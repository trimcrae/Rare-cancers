## Abstract
The limb anatomy displays well‐defined dorsal and ventral compartments, housing extensor, and flexor muscles, which play a crucial role in facilitating limb locomotion and manipulation. Despite its importance, the study of limb dorsoventral patterning has been relatively neglected compared to the other two axes leaving many crucial questions about the genes and developmental processes implicated unanswered. This review offers a thorough overview of the current understanding of limb dorsoventral patterning, synthesizing classical literature with recent research. It covers the specification of dorsal fate in the limb mesoderm and its subsequent translation into dorsal morphologies—a process directed by the transcription factor Lmx1b. We also discuss the potential role of dorsoventral patterning in the evolution of paired appendages and delve into the involvement of LMX1B in Nail‐Patella syndrome, discussing the molecular and genetic aspects underlying this condition. Finally, the potential role of dorsoventral polarity in digit tip regeneration, a prominent instance of multi‐tissue regeneration in mammals is also considered. We anticipate that this review will renew interest in a process that is critical to limb function and evolutionary adaptations but has nonetheless been overlooked.

## Key Findings

We offer a comprehensive overview of DV patterning during limb development, integrating classical works with new knowledge.
We review the mechanisms leading to activation of Lmx1b, the dorsal limb determinant, its targets and mode of action.
We cover LMX1B implication in the Nail‐Patella Syndrome.
We identify crucial gaps in our current understanding of DV patterning, unraveling the complexities of limb DV patterning and encouraging further research.

The skin and subcutaneous tissue on the dorsal and ventral surfaces differ in thickness, texture, and sensitivity to touch. Furthermore, the epidermal derivatives differ between the dorsal (e.g., nail) and ventral (e.g., fingerprints/volar pads) surfaces. These asymmetries may seem minor, but they can have dramatic functional consequences, such as affecting grip strength, sensitivity to pressure, and overall dexterity of the limb. Hence, optimal limb function necessitates the correct DV polarity, both internally and on the surface.

## ESTABLISHMENT OF DORSOVENTRAL POLARITY IN LIMB DEVELOPMENT
Grafting experiments in chick embryos showed that DV polarity is established very early in the limb field before the limb bud emerges. , This was later confirmed with the discovery of the DV regulatory genes. Through very elegant studies in chick and mouse, the current model for limb DV patterning, which centers on Wnt7a, En1, and Lmx1b, was devised (Figure  ). En1 functions in the ventral ectoderm to restrict the expression of Wnt7a to the dorsal ectoderm. , Wnt7a, emanating from the dorsal ectoderm, induces Lmx1b expression in the underlying mesoderm. , , , , , In both chick and mouse, the onset of Wnt7a in the ectoderm and that of Lmx1b in the mesoderm occur simultaneously. , 
Lmx1b is transcribed precisely in the limb progenitors located in the dorsal half of the bud forming a crisp ventral mesodermal boundary (Figure  ). Cells that express Lmx1b adopt a dorsal fate leading to the development of dorsal morphologies of skeletal elements (bones, tendons, and muscles), and skin adnexa (glands, hair, nails/claws). Therefore, Lmx1b is the primary patterning factor within the mesoderm shaping and organizing the dorsal side of the distal limb (zeugopod and autopod), critical for limb function (Box  ). Because no ventral mesodermal specific marker has been found, and because both the Wnt7a knockout and the Wnt7a;En1 double knockout display similar double‐ventral phenotypes, corresponding to the absence of Lmx1b, the ventral limb fate is considered the default state. ,

## 
LMX1B EXPRESSION PATTERN DURING LIMB DEVELOPMENT
As mentioned, Lmx1b expression is detected in the presumptive limb mesoderm before the emergence of the limb bud both in chick and in mouse. , , During mouse limb development it is first detected in the lateral plate mesoderm at the presumptive forelimb level at E8.5 and slightly later in the hindlimb. , In the emerging limb bud, Lmx1b is expressed throughout most of the limb bud mesoderm becoming restricted precisely to the dorsal half during E9.5‐E10. , The initial broad expression of Lmx1b remains somewhat enigmatic, as fate mapping experiments showed that the number of cells initiating Lmx1b expression and subsequently downregulating it is very low. Further experiments are needed to clarify if this result is due to late labeling timing or reflects some delayed incorporation of ventral (non‐expressing Lmx1b) cells to the nascent bud, concurrent with the process of compaction and distal positioning of the mature AER.
The expression of Lmx1b in the undifferentiated dorsal mesoderm looks homogeneous and is generally considered binary, either expressed or not, suggesting that once a threshold level of the dorsal ectodermal signal is attained, Lmx1b is induced (Figure  ). , Nevertheless, the use of the HCR (RNA‐fluorescence in situ hybridization with amplification by hybridization chain reaction), which is more sensitive, suggests some decrease in expression near the DV boundary (Figure  ’). It is important to note that not all cells within the Lmx1b positive dorsal domain express Lmx1b. The muscle precursors and nerves that migrate into the dorsal compartment are Lmx1b negative suggesting that Lmx1b expression may be restricted to the lateral plate lineage. 

 Variations in the level of Lmx1b expression along the PD axis become evident as development progresses (by E11.5; Figure  ). As proximal tissues differentiate, higher expression levels are observed toward the distal end of the limb bud. 
Lmx1b expression wanes first from the chondrogenic condensations while remaining for longer in the developing tendons, ligaments, and dorsal joint‐associated tissues. , As the digit condensations differentiate in the hand plate, Lmx1b concentrates in the developing digits while diminishing in the interdigits (Box  and Figure  ). Differences in the level of expression along the anterior–posterior axis have also been reported, with a higher level in the anterior mesoderm. Over time, Lmx1b expression becomes more dorsally restricted, remaining on the dorsal side of the digits during late fetal stages, and eventually localizing to the dermis beneath the nail and the proximal nail fold, where it persists throughout adulthood (Figure  ). , , , The persistent expression of Lmx1b is reminiscent of that exhibited by other limb determinants, such as Hox factors, which contribute to positional memory. 

While other coding genes show some DV asymmetry in their expression, none have been reported to exhibit a distinct dorsal or ventral pattern of expression to the extent observed in Lmx1b. However, it is worth noting that two long non‐coding RNAs associated with Lmx1b also display similar dorsal‐restricted patterns of expression. , ,

## 
LMX1B FUNCTION DURING LIMB DEVELOPMENT
In the absence of Lmx1b (Lmx1b knockout), the limbs that develop are double‐ventral at zeugopod and autopod level. Limbs are termed “double‐ventral” or “bi‐ventral” when the dorsal half has been converted into a mirror image of the ventral side. At the skeletal level, dorsal structures such as the patella are absent while ventral structures such as the ventral sesamoid bones are duplicated (Figure  ). Additionally, the tendons and muscles in the dorsal compartment display a symmetrical configuration, mirroring their counterparts in the ventral region (Figure  ). Lmx1b‐null double‐ventral limbs also display loss of dorsal epidermal derivatives, namely hair, and nails, while ventral ectodermal derivatives such as pads and eccrine glands develop on the dorsal side. , 

 Reciprocally, GOF of Lmx1b in the ventral limb mesoderm results in double‐dorsal distal limbs. For instance, retroviral misexpression of Lmx1b in the chick ventral limb mesoderm leads to the development of limbs with bi‐dorsal feather patterns and conversion of ventral elements into their dorsal counterparts. , Similarly, misexpression of Lmx1b in the mouse ventral mesoderm due to the activation of Wnt7a in the ventral ectoderm secondary to the loss of En1, leads to a double‐dorsal limb phenotype of the distal limb. , This phenotype in mouse is characterized by circumferential/conical nails/claws, volar hair, and absence of digit pads.
Together, these findings establish Lmx1b as the key determinant of dorsal limb development, unequivocally demonstrating that it is both necessary and sufficient to specify dorsal morphologies. , , However, the precise mechanism, including the molecular pathways and downstream effectors, by which Lmx1b orchestrates asymmetrical limb development remains unknown. Importantly, in the limb, Lmx1b may have other roles in addition to DV patterning, as suggested by some skeletal abnormalities in Lmx1b‐null mice not clearly related to DV patterning such as the hypoplasia of the scapula and ilium and the absence or underdevelopment of the ulna (Figure  ).

## 
LMX1B IS THE SELECTOR GENE FOR THE DORSAL COMPARTMENT
As mentioned, the domain of expression of Lmx1b in the limb bud is characterized by a sharp boundary that extends along the DV interface. This boundary doesn't correspond to any anatomical structure, and even elements that cross it, like cartilage and joint anlagen, only express Lmx1b in the part included in the dorsal Lmx1b domain. Several fate mapping approaches showed that the dorsal and ventral domains were composed of distinct lineages in correlation with Lmx1b expression. , , This is consistent with the classical concept of developmental compartment. Therefore, Lmx1b can be considered the selector gene that defines and maintains a dorsal mesenchymal compartment, probably controlling differential cell adhesive properties between dorsal Lmx1b‐expressing and ventral Lmx1b non‐expressing mesodermal cells. This notion is supported by the non‐random distribution of Lmx1b mutant cells, excluded from dorsal tissues, in the limbs of chimeric mice made of mixtures of wild‐type and mutant cells. 

The ability of Lmx1b‐expressing cells to segregate from non‐Lmx1b‐expressing cells was also studied in the experimental model of chick recombinant limbs (RLs), which are limb buds experimentally assembled by recombining a mesodermal core with an ectodermal cover. , To explore patterning mechanisms, the limb bud mesoderm can be isolated, dissociated to single cell level and randomly reaggregated before encased in an ectodermal jacket. The analysis of the DV axis in such RLs showed that the expression domains of Wnt7a, Fgf8, and En1 in the limb ectoderm, once established, were stable and autonomous, or at least not dependent upon organized signals from the subjacent mesoderm. In these RL, the initial mixture of cells expressing and non‐expressing Lmx1b evolves to a normal DV pattern in the growing distal mesoderm. Two possible explanations can account for this result: the sorting out of cells expressing and not expressing Lmx1b or the accommodation of Lmx1b expression to the new environment. The latter seems more likely, as no major movement of cells is observed. The fact that this adjustment is only observed in the distal region confirms a differential stability of Lmx1b expression along the PD axis. This notion was previously proposed based on the persistence of Lmx1b expression in the proximal region of Wnt7a mutants or after the removal of the dorsal ectoderm or 180° ectodermal rotations in chick. , , The mechanism responsible for the more labile distal Lmx1b transcription remains unknown. , ,

## 
LMX1B TRANSCRIPTIONAL REGULATION
Wnt7a is the best known regulator of Lmx1b transcription. , , , However, Wnt7a null mutants only lose Lmx1b expression in the anterior autopod indicating that Wnt7a is not necessary for Lmx1b activation and only required for later distal Lmx1b expression. , Curiously, Wnt7a also appears insufficient to induce Lmx1b in the proximal mesoderm as even though Wnt7a is ectopically activated across the entire ventral ectoderm of En1‐null mutants, ectopic ventral Lmx1b activation exclusively occurs distally. , 

Wnt7a, although initially viewed as a “non‐canonical” Wnt, was later shown to signal via the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. , By mouse genetics, it was shown that Dkk1, a Wnt antagonist that binds Lrp6 interfering with signal stabilization, can modulate the function of Wnt7a. , In line with this, conditional loss off function (LOF) of β‐catenin in the limb mesoderm using the Prrx1‐Cre line resulted in a reduction of Lmx1b expression in the dorsal mesoderm while GOF of β‐catenin with the same Cre line led to expansion of Lmx1b expression toward the ventral mesoderm although, curiously, without reaching the ventral ectoderm. The normal expression of both Wnt7a and En1 in these limbs was consistent with Lmx1b responding to other Wnt‐βcatenin ligands, not just to Wnt7a. This is further supported by the differences in the timing and domain of Lmx1b's response following manipulations of Lrp6 (late and distal) or βcatenin (early and proximal). 

Recently, it has been shown that Wnt7b, which is expressed in the dorsal and also in the ventral ectoderm, at least at certain stages, also contributes to Lmx1b regulation. , , , Wnt7a and Wnt7b specifically require interaction with the receptor co‐factors Gpr124 and Reck to bind the frizzled receptors. 

In addition to regulation by Wnt signals, Lmx1b transcription in the limb is also subject to autoregulation through two limb‐specific enhancers, termed LARM1 and LARM2 (Lmx1b associated regulatory module 1 and 2) (Figure  ). 
LARM1 and LARM2 are located 60 Kb and 66 Kb upstream of Lmx1b and were identified in a ChIPseq for Lmx1b binding sites. , These LARM enhancers contain several canonical Lmx1b binding sites that are necessary for their activity as shown in enhancer reporter assays in chick. Based on these observations, LARM1 and LARM2 are considered to mediate a positive Lmx1b autoregulatory loop (Figure  ). Positive autoregulation may account for the stabilization, amplification, and fine‐tuning of Lmx1b's own transcription providing a mechanism for its autonomous maintenance of expression. , , With the LARM sequences mediating autoregulation, the presumed Lmx1b inducer would only be necessary transiently to initiate transcription. However, during late stages, this mechanism appears insufficient, at least in the anterior autopod, as Wnt7a is required for Lmx1b maintenance of expression. 

 The enhancer activity and limb‐specificity of the LARMs are demonstrated by their CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated deletion. 
ΔLARM1/2 mutants replicate the double‐ventral limb phenotype of Lmx1b‐null mice with volar‐like pads on both surfaces and no nails while lacking other pleiotropic tissue/organ defects related to Lmx1b (Figure  ). Remarkably, the individual deletion of each enhancer resulted in the double‐ventral phenotype limited to the posterior half of the autopod in ΔLARM1 mutants and to the anterior half in ΔLARM2 mutants, revealing an intriguing anterior–posterior spatial modularity. Initially, these enhancers function cooperatively and redundantly, as evidenced by the absence of phenotypic changes in the stylopod and zeugopod of individual ΔLARM mutants. However, as development advances, they transition to a modular mode of action, with each enhancer predominantly active in a distinct location, LARM2 in the anterior and LARM1 in the posterior autopod. The similarity in phenotype between LARM2 and Wnt7a mutants is noteworthy, raising the intriguing possibility that Wnt7a specifically functions through LARM2. , , 

The spatially restricted activity of the LARM sequences along the AP axis suggests their responsiveness to positional cues in that axis underscoring an intriguing linkage between the DV and AP axes. The higher expression level of Lmx1b in the anterior mesoderm and the AP gradient of severity observed in human pathology , further underscore the connection between the DV and AP axes. , The ulna hypoplasia, while possibly indicative of Lmx1b's involvement in osteogenesis (see below), also reflects an inherent AP bias. Additionally, it has been reported that overexpression of Lmx1b may, in some cases, produce anterior ectopic activation of Shh, the major regulator of limb AP patterning. Thus, in view of this information, it is clear that the relationship between the DV axis and the AP axis merits an in‐depth study.
In the initial studies that identified Lmx1b, positive autoregulation was considered but rejected because retroviral misexpression of Lmx1b in the ventral mesoderm did not induce endogenous Lmx1b. The contrast between the lack of induction of endogenous Lmx1b in the RCAS‐Lmx1b overexpression experiments and the induction of LARM activity by LMX1B in enhancer reporter assays remains undetermined. , One potential explanation is that the endogenous Lmx1b regulatory sequences and/or promoter are either not accessible or silenced in ventral cells preventing response to Lmx1b. This is consistent with the suggested presence of a silencer sequence within the LARM1 enhancer that could suppress or inhibit LARM activity, leading to the repression of Lmx1b expression in specific contexts such as the ventral mesoderm (Figure  ). Since the silencer activity depends on the Lmx1b binding site it contains, it is plausible to consider that the hypothetical repressor would bind to this site to regulate its activity, competing with the activator Lmx1b in dorsal cells. In the endogenous chromatin configuration of ventral cells, the repressor would resist displacement by Lmx1b, unlike in enhancer reporter assays which do not recapitulate the endogenous situation.
Indeed, the presence of an Lmx1b repressor in the ventral mesoderm (Figure  ) , would explain several observations: (i) the lack of activation of endogenous Lmx1b in the ventral mesoderm by retroviral Lmx1b‐expression, as just discussed; (ii) the failure of overexpressed β‐catenin to ectopically induce Lmx1b in the ventral‐most mesoderm; (iii) the loss of Lmx1b positive cells from the now “ventral” mesoderm in dissociated and reaggregated RLs, and (iv) the time delay between ectopic activation of Wnt7a in the ventral ectoderm and the activation of Lmx1b in the ventral mesoderm in En1 mutants. Because of the lack of genes with only ventral expression, the repressor transcription factor is anticipated to be expressed throughout the entire mesoderm or, alternatively, emanate from the ventral ectoderm possible under En1 regulation. It is tempting to propose that En1 itself could be the factor, given that it has been shown to be secreted although, obviously, this explanation is not valid for the En1 mutant. , , , An alternative possibility is that dorsal and ventral cells, having distinct developmental origins, may exhibit different competencies in responding to Wnt7a. Further investigation using advanced “omic” techniques has the potential to yield crucial insights into these issues.

## DORSOVENTRAL PATTERNING POSSIBLE IMPLICATION IN THE EVOLUTION OF PAIRED APPENDAGES
Unlike Lmx1b‐null mice, which die perinatally because of systemic defects, ΔLARM1/2 mutants survive, as expected from limb‐specific enhancers, providing the opportunity to observe, for the first time, the morphology and function of fully developed double‐ventral limbs. The double‐ventral limbs of ΔLARM1/2 mutants fail to support locomotion, and they are even unable to lift the body weight. 

This finding clearly highlights the importance of limb DV patterning for stance and locomotion. It stresses the notion that the gradual development of DV polarity was a necessary step in the transition from fins to appendages capable of supporting body weight, as observed in Tiktaalik. In support of this view, it has been recently shown that the adaptation of the fin prior to the origin of the digits involved a trend toward increased asymmetry between dorsal and ventral hemitrichia. Thus, considering the deep roots of regulatory networks, , , , it seems worth to investigate whether the elaboration of DV pattern, providing increasingly adaptive differentiation between dorsal and ventral sides, was a critical adaptation of appendage evolution.

## LMX1B DOWNSTREAM TARGETS
To fully understand the functional significance of Lmx1b in limb development, it is crucial to identify and examine its targets, the specific genes, and molecular pathways that it regulates. Yet, uncovering these precise targets has proven surprisingly elusive, likely due to the complexity of the genetic interactions Lmx1b is involved in.
Several studies used microarrays to detect gene expression differences between wild‐type and Lmx1b‐null limb buds which could include both direct targets and indirectly affected genes (Table  ). , , The comparison of WT and Lmx1b mutant E11.5 whole limb buds using microarray analysis surprisingly failed to identify significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). However, when the analysis focused on the proximal limb, hundreds of DEGs were identified suggesting that Lmx1b may control DV identity through distinct molecular mechanisms in the proximal and distal limb. The majority of DEGs were downregulated in the absence of Lmx1b, indicating its role as a transcriptional activator. A subsequent analysis performed at E13.5, identified another set of potential target genes that exhibited DV asymmetric expression patterns dependent on Lmx1b. A third comprehensive microarray study spanning from E11.5 to E13.5 limbs identified a set of 54 genes with persistent differential expression throughout all stages. Altogether, these studies converged in identifying Lmx1b as a regulator of genes associated with joint/skeletal development (Gdf5, Acan, Ibsp, Panx3, Matn1, Col9a3, and Col11a2), with connective tissue and extracellular matrix organization (Kera, Lum, and Dcn), and with nerve guidance (Ntn1, Epha7, and Ebf2), among others. However, overall the results were limited and the list of Lmx1b target genes identified so far is incomplete (Table  ). , , It is important to note that despite the hypothesis that Lmx1b controls differential cell–cell adhesion, the microarray studies did not identify genes involved in this process. Thus, cell adhesion might not be the dominant process underlying sorting between dorsal and ventral cells.
 The crucial question is whether Lmx1b has a direct influence on the transcription of these target genes, or if its role is primarily to regulate the development of the structures where these genes are expressed. Thus, in the absence of Lmx1b, genes that are expressed primarily or exclusively in ventral structures, such as Eya1 or Six2 in ventral tendons, exhibit an ectopic duplication of their expression domain on the dorsal side, which may result from the conversion of dorsal limb structures to ventral rather than from direct regulation by Lmx1b. More recently, a ChIP‐seq for Lmx1b in E12.5 limbs provided valuable information on regulatory elements bound by Lmx1b, aiding in the identification of its direct targets. Next, we will briefly delve into the available information on the Lmx1b direct targets in musculoskeletal development and in axon guidance.
8.1

## Lmx1b function in the development of the musculoskeletal system
As mentioned, some of the skeletal abnormalities in Lmx1b‐null mutants, such as the hypoplastic or absent ulna, cannot be attributed to DV patterning defects. It was suggested that Lmx1b may have a role in early bone morphogenesis. Consistent with this, several genes associated with ossification such as Ibsp and Panx3, or with skeletal development, such as Matn1, Col9a3, or Col11a2
 were identified, in microarray analyses, as upregulated in the presence of Lmx1b. Given that only a subset of these DEGs possess Lmx1b‐bound regulatory elements, it can be inferred that many of them are indirectly affected by the absence of Lmx1b.
In contrast with this positive effect of Lmx1b in osteogenesis, Lmx1b appears to negatively regulate calvarial osteoblast differentiation. For instance, an anti‐osteogenic role for Lmx1b has been demonstrated in the apical mesenchyme of the mouse fetal calvaria. In the absence of Lmx1b, the bony plates fuse causing craniosynostosis and a smaller malformed cranial vault. Also, Lmx1b has been shown to negatively regulate osteoblast differentiation and function in culture using mouse osteoblast precursor cells derived from the calvaria. This function is mediated by the inhibition of Runx2, a critical transcription factor in bone formation. Interestingly, Lmx1b also inhibits osteoclast differentiation in bone marrow‐derived cells. Thus, the precise roles of Lmx1b in bone development and homeostasis, as well as whether it functions differently in membranous and endochondral ossification, are currently unclear.
In addition to its role in bone development, Lmx1b function during joint and tendon formation has also been studied. , During limb development, Lmx1b is expressed in dorsal tendons and ligaments, as well as in the dorsal region of joints overlapping with the expression of tissue‐specific markers such as Scx and Gdf5 which, however, exhibit no DV differences in their expression patterns. Interestingly, Gdf5 possesses a regulatory region bound by Lmx1b whose activity in the dorsal aspect of the joints depends on Lmx1b. , Other tissue‐specific markers, like the tendon‐associated genes Six1 and Six2 and the join‐associated gene sFrp2, do show DV asymmetric patterns of expression that become symmetric in the absence of Lmx1b. However, among these genes, the ChIP‐seq dataset only supports direct regulation for Six2 through associated Lmx1b‐bound elements. 

Lmx1b also controls the organization and shape of the dorsal musculature despite not being expressed in muscle. Muscle progenitors that enter the limb derive from somites and are not prespecified to a specific pattern. , As these muscle progenitors migrate into the dorsal connective tissues, they remain Lmx1b‐negative, though they become invested in Lmx1b‐positive epimysium. , Interestingly, there is a significant size difference between the dorsal and ventral muscle masses with the dorsal muscle mass being smaller. It has been suggested that this asymmetry depends on Lmx1b negatively regulating Sim2, in a cell non‐autonomous manner, leading to its predominant expression in the ventral muscle masses. Sim2 controls the initiation of the myogenic program by repressing MyoD thus allowing the expansion of the progenitor pool before differentiation and resulting in the size asymmetry of dorsal and ventral muscular masses. Therefore, Lmx1b' role in muscle patterning and development is indirect, likely mediated by the connective tissue.

8.2

## Lmx1b function in axon guidance
During development, the axons of spinal motor neurons navigate toward the base of the limb where they segregate into a dorsal and a ventral nerve branch that invade the dorsal and ventral compartment, respectively. This process involves the selection of dorsal or ventral trajectories to establish precise patterns of nerve‐muscle connectivity, which are regulated by a complex set of guidance cues. , Specific ephrin:Eph signaling guides lateral and medial motor axons of the lateral motor column (LMC) into the limb dorsal or ventral compartments, respectively. The axons of medial LMC neurons are directed into the ventral limb mesoderm by a repellant signaling system that involves axonal EphB receptors and ephrin‐B ligands in the dorsal mesoderm. Conversely, the axons of lateral LMC neurons are guided into the dorsal limb mesoderm by the repulsion between axonal EphA receptors and ephrin‐A ligands in the ventral mesoderm. This core ephrin:Eph signaling system is modulated by other guidance systems such as semaphorins, , cadherins, Netrin1:Unc5c/Dcc, Paxillin, and Gdnf:Gfrα1/c‐Ret. , 

The crucial role of Lmx1b in axon guidance is evidenced by the atypical pathways neurons take in Lmx1b mutant limbs with random migration of lateral LMC neurons into the ventral or dorsal limb mesoderm. Lmx1b role must rely on establishing differential DV expression of positional cues/guide molecules in the limb mesoderm. , , , Although loss of Epha4 and gain of EphrinA5 ligand in the dorsal mesoderm has been observed in Lmx1b‐null mutants, the microarray analyses did not reveal clear distribution changes in Lmx1b mutant limbs. In the microarray analyses, the following genes related to axonal guidance were upregulated in Lmx1b mutants: Calb2, Cbln1, Cbln2, Cbln4, Ntn1, Epha7, Epha4, Nrp2, and Ebf2 while Rfx4 was downregulated. Of these, Cbln1, Cbln4, Epha7, Nrp2, Ntn1, Rfx4 have ChIP‐seq assigned peaks and can be considered direct Lmx1b targets.
In conclusion, our understanding of how Lmx1b acts shaping the limb along the DV axis is strikingly limited. Additional Lmx1b ChIP‐seq analyses covering a broader range of developmental stages, including earlier ones, along with comparisons of transcriptional profiles between dorsal and ventral cells and functional studies of putative target genes would be necessary to uncover the precise molecular mechanisms by which Lmx1b exerts its function.

## LMX1B IN HUMAN PATHOLOGY
In humans, pathologic variations in the LMX1B gene are responsible for the autosomal dominant Nail‐Patella Syndrome (NPS; OMIM #161200; ORPHA:2614), also known as hereditary osteo‐onychodysplasia (HOOD syndrome) or Fong disease. , , , NPS is a rare disease with an estimated incidence of 1 in 50,000 live births and is predominantly inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with haploinsufficiency as the underlying mechanism. The disorder is considered fully penetrant but has variable expressivity among patients even within families. Most NPS patients present with fingernail (98%) and patellar hypoplasia (80%). Joint abnormalities and contractures of the elbow and limb girdles are also frequent (70%). Conical, bony processes that project posteriolaterally from the pelvis, called iliac horns, are radiologic features that are considered pathognomonic of NPS (70%–80%). , , Other pleiotropic manifestations such as nephropathy (40%), glaucoma (10%–20%), and brain‐related pathology (epilepsy, Parkinson disease, and schizophrenia) can also be present. , , , The phenotypic features observed in NPS patients resemble those of homozygous Lmx1b mutant mice but are much milder. 

A significant percentage (10%) of patients with NPS have no changes in the LMX1B coding region suggesting the involvement of other genes or regulatory regions/elements. Interestingly, some NPS patient families that lacked an LMX1B mutation were found to have variant LARM sequences, , two of them carrying small deletions in heterozygosis encompassing LARM2 and the other with several rare single nucleotide variations (SNVs) in and nearby LARM2 in homozygosity. In full agreement with the limb‐specificity of the LARMs sequences, the NPS defects in these two cases were restricted to the limbs with no other Lmx1b‐related organ manifestations. These cases are good examples of enhanceropathies, in which the disruption of a regulatory element leads to disease. It is worth mentioning here that patients with isolated organ‐specific LMX1B‐related manifestations (nephropathy, glaucoma, etc.) without the typical features of the NPS, could also harbor pathologic variations in the corresponding organ‐specific regulatory elements that have yet to be identified.
There are about 200 pathogenic mutations in the LMX1B gene reported within the Human Gene Mutation Database. The majority of them disrupt the functional domains (the LIM or the HD) but no clear genotype–phenotype correlation has been demonstrated. , , , , Initially, a dominant‐negative effect of LMX1B mutations was considered. However, subsequent functional analyses and the identification of families with the disease‐carrying deletions of the entire LMX1B gene in heterozygosity confirmed the role of haploinsufficiency. , Haploinsufficiency refers to the situation where having only one functional copy of a gene results in an abnormal phenotype; then, LMX1B can be classified as a haploinsufficient gene in humans (autosomal‐dominant) but not in mice (autosomal‐recessive), a scenario that is not uncommon. It is assumed that human LMX1B homozygous LOF mutations are lethal at early developmental time, as this genotype has not yet been identified.
Because a specific threshold level of LMX1B activity is essential for correct function, in cases of hypomorphic alleles, the disease can manifest only in homozygous patients if the activity of the hypomorphic proteins from both alleles does not reach the required threshold. For instance, an autosomal recessive form of NPS has been reported with a missense mutation that involves a change of Leucine to Phenylalanine (c.268C > T) in a highly conserved amino acid of the LIM‐A domain. , The parents were heterozygotes for this mutation but did not display any features of NPS suggesting that this variation reduced LMX1B function, but not to a level sufficient to produce a phenotype. Another example pertains to the patient homozygous for the rare SNVs in LARM2 referred to above which can be inferred to result in a reduction of function below the necessary threshold in homozygosity.

## DORSOVENTRAL POLARITY IN REGENERATION
Mammals are known to possess a rather limited capacity for regeneration though a notable exception lies in their ability to regenerate digit tips following amputation. This is observed in several rodents and primates, including human children, who can successfully regenerate the tips of amputated digits. , The regenerative response is mediated by a blastema which is a group of undifferentiated cells that transiently forms at the tip of the stump and proliferates and differentiates to form the missing tissues. The regeneration of the digit tip has been linked to the nail organ as amputations that do not preserve the nail matrix do not regenerate. The influence of the nail in digit tip regeneration has been attributed to Wnt signaling emanating from the distal nail matrix that promotes nail differentiation (reviewed in ). The nail/claw is a dorsal ectodermal appendage whose formation depends on Lmx1b. , , 

Recently, the ΔLARM1/2 mutants, which exhibit double‐ventral digits and lack nails, have permitted a more direct test of the nail's role in digit tip regeneration. The Lmx1b‐null mice would also have been an excellent model for this test, but these mutants die perinatally due to the associated systemic defects. Interestingly, ΔLARM1/2 mutants, both in adult and neonatal stages, show no ability to regenerate their digit tips after amputation underscoring the necessity of the nail and indirectly implying Lmx1b involvement in mediating the formation of the nail apparatus (Figure  ).
 The lack of regeneration capacity in LARM mutants cannot be attributed to the lack of polarity, as has been suggested in some amphibian species , , , , , since the mouse mutant Del (27), which also lacks DV polarity due to the specific loss of En1 in the limbs, does regenerate. , The fact that double‐ventral digits without nails (ΔLARM1/2) do not regenerate while double‐dorsal digits with circumferential nails (Del (27)) do, adds support to the indirect influence of Lmx1b on regeneration through its control over nail formation (Figure  ). Indeed, DV patterning may not be redeployed in digit tip regeneration, as the conditional deletion of Lmx1b prior to the amputation has no impact on the DV pattern of the regeneration. 

Lmx1b may have more direct functions in digit tip regeneration, separate from providing DV information, through its expression in the nail dermis and the blastema (Figure ). , , The nail dermis is an important source of blastema cells. , The transcriptional signature unique to the nail dermis includes Lmx1b
 , and Msx1, which is also required for digit tip regeneration and seems to be downstream of Lmx1b. , , Curiously, ablation of the Lmx1b expressing cells of the nail dermis, using the diphtheria toxin, significantly impaired blastema formation while removal of Lmx1b in these cells did not produce any significant defect in digit tip regeneration. These observations suggest that the main function of Lmx1b may be the generation of the nail dermis rather than regulating nail dermis cell functions during regeneration. This conclusion is supported by the small dorsal dermis that forms in the absence of Lmx1b in ΔLARM1/2 mutants. 

The expression of Lmx1b in the blastema, which occurs at a very low level and without positional specificity (Figure ), also suggests a potential direct function of Lmx1b in regeneration distinct from providing DV information. Although the dorsal dermis significantly contributes to the blastema, its contribution is limited to its dorsal part with no cell movements observed suggesting that cells in the ventral blastema de novo activate expression of Lmx1b. It is worth mentioning that, since Msx1 is not expressed in the blastema, the conditional deletion of Lmx1b in the nail dermis using the Msx1Cre line would not prevent de novo Lmx1b expression in the blastema cells that do not derive from Msx1 expressing cells, and thus it does not completely rule out a putative function for Lmx1b in the blastema. In support of this view, the small blastema that forms in the absence of Lmx1b (ΔLARM1/2 mutant) fails to transition into a regenerative state and instead remains in an inflammatory state that cannot undergo regeneration (Figure  ).
 To confirm a direct Lmx1b role, it would be necessary to disentangle it from the nail. This can be achieved by forcing Lmx1b in the ΔLARM1/2 model or by forcing nail signals in a digit without nails.

## CONCLUSIONS
It is well established that Lmx1b has a pivotal function in DV limb patterning. Surprisingly, however, it has been difficult to identify both the upstream regulators and the downstream gene regulatory network controlling DV polarity. A current challenge is understanding the mechanisms that control the precise activation of Lmx1b in dorsal cells breaking the DV symmetry. Another challenge is understanding how Lmx1b expression is interpreted to orchestrate the organization of limb elements into specific dorsal morphologies. We are currently in an ideal position to address these issues, thanks to recent advancements and high‐throughput omics technologies. Closing this knowledge gap will provide new insights on the mechanisms underlying the generation of specific shapes as well as the evolution of appendages.

## The Lmx1b Gene and its Protein
The human LMX1B (LIM homeobox transcription factor 1 beta) gene is located at chromosome 9q33.3 and contains 8 exons (top). The mouse and chicken Lmx1b genes are located on chromosome 2 and 17 respectively and retain similar structure as the human LMX1B gene. Initially, only one Lmx1 gene was identified in chick , although more recent annotations have identified two homologs as in the other species (cLmx1a ortholog in chromosome 8 and cLmx1b in chromosome 17; GRCg6a/galGal6).

Lmx1b codes for a member of the LIM‐domain homeodomain (LIM‐HD) family of transcription factors, which is characterized by the presence of two LIM domains and one homeodomain (HD) (bottom). Lmx1b includes two N‐terminal LIM domains (LIM‐A in exon 2–3 and LIM‐B in exon 3), which are double zinc fingers involved in protein–protein interactions, a HD (in exons 4–6), which is required for DNA binding and transcriptional regulation, and a C‐terminal glutamine/serine‐rich domain, which may be involved in protein–protein interactions and transcriptional activation. , ,

## The Pleiotropic Nature of Lmx1b Function

Lmx1b is expressed in multiple sites during embryonic development including the midbrain and rostral hindbrain, the isthmus and the prechordal plate rostral head mesenchyme, foregut endoderm, anterior segment of the eye, otic placode and vesicle, mammary glands, mesonephros and dorsal limb bud mesoderm. , , , , , , , , The expression of Lmx1b during development is complex, dynamic, and varies depending on the tissue and developmental stage. The diverse sites of Lmx1b expression suggest its involvement in various tissue and organ patterning and differentiation processes. Correspondingly, Lmx1b knockout mice display a spectrum of severe developmental abnormalities including significant calvarial defects, absence of the cerebellum and posterior hindbrain, kidney and ocular malformations, and double‐ventral limbs , (see text and Figure  ). Due to these malformations, homozygous Lmx1b mutants die shortly after birth although the specific cause of death remains unknown.
