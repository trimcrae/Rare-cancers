## Abstract
To advance the development of fusion protein–targeting therapeutics, it is crucial to understand how patients with fusion oncoproteins have been treated and what small molecules have been studied. To fill this gap, we developed FusionPub, a knowledgebase of the therapeutic landscape of human fusion genes. We searched PubMed abstracts for 107K human fusion genes with 14K drugs. We also searched PubMed abstracts for 18K human fusion proteins, considering all gene synonyms of individual partner genes with 14K drugs. After manual curation, we found 17 342 records from 9623 PubMed abstracts. For these fusion genes and drugs, we provide a summary sentence of their relationship using the generative AI, details of the drugs, clinical trial records, common drug records from patient treatment history, etc. FusionPub will be a valuable and unique resource providing knowledge of the development of fusion gene targeting therapeutics.
Keywords: fusion gene, drug, therapeutics, PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov

## Introduction
Gene fusion is one of the hallmarks of the cancer genome, resulting from chromosomal rearrangement initiated by DNA double-strand breakage. Once the fusion gene is formed, it can be transcribed to the fusion mRNA. If the open reading frame of the fusion mRNA is in-frame, then this one can be translated to fusion protein and form a 3D structure to be functional. Fusion genes/proteins are used as the targets of the therapeutics of cancer patients. Specifically, kinase inhibitors are the most frequently treated drugs for driver fusion gene–positive patients [ , ]. For example, BCR::ABL1 is the first designed drug targeting the expressed structural variants between chromosomes 9 and 22. Since the first report of the excessive white blood cells of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) in 1845, multiple facts [i.e. chromosomal abnormality, reciprocal translocation, and fusion gene (BCR::ABL1 fusion mRNA)] were identified following the development of molecular biology study technologies (i.e. high-resolution karyotyping, chromosome banding technology, and sequencing technology) with several decades. In 1996, STI571, a kinase domain inhibiting small molecule targeting BCR::ABL1, was identified. Finally, in 2001, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Gleevec for CML treatments. BCR::ABL1 is a marker of CML (20% of adult leukemia) and is also expressed in a minority of acute myelogenous leukemia (AML, 80% of adult leukemia) [ ]. Since the approval of imatinib in 2001, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several additional tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting the BCR-ABL1 fusion protein for treating CML. These include second-generation TKIs such as dasatinib, nilotinib, and bosutinib, as well as the third-generation TKI ponatinib. In October 2024, the FDA approved asciminib (Scemblix) for treating adults with newly diagnosed Philadelphia chromosome-positive chronic myeloid leukemia (Ph + CML) in the chronic phase. Unlike traditional inhibitors, asciminib is an allosteric BCR::ABL1 inhibitor, targeting the myristoyl pocket to disrupt fusion protein activity through a unique mechanism (FDA.gov, 2024).

EML4::ALK was identified in 2007 in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, and the targeting kinase inhibitor, crizotinib, was approved to treat EML4::ALK-positive patients in 2011. After crizotinib, the FDA has approved six additional ALK inhibitors for EML4-ALK-positive NSCLC, such as ceritinib in 2014, alectinib in 2015, brigatinib in 2017, lorlatinib in 2018, and repotrectinib in 2023. In April 2024, the FDA approved alectinib (Alecensa) as an adjuvant therapy for patients with ALK-positive NSCLC following tumor resection. As a second-generation ALK inhibitor, alectinib effectively targets the EML4-ALK fusion protein, providing a treatment option specifically for patients with this genetic alteration in 2024.
Tropomyosin-related kinase (TRK) proteins—TRKA, TRKB, and TRKC—are encoded by the NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 genes, respectively [ , ]. When activated by neurotrophins, these proteins undergo dimerization, triggering signaling pathways that promote cell growth and differentiation. NTRK fusion proteins result in continuous activation and uncontrolled cellular proliferation. Though rare, NTRK fusions have been identified across various cancers, with higher occurrences in rare cancers like secretory carcinoma of the breast and salivary gland and in pediatric conditions such as infantile fibrosarcoma [ ]. Larotrectinib and entrectinib are targeted inhibitors for TRK proteins, with entrectinib also showing activity against ROS1 and ALK (FDA.gov, 2018, 2019). Both drugs received accelerated FDA approval based on their effectiveness in tumor response and durability of treatment outcomes.
RET gene fusions are rare oncogenic changes primarily found in papillary thyroid cancer (5%–10%) and NSCLC (1%–2%), while their occurrence in other solid tumors is <1%. These fusions are usually distinct and not seen alongside other oncogenic drivers. Due to their rarity outside of NSCLC and thyroid cancer, limited information is available regarding their impact on prognosis. However, outcomes for advanced solid tumors that progress after prior treatment are generally poor, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 3% to 40%. On 21 September 2022, the FDA granted accelerated approval for selpercatinib (Retevmo) for adults with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring RET fusions who have exhausted prior treatments or lack alternative options (FDA.gov, 2022). The approval was supported by findings from the LIBRETTO-001 clinical trial, which investigated the effectiveness of selpercatinib in patients with advanced solid tumors carrying RET alterations.
In June 2024, the FDA approved Augtyro (repotrectinib), an advanced TKI, for treating locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors with NTRK gene fusions in both TKI-naïve and pretreated patients. This approval extends to adult and pediatric patients aged ≥12 years and is based on the TRIDENT-1 phase I/II clinical trial results. Additionally, repotrectinib is approved for ROS1-positive NSCLC (FDA.gov, 2024). It offers a promising option for patients where surgical intervention could lead to significant complications. Despite previous advancements, there remains an unmet need for effective therapies that provide durable responses and address resistance to existing treatments, making repotrectinib a valuable addition to available treatment options.
For these, kinase fusion genes have efficient inhibitors. However, many studies are still underway to identify more effective inhibitors. The fusion gene frequency in pediatric cancer is very high compared to adult cancers. For example, EWSR1 fusion genes are identified in all children patients of Ewing sarcoma. Specifically, ~85% of Ewing sarcoma patients expressed the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion gene [ ]. The t(2;13)(q35;q14) translocation joins the PAX3 and FOXO1 (FKHR) genes in ~60% of alveolar rhabdomyosarcomas [ ], while the less common t(1;13)(p36;q14) joins PAX7 with FOXO1 in ~20% of cases. The SS18-SSX fusion gene is found in >95% of synovial sarcoma [ ]. These fusion gene–positive pediatric cancer patients have a poor prognosis as it is prone to local recurrence and distant metastasis.
These previous studies can provide valuable knowledge on developing new targeted therapies targeting human fusion proteins. We need a well-arranged, in-depth knowledge base of human fusion genes’ therapeutic study records to develop the targetable fusion genes and fusion-specific targeted therapeutics. To fill this gap, we tried integrating all drug treatment studies/records targeting human fusion proteins to help with drug development planning, studies, and insights. FusionPub is the first knowledgebase to compile all known studies on human fusion proteins and their drug interactions. By integrating data on drug treatment studies, clinical trials, and fusion protein–drug interaction studies, FusionPub aims to support drug development planning and provide valuable insights for biological and pharmaceutical research. The impact of this work will be huge and broader than we think in the bio/health/drug scientific communities.

## Results

## Overview of FusionPub

 illustrates the overall workflow of FusionPub. First, we downloaded the known human fusion genes from FusionGDB 2.0, COSMIC, and CCLE. In a total of 108K fusion gene names recorded in these resources, 99 550 fusion genes were composed of gene pairs annotated in the GENCODE database. These correspond to 91 915 unique fusion gene IDs (82 677 FusionGDB IDs). For these fusion genes ( ) and ~14K drugs/small molecules ( ), we conducted large-scale PubMed abstracts and PubMed Central (PMC) full-text searches, investigating potential relationships between the fusion genes and drugs. Theoretically, the total number of combinations between ~108K fusion genes and ~14K drugs can involve >1.5 billion combination queries in PubMed abstracts and an equal number in PMC full-text searches. We identified 1047 relevant abstracts from this search, including 328 from abstract searches and 719 from full-text searches. Of these, 270 fusion gene IDs (representing 336 unique fusion gene names) were found to interact with 981 distinct drugs or small molecules.
 Classical cancer drugs have been directly used to target the functional domains of fusion proteins, such as the kinase or DNA binding domains. However, not all fusion genes can make fusion proteins. Only the fusion genes with the open reading frame (ORF) of the in-frame case can form the fusion protein structure and be targeted by drugs. Therefore, to develop the targeted therapy of fusion proteins, we need to have a deep knowledge of all previous studies on targeting fusion proteins, which are the in-frame fusion genes. Therefore, next, we more focused on the potentially translatable in-frame fusion genes (18 670 in-frame fusion gene names corresponding to 18 408 unique fusion gene IDs from 16 945 FusionGDB IDs) ( ); we repeated this search. However, this time, we also considered the gene synonyms. There can be a 1.8 quintillion times possible combination of searching between ~18K fusion gene names × 8.5K gene synonyms of 5′-genes × 8.3K gene synonyms of 3′-genes × ~14K drugs. Here, the 5′-gene is the first gene, and the 3′-gene is the second gene in the fusion gene name. We identified 10 795 relevant abstracts, including 3903 from abstract searches and 6892 from full-text searches. Combining and manually curating two types of searches finally made 17 342 findings from 9623 PubMed abstracts between 270 unique FusionGDB IDs and 981 drugs ( ). Among these findings, after investigation of ClinicalTrials.gov, we found 989 unique clinical trials (NCT IDs) among 44 specific fusion genes (with both gene names) and 15 nonspecific fusions (like ALK fusion and NTRK fusion) against 284 drugs ( .A). ## Case study: BCR::ABL1
BCR::ABL1 is a hallmark fusion gene in CML and has revolutionized targeted therapy with TKIs. The first breakthrough came with imatinib (Gleevec), which was FDA approved in 2001 and dramatically improved patient survival rates by inhibiting the constitutively active ABL kinase. Following resistance development, second- and third-generation TKIs, including dasatinib, nilotinib, and ponatinib, were introduced to overcome imatinib-resistant mutations such as T315I. In this database, we collected BCR::ABL fusion gene samples from three sources: CCLE (15), COSMIC (3010), and ChiTaRS 5.0 (27). Clinical trials indicate that BCR::ABL1-targeted therapies remain among the most extensively studied, with 612 trials indexed in ClinicalTrials.gov. In FusionPub, we have reported 1202 imatinib, 435 dasatinib, and 354 nilotinib to BCR-ABL related report present in PubMed.

## Case study: TMPRSS2::ERG
The TMPRSS2::ERG fusion is the most common genetic alteration in prostate cancer, present in ~50% of cases. This gene fusion results in the overexpression of ERG, a transcription factor that drives oncogenesis by disrupting normal androgen receptor (AR) signaling. While TMPRSS2::ERG itself is not directly targetable, its downstream effects have been explored for therapeutic interventions. In this database, we collected TMPRSS::ERG fusion gene samples from three sources: CCLE (2), COSMIC (254), and ChimerDB4 (7). Androgen deprivation therapy remains the standard treatment for prostate cancer, with agents like enzalutamide and abiraterone showing efficacy in TMPRSS2::ERG-positive cases. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating novel inhibitors targeting ERG-mediated pathways and synthetic lethality approaches, such as PARP inhibitors. In FusionPub, we have reported PMIDs related to TMPRSS2::ERG and drugs such as abiraterone, enzalutamide, and docetaxel.

## Discussion
So far, the existing studies of fusion genes are mainly focused on the lower level bioentity such as DNA breakage, fusion transcript, and the small number of fusion proteins due to the limited knowledge of the fusion amino acid sequences and structures. In this need, our previous studies of FusionGDB [ ] and FusionGDB 2.0 [ ] have served as the unique systematic functional annotation of human fusion genes. In FusionGDB 2.0, we reported ~43K of human fusion protein sequences, translated from 82K fusion transcripts and transcribed from 14.7K in-frame fusion genes. Based on these predicted fusion protein sequences and recently available AI approaches to predict novel proteins like AlphaFold2, we can now predict the protein structures of fusion proteins (or new proteins) in FusionPDB and perform further analyses.
Our comprehensive analysis of PubMed abstracts and clinical trials related to fusion gene–drug interactions has revealed important insights into the gaps in fusion protein–targeted drug development. The results indicate that while many fusion genes have been identified, the number of drugs currently targeting these genes remains limited, with only a subset of fusion genes receiving extensive clinical attention. This limitation underscores the need for continued research into underexplored fusion genes to expand the therapeutics for cancer treatment. The data presented in highlight the concentration of research efforts around specific fusion genes, such as BCR::ABL1, PML::RARA, and EML4::ALK, which have been well characterized and targeted by drugs like imatinib, dasatinib, and crizotinib. These findings reflect the success of TKIs in treating cancers like CML and NSCLC. However, our study also reveals the significant underrepresentation of other fusion genes, many of which have fewer associated clinical trials or PubMed-indexed studies. This disparity may be due to the rarity of certain fusion events or the complexity of targeting specific fusion proteins, which presents both a challenge and an opportunity for drug development.
The clinical trial data in and provide a detailed view of the drugs most frequently studied in terms of fusion genes. Notably, BCR::ABL1 emerges as the most clinically studied fusion gene, with 612 trials reflecting its well-established role in CML and the success of targeted therapies such as imatinib and dasatinib. Similarly, NPM-ALK and CBFB-MYH11, involved in lymphomas and leukemias, are also prominent in clinical trials, underscoring the focus on hematologic malignancies in fusion gene research. On the drug side, agents such as TKIs, cytarabine, and etoposide dominate the landscape of clinical trials, aligning with their established use in cancer therapies targeting fusion genes and other oncogenic drivers. The increasing number of clinical trials involving newer agents like brigatinib, lorlatinib, and lurbinectedin suggests a growing interest in developing precision therapies for solid tumors, particularly sarcomas and lung cancers driven by fusion genes such as ALK fusions and EWSR1::FLI1. The variation in the number of trials across different drugs and fusion genes highlights the disparity in research attention. It provides opportunities for expanding clinical studies into less-explored fusion gene targets.
The clinical trial data in illustrate ongoing efforts to develop targeted therapies for the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion gene, a key driver of Ewing sarcoma. Drugs like TK216, trabectedin, and irinotecan are currently being evaluated in phase I and II clinical trials, with promising outcomes expected in the coming years. The emergence of novel compounds, such as lurbinectedin, highlights the growing interest in precision medicine approaches to treating fusion gene–driven sarcomas. However, the termination of several trials, such as those involving plicamycin and vincristine, suggests that significant challenges remain in identifying effective and tolerable treatments for these aggressive cancers.
FusionPub is a unique and valuable resource for researchers and clinicians, providing a centralized database of fusion gene–drug interactions. The breadth of information in FusionPub, from molecular data to clinical trial outcomes, will undoubtedly facilitate the discovery of new therapeutic strategies and guide future drug development efforts. For biologists, the extensive structural data derived from AlphaFold2 and the catalog of fusion proteins provide a foundation for understanding the mechanistic roles of fusion proteins in oncogenesis. For bioinformaticians, FusionPub offers a wealth of data that can be further mined for predictive models, facilitating novel insights into fusion protein–drug interactions and resistance mechanisms. By utilizing FusionPub’s comprehensive data, clinicians could better stratify patients based on fusion gene profiles and optimize treatment strategies. Finally, for pharmacologists, the resource points to new directions for drug development, especially in targeting underrepresented fusion genes with novel compounds.

## Methods

## Fusion gene information
We obtained the fusion gene data from several publicly available databases, including FusionGDB 2.0, COSMIC, and CCLE. It has 39 485, 14 970, 12 861, and 1449 samples of 35 834, 47 379, 335, and 25 771 fusion genes from ChiTaRS 5.0, ChimerDB4, COSMIC, and CCLE, respectively. The fusion genes collected involved protein-coding genes with annotations from GENCODE. A total of 99 550 fusion genes were retrieved, representing 91 915 unique fusion gene IDs. Out of these, 82 677 fusion gene IDs were derived from FusionGDB.

## In-frame fusion gene information
We identified in-frame fusion genes using ORF annotations from FusionGDB 2.0 to focus on potentially translatable fusion genes. In total, 18 408 unique in-frame fusion gene IDs (corresponding to 18 670 in-frame fusion gene names) were identified for further analysis.

## PubMed abstract search
Our PubMed abstract search approach involved comprehensive searches across billions of combinations of fusion genes, fusion proteins, and drugs, all systematically executed and analyzed to ensure no potential therapeutic interactions were missed. We began by searching PubMed abstracts using fusion gene names formatted as “A-B” or “A::B,” where genes A and B represent the two partners in a gene fusion. Each fusion gene was combined with a list of ~14 000 drugs and small molecules. This resulted in a total of 1.5 billion search combinations, providing a thorough exploration of possible interactions between fusion genes and therapeutic agents. Similarly, we expanded the search to include 18 000 human fusion proteins. To capture the full range of potential gene–drug interactions, we used gene synonyms for the 5′- and 3′-fusion partners (8500 for 5′-partners and 8300 for 3′-partners), again combined with 14 000 drugs and small molecules. This expanded search included PubMed abstracts and full-text articles from PMC, generating a total of 1.8 quintillion search combinations.
To efficiently manage extensive searches, we created custom Python code using the Entrez library to access PubMed for information on fusion genes and inhibitors programmatically. The code employed Bio.Entrez for article retrieval, regular expressions for identifying gene–drug mentions in abstracts, pandas for organizing data, and itertools.product for generating combinations of fusion genes and drugs. We also created key Python functions, including one for executing searches and retrieving article IDs, another for fetching abstract text using PMIDs, and another for extracting relevant keywords from the abstracts.

## PubMed search result curation
After completing the literature search, we manually curated the identified records to extract relevant information. For example, if a fusion gene name is between the same gene, like DES-DES, CIC-CIC, or FOS-FOS, then we deleted this case. Sometimes, the gene synonyms are the same with a chemical name like “STA-II” in PMID 21899708, so we deleted this case. Most of all, we checked each paper for individual identified PubMed IDs to see whether it was about a fusion gene. If the paper was not about a fusion gene, we deleted it. We used generative AI to create a summary sentence for each identified interaction between a fusion gene and a drug based on the abstracts found. We extracted the PubMed ID (PMID), drug names, and study titles for each identified interaction. This process resulted in 17 342 curated records across 9623 PubMed abstracts, with interactions involving 270 unique fusion gene IDs (representing 336 fusion gene names) and 981 drugs.

## Clinical trials search
Using the curated fusion gene and drug lists from our PubMed search, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov for relevant clinical trials (NCTIDs). We utilized the most recent version of API from ClinicalTrials.gov to streamline this process, specifically the API/v2/studies endpoint. This allowed us to customize our search queries and specify the fields to retrieve and the desired return format. For each fusion gene and drug pair, we first compiled all known synonyms for the fusion gene, followed by three rounds of searches.
In the first round, we entered the drug in the “Intervention/treatment” search box and each fusion gene synonym in the “Condition/disease” search box, performing separate searches for each synonym. In the second round, we again entered the drug in the “Intervention/treatment” box but used each fusion gene synonym in the “Other terms” search box for each search. In the final round, we only entered the drug in the “Intervention/treatment” box and applied additional filters. For each trial returned in the final round, we reviewed the “Detailed Description” to check if any synonyms of the fusion gene appeared. If a synonym was present, we recorded the trial. For each identified trial, we documented the fusion gene name, drug, NCTID, study status, trial phase, disease, number of enrollments, and study completion date.
In addition, we also searched kinase name-based for ClinicalTrials.gov to cover the kinase fusion genes, not mentioning the specific fusion gene but just kinase fusion. From searching for the PubMed records of human kinase fusion genes, we found previous studies on 112 kinase fusion genes involving 21 kinases. For each of these kinases, we searched the ClinicalTrials.gov using “[kinase name] fusion and [drug name].” We found 3386 lines of “Gene, Drug, NCTID, Status, Condition, Phase, Enrollment, StartDate.” We found 193 NCTIDs between 15 kinase fusions (nonspecific fusion names, such as ALK fusion, BCR fusion, ABL1 fusion, BRAF fusion, FGFR1 fusion, FGFR2 fusion, JAK2 fusion, MET fusion, NTRK1 fusion, NTRK2 fusion, NTRK3 fusion, PDGFRB fusion, RAF1 fusion, RET fusion, and ROS1 fusion) and 108 drugs. This is additional information to our previously found clinical trial studies based on the fusion gene name search (A-B). We shared this information in the .

## Clinical trials search result curation
Each clinical trial entry was manually curated, and detailed information was extracted. Trials were categorized based on their phase (phase I to phase IV), status (recruiting, active, completed, terminated), and disease focus. This resulted in the identification of several trials involving fusion genes like EWSR1::FLI1 and drugs such as irinotecan, lurbinectedin, and TK216, with the trial data incorporated into FusionPub.

## Prompt used for generative AI (ChatGPT)
We have generated one summary sentence for each fusion gene and drug molecule relationship association by using the prompt “Generate a one-sentence summary describing the relationship between the fusion gene [Fusion Gene] and the drug [Drug Name] based on scientific literature. If the drug is known to target the fusion gene, mention its mechanism of action and clinical relevance. If no direct relationship exists, state that clearly.”

## Database development
FusionPub was a web-accessible platform to provide comprehensive information about fusion genes, their associated drugs, and relevant clinical trials. The FusionPub system is based on a three-tier architecture: client, server, and database. It includes a user-friendly web interface, Perl’s DBI module, and MySQL database. This database was developed on MySQL 3.23 with the MyISAM storage engine. Data were organized in a manner that allows users to retrieve information on fusion gene names, drugs, associated PMIDs, and NCTIDs.

## Key Points
FusionPub is a comprehensive resource for exploring human fusion genes and their clinical significance.
The platform integrates data from PubMed abstracts, clinical trials, and patient treatment histories to provide a detailed therapeutic landscape of fusion genes.
FusionPub identifies 9623 PubMed abstracts linking 270 unique fusion genes to 981 drugs, revealing critical insights into fusion gene targeting therapeutics.
Generative AI is employed to summarize relationships between fusion genes and drugs, enhancing data interpretation for cancer research.
