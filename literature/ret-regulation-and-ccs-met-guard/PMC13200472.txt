## Abstract
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of global mortality and disease burden, with pathogenesis involving metabolic remodelling and inflammatory responses. Metabolic remodelling is also increasingly regarded as a key factor in the pathogenesis of CVDs, leading to the abnormal accumulation of metabolic intermediates. Current research has shown that succinate, an intermediate product of the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), significantly accumulates in CVDs, promoting the occurrence of disease. In recent years, the role of epigenetics in CVDs has received increasing attention. Compared with its modification, succinylation modification directly links metabolism and epigenetics. Therefore, in CVDs where metabolic remodelling is a key factor, succinylation modification may play a crucial role. This article describes the role of succinate and succinylation modifications in CVDs. For example, in ischaemia‒reperfusion injury (IRI), the abnormal accumulation of succinate during ischaemia and the oxidation of succinate by succinylation dehydrogenase (SDH) during reperfusion lead to the production of large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the succinylation modification of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2) at lysine 424 increases the lipotoxicity to diabetic hearts. In addition, given the significant role of succinate and succinylation modifications in the cardiovascular system, we focused on therapeutic strategies targeting succinate and succinylation modifications, including inhibitors of SDH, inhibitors of succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1), and activators of sirtuin 5 (SIRT5), providing new insights for CVD intervention.
Keywords: Cardiovascular diseases, Succinate, Succinylation, Epigenetics, SDH

## Highlights

The intracellular accumulation of succinate drives the production of ROS to stabilize HIF-1α.
Succinate released outside the cell acts as a signalling molecule to recognize SUCNR1.
Succinylation is regulated by succinyl donor, writer and eraser enzymes.
Changes in succinate and succinylation levels may affect a variety of CVDs.
Succinate and succinylation are expected to serve as therapeutic targets for CVD.

## Introduction
CVDs, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, myocardial hypertrophy, and heart failure, are major contributors to the global disease burden [ ]. Global ageing has led to a steady increase in the incidence and mortality of CVDs. In 2019, CVDs were the root cause of death for 9.6 million men and 8.9 million women, accounting for approximately one-third of global deaths [ ]. It is vital to actively improve the pathogenesis of CVDs and seek safer and more effective drugs. Metabolic remodelling is regarded as the fundamental mechanism of CVD occurrence [ ]. In recent years, the level of succinate in plasma has been shown to increase in patients with various CVDs [ – ]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the abnormal accumulation of succinate is closely related to the occurrence of CVDs. During this process, metabolic remodelling promotes abnormal accumulation of succinate, and subsequently, the accumulated succinate is oxidized by SDH to produce ROS, leading to cell damage [ ]. In addition, the accumulated succinate within cells can also be released through monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), where it acts as a signalling molecule to activate its receptor SUCNR1 and exert a proinflammatory effect [ ].
At present, increasing evidence has demonstrated the connection between metabolic remodelling and epigenetics. Compared with posttranslational modifications (PTMs), such as acetylation and methylation, succinylation, an emerging modification that is closely linked to cellular metabolism, may play distinct functional roles. Treatment with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induces the accumulation of succinate in macrophages, leading to an increase in the level of active succinyl-CoA. The accumulated succinyl-CoA can directly modify proteins through succinyl modifications and regulate their function [ , ]. Succinylation modification indicates a significant expansion of the pathogenic effect of succinate. For instance, the succinylation modification of key enzymes alters their enzymatic activity, thereby regulating metabolic pathways, and histone succinylation promotes gene transcription [ , ]. Because of the significant role of succinate and succinylation modification in CVDs, increasing numbers of people are turning their attention to small molecules that inhibit the pathogenic pathway of succinate and succinylation modification. Malonate, itaconate and its derivatives, NF-56-EJ40, and NAD+ can significantly inhibit the pathogenic effects of succinate or succinylation [ , – ] (Fig. ).
 In this review, we focus on the cardiovascular field, summarize the latest progress in succinylation in this area, determine the role of succinate and succinylation in cardiovascular diseases, and integrate basic and clinical perspectives to discuss the clinical translation potential and translation obstacles of current therapeutic strategies targeting succinate and succinylation. This review greatly expands our previous work on this topic.

## Succinate metabolism and its signalling roles

## Succinate metabolism
Succinate is an intermediate metabolite of the TCA cycle in mitochondria and plays an indispensable role in metabolism. It is generated from α-ketoglutarate through the catalytic action of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, forming succinyl-CoA, which is subsequently hydrolysed to succinate. Succinate is then oxidized to fumarate by succinate dehydrogenase [ , ]. Recent studies on LPS-activated macrophages have fully clarified the mechanisms regulating succinate accumulation, with potential mechanisms including the γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) shunt, glutamine anaplerosis, and the glyoxylate shunt [ ]. Furthermore, in ischaemic heart disease, succinate accumulation is primarily increased through the malate/aspartate shuttle (MAS) and the purine nucleotide cycle (PNC) [ ]. (1) In the GABA shunt, glutamate is catalysed by glutamate decarboxylase, GABA transaminase, and succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase to produce succinate, allowing GABA carbon skeletons to enter the TCA cycle [ ]. (2) Glutamine anaplerosis generates α-ketoglutarate, which enters the TCA cycle and promotes succinate accumulation [ ]. (3) The glyoxylate shunt combines acetyl-CoA (a product of fatty acid β-oxidation) with oxaloacetate to produce succinate and glyoxylate [ ]. (4) Under ischaemic conditions, a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio activates the malate/aspartate shuttle and purine nucleotide cycle, leading to malate formation. Malate is subsequently converted to succinate via reverse catalysis by fumarate dehydrogenase (FH) and SDH [ ]. The microbial fermentation of dietary fibre also results in the production of large amounts of succinate in the intestine [ , ]; there is increasing evidence that microbial variations are involved in the pathogenesis of CVDs [ ]. A cohort study revealed a population-based cohort of 10,194 Finnish men (age = 57.65 ± 7.12 years) with metabolic syndrome in men (METSIM). After adjusting for traditional risk factors and correcting for multiple comparisons, higher plasma levels of succinate were significantly associated with the risk of CVD [ ]. Recent evidence has shown that the accumulation of succinate, driven by an increased abundance of succinate-producing bacteria in the intestines of mice such as B. vulgatus, significantly exacerbates cardiac fibrosis following helminth infection [ ]. Under hypoxic conditions, the glyoxylate shunt in microorganisms becomes markedly active and serves as a key pathway for succinate synthesis, which differs from that in humans [ ].

## Intracellular succinate signalling mechanisms
Accumulated succinate has two fates: intracellular mitochondrial oxidation via SDH and extracellular release. Intracellular succinate couples with SDH to drive mitochondrial ROS generation, and the accumulated ROS can stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) [ ].
Intracellular succinate can regulate cellular adaptation by inhibiting α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)-dependent dioxygenases. Proline hydroxylase (PHD) is an α-KG-dependent dioxygenase that hydroxylates proline residues in the HIF-1α subunit. Succinate inhibits PHD, reducing the hydroxylation of HIF-1α and thereby stabilizing HIF-1α, which is termed “pseudohypoxia” [ , ]. Stabilized HIF-1α activates genes containing HIF response elements (HREs), many of which are involved in glycolysis, such as glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), LDHA, and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isozyme 1 (PDK1) [ , ]. In addition, succinate also inhibits DNA demethylases (another α-KG-dependent dioxygenase), resulting in a change in the myocardial transcriptomic landscape. Succinate-induced DNA hypermethylation decreases the expression of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) genes in the myocardium, leading to an imbalance in FAO/glycolysis [ , ] (Fig.  ).

## Extracellular succinate signalling roles
Succinate is transferred into the extracellular space and may act as a signalling molecule when the intracellular succinate level is markedly increased [ ]. The succinate receptor—SUCNR1—is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that was initially identified as predominantly expressed in the kidney. Recent studies have demonstrated its critical role in diverse cell types, including dendritic cells, T cells, platelets, endothelial cells, and macrophages [ ].
The succinate receptor is among the most highly expressed receptors in human platelets and can activate platelets by increasing P-selectin and glycoprotein IIb–IIIa complex (GPIIb–IIIa) expression [ ]. In endothelial cells, succinate drives succinate/IL-1β signalling in a SUCNR1-dependent manner and subsequently exacerbates inflammatory responses to promote the development of atherosclerosis [ ]. The dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics and functional alterations are the primary initiating factors of cardiac injury [ ]. In murine ventricular cardiomyocytes, succinate activates protein kinase C-δ (PKCδ) via SUCNR1, leading to the phosphorylation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) at Ser 585 (equivalent to Ser 616 of human Drp1 isoform 1), which promotes Drp1 translocation to mitochondria, induces mitochondrial fission, and induces mitochondrial fission factor (MFF) phosphorylation via extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) activation in a SUCNR1-dependent manner, which enhances the localization of MFF and Drp1 in mitochondria and promotes mitochondrial fission, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ]. Succinate-SUCNR1 signalling is critical for diabetic nephropathy, which may lead to end-stage renal failure and is characterized by hypertension. Additionally, the distal nephron-collecting duct system, the major source of renin in diabetes, has the highest level of SUCNR1 expression in the kidney. Succinate stimulation and SUCNR1 activation may further activate ERK1/2 and cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) to induce prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) release, Ca2 + transduction, and nitric oxide (NO) production. These three elements stimulate renin release and activate the renin‒angiotensin system (RAS) [ , ] (Fig.  ).
 In conclusion, in some cases, succinate is a causal mediator. For instance, when succinate stimulates SUCNR1, it leads to an inflammatory response and acts as a donor for succinylation modification, altering the enzymatic activity of proteins and the transcription of genes [ ]. However, in some other cases, succinate merely serves as a biomarker reflecting metabolic conditions. For instance, in individuals with SDH gene mutations, the level of succinate in their blood is extremely high [ ]. Critically, these roles are not mutually exclusive. In complex disease processes, a vicious cycle may lead to a vicious cycle in which initial metabolic dysfunction (hypoxia, mitochondrial damage) causes succinate accumulation making it a biomarker, which in turn drives inflammatory and epigenetic pathways making it a causal mediator, thereby amplifying initial injury. Therefore, the specific functions of succinate should be carefully verified and distinguished on the basis of specific circumstances.

## Succinylation and its pathological roles

## Mechanism of succinylation
Lysine succinylation, the reversible, dynamic, and evolutionarily conserved transfer of a succinyl group from succinyl-CoA to a lysine residue on a target protein, is a key PTM in the field of cardiac metabolic regulation and cardiac pathology [ ]. This modification is notable for its ubiquity; in the heart, succinyl-CoA is the most prevalent acyl-CoA identified to date [ ]. During metabolic remodelling, the accumulated succinyl-CoA, as a key intermediate in the TCA cycle, increases the level of protein succinylation modification [ ]. Whether or not there is enzymatic action, succinyl-CoA can transfer succinyl group to the lysine residues of proteins, thereby achieving succinylation [ , – ]. Therefore, succinyl-CoA plays a crucial role in regulating cardiac metabolism and function. The enzymatic regulation of succinylation formation involves multiple succinylation transferases, including lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A), histone acetyltransferase 1 (HAT1), and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) [ ].
Based on the subcellular localization of cells where succinylation occurs, these modifications can be classified as histone or nonhistone succinylation (Table  ). For example, KAT2A catalyzes the succinylation of H3K79, which promotes gene transcription [ – ]. HAT1 is an acetyltransferase that regulates the acetylation of histone and nonhistone proteins. However, a recent study revealed that HAT1 is a novel succinyltransferase that modulates the succinylation of histones and nonhistones, such as HAT1, which succinylates histone H3 on K122 to affect gene expression. With respect to nonhistones, HAT1 catalyses the succinylation of phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) at K99 to increase its enzyme activity, promoting glycolysis in cells [ , ]. Another enzyme in mammalian cells that possesses lysine succinyltransferase activity is CPT1A [ , ], which is found on the outer mitochondrial membrane and converts acyl-CoA to acyl-carnitine to transfer fatty acids into the mitochondria for further oxidation [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated that CPT1A maintains the normal function of mitochondria by promoting the succinylation of (tRNA methyltransferase 10C) TRMT10C [ ]. As a histone acetyltransferase, E1A-binding protein (p300) plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CVDs. Recent studies have revealed that p300 regulates the succinylation of several glycolytic enzymes, particularly phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), thereby promoting glucose metabolic remodelling [ , ]. Moreover, the succinylation of H3K122 can be mediated by p300/CBP, leading to nucleosome destabilization and the possible displacement of the histone octamer from DNA, resulting in increased DNA accessibility [ ]. However, the mechanistic role of p300 as a succinyltransferase in CVDs remains unexplored. The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex (KGDHC) can serve as a succinyltransferase that mediates succinylation in an α-ketoglutarate-dependent manner. KGDHC-dependent succinylation is strikingly efficient in the presence of KGDHC, which readily transfers succinyl groups to substrate proteins [ ]. Succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase 1 (OXCT1) catalyses ketone body oxidation. A recent study demonstrated that the expression of OXCT1 significantly increased after acute myocardial infarction. Mechanistically, OXCT1 could bind to mitochondrial transcription factor A (Tfam) and increased its succinylation, promoting Tfam degradation. Downregulation of Tfam resulted in ROS production and mtDNA damage, while mtDNA released into cytoplasm could activate Z-DNA binding protein 1(Zbp1) and induce cascade reaction of PANoptosis (Fig.  ) [ ].
 

## Mechanisms of desuccinylation
SIRT5, a member of the NAD⁺-dependent lysine deacetylase family, regulates critical biological processes in mammals. It was initially recognized for its deacetylase activity and has been demonstrated to exhibit potent desuccinylase enzymatic activity [ ]. SIRT5 removes succinyl groups from lysine residues on SDH and propane dehydrogenation (PDH), thereby suppressing the activity of these enzymes [ ]. When SIRT5 is knocked out, lysine succinylation predominantly occurs in the heart. Previous mass spectrometry analysis revealed 124 succinylated proteins that are potentially regulated by SIRT5. Among all the identified succinylated proteins, more than 75% were mitochondrial proteins [ ]. Sirtuin 7 (SIRT7)-catalysed desuccinylation primarily occurs in the nucleus; for example, SIRT7 erases the succinyl group of H3K122 to modulate the DNA damage response and genome stability [ , ]. In addition, sirtuin2 (SIRT2) can also act as a succinylase. Studies have demonstrated that SIRT2 catalyses the desuccinylation of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ ATPase type 2a (SERCA2a) K352, which inhibits the formation of its ubiquitin chain and maintains its stability. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that histone desuccinylation is another process that is primarily catalysed by class I HDAC1/2/3, which plays a critical role in suppressing histone succinylation at promoter regions to maintain transcriptional homeostasis (Table  ) [ ].
In addition, succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS) is responsible for catalysing the reversible conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate and the release of GTP/ATP. In eukaryotes, the SCS is a heterodimeric complex with a single catalytic α subunit, SUCLG1, and two isoforms of the β subunit, ATP-generating SUCLA2 and GTP-forming SUCLG2 [ ]. The SCS-mediated conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate reduces the pool of succinyl-CoA available for succinylation modifications, leading to decreased succinylation (Table  ) [ ].
 

## Pathological implications of succinylation
Lysine residues are key to regulating the spatial structures and functions of proteins, and their succinylation may significantly affect the activity of substrate proteins [ ]. The overrepresentation of succinylation at sites on mitochondrial proteins can regulate metabolic pathways, such as ATP biosynthesis, the urea cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and fatty acid β-oxidation [ ]. Succinylation has been shown to regulate pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), carbamoyl-phosphate synthase 1 (CPS1), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2), PDH, and SDH [ ]. PKM2 is a key target of succinylation in macrophages. The succinylation of PKM2 at the K311 site has been shown to impair its glycolytic activity, induce its nuclear translocation, and increase IL-1β production [ , ]. CPS 1 is the rate-limiting enzyme of the urea cycle. Under succinylation, the activity of CPS 1 is reduced [ ]. In the ketogenesis pathway, HMGCS2, the enzyme responsible for the rate-limiting conversion of acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA into 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA), contains the greatest number of succinyl lysine modification sites and SIRT5 target sites, and the succinylation of its lysine residues inhibits HMGCS2 activity [ ]. In contrast, PDH and SDH exhibit enhanced activity after succinylation, thereby influencing mitochondrial metabolism and cellular respiration [ , ]. Additionally, evidence suggests that histone succinylation modulates nucleosome structure and gene expression [ , ]. For example, compared with unmodified nucleosomes, H3K122-synccinylated nucleosomes exhibit reduced stability. This phenomenon occurs because histone succinylation weakens DNA‒histone affinity, potentially increasing the accessibility of DNA and thereby facilitating transcriptional activation [ , ]. A recent study utilizing chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) revealed that chromatin succinylation exhibits distinct localization patterns near gene promoters. Succinylation at these promoter regions was identified as a hallmark of transcriptional activation. Furthermore, chromatin succinylation serves as a functional nexus linking the TCA cycle status to epigenetic transcriptional regulation [ , ].

## Role of succinate and succinylation in cardiovascular pathologies

## Role of succinate in cardiovascular diseases

## Aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD)
AAD is the leading cause of death in Asia and globally; this condition often remains asymptomatic until death, contributing to alarmingly high mortality rates [ ]. Recent clinical sample studies utilizing untargeted metabolomics have identified succinate as both a biomarker and therapeutic target for AAD. In animal model experiments, succinate administration increased mitochondrial ROS levels in vascular tissues, exacerbating AAD in Ang II-, β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN)-, and BAPN/Ang II-induced model mice. Further mechanistic investigations revealed that p38α regulates oxoglutarate dehydrogenase expression via the phosphorylation of the transcription factor cyclic adenosine monophosphate-responsive element-binding protein 1 (CREB) and subsequently affects the expression of succinate, thereby modulating macrophage inflammatory phenotypes [ ]. In this study, untargeted metabolomics identified plasma succinate as a biomarker for AAD. These findings support the hypothesis that elevated plasma succinate in AAD patients drives increased succinate uptake by macrophages. The accumulated intracellular succinate is subsequently oxidized by SDH, generating excessive amounts of ROS that exacerbate AAD progression. Additional studies have shown that increased intracellular succinate can stabilize HIF-1α by directly inhibiting PHDs or indirectly through the generation of ROS via SDH-mediated oxidation driven by reverse electron transport (RET) [ , ]. Moreover, in vivo, macrophage HIF-1α activation induces the expression of a disintegrin and metalloprotease 17 (ADAM17), which triggers vascular inflammation, extracellular matrix degradation, and disruption of elastic laminae, thereby promoting the progression of AAD [ ] (Fig.  A).
 

## Atherosclerosis
In the early stages of atherosclerotic development, monocytes are recruited from the blood to the damaged arterial vascular wall and locally differentiate into inflammatory macrophages [ ]. As demonstrated by an in vitro study, activated macrophages undergo a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, during which succinate, a key accumulated metabolite, serves as a novel extracellular inflammatory driver that is sensed extracellularly through SUCNR1 [ , , ]. In macrophages, accumulated succinate is partially oxidized to generate ROS, stabilizing HIF-1α and promoting IL-1β production, while the remaining succinate is released via MCT1 [ , ]. Extracellular succinate released through this process binds to SUCNR1 on cardiomyocytes, activating nuclear HIF-1α, which induces the expression of pro-IL-1β and NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3). Subsequently, NLRP3 interacts with pro-caspase-1 to release active caspase-1, which catalyses the processing of pro-IL-1β into mature IL-1β, exacerbating inflammatory responses [ ]. Additionally, ROS generated via succinate oxidation through SDH directly impair the vasodilatory effects of NO in the endothelium, thereby exacerbating endothelial dysfunction (Fig.  B) [ ].

## Myocardial hypertrophy
One study demonstrated that elevated succinate levels in the bloodstream can activate the RAS via SUCNR1 to increase blood pressure, thereby inducing myocardial hypertrophy [ , ]. However, succinate-induced myocardial hypertrophy is not solely caused by elevated blood pressure. Further linking cardiac hypertrophy to the succinate–SUCNR1 axis revealed that SUCNR1 is necessary for succinate-induced myocardial hypertrophy (Fig.  C). In vitro studies have shown that the accumulation of succinate also promotes the progression of myocardial hypertrophy through several SUCNR1-mediated pathways. In one pathway, SUCNR1 activates mitogen-activated protein kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2), resulting in ERK1/2 phosphorylation. Phosphorylated ERK1/2 is translocated to the nucleus, where it activates the expression of cardiac hypertrophy-associated genes. The second involves SUCNR1 activation of phospholipase C (PLC), generating inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 binds to receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum, triggering intracellular Ca2⁺ release. Elevated Ca2⁺ activates calcium calmodulin kinase II δ (CaMKIIδ), which moves to the nucleus and phosphorylates histone deacetylase 5 (HDAC5), which is released from myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2), allowing the transcription of hypertrophic genes and thereby promoting the expression of myocardial hypertrophy-related genes [ , ]. In addition, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) axis acts as a downstream effector of succinate‒SUCNR1 signalling in cardiac hypertrophy, where SUCNR1 activates AKT through PI3K. AKT phosphorylates tuberous sclerosis protein 2 (TSC2), suppressing its activity and thereby alleviating the TSC2-mediated negative regulation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). Enhanced activation of mTOR and its downstream targets facilitates cellular proliferation, promoting the development of myocardial hypertrophy [ ].

## IRI
In ischaemic hearts, the accumulation of succinate is increased because of the reverse catalytic activity of SDH (Fig.  D). This process is closely linked to the MAS and PNC [ ]. Both the MAS and PNC pathways promote the formation of fumarate, which is subsequently converted into succinate through the reverse action of SDH. Under ischaemic conditions, an elevated NADH/NAD+ ratio significantly impairs the normal conversion of α-ketoglutarate to succinate via succinyl-CoA, which has been demonstrated in animal models [ ]. Consequently, the reverse catalysis of fumarate to succinate via SDH becomes the primary pathway for succinate accumulation during ischaemia. The accumulation of succinate appears to be a hallmark of ischaemia [ ]. Elevated succinate inhibits PHDs, thereby stabilizing HIF-1α [ ]. During reperfusion, a portion of the accumulated succinate is rapidly oxidized by SDH, driving ROS production via reverse electron transfer at mitochondrial complex I [ ]. ROS contribute to the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores (MPTPs) and the excessive contraction of cardiomyocytes during reperfusion, which represent two primary mechanisms of cell death in myocardial infarction [ ]. Moreover, elevated levels of intracellular succinate can inhibit PDH activity, reduce energy production and further exacerbate IRI [ ]. Additionally, partial succinate is released extracellularly via MCT1, a process that is promoted by ischaemic acidosis in the myocardium. MCT1 cotransports protons and monocarboxylates; thus, during the reperfusion of ischaemic tissue, the pH gradient between tissues and the circulation drives succinate efflux [ ]. The released succinate binds to its receptor SUCNR1, activating succinate-mediated HIF-1α/IL-1β signalling in effector cells and aggravating cardiomyocyte injury [ ].

## Role of succinylation in cardiovascular diseases

## AAD
PTMs are among the most critical regulatory mechanisms for protein functions, including stabilization, cellular localization, and activity. Tandem mass tag labelling combined with mass spectrometry is employed to analyse differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Clinical cohort studies have shown that the level of lysine succinylation in patients with AAD is significantly greater than that in healthy individuals [ ]. Through proteomic analysis, 197 common DEPs were identified in the thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD) tissues of clinical patients compared with those in the control group. Among these genes, OXCT1 was selected as a target protein involved in the pathogenesis of TAAD [ ].

## Ischaemic heart disease
Clinical studies have shown that in ischaemic heart disease, the lysine succinylation of myofibrillar and mitochondrial proteins is reduced in failing human ischaemic hearts. Pathway analysis revealed that proteins with decreased lysine succinylation in ischaemic heart failure samples were highly represented in pathways involved in myofibril function, catabolic pathways, and ATP synthesis pathways [ ]. Additionally, compared with wild-type controls, liver SIRT5-overexpressing mice presented significantly elevated levels of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) in plasma and cardiac tissue after myocardial infarction. Furthermore, proteins involved in hepatic mitochondrial pathways, including the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid β-oxidation, were markedly upregulated. These findings suggest that SIRT5 may exert cardioprotective effects during acute ischaemia by increasing FGF21 secretion and improving energy metabolism through a liver‒heart axis mechanism [ ].

## IRI
A mechanistic study revealed that IRI induces the downregulation of SIRT5 expression, an effect that is accompanied by reduced autophagy and increased cell death in an animal model. SIRT5 overexpression effectively promotes autophagy and suppresses cell death. Coimmunoprecipitation assays revealed that SIRT5 interacts with its downstream target myb1 membrane trafficking protein (TOM1), reduces the succinylation of TOM1 at K48, and increases the expression of TOM1. This mechanism inhibits autophagy and ameliorates myocardial injury in a myocardial IRI cell model [ ]. Compared with their wild-type (WT) littermates, SIRT5 knockout (SIRT5−/−) mice exhibit increased infarct sizes in the heart after ischaemia‒reperfusion. The loss of SIRT5 activity enhances the succinylation of SDH, which promotes SDH enzymatic activity and increases postischaemic succinate oxidation. These changes may constitute one of the mechanisms by which SIRT5 deficiency exacerbates infarct size in IRI [ ].

## Sepsis-induced heart dysfunction
SERCA2a is a key ATPase involved in the reuptake of calcium from the cytosol back into the SR during the excitation‒contraction coupling process. Decreased expression and activity of SERCA2a are markers of HF. A mechanistic study revealed that in sepsis-induced heart dysfunction, the succinylation of SERCA2a at the K352 site increases, which disrupts the stability of SERCA2a by promoting the formation of its K48 ubiquitin chain and facilitating its degradation by the proteasome via crosstalk. These changes might be attributed to the increased succinylation of SERCA2a upon LPS treatment, which led to alterations in its protein structure, with more lysine residues exposed for binding by ubiquitin, ultimately causing protein degradation. LPS inhibits the desuccinylation of the K352 site of SERCA2a by SIRT2, resulting in a further increase in the succinylation modification of SERCA2a (Fig.  A). These findings suggest that the regulation of SERCA2a desuccinylation has potential for the treatment of sepsis-induced heart dysfunction [ ].
 

## Coronary microembolization (CME)
CME refers to coronary microcirculation embolism and its series of complications caused by myocardial microinfarction. In a recent study using in vivo and in vitro CME models, decreased expression of CPT1A was observed to inhibit the succinylation of TRMT10C and promote its binding to karyopherin subunit alpha 4 (KPNA4), resulting in its abnormal accumulation in the nucleus. The accumulation of TRMT10C in the cell nucleus, rather than in the mitochondria, under hypoxic conditions likely disrupts mitochondrial function. Further studies revealed that TRMT10C in the cell nucleus may cause the degradation of TAFAZZIN and NLR family member X1 (NLRX1) through N1-methyladenosine (m1A) modification, which may lead to ROS production. Additionally, decreases in the levels of TAFAZZIN and NLRX1 facilitate the activation of inflammatory signalling pathways, such as the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and NLRP3 pathways (Fig.  B). A reduction in NLRX1 expression promotes the interaction between apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC) and NLRP3, thereby activating the NLRP3 signalling pathway and inducing the production of inflammatory factors [ ]. However, the available evidence in this area is limited and should be interpreted with caution.

## Diabetic cardiomyopathy
SIRT5 expression is significantly reduced in a mouse model of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) heart failure. SIRT5 deficiency impairs FAO in diabetic hearts without affecting fatty acid uptake capacity, leading to the accumulation of fatty acid (FA) intermediate metabolites. Mechanistically, succinylomic analyses have revealed that CPT2, a crucial enzyme involved in the conversion of fatty acylcarnitine to fatty acyl-CoA and the facilitation of FAO, is the functional succinylated substrate of Sirt5. Loss of SIRT5 expression significantly increases the succinylation of CPT2 at Lys424, resulting in CPT2 inactivation and ultimately contributing to cardiac lipotoxicity in diabetic hearts [ , ].

## Transverse aortic constriction
Additionally, in transverse aortic constriction (TAC) animal models with SIRT5 overexpression, the level of succinylation is significantly reduced. SIRT5 overexpression also alleviates the elevated expression of atrial natriuretic peptide (Nppa), skeletal muscle actin (Acta1), and myosin heavy chain isoform α (Myh6) in TAC hearts. The remodelling and fibrosis of extracellular matrix fibroblasts are critical components of adverse remodelling in heart failure (HF). The expression of periostin (Postn), a key marker of cardiac fibroblast activation and differentiation, is strongly induced in wild-type TAC hearts but is reduced in SIRT5-overexpressing TAC mice [ ]. These findings suggest that succinylation may exacerbate the progression of heart failure.

## Comparison of succinylation and other modifications in CVDs
PTM is a dynamic research direction in the field of cardiovascular medicine. Dynamic changes in gene expression and protein activity have been studied primarily under the premise that the DNA sequence remains unchanged. These changes profoundly affect the development of CVDs and the maintenance of normal functions. Succinylation mainly affects metabolic enzyme activity, mitochondrial function and the inflammatory response, which together with other posttranslational modifications, regulate the delicate cardiovascular system. However, compared with other modifications, the uniqueness of succinylation lies in its ability to directly link metabolism with posttranslational modifications. Its level can reflect the metabolic status of the organism and is highly dynamic; it is catalysed mainly by nonenzymes [ ] and changes the charge of lysine residues from + 1 to -1, greatly altering protein conformation and activity.
Among the modifications of histones, histone acetylation is regulated by histone acetylases and histone deacetylases, which extensively fine-tune gene transcription in cells [ ]. In human valvular interstitial cells, histone acetylation alters the accessibility of chromatin and upregulates the expression of Notch1, inhibiting valvular calcification [ ]. Histone methylation functions by establishing stable and long-term epigenetic markers [ ]. Suppressor of variegation 3–9 homologue 1 (SUV39H1) catalyses the trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9me3), a repressive heterochromatin-associated epigenetic marker, which promotes the repressive state of contractile gene promoters and the proliferation of human coronary artery smooth muscle cells [ ]. Succinylation serves as a molecular bridge between metabolic status and gene transcription, loosening the structure of chromatin and promoting gene transcription. Compared with other modifications, this characteristic is unique (Table  ).
 Among the modifications of nonhistones, succinylation mainly affects mitochondrial metabolism and amino acid and fatty acid metabolism by altering enzyme activity. For instance, in diabetic cardiomyopathy, the succinylation level of CPT2 increases, inhibiting its activity and enhancing lipid toxicity in the myocardium [ ]. Acetylation contributes to cellular metabolism by altering the conformation of proteins. For instance, an increase in the acetylation of Drp1 promotes the transfer of its mitochondria, leading to the dysfunction and death of cardiomyocytes [ ]. The sites of phosphorylation are mainly serine, threonine, and tyrosine, which are widely involved in signal transduction. For instance, in myocardial hypertrophy, the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 promotes the transcription of hypertrophy genes [ , ]. Although the available evidence for succinylation in CVDs is relatively weak, its direct connection to metabolic remodelling supports its research potential in metabolic CVDs. ## Diagnostic value of succinate and succinylated proteins and their detection methods in clinical samples

## Significance of succinate and succinylated proteins as diagnostic markers for CVDs
AAD is a severe vessel disease with a high mortality rate [ ]. The earlier the condition of AAD patients is assessed, the more scientifically disease development can be managed. Therefore, timely and accurate diagnosis of AAD is important. Blood biomarkers, as a liquid biopsy method, have advantages such as speed, convenience, noninvasiveness, and the absence of radiation, which can increase AAD diagnostic efficiency. In inflammatory macrophages, succinate is released into the extracellular space, where it promotes the progression of aortic diseases [ ]. Consequently, higher concentrations of succinate can be detected in the circulating blood of AAD patients than in that of healthy controls.
A comparison of plasma succinate levels between 700 AAD patients and 692 healthy controls revealed that the levels of the former were approximately twice those of the latter. Even after using propensity score matching to minimize differences in baseline characteristics, this difference still exists. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was subsequently used to evaluate the diagnostic ability of succinate, and the value obtained was 0.83, indicating better discriminative performance than that of the baseline module [ ]. AAD shares similar clinical symptoms with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and pulmonary embolism (PE); however, distinct treatment strategies are needed. Research analysing the sensitivity and specificity of plasma succinate levels in these diseases has revealed that plasma succinate levels can distinguish AAD from AMI and PE [ , ]. These findings demonstrated that plasma succinate can be regarded as a specific biomarker for the diagnosis of AAD. Additionally, younger individuals with higher plasma succinate levels tend to have elevated levels of visceral adipose tissue, which is a marker of increased cardiovascular risk. Plasma succinate levels may reflect the cardiovascular status of young individuals and serve as a marker for CVD risk [ ].
Currently, PTMs of serum proteins have been utilized as biomarkers for diseases. Clinical research has revealed the presence of more than 100 malonylated, succinylated, and glutarylated lysine sites on more than 20 human serum proteins. Succinylation is the most abundant PTM in human blood. In AMI, the level of plasma albumin succinylation is significantly reduced. Specifically, succinylation at several lysine sites on albumin markedly decreases after 24 h and 72 h of induction of myocardial infarction [ ].

## Assays for reliable measurement of succinate and succinylation in clinical samples
At present, there is a lack of standardized detection methods for succinate and protein succinylation. The main methods for determining the concentration of succinate in plasma include mass spectrometry (MS) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [ , ]. Although MS detection results are relatively accurate, owing to its low detection efficiency and high detection cost, MS cannot be used for large-scale detection, which limits its practical clinical application. However, although ELISA can perform high-throughput detection, its linear detection range is limited, and it is easily interfered with by other nonspecific impurities in the sample. Therefore, it is highly necessary to develop a high-throughput and specific detection method in the future.
Protein succinylation modifications are often detected by Western blotting (WB), coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and MS. WB and Co-IP are highly dependent on antibodies [ ]. Despite their high specificity and affinity, they face challenges such as epitope occlusion, cross-reactivity, and uncertain binding efficiency. In contrast, high-resolution MS has high compatibility and universal applicability among different biological samples. It can not only detect classic posttranslational modifications but also promote the discovery of new modifications. However, MS is primarily used in research settings and requires specialized operators. To date, the lack of standardized detection protocols has hindered its clinical translation, necessitating the development of specific probes or antibodies to advance its applications.

## Therapeutic potential of succinate metabolism and succinylation modifications

## Malonate and itaconate inhibit SDH activity
In various CVDs, SDH plays a core role [ ]. For instance, in IRI, SDH exerts reverse catalytic activity during ischaemia, leading to a significant accumulation of succinate. During reperfusion, the accumulated succinate is rapidly oxidized by SDH to generate a large amount of ROS, driving the production of IL-1β [ , ]. Therefore, SDH inhibitors may be helpful in treating these diseases. Malonate and itaconate both have structural similarities to succinate; thus, they can be used as inhibitors of SDH [ ]. Itaconate is a major marker of macrophage metabolic remodelling [ , ] and is formed by decarboxylation catalysed by cis-aconite decarboxylase (ACOD1) [ , ]. Itaconate, as an endogenous metabolite, can be processed into drugs for treating diseases without the need for gene editing tools [ ]. Studies have shown that both malonate and itaconate can inhibit the activity of SDH and alleviate the accumulation and oxidation of succinate, thereby preventing the production of mitochondrial ROS driven by complex I [ , ]. Suppressing mitochondrial ROS can maintain the activity of PHD and stabilize HIF-1α. This process blocks the transcription of IL-1β [ , , – ]. Their derivatives have very high membrane permeability, which increases the possibility of their clinical transformation [ ]. During localized ischaemia, the intravenous administration of dimethyl itaconate (DI), a derivative of itaconate, significantly reduces the myocardial infarct size, similar to the effects observed with dimethyl malonate [ ]. However, whether derivatives of itaconate act through the same mechanisms as itaconate in the regulation of immune responses remains highly debated [ ]. One study revealed that mice can tolerate large, repeated doses of malonate without adverse effects. Acidic malonate enhances its uptake in cardiomyocytes through MCT1 (widely expressed in human cardiac tissue), which enables even low doses of malonate to achieve long-term cardioprotective effects [ ]. Notably, during ischaemia, the local PH of myocardial tissue decreases, promoting the selective uptake of malonate in this high-risk tissue [ ].
In addition, after myocardial infarction, the inhibition of SDH by malonate might promote cardiomyocyte cell cycle activity via metabolic remodelling of the adult heart to a regenerative state, promoting the proliferation of myocardial cells and cardiac regeneration in adults [ ].
Itaconate also has other functions, such as antimicrobial effects. It inhibits the activity of bacterial isocitrate lyase [ ], thereby suppressing the glyoxylate shunt during bacterial infections. It also acts as an activator of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a modifier of cysteine residues, and a regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome [ ]. In summary, the use of malonate and itaconate may be an effective strategy for treating CVDs (Table  ).
 

## Inhibition of the succinate signalling pathway
SUCNR1 is widely expressed in human tissues, including epithelial cells of the intestine and kidney, white adipose tissue, and immune organs [ ]. A significant portion of accumulated succinate is selectively released into the circulation via MCT1. Once released into the circulation, it can be detected by the succinate receptor SUCNR1, triggering a series of signalling effects mediated by succinate [ ]. Therefore, inhibiting the binding of succinate to its receptor, SUCNR1, is equally important.
The succinate-SUCNR1 axis is involved in various pathological conditions and diseases, including inflammatory diseases, IRI-mediated organ infarction, and postinjury functional failure [ , ]. Studies have shown that SUCNR1 is selectively activated by succinate but not by any other intermediates of the TCA cycle or known ligands of G protein-coupled receptors [ ]. The half-maximal response concentrations for the succinate-induced activation of human and mouse SUCNR1 were 56 ± 8 μM and 28 ± 5 μM, respectively. Under normal physiological conditions, the plasma succinate level is too low to activate the receptor. However, since the plasma succinate concentration under normal physiological conditions is only approximately twofold lower than the level required for the half-maximal response, even a slight increase in the plasma succinate concentration could fully activate SUCNR1 [ ]. Therefore, inhibiting succinate-induced SUCNR1 signalling is crucial for treating diseases caused by elevated succinate levels [ ]. Recent studies have identified inhibitors of SUCNR1, such as the novel antagonist AK-968/12117473 (Cpd3) [ ] and the high-affinity, human-selective antagonist NF-56-EJ40 (Table  ) [ , , ].
The SUCNR1 inhibitor NF-56-EJ40 significantly blocks succinate/IL-1β signalling in human umbilical vein endothelial cells and macrophages. SUCNR1 is the sole pathway involved in succinate-mediated extracellular HIF-1α/IL-1β signalling, and research has revealed the potential to reduce the production of the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β by blocking SUCNR1 [ ]. Notably, succinate, as an extracellular signal of metabolic stress, initially acts primarily through SUCNR1 on M2 macrophages to help achieve a protective anti-inflammatory response. During prolonged metabolic stress, when other proinflammatory drivers shift the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages towards M1, SUCNR1 expressed on proinflammatory M1 macrophages can exacerbate the inflammatory response, which may be detrimental in chronic conditions. Therefore, the potential targeting of SUCNR1 should be approached in different ways according to the specific disease and stage of disease progression [ ]. Therefore, in the future, we can focus our attention on the development of highly specific and selective SUCNR1 inhibitors.

## Targeting SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation
Sirtuins are present in mammals and include seven members, namely, Sirt1 to Sirt7, which are a group of proteins with nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺)-dependent histone deacetylase and ADP-ribosyltransferase activities [ , ]. NAD⁺, also known as coenzyme I, is an essential electron carrier in the mitochondrial TCA cycle and the electron transport chain and plays a vital role in energy production. In addition, as a fundamental substrate for sirtuins, NAD⁺ is crucial for metabolic regulation [ ].
Research has demonstrated that SIRT3 and SIRT5 exert their effects by influencing related metabolic targets. Succinate dehydrogenase complex flavoprotein subunit A (SDHA) is a substrate for the deacetylase activity of SIRT3. The absence of SIRT3 leads to a decrease in SDH enzyme activity, indicating that SIRT3 may be an important physiological regulator of SDH activity [ ]. As a desuccinylase, SIRT5 desuccinylates and inactivates SDH [ ], which has protective effects on the myocardium. The continuous administration of NAD+ for 14 days significantly increases the total NAD+ concentration in myocardial tissue and promotes the interaction between SIRT5 and SDHA [ ], reducing SDH-dependent oxidation and exerting cardioprotective effects (Table  ).
Additionally, quercetin may promote the desuccinylation of IDH2 through SIRT5 (Table  ), maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, protect mouse cardiomyocytes under inflammatory conditions, and alleviate myocardial fibrosis, thereby reducing the incidence of heart failure [ ].
In addition, in CoCl2-induced H9C2 cells, catalpol increases myocardial enzyme levels, the NAD+/NADH ratio, and ATP levels while inhibiting lactate, tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, and IL-6 levels in CoCl2-induced H9c2 cells. SIRT5 knockdown abolishes the effects of catalpol on cellular ATP levels, LD content, and the expression of inflammatory factors. Therefore, catalpol likely exerts its protective effects on myocardial function through a SIRT5-mediated signalling pathway (Table  ) [ ].

## Conclusion and future perspectives
With the continuous progress of research, it is becoming increasingly clear that succinate and its succinylation play critical roles in the development and progression of CVDs. Multiple clinical studies have shown that plasma and tissue levels of succinate are significantly elevated in patients with various cardiovascular conditions, including atherosclerosis, AAD, and myocardial hypertrophy, suggesting that succinate has potential as a key metabolic biomarker of disease progression.
During the occurrence of CVDs, metabolic remodelling promotes the accumulation of succinate, which aggravates CVDs through several key mechanisms. Intracellularly, succinate promotes substantial ROS generation under the catalysis of SDH, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Extracellularly, succinate acts as a signalling molecule by binding to its receptor SUCNR1, activating downstream inflammatory pathways and promoting vascular inflammation and remodelling. Furthermore, elevated succinate levels directly increase protein succinylation, which links the metabolic state with PTMs, making it unique compared with other PTMs. Succinylation modulates enzyme activity and gene transcription and extensively participates in the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism, thereby playing unique roles in cardiovascular metabolic remodelling. However, some limitations exist in the mechanistic exploration of succinate and succinylation in CVDs. First, current mechanistic studies rely primarily on cell and animal models, with limited human tissue data. Species differences may restrict the clinical applicability of the research findings. Second, the exploration of succinylation modification in CVDs remains relatively scarce, and complete evidence supporting the role of succinylation modification in CVD is lacking.
Targeting the succinate metabolic pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic direction. For instance, competitive SDH inhibitors such as malonate and itaconate effectively inhibit succinate oxidation, reduce ROS production, and alleviate inflammatory responses, demonstrating protective effects in animal models of ischaemic heart disease and aortic dissection. Their derivatives, including 4-OI, exhibit improved cellular permeability and stability. On the other hand, the SUCNR1 inhibitor NF-56-EJ40 specifically blocks extracellular succinate signalling, suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and has anti-inflammatory and anti-remodelling effects in models of atherosclerosis and myocardial hypertrophy. Additionally, SIRT5, a desuccinylase, can reverse pathological protein succinylation when activated by agents such as NAD⁺, mitigating oxidative damage in myocardial ischaemia‒reperfusion injury. Among these compounds, malonate and itaconate are currently in the preclinical stage, whereas NAD+ is in the experimental validation phase. There is a lack of developed small-molecule drugs with high specificity, low toxicity, and favourable pharmacokinetics, indicating that clinical translation remains a somewhat distant future endeavour. However, high-affinity human-selective small molecules targeting SUCNR1 inhibitors have been identified, demonstrating potential for clinical translation, and are currently in the transition stage from preclinical to clinical development; however, there is still a lack of approved drugs. The greatest current obstacle is whether drugs can be precisely delivered to the disease site without causing adverse reactions in other areas. Recent studies have demonstrated that itaconate promotes inflammatory responses in alveolar macrophages, which is distinct from its effects on bone marrow-derived macrophages [ ]. The off-target effects of malonate and itaconate must be addressed before clinical translation. Moreover, since succinate and succinylation are involved in fundamental energy metabolism, broad inhibition may lead to systemic side effects. Therefore, targeted delivery of drugs becomes crucial.
To understand the relationships between succinate and succinylation modifications and human CVDs, it is necessary to conduct large-scale prospective cohort studies that integrate metabolomics, proteomics, succinylation omics and metagenomics data to construct predictive disease models. Moreover, there is a need to develop noninvasive dynamic monitoring technologies to overcome the limitations of tissue biopsy. Because few studies have investigated succinylation in CVDs, there is a need for increased research on succinylation in CVDs and elucidation of the pathogenic mechanisms of succinylation in these diseases. In terms of therapeutic strategies, emphasis should be placed on advancing targeted delivery systems, utilizing functionalized nanoparticles to achieve precise drug distribution at lesion sites, and optimizing the pharmacokinetic properties of lead compounds through medicinal chemistry approaches. Furthermore, systematic evaluation of drug tissue specificity and safety is essential. In addition, regulators of succinyltransferase may be promising measures for the future treatment and intervention of CVDs. John R. Ussher’s team demonstrated that pimozide can inhibit the activity of OXCT1. Preclinical models have shown that pimozide can suppress OXCT1-induced hyperglycaemia. This small molecule has been approved by the FDA and has excellent safety. However, it is able to cross the blood‒brain barrier and is mostly used to treat mental disorders. Systematic screening of succinyltransferase inhibitors may become a future research direction targeting the succinylation pathway. In addition, metabolic regulators may be able to suppress the levels of succinate and succinylation by restoring normal metabolism. For instance, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors protect the heart by regulating metabolic remodelling [ ], whereas histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors inhibit metabolic remodelling by targeting superenhancers, inhibiting glycolysis and promoting oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. Cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) inhibitors reduce inflammation by inhibiting glycolysis [ ], all of which may slow the occurrence of the disease by reducing the pathological damage induced by succinate and succinylation. Among these inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, have demonstrated protective effects against heart failure and diabetes in clinical trials [ ] and have been approved by the FDA for these indications, with high potential for transformation. HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat have been used to treat cancer [ ], but their role in CVD progression is still unclear. The CDK5 inhibitor roscovitine is still in the preclinical research stage and mainly targets neurological diseases, but it faces challenges in clinical translation, such as selectivity and toxicity issues [ ].
In summary, the succinate-succinylation axis serves as a critical bridge linking metabolic dysregulation and cardiovascular pathological changes, not only deepening our understanding of CVD mechanisms but also providing new targets and strategies for metabolic intervention. Through multiomics integration and precision medicine approaches, this field holds great promise for achieving breakthroughs in both diagnosis and treatment.
