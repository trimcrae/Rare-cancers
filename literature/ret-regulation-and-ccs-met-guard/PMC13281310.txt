## Abstract

## Background
Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) is a widely used biomarker for immunotherapy in melanoma. The expression of PD-L1 in melanoma cells is known to vary considerably among and within patients’ tumor samples. Recent studies suggest that there may be a link between PD-L1 expression and the differentiation status of melanoma cells which is known to fluctuate in response to external stimuli and to be influenced by a multitude of regulators. Here, we systematically review which differentiation regulators affect PD-L1 expression in melanoma.

## Methods
A systematic review was performed on studies collected through PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Knowledge up until February 13th, 2026. Screening of abstracts and titles was performed independently by two reviewers. Screening of full-text articles, data extraction, and validity assessments were performed by one reviewer. Studies published in English reporting changes in PD-L1 expression associated with a known differentiation regulator in a melanocytic/melanoma model were included. Studies were assessed for risk of bias regarding imprecision in experimental reporting and model validity, using previously defined assessment tools. Synthesis of results was performed narratively.

## Results
Out of 496 identified articles, 57 studies met the inclusion criteria. A total of 16 differentiation regulators were significantly associated with PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells. Most studies (45/57) reported a single regulator, while 12/57 reported multiple. STAT3 appeared in 20/57 studies; all other regulators were reported in eight or fewer. PD-L1 expression was positively associated with all dedifferentiation-linked regulators (9/9). Among differentiation-linked regulators, 3/4 (MITF, SOX10, IRF4) showed contrasting associations depending on the PD-L1 expression mode and study. In total, 120 human and 10 mouse melanoma cell lines were used. The A375, SK-MEL-28, and B16 cell lines were used in 20, 18, and 28 studies, respectively, suggesting lineage bias. Most studies had unclear risk of bias regarding imprecision (82%) and model validity (70%).

## Conclusions
PD-L1 expression is commonly affected by differentiation regulators in melanoma cells, linking high PD-L1 expression to dedifferentiated cell states. The various regulators and inducible pathways that affect PD-L1 may in turn explain the heterogeneity observed in PD-L1 expression between and within patients with melanoma. Owing to the clinical significance of PD-L1 expression as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response, it is crucial to better understand the impact of various regulators on its expression.

## Background
Programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) is a predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor response in multiple solid cancers, including cutaneous melanomas [ – ]. Cutaneous melanomas tend to have high PD-L1 expression relative to other solid tumors [ ] and other melanoma subtypes [ , ]. However, PD-L1 expression is also known to vary considerably among melanoma patient samples [ , ] and between primary tumors and different metastatic sites within the same patient [ ]. Consequently, the reliability of PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy is actively debated [ , ].
This dilemma is also reflected in vitro, where efforts to characterize the molecular mechanisms of PD-L1 regulation have revealed highly heterogeneous PD-L1 expression levels between melanoma cell lines, under both basal and inducible conditions [ – ]. Being an immunological modulator, PD-L1 is typically regulated through pro-inflammatory cytokine-mediated signaling cascades [ , ]. However, inflammatory signaling is also capable of inducing phenotype switching in melanoma cells, a process characterized by reversible dedifferentiation and increased inflammatory responsiveness [ – ]. This creates a potential crosstalk between the differentiation state of melanoma cells and their ability to express PD-L1. Essentially, melanoma dedifferentiation occurs through loss of microphthalmia associated transcription factor (MITF), the master regulator of the melanocytic lineage [ , ] followed by gradual loss of melanocytic gene expression and a gain of neural crest markers, along with alterations in inflammatory responses [ , , – ].
We have shown an interaction between differentiation state and interferon gamma (IFNγ)-induced PD-L1 expression, indicating that PD-L1 expression is contextually tied to differentiation status in melanoma cells [ ]. Similar results have been published by others, including single-cell transcriptomic analyses hinting at increased PD-L1 expression among dedifferentiated melanoma cells [ , ]. Given the fact that PD-L1 expression is widely characterized in melanoma, and that melanoma dedifferentiation may be induced by multiple distinct pathways and transcriptional regulators [ – , – ], we hypothesized that the existing literature may collectively reveal a clearer picture of the association between differentiation status and PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells, if reviewed systematically. Thus, the aim of this systematic review is to clarify the relationship between differentiation regulators and PD-L1 expression in melanoma model systems.

## Methods
This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) checklist [ ]. The review was not registered.

## Definitions
We focused on regulators known to affect the differentiation status of melanoma by affecting the expression or activity of MITF. Their respective association with differentiation status was based on whether they affected MITF in a positive or negative manner. First, regulatory factors were identified in relevant reviews on MITF and melanoma biology [ , , ] and by assessing the literature covering immune evasion in melanoma. Then, additional literature searches were performed to curate further evidence for the effect of each factor on MITF expression or activity, and to assess which factor should be considered in the search strategy. A list of all factors identified is provided in Supplementary File S1. Note that this should by no means be viewed as an exhaustive list of all factors that can influence MITF expression and/or activity in melanoma.
Different modes of PD-L1 expression were defined as either basal, induced, constitutive, or unclear. The basal category encapsulates PD-L1 expression reported under standard cell culture conditions. Reports of PD-L1 upregulation following treatment with exogenous stimulants or environmental manipulation (e.g., IFNγ, hypoxia) were categorized as induced. Importantly, the definition of induced PD-L1 expression was kept open-ended, and any stimulant that was shown to cause increased PD-L1 expression compared to an untreated control was classified as PD-L1 inducing for the purposes of this review. The definition of constitutive expression relied on the descriptions provided in each respective study that used the term. In essence, constitutive expression included high baseline expression of PD-L1 following the development of therapy resistance compared with treatment naive cells and/or high baseline expression of PD-L1 along with reduced/limited responses to known PD-L1 stimulants. The unclear category included cases where the experimental descriptions and/or complex sample material did not provide a transparent way of deducing whether PD-L1 was being induced or not (e.g., patient biopsy samples and in vivo tumor samples).

## Literature search
The purpose of the search was to identify studies providing evidence that link known regulators of melanoma differentiation status to PD-L1 expression in the melanocytic lineage. To that end, three databases were searched on May 2nd, 2024: PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Scopus (https://www.scopus.com/search/form.uri?display=basic#basic), and Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/wos/woscc/basic-search). Following a retroactive addition of STAT3 to the list of relevant MITF regulators, a separate search was conducted in all three databases on August 20th, 2024, utilizing the same databases to identify studies linking STAT3 to PD-L1 expression in melanoma. Studies were also identified by manually searching the reference lists of relevant reviews and the already included articles. The main search strategy was as follows:
(“melanoma”) AND (“differentiation” OR “differentiated” OR “dedifferentiation” OR “dedifferentiated” OR “undifferentiated” OR “senescent” OR “EMT” OR “Epithelial-mesenchymal transition” OR “MITF” OR “SOX10” OR “AP-1” OR “JUN” OR “c-JUN” OR “JUNB” OR “c-MYC” OR “MYC” OR “NGFR” OR “CD271” OR “AXL” OR “NF-κB” OR “NF-kappaB” OR “HIF-1α” OR “Hypoxia-inducible factor 1alpha” OR “SOCS1” OR “ZEB1” OR “SOX9” OR “IRF4” OR “NRF2” OR “GLI2” OR “CREB” OR “BRN2”) AND (“PD-L1” OR “PDL1” OR “B7H1” OR “CD274”).
The second search strategy specific to STAT3 was as follows:
(“melanoma”) AND (“STAT3”) AND (“PD-L1”).
An additional search was performed on February 13th, 2026, to update the review. This search was limited to the years spanning 2024–2026, and the search strategy consisted of the main search strategy with the addition of STAT3 among the other differentiation regulators.
Searches were limited to language (English) and document type (article). Searches were not limited by publication date nor publication status. Search settings were set to scope abstracts, titles, and keywords. All search results from each respective database were exported as RIS or NBIB files.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Original publications reporting changes in PD-L1 expression associated with phenotypic plasticity and/or differences in regulator expression/activity in melanoma and/or melanocyte models were included. The inclusion of melanocyte models was based on the considerable overlap of their molecular biology with that of melanoma cells, as melanocytes are the precursor cells of the disease [ ]. The inclusion criteria are summarized in Supplementary File S2.

## Study selection
Search results were uploaded to the reference manager Zotero (https://www.zotero.org/), which was then used to manually screen the records according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Prior to title and abstract screening, duplicate articles were removed and filtered based on the presence of both the terms “melanoma” and “PD-L1” in the title and/or abstract of the article. Two reviewers (T.S., H.N.V.) independently assessed the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles, on the basis of the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were solved through discussion. Records included after the title abstract screen were acquired in full-text and assessed according to the inclusion criteria by one reviewer (T.S.). Papers excluded at this stage were logged along with the reason for exclusion, available in Supplementary File S3.

## Quality assessment
Quality assessment was performed by one reviewer (T.S.). The included studies were evaluated in terms of imprecision and model validity using the respective imprecision assessment and model validity assessment tools developed and shared by Collins et al. (2017) [ ]. The model validity assessment tool was modified to better address the relevant features of cell culture experiments. Individual results for each included study and the details for the imprecision and model validity tools can be found in Supplementary Files S4 and S5, respectively.

## Data extraction and synthesis
A narrative summary of all included studies was compiled. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer (T.S.). Included studies were identified by the first author’s surname and year of publication. Data was collected on which country the study was conducted in, which differentiation regulator was reported, the type of evidence (correlational or functional), mode of PD-L1 expression, the melanoma models used in each study, the level of PD-L1 detection as well as method(s) of detection. The extracted data are reported alongside quality assessment results in Supplementary File S6. A direction score metric was derived for each regulator on the basis of how many studies reported a positive, negative, mixed, or nonsignificant association with PD-L1 expression, calculated as:
 Where the direction score of any regulator (i) is equal to the difference between the number of studies reporting a strictly positive association with PD-L1 expression and those reporting a strictly negative association, divided by the total number of studies that report said regulator.

## Results

## Literature searches and inclusion assessments
A summary of the study identification and selection processes for this systematic review is presented in Fig.  , in line with the PRISMA reporting guidelines [ ]. Collectively, searches of electronic databases yielded 1163 records. After removing duplicate studies and additional filtering for the keyword’s “melanoma” and “PD-L1,” a total of 487 records remained. Title and abstract screening resulted in a total of 73 included studies, which were subsequently retrieved in full-text form. Manual searching resulted in nine additional studies being included for full-text screening. Full-text screening resulted in a total of 57 studies being included, with a total of 21 studies being excluded at this stage for reasons depicted in Fig.  . Four studies proved unobtainable.
 

## Differentiation regulators and PD-L1 expression in melanoma models
A summary of all included studies, their respective study designs, regulators reported, assays used to measure PD-L1 expression, and the main result of each study relevant to this review are depicted in Table  . An additional list of all included studies, study country, the reported differentiation regulators in each study, type of evidence, association with PD-L1 expression, the melanoma models used, mode of PD-L1 expression, and detection are presented in Supplementary File S6.
 We identified 57 relevant studies with publication years ranging from 2013 to 2026. Most studies (45/57, 79%) reported only one regulator, while 12/57 (21%) studies reported more than one (Table  , Supplementary File S6). STAT3 was most frequently associated with PD-L1 expression in melanoma models, appearing in 20/57 studies (35%) (Fig.  A). Of the other regulators, only MITF, SOX10, MYC, HIF1α, NF-κB, and JUN appeared in more than five studies (Fig.  A). Regulators associated with dedifferentiation generally had a consistent positive association with PD-L1 expression across the literature, while the evidence for differentiation-linked regulators was less consistent (Fig.  B). Notably, the evidence for the association of MITF and IRF4 on IFNγ-induced PD-L1 proved inconsistent across the included studies, while SOX10 had a consistent negative association and MYC had a consistent positive one (Fig.  B). MITF and SOX10 similarly showed inconsistent associations with basal PD-L1 expression, while IRF4 and MYC were positively and consistently associated with this mode of expression (Fig.  B).
 The majority of the studies reported results from multiple cell lines (46/57, 81%), whereas 11/57 studies (19%) only reported results from a single melanoma cell line (Table  , Supplementary File S6). Collectively, a total of 120 human melanoma or skin melanocyte cell lines and 10 mouse melanoma cell lines were used among the 57 studies (Table  , Supplementary File S6). Among the human melanoma cell lines, two appeared to be most frequently utilized, with the human melanoma cell lines A375 (and its derivatives) and SK-MEL-28 each appearing in 20/57 (35%) and 18/57 (32%) of studies, respectively (Fig.  A). Similarly, the mouse melanoma cell line B16 (and its derivatives) was used in 28/33 (85%) studies reporting the use of mouse melanoma cell lines (Fig.  B). A summary of cell lines/sample material, treatments and risk of bias assessment per study is presented in Table  .
 Collectively, this shows that PD-L1 expression has been quantified in a wide variety of melanoma cell lines. Simultaneously, much of the evidence regarding PD-L1 regulation by the regulators in question may be restricted to only a handful of these cell lines. By extension, the evidence may also be restricted to specific phenotypic contexts, in terms of the mutational background and baseline differentiation of the respective cell lines.

## Omics studies indicate a negative association between differentiation and PD-L1 expression in cutaneous melanoma
Three studies (3/57, 5%) primarily focus on omics-based research utilizing RNA sequencing [ , ] or methylation bead chip arrays [ ]. While analyzing different subgroups of melanoma cells, these studies collectively indicate an inverse relationship between melanoma differentiation and PD-L1 expression.
In one study, Ahn et al. (2021) [ ] demonstrated that melanoma cell lines displaying constitutive PD-L1 expression (PD-L1CON) in the absence of external stimuli are undifferentiated, with significant downregulation of both MITF and SOX10 [ ]. This was compared with melanoma cell lines without constitutive PD-L1 expression and low levels of inducible PD-L1 expression (PD-L1IND), which displayed either melanocytic or neural-crest-like transcriptional profiles relative to the PD-L1CON lines [ ]. Furthermore, the PD-L1CON cell lines were characterized by a loss of oxidative phosphorylation gene set signature and increased TNF and IFN response signatures compared with the PD-L1IND group [ ], characteristics which have previously been linked to dedifferentiation in melanoma [ , ]. The authors proposed that the activation of viral mimicry pathways through the expression of endogenous retroviral elements could facilitate the constitutive activation of IFN pathways and PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells. Supporting this hypothesis, the PD-L1CON melanoma cells also displayed significant upregulation of genes involved in dsRNA sensing and viral mimicry responses [ ]. However, functional evidence of viral mimicry pathways regulating PD-L1 in the melanoma cell lines was not presented.
Another study demonstrated how heterogeneity in differentiation influences PD-L1 RNA expression in melanoma cells [ ]. Subhadarshini et al. (2023) [ ] conceptualized a minimal gene regulatory network to determine the differentiation states of melanoma cells, composed of the transcription factors MITF, SOX10, SOX9, JUN, and ZEB1 [ ]. Both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression appeared to be increased in the invasive/undifferentiated melanoma cells (MITFlow/SOX10low/JUNhigh/ZEB1high/SOX9high) [ ]. These findings were partially corroborated by in vitro experiments using three melanoma cell lines, A375, Malme-3M, and SK-MEL-5, but were not strongly recapitulated in other datasets, such as the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [ ].
In the third study, Conway et al. (2022) [ ] characterized the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP), an aggressive melanoma subphenotype associated with poor prognosis [ , ], using 89 samples from primary invasive melanomas. Regions at the transcription start site (TSS) of MITF and multiple other genes associated with melanocytic differentiation were significantly hypomethylated in CIMP compared with low methylation (LM) in melanoma [ ]. Simultaneously, the transcription start site of CD274, the gene encoding PD-L1, was significantly hypermethylated in CIMP samples [ ]. However, the expression of MITF and PD-L1 was not reported in the melanoma samples used for the methylation analysis. Using data from TCGA, the authors demonstrate that CD274 expression tends to be inversely correlated with CpG methylation [ ]. The same has been reported for MITF and other melanocytic genes [ ]. As such, these external sources suggest that CIMP represents a relatively differentiated melanoma phenotype with low PD-L1 expression. However, this remains unclear without direct analysis of the differentiation status and PD-L1 expression in CIMP and LM melanoma samples.
Despite applying different approaches in terms of methodology and groups of comparison, these three studies consistently indicate that the degree of differentiation in melanoma cells inversely influences both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression [ , , ].

## Contrasting evidence for the regulation of PD-L1 by differentiation factors
A link between PD-L1 and MITF was observed in seven studies. MITF expression was negatively associated with PD-L1 expression in four studies [ , , , ], positively associated in three studies [ , , ], and contextually associated in one study [ ]. All three aforementioned omics-based studies reported a negative correlation between MITF and PD-L1 in terms of RNA expression [ , ] or TSS methylation status [ ] in multiple melanoma cell lines and patient-derived samples. In the fourth study reporting a negative link between MITF and PD-L1, Zingg et al. (2017) [ ] demonstrated that a gain in Mitf gene expression following Ezh2 knockdown in the mouse melanoma cell lines B16F10 and RIM-3 was accompanied by reduced PD-L1 expression, measured by RNA sequencing and immunofluorescent staining [ ]. Of the two studies reporting a positive correlation between MITF and PD-L1 expression, Vučinić et al. (2022) [ ] reported coexpression of MITF and PD-L1 proteins as measured via immunohistochemical staining of 52 melanoma patient biopsies [ ], while Lo (2016) [ ] showed how MITF knockdown resulted in reduced PD-L1 RNA expression in primary human melanocytes and the melanoma cell lines 501Mel and SK-MEL-30, with PD-L1 protein expression similarly reduced in both melanoma cell lines [ ]. Furthermore, Lo (2016) [ ] showed by using a ChIP assay that MITF can bind to a putative PD-L1 enhancer element in primary human melanocytes, and luciferase assays in 501Mel and U62 melanoma cells indicated a positive regulatory effect by MITF [ ]. Similarly, Cerioli et al. (2025) [ ] reported concordant downregulation of MITF and both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells following esomeprazole treatment. In contrast, Sævarsson et al. (2024) [ ] reported that MITF knockdown led to a significant increase in IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in 624Mel melanoma cells, mediated through a JAK/STAT1/IRF1 regulatory axis [ ]. The same was not observed in A375P, SK-MEL-28, Malme-3M, YUMM1.7, and B16 cells, where both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression was either slightly negatively affected (B16) or unaffected by MITF knockdown [ ]. The same study additionally reported a significant albeit weak (r = −0.4) negative correlation between MITF and PD-L1 gene expression in 45 additional melanoma cell lines [ ]. PD-L1 regulation by MITF in melanoma cells seems supported by conflicting evidence, indicating that the relationship between the two is dependent on a wider molecular context that is not encapsulated by MITF expression or differentiation levels alone.
Similar to MITF, SOX10 was reported to have a negative correlation with PD-L1 expression in all three omics studies [ , , ] and following Ezh2 suppression in mouse melanoma cells [ ]. However, the functional evidence presented in four additional studies was not fully consistent with these findings [ , , , ]. Yokoyama et al. (2021) [ ] reported significantly increased basal PD-L1 expression following SOX10 knockdown in UACC257, A2058, A375, and MeWo melanoma cells [ ]. Similarly, IFNγ-induced expression of PD-L1 was significantly increased in UACC257, A2058, SK-MEL-28, and Malme-3M cells following SOX10 loss [ ]. This was mediated through an inhibitory axis of SOX10/IRF4/IRF1, and independent of JAK activity [ ]. In addition, Martinkova et al. (2024) [ ] provided further evidence for negative regulation of PD-L1 by SOX10 through IRF1 suppression in A375 cells [ ]. SOX10 itself was shown to be dependent on p53 status, as p53 knockout in A375 cells resulted in SOX10 loss and a subsequent gain in IRF1 and PD-L1 expression [ ]. However, these results were not recapitulated in a panel of p53 wild-type and p53-mutated melanoma cell lines [ ]. In contrast, Sasaki et al. (2023) [ ] reported a gain in both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression upon SOX10 overexpression in A375, resulting in suppressed immunogenicity in co-culture with TCR-redirected T cells [ ]. Similarly, a significant positive relationship between SOX10 and PD-L1 RNA expression was reported by Sasaki et al. (2023) [ ] in SK-MEL-28 and 888-mel cells by applying both knockdown and overexpression of SOX10 [ ]. Likewise, Zhang et al. (2025) [ ] reported an increase in basal PD-L1 expression following SOX10 overexpression in A375 and B16F10 cells, and a corresponding decrease upon SOX10 knockdown.
Three studies reported functional but contradicting evidence for the role of IRF4 in PD-L1 regulation [ , , ]. As previously mentioned, Yokoyama et al. (2021) [ ] reported that SOX10 negatively regulates PD-L1 through IRF4 [ ]. The involvement of IRF4 was further confirmed in UACC257 melanoma cells by siRNA-mediated IRF4 knockdown, which resulted in significantly increased PD-L1 expression [ ]. However, Liu et al. (2021) [ ] reported increased PD-L1 levels mediated by an IRF4/EGR1 axis following inhibition of the cystine/glutamate transporter (xCT) in B16F10 and SK-MEL-5 melanoma cells [ ]. This was further supported by direct knockdown of the SLC7A11 gene (encoding xCT) in both melanoma cell lines, as well as by IRF4 and EGR1 overexpression in SK-MEL5 and SK-MEL-28 cells [ ]. In a dual luciferase reporter assay, both IRF4 and EGR1 were shown to bind to the PD-L1 promoter and mediate luciferase expression when transfected into HEK-293T cells, but those experiments were not extended to the melanocytic lineage [ ]. Lastly, a single nucleotide polymorphism in intron 4 of the IRF4 gene, termed rs12203592*C/T, was shown by Chhabra et al. (2018) [ ] to influence both melanocytic gene expression and PD-L1 expression in human primary melanoblasts [ ]. More specifically, the rs12203592 T/T allele was reported to cause decreased expression of melanocytic proteins (MITF and TYR) and significantly decreased IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression compared with the C/C allele in multiple strains of melanoblasts, while direct IRF4 knockdown reduced both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in C/C melanoblast strains [ ].
The proto-oncogene MYC has been reported to facilitate differentiation in human primary melanocytes [ ] and was defined as a differentiation regulator for the purpose of this review. Five studies [ , , , , ] associate significant downregulation of either basal or IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression with loss of MYC expression. In these studies, MYC expression was affected by in vitro treatment of melanoma cell lines with either oxymatrine [ ] or the BRD4-targeting drugs JQ1 [ , , ], dBET1, and dBET6 [ ]. Li et al. (2023) [ ] and Casey et al. (2016) [ ] provided additional evidence that MYC knockdown alone directly results in significant, albeit moderate, downregulation of basal PD-L1 expression in B16 [ ] and SK-MEL-28 [ ] melanoma cells. Additionally, Li et al. (2023) [ ] showed that oxymatrine treatment had similar effects on PD-L1 levels in vivo when administered to C57BL/6 mice engrafted with B16 melanoma cells [ ]. Furthermore, Casey et al. (2016) [ ] reported that MYC binds directly to the PD-L1 promoter in mouse lymphoblastic leukemia cells [ ], but whether those results apply to the melanocytic lineage is not addressed. Lastly, Tong et al. (2022) [ ] reported coexpression of the PD-L1 and MYC proteins in three melanoma cell lines following IFNγ treatment, without claiming a regulatory relationship between the two [ ]. Collectively, these studies indicate that MYC might positively affect PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells, while functional evidence remains lacking.

## PD-L1 regulation by factors with contextual ties to melanoma differentiation
CREB is known to support melanocytic differentiation by directly regulating MITF expression via cAMP signaling [ ]. However, metabolic rewiring of melanocytes towards increased glycolysis can lead to MITF suppression by AMPK, which inhibits the CREB cofactors CRTC2/3, while maintaining CREB activity [ ]. CREB was defined to have contextual ties to melanoma differentiation. Liu et al. (2023) [ ] reported a positive association between CREB and basal PD-L1 expression following TCA inhibition and increased glycolytic activity in SK-MEL-5 and A375 melanoma cells [ ]. Upregulation of PD-L1 was observed following TCA inhibition and attributed to an AMPK/CREB/ATF3 regulatory axis [ ]. ATF3 knockdown confirmed its involvement in PD-L1 regulation, but functional evidence for the involvement of CREB upstream of ATF3 was not provided [ ]. In contrast, Berzaghi et al. (2017) [ ] observed an increase in CREB activity along with reduced PD-L1 expression in BF16F10-Nex2 melanoma cells with no functional consequence reported. Collectively, the evidence linking CREB with basal PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells is contradictory [ , ]. Similarly, functional evidence for the direct involvement of CREB in PD-L1 expression is lacking.
Another contextual regulator is HIF1α, which can facilitate both up- and downregulation of MITF in response to hypoxia [ , ]. HIF1α has been positively associated with PD-L1 expression in six studies [ , , , , , ]. Using a transgenic C57BL/6 mouse model expressing the RET oncogene specifically in melanocytes, Flores-Guzmán et al. (2020) [ ] reported a stem-like CD133-positive subpopulation of melanoma cells which had elevated levels of HIF1α, PD-L1, and NGFR compared with CD133-negative melanoma cells [ ]. Four studies provide correlational evidence of a positive association between HIF1α and PD-L1 expression under hypoxic conditions, with or without IFNγ stimulation in B16 [ ], B16F10 [ , ], A375, SK-MEL-28, and WM115 [ ] melanoma cell lines. However, despite seeing PD-L1 expression positively correlating with HIF1α gene signatures in melanoma tumors, van Duijn et al. (2022) [ ] reported that hypoxic conditions have different effects on PD-L1 mRNA expression depending on the melanoma cell lines studied [ ]. Subjecting melanoma cell lines to hypoxic conditions consistently induced HIF1α expression [ ]. PD-L1 mRNA expression was either significantly upregulated (in MelWBO, Mel136.2, and B16F10 cells) or downregulated (in MelAKR, MelJUSO, and Mel88.23 cells) depending on the cell line, whereas PD-L1 protein expression was not significantly affected in any of the melanoma cell lines tested (MelAKR, MelJUSO, Mel88.23, Mel136.2, and MelWBO) [ ]. Furthermore, while the HIF stabilizing agent and hypoxia mimic DFO (deferoxamine mesylate salt) induced PD-L1 expression in MelJUSO, MelWBO, and Mel136.2 melanoma cells, HIF1α only seemed to positively affect the DFO-induced PD-L1 expression in the MelWBO cell line, analyzed via HIF1α knockdown [ ]. Similarly, depending on the cell line, the combination of IFNγ treatment and hypoxia led to either an increase or decrease in PD-L1 mRNA expression compared with IFNγ treatment alone [ ]. Again, PD-L1 protein expression was rarely affected, only showing significant changes in the MelAKR cell line [ ]. Additionally, while HIF1α seemed to influence IFNγ-induced PD-L1 promoter activity in a contextual manner, no HIF1α consensus binding sites are present in the PD-L1 promoter region [ ]. Collectively, HIF1α may influence PD-L1 mRNA expression in an indirect and contextual manner depending on the melanoma cells in question. So far, PD-L1 protein expression has mainly been associated with HIF1α in the B16 melanoma cell lineage. Functional evidence for a regulatory role of HIF1α has not yet been presented.

## PD-L1 is frequently upregulated by dedifferentiation factors in cutaneous melanoma
Zingg et al. (2017) [ ] provided detailed evidence for how the histone methyltransferase EZH2 regulates both differentiation and immunogenicity in B16F10 and RIM-3 melanoma tumors [ ]. EZH2 will be considered an independent regulator in this review, despite not appearing in the initial search strategy. Inhibiting EZH2 activity was shown to facilitate differentiation in B16F10 tumors, characterized by significantly increased expression of melanocytic genes including Mitf, Sox10, Tyr, Dct, Tyrp1, Pmel, and Mlana. Genes associated with dedifferentiation and/or EMT in melanoma (Zeb1, Cd271, Zeb2, Twist, Snai1) where significantly downregulated along with Pdl1 gene expression [ ]. EZH2 inhibition led to decreased PD-L1 protein expression in RIM-3 melanoma tumors, irrespective of tumor T cell infiltrate levels [ ]. Whether the PD-L1 expression was basal or induced in the melanoma cells could not be properly deduced. However, the association between EZH2 and PD-L1 expression seems consistently negative between models in this single study.
STAT3 was positively associated with basal [ , – , , , , , , , , ], constitutive [ ], IFNγ-induced [ , , ], IL-6 induced [ ], and nicotine-induced [ ] PD-L1 expression. Some studies provide solely correlational data, linking STAT3 to basal [ , – , , , , ] or IFNγ-induced [ , ] PD-L1 expression in melanoma without direct functional evidence. Chen et al. (2022) [ ] and Zeng et al. (2025) [ ] specifically associated increased PD-L1 expression with EMT-like melanoma phenotypes, characterized by increased expression of the EMT markers ZEB1, SNAIL, N-cadherin, Vimentin, MMP2, and MMP9 in addition to STAT3 [ , ]. Functional evidence provided in other studies collectively indicates that STAT3 physically binds to the PD-L1 promoter in melanoma cells [ , , , ]. This binding may be dependent on cofactors such as EGFR [ ] and HOXA5 [ ], and can be further influenced by epigenetic regulators such as HDAC6 and HDAC8 [ , ]. However, evidence for the regulatory relationship between STAT3 and PD-L1 is inconclusive. While many studies showed direct manipulation of STAT3 activity consistently affecting PD-L1 expression across melanoma cell lines [ , , , , , , ], four studies reported either minor or no effects on PD-L1 expression, be it constitutive [ ] or induced by either IFNγ [ , ], IL-6, or IL-27 [ ]. In summary, cumulative evidence points towards a positive yet incompletely defined role for STAT3 in PD-L1 regulation through multiple distinct pathways.
JUN was positively associated with basal [ , , ] PD-L1 expression, with either positive or negligible effects on constitutive [ , ] and IFNγ-induced [ , , ] PD-L1 expression in different melanoma cell lines. Jiang et al. (2013) [ ] and Yu et al. (2022) [ ] reported decreased basal, constitutive, and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells following JUN knockdown or pharmacologically induced degradation of JUN [ , ]. Furthermore, Yu et al. (2022) [ ] reported an increase in PD-L1 expression following JUN overexpression in B16F10 melanoma cells. They also reported that JUN is capable of binding to the PD-L1 promoter in SK-MEL-28 melanoma cells [ ]. In addition, Bhattacharyya et al. (2024) [ ] described a molecular mechanism where JUN positively influences basal PD-L1 expression by suppressing HDAC3 expression and/or activity, thus maintaining chromatin access at the PD-L1 locus, but the activity of JUN itself was regulated by the availability of free Galectin-3 in human primary melanocytes and A375 and B16F10 melanoma cells. Collectively, these studies indicate that JUN may directly regulate PD-L1 expression in melanoma, but that this association is likely dependent on a wider molecular context.
YAP1 was positively associated with basal [ ] and constitutive PD-L1 expression [ , ] in three studies. Ahn et al. (2021) [ ] reported enrichment of YAP1 signature genes in melanoma cells with constitutive PD-L1 expression [ ]. The other two studies provided functional evidence. Wang et al. (2024) [ ] reported that YAP1 facilitated the regulation of basal PD-L1 expression in A2058 melanoma cells; YAP1 mediated the binding of TFCP2 to the PD-L1 promoter which then induced PD-L1 expression [ ]. This was dependent on YAP1 stabilization via TRAF6 mediated K63-polyubiquitination, but the YAP1 dependency on downstream mechanisms was demonstrated through both YAP1 overexpression and knockout/knockdown experiments in A2058 melanoma cells [ ]. In another study, Kim et al. (2018) [ ] showed that constitutive PD-L1 expression in BRAFi resistant melanoma cells is specifically dependent on YAP1 [ ]. Overexpressing a constitutively active YAP1 protein was sufficient to induce constitutive PD-L1 expression, whereas YAP1 knockdown caused significant decreases in PD-L1 expression in BRAFi resistant melanoma cell lines [ ]. PD-L1 regulation by YAP1 was further associated with YAP1 binding to an enhancer element located ~13 kilobases upstream of the PD-L1 transcriptional start site in SK-MEL-28 and WM3248 melanoma cells [ ]. This element was characterized as a YAP-TEAD binding site, and inhibiting the interaction of YAP1 with TEAD transcription factors similarly decreased PD-L1 expression in the melanoma cell lines [ ]. Despite the limited number of studies, there is detailed evidence for a consistent positive regulation of PD-L1 by YAP1 in melanoma cells.
SOCS1 was positively associated with basal PD-L1 expression [ ]. Berzaghi et al. (2017) [ ] applied stable knockdown of SOCS1 in B16F10-Nex2 mouse melanoma cells, resulting in decreased PD-L1 expression as well as decreased p65 expression and STAT3 phosphorylation at serine residue 727 [ ]. Additionally, CREB was increasingly phosphorylated at serine residue 133 following SOCS1 knockdown [ ]. Functional evidence describing the involvement of SOCS1, STAT3, p65, or CREB in the regulation of PD-L1 in this melanoma model was not provided.
NGFR was associated with PD-L1 expression in five studies [ , , , , ]. Two studies, Alavi et al. (2018) [ ] and Furuta et al. (2014) [ ], specifically associate NGFR with IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression, where the two are co-expressed following IFNγ treatment [ , ]. Both studies reported that this relationship varies on the basis of the IFNγ responsiveness of individual cell lines [ , ]. Furthermore, Furuta et al. (2014) [ ] provided supplementary data showing that activating NGFR signaling via β-NGF treatment did not affect PD-L1 expression in 501Amel melanoma cells [ ]. Wirbel et al. (2025) [ ] reported long-term dual treatment with TNFα and TGFβ resulting in melanoma dedifferentiation, generating a NGFRhigh/ZEB1high/MITFlow cell population with significantly higher PD-L1 expression than their untreated counterparts [ ]. The other two studies have already been addressed, where PD-L1 and NGFR were reported to be coexpressed in CD133-positive melanoma cells [ ] and dedifferentiated melanoma cells in relation to the phenotypic regulation of EZH2 in melanoma [ ]. Collectively, this indicates that PD-L1 and NGFR associate by virtue of being co-regulated by IFNγ signaling, TNFα/TGFβ, EZH2, and an unknown mechanism which induces CD133-positive melanoma subphenotypes, whereas NGFR itself may not necessarily have direct effects on PD-L1 regulation.
NF-κB was positively associated with basal [ , , ], IFNγ-induced [ ], UVB-induced [ ], IL-6-, and TNFα-induced PD-L1 expression [ ] in melanoma cells. Associations with basal PD-L1 expression are correlational [ , , ]. Other studies provided functional evidence for the regulatory role of NF-κB. Gowrishankar et al. (2015) [ ] demonstrated that inhibiting NF-κB activity using two small molecule inhibitors, IBET or BMS-345541, abrogated IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression, whereas siRNA mediated knockdown of the NF-κB subunits p65, p50, and p105 had no significant effect on constitutive PD-L1 expression in KMJR138 melanoma cells [ ]. Similarly, Li et al. (2020) [ ] showed that NF-κB inhibition via withaferin A abrogated PD-L1 induction by IL-6 and TNFα stimulation in B16F1 melanoma cells [ ]. Lastly, Wang et al. (2019) [ ] showed that suppression of NF-κB via either p65 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition abrogated PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells following UVB radiation [ ]. Furthermore, Wang et al. (2019) [ ] showed via ChIP that NF-κB occupies predicted NF-κB binding sites in the PD-L1 promoter and facilitates its activation in SK-MEL-28 melanoma cells following UVB treatment [ ]. UVB induced PD-L1 expression was further shown to rely on the cooperation of NF-κB and IRF3, which in turn depend on autocrine and/or paracrine HMGB1 signaling through RAGE receptors on melanoma cells following UVB radiation [ ].
ZEB1 was positively associated with basal [ , ], IFNγ-induced [ ], TNFα-, and TGFβ-induced [ ] PD-L1 expression as well as one instance where the mode of PD-L1 expression is unclear [ ]. While three out of four studies provide solely correlational results, they consistently indicate that melanoma dedifferentiation via either Ezh2 [ ], looser matrix stiffness, or SNF5 overexpression [ ] leads to increased ZEB1 and PD-L1 expression. Similarly, ZEB1 expression has been linked to high PD-L1 levels in multiple melanoma datasets [ ]. Wirbel et al. (2025) [ ] provided functional evidence, reporting that inducing dedifferentiation through long-term treatment with TNFα and TGFβ leads to increased PD-L1 expression in GLO and C-09.10 melanoma cells [ ]. This was associated with MITF loss, whereas the expression of ZEB1 and NGFR was significantly increased [ ]. ZEB1 was furthermore shown to influence PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells, as ZEB1 knockout caused a significant, albeit moderate, decrease in PD-L1 expression in A375 melanoma cells [ ]. ChIP experiments further confirmed that ZEB1 binds to the PD-L1 promoter in all three melanoma cell lines, and that ZEB1 binding is enriched upon TNFα treatment in GLO and C-09.10 melanoma cells [ ].
NRF2 was positively associated with both UVB- and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in a single study [ ]. Zhu et al. (2018) [ ] provided functional evidence that NRF2 knockdown leads to decreased PD-L1 expression in human primary melanocytes (HPMs) following UVB or IFNγ treatment, and in engrafted B16-BL6 mouse melanoma cells [ ]. Similarly, overexpression of NRF2 resulted in increased mRNA and protein PD-L1 levels in HPMs [ ]. Lastly, following UVB treatment, NRF2 was shown to occupy a predicted binding site within an enhancer element of the PD-L1 regulatory region in HPMs [ ].
SOX9 was positively associated with basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in a single study [ ], whereas another showed no implications between SOX9 and PD-L1 in mouse melanoma cells [ ]. Both studies provide correlational evidence only and have already been addressed in this review.

## Risk of bias assessment
All studies were evaluated for risk of bias using the imprecision and model validity assessment tools conceptualized by Collins et al. (2017) [ ]. Detailed assessments for each domain of the imprecision and model validity tools are available in Supplementary Files S4 and S5, respectively. A minimum requirement for low risk of imprecision was to report technical and biological replicates as well as a measure of variation for relevant experiments. Most studies (82%, 47/57) were judged to have “unclear” risk of imprecision, while four (7%) studies were judged to have low risk and another six (11%) were deemed to have high risk of imprecision (Fig.  A). Similarly, the majority of studies (70%, 40/57) were judged to have “unclear” risk of model validity bias, whereas 10 (18%) studies had low risk, and seven (12%) studies were judged to have high risk of bias arising from model validity (Fig.  A).
 In most cases, authors did not disclose how many technical replicates were included in their experiments (Fig.  B). Similarly, while inter-assay repeats/biological replicates (i.e., “independent experiments”) were consistently reported in over half of the included studies, others either reported the number of inter-assay repeats for a limited number of relevant experiments, or not at all. The vast majority of studies consistently included a measure of variance in their quantitative data. However, only half of the studies provided clear descriptions of the statistical methods used to evaluate quantitative results (Fig.  B). Estimates of sample sizes, statistical power, or reports of how indeterminate results were handled were missing from all studies.
Regarding model validity, while most studies provided clear descriptions of model details and how models were manipulated during experimental procedures, only 8/54 (15%) applicable studies [ , , , , , , , ] disclosed that cell cultures were routinely checked for contaminants. Similarly, only 8/54 (15%) applicable studies [ , , , , , , , ] reported taking appropriate measures of cell line authentication (Fig.  B). In addition, less than half of all studies provided a clear description of routine maintenance (Fig.  C). All applicable studies provided an ethical statement, while 5/7 (71%) applicable studies [ , , , , ] did not provide proof of origin for primary tissues (Fig.  C).

## Discussion
The reliability of PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy in cutaneous melanoma is challenged by its heterogeneous expression levels in melanoma tumors and lack of standardized clinical assessment protocols [ ]. The fact that PD-L1 can also be expressed by other tumor-residing cells may further confound the clinical meaning of PD-L1 expression in any given case. Recent studies indicate that the clinical reliability of PD-L1 assessment may increase by stratifying tumor PD-L1 expression by cell type, reporting that tumor cell specific PD-L1 expression may have greater predictive and/or prognostic value over that of PD-L1 expression by infiltrating immune cells [ – ]. In addition, a recent study reported that the predictive value of PD-L1 expression in the adjuvant immunotherapy setting may itself be both cell-type and tissue-site specific, with tumor cell-specific PD-L1 expression having greater predictive value in melanoma metastasis, whereas the PD-L1 expression by tumor infiltrating immune cells appeared more predictive in primary tumors [ ]. Given these revelations, further understanding of the molecular mechanisms that facilitate PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells specifically and how they may or may not be intertwined with the phenotypic plasticity of the disease is of high relevance. Better understanding of the different molecular phenotypes that facilitate PD-L1 expression by melanoma cells may in turn help stratify PD-L1 positive patients that are more or less likely to benefit from immunotherapies. In addition, the established effects of dedifferentiation on the inflammatory responsiveness of melanoma cells and vice versa [ – ] provides a further rationale for combining immunotherapies with treatments aimed at modulating the differentiation state of cutaneous melanoma cells, as such phenotypic conditioning may help predispose melanoma cells to immunotherapies, but also help to alleviate the negative effects otherwise brought about by inflammation-induced shifts in differentiation levels.

## Summary of evidence
We have systematically reviewed the existing literature linking regulators of differentiation to PD-L1 expression in cutaneous melanoma. Among the 57 studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria, a total of 16 regulators (counting Ezh2) were found to have significant associations with PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells. In addition, PD-L1 expression was found to be inducible through a wide variety of stimulants, including inflammatory cytokines (IFNγ, IL-6, IL-27, and TNFα), growth factors (TGFβ), UVB irradiation, and a commonly consumed addictive alkaloid (nicotine) (Table  ). This is worth noting, as the number of inducible pathways acting on PD-L1 expression are likely to contribute to the heterogeneity in PD-L1 expression observed in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, some of the inflammatory cytokines mentioned, e.g., IL-6, are expressed by melanoma cells, especially following MITF knockdown [ ]. This invites the possibility that dedifferentiated melanoma cells engage in auto- and paracrine induction of PD-L1. This was for example noted by Garcia-Diaz et al. (2017), as they observed consistent IL-6 pathway activity in their melanoma cell lines [ ]. Auto- and paracrine IL-6 signaling may in turn partly explain the contradicting associations of STAT3 with IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression, as the former is known to be upregulated via IL-6 signaling [ ]. This, and other similar scenarios are otherwise rarely addressed in studies of PD-L1 expression between different melanoma phenotypes.
Importantly, associations between PD-L1 expression and that of any single differentiation regulator following bromodomain inhibitor treatment should be considered with care. Bromodomain inhibitors function by displacing bromodomain proteins from acetylated super-enhancer sequences, and can thus simultaneously affect multiple molecular pathways, including JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways, as well as influencing MYC expression [ ]. As such, it is probable that the effects of such treatments on PD-L1 expression are mediated through indirect effects on multiple regulators simultaneously.
All regulators shown to mediate dedifferentiation in melanoma cells were found to positively influence basal PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells, while their effects on inducible and constitutive modes of PD-L1 expression were not significantly positive across all studies (Fig.  A, B). Regulators mediating differentiation had differing and at times contradicting associations with PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells (Fig.  A). while studies on MYC indicated a consistent positive effect on both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in the melanoma models tested; reports on MITF, SOX10, and IRF4 are either positive or negative between and within studies when it comes to basal and/or IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression (Fig.  A, B). Similarly, regulators with contextual ties to differentiation such as CREB have evidence supporting either a positive or negative effect on PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells (Fig.  A, B). Together with the omics-based studies included in this review [ , , ], this suggests that PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells is associated with dedifferentiated cell states. In addition to direct regulation of PD-L1, certain dedifferentiation factors, e.g., JUN, may also indirectly influence PD-L1 expression through their downregulation of MITF [ ].
The inconsistency between studies reporting the effects of the same regulator (e.g., MITF, SOX10, and IRF4) on PD-L1 expression may be influenced by the mutational background of the cell lines in question. One included study demonstrated how p53 status influences the effects of SOX10 on PD-L1 expression in A375 melanoma cells [ ]. p53 is known to affect IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in melanoma cells [ ]. Similar to autocrine signaling, the mutational backgrounds of melanoma cell lines are rarely put into context with the results in the included studies, apart from the BRAF and/or NRAS mutational status. Publicly accessible resources, such as the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia, the DepMap project (https://depmap.org/portal) [ ] and the Cellosaurus database [ ] hold information on the mutational status of many melanoma cell lines that are commonly used in the included studies. Utilizing these resources better could provide additional context for how the phenotypic plasticity in melanoma cells affects their PD-L1 expression. In this regard, it is plausible that the contradicting evidence for the association of IRF4 with IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression stems from model differences in terms of mutational burden and/or developmental context. The evidence for a positive association of IRF4 with IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression presented by Chhabra et al. (2018) [ ] is derived from melanocytic cells induced from primary neonatal foreskin melanoblasts, while the evidence for a negative association presented by Yokoyama et al. (2021) [ ] is from immortalized melanoma cell lines, which originally derive from adult patients. Thus, this inconsistency may stem from the differences between healthy- and disease conditions or temporospatial differences in melanocytic function [ ].
A potential key difference between the studies reporting contrasting associations between MITF and PD-L1 expression is intracellular pH levels. Cerioli et al. (2025) [ ] applied esomeprazole treatment to raise the intracellular pH in 501Mel, LM38, LM47, and LM56 melanoma cells, and noted downregulation of MITF and both basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression. In contrast, the in vitro assays performed by Subhadarshini et al. (2023) [ ] include cytokine treatments with TNFα and TGFβ along with IFNγ. These cytokines have all been shown to facilitate acidification of intracellular compartments [ – ]. MITF is directly involved in pH regulation in melanocytes, and it has been demonstrated that PD-L1 expression by cancer cells is elevated as acidity increases [ , ]. As such, it is plausible that the association observed in esomeprazole-treated melanoma cells reflects concordant yet independent effects of increased cellular alkalinity on the expression of MITF and PD-L1.
Another potential explanation for the inconsistent findings regarding MITF may lie in codependency and/or redundancy with other differentiation regulators. For instance, Hejna et al. (2019) [ ] have demonstrated that MYC-MAX heterodimers compete with MITF for binding of specific E-box motifs, where decreasing binding by one factor coincided with increased binding by the other in COLO829 melanoma cells. Furthermore, E-box motifs favoring binding by MITF were characterized by colocalization of a SOX10 motif and DNA binding by SOX10 [ ]. This would indicate that SOX10 mediates MITF binding to DNA [ ] in addition to influencing MITF gene expression [ ]. As such, it is possible that the discrepancies observed between and within the included studies regarding PD-L1 regulation by MITF may in turn be influenced by the extent of SOX10 and/or MYC-MAX expression and their interactions with MITF in the different melanoma cell lines used. In addition, ZEB1 and p53 binding motifs were found to coincide with E-boxes preferentially bound by MYC-MAX and not by MITF [ ], which may suggest a link between PD-L1 regulation by MYC and ZEB1.
Similarly, post-translational modifications (PTMs) of the differentiation regulators may partly explain the contradicting evidence observed among the included studies. For instance, MITF, SOX10, and IRF4 can all be subjected to multiple different phosphorylation events which can either elevate or repress their transcriptional activity, as reviewed by Vu et al. (2021) [ ], Williams et al. (2020) [ ], and Wang et al. (2024) [ ], respectively. Variations in PTMs between melanoma cell lines may influence to which extent PD-L1 is regulated by these factors. However, PTMs were rarely investigated in the included studies. Current knowledge of the role of PTMs in regulating the transcriptional activity of MITF, SOX10, and IRF4 in melanoma is limited. The transcriptional activity of SOX10 has been shown to rely on SUMOylation at K55, and that the event may be interrupted by phosphorylation at T240 and/or T244 in melanoma cells [ ]. Additionally, SOX10 phosphorylation sites at S24 and S45 have been shown to impact protein stability and the activation of the MITF promoter in a cell type-dependent manner [ ]. Similarly, nuclear localization and stability of IRF4 is known to depend on UBC9-mediated SUMOylation at K349 in macrophages [ ], and phosphorylation at Y121 and/or Y124 stimulate the transcriptional activity of IRF4 in 293 cells and transformed B cell lines [ ]. Lastly, despite the multitude of known post-translational modification events affecting MITF (reviewed by Goding and Arnheiter (2019) [ ] and Vu et al. (2021) [ ]), the only known transcriptional effects of MITF PTMs is that of phosphorylation at S73, which has been demonstrated to facilitate interactions between MITF and EP300 and resulting in transactivation of the TYR promoter [ ]. How any of these PTMs, or those with as of yet unknown transcriptional effects, may impact PD-L1 regulation by SOX10, IRF4 or MITF in melanoma is currently unknown and requires elucidation.
Furthermore, the PD-L1 protein itself is also regulated post-translationally, where N-linked glycosylation plays an important role in stabilizing the PD-L1 protein, whereas serine and/or threonine phosphorylation by GSK3β are known to trigger degradation of PD-L1 (reviewed by Hsu et al., 2018) [ ]. Notably, Hsu et al. (2018) [ ] reported that PD-L1 glycosylation is increased following EMT in breast cancer cells, mediated by increased expression of STT3, the catalytic subunit of the oligosaccharyl transferase complex. The upregulation of STT3 was mediated by β-catenin and resulted in increased PD-L1 accumulation owing to the increased stability of the protein [ ]. Potential alterations in post-translational regulation of PD-L1 following EMT in melanoma cells are not addressed in any of the included studies, but such queries might be warranted given the already established implications of β-catenin in both melanoma differentiation [ ] and immune evasion [ ].
All the included studies were evaluated for risk of bias regarding imprecision in experimental reporting and model validity (Fig.  ). Most studies were assessed to have unclear risks of bias with regards to imprecision (82%) and model validity (70%). In short, these results are similar to those observed by Collins et al. (2017) [ ], reflecting that many in vitro studies do not present sufficient information to properly assess the statistical reliability of their results (Fig.  B). Similarly, while the melanoma models and experimental procedures were usually well-described in the included papers, cell lines were rarely reported to be authenticated nor routinely checked for contaminants such as mycoplasma (Fig.  C). Cases of misidentified or cross-contaminated cell lines are more common than one might expect. For instance, one study found that 128/278 (46%) of the cell lines investigated from among 22 research institutions in China proved to be either misidentified or cross-contaminated with another cell line [ ]. This underpins the importance of authenticating cell lines prior to experimentation. The ramifications of using misidentified cell lines have been outlined numerous times in an effort to raise awareness of this issue [ – ]. Yet, the studies included for this review indicate that reporting cell line authentication remains relatively uncommon. This poses another potentially confounding factor when it comes to the contrasting evidence seen for differentiation-linked regulators. For instance, three studies that collectively have contrasting evidence for the functional role of SOX10 in basal PD-L1 regulation in A375 cells [ , , ] do not report whether the cell line was properly authenticated. This makes it hard to objectively assess the meaning of these contrasting results.

## Limitations
This systematic review has several methodological limitations that should be considered. Apart from the title/abstract screening process, the full text screening and data extraction was conducted by a single reviewer. This was first and foremost because of time constraints. As a result, there was a greater risk of selection and interpretation bias in the screening and data extraction phases. While all systematic reviews are subject to these risk factors, having at least two reviewers independently perform the screening and data extraction minimizes their effects. However, we do not believe that these limitations drastically affect the conclusions of this review. We followed two transparent risk of bias assessment tools to ensure consistency in quality evaluation to mitigate this potential bias. Additionally, by limiting the searches to the English language, we may have missed some relevant studies published in different languages (e.g., Chinese, Spanish, or German).
The definition of the regulators of differentiation may also pose a conceptual limitation of this review. As mentioned before, the list of factors affecting MITF identified for this study should not be considered exhaustive. As is true for systematic reviews in general, it is important to regard our results and conclusions within the framework of the search strategy.

## Conclusions
This study demonstrates that regulators of differentiation commonly influence PD-L1 expression in cutaneous melanoma cells through a diverse range of regulatory pathways. Regulators that associate with dedifferentiated cell states have consistent positive effects on PD-L1 expression across studies, corroborating the notion that dedifferentiation may predispose melanoma cells to elevated PD-L1 levels. However, improved efforts regarding experimental reporting and model validation in in vitro melanoma research are warranted to increase the reliability of the results.
