## Introduction
As a prevalent cancer type in adults, renal cell carcinoma (RCC) constitutes approximately 3% of all cancer cases diagnosed in adults worldwide [ ] and ranks third among urologic tumours [ ]. At present, there are more than 20 subtypes of malignant renal cell tumours [ ]. The 3 dominant histological subtypes are clear cell (75%–80%), papillary (10%−15%), and chromophobe (5%) [ ]. Despite conventional surgical treatment being the standard approach for RCC, approximately 30% of patients with localized or locally advanced disease experience local recurrence or metastasis after surgery. For those with metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the five-year survival rate following treatment is only approximately 10% [ , ]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive investigation into the pathogenic mechanisms of RCC, with the goal of identifying more efficacious treatment options.
Epigenetic regulation can adjust gene expression and function without altering the DNA sequence. Thus, epigenetic regulation has different biological effects in different contexts [ , ]. Current evidence indicates that epigenetic regulation can promote the malignant progression of RCC, including tumour growth [ ], metastasis [ ], and invasion [ ]. Furthermore, considering the reversible nature of epigenetic regulation, exploiting its mechanism of promoting RCC formation could be key to the development of novel RCC therapeutic strategies.
In conclusion, we review various epigenetic regulatory mechanisms and their functions and focus on the role of epigenetics and epigenomics in RCC. Furthermore, we explore how epigenetic factors and RNA modifications, as well as noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), serve as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for RCC. Finally, we discuss the clinical value of epigenetic regulation as a potential therapeutic approach for RCC. These discussions not only highlight the importance of epigenetic regulation in RCC research but also provide new ideas and directions for future clinical applications.

## Method
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE and Web of Science databases to identify relevant publications on epigenetic mechanisms in renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The search was performed using the following terms: ("renal cell carcinoma" OR "kidney cancer") AND ("epigenetic" OR "DNA methylation" OR "histone modification" OR "non-coding RNA" OR "RNA modification" OR "chromatin remodeling"). The search encompassed English-language articles from early seminal studies to 2025.

## RCC pathogenesis
Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a malignant neoplasm derived from renal cortical epithelial cells, among which clear cell RCC represents the predominant histological subtype (75%–80%) and is distinguished by its characteristic golden-yellow, clear cytoplasm [ , ]. The molecular pathogenesis of clear cell RCC centers on inactivation of the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) tumor suppressor gene—a genetic event occurring in up to 90% of tumors through deleterious mutations coupled with deletion of chromosome 3p, a critical locus that also harbors other frequently mutated tumor suppressors including SETD2, BAP1, and PBRM1. VHL loss precipitates constitutive activation of hypoxia and angiogenic pathways, fundamentally disrupting cellular oxygen-sensing homeostasis [ ].
Under normoxic conditions, the VHL protein (pVHL) functions as the substrate recognition component of an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that mediates oxygen-dependent degradation of hypoxia-inducible factor α (HIFα) subunits (HIF1α, HIF2α, and HIF3α) [ ]. This proteolytic regulation is orchestrated by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) proteins, which hydroxylate HIFα, enabling its binding to pVHL and subsequent proteasomal destruction [ ]. In clear cell RCC, VHL inactivation dismantles this regulatory axis, leading to HIFα stabilization, nuclear translocation, and heterodimerization with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) [ , ]. The resulting constitutively active HIFα-ARNT transcriptional complex drives expression of numerous hypoxia-responsive genes, thereby promoting angiogenesis, glycolysis, and cellular proliferation. This dysregulated oxygen-sensing cascade constitutes the central oncogenic driver of clear cell RCC tumorigenesis [ ].
VHL inactivation triggers upregulation of multiple oncogenic genes, most notably vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), which drives the characteristic tumor hypervascularity and associated bleeding diathesis [ ]. Additionally, erythropoietin (EPO) overproduction accounts for the paraneoplastic erythrocytosis observed in 2–4% of patients. Concurrent upregulation of platelet-derived growth factor (PDGFA) and transforming growth factor α (TGFα) promotes cellular proliferation and metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis [ ]. Collectively, these molecular perturbations produce a highly vascularized tumor phenotype and underlie the development of paraneoplastic syndromes, which affect 10–40% of patients. Common manifestations include hypercalcemia (1–30%), anemia (22–52%), and Stauffer syndrome—hepatic dysfunction without metastatic involvement occurring in approximately 3% of patients [ ]. Persistence of these syndromes following nephrectomy may signal residual disease, reflecting the profound systemic impact of VHL-driven pathophysiology.
Genetic predisposition significantly contributes to RCC pathogenesis, accounting for 5–16% of advanced cases through hereditary renal cancer syndromes [ ]. Key syndromes include von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease (2% of all RCC cases), hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC syndrome, and hereditary papillary renal carcinoma syndrome. These disorders involve germline mutations in VHL, FH, MET, and FLCN that disrupt the same hypoxia-sensing pathways implicated in sporadic RCC [ ]. Identifying hereditary etiology is crucial in patients diagnosed before age 50, those with bilateral tumors, or multiple unilateral lesions, as it mandates screening for associated malignancies and genetic counseling of at-risk relatives.

## Epigenetic mechanisms
Epigenetic regulatory mechanisms act as “switches” to dynamically regulate gene expression. Their complexity and importance are of increasing interest. This section systematically describes the mechanisms by which various types of epigenetic regulation occur (Fig.  ). 

## Histone modifications: histone acetylation and methylation
Histones are involved in the formation of chromatin in eukaryotic cells. The nucleosome, the fundamental repeating unit of chromatin, comprises an octamer of core histones wrapped with approximately 147 base pairs of DNA [ ]. The core histones, which are basic or positively charged, bind to the surrounding negatively charged DNA base pairs through electrostatic forces [ ]. Interactions between histones and DNA, or among histones, alter the structure of chromatin, affecting cellular processes involving DNA [ ].
Histone posttranslational modifications (PTMs) enable cells to dynamically regulate chromatin structure and function through covalent modifications of core histones. Studies have shown that histone modifications occur primarily in the highly basic histone amino (N)-terminal tail [ ]. Dozens of covalent modifications, such as acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitylation, sumoylation, ADP-ribosylation, citrullination, biotinylation, crotonylation, butyrylation, propionylation, and glycosylation, have been identified [ , , ]. However, in histone PTM, acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation are the primary forms of modification, with lysine, serine, threonine, and arginine being the most crucial amino acids involved [ ].
The balance of histone acetylation is maintained by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs). By using acetyl-CoA as a cofactor, HATs are capable of acetylating the ε-amino group of lysine side chains. Additionally, histone acetylation reduces the electrostatic affinity between histones and DNA chains, promoting gene expression by regulating chromatin structure. In contrast, HDACs catalyse the deacetylation of acetylated lysine residues [ , ]. HATs include type A and type B HATs, with type B HATs primarily acetylating histones after their formation rather than during their assembly into chromatin [ ]. Current research categorizes HATs into three families: the MYST, GNAT and p300/CBP families [ ]. Moreover, HDACs include four superfamilies (class Ⅰ, Ⅱ, Ⅲ and Ⅳ): classes Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅳ, which are dependent on the zinc finger structure, and class Ⅲ, which uses NAD + as a reactant to deacetylate acetyl lysines [ , ].

## Histone methylation
The enzyme histone methyltransferase (HMT) facilitates the transfer of one or more methyl groups (-CH3) to particular amino acid residues in histones (especially lysine and arginine). S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) provides methyl groups during this process. Specifically, the ε-amino group of lysine can be mono- (me1), di- (me2) or trimethylated (me3), whereas arginine can be monomethylated (me) or dimethylated symmetrically (me2s) or asymmetrically (me2a) [ ]. Histone methylation has multiple effects, including but not limited to the regulation of gene expression, alteration of chromatin structure, regulation of the DNA damage response (DDR), or promotion of disease (especially cancer).
In terms of different catalytic objects, HMTs include two families: lysine methyltransferase (KMT) and arginine N-methyltransferase (PRMT) enzymes. Specifically, KMTs include the SET domain family and non-SET domain family [ ]. Given the different catalytic mechanisms, the identified histone demethylases (HDMs) include two families: flavin-dependent lysine demethylases (LSD1/LSD2 family, including KDM1a and KDM1b) and conserved Jumonji C domain-containing proteins (JmjC family, including KDM2-8) [ ].

## DNA modifications: DNA methylation
DNA methylation dynamically regulates gene expression when a –CH3 group is added to DNA without altering its sequence. Specifically, this process predominantly occurs at cytosine–phosphate–guanine (CpG) sites, where SAM provides a -CH3 group to the 5-carbon position of cytosine residues [ , ]. Enriched short sequence regions of CpG dinucleotides are called CpG islands (CGIs) and are usually located near the promoter of a gene. Current studies suggest that CpG sites distant from promoters are heavily methylated, whereas CGIs are largely unmethylated [ ]. However, abnormal methylation of CGIs can lead to gene silencing or repression, which may be linked to disease [ , ].
DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), which can catalyse DNA methylation, are referred to as DNA methylation writers. On the basis of their function and mechanism of action, DNMTs are classified into two subfamilies: DNMT1 and DNMT3 (which include DNMT3A/B/L). DNMT1 is a maintenance DNA methyltransferase that is responsible mainly for catalysing the same methylation pattern between the template strand and the newly synthesized strand during DNA replication [ ]. Unlike DNMT1, the DNMT3 subfamily functions as a de novo methyltransferase. Specifically, DNMT3A and DNMT3B can methylate target DNA without a template strand to form a new methylation pattern. Although DNMT3L lacks methyltransferase activity itself, it can promote DNMT3A- and DNMT3B-mediated methylation processes [ , ]. Interestingly, DNMT2 was originally discovered as one of the three members of the DNMT family but has been shown to be a tRNA methyltransferase [ ].
Enzymes or proteins that can remove -CH3 groups from DNA are called DNA methylation erasers. The demethylation process of DNA is relatively complex and involves multiple proteins and enzymes. First, the ten-eleven translocation (TETs, including TET1/2/3) family of enzymes oxidize 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which is then progressively oxidized to 5-formylcytosine (5fC) and 5-carboxycytosine (5caC). Next, the oxidized groups of 5fC and 5caC are excised by thymine-DNA glycosylase (TDG), and the excision sites are repaired by base excision repair (BER) to form unmethylated cytosine [ , ].

## RNA modifications: m.6A (writers, erasers and readers)
RNA can also undergo chemical modifications, which are alterations that occur at the posttranscriptional level. They can alter the function of RNA, thereby regulating gene expression. In particular, this process exerts its influence through a range of effects on the structure, stability, localization and translation efficiency of RNA. To date, more than 170 different RNA modifications have been identified, including N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), and 7-methylguanosine (m7G) [ ], which are seen in both coding and noncoding RNAs.
Initial studies have suggested that this process of modification is static and irreversible. However, recent studies have debated this view: the process is, in fact, fully reversible [ , ]. The process of RNA modification generally involves three components: (1) enzymes that add the appropriate chemical group to the RNA, which are referred to as “writers” [ ]; (2) enzymes that remove the appropriate chemical modifications from the RNA, which are called “erasers” [ ]; and (3) proteins that can recognize and bind to specific RNA modifications, which are known as “readers” [ ]. As research has progressed, the reversible modification of RNA has emerged as a highly promising gene regulatory modality for further investigation. In the next section, we provide a detailed account of the regulatory mechanisms and functions of m6A.
The m6A, consisting of the addition of a -CH3 group to the 6th nitrogen of RNA adenosine, is a prevalent modification that occurs in many types of RNAs, particularly in mRNAs [ ]. The m6A modification was initially identified in 1974 [ ]; however, there was a prolonged period of inactivity in the field, and the discovery of the obesity-related m6A eraser fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) by Prof. He Chuan in 2011 led to a second period of revitalized research in this field [ ]. In the context of the current surge of interest in m6A, there has been a particular emphasis on its specific role in eukaryotic RNA [ ]. In comparison to the original RNA, RNA containing m6A modifications has distinctive characteristics, including alterations in stability, localization, splicing and translation efficiency [ ]. Next, we describe the RWE (reader, writer and eraser) proteins associated with m6A modification.
The METTL family of methyltransferases (comprising METTL3/14/16) serves as the “writer” of m6A modifications. The writing process occurs within the nucleus and involves the posttranscriptional installation of m6A by the writer within the consensus sequence of A(m6A)C (30%) or G(m6A)C (70%) [ ]. Specifically, this reaction is catalysed by the m6A methyltransferase complex, in which METTL3 and METTL14 form the active catalytic centre [ ]; whereas the former catalyses methyl transfer, the latter stabilizes the target RNA structure. In addition, certain cofactors, such as Wilms tumour 1-associated protein (WTAP), vir-like m6A methyltransferase associated (VIRMA), RNA binding motif protein 15A/15B (RBM15A/RBM15B) and zinc finger CCCH-type containing 13 (ZC3H13), are involved in the complex formation [ – ]. During the process of methylation, the complex initially identifies and binds to the target RNA, subsequently transferring the -CH3 group of SAM to the RNA [ ]. This process is dynamic and reversible. Although studies on METTL16 are lacking, METTL16 has been demonstrated to catalyse the m6A modification of U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) [ ].
The study of RNA demethylases, which are often referred to as “erasers,” is becoming a prominent area of research in the field of epigenetics. Among these enzymes, FTO and ALKB homologue 5 (ALKBH5) have attracted significant attention because of their critical role in the regulation of m6A modification. Both FTO and ALKBH5 are members of the ALKB family of iron (II)- and α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, which catalyse the removal of methyl groups from specific RNA nucleotides [ ]. FTO was the first demethylase to be identified, and the demethylation process occurs primarily at m6A and N6,2’-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am) [ ]. ALKBH5 was subsequently identified as the second m6A-specific demethylase, further revealing how m6A dynamically affects RNA [ ].
Specific RNA molecules with m6A modifications can be identified and attached to by readers, thereby influencing the biological effects of these RNAs. The most widely studied reader proteins include the YTH domain family (YTHDF1-3, YTHDC1/2), insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding proteins (IGF2BPs; including IGF2BP1/2/3) and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs). The biological effects of reading proteins have been observed to vary. For example, YTHDF1 enhances the translation efficiency of m6A-modified mRNA, thereby promoting protein synthesis from these transcripts [ ]. Conversely, YTHDF2 facilitates the degradation or destabilization of m6A-modified mRNA [ ]. Interestingly, YTHDF3 exhibits a dualistic nature, interacting with disparate partners to perform different functions: it synergizes with YTHDF1 to stimulate protein synthesis and binds to YTHDF2 to promote RNA degradation [ ]. In addition, IGF2BP can bind to target RNA through its KH structural domain, thereby promoting its stabilization and transcription [ ]. hnRNPs can bind target RNA and mediate the splicing of endogenous pre-mRNAs and/or pri-miRNAs. Furthermore, although hnRNP protein family members share numerous common RNA recognition sequences, different members exhibit increased affinity for their specific RNA sequences [ ].

## ncRNAs: miRNAs and lncRNAs
Recent studies have demonstrated that a significant proportion of posttranscriptional RNAs are ncRNAs [ , ], which can exert a pivotal influence on gene expression despite their inability to directly encode proteins. The classification of ncRNAs is based on the length of the nucleotide chain, with the designation of short ncRNAs (< 200 nucleotides) and long ncRNAs (lncRNAs) (> 200 nucleotides) [ ]. The scientific literature on RNA modification has concentrated on lncRNAs and microRNAs (miRNAs). These studies have demonstrated that these molecules play a significant role as epigenetic regulators.
miRNAs are a class of endogenous, single-stranded RNA molecules with a length of ~ 22nt [ ]. Typically, miRNAs can directly target the 3’UTR of target gene mRNAs and inhibit target gene expression by promoting their degradation [ , ]. miRNA biogenesis involves five stages. 1) The genes encoding miRNAs are transcribed into long RNA precursors (pri-miRNAs), which is catalysed by RNA polymerase Ⅱ (Pol Ⅱ). 2) Subsequently, pri-miRNA is subjected to processing in the nucleus by the RNase Ⅲ enzymes Drosha and DGCR8, which results in the generation of the shorter precursor miRNA (pre-miRNAs) [ – ]. 3) Following the formation of the pre-miRNAs, the molecules are exported from the nucleus via the action of exprotin-5 [ , ]. 4) Next, the pre-miRNAs are cut by Dicer (another RNase Ⅲ enzyme) [ ]. 5) After cleavage by Dicer, the resulting RNA is a double-stranded structure. However, the mature miRNA will eventually be formed by loading only one of these strands into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [ ]. Subsequently, miRNA binds to the 3’-untranslated region (3’UTR) of the target mRNA, primarily through complementary base pairing, and inhibits translation of the mRNA, thereby regulating gene expression [ ].
Several miRNAs are associated with kidney disease progression. For example, some miRNAs (e.g., miR-21) have been demonstrated to facilitate renal tubular cell fibrosis, which in turn promotes the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [ ]. The exosomal miRNA miR-181d-5p can promote RCC stemness and tumour metastasis [ ]. Thus, miRNAs represent a promising avenue of research in the field of kidney disease.
lncRNAs are ubiquitous within the cellular milieu, with each molecule performing a specific function. Specifically, these functions pertain to the location of the lncRNAs, their relative abundance, and the molecules with which they interact [ ]. For example, nuclear lncRNAs primarily modulate nuclear processes such as chromatin organization, RNA transcription and splicing. In contrast, cytoplasmic lncRNAs mainly regulate mRNA transport, stability, and translation. Like miRNAs, the transcription of most lncRNAs is mediated by Pol Ⅱ. Transcribed lncRNAs undergo splicing and are subsequently capped with m7G at the 5’ end and polyadenylated with a poly(A) tail at the 3’ end. Following these modifications, they are transported through the nuclear pore complex to various locations within the cell to perform their biological function [ ]. Recently, some studies have elucidated how lncRNAs function in kidney diseases, highlighting their significant potential for future research.
At the most basic level, gene expression involves standard transcription processes and subsequent mRNA formation, degradation, and protein synthesis. The additional layers of epigenetic regulation and ncRNA function contribute to a complex and multifaceted mechanism for controlling gene expression. In addition, epigenetic modifications not only play a single role but also engage in multiple modifications that crosstalk with each other and together form a complex epigenetic regulatory network. Disruption of this intricate epigenetic landscape leads to various pathological conditions, including kidney diseases.

## Epigenetic regulations in TME
The tumour microenvironment (TME), which consists of noncancerous elements surrounding tumour cells, significantly promotes cancer cell survival and is linked to tumour growth, metastasis, and invasion. It comprises both cellular and noncellular components, which include immune cells, stromal cells, the extracellular matrix, and blood vessels.
In the TME, the tumour immune microenvironment (TIME) is the most critical component. Infiltrating immune cells are a distinguishing feature of the TIME and include T cells, B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic (DC) cells, and myeloid suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ – ]. Despite immune surveillance, RCC has a more ingenious immune escape mechanism. Immune checkpoints often act as “brakes” against excessive immune activation in normal tissues; however, RCC cells utilize this mechanism for immune escape. Specifically, tumor cells highly express programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and B7 family molecules (e.g., CD80 and CD86), which bind to PD-1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) on T cells, thereby inhibiting T-cell activation and antitumour immune responses [ , ]. Furthermore, the recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells are important mechanisms through which RCC can escape the immune system. For instance, tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) in the TME acquire an M2 phenotype in response to specific signalling molecules. M2 macrophage polarization can promote tumour progression [ ].
The mechanisms that form the TIME are extremely complex; however, there is now growing evidence that epigenetic regulation is involved. DNA hypomethylation-mediated pleckstrin homology-like domain family a member 2 (PHLDA2) expression in ccRCC is associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, suggesting that this mechanism is related to poor immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy [ ]. Aberrant m6A modification promotes M2 macrophage polarization in RCC [ , ]. IGF2BP3-mediated m6A modification may promote the infiltration of Th2 cells, prompting immune escape in ccRCC [ ].
Another major event in TME formation is angiogenesis [ ]. This process is tightly linked to immunity and influences the progression of RCC [ ]. The key to RCC angiogenesis is the dysregulation of VHL, especially in ccRCC. Specifically, upon VHL dysregulation, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) accumulate in tumours and promote angiogenesis by activating the production of downstream target genes, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), through the hypoxic response [ , ]. Given the critical role of VEGF in angiogenesis, therapeutic strategies for many cancers include targeted therapy against VEGF. Currently, the most commonly used VEGF-targeted agents in the clinic include small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and anti-VEGF monoclonal antibodies.
Epigenetic regulation is actively involved in tumour angiogenesis. ncRNAs regulate the expression of angiogenesis-inducing molecules [ ]. FTO promotes tumour VEGFA and EGR1 expression in a m6A modification-dependent manner [ ]. Hypermethylated silencing of some tumour suppressors promote tumour VEGF expression [ ].

## Epigenetic regulations in RCC
Epigenetic regulatory mechanisms are widely involved in life processes and can act individually or in tandem to drive them. However, a multitude of studies have indicated that aberrant epigenetic regulation contributes to RCC progression, providing insight into how RCC forms. This section focuses on their mechanisms and role in RCC.

## Histone modifications in RCC: histone acetylation and methylation
Histone modifications, as key epigenetic modes of regulation, are aberrantly associated with the occurrence of RCC. Many gene expression abnormalities are precisely triggered by aberrant histone modifications (Fig.  ). Given that existing studies have focused mainly on the mechanisms of histone methylation and acetylation in RCC, the following section focuses on the effects of these two modifications on RCC. 
Trimethylation of lysine 36 on histone H3 (H3K36me3) is an extremely important histone PTM in human cells. H3K36me3 has many genetic effects, including transcription elongation, DNA mismatch repair (MMR), DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair by homologous recombination (HR), and co-transcribed RNA processing [ – ].
In humans, the formation of H3K36me3 is catalyzed by a specific HMT: the SET domain-containing protein 2 (SETD2) [ ]. Specifically, SETD2 is responsible for trimethylating H3K36me2 to H3K36me3, whereas the previous two steps (from unmethylated H3K36 to mono- and dimethylated H3K36) are catalyzed by NSD1/2 [ ]. As one of the chromatin repair genes, SETD2 is particularly mutated and can be found in both solid and non-solid tumors [ ]. TCGA dataset shows that of all the tumors in which SETD2 mutations have been identified, ccRCC has the highest mutation rate, at 13% [ ]. The von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) gene, which is mutated or silenced in more than 90% of ccRCC, is considered an important tumor suppressor gene. In earlier times, the prevailing view was that VHL reduction was the primary driver of ccRCC. However, current studies have shown that VHL reduction alone is not sufficient to cause ccRCC, but needs to be mutated simultaneously with other genes, including SETD2 [ , ].
Mutation of SETD2, a tumour suppressor during progression, is linked to adverse outcomes, especially metastasis. The incidence of SETD2 mutation in ccRCC metastasis is much greater than that in primary ccRCC [ , ]. Rao et al. have found that mutation of SETD2 promotes the progression of polycystic kidney disease (PKD) to RCC [ ]. This process is driven by the promotion of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in renal tubular epithelial cells. During EMT, epithelial cells lose their epithelial characteristics. These cells then gain the characteristics of mesenchymal cells. This change from epithelial to mesenchymal characteristics is a critical indicator of tumour invasion and metastasis [ ]. Specifically, SETD2 can inhibit the EMT of renal tubular epithelial cells by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway, thereby inhibiting the transition from PKD to RCC and the invasion and metastasis of RCC. Mechanistically, SETD2 inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through two mechanisms: On the one hand, SETD2 and β-catenin competitively bind to the target gene promoter region to inhibit downstream signalling; on the other hand, SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 can maintain the expression of the β-catenin destruction complex, leading to the degradation of β-catenin.
Wang et al. further demonstrated that the EMT of renal tubular epithelial cells is independent of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) by establishing a nontumorigenic epithelial cell line model and reported that the transcription factors SOX2, POU2F2 (OCT2) and PRRX1 are downstream effector molecules [ ]. They may activate the transcription of cancer metastasis genes. Specifically, these factors may promote the recruitment of histone companions such as ASF1A and ASF1B to chromatin and increase the accessibility of chromatin across the genome (such as MMP1 promoters and introns) to construct an epigenetic landscape suitable for RCC development, thereby enhancing oncogene expression [ ]. Subsequently, Javaid et al. demonstrated that elevated expression of these downstream effector molecules is linked to aberrant DNA methylation [ ]. These findings suggest that SET2 mutation can promote RCC metastasis through the interaction of histone modification and DNA methylation. Notably, deletion of H3K36me3 may disrupt its synergistic effect with H4K16ac, leading to dysfunction of RWE proteins and exacerbating epigenetic disorders, suggesting that RNA modifications are also involved in this complex regulatory network [ ]. These findings are consistent with the broader context of epigenetic regulation and highlight the role of SETD2 mutation in shaping the epigenetic landscape that influences RCC progression and metastasis.
While the downregulation of SETD2 expression is typically linked to poor prognosis, in certain specific cases, it may be advantageous for treatment. In contrast to programmed cell death, such as apoptosis or necrosis, ferroptosis is an iron-dependent mode of cell death that is characterized by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [ , ]. Ferrochelatase (FECH) plays a role in the formation of haemoglobin through the insertion of ferrous iron into protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) in the final step of haem synthesis [ ]. Yang et al. demonstrated that FECH downregulation can promote ferroptosis in cancer [ ]. A subsequent study further demonstrated that the SETD2 mutation-mediated reduction in H3K36me3 in ccRCC inhibits FECH expression, which in turn increases lipid ROS and Fe2+ accumulation, ultimately contributing to erastin-induced ferroptosis [ ]. This finding is consistent with the findings of Rao et al., who reported reduced lipid accumulation in renal tubular epithelial cells through the use of Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors [ ]. These findings suggest that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway may be involved in lipid accumulation. Therefore, SETD2 and H3K36me3 represent extremely attractive research directions with significant research potential for the personalized treatment of RCC patients.
Ndc80 kinetochore complex component (NUF2), a component of the mitotic process, can establish stable attachments between chromosomes and spindle microtubules during cell division. Given that abnormal chromosome segregation during mitosis is a common cause of genomic instability, which can contribute to carcinogenesis, it is not surprising that aberrant NUF2 function is associated with a series of malignant changes. A recent study revealed the carcinogenic role of NUF2 in ccRCC, showing that NUF2 sustains the H3K36me2 modification level in the HMGA2 promoter region by inhibiting lysine demethylase 2 A (KDM2A), thereby promoting its transcription [ ]. Normally, HMGA2 is highly expressed during the embryonic period but is expressed at low levels during late development and adulthood. However, it is aberrantly re-expressed at high levels in various malignant human tumours, where it promotes tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms [ – ].
The D4, zinc and double PHD fingers, family 3 (DPF3), a noncatalytic subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling complex, is upregulated in ccRCC due to increased HIF levels resulting from VHL loss [ ]. In normal cells, SMAD nuclear-interacting protein 1 (SNIP1) can interact with SMAD4 and P300 HAT and inhibit p300 activity [ ]. However, in ccRCC, the interaction of DPF3 with SNIP1 deregulates the inhibition of p300 by SNIP1, thereby increasing the local histone acetylation level. This mechanism in turn upregulates metastasis-associated target genes downstream of SMAD4, ultimately facilitating the metastatic process in ccRCC [ ].
In addition, aberrant histone acetylation of another gene promotes the metastatic process of RCC. Snail family zinc finger 1 (SNAI1), a key transcriptional repressor, promotes the EMT process and tumour metastasis by downregulating E-cadherin expression [ ]. However, the expression of SNAI1 is regulated by histone acetylation involving ACSS2. Specifically, acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) expression is significantly upregulated in a glucose-limited RCC environment. Its catalytic production of CoA, a key substrate for histone acetylation, is directly involved in the H3K27ac process of SNAI1, thereby increasing the expression level of SNAI1 [ ]. This study revealed a strong link between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation in RCC progression.
Human organic cation transporter member 2 (OCT2) is a cation transporter protein that promotes the accumulation of several platinum-based anticancer drugs (especially oxaliplatin) in cancer cells, thereby increasing their cytotoxicity [ ]. In RCC, however, the histone deacetylases HDAC7 and HDAC9 mediate H3K18ac and H3K27ac deacetylation of OCT2, which in turn inhibits OCT2 expression. This process ultimately leads to resistance to platinum-based anticancer drugs in RCC [ ].
GM2 synthase catalyses the synthesis of GM-2-type gangliosides, the aberrant expression of which can promote tumour formation through EMT [ ]. SP1 usually binds to the P region of the GM2-synthase gene and recruits HDAC1 to reduce levels of H3K9ac and H3K14ac modifications of GM2-synthase, thereby inhibiting its expression. However, SP1 is degraded because of high histone acetylation, which increases GM2-synthase expression in RCC [ ].

## DNA modifications in RCC: DNA methylation
Aberrant DNA methylation of genes is prevalent in RCC and is strongly associated with the progression of RCC (Fig.  ). Specifically, this process includes low DNA methylation levels and high expression of oncogenes, and high DNA methylation levels and low expression of cancer suppression genes. 
The Janus kinase (JAK) family of tyrosine kinases, including JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2, are key proteins involved in cell signaling [ ]. JAK proteins and signal transducers and activators of transcription (STAT) proteins constitute an extremely important signalling pathway that mediates almost all immunoregulatory processes [ ]. Evidence indicates that JAK expression is correlated with poor prognosis in cancer patients, suggesting that it plays an oncogenic role in cancer progression [ ]. Long et al. reported that the JAK3 promoter is hypomethylated and that the expression of JAK3 is aberrantly elevated in patients with ccRCC, with poor survival outcomes. Subsequent investigations demonstrated that high levels of JAK promote the expression of pivotal immune regulators (including LAG3, PD1, CTLA4, TIGIT, and PD-L2), resulting in the formation of a tumour immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ], which may be achieved through the JAK-STAT pathway. Given the aberrant activity and oncogenic role of the JAK-STAT pathway in ccRCC, the use of JAK inhibitors represents a viable therapeutic strategy, and as of 2021, six JAK inhibitors have been approved by the FDA for clinical treatment [ ].
The E74-like transcription factor 5 (ELF5) is a member of the E26 transformation-specific family and is also known as ESE2 in human cells [ ]. ELF5 has epithelial specificity and can affect human life processes. ELF5 determines trophectoderm cell fate in human embryos and regulates the genesis and development of numerous organs, especially the mammary gland [ ]. It is also expressed in normal human tissues, including kidney, prostate, lung, and epithelial exocrine glands. Furthermore, the regulatory mechanisms of ELF5 in human malignant tumours are complex and context dependent; however, in general, they primarily inhibit cancer progression. For example, ELF5 can inhibit EMT in prostate cancer by inactivating SMAD3 [ ]; ELF5 loss promotes breast cancer progression via interferon-γ signalling [ ]. However, ELF5 mRNA expression levels are elevated in a cell line model of prostate cancer progression involving the acquisition of androgen independence [ ]. Thus, dysregulation of ELF5 expression is an important feature of cancer development.
Li et al. reported that ELF5 regulates tumour microvessel formation during RCC development [ ]. Specifically, ELF5 transcriptionally activates USP3 (a deubiquitinating enzyme), which in turn inhibits the ubiquitinating degradation of WDTC1, thereby increasing its stability and reducing VEGFA expression. This regulatory mechanism inhibits the malignant progression of RCC cells. Unfortunately, DNMT1/DNMT3b mediates hypermethylation of the ELF5 gene in RCC, resulting in suppression of its expression. However, these findings also suggest that the ELF5-USP3-WDTC1 axis is highly promising for RCC treatment.
The tumour suppressor ras-association domain family comprises ten members (RASSF1-10). The defining characteristic of these proteins is the presence of a Ras-association (RA) domain in their N- or C-terminus, which is capable of binding to the Ras GTPase protein family. Furthermore, these proteins possess certain functional domains that are associated with the regulation of other proteins. Collectively, these domains are instrumental in their tumour-suppressive effects [ ]. RASSF members have been reported in a multitude of tumour types, and in renal carcinomas, RASSF1 and RASSF10 are the most frequently observed.
The RASSF1 gene is located within the 3p21 region of the human chromosome and is associated with eight distinct splice variants (A-H) [ ], with RASSF1A and RASSF1C representing the most extensively investigated. The epigenetic methylation of these two genes has disparate biological effects. The promoter region of the RASSF1A gene contains CpG islands, and hypermethylation of this gene is associated with a poor clinical prognosis for patients with ccRCC. In contrast, this association is not observed for RASSF1C [ ]. Furthermore, RASSF1A is associated with many childhood malignancies. For example, compared with normal tissue, RASSF1A methylation is a distinct feature of childhood Wilm’s tumour [ ], and this phenomenon is similarly observed in rhabdoid tumour of the kidney (RTK) and clear cell sarcoma of the kidney (CCSK) and is associated with an unfavourable prognosis [ ].
The RASSF10 gene is located within the 11p15 region of the human chromosome and does not exhibit any splice variants [ ]. Similar to RASSF1A, RASSF10 methylation levels are elevated in various renal carcinomas, including chromophobe carcinoma, papillary carcinoma, and clear cell carcinoma, indicating epigenetic inactivation. In human kidney tumour cells, epigenetically silenced RASSF10 leads to the activation of the IL6-JAK-STAT pathway, which subsequently results in the aberrant expression of downstream effector molecules (e.g., MCV and VEGF) and facilitates tumour development [ ]. These results indicate that combining RASSF10 with other biomarkers (such as RASSF1A, MCY, or VEGF) is highly valuable for the postoperative diagnosis of renal malignancies. This conclusion aligns with that of another study, which highlights the clinical significance of the combination of RASSF10 and a tumour-associated macrophage (TAM) assay for predicting ccRCC after surgery [ ].
The death-associated protein kinase-1 (DAPK1), a target gene of p53, promotes apoptosis in tumour cells. Its promoter has been shown to be hypermethylated in ccRCC, leading to a concomitant downregulation of expression, which in turn promotes tumour progression [ ]. Alternative polyadenylation (APA) can regulate the stability of mRNA by altering the length of its 3’UTR, consequently regulating gene product expression. This process affects the binding of miRNAs to the 3’UTR of target mRNAs [ ]. Normally, APA is regulated by APA regulators, including NUDT21. However, in cancer, APA is reprogrammed due to dysregulation.
Recently, Tan et al. elucidated the role of NUDT21 and DAPK1 in ccRCC [ ]. Specifically, DNMT3B-induced hypermethylation of the NUDT21 promoter resulted in the downregulation of its expression, which in turn led to a shortening of the 3’UTR of the chromatin modifier MORC2, causing an increase in its expression. Although MORC2 has no DNA methyltransferase activity, it can hypermethylate the promoter of DAPK1 by recruiting DNMT3A, which represses its expression and ultimately promotes tumour progression. In the NUDT21-APA-MORC2-DAPK1 axis, the expression of NUTD21 and DAPK1 is silenced by hypermethylation, indicating that demethylation in this pathway may have therapeutic potential for ccRCC.
CTLA-4, a well-known immune checkpoint, is hypomethylated in ccRCC, and its methylation level is correlated with the response to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [ ]; cadherin 3 (CDH3) has been reported to be related to malignant progression in tumours. In RCC, CDH3 is hypermethylated, which is correlated with tumour aggressiveness, revealing that this gene could be a potential future therapeutic target [ ]. Ubiquinol cytochrome c reductase hinge protein (UQCRH) is a cytochrome c-dependent pro-apoptotic factor. In ccRCC, UQCRH expression is essential for the induction of apoptosis in tumour cells. However, DNMT3B-mediated hypermethylation of the UQCRH promoter inhibits its expression or inactivation, which promotes tumour progression [ ].

## RNA modifications in RCC: m6A and other RNA modifications
Over the past decade, researchers have increasingly focused on exploring RNA modification mechanisms in RCC. The expression and function of key proteins in RCC are collectively affected by RWE-regulated RNA modifications (Fig.  ). Given that current studies on RNA modification in RCC focus mainly on m6A modification, in the next sections, we will focus on the mechanism and role of m6A modification. 
FTO regulates autophagy in ccRCC cells through epigenetic modifications. Autophagy is a process of cellular self-degradation through which cells remove damaged proteins, organelles, and other substances to maintain the stability of the internal environment [ ]. Salt-inducible kinase 2 (SIK2) plays a crucial role in regulating intracellular signalling pathways involved in apoptosis, tumorigenesis, and metastasis. IGF2BP2 specifically recognizes m6A sites in SIK2 mRNA, stabilizing its expression and enhancing its translation efficiency. Conversely, FTO, a m6A demethylase, reduces SIK2 mRNA stability through a mechanism dependent on m6A and IGF2BP2. FTO decreases the binding of IGF2BP2 to SIK2 mRNA by reducing m6A modification, leading to decreased mRNA stability and expression. This phenomenon results in reduced intracellular autophagic flux, ultimately promoting the growth and metastasis of ccRCC [ ].
In addition, FTO has been used to modulate other factors in ccRCC and promote tumour progression: 1) regulate the expression of POLQ (a protein essential for cell survival) by modulating the level of m6A modification of its mRNA [ ]; 2) reduce the level of m6A modification of PDK1 to prevent YTHDF2-induced PDK1 mRNA degradation, which then enhances AKT phosphorylation via PDK1 [ ]; and 3) reduce the level of m6A modification and mRNA expression of ALDH9A1 (a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase superfamily), leading to increased NPM1 content in the nucleus, resulting in the inhibition of IQGAP2 transcription and subsequently the activation of AKT-mTOR signalling, promoting tumour progression and lipid accumulation [ ]. Taken together, these studies highlight FTO inhibitors as a potential new opportunity for RCC treatment.
Another “eraser” of m6A modification was also found to be involved in the progression of RCC. ALKBH1 increases the expression level of G-protein-coupled receptor 137 (GPR137) by mediating the demethylation of m6A modifications on GPR137 mRNA, thereby promoting the expression of migration-related proteins [ ].
METTL3 is involved in the progression of RCC through multiple pathways. For example, it epigenetically mediates m6A modification to increase the expression of HHLA2 (a member of the B7 family), which in turn promotes the progression of ccRCC by regulating immune function. Although previous studies have indicated that high HHLA2 expression in ccRCC might be correlated with a favourable prognosis, current evidence predominantly reveals that high HHLA2 expression is associated with poor outcomes [ – ]. Similarly, METTL3 epigenetically represses ABCD1 expression in RCC in a m6A-dependent manner, affecting lipid metabolism in tumour cells and thereby promoting tumour growth and metastasis [ ]. Under prolonged hypoxia, HIF-driven METTL3 expression increases PLOD2 expression, which in turn promotes RCC progression by regulating EGFR-dependent AKT pathway activation [ , ].
NSUN1 mediates m5C modification of APOL1 mRNA. It is subsequently recognized by YBX1 and further stabilizes APOL1 mRNA, thereby promoting its expression. Activation of the PI3K/Akt signalling pathway via this process has been demonstrated to drive ccRCC progression [ ].
Although studies on m1A and m5C modifications in RCC are relatively limited, the functions of these two modifications in other cancer types have been widely reported. Histone acetylation-driven ALKBH3 expression promotes malignant transformation in promyelocytic leukaemia by contributing to attenuated m1a methylation of SP100A [ ]. In colorectal cancer, microfibril-associated protein 2 (MFAP2) mRNA exhibits high levels of m1A modification and enhances its invasive properties through the action of CDC-like kinase 3 (CLK3) [ ]. YBX1 promotes the progression of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) through m5C-dependent SMOX mRNA [ ]. In endometrial cancer, histone modification-driven upregulation of NSUN2 increases the m5C modification level of SLC7A11 mRNA, which is recognized by YBX1 and leads to increased expression of SLC7A11, thus enhancing the resistance of cancer cells to ferroptosis [ ].

## ncRNAs in RCC: miRNAs and lncRNAs and other ncRNAs
As important components of epigenetic regulation, ncRNAs are involved in the formation of RCC in several ways (Fig.  ). Considering that ncRNAs may present new opportunities for clinical applications in RCC, understanding their role in RCC is necessary. In this section, miRNAs and lncRNAs in RCC are highlighted (Table  ). 
miRNAs are capable of regulating the expression and function of genes and proteins. Typically, abnormal expression levels (increased or decreased) affect the expression of target genes: overexpressed miRNAs can promote tumorigenesis by reducing the expression and functional activity of tumour suppressors, whereas miRNAs with downregulated expression usually function as tumour suppressors because they tend to inhibit the expression and functional activity of oncogenic factors [ ].
Increased levels of miR-196a in RCC directly target and inhibit bone morphogenesis protein receptor-associated molecule 1 (BRAM1) expression, which consequently leads to increased migration and invasion of RCC through the BMP and MAPK pathways [ ]. miR-340 promotes RCC malignant progression by directly inhibiting frizzled class receptor 3 (FZD3) expression. This process may promote glycolysis by upregulating alanine levels, thereby constructing a microenvironment that is favourable for RCC survival [ ]. By inhibiting argonaute RNA-induced silencing complex catalytic component 1 (AGO1) expression and activating the PI3K/Akt signalling pathway, miR-153-5p promotes RCC proliferation and metastasis. [ ]. The increased expression of the above miRNAs in RCC suggests that these miRNAs are carcinogenic.
The miR-204-5p inhibits the malignant progression of RCC by suppressing glucoside xylosyltransferase 2 (GXYLT2) expression. However, studies on how GXYLT2 is involved in tumour progression have not yet been reported [ ]. Hairy and enhancer of split 1 (HES1), a downstream effector of the NOTCH signalling pathway, is influenced by miR-138–3/5p, which targets NOTCH1 [ ]. In ccRCC, HES1 binds to the promoter of miR-138–2, inhibiting its transcription into mature miR-138–3/5p and thereby upregulating NOTCH1 expression. This positive feedback regulatory mechanism promotes malignant tumour progression induced by the NOTCH signalling pathway [ ]. In RCC, promoter hypermethylation of miR-30a/c-5p leads to the repression of their expression. As a result, the expression of their target gene, TWF1, is increased. TWF1 regulates the expression of NCAD and vimentin, which in turn facilitates the EMT process in RCC [ ]. These findings further highlight the intriguing connection between DNA methylation and ncRNAs. In addition, another miRNA is involved in the EMT process. Specifically, miR-9-5p suppresses the expression of HMMR (CD168) to inhibit the EMT and JAK/STAT signalling pathways [ ]. The suppression of the expression of these miRNAs in RCC indicates that these miRNAs function as tumour suppressors.
As important components of the epigenome, lncRNAs have many different biological functions, among which serving as molecular sponges for miRNAs are the most common. Specifically, lncRNAs bind to miRNAs as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) to reduce their binding to targeted mRNAs [ ]. The lncRNA DMDRMR, a DNA hypomethylated lncRNA in tumours, acts as a ceRNA and directly upregulates the expression of enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) and SMAD-specific e3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (SMURF1) by binding to miR-378a-5p, thereby inhibiting the expression of DAB2IP and ultimately indirectly activating the VEGFA/VEGFR2 signalling pathway to promote RCC angiogenesis and metastasis [ ]. Like DMDRMR, the lncRNA RCAT1 binds to miR-214-5p to promote E2F2 expression for the malignant progression of RCC [ ], and the lncRNA linc00926 binds to miR-30a-5p to upregulate SOX4 expression and promote RCC progression [ ].
In addition to affecting RCC cells, lncRNAs can be delivered to and function in other tumour-adjacent cells. For example, lncARSRC, derived from RCC, is transported via exosomes to macrophages in the TME, where it binds to miR-34 or miR-449 to increase STAT3 expression, thereby promoting macrophage conversion from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype [ ]. In addition, the expression of lncRNAs in RCC is regulated in multiple ways. For example, in the context of VHL loss, HIF-2α can directly regulate the transcription of the lncRNA lnc02609, thereby affecting its binding to miR-149 [ ]; the lncRNA COL18A1-AS1 acts as a tumour suppressor that binds to miR-1286 to promote Krüppel-like factor 12 (KLF12) expression, resulting in the inhibition of ccRCC progression through the regulation of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)-mediated lipid browning. However, increased methylation in the promoter region suppresses the expression of COL18A1-AS1 [ ].
In addition, lncRNAs regulate the translation of mRNAs. The lncRNA MILIP forms a duplex structure with Snai1 mRNA, facilitating the recruitment of Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) to increase Snai1 translation and thereby promoting the metastasis of ccRCC [ ]. Hypoxia induces increased expression of TR4 in RCC, consequently transcriptionally activating lncRNA TASR. TASR subsequently binds to AXL mRNA, stabilizing it and promoting AXL expression, thereby enhancing sunitinib resistance in RCC [ ].
The lncRNAs can bind to transcription factors to regulate their biological effects. The lncRNA SNHG12 promotes cell division cycle associated 3 (CDCA3) transcription by binding to the transcription factor SP1 and stabilizing SP1 against ubiquitination-dependent protein hydrolysis, which in turn promotes RCC progression and enhances resistance to sunitinib [ ]. In fumarate hydratase-deficient RCC (FH-deficient RCC, a rara subtype of RCC), fumarate promotes the expression of the lncRNA MIR4435-2HG via H3K4me3 modification. MIR4435-2HG subsequently interacts with the transcription factor STAT1 to transcriptionally activate glutaminase 1 (GLS1), which is involved in glutamine reprogramming in FH-deficient RCC [ ]. HIF-2α and the lncRNA PVT1 promote the formation and metastasis of RCC through positive feedback regulation. On the one hand, PVTL1 binds to HIF-2α, protecting it from ubiquitin-dependent degradation. On the other hand, HIF-2α directly targets the enhancer region of PVTL1, thereby promoting its expression [ ].
Moreover, lncRNAs can interact with RNA-binding proteins. The lncRNA IGFL2-AS1 binds to hnRNPC in an m6A-dependent manner, thereby inhibiting hnRNPC-mediated alternative splicing of tumour protein P53 inducible nuclear protein 2 (TP53INP2). Increased expression of TP53INP2 promotes resistance to sunitinib in RCC. Furthermore, IGFL2-AS1 bound to hnRNPC can be transferred to other cells via extracellular vesicles (EVs), facilitating the dissemination of sunitinib resistance [ ]. miR-155 directly targets and suppresses the expression of the lncRNA lncTCL6. However, lncTCL6 binds to the RNA-binding protein STAU1, recruits it to Src mRNA, and promotes its degradation. This process inhibits RCC metastasis through the Scr/Akt/EMT network [ ].
The lncRNAs are involved in histone modifications. The recruitment of CREB-binding protein (CBP) and epigenetic modification complex 3 (SMYD3), which contain SET and MYND structural domains, to the promoter region of NUF2 by the IGF2BP3-stabilized lncRNA CDKN2B-AS1 increases the levels of H3K27ac and H3K4me3 modifications in this region, which results in epigenetic activation of NUF2 and its increased transcription. This process promotes the malignant progression of RCC [ ].
In addition, lncRNAs are involved in RNA modifications. DMDRMR binds to IGF2BP3 and promotes its m6A-dependent interaction with cell cycle-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) mRNA. This interaction stabilizes CDK4 mRNA and enhances its expression, thereby promoting RCC proliferation. Notably, while CDK4/6 inhibitors are widely recognized as effective targeted therapies for treating breast cancer, in ccRCC, DMDRMR has been shown to mediate resistance to these inhibitors, thereby limiting their therapeutic efficacy [ ]. The expression of the lncRNA TRAF3IP2-AS1 is suppressed in NONO-TFE3 tRCC (a type of translocation RCC). Upon restoration of TRAF3IP2-AS1 expression, it is able to bind directly to poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP1) mRNA and increase the level of m6A modifications of PARP1 mRNA by recruiting the methyltransferase complex. YTHDF2 subsequently recognizes these modifications and mediates PARP1 mRNA degradation. This process ultimately inhibits the malignant progression of NONO-TFE3 tRCC [ ].
The roles and mechanisms of other types of ncRNAs in RCC have also been reported. CircRNA primarily functions as an oncogenic driver in RCC. CicrPPAP2B influences the proliferation and metastatic capabilities of ccRCC cells through two parallel mechanisms [ ]. On one hand, circPPAP2B modulates tumor cell alternative splicing through its m6A-dependent regulation; on the other hand, it sponges miR-182-5p, thereby abolishing its tumor-suppressive effects. Hsa_circ_0057105 acts as a double-edged sword in RCC by sponging miR-577, thereby unveiling the expression of its downstream targets. This action concomitantly promotes tumor progression via COL1A1-induced EMT and, paradoxically, enhances therapeutic vulnerability by increasing ferroptosis sensitivity through VDAC2 upregulation [ ]. By serving as a molecular scaffold to colocalize UBE3C and PEBP1, circPOLR2A facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of PEBP1 by UBE3C, which in turn activates the ERK signaling pathway and promotes ccRCC progression [ ]. In addition to functioning as molecular sponges, circRNAs can also be translated generate oncogenic peptides through IRES-dependent (Internal Ribosome Entry Site) translation. Driven by HIF-2A, the translation of circPDHK1 produces the PDHK1-241aa peptide, which promotes tumor progression by affecting PPP1CA nuclear localization and consequently activating the AKT-mTOR signaling pathway [ ]. circPVT1 enhances c-MYC expression and stability via two simultaneously acting pathways. On the one hand, the translated cP104aa peptide stabilizes c-MYC through its interaction with HNRNPK. On the other hand, the circPVT1 RNA itself acts as a sponge for EIF4A3, thereby promoting c-MYC expression [ ].
Although the mechanism of action of piRNAs in RCC remains poorly understood, existing research suggests its role is multifaceted. piR-1742 enhances the expression of the deubiquitinating enzyme USP8 in an hnRNPU-dependent manner, thereby stabilizing MUC12 and leading to the malignant progression of RCC [ ]. The tumor suppressor piR-hsa-28489 is downregulated in RCC. Mechanistically, it binds to YBX-1 and prevents its nuclear translocation, thereby alleviating the transcriptional repression of the EHF gene by YBX-1 and ultimately inhibiting the malignant progression of RCC [ ].
Recent studies on snoRNAs in RCC have predominantly focused on investigating their promise as diagnostic biomarkers [ , ].

## The interplay of diverse epigenetic modifications in RCC
Emerging evidence has shifted our understanding of epigenetic deregulation in RCC from a model of parallel, independent alterations in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and RNA modifications, to a paradigm of a highly interconnected regulatory network. These epigenetic layers do not function in isolation; instead, they engage in extensive crosstalk, whereby a change in one layer can directly initiate, reinforce, or antagonize changes in another. This dynamic interplay creates a self-reinforcing oncogenic epigenetic landscape that drives RCC tumorigenesis, progression, and therapy resistance. For instance, in RCC, driving events like SETD2 mutation compromise H3K36me3 levels, thereby disrupting the chromatin occupancy and function of RWE proteins. Such disruption systematically rewires the cellular RNA modification landscape, promoting renal carcinogenesis and progression [ ]. Furthermore, SETD2 mutation-mediated loss of H3K36me3 can dysregulate DNA methylation in RCC. Mechanistically, H3K36me3 is essential for stabilizing enhancer chromatin states. Its loss leads to enhancer instability, consequently provoking aberrant DNA methylation patterns and subsequent gene expression dysregulation [ ]. The lncRNA MIR503HG functions as a pivotal epigenetic hub. It binds the histone variant H2A.Z and orchestrates the normal deposition of the repressive H3K27me3 mark, thereby epigenetically repressing NOTCH1 expression and suppressing lymphangiogenesis. In papillary renal cell carcinoma (pRCC), this protective mechanism is silenced by promoter CpG island hypermethylation [ ]. Collectively, these interactions establish the cross-talk between distinct epigenetic layers as a central driver of renal cell carcinoma pathogenesis and progression. The intricate, multi-dimensional regulatory network they form presents a compelling framework for future therapeutic exploration.

## Clinical prospects of epigenetic regulation in RCC

## RCC biomarkers: diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers
As with the vast majority of malignant tumours, the survival of RCC is correlated with tumour stage. When the infiltration of early RCC is more limited, surgical treatment can be used to achieve significant results. However, because RCC has no specific clinical manifestations in its early stage, 50% of cases are incidentally identified during unrelated medical examinations. Furthermore, approximately 1/3 of patients already show cancer spread at initial diagnosis [ , ]. Thus, early detection of RCC is extremely important for overall survival. Moreover, the efficacy of radiotherapy and chemotherapy is limited in RCC. Despite combination regimens that include TKIs, such as pazopanib and sunitinib, interferon and VEGFR ligand monoclonal antibodies, such as bevacizumab, the treatment outlook remains poor [ ]. Therefore, prognostic prediction for these RCC patients is particularly important and provides a key basis for personalized treatment (Table  ) Recent research has highlighted the potential for diagnosing RCC by measuring the degree of epigenetic modifications in target genes within tissues, blood, or urine. A study performed cfMeDIP-seq on 99 patients with histologically confirmed RCC (69 plasma and 30 urine samples) and 28 cancer-free controls (13 plasma and 15 urine samples). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that plasma samples could accurately classify RCC patients at all stages (AUC = 0.99) and confirmed the efficacy of using urinary cell-free DNA for RCC diagnosis (AUC = 0.86) [ ]. Another study analysed genome-wide DNA methylation levels in tumours and adjacent benign tissues from 96 RCC patients with different histological types and identified a set of DNA methylation biomarkers that could reliably distinguish tumour tissue from adjacent benign tissue [ ]. 
Active surveillance of small renal masses (SRMs) is important for the early diagnosis of RCC. Žalimas et al. collected and measured the methylation levels of the TFAP2B, TAC1, PCDH8, ZNF677, FLRT2, and FBN2 genes in 165 urine samples from 39 patients diagnosed with SRM (specifically RCC) and in samples from 92 healthy volunteers serving as a control group. In terms of the diagnostic potential for individual genes, PCDH8 demonstrated the best performance, with a sensitivity of 48.7% and a specificity of 88%. A group of four biomarkers, namely, ZNF677, PDCDH8, TAC1, and FLRT2, had the highest diagnostic power (AUC = 0.736, P < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 92% and a specificity of 52% [ ].
Currently, diagnostic biomarkers for epigenetic modifications focus primarily on DNA methylation. Although these studies show promising potential, research in this area remains limited, and there are no reports of clinical applications of such technologies yet.
In a cohort study, PCHD17 and NEFH promoter methylation levels were determined by quantitative methylation-specific PCR in surgical specimens and normal adjacent tissues from 41 RCC patients with long-term follow-up. The results showed that high methylation of PCDH17 is associated with shorter disease-specific survival (DSS) and disease-free survival (DFS), while high methylation of NEFH is linked to shorter DSS. This result suggest that the promoter methylation levels of these two genes are of good value as prognostic biomarkers for RCC [ ].
The metastasis of cancer cells often portends a poor prognosis. In one study, computational analysis was used to examine 154 tumour-adjacent normal tissue samples, 189 RCC tumour samples, and 194 metastatic tissue samples from 95 patients with metastatic RCC. DNA methylation at the NKX6-2 locus was associated with cancer cell metastasis, suggesting that its methylation level could serve as a biomarker for metastatic prognosis [ ].
Klümper et al. found that low methylation of the CTLA4 promoter is an independent predictor of improved prognosis in RCC patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade therapy [ ]. In addition, several single loci DNA methylation sites have the potential to serve as prognostic biomarkers, including RUNX1 [ ], RAB17 [ ], LRRK2 [ ], IL18 [ ], and TACSTD2 [ ]. Despite the promising predictions shown by these studies, there are certain shortcomings, including flawed experimental designs and small sample sizes.
Multigene combination prediction improves the accuracy of prognostic assessment. A study developed a prognostic risk model using tissue samples from 336 patients with non-metastatic ccRCC, and validated the model using tissue samples from 64 ccRCC patients and 232 additional cases. The results showed that a prognostic biomarker model combining the methylation levels of GREM1, GATA5, LAD1, NEFH, and NEURL with clinical models can effectively predict patient prognosis [ ]. Similarly, another study constructed a five-gene methylation signature prognostic prediction model, including CCNB2, CDKN1C, CTSH, E2F2, and ERMP1. And this model also showed good prognostic predictive ability [ ].
Current studies reported the promise of RNA modification-associated regulators as biomarkers of RCC-independent prognosis. For example, one study analyzed the expression data of m6A regulators in 529 ccRCC patient samples and 72 adjacent normal kidney tissues, finding that the level of YTHDF2 expression significantly predicts the overall survival (OS) of ccRCC patients and can be considered as an independent prognostic factor (P < 0.001) [ ]. Other studies have focused on the METTL family as a single-gene prognostic biomarker, including METTL1 [ ], METTL14 [ ], and METTL24 [ ]. These genes have all demonstrated excellent prognostic predictive capabilities in ccRCC.
Multifactor joint prediction models outperform single-factor prediction models. Chen et al. constructed a dual-gene predictive risk model that incorporates METTL3 and METTL14 and validated its predictive ability in ccRCC [ ]. In another study, a dual-gene prognostic model incorporating IGF2BP3 and RNPC was constructed and identified them an independent prognostic indicators of papillary renal cell carcinoma (pRCC) [ ]. Zhang et al. constructed a three-gene predictive risk model incorporating NOP2, NSUN6, and TET2. The model demonstrated excellent prognostic ability (1-year AUC = 0.798,3-yyear AUC = 0.750, and 5-year AUC = 0.768) [ ]. Moreover, Li et al. developed a four-gene predictive risk model by adding NSUN4 to the genes identified by Zhang et al. This model exhibited a strong ability to distinguish between high-risk and low-risk patients (training cohort AUC = 0.723; testing cohort AUC = 0.689), with a significantly shorter OS in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group [ ].
Currently, ncRNAs have also been reported as prognostic biomarkers for RCC. Du et al. investigated the ability of plasma exosomal miRNAs to predict metastatic RCC (mRCC). This study revealed that miR-Let-7i-5p demonstrates significant value as an independent prognostic indicator, showing a significant correlation with OS (P = 0.018, HR = 0.49, 95% CI = 0.21–0.84) in patients with mRCC. Furthermore, miR-Let-7i-5p levels and the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) score were used to perform a multivariate analysis, which significantly improved the predictive power of the MSKCC score alone for survival prognosis in patients with RCC [ ]. In another study, a 4-miRNA score (miR-30a-3p/−30c-5p/−139-5p/−144-5p) was constructed to predict the risk of metastasis in RCC. Multivariate Cox regression analysis has shown that the model (C-index 0.86, p = 1.1E-06) is more accurate than the Leibovich score (C-index 0.74, p = 1.0E-3) for predicting individual risk of metastasis as an independent prognostic factor [ ].
Furthermore, rather than being limited to a single type of epigenetic regulation, prognostic prediction models constructed by integrating multiple epigenetic modification-related genes have demonstrated even greater predictive power. For example, Yang et al. constructed a risk prediction model grounded in genes linked to m6A modification and histone acetylation by analysing gene expression data from 541 tumour tissues and 72 normal tissue samples. Using this model, they successfully predicted the OS of ccRCC patients at 1 year (TCGA cohort AUC = 0.863; validation cohort AUC = 0.900), 3 years (TCGA cohort AUC = 0.819; validation cohort AUC = 0.903), and 5 years (TCGA cohort AUC = 0.809; validation cohort AUC = 0.912) [ ].
In summary, although the clinical application of epigenetic regulation in the prognostic diagnosis of RCC has not been widely reported, given the current enthusiasm of the scientific community in this field and its broad prospects, major breakthroughs are expected to be achieved in the near future.

## Epigenetic modifications and therapy
Currently, RCC treatment employs a stage-adapted strategy, tailoring interventions based on disease extent and patient risk factors. For localized disease (70% of cases), partial or radical nephrectomy remains the preferred treatment, with partial nephrectomy favored for tumors ≤ 7 cm to preserve renal function, achieving 5-year cancer-specific survival > 94% [ ]. Alternatives include active surveillance for small renal masses (< 2 cm) in elderly or comorbid patients, and ablative techniques (cryoablation, radiofrequency ablation, stereotactic radiotherapy) for tumors < 3 cm in surgically high-risk individuals [ ]. Adjuvant pembrolizumab for one year is recommended for intermediate- and high-risk clear cell RCC after nephrectomy, representing the first systemic therapy demonstrating overall survival benefit in this setting.
For advanced or metastatic RCC, first-line treatment consists of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combination therapy, either dual ICI blockade (ipilimumab plus nivolumab) or ICI plus tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) regimens, yielding objective response rates of 42–71% based on IMDC risk stratification [ ]. Cytoreductive nephrectomy is no longer routinely recommended. For hereditary VHL disease-associated RCC, the HIF2α inhibitor belzutifan offers a targeted option, while management of treatment-related toxicities requires multidisciplinary collaboration due to immune-related adverse events and TKI-associated toxicities [ ].
Owing to the drug resistance of RCC and interindividual variability in drug tolerance, the efficacy of immunotherapy and targeted therapy as first-line nonsurgical treatments for RCC is often suboptimal. Given the extensive roles of epigenetic modifications in RCC and their reversible nature, modulating these modifications is becoming a promising therapeutic strategy for RCC.
MLO1302, a newly synthesized 3-nitroflavone, alters global DNA methylation levels in RCC cell lines, resulting in both antitumour activity and demethylating activity [ ]. Moreover, many tumour suppressor genes (including SETD2 [ , ], PCK2 [ ], and UQCRH [ ]) are silenced due to hypermethylation in RCC, suggesting the therapeutic potential of DNMT inhibitors such as MLO1302.
High-dose injections of vitamin C can activate TET2 and maintain 5hmC levels in RCC, leading to the hypomethylation of IRF1 and thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy. This research revealed that the use of vitamin C together with immune checkpoint inhibitors is promising for RCC therapy [ ].
Epigenetic mechanisms can increase the sensitivity of RCC to anticancer drugs. For instance, epigenetic silencing of TRIM37 [ ] and promotion of PCK2 [ ] expression can increase RCC sensitivity to sunitinib. Epigenetic silencing of IGFL2-AS1 can increase RCC sensitivity to pazopanib [ ]. These findings suggest that targeting these factors could provide new therapeutic options for RCC patients who are insensitive to targeted drugs.
METTL14 typically functions as a tumour suppressor in RCC. YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification by METTL14 suppresses ITGB4 expression [ ] and promotes PTEN expression [ ], thereby inhibiting the PI3K/Akt pathway and blocking the EMT process. In addition, METTL14 mediates m6A modification of Lnc-LSG1, recruiting YTHDC1 to Lnc-LSG1 and thereby blocking the interaction between Lnc-LSG1 and the ESRP2 protein. This inhibition prevents the ubiquitination and degradation of ESRP2, ultimately suppressing the progression of ccRCC [ ]. Similarly, METTL14 mediates m6A modification of the lncRNA NEAT1_1, which is subsequently recognized by YTHDC2 and contributes to NEAT1_1 degradation, thereby inhibiting the EMT triggered by NEAT1_1 [ ]. Although METTL14 appears to play a beneficial role in RCC, its true effect on RCC may be two-fold. For example, Chen et al. reported that METTL14 enhances the stability and expression of TRAF1 mRNA in an IGF2BP2-dependent manner, thereby promoting resistance to sunitinib in RCC patients [ ].
Given the positive promotional effect of the PVT1-HIF-2α positive feedback loop in ccRCC, HIF-2α inhibitors are expected to exert significant antitumour effects. Specifically, the HIF-2α inhibitor PT2385 strongly inhibits tumour growth in an in vivo xenograft tumour model [ ].
Recent studies have shown that resveratrol, a nonflavonoid natural polyphenolic compound, has unique efficacy in the treatment of RCC. Resveratrol inhibits the progression of a human renal cell line (ACHN) through histone acetylation [ ]. Another study further confirmed this phenomenon. Specifically, resveratrol inhibits the expression of EP300 and CBP proteins, reducing histone acetylation at the RBM15 promoter and thereby suppressing CCNB1 expression. Ultimately, this phenomenon promotes M1 macrophage polarization in ccRCC and affects ccRCC through the induction of cellular senescence [ ]. Erianin is a natural product of the bibenzyl group extracted from the traditional Chinese medicine Dendrobium chrysotoxum [ ]. A recent study demonstrated that Erianin promotes m6A modification of ALOX12/p53 mRNA, thereby enhancing ferroptosis in renal cancer stem cells [ ]. Ginsenosides, including ginsenosides RB1, RG1, and RG3, are the active ingredients of ginseng [ ]. They have multiple effects on disease treatment, including RCC. A recent study demonstrated that the ginsenoside RG3 can promote the demethylation and histone acetylation of tumour suppressor genes (including p53, p21, and p16) to increase their expression, which in turn inhibits the progression of RCC and promotes apoptosis [ ]. These studies suggest that natural organics are promising for the treatment of RCC.
The reversible nature of aberrant epigenetic modifications provides a fundamental rationale for their therapeutic targeting in cancer. Consequently, clinical strategies have been developed to directly reverse these modifications, as exemplified by histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTi). Evidence from preclinical studies indicates that HDAC inhibitors (HDACi) suppress RCC metastasis to the lungs in mouse models [ ]. Evidence from a clinical trial suggests that the HDAC inhibitor vorinostat, combined with isotretinoin, exhibits clinical activity in patients with refractory metastatic RCC [ ]. However, clinical validation of HDACi in RCC is still lacking, especially when compared to their well-established efficacy in certain other cancer types, such as colorectal cancer [ ], non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ], and prostate cancer [ ]. Similarly, while DNMTi have revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies, their clinical activity in RCC as monotherapies has been limited. A phase II trial of guadecitabine (a DNMTi prodrug) in SDH-deficient tumors, which included an HLRCC-RCC patient, showed no objective responses despite evidence of biological demethylation [ ],underscoring the challenge of translating epigenetic reversal into efficacy in RCC.
Despite the limited efficacy of DNMTi and HDACi as monotherapies in RCC, their synergistic effects in combination with conventional therapeutics have shown considerable promise. One study demonstrates that in SETD2-deficient ccRCC, decitabine triggers an effective antiviral immune response within the tumor microenvironment by inducing RNA mis-splicing and the subsequent viral mimicry response. This process serves as a critical immune "priming" event in vivo, which significantly enhances the efficacy of subsequent anti-PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade. Consequently, the combination therapy achieved synergistic tumor growth suppression in preclinical models [ ]. Notably, emerging evidence also indicates that HDAC inhibitors can potentiate the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. For instance, the novel agent OBP-801 demonstrated a robust synergistic effect with anti-PD-L1 therapy, resulting in a significantly more potent inhibition of tumor growth in murine models than either agent alone [ ]. Besides, in a clinical trial, the combination of the histone deacetylase inhibitor abexinostat and the VEGF inhibitor pazopanib was associated with a prolonged median overall survival, particularly in patients with high HDAC2 expression [ ]. Collectively, although the current body of evidence supporting the combination of DNMTi and HDACi with other therapeutics in RCC remains limited and primarily derived from early-stage investigations, these initial findings collectively suggest a promising therapeutic avenue that warrants further rigorous exploration.

## Conclusion
RCC poses a significant global health challenge because of the suboptimal efficacy of its diagnosis and treatment. This review discusses the underlying mechanism of epigenetic regulation in RCC and its potential clinical application. Epigenetic regulation, especially DNA methylation and m6A modification of RNA, can promote the growth, metastasis and invasion of RCC. For instance, the chain reaction initiated by abnormal epigenetic regulation of SETD2 involves multiple types of epigenetic regulation. Aberrant methylation of genes such as JAK3 and ELF5 is also involved in the progression of RCC. Furthermore, there are significant differences in the expression levels of epigenetic regulators between RCC tumours and normal tissues. Given the reversibility of epigenetic regulation, these findings present novel opportunities for the treatment of RCC. Therefore, it is imperative to not only elucidate the mechanisms of action of epigenetic regulation but also utilize this knowledge to develop novel therapeutic interventions.
