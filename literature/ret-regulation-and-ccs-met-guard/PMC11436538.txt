## Introdction
Gliomas stand as the predominant malignant primary tumors within the central nervous system (CNS), comprising around 30% of all primary brain tumors and 80% of malignant instances. Furthermore, they constitute a significant factor contributing to mortality rates associated with primary brain tumors [ , ]. Despite the utilization of intensified multimodal therapies, including surgical resection in conjunction with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and the recent introduction of tumor-treating fields (TTFs), the prognosis for GBM remains discouraging. This is primarily due to the tumor's ability to evade anti-tumor treatments by employing various signaling pathways [ , , ]. The recently discovered GSCs also demonstrate a unique resistance to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy [ ]. The existence of the Blood–Brain Tumor Barrier and Blood–Brain Barrier further exacerbates the challenges associated with administering medications to brain tumors [ ]. Typically, individuals diagnosed with GBM experience disease relapse within a span of 7–9 months on average. This unfortunate recurrence often leads to a rapid progression of the illness, ultimately resulting in fatality. The median survival period for GBM patients does not surpass 15 months, and the cumulative five-year survival rate stands at approximately 5% [ , ]. The urgent necessity lies in the advancement of efficacious approaches aimed at enhancing the treatment outcomes for GBM.
Over the past few years, researchers have recognized EphA2, a member of the Eph receptor family, as a prominent contributor to GBM. Previous investigations have suggested that EphA2, a member of the Eph receptor family, experiences significant upregulation in GBM cells. Furthermore, heightened expression of EphA2 has been linked to unfavorable survival outcomes among GBM patients [ , , ]. EphA2 plays a pivotal role as a regulatory factor in both tumor initiation and the progression of cancer [ ]. The influence of typical ligands and atypical gametes varies in their impact on the transformation and progression of tumors [ ]. The potential for GBM treatment lies in targeting EphA2. By comprehending the precise mechanisms through which EphA2 contributes to the development and progression of GBM, researchers can pinpoint potential intervention targets for therapeutic approaches. Inhibition of EphA2 signaling pathways could potentially circumvent treatment resistance, augment the effectiveness of current therapies, and enhance patient outcomes. Moreover, targeting EphA2 presents a distinct opportunity to disrupt the resistance of GBM stem cells to chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
In this review, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of the important roles of EphA2 in GBM development and treatment. We will discuss the oncogenic functions of EphA2 in GBM initiation and progression, as well as the potential of targeting EphA2 as a therapeutic approach. By understanding the clinical significance of EphA2 in GBM, we hope to contribute to the development of novel treatment strategies that can prolong survival and enhance the quality of life for GBM patients.

## EphA2-ephrin A1 signaling

## Basic structure of EphA2
Based on the homology pertaining to the sequence, structure, and binding affinity of the extracellular domain, the Eph receptors and their corresponding ephrin ligands can be categorized into two distinct subtypes, namely A and B [ ]. The EphA2 gene is situated on chromosome 1 and encodes a 130 kDa EphA2 protein comprising roughly 976 amino acids [ ]. EphA2 is characterized as a type I transmembrane receptor protein and is a member of the Eph receptor family, which constitutes the largest subfamily within the receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) [ , ]. The human genome encodes a total of nine EphA receptors (EphA1-8, EphA10) and five EphB receptors (EphB1-4, EphB6). These receptors exhibit a shared and unified structural framework. This framework encompasses a conserved N-terminal globular ligand-binding domain (LBD), a sushi domain, an epidermal growth factor-like motif housed within a cysteine-rich domain, as well as two fibronectin type III repeats (FN III 1 and FN III 2) found in the extracellular domain. The extracellular and intracellular regions are connected through a transmembrane helix (TM), while the intracellular region encompasses a juxtamembrane region (JM) that facilitates interactions with effector molecules. Additionally, it contains a tyrosine kinase domain (KD), a sterile alpha motif (SAM), and a PDZ-binding motif that initiates interactions with scaffold proteins such as PSD-95, SAP97, and MAGI-1 [ , ]. Eph receptors are associated with a set of eight ligands known as ephrins, which can be categorized into subclasses A and B. A-class Eph receptors predominantly interact with A-class ephrins, which are anchored to the cell membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) linkage. In contrast, B-class Eph receptors primarily bind to B-class ephrins that possess both transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains. The cytoplasmic domain of these ephrins harbors a tyrosine residue-rich cytoplasmic tail as well as a PDZ-binding motif, while the receptors themselves remain attached to the cell membrane through a transmembrane helix [ – ]. Despite the ability of an Eph receptor to engage with various ephrins, it is commonly observed that the EphA2 receptor predominantly interacts with ephrin A1 as its principal ligand [ ]. Receptor homotypic and heterotypic oligomerization encompass the participation of the extracellular domains, which include the ligand-binding domain (LBD) and the cysteine-rich domains [ , ]. The SAM domain actively engages in receptor-receptor interactions and has the potential to facilitate both homotypic and heterotypic oligomerization [ ] ( Fig.  ). 

## Canonical pathway of EphA2
The conventional pathway of EphA2 involves the interaction between an ephrin ligand (either subclass A or B) and the extracellular domain of EphA2. This binding event triggers receptor dimerization and subsequent autophosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues within the intracellular domain. Such autophosphorylation facilitates the formation of docking sites for downstream signaling molecules. In the context of EphA2, the preferred ligand is ephrin A1 [ ]. The association between EphA2 and hepatocyte growth factor receptor A1 on the cell membrane involves distinct interaction mechanisms that set them apart from other receptor tyrosine kinase families. In this classical pathway, both Eph receptors and hepatocyte growth factor receptor ligands are bound to the cell membrane. Activation by ligand binding initiates a unique bidirectional signaling mechanism known as "forward signaling" in Eph receptor-expressing cells and "reverse signaling" in hepatocyte growth factor receptor-expressing cells.In forward signaling, the hepatocyte growth factor receptor ligand triggers signaling that is directed towards the Eph receptor, specifically EphA2 in this case. This process, referred to as EphA2 forward signaling, induces receptor oligomerization and clustering near the membrane domain, leading to reciprocal phosphorylation of tyrosine residues and activation of the receptor's kinase activity. Multiple proteins, such as Ras family GTPase activating proteins, guanine nucleotide exchange factors, Vav, ephexin, focal adhesion kinase (FAK), cytoplasmic tyrosine kinases of the Src family, and the p85 subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), have been reported to interact with activated receptors and regulate signal transduction.
On the other hand, reverse signaling originates from the Eph receptor and is directed towards the hepatocyte growth factor receptor ligand. This phenomenon, known as EphA2-ephrin reverse signaling, involves the activation of hepatocyte growth factor receptor and its tyrosine kinase activity through interaction with transmembrane proteins like p75 NTR, TrkB, and Ret receptors.Ephrin-A1-induced activation of EphA2 results in the inhibition of FAK, Akt, and ERK phosphorylation, which helps control cancer cell motility, viability, and proliferation [ – ].

## Noncanonical pathway of EphA2
Eph receptors possess the capability to convey signals through non-classical signaling mechanisms as well. Within these mechanisms, they engage in interactions with other surface receptors and intracellular kinases, even in the absence of ligand binding and tyrosine kinase activation. This classification is commonly referred to as non-classical signaling. Diverging from the classical pathway, the non-classical pathway does not encompass the autophosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues in the intracellular domain of EphA2, nor does it trigger the activation of Src family kinases or the PI3K pathway. The non-classical pathway seemingly initiates distinct downstream signaling pathways, exemplified by Akt-mTORC1, Raf-MEK-ERK, and Pyk2-Src [ ]. EphA2 has been evidenced to engage in non-classical, ligand-independent interactions with E-cadherin, EGFR, HER2, and integrins. These interactions effectively regulate downstream signal transduction processes [ – ]. Furthermore, the activation of EphA2 involves phosphorylation events at serine 897, which are mediated by various kinases including AKT, RSK, PKA, and other members of the AGC family [ – ]. The non-classical pathway of EphA2 is intricately linked to numerous biological processes. Harada et al. propose that the phosphorylation of EphA2 at Ser-897 plays a pivotal role in both the establishment and preservation of epithelial tissues [ ]. Koshikawa et al. have substantiated the significant involvement of the non-canonical pathway of EphA2 in the regulation of MT1-MMP expression within the human tumor microenvironment [ ]. Miao et al. have elucidated the paramount significance of the Akt-EphA2 pathway in governing the invasive nature and self-renewal capacities of glioblastoma stem-like cells [ ]. In contrast, Zhou et al. elucidated that the functional activity of tumor-initiating cells in gliomas predominantly relies on the non-classical EphA2 pathway. This intricate pathway governs crucial aspects such as tumorigenicity, proliferation, stemness, and resistance to therapy through AGC kinase-mediated Ser-897 phosphorylation, consequently fostering the malignant progression of the tumor [ ]. The most recent study conducted by Zhou et al. illustrates that malignant cells within the tumor microenvironment are subjected to cellular stress, encompassing factors such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and therapeutic anticancer medications. In this context, cellular stress triggers the unconventional activation of the receptor tyrosine kinase EphA2 via the p38-MK2-RSK signaling pathway. This activation stimulates the migration of tumor cells [ ]. These alternative signaling pathways have been demonstrated to yield markedly distinct outcomes in contrast to ligand-dependent activation of EphA2, thereby playing a consequential role in the promotion of tumorigenesis in GBM (Fig.  ). 

## The expression of EphA2 in GBM
Previous research has provided evidence of EphA2's aberrant upregulation in diverse tumor types including breast cancer, ovarian cancer, prostate cancer, and colorectal cancer. In these malignancies, the heightened expression of EphA2 is closely linked to tumor aggressiveness, invasiveness, and unfavorable prognoses. By effectively inhibiting the expression or functionality of EphA2, it becomes possible to suppress the growth, invasiveness, and metastatic potential of tumor cells.In GBM, the transmembrane receptor protein EphA2 has been identified as being upregulated [ ]. The increased expression of EphA2 in GBM has been positively associated with tumor stage, progression, and patient survival [ ]. Miao et al. conducted a study that specifically pinpointed the phosphorylation of EphA2 at Ser-897 in grade IV human GBM samples. The presence of this phosphorylation suggests a correlation with the aggressive nature of GBM. Additionally, the study revealed strong staining of phosphorylated EphA2 in areas enriched with growth factors and invasive cells [ , , ]. The expression of EphA2 is primarily restricted to tumor cells in GBM and is rarely detected in normal brain tissue. This highlights its potential as a diagnostic marker for GBM [ ]. The regulation of EphA2 expression in GBM involves various factors, including gene amplification, transcriptional activation, and post-transcriptional regulation. It has been reported that gene amplification of EphA2 is a frequent occurrence in GBM and is linked to elevated levels of EphA2 expression [ ]. Moreover, studies have shown that the transcription factor E2F1 plays a role in activating EphA2 expression in GBM cells. This activation leads to enhanced proliferation and invasion capabilities in these cells [ ]. Furthermore, the post-transcriptional modulation of EphA2 expression through microRNAs has been identified in GBM [ , ].
The elevation of EphA2 expression in GBM is thought to be influenced by multiple factors, including genetic mutations. GBM cells often harbor genetic mutations in signaling pathways that can lead to increased EphA2 expression. For instance, the frequent amplification of the EGFR gene in GBM can activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, subsequently resulting in the upregulation of EphA2 expression [ , ]. In the tumor microenvironment, hypoxia is frequently observed in solid tumors, including GBM. This condition can lead to the upregulation of EphA2 expression through the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) [ , ]. Furthermore, the inflammatory microenvironment commonly associated with GBM has been linked to the upregulation of EphA2 expression. Inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) can activate signaling pathways that contribute to the elevation of EphA2 expression. Additionally, the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM), a crucial aspect of tumor progression, can also result in the increased expression of EphA2. ECM proteins such as fibronectin and laminin can activate signaling pathways mediated by integrins, thereby promoting the upregulation of EphA2 expression [ ]. Epigenetic alterations, encompassing DNA methylation and histone acetylation, have been acknowledged as supplementary regulators influencing EphA2 expression [ ]. Kung et al. have observed atypical DNA methylation and histone acetylation patterns in GBM, leading to the enhanced expression of EphA2 [ ]. These intricate and multifarious mechanisms collectively contribute to the amplification of EphA2 expression in GBM, emphasizing its importance as a prospective therapeutic target and biomarker in the management of this formidable cerebral neoplasm.

## Roles of EphA2 in GBM

## Tumor growth and invasion
The unequivocal demonstration of EphA2 overexpression in GBM cells has undeniably revealed its paramount role in fostering cellular proliferation, invasion, and metastasis within the tumor [ ]. The tumorigenic effects orchestrated by EphA2 are thought to be instigated by the activation of cascading signaling pathways [ ]. Yuho et al. have illuminated that ligand-independent EphA2 signaling takes place downstream of the MEK/ERK/RSK pathway, thereby facilitating the epidermal growth factor (EGF)-induced proliferation of GBM cells [ ]. Miao et al. have furnished evidence establishing the association between phosphorylation of S897, tumor grading, and Akt activation in GBM cells. The Akt-mediated phosphorylation of S897 stands as the principal mechanism underlying cell migration induced by ligand stimulation of EphA2. This discovery implies the involvement of Akt-EphA2 crosstalk in the migration and invasion of cerebral neoplasms [ ]. In addition, EphA2 has the ability to control the expression of critical genes that play a role in regulating the tumor microenvironment. These genes include cytokines, chemokines, and components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [ , ]. Furthermore, recent research has emphasized the crucial involvement of EphA2 as a cellular factor in the infection of Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV) within GBM cells. The upregulation of EphA2 enhances HCMV infection, thereby promoting the advancement of GBM [ ].

## Angiogenesis
Over the last few decades, extensive scientific investigations have unveiled that GBM is among the most profoundly angiogenic solid tumors [ ]. Recent studies have conclusively demonstrated the contribution of EphA2 to GBM angiogenesis, supported by its heightened expression within the tumor's vascular system. This discovery underscores the potential involvement of EphA2 in the formation of new blood vessels (neovascularization) [ , ]. The involvement of EphA2 in cancer-related angiogenesis is well-established and operates through the recruitment of phosphatidylinositdata: ol 3-kinase (PI3K) and guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs). This mechanism is partially mediated through interplay with angiogenic molecules, including growth factor receptors and adhesion molecules like integrins and cadherins. Importantly, EphA2 has been found to regulate the expression and activation of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR-2) at both the genetic and protein levels, thus exerting modulatory effects on tumor-associated angiogenesis [ ]. In GBM tumors, glioma stem cells have the capacity to undergo differentiation into endothelial-like cells, which plays a role in a phenomenon known as vasculogenic mimicry (VM) [ ], which leads to the formation of new blood vessels.In gliomas, the occurrence of VM positivity is closely associated with tumor grade and serves as a negative prognostic factor. GBM exhibits a remarkable propensity for vasculogenic mimicry (VM) formation compared to VM-negative gliomas. Notably, heightened expression of EphA2 has been observed in VM-positive gliomas, further emphasizing its association with VM formation. Studies suggest that EphA2 activates focal adhesion kinase (FAK), subsequently triggering the phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2). This signaling cascade ultimately results in the degradation of laminin 5 γ2 into γ20 and γ2x by matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and membrane type 1-matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP), stimulating the development of structures resembling blood vessels through a vasculogenic-like process [ , ].

## Resistance to therapy
Accumulating evidence strongly suggests that the expression of EphA2 plays a pivotal role in conferring resistance to various cancer treatments, including chemotherapy and radiation therapy. This role is believed to be mediated through the activation of downstream signaling pathways that promote cell survival and resistance mechanisms. An example is a study indicating that EphA2 mediates the oncogenic function of FLOT2 in gliomas. FLOT2 interacts with EphA2, leading to the activation of downstream oncogenic signaling pathways, such as PI3K/Akt3, Akt/FOXO, NF-kB, and TGF-b. These pathways contribute to cellular resistance against apoptosis, facilitate cell cycle progression, and promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in gliomas, ultimately culminating in treatment resistance [ ]. Moreover, EphA2 has been recognized as a prospective receptor for PDGFA. It is postulated that EphA2 might collaborate with PDGFRA in facilitating the transmission of PDGFA signals, thus playing a role in the resistance of GBM cells to PDGFRA inhibitors. This emphasizes the significance of EphA2 in the resistance mechanisms exhibited by GBM cells [ ]. Furthermore, recent studies have unveiled that EphA2 can facilitate the development of resistance to radiotherapy in cancer cells. It triggers the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway via DNA-PK/AKT/cyclin D1 signaling, thereby augmenting DNA repair mechanisms and promoting cell viability subsequent to radiation therapy [ ].

## Cancer stem cell maintenance
Apart from its elevated expression in GBM tumor cells, EphA2 also plays a crucial role in governing the proliferative potential and population size of GSCs. Recent investigations have illuminated the significant involvement of cancer stem cells in the development of GBM, as they are implicated in tumor initiation, progression, and resistance to treatment [ ]. The preservation of stemness in GBM is regulated by a multitude of molecular mechanisms, many of which center around the EphA2 signaling pathway. EphA2 is observed to be upregulated in human GBM stem cells and plays a role in fostering their self-renewal and maintenance of an undifferentiated state [ , ]. Another study has showcased that impeding the self-renewal and intracranial tumorigenic capabilities of cancer stem cells can be accomplished by provoking the degradation of EphA2 receptors using ephrin-A1, or by employing RNA interference (RNAi) to silence relevant genes. The consequent decrease in the population of cancer stem cells and/or their invasive phenotype subsequent to EphA2 silencing is correlated with the activation of ERK and AKT signaling pathways [ ]. Moreover, recent studies indicate that the intercommunication between EphA2 and other signaling cascades, such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, might play a role in sustaining cancer stem cells, thereby emphasizing the intricate control of CSCs in glioblastoma multiforme [ ].

## Prognostic biomarker
Extensive and conclusive scientific investigations have undeniably established that the presence of EphA2, a transmembrane tyrosine kinase, serves as an immensely valuable prognostic biomarker in GBM, an exceptionally invasive and malignant brain neoplasm. The unanimous conclusions drawn from these studies consistently indicate that patients with heightened EphA2 expression within their tumor tissue face considerable hurdles in terms of clinical outcomes. They are prone to a higher risk of diminished overall survival, implying constraints on their life expectancy. Furthermore, there is an escalated likelihood of tumor recurrence subsequent to treatment, effectively highlighting the potential menace of tumor reappearance in these individuals. Hence, the evaluation and monitoring of EphA2 expression levels not only provide invaluable prognostic insights but also underscore the possibility of disease management and treatment decision-making for GBM patients. With precise assessment of EphA2 expression, clinicians and researchers can more accurately foresee the clinical progression and treatment response in GBM patients, thereby enabling the formulation of targeted therapeutic strategies to enhance prognosis and survival rates. This knowledge can guide treatment selection, identify patients who may derive greater benefit from more intensive interventions, and facilitate the development of clinical trials evaluating novel therapies targeting EphA2. Ultimately, the incorporation of EphA2 as a prognostic biomarker holds the potential to optimize personalized medicine approaches and overall patient outcomes in the challenging landscape of GBM [ , ].

## Targeting EphA2 in GBM
Given the elevated expression of EphA2 in GBM cells and its limited expression in normal brain tissue, EphA2 has emerged as a highly promising candidate for immunotherapy in both adult and pediatric malignant gliomas [ , ]. At present, there are several clinical strategies being investigated for the treatment of GBM that specifically target EphA2. Some of these strategies include: (Fig.  ). 

## Peptide-based vaccines (PDC)
Peptides derived from EphA2 are being utilized to develop therapeutic vaccines that elicit an immune response to target and combat GBM cells expressing EphA2. These vaccines aim to provoke a focused immune reaction against tumor cells while preserving healthy tissue. During a phase I trial involving children diagnosed with brainstem glioma (BSG) and high-grade glioma (HGG) who tested positive for HLA-A2, researchers administered peptide vaccines targeting neuroglioma-associated antigens EphA2, IL-13Ra2, and survivin. These vaccines were administered via subcutaneous injection of GAA epitope peptide. Significantly, no severe systemic toxicity of grade III or higher was observed throughout the trial. Out of the 26 patients, only two exhibited disease progression during the initial two vaccine courses, while 19 patients maintained stable conditions. Moreover, two patients experienced partial relief, one had mild relief, and two patients achieved long-term disease-free status after undergoing surgery. Regarding survival rates, at the six-month mark, the rate of progression-free survival stood at 33%, with an overall survival rate of 73% [ , ]. These findings demonstrate the favorable advantages of this approach compared to other studies in terms of its efficacy and safety profile.

## Antibody–drug conjugates (ADC)
Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a promising and innovative therapeutic strategy. In a study conducted by Debinski, a pioneering EphA2-targeting agent called ephrinA1-PE38QQR was developed. This cutting-edge conjugate functions as a potent cytotoxin. Remarkably, this conjugate has exhibited remarkable efficacy and dose-dependent cytotoxicity against GBM cells that exhibit an overexpression of EphA2 receptors. Such efficacy has been demonstrated through various assays measuring cell viability and colony formation survival. The average IC50 of approximately 10–11 mol/L further exemplifies the potency of this conjugate.Significantly, the cytotoxic activity of this conjugate is selectively targeted towards GBM cells with high EphA2 expression. Normal human endothelial cells, cancer cells with moderate EphA2 expression, and GBM cells with downregulated EphA2 are not adversely affected by this therapeutic intervention. This specificity accentuates the exceptional selectivity and targeted nature of ephrinA1-PE38QQR within the realm of GBM treatment [ ]. The co-expression of EphA2, EphA3, EphB2, and IL-13RA2 has been found to be present in nearly 100% of patients diagnosed with GBM. Interestingly, these receptors are not only expressed within the tumor cells themselves but also within tumor-infiltrating cells, tumor-initiating cells, or GSCs, as well as the neovascular system [ ]. Hence, the concomitant targeting of these four receptors using cytotoxic agents presents a remarkable prospect for substantial disruption of the tumor ecosystem in GBM, leading to a notable reduction in disease recurrence. Ferluga et al. employed ephrinA5 (eA3) as a ligand, which binds to EphA2, EphA2, and EphB5 receptors, thereby devising a groundbreaking, selectively targeted cytotoxin for GBM cells. The exceptional efficacy and specificity of the eA50-based cytotoxin were evidenced by its ability to selectively eliminate GBM cells with an IC50 value of no less than 10–11 M. Significantly, this cytotoxin exhibited the capacity to concurrently target diverse compartments within GBM tumors, addressing the issue of tumor heterogeneity and establishing the potential of a multifaceted, multi-targeted approach in the treatment of GBM [ ].

## EphA2 agonist
Increased expression of EphA2 within tumor tissue is frequently accompanied by the loss or misplacement of its corresponding ligand, ephrinA1 [ ]. The upregulation of EphA2 and the concurrent decrease in ligand levels are associated with the frequent activation of the Akt pathway. This activation results in ligand-independent and invasive Akt-EphA2 crosstalk, which is implicated in aberrant signaling processes and has been linked to a negative prognosis in cancer [ , ]. The reinstatement of the EphA2 ligand has demonstrated the restoration of its tumor suppressive activity. This remarkable finding paves the way for innovative therapeutic approaches in cases where EphA2 is overexpressed in tumors. One such example is the utilization of dasatinib, a novel small molecule agonist for EphA2 and EphA4, which has been employed to induce the internalization of EphA2 receptors and inhibit chemotaxis in prostate cancer, breast cancer, and GBM cells [ ]. Comparable to the endogenous ligand ephrinA1, activation with dasatinib elicits the internalization and degradation of EphA2. Furthermore, dasatinib impedes the downstream Akt and ERK 1/2 kinase activities that are induced by EphA2. In vitro experiments have substantiated that dasatinib attenuates the migratory capability of tumor cells, aligning with the acknowledged ligand-dependent negative modulation of cellular locomotion mediated by Eph receptors. It is postulated that this effect is achieved through the inhibition of the AKT-mTORC1 and RAS-ERK oncogenic pathways, as well as the suppression of cellular adhesion and migration [ – ].

## tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI)
Within the majority of GBM tumors, there is an augmented expression and activation of tyrosine kinases. This highlights the notable role played by tyrosine kinases in the progression, recurrence, and resistance to treatment in GBM. Accordingly, small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have emerged as a compelling therapeutic approach for GBM, capturing considerable attention [ ]. Research findings have elucidated that the process of phosphorylation on EphA2 at position S897 assumes a vital role in determining the tumor's response to dasatinib. The presence of elevated levels of EphA2 phosphorylation at this specific site exhibits utmost importance for achieving sensitivity to dasatinib. The mechanism underlying the response to dasatinib involves intricate communication facilitated by caveolin-1 (CAV-1) between EphA2 and BRaf. This interplay induces the localization of BRaf and EphA2 to the plasma membrane, effectively disrupting the formation of BRaf/CRaf heterodimers and subsequently leading to the downregulation of the MAPK pathway in cells sensitive to dasatinib [ ]. The amalgamation of crizotinib and dasatinib has exhibited the utmost cytotoxicity when evaluated in both established and primary human GBM cell lines. The simultaneous administration of dasatinib and crizotinib leads to diminished migration and invasion of tumor cells, while also restraining vascular formation and vascular mimicry in in vitro models [ ].

## Adoptive cell therapy (ACT)
This strategy entails modifying immune cells obtained from patients, including T cells or natural killer cells, to express receptors that specifically bind to EphA2, such as chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) or T-cell receptors (TCRs). By engineering these cells, they become capable of recognizing and eliminating GBM cells that express EphA2. This targeted approach leads to a potent anti-tumor response [ ]. Preclinical studies have shown encouraging results regarding the anti-tumor activity of EphA2-directed CAR-T cells in the treatment of high-grade glioma (HGG). Recently, the initial human trial involving the intravenous administration of EphA2-CAR-T cells to advanced GBM patients has demonstrated both safety and temporary clinical responses [ ]. In specific, the introduction of constitutively signaling IL-7 receptor (C7R) expression on EphA2-CAR-T cells has exhibited promising outcomes concerning their proliferation, survival, and anti-tumor activity. These positive results have been observed in both in vitro CAR-T cell assays and in vivo models using HGG xenografts [ ]. Indeed, the study conducted by Zhijing et al. has developed two third-generation CARs that target distinct epitopes of EphA2. The EphA2 CAR-T cells generated in this manner have demonstrated significant efficacy by effectively eliminating 20%-50% of GBM tumors in an orthotopic xenograft SCID mouse model [ ]. Muhammad et al. have enhanced CAR-T therapy for GBM through the creation of a groundbreaking TanCAR construct, which incorporates concatenated IL 13 (4 MS) and EphA2 scFv molecules. This innovative TanCAR approach has demonstrated heightened effectiveness and specificity in the eradication of GBM cells, surpassing the individual IL 13 CAR or EphA2 scFv CAR methods. This advancement holds promise in terms of preventing antigen escape and mitigating off-target cell toxicity [ ]. The application of CAR-T cell therapy in treating GBM encounters several hurdles, such as the transport of cells, the presence of a profoundly immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and the existence of varying antigen expression and loss. Moreover, limitations regarding safety, toxicity, and the manufacturing procedures for CAR-T cells persist. Nevertheless, EphA2-CAR-T cell therapy presents itself as a viable alternative for GBM treatment, as it has exhibited at least temporary responses and tolerable adverse effects. By further enhancing efficacy, adaptability, and safety measures, it holds the potential to become a promising therapeutic option for GBM.

## GSC
Cancer stem cells constitute a distinct population of cancer cells characterized by their remarkable adaptability. These cells possess the ability to withstand therapy-induced stress, driving tumor recurrence and metastasis while facilitating clonal rearrangements within cancer cell subpopulations. The identification and targeted therapy of new biomarkers for GSCs hold critical importance in the treatment of GBM. EphA2 has been demonstrated to impede dislodging apoptosis, a key trait observed in CSCs [ ]. Co-expression of EPHA2 and EPHA3 marks the population of GSCs in treatment-resistant recurrent GBM (rGBM) [ ]. Affinito et al. delineated a pair of RNA aptamers, specifically the 40L sequence and its abbreviated variant A40s, which exhibit affinity for the cellular outer layer of human GSCs by means of the EphA2, employing a distinct technique known as cell SELEX (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment). These aptamers exert a suppressive effect on GSC proliferation, maintenance of stem-like properties, and migratory behavior. Notably, A40s possesses the capability to traverse the formidable blood–brain barrier while retaining robust stability in serum during in vitro examinations [ ].
It is crucial to acknowledge that the clinical approaches aimed at EphA2 in GBM are presently undergoing investigation, necessitating further research to enhance their effectiveness and ensure safety. Nevertheless, the prospect of EphA2 as a therapeutic target in GBM presents a hopeful avenue for enhancing the treatment outcomes of this formidable brain malignancy.

## Conclusions
Over the years, a wealth of research has demonstrated that EphA2 expression is primarily confined to tumor cells in GBM, with minimal presence in normal brain tissue. Elevated levels of EphA2 in GBM have been correlated with unfavorable prognosis, and its interaction with ligands plays a pivotal role in the development, growth, and metastasis of GBM. Consequently, EphA2 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in GBM, and significant strides have been made in comprehending its mechanisms and the application of targeted therapy. However, the precise workings of EphA2, particularly its involvement in EphA2-ephrin reverse signaling and non-canonical signaling pathways, remain largely elusive, making it challenging to predict its regulatory outcomes reliably. The diverse signaling modes of EphA2 receptors further complicate targeted strategies, and consensus regarding the relative efficacy of agonists and antagonists has yet to be reached.Nonetheless, inhibition or targeting of EphA2 continues to exhibit substantial benefits in experimental studies, with several targeting approaches such as PDCs, ADCs, EphA2-CAR-T, and TKIs demonstrating noteworthy anti-tumor activity in GBM treatment. In broader clinical trials, EphA2 has been successfully employed as a tumor antigen for targeting GSCs and inhibiting GSC cell growth, stemness, and migration, showcasing considerable progress. Additionally, the success of various EphA2-targeted therapies in clinical settings provides additional options for resistant diseases, paving the way for combination therapies that synergize with anti-EphA2 treatments. Nevertheless, extensive clinical trials are warranted to establish their clinical utility conclusively.It is believed that in the near future, immunotherapies focusing on EphA2 hold promise for clinical implementation. To summarize, EphA2 represents an immensely promising therapeutic target in GBM. Further research is imperative to elucidate the functional mechanisms of EphA2, its interactions with proteins, and the upstream regulatory mechanisms. Preclinical and clinical investigations should continue to develop more EphA2-targeted therapies.
