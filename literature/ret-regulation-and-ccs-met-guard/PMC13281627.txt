Here, we report a mitochondrial suicide program which is activated in response to thermal flux to terminate this early stage of mitochondrial hyperactivity. A conserved stem loop at the mitochondrial origin of replication of the light strand operates as a thermal sensor, denaturing upon enhanced thermal flux and repressing the replication of the parental heavy strand of mitochondrial DNA. This triggers a quasi-replication of mitochondrial DNA characterised by replication of only the parental light strand. The non-replicated single-stranded heavy strand is then released and operates as a natural antisense DNA which sequesters complementary mRNAs encoded by the heavy strand. Subsequent degradation of the bound mRNAs by RNase H1 completes the cycle by triggering mitochondrial transcriptional decline and ultimately mitochondrial death.

## Introduction
In a brief temporal window after induction of neural differentiation, mitochondrial hyperactivity [ , ] primes progenitor cells for adoption of a differentiated state [ – ]. The contribution of mitochondrial hyperactivity to differentiation dynamics in this early stage appears to extend beyond energetic support of differentiating cells. We recently reported that hyperactive mitochondria transiently fuse with the nuclear membrane to acquire nuclear encoded RNAs at an early stage of neuronal differentiation [ ]. Subsequently, mitochondrial polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase) degrades the acquired RNAs in the intermembrane space of the organelle [ ]. This interaction remodels the transcriptome of progenitor cells and facilitates emergence of the neuronal transcriptome [ ]. While remodelling of the transcriptomic landscape of neural progenitor cells (NPCs) by mitochondrial activity facilitates neuronal differentiation, a form of negative feedback appears to be necessary to regulate the scope of inter-organellar communication to ensure that the resulting transcriptomic stress remains within the adaptive capacity of NPCs.
Feedback mechanisms typically operate based on an “ATP surplus” principle by dampening mitochondrial activity after a period of ATP synthesis. To date, two respiratory control mechanisms have been described. The first is triggered by inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase upon mitochondrial transition to a state of increased proton motive force (Δp) after a period of ATP synthesis [ , ]. The diminished activity of cytochrome c oxidase reduces the rate of ATP synthesis in state 4 mitochondria. The second mechanism of respiratory control refers to allosteric inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase at a high ATP/ADP ratio. [ , ] ATP exerts this inhibitory effect by binding to the matrix domain of subunit IV of cytochrome c oxidase [ ]. In a repressed state, the activity of the complex diminishes by five- to ten-fold [ ]. While both respiratory control mechanisms are based on ATP surplus, early neural differentiation is characterised by a transient shift to a negative ATP economy [ ]. Hence, an alternative negative feedback mechanism, invoked by inter-organellar communication, appears to be required to regulate mitochondrial activity during neuronal differentiation.
Thermal flux is a by-product of mitochondrial metabolic activity [ ] that contributes to the early stage of neural differentiation [ ] by rewiring signalling pathways of NPCs [ , ]. Thermal flux in a differentiating progenitor cell is sensed by specific proteins and nucleic acids. Here, “sensing” is used to denote a molecular conformational change that directly gives rise to a calibrated negative feedback signal. At a protein level the ankyrin domain of Notch-1, a pro-stemness transcription factor, exemplifies a thermal receptor which converts thermal energy to micromechanical oscillations [ ]. These vibrations lead to destabilization of the Notch1/RbpJ complex and trigger eventual dissociation of the transcriptional complex upon a rise in nuclear temperature of 3–4 °C thereby switching off the Notch1 pathway [ ]. At an expression level, modulation of the abundance of heat shock proteins appears to regulate neurodevelopment [ ] via a temperature-based feedback input. In this context, certain heat shock proteins are emerging as critical regulators of neuronal differentiation in addition to functioning as generic chaperones [ ]. To modulate the abundance of heat shock proteins, ribosomal accessibility of the encoding mRNAs is regulated at a post-transcriptional level by the action of cryptic thermo-sensory elements embedded within the 5´-untranslated region of mRNA [ , ]. A similar mechanism facilitates adaptation of bacteria to alterations of exogenous temperature [ ]. Repression of heat shock gene expression (ROSE) exemplifies a well-studied thermal riboswitch composed of 2–4 hairpins (60-100bps in total) that mask the Shine-Dalgarno sequence at 5’-untranslated regions (UTR) of heat shock proteins [ , ]. Gradual melting of the riboswitch between 30 and 42 °C exposes the Shine-Dalgarno sequence enabling ribosome binding and translation of mRNA [ ].
Here we report that the highly conserved stem loop in the mitochondrial origin of replication of the light strand, required for initiation of light-strand DNA synthesis [ ], operates as a thermal sensor/switch. We demonstrate that temperature-dependent melting of the thermal switch uncouples synthesis of leading and lagging strands of the mitochondrial genome leading to release of the paternal heavy strand as a single-stranded DNA. The single-stranded DNA operates as a natural antisense DNA that hybridises to mitochondrial-encoded RNAs (meRNAs) and triggers degradation of the associated mRNAs by activity of RNase-H1. This activity expands the reported functions of RNase-H1 in mitochondria that include directing replication of mDNA at origin-specific sites [ ], removal of RNA primers at origins of replication [ ], and removal of R-loops [ ]. RNase-H1 mutations in the catalytic domain of the enzyme are characterised by diminished activity of the mitochondrial complexes I and IV and a reduced copy number of mDNA together with multiple deletions [ – ]. RNase-H1-mediated depletion of meRNAs, reported herein, is a programmed event invoked by generation of single-stranded mDNA, as opposed to the known function of this enzyme in removing accidental R-loops. Programmed RNase-H1-mediated depletion of meRNAs amplifies production of reactive oxygen species and triggers mitochondrial degeneration. We demonstrate that the described mitochondrial suicide program restricts the pace and scope of inter-organellar communication to prevent a loss of cell viability during neuronal differentiation.

## Results

## A decline of mitochondrial population occurs during early neuronal differentiation
Our previous work demonstrated that a phase of mitochondrial hyperactivity contributes to differentiation dynamics of mouse NPCs in the subventricular zone of developing mouse brain by direct remodelling of the transcriptomic profile of NPCs [ ]. This phenomenon occurs at embryonic day (E) 13 by transient fusion of mitochondrial outer membrane and nuclear membrane leading to a direct communication between these organelles (inter-organellar communication or IOC). Further exploration of the zone of mitochondrial hyperactivity revealed a pronounced (≈ 50%) depletion of mitochondrial outer membrane protein TOMM20 in clusters of cells within the outer subventricular zone relative to cells in the ventricular and marginal zones (Fig.  A). Further investigation of the TOMM20low zone by employing laser capture microdissection to isolate the residing cells and to quantify the copy number of mitochondrial DNA (mDNA) disclosed that in TOMM20low cells the ratio of mDNA/gDNA was ≈ 50% lower than the adjacent TOMM20high cells of the ventricular/inner subventricular zones (Fig.  B). The proportional reduction of TOMM20 and mDNA prompted investigation of the diminished level of TOMM20 as representing a true decline of the mitochondrial population. To address this question, ultrastructural analysis of cells in the TOMM20low zone revealed a decline of mitochondrial populations (Fig.  C). This mitochondrial decline was a consistent finding (observed by TEM in > 100 cells in ultrathin sections) in NPCs residing in the outer subventricular zone (for additional evidence see supplementary Fig. ). Further analysis by TEM provided evidence of mitochondrial degeneration in cells populating the subventricular zone of the neural tube at E13 (Fig.  D). Decline of mitochondrial populations in NPCs within the subventricular zone suggested that mitochondrial degeneration could potentially be associated with neuronal differentiation. To explore this possibility, an established in vitro model of neuronal differentiation, was employed.

 

In this model system, uniform neuronal differentiation across the cell population was triggered by withdrawal of growth medium and subsequent incubation of C17.2 NPCs in neural induction medium (NIM: Neurobasal medium/Glutamax/N2 supplement). To track the fate of mitochondria, the organelles were pre-labelled with PKmito ORANGE FX (50nM) for 30 min in the spent growth medium (i.e. after 16 h of incubation). Differentiation was then induced, and NPCs were fixed at regular intervals to visualise the mitochondria using a fluorescence microscope. Shortly after induction of differentiation, some mitochondria adopted a dilated spherical morphology by t=30 min post induction of differentiation (Fig.  E, top). The magnitude of this transformation increased by t=60 min post-induction of differentiation (Fig. 1E, top). Analysis of differentiating NPCs by electron microscopy provided insight into the nature of this transformation. Ultrastructural study of NPCs at t=60 min post-induction of differentiation revealed mitochondrial degeneration, evidenced by disappearance of mitochondrial internal cristae and emergence of disordered multilayered aggregates within mitochondria, adjacent to a region characterised by fusion of mitochondria and the nucleus (Fig.  E, bottom left). Corroborating the ultrastructural evidence of mitochondrial degeneration, we noted significant reduction of the level of ATP after t=45 min post-induction of differentiation (Fig.  E, bottom right). This trend continued linearly until the last studied time point at t=135 min (Fig.  E, bottom right). Also, both immunocytochemical fingerprinting (supplementary Fig. ) and Western blot analysis of TOMM20 in differentiating NPCs indicated depletion of the protein by t=60 min despite a modest increase by t=30 min (Fig.  F). The results suggested that induction of differentiation in vitro leads to a phase of mitochondrial hyperactivity that peaks by t=30 min followed by a rapid decline of mitochondrial populations. To validate this assertion qPCR analysis of mitochondrial DNA was performed. The DNA of isolated mitochondria was amplified by application of specific primers designed to detect regions within Rnr1, Cox1 and Nd5 loci of mDNA. These selected regions correspond to mtDNA643–748, mtDNA5594–5727, and mtDNA11857–12070 providing quantitative insight into different regions of mtDNA and hence improving the robustness of mtDNA measurements. The analysis revealed ≈ 50% reduction of the abundance of mtDNA by t=60 min relative to baseline (Fig.  G), corroborating the findings in situ regarding depletion of mtDNA in differentiating neurons (Fig. 1A). Such reduction of mtDNA was aligned to the morphological and biochemical evidence of mitochondrial degeneration between t=30 min and t=60 min after induction of differentiation. We wondered if mitochondrial degeneration is a negative feedback mechanism that is linked to inter-organellar communication.
To test this hypothesis, live imaging of mitochondrial dynamics was undertaken. The inner membrane of the organelles was labelled with the photostable fluorescent marker, PK Mito Orange (PKMO), prior to induction of differentiation and NPCs were then visualised by live-cell Tau-STED nanoscopy at a high frame rate (1frame/sec).
Our attempts to include a nuclear marker resulted in significant phototoxicity due to the high frame rate of Tau-STED nanoscopy required to capture the fast dynamics of inter-organellar communication in differentiating cells (as per Fig.  H). As such, we relied on characteristic association of mitochondria and the nucleus separated by the nuclear envelope to study the directionality of IOC (i.e. extension of mitochondrial extrusions toward the nucleus). Corroborating the findings of Fig.  E regarding mitochondrial spherical degeneration, mobile PKMO+ mitochondria became static and adopted a dilated spherical morphology shortly after induction of differentiation (Fig.  H, supplementary Movie 1). Further, mitochondrial degeneration only affected mitochondria undergoing inter-organellar communication (IOC) characterised by a cycle of protrusion into the nucleus and subsequent retraction of the mitochondrial inner membrane (Fig.  H, supplementary Movie 1). This observation was aligned to ultrastructural findings in situ hinting at potential association of IOC and mitochondrial degeneration (Fig.  E, 1I). It appeared that mitochondrial degeneration not only is a facet of neuronal differentiation, but is also a consequence of IOC during the early stage of neuronal differentiation. A relevant question was if association of IOC and mitochondrial degeneration reflected more than a temporal co-incidence and whether the two events were mechanistically or causally linked. A causal link would imply that mitochondrial degeneration could not be invoked independently of IOC during neuronal differentiation. This notion was investigated. It was previously shown that IOC can be prevented by inhibiting the retrograde movement of mitochondria towards the nucleus [ ]. To this end, application of a specific inhibitor of dynein, dynarrestin (25µM), one hour prior to induction of differentiation prevented replication of mitochondrial DNA occurring within 30 min of induction of differentiation and the subsequent ≈ 4-fold reduction of the copy number of mDNA between t=30 min and t=60 min (Fig.  J). The rapid pace of IOC (minutes) provided an opportunity to abolish IOC while avoiding most other bystander effects of dynarrestin that either require a longer duration of exposure to the inhibitor or that emerge beyond the studied temporal window of 2 h. Subsequent analysis of the metabolomic profile of dynarrestin-treated NPCs supported the lack of a significant bystander effect (See supplementary Fig ). As dynein resides in an autoinhibited state and the activity of Lis1 protein, encoded by the Lissencephaly 1 gene, is required to dis-inhibit dynein [ ], this component was also targeted. RNAi-mediated inhibition of Lis-1 using a cocktail of two DsiRNAs (see methods) 18 h prior to induction of differentiation, effectively prevented the loss of mDNA during early neuronal differentiation (Fig.  K). Findings suggested that while IOC drives neuronal differentiation [ ], it triggers a concomitant wave of mitochondrial degeneration, potentially as a negative feedback mechanism to regulate the pace or extent of IOC. Further validation of the latter interpretation was contingent upon identification of the mechanism of mitochondrial degeneration to enable targeted manipulation of the degeneration process during neuronal differentiation.

## Induction of differentiation rewires the metabolome of progenitor cells
With the aim of uncovering clues regarding the mechanism of organellar degeneration, the metabolic profile of differentiating NPCs was investigated. A combination of metabolomic and lipidomic analysis of NPCs was employed to generate a blueprint of an emergent metabolic shift upon induction of neuronal differentiation (supplementary Data S1 and S2). To separate the IOC-mediated mitochondrial reprogramming from a generic metabolic shift induced by neuronal differentiation medium, dynarrestin was employed (25µM, applied one hour prior to induction of differentiation) to prevent IOC. LC/MS was used to build the metabolomic and lipidomic profiles of NPCs upon induction of differentiation.
Induction of neuronal differentiation resulted in an increased intracellular level of glucose at t = 0.5 h, a trend that was reflected in the level of the key downstream metabolites of the upper glycolysis pathway, namely, fructose 1,6-diphosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (Fig.  A). Activation of the upper glycolysis (i.e. conversion of glucose to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate) pathway was only partially related to the intermediates in the lower glycolysis pathway (3-phosphoglycerate, 2-phospho glycerate, and phosphoenol pyruvate) as evidenced by a modest 1.5-fold increased level of these metabolites (supplementary Fig. ). The intracellular level of the last metabolite in glycolysis, pyruvate, decreased below the baseline level of the metabolite upon induction of neuronal differentiation (Fig.  A). While the amplified synthesis of upper glycolysis intermediates is an expected outcome of exposure to neural induction medium, which contains 0.086mM insulin/25mM D-glucose, the restricted metabolic flux downstream to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate suggested reduced activities of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase [ ] and pyruvate kinase [ ], two key rate-limiting enzymes of the lower glycolysis pathway. Both these enzymes are directly inhibited by oxidative stress [ , ] diverting the metabolic flux from upper glycolysis into the pentose phosphate pathway to regenerate the NADPH pool and the associated antioxidants, thereby boosting the adaptive capacity of cells in an oxidising milieu. Consistent with this notion, increased abundance of intermediates of the pentose phosphate pathway and a concomitant elevation of NADPH/NADP ratio was detected during neuronal differentiation (Fig.  B, supplementary Fig. ). Further, repression of this metabolic shift in dynarrestin-treated NPCs hinted at contribution of IOC to this early reprogramming event.

 

A second metabolic imprint of differentiating NPCs was the elevated level of acetyl-CoA (Fig.  C). Given the repression of the lower glycolysis intermediates, in particular the diminished level of pyruvate, ß-oxidation of fatty acids could provide an alternative route for production of acetyl-CoA. Supporting this inference, increased levels of acylcarnitines and reduced abundance of triacylglycerols were detected (Fig.  D) corroborating the notion that free fatty acids were utilised to fuel ß-oxidation. Also noted was increased abundance of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and a delayed increase of lysophosphatidic acids (from t = 0.5 to 1 h) (Fig.  E) that function as precursors of membrane glycerophospholipids and triacylglycerols. Corroborating this finding, increased abundance of phosphatidyl serine and phosphatidyl choline was detected at t = 2 h post-induction of differentiation (Fig.  F). This observation suggested that the metabolic flux from upper glycolysis was in part diverted to synthesis of metabolites that could eventually be utilised to replenish the lipids catabolised by ß-oxidation (Fig.  C) via Land’s cycle [ ]. Repression of the level of phospholipids and triacylglycerols in dynarrestin-treated NPCs highlighted interpretation of the role of IOC in this metabolic reprogramming.
Another facet of metabolic reprogramming was concerned with increased abundance of succinyl-CoA/succinate and oxaloacetate despite repression of all other tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) metabolites (Fig.  G). Significant depletion of triacylglycerols containing odd chain fatty acids (e.g. TG 49:1-(15:0) and TG 51:1-(15:0), see supplementary Data S2) suggested that some odd chain fatty acids (pentadecenoyl acid in the example above) fuel the ß-oxidation cascade, an interpretation that was strengthened by lack of significant enrichment of odd-chain fatty acids amongst the detected species of phosphatidyl choline (e.g. a diminished level of PC(33:1) containing pentadecenoyl acid, see supplementary Data S2). The increased level of oxaloacetate/glutamate, on the other hand, aligns with concomitant reduction of the level of aspartate/α-ketoglutarate suggesting potential involvement of a transamination reaction in generating oxaloacetate. Notably, oxaloacetate is a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase (complex II of the electron transport chain) [ ] and one may surmise that elevation of oxaloacetate in parallel to succinate was necessary to fine-tune the activity of mitochondrial complex II, a notion that is validated in a subsequent section of the present paper.
Together, the emerging picture was consistent with a biphasic metabolic reprogramming of NPCs upon differentiation that is best described as an early (i.e. within t = 0.5 h) shift to an oxidising milieu that triggered a late phase (> t = 0.5 h) of lipid ß-oxidation and shunting of glycolytic flux towards lipid biogenesis due to the repression of glycolysis intermediates downstream to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. Supporting this inference, an ultrastructural feature of NPCs within the first 2h of inducing differentiation was expansion of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum proximal to mitochondria (Fig.  H, supplementary Fig. ). It is known that lipid transfer between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum occurs at the sites of contact between these organelles [ ] and therefore expansion of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum would support the shift to lipid-based metabolism in differentiating NPCs. To confirm the validity of this interpretation a proteomic profile of NPCs was generated by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS, supplementary Data S3).
After induction of neural differentiation, NPCs (2 × 107 cells/time point) were harvested at t = 0.5 h, 1 h, and 2 h and processed for further analysis by LCMS (methods). Gene ontology analysis of the LCMS profile revealed that proteins regulating the pentose phosphate pathway, glutathione metabolism and oxidative stress response were more abundant at t = 0.5 h compared to control NPCs (Fig.  A, supplementary Fig. ). In parallel, reduction of proteins that were mapped to ribosome assembly, RNA processing, and cap-dependent RNA translation at t = 0.5 h was noted (Fig.  A). Such depletion of ribosomal proteins could be related to the documented susceptibility of cysteine and methionine residues of these proteins to oxidative damage [ , ]. Likewise, it is known that amplified oxidative stress leads to a global inhibition of various stages of RNA translation [ ]. Together, the remodelled metabolomic, lipidomic, and proteomic profiles suggested mitochondria experienced an enhanced oxidative stress along with a shift to ß-oxidation of fatty acids upon induction of neural differentiation.

 

Evidence of an amplified oxidative stress in the zone of mitochondrial decline in developing (E13) mouse neural tube was probed. Analysis of distribution of 8-hydroxyguanine (8-OHG: a marker of oxidative DNA damage) by immunohistochemical staining of E13 neural tube revealed that 8-OHGhigh NPCs exhibited a TOMM20low profile in the subventricular zone of mouse developing neural tube (Fig.  B). Findings in vitro disclosed a similar co-localization of 8-OHG and TOMM20 in NPCs at t=30 min post-induction of differentiation (Fig.  B). To complement DNA-level evidence of oxidative damage, the abundance of malondialdehyde (MDA, a by-product of lipid oxidation) was assessed in differentiating NPCs in vitro. Fluorometric quantification of the level of MDA showed increasing concentration of the compound in differentiating NPCs from t=30 min to t = 2 h (Fig.  C). Evidence of enhanced oxidative stress compelled us to ask whether mitochondrial degeneration was in part or entirely a consequence of the oxidative damage. To answer this question, we were required to manipulate mitochondrial production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), a task which was not possible without deeper mechanistic insight into mitochondrial ROS production upon induction of differentiation.
Generation of ROS by a forward electron flow or a reverse electron transport (RET) [ ] in the mitochondrial electron transport chain was investigated. NPCs were harvested and incubated with a complex I inhibitor, rotenone (100nM), and a complex III inhibitor, antimycin A (1µM), for 10 min in DPBS. Rotenone, upon association with complex I, decreases ROS production via RET in mitochondria oxidising the complex II substrate succinate but amplifies ROS production in mitochondria respiring on complex I substrates [ ]. Antimycin A amplifies generation of ROS in mitochondria respiring on complex I or II substrates [ ]. After incubation with the inhibitors for 10 min, neural induction medium was added to trigger production of ROS and oxidation of DCFH (2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein) was used to detect production of ROS [ ] (Fig.  D). While inhibition of complex III by antimycin-A resulted in slight reduction of the level of ROS, exposure to rotenone triggered a marked decrease in production of ROS (Fig.  D, left). This pattern of responsiveness to inhibitors was consistent with RET (reverse electron transport) as the main mechanism for amplification of ROS immediately after induction of differentiation. Notably, application of dynarrestin to prevent IOC, reduced the level ROS with an effectiveness comparable to that of rotenone (Fig.  D, right) suggesting that fusion of mitochondrial and nuclear membranes somehow triggers RET and amplifies ROS. Two proposed mechanisms for induction of RET include an over-reduction of the available pool of coenzyme Q and a high proton motive force (Δp), both of which render the forward electron flow thermodynamically unfavourable. Given that IOC amplifies ΔpH component of Δp due to influx of protons associated with nuclear-encoded RNAs [ ], we investigated whether an amplified ΔpH exceeds the thermodynamic threshold for induction of forward electron flow. Mitochondria were isolated from NPCs at baseline and T = 0.5 h post-induction of differentiation and resuspended in 500 µl of RNase-free water. RNase-A was then employed to degrade RNAs in the mitochondrial intermembrane space and release the RNA-associated protons [ , ]. The difference in proton release after RNase-A treatment at baseline and T = 0.5 h, denoted ΔΔpHT0:T0.5 (ΔpHT0.5-ΔpHT0), was ≈ 0.17 units (Fig.  E). Based on a conservative calculation that the apparent ΔΔpHT0:T0.5 reflects a ≈ 5314-fold dilution of protons upon release from the mitochondrial intermembrane space to the water-electrode interface (see methods and supplementary Fig. for details), the corrected ΔΔpHT0:T0.5 within the intermembrane space was estimated as ≈ 3.4. Using the Nernst equation at 37 °C, a ΔΔpH of 3.4 corresponds to an electrical potential difference of 210mV (see methods). That is, the locally generated Δp via activity of the electron transport chain is amplified by 210mV owing to imported protons via IOC. A favourable thermodynamic condition for forward electron transport requires that the reduction potential difference of the NAD+/NADH and CoQ/CoQH2 redox couples (ΔEh) for 2 electrons to transport across complex I must be large enough to allow pumping of 4 protons against the proton motive force (Δp) across the mitochondrial inner membrane or 2ΔEh> 4Δp [ ]. Findings suggest that a conservative 4Δp of 840mV contributed entirely by imported protons exceeds 2ΔEh (760mV), a condition that is conducive to RET (Fig.  E). It was concluded that the early shift to an oxidizing milieu is a consequence of RET-induced ROS in response to IOC-mediated import of RNA-associated protons. Surprisingly, when NPCs were harvested at T = 0.5 h post-induction of differentiation and the impact of rotenone and antimycin-A on ROS activity was probed, oxidation of DCFH was not impacted by either inhibition of complex I, or by suppression of complex III (Fig.  F, left). However, application of 1mM oxaloacetate diacetate, a competitive inhibitor of complex II, attenuated the production of ROS (Fig.  F, right). The latter finding is in agreement with the suggested role of complex II in contributing to ROS production [ ]. Further, pre-exposure to oxaloacetate prevented mitochondrial degeneration as evidenced by an increased copy number of mitochondrial DNA upon induction of differentiation (Fig.  G). Together, findings suggested that while an early wave of ROS production by complex I is triggered by IOC-mediated RET, a second wave of ROS production ensues owing to the activity of complex II respiring on succinate. Given that accumulation of succinate is partially dependent on β-oxidation of odd chain free fatty acids, we tested whether suppression of β-oxidation reduces oxidative stress in mitochondria and abolishes mitochondrial decline following the induction of differentiation.
NPCs were incubated with 1µM etomoxir, an inhibitor of carnitine palmitoyl transferase-I (CPT-I), which prevents shuttling of long chain free fatty acids to mitochondria [ ] (supplementary Fig. S7). In parallel, (R)-Bromoenol lactone ((R)-BEL) was used at a concentration of 10µM to inhibit mitochondrial calcium-independent phospholipase A2-γ (iPLA2γ). Enzymatic activity of iPLA2γ protects mitochondria from oxidation-induced lipid peroxidation and dysfunction [ ] and inhibition of the enzyme is expected to amplify the impact of ß-oxidation on mitochondria. To determine the efficiency of inhibitors in altering mitochondrial redox state by modulating ß-oxidation, we deployed MitoTracker red CMXRos (50nM) which is retained in mitochondria by forming covalent bonds with the redox-sensitive thiol groups of proteins. Hence, a shift to an oxidizing milieu can be detected by diminished retention of the dye within mitochondria [ ]. NPCs were pre-treated by adding the inhibitors to spent growth medium one hour before induction of differentiation. NPCs were then detached, washed, and incubated with neural induction medium containing MitoTracker Red CMXRos. Cells were immediately transferred to an optical reader to record the intensity of MitoTracker Red CMXRos (recording frequency: 1/sec) at a baseline temperature of 37 °C. Upon induction of neural differentiation, etomoxir-treated NPCs showed 1.5-fold increased retention of the dye which contrasted sharply with pronounced reduction of the retained dye in mitochondria of (R)-BEL-treated NPCs (supplementary Fig. S7). The observed impact of etomoxir confirmed previous findings that ß-oxidation of fatty acids is a major contributor to oxidative stress within mitochondria. MitoTracker Green was employed to provide an indication of mitochondrial protein mass in differentiating NPCs. Etomoxir increased the apparent mitochondrial protein mass by 1.6-fold as opposed to a reduction by 1.6-fold of the mitochondrial protein mass in the presence of (R)-BEL (supplementary Fig. S7). Notably, combined application of the inhibitors effectively offset the impacts observed upon application of individual compounds (supplementary Fig. S7). NPCs were pre-treated with etomoxir (1 h) prior to induction of differentiation and the impact of this treatment on mitochondrial degeneration during differentiation was explored by quantification of mitochondrial DNA (mDNA). This treatment partially abolished the metabolic flux of mitochondrial populations (Fig.  H) observed during differentiation of control NPCs (Fig.  F). To confirm the role of ROS in triggering mitochondrial degeneration, cultured NPCs were exposed to 50µM H2O2 in spent growth medium (supplementary Fig. S7). Ultrastructural analysis of H2O2-treated cells disclosed features of mitochondrial degeneration within 15 min of exposure (supplementary Fig. S7) that were indistinguishable from degenerative changes triggered by neural induction in vivo and in vitro (Fig.  D, E). It was concluded that while RET-induced ROS were essential for initiation of the metabolic reprogramming that eventually leads to mitochondrial degeneration, the activity of complex II is essential for continued accumulation of ROS and for eventual degeneration of mitochondria.
Considering the evidence for the involvement of complex II in triggering mitochondrial degeneration we investigated the molecular basis for this phenomenon. One possibility was that RET-generated ROS damage mitochondrial complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) [ ], prompting a search for evidence of depletion of components of this complex by analysing the LC/MS data. This analysis revealed decreased abundance of the nuclear-encoded components of complex I by t = 0.5 h post-induction of differentiation, an observation that contrasted sharply with the increased abundance of subunits of complex II (Fig.  I, left). Mitochondria-encoded subunits of the electron transport chain were also depleted at t = 0.5 h post-induction of differentiation to a considerably greater extent than nuclear-encoded proteins (Fig.  I, right). Corroborating the LCMS profile, the analysis of mitochondrial-encoded mRNAs (meRNAs) isolated at defined time points after the induction of neural differentiation revealed significant depletion of three selected meRNAs encoded by the heavy strand (or H-strand) of the mouse mitochondrial genome, namely Rnr1, Nd5 and Cox1 (Fig.  J). Succinate dehydrogenase is the only component of the electron transport chain whose subunits are not encoded by mitochondrial DNA. Therefore, the emerging picture suggested that RET-induced ROS production by complex I not only initiates a redox-dependent metabolic reprogramming that leads to accumulation of succinate, but it also causes a depletion of the mitochondrial electron transport chain components except for nuclear-encoded complex II which respires on succinate and amplifies ROS production. Central to this phenomenon was a decline of mRNAs encoded by the mitochondrial genome concurrent with ROS-mediated metabolic reprogramming so that electrons transferred to complex II were used to generate ROS rather than being transported to upstream or downstream components of the electron transport chain. The basis for elimination of mRNAs encoded by the mitochondrial genome was investigated.

## A single-stranded mitochondrial DNA remodels mitochondrial transcriptome
A preliminary clue regarding transcriptional decline of mitochondria was provided by ultrastructural analysis of the replicating mitochondrial DNA (Fig.  A, left). While ultrastructural features typical of the strand displacement model of mitochondrial DNA replication were present, a second low molecular weight product that appeared to be a single stranded DNA (ssDNA) was also observed (Fig.  A, right). Partial degradation of this band following incubation with exonuclease I (2 units/20µl reaction buffer) confirmed that the band mainly consisted of a single stranded DNA (Fig.  B). However, the low molecular weight fragments generated as by-products of exonuclease I activity suggested the presence of duplex regions that hindered the enzymatic activity of exonuclease I. Such duplex regions have been reported to be mitochondrial DNA replication intermediates composed of RNA hybridised to the H-strand of mtDNA [ ]. To explore whether the duplex region contains hybridised RNA, isolated mitochondrial DNA was incubated with 2U of RNase H for 30 min (Fig.  B). This led to reduced intensity of the ssDNA suggesting the presence of RNA/DNA duplex regions.

 

We then used a gel electrophoresis mobility shift assay (EMSA) by employing S9.6 antibody that recognises RNA/DNA duplex. To exclude any non-specific binding of S9.6, we utilised a double-stranded DNA corresponding to a PCR-amplified fragment of Rnr1 locus of mDNA. Further, a higher molecular weight band corresponding to the double-stranded mDNA served as the internal control for the specificity of binding of S9.6 to RNA-DNA duplex. After 20 min of incubation with S9.6, delayed migration of the ssDNA both at t = 30 and 60 min after induction of differentiation was noted, while migration of the double stranded high molecular weight band corresponding to mtDNA remained unaffected (Fig.  C). These results supported the interpretation of RNase H treatment indicating that the ssDNA contains RNA/DNA duplex regions. To determine the identity of ssDNA, two biotinylated oligonucleotide baits complementary to the L- and H-strands of mtDNA were utilised. After isolation of the ssDNA using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1, only the H-strand of mDNA was detected (Fig.  D). This finding accorded with previous reports that the parental H-strand of mtDNA can be identified as mitochondrial replication intermediates in association with L-strand transcripts [ ]. By employing 11 specific primer sets spanning mouse mtDNA, it was confirmed that ssDNA encompasses the full length of the H-strand of mitochondrial DNA (Fig.  D). Having confirmed the identity of ssDNA as mitochondrial H-strand, we then explored the occupancy pattern of this ssDNA by meRNAs.
Isolated ssDNA-bound meRNAs were reverse transcribed using superscript III. The resultant cDNA library was analysed using qPCR in the background of ssDNA. Unequal representation of hybridised meRNA characterised by a reduction of the level of bound transcripts from the 5′ towards the 3′ end of the H-strand was noted (Fig.  E). A potential explanation for this observation was that preferential degradation of these meRNAs by processive exonuclease activity of mitochondrial RNase-H1 in 3′ to 5′ direction [ ] would lead to under-representation of 3′-meRNAs. To test the validity of this hypothesis mitochondrial RNase-H1 and Mgme1 (Mitochondrial genome maintenance exonuclease 1) were inhibited by employing RNAi. The underlying logic was that just as inhibition of RNase-H1 is expected to increase the level of meRNAs encoded on H strand of mtDNA in RNAiRNase−H1-treated cells (e.g. Nd-5), inhibiting degradation of ssDNA by targeting the nuclease Mgme1 should increase the level of ssDNA and thus expand the scope of activity of RNAse-H1 leading to amplified degradation of the meRNAs. In this experiment Nd-6 was selected as a control gene encoded on the L strand of mtDNA. After application of RNAi, the level of Nd-5 meRNA encoded by the H-strand of mDNA increased significantly, whereas the level of Nd-6 which is encoded by the L-strand of mDNA remained unaffected by RNAi treatment of NPCs (Fig.  F). Findings were consistent with a model of DNA replication in which the parental H-strand of mDNA is generated in an uncoupled manner from the L-strand (Fig.  G). This strand could function as a natural antisense DNA which hybridises to the meRNAs triggering RNase-H-mediated degradation of the transcripts (Fig.  G). Three specific questions needed to be addressed to gain functional insight into the proposed model of post-transcriptional regulation of meDNAs. Why is the single-stranded H-strand not protected by mitochondrial single-stranded DNA binding protein-1 (Ssbp1)? Why are the meRNAs hybridised to the H-strand despite a capacity to form a secondary structure? Finally, why is the replication of H and L-strands uncoupled leading to generation of the single-stranded H-strand during neural differentiation?
Of the three questions, the most accessible concerned the hybridization of meRNAs which indicated a loss of RNA secondary structure following the induction of differentiation. It was reasoned that mitochondrial generation of heat during neuronal differentiation [ ] could potentially explain a loss of meRNA secondary structure and hence the next set of experiments were designed to address the mitochondrial thermal flux. To record the temperature at nanoscale with high accuracy, an L-DNA molecular beacon (l-MB) [ ] probe was introduced into NPCs via electroporation [ ]. This approach, however, provides restricted information due to variation of the intracellular copy number of the probe across the population coupled with limitations of assessing the probe by live imaging microscopy including phototoxicity at a high frame rate. In this study, NPCs were detached, mixed with l-MB (at a ratio of ≈ 1010 probes/NPC) and suspended in 20 µl of spent growth medium (18 h post-incubation) or neural induction medium. The mixture was immediately transferred to a qPCR thermal cycler and the florescence signal of l-MB was recorded at a baseline temperature of 37 °C and at 1 s intervals (Fig.  H). While the temperature of NPCs in spent growth medium remained stable at 37 °C, we recorded a mean cross-population temperature of 44.6 °C within t=5 min of inducing differentiation followed by a gradual decline of the temperature to 42 °C by t=10min (Fig.  H). This thermal output approximates to thermogenesis at an average rate of 10nW/NPC which is higher that the reported average energetic output of 2.2 nW for a typical eukaryotic cell [ ], but lower that the thermal output of zebrafish embryos at the 2-cell stage (30nW/cell) [ ]. A mitochondrial lysate was then prepared using a sucrose gradient, mixed with l-MB and transferred to a qPCR thermal cycler and the florescence signal was recorded at a baseline temperature of 37 °C and at 1 s intervals. In state 3 mitochondria (with access to 1mM pyruvate, 1mMADP and 2mMPi) temperature approached 55°C within seconds and then gradually elevated to 58 °C (Fig.  I). This recorded peri-mitochondrial temperature using l-MB was in close agreement with the reported mitochondrial temperature of 54 °C by Terzioglu et al. who used protein reporters of temperature to record the thermogenic output [ ]. Findings together with those of others [ , ] suggest that mitochondrial temperature could rise significantly (≈ 15 °C) upon full activation of oxidative phosphorylation which is expected to establish an ultimate Δp of 210mV. While metabolomic reprogramming upon induction of neuronal differentiation renders the electron transport chain inactive, it was noted above that imported protons via IOC amplify ΔpH component of Δp by 210mV (Fig.  E). Replicating the measurement of thermal flux in dynarrestin-treated NPCs following incubation in NIM revealed an attenuated thermogenic capacity thereby strengthening the role of IOC in amplifying thermal flux (Fig.  J). Further supporting evidence for the notion of IOC-mediated thermogenesis was afforded by RNase-A treatment of the isolated mitochondria in the presence of l-MB to record the temperature (Fig.  K). While RNase-A treatment triggered a spike in temperature to 48 °C within seconds of application, the RNase-A-treated mitochondria lost capacity to maintain this temperature afterwards. The latter observation is compatible with the proposed role of RNA as a carrier for protons in the intermembrane space [ ], whose loss diminishes the capacity of the organelle to generate an effective ΔpH. Therefore, it is proposed that IOC-mediated import of protons from the nucleus not only triggers RET, but it also leads to a concurrent elevation of temperature upon dissipation of the proton gradient (Fig.  L), a notion that was consistent with an increased level of mRNAs encoding mitochondrial uncoupling proteins upon induction of differentiation (supplementary Fig. S8).

## A critical mitochondrial adaptation facilitates binding of meRNAs to mitochondrial single-stranded H-strand
To investigate the impact of temperature on the global melting profile of mitochondrial-encoded RNAs (meRNAs), we applied the intercalating dye Sybrgreen I, which separates from double-stranded regions of meRNAs upon denaturation of the transcript. As a complementary method, the metachromatic property of acridine orange was utilised. Mitochondria were isolated from NPCs and total mitochondrial RNAs were extracted and dissolved in RNase-free 18mΩ water. The RNA binding dyes were then added, and an optical reader was used to record the fluorescence signal. Both methodologies reported a similar melting profile with a population-level average Tm of 51 °C for meRNAs at a concentration of 2 µg in 50 µl of RNase-free 18mΩ water (Fig.  A, left). To complement this finding, we studied the Tm of select meRNAs using a more accurate methodology. We initially used the UNAfold platform to identify regions within mouse meRNAs that can form a double-stranded helix and to estimate the ΔG of these helices (Fig.  A, middle). From the thermodynamic profile of helices, tRNAs appeared to form more extensive and stable helices. Two tRNAs were selected, tRNA-Ala and tRNA-Arg, with a ΔΔG of 5 kcal/mol. The synthetic tRNAs (1 µg in 50 µl of RNase-free 18mΩ water) were labelled with a fluorescence reporter, 5′-FAM, and a quencher, 3′-TAMRA, to study the denaturation kinetics of the tRNA. In a control experiment, mixed meRNAs were added to the synthetic tRNAs anticipating that in a mixed population helices with a low ΔG will absorb the thermal energy and will hence function as a thermal buffer delaying the denaturation of tRNAs (Fig.  A, right). While tRNA-Ala showed a Tm of 53 °C in a mixed pool of meRNAs, tRNA-Arg had a Tm of 38 °C. The measured ΔTm of 15 °C was consistent with the calculated ΔΔG of 5 kcal/mol as for a typical 10–20 bp RNA duplex. Hence, the thermal output of mitochondria triggered by IOC (Fig.  I) is within an optimal range (38 °C to 53 °C) to eliminate the secondary structure of a majority of the mouse meRNAs with melting propensities within the Tm range measured for tRNA-Arg and tRNA-Ala. While denaturation of meRNAs facilitates hybridisation to mitochondrial ssDNA, the competing single stranded binding protein 1 (Ssbp1 is expected to associate with mitochondrial ssDNA and prevent binding of meRNAs. Hybridisation of meRNAs to mitochondrial ssDNA suggests that either the protein is not present in sufficient quantities or that binding of Ssbp1 to ssDNA is somewhat compromised during early neuronal differentiation.

 

Analysis of proteomic data revealed that the level of Ssbp1 increased from T0 to 0.5 h post-induction of differentiation (supplementary Data S3). It was therefore hypothesized that binding of Ssbp1 to ssDNA could potentially be compromised during early neuronal differentiation. A possibility that thermal flux affects binding of Ssbp1 to ssDNA was probed using a thermal shift assay, with Sypro orange as the reporter, and employing recombinant mouse Ssbp1 protein alone or in association with a circular ssDNA (corresponding to OriL flanked by the upstream region (-40 bp: C5119-G5158) and the downstream region (+ 45 bp: A5196-T5242) of the OriL). In parallel control experiments, we used recombinant mouse DNA polymerase gamma (Pol-γ), mitochondrial RNA polymerase (Polmrt) alone, and Polmrt bound to the mitochondrial origin of replication of light strand (OriL). The recombinant Polmrt and Polmrt/OriL were not destabilised as a function of increased temperature (Fig.  B). Likewise, the stability of recombinant Pol-γ did not appear to be affected by an increasing temperature. This is evidence by an absence of significant increase in Sypro orange signal intensity up to 48 °C, indicating that the hydrophobic residues of recombinant Pol-γ remain buried in the interior (core) of the folded protein. The recombinant Ssbp1, on the other hand, became destabilised as a function of temperature with the peak of instability occurring at 49 °C (Fig.  B). This surprising finding prompted search for a molecular basis for the thermal instability of Ssbp1. To find a clue, we simulated molecular dynamics of Ssbp1 at 37 °C and 50 °C using Gromacs package. A model of mouse Ssbp1 (AF-Q9CYR0-F1-v4) was used in the analysis. Figure  C shows the RMSF (root mean square fluctuation), which correlates the fluctuation, per amino acid during the trajectory of the dynamics, for (α)-carbon of amino acids. A region corresponding to W84-M119 of Ssbp1 fluctuated significantly more than other regions of the protein at 50 °C (Fig.  C). Notably, the stacking energy of W84 contributes to the binding of Ssbp to ssDNA [ ] and fluctuations of this region could potentially compromise the association of the protein and DNA. To understand the molecular basis for thermal instability of mouse Ssbp1, we mapped and compared adaptations that underpin a divergent evolution of the protein across the prokaryotic and eukaryotic domains of life.
Studying E. coli single-stranded DNA-binding protein (Ssb) using thermal shift assay disclosed remarkable thermal stability of the protein at 50 °C, a behaviour that clearly contrasted with thermal instability of mouse Ssbp1 (Fig.  D, top). Corroborating the experimental results, MD analysis revealed that temperature-dependent fluctuation of (α)-carbons of E. coli Ssb at 37 °C and 50 °C were within the same atomic range (Fig.  D, bottom). Local alignment of the region corresponding to W84-M119 of mouse Ssbp1 to E. coli Ssb indicated that the region was highly conserved both in prokaryotic and eukaryotic domains of life (Fig.  D), an observation that is not surprising given the reported role of this region in binding to single-stranded DNA [ ]. Interestingly, P92 appeared to be a structural signature of the mitochondria protein that is absent in all the other studied prokaryotes (supplementary Fig. S9). This highly conserved proline preceded an alpha helix “(G93-Y101), an observation that was at odds with the capacity of the amino acid to function as a disruptor of helices [ ], unless it was conserved to operate as a thermal destabiliser. We tested whether P92 could potentially contribute to the thermal instability of W84-M119. A conservative in silico mutation of proline to leucine (P92> L92) followed by molecular dynamics analysis provided theoretical validation that P92 is a major contributor to the thermal instability of mouse Ssbp1 (Fig.  E, left, supplementary Fig. S9). Consistent with these findings, uploading mouse mitochondria with SsbpP92> L92 or E. coli Ssb after transfection of NPCs using a eukaryotic expression vector followed by induction of neuronal differentiation, we observed lower abundance of RNA/DNA duplex (Fig. 5E, right). Also, the level of meRNAs in NPCs expressing SsbpP92> L92 or E. coli Ssb did not decrease upon induction of neuronal differentiation providing further assurance that ssDNA operates as a natural antisense DNA triggering RNase H-mediated degradation of meRNAs (Fig. 5F). This led us to question if offsetting this mitochondrial adaptation (thermally unstable Ssbp1) would be sufficient to prevent mitochondrial decline during neuronal differentiation. Quantifying the level of mtDNA with primers that hybridised to Nd5 locus provided evidence that mitochondria remain completely viable in NPCs expressing E. coli Ssb, while SsbpP92> L92 partially prevents mitochondrial decline (Fig. 5G). We concluded that mitochondrial thermogenesis not only triggers partial denaturation of meRNAs but it also destabilises Ssbp1 providing an opportunity for formation of RNA/DNA duplexes. We next asked what uncouples replication of mitochondrial light and heavy strands leading to generation of single-stranded H-strand.

## Mitochondrial OriL functions as a nanothermometer regulating generation of mitochondrial ssDNA
Given the evidence for asymmetric replication of mitochondrial DNA by uncoupling the polymerisation of the light and heavy strands, we focused on investigating the putative uncoupler. It is known that both the secondary structure (i.e. a conserved stem loop) and the primary sequence of OriL are required for initiating replication of the light strand of mDNA [ ]. To investigate if melting of the secondary structure of OriL uncouples the replication of the L-strand and H-strand of mDNA, a synthetic 37-mer oligonucleotide corresponding to A5159-T5195 of mouse mtDNA with a 5′ fluorophore, 6-FAM, and a 3′ quencher, ZEN-Iowa Black FQ, was utilised to study the melting kinetics of OriL. The probe was dissolved in 50ul of 0.1 mM TE buffer (final concentration: 1mM) and transferred to a qPCR thermal cycler. The intensity of fluorescent signal was recorded at 1 s intervals as a function of increasing temperature (25 °C to 75 °C). The synthetic OriL analogue exhibited a melting temperature (Tm) of 51 °C with > 90% of melting events occurring between 41 °C and 55 °C under the specified ionic conditions (Fig.  A). This initial analysis of Tm was done in the absence of any counter-ion. As expected, increasing the concentration of Na+ increased the Tm of the synthetic OriL analogue by presumptive shielding of the phosphate groups (Fig.  A). However, under physiological conditions ions are unlikely to interact entirely with the stem loop, as proteins function as a competing pool for sequestering ions. To address this scenario, we repeated the previous experiment with the highest concentration of NaCl in the presence of acetylated bovine serum albumin (5%) (Fig.  B). Inclusion of 5% BSA alone did not alter the melting kinetics of the synthetic OriL (Fig. 6B). Upon addition of 40mM NaCl, however, BSA completely neutralised the stabilising impact of counter-ions (Fig.  B) while inclusion of the osmolyte PEG200 in TE buffer reduced the Tm of the OriL in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig.  C). The apparent destabilisation of OriL by osmolytes is consistent with the reported impact of these molecules in reducing the stability of a DNA helix [ ]. Ca2+, a critical regulator mitochondrial function, had little impact on destabilisation of the synthetic OriL at low nM concentrations (Fig.  D, left). However, incubation of isolated mitochondria with Ca2+ triggered significant enrichment of the mitochondrial ssDNA consistent with the open state of the OriL and uncoupled replication of the heavy and light strands of mitochondria (Fig.  D, right). This was expected as Ca2+ functions as an allosteric activator of key enzymes in the Krebs cycle, particularly isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase [ ], thereby enhancing mitochondrial thermogenic capacity. We concluded that while the synthetic minimal OriL has an inherent Tm of 51 °C, the apparent Tm could be fine-tuned to adapt to the functional state of mitochondria. We then sought an alternative method of determining the Tm of OriL as a molecular beacon reports the separation of the fluorophore and the quencher due to melting of the 5′ and 3′ termini of the synthetic OriL raising the possibility of reporting a partial melting as full melting.

 

A variation of the hybridisation chain reaction (HCR) was devised in which the need for a reaction initiator was eliminated by increasing the temperature to melt two hairpin DNAs, a synthetic analogue of OriL (A5159-T5195) and another half-complementary hairpin with a similar Tm to OriL (Fig.  E). At a temperature corresponding to Tm, denatured hairpins could hybridise in a chain reaction and hence Thybridisation=Tm. This approach (thermal chain reaction) allowed determination of the Tm of hairpins in a reporter-independent manner. Thermal chain reaction was most efficient between 49 and 55 °C (Fig.  E), a range that accorded with the reporter-dependent range of Tm (Fig.  A). Finally, we questioned whether inclusion of OriL in a circular closed DNA could potentially alter the apparent Tm of OriL due to forces applied to the 5′ and 3′ termini of the OriL. A single-stranded mini-chromosome corresponding to the H-strand of mDNA was synthesized by CircLigaseII-mediated circularisation of the region of mDNA containing the OriL and extending to the proximal upstream region (-40 bp: C5119-G5158) and the downstream region (+ 45 bp: A5196-T5242) of the OriL. The melting dynamic of the mini-chromosome was compared to that of an isolated OriL using the intercalating dye Sybrgreen I (Fig.  F). Inclusion of OriL in a mini-chromosome altered the melting dynamics of OriL from a sigmoidal to a hyperbolic mode leading to a reduction of the apparent Tm by 7 °C (Fig.  F). An explanation for reduction of the apparent Tm of the OriL in a mini-chromosome was that forces applied to the 5′ and 3′ termini of the hairpin amplify the breathing kinetics [ ] of the DNA helix leading to a reduction of the Tm of OriL. To probe the impact of stem and loop breathing kinetics on melting dynamics of OriL, two mutations were introduced into OriL and the impact on Tm was studied. To attenuate breathing of the DNA helix, C5183 was mutated to T5183, a substitution that increases the length of the stem by 1 bp while simultaneously reducing the size of the loop by 2 bp (ΔTmhigh OL). To amplify the breathing dynamics of the DNA helix, an intra-stem mismatch was introduced by mutating C5163 to T5163 (ΔTmlow OL). A 5′ fluorophore, 6-FAM, and a 3′ quencher, ZEN-Iowa Black FQ, was utilised to study the melting kinetics of the mutated OriLs. While the Tm of ΔTmhigh OL increased by 4 °C, that of ΔTmlow OL decreased by 11 °C confirming the notion that amplifying the breathing dynamics of the DNA helix alters the melting dynamics of OriL (Fig.  G). Together, findings indicated that OriL functions as a thermal switch with melting kinetics that are within the range of the thermogenic capacity of mitochondria. Considering the importance of DNA secondary structure in recruiting mitochondrial RNA polymerase to OriL [ ], we explored whether thermal flux suppresses the polymerisation of the parental H-strand of mitochondrial DNA by denaturing the OriL stem loop.
We initially probed binding affinity of mouse recombinant Polmrt to a single-stranded circular DNA corresponding to mouse mitochondrial H-strand containing the OriL along with the proximal upstream region (-40 bp: C5119-G5158) and the downstream region (+ 45 bp: A5196-T5242) of the OriL (OriLWt). Variations of this circular single-stranded mini-chromosome were also synthesized in which a C5163 to T5163 (ΔTmlow OL) and a C5183 to T5183 (ΔTmhigh OL) were introduced (Fig.  H) to alter the Tm of the OriL as discussed above (Fig.  G). The mini-chromosome and Polmrt were incubated in TE buffer for 15 min, at a temperature of 25 °C and 37 °C in a thermal cycler. To stabilise the association of Polmrt and OriL via molecular crowding [ ] and to precipitate the complex [ ], polyethylene glycol (mw:8000, PEG8000) was added to a final concentration of 10% just before collecting the polymerase/OriL complex by centrifugation at 20000g for 2 min. The ssDNA was then purified using phenol-chloroform extraction and quantified using qPCR. No significant difference was observed in the quantity of pulled-down ΔTmhigh OL between samples incubated at 25 °C and 37 °C (Fig.  H). However, a significantly smaller amount of OriLWt and even lower quantity of ΔTmlow OL were recovered from the sample incubated at 37 °C compared to that incubated at 25 °C (Fig.  H). This is consistent with as estimated Tm of 47 °C for ΔTmhigh OL (7 °C lower than the isolated ΔTmhigh OL) which retains its secondary structure both at 25 °C and 37 °C, whereas OriLWt (Tm = 42 °C) and ΔTmlow OL (Tm = 31 °C) reside in a partially denatured state at 37 °C decreasing the affinity of Polmrt to OriL. Findings were validated using an in vitro model of mitochondrial H-strand replication adapted from Korhonen et al. [ ]. Mitochondrial mini-chromosomes were incubated with mouse recombinant Polmrt, recombinant DNA polymerase gamma (Pol-γ), and Ssbp1 in a reaction mix containing rNTPs and dNTPs for 40 min at 25 °C, 37 °C and 50 °C (Fig.  I). DNA was purified using phenol-chloroform extraction and quantified using qPCR. When OriLWt was used as a template, the quantity of replicated DNA at 37 °C was > 50% less than for incubation at 25 °C. Increasing the reaction temperature to 50 °C decreased the rate of replication of DNA by > 80% compared to incubation at 25 °C (Fig.  I). Further, the rate of replication of DNA in reactions with ΔTmhigh OL was higher than that of OriLWt at 37 °C and 50 °C, whereas no such difference was observed between the rate of replication of ΔTmlow OL and that of OriLWt (Fig.  I). It is noteworthy that these results are conservative estimates of the impact of stem loop on polymerisation rate as DNA polymerases follow Arrhenius kinetics [ ] and increasing the temperature from 25 °C to 37 °C (or from 37 °C to 50 °C) is expected to increase the extension rate of the polymerase by an average of ≈ 31% for a reported activation energy of 17 kcal/mol [ ]. We therefore concluded that OriL operates as a nanothermometer that melts in response to an increased thermal flux, thereby reducing the affinity of Porlmrt to OriL and uncoupling the replication of the heavy and light strands of mDNA (Fig.  J).That is, thermal flux not only triggers partial denaturation of meRNAs and a concurrent destabilization of Ssbp1, but also suppresses the replication of the H strand of mDNA leading to release of the parental H strand as a single-stranded DNA which hybridises to denatured meRNAs, followed by RNaseH-mediated degradation of these transcripts. An outstanding question was whether IOC not only uncouples the replication of mitochondrial H- and L-strand, but also triggers replication of the mitochondrial genome.

## Mitochondrial DNA replication is triggered by inter-organellar communication
To probe the relevance of IOC to replication of the mitochondrial genome, we asked if accumulation of the ssDNA by asymmetric replication of mtDNA would occur in the absence of inter-organellar communication. IOC was prevented by application of dynarrestin (25µM, 1 h prior to induction of differentiation) or by RNAi-mediated inhibition of Lis-1 using a cocktail of two DsiRNAs (18 h prior to induction of differentiation). Following the induction of differentiation, we did not observe significant accumulation of ssDNA in either treatment group (Fig.  A). To complement these results, mitochondria were isolated at t=30 min after induction of differentiation (mito0.5 h). The isolated mitochondria, which contained ssDNA/meRNA duplex, were incubated in EB buffer (2mM Pi) supplemented with pyruvate and ADP (Fig.  B, left). In isolated mito0.5 h, the ssDNA/RNA duplex was rapidly degraded. Supplying ADP/Pi (state 3 mitochondria) accelerated the degradation rate of the duplex compared to state 0 mitochondria (Fig.  B, right). We concluded that isolated mitochondria appeared to be deprived of DNA replication cues. It also appeared that amplified thermal flux (triggered by supplying ADP/Pi) was not sufficient to trigger the replication process. To confirm this, we replicated the experiment with state 3 isolated mito0.5 h at different temperatures to test whether the thermal flux could trigger generation of ssDNA (Fig.  C, left). The rate of degradation of ssDNA/meRNA duplex increased in a temperature-dependent manner (Fig.  C, right). Further, a reduction of double-stranded mtDNA in a temperature-dependent manner suggestive of mitochondrial degeneration was noted (Fig.  C, right). Therefore, it appeared that thermal flux increases the enzymatic activity of RNase-H1 in a temperature-dependent manner but does not trigger replication of mtDNA. A conclusion derived from these experiments was that isolated mitochondria lack a factor required for DNA replication. An essential clue was provided by the observation that in mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome, mutation targeting the Rrm2B subunit of ribonucleotide reductase reduces the mitochondrial pool of dNTPs leading to mDNA replication arrest [ ]. Likewise, other reports highlighted a competition between nucleus and mitochondria for acquisition of dNTPs [ ].

 

We therefore tested if addition of dNTPs rescues synthesis of ssDNA by triggering mitochondrial replication. After isolation of mitochondria from NPCs, dNTPs were added in an equimolar ratio of 2mM, along with 10mM pyruvate, 2mM ADP, and 2mM Pi and the isolated organelles were incubated at 25 °C and 42 °C for 40 min (Fig.  D). Increased copy number of mtDNA suggested that supplying dNTPs triggers the replication of mitochondrial genome (Fig.  D, right). Simultaneously, access to dNTPs resulted in significant accumulation of ssDNA in isolated mitochondria after 40 min of incubation (Fig.  D). While increased temperature appeared not to affect the level of ssDNA (Fig.  D, middle), a comparison with Fig.  C revealed that equal levels of ssDNA 25 °C and 42 °C occurred despite significant amplification of RNAseH1-mediated degradation of ssDNA at 42 °C, suggesting a significant boost of ssDNA generation at 42 °C. On the other hand, RNAi-mediated targeting of the subunits of ribonucleotide reductase, Rrm1 and Rrm2, to restrict the supply of dNTPs to mitochondria 16 h prior to induction of differentiation resulted in a significant reduction of ssDNA/meRNA duplex following induction of neuronal differentiation (Fig.  E). Further, mitochondrial degeneration in RNAiRrm1 and RNAiRrm2 NPCs was markedly less than the control NPCs undergoing neuronal differentiation (Fig.  F). Considering that ribonucleotide reductase catalyses the conversion of nucleoside diphosphates (NDPs) or triphosphates (NTPs) to deoxynucleotides [ ], it seems plausible that IOC supports replication of mtDNA by supplying NDPs to ribonucleotide reductase after importing and degradation of nuclear-encoded RNAs via PNPase [ ]. However, the trigger for initiation of replication at OH (Origin of replication of H-strand) remains unclear. One possibility is that while some 95% of mitochondrial replication events are terminated after about 650 nt [ ], supplying dNTPs could potentially reduce the frequency of aborted replication events [ ] rather than directly inducing replication of mtDNA. Nonetheless, validation of this hypothesis requires extensive experimentation beyond the scope of the present paper.

## IOC-mediated mitochondrial degeneration gives rise to a negative autoregulatory loop
Finally, we asked if IOC-mediated mitochondrial degeneration operates as a negative autoregulation mechanism that is invoked upon mitochondria-nucleus fusion to diminish the number of mitochondria poised for successive waves of interaction with the nucleus. Integration of such negative feedback is expected to reduce the rate of neuronal differentiation driven by IOC [ ]. To test this proposition, we employed two strategies to reduce the level of mitochondrial ssDNA. Cells were treated with 1mM hydroxy urea (HU), a specific inhibitor of ribonucleotide reductase, for 1 h prior to induction of neuronal differentiation. HU-treated NPCs exhibited an increased level of pro-neural transcription factor Ngn1 at t = 2 h post-induction of differentiation compared to the control cells (Fig.  A). Mouse NPCs were then transfected to express SsbpP92> L92 or E. coli Ssb followed by induction of neuronal differentiation. Quantifying the level of mRNA encoding pro-neural transcription factor, Ngn1, revealed significant upregulation of the level of this transcript in NPCs expressing SsbpP92> L92 (≈ 3 fold) or E. coli Ssb (≈ 4 fold) by day 3 of differentiation (Fig.  B). An interpretation of this finding was that enhanced stability of SsbpP92> L92 (or introduction of the thermally stable E. coli Ssb into NPCs) boosts the impact of IOC on driving neuronal differentiation by preventing mitochondrial degeneration (Fig.  C). We studied neuronal markers in differentiating NPCs to confirm these findings. Interestingly, immunohistochemical detection of neuronal differentiation markers, Tubulin-β3 and NeuN, revealed a different picture; the level of these proteins in NPCs expressing SsbpP92> L92 or E. coli Ssb at day 1 and 3 of differentiation was lower than that of the control cells (Fig.  D). In contrast, the level of phalloidin/F-actin was higher in the control cells compared to the transfected cells (Fig.  D). Considering that induction of autophagy is required to facilitate emergence of a new proteomic profile during the early stage of neuronal differentiation [ ], we wondered if inhibition of mitochondrial degeneration in SsbpP92> L92 or E. coli Ssb is responsible for uncoupling of the transcriptional and proteomic profiles in differentiating NPCs. We initially asked if the autophagic flux corresponds to the timeline of mitochondrial degeneration upon induction of differentiation. Probing the timeline of macro-autophagy revealed increased levels of Sqstm1 (p62), LC3, and ubiquitinated proteins by t=60 min followed by a reduction of the level of these proteins between t=60 min and t=90 min (Fig.  E). We then asked if mitochondrial decline contributes to the observed amplification of autophagy during neuronal differentiation. To probe this notion, we studied the autophagy in NPCs expressing SsbE. coli using a fluorescent reporter of autophagic flux. Cells expressing SsbE. coli were co-transfected with a reporter vector of autophagy, ptfLC3, and neuronal differentiation was induced after 16 h. Monitoring for GFP and RFP, to visualize autophagosomes (appearing yellow) and autolysosomes (appearing red), revealed a significant reduction of the autophagic flux in SsbE. coli NPCs after 24 h of incubation in the differentiation medium (Fig.  F). This finding was consistent with a reduction of autophagic flux in SsbE. coli NPCs. Therefore, we asked whether the reduced autophagic flux was linked to events that trigger mitochondrial degeneration or whether it is a direct energetic consequence of mitochondrial degeneration.

 

To arrest mitochondrial transcription and induce mitochondrial degeneration we employed the established method of using the intercalating dye, ethidium bromide (EtBr: 2 µg/mL), for 2h [ ]. Analysis of two meRNAs, Nd5 and Nd6, encoded on H and L-strands respectively of the mitochondrial genome, disclosed near-linear reduction of the level of both mRNAs within a period of 2 h from the application of the intercalating dye (Fig.  G), a reduction rate equivalent to that observed in differentiating NPCs. Mitochondria from EtBr-treated NPCs showed ultrastructural features similar to those of NPCs 1 h after induction of differentiation, namely a reduction of the number of internal cristae and disappearance of the matrix (Fig.  ). However, we did not observe noticeable evidence of enhanced autophagic flux upon mitochondrial decline in EtBr-treated cells (Fig.  G). We therefore focused on an alternative hypothesis that amplified production of ROS is responsible for induction of autophagy [ ]. TEM analysis of H2O2-treated cells (supplementary Fig. S7) provided evidence of amplified autophagosome formation (Fig.  H) corroborating reports that oxidative stress is a major inducer of autophagy [ ]. We concluded that ssDNA-dependent production of ROS not only propels a decline of mitochondria but also induces a concurrent wave of macro-autophagy (Fig.  I). In the proposed model, mitochondrial decline operates as a negative autoregulatory mechanism to regulate the rate of IOC and hence the scope of transcriptional remodelling of NPCs by IOC [ ]. The concurrent induction of autophagy facilitates remodelling of the proteome by eliminating the existing proteins and emergence of a new protein profile after translation of the remodelled transcriptome (Fig.  I).

## Discussion
Here, we provide evidence for a novel mitochondrial degeneration mechanism that is triggered by inter-organellar communication (Fig.  J). Central to this negative autoregulation mechanism is IOC-mediated thermal flux that uncouples replication of the heavy and light strands of mitochondrial DNA by denaturing the Origin of replication of the light strand. Accordingly, completion of the replication of the L-strand leads to release of the non-replicated parental H-strand as a single stranded DNA which hybridises to mRNAs encoded by the heavy strand. Degradation of ssDNA-bound mRNAs by mitochondrial RNase H completes this cycle by shutting down electron transport chain components encoded by the mitochondrial genome. Continued activity of complex II respiring on succinate leads to significant amplification of ROS and degeneration of mitochondria. The resultant decline of mitochondrial populations operates as a negative autoregulation mechanism that reduces the rate of neuronal differentiation driven by IOC. A key facet of IOC that triggers the cascade of events culminating in mitochondrial degeneration is significant amplification of mitochondrial proton motive force (Δp) owing to the release of RNA-associated protons imported from the nucleus.
Under homeostatic conditions, mitochondrial Δp is established by the activity of the electron transport chain pumping protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space of mitochondria. Hence establishment of Δp by this mechanism is subject to regulation by two respiratory control mechanisms discussed earlier. While the first control mechanism is triggered by inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase in an amplified state of proton motive force (Δp) [ , ], the second mechanism of respiratory control requires allosteric inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase at a high ATP/ADP ratio [ , ]. ATP exerts this inhibitory effect by binding to the matrix domain of the subunit IV of cytochrome c oxidase [ ]. IOC-mediated acquisition of RNA-associated protons from the nucleus provides an alternative route for establishment of an amplified Δp [ , ] that is independent of the activity of the electron transport chain and hence is not subject to regulation by known respiratory control mechanisms. A corollary of such an amplified Δp is enhanced mitochondrial thermogenic capacity which uncouples replication of the L- and H-strand of mitochondrial genome.
Mitochondrial DNA appears to replicate via two different mechanisms [ ]. According to the strand displacement model of DNA replication [ ], synthesis of L- and H-strand DNA proceeds continuously (i.e. without synthesis of Okazaki fragments) but in an unsynchronised manner [ ]. Synthesis of L-strand beings only after 11 kb of H-strand has been synthesized. During replication of the L-strand, the parental single-stranded H-strand is covered and protected by mitochondrial single-stranded DNA binding protein (mtSsbp) [ ]. General dynamics of the alternative RITOLS model (ribonucleotide incorporation throughout the lagging strand) of mitochondrial DNA replication are similar to those of strand displacement replication with a key difference [ , ]. In the RITOLS model, processed RNA molecules rather than mtSsbp cover the displaced single-stranded parental H-strand [ ]. While the physiological significance of RITOLS and underlying circumstances that promote this mode of DNA replication remain largely unknown, findings presented herein partially address these questions. Our findings complement the RITOLS model of DNA replication by demonstrating an inherent thermal instability of mtSsbp which enables binding of the competing meRNAs to the single-stranded mtDNA. This instability of mtSsbp is underpinned by Pro92-mediated destabilisation of the protein which triggers its dissociation from ssDNA at an elevated temperature. Supporting our findings, Cha et al. identified a point mutation in mtSsbp which leads to Arg91 > Gln91 [ ] leading to further destabilisation of the protein due to a diminished capacity for salt bridge formation in this region. This mutation impeded multimer formation, reduced DNA-binding affinity of mtSsbp, and led to significant reduction of mtDNA copy number and mitochondrial ATP synthesis [ ]. Similarity of our disclosed mechanism of mitochondrial replication to RITOLS suggest that both describe a single phenomenon, a quasi-replication that attenuates the transcriptional profile of mitochondria by RNase-H-mediated degradation of ssDNA-bound meRNAs. This quasi-replication phenomenon is similar to asymmetric replication of plasmid DNA that generates a single-stranded DNA with potential to be converted to double-stranded DNA [ ]. In bacteria, the asymmetric replication and generation of ssDNA has an important pro-survival function. During conjugation of Gram-negative bacteria, rolling circle replication occurs in an asymmetric manner and while the leading strand is replicated in the donor organism, the single-stranded lagging strand is transferred and replicated in the recipient cell [ ]. It appears that the quasi-replication of the mitochondrial genome is yet another variation of the asymmetric rolling-circle mechanism adapted to alter mitochondrial activity by regulating mitochondrial transcriptional profile.
Subsequent to the generation of mitochondrial ssDNA, meRNAs hybridize to it leading to emergence of RNA-DNA hybrids that are targeted by mitochondrial RNase-H1. RNase-H is present in all major kingdoms of life with the conserved functionality of eliminating R-loops (RNA/DNA hybrids) that arise during replication of DNA [ ]. In mitochondria, RNase-H1 performs a more specialised function of processing RNA primers to allow site-specific initiation of mtDNA replication [ ]. Here we report that RNase-H performs an additional task of modulating the transcriptional landscape of mitochondria. This function is similar to the activity of synthetic DNA antisense oligonucleotides in reducing the level of both cytoplasmic mRNAs and nuclear-retained RNAs in an RNase H1-dependent manner [ ]. A major consequence of mitochondria-intrinsic RNA degradation by an RNase H1-dependent mechanism is that all the components of the electron transport chain have subunits encoded by the mitochondrial genome except complex II whose components are nuclear encoded. Therefore, selective degradation of meRNAs provides an opportunity for complex II to function in isolation leading to an amplified generation of ROS.
In recent years, mitochondrial complex II has emerged an important producer of ROS [ , – ]. Aside from ROS production via forward and reverse electron flow at high concentration of succinate (> 5mM), complex II can produce significant quantities of ROS directly when the concentration of succinate is 0.5mM or less. While respiration on succinate is a prerequisite for generation of ROS by succinate dehydrogenase, higher concentrations of the metabolite appear to prevent access of oxygen to FAD of the complex [ ]. A source of succinate is β-oxidation of odd chain fatty acids. This provides a partial explanation for our finding that β-oxidation of fatty acids contributes to neuronal differentiation. Corroborating this notion, Yanes et al. reported that a metabolic shift to oxidize fatty acids promotes differentiation dynamics of NPCs [ ]. Maximal ROS generation by mitochondrial complex II at the FAD site also requires inhibition of electron transfer both toward complex I via reverse electron flow and toward complex III via forward electron flow. As noted above, ssDNA-mediated degradation of meRNAs encoding components of the electron transport chain enables complex II to function in isolation leading to amplified ROS production by this complex. Attenuation of meRNAs is complemented by RET-induced oxidative damage to mitochondrial proteins. Triggering of RET by IOC is central for initiation of β-oxidation of free fatty acids by shunting of the metabolic flux from the upper glycolysis pathway to lipogenesis. Sufficient amplification of proton motive force (Δp) by IOC to fulfill the thermodynamic requirement for RET means that the Δp for pumping 4 protons against the proton motive force (4Δp) must exceed the reduction potential difference of the NAD+/NADH and CoQ/CoQH2 redox couples in transporting 2 electrons across the complex I (ΔEh=760mV) [ ].
Due to biophysical constraints, such as increased permeability of mitochondrial membranes at a high voltage [ ], surpassing the thermodynamic threshold for induction of RET by importing protons from the mitochondrial matrix would be challenging. IOC, on the other hand, bypasses these biophysical constraints by importing RNA-associated protons from the nuclear matrix [ , ]. The imported RNA is degraded via exonuclease activity of mitochondrial polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase) which releases H+ along with nucleoside diphosphates (NDPs) [ , ]. The released protons not only trigger RET but also, upon dissipation, amplify the thermal flux. While the role of an amplified thermal flux in neuronal differentiation has been reviewed elsewhere [ ], our findings add a novel dimension to the existing knowledge by demonstrating that mitochondria accommodate evolutionary adaptations that enable sensing the thermal flux aside from the thermogenic capacity. These adaptations include a replication mode that responds to thermal flux and a single-stranded DNA binding protein with a structural adaptation that brings about thermal instability. Considering that thermal flux plays a major role in mitochondrial degeneration, we propose the term “Calamortis” (Cala: from calor, meaning heat, Mortis: death) to describe this mode of mitochondrial degeneration.

## Scope and limitations of the study
An important limitation of our findings in vitro is that we have not validated the results in different cell lines or primary cells. This concern is partially addressed by findings in vivo that corroborate the in vitro results. However, it remains to be investigated whether the reported mitochondrial degeneration occurs upon differentiation of other cell lineages. From a mechanistic perspective, the exact role of ROS in induction of mitochondrial degeneration remains unclear at this stage. One explanation is that ROS-mediated DNA damage triggers the depletion of mtDNA. However, the outcome of RNAseH1 inhibition and introduction of the stable mtSsbp along with experiments in which the biphasic nature of ROS generation was investigated argue against this likelihood to some extent. We noted that inhibition of RNaseH1-mediated degradation of meRNAs and transfection of mitochondria with the stable mtSsbp (P21 > L92 or E coli SSB) was sufficient to prevent the reduction of the level of mtDNA. Arguably, these interventions affect the second phase of ROS production (i.e. the rise of complex II as the generator of ROS). However, significant production of ROS via IOC-mediated RET (phase I) remains unaffected in these experiments. Therefore, while oxidative damage is the root cause of organellar degeneration, it does not appear to be caused by direct oxidative damage to mtDNA. Other possibilities, that remain to be investigated, include mitochondrial lipid peroxidation or oxidative damage to key susceptible mitochondrial proteins such as Voltage-Dependent Anion Channel 1 (Vdac1). From a methodological perspective, while the reduction of the abundance of mRNAs of interest is indicative of the efficiency of RNAi, the absence of protein-level validation of RNAi remains a current limitation of this study. Issues such as the absence of antibodies that recognise the alternatively spliced mitochondrial isoform of RNaseH1 rendered this task challenging.

## Conclusion and future directions
The functional significance of IOC-mediated mitochondrial degeneration is to provide a negative autoregulatory mechanism to decelerate neuronal differentiation. Accordingly, the described phenomenon can be viewed as an anti-differentiation mechanism. Several interesting questions arise from this observation. In our experiments, we noted that a subpopulation of mitochondria undergoing IOC enter the degenerative pathway. A potential explanation for this apparent heterogeneity of response is an inherent resistance to the redox-mediated degeneration. Potentially, this could be the result of heterogeneity of mitochondrial antioxidant response giving rise to a resistant subpopulation. A second plausible explanation is concerned with the mechanism of heat build-up as mitochondria become metabolically active. It can be argued that mitochondria not only generate heat endogenously but also receive heat from other surrounding metabolically active mitochondria; a bystander effect. Hence, it is expected that once mitochondrial degeneration exceeds a threshold, the thermal flux (due to mitochondrial decline) will decrease, sparing the remaining mitochondria. It is also conceivable that a combination of these mechanisms may be involved in setting up an upper limit for mitochondrial decline. At a cellular level, it is of interest whether IOC-mediated mitochondrial degeneration can be calibrated to orchestrate the rate of neuronal differentiation. Also, it remains to be established whether this form of mitochondrial degeneration is specific to neuronal differentiation or represents a general mechanism underlying cellular differentiation. Beyond developmental relevance, it is intriguing to consider whether the observed mechanism represents a vulnerability that is exposed in pathological conditions affecting the nervous system.

## Mice

## Cells
The established mouse multipotent neural stem cells (C17.2) were purchased from CellBank Australia (Children’s Medical Research Institute, Sydney, Australia). ## Immunohistochemistry
Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in Sorensen’s phosphate buffer pH: 7.4, for 4 h at 4 °C. Cultured cells were fixed in the same fixative for 25 min at room temperature. For IHC staining, sections/cells were incubated with 0.1 M PBS, 1% BSA, 0.1% Tween-20, and 5% normal goat serum for 40 min. Sections/cells were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 ˚C and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Specificity controls were carried out by incubating sections with rabbit or mouse IgG negative control antibodies.

## Image analysis
To measure the linear intensity of fluorescence signals along a designated path, the intensity of a signal of interest was recorded along a traced line using the plot profile plugin of ImageJ software.

## Live-cell tau-STED nanoscopy

## Transmission Electron Microscopy
For TEM analysis, tissues were fixed in Karnovsky’s fixative overnight at room temperature followed by post-fixation in OsO4 for 1 h. Preparations were dehydrated in graded alcohols and embedded in low viscosity resin (TAAB Laboratory and Microscopy, United Kingdom). Ultrathin sections were mounted on Pioloform/formvar coated slot grids, stained in uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined in a JEM-2200FS field emission electron microscope.
For ultrastructural analysis of replication intermediates, DNA was extracted from mitochondria isolated from NPCs at t = 0.5 h after induction of differentiation using phenol-chloroform isolation method. TEM copper grids 600 mesh (EMS) were covered with a very thin carbon film (2–4 nm) to facilitate deposition and spreading of replicating mitochondrial DNA. Seven microliters of the isolated DNA (conc. 280ng/µl) were deposited on the carbon-coated grid for 5 min and a 2% (w/vol) solution of aqueous UrAc was used for rinsing and staining of DNA molecules. Blotting was done using a filter paper and the grids were visualised in a JEM-2200FS field emission electron microscope.

## Laser capture microdissection

Tomm20 immuno-stained samples were visualized by using a 20× objective and FITC filters, and LMPC was performed by using the same objective under freeze mode (UV energy: 68–72, UV focus: 76–78, cutting speed: 25).

## Gene expression analysis
RNA was isolated using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). After DNase treatment, reverse transcription of the extracted RNA was carried out using a mixture of 1 µL of oligo-dT/random hexamer, 4 µL of total RNA, 1 µL of dNTP Mix (10 mM each), 4 µL of 5x First-Strand synthesis Buffer, 1 µL of 0.1 M DTT, 1 µL of RNaseOUT (40 units/µL), 1 µL of SuperScript-IV reverse transcriptase (200 units). To design primers, gene sequence data and exon/intron boundaries were obtained from GenBank database. ## Isolation and functional analysis of mitochondria
Mitochondria were isolated based on the method developed by Frezza et al. [ ]. Briefly, NPCs (107 cells) were harvested, washed and resuspended in 3 ml of ice-cold IB buffer (10mM Tris–MOPS, 1mM EGTA/Tris, 0.2 M sucrose, pH to 7.4). The cells were then homogenized using a Teflon pestle. Homogenized cells were centrifuged twice at 600 g for 5 min at 4 °C to isolate nuclei. Mitochondria were isolated by additional centrifugation of the supernatant from the previous step at 7,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Mitochondria were then resuspended in EB buffer (10mM KCl, 10mM Tris/MOPS, 1mM EGTA/Tris, and 1mM KH2PO4, pH to 7.4) and checked using Mitotracker Red under a fluorescence microscope to ensure that mitochondria were viable before further experimentation.

## Quantification of ROS
The cell permeable probe, 2′-7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), was used to monitor generalised oxidative stress (i.e. ROS production). To measure ROS production, NPCs were harvested at defined time points, resuspended in PBS and incubated for 10 min with 2µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C. The cells were then washed and resuspended in GM or NIM at a final density of 106 cells/well and a total volume of 100 µl in a 96-well plate. The plates were transferred into a real-time PCR machine and accumulation of DCF was recorded at 10-second intervals by recording fluorescence at 530 nm after excitation of the sample at 485 nm.

## Induction of mitochondrial depletion

## Measurement of thermal flux
The L-DNA molecular beacon (MB) was designed as described elsewhere. Molecular beacons were labelled with a 6-FAM reporter dye at the 5′ end, and an Iowa Black FQ quencher at the 3′ end (Integrated DNA Technologies).
The sequence of MB is as follows:
FAM-GCGAGTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTCTCGC-IBFQ
To measure the temperature, MB was mixed with thermogenic units (cells/mitochondria) to a final concentration 1µM in a total volume of 50 µl. The mixture was then transferred to a real-time PCR machine (Stratagene® Mx3000P) to record the intensity of FAM signal that is detected upon melting of the molecular beacon and separation of the fluorochrome from the quencher. During an initial equilibration step at 25 °C for 30 s, the signal was recorded at a rate of 1/sec. In a second incubation step at 37 °C, thermal flux of mitochondria/cells was recoded for 30 min at a rate of 1/sec. The fluorescence signal intensity was then converted to temperature (°C) using a calibration curve generated from inactive thermogenic units (i.e. cells in PBS without access to glucose or mitochondria in EB buffer without access to pyruvate). This method ensures accurate conversion of fluorescence signal intensity to temperature (°C) as the presence of inactive thermogenic units alters the emission characteristics of the FAM signal. To generate a calibration curve, FAM signals were collected after an initial equilibration step at 25 °C for 30 s by increasing the temperature in steps of 5 °C (each step 20 s). Mean values of 20 recordings for every thermal step were used generate a calibration curve used for conversion of fluorescence signal intensity to temperature (°C).

## Metabolomic analysis
For cell metabolite extraction (refer to Rabinowitz’s method), a buffer consisting of acetonitrile: methanol: water in the ratio between 4:4:2 (20% water) and 0.1mM formic acid was used. The extraction buffer was then pre-chilled in a -30 °C freezer. To extract the metabolites, 5 ml of the pre-chilled extraction buffer was added to the T7 flasks containing NPCs (~ 2 × 107 cells/time point), pre-washed gently with 10 ml ice-cold PBS. Flask surface was scraped and then incubated on ice for 3 min. 435uL of neutralizing buffer (15%w/v NH4HCO3) was added and the cell slurry transferred into 15 ml falcon tubes and stored at -80 C. Just before LCMS metabolite analysis, the cell slurry was thawed and vortexed before taking e 200uL into a fresh microfuge tube and centrifuged at 15,000 g 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred into HPLC vials and assayed promptly. LCMS was carried out using a Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z column (2.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm, Agilent) at 30 °C on a Vanquish-TSQAltis LC-MS/MS system. The pair of mobile phase buffers used were 95:5 (v/v) water: acetonitrile containing 20 mM ammonium hydroxide and ammonium acetate (Buffer A) and 100% acetonitrile (Buffer B) flowed at 200 µL/min. MS acquisition was performed in positive and negative SRM mode to measure energy cofactors, amino acids, glycolytic and TCA cycle metabolites. Authentic standards were used to calibrate metabolite MRMs and determine analytes’ retention times. The injection volume was 5 µL. HESI source settings were 3500 V (positive) and 2500 V (negative) and vaporizer temperature 275 °C. Scheduled acquisition was carried out across the 30-minute LC program. Instrument data was extracted using ProteoWizard MSConvert (3.0.21128-7376ae988) and in-house MATLAB (2024a) scripts.

## Lipidomic analysis
A 1-butanol/methanol (BUME, 1-butanol: methanol = 1:1 v/v containing 5 mM ammonium formate) single phase lipid extraction method was used for lipid extraction. Briefly, washed cell pellets contained in 15 mL tubes were resuspended in 500 µL 0.1 mM Tris-HCl with 100 µM BHT (pH 7.9) before being transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Cells were then sonicated to generate cell lysates (Branson, Amp 30% for 30 s). All the above steps were done on crushed ice. 10 µL of cell lysate was transferred into a fresh tube and first spiked with 10 µL of diluted Avanti Splash Lipidomix (10% dilution), serving as lipid class specific internal standards (ISTD). Then, 100 µL BUME solvent was added and mixed thoroughly in a water bath sonicator for 60 min and finally centrifuged 16,000 g for 10 min (room temperature). Supernatants were then carefully transferred to inserted LC glass vials for subsequent LC/MS analysis.
A targeted LC/MS lipidomics method was applied, capable of detecting > 600 lipid species encompassing 36 lipid classes/subclasses, using a TSQ Altis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with a H-ESI source and coupled with a Vanquish Horizon liquid chromatography (LC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Briefly, autosampler injection volume was 5 µL and temperature was set 10 °C. ## Proteomic analysis
The samples were solubilized using 150 µl of 1% SDC in 100 mM TEAB, heated to 95 °C for 5 min, cooled then incubated with 1U of Benzonase for 15 min at 37 °C. An aliquot (25 µl) of the suspension was transferred to two PBI Barocycler tubes and lysed, reduced and alkylated in the PBI Barocycler for 60 min at 56 °C. The mixture was diluted with 120 µl of Rapid digestion buffer and 1.5 µg of trypsin added. Enzymatic digestion was in the PBI Barocycler for 30 min at 56 °C. The reaction was terminated and SDC precipitated by adding 5 µl of 100% formic acid, vortexed and centrifuged for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, diluted with 760 µl of 0.5% formic acid and loaded onto a Phenomenex C18 SPE cartridge. Peptides were eluted and dried to completeness in the RVC. The peptides were resuspended in 30 µl of 0.5% formic acid, centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to clean low-bind tubes. Peptide quantitation was performed using the nanodrop N60 using setting OD = 1.0. The samples were acquired using the TripleTOF5600 + mass spectrometer. A total of 2 µg of total peptide/protein was used for LC-MS/MS analysis on the TripleTOF 5600 + mass spectrometer. The tryptic peptides were first loaded onto the TRAP column and then separated using a Waters Acuity UPLC M-class CSH C18 1.7 μm particle size analytical column using a 95 min LC-MS/MS method. Data was acquired using Information Dependent Acquisition (IDA) LC-MS/MS method. The raw LC-MS/MS data was processed using SCIEX Protein Pilot 5.0 and MS-GF+ software. The processed data was searched against the SWISS-PROT database.

## Analysis of meRNA melting profile
After isolating mitochondria from NPCs, meRNAs were extracted using Qiagen RNeasy kit. ## Molecular dynamics
The open-source GROMACS tool was used to simulate the temperature-dependent dynamics of mouse mitochondrial Ssbp1 (AlphaFold ID: AF-Q9CYR0-F1-v6). Simulations were performed using the water model of SPC/E and OPLS/AA force-field. Equilibrating simulations under an NVT (isothermal-isochoric) ensemble, followed by an NPT (isothermal-isobaric) ensemble were performed at 298 K until the root mean-square deviation (RMSD) was stable. The root mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values were calculated with Gromacs 4.5 through the Galaxy platform. Mutation of Pro-92 to leu-92 in silico was performed using PyMOL. Both RMSD and RMSF were calculated with Gromacs package.

## Thermal hybridization chain reaction
Thermal hybridisation chain reaction (t-HCR) is based on iterative hybridization of two hairpin DNAs, a synthetic analogue of OriL (A5159-T5195) and another half-complementary hairpin with a similar Tm to OriL.
OriL: 5′- TGAAGATGGCCCCTTTTTTTTTTTACCGCCATCTTCA − 3′.
Complementary OriL: 5′-AAAAGGGGCCATCATCATGAAGATGGCGGTAAAAAAA-3′.
As the temperature approaches the Tm of OriL, denatured hairpins could hybridise in a chain reaction whereby 3′-complementary OriL (highlighted orange) hybridises to 3′-OriL (highlighted red). This generates a 3′overhang in double-stranded DNA corresponding to 5′-complementary OriL (highlighted green) to which another 5′-OriL (highlighted blue) can hybridise exposing 3′-OriL as a single-stranded DNA. This event completes one hybridization cycle, only to start a new hybridization cycle for as long as the condition of Thybridisation=Tm is met. Therefore, the length of the double-stranded product corresponds to the duration of time in which Thybridisation=Tm. When the temperature exceeds the annealing temperature of OriL and complementary OriL (Thybridisation> Tm), the hybridization reaction will cease despite existence of the two stem loops in a denatured stage.

## Synthesis of mitochondrial single-stranded mini-chromosome
To synthesize a single-stranded mitochondrial H-strand, a methodology was required to enable circularization of a single-stranded DNA oligomer in a splint-independent manner. A single-stranded DNA oligomere corresponding to the H-strand of mDNA was synthesized corresponding to a region of mDNA containing the OriL and extending to the proximal upstream region (-40 bp: C5119-G5158) and the downstream region (+ 45 bp: A5196-T5242) of the OriL:
TTTTTCGGCGGTAGAAGTAGATTGAAGCCAGTAATAGGGTATTTACCTGTCGTAGCAAATTAATTCTTGTAGAGAAATCTCTACTAAGACTTCTACCGCCATTTTTT
Annealing of the complementary stem region of the oligomer facilitates approximation of the 5′ and 3′ terminals prior to ligation. To circularise the synthetic H-strand, 2µL of the oligomer (0.5 pmol/µL), 2µL CircLigase II 10X Reaction Buffer, 1µL 50 mM MnCl2, 1µL CircLigase II ssDNA Ligase (100 U), and 14µL nuclease-free water were mixed and incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. The enzyme was then inactivated by incubation at 80 °C for 10 min. The efficiency of circularization was checked by running a DNA gel in which near-complete conversion to a circular DNA was detected. The circularised D-strand was then purified using a phenol-chloroform isolation method.

## Expression and purification of recombinant mitochondrial proteins
Synthesis of recombinant proteins was achieved using a methodology described by Korhonen et al. [ ]. Synthetic DNA fragments encoding mouse mitochondrial Ssbp1, Polγ (DNA polymerase subunit γ-1), and Polmrt containing a His6 tag at the N terminus were cloned into the pBacPAK9 vector (Clontech). The plasmid constructs were used to prepare Autographa californica nuclear polyhedrosis virus recombinant for the proteins of interest. Expression of the recombinant proteins was achieved in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells maintained in suspension in SFM 900 medium (Gibco-BRL) and containing 5% fetal calf serum at 27 °C. To purify the proteins, cells were transferred to a lysis buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 20 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma) and lysed using a Dounce homogeniser type B. The recombinant proteins were purified using a Ni2+-NTA matrix using the methodology described by Falkenberg et al. [ ]. The purity of isolated proteins was checked by SDS-PAGE under reducing condition (purity > 90%) and the identity of recombinant proteins was confirmed by LC/MS.

## In vitro mitochondrial mini-replisome
The circularised single-stranded mitochondrial H-strand (OriLWt, or the mutated versions (ΔTmlow OL, or ΔTmhigh OL); final concentration 5 nM) was added to a reaction mixture containing 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.6), 5 mM magnesium chloride, 1 mM DTT, 100 µg/ml BSA, 4 mM ATP, 10% glycerol, 250 µM dATP, 250 µM dTTP, 250 µM dGTP, 250 µM dCTP, 100 µM UTP, 100 µM GTP, 100 µM CTP, and 2U of SUPERase·In RNase Inhibitor.
