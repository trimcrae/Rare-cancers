## Summary
Telomere length maintenance, critical for cancer progression, can be driven by TERT promoter mutations (TERTpMUT), which are markers of poor prognosis across multiple cancers. Their tumor-specificity also makes them attractive chemotherapeutic targets. Here, we identified previously uncharacterized pathways regulating TERTpMUT activity by inserting a luciferase reporter downstream of TERTpMUT in SW1736 anaplastic thyroid cancer cells via CRISPR-Cas9, generating SW1736TERT/LUC, and screening a 218-kinase inhibitor library. Beyond MAPK regulation, we found co-regulatory roles for PI3K/AKT/mTOR1 signaling and the cell cycle via Aurora kinase B (AURKB). Further analyses using quantitative PCR, immunoprecipitation (IP), and chromatin IP in thyroid cell models revealed that although both TERTpMUT and wild-type promoters are governed by these pathways, distinct factors mediate each mechanism. Specifically, AURKB, via REST, is recruited to TERTpMUT by TRIM28. These findings highlight TRIM28 as a promising therapeutic target for TERTpMUT-driven thyroid cancers.

## Introduction
The maintenance of telomere length is a major mechanism for cancer development. , Telomeres shorten with each cell division, which in due course leads predictably to cellular senescence and growth arrest. If cells continue to replicate through this phase then cellular crisis and cell death will occur unless telomere length can be maintained. Two such mechanisms exist: reactivation of telomerase enzymatic subunit encoded by TERT or alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT). In the case of TERT, reactivation in cancer occurs by various methods, the most common of which is somatic activating mutation in the TERT promoter (TERTpMUT). TERTpMUT comprises two mutually exclusive hotspot mutations, both being C>T transitions, located −124 and −146 bp upstream of the TERT start codon (chr5:1,295,228 and chr5:1,295,250, or C228T and C250T, respectively; GRCh37/hg19 genomic coordinates). TERTpMUT constitutes the most significant hotspot in 237/2,633 (9%) tumors in Pan-Cancer Genome Analysis of Whole Genomes and occurs at high incidence in bladder cancer (70%), liver cancer (45%), melanoma (72%), low grade glioma (45%), and glioblastoma (89%). 
In thyroid cancer, TERTpMUT was initially noted to occur relatively infrequently in better prognosis tumors (papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) and follicular thyroid cancer (FTC)) and with increasing frequency in progressively worse prognostic subtypes (i.e., poorly differentiated and anaplastic thyroid cancers (ATC)) , , ; even within differentiated PTCs and FTCs, TERTpMUT was correlated to adverse prognostic features such as older age, larger tumor size, and male gender. , , Subsequent work has confirmed not only is TERTpMUT associated with cancer-specific mortality, but wild-type TERTp is also associated with excellent survival. , , Moreover, the co-occurrence of MAPK pathway activating BRAF p.V600E (BRAFV600E) and TERTpMUT promotes aggressive thyroid tumorigenesis. , 
The TERTpMUT creates de novo consensus binding motifs for ETS transcription factors. , As many ETS members act as transcriptional effectors of the MAPK pathway, there is significant interest in their role in activating mutant TERTp. Indeed, for the ETS factor GA-binding protein (GABPA), strong evidence supports this mechanism. In several cancers, most notably in glioblastoma models, the c.-124C>T or c.-146C>T mutation, together with native ETS-binding sites, generate bipartite sequences that permit the selective recruitment of a tetrameric GABP complex, comprising two GABPA (GABP-alpha) subunits paired with two non-DNA binding GABPB (GABP-beta) subunits, which in turn facilitates long-range enhancer–promoter interactions. , However, while GABP activity has been observed at the TERTpMUT in certain well-differentiated thyroid cancer models, , , , , it does not seem to play as dominant a role in many thyroid-derived tumors as in glioblastoma. Emerging evidence suggests a nuanced interplay between GABPA and other ETS factors in thyroid-derived tumors, , , a complexity that is being recognized in glioblastoma too. 
Irrespective of the initial mechanism behind TERTpMUT activation, the mutant allele is generally characterized by reduced 5-methyl-cytosine (5 mC) levels in comparison to the wild-type allele, across a CpG island that spans both the proximal promoter region and the first exon of TERT. This impedes Polycomb-repressive complex 2 (PRC2) binding, which favors methylated CpG islands, and which contains the methyltransferase Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2), responsible for deposition of the repressive chromatin mark, tri-methylation of K27 of histone H3 (H3K27Me3). , , Concurrently, there is an increase in active marks H3K4Me2/3, and recruitment of RNA Polymerase II, culminating in monoallelic expression of TERT from the mutant allele. , , 
If TERTpMUT activation is distinct from wild-type TERT regulation in stem and immune cells, it could pave the way for therapeutics that selectively inhibit TERTpMUT in cancer cells, sparing non-malignant cells from cytotoxicity. Growing data pointing to GABP not being a tractable therapeutic target for thyroid cancer prompted us to explore alternative druggable pathways and targets that can control TERTpMUT. In this study, we engineered a high-throughput platform to systematically investigate endogenous TERT transcription and identify kinases and signaling pathways that can modulate mutant promoter-driven TERT expression. To achieve this, we utilized a human kinase inhibitor library for screening, given that kinases are druggable targets and that kinase pathways are frequently involved in transcriptional regulation.

## Results

## Generation of TERT reporter cell line
To identify regulators of the TERT promoter, we performed CRISPR-Cas9-nickase (Cas9n)–guided precision editing of the SW1736 ATC cell line, which harbors the c.-124C>T TERT promoter and BRAFV600E mutations. This editing generated seven isogenic reporter subclones carrying the firefly luciferase gene immediately downstream of the endogenous TERT transcriptional start site ( A provides a schematic diagram of the knock-in strategy). We confirmed that these subclones express robust levels of luciferase, and that this expression can be readily repressed following treatment with known thyroid cancer drugs that inhibit the MAPK pathway ( ). Clone seven was selected for further study based on having the highest luciferase expression and is henceforth referred to as SW1736TERT/LUC in our study. Further characterization by karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed that, like unmodified SW1736 cells, SW1736TERT/LUC exhibited a near-tetraploid genome with significant instability, even within clonal cells of the same passage. Predominantly, the cells carried 3–4 copies of chromosome 5, along with a dicentric chromosome harboring two additional copies of TERT ( ). Copy-number droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) and gene expression RT-qPCR analysis over 30 passages post-puromycin selection confirmed that, an average, two of the five TERT copies in SW1736TERT/LUC cells were stably edited ( B). Importantly, the remaining unedited copies were sufficient to sustain endogenous TERT expression at levels comparable to those in the parental SW1736 cells ( B). Next, using Oxford nanopore sequencing of high-molecular-weight gDNA from SW1736TERT/LUC, we mapped the LUC transgene integration to the intended site at chr5:1,295,015 (hg38) ( C). The 3.5 kb reporter cassette was verified to be free of unintended genomic rearrangements, with all known regulatory elements within the upstream TERTp intact. This was further confirmed by PCR amplification followed by Sanger sequencing, with an example sequence of the TERT 5′UTR from SW1736TERT/LUC shown in D.
 

## Inhibitor library screen
To identify possible pathways other than MAPK responsible for TERTp activation, we screened a library of 218 well-defined kinase-inhibitors using SW1736TERT/LUC, across a broad spectrum of intracellular signaling pathways. The dose-response data were collected at ten concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 10 μM, representing the dynamic range of the assay ( ). We constructed a heatmap and applied unsupervised hierarchical clustering methods to discern inhibitors with similar dose-response patterns ( E). The inhibitors of cluster-1 and -2 exhibited inhibitory action on the TERTpMUT reporter at sub-micromolar concentrations ( E, cluster-1 and -2 highlighted with red and blue, respectively). As anticipated, given MAPK signaling consequent to BRAFV600E stimulates TERTpMUT, the clusters featured several MAPK kinase (MEK) inhibitors: AZD8330 with an IC50 of 10.0 nM, PD0325901 at 3.4 nM, PD318088 at 4.6 nM, and PD184352 at 25.9 nM; and a BRAFV600E-specifc inhibitor RAF265 with an IC50 of 9.3 nM. These clusters also included inhibitors targeting the Phosphoinositide 3-kinase/AKT/mammalian Target of Rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) pathway: CUDC-907 targeting PI3Kα with an IC50 of 3.7 nM; GSK2126458, a pan-PI3K and mTOR Complex 1 and 2 (mTORC1/2) inhibitor at 4.6 nM; PF-04691502, a pan-PI3K and mTOR inhibitor at 3.7 nM; and AZD8055 targeting mTORC1/2 at 1.9 nM. We also identified cell-cycle regulators, such as Hesperadin targeting Aurora Kinase B (AURKB) with an IC50 of 183 nM; Flavopiridol, a pan-cyclin dependent kinase (pan-CDK) inhibitor at 101 nM; SNS-032, a pan-CDK inhibitor at 249 nM; AZD7762 targeting Checkpoint Kinase 1(CHK1) at 523 nM; and CHIR-124 also targeting CHK1 at 356 nM.
We verified the pathways identified in our screen with additional dose-response experiments using SW1736TERT/LUC cells. AZD8055 (mTORC1/2) was selected as the most potent mTOR inhibitor, and its efficacy was compared to the MEK1/2 inhibitor AZD8330, as well as FDA-approved Lenvatinib (multikinase inhibitor), Dabrafenib (RAF inhibitor), and Everolimus (mTORC1). Cells were treated with a range of inhibitor doses for 24 h, followed by luciferase activity measurement. The mTOR inhibitors showed comparable efficacy to RAF and MEK inhibitors, indicating that PI3K/AKT/mTOR is equally important in TERT regulation in this model. Notably, Everolimus, which selectively targets mTORC1 within the 24 h treatment period, showed TERTp inhibition equivalent to the dual mTOR inhibitor, indicating regulation of TERT is mediated through the mTORC1 complex ( F).

## TERT expression is positively correlated with PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling activity
Having identified that TERT is regulated by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, we sought to probe this relationship further by quantifying TERT mRNA (via RT-qPCR) and phospho-AKT (S473) levels (via semi-quantitative Western blotting) in four TERTp–mutated cell lines —TPC1 (PTC), and SW1736, 8505C, and C643 (all ATC)— as well as the non-malignant NThy-ori-3.1 cell line. We detected a significant positive correlation between TERT expression and phospho-AKT (S473) (r2 = 0.9265, p = 5.6 × 10−3) ( A). TERT levels were highest in C643 (HRAS p.G13R, MTOR p.H419R, and PTEN p.F341L), intermediate in TPC1 (CCDC6-RET fusion, PIK3R3 p.N317S, and STK11 p.F354L), and 8505C (STK11 p.S216F and PIK3R3 p.N317S), and lowest in SW1736, which lacks reported PI3K/AKT/mTOR mutations (cell-line genotypes taken from published dataset ).
 As noted, C643, TPC1, and 8505C each carry at least one potential PI3K/AKT/mTOR variant. However, beyond recognized driver events, many of these mutations remain unvalidated and may represent passenger events. Intrinsic differences in signaling properties across these cell lines may also contribute to their distinct phenotypes.
Next, we compared TERT expression with phospho-ERK1/2 (T202/Y204) activity ( B). A positive trend was observed among lines bearing BRAFV600E or RET mutations, but the HRAS p.G13R–mutated C643 cells—despite exhibiting the highest TERT levels—showed minimal MAPK pathway activity. Next, an analysis of whole-genome sequencing and TERT RT-qPCR data from a small cohort of poorly differentiated thyroid cancers further suggested that PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation can promote TERT expression: TERT-high tumors lacking detectable MAPK drivers commonly harbored activating mutations in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway ( C). We also examined publicly available transcriptomic data to examine whether TERT expression correlated with the BRAFV600E-RAS score (BRS) of PTCs ( A) and advanced thyroid cancers ( B). Across tumor types there was a weak negative correlation between the two variables, with a higher level BRS (more RAS-like) associated with lower levels of TERT. However, this correlation did not reach statistical significance. These findings collectively indicate a connection between TERT expression and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, further supported by our mechanistic data below. However clearly the regulation of TERT by kinase signaling in thyroid cancer is nuanced.
Considering Everolimus’ existing clinical use, we next investigated whether it selectively inhibited TERT expression in TERTpMUT versus wild-type thyroid cancer cell lines. For wild-type controls, we used CAL62, a KRAS p.G12R -mutant ATC cell-line, and MDAT-T41, a BRAFV600E-mutant PTC cell-line. Treatment of all cell-lines with 2 nM Everolimus resulted in a progressive decrease in TERT expression from 24 to 72 h (RT-qPCR, D) and a subsequent reduction in telomerase activity from 72 to 120 h (qTRAP, E) (all t-tests p < 0.05). In TERTpMUT cell lines, vehicle-treated (0.02% v/v DMSO) cells exhibited a less pronounced decline in TERT expression and telomerase activity compared to the significant reductions observed post-Everolimus treatment (all t-tests p < 0.05, D).

## AURKB and TERT expression are correlated across thyroid cancer subtypes
AURKB is a multifaceted kinase which was also identified by the screen. While its canonical function is mitotic regulation, work indicating its role in regulating gene promoters motivated us to investigate its relationship with TERT more closely. To validate the findings of the library screen, we conducted dose-response experiments (0.1–10 μM) on SW1736TERT/LUC using Hesperadin and two AURKA/B inhibitors, AT9283 and ENMD-2076. All three inhibitors decreased TERTp activity in a dose-dependent manner, yielding IC50 values of 180, 1600, and 670 nM, respectively ( ). To ascertain specificity of the effect to AURKB, we employed three distinct short hairpin RNAs (shRNA) plasmids (1–3) to establish TERTpMUT and wild-type thyroid cancer cells with stable AURKB knockdown. After four days of puromycin selection, we harvested both protein and total RNA to evaluate AURKB and TERT levels using western blotting and RT-qPCR. Across all lines tested, compared to scrambled shRNA and untransfected control cells, all three shRNA achieved significantly reduced AURKB protein (representative western blots shown in ). Importantly, all cells with reduced AURKB also displayed a notable decrease in TERT expression relative to controls (t-test p < 0.05) ( A).
 The correlation between native AURKB protein expression and TERT expression was assessed in our thyroid cancer cell lines using semi-quantitative western blotting for AURKB and RT-qPCR for TERT. A strong positive correlation was observed between AURKB and TERT expression levels (linear regression, r2 = 0.756, p = 0.0353) ( B). Analysis of publicly available transcriptomic data also showed similar positive correlations in PTCs ( C) and advanced thyroid cancers ( D). Additionally, correlations were observed across tumor types between TERT expression and genes associated with Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to mitosis ( ; Full GO results in and ).

## AURKB binds the TERT promoter and epigenetically regulates its transcription
Building on prior evidence that AURKB phosphorylates histone 3 at Polycomb regulated gene promoters, , we investigated its possible interaction with TERTp: to ascertain whether AURKB binds to TERTp, to evaluate if this DNA-binding activity is allele-specific, and to understand the ramifications of AURKB inhibition by Hesperadin on the epigenetic modulation of the promoter by Polycomb proteins. We performed ChIP-ddPCR assays on formaldehyde-crosslinked chromatin from TPC1 and C643 cells, both heterozygous for c.-124C>T. Our prior research showed both cell-lines exhibit monoallelic expression from the H3K4Me3-marked c.-124C>T strand. By utilizing an allele-specific probe assay spanning the TERTpMUT site we discerned a predominant interaction of the kinase with the mutant allele at a 3:1 ratio in both cell-lines. This observation was corroborated by PCR-cloning of AURKB-precipitated ChIP DNA followed by Sanger sequencing. Moreover, in both TPC1 and C643, although a 24 h exposure to 180 nM Hesperadin did not perturb AURKB’s binding dynamics, it did lead to a discernible drop in its target, H3S10ph. Concurrently, we observed an increase in the binding of PRC2 subunit EZH2, along with reciprocal shifts in H3K4Me3 and H3K27Me3 levels, indicating a downregulation in transcriptional activity (all t-tests p < 0.05) ( E). Similar changes were also observed at the mutant promoter in homozygous SW1736 cells ( E).
However, this pattern was also observed in wild-type CAL62 and MDAT-T41 cells (all t-tests p < 0.05) ( F), indicating that Hesperadin inhibition was not unique to TERTpMUT. This suggested that AURKB also regulates the wild-type promoter in these cells.
We next investigated whether Hesperadin-mediated inhibition of TERTp resulted in a corresponding downregulation of TERT expression. As shown in A, 24 h treatment with Hesperadin led to a dose-dependent inhibition of TERT expression across several cell lines, including TPC1, the TERTpMUT cell lines SW1736TERT/LUC and parental SW1736, as well as C643, 8505C, TPC1, and the wild-type cell lines CAL62 and MDAT-T41. The inhibition pattern was consistent across all cell lines, demonstrating a similar dose-responsive downregulation of TERT expression.
 Next, treating each line with the IC50 dose of Hesperadin over a 24–72 h time-course, TERT expression notably decreased across all cell lines ( B). Similarly, qTRAP assays revealed Hesperadin profoundly inhibited telomerase activity (all t-test p < 0.05 compared with 0 h vehicle control) ( C). Although vehicle-treated controls (≤0.2% v/v DMSO for TPC1, SW1736 and C643, respectively) of TERTpMUT cells exhibited a gradual decline in TERT expression and telomerase activity over time, this reduction was markedly less pronounced than the inhibition induced by Hesperadin.
To determine whether TERT downregulation was directly due to AURKB inhibition at the promoter or indirectly from mitotic dysregulation, SW1736 and CAL62 cultures were synchronized for G2/M arrest using the microtubule poison Nocodazole. After synchronization, cells were treated with Hesperadin or vehicle for up to 6 h, followed by RT-qPCR and cell cycle analysis at 0 h, 3 h, and 6 h (experimental design depicted in D). Cell cycle analysis showed that 24 h Nocodazole treatment synchronized over 90% of cells into mitosis, and this synchronization was maintained for 6 h after Hesperadin addition ( E). In both SW1736 and CAL62, TERT levels significantly decreased by 3 h and remained low at 6 h following Hesperadin treatment, compared to the 0 h baseline (both p < 0.05) ( F).

## Inhibition of mTORC1 leads to the dissociation of TRIM28-TRIM24 and AURKB from the TERT promoter
Agarwal et al.'s recent work identified that in TERT promoter mutated bladder cancer, the Tripartite Motif Containing-28 (TRIM28) and −24 (TRIM24) complex is recruited by GABPA to the mutant allele. Furthermore, they found that TRIM28 phosphorylation by mTORC1, inhibiting binding of TRIM24, is crucial for transactivating TERT. Our Western blot analyses of total lysates ( A) and co-immunoprecipitated endogenous proteins ( B) across various cell lines showed both TRIM28 and TRIM24 are highly expressed and interact, indicating a similar regulatory mechanism in thyroid cancer. However, under the same experimental conditions, no interaction was detected between TRIM28 and GABPA, or with other ETS factors known to bind the TERTp, such as ELK1 and ETV5. No significant endogenous interaction with AURKB was observed (data not shown). We reasoned this might be due to the assay’s insensitivity to low expression levels, such as GABPA which is expressed at very low levels in SW1736 cells. Additionally, the interactions may have been weak, possibly due to being transient or mediated indirectly via other proteins. To test this, we overexpressed flag-tagged versions of these proteins in SW1736 cells and immunoprecipitated TRIM28, revealing a clear but likely indirect interaction with AURKB. However, any interactions with GABPA, ELK1, and ETV5 remained undetectable in the overexpression system ( C). Considering recent reports that REST (RE-1 silencing transcription factor) interacts with both AURKB and TRIM28, we hypothesized that REST could act as an intermediary at the TERT promoter. Western blotting confirmed REST expression across the cell lines, and co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous REST in SW1736 cells revealed clear interactions with both AURKB and TRIM28.
 ChIP-ddPCR analysis of heterozygous TPC1 and C643 lines revealed baseline co-occupancy of TRIM28, REST, and AURKB at the TERT promoter, with allele-specific assays showing a 3:1 enrichment of each factor at the mutant allele ( E). Similar occupancy levels were observed in homozygous SW1736 cells ( E). Treatment with Everolimus for 6 h led to the loss of TRIM28. TRIM24, AURKB, and its histone phosphotarget H3S10ph, while increasing EZH2 binding (all t-tests p < 0.05), mirroring the effects of Hesperadin treatment. In contrast, REST occupancy at the TERT promoter remained unchanged.
In wild-type CAL62 and MDA-4T1 cells, no detectable TRIM28 or TRIM24 occupancy was observed, confirming their interaction with the mutant allele. However, REST, AURKB, and H3S10ph were present, and their levels were unaffected by 6 h Everolimus treatment ( F).

## Discussion
In this study, we utilized a CRISPR-engineered SW1736 ATC-derived subclone, named SW1736TERT/LUC, where the native −124C>T-mutated TERTp controls the expression of a firefly luciferase reporter gene, to assess a kinase inhibitor library and discover previously unknown kinase regulators of TERTpMUT activation in thyroid cancer. Our approach was influenced by prior in vitro studies on TERTp activity. , , , Most of these studies utilized transient gene reporter assays featuring reporters that captured only a truncated portion of the TERT proximal promoter. As a result, these assays do not account for the influence of distal regulatory elements and fail to mimic chromatin structure and epigenetic modifications inherent to the endogenous gene promoter.
Our screen’s identification of MEK and RAF inhibitors, coupled with the observed correlation between TERT expression and MAPK activity in both cell lines and PTC tissues, is consistent with established evidence linking BRAFV600E with TERTpMUT activation in thyroid cancer cells harboring these mutations. , Given that many ETS factors are activated either transcriptionally or through phosphorylation by ERK, the prevailing hypothesis has been that these mutations prime the TERTp for canonical BRAF-MEK-ERK MAPK signaling. This activation is thought to be facilitated mainly through the binding of ETS factors. Interestingly, while the ETS-factor GABPA activates TERTpMUT in specific well-differentiated thyroid cancer models, , , , it may be less prominent in thyroid-derived tumors than in glioblastoma. Recent studies indicate a more complex relationship in TERTpMUT activation in thyroid cancers, suggesting some redundancy between GABPA, other ETS factors, and potentially other transcriptional regulators. , , In this regard, our finding that PI3K and mTOR inhibitors exhibited comparable efficacy to MEK inhibitors represents the first experimental data implicating these kinases in TERTpMUT activation in thyroid cancer. Interestingly, two recent studies using BrafV600E-driven mouse models of thyroid cancer found that upregulated Tert also enhanced mTORC1 signaling, a function independent of its canonical role in telomere maintenance. , In light of these findings, it is plausible that TERT may participate in a positive feedback loop, where its upregulation enhances mTORC1 signaling, which in turn promotes further TERT expression. This potential autoregulatory mechanism could amplify TERTpMUT-driven transcription, contributing to sustained TERT activation in thyroid cancer. Further investigation is warranted to explore this hypothesis and its implications for tumor progression.
Earlier research by Faustino et al., found the AKT/mTOR pathway was notably overactivated in classical PTC, especially in those with the BRAFV600E mutation. This overactivation was further supported by their in vitro findings, suggesting the potential underlying mechanism was BRAF-induced phosphorylation (S428) and inactivation of Serine/threonine-protein kinase 11 (STK11)/liver kinase B1 (LKB1, the key upstream activator of AMPK), thereby relieving AMPK’s inhibition of mTORC1. It is also worth mentioning mutations that inactivate STK11 have been identified in 1% of PDTC and 6% of ATC. A paradigm is emerging that accumulation of additional oncogenic mutations, especially in key effectors of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, correlates with the progression to more advanced disease. , Given the strong link between TERT expression levels and adverse clinicopathological features, it is tempting to speculate an augmentation in mTORC1 signaling, and consequently TERT expression, might be a driving force behind disease progression. , Our expression findings, indicating a correlation between TERT expression and PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation, further underpin this model. These preliminary results are based on a limited set of thyroid cancer lines and patient samples, and they will need to be verified in more extensive studies.
Building on Agarwal et al.’s discovery that the TRIM28-TRIM24 complex regulates the TERTpMUT in bladder cancer, our research supports the involvement of the TRIM28-TRIM24 mechanism in thyroid cancer cells. We further demonstrate that TRIM28 indirectly recruits AURKB via REST at the TERTpMUT, facilitating phosphorylation of H3S10, which correlates with the detachment of PRC2 and the establishment of a transcriptionally repressive epigenetic state. In contrast to Agarwal et al.'s findings, which showed that mTORC1 inhibition disrupted TRIM24 binding to TRIM28, we observed that both TRIM28 and TRIM24 dissociated from the promoter following mTORC1 inhibition. This discrepancy may be due to the timing and dynamics of the mechanism, where the initial loss of TRIM28 triggers a cascade of events that ultimately leads to reduced occupancy of both TRIM28 and TRIM24 at the promoter.
Our findings also suggest that GABPA —consistent with its proposed minor role in thyroid cancer— and other ETS factors like ELK1 and ETV5 do not mediate the recruitment of this complex. While the lack of interaction could be due to technical factors, such as epitope masking in protein complexes, this insight encourages further investigation into alternative, non-ETS factor-mediated transcriptional complexes that may facilitate the engagement of the TRIM28-TRIM24 complex with TERTpMUT in thyroid cancer.
Concerning our finding that AURKB interacts with and regulates the activity of wild-type TERTp, a recent study by Sharma et al. provides additional insights. They demonstrated that non-telomeric telomere-repeat-binding-factor 2 (TRF2) represses TERT transcription by binding to the G-quadruplex structure of the wild-type promoter and recruiting the REST/PRC2 complex, leading to transcriptional repression It is plausible AURKB modulates the TERTp chromatin state by regulating REST/PRC2-mediated repression, whether recruited by TRIM28 at the mutant promoter or by TRF2 at the wild-type promoter. Further studies are needed to explore this regulatory mechanism in more detail.
Our study has revealed a previously unrecognized role for AURKB in TERT transcriptional regulation, extending beyond its well-documented function as the enzymatic subunit of the chromosomal passenger complex (CPC). As a CPC component, along with the scaffold protein Inner Centromere Protein (INCENP) and two non-enzymatic subunits, Survivin and Borealin, AURKB ensures accurate kinetochore alignment and cytokinesis progression. , Interestingly, our kinase inhibitor library screen found CHK1 inhibitors, which regulate AURKB via post-translational modifications in response to DNA replication errors, co-clustered with Hesperadin, suggesting a shared pathway influencing TERT expression. Moreover, analysis of TCGA thyroid cancer RNA-seq data highlighted an enrichment for GO terms related to mitosis and demonstrated that Survivin, similarly to AURKB, correlates with TERT expression. Collectively, these data imply a cell-cycle regulatory mechanism controlling TERT transcription and builds upon existing literature around AURKB’s role in cell-cycle regulation of telomere maintenance. , In cancer, the perturbation of cell cycle dynamics by aberrant AURKB expression is known to promote oncogenic transformation. Elevated AURKB expression levels have been documented in several cancers, including breast, colon, thyroid, oral, and non-small cell lung carcinomas. Preclinical studies have shown AURK inhibitors demonstrate promising anti-tumorigenic effects in thyroid cancer in vitro models. , , , However, the impact of these agents on TERT expression and telomerase activity remains unexplored.
In conclusion, our study highlights the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and AURKB as key regulators of TERT expression in thyroid tumors, suggesting that targeting mTORC1 and the TRIM28-REST-AURKB interaction could be a promising therapeutic approach for TERTpMUT-driven cancers. These findings not only expand our understanding of TERT regulation but also point to new strategies for treating advanced thyroid cancers, where conventional therapies are limited. Further exploration of these pathways may provide critical insights into overcoming resistance and improving patient outcomes.

## Limitations of the study
Our study faces a few limitations. Although our initial goal was to compare the responsiveness of mutant and wild-type TERT promoters to inhibitors, we were unable to generate isogenic clones that would allow a direct comparison of endogenous promoter activity. SW1736, which harbors five copies of the mutant TERTp (two of which were successfully edited), was the only cell line we could modify. In contrast, attempts to modify other lines, such as TPC1 or C643 —both of which carry fewer TERT copies— were unsuccessful, potentially because reporter insertion lowered TERT expression below the critical threshold required for telomere maintenance and cell survival. In addition, while MDAT-T41 and CAL62 were selected as a wild-type TERTp cell lines, they nonetheless exhibit TERT expression levels comparable to TERTpMUT lines, likely due to alternative, as-yet unidentified mechanisms of TERTp activation. We also acknowledge that intrinsic differences among these cell lines may influence their signaling properties and therapeutic responses, which could limit the generalizability of our findings.
Regarding the influence of demographic factors such as age, gender identity, ancestry, race, or ethnicity, these variables were not studied in either the cell line or human participant samples used in this research. The absence of such data may affect the generalizability of our findings, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the results. Future studies will aim to address these variables to better understand their potential impact.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Human cell lines and culture conditions
The TPC1 cell line, derived from a primary PTC cell culture, was kindly provided by Prof. James Fagin of the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, USA. The NThy-ori-3.1 (RRID:CVCL_2659), 8505C (RRID:CVCL_1054) and U2OS (RRID:CVCL_0042) cell lines, representative of simian virus 40 (SV40)-immortalized normal thyroid epithelial cells, undifferentiated PTC and osteosarcoma respectively, were procured from CellBank Australia, Sydney. Both the SW1736 (RRID:CVCL_3883), established by Drs. Leibowitz & McCombs in 1977, and C643 (RRID:CVCL_5969) cell lines, established from primary ATCs, were acquired from CLS Cell Lines Service GmbH, Eppelheim, Germany. The MCF7 breast adenocarcinoma line was kindly gifted by Prof. Robert Baxter of the University of Sydney. Prior to the study, cell line identities were confirmed through short tandem repeat profiling (CellBank Australia). Additionally, the mutational status of each cell line was ascertained via Sanger sequencing (Australian Genome Research Facility, Sydney). All cell lines used in this study were negative for mycoplasma contamination as determined by routine PCR-based testing.
SW1736, C643, and TPC1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, whereas MCF7 and U2O2 were cultured in DMEM. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. In contrast, 8505C cells were cultured in MEM medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% non-essential amino acids, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All reagents were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA. Cell cultures were consistently maintained at 37°C within a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere.

## Human participants
Informed and written consent was obtained from all patients for the collection and study of their samples, under the approval of the Northern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (Protocol RESP/16/66). Six patients with poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (three female, three male) were included in this study. Samples were obtained through routine, ethically approved biobanking at Royal North Shore Hospital and were not part of any clinical trial. Patients were selected based on histopathological diagnosis. All samples were deidentified prior to analysis, and no additional demographic or clinical information (including age, gender identity, ancestry, race, or ethnicity) was available to the researchers.

## Method details

## CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in of luciferase reporter cassette
SW1736 cells were plated in a 35 mm tissue culture dish at a density of 1×105 cells per well. The growth medium used throughout the entire procedure was RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The next day the cells were transfected with three plasmids: (i) 500 ng pCas-Guide-Nickase-forward strand, (ii) 500 ng pCas-Guide-Nickase-reverse strand and (iii) 1000 ng TERTp-Luc Donor DNA plasmid, using 4 μL XtremeGene HP transfection reagent (Roche), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The sgRNA target sequences are provided in . The transfected cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C for 48 h. The cells were trypsinized, and 1/10 of the cells transferred to a 25 cm3 tissue culture flask. From this time forward cell were maintained in medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL puromycin. Puromycin-resistant cells were collected, diluted and transferred to a 96-well tissue culture plate at an average seeding density of 1 cell/well. Clones were grown in the presence of puromycin selection over several weeks. Upon reaching 90%-100% confluency, clones were transferred to a 25 cm3 flask. Genomic DNA was extracted from half of the cells, for genetic analysis. Clones with the intended DNA integration were maintained.

## Genomic DNA purification
For long read whole genome sequencing, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using a Monarch HMW DNA Extraction Kit for Tissue (#T3060, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA extraction from cells was performed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (#51306, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), also adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines.
The extracted DNA underwent a comprehensive quality assessment. This included measurements using the Nanodrop UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), sizing and integrity checks with Tapestation 4200 Genomic DNA tapes (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and quantification via the Qubit DNA Broad Range Assay Kit (#Q32850, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Luciferase reporter assay
For assays involving SW1736TERT/LUC, the cells were seeded in a 96-well culture plate at 1x104 cells/well prior to treatment with drug (All inhibitors from Selleck Chem, Houston TX, USA) or DMSO vehicle control (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h. Following treatment, the confluency of each well was measured using the IncuCyte® S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Essen BioScience, Michigan, USA), and the cells were then lyzed in 50 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer (PLB) (Promega, Madison WI, USA). The luciferase activity was assessed using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Reagents (Promega) and quantified on a Veritas luminometer (Promega), as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RNA purification
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Plus Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), incorporating in-solution digestion with RNase-Free DNAse I (Qiagen). RNA concentration and quality were assessed via Qubit 4 Fluorometer with RNA HS Assay Kit (#Q33225, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the NanoDrop-1000 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples with 260/280 ratio 1.8-2.2 and 260/230 ratio >1.7 were considered acceptable for downstream analysis.

## Long read whole genome sequencing (WGS) of SW1736TERT/LUC
Three μg of gDNA from SW1736TERT/LUC cells was prepared (The Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics, Sydney NSW) as per manufacturer's protocol, with minor changes described below, using the Oxford Nanopore DNA by Ligation SQK-LSK114 kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Cambridge, UK).
Briefly, DNA was end repaired and dA-tailed using the NEB Companion module for ONT (New England Biolabs) at 20°C for 10 min and 65°C for 5 min. A 1x bead cleanup was performed with on bead incubation increased to 10 min. The elution step was performed for 10 min at 37°C. Adapter ligation was performed followed by 0.4x bead clean up with on bead incubation increased to 10 min. For the elution step, the beads were resuspended 26 μL elution buffer which was incubated for 30 min at 37°C. All hula rotation incubation steps were performed for 10 min.
The library was sequenced (The Garvan Medical Research Institute, Sydney, Australia) on a R10.4.1 flow cell in high accuracy (400bps) mode on the ONT PromethION sequencing device (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Minimap2 (version 2.36-r1175) with the ‘-x map-ont’ option was used to align the nanopore reads to GRCh38 (hg38) reference assembly and TERTp-Luc Donor reference.

## Short read WGS of poorly differentiated thyroid cancer patients
WGS was performed on paired DNA from fresh frozen tumor and peripheral blood. Sequencing libraries were prepared from 1 μg DNA using the TruSeq Nano DNA HT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), as per manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia).
Following GATK’s Best Practice Guidelines for mapping and variant calling with short read WGS data, nf-core’s Sarek pipeline (2.7.1) was utilised to map reads to the GRCh38 (hg38) reference assembly, and further processed to remove systematic technical errors introduced during the sequencing process (marking of duplicate reads and base quality score recalibration). Candidate somatic short variants were identified using the Mutect2 tool. After filtering these candidate variants, we utilized SnpEff, a genomic variant annotation and functional effect prediction tool, to annotate the filtered variants.

## Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
Reverse transcription was performed using the SensiFAST cDNA synthesis kit (#BIO-65053, Bioline Australia, Eveleigh, Australia), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was quantified by RT-qPCR using SensiFAST probe Hi-ROX mastermix (#BIO-82005, Bioline) with gene-specific TaqMan hydrolysis probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Preliminary experiments found 18S to be stably expressed across all conditions in our models, thus it was selected as the normalization control. Samples were amplified in triplicate on a QuantStudio 12K Flex real-time PCR machine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and relative gene expression levels calculated from Ct values generated by ExpressionSuite Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Quantitative telomeric repeat amplification protocol (qTRAP)
Depending on treatment with either DMSO or a specific drug, cells were seeded in 25 cm2 or 75 cm2 culture flasks at densities of 2×105 and 1×106 cells respectively, and treated for 72 to 120 h. At each time-point, 1×106 cells were harvested and lyzed with 150 μl CHAPS lysis buffer (0.5% CHAPS, 10 mM Tris 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA and 10% glycerol, pH 7.5) containing 1% β-mercaptoethanol (all reagents from Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% Halt inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were similarly prepared from non-treated MCF7 and U2OS cells to be utilized as the telomerase-positive and -negative control, respectively. The lysates were incubated on ice for 45 min with occasional vortexing and centrifuged at 16,000×g at 4°C for 20 min. In a two-step process of telomerase product formation and amplification, 6 μL of lysate was first incubated with 18 μL extension mixture containing 150 ng M2 primer (5′-AATCCGTCGAGCAGAGTT), 10 mM dNTP mix (Bioline), RNAse OUT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 1× TRAP buffer (1 M Tris HCl, 1 M MgCl2, 1 M KCl, Tween-20 and 0.5 M EGTA, pH 8.0). The reaction was incubated at 30°C for 35 min, then 90°C for 20 min. As negative control, 50 μL of the supernatant was heated for 20 min at 99°C. 6 μL of undiluted sample solution or heat-treated controls were added in triplicate to a 384-well plate with 14.6 μL PCR reaction mixture, consisting of 800 ng M2 primer, 40 ng of ACX primer (5′-GCGCGGCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTAACC) diluted in 1× SensiMIX SYBR® Hi-ROX Kit (Bioline). Samples were amplified using a QuantStudio 12K Flex PCR machine, with the following cycling parameters: 50 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 50°C for 30 s and 72°C for 30 s, followed by a melt curve consisting of 95°C for 10 s, 65°C for 60 s and 72°C for 1 s. A standard curve was generated from the Ct values of the MCF7 serial dilution samples, and used to calculate relative telomerase expression.

## Western blotting
Whole-cell lysates were harvested in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (containing 1% NP40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.5) (all reagents from Sigma-Aldrich) and supplemented with Halt Phosphatase and Protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA (bicinchoninic acid assay) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 10 μg of each protein sample was separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a Hybond ECL membrane (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL).
The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-AURKB (Abcam Cambridge, UK; Cat# ab2254, RRID:AB_302923), anti-KAP1(TRIM28, Abcam Cat# ab10483, RRID:AB_297222), anti-TRIM24 antibody (Abcam Cat# ab70560, RRID:AB_1271220), anti-phospho ERK (T202/Y204) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; Cat# 4370, RRID:AB_2315112), anti-total ERK (CST Cat# 4695, RRID:AB_390779), anti-phospho AKT (S473) (CST Cat# 4060, RRID:AB_2315049) anti-total AKT (CST Cat# 4691, RRID:AB_915783), anti-phospho S6 (S235/236) (CST Cat# 4958, RRID:AB_916156), anti-total S6 (CST Cat# 2217, RRID:AB_331355) or anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2118 (also 2118L), RRID:AB_561053) following manufacturer-recommended dilutions. The membrane was subsequently probed for 1 h at room temperature using either a goat anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 7074 (also 7074S, 7074V, 7074P2), RRID:AB_2099233) or anti-mouse (Agilent Cat# P0447 (also P044701-2), RRID:AB_2617137) IgG horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibody, diluted at 1:10,000. Visualization was achieved using the Supersignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Densitometric quantification was performed using a multigauge imaging system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

## Short-hairpin RNAs (shRNA) knockdown
Cells were silenced for AURKB using the Human AURKB shRNA Plasmid Kit (#TL320607; Origene, Rockville, Maryland, USA) and pGFP-V-RS scrambled shRNA (#TR30013; Origene), following the manufacturer's guidelines. After two weeks of selection with 1 μg/mL puromycin, cells were collected for western blotting and RT-qPCR analysis.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays
Formaldehyde cross-linked chromatin was prepared from cells using a protocol adapted from the methodology developed by the Myers laboratory (HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology, Huntsville, AL, USA). Briefly, per assay, 2×106 cells were fixed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% formaldehyde, incubated at room temperature for 10 min; and then inactivated by the addition of Glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM. Fixed cells were then lyzed in 1 mL ice-cold Farnham lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40, 85 mM KCl and 5 mM PIPES, pH 8.0), and this was then centrifuged at 16,000×g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the nuclear pellet was resuspended in 1 mL ice-cold RIPA buffer. The nuclear lysates were sonicated on ice for 10 min (20 × 30 sec bursts), and the shearing of the chromatin into 100-300 bp fragments was confirmed by assaying a reverse-crosslinked sample with a Bioanalyzer 2100 system using the DNA 12000 Assay Kit (#5067-1508, Agilent Technologies). The preparation was then cleared of debris by centrifugation at 16,000×g for 5 min at 4°C; and the supernatant transferred to a new tube. For each ChIP purification, the nuclear lysates were combined with 50 μl Dynabeads® Sheep anti-Rabbit IgG combined with anti-AURKB (Abcam Cat# ab2254), anti-KAP1(TRIM28, Abcam Cat# ab10483), anti-TRIM24 antibody (Abcam Cat# ab70560, Abcam), anti-phospho (S10) Histone H3 (Abcam Cat# ab5176, RRID:AB_304763), anti-H3K4Me3 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9751, RRID:AB_2616028), anti-H3K27Me3 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9733 (also 9733S, 9733P), RRID:AB_2616029), precipitating primary antibody (or IgG isotype negative control), and incubated overnight at 4°C. The following day, the beads were subjected to six 6 mL washes with ice-cold LiCl wash buffer (500 mM LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 100 mM Tris, pH 7.5); and then a single wash with 1 mL ice cold Tris-EDTA buffer (0.1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5). Purified ChIP DNA was then eluted from the beads in 200 μl elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3), incubated at 65°C for 1 h. Finally, the DNA was reverse cross-linked by a further overnight incubation at 65°C and then purified using a ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA).
Each ddPCR reaction was prepared to a volume of 20 μL containing 1× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP) (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), 900 nM of each primer, 250 nM probe) and which 2 μL ChIP DNA was added. contains the primers and FAM/HEX-labeled probe sequences measure enrichment across the TERT locus. Droplets were then generated using the AutoDG Instrument (Bio-Rad) which combined 20 μL aqueous phase with 70 μL of droplet generation (DG) oil (Bio-Rad). Samples were transferred to a 96-well reaction plate and then sealed using the PX1 PCR plate sealer (Bio-Rad) for 10 s at 180°C prior to thermal cycling. The plate was then transferred to a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad), with cycling conditions as follows: 95°C for 10 min, and then 95°C for 30s and 60°C for 1 min for 40 cycles, followed by 98°C for 10 min and then finally held at 4°C. Droplets were then aspirated and read by the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad). Calculation of absolute number of positive events for a given channel (FAM or HEX) was performed by the Quantasoft Software (Bio-Rad).

## Co-immunoprecipitation
Whole cell lysates were prepared with chilled protein lysis buffer (0.1% Triton-X100, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM EDTA and 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5), containing 1X Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For overexpressed exogenous protein, 300 μL of lysate was prepared from 1×106 SW1736 cells transfected with 1 μg expression plasmid using Roche’s XtremeGene HP reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Endogenous proteins were harvested in 1 mL of lysis buffer isolated from approximately 2×107 SW1736 or C643 cells. For each immunoprecipitation, the lysates were combined with 50 μL Dynabeads Sheep anti-Mouse or anti-Rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) combined with 1 μg of precipitating primary antibody or IgG negative control, and incubated overnight at 4°C. Flag-tagged proteins were precipitated with anti-Flag M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804, RRID:AB_262044). Endogenous TRIM28 was precipitated with anti-KAP1(TRIM28, Abcam Cat# ab10483, RRID:AB_297222). The following day, the beads were subjected to six 1 ml washes with ice-cold lysis buffer. Finally, the purified proteins were eluted in 20 μL laemmeli buffer incubated at 95°C for 10 min.

## Cell cycle analysis
To investigate the impact of inhibitors on cell cycle progression, 1×106 treated cells were fixed with cold 50% ethanol in PBS and stored at -20°C overnight. After fixation, cells were centrifuged at 600xg for 5 min, washed with PBS, and subsequently resuspended in 1 mL of propidium iodide staining solution (1 mg/mL propidium iodide, 100 mg/mL RNase I, and Triton 100). Following a 15 min incubation at 37°C, cellular DNA content was analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA; RRID:SCR_002798) and the compare_means function from the R package ggpubr (version 0.4.0). Linear regression analysis was conducted using the lm function from the R package stats (version 3.6.2). All data were collected from biologically distinct samples and plotted as mean ±SD. QTRAP, qRT-PCR and ChIP assays were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test with the assumption of equal variances. p values < 0.05 were considered significant in all cases.
