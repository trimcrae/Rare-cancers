## Summary
Erythropoiesis, the process of red blood cell (RBC) development from haematopoietic stem cells, is crucial in haematology research due to its intricate regulation and implications in various pathologies such as anaemia and haemoglobinopathies. Humanized mice, created by introducing human cells or tissues into immunodeficient mice, offer a promising avenue in vivo approach. However, challenges persist in fully replicating human erythropoiesis in these models, particularly in generating mature human RBCs capable of sustained circulation. This review discusses the differences between human and mouse erythropoiesis, recent progress made using refined humanized mouse models for studying human erythropoiesis and erythropoietic disorders, the challenges that impede a faithful mimicking of human phenotypes in these mice and recommendations for future research improvements. Despite progress being made, enhancing the translational potential of humanized mouse models for human erythropoiesis research remains a priority.

## INTRODUCTION
Erythropoiesis, the stringently regulated process of forming red blood cells (RBCs) from haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), is a critical area of haematological research for its complexity and role in maintaining physiological balance. Conditions such as anaemia and haemoglobinopathies highlight the importance of understanding the mechanisms underlying erythropoiesis for developing treatments. Although in vitro models involving human‐induced pluripotent stem cells or primary HSCs are widely studied, employing rodent models for human erythropoiesis research is limited due to species‐specific differences.
The lack of a comprehensive in vivo model for human erythropoiesis poses significant challenges, particularly in studying mature erythrocytes. Humanized mice, created by introducing human cells or tissues into immunodeficient mice, show promise for investigating human diseases and drug screening. , , However, early models faced limitations in replicating human immune systems and erythropoiesis. Although substantial advancements have been made, , getting mature human RBCs to circulate in these humanized mice still remains great challenges. , , , The rejection of human erythrocytes by the mouse immune system and physiological differences between species hinder accurate replication of human erythropoiesis. , , Therefore, current research efforts are focused on refining humanized mouse models, making them more accurate and efficient in studying human erythropoiesis and related diseases.
In this review, we begin with a brief overview of the complex and dynamic process of RBC formation. We then explore the disparities between human and murine erythropoiesis and recent advancements in replicating human erythropoiesis in humanized mice. Although progress has been made, challenges remain in fully supporting the development of human RBCs within these models. Additionally, we highlight the potential applications of humanized mouse models and offer recommendations for future research improvements.

## DYNAMIC AND COMPLEX: THE JOURNEY OF HUMAN RBC GENERATION
The process of generating human RBCs is a dynamic but complex process. Throughout embryonic development, mammalian haematopoiesis unfolds in three distinct waves. Most of our understanding of this process comes from extensive mouse studies. The first wave starts in the yolk sac at approximately embryonic day 7.5 (E7.5) and produces nucleated megaloblastic erythroblasts with highly embryonic globin gene expression, known as primitive erythroid precursors (PEs). The second wave begins on approximately embryonic day 8.25 (E8.25) and is characterized by the appearance of erythro‐myeloid progenitors (EMPs) in the yolk sac. Yolk sac EMPs can generate erythroid colonies similar to those from adult bone marrow (BM) and have the potential to produce various other myeloid lineages. , EMPs eventually migrate to the fetal liver to generate definitive erythroblasts, which predominantly express adult globin. The third wave occurs on approximately embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5) and is driven by specialized endothelial cells in the dorsal aorta of the aorta–gonad–mesonephros (AGM) region of the embryo, resulting in the formation of HSCs. These HSCs travel to the fetal liver, undergo an expansion phase and eventually settle in the BM. 

Erythropoiesis in mouse embryos serves as the foundation for understanding RBC development during human embryonic development, as the process of erythropoiesis in human embryos cannot be directly observed. Both exhibit certain similarities, which serve as an important foundation for using humanized mice to study human erythropoiesis. Overall, throughout human embryonic development, erythropoiesis can be divided into three stages. The first stage (3rd–6th week) of embryogenesis involves the yolk sac producing platelets, PEs and macrophages, with erythrocyte‐containing PEs entering circulation. In the second stage (3rd–6th month), yolk sac‐derived EMPs migrate to the liver, where they proliferate and differentiate into RBCs. By the third stage (after 6 months), haematopoiesis shifts to the BM, where HSCs generate RBCs that enter the circulation.
While studies in mice have significantly improved our understanding of embryonic haematopoiesis, notable differences between mouse and human development have limited our understanding of human RBC production during embryonic development. The relative contribution of cells derived from HSCs during embryonic development and the specific time at which EMP contribution decreases remain unknown. Moreover, whether EMP‐derived definitive haematopoietic cells are similar to or differ from HSC‐derived definitive haematopoietic cells requires investigation. The precise number of HSCs that migrate to the BM to participate in mature adult haematopoiesis also remains elusive.
Following birth, HSCs establish residence in the BM, and the generation of adult RBCs involves two main stages (Figure  ). First, within the BM microenvironment, influenced by intercellular interactions and exogenous factors, haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) differentiate into burst‐forming unit‐erythroid cells (BFU‐Es), which subsequently differentiate into colony‐forming unit‐erythroid cells (CFU‐Es). CFU‐Es undergo terminal erythropoiesis, progressing from immature erythroblasts to proerythroblasts, basophilic erythroblasts, polychromatic erythroblasts and finally orthochromatic erythroblasts. During this process, the nucleus undergoes progressive condensation and is ultimately extruded, forming nascent reticulocytes. These reticulocytes undergo further maturation through membrane and proteome remodelling to transform into RBCs. Although classical models of haematopoietic differentiation have been established through mouse studies, emerging research suggests the existence of alternative differentiation pathways under specific condition. Therefore, developing animal models that more accurately reflect human haematopoiesis is essential for understanding erythropoiesis and its regulation.
 

## BRIDGING THE GAP: COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF MURINE AND HUMAN ERYTHROPOIESIS

## BM microenvironment
Extensive comparisons between mice and humans have highlighted the conservation of erythropoietic pathways across species, supporting the use of mice as valuable models for investigating human erythropoiesis. The BM niche provides structural support and biological signalling essential for erythropoiesis. This niche is composed of various cell types and extracellular matrix components, including osteoblasts, endothelial cells and stromal cells. It is also enriched with multiple cytokines and growth factors, such as erythropoietin (EPO), stem cell factor (SCF) and interleukin‐3 (IL‐3), which collectively regulate the development and maturation of erythrocytes. The erythroblastic island (EBI) serves as the primary haematopoietic niche for erythropoiesis, consisting of a central macrophage surrounded by maturing erythroblasts. It has been demonstrated in a number of settings that both autocrine and paracrine interactions between developing erythroid cells and macrophages are essential for the normal development of mature erythroid cells. The central macrophage secretes cytokines that promote the enucleation of erythroblasts and provides iron for haem synthesis. 

However, there are significant differences in the haematopoietic niche between mice and humans. Studies have shown that although the basic structure of EBI is similar in both species, the central macrophages exhibit differences in surface marker expression and gene expression profiles. Mouse central macrophages highly express F4/80, VCAM1 and CD169, while human central macrophages express CD163 and CD169. Erythropoiesis predominantly occurred in the fetal liver during embryonic development in mice and initiated in the yolk sac before transitioning to the fetal liver and BM in humans underscores species‐specific adaptations and suggests distinct regulatory mechanisms. In humans, definitive haematopoiesis is exclusively located within the BM. Conversely, definitive haematopoiesis in mice occurs not only in the BM but also in the spleen, a fetal feature conserved in mice but lost in human. The BM cellularity is higher for mice than other species and does not decrease with age. The blood vessel architecture in human BM is more complex and features more arterial vessels and sinusoidal veins compared to those in mice. Mouse BM displays lower adiposity than human BM, which may lead to differences in the HSC niche compared to humans. In the human BM microenvironment, adipocytes are thought to potentially influence the efficiency and regulation of erythropoiesis. In addition, the expression patterns of certain niche signals influencing erythroid differentiation, such as EPO, SCF and IL‐3, also differ between humans and mice (Figure  ). For example, SCF exerts a stronger effect than FMS‐like tyrosine kinase 3 (Flt‐3) on mouse HSCs, whereas the reverse is true for human cells. 

 Mice and humans also exhibit significant differences in lineage‐specific markers, suggesting that their respective HSCs follow distinct differentiation and maturation patterns (Figure  ). For example, human LT‐HSCs are derived from the Lin− CD34+ CD38− subset and express Flt‐3, whereas mouse LT‐HSCs do not express CD34 or Flt‐3 but do express CD38. Megakaryocyte–erythrocyte progenitors (MEPs) exhibit different lineage markers between mice and humans. Mouse MEPs show low expression of CD16/32 and CD34 and do not express Sca1, while human MEPs are characterized as CD34+/CD38+/CD45RA−/CD135−. Differences in receptors, molecular signalling and expression patterns result in a haematopoietic bias towards different lineages commitment in both species. Overall blood composition in mice and humans is also extremely different (Figure  ), and RBCs account for approximately 40% of blood cells in mice and 45% in humans, underlining divergent haematopoietic/immunologic needs.
 

## Erythrocyte maturation and characteristics
There are significant differences between mice and humans in the later stages and characteristics of mature erythrocytes (Table  ). In mice, it takes approximately 2–3 days for proerythroblasts to become reticulocytes, while in humans, it takes about 5–7 days. Mouse reticulocytes complete maturation and shed their reticular network within 1 day after entering the bloodstream. In contrast, human reticulocytes take approximately 2 days to migrate from the BM to the bloodstream, where they take an additional 1–2 days to mature into erythrocytes. Mature erythrocytes also exhibit distinct characteristics between the two species, with mouse erythrocytes being smaller (MCV: 49.0 fL) than those of humans (MCV: 89.5 fL). Murine erythrocytes have a shorter life span, lasting in circulation for only 38–52 days, compared to the 120 days for human erythrocytes.
 

## Transcriptional regulation and gene expression
Significant differences in gene expression and transcriptional regulation levels exist between humans and mice. Approximately 30% of the genes involved in erythropoiesis show distinct expression patterns between the two species. Mouse erythrocytes also demonstrate unique physiological functions and adaptability, with distinct expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms in their transcription factors, membrane proteins and iron metabolism compared to human erythrocytes. Mice have one predominant form of the Band 3 protein, whereas humans possess two major splice variants. , , Additionally, the cytoplasmic domain of Glycophorin A (GYPA) exhibits only 46% sequence conservation between humans and mice, with mice expressing higher levels of CD44. , 

However, the most pronounced differences are observed in the haemoglobin genes. Although both human and mouse haemoglobin genes are organized into α‐ and β‐globin gene clusters, the composition of the β‐globin gene differs. In humans, the α‐globin gene cluster is situated on chromosome 16, whereas in mice, the equivalent α‐globin genes are located on chromosome 11. ## Haemoglobin switching
The haemoglobin switching in mice and humans exhibits distinct stage‐specific characteristics. In mice, embryonic haemoglobin (EI, x2y2; EII, α2y2; and EIII, α2z2) is present on the 12th day after conception and disappears by the 15th day, whereas in humans, it (Hb Gower‐1, Hb Gower‐2 and Hb Portland) appears 2–3 weeks after conception and is present in circulating blood from 4 to 10–12 weeks. In humans, the timing of the embryonic–fetal haemoglobin switch occurs at approximately 7–10 weeks. In contrast, fetal haemoglobin (HbF) is absent in mice, and the disappearance of the embryonic component is followed by the adult component. All mammals have two distinct erythroid lineages: primitive and definitive erythroid lineages. A distinguishing feature of the primitive and definitive lineages is the presence of different globin gene expression patterns. As primitive cells are progressively replaced during fetal development by the definitive erythroid cells, a switch from the embryonic globin genes to the definitive globin genes occurs. In mice, definitive erythrocytes formed later in the fetal liver, spleen and BM contain only adult haemoglobin (HbA, α2βmaj and HbA2, α2βmin). These definitive cells first appear on days 11.5 and 12.5 in the blood and fetal liver respectively. Conversely, adult haemoglobin (HbA, α2β2 and HbA2, α2δ2) progressively replaces HbF until approximately 6 months post‐birth in humans. These differences reflect evolutionary adaptations in haemoglobin expression.
Certain mutations that affect human erythropoiesis may not be fully replicated in mouse models, highlighting the limitations of using rodent models to accurately mimic human erythroid pathology. , , , Furthermore, the inherent genetic homogeneity of in‐bred mouse strains contrasts with the genetic diversity present in the human population, posing challenges in predicting phenotypic outcomes in mouse studies. Therefore, with the growing recognition of species‐specific biological and haematological differences and their direct relevance in the development of therapeutic approaches, researchers are increasingly using gene‐editing techniques and primary human cells to construct more humanized models.

## RECENT ADVANCEMENTS IN HUMANIZED MOUSE MODELS FOR STUDYING HUMAN ERYTHROPOIESIS
The development of animal models that accurately replicate human erythropoiesis is crucial for investigating the biological functions of human RBCs in both normal and disease conditions, as well as for assessing protocols for in vitro RBC differentiation. However, in vivo models that accurately replicate human erythropoiesis with the persistence of circulating RBCs remain elusive. We need to consider two critical aspects: One is the engraftment and differentiation of human erythroid cells and the other is the maturation and maintenance of human RBCs. Due to the inability of the murine haematopoietic niche to provide the structural support and regulatory network required for human erythropoiesis, the engraftment rate of human erythroid cells is low and differentiation defects occur. Some haematopoietic regulatory factors in mice may have insufficient effects on human haematopoietic cells or lack corresponding molecular expression similar to humans. As a result, their regulation and stimulation of human haematopoietic development are inadequate. Moreover, even in immunodeficient mice, such as NSG mice, functionally active macrophages are present, which can strongly reject human RBCs, making it difficult for human RBCs to be maintained in circulation. We need to consider multiple aspects to evaluate the rationality and scientific validity of using humanized mouse models for studying human erythropoiesis.
While humanized mouse models (Table  ) display immature human erythrocytes in the BM, the differentiation rate from HSCs to mature human RBCs is low. The c‐KIT tyrosine kinase, which is expressed in stem cells, plays an essential role in their maintenance. Functional loss mutations in the mouse Kit gene (Kit

Wv/Wv
 and Kit

) result in the decreased renewal of mouse HSCs. This reduction in HSC renewal creates open niches, thereby facilitating the homing and development of transplanted human cells in the mouse model. For example, NSGW41 and BRGSK mice have shown improvements in the long‐term stable engraftment of human HSCs and better support for human erythropoiesis compared to traditional NSG mice. , 

 Despite successful human RBC production in the BM of NSGW41 and BRGSK mice, human RBCs are absent in the peripheral circulation. Attempts to improve this by utilizing clodronate to deplete host macrophages only result in a transient presence of human RBCs in circulation due to the involvement of the mouse innate immune response. , However, liposomal clodronate is toxic, has a high mortality rate and provides only short‐term benefits due to the regeneration of phagocytes within 1–2 weeks.
Certain efforts have been made to design a mouse strain with modified mouse protein murine C3 (muC3) to improve human RBC cell survival. Yamaguchi et al. established a novel non‐obese diabetic (NOG) sub‐strain harbouring a truncated version of the muC3 gene (NOG‐C3ΔMG2–3); however, the in vivo depletion of muC3 only improved human RBC survival when phagocytes were concurrently suppressed by clodronate treatment.
A new humanized mouse model that combines human liver and cytokine features has been introduced, resulting in the improved production and survival of human RBCs in blood circulation. This model was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to delete a specific gene called fumaryl‐acetoacetate‐hydrolase (Fah) in mice with the cytokine‐humanized MISTRG background to generate MISTRGFah

−/−
 mice. The liver of these mice was humanized via intrasplenic injection of primary human hepatocytes (huHepMISTRGFah). Liver humanization eliminated muC3, reduced murine Kupffer cell density and prolonged the survival of transfused human RBCs. Human CD34+ stem and progenitor cells engrafted in huHepMISTRGFah mice showed enhanced human erythropoiesis, resulting in the production of circulating and mature enucleated human RBCs.
Multiple factors contribute to the abnormal development of human cell lineages in humanized mouse models. Factors such as EPO and SCF in mouse models have limited effects on human RBCs, resulting in a weak capacity for the generation of transplanted human RBCs. Moreover, certain human‐specific factors lack corresponding expression in mice. In NSG mice transplanted with CD34+ HSPCs, hydrodynamic tail‐vein injection of plasmid DNA encoding human EPO and IL‐3 enables the mice to express EPO and IL‐3, enhancing human erythroid differentiation. The strong rejection of human RBCs by mouse macrophages results in the absence of mature human RBCs in circulation. , Previous studies have shown that human RBCs in the murine host are coated with muC3, which likely targets human RBCs to murine macrophages and other phagocytes. Xenogeneic antigens on human RBCs can activate mouse macrophages, thereby triggering the phagocytosis and clearance of RBCs, a mechanism that may be independent of the CD47‐SIRPα axis. The expression of haem oxygenase‐1 (HMOX‐1) in humanized mice restricts the engraftment of human RBCs, whereas mature human RBCs are increased in the peripheral blood of HMOX‐1‐deficient humanized mice. Therefore, strategies such as depleting mouse macrophages and combining liver humanization with cytokine humanization can promote the maintenance of human RBCs in humanized mice.
Exploring these factors will help optimize humanized mouse models to better simulate the physiological complexity of human erythropoiesis. In addition, given the limitations of current in vivo systems in replicating the human bone BM environment, particularly in erythropoiesis and its maintenance, the development of 3D BM models or co‐culture systems represents a promising direction. The 3D BM models feature a complete BM structure, including vasculature and the endosteum, and are capable of replicating in vivo BM functions. They support the differentiation and maintenance of human HSCs, while also demonstrating excellent performance in simulating erythropoiesis. , Elvarsdottir et al. developed an efficient and robust 3D scaffold culture model supporting terminal erythroid differentiation from CD34+‐enriched primary BM cells from healthy donors. CD34+ cells cultured in 3D facilitated the highest expansion and maturation of erythroid cells, including the generation of erythroblastic islands and enucleated erythrocytes.
Each model has unique advantages and limitations, and the choice of an appropriate model should be based on specific research purposes and conditions. Combining humanized mice and gene editing technologies can be used to study the impact of specific conditions or genes on erythropoiesis and haematological diseases. For example, Bhoopalan et al. transplanted CRISPR/Cas9‐edited RPS19+/− HSPCs into NSGW41 mice to create a Diamond‐Blackfan anaemia (DBA) model and confirmed that RPS19 mutations impair the haematopoietic function of HSCs. In addition, CD34+ HSPCs derived from DBA patients were transplanted into NBSGW mice to establish a patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) model. Using this model, it was confirmed that gene therapy with hG1E‐GATA1‐IRES‐GFP could stimulate in vivo erythroid differentiation, providing a new therapeutic strategy for the clinical treatment of DBA. In vivo HSC gene therapy is an emerging and promising area of focus in the gene therapy field. Humanized mouse models are frequently used to evaluate novel HSC gene therapy approaches. Further development and improvement of these models are expected to advance our understanding of the fundamental principles of erythropoiesis and its abnormalities in various diseases, while also enabling their better application in the preclinical evaluation of new drugs or therapeutic strategies.

## INVESTIGATING ERYTHROPOIETIC DISORDERS IN HUMANIZED MICE
Disruption in human erythropoiesis or related inefficiencies can result in a multitude of haematological disorders, and these disorders exhibit significant heterogeneity and are characterized by their complex aetiologies and pathological mechanisms affecting RBC production. Using humanized mice could help establish refined preclinical models to investigate specific defects and molecular pathways implicated in these diseases.

## SICKLE CELL DISEASE
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited monogenic disease caused by mutations in the β‐globin gene that result in sickle haemoglobin (HbS) production. Advances in gene editing technology have led to the development of several humanized SCD mouse models , , , , (Table  ). Developed in 1997, HbSS‐BERK mice express human α‐ and βS‐globin chains with approximately 99% human HbS and exhibit cutaneous and deep tissue hyperalgesia, rendering them suitable for researching vascular occlusion and chronic pain in SCD. , The Townes model was developed later and created using a mixed genetic background by replacing mouse α‐ and β‐globin genes with human α‐globin genes and linked fragments of human β‐sickle and fetal α‐globin genes respectively. , 

 However, even these models do not fully capture the genetic heterogeneity and biological properties present in patients with SCD. To address this limitation, HSPCs from patients with SCD were implanted into huHepMISTRGFah mice to construct a novel humanized SCD model. This model could reproduce acute SCD pathology with circulating sickle‐shaped human RBCs, which were detected in peripheral blood. Moreover, pathological changes in the lung, spleen, liver and kidney were comparable to those in patients with SCD.

## β‐THALASSAEMIA
β‐Thalassaemia is a group of heterogeneous autosomal recessive anaemias caused by reduced (β+) or absent (β0) synthesis of β‐globin chains of haemoglobin. Table  summarizes the humanized β‐thalassaemia mouse model established in previous studies. Knockout mouse models, including a natural deletion (Hbbth‐1), insertional disruption (Hbbth‐2) and complete deletion of mouse β‐globin genes (Hbb0 and Hbbth‐3), have been developed to phenotypically mimic human β‐thalassaemia. , , , , , , These models do not fully replicate the genotypic characteristics and physiological changes of human β‐thalassaemia. , 

 The first humanized mouse model, DHΔ4bp mice, carries a common β‐thalassaemia splicing mutation, the codons 41–42 (–TTCT) deletion, which can be used to investigate gene correction strategies in HSCs and to identify HbF inducers. The IVSI‐110 mouse model carries the IVSI‐110 splicing mutation and exhibits a 90% decrease in human β‐globin chain synthesis, which provides a valuable platform for testing strategies aimed at restoring normal splicing. Ryan and colleagues used a delayed fetal to adult haemoglobin‐switching cassette to generate humanized Cooley's anaemia CA mice that more accurately replicate the haemoglobin switch that occurs in humans. , This model synthesizes the high levels of human HbF at birth but becomes severely anaemic upon the completion of their haemoglobin switch to a non‐functional human β0‐globin allele after birth, which can be used to investigate the mechanisms of human haemoglobin switching.

## BLOOD‐STAGE INFECTION OF PLASMODIUM FALCIPARUM

Plasmodium falciparum develops in the human body in two stages: the liver and blood stage. Humanized mouse models have been developed to explore P. falciparum blood‐stage infection in vivo (Table  ), with early models transplanting human RBCs into immunodeficient mice. , , , , , , These early models had limitations, including inconsistent engraftment and rapid clearance by mouse macrophages. Improved models, such as those using NOD/SCID‐β2m

−/−
 and NSG mice, have demonstrated the high levels of human RBC chimerism and parasitaemia, with the NSG (NOD/SCID/IL2rγnull) strain supporting a 10‐fold higher infectious burden than NOD/SCID/β2m

−/−
 mice. , Although previous mouse models have displayed the high levels of human RBC maintenance and parasitaemia, they were limited in their ability to host various P. falciparum strains. Arnold et al. , developed a refined NSG mouse models that accommodated multiple malaria parasite strains without prior adaptation and demonstrated significant growth of the P. falciparum‐infected human RBC population.
 The major advancement in humanized mouse models of blood‐stage malaria is the xenotransplantation of HSCs that can represent the entire cycle of erythrocyte development within the mouse. The HLA‐DR4.RagKO.IL2RγcKO.NOD (DRAG) mice were developed using human erythrocytes and reticulocytes and comprised approximately 0.2% of the red cell pool. This model supports the complex life cycle of P. falciparum. , However, DRAG mice have a low human haematocrit level due to the poor differentiation of stem cells into the erythroid lineage.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS
Humanized mice have profoundly advanced our understanding of erythropoiesis in humans under both healthy and diseased conditions. The use of humanized mouse models allows for a more accurate representation of the human erythroid development process, aiding in a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying RBC differentiation and maturation. Building on this foundation, we can gain a better understanding of the pathogenesis of erythroid‐related diseases, thereby facilitating the discovery of new therapeutic targets. For example, humanized mouse models have been used to establish disease models for SCD and β‐thalassaemia, investigating specific defects and molecular pathways implicated in these diseases. In addition, these models serve as experimental platforms for testing pharmaceutical and gene therapies.
However, they require further improvements and innovations to overcome current limitations and to reflect human physiological and pathological states more accurately. A key shortcoming of these systems is the lack of human environmental factors. First, differences in RBC development between humans and mice limit the accuracy of mouse models in simulating human erythropoiesis. Second, the innate immune system of mice strongly rejects human RBCs. The activation of mouse macrophages results in the clearance of transplanted RBCs or progenitor cells, thereby hindering their effective reconstitution in humanized mice. Even after interventions, such as liver humanization to deplete mouse macrophages, the quantity of mature human RBCs in humanized mice remains insufficient. Furthermore, in the study of various RBC diseases, numerous mouse models are created through genetic editing techniques to modify mouse gene expression and express human disease‐related proteins. Such aberrant erythropoiesis models do not truly embody the concept of ‘humanization’ as they are produced within the context of mouse haematopoietic and immune systems, failing to accurately reflect the pathophysiology of human erythrocytic diseases.
The BM microenvironment, as the primary niche for erythropoiesis and development, provides crucial structural support and regulatory networks for human erythroid development. By introducing key components such as human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and macrophages, a partial reconstruction of the human BM microenvironment can be achieved. For example, introducing human BM MSCs or central macrophages may effectively enhance erythroblastic island function and improve erythropoiesis. Ectopic ‘humanized ossicles’ (hOss) are miniaturized, engineered human bone organs in mice displaying a similar structure and function to native mouse bones. However, they are composed of human mesenchymal‐derived cells forming a humanized BM niche. Compared to conventional humanized mice (e.g., NSG), hOss models are reported to support both lymphoid and myeloid engraftment in a more balanced fashion and better maintain the quiescent status and self‐renewal properties of human HSCs as compared to mouse bones. In addition, using 3D bioprinting technology to create in vitro models similar to the human BM environment, such as human erythroblastic islands, can make it easier to observe and study how RBC development interacts with the microenvironment.
Currently, gene editing technology can be used to express key molecules required for human haematopoietic development in humanized mice, including cytokines, transcription factors and growth factors, which promote the maintenance and differentiation of human HSCs in mice. However, erythroid differentiation and the maintenance of mature RBCs in humanized mice remain insufficient. While expressing human molecules, it is crucial to consider the compatibility of the mouse host with the transplanted human cells. It is necessary to optimize cross‐species signal compatibility. Genetic engineering techniques can be used to modify mouse EPO or SCF to make them more effective in acting on human erythroid progenitor cells. Alternatively, knocking out endogenous competitive factors in mice can alleviate the inhibitory effects on transplanted human cells. Mouse liver macrophages are the primary agents responsible for clearing human RBCs. Therefore, co‐transplanting human fetal liver to replace the mouse liver can, to some extent, improve the engraftment and maintenance of human RBCs. Equally important for erythroid disease‐related PDX models is the implementation of more personalized and autologous experiments. This could involve co‐transplanting HSCs and immune cells from the same patient to more accurately mimic disease progression and therapeutic responses. In addition, studying the differences in gene expression and signalling mechanisms between humans and mice during erythropoiesis can help propose numerous suggestions and strategies to improve the accuracy of humanized mouse models.
In conclusion, the application of humanized mouse models in erythropoiesis research has numerous challenges, but with technological innovation and interdisciplinary collaboration, there is hope to resolve these issues in the future, thereby providing an important platform for the investigation and treatment of human blood diseases.
