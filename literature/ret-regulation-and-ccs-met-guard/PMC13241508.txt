## Abstract
Cold tumors, including epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), mostly arise from sporadic factors, particularly hormonal and environmental influences, and are characterized by limited immune cell infiltration. Growth differentiation factor 15 (Gdf15) is increasingly recognized as a prognostic marker for EOC, but its role in harsh tumor environments, including immune surveillance and xenobiotic stress, remains underexplored. Analysis via clinical and cellular analyses revealed that EOC cell-derived Gdf15 facilitates the biological actions of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling in the tumor immune niche. Notably, AhR signaling is enriched in natural killer (NK) cells following chemotherapy and during relapse, which is correlated with poor patient prognosis. Moreover, elevated Gdf15 levels are associated with reduced postprogression survival, indicating the involvement of the Gdf15-AhR axis in chemoresistance and tumor progression. While Gdf15-high EOC cells exhibit resistance to NK actions, EOC cell-derived Gdf15 enhances NK cell surveillance against Gdf15-low EOC cells, a predominant population in primary tumors. Despite the initial anticancer activity of NK cells, the AhR signaling-high NK cell population eventually displays stress-associated features of cellular exhaustion with diminished immunological surveillance markers during malignancy. This AhR-associated exhaustive stress process in NK cells was further validated in allograft and xenobiotic exposure models, closely mirroring clinical observations. Although Gdf15-modulated AhR signaling initially mediates anticancer effects, the prolonged interplay between Gdf15 and AhR is linked to impaired NK cell surveillance. The prediction of adverse outcomes via the Gdf15-AhR axis provides new insights into the malignant evolution of the tumor-NK cell niche and the environmental susceptibility of EOC progression.

## Introduction
NK cells can kill tumor cells without prior sensitization, and activated NK cells downregulate tumor cell populations, as determined in both clinical and experimental investigations. , From an immune perspective, the 2D NK group (NKG2D, CD314) plays a pivotal role in the recognition and elimination of potentially dangerous cells, including tumor cells. Moreover, NK cells respond to various environmental factors through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which can also modulate NK cell activity. , Notably, local tissue-rich NK cells, such as CD56high NK cells, are highly responsive to AhR ligands in the microenvironment. Despite the antitumor actions of NK cells, their functionality is often compromised within the tumor microenvironment. In cancers such as ovarian, breast, and endometrial carcinoma, NK cells shift toward an NK2 subset, characterized by low expression of cytotoxic molecules, reducing their tumor-killing ability. The transition to the NK2 phenotype could be a clue to this dysfunction, as it weakens NK cell cytolytic activity, enabling the tumor to evade immune detection. Understanding these complex mechanisms could provide key insights for developing therapies that restore NK cell function in cold tumors. Although NK cell dysfunction is recognized, a clear molecular signature of dysfunctional NK cells remains undefined. Studies show that factors such as TGFβ, PGE2, or HIF-1α alone are insufficient to drive NK cell changes, suggesting that multiple signals coordinate the differentiation process. Understanding these mechanisms could lead to strategies to restore NK cell activity in cancer immunotherapy.
AhR mediates a wide range of biological and toxicological responses to internal or external stressors, contributing to human physiology and adverse outcomes of the exposome, including tumorigenesis, metabolic distress, and inflammation. , The AhR receptor is a potential intervention target in a variety of epithelial cancers. , Moreover, AhR signaling plays a crucial role in shaping both innate and adaptive immune responses. Specifically, AhR is notably involved in the development of innate lymphoid cells, including NK cell precursors. , The infiltration and activation of lymphocytes and NK cells into tumors are positively correlated with survival in patients with cancer. , 
Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the leading cause of mortality in patients with gynecologic malignancies in developed countries, as ovarian cancer is rarely detected in the early stages until it spreads to the abdomen and pelvis. This late-stage diagnosis is a hallmark of “cold” tumors, which are characterized by a lack of immune cell infiltration, particularly natural killer (NK) cells, and a generally immunosuppressive microenvironment. Cold tumors fail to generate an effective immune response, allowing cancer cells to evade detection and grow uncontrollably. The majority of patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer have a sporadic form of this malignancy owing to hormonal, lifestyle, and environmental risk factors, including chemical stressors, obesity, and smoking. , , In particular, a recent report demonstrated that ultra-processed food consumption is associated with a notably increased incidence of ovarian cancer among all types of human cancers. Furthermore, patients with relapsed ovarian cancer have a poor prognosis despite receiving superior treatment strategies, including radiotherapy and chemotherapy. , Based on their chemoresistance, EOC cells can be classified into two groups: chemoresistant (type I) and chemosensitive (type II). , Type II EOC cells are classical ovarian cancer cells and are characterized by rapid growth, cell division, and a lack of cell-to-cell contact inhibition. In contrast, type I EOC cells are similar to progenitor cells and are characterized by high levels of toll-like receptor 4, MyD88, and stress-responsive growth differentiation factor 15 (Gdf15), , which contribute to ovarian cancer progression by facilitating EOC cell survival, , paclitaxel chemoresistance, , and metastasis. , Notably, type I EOC cell-derived Gdf15 characterizes EOC cell differentiation and resistance following standard first-line chemotherapy, contributing to the prognosis of patients with EOC. , The level of serum cancer antigen 125 (CA125), a clinically representative biomarker of EOC, has been used to diagnose and predict treatment response, although it is elevated in 50% of patients with cancer stages I and II. As CA125 alone cannot precisely predict therapeutic responses, new biomarker candidates, such as Gdf15, have been developed. , Gdf15 is a stress-responsive biomarker of clinical inflammatory and oncogenic distress in various types of diseases. Previously, we identified Gdf15 as a potent mediator of paclitaxel resistance in type I EOC cells. 
In the present study, we assessed stress-responsive biomarkers to address the biological process of EOC progression in response to immunological surveillance or xenobiotic stress. Gdf15 was assessed to determine its impact on progression-associated prognosis and EOC responses to NK-mediated surveillance via regulation of AhR, a promising environmental receptor. The findings of the present investigation provide novel insights into the unproven etiology and progression of cold tumors, including EOC.

## Results

## Stress-responsive Gdf15 is associated with AhR-linked features in EOC
EOC cell-derived Gdf15, a stress-responsive biomarker of oncogenic distress, plays a pivotal role in regulating cancer cell survival or chemoresistance. , We evaluated the prognostic patterns of EOC in response to Gdf15 levels, considering progression status. In terms of progression-free survival, high Gdf15 levels were associated with a good prognosis in patients with EOC (Fig. a– ). In contrast, postprogression survival analysis revealed that high Gdf15 levels were associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. c– ). The progression-associated dual patterns of Gdf15 suggested that Gdf15 may be involved in the malignant process of cancer progression despite its early association with a good prognosis. Moreover, in the functional prediction of the Gdf15-associated gene cluster via the clinical transcriptome, Gdf15 expression was positively associated with the levels of genes encoding kynurenine (Kyn) production enzymes, including indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), and xenobiotic metabolism (Fig. ). Assuming that cancer cell-derived Kyn is a promising endogenous AhR ligand for cell regulation, Kyn levels were analyzed in patients with EOC. While there were no significant differences in the levels of Kyn and Gdf15 among the ovarian cancer types (high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC), and ovarian endometrial carcinoma, Supplementary Fig. – ), patients with HGSOC presented increasing plasma Kyn levels in a stage-dependent manner, with remarkable elevation in the advanced stage (stage 4) (Fig. ). Moreover, subjects with high Gdf15 levels presented increased Kyn secretion (Fig. ).
 Since Gdf15 is positively associated with AhR ligand production, it was hypothesized that AhR is involved in tumor progression, including relapse. We further investigated the features of AhR signaling in single-cell sequencing-based cell clusters from patients with progressive ovarian cancer receiving cancer treatment (Fig. h– ). Notably, AhR signaling-associated features were enriched in NK cells after chemotherapy despite a decrease in the majority of cancer cell and lymphoid cell populations following treatment (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Of note, NKG2D-, AhR-, or AhR target-expressing NK cell levels increased in patients with EOC after cancer treatment (Supplementary Fig. – ). Consistent with the gene expression profiling of NK cells in response to cancer treatment, recurrent ovarian cancer tissue-derived NK cells presented increased levels of AhR-related features compared with those of NK cells from primary tumor tissue (Fig. ). Considering the present evidence, postprogression survival analysis revealed that high levels of AhR signatures in NK cells were associated with poor prognosis in patients with EOC after cancer treatment (Fig. ), which was notable compared with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) (Supplementary Fig. and , respectively).

## Gdf15 facilitates IDO1 expression and metabolite production in EOC cells
Consistent with the clinical features, cellular evaluation demonstrated that Gdf15-high type I EOC cells (R182) secreted more Kyn than Gdf15-low type II EOC cells (A2780) (Fig. ). Moreover, Gdf15 overexpression or treatment with recombinant human Gdf15 increased Kyn production in A2780 cells. Biochemically, indoleamine-pyrrole 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), the main enzyme involved in kynurenine-producing tryptophan metabolism, was markedly upregulated in R182 cells compared with A2780 cells (Fig. ). Moreover, IDO1 expression was dependent on Gdf15 in R182 cells (Fig. ), whereas IDO1 suppression marginally affected Gdf15 expression (Supplementary Fig. ). Moreover, Gdf15 overexpression was found to increase IDO1 levels in A2780 cells (Fig. ), confirming the positive regulation of IDO1 by the Gdf15 protein in EOC cells. Consequently, inhibition of IDO1 enzymatic activity or genetic ablation of IDO1 expression in R182 cells led to a reduction in cellular kynurenine secretion (Fig. ). Considering signal transduction in response to cytokine and inflammatory stimuli, the main transcription factors, such as interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) and NF-κB, are reportedly involved in IDO1 induction. , Accordingly, we assessed whether IRF1 and p65 are involved in Gdf15-induced IDO1 expression in R182 type I EOC cells. Compared with A2780 cells, R182 cells presented elevated expression of IRF1 and phosphorylated p65 (Fig. ). Moreover, suppression of Gdf15 notably downregulated IRF1 and p-p65 levels in R182 type I EOC cells (Fig. ). Based on our previous study on Gdf15 binding to receptors, including TGFRs (TβRII and TβRI), and subsequent activation of the NF-κB-dependent SMAD and TAK1 signaling pathways, we assessed the TGFR/SMAD and TAK1-linked signaling pathways to determine their involvement in IDO1 induction in type I EOC tumor cells. We observed that IDO1 expression was downregulated following specific inhibition of signaling using the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082, the TAK1 inhibitor 5Z7OXO, and the TGFβ receptor (TGFβRI) inhibitor SB431542 (Fig. ). Moreover, genetic knockdown of SMAD4 and IRF1 decreased IDO1 levels in type I EOC cells (Fig. i and , respectively). Both chemical and genetic suppression robustly or partially downregulated the levels of the active form of p65, indicating the positive regulation of NF-κB by SMAD, TAK1, and IRF1, all of which are required for inducing high levels of IDO1 in R182 type I EOC cells. In addition to the conventional receptor of the TGFβ superfamily, Gdf15 can bind to the glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor family receptor α-like (GFRAL) and its coreceptor, the rearranged during transfection (RET) tyrosine kinase. Chemical blockade of GFRAL-RET signaling also downregulated IRF1 and IDO1 levels with a partial reduction in NF-κB signaling in R182 cells (Fig. ). Overall, type I EOC cell-derived Gdf15 is involved in IDO1 expression via the regulation of receptor-linked NF-κB, SMAD, TAK1, and IRF1, all of which contribute to IDO1-mediated AhR ligand production in EOC cells for NK cell regulation (Fig. ).
 

## Type I and II EOC cells differentially alter NK cell responses
Next, we assessed whether cancer cell-derived Gdf15 modulates NK-mediated antitumor immunity via a cell-based model. We compared the responses of EOC cells to those of the NK-92 cell line, which exhibits consistent anticancer activities with numerous hallmark activating receptors and has been used as a representative allogeneic cell source with high safety in several anticancer immunotherapy clinical trials. As shown in Figure 3a, the cell viability assay demonstrated that A2780 type II cells were susceptible to NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, whereas R182 cells were resistant. A similar pattern of responses to NK cells was verified via the calcein-acetoxymethyl assay (Fig. ). Compared with A2780 cells, R182 cells presented attenuated calcein release in response to NK attack. In addition, biochemical evaluation confirmed the differential responses of EOC cells to NK cells. The levels of cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) 1, a representative readout of apoptotic cell death, were markedly greater in A2780 cells than in R182 cells (Fig. ). These findings indicate that type I EOC cells are more resistant to NK cell-mediated antitumor actions than type II EOC cells. Furthermore, we assessed tumor cell-derived NKG2D ligands, which play crucial roles in the sensitivity of cells to NK-mediated recognition. Consistent with their differential responses to NK cells (Fig. a– ), A2780 type II EOC cells presented higher expression levels of MHC class I-related chain A and B (MICA/B) mRNAs and surface proteins than R182 cells (Fig. d and , respectively). Mechanistically, we examined whether Gdf15 could be linked to low expression of NKG2D ligands in R182 cells via shRNA expression. However, Gdf15 did not participate in the regulation of MICA/B and UL16-binding protein (ULBP) levels in R182 cells (Fig. ).
 The functional analysis demonstrated that genetic knockdown of Gdf15 and IDO1 downregulated NK cell actions against EOC cells (Fig. ), indicating that Gdf15 and IDO1 positively contributed to NK-mediated anticancer actions. Furthermore, Gdf15 overexpression enhanced NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Fig. ), as evidenced by elevated levels of cleaved PARP1 in EOC cells (Fig. ). These results indicate that Gdf15 is an NK-activating factor that facilitates cytotoxic anticancer actions.
However, we cannot exclude the possibility that Gdf15 directly mediates cancer cell apoptosis regardless of NK cell activation, given that Gdf15 overexpression increased A2780 cell cytotoxicity, as shown in the present study. Accordingly, if Gdf15 is a proapoptotic factor in cancer cells, Gdf15-high R128 cells should be more susceptible to NK-mediated cytotoxicity than Gdf15-low A2780 cells. However, R182 cells exhibited greater resistance to NK-linked cell death than A2780 cells (Fig. a– ), indicating that Gdf15 is likely involved in facilitating NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity rather than directly regulating cancer cell growth. Based on the assumption that cancer-derived Gdf15 can promote the actions of NK cells, the NK-mediated cytotoxicity of EOC cells was assessed under tumor niche-simulating culture conditions containing type I/II EOC cells and NK cells; NK-susceptible A2780 cells were monitored in this simulation model. As the NK cell-to-target ratio increased, R182-cocultured A2780 cells underwent apoptotic cell death within the microenvironment. However, coculture with Gdf15-deficient R182 cells notably decreased NK cell activity against A2780 cells (Fig. ), indicating that type I EOC cell-derived Gdf15 facilitates NK cell activity against type II EOC cells.

## Gdf15 modulates AhR-associated NK signaling features in EOC
EOC cell-derived Gdf15 and AhR products were further assessed for their effects on NK cell regulation. In particular, the protein and mRNA expression levels of NK cell AhR were dependent on EOC cell-derived Gdf15 (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. , respectively). NK cells cocultured with Gdf15-deficient R182 cells exhibited reduced AhR expression. Furthermore, the ability of EOC cell-derived Kyn, an endogenous AhR ligand, to promote NK cell growth was tested. Kyn levels corresponding to the R182 EOC cell secretion dose (approximately 1 μM) were marginally detrimental to NK cells and even improved NK cell growth and DNA synthesis, although higher doses of Kyn tended to retard NK cell growth (Fig. ). Moreover, AhR inhibition partially decreased the levels of NK cell activation markers (NKp46 and NKG2D) (Supplementary Fig. ) despite marginal effects on NK cell viability and the cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. ).
 We next tested whether cancer cell-derived Gdf15 and Kyn, which are endogenous AhR ligands, affect the regulation of NK cell signaling. Gdf15-associated molecular interactions were predicted in patients with EOC via a multilayered biological network (Fig. ). The hierarchical layout of the network was composed of GATA-binding factor 2 (GATA2)-governed nodes with high betweenness centrality. Moreover, GATA2 and CD56 expressions were positively associated with Gdf15 levels during EOC progression (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Previous investigations have demonstrated that GATA2 plays a pivotal role in human NK cell maturation, specifically maintaining the CD56bright subset. , Herein, we hypothesized that cancer cell-derived Gdf15 and Kyn regulate GATA2 expression in NK cells in a tumor-associated context. In the cell-based model, Gdf15-high R182 EOC cell-exposed NK cells presented higher GATA2 and NKP46 levels than NK cells in the normal state or in the presence of Gdf15-low A2780 EOC cells (Fig. ). Moreover, Gdf15 suppression in R182 cells attenuated GATA2 levels in NK cells, indicating the positive regulation of GATA2 by tumor cell-derived Gdf15. As stated in the introduction, NK cells can respond to various environmental factors via AhR, along with NKG2D-linked conventional NK cell activation. As AhR mainly modulates the CD56bright subset of NK cells, we also examined whether Gdf15 is involved in the ability of NK cells to modulate AhR signaling. Compared with Gdf15-deficient R182 and A2780 EOC cells, R182 EOC cells presented increased expression of AhR target genes, including CYP1A2 and CYP2C9, in NK cells (Fig. ). Furthermore, genetic knockdown or enzymatic inhibition of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), a Gdf15-associated Kyn-synthesizing enzyme (Fig. ), suppressed the levels of GATA2 and AhR target genes (CYP1A2 and CYP2C9) (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). In terms of the signaling pathway, AhR was positively involved in regulating GATA2 expression in NK cells (Fig. ). We also tested whether Kyn, a main metabolite of IDO, is involved in the regulation of NK cell features. Kyn was a positive inducer of the GATA2 and CYP genes in an AhR signaling-dependent manner (Fig. ). Moreover, we evaluated the A2780-originated cell line (A2780-DOXR), which has type II EOC cell features, including chemoresistance and cancer stemness. Consistent with R182 cells, A270-DOXR was proven to produce more L-Kyn than A2780 cells (Fig. ). Similarly, Kyn levels in A2780-DOXR or R182 cells were marginally detrimental to NK cells (Fig. ) and even improved NK cell activity against A2780 cells (Fig. ). TCDD, a highly specific AhR agonist, also improved the anticancer actions of NK cells. Overall, these findings indicate that EOC cell-derived Gdf15 and subsequent levels of Kyn are positive regulators of the expression of GATA2, which is a key differentiation marker of AhR-linked NK cells and anticancer actions.

## AhR-positive NK cells are associated with Gdf15 expression during EOC progression
To establish the physiological relevance of the Gdf15–AhR axis, we first examined whether tumor-derived Gdf15 directly modulates the effector functions of primary human NK cells. Consistent with our observations in NK-92 cells, genetic knockdown of Gdf15 in EOC cells significantly attenuated the cytotoxic activity of primary NK cells obtained from healthy donors (Fig. ). These results suggest that Gdf15 initially serves as a positive regulator of NK cell-mediated immunosurveillance against ovarian cancer. However, analysis of clinical specimens revealed a more complex involvement of this axis during EOC progression. Using tumor tissues from patients with EOC, we found that the frequency of AhR-expressing NK cells was positively correlated with advanced disease stages; specifically, stage III–IV tumors exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of AhR-positive NK cells than early-stage tumors (Fig. ). Furthermore, EOC specimens with high Gdf15 expression exhibited a markedly increased infiltration of these AhR-positive NK cells (Fig. ). Notably, while Gdf15 initially promotes NK cell recruitment and activation, the sustained enrichment of AhR-positive NK cell populations in Gdf15-high, advanced-stage tumors suggests a phenotypic shift associated with EOC progression.
 To complement these findings, we employed a syngeneic mouse model using ID8 ovarian cancer cells, which closely recapitulate the pathophysiological characteristics of human EOC. We acknowledge that ID8 cells produce only a marginal amount of Gdf15, which initially seems inconsistent with our hypothesis regarding NK cell regulation by cancer cell-derived Gdf15. However, this limitation provides an opportunity to explore alternative, physiologically relevant sources of Gdf15 and their effects on immune cell regulation and tumorigenesis. Given that Gdf15 production by EOC cells is negligible in the syngeneic ID8 model, we hypothesize that the primary source of Gdf15 activity likely originates from the tumor stroma or circulating levels of this cytokine. By using Gdf15-deficient mice, we were able to assess the impact of reduced systemic Gdf15 on tumor growth and immune cell dynamics, particularly focusing on the contribution of Gdf15 to immune cell populations and tumor progression. Tumor burden and growth were significantly reduced in Gdf15 knockout (KO) mice, indicating that Gdf15 promotes tumor progression (Fig. a– ). Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed that AhR-positive NK cells were abundant in tumors from wild-type mice but were markedly reduced in Gdf15-deficient mice (Fig. c– ). Interestingly, while circulating NK cell levels were slightly elevated in the absence of Gdf15, circulating T cells were reduced (Fig. ). In tumor tissues, the infiltration of both T cells and macrophages was diminished in Gdf15 KO mice (Fig. f– ).
 Notably, the frequency of AhR-positive NK cells was more strongly correlated with tumor mass than with T cells or macrophages (Fig. h– ), suggesting a potential link between tumor progression and the expansion of this NK cell subset. Together, these findings support a model in which Gdf15 promotes the emergence and intratumoral accumulation of AhR-positive NK cells, thereby contributing to ovarian tumor progression in both human and murine models. The ID8 model, therefore, serves as a complementary approach to our in vitro human cancer cell studies, allowing us to investigate how extrinsic Gdf15 (whether from stromal sources or systemic circulation) influences NK cell recruitment and function in the tumor microenvironment. This distinction between cancer cell-intrinsic Gdf15 production (demonstrated in human EOC cell lines) and noncancer cell-derived Gdf15 (evaluated in the ID8 model) provides a more comprehensive understanding of Gdf15’s multifaceted role in regulating NK immunity.

## Sustained AhR-associated NK cells predict stress-responsive reprogramming
To classify human NK cells systematically and comprehensively, we utilized human transcriptome datasets derived from multimodal analyses (gse164378 and gse119562). , The cluster profiling of proliferating NK cell transcriptomes exhibited three primary subsets of NK cells (NK1, NK2, and NK3) with differentially expressed gene profiles (Supplementary Table ). The NK1 cluster is characterized by high levels of CD16, CD161, CD38, and genes encoding cytotoxic molecules and markers of NK cell maturity. The NK2 cluster exhibits high levels of CD56, CD27, NKG2D, NKp46, and ribosomal genes, whereas levels of the classic NK cell markers, including CD57 and CD16, are marginal. The NK3 cluster preferentially expresses CD57 and PRDM1, which are features of adaptive NK cells. Of note, NK2 is known to be mostly abundant in cold tumors such as ovarian and breast cancers and characterized as CD56bright NK cells derived from innate lymphoid cell precursors, whereas NK1 and NK3 populations are connected to CD56dim NK cells from early NK progenitors. 
Despite the AhR-linked anticancer actions of NK cells, the AhR-linked signaling features of NK cells are associated with poor prognosis in EOC patients after progression or relapse (Fig. j– ). The NK-immune cell transcriptome-based trajectory was evaluated in patients with multiple phases of EOC progression (Fig. ). Considering the dataset-derived NK subset profiling, the NK2 signature was the most dominant in all pseudotime groups (T1-T5) in the EOC tissues (Supplementary Fig. ). In particular, the levels of the AhR signaling signature were significantly elevated in NK cells at late pseudotime points (T4 and T5, Fig. ). Moreover, NK cells with an elevated AhR signaling signature were more prone to exhibiting higher levels of NK2 characteristics than NK1 and NK3 (Supplementary Fig. ). We further compared the DEG profiles at each pseudotime point, which displayed typical functional patterns (Fig. ). Although the early state exhibited the conventional features of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity and antigen processing, late-phase cell functions were remarkably associated with oxidative stress and cell death responses (Fig. ). In particular, the late-phase genes of NK immune cells included stress response genes (GADD45A/B/G, HAVCR2) and cell exhaustion-related genes (CTLA4, HSPA1A) (Fig. ). Therefore, the late pseudotime populations were enriched with cell stress- and exhaustion-associated signatures, along with an attenuation of the immunosurveillance features (Fig. ).
 We further analyzed the DEG profiles in the NK cell population after recurrence in patients with EOC. While the expression feature of NK cells in primary EOC tissue was attributed to cellular proliferation and cytokine profiling for lymphocyte actions, recurrent tissue NK cells presented increased levels of genes involved in ER stress responses and TGF beta signaling (Fig. g– ). Notably, recurrent EOC-derived NK cells exhibited increased levels of DDIT3 (DNA damage-inducible transcript 3) and LEFTY1 (Left-right determination factor 1), which are dominant predictors in stress response functions and TGF beta signaling, respectively (Fig. g– ).
Compared with those in primary EOC, NK cells from patients with recurrent EOC presented notably increased expression of AhR, DDIT3, and LEFTY1 (Fig. ). Notably, AhR-high subjects exhibited increased expression of DDIT3 and LEFTY1 in patients with recurrent EOC (Fig. ). DDIT3 levels are strongly correlated with AhR levels (Supplementary Fig. ). Moreover, the recurrence-associated CD56high NK cell population presented increased levels of GATA2, DDIT3, and LEFTY1 as well as AhR (Supplementary Fig. ), which is consistent with the previous observation that CD56high NK cells are primarily responsive to AhR ligands in the microenvironment. Taken together, these findings suggest that NK cells from recurrent EOC patients are associated with cell stress responses in an AhR signaling-dependent manner.
 Stress-associated NK cell responses were further verified in the ovarian tissue of AhR ligand-exposed animals (Fig. ). Phthalates are extensively used in daily plasticizing or other diverse chemical supplies, modulating cancer stem cells, cancer cell progression, and chemoresistance by binding to AhR. Experimentally, phthalate-exposed murine ovarian NK and immune cells exhibited stress-responsive signaling pathways related to apoptosis, cell senescence, and TGF-beta signaling (Fig. ). Consistent with the findings of the clinical evaluation of EOC, AhR-high subjects presented increased expression of DDIT3, a key mediator of the ER stress response to phthalate exposure in the animal model (Fig. ), indicating that AhR-associated NK cell stress occurs in the environmentally compromised population. Chemical exposure-linked AhR signaling was confirmed in vitro in human NK cells under oncogenic insult. Human NK cell-derived AhR signaling was also involved in inducing the expression of DDIT3 in response to 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (FICZ) or 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) in the presence of EOC cells (Fig. ). Moreover, the expression of other key ER stress-linked markers, including ATF4 and GRP78, was dependent on AhR signaling in NK cells (Fig. ), indicating the involvement of AhR-associated signaling in the NK cell stress response and exhaustion. Moreover, prolonged AhR activation by either endogenous (L-Kyn) or exogenous (TCDD) elevated the protein levels of DDIT3 and the genotoxicity marker γH2AX in NK cells (Fig. ). Crucially, pharmacological AhR inhibition effectively attenuated the induction of these markers, confirming that AhR acts as a positive regulator in modulating the transition of NK cells toward a stressed and genotoxic state. Consequently, pharmacological blockade of AhR signaling markedly restored the cytotoxic activity of NK cells against Gdf15-high type I EOC cells, whereas it exerted only a marginal effect on their activity against Gdf15-low type II EOC cells (Fig. ).
We next characterized the stress-related transcriptional profiles of AhR-activated NK cells. To simulate pathological conditions, NK cells were challenged with pathophysiologically relevant concentrations of L-Kyn (10 μM). While physiological levels of L-Kyn ( < 1 μM) were positively associated with NK cell expansion and effector functions (Fig. ), higher doses, mirroring the serum kynurenine levels (1.5–20 μM) observed in EOC patients (Fig. ), markedly suppressed NK cell proliferation. Under these high-dose conditions, stress response markers (DNAJB1, HSPA1B, and HSPH1) and immune exhaustion signatures (HAVCR2, CTLA4, and LAG3) were significantly upregulated in an AhR-dependent manner (Fig. ).
 Beyond AhR signaling, we investigated the regulatory role of Gdf15-responsive receptors, as NK cells significantly express GFRAL, TGF-βR1, and TGF-βR2 (Supplementary Fig. ). Notably, pharmacological inhibition of GFRAL-RET or TGF-β receptor signaling enhanced NK cell efficacy against EOC cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, EOC cell-conditioned media triggered the expression of exhaustion-related genes via a TGF-β receptor-dependent pathway; however, this induction was marginally affected by dual inhibition of AhR and RET signaling (Supplementary Fig. ). Crucially, our findings suggest that while Gdf15 is vital for the initial recruitment and activation of NK cells, its primary role in malignancy is to facilitate the AhR-mediated exhaustion axis by driving kynurenine-rich environments. Although Gdf15 alone exerted marginal effects on direct stress gene induction (Supplementary Fig. ), it effectively sensitized NK cells to AhR-mediated exhaustion, bridging the transition from active surveillance to immune failure. Clinical transcriptomic analysis further supported this, showing that GATA4, a critical factor for NK cell differentiation, was inversely correlated with stress (DNAJB1) and exhaustion (LAG3) markers in EOC patients (Fig. ). Collectively, these findings underscore a complex reprogramming of NK cell function, where the interplay between the Gdf15-receptor axis and AhR signaling drives a transition toward a stressed, exhausted state, ultimately facilitating the formation of a “cold” tumor microenvironment dominated by Gdf15-high EOC cells (Fig. ).

## Discussion
These type I EOC cells are the main producers of Gdf15 and Kyn, although they constitute a minor group in the early stage. In contrast, type II EOC cells, which constitute the main population in the primary tumor mass, are susceptible to NK cell-induced cytotoxicity and are well controlled during the early phases of cancer treatment. In contrast, type I EOC cells may survive primary cancer treatment and constitute the main subpopulation of the progressing or recurrent tumor mass. The differential fate of EOC cells with compromised immunity to exhausted NK cells accounts for the prevalence of treatment-resistant EOC cells and poor prognosis after relapse (Fig. ). Tumor cell-derived Gdf15 facilitates the production of AhR ligands for NK cells, which contributes to the efficient control of EOC cells. However, chronic activation of AhR signaling ultimately triggers stress-responsive reprogramming and NK cell exhaustion, which is predicted to be prevalent in postprogression NK cells within the tumor niche. Experimentally, AhR signaling-attenuated NK cells displayed improved antitumor activity against EOC cells. Both exogenous (environmental) and endogenous AhR ligands participate in the pathologic process of NK cells in immunocompromised subjects. Chronically stress-exhausted NK cells are inefficient in clearing EOC cells, leading to EOC recurrence with immunosurveillance-survived cancer cells. All the key events from the present study can constitute a provisional map of an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) for EOC progression or recurrence via modulation of the AhR signaling axis (Fig. ).
Although Gdf15 is a promising prognostic marker of EOC, the role of Gdf15 in the harsh tumor environment, including during cancer treatment and immune surveillance, remains unclear. In the present study, clinical transcriptomic analysis also revealed that Gdf15 contributed to increases in cancer stemness markers in patients with EOC (data not shown). Based on the cell-based evaluation, type I EOC cells presented high levels of Gdf15, which facilitated NK cell-mediated anticancer actions against type II EOC cells. Although Gdf15-high type I EOC cells exhibit NK resistance compared with type II EOC cells, Gdf15 is not involved in regulating NKG2D ligand expression. Instead, Gdf15 is involved in AhR activation, which could facilitate NK cell actions. In particular, Kyn produced by tumor-derived IDO has been suggested to be an AhR agonist, and it plays an important role in activating NK cell differentiation or functionality through AhR within the tumor microenvironment. In particular, lower levels of L-Kyn (approximately <1 μM) were positively correlated with NK cell growth and anticancer effects in the present study. Mechanistically, Gdf15 derived from type I EOC cells enhances IDO1 metabolism and promotes the production of physiological levels ( < 1 μM) of L-Kyn. This process contributes to the activation of AhR-linked NK cell features, such as the upregulation of GATA2, a key transcription factor involved in NK cell differentiation, which plays a crucial role in anticancer immunity.
However, higher doses of L-Kyn counteract NK cell growth and their anticancer activities. This dosing regime is consistent with the serum kynurenine levels (1.5–20 μM) observed in patients with EOC and other cancers, , as well as the clinical observations in this study (Fig. f– ). Additionally, both clinical tissue analysis and mouse allograft models revealed a correlation between AhR-positive NK cells and disease progression in a Gdf15-dependent manner. Mechanistically, exposure to high doses of kynurenine induced the expression of genes associated with stress responses and immune cell exhaustion in our model. Several reports have demonstrated that kynurenine may inhibit NK cell proliferation via AhR signaling. , Moreover, the adverse effects linked to AhR signaling are closely associated with cancer cell-derived Gdf15. As the disease progresses, Gdf15-positive EOC cells become dominant in driving malignancy, as evidenced by our results. Since Gdf15 receptor signaling is also involved in promoting the expression of NK cell exhaustion markers, the interplay between AhR and Gdf15 receptors appears to contribute to NK cell stress and exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment, leading to impaired immune surveillance.
AhR-associated signaling is involved in diverse cell types, such as epithelial cells, B cells, T cells, macrophages, NK cells, and other specialized innate lymphoid cells. , The findings of the present study indicate that Gdf15-high type I EOC cells are highly capable of triggering the expression of NK cell differentiation markers, including GATA2. As mentioned in the introduction, local tissue-rich NK cells, such as CD56-high NK cells, are highly responsive to AhR ligands in the microenvironment. Moreover. GATA2 is required for human NK cell maturation, typically maintenance of the CD56bright subset pool in the periphery. In the present analysis of the single-cell transcriptome in patients with EOC, chemotherapy-responsive GATA2 was enhanced in the immune cell cluster rather than in the mature NK cell cluster. AhR-linked signaling was positively associated with the induction of GATA2 in NK cells. GATA2 is predicted to promote the maturation of AhR-responsive CD56high NK cells, which play crucial roles in modulating differential anticancer actions against EOC cells. However, AhR-positive NK cells experience chronic inflammatory and exhaustive stress responses driven by elevated levels of type I EOC cell-derived Gdf15 and AhR ligands from both endogenous and exogenous sources. Tumor-derived Gdf15 levels are positively correlated with increased infiltration of AhR-positive NK cells. In contrast, Gdf15 deficiency suppressed tumor formation, with reduced populations of T cells and macrophages, whereas total circulating NK cell levels remained intact. Collectively, these findings suggest that Gdf15 potently facilitates the expansion of AhR-positive stressed NK cells, thereby impairing NK cell-mediated anticancer responses within the cold tumor microenvironment.
Previous reports have also demonstrated that inhibition of IDO expression decreases the cytotoxic actions of NK cells and progressively accelerates tumor growth, suggesting that IDO plays a crucial role in antitumor immunity. Similarly, accumulated evidence has indicated that AhR ligands, including IDO1 metabolites, facilitate NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity in the tumor microenvironment. , The results of the present study examining the Gdf15-AhR signaling axis also support the crucial role of IDO1 in the anticancer actions of NK cells. Therefore, we postulate that Gdf15-high cells can facilitate AhR-linked NK reprogramming, which would contribute to enhanced anticancer actions mediated by NK cells. Activated NK cells then suppress NKG2D ligand-high type II EOC cells but are less detrimental to NK-resistant type I EOC cells, which may survive and comprise the recurring tumor mass. However, if the levels of the IDO1 metabolite Kyn are markedly high in the tumor niche, Gdf15-high EOC cells can suppress NK cells more efficiently than can Gdf15-low type II EOC cells. Ultimately, Gdf15-high tumor cells led to stress-associated cellular reprogramming of NK cells, allowing them to evade NK-mediated immune surveillance. In any case, Gdf15-high and IDO1-high cancer cells are predicted to overcome NK cell-mediated anticancer actions with progression.
The tissue expression of Gdf15 is reportedly elevated in several types of cancer biopsies. , Notably, patients with high-grade dysplasia and metastatic cancers display prominently increased serum Gdf15 levels. , Although Gdf15 is a well-defined malignancy-related biomarker in numerous tumors, the association of Gdf15 with tumor prognosis remains debatable, given that Gdf15 can be either protumorigenic or antitumorigenic. Gdf15 is acutely induced by most cancer treatments, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and natural anticancer agents such as phytochemicals. , In response to harsh tumor niches during progression, Gdf15 induces various cellular stress molecules, such as low levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can regulate cell stemness via antioxidant response systems to maintain genomic integrity. , In terms of signaling pathways, Gdf15-induced SMAD signaling is known to regulate cancer cell stemness and subsequent radioresistance in head and neck cancers. In the present study, the Gdf15-activated SMAD signaling pathway was crucial for IDO1 induction in chemoresistant type I EOC cells. Given that IDO1 is highly induced in some cancer stem cells, it can be postulated that IDO1 could be involved in stemness marker expression in Gdf15-high EOC cells. Additional complex molecular networks are thought to associate Gdf15 with EOC progression, which needs to be addressed in future investigations. Although acutely induced Gdf15 mainly tends to exhibit anticancer activity via growth inhibition of tumor cells, tumor cell-derived Gdf15 can be associated with poor prognosis in patients with malignant cancers due to radioresistance, cancer stemness, and metastasis. , Notably, Gdf15-linked malignancy is mechanistically mediated by cellular ROS levels via a SMAD-associated signaling pathway. Gdf15 also facilitates cancer cell migration during metastasis by regulating intracellular signals such as focal adhesion kinase and RhoA small GTPase signaling for polarity and cytoskeleton rearrangement. , 
In addition to the IDO1-AhR-associated regulation of NK cells, we cannot exclude the possibility that cancer cell-secreted Gdf15 directly affects NK cells in the tumor microenvironment since NK cells express Gdf15 receptors such as GFRAL, TGF-βR1, and TGF-βR2. A recent investigation demonstrated that circulating Gdf15 is associated with the dysfunction of human CD56high NK cells via TGFβRI during infection and subsequent severe inflammation in patients with infectious diseases. Likewise, EOC cell-derived Gdf15 regulates NK cells via TGFβRI-associated signaling, as shown in human EOC cells. Moreover, the clinical dataset analysis verified that postprogression NK cells presented TGFβ-associated responses rather than conventional immune signaling (Fig. ). Given that various molecular machineries, including transcriptional regulation, epigenetic regulation, the miRNA machinery, and metabolic reprogramming, are involved in regulating NK cell activity, additional studies could elucidate the dynamic molecular mechanisms underlying the AhR-mediated regulation of NK cells in EOC.
Although extensive clinical evaluations are warranted, the present study provides novel predictive insights into AhR-associated dynamic waves in the NK-tumor niche in a changing environment. Gdf15 was initially associated with AhR-linked NK cell actions against tumor cells. Gdf15 facilitates EOC cell NK immunity via the regulation of AhR ligand production. While Gdf15-high EOC cells exhibit resistance to NK actions, Gdf15 skews NK cell surveillance toward Gdf15-low EOC cells, the main cell population of primary EOC. Despite initial anticancer actions, NK cells in an AhR signaling-frequent environment exhibit stress-associated features of cell exhaustion with attenuated levels of immunological surveillance markers. In conclusion, the Gdf15-AhR axis provides a novel prediction of treatment-resistant cancer recurrence and the environmental susceptibility of the malignant tumor-NK niche.

## Materials and methods

## Cell line, culture maintenance, and reagents
The experimental approach was modified based on a previously published method. The human epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) type I cell line R182 was generously provided by Professor Gil Mor (Yale University, CT, USA), whereas the EOC type II cell line A2780 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). R182 cells were originally developed at Yale University and are characterized by co-expression of MyD88 and CD44, markers known to be involved in chemoresistance and stem cell-like behavior in ovarian cancer. , Notably, R182 cells displayed enhanced resistance to paclitaxel mediated through Toll-like receptor-associated MyD88 signaling, in contrast to A2780 cells. In addition, the A2780-DOXR cell line, derived from the same parental A2780 lineage and exhibiting cancer stemness-like characteristics, was kindly provided by Professor Jae Ho Kim (Pusan National University, Yangsan, Korea). Both R182 and A2780-DOXR cells were chosen for their resistance to paclitaxel and doxorubicin, respectively, and demonstrated in vitro resistance to cisplatin (see Supplementary Fig. ). For cell culture, R182, A2780, and A2780-DOXR cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing either 10% or 20% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Welgene, Daegu, South Korea) under standard humidified conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The mouse ovarian epithelial cancer cell line ID8 (C57BL/6 background) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and cultured in DMEM (Welgene) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Welgene).

## NK cell isolation and culture
Primary human NK cells were purified from healthy donor blood samples, following approval by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Pusan National University Hospital (Approval No. 2406-014-140). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Initially, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were stratified from whole blood using Ficoll-Paque (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) density gradient centrifugation. To achieve high-purity, untouched NK cell populations, we employed the MagniSort™ Human NK Cell Enrichment Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for immunomagnetic negative selection. Briefly, a suspension of 1 × 10⁷ PBMCs was resuspended in isolation buffer. To deplete nontarget cells, the suspension was incubated with a biotinylated antibody cocktail followed by streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. The mixture was then placed in a magnetic field, where the bead-bound undesired cells (including B, T, and NKT cells) were immobilized. The untouched NK cells were subsequently collected from the flow-through fraction and used for further analysis. The isolated NK cells were cultured in AIM V™ Medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with CTS™ Immune Cell Serum Replacement (SR; Thermo Fisher Scientific). To promote cell proliferation and activation, recombinant human interleukin-2 (IL-2; PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 200 IU/mL. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The medium was partially replenished every 2 days to maintain optimal cell density and cytokine activity for subsequent functional assays.
NK-92 cells were selected for their well-characterized cytotoxic properties, reproducible functional characteristics, and established clinical relevance. This cell line maintains the essential functional attributes of primary NK cells, including the expression of activating receptors and potent cytotoxic activity against tumor cells, while providing experimental consistency critical for mechanistic and clinical investigations. NK-92 cells were cultured in alpha MEM medium (Welgene) supplemented with 12.5% FBS, 12.5% horse serum, and recombinant interleukin-2 (IL-2; 100 IU/mL), along with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cell counts were performed using the trypan blue exclusion method (Sigma Aldrich) and a hemocytometer, as described previously 151515. Human recombinant Gdf15 (hrGdf15) and IL-2 proteins were sourced from PeproTech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), and all other chemicals used in this study were obtained from Sigma Aldrich.

## Transient transfection and plasmid construction
The experimental procedure was modified from our previously published work. R182 or A2780 cells (4 × 10⁵) were plated in 60 mm culture dishes and transfected with the indicated plasmids using either jetPRIME (Polyplus Transfection, Illkirch, France) or OmicsFect (Omics Biotechnology Co., Taipei City, Taiwan), following the respective manufacturer’s instructions. For luciferase reporter assays, a transfection mixture was prepared containing 1 µg of luciferase reporter plasmid and 0.1 µg of pRL-null Renilla luciferase vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), which was diluted in 1 µL of Carrigene reagent (Kinovate, Seoul, Korea) and added to each well of a 12-well plate. Following transfection, cells were exposed either to vehicle control (dimethyl sulfoxide [DMSO]) or to combinations of chemical treatments for specified time intervals, after which they were harvested for luciferase activity measurement. According to a prior report, transfection efficiency was maintained between 50–60%, as verified by fluorescence microscopy of cells transfected with a pMX-GFP expression plasmid. A Gdf15-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) vector was generously provided by Dr. Jong-Sik Kim (Andong National University, South Korea) and Dr. Seong-Joon Baek (University of Tennessee, TN, USA). The SMAD4 shRNA plasmid was obtained from Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA). CMV-linked shRNAs were constructed by inserting target sequences into the pSilencer 4.1-CMV-neo vector (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). The control and target-specific shRNA constructs used in this study were referred to as shControl, shGdf15, shSMAD4, shIDO1, and shIRF1, respectively. Inserts of Gdf15, SMAD4, IDO1, and IRF1 shRNA targeted 5′-ACA TGC ACG CGC AGA TCA A-3′, 5′-GGT GTG CAG TTG GAA TGT A-3′, 5-CCC TTC AAG TGT TTC ACC A-3′, and 5′-AGA CCA GAG CAG GAA CAA G-3′, respectively. The xenobiotic responsive element promoter binding site was generated by oligonucleotide synthesis with the following primers: 5′-CTG AGT TCT CAC GCT AGC AGAT TGA GTT CTC ACG CTA GCA GAT TGA GTT CTC ACG CTA GCA GAT C-3′ and 5′-TCG AGA TCT GCT AGC GTG AGA ACT CAA TCT GCT AGC GTG AGA ACT CAA TCT GCT AGC GTG AGA ACT CAG AGC T -3′. The 63 bp double-stranded fragment was generated, excised using SacI and XhoI restriction enzymes, and ligated into the pGL3-enhancer-luc-hygro vector in the sense direction, following the cloning protocol described in our previous study. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the presented data represent three biologically independent repetitions.

## Flow cytometry
After trypsinization, cells were collected and processed into a single-cell suspension. The suspension was then adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL using cell staining buffer. Primary antibodies were added at the optimal dilutions recommended by the manufacturer and incubated with the cells for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. Unbound antibodies were removed by washing the cells twice with staining buffer. Subsequently, the cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 3 min, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied at the suggested concentration, followed by another incubation at 4 °C for 1 h in the dark. The cells were then washed three times with staining buffer and finally resuspended in 500 µL of the same buffer. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted using a FACSCanto™ II system (Becton Dickinson Biosciences).

## Measurement of cell viability
All procedures were based on the methods reported in our previous study. In brief, R182 and A2780 cells (5 × 10³) were seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate and incubated for 48 h. Following this incubation period, cells were treated with either vehicle (control) or the indicated chemical combinations for the appropriate time points. For coculture assays with natural killer (NK) cells, tumor cells were incubated with NK-92 cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios for 4 h. Upon completion of treatment or coculture, NK cells were carefully removed by washing. To assess cell viability, 50 μL of MTT solution (1 mg/mL of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h to allow the formation of formazan crystals through mitochondrial metabolic activity. Following incubation, 150 μL of DMSO was added to each well to solubilize the formazan product, and the plates were further incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data presented are representative of three biologically independent replicates.

## Calcein AM assay
R182 and A2780 cells (5 × 10³) were initially plated in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere and grow for 48 h. After incubation, the cells were labeled with 3 µM calcein-AM and further incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Excess dye was removed by discarding the staining solution and washing the cells twice with complete growth medium. To evaluate NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the labeled tumor cells were cocultured with NK-92 cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios for 4 h. Following the coculture period, the supernatant from each well was carefully collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min to remove cellular debris. Subsequently, 150 µL of the clarified supernatant was transferred into a dark-colored 96-well plate for fluorescence analysis. Fluorescence was detected using the Spectra Max M2e Microplate Reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. Elevated fluorescence levels reflected increased calcein release, indicative of enhanced cytolytic activity against the target cells. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the data represent results from three biologically independent repetitions.

## Measurement of IDO activity
To evaluate IDO activity, kynurenine (Kyn), the first stable metabolite formed downstream of IDO, was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), following the method reported by Yong and Lau. In summary, the target cells were seeded and maintained in culture for 48 h, after which the culture supernatants were collected. A 150 µL aliquot of each supernatant was mixed with 1.3 mL of chilled methanol, and the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 1.3 mL of the clarified supernatant was transferred to a speed vacuum concentrator and dried for 4 h. The resulting residue was reconstituted in potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.4), and 20 µL of the sample was analyzed by monitoring UV absorbance at 360 nm with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. IDO activity was expressed as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the production of 1 nmol of Kyn per sample of cellular protein, as described previously.

## Mouse experimental model of ID8 allograft and blood analysis
All animal experiments and care of the ID8 cell allograft model were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Pusan National University (PNU-2019-2365), and the methods were performed under the Declaration of Helsinki and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals as adopted and promulgated by the United States National Institutes of Health. For the allograft experiments, ID8 cells were dissociated into single cells using trypsin-EDTA and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A total of 3 × 10⁶ cells suspended in 200 μl PBS were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank of 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Hyochang Science, Daegu, Korea) and whole-body Gdf15 knockout (KO) C57BL/6 mice (kindly provided by Dr. Se-Jin Lee, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD, USA). Tumors were allowed to grow for 6 weeks, after which they were surgically excised for further analysis. Moreover, peripheral blood was collected from mice in the ID8 allograft model, and red blood cells were promptly lysed using ACK lysis buffer (150 mM NH₄Cl, 10 mM NaHCO₃, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) for 5 min at 4 °C. Following lysis, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and subsequently stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies targeting mouse CD3 (FITC) and NKp46 (PE) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Staining was conducted for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark to prevent photobleaching. Afterward, the stained cells were washed, resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS), and analyzed for CD3 and NKp46 expression using a FACS Canto II and AccuriTM C6 plus flow cytometry system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

## Clinical tissue sampling and blood analysis
Ovarian cancer tissue samples were obtained from patients diagnosed with epithelial ovarian cancer. All participants provided informed consent, and the use of patient samples was approved by the institutional review board of Pusan National University Hospital (PNUH #1007-006-001, PNUH #1007-007-001). Based on the CONSORT statement (Supplementary materials), a total of 77 patients diagnosed with epithelial ovarian cancer were initially considered for inclusion in this retrospective study as follows: HGSOC (n = 61), endometrioid ovarian cancer (n = 7), and ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC, n = 9). All patients met histological confirmation criteria, and no exclusions were made based on the available clinical data. At the time of initial diagnosis, clinical cancer staging was as follows: stage I (n = 24), stage II (n = 3), stage III (n = 37), and stage IV (n = 8). FIGO staging identified 20 patients with Stage I disease, 3 with stage II, 42 with stage III, and 12 with stage IV. Moreover, plasma samples from patients with EOC were provided by the Biobank of Pusan National University Hospital, a member of the Korea Biobank Network, which was approved by the Pusan National University Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB #2201-008-111). Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all subjects 24 h before surgery in EDTA-containing tubes for deposition at the Biobank. Human plasma Gdf15 and kynurenine were measured using the human Gdf15 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, catalog no. DGD150, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and the Kynurenine-Tryptophan ELISA Kit (ImmuSmol, Bordeaux, France), respectively. The ELISAs were used according to the manufacturer’s directions. Each terminated reaction solution was analyzed using a microplate reader set to 450 nm. The concentrations were calculated according to the standards.

## Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence
Tumor tissues were processed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning into 5 µm-thick slices for further examination. For immunostaining, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor sections derived from mouse flank tumors underwent deparaffinization in xylene, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 9.0) at 121 °C for 5 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the samples in 3% hydrogen peroxide diluted in PBS for 10 min at room temperature in the dark.
For immunohistochemistry (IHC), the tissue sections were rinsed with 0.5% Tris-HCl-Tween buffer and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Bovogen Biologicals, Melbourne, Australia) in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied at a 1:200 dilution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following washes with PBS, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, and then washed again with PBS. Visualization of the signal was achieved by treating the sections with freshly prepared substrate solution containing 0.05% diaminobenzidine (DAB; Sigma Aldrich) and 0.015% hydrogen peroxide in PBS for 2 min. After washing with PBS and distilled water, sections were counterstained with 50% hematoxylin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 min, dehydrated through ascending concentrations of alcohol (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), and mounted. Microscopic examination was carried out using an Axio Imager M2 microscope (Carl Zeiss MicroImaging, GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany). Quantitative analysis of positively stained cells was performed on at least four representative fields using HistoQuest software version 4.0 (TissueGnostics GmbH, Vienna, Austria).
For immunofluorescence (IF), sections that were deparaffinized and rehydrated were washed with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) and blocked with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in PBST for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied at a 1:200 dilution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST, the sections were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies (diluted 1:200) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. This included goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) DyLight® 488 (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA) and goat anti-rabbit IgG Texas Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, IHC-Tek™ DAPI solution; IHC WORLD, Woodstock, MD, USA) for 5 min, and the slides were mounted using anti-fade medium. Fluorescence images were captured with an Andor BC43 benchtop confocal microscope (Oxford Instruments, Belfast, UK).

## Clinical transcriptomic analysis
Through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, whole transcriptomic datasets from patients with ovarian cancer (gse9891 (n = 285), gse63885 (n = 101), and gse26193 (n = 107)) were acquired for analysis. The majority of patients with invasive ovarian cancer (gse9891) underwent laparotomy for diagnosis, staging, and debulking, followed by first-line chemotherapy with a platinum/taxane-based regimen. In the gse63885 study, 32 patients were treated with platinum with cyclophosphamide, while 40 patients were in the taxane and platinum regimen. In the gse26193 study, patients were treated with a combination of surgery and chemotherapy, such as alkylating or alkylating-like agents with or without taxane as a first-line treatment. Progression-free survival (PFS) is a measure of how long someone is on treatment before their cancer starts to grow, while postprogression survival (PPS) is defined as the survival time following the first disease progression during cancer treatment.
For the single-cell-based gene expression analysis, the scRNA-Seq dataset from 11 patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer under chemotherapy (gse165897), 6 patients with or without recurrent EOC (gse130000), and murine ovarian tissues (CRA003354) were downloaded through the GEO database. The gse165897 dataset of patient-derived prospective tissue samples obtained before and after treatment was analyzed using single-cell resolution. Our study includes scRNA-seq data from matched tissue samples, one collected prior to treatment and the other collected following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, from 11 patients with homogeneously treated high-grade serous ovarian cancer (gse165897). All data analyses were performed with version R v4.1.3. The scRNA-Seq analysis was performed using the Seurat R package v4.1.0, with the batch effect of the data eliminated. The UMAP algorithm was adopted for nonlinear dimension reduction of the scRNA-Seq data. Marker genes of each cell cluster were found with the FindAllMarkers function. Cell clusters were annotated using the SCINA R package v1.2.0 (https://github.com/jcao89757/SCINA).

## Statistical analysis
All statistical evaluations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 5.01 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons between the two experimental groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. For datasets involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized, followed by the Newman–Keuls post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Pearson correlation coefficients (R) were calculated to evaluate relationships within CRC-associated datasets. Each in vitro experiment was independently repeated at least twice or three times. The exact number of replicates and technical details for each assay are described in the corresponding figure legends.
