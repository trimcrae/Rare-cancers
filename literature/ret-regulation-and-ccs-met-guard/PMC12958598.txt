This comprehensive review synthesizes the intricate roles of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) in the pathogenesis and progression of ovarian cancer, highlighting their underlying molecular mechanisms and emerging therapeutic implications. LncRNAs, a diverse class of non-protein-coding transcripts, exert profound regulatory control over gene expression at transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and epigenetic levels, acting as both oncogenes and tumor suppressors in EOC. Concurrently, VEGF, a pivotal cytokine, is a master regulator of angiogenesis, fostering tumor growth, metastasis, and the characteristic malignant ascites in EOC. Recent research has unveiled complex regulatory networks, and this review specifically synthesizes the evidence for hypoxia-driven positive feedback loops and regulation within the unique peritoneal tumor microenvironment. In these contexts, lncRNAs intricately modulate VEGF expression and signaling, often through hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) and microRNA (miRNA) sponging, thereby locking in aggressive angiogenic and chemoresistant phenotypes. While anti-VEGF therapies have shown clinical benefit, their efficacy is often limited by acquired resistance. This review explores the potential of targeting specific lncRNAs, either alone or in combination with anti-VEGF agents, as a promising strategy to overcome resistance and enhance personalized medicine in EOC. The discussion includes preclinical advancements in lncRNA-based therapeutics and underscores the critical need for continued research to translate these findings into effective clinical interventions.

## Introduction
Ovarian cancer (OC) stands as the most formidable gynecological malignancy worldwide, characterized by a staggering mortality rate that largely stems from its asymptomatic nature in early stages, leading to diagnosis predominantly at advanced, metastatic disease [ , ]. Despite advancements in cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, the five-year survival rate remains disappointingly low, hovering around 30–40%, largely due to frequent relapse and the acquisition of chemoresistance [ ]. This grim prognosis underscores an urgent need for a deeper understanding of the molecular underpinnings of EOC progression and the development of novel, targeted therapeutic strategies, particularly those aimed at the tumor microenvironment and its complex, interconnected angiogenic signaling networks that are crucial for tumor survival and resistance [ , ]. The inherent difficulty in early detection of OC, often described as a “silent killer,” necessitates the discovery of new biomarkers and therapeutic targets [ ].
In recent decades, the landscape of cancer biology has been revolutionized by the discovery of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), a heterogeneous class of RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length that lack protein-coding potential [ , ]. Far from being “junk RNA,” lncRNAs are now recognized as critical regulators of gene expression, participating in a myriad of cellular processes including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, metabolism, and tumorigenesis [ , ]. Their dysregulation is a hallmark of cancer, with specific lncRNAs acting as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors, profoundly influencing EOC initiation, progression, and therapeutic response [ , ]. The pervasive transcription across the mammalian genome has identified tens of thousands of lncRNAs, many exhibiting tissue-specific or time-specific expression patterns, highlighting their crucial roles in fine-tuning gene expression and cellular processes [ , ].
Concurrently, Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) has long been established as a central player in cancer progression, primarily through its potent pro-angiogenic activity [ , ]. VEGF drives the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), which is essential for tumor growth beyond a minimal size, supplying nutrients, and facilitating waste removal [ ]. In ovarian cancer, VEGF’s role extends beyond direct angiogenesis to include enhancing vascular permeability, contributing significantly to the development of malignant ascites and peritoneal dissemination, characteristic features of advanced disease [ , ]. The VEGF/VEGFR signaling axis is a well-documented therapeutic target, with anti-VEGF agents like bevacizumab widely employed in OC management [ , ].
Emerging evidence reveals that lncRNAs directly modulate VEGF signaling in epithelial ovarian cancer, creating a critical regulatory intersection. LncRNAs are increasingly recognized for their ability to intricately regulate VEGF expression and its associated pathways, thereby influencing tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and response to therapy [ , ]. While previous reviews have discussed lncRNAs and VEGF individually, this review will uniquely synthesize their interplay through the lens of key microenvironmental pressures, specifically the hypoxia-driven feedback loops that amplify angiogenic signaling and the distinct regulatory landscape of the peritoneal niche. By focusing on these critical contexts, we aim to provide a more integrated understanding of the mechanisms that govern their interactions. Furthermore, the exploration will extend to the therapeutic implications of targeting these pathways, including current anti-VEGF strategies, the promising potential of lncRNA-based therapeutics, and synergistic approaches to overcome the persistent challenge of drug resistance in this devastating disease.

## Long non-coding rnas: diverse roles in epithelial ovarian cancer pathogenesis
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) represent a vast and functionally diverse class of RNA molecules, defined by their length exceeding 200 nucleotides and their lack of protein-coding capacity [ ]. Despite initial skepticism regarding their biological significance, high-throughput sequencing technologies have revealed pervasive transcription across the mammalian genome, identifying tens of thousands of lncRNAs, many of which exhibit tissue-specific or time-specific expression patterns [ , ]. This specificity suggests their crucial roles in fine-tuning gene expression and cellular processes. In EOC, the dysregulation of lncRNAs is a consistent finding, profoundly impacting various hallmarks of malignancy, including sustained proliferative signaling, evasion of apoptosis, induced angiogenesis, and tissue invasion and metastasis [ , ].

## Characteristics and molecular mechanisms of LncRNAs
LncRNAs exert their regulatory influence through diverse molecular mechanisms, which can be broadly classified into four functional archetypes: signals, decoys, guides, and scaffolds [ ]. Signals act to regulate gene expression in response to cellular cues. Decoys bind to and titrate away regulatory molecules, such as transcription factors or microRNAs, thereby preventing them from interacting with their intended targets. Guides direct specific molecules (e.g., protein complexes or chromatin modifiers) to target genomic loci. Scaffolds facilitate the assembly of macromolecular complexes by bringing multiple proteins together. These functional categories are not mutually exclusive, and a single lncRNA can act through multiple modes depending on its context.
LncRNAs exert their regulatory influence through diverse molecular mechanisms, often by interacting with DNA, RNA, and proteins [ ]. These mechanisms can be broadly categorized based on their impact on gene expression:

Epigenetic modification: Many lncRNAs, particularly those in the nucleus, function by acting as guides or scaffolds for chromatin-modifying complexes. By recruiting these complexes to specific genomic loci, they can induce epigenetic silencing or activation of target genes [ , ].
Example (Oncogenic): The lncRNA HOTAIR acts as a scaffold, binding to both the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) and the LSD1 histone modification complex [ , ]. In breast cancer, this guides the coordinated silencing of tumor suppressor genes like PCDH10 {Bhan, 2015 #3945;Moradi, 2023 #3944}.
Example (Tumor Suppressive): Conversely, the lncRNA MEG3 associates with PRC2 (via EZH2) and can induce the transcription factor p53, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation in cancers like glioma [ , , ].

## Transcriptional regulation
LncRNAs can directly modulate gene transcription by interacting with transcription factors or components of the transcriptional machinery, such as RNA polymerase II [ , ].

Cis-acting Regulation: Some lncRNAs regulate the expression of neighboring genes on the same chromosome [ , ]. For example, in prostate cancer, the lncRNA PCAT-1 represses the adjacent tumor suppressor gene BRCA2 by recruiting PRC2 {Yang, 2019 #3948;Prensner, 2014 #3947;Malik, 2016 #3946}.

Trans-acting Regulation: Other lncRNAs can regulate distant genes, often by facilitating long-range chromatin interactions. In colorectal cancer, the lncRNA CCAT1-L promotes the expression of the distant oncogene MYC by facilitating chromatin looping between the MYC promoter and its enhancers {Xiang, 2014 #3949}.

## Post-transcriptional regulation
 A primary function of cytoplasmic lncRNAs is to control the fate of mRNAs after transcription [ , ].

 Competing Endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs)/miRNA Sponges: Many lncRNAs act as decoys for microRNAs (miRNAs) [ ]. By binding to and sequestering miRNAs, lncRNAs prevent them from repressing their target mRNAs [ ]. For example, in ovarian cancer, the oncogenic lncRNA H19 sponges the tumor-suppressive miRNA let-7, leading to the de-repression of let-7’s oncogenic targets like HMGA2 [ , ].
 Modulation of mRNA Stability and Translation: LncRNAs can directly bind to mRNAs or proteins to influence their stability or translation [ , ].
 Splicing Regulation: Nuclear lncRNAs can modulate alternative splicing. For example, in breast cancer, the lncRNA MALAT1 recruits and modifies the activity of serine/arginine-rich (SR) splicing factors like SRSF1, altering the splicing patterns of key cancer-related genes {Ouyang, 2021 #3950}.

The repeated observation of lncRNAs functioning as both oncogenes and tumor suppressors points to a fundamental characteristic of their biology. This dual functionality is not a contradiction, but rather a reflection of their context-dependent roles, where the same lncRNA may exert opposing effects based on the specific cancer type, cellular environment, or the particular signaling pathways involved. This implies that therapeutic strategies targeting lncRNAs must be highly specific and tailored to the context of the individual tumor. A broad-spectrum lncRNA inhibitor might have unintended consequences or prove ineffective if the targeted lncRNA acts as a tumor suppressor in certain healthy tissues. This necessitates precision medicine approaches, where lncRNA expression profiles are thoroughly characterized for individual patients and tumor subtypes. Furthermore, the precise mechanism of action (e.g., sponging a specific miRNA versus recruiting a chromatin modifier) may be more critical than the overall expression level when designing therapeutic interventions [ ].
Beyond their direct influence on gene expression, lncRNAs are increasingly recognized as integrators of cellular stress and metabolism. While many studies focus on their roles in traditional cancer hallmarks, lncRNAs also regulate mitochondrial function and glutamine metabolism {Basu, 2025 #3835;Wang, 2021 #76;Roh, 2022 #3943}. Cancer cells are known to undergo significant metabolic reprogramming, often relying on altered mitochondrial function and increased glutamine metabolism for their rapid proliferation and survival [ , ]. The involvement of lncRNAs in regulating enzymes central to glutaminolysis, such as GLS1 and GLS2 (e.g., CCAT2, UCA1) [ , ], highlights their deep integration into the metabolic rewiring that characterizes cancer. By influencing glutamine utilization, lncRNAs can impact the supply of precursors for macromolecule synthesis and the maintenance of intracellular redox balance, both of which are essential for sustained cancer cell growth [ ]. This understanding opens new avenues for targeting lncRNAs to disrupt cancer cell metabolism, potentially synergizing with existing or emerging metabolic inhibitors.
The prevalence of “scaffold,” “decoy,” “guide,” and “signal” mechanisms employed by lncRNAs represents a significant organizational principle in cellular regulation [ ]. Multiple reports describe lncRNAs acting as scaffolds to bring proteins together (e.g., HOTAIR with PRC2/LSD1, FAL1 with BMI1) or as decoys to sequester miRNAs or transcription factors (e.g., HOST2, H19 sponging miRNAs) [ , , , ]. These are not merely isolated regulatory mechanisms but function as central hubs that orchestrate complex protein-protein or RNA-protein interactions. Understanding these scaffolding and decoy functions provides a powerful conceptual framework for drug discovery. Instead of attempting to target individual proteins or miRNAs, a more effective strategy could involve designing molecules that specifically disrupt the lncRNA’s ability to act as a scaffold or decoy. Such an approach could simultaneously impact multiple downstream pathways, potentially leading to more robust therapeutic responses and a reduced likelihood of developing resistance compared to therapies that target only a single molecular component [ ]. ## LncRNAs as orchestrators of ovarian cancer hallmarks
The dysregulation of lncRNAs in EOC contributes directly to the acquisition and maintenance of the core cancer hallmarks. They function as central nodes in regulatory networks, influencing a wide array of pathological processes.

Proliferation and Cell Cycle Control: Many oncogenic lncRNAs, such as MALAT1 and HOTAIR, promote uncontrolled cell growth by modulating key cell cycle regulators. They can sponge miRNAs that target cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) or epigenetically silence tumor suppressors that enforce cell cycle checkpoints, like the CDKN2A/B locus targeted by ANRIL {Kong, 2018 #3953}{Tripathi, 2013 #3952;Fan, 2022 #3951}.
Invasion, Migration, and Metastasis: LncRNAs are key drivers of the metastatic cascade in EOC, particularly in promoting peritoneal dissemination. LncRNAs like H19 and MALAT1 can induce an Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), a process that endows cancer cells with migratory and invasive properties. They achieve this by regulating EMT-associated transcription factors (e.g., ZEB1) and extracellular matrix remodeling enzymes (e.g., MMPs) {Xu, 2016 #3955;Lin, 2016 #3954}.
Chemoresistance: A major clinical challenge in EOC is the development of resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy. LncRNAs like HOTAIR and PVT1 are strongly implicated in this process. Their mechanisms include activating pro-survival pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT), promoting drug efflux, and inhibiting apoptosis, thereby allowing cancer cells to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy {Alkhathami, 2025 #3959;Jasim, 2024 #3958;Nazari, 2024 #3957;Uppaluri, 2023 #3956}.
Cancer Stemness: The ability of a small population of cancer stem cells (CSCs) to self-renew and initiate tumors is linked to relapse and chemoresistance. LncRNAs, including MALAT1 and H19, play a crucial role in maintaining the stem-like properties of these cells by regulating key stemness-associated transcription factors (e.g., SOX2, OCT4) {Nourbakhsh, 2025 #3962;Schwerdtfeger, 2021 #3961;Chen, 2017 #3960}.
Metabolic Reprogramming: Cancer cells undergo significant metabolic shifts to fuel their rapid growth. LncRNAs are deeply integrated into this reprogramming. For instance, some lncRNAs can upregulate key enzymes in glutamine metabolism (e.g., GLS1), which provides EOC cells with essential biosynthetic precursors. Others can promote the switch to glycolysis (the Warburg effect), ensuring energy production even in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment {Huang, 2021 #3965;Lin, 2020 #3964;Guo, 2021 #3963}.

## Key oncogenic and tumor suppressor LncRNAs in epithelial ovarian cancer
Numerous lncRNAs have been identified with aberrant expression in epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), playing critical roles in disease progression [ , , ]. The following lncRNAs represent some of the most well-validated players in EOC pathogenesis.

HOTAIR (HOX transcript antisense RNA): Located on chromosome 12q13.13, HOTAIR is a well-studied oncogene in EOC that functions primarily as a scaffold and guide. Mechanistically, it acts as a scaffold for PRC2 and LSD1 histone modification complexes, guiding them to specific gene loci to induce epigenetic silencing. It can also activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and may function as a ceRNA for miR-193a. Clinically, high HOTAIR expression is a robust biomarker for poor prognosis, correlating with advanced FIGO stage, high histological grade, metastasis, and reduced overall survival. It is also implicated in promoting cisplatin resistance [ , – ].

H19 (H19, imprinted maternally expressed transcript): Located at 11p15.5, H19 is an oncogene that acts as a decoy and scaffold. It is well-known for sponging the tumor suppressor let-7 miRNA family, thereby promoting migration and invasion. It also serves as the precursor for the oncogenic miR-675. Clinically, its overexpression is associated with greater tumor size, higher disease stage, and the acquisition of chemoresistance to cisplatin [ , , ].

MALAT1 (Metastasis-Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1): Located at 11q13.1, this oncogenic lncRNA functions as a decoy and scaffold. Highly expressed in EOC, it participates in pre-mRNA splicing and functions as a ceRNA for multiple miRNAs, including miR-145 and miR-503-5p. Its dysregulation activates pathways like PI3K-AKT and JAK2/STAT3. High expression is a strong predictor of poor prognosis, chemoresistance, and tumor recurrence, and exosomal MALAT1 promotes angiogenesis [ – ].

NEAT1 (Nuclear Paraspeckle Assembly Transcript 1): Located at 11q13.1, NEAT1 is an oncogenic scaffold lncRNA essential for the formation of nuclear paraspeckles. In EOC, its upregulation is associated with metastasis and resistance to paclitaxel, a mechanism involving its function as a decoy for miR-194 to increase ZEB1 expression. Clinically, elevated NEAT1 levels are correlated with advanced FIGO stage and poor overall survival [ , ].

UCA1 (Urothelial Carcinoma Associated 1): Located on chromosome 19p13.12, UCA1 is an oncogene that acts as a guide and decoy. It promotes EOC invasion and metastasis by upregulating MMP2 and MMP9 and enhances cisplatin resistance by modulating apoptosis-related factors. It can also sponge miR-143 to upregulate its target FOSL2. Clinically, high UCA1 expression is associated with advanced stage, lymph node metastasis, and decreased overall survival [ , ].

ANRIL (CDKN2B antisense RNA 1): Located at 9p21.3, the oncogenic lncRNA ANRIL functions as a scaffold and guide. It epigenetically silences the neighboring CDKN2A/B tumor suppressor locus by recruiting PRC1 and PRC2 complexes. This leads to reduced senescence and promotes migration, invasion, and angiogenesis. High ANRIL expression is an independent prognostic factor for poor overall survival in serous EOC [ , , , ].

PVT1 (Pvt 1 oncogene): Located on chromosome 8q24, PVT1 is a frequently amplified oncogene that can act as a decoy. It enhances EOC survival and cisplatin resistance by stabilizing the MYC protein and sponging tumor-suppressive miRNAs like miR-152-3p. Its upregulation is found in cisplatin-resistant EOC tissues and is associated with reduced survival [ , – ].

GAS5 (Growth Arrest Specific 5): Located on chromosome 1q25.1, GAS5 is a tumor suppressor that primarily functions as a decoy and scaffold. It is often downregulated in EOC, and its re-expression promotes apoptosis and enhances cisplatin sensitivity. It can act as a sponge for oncogenic miRNAs like miR-21 and is involved in the ESM1/GAS5/miR-23a-3p/PTEN axis. Clinically, decreased GAS5 expression predicts poor prognosis and chemoresistance [ , ].

MEG3 (maternally expressed 3): Located at 14q32.2, MEG3 acts as a tumor suppressor guide, often silenced in EOC tissues. Its expression inhibits cell growth by associating with PRC2 (via EZH2) and inducing p53. Its loss is associated with tumor progression, making its restoration a potential therapeutic strategy [ , – ].

XIST (X-inactive specific transcript): Located on the X chromosome (Xq13.2), XIST is best known as a guide and signal for X-chromosome inactivation. In EOC, it is often dysregulated and acts as an oncogene, promoting proliferation and migration by sponging miRNAs like miR-150-5p. Its expression levels have been linked to prognosis and response to chemotherapy [ , ].

HOST2 (Human ovarian cancer specific transcript 2): Located at 10q23.1, HOST2 is an overexpressed oncogene that functions as a decoy. It promotes proliferation and migration in EOC specifically by acting as a molecular sponge for the let-7b miRNA, preventing it from suppressing its oncogenic targets [ , ].

SNHG15 (Small Nucleolar RNA Host Gene 15): Located on chromosome 7p13, SNHG15 is an oncogene in EOC that primarily functions as a decoy. It promotes proliferation and migration by sponging tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miR-370-3p, thereby upregulating targets like CDK6. It has also been shown to activate the pro-survival Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Clinically, its upregulation in EOC tissues is a significant predictor of poor progression-free survival (PFS), marking it as a valuable prognostic biomarker [ , , ].

HOXA11-AS (Homeobox A11 Antisense): Located on chromosome 7p15.2, HOXA11-AS is a tumor suppressor lncRNA in EOC. Its functional archetype is likely a guide or cis-regulator, as it influences the expression of the adjacent HOXA11 gene. Its expression is reduced in ovarian tumors, and experimental overexpression inhibits cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Clinically, lower expression levels are associated with a more aggressive phenotype, and it may serve as a favorable prognostic marker [ , ].

AB073614: Located on chromosome 3q24, AB073614 is an oncogene whose functional archetype is not yet fully determined. Its overexpression promotes tumorigenesis by activating the ERK1/2 and AKT signaling pathways, leading to increased expression of proteins involved in proliferation (PCNA) and invasion (MMP2, MMP9). Clinically, high expression of AB073614 is a strong independent predictor of shorter 5-year overall survival in EOC patients, highlighting its utility as a poor prognostic biomarker [ , , ].

ZNF300P1: Located on chromosome 5q33.1, ZNF300P1 is a pseudogene lncRNA that acts as a tumor suppressor in EOC. Its molecular archetype is not well defined. Mechanistically, its downregulation is associated with a loss of cell polarity and increased adhesion of cancer cells to the peritoneum, a key step in ovarian cancer metastasis. Clinically, its epigenetic silencing and reduced expression are features of aggressive EOC, suggesting a role in metastatic progression [ , , ].

## Vascular endothelial growth factor: a cornerstone of epithelial ovarian cancer angiogenesis
Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) is a potent cytokine and a master regulator of both physiological and pathological angiogenesis [ ]. Its critical role in EOC pathogenesis is well-established, as OC is a highly vascularized tumor that is significantly dependent on VEGF-mediated processes for its growth and dissemination [ , ]. Beyond its canonical role in building blood vessels, emerging evidence also highlights VEGF’s angiogenesis-independent functions, where it acts directly on cancer cells in an autocrine fashion to promote their survival and malignant progression [ , ].

## VEGF family, isoforms, and receptor signaling pathways
The human VEGF family is a group of structurally related cytokines that are central to vascular biology. While several members exist, the most relevant to EOC pathogenesis are:

VEGF-A: This is the archetypal and most potent member of the family, often referred to simply as VEGF. It is the primary driver of tumor angiogenesis. By binding mainly to VEGFR-2, it potently stimulates endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival. Critically, it also increases vascular permeability, a key factor in the formation of malignant ascites in EOC {E, 2012 #3967;Duffy, 2013 #3966}.
Placental Growth Factor (PlGF): Structurally similar to VEGF-A, PlGF primarily binds to VEGFR-1. While it has weaker angiogenic potential on its own, it plays a significant role in pathological angiogenesis by recruiting pro-angiogenic inflammatory cells to the tumor microenvironment. It can also displace VEGF-A from VEGFR-1, making more VEGF-A available to bind to the highly pro-angiogenic VEGFR-2 {Autiero, 2003 #3969;Shibuya, 2011 #3968}.
VEGF-B: This ligand also binds specifically to VEGFR-1. Its role in tumor angiogenesis is less pronounced than VEGF-A’s, but it is involved in endothelial cell survival and has been implicated in metabolic regulation within the TME {Li, 2009 #3970}.
VEGF-C and VEGF-D: These two ligands are the primary drivers of lymphangiogenesis—the formation of new lymphatic vessels. They exert their effects by binding to and activating VEGFR-3, which is highly expressed on lymphatic endothelial cells. In EOC, the upregulation of VEGF-C and VEGF-D is strongly associated with the formation of lymphatic metastases, providing a key route for tumor dissemination to lymph nodes {Zheng, 2014 #3972;Valtola, 1999 #3971}.

These ligands exert their effects by binding to three main receptor tyrosine kinases (VEGFRs), which are expressed on vascular and lymphatic endothelial cells, as well as on tumor cells themselves:

 VEGFR-1 (Flt-1): Binds VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF [ ]. While expressed predominantly on vascular endothelial cells, it can act as a negative regulator of VEGF-mediated angiogenesis during development and a stimulator of pathological angiogenesis when activated by PlGF and VEGF-B [ , ].
 VEGFR-2 (KDR/Flk-1): Primarily activated by VEGF-A and is considered the major angiogenic pathway, exhibiting the strongest pro-angiogenic activity and higher tyrosine kinase activity than VEGFR-1 [ ]. Its activation triggers intracellular signaling cascades crucial for endothelial cell function [ , ].
 VEGFR-3 (Flt-4): Primarily activated by VEGF-C and VEGF-D, inducing lymphangiogenesis and also important for angiogenesis, cooperating with VEGF/VEGFR-2 signaling [ , – ].

Ligand-receptor binding leads to receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation on tyrosine residues, initiating diverse intracellular signaling cascades involving components such as JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and MAP kinases [ ]. Endothelial cells also express co-receptors like neuropilin-1 (NP-1) and neuropilin-2 (NP-2), which modulate VEGFR activity [ ]. Importantly, both VEGF and its receptors are expressed on endothelial and non-endothelial cells, including tumor cells themselves [ , ] (Table ; Fig. ).

 

## Regulation of VEGF by oncogenic drivers in EOC
The overexpression of VEGF in epithelial ovarian cancer is not a random event but a direct consequence of the hallmark genetic alterations that drive the disease. Several core oncogenic pathways converge to create a powerful and sustained pro-angiogenic stimulus, primarily by upregulating VEGF expression.

 Loss of TP53 Function: The TP53 tumor suppressor gene is mutated in over 96% of high-grade serous EOC. Wild-type p53 can directly repress the VEGF gene promoter. Consequently, the loss of functional p53 removes this transcriptional brake, leading to the de-repression and subsequent overexpression of VEGF [ ].
 Activation of Oncogenic Signaling Pathways: Pathways such as RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT, which are frequently hyperactivated in EOC through mutations in genes like KRAS, BRAF, or loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN, are potent inducers of VEGF. These signaling cascades enhance the stability and transcriptional activity of Hypoxia-Inducibly Factor-1α (HIF-1α). HIF-1α is a master transcription factor that binds to the Hypoxia Response Element (HRE) within the VEGF gene promoter, driving its expression even under normal oxygen conditions (normoxia). This establishes a direct mechanistic link between the cancer’s core proliferative signals and its ability to build a blood supply [ , ].

These genetic events ensure that angiogenesis is intrinsically coupled to tumor growth, making the VEGF pathway a central node in EOC biology (Fig.  ).

 

## VEGF’s contribution to epithelial ovarian cancer progression
VEGF’s multifaceted contributions to EOC progression, which are summarized in Fig.  , extend beyond simple blood vessel formation and can be categorized into several key functions:

## Tumor angiogenesis and growth
VEGF is the principal driver of tumor angiogenesis. It promotes the survival, proliferation, and migration of endothelial cells, which is an absolute requirement for tumors to grow beyond a minimal size of 1–2 mm [ , ]. Consequently, high expression levels of VEGF in the tumor tissue are strongly associated with accelerated tumor growth and a more aggressive disease phenotype [ , ].

## Peritoneal dissemination and malignant ascites formation
The peritoneal cavity in advanced EOC is not merely a passive site for fluid accumulation but an active, VEGF-driven microenvironment. VEGF is a central player in this niche, dramatically enhancing vascular permeability to cause the formation of malignant ascites [ ]. Furthermore, it has an anti-apoptotic role, protecting cancer cells shed into the ascitic fluid and contributing to vasculogenic mimicry—a process where tumor cells form vessel-like structures, which is associated with aggressive metastatic features {Masoumi Moghaddam, 2012 #3973}. This comprehensive role suggests that therapeutic strategies should specifically aim to disrupt this peritoneal niche [ ].

## Direct autocrine effects on tumor cells (angiogenesis-independent)
The understanding of VEGF’s role has evolved beyond traditional angiogenesis. Evidence indicates that VEGF can exert direct effects on the cancer cells themselves. EOC cells can express VEGF receptors like neuropilin-1 (NRP-1), allowing tumor-produced VEGF to act in an autocrine loop to directly promote cancer cell growth, survival, and dedifferentiation [ , ]. This expanded view suggests that VEGF inhibitors may not only starve tumors of blood supply but also directly impact tumor cell malignancy, implying that resistance could arise from adaptations in these non-angiogenic pathways [ ].

## Convergence with oncogenic signaling pathways
VEGF signaling is not an isolated pathway but a convergence point for several core oncogenic cascades. The activation of VEGF receptors initiates diverse intracellular signaling pathways, including JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and MAP kinases, all of which are well-known drivers of cancer progression [ , ]. This integration means that targeting VEGF can have pleiotropic effects on tumor cells by dampening multiple pro-survival and pro-proliferative pathways simultaneously, though dysregulation of these downstream pathways can also contribute to resistance [ ].

## Correlation with poor prognosis
Consistent with its central biological roles, the level of VEGF expression is a powerful prognostic biomarker in EOC. Elevated levels of VEGF in both serum and tumor tissue are significantly and consistently associated with poor progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) for patients, highlighting its fundamental importance from the very beginning of tumor progression [ ]{Alves, 2023 #3975;Guo, 2018 #3974;Masoumi Moghaddam, 2012 #3973}.

## The interplay: lncRNA-VEGF regulatory networks in epithelial ovarian cancer
The progression of ovarian cancer is not driven by isolated molecular events but by complex, interconnected regulatory networks. A critical area of emerging research highlights the intricate interplay between long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) signaling, where lncRNAs profoundly modulate VEGF expression and activity, thereby influencing tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic response [ , ].

## Key lncRNA-VEGF regulatory axes and their mechanisms in EOC
The progression of epithelial ovarian cancer is driven by complex regulatory networks where lncRNAs profoundly modulate VEGF expression and activity. These interactions are not random but occur through distinct molecular mechanisms, creating specific regulatory axes that control angiogenesis and therapeutic response. The core mechanisms of these interactions are illustrated in Fig.  .

## Regulation via Hypoxia-Inducible factors (HIFs)
Hypoxia is a central feature of the EOC microenvironment and a primary trigger for VEGF expression via the transcription factor HIF-1α. Several lncRNAs are key players in this process, creating robust signaling axes.

 The HOTTIP-HIF-1α-VEGF Axis: The lncRNA HOTTIP is highly expressed in hypoxic EOC cells where it stabilizes the HIF-1α protein. Evidence suggests HOTTIP may act as a scaffold to inhibit the E3 ubiquitin ligase-mediated proteasomal degradation of HIF-1α. This enhanced stability leads to increased HIF-1α activity, potent upregulation of VEGF transcription, and a more aggressive, invasive tumor phenotype [ – ].
 The ANRIL-miR-411-3p-HIF-1α-VEGF Axis: The oncogenic lncRNA ANRIL can indirectly activate HIF-1α by functioning as a decoy for miR-411-3p. By sponging this miRNA, ANRIL prevents it from repressing its target, HIF-1α mRNA. This leads to increased HIF-1α translation, subsequent VEGF transcription, and enhanced cancer cell migration and invasion [ , ].
 The MIR210HG-HIF-1α Axis: The lncRNA MIR210HG (MIR210 Host Gene) is another critical component of the hypoxic response in EOC. Its expression is directly and strongly induced by HIF-1α under low-oxygen conditions. While MIR210HG is the precursor for the well-known pro-angiogenic microRNA, miR-210, the lncRNA transcript itself also has independent functions {Liu, 2021 #3979;Saigusa, 2023 #3978}. It can act as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to regulate other pathways involved in cell survival and proliferation under hypoxic stress. This creates a positive feedback loop: hypoxia activates HIF-1α, which upregulates MIR210HG and miR-210, both of which promote a pro-angiogenic and pro-survival phenotype that helps the tumor adapt to the hypoxic environment it creates {Peng, 2020 #3982;Basheeruddin, 2024 #3981;Lian, 2023 #3980}.

## Modulation via MiRNA sponging (ceRNA Mechanism)
One of the most common mechanisms for cytoplasmic lncRNAs is to act as decoys or “sponges” for miRNAs, thereby preventing them from repressing their target genes, including VEGF.

 The circRhoC-miR-302e-VEGFA Axis: The circular RNA circRhoC is overexpressed in EOC and enhances angiogenesis by acting as a molecular sponge for miR-302e. By sequestering miR-302e, circRhoC prevents it from binding to and degrading VEGF-A mRNA, leading to increased VEGF expression and enhanced tumorigenicity [ ].
 The MALAT1-miRNA-VEGFA Axis: MALAT1 is a powerful regulator of angiogenesis that acts as a ceRNA for numerous VEGF-targeting miRNAs, including miR-126-5p, miR-145, and miR-206. By sponging this diverse pool of miRNAs, MALAT1 provides a broad and robust mechanism for maintaining high levels of VEGF-A expression in EOC [ – ].

## Direct interaction with VEGF mRNA
While many lncRNAs regulate VEGF indirectly, some can exert control through direct physical interaction with the VEGF mRNA transcript itself. A key example of this mechanism involves the lncRNA UCA1. In bladder cancer, UCA1 has been shown to directly bind to the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of VEGF-A mRNA. This interaction shields the mRNA from degradation by endogenous ribonucleases, thereby increasing its stability and leading to elevated VEGF protein expression and enhanced angiogenesis {Tang, 2023 #3977;Epis, 2011 #3976;Alves, 2023 #3975}. This illustrates how a lncRNA can function similarly to an RNA-binding protein to directly control the fate of the VEGF transcript post-transcriptionally (Table ).

 

 

## Therapeutic strategies and challenges: targeting LncRNAs and VEGF
The critical roles of both VEGF and lncRNAs in EOC progression have positioned them as attractive targets for therapeutic intervention. Anti-angiogenic therapy, in particular, has become a cornerstone of modern EOC management, complementing standard chemotherapy and targeted agents. The strategies employed are diverse and can be broadly categorized based on their mechanism of action:

 Monoclonal Antibodies against VEGF: The most prominent agent in this class is bevacizumab, a humanized antibody that directly binds to and neutralizes circulating VEGF-A, preventing it from activating its receptors on endothelial cells [ , ].
 Small Molecule Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs): These are orally administered drugs that target the intracellular kinase domains of VEGFRs and other pro-angiogenic receptors (e.g., PDGFR, FGFR). Multi-targeted TKIs like cediranib, nintedanib, and pazopanib block downstream signaling cascades, thereby inhibiting endothelial cell proliferation and survival [ – ].
 Soluble VEGFRs (VEGF Traps): Agents like aflibercept are fusion proteins that act as high-affinity decoy receptors, sequestering VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF, offering a broader ligand blockade than anti-VEGF-A antibodies alone [ , ].
 Inhibitors of Alternative Pathways: Recognizing that tumors can escape VEGF blockade, newer strategies target other angiogenic pathways. For example, inhibitors of the angiopoietin-TIE2 receptor axis are being explored to counteract resistance mechanisms [ , ].

Crucially, these agents are rarely used as monotherapy in EOC. Their greatest clinical benefit has been demonstrated when integrated with other treatments. They are most commonly used in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy (in both newly diagnosed and recurrent settings) and, more recently, have shown significant synergistic effects when combined with poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, particularly in patients with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) [ – ]. This combination approach, which simultaneously targets DNA repair and angiogenesis, has shifted the treatment paradigm. Despite these advances, the efficacy of anti-angiogenic agents is often limited by eventual resistance, creating a pressing need for novel strategies, such as targeting the lncRNA networks that regulate these pathways, to achieve more durable responses.

## Mechanisms of resistance and associated toxicities
The clinical success of anti-angiogenic therapies is frequently undermined by the development of adaptive resistance. This is not a single event but a multi-faceted process driven by intricate biological adaptations within the tumor and its microenvironment.

Compensatory Angiogenic Pathway Activation: The most common resistance mechanism is “angiogenic redundancy.” When the VEGF pathway is blocked, tumors can upregulate a host of alternative pro-angiogenic factors to restore blood supply. These include members of the Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) family, Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF), and Angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2). These factors signal through different receptors (FGFR, PDGFR, TIE2), effectively creating a bypass circuit that renders anti-VEGF agents ineffective [ – ].
 Hypoxia-Induced Cellular Adaptation: By design, anti-angiogenic therapy prunes blood vessels, which intensifies tumor hypoxia. This severe hypoxic stress acts as a powerful selective pressure.◦ Metabolic Reprogramming: Tumor cells adapt their metabolism to survive in low-oxygen, nutrient-poor conditions. They often switch to a state of heightened glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and increase their reliance on alternative fuels like glutamine, allowing them to generate energy and biomass anaerobically [ – ].
◦ Selection for Aggressive Clones: The hypoxic environment preferentially selects for more malignant and resilient cancer cell populations. In EOC, studies have shown that VEGF inhibition can enrich for tumor cells that have undergone an Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT). These cells are more invasive, motile, and often express cancer stem cell (CSC) markers, making them inherently resistant to therapy and prone to metastasis [ – ].

 Tumor Microenvironment (TME) Remodeling: The TME actively contributes to resistance. Hypoxia can stimulate the recruitment and activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which secrete their own pro-angiogenic factors (like FGF2 and SDF-1) and remodel the extracellular matrix to promote invasion. Furthermore, the hypoxic and acidic TME fostered by anti-angiogenic therapy promotes immune evasion by suppressing the activity of cytotoxic T cells and recruiting immunosuppressive cells like regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ – ].

## Toxicity profile of anti-angiogenic agents
Beyond resistance, the clinical use of these agents is also shaped by their unique toxicity profiles. Because VEGF signaling is crucial for maintaining normal vascular homeostasis, its inhibition can lead to characteristic class-specific adverse effects. The most common toxicities include hypertension (HTN), resulting from decreased nitric oxide production and endothelial dysfunction, and proteinuria, caused by damage to the glomerular filtration barrier in the kidneys [ – ]. Other significant side effects include an increased risk of arterial thromboembolic events, impaired wound healing, and, rarely, gastrointestinal perforation [ ]. Managing these toxicities is a key component of patient care when utilizing anti-angiogenic therapies.

## LncRNAs as emerging therapeutic targets: preclinical approaches and delivery systems
LncRNAs’ critical roles in cancer hallmarks and their dysregulation in OC make them promising therapeutic targets [ ]. Preclinical research is exploring various strategies to modulate lncRNA expression or function [ ]:

 Small Interfering RNAs (siRNAs): Induce lncRNA degradation by recruiting the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Successfully used in preclinical models to reduce tumor progression (e.g., DANCR siRNA in breast cancer, LINC01296 knockdown in NSCLC) [ ]. Challenges include off-target effects.
 Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs): Short single-stranded oligonucleotides that bind to complementary RNA, leading to RNA degradation (via RNase H) or inhibition of translation. Chemical modifications (e.g., phosphorothioate, 2’-O-substituted, locked nucleic acids (LNAs)) improve stability and binding. Preclinical examples include ASOs targeting MALAT1, which have been shown in mouse models of breast cancer to inhibit primary tumor growth and reduce lung metastasis by over 70% [ ]. Similarly, a NEAT1 LNA gapmeR has demonstrated potent antitumor activity in multiple myeloma models, significantly extending the survival of treated mice [ ]. While these quantitative results are highly promising, challenges such as specific delivery and potential off-target effects must be addressed for successful clinical translation [ ].
 CRISPR/Cas9 System: A gene-editing tool that can target specific DNA sites for cleavage, used to knockout lncRNAs and explore their function (e.g., TTTY15 knockout in prostate cancer, NEAT1 knockout in glioblastoma). Therapeutic effects are currently limited to basic research due to unpredictable clinical risks and off-target effects [ , ].
 Small-Molecule Compounds: Can bind to or block specific lncRNA structures, disrupting their spatial structure or lncRNA-protein interactions (e.g., ellipticine inhibiting BDNF-AS–EZH2 and HOTAIR–EZH2 interaction, DPFs targeting MALAT1 triple helix). Advantages include lower cost and convenient administration, but research is limited [ ].
 LncRNA Regulators from Natural Plants: Plant-derived compounds (e.g., Resveratrol, Curcumin) have shown regulatory effects on lncRNAs in disease models [ , ].
 Delivery Methods: Crucial for lncRNA drugs, requiring specificity, stability, cell permeability, and low immunogenicity.◦ Exogenous Vectors: Viral vectors (lentiviral, adenoviral) offer high transfection efficiency but have safety concerns. Non-viral vectors like liposomes and lipid nanoparticles (NPs) are promising, often surface-modified for targeted delivery (e.g., RGD-PEG-ECO/siRNA DNCR NPs, ASO-gold-TAT NPs for nuclear MALAT1) [ ].
◦ Endogenous Vectors: Exosomes: Naturally secreted membrane-bound vesicles that can carry lncRNAs. They offer lower immunogenicity and higher stability in vivo and are promising for individualized drug delivery, though purification and stability remain challenges [ ].

A critical challenge in OC therapy is the development of resistance, not only to chemotherapy but also to targeted agents like bevacizumab. Several of the lncRNAs detailed in Table are key drivers of this phenomenon. For instance, the overexpression of HOTAIR, MALAT1, and PVT1 has been directly linked to cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, a major therapeutic hurdle [ , , ]. While direct clinical evidence linking these specific lncRNAs to bevacizumab resistance is still emerging, their established roles in promoting chemoresistance and regulating pro-angiogenic pathways suggest they are strong candidates for mediating resistance to anti-VEGF therapies. Targeting these lncRNAs could therefore represent a powerful strategy to re-sensitize resistant tumors.

## Clinical translatability and delivery challenges in EOC
The preclinical progress in lncRNA targeting, utilizing strategies such as siRNAs, ASOs, CRISPR/Cas9, and small molecules, is indeed promising [ ]. However, significant hurdles remain before clinical translation, most notably the challenge of achieving specific and efficient delivery while avoiding off-target effects. These delivery challenges are particularly formidable in the context of epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), given its unique pattern of peritoneal dissemination.
Systemically delivered agents must overcome the diluting effect of malignant ascites and achieve sufficient penetration into dense, often poorly vascularized tumor nodules scattered throughout the peritoneum. To address this, EOC-specific strategies such as direct intraperitoneal (IP) delivery or the use of advanced nanoparticles engineered to target EOC cells (e.g., via folate receptor alpha) are critical areas of research [ , ]. While no lncRNA-based therapeutic has yet entered clinical trials, and the complexity of lncRNA structures demands sophisticated validation, overcoming these delivery barriers holds immense potential for revolutionizing EOC treatment [ ] (Fig. ).

 

## Synergistic therapeutic approaches: combining LncRNA and VEGF targeting to overcome resistance
Given the interconnectedness of lncRNAs and VEGF in OC pathogenesis and resistance, combining strategies that target both pathways holds immense therapeutic potential (Table  ).

 Rationale: Targeting a single pathway (e.g., VEGF) can lead to compensatory activation of alternative pathways, often modulated by lncRNAs. A dual-targeting approach could block these escape routes [ ].
potential strategies:◦ LncRNA inhibition to sensitize to anti-VEGF: Inhibiting oncogenic lncRNAs that promote VEGF expression (e.g., HOTTIP, circRhoC) or those that drive alternative angiogenic pathways could enhance the efficacy of anti-VEGF agents.
◦Addressing lncRNA-mediated resistance: While direct preclinical evidence of lncRNA modulation overcoming anti-VEGF resistance specifically in EOC models is still an emerging field, proof-of-concept exists in other cancers. For example, in glioblastoma, upregulation of the lncRNA XIST was shown to confer resistance to bevacizumab by sponging miR-137, thereby promoting an aggressive phenotype [ , ]. Targeting the XIST/miR-137 axis restored sensitivity to anti-angiogenic therapy in preclinical models [ ]. Validating similar lncRNA-driven resistance axes in EOC, particularly those that control the compensatory activation of factors like FGF or PDGF under VEGF blockade, is a critical next step and represents a promising avenue for developing effective combination therapies.
◦ Combination with other therapies: Integrating lncRNA-targeted therapies with anti-VEGF agents and conventional chemotherapy or PARP inhibitors (as seen with bevacizumab + PARPi combinations) could lead to more profound and durable responses [ , ].
◦ Challenges and Future Directions: Developing effective combination therapies requires a deep understanding of the specific lncRNA-VEGF axes active in individual tumors, necessitating advanced bioinformatics and omics approaches, including single-cell sequencing, to unravel tumor heterogeneity and identify patient-specific biomarkers [ ].

Beyond their role as therapeutic targets, lncRNAs hold immense promise as predictive biomarkers to guide personalized treatment strategies. A patient’s lncRNA expression profile could potentially predict their response to anti-VEGF therapy, allowing for better patient stratification [ , ]. For example, in other malignancies such as glioblastoma, high expression of the lncRNA HOTAIR has been correlated with a poor response and acquired resistance to bevacizumab by promoting an alternative, VEGF-independent angiogenic program [ – ]. Although this specific link is yet to be fully established in ovarian cancer, it highlights a critical area for future investigation. Identifying a panel of lncRNAs that predict sensitivity or resistance to anti-angiogenic agents could revolutionize OC management, enabling clinicians to select the most effective treatment combinations from the outset and sparing predicted non-responders from ineffective therapies and their associated toxicities [ , , ].

## Conclusions and future directions
Epithelial ovarian cancer remains a formidable challenge, driven by a complex interplay of molecular pathways that foster angiogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. This review has synthesized the pivotal and interconnected roles of long non-coding RNAs and VEGF, highlighting how their crosstalk, particularly within the hypoxic peritoneal niche, orchestrates the aggressive biology of EOC. While anti-VEGF therapies have provided clinical benefit, adaptive resistance remains a major barrier. The emerging field of lncRNA therapeutics offers a promising path to dismantle these resistance mechanisms and unlock more durable responses.
Looking forward, translating these complex molecular insights into clinical benefit requires a multi-pronged approach that leverages cutting-edge technologies and innovative therapeutic strategies:

 Functional Genomic Mapping: A crucial first step is to systematically map the functional landscape. High-throughput CRISPR-based screening technologies can be employed to define the complete network of lncRNAs that drive angiogenesis and resistance, moving beyond single-gene studies to a systems-level understanding.
 Rational Combination Therapies: This deeper understanding will enable the rational design of synergistic therapeutic combinations. For instance, based on preclinical data, a promising strategy would be to combine bevacizumab with a targeted inhibitor of the lncRNA HOTTIP to simultaneously block VEGF and dismantle the hypoxia-induced feedback loop that drives resistance. While clinical trials specifically combining anti-angiogenics with lncRNA-targeting agents in EOC have yet to be initiated, early-phase trials using RNA-based therapeutics for other targets are paving the way [ , ].
 Advanced Computational and Omics Approaches: Identifying the most relevant lncRNA targets and predicting patient response will be greatly accelerated by Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML). Advanced algorithms can analyze multi-omics data to predict lncRNA function, patient-specific vulnerabilities, and potential resistance trajectories, thereby guiding personalized treatment selection [ , ]. Furthermore, multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics will be essential for mapping the lncRNA-VEGF crosstalk directly within the peritoneal tumor niche. This will allow us to understand how these networks shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment and identify novel co-targetable pathways to restore anti-tumor immunity [ – ].
 Innovations in Therapeutic Delivery: The clinical success of these strategies hinges on overcoming delivery barriers. Future innovations will focus on engineering tumor-selective nanocarriers (e.g., lipid nanoparticles, exosomes) designed to penetrate peritoneal nodules and deliver lncRNA-based payloads (like ASOs or siRNAs) with high precision, maximizing efficacy while minimizing off-target effects.

In summary, the integration of functional genomics, computational biology, and advanced drug delivery holds the key to unlocking the therapeutic potential of the lncRNA-VEGF axis, offering new hope for patients with epithelial ovarian cancer.
