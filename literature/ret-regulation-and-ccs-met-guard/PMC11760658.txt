## Abstract

## Background
Pediatric pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) is an exceptionally rare and poorly understood malignancy with a challenging prognosis. Its clinical presentation is often atypical, and standardized treatment guidelines are currently unavailable. While genetic alterations in adult PACC have been studied to some extent, knowledge of genetic abnormalities in pediatric cases remains limited.

## Case presentation
We report a case of pediatric PACC in a 7-year-old male presenting with a large, non-tender abdominal mass (11 cm x 11 cm) on the right side. Pathological and imaging evidence confirmed the diagnosis of PACC, with no lymph node infiltration or distant metastasis. Comprehensive genomic profiling by next-generation sequencing identified a non-canonical BRAF fusion with KMT2C at the DNA level and a classic SND1-BRAF fusion at the RNA level. The patient underwent surgical resection through a Whipple operation followed by six cycles of mFOLIRINOX chemotherapy and radiation therapy, achieving a favorable outcome up to now.

## Conclusions
Next-generation sequencing has demonstrated significant value in identifying genetic fusions in pediatric PACC. In our case report, we identified both the classical SND1-BRAF fusion, commonly associated with PACC, and a previously unreported nonclassical BRAF-KMT2C fusion. These findings underscore the critical role of BRAF alterations as key drivers of oncogenesis in PACC. A multidisciplinary treatment strategy integrating surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy offers a promising precedent for improving therapeutic outcomes and prolonging survival in pediatric PACC cases.

## Introduction
Pancreatic cancer poses significant clinical challenges due to its aggressive nature and poor prognosis [ ]. Despite advances in cancer treatment, high mortality rates persist, largely attributed to challenges in early detection and widespread drug resistance [ ]. Notably, pediatric pancreatic malignancies are exceedingly rare, comprising only 0.6–0.8% of all childhood tumors [ ]. Among these, pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) is particularly uncommon, constituting only 7–15% of all exocrine pancreatic tumors in pediatric population [ ]. PACC originates from pancreatic follicular cells and terminal branching pancreatic ducts [ ], with its first documented report dating back to 1908 by the Japanese scholar Berne [ ].
PACC is characterized by diverse genetic and molecular alterations, contributing to its marked heterogeneity [ ]. These genetic abnormalities define distinct molecular subtypes of PACC, each with unique clinical and pathological features that pose challenges for accurate diagnosis and optimal therapeutic management [ ]. Targeting specific vulnerabilities associated with these molecular subtypes holds promise for improving treatment outcomes. However, research into pediatric pancreatic cancer remains limited, primarily consisting of registry data, case reports, or bioinformatics analyses using public databases [ , ]. Consequently, the pathogenesis, particularly the genetic alterations and molecular subtypes of pediatric PACC, remains poorly understood.
In this report, we present a case of pediatric PACC characterized by specific fusions and review recent advancements in understanding its clinical characteristics, pathogenesis, and therapeutic strategies. This article aims to provide deeper insights into the management of this rare and challenging malignancy.

## Case presentation

## Patient information and clinical findings
A 7-year-old Chinese male child was admitted to our hospital presenting with an abdominal mass for a week. The child was delivered naturally at full term, with a personal history indicating a balanced diet. The patient had no significant medical or family history, no history of trauma, or allergies, and lived in a favorable environment.
Upon physical examination, a non-tender mass measuring approximately 11 cm x 11 cm was palpable on the right side of the abdomen, with fixed localization. There were no signs of jaundice, lymph node enlargement, or other notable indicators. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a solid mass located on the right side. Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed an abnormal mass in the pancreatic head, accompanied by mild dilation of the pancreatic ducts, while its anatomical location was independent to the superior mesenteric artery or vein (SMA/SMV). Compression effects were noted on the choledochal ducts, with slight dilation of the right renal pelvis and a small amount of pelvic effusion (Fig.  A).
 The preoperative serum AFP level was abnormally elevated at 23.3 ng/ml, while other tumor markers, including CA125, CA199, and CEA, remained within physiological serum levels. Cardiac ultrasound, electrocardiograms, peripheral blood routine, amylase, liver, and kidney function tests yielded normal results. Subsequently, surgical resection was performed on the child.

## Surgical treatment and diagnostic assessment
In this case, surgical exploration detected a large intra-abdominal mass. The tumor was located in the posterolateral aspect of the duodenum, with its lower edge pressing against the transverse colon. The common bile duct traveled slightly lateral to the surface of the tumor, about 4 cm from the duodenum, accompanied by the superior duodenal artery. Additionally, the inferior pancreaticoduodenal vein was observed on the medial side of the duodenum, converging into the SMV, supporting that the tumor did not encase the SMA/SMV. Consequently, the patient underwent a Whipple operation including radical pancreaticoduodenectomy, partial gastrectomy, cholecystectomy, biliary drainage, and percutaneous abdominal drainage under general anesthesia. The resected tumor measured approximately 11.2 × 11.0 × 10.8 cm featuring a well-demarcated, consistent with the preoperative ultrasound findings. It was predominantly lobulated and displayed a dense surface vascular network (Figure ). The surgery lasted six and a half hours, with approximately 50 ml of intraoperative bleeding. No blood transfusion was required, and the urine output was about 800 ml. Based on the pre-operative examination results, it was determined that there was no regional lymph node metastasis or swelling in this child. Therefore, deep lymph node dissection, such as D2, was conservatively not performed.
Postoperatively, the patient received anti-infective agents, hemostatic therapy, rehydration, acid suppression, and supportive care including maintaining abdominal drainage unobstructed. Biopsy results confirmed a malignant mass originating from the pancreatic head follicle, with tumor components detected at the incision margins.
Immunohistochemistry revealed positive staining for BCL10 and CD10, membrane positivity for β-catenin and positive staining for E-cadherin. Partial positivity was noted for CgA and CK19, while staining was negative for Vim, PR, Syn, CK7, AFP (with high background), glypican-3, PAX-8, CD56, NSE, and EMA. Additionally, PAS staining was positive (Fig.  B-C). Histological examination of the gallbladder and duodenal tissues indicated chronic inflammatory changes with submucosal edema and no tumor involvement.
Positron emission tomography/CT revealed no evidence of nearby lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis. According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM staging system for pancreatic cancer [ ], the patient was diagnosed with PACC classified as T3N0M0, stage IIA.

## Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified a previously unreported BRAF-KMT2C fusion at the DNA level and a classic SND1-BRAF fusion at the RNA level
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was conducted on day 1 post-surgery to elucidate the molecular pathology. The analysis identified a novel gene fusion located on chromosome 7, where BRAF exon 8 (NM_004333.6) was found to be fused in-frame with KMT2C exon 7 (NM_170606.3) at a frequency of 27.06% (Fig.  A). NGS profile further confirmed that the fusion gene indeed involved BRAF exon 8 and KMT2C exon 7 (Fig.  B). No other pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected. Additional investigations revealed a low mutational burden in the tumor (0.0 mutations/megabase). Copy number alterations were absent, and the microsatellite status was stable, with no mutations detected in mismatch repair related genes. To investigate potential actionable pathogenic fusions in this case, bulk RNA sequencing was also conducted. The analysis revealed a transcript harboring an in-frame fusion between staphylococcal nuclease domain-containing protein 1 (SND1) exon 9 (NM_014390.4) and BRAF exon 9 (NM_004333.6) at a frequency of 4.28% (Fig.  A). The fusion junction was noted to exclude the upstream autoinhibitory region that normally regulates BRAF signaling (Fig.  B).
 

## Therapeutic intervention
Following surgery, to be protected from relapse, the patient completed six cycles of chemotherapy treatment with modified FOLFIRINOX (mFOLIRINOX) regimen (85 mg/m2 oxaliplatin, 150 mg/m2 irinotecan, and 400 mg/m2 leucovorin on day 1, and 2400 mg/m2 fluorouracil infusion continued for 46 h), administered every 2 weeks. Post-chemotherapy, the child experienced myelosuppression and leukocyte depletion, which were successfully managed with leukocyte-boosting treatment.
After the 4th cycle of mFOLIRINOX, abdominal MRI showed an increase in the size and number of retroperitoneal and mesenteric lymph nodes. Given their anatomical location, the child’s parents opted against biopsy of the enlarged lymph nodes due to the invasiveness of the procedure. Therefore, histological confirmation of recurrence was not obtained, leaving the diagnosis of recurrence uncertain. After thorough discussions with the parents and radiologist, radiation therapy was recommended. The radiation therapy field encompassed the preoperative tumor beds and retroperitoneal lymph node drainage. Due to the tumor’s large size and intact periphery, complete resection and intestinal reset were deemed necessary. The radiation therapy plans followed guidelines for pancreatic head cancer (DT45Gy/25F-5WK). Although no enlarged lymph nodes were noted preoperatively, retroperitoneal lymph nodes were identified during postoperative follow-up, warranting prophylactic radiation (DT50Gy/25F-5WK).
The patient received a total of 18 radiation therapy sessions in combination with chemotherapy (albumin-bound paclitaxel 200 mg) and deep thermal therapy to consolidate the effects of pre-treatments. The timeline of the child’s diagnosis and treatments is depicted in Fig.  .
 

## Follow-up and outcomes
Throughout the treatment period, the child underwent monthly physical examinations, laboratory tests (including routine blood tests, and assessment of liver and kidney function), as well as imaging studies ( cardiac ultrasound, abdominal CT scans, and MRI). These evaluations consistently showed no significant abnormalities.
Nearly two years post-surgery, the child remains alive and thriving, with no evidence of recurrence. However, in the unlikely event of a future relapse, an aggressive treatment strategy combining chemotherapy with a BRAF inhibitor may be considered. Regular follow-ups and periodic checkups remain essential to ensure the child’s continued health and well-being.

## Discussion

## Clinical features of pediatric PACC
Pediatric PACC typically manifests when a large abdominal mass (tumor diameter > 6 cm) is discovered, often without specific clinical signs or abnormal laboratory findings. Common symptoms at presentation include abdominal pain, distension, jaundice, nausea, and vomiting [ ]. Therefore, thorough abdominal examination and imaging studies—such as ultrasound, CT, and MRI—are crucial for initial diagnosis [ ]. On CT scans, PACC appears as an enhancing, predominantly oval, solid pancreatic mass typically without bile duct dilation, lymph node enlargement, or involvement of adjacent organs, distinguishing it from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [ ]. MRI is preferred for evaluating tumor margins, intra-tumoral hemorrhage, tissue invasion, and ductal dilation compared to CT [ ].
Diagnosis usually relies on the morphology and immunohistochemistry of adenoidal cell carcinoma cells, guiding subsequent treatment and prognosis for affected children [ ]. Histopathologically, PACC is characterized by relatively uniform tumor cells arranged in clusters or clumps. These cells typically exhibit round or oval shapes with moderately granular amphipathic cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and positive immunohistochemical staining for α-AT, α-ACT, CK, while testing negative for CgA and Syn [ ]. Notably, tumor markers CA19-9 and exocrine enzymes are generally not significantly elevated in pediatric PACC [ ]. The case at hand underscores these distinctive clinical features and diagnostic considerations.

## NGS analysis of the patient
NGS analysis of this patient revealed a novel BRAF-KMT2C fusion at the DNA level, which has not been previously reported. We are intrigued by this unique fusion and its potential role in the pathogenesis of PACC—whether it promotes, inhibits, or has no impact on tumor biology. However, the absence of a PACC cell line poses significant challenges for constructing a PACC model harboring the BRAF-KMT2C fusion, limiting our ability to conclusively assess its biological significance in pediatric PACC. A 2020 study in Korea analyzed 13,698 gene fusions via DNA-based NGS, classifying 28% as gene-gene region fusions, 34% as intergenic region fusions, and 38% as conventional intergenic fusions [ ]. This indicates that DNA-based NGS often cannot fully predict actual splicing patterns at the RNA level due to factors such as chromosomal fragmentation or complex RNA splicing of fusions [ ]. Thus, determining the functional implications of the BRAF-KMT2C fusion requires creating in vivo or in vitro PACC models to study its role in tumor biology.
At the RNA level, a classic SND1-BRAF fusion was identified in this patient, a finding rarely reported in pediatric populations. This case, combined with prior research, underscores the importance of BRAF fusions in the tumorigenesis of PACC and other cancers. In a study of 49 PACC cases (median age 59; range 15–80 years), BRAF gene rearrangements were identified in 20% (12/49) of cases, with SND1 as the most frequent fusion partner (50%), followed by HERPUD1 (18%) [ ]. These fusions typically retain exons 9–18 of the BRAF gene, preserving the BRAF kinase domain, which leads to constitutive activation of the BRAF kinase and downstream MAPK pathway activation, driving tumorigenesis [ ]. A separate study identified five recurrent variants of SND1-BRAF fusions in six out of 44 pancreatic follicular carcinoma patients, showing that the induced MAPK pathway activation could be effectively suppressed using the MEK inhibitor trametinib [ ].
Interestingly, this patient harbored two distinct BRAF fusions at the DNA and RNA levels, suggesting several possible mechanisms: (i) Alternative splicing: A single complex rearrangement could generate distinct splice variants, resulting in different fusions depending on transcription and splicing patterns. (ii) Subclonal variability: Tumor heterogeneity may result in some cells expressing the BRAF-KMT2C fusion, while others express the SND1-BRAF fusion. (iii) Transcriptional regulation differences: The presence of the SND1-BRAF fusion at the RNA level may indicate a functional preference within the tumor, with SND1-BRAF fusion mRNA being more stable or actively transcribed. It must be pointed out that third-generation sequencing, which directly reads DNA or RNA base information with read lengths reaching thousands of base pairs, could more accurately elucidate such gene fusion events. However, its high cost compared to second-generation sequencing remains a practical limitation in clinical settings.

## Other genetic abnormalities in pediatric and adult PACC
Based on this case, we reviewed additional genetic abnormalities in adult and pediatric PACC to explore distinctions between the two (Table  ). Molecular profiling has identified several genetic alterations associated with adult PACC, including SMAD4, TP53, RB1, MEN1, and JAK-1, some of which are actionable targets for existing therapies [ ]. Mutations in TP53 were detected in 13% of adult PACC cases, with TP53 deletion observed in 53%, often accompanied by promoter hypermethylation [ ]. Additionally, approximately 22% of adult PACC patients exhibit deficiencies in BRCA1/2, along with mutations in these genes [ ]. Genomic loss-of-heterozygosity in PACC often correlates with mutations in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 [ ]. In pediatric PACC, a notable case involved a BRAF (V600E) mutation, which responded well to a combination of BRAF and MEK inhibitors, resulting in a durable response [ ]. AGAP3 was identified as a novel BRAF fusion partner in pediatric PACC, establishing a molecular link between adult and pediatric cases [ ]. Recently, a novel SEC31A-BRAF fusion gene and amplification of 18p has been reported in pediatric PACC [ ]. In contrast, RAF1 gene rearrangements are relatively common in older PACC, observed in 18.5% of cases among a cohort of 25, detectable by FISH and subsequent molecular assays [ ]. A study also found RET gene rearrangements were found in 7.5% of adult PACC cases (3 out of 36) [ ]. Additionally, a case report described a SEL1L-NTRK1 fusion in an adult PACC patient, which was treated effectively with arotrectinib, an NTRK inhibitor, resulting in a favorable response [ ].
 It is important to note that the genetic profiles of pediatric and adult PACC differ significantly, underscoring the importance of further research into pediatric-specific genetic abnormalities. Reports on pediatric PACC are scarce, likely due to the lower mutation burden in pediatric tumors and limited case reports. Notably, some studies combine PACC cases across a wide age range (e.g., 7–79 years), lumping pediatric and adult PACC molecular characteristics together. Therefore, we advocate for increased clinical case reports of pediatric PACC with diverse genetic variants, as illustrated in this study. Such efforts will provide crucial insights into the distinct molecular drivers of pediatric PACC and guide the development of targeted therapies.

## Treatment strategy and prognosis for pediatric PACC
Despite genetic differences between adults and children, the overall treatment principles for PACC in both groups are largely similar. The cornerstone of treatment for pediatric PACC is surgical resection of the tumor. Evidence indicates that complete tumor removal leads to significantly better prognosis compared to non-surgical approaches [ ]. Surgical strategy depends on factors such as tumor location, size, and invasiveness. Pancreatic tumors in children are typically well-defined and non-invasive lesions [ ]. Common surgical procedures include pancreaticoduodenectomy and distal pancreatectomy, focusing on maintaining gastrointestinal continuity, achieving precise surgery with minimal bleeding, and preserving normal pancreatic tissue. Tumor stage is a crucial factor in determining the treatment strategy for pediatric patients. We propose that administering 6 cycles of chemotherapy following complete tumor resection to reduce recurrence. Additional cycles of chemotherapy or targeted therapy should be considered if the patient presents with other poor prognostic factors, such as a tumor size greater than 10 cm, elevated serum lipase levels, or the presence of malignant genetic mutations [ ]. Accumulated evidences have proved that chemotherapy FOLFIRINOX are effective to control PACC progress in surgical or non-surgical patients, even in recurrent patients [ , ]. Whereas, the side effects associated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, such as mFOLFIRINOX, in pediatric patients are necessary concerns for pediatrician. One of the primary issues is the high incidence of gastrointestinal reactions, which must be carefully differentiated based on the specific agents [ ]. Oxaliplatin and fluorouracil typically cause symptoms like nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite, while irinotecan tends to cause diarrhea within the first 24 h of administration. This diarrhea is characterized by an increased frequency of stools, as well as changes in stool consistency. However, this form of diarrhea is dose-limiting toxicity and does not accumulate with repeated dosing. The second most common toxic effect is bone marrow suppression, leading to a decrease in peripheral blood leukocytes [ ]. This typically reaches its lowest point within 2 to 3 weeks after the regimen begins and generally returns to normal after 3 to 4 weeks. In our case, the patient experienced both toxicities above during the chemotherapy. With supportive therapies, the child successfully navigated through this period of toxicity.
Notably, PACC exhibits significant genetic intertumoral heterogeneity, involving multiple pathways that suggest the potential for targeted therapies. We have reviewed these specific genetic alterations and corresponding therapeutic approaches as follows: (i) BRCA1/2 mutations [ ] and RAF1 gene rearrangements [ ] are relatively common in PACC. These mutations contribute to the inactivation of DNA repair genes and genomic instability in tumors, making them potentially responsive to platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents and PARP inhibitors [ ]. (ii) BRAF alterations are critical genetic alterations that promote PACC progression by abnormally activating the MAPK pathway. Thus, BRAF inhibitors (e.g., dabrafenib) or MEK inhibitors (e.g., trametinib) or the combination of both two drugs may be effective to overcome the acquired resistance [ , ]. (iii) RET gene rearrangements [ ] leads to constitutive activation of the RET kinase domain, promoting tumor cell growth, proliferation, and survival. Selpercatinib and pralsetinib, selective RET inhibitors, have shown significant clinical benefits in clinical trials. (iiii) PACC harboring NTRK gene fusions may respond to TRK inhibitors such as larotrectinib or entrectinib [ ]. (iiiii) Other genetic alterations, such as KRAS mutations, which are common in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma but less frequent in PACC, can be targeted by drugs that inhibit downstream signaling pathways including MEK inhibitors and ERK inhibitors [ ]. Additionally, if ALK or ROS1 fusions are present, targeted therapies such as ALK inhibitors (e.g., crizotinib) or ROS1 inhibitors can be used [ , ].
In addition, deep thermal therapy has emerged as a promising adjunctive treatment for pancreatic cancer [ ]. It works by raising the temperature of the cancerous tissue to between 40 and 45 °C, which can induce several biological effects that are beneficial in cancer treatment, such as increased blood flow for improving the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, enhanced oxygenation within the tumor for sensitizing cancer cells to radiation therapy, direct cytotoxicity to kill cancer cells without harming surrounding healthy tissues, and improve the anti-tumor immunomodulation [ , ]. Several clinical studies and trials have investigated that the combination of hyperthermia with chemotherapy or radiation therapy can improve response rates and achieve better outcomes in pancreatic cancer [ , ]. Additionally, for advanced pancreatic cancer, hyperthermia can be used to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life by reducing tumor size and associated pain [ ]. Ongoing research and clinical trials will continue to refine its application and establish its role in the standard care, making it a valuable addition to the multimodal treatment approach for pancreatic cancer.
Despite the various treatments mentioned above, the prognosis for PACC remains poor. In adults, the average survival time following definitive diagnosis is reported to be 22 months [ ]. While cohort study data for pediatric PACC is lacking, case reports suggest that pediatric PACC generally has a better prognosis compared to adult cases, with a higher mean survival time. This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher levels of chromosomal instability seen in adult PACC, a feature not observed in the pediatric cases included in this study, which lacked chromosomal imbalances associated with fusion genes [ ].
Overall, surgery remains the most common and effective treatment for pediatric PACC. Radical resection should be pursued, particularly in early-stage patients, with tail and splenectomy for tumors located in the body of the pancreas, and pancreaticoduodenectomy for those located in the head. Postoperative combination chemotherapy is recommended; however, there is currently no prospective data to guide chemotherapy decisions, and more relevant studies are required in the future. Furthermore, progress in targeted therapy for PACC has been developed. While there is some research on genetic abnormalities such as BRAF, BRCA2 mutations and the clinically approved drugs, targeted therapy for pediatric PACC holds potential but still requires extensive investigation to be fully realized.

## Conclusions

## Patient perspective
When our child was diagnosed with a rare form of pancreatic cancer, we were deeply concerned and overwhelmed. However, we are immensely grateful to the doctors and staff at Tongji Pediatrics for their unwavering support throughout the treatment process. Their exceptional dedication ensured our child underwent therapy smoothly, with regular monthly follow-ups that provided crucial reassurance. Today, our child is thriving at school and enjoying good health. We share our story in the hope that it offers inspiration and encouragement to physicians and families facing similar challenges worldwide.
