## Abstract

## Objective
SMARCA4-deficient thoracic tumors (SDTTs) mainly include SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated thoracic tumor (SMARCA4-dUT) and SMARCA4-deficient non–small cell lung cancer (SMARCA4-dNSCLC). Comparative studies between these subtypes remain limited, and no standardized therapeutic guidelines have been established.

## Design
This study retrospectively analyzed 102 patients with SDTT from January 2020 to May 2024. We evaluated the efficacy of different treatments for SDTT and compared the clinicopathological features and prognostic outcomes between SD-UT and SD-NSCLC.

## Results
SDTT predominantly occurs in older male smokers. SMARCA4-dUT exhibits high NSE expression, higher risk of lymph node metastases, and lower rates of early diagnosis than SMARCA4-dNSCLC. SMARCA4-dNSCLC can be distinguished from SMARCA4-dUT by gland architecture and the diffuse expression of epithelial markers, whereas SMARCA4-dUT shows positive expression for Syn and stem cell markers. Common mutations include TP53, SMARCA4, KRAS, and STK11, with STK11/KRAS co-mutations associated with poorer prognosis. SMARCA4 protein loss was observed not only in class I mutations but also in class II mutations and wild-type cases. Both SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC carry a high recurrence risk after radical treatment. Immune-based therapy significantly improved median progression-free survival (mPFS) and overall survival (mOS) compared with non-immunotherapy (mPFS, 8.6 vs. 3.3 months, P < .0001; mOS, 13.1 vs. 8.0 months, P = .048). Central nervous system metastasis, pleura metastasis, and primary tumor resection were independent factors for OS.

## Conclusions
This study provides a comprehensive analysis of SDTT to date, based on a relatively large sample size. STK11/KRAS co-mutations are associated with poorer prognosis. SMARCA4-dNSCLC can be distinguished from SMARCA4-dUT by epithelial markers and stem cell markers. Immune-based therapy is the optimal option in the current treatment strategy.

Implications for PracticeSMARCA4-deficient thoracic tumors (SDTTs) are rare malignancies, and no standard guidelines have been established for its treatment. Owing to the lack of prospective studies, clinical evidence primarily relies on real-world data. This study presents a comprehensive analysis of SDTT and highlights the clinical and pathological differences between SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Immune-based therapy demonstrated significant survival benefits, providing essential evidence to guide treatment strategies for this aggressive malignancy.

## Introduction
The switch/sucrose nonfermentable (SWI/SNF) complex, an ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling unit, is pivotal in transcriptional regulation and DNA damage repair. SWI/SNF complex variations are closely associated with tumorigenesis.  SMARCA4 is a tumor suppressor gene that encodes the transcriptional activator BRG1. , SMARCA4/BRG1 is an ATPase subunit of the SWI/SNF complex ( ). Aberrant SMARCA4 mutations and protein expression have been identified in various cancers. In thoracic tumors, approximately 8% of non–small cell lung carcinomas (NSCLC) harbor SMARCA4 mutations, whereas 5%-10% exhibit SMARCA4/BRG1 protein deficiency. , Notably, SMARCA4 mutations were classified into two groups based on the type of genomic abnormality: class 1 mutations included nonsense, frameshift, and truncating mutations, gene fusions, and homozygous deletions. Class 2 mutations included all missense mutations and other variants of unknown significance. SMARCA4 protein loss was significantly associated with class I mutations, whereas class II mutations usually do not lead to SMARCA4 protein deficiency. , Currently, SMARCA4-deficient thoracic tumors (SDTTs) are diagnosed mainly based on immunohistochemistry (IHC) results, specifically, SMARCA4/BRG1 protein loss.
 SDTTs are mainly classified into SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated thoracic tumor (SMARCA4-dUT) and SMARCA4-­deficient non–small cell lung cancer (SMARCA4-dNSCLC). SMARCA4-dUT is a newly recognized entity in the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of ­Thoracic Tumors (2021). This newly added entity was initially identified as “SMARCA4-deficient thoracic sarcoma” by Le Loarer et al. Nevertheless, accumulating molecular evidence suggests its close genomic relationship with NSCLC, and most examples are thus increasingly reinterpreted as dedifferentiated/undifferentiated lung carcinoma. This has led to the decision that the entity be renamed and placed under the category of “other epithelial tumors of the lung.” Nonetheless, the WHO classification recognizes SMARCA4-dUT as a separate entity from conventional SMARCA4-deficient NSCLC because of distinct phenotype. 
SMARCA4-dUT originates from epithelial cells. It predominantly affects young to middle-aged adults and is typically located in the mediastinum, hilum, lungs, or pleura. These tumors often present as large masses, causing symptoms related to tumor compression. Histologically, SMARCA4-dUT exhibits dedifferentiated or undifferentiated characteristics, with the loss of typical epithelial structures, such as glandular or squamous differentiation. The tumor cells exhibit rich cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. SMARCA4-dNSCLC is a rare primary lung malignancy arises from the bronchial mucosa, bronchial glands, and alveolar epithelium. It predominantly occurs in the lung parenchyma and exhibits a higher degree of differentiation, commonly presenting morphological features of either adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma. Immunohistochemically, SMARCA4-dUT exhibits negative or focal epithelial marker expression, whereas SMARCA4-dNSCLC demonstrates strong/diffuse positivity. , 
Currently, early-stage SDTT remains extremely under-researched. This is primarily due to the highly aggressive behavior and poor prognosis of SDTT, with most cases being diagnosed at advanced stages. Additionally, given the rarity of SDTT, no standard guidelines have been established for the treatment of this condition. , First, SMARCA4 deletion inhibits chemotherapy‐induced apoptosis by disrupting Ca2+ release, thereby limiting the efficacy of chemotherapy. Second, large-scale cohort studies have revealed that epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusions occurred in <5% of patients with SMARCA4 alterations, highlighting extremely limited applicability of targeted therapies in this population. , Third, the efficacy of immunotherapy in SDTT remained controversial. Patients with SDTT often carried a high tumor mutation burden (TMB), which may make them responsive to immunotherapy. In some cases, patients with SMARCA4 deficiency could benefit from PD-1 inhibitors. , Conversely, some cases showed inconclusive results. , According to Gantzer et al., SMARCA4-dUT was characterized by a distinct immune-desert microenvironment, with low immune cell infiltration and limited responsiveness to immunotherapy. 
Therefore, whether patients with SDTT can benefit from existing treatment options is still unclear. Moreover, reports on survival outcomes in operable early-stage SDTT are notably insufficient. Accordingly, this comprehensive retrospective analysis was conducted to further explore survival outcomes and optimal treatment approaches and investigate the clinicopathologic and genomic differences between SMARCA4-­dNSCLC and SMARCA4-dUT.

## Methods

## Study population
A total of 102 patients diagnosed with SDTT by IHC between January 2019 and May 2024 in Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST), were retrospectively included in this study. These patients were diagnosed with SDTT presenting complete absence or diffuse reduction of SMARCA4/BRG1 expression. Two seasoned pathologists independently conducted the pathological diagnosis. The classification of SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC follows the fifth edition of the 2021 WHO Classification of Thoracic Tumors. The flowchart of this study is illustrated in .
 The need for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design of the study. The Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College of HUST, approved this study (No.202412102).

## Data collection
Demographic and clinicopathologic data were collected from the electronic medical records. Additionally, treatment strategies (including surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies) and survival outcomes were analyzed. The TNM staging was conducted following the eighth edition of the Cancer Staging Manual of the American Joint Committee on Cancer. Differentiation and histological subtypes were classified based on the fifth edition of the 2021 WHO Thoracic Tumors Classification. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was evaluated using the DAKO 22C3 assay (positive was defined as ≥1%). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples from eight patients with SMARCA4-dUT and 45 with SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Follow-up details were obtained from medical records and telephone interviews, with a cutoff date of August 15, 2024.

## Efficacy assessment
Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from radical treatment to disease recurrence, metastasis, death, or last follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured from the time of treatment initiation to disease progression, death, or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was estimated as the time from diagnosis to death or last follow-up, whereas OS from first-line treatment was measured from its initiation until death or last follow-up.

## Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test, whereas categorical variables were analyzed with the ­chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with log-rank tests for subgroup comparisons. Additionally, Cox regression analyses were conducted to assess the effect of multiple factors on OS. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.4 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## SMARCA4-dUT exhibits high NSE expression, higher risk of lymph node metastases, lower rates of early diagnosis, and fewer opportunities for radical surgery than SMARCA4-dNSCLC
A total of 102 patients diagnosed with SDTT (SMARCA4-dUT, n = 28; SMARCA4-dNSCLC, n = 74) were included in the study. Most patients were male and had a history of smoking. The median age was 62 (range, 45-79) years in the SMARCA4-dUT group and 63 (range, 44-82) years in the SMARCA4-dNSCLC group. Histologic subtypes of SMARCA4-dNSCLC included adenocarcinoma (66.2%), squamous cell carcinoma (12.2%), mixed (5.4%), sarcomatoid carcinoma (4.1%), large cell carcinoma (2.7%), and not otherwise specified (NOS) (9.4%) ( ). SMARCA4-dUT had significantly higher mean neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels than SMARCA4-dNSCLC (28.37 vs. 18.78 µg/L, P = .01), whereas SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibited markedly high carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels (216.44 vs. 8.45 ng/mL, P < .001). Regarding tumor growth behavior and metastatic patterns, SMARCA4-dUT exhibited larger tumor diameters (55.00 vs. 43.00 mm, P = .056) and a higher incidence of lymph nodes metastases (85.7% vs. 60.8%, P = .016) than SMARCA4-dNSCLC. The distant metastatic pattern of SMARCA4-dUT was similar to that of SMARCA4-dNSCLC, and common metastatic sites included the bone (25.0% vs. 28.4%, P = .733), adrenal gland (21.4% vs. 12.2%, P = .386), central nervous system (CNS, 17.9% vs. 12.2%, P = .672), pleura (14.3% vs. 16.2%, P = .811), liver (14.3% vs. 10.8%, P = .887), and lung (10.7% vs. 10.8%, P = .993). Compared with the SMARCA4-dNSCLC group, more patients in the SMARCA4-dUT group were diagnosed with stage III-IV disease (96.5% vs. 67.5%, P = .01), with fewer opportunities for radical surgery (17.9% vs. 47.3%, P = .007). However, no significant differences were observed in other clinical characteristics between SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC ( and ).
 

## Distinguishing SMARCA4-dNSCLC from SMARCA4-dUT based on pathological features and immunohistochemical markers
Specimens from 102 patients with SDTT were obtained via core needle biopsy (n = 46, 45.1%), endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (n = 16, 15.7%), and pulmonary resection (n = 40, 39.2%).
Among the 74 patients with SMARCA4-dNSCLC, 14 (18.9%) had solid architecture with focal glandular formations, and 11 (14.9%) showed papillary and acinar patterns. Notably, SMARCA4-dUT lacked solid architecture with focal glandular formations (P = .031) and exhibited a higher prevalence of rhabdoid morphology compared with SMARCA4-dNSCLC (32.1% vs. 12.2%, P = .038). Additionally, solid architecture and necrosis were similarly common in both groups (46.4% vs. 66.2% and 60.7% vs. 51.4%, respectively; P > .05) ( ).

 summarizes the immunohistochemical profile of SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Syn (45% vs. 11.1%, P = .006) and stem cell markers SALL4 (50% vs. 6.3%, P = .002) and CD34 (54.5% vs. 15.4%, P = .001) were more frequently expressed in SMARCA4-dUT than in SMARCA4-dNSCLC. SMARCA4-dNSCLC displayed diffuse or strong positive expression of epithelial markers, such as cytokeratin 7 (CK7), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and pan-cytokeratin (PCK). In contrast, SMARCA4-dUT exhibited weaker or only negative expression of these epithelial markers (all P < .05). Of note, a few SDTT cases expressed lung adenocarcinoma markers (napsin aspartic peptidase A [Napsin A] and thyroid transcription factor 1 [TTF-1]) and squamous cell carcinoma markers (p40 and p63). The median Ki-67 index in SMARCA4-dUT was higher than that in SMARCA4-dNSCLC (70% vs. 60%, P < .001), suggesting the greater malignancy and aggressiveness of SMARCA4-dUT.

## CNS metastasis, pleura metastasis, and primary tumor resection are independent factors affecting OS in SDTT
As of August 15, 2024, the median follow-up for 102 patients was 14.4 (13.1-15.7 months). By the end of follow-up, 48% (49/102) of the patients had died, including 16 (57.1%) with SMARCA4-dUT and 33 (44.6%) with SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Both thoracic SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibited poor survival outcomes. The mOS for overall patients was 14.2 (95% confidence interval [CI] 8.554-19.846) months ( , see for a color version of this figure). The mOS of patients with SMARCA4-dUT was shorter than that of patients with SMARCA4-dNSCLC, but no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (9.6 vs. 17.3 months, P = .338, , see for a color version of this figure).
Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the prognostic factors for OS in patients with SDTT. In the univariate analysis, five negative prognostic factors were identified: bone metastasis (hazard ratio [HR] 2.149, 95% CI 1.179-3.914; P = .012), CNS metastasis (HR 3.815, 95% CI 1.899-7.664; P < .001), pulmonary metastasis (HR 2.431, 95% CI 1.133-5.217; P = .023), pleural metastasis (HR 5.028, 95% CI 2.632-9.605; P < .001), and stage IV disease at diagnosis (HR 3.863, 95% CI 2.059-7.247; P < .001) ( ). In contrast, primary tumor resection was identified as a positive prognostic factor (HR 0.259, 95% CI 0.526-1.631; P < .001). Other factors, such as age, smoking history, lymph node metastasis, adrenal gland metastasis, liver metastasis, and the Ki-67 index, did not significantly influence OS (P > .05).
 Variables with P < .05 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate Cox regression, which identified CNS metastasis (HR 2.674, 95% CI 1.226-5.828; P = .013), pleural metastasis (HR 4.308, 95% CI 1.995-9.305; P < .001), and primary tumor resection (HR 0.396, 95% CI 0.164-0.957; P = .04) as independent factors that affect OS. Both CNS and pleural metastases were associated with worse OS, whereas primary tumor resection was favorable ( ).

## Both SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC carry a high recurrence risk following radical treatment, with no significant difference in prognosis
A total of 41 patients received radical treatment, including 38 who underwent radical surgery and 3 who received radical chemoradiotherapy. Among these, 7 patients had SMARCA4-dUT and 34 had SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Overall, 43.9% (18/41) of the patients experienced tumor recurrence, including 4 with SMARCA4-dUT and 14 with SMARCA4-dNSCLC.The most common recurrence sites were regional lymph nodes (6/18) and adrenal gland (6/18), followed by the lung (5/18), bone (4/18), pleura (3/18), brain (3/18), liver (2/18), chest wall (1/18), and abdominal cavity (1/18). Specifically, the adrenal glands are the most frequent initial metastasis site after treatment ( ).
The median DFS (mDFS) for patients with SDTT was 15 (95% CI 8.583-21.417) months, demonstrating a rapid relapse tendency ( , see for a color version of this figure). At the time of last follow-up, the mOS had not been reached among patients receiving radical treatment. ( , see for a color version of this figure). The mDFS was 7.4 months for the SMARCA4-dUT group and 18.5 months for the SMARCA4-dNSCLC group, with no significant difference between them (P = .460). Similar trends were observed for the mOS in both groups ( , see for a color version of this figure).

## Immune-based therapy significantly improves prognosis in both SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC
A total of 55 patients with SDTT received first-line treatment at our center, including 16 with SMARCA4-dUT and 39 with SMARCA4-dNSCLC. PD-L1 expression data were available for 38 patients ( ). In this study, PD-L1 expression was positive (TPS ≥ 1%) in 83.3% (5/6) of patients with SMARCA4-dUT, whereas 65.6% (21/32) of patients with SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibited negative expression (TPS < 1%) ( , see for a color version of this figure).
Among 55 patients receiving first-line treatment, 29 (52.7%) received immune-based therapy; 21 (38.2%) received chemotherapy-based regimens; 3 (5.5%) received targeted therapy; and 2 (3.6%) received anti-angiogenic therapy ( , see for a color version of this figure). After excluding three patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations who received targeted therapy, the remaining 52 patients were divided into the immune-based therapy group (N = 29) and the non-immunotherapy group (N = 23).
Within the immune-based therapy group, 27 patients received immunotherapy plus chemotherapy (IO + C): 16 patients received immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) plus paclitaxel-based regimens (albumin paclitaxel, paclitaxel, or docetaxel), 10 received ICI plus pemetrexed-based chemotherapy, and 1 received ICI plus etoposide-based chemotherapy. Additionally, 1 patient received immunotherapy plus anti-angiogenic therapy (IO + A), and 1 received immunotherapy alone (mono-IO).
In the non-immunotherapy group, 6 patients received chemotherapy plus anti-angiogenic therapy (C + A), 15 received chemotherapy alone (C alone), and 2 received anti-angiogenic therapy alone (A alone). Regarding chemotherapy drugs, 11 (11/21, 52.4%) patients received nab-paclitaxel‌ plus platinum, 8 (8/21, 38.1%) received pemetrexed plus platinum, and 2 (2/21, 9.5%) received gemcitabine plus platinum ( , see for a color version of this figure). In the overall first-line treatment cohort, 19 patients presented with recurrent or locally advanced unresectable ­disease, whereas 36 were initially diagnosed with metastatic disease. Detailed treatment trajectories and clinical information are illustrated in the swimming plots ( , see for a color version of this figure).
Immune-based therapy as first-line treatment significantly prolonged mPFS in patients with SDTT compared with patients with non-immunotherapy (8.6 vs. 3.3 months, P < .0001; ). Subgroup analysis revealed that the SMARCA4-dUT group had a significantly longer mPFS with immune-based therapy versus non-immunotherapy (8.1 vs. 3.0 months, P = .011; ). This trend was consistently observed in the SMARCA4-dNSCLC group (10.4 vs. 3.3 months, P < .001, ). No significant differences were observed in immune combination chemotherapy (paclitaxel-based or pemetrexed-based) regimens ( , see for a color version of this figure).
 Moreover, patients with SDTT receiving first-line immune-based therapy had significantly better mOS than those treated with non-immunotherapy (13.1 vs. 8.0 months, P = .048; ). This survival benefit was consistently observed in both SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC subgroups ( ).

## SDTTs derive significantly greater benefit from first-line immune-based therapy than from non–first-line immunotherapy
A total of 11 patients with SDTTs received at least one line of immune-based therapy during their treatment course. Survival outcomes were compared between patients treated with ICI as first-line and those treated with ICI as non–first-line therapy. Notably, first-line immunotherapy was associated with a significantly longer mPFS (8.6 vs. 5.6 months, P = .039; , see ). A similar trend toward improved mOS was observed; however, it did not achieve significance ( , see for a color version of this figure).

## No significant differences in mPFS and mOS were observed between the SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC groups, regardless of treatment regimens
Regarding prognostic variances, no significant differences in either mPFS or mOS were detected between the SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC groups, regardless of whether treatment was immune-based therapy or non-immunotherapy ( , see for a color version of this figure).

## SDTTs frequently harbor TP53, SMARCA4, KRAS, and STK11 mutations, with STK11/KRAS co-mutations associated with poorer prognosis
Data on genetic characteristics were available in 53 patients with SDTT, including 8 with SMARCA4-dUT and 45 with SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Among all the tested SDTT cases, the most common mutated gene was TP53 (39.1%), followed by SMARCA4 (36%), KRAS (32%), and STK11 (17.8%) ( ). Additionally, patients with SMARCA4 deficiency rarely harbor common actionable oncogenic driver mutations. Three patients with EGFR mutation (an exon 19 deletion, an exon 21 missense mutation, and an exon 18 missense mutation) received first-line EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (almonertinib, gefitinib, and afatinib), with PFS ranging from 3.3 to 10.6 months, including one patient with progression at 5.5 months. Additional driver alterations included two NRAS missense mutations, one ALK missense mutation, one ROS1 missense mutation, one RET frameshift mutation, one MET amplification, and one ERBB2 amplification. The mutational profiles of SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC were similar ( ).
 SMARCA4 sequencing was conducted on 25 patients, and 9 of them had genetic mutations. Importantly, instances of SMARCA4 protein deficiency were also observed in SMARCA4 wild-type cases, indicating that SMARCA4 protein deficiency does not always correlate directly with genetic alterations. Notably, mutation patterns observed in patients with SDTTs included class I mutations (eg, frameshift and nonsense mutations) and class II alterations (eg, missense mutations) ( ), suggesting that protein loss is not merely associated with class I mutations.
The influence of mutated genes on mOS in patients with SDTT was further investigated. Among the 53 patients, 45 underwent NGS testing with a panel of at least 26 genes, in which 17, 14, and 8 harbored TP53, KRAS, and STK11 mutations, respectively. Notably, STK11 mutations consistently co-occurred with KRAS mutations, indicating a strong genetic association. Moreover, patients with STK11 mutations exhibited significantly worse mOS than those without STK11 mutations (9.7 vs. 18.2 months, P = .008) ( ). This conclusion remained consistent in patients with KRAS mutations (9.7 vs. 18.2 months, P = .019) ( ). However, TP53 mutation status did not significantly affect mOS (13.8 vs. 26.5 months, P = .432) ( , see for a color version of this figure).
 In this study, we encountered a 64-year-old male patient with stage IIIA resectable SMARCA4-dNSCLC harboring KRAS G12C/STK11 co-mutations, who exhibited aggressive disease progression ( , see for a color version of this figure). Despite six cycles of postoperative chemotherapy, his disease recurred with adrenal and right lung metastases, with a DFS of 6.9 months. Subsequently, he received sotorasib and local radiotherapy for adrenal ­metastasis; however, he developed PD again with a PFS of 3.0 months, accompanied by multiple lung and liver metastases. Then, the patient was treated with docetaxel, carboplatin, and sintilimab. Unfortunately, he died 1.9 months later, underscoring the therapeutic challenges in SDTTs with KRAS/STK11 co-mutations.

## Discussion
To date, most studies on SDTTs have focused on advanced-stage cases, many of which have been published as individual case reports. The present study provides a comprehensive analysis based on a large cohort, exploring the clinicopathological and molecular characteristics, and prognostic differences between SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Furthermore, recurrence patterns and survival in potentially curable patients with SDTT were investigated, and optimal treatment strategies for locally advanced unresectable or advanced disease were evaluated.
The 2021 WHO Classification of Thoracic Tumors identified SMARCA4-dUT as a distinct entity separate from SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Previously, Lin reported a patient with SMARCA4-dNSCLC at the primary site and SMARCA4-dUT at the metastatic site, suggesting a potential correlation between the two diseases. In the present series, SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC shared some overlapping clinical characteristics. Both thoracic SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC predominantly occurred in older male smokers. However, Perret described SMARCA4-dUT as a highly aggressive tumor primarily affecting younger male patients (median age 48 years). In the present study, adenocarcinoma was the most common subtype of SMARCA4-dNSCLC, whereas large cell carcinoma rarely occurred, consistent with the findings of Zhou et al. In contrast, an Asian patient cohort study predominantly observed SMARCA4 aberrations in large cell carcinoma. These differences may be due to racial and regional variations in patient cohorts.
A previous study reported that SMARCA4-deficient tumor cells exhibit differentially attenuated expression of Napsin A and P40 compared with SMARCA4-intact cells. As tumor cells were hypothesized to progress toward poor differentiation, lineage-specific markers expression gradually diminished. Although the current WHO classification does not recommend routine SMARCA4 IHC testing in lung cancer, increasing evidence shows that this biomarker has significant prognostic implications. The present analysis demonstrates that SDTTs frequently exhibit solid architecture and necrosis, with some showing rhabdoid-like morphology. IHC suggests SMARCA4 deficiency, with low expression of TTF1 and Napsin A. Based on current evidence, we propose that SMARCA4 IHC should be performed when tumor cells lack or display poor epithelial differentiation, exhibit solid architecture, necrosis, and rhabdoid morphology, negative TTF1, Napsin A, and p40 immunostaining, and absence of driver gene mutations. These characteristics may help identify a subset of patients more likely to harbor SMARCA4 deficiency. Moreover, epithelial and stem cell markers exhibit distinct expression profiles that can precisely and efficiently distinguish SMARCA4-dUT from SMARCA4-dNSCLC. Some SMARCA4-dUT shows a neuroendocrine-like phenotype, characterized by significantly high serum NSE levels and positive immunohistochemical staining for Syn.
SDTTs are rare, with most studies primarily focusing on advanced-stage disease. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report mDFS and mOS in patients with SDTTs who underwent radical treatment. Furthermore, prognostic differences between SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC were analyzed. Peter et al. and Enriqueta et al. reported that the mDFS for NSCLC ranged from 28.7 to 53.6 months. , In the present study, 57.1% of patients with SMARCA4-dUT and 32.4% with SMARCA4-dNSCLC experienced recurrence within 1 year after radical treatment. Both SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibited unsatisfactory survival outcomes. These findings indicated that patients with SDTTs had a high recurrence risk after radical treatment, thus optimal perioperative therapeutic strategies warranted further investigation.
Despite the lack of significant prognostic differences between the SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC groups, this lack of significance may be attributed to the limited sample sizes in each group. Nevertheless, these findings provide valuable clinical insights, and further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these results.
Cox multivariate analysis identified CNS metastasis, pleura metastasis, and primary tumor resection as independent factors influencing OS. Both CNS and pleura metastases were associated with poorer OS, whereas primary tumor resection was associated with better OS.
A study showed that some SWI/SNF-deficient tumors are characterized by immune cell infiltration and exhibit responses to immunotherapy in preclinical and clinical models. In contrast, Gantzer et al. have shown that SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer is characterized by the infiltration of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells and neutrophils rather than CD8+T cells, indicating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. , Furthermore, Cao analyzed 9 patients with SMARCA4-dNSCLC treated with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy plus immunotherapy; however, only 1 patient achieved a partial response after 6 cycles of treatment.
In the present cohort, the majority of patients had a smoking history. As reported in the literature, long-term tobacco exposure correlates with increased TMB. Furthermore, SMARCA4 deficiency disrupts DNA mismatch repair, leading to an increase in TMB, which may promote neoantigen formation. These factors may explain why patients with SMARCA4-deficiency could benefit from immune-based therapies in the present study. Differences in treatment regimens may explain the contrasting outcomes between the present study and those reported by Gantzer and Velut. , Their patients received ICI monotherapy, which showed limited survival benefits. In contrast, most patients in the present study received immune-based combination therapies, incorporating chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic treatments. These therapies improve the immune microenvironment and enhance the antitumor response when combined with ICIs.
For the combination regimen, Zhang et al. reported that paclitaxel-based chemoimmunotherapy achieved better mPFS than pemetrexed-based therapy because of SMARCA4 deficiency, leading to the upregulation of thymidylate synthase, which may induce resistance to pemetrexed. However, our subgroup analysis revealed the lack of significant outcome differences between the regimens. This discrepancy may stem from limited subgroup sample sizes, warranting further in-depth exploration.
Previous studies have confirmed that potential biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, TMB, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, are associated with immunotherapy responses in lung cancer. , Takada described a patient with SMARCA4-dUT and PD-L1 overexpression who achieved a partial response after just one cycle of pembrolizumab. Although Zhou et al. reported 6 patients with SMARCA4-dUT who received ICI combination therapy, among whom 4 achieved complete or partial response, despite negative PD-L1 expression in all 4 responders. Owing to the limited number of patients who underwent PD-L1 expression testing, a subgroup analysis of PD-L1 expression and immunotherapy response was not conducted. Further research is needed to identify biomarkers associated with responses to immunotherapy.
This study found that patients with SDTTs harboring STK11 or KRAS mutations were more likely to develop distant metastases. Studies have reported that STK11 mutations are associated with tumor growth, resistance to ICIs, and early mortality. SMARCA4 and KRAS co-mutations were associated with reduced CD8+T cell infiltration in NSCLC tissues. These factors may contribute to the poor prognosis in patients with STK11 and KRAS mutations. , In this study, 70% of the patients exhibited Ki-67 expression ≥60%, indicative of rapid proliferation and high invasiveness in SDTTs, which was significantly linked to an increased risk of recurrence and mortality. 
Gregorys demonstrated that SMARCA4 deficiency occurs exclusively in patients with class I mutations, whereas no deficiency is detected in patients with class II mutations. Inconsistent with these findings, our results indicated that SMARCA4 deficiency also occurred in a subset of class II alterations, although class I mutations remained more prevalent. Class I mutations disrupt full-length protein translation, resulting in the complete loss of protein expression as demonstrated by IHC, whereas class II missense mutations induce minimal protein changes that still allow recognition by the SMARCA4 antibody. 
Furthermore, some patients with SMARCA4 deficiency lack SMARCA4 gene mutations. This may be due to epigenetic alterations (eg, promoter methylation and protein post-translational regulation), microRNA-mediated post-transcriptional repression, or mutations in other SWI/SNF complex subunits such as ARID1A. , Zhang et al. comprehensively analyzed SMARCA4 protein and gene status, revealing that the correlation between protein deficiency and genetic alterations is not completely parallel, with protein deficiency as the key prognostic factor. These findings underscore IHC as the gold standard for diagnosing SMARCA4-deficient tumors.
However, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the findings require validation in further multicenter, large prospective studies. Second, given the limited tumor tissue availability, not all patients underwent complete NGS and immunohistochemical testing, leading to incomplete molecular data and the absence of certain immunohistochemical markers. The relationship between PD-L1 expression, TMB, co-mutation status, and immunotherapy efficacy was not explored through subgroup analysis. Finally, although SDTTs tend to progress rapidly, long-term follow-ups are essential.
In conclusion, this study represents a comprehensive analysis of SDTT, leveraging a relatively large sample size. CNS metastasis, pleura metastasis, and primary tumor resection are independent factors for OS. Combined STK11/KRAS co-mutations are associated with poorer prognosis. SMARCA4-dNSCLC can be distinguished from SMARCA4-dUT by epithelial markers and stem cell markers. Immune-based therapy is the optimal option in the current treatment strategy.
