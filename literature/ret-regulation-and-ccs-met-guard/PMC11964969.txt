## INTRODUCTION
The Asian Society of Gynecologic Oncology (ASGO) held a scientific meeting in 2024 under the theme of “Close the Gap in Gynecologic Cancer Care in Asia.” Continuing the legacy of ASGO meetings in many Asian countries since 2009 [ ], The Indonesian Society of Gynecologic Oncology (INASGO) has been given the honor to host ASGO 2024 meeting in Bali, Indonesia. The meeting was held in Bali Nusa Dua Convention Center, Nusa Dua, Bali from 29th November to 1st December 2024. As the theme stated, this meeting aimed to close the gap in gynecologic cancer care in Asia by providing the latest insights and updates in management of gynecologic cancer across Asia. This meeting was organized as a collaborative meeting with many other professional divisions and departments such as radiation oncology, radiology, pathology, palliative care, nuclear medicine, cancer registration, reproductive gynecology, and medical writing with Journal of Gynecologic Oncology (JGO). The program was designed to be both impactful and enjoyable, as the meeting took place in one of the most stunning places on Earth, bringing together the brightest minds in the field of gynecologic oncology from across Asia.
The meeting included 1,024 participants globally ( ), featuring 141 international speakers and moderators, and 343 submitted abstracts. The quantity of submitted abstracts was the greatest ever recorded in any ASGO meeting, according to ASGO’s secretary general, Jeong-Yeol Park. The scientific program consisted of 5 plenary sessions, 5 master sessions, and 15 parallel sessions. Preceding the symposium, 7 workshops were held, namely ‘Advanced Gynecologic Surgery Course,’ ‘Medical Writing in Conjunction with JGO,’ ‘Mastering Ultrasonography in Gynecology,’ ‘Precancerous Cervical Lesion Management,’ ‘Laparoscopic Suturing,’ ‘Laparoscopic Surgery (with animal wet workshop),’ and ‘Mastering Hysteroscopy’ ( ). Not only to improve knowledge and to engage in an interactive discussion during the symposium, but these workshops also provided a chance for all participating professionals to enhance their skills in the diagnosis and management of gynecologic diseases and gynecologic oncology cases.
 As the opening ceremony began, the distinguished audience was presented with the traditional Pendet dance of Bali that symbolizes hospitality and blessings as a welcome to the Land of Gods. During the opening ceremony, Dr. Brahmana Askandar Tjokoprawiro, Professor Masaki Mandai, Professor Yudi Mulyana Hidayat, and Dr. Tricia Dewi Anggraeni delivered their inaugural speeches following their respective positions as Chief of Congress of ASGO Bali 2024, President of ASGO, President of the Indonesian Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ISOG), and Chair of the Scientific Committee of ASGO Bali 2024. The meeting commenced with a gong bell struck by Professor Masaki Mandai, alongside Professor Suk Joon Chang (President-Elect of ASGO), Dr. Brahmana Askandar Tjokroprawiro, Professor Yudi Mulyana Hidayat, and Dr. I Nyoman Gede Anom (Head of the Bali Provincial Health Department) ( ).
 

## PLENARY SESSION 1: DIFFERENCES IN GYNECOLOGIC CANCER MANAGEMENT BETWEEN DEVELOPING AND DEVELOPED COUNTRIES
The first plenary session was led by Professors Masaki Mandai, Joseph Ng, and Yudi M Hidayat ( ). This session highlighted the differences in gynecologic cancer management between developed and developing countries, concentrating on possible solutions and important lessons that can be learned from the practices of developed nations. Dr. Brahmana Askandar represented professionals from developing countries, emphasizing challenges like the notable lack of radiation oncologists and radiotherapy centers, the inefficient functioning of associated equipment, and the high costs of chemotherapy. Professor Suk Joon Chang represented specialists from developed countries, who communicated the situation in South Korea. According to Professor Chang, referencing Yukiya Amano, the Director General of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), cancer is becoming more manageable in developed nations. However, numerous developing countries face challenges due to insufficient equipment and human resources to adequately address the rising epidemic of this devastating illness [ ]. Many developed nations allocate greater resources to health care and strive to meet the fundamental health requirements of their citizens. Conversely, developing nations face challenges in delivering effective medical services to their populations [ ]. Additionally, the infrastructure of the nations is also significant. This indicated that the overall environment, concepts, processes, fundamental structure, and government policy are crucial in providing health care services to patients [ ]. Professor Pearly Khaw, an Australian radiation oncologist, highlighted the important gap left by the 2 discussions by providing valuable insights into radiation oncology, a crucial issue affecting cancer care management in numerous developed countries. The primary aim of health promotion for individuals is to establish enhanced cancer control strategies in each country [ ]. The four key topics for Asian countries can be outlined as follows: 1) A coordinated investigation into etiology, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, 2) Dissemination of health information, 3) Development of infrastructure capabilities, 4) Sharing and exchanging cancer control initiatives through international collaborations among various organizations in the region. Discrepancies exist in the management of gynecologic cancer across Asian countries. The role of ASGO is crucial in minimizing this.
 

## PLENARY SESSION 2: ASGO COLLABORATORS – CAN WE MAKE SYNERGY?
The second plenary session was moderated by Professor Paul Cohen from Australian Society of Gynecologic Oncology, Dr. David Tan, and Dr. Fitriyadi Kusuma ( ). This session aimed to enhance collaborative efforts among Asia Pacific countries, which include Australia. The synergy between all countries in Asia Pacific region is bound to greatly enhance the development of gynecologic cancer care in Asia Pacific. Professor Byoung Gie Kim elaborated on the progress of clinical trials in Asia Pacific. He highlighted the importance of developing clinical trials in Asia Pacific, for approximately 45% of cancer patients worldwide are from Asia [ ]. However, recent pivotal trials are mostly conducted in European countries with non-Asian participants. This might be due to the highly different cultural and economic environments among each country with vastly different infrastructures, and the unavailability of a common regulatory organization like the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to get drug approval for the Asia Pacific region. In the present era, many Asian trial groups have risen, such as Asia-Pacific Gynecologic Oncology Trials Group, East Asian Gynecologic Oncology Trial Group, and Asian Gynecologic Oncology Group [ , ]. Instead of bringing competitions, trial groups should be supportive to improve research in Asia Pacific region. We have already had an official representative academic society (ASGO) for the Asia Pacific region which can support collaborative Asia Pacific region clinical trial groups.
 Cervical cancer incidence remains the highest in the Asia Pacific region, this issue was reported thoroughly by Professor Jae Kwan Lee. Although it is a preventable disease, human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination coverage is still very low. Professor Jae Kwan Lee, stated that Standard Development of Growth targets in 2023 that there will be 90% of girls should be fully vaccinated with HPV vaccine by 15 years of age, 70% of women should be screened with a high precision test at 35, and 45 years of age, and 90% of women should be identified with the cervical disease receive treatment and care [ ]. There should also be a 3.4%–30% reduction in mortality from cervical cancer. In reality, it was estimated that only 12% of adolescent girls completed the full course of HPV vaccination in 2021 worldwide. Only 3 Asian National Cancer Centers Alliance (ANCCA) member countries have national vaccination coverage of above 90% for girls by 15 years, while 52% reported a lack of national surveillance data on HPV vaccination coverage [ ]. Cervical cancer screening coverage in 2023 ANCCA survey, among 21 ANCCA member countries 24% have a screening coverage of over 50%, where 38% of countries utilize visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) [ ]. However, HPV testing alone should serve as a primary screening method, as it is now recommended by World Health Organization (WHO) and Asia-Oceania Research Organisation in Genital Infection and Neoplasia. During the transition period to HPV testing, cytology or VIA could be used as a screening method [ ]. Self-sampling methods should also be introduced to the community to increase screening coverage [ ]. Proposed strategies to mitigate the problems are to introduce HPV national immunization program (consider one dose vaccination) [ ] and HPV test for cervical cancer screening (consider self-sampling) [ ].
This session also discussed the role of radiation therapy in endometrial cancer (EC). According to Professor Pearly Khaw, molecular profiling is rapidly changing the landscape in the management of EC. The opportunities are now arising that allow us to better tailor treatments accordingly [ ]. She emphasized the importance of radiotherapy and the current updates in trials regarding the combination of targeted immunotherapy. These trials are indeed important, in concordance with the updated molecular classification of EC. As the knowledge of EC is rapidly changing, new guidelines for the application of radiotherapy are needed.

## PLENARY SESSION 3: MANAGEMENT OF OVARIAN CANCER
The third plenary session was moderated by Professor Anna Fagotti, Professor Yoo-Young Lee and Dr. Brahmana Askandar ( ). It was our honor to have an important representative from European Society of Gynecologic Oncology (ESGO) to take part in this plenary session. The aim of this session is to enlighten professionals regarding the most updated approach to the management of ovarian cancer. As the resectability and debulking of ovarian cancer remain a debatable issue, Professor Anna Fagotti delivered her talk regarding “Comparison of Scoring Methods in Predicting Resectability and Prognosis in Gynecologic Malignancies.” The scoring method developed by Professor Fagotti Scoring methods to predict resectability and prognosis in ovarian cancer are useful, but not perfect. However, the rapid changing of the landscape of treatment of ovarian cancer may require new scores [ , ].
 The outcome of ovarian cancers is often reliant on advancements in surgical and chemotherapeutic approaches. Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) has emerged as a potential intervention, particularly for primary and recurrent ovarian cancer [ ]. Professor Myong Cheol Lim elaborated on the role of HIPEC in ovarian cancer. HIPEC combines cytoreductive surgery with localized chemotherapy, leveraging hyperthermia to enhance cytotoxic effects and drug penetration [ , , ]. After neoadjuvant chemotherapy, HIPEC after interval cytoreductive surgery increased progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in primary advanced ovarian cancer from the randomized trial of OV-01 and KOV-01. Meta-analyses have demonstrated the efficacy of HIPEC in prolonging PFS and OS in ovarian cancer patients, with recent exposure to systemic chemotherapy [ ]. This finding has been confirmed again by the recent publication of CHIPOR and HORSE trials. Ongoing clinical trials, including KOV-02R, KOV-04, and HOTT trials, continue to explore the utility of HIPEC in the era of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and bevacizumab.
The study of genomics and its application in ovarian cancer has also been of popularity. We had the opportunity for Dr. Jack Junjie Chan to deliver the talk. Ovarian cancer is a genomically complex and heterogeneous disease. Genomic testing aids in diagnosis, prognostication, selection of patients for specific treatments, and prediction of treatment response [ ]. Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) testing guides the selection of frontline maintenance PARP inhibitor in advanced high-grade serous ovarian cancer/high-grade endometrioid ovarian cancer. Alternative or academic HRD assays should be validated before routine use [ ]. While there are few readily actionable genetic variants apart from BRCA1/2, next-generation sequencing testing may potentially inform personalized later-line treatment selection in patients with previously treated ovarian cancer [ , ].
The incidence of ovarian cancer in the reproductive-age population is increasing, and this highlights the importance of a fertility-sparing management strategy [ ]. Many cancer survivors can now seek to obtain a live birth [ ]. Oncofertility is a medical treatment that preserves the fertility of patients by any means that may be eradicated by cancer treatment, with the mission of preserving options for the future. Since the target patients are cancer patients, oncofertility is a part of cancer care [ ]. The number one issue to be resolved in the field of oncofertility is how oncologists can responsibly address the issue of oncofertility for cancer patients. Until today there has not been a statement regarding patients with borderline tumors (BOTs), which is an issue to be addressed [ , ]. Some questions that have been answered through Professor Nao Suzuki’s presentations are, platinum-based chemotherapy is not needed for stage II–III BOT, therefore there is no risk of ovarian toxicity; hysterectomy does not need to be performed and routine completion surgery is not recommended; ovarian stimulation is not contraindicated for BOT, and oocyte retrieval is not contraindicated in both early and advanced stage BOT, as long as there has been a complete resection and pathological evaluation of visible peritoneal lesions confirming non-invasive implants; ovarian tissue cryopreservation and thawed ovarian tissue transplantation are options for ovarian function preservation, however, long-term outcomes should still be evaluated [ , ]. Professor Nao Suzuki highlighted that, as the primary caregiver for patients with BOT, gynecologic oncologists should be involved not only in the management of the tumor by fertility-sparing surgery (FSS), but also in the subsequent medical care including assisted-reproductive technology and fertility preservation treatments since the goal of FSS is to try to obtain a live birth. Consequently, gynecologic oncologists ought to have a comprehensive understanding of oncofertility [ ].

## PLENARY SESSION 4: MANAGEMENT OF CERVICAL CANCER
In the fourth plenary session, we had the utmost honor to have Professor David Cibula to moderate the session and share his knowledge regarding the specific issue, together with Professor Jeong-Yeol Park and Dr. Tricia Dewi Anggraeni ( ). The aim of this session is to deliver and discuss the most updated approach in managing cervical cancer. While the traditional approach in the early stage of cervical cancer supports extreme radicality in surgery, Professor David Cibula presented evidence regarding de-escalation in management and how there were no significant differences. Some important points that were made by him were: lymph node staging should be avoided in stage IA, except for T1a1 LVSI0 [ ]; sentinel lymph node mapping can be done instead of pelvic lymphadenectomy in IA/L1-IB2 cervical cancer; simple hysterectomy can be done instead of radical hysterectomy in cases with ≤2 cm tumor size which also fulfils the SHAPE criteria (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics [FIGO] stage IA2/IB1, tumor size ≤ 2 cm, limited stromal invasion [less than 10 mm in cone biopsy, less than 50% involvement of stromal invasion in MRI], histology of squamous cell carcinoma/adenocarcinoma/ adenosarcoma), and N0 on imaging) [ , ]; Decrease radicality of parametrectomy can be done in smaller tumors [ ]. The purpose of de-escalation is to minimize the surgery-related morbidity and complications. However, these patients need to undergo adjuvant radiotherapy due to inadequate radicality [ ].
 A further issue in the management of cervical cancer is the management of locally advanced cervical cancer. Management of bulky early cervical cancer with FIGO stage IB3–IIA2 remains controversial until today [ ]. While many treatment modalities are available, including surgical and non-surgical, Professor Jeong Yeol Park stated that the most frequently employed primary treatment modality for stage IB3 cervical cancer in Korea during the past 10 years was radical hysterectomy [ ]. However, recommendations differ depending on the regions. According to studies, radical hysterectomy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) both can be recommended, however, combination with CCRT is deemed to be not cost-effective, for RH alone resulted in similar survival outcomes [ ].
Professor Tadaaki Nishikawa addressed another issue regarding the management of metastatic and recurrent cervical cancer. As the advanced cases rely solely on chemoradiation therapy, the development antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) has been very progressive, namely the discovery and clinical application of trastuzumab deruxtecan and sacituzumab tirumotecan and immune-checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. Chemotherapy for metastatic cervical cancer began with the development of many cytotoxic anti-cancer agents and has achieved a paradigm shift with the appearance of immune-checkpoint inhibitors and ADCs. Trastuzumab deruxtecan is one of the most promising ADC for patients with metastatic cervical cancer [ ], but interstitial lung disease has potentially life-threatening side effects and we need to be well-prepared to manage it [ ]. Many other ADCs, such as sacituzumab tilmotecan, are in development for patients with metastatic cervical cancer, and ADCs are also being developed in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors and other agents [ ]. Although the appearance of new ADCs and the development of combination therapies are expected to improve the prognosis of patients with metastatic cervical cancer, but novel side effects have also been reported, and we need to share the knowledge, including the development of guidelines for their management all over the world [ ].

## PLENARY SESSION 5: IMMUNOTHERAPY AND ADC IN EC
The final plenary session was moderated by Professor Kenichi Harano, Professor Byoung-Gie Kim and Dr. Tofan Widya Utami ( ). As the staging of EC has integrated molecular classification, the role of ADC in EC management is important to increase survival [ ]. As Professor Byoung-Gie Kim introduced, ADCs are novel class of cancer therapeutics combining the specificity of a monoclonal antibody with the anti-tumor activity of potent cytotoxic drugs. ADCs offer promising therapeutic options, especially for patients with advanced disease and limited treatment options. Early data about the efficacy and safety of various ADCs are encouraging in EC [ ]. Ongoing clinical trials with several most promising ADCs will provide the improvement of patient outcomes and change our future clinical treatment options for EC.
 Immunotherapy has also gained a significant role, in which the efficacy of several immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been demonstrated in the first-line treatment of advanced or recurrent EC [ , ]. Some studies highlighted by Professor Kenichi Harano regarding his talk about this were ENGOT-EN6-NSGO/GOG-3031/RUBY trial (addition of dostarlimab to carboplatin and paclitaxel in advanced/recurrent EC) [ ], NRG GY018 trial (carboplatin and paclitaxel ± pembrolizumab for stage III/IVA, stage IVB, or recurrent EC) [ ], and AtTEnd study (addition of atetzolizumab to chemotherapy) [ ]. PARP inhibitor as an addition to chemotherapy and ICIs were also highlighted, namely DUO-E study (durvalumab + olaparib) [ ] and RUBY Part 2 (dostarlimab + niraparib) [ ]. In the deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) population, there is no significant difference of efficacy between ICIs. The addition of PARP inhibitors had no impact on improving prognosis. In the proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) population, addition of pembrolizumab to chemotherapy was shown to improve PFS, while other ICIs had a poor prognostic effect. Maintenance PARP inhibitor therapy plus ICI improved PFS in the pMMR population [ ]. However, careful consideration is required to balance therapeutic efficacy against toxicities. It is important to identify subgroups that are particularly benefited by the combination of ICI and PARP inhibitors.
Analysis of subpopulation in Asian populations showed that efficacy of ICIs and PARP inhibitor in the subgroup analysis of the DUO-E trial showed that Asian patients had a trend toward lower efficacy of durvalumab and durvalumab + olaparib therapy compared to non-Asian patients [ ]. In the AtTEnd study, MMR status did not differ between Asians and non-Asians. In both dMMR and pMMR populations, Asians were found to be younger, thinner, and more programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) positive than non-Asians. In pMMR population, there was a trend toward worse prognosis with atezolizumab in Asians, but improved prognosis with atezolizumab in non-Asians. In dMMR population, both Asians and non-Asians had improved PFS with atezolizumab [ ]. Asians were also found to have a better prognosis in the chemotherapy group. Further studies are still needed to determine the efficacy of ICIs in the Asian population.

## LATE-BREAKING ABSTRACTS (LBAs)
Seven LBAs were presented in ASGO 2024 meeting, namely the DUO-O, NIRVANA-R, LARA, OSCAR, DUO-E, E7386 + LEN, and KEYNOTE A18 trials. Dr. Se Ik Kim presented results of an interim analysis from DUO-O/ENGOT-ov46/GOG-3025 trial [ ], which compared carboplatin/paclitaxel (CP) + bevacizumab (arm 1), CP + bevacizumab followed by durvalumab (arm 2) and CP + bevacizumab followed by durvalumab and olaparib maintenance in newly diagnosed advanced ovarian cancer without a tumor BRCA1/BRCA2-mutation (non-tBRCAm). From the 3 groups, clinically meaningful PFS was demonstrated in arm 3 versus arm 1. Median PFS of 45.1 months in arm 3 is the longest reported for non-tBRCAm HRD-positive patients in the first line setting to date. Regarding the OS, the difference between arm 3 and control was not statistically significant in the non-tBRCAm population; hazard ratio (HR) for arm 3 versus control in the non-tBRCAm HRD-positive population was 0.84 (95% confidence interval [CI]=0.51–1.37). Safety continues to be generally consistent, with neutropenia found to be the most common adverse effect. DUO-O trial is ongoing, and further insight into the long-term benefit of the combination will be provided with additional follow-up.
NIRVANA-R trial [ ] was presented by Dr. Hyun Woong Cho. This is the first report of doublet maintenance which is niraparib + bevacizumab maintenance therapy in platinum-sensitive relapsed ovarian cancer (non-mucinous histology) patients previously treated with PARP inhibitor. As of the data cutoff on June 1, 2024, the median follow-up time was 11.8 months. The benefits were particularly evident in patients who responded well to previous platinum-based chemotherapy, namely patients with treatment-free interval of 24 months or longer after the penultimate chemotherapy; complete response or normal CA-125 level in response to the most recent chemotherapy. Regarding the adverse effects, only 6.8% had to discontinue treatment due to the adverse effects, whereas 50% had to receive treatment with interrupted dose and 22.7% had to receive treatment with reduced dose. Most common adverse effects were hypertension, anemia and thrombocytopenia. From this trial, it was showed the combination maintenance of niraparib + bevacizumab is promising, warranting further clinical research.
LARA trial [ ] was presented by Dr. David Tan. He presented phase II results of pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib in recurrent clear cell gynecological cancer, mainly ovarian clear-cell ovarian carcinoma (OCCC). LARA is the first clinical study reporting efficacy for pembrolizumab and lenvatinib in clear cell ovarian and endometrial carcinomas (89% were OCCC). Objective response rate (ORR) reaches 44%, whereas PFS reached 23.4 weeks. In comparison to MOCCA trial (durvalumab) [ ], PFS only reached 14 weeks. No new safety concerns with pembrolizumab/lenvatinib – treatment was tolerable with manageable toxicity. This finding supports that pembrolizumab/ lenvatinib may represent a new standard of care for recurrent clear cell gynecologic cancers of the ovary.
DUO-E trial [ ] was presented by Professor Byoung Gie Kim, reporting the ORR, duration of response (DoR) and time to treatment discontinuation (TDT) analyses of durvalumab plus CP followed by durvalumab with/without olaparib in EC. Immunotherapy and chemotherapy combinations have shown activity in EC, particularly in dMMR disease, and adding a PARP inhibitor to immunotherapy in the maintenance setting has the potential to further improve antitumor activity. This study found that in newly diagnosed FIGO 2009 stage III/IV or recurrent EC, durvalumab plus CP followed by durvalumab with/without olaparib improved ORR, DoR, and TDT. Specific in the dMMR subpopulation, the addition of durvalumab to CP showed a consistently improved benefit in ORR, DoR, and TDT. In the pMMR subpopulation, addition of olaparib to durvalumab plus CP further extended the median DoR by 11.1 months versus CP alone (18.7 vs. 7.6 months), and further extended median TDT. Both treatment regimens were generally well tolerated with a manageable adverse event profile and a low frequency of treatment discontinuations due to adverse effects, most commonly anemia and neutropenia. In conclusion, DUO-E results support CP plus durvalumab as a new treatment option for patients with EC, including dMMR disease, and CP plus durvalumab plus olaparib as a new treatment option for pMMR disease.
KEYNOTE-A18 [ ], presented by Professor Yang Xiang, reported the results of their phase 3 study evaluating pembrolizumab plus CCRT versus placebo plus CCRT for patients with newly diagnosed, previously untreated, high-risk, locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC). This study included FIGO 2014 stage IB2–IIB (node-positive disease) or FIGO 2014 stage III–IVA (either node-positive or node-negative disease). The experimental group was given CCRT (cisplatin + external beam radiotherapy [EBRT]) followed by brachytherapy + pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks for 5 cycles) continued with pembrolizumab 400 mg every 6 weeks for 15 cycles. The control group was given only CCRT without pembrolizumab at all. At IA2, pembrolizumab plus CCRT continued to demonstrate PFS (HR=0.61; 95% CI=0.40–0.93) benefit in patients with high-risk locally advanced cervical cancer enrolled in Asia, comparable to the overall population (HR=0.68; 95% CI=0.56–0.84). The safety profile for pembrolizumab plus CCRT in patients from Asia continued to be manageable and consistent with the overall population, most commonly decrease in white blood cells and anemia. These data support pembrolizumab plus CCRT as a new potential standard of care for patients with newly diagnosed, previously untreated, high-risk LACC in Asia.
The results of phase 1b study of E7386 + lenvatinib [ ] in patients with advanced EC that progressed on platinum-based chemotherapy and an anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy was presented by Professor Jung Yun Lee. Currently, no standard-of-care therapy exists for patients with advanced EC in the post-immunotherapy setting, adding to the unmet need for this population. Lenvatinib monotherapy has shown antitumor activity in patients with unresectable EC that relapsed after 1 prior systemic platinum-based chemotherapy. E7386 inhibits the interaction between β-catenin and the CREB-binding protein. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that E7386 enhanced the antitumor activity of lenvatinib. This interim study evaluated the efficacy of E7386 + levantinib combination on EC patients that progressed after a platinum-based chemotherapy and anti-PD-L1 therapy, and patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of 0–1. E7386 was given in 120 mg twice a day dose + lenvatinib 14 mg once a day. In this study, E7386 + lenvatinib demonstrated promising preliminary antitumor activity, with a high objective response rate (43.8%). Nausea and vomiting were the most frequent adverse effects (mostly grade 1 or 2). The study is now in a dose-optimization phase and further results will continue to be reported.
OSCAR trial [ ] was presented by Professor Tadaaki Nishikawa. In this trial, efficacy and safety of nivolumab in patients with unresectable clear cell sarcoma (CCS) and alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) was evaluated. Although this histologic type is very rarely found in gynecologic cancer, the result of this trial with nivolumab may be of significance to the addition of knowledge in chemotherapy. In patients aged ≥ 18 with unresectable CCS or ASPS, nivolumab 240 mg/body surface area every 2 weeks was given until progression or intolerable toxicity. A total of 25 patients (11 CSS, 14 ASPS) were included in the study, with median age of 34. There was no responder of CCS and 3 responders of ASPS. The primary endpoint of response rate was not met for CCS and ASPS. The incidence of any adverse event with grade 3 or 4 was 42%. Further studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy of ICIs especially for ASPS.

## PARALLEL SESSIONS
ASGO 2024 held 5 master sessions and 10 parallel sessions. Master sessions discussed about by about current treatment of EC, gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, latest updates in imaging modalities in gynecologic cancer, new uterine sarcoma guideline, and video session in cervical cancer surgery. Parallel sessions discussed about novel systemic agents in gynecologic oncology, palliative care, perioperative care, rare tumors in gynecologic oncology, networking for Asian-wide oncology trial groups, women’s session, WHO/FIGO/ASGO session, progress in pathology in gynecologic oncology, radiation therapy, and precision medicine.
The Women in Gynecologic Oncology session seeks to advance the initiative of female empowerment within the domain of gynecologic oncology. The movement was initiated by Dr. Ga Won Yim, Dr. Tricia Dewi Anggraeni, Professor Kaoru Abiko, and Professor Laila Nuranna ( ). The committee welcomed Professor Anna Fagotti to present insights from the ESGO. This session facilitated the collection of numerous insights and perspectives from female gynecologists and gynecologic oncologists around Asia, as gathered through surveys performed by Dr. Ga Won Yim, Dr. Tricia Dewi Anggraeni, and Professor Kaoru Abiko. They concurred that the number of female gynecologic oncologists is rising, although those in leadership roles remain limited. Many institutions lack support for female physicians, resulting in pressure for them to adapt to their female nature as mothers and partners [ ].
 ESGO, European Reference Network on Rare Adult Cancers and Gynecologic Cancer Inter Group has developed a new guideline for uterine sarcoma [ ] and it was introduced in ASGO 2024. Professor Jae-Weon Kim and Dr. Se-Ik Kim took part in the development and publishment processes of this guideline ( ). While the incidence of uterine sarcoma is relatively low compared to the Western countries, number of cases are increasing in some part of Asia.
 In Republic of Korea, the incidence of uterine sarcoma was found to be 0.25 per 100,000 in 2018 (annual percentage change [APC], 13.9%; p<0.001), compared to 0.02 per 100,000 in 1999, with APC of 13.9% (p<0.001) [ ]. The principle of diagnosis in uterine sarcoma is that the diagnosis should be based on histology, immunohistochemistry and molecular analyses. Surgery in early-stage disease should include total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, advanced stage should include tumor debulking resection of metastases, and recurrence diseases should include tumor debulking. ASGO and ESGO will be conducting a joint webinar in February 2025 to discuss how these guidelines can be applied to Asian countries.
