This leads to significant myocardial ischaemia and hypoxia, disrupting the heart’s energy metabolism and ultimately resulting in irreversible injury to cardiomyocytes. Despite advancements in reperfusion therapies, ischaemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) remains a critical contributor to complications such as arrhythmias and heart failure. Key mechanisms, including the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), succinate accumulation, and the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), are highlighted as drivers of cellular injury. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial protection, and ischaemic conditioning are discussed, highlighting their potential to mitigate IRI. By integrating preclinical and clinical evidence, this review highlights the promise of metabolic modulation as a significant approach to enhancing outcomes in AMI patients.

## Background
Myocardial infarction (MI) is one of the most severe manifestations of heart disease. It remains a leading cause of death worldwide despite significant advancements in clinical management over the past few years [ , ]. During a sudden ischaemic event in the heart, cardiac metabolism undergoes rapid changes. The lack of oxygen hinders the oxidative metabolism of fatty acids (FAs), carbohydrates, ketones, and amino acids while promoting anaerobic glycolysis to conserve the limited oxygen available [ ]. Modern management therapies, such as mechanical interventions (like balloon angioplasty) and pharmacological approaches (like thrombolysis), aim to restore blood flow in MI patients by removing plaque or occluded thrombus from blocked coronary arteries[ – ]. Once reperfusion occurs, bringing oxygen back to the tissue and eliminating the metabolites accumulated during ischaemia, there is a swift normalisation of intracellular pH and a resumption of oxidative metabolism for various substrates. However, increased oxidative stress and the imbalanced production and accumulation of ROS can exacerbate tissue damage, leading to a phenomenon known as reperfusion injury [ , ]. IRI accounts for approximately 50% of the final MI size, leading to heightened damage to myocardial cells and contributing to increased fibrosis and remodelling of the heart tissue [ ].
Emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of modulating myocardial metabolism to mitigate IRI. Drugs such as trimetazidine (TMZ) exemplify this strategy by shifting substrate preference from FAO to glucose oxidation, thereby improving oxygen efficiency and reducing cytotoxic lipid accumulation [ ]. Concurrently, targeting mitochondrial dysfunction and ischaemic conditioning has been demonstrated to preserve cardiomyocyte viability. Mitochondrial transplantation, a novel approach, further underscores the centrality of metabolism in cardioprotection. This review examines the current literature on key metabolic pathways and concepts related to healthy and ischaemic hearts, summarising the development of treatment methods for AMI and myocardial ischaemia and reperfusion (MIRI).

## Cardiac energy metabolism in health and infarction

## Metabolic pathways in the healthy heart
The human heart requires a substantial amount of energy and relies on a continuous supply of nutrients and oxygen to maintain stable adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels within its cells [ , ]. Over the course of a day, the heart produces and consumes approximately 3.5–5 kg of ATP, which is 15–20 times its own weight [ ]. Despite glucose metabolism having a greater capacity to generate ATP, the energy metabolism in the healthy adult human heart heavily relies on FAO. Mitochondrial FAO accounts for 60–80% of cardiac ATP production, while glucose accounts for approximately 20% [ ]. This may be because long-chain (LC) FAO is 3–4 times more efficient in producing ATP per substrate molecule than glucose oxidation [ ]. Randle and colleagues demonstrated in 1963 that FA and glucose metabolism are mutually exclusive, allowing heart metabolism patterns to change in response to physiological conditions, a regulation known as the Randle cycle [ ]. Metabolic flexibility is crucial for meeting the heart’s energy demands, especially during periods of overload or increased stress [ ].
In cardiomyocytes, FAs and glucose undergo metabolism, converting to acetyl-CoA, a common intermediate that can produce energy. FAs are transported to the CMs by fatty acid translocase (FAT)/CD36. After cellular uptake, FA can be stored as triglycerides or metabolised in the mitochondria. It can also be catalysed by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT-1), which synthesises LC acylcarnitine. This step limits the metabolic rate of FAs and is regulated by the concentration of malonyl coenzyme A (malonyl-CoA). LC acylcarnitine then moves from the intermembrane space into the mitochondrial matrix, where it undergoes β-oxidation to generate acetyl-CoA [ ]. In the meantime, glucose, entering the CMs via glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) or glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), is converted to pyruvate by glycolysis. Then, Pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA by pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and ultimately enters the mitochondria, where fatty acyl-CoA is oxidised to produce acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA subsequently enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to produce ATP [ ] (Fig.  ). 
Meanwhile, other substrates, such as lactate, ketone bodies, and amino acids, also contribute to mitochondrial ATP production. Lactate is converted to pyruvate through the action of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Ketone bodies are transformed directly into acetyl-CoA. Furthermore, the breakdown of amino acids produces keto acids that can enter the TCA cycle [ ]. However, these substrates are considered minor contributors due to their low availability under normal physiological conditions [ , ]. During the TCA cycle, acetyl-CoA generates substantial amounts of reducing equivalents, including NAD+ and H+ [ ]. The reducing equivalents from the cytosol are primarily transferred into the mitochondria via the malate-aspartate shuttle (MAS) [ ]. These reducing equivalents are involved in oxidative phosphorylation within the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), producing large quantities of ATP.
The mitochondrial ETC is composed of four multi-subunit complexes (complexes Ⅰ–Ⅳ) coupled with electron carriers [coenzyme Q (CoQ) and cytochrome c (cyt c)]. Complex Ⅰ is the initial site where electrons derived from reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) are introduced into the ETC, and it simultaneously generates ROS in a complex manner [ ]. Complex Ⅱ transfers electrons from reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2) to CoQ but does not pump protons across the inner membrane [ ]. SDH, also known as Complex Ⅱ, is a crucial link between the essential metabolic pathways of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and the TCA cycle. It catalyses the conversion of succinic acid into fumaric acid within the TCA cycle, a process coupled with the reduction of flavin-adenine dinucleotide (FAD) to form FADH2. This reduction enables the transfer of electrons to Q in the ETC [ , ]. Complex Ⅲ facilitates the transfer of electrons from CoQ to cyt c by actively pumping protons across the membrane. Cyt c transfers electrons to Complex Ⅳ, enabling the conversion of molecular oxygen into water [ , ].
All in all, the ETC can be divided into two circuits: (1) complex Ⅰ → complex Ⅲ → complex Ⅳ; (2) complex Ⅱ → complex III → complex Ⅳ. During regular metabolic activity, these complexes and mobile carriers facilitate the transfer of electrons released from oxidative substrates along the components of the ETC, ultimately converging on complex Ⅳ (cytochrome oxidase), where oxygen is reduced to water [ ]. Complex Ⅴ, also known as ATP synthase, utilises the proton electrochemical gradient generated by the ETC to convert adenosine diphosphate (ADP) into ATP [ ]. The produced ATP and creatine are converted into creatine phosphate and ADP by mitochondrial creatine kinase. ATP and creatine phosphate are transported to the myocardial myofibrils, where myocardial creatine kinase reverses the reaction to synthesise ATP, thereby continuously meeting the energy metabolism needs of cardiomyocytes [ ] (Fig.  ). 

## Shift of metabolic pathways in the progression of AMI
The oxygen supply to myocardial mitochondria is limited during AMI. As a result, glucose, which requires less oxygen than FAs to produce ATP, becomes the primary energy source for the heart [ ]. Regarding other substrates, ketone body accumulation is active in the ischaemic heart and improves functional recovery during reperfusion [ ]; glutamate uptake is further enhanced in the ischaemic heart and exhibits cytoprotective effects [ ]. However, the protective significance of these mechanisms remains uncertain.
Under ischaemia, the TCA gets blocked. Therefore, oxidative phosphorylation does not provide energy, which ultimately leads to the accumulation of NADH; however, glycolysis maintains the ATP level. Multiple mechanisms contribute to an increased glycolytic rate during ischaemic conditions. These include enhanced glucose extraction and improved activity of glycolytic enzymes, among other effects [ , ]. Prolonged glycolytic activity accumulates protons, resulting in metabolic acidosis and lowering the intracellular pH to below 7.0 during acute myocardial ischaemia. This change also affects the activity of ion exchangers, which can cause intracellular and, ultimately, mitochondrial calcium (Ca2+) overload [ ]. Moreover, the accumulation of glycolysis-derived metabolic products, such as lactate, protons, and NADH, can inhibit glycolysis, thereby exacerbating the energy deficit [ , ].
Elevated plasma succinate levels observed in patients with AMI clinically underscore the well-characterised accumulation of succinate in the myocardium during MI, highlighting its pathophysiological relevance [ ]. Ischaemic succinate accumulation arises from the reversal of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), which is driven by fumarate overflow from purine nucleotide breakdown and partial reversal of the malate-aspartate shuttle [ ]. Excess succinate worsens cardiomyocyte injury by disrupting electron flow through complexes I, II, and III of the respiratory chain, disturbing the balance of ROS production and antioxidant defences. During myocardial IRI, ROS include free radicals, such as superoxide (O2•−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). O2•−, and hydroxyl radical (•OH), as well as reactive nitrogen species like peroxynitrite [ ]. Mitochondrially generated ROS can cross membranes or exit through channels such as the voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) to cause oxidative damage throughout the cell. Their very short half-lives make such damage rapid and largely irreversible [ ].

## MI of metabolic syndrome
Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of risk factors that can increase the likelihood of developing heart disease. These factors include obesity, abnormal lipid levels, high blood pressure, and insulin resistance or elevated blood glucose levels. Alterations in substrate availability and utilisation, as well as impairment in the transcriptional regulation of oxidation pathways, are often noted in MetS [ ]. They impact both susceptibility to acute myocardial IRI and the efficacy of cardioprotective therapies [ ]. A character of MetS is the pathological accumulation of FAs in cardiomyocytes, driven by sarcolemmal translocation of the FA transporter CD36 and enhanced mitochondrial CPT-1 activity, which promotes excessive LCFA uptake and oxidation [ ]. Several vital enzymes that drive the traditional TCA cycle, including isocitrate dehydrogenase, mitochondrial succinyl-CoA synthetase, subunit B of the mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase, and malate dehydrogenase, are all transcriptionally downregulated in MS [ ]. This metabolic shift disrupts the transcriptional regulation of oxidative pathways, induces mitochondrial dysfunction, and amplifies oxidative stress through the activation of NADPH oxidase (NOX) and a decrease in antioxidant capacity [ ].
This metabolic inflexibility depletes ATP reserves during ischaemia by limiting the activity of the glycolytic pathway. At the same time, mitochondrial uncoupling induced by FAs and the excessive production of uncoupling proteins have a detrimental effect on Ca2+ storage and mitochondrial energy production, leading to an elevated production of ROS and less efficient ATP production [ ]. High-fat environments facilitated lipid peroxidation when ROS were present. Lipid peroxidation could be the underly driver that accelerates atherothrombosis and eventually leads to AMI [ ]. Post-ischaemic reperfusion amplifies injury as overwhelmed antioxidant systems fail to neutralise ROS overproduction, exacerbating oxidative damage and inflammatory cascades [ ].

## Pathological mechanisms of AMI

## Oxidative stress reaction
Ischaemia Oxidative stress is the imbalance between the production of ROS and the decrease of antioxidant defences in the body, leading to the inability of ROS to be effectively removed, which damages cellular molecular structures and disrupts signalling regulation [ ]. Mitochondrial ROS generation occurs partly during ischaemia and more extensively during the early reperfusion period [ ]. Mitochondrial ROS can increase due to residual oxygen availability early in the ischaemia process [ ]. However, during ongoing anoxia, ROS production stops because ischaemia causes a conformational change in complex I that deactivates its activity, preventing ROS generation by inhibiting both forward and reverse electron flow through the ETC [ ].
Ischaemia induces mitochondrial alterations that predispose mitochondria to overproduce ROS during reperfusion. During ischaemia, succinate levels can rise dramatically within the mitochondria, thoroughly saturating the succinate dehydrogenase subunit A (SDHA) active site and protecting the reduced FAD cofactor from oxidation by oxygen. While this disease state efficiently prevents direct ROS formation by Complex II, it, unfortunately, leads to a much more potent and damaging source of ROS known as reverse electron transfer (RET). A substantial influx of electrons from succinate oxidation rapidly depletes the CoQ pool upon reperfusion. This sudden surge forces electrons to flow backwards from CoQ to Complex I rather than moving forward to Complex III. As a result, a significant quantity of electrons is leaked to oxygen during this process [ ]. Meanwhile, oxidative damage to cardiolipin, a phospholipid located in the mitochondrial inner membrane, leads to the dissociation of complexes I and III from the supercomplex, leading to electron leakage from ETC and ROS generation [ ]. Succinates from ischaemic accumulation are rapidly re-oxidised by the succinate dehydrogenase of complex Ⅱ, which transfers electrons from complex II to complex I, producing O2•−. Complex Ⅲ also triggers O2•− production through the redox reactions between oxygen and ubisemiquinon [ ](Fig.  ). 
Complexes Ⅰ and Ⅲ are the primary sources of ROS during myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion [ , ]. Additionally, the outer mitochondrial membrane-bound monoamine oxidases (MAO-A, MAO-B) [ ], along with mitochondrially localised NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) [ ], catalyse the oxidation of their respective substrates, leading to superoxide and hydrogen peroxide generation independently of the canonical electron transport chain. NOX4-derived ROS can oxidise tetrahydrobiopterin, a vital cofactor for endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), resulting in eNOS uncoupling and the production of superoxide rather than nitric oxide, significantly amplifying oxidative stress [ ]. p66Shc is an atypical redox enzyme that functions as a mitochondrial signal transducer by diverting electrons from the mitochondrial electron transport chain via cyt c oxidation to catalyse the partial reduction of molecular oxygen, thereby generating hydrogen peroxide [ ]. Furthermore, cytochrome P450 monooxygenases contribute to ROS buildup by generating ROS and eicosanoids near calcium-handling proteins like SERCA2 and NCX [ ].
Proteins are among the earliest targets of oxidative damage [ ]. Amino acid side chains, particularly those of cysteine and methionine residues, are highly susceptible to ROS oxidation [ ]. Such oxidation harms enzymes essential for metabolism, proteases, phosphatases, and key regulators of apoptosis [ ]. The glycolytic enzyme glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is especially vulnerable to ROS-mediated oxidation of its catalytic cysteine, resulting in reversible inhibition of glycolytic activity and redirecting glucose flux towards the pentose phosphate pathway, thereby impacting energy metabolism and redox balance [ ]. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) undergoes oxidative modification at conserved cysteine residues within its DNA-binding domain, impairing its transcriptional activity and affecting mitochondrial and metabolic regulation [ ]. The extracellular-signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway may be disrupted by ROS, such as through ER stress-dependent activation, but prolonged oxidative conditions can interfere with signalling accuracy and apoptotic control [ ]. The phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) p110α subunit is prone to oxidation at critical cysteines, which disrupts its interaction with upstream regulators and diminishes downstream Akt-mediated survival signals. Lastly, ROS induces oxidation-driven conformational changes in Bax, increasing its mitochondrial translocation and oligomerisation, which facilitates cyt c release and initiates apoptosis [ ]. Overall, oxidative modifications can cause protein dysfunction, increase proteolysis, and lead to severe cytotoxic effects.
Lipid peroxidation happens when oxidants like free radicals attack lipids with carbon–carbon double bonds, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), resulting in various oxidation products [ ]. Lipid peroxidation yields lipid hydroperoxides as its primary products and subsequently generates secondary oxidative breakdown products, including propanal, hexanal, malondialdehyde (MAD), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), and others [ , ]. MDA, long established as a clinically reliable marker of oxidative stress [ ]. It has been demonstrated that MDA depresses cardiac contraction and relaxation velocities in isolated ventricular myocytes, an effect linked to a diminished Ca2+ transient amplitude, increased oxidative stress, and activation of p38 MAP kinase [ ]. By contrast, 4-HNE, regarded as the most toxic product of lipid peroxidation [ ], plays a dual role that depends on its intracellular concentration. At low levels, it has a growth-regulating effect, whereas higher concentrations are primarily cytotoxic [ ]. 4-HNE, produced in large quantities during MI/R injury [ ], influences pathological processes in several ways. 4-HNE can bind to cysteine, histidine, and lysine amino acid residues and can also directly modify four DNA bases [ , ], thereby regulating apoptosis or autophagy differently [ , ]. Elevated 4-HNE binds directly to RIP1, resulting in reduced ubiquitin-mediated degradation of RIP1, which activates the necroptosis pathway and promotes myocardial necroptosis [ ].
Cardiolipin (CL) is a distinctive dimeric phospholipid found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) in eukaryotic cells. Due to its high content of unsaturated fatty acids, CL is particularly susceptible to oxidative attacks triggered by ROS [ ]. CL is responsible for maintaining and stabilising the proteins of the respiratory chain, including complexes I, III, and IV, as well as ATP synthase. Additionally, CL is essential for aggregating and anchoring oxidative phosphorylation proteins [ ]. A loss of CL caused by ROS-induced peroxidation in the heart following ischaemia/reperfusion (I/R) has been well documented. This depletion of CL appears to contribute, at least in part, to the reduced activity of respiratory chain complexes (I, III, and IV) [ – ], mitochondrial dysfunction, and subsequent heart injury [ ].
Importantly, under normal conditions, non-oxidised CL securely anchors cyt c to the IMM.
However, CL releases cyt c out of the mitochondria via mPTP once CL itself is oxidised [ ]. The release of cyt c increases ROS production and triggers apoptosis cascades [ , ].
Guanine is the most oxidation-prone of the four DNA bases, producing 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxo-dG) when modified by ROS [ ]; this lesion can mispair with adenine via Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding, thereby increasing the risk of mutations and genomic instability [ ]. Regions rich in guanine, such as the telomeric 3′ overhang with its (TTAGGG)n repeats and G-quadruplex structures, serve as hotspots for oxidative damage [ , ]. Mitochondrial DNA is particularly vulnerable to such damage because of its proximity to ROS generation sites and its lack of protective chromatin [ ]. Lesions in mtDNA impair the encoding of key electron transport chain subunits, resulting in electron leakage at complexes I and III, premature oxygen reduction, and further ROS formation [ , ]. In the context of myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion, mitochondrial telomerase reverse transcriptase, but not its nuclear equivalent, mitigates these harmful effects by preventing excessive accumulation of prohibitin and normalising the balance between matrix arm and membrane subunits of complex I [ ].

## Calcium overload
Cytosolic Ca2+ overload is a common event that leads to ischaemic heart disease. As an essential second messenger in cells, Ca2+ regulates excitation–contraction coupling, mitochondrial metabolism, and oxidative stress signalling, thereby controlling the function and destiny of the cell[ ]. Studies have shown that Ca2+ regulation undergoes significant changes during myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion. Maintaining or correcting Ca2+ homeostasis can help alleviate myocardial cell damage and reduce the extent of MI [ ].
Ca2+ levels in myocardial cells are regulated by L-type voltage-dependent calcium channel (L-VDCC), Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCE), Na+/H+ exchanger mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca2+ release channel—ryanodine receptor (RyR), sarco(endo)plasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA), etc., which participate in Ca2+ overload during MIRI [ ]. The L-type voltage-dependent calcium channel (L-VDCC) and the NCE are two key factors in regulating calcium homeostasis. The Ca2+ increase can be generally divided into two phases: the early phase is partially mediated by calcium channels, such as L-VDCC; the NCE mainly mediates the latter phase. Under normal conditions, the extracellular Ca2+ concentration is much higher than the intracellular one. After a period of ischaemia, cardiomyocytes exhibit anaerobic metabolism, resulting in a decrease in ATP production, which in turn reduces the function of the NCE. Ca2+ cannot be effectively excreted from the cell, and hypoxia leads to an enhanced anaerobic enzymatic process of the tissue, producing large amounts of H+. Excessive H+ concentration in the cell results in a decrease in pH. In this case, the H+/Na+ exchanger (HNE) compensation mechanism on the cell membrane is activated to restore the pH balance, which in turn extrudes protons from the cell in exchange for Na+ entry. Together with a reduction in Na+/K+ ATPase activity due to ATP depletion, intracellular Na+ overload ensues. The intracellular and subsequent mitochondrial Ca2+ concentration continues to increase, leading to Ca2+ overload [ , ]. This remodelling process results in an increased susceptibility for arrhythmias with the myocardium as the substrate on which specific events trigger arrhythmias [ ]. As cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations rise to micromolar levels, the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) is activated in energised mitochondria, permitting significant Ca2+ transfer into the matrix [ , ].
Upon reperfusion, mitochondrial respiration resumes, quickly replenishing cellular ATP. This renewed ATP activates sarcoplasmic reticulum ATPase 2A (SERCA2A), allowing the reuptake of cytosolic Ca2+ into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. However, hyperactivation of the sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release channel, RyR2, triggers destabilising cycles of rapid Ca2+ uptake and spontaneous release. This dysregulation, combined with persistently elevated cytosolic Ca2+ levels despite the restored ATP, causes pathological hypercontraction of cardiomyocytes, a possible mechanism of cell death [ , ]. This persistent Ca2+ overload disrupts cellular metabolism and antioxidant defences by promoting Ca2+-dependent dephosphorylation of enzymes such as pyruvate dehydrogenase and manganese superoxide dismutase, thereby impairing ATP production and ROS detoxification [ ]. It also activates calpains that proteolytically cleave structural and functional proteins, including Na+/K+ ATPase, NCE, SERCA, and the RyR, thereby perpetuating Ca2+ influx and worsening myocardial injury [ , ]. Excessive Ca2+ entry into the mitochondrial matrix further damages the cell by working together with increased ROS levels to trigger the opening of the mPTP [ ]. Ca2+ serves as the indispensable trigger for mPTP opening, which proceeds exclusively in a Ca2+-dependent manner [ ]. Notably, ROS production often occurs before and promotes intracellular Ca2+ accumulation, lowering the threshold for pore activation and driving lipid peroxidation-mediated mitochondrial swelling [ ]. The timing of peak mitochondrial Ca2+ load and maximum ROS during early reperfusion indicates the critical period for irreversible pore opening and cell death [ , ](Fig.  ). 

## Mitochondrial dysfunction
Mitochondria are recognised as the powerhouses orchestrating cellular activities. This core role is well-established based on the electrochemical gradient produced by the respiratory chain [ , ]. Abnormal dynamics and quality control, dysfunctional proteostasis, inhibited ATP production, calcium dyshomeostasis, and metabolic reprogramming often occur simultaneously and interact within MIRI pathological conditions, thereby affecting mitochondria’s ability as biosynthetic and signalling hubs [ ].
The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) results from proton translocation by respiratory complexes I, III, and IV and, together with the proton gradient (ΔpH), creates the electrochemical driving force for ATP synthesis [ ]. During myocardial ischaemia, impaired oxygen delivery quickly stops electron flow through the respiratory chain, first causing early defects in complex I and, with ongoing ischaemia, leading to progressive damage of complexes III and IV [ ]. This disruption in the chain and the resulting failure of ATP production cause fluctuations and a decline in ΔΨm, which both indicate and worsen mitochondrial dysfunction by further impairing oxidative phosphorylation [ , ].
Building on the collapse of membrane potential and impaired oxidative phosphorylation, the mPTP emerges as a pivotal mediator of IRI [ ]. Although the exact molecular makeup of the mPTP remains debated, recent genetic and electrophysiological research suggests that the F1F0 ATP synthase complex, especially its c-subunit ring and dimeric interface, is the main pore-forming component regulated by cyclophilin D (CypD). Meanwhile, the adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT) contributes to low-conductance channel activity. Proteins like VDAC and the phosphate carrier serve secondary regulatory roles [ , ]. During IRI, mPTP opening is triggered by mitochondrial calcium and phosphate overload accumulated during ischaemia, the restoration of normal pH, a decreased ADP:ATP ratio upon reperfusion, increased uptake of stored calcium into energised mitochondria via the uniporter, and a burst of ROS from reverse electron transport. These factors collectively induce CypD-sensitive conformational changes in the F1F0 ATP synthase complex [ , ]. Sustained mPTP opening equalises protons across the membrane, dissipating the membrane potential and uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation, which results in ATP depletion and further disruption of the respiratory chain [ ]. At the same time, the pore allows the release of cyt c and other intermembrane proteins into the cytosol, where they activate caspase-9 and caspase-3 to trigger mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis [ , ]. Additionally, mPTP-mediated respiratory dysfunction enhances ROS production in a feedback loop of ROS-induced ROS release (RIRR), thereby promoting the shift from reversible to irreversible cardiomyocyte injury [ ].
In response to these bioenergetic and redox disturbances, mitochondrial morphology dynamically adjusts through the balanced actions of fission and fusion [ ], with fission driven by the GTPase Drp1 along with its adaptors Mff, Fis1, and MID49/51, and fusion reliant on mitofusins and Opa1 [ ]. During ischaemia–reperfusion, elevated ROS activate calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), leading to the phosphorylation of Drp1 and Mff, disruption of Opa1-mediated fusion, and degradation of F-actin, collectively shifting the balance towards mitochondrial fragmentation [ , ]. The resulting shift towards mitochondrial fragmentation reduces ATP production efficiency, dissipates membrane potential, and predisposes organelles to selective removal. This elimination takes place via mitophagy, a process initiated by the loss of membrane potential in damaged mitochondria [ , ]. Three interconnected recognition mitophagy pathways are well known: the PINK1/Parkin-dependent ubiquitination route, the BNIP3/NIX receptor-mediated direct binding to LC3, and the FUNDC1 receptor-driven dephosphorylation activation under hypoxic conditions. In ischaemia–reperfusion models, the PINK1/Parkin pathway can provide cytoprotection but may also induce cell death if overactivated [ ]. BNIP3/NIX supports mitophagy during hypoxia alongside apoptotic or necroptotic signalling, demonstrating bidirectional regulatory capacity [ ]. Conversely, FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy, once dephosphorylated, mainly exerts protective effects on the heart by removing damaged mitochondria, reducing ROS production, and inhibiting mPTP opening, thereby improving outcomes in myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion injury [ , ]. Mitophagy can eliminate defects during IR injury and is seen as a protective or adaptive mechanism. However, uncontrolled or excessive mitophagy may lead to a shortage of functional or healthy mitochondria capable of producing ATP, resulting in impaired cell survival [ ]. The removal of defective mitophagy mainly depends on their low ΔΨm [ ](Fig.  ). 

## Targeting energy metabolism as a therapeutic intervention for AMI
Normal energy metabolism is critically essential for maintaining cardiac function. At rest, mitochondrial activity generates 6– 30 kg of ATP daily [ ], enabling the heart to meet the energetic demands of approximately 100,000 beats per day [ ]. Protecting mitochondrial integrity is thus crucial for preserving cardiac energy output. During ischaemia, fatty acid and glucose oxidation are inhibited, and anaerobic glycolysis is increased [ ]. During the period of reperfusion, the oxidation of fatty acids and glucose is restored, whereas high rates of FAO can inhibit glucose oxidation. Abnormally high rates of fatty acid oxidation and low rates of glucose oxidation are essential contributors to the severity of ischaemic heart disease [ ]. This highlights the critical need for therapies targeting mitochondrial energetics to mitigate such injury. Acute interventions should prioritise maintaining cardiac energy homeostasis, preventing mitochondrial dysfunction and mitochondrial-mediated cell death, and reducing oxidative stress.

## Drug therapy through different pathologic mechanisms

## Alter metabolic substrates
In 1962, Sodi-Pallares demonstrated in experimental models that glucose-insulin-potassium (GIK) therapy could enhance glycolytic flux and protect the myocardium from ischaemic damage [ ]. The rationale behind GIK lies in its ability to reduce cardiac oxygen demand by favouring glucose utilisation over FAO, thereby compensating for diminished ATP production caused by hypoxia. Glycolysis is a crucial source of ATP during ischaemia and mitigates reperfusion injury when sustained during early reperfusion, thereby enhancing functional recovery and reducing cell death [ ]. Although boosting glucose availability may not rescue cardiomyocytes in the core ischaemic zone, it helps preserve viability in the border regions, thereby limiting infarct expansion and adverse remodelling [ ].
Emerging evidence highlights multiple pharmacological agents that protect cardiomyocytes during MIRI by modulating cardiac metabolism to enhance glucose utilisation. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, initially developed for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes, exhibit cardioprotective effects. Clinical studies demonstrate that SGLT2 inhibitors significantly reduce cardiovascular mortality and heart failure hospitalisation in patients with type 2 diabetes and prior MI. However, despite preconceptions, patients with acute AMI have been relatively understudied in SGLT2 inhibitor outcome trials to date [ ]. Mechanistically, SGLT2 inhibitors limit infarct size and fibrosis by suppressing excessive autophagy via Na+/H+ exchanger 1 (NHE1) inhibition [ ] while also activating cardioprotective pathways like STAT3 and PI3K to mitigate IRI [ ]. Dichloroacetate (DCA) directly promotes glucose oxidation by inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK), the enzyme that phosphorylates and inactivates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, thereby activating PDH and restoring aerobic glycolysis during reperfusion [ , ]. DCA treatment significantly reduced the infarction size caused by I/R and improved cardiac function in mice through the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signalling pathway [ ]. Thiazolidinediones (e.g., rosiglitazone, pioglitazone) enhance myocardial glucose uptake and oxidation while acting as insulin sensitizers [ , ]. Similarly, metformin, an activator of AMPK, enhances glucose uptake by inducing the recruitment of GLUT4 to the plasma membrane [ ], thereby improving systolic function, reducing myocardial cell apoptosis, attenuating post-infarction fibrotic remodelling, and exerting anti-inflammatory activity in cardiac tissues [ ]. Finally, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogues promote myocardial glucose utilisation by stimulating insulin secretion and enhancing insulin sensitivity, thereby optimising the selection of energy substrates during ischaemic stress [ , ].
Studies have demonstrated that shifting mitochondrial substrate preference from FAs to glucose by inhibiting myocardial FAO while promoting carbohydrate oxidation represents a promising dual strategy to attenuate myocardial IRI. This metabolic reprogramming enhances clinical outcomes in myocardial ischaemia by improving ATP production efficiency and reducing oxidative stress, a principle that is increasingly validated in therapeutic applications. Mechanistically, excessive FAO during I/R exacerbates lipid toxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction, whereas enhancing glucose oxidation restores metabolic flexibility, limits ROS generation, and mitigates cellular damage [ , , ]. Agents targeting this balance highlight the synergistic potential of simultaneously suppressing harmful lipid pathways and activating protective carbohydrate metabolism.
Malonyl-CoA is a key endogenous regulator of FAO that inhibits mitochondrial fatty acid uptake by suppressing CPT-1 [ ]. Preclinical studies in mice and pigs demonstrate that inhibiting malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MCD), the enzyme responsible for its degradation, increases cardiac malonyl-CoA levels and shifts energy metabolism from FAO to glucose oxidation [ , ]. This metabolic reprogramming reduces proton accumulation, enhances the coupling between glycolysis and glucose oxidation, and improves post-ischaemic cardiac function, positioning MCD inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for IRI [ ]. TMZ is a metabolic modulator and antianginal agent that protects the heart by shifting energy metabolism from FAO to glucose oxidation. It achieves this by selectively inhibiting mitochondrial LC 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase, the final enzyme in the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway, thereby enhancing PDH activity to restore the glucose oxidation-glycolysis balance, reduce oxygen demand, and mitigate proton and Ca2+ overload, as well as oxidative stress [ , ]. Clinically, a randomised, double-blind trial in patients with ST-Segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) demonstrated that early TMZ administration before reperfusion significantly reduced infarct size, decreased microvascular obstruction, and improved the myocardial salvage index compared to placebo [ ]. These outcomes align with its antioxidant effects, which enhance superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase while reducing lipid peroxidation, stabilising cardiomyocyte membranes, and suppressing apoptosis, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Building on this metabolic reprogramming paradigm, other agents, such as carvedilol, 4-[ethyl (dimethyl) ammonio] butanoate (methyl-GBB), and sphingosylphosphorylcholine (SPC), further expand therapeutic options. Carvedilol activates AMPK signalling to suppress FAO and enhance glucose oxidation, reducing infarct size in mice [ ]. Methyl-GBB inhibits L-carnitine transport to limit toxic lipid accumulation [ ], while SPC modulates the transcription factor GATAD1 to balance FAO and glucose oxidation, synergistically mitigating IRI through complementary metabolic targets [ ](Table  ). 

## Prevention of mitochondrial dysfunction

## Antioxidants
Antioxidants play a crucial role in mitigating MIRI by scavenging ROS, suppressing apoptosis, and maintaining mitochondrial integrity. Therapeutic strategies targeting oxidative stress encompass three synergistic approaches: metabolic modulators to suppress ROS generation at its source, direct antioxidants to neutralise existing free radicals, and multitarget agents that concurrently enhance endogenous defences and disrupt downstream pathological signalling cascades.
Metabolic modulators, such as malonate and aminooxyacetate (AOA), target the root of ROS overproduction. Malonate, a competitive inhibitor of SDH, prevents succinate-driven ROS surges during reperfusion by blocking RET at mitochondrial complex Ⅰ, thereby reducing infarct size in mice with AMI [ , ]. Similarly, AOA transiently inhibits the MAS, thereby enhancing mitochondrial complex Ⅰ-linked oxidative phosphorylation capacity while attenuating ROS emission during late reperfusion [ , ].
Direct antioxidants, exemplified by vitamin C, act as frontline defenders against oxidative damage [ ]. Vitamin C not only neutralises free radicals but also upregulates endogenous enzymes, such as manganese superoxide dismutase (Mn-SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thereby synergistically enhancing the efficiency of the mitochondrial ETC [ ]. Clinical evidence further supports its therapeutic value in improving outcomes after MI [ ].
Multitarget agents bridge the upstream regulation of ROS and the downstream pathological cascades. Idebenone, a synthetic analogue of coenzyme Q10, exemplifies this dual functionality. Unlike conventional antioxidants, it bypasses mitochondrial complex Ⅰ dysfunction by replenishing the quinone pool, restoring electron flux downstream of damaged complex Ⅰ, and sustaining oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. Beyond metabolic salvage, idebenone attenuates ROS-triggered ferroptosis and excessive autophagy by modulating the ROS-AMPK-mTOR axis, thereby preserving cardiomyocyte viability [ ]. Natural compounds, such as dioscin, further demonstrate multimodal efficacy by downregulating oxidative stress mediators, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhancing the activities of TCA cycle enzymes and respiratory chain complexes [ , ]. Similarly, protocatechualdehyde, a bioactive component of Salvia miltiorrhiza, converges ROS scavenging with the inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways, thereby disrupting the cycle of oxidative damage and apoptosis [ , ](Table  ). 

## Calcium antagonist
Calcium antagonists mitigate MIRI by inhibiting Ca2+ influx, suppressing Na+/Ca2+ exchange, and reducing H⁺ efflux. These mechanisms collectively attenuate Ca2+ overload while preserving mitochondrial integrity and cellular homeostasis. Among pharmacological interventions, dexmedetomidine emerges as a multifunctional agent. By activating α2-adrenergic receptors, dexmedetomidine enhances STAT3 phosphorylation, which in turn suppresses Ca2+ overload, inflammatory cascades, and oxidative stress [ ]. Furthermore, it modulates metabolic pathways by promoting the phosphorylation of the glucocorticoid receptor, leading to the downregulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4). This metabolic shift reduces lactate accumulation, stabilises mitochondrial morphology, and inhibits ferroptosis, ultimately alleviating MIRI through dual protection of mitochondrial function and redox balance [ ].
In contrast, classical calcium channel blockers, such as nifedipine and verapamil, directly target L-VDCCs, thereby limiting Ca2+ influx during ischaemia. Beyond their channel-blocking effects, these agents influence the Ca2+/cAMP/ADO pathway, where adenosine (ADO) activation of A1 receptors (A1R) demonstrates therapeutic potential in preventing fatal arrhythmias linked to AMI [ ]. Verapamil also exhibits antioxidant properties through the activation of SIRT1 signalling, offering a novel mechanism to counteract ischaemic heart disease by enhancing cellular resilience to oxidative damage [ ].
Expanding the therapeutic arsenal, SGLT2i exert cardioprotection by inhibiting the NHE. By reducing intracellular sodium and Ca2+ overload, SGLT2i preserve ionic equilibrium in cardiomyocytes, thereby mitigating ischaemic injury [ ]. Dapagliflozin, a prominent SGLT2i, further amplifies cytoprotective signals by upregulating hypoxia-suppressed pathways involving SIRT1, SIRT3, FOXO4, and TP53. This multimodal action enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation while suppressing apoptosis and oxidative stress, fostering cell survival under hypoxic conditions [ ].
Xin-Ke-Shu (XKS), Guanxinshutong capsule (GXSTC), modified Yiqi decoction (MYQ), and other Chinese herbal compound preparations also regulate calcium signalling to alleviate Ca2+ overload during MI treatment, bridging ancient therapeutic wisdom with modern mechanistic insights to offer holistic cardioprotection [ ] (Table ). 

## Inhibitors of mPTP opening
mPTP is a critical therapeutic target in MIRI. Pharmacological strategies to inhibit mPTP opening can be categorised into two primary types: direct pore inhibitors and kinase pathway modulators. Cyclosporin A (CsA) directly blocks mPTP activation, reducing infarct size and preserving myocardial viability [ – ]. In studies conducted with obese rats, the combination of Tanshinone IIA (TIIA) and CsA has been shown to decrease myocardial cell apoptosis. This effect occurs through the modulation of mitochondrial function via the PI3K/Akt/Bad signalling pathway [ ]. Natural compounds like morin and rhynchophylline exhibit a broad range of protective effects. Morin directly inhibits the opening of mPTP while maintaining ΔΨm. It also downregulates apoptotic markers such as cyt c, caspase-9/3, and the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, ultimately reducing infarct size [ ]. Similarly, rhynchophylline alleviates oxidative stress and regulates genes related to mitochondrial function, providing a dual mechanism for suppressing mPTP activation and apoptosis [ ].
Kinase pathway modulators target upstream signalling cascades that regulate the opening of mPTP. Levosimendan is a calcium-sensitising inotropic agent that activates reperfusion injury salvage kinases (RISK) and mitochondrial ATP-sensitive potassium (mKATP) channels. This process effectively inhibits the opening of mPTP through Akt-mediated phosphorylation [ ]. Additionally, statins enhance this cardioprotective strategy by delaying the opening of mPTP through two mechanisms: the inactivation of glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) and the activation of mitochondrial uncoupling [ ]. Remifentanil preconditioning confers cardioprotection primarily through the activation of JAK2/STAT3 signalling, which can function independently of PI3K/Akt activation. Glycogen synthase kinase-3β is a crucial downstream effector of remifentanil-induced cardioprotection, serving as a critical checkpoint that stabilises mPTP closure and suppresses apoptotic signalling, promoting glycogen synthesis [ , ]. Furthermore, quercetin administered before reperfusion significantly increases STAT3 phosphorylation at Ser727, thereby maintaining mitochondrial integrity by preventing the opening of the mPTP and the subsequent release of cyt c and activation of caspase-3, ultimately reducing apoptotic cell death in the I/R myocardium [ ] (Table  ). 

## Mitochondrial transfer
Various therapeutic approaches, such as antioxidant treatment and gene therapy, can only partially improve mitochondrial function while failing to replenish the damaged mitochondria within a short time, thereby limiting the treatment efficiency for cardiac diseases. This limitation underscores the need for innovative replenishment strategies.
Mitochondrial transplantation presents a promising therapeutic approach by directly targeting both the quantity and quality of mitochondria. This strategy encompasses two primary methods: naked mitochondrial transplantation and cell-mediated transfer [ ]. Naked mitochondrial transplantation involves the direct administration of isolated mitochondria through intracoronary or intramyocardial injection. Preclinical studies in porcine models have demonstrated that administering autologous mitochondria at the time of reperfusion can reduce infarct size by 40– 60% and preserve left ventricular ejection fraction by restoring ATP levels and integrating mitochondrial DNA [ ]. In a notable clinical pilot study, five pediatric patients with post-surgical myocardial injury showed improved ventricular strain within 48–72 h following intramyocardial injection of autologous skeletal muscle-derived mitochondria, with no adverse effects reported [ ].
Cell-mediated transfer utilises natural intercellular communication mechanisms, such as tunnelling nanotubes (TNTs), gap junctions, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), to deliver functional mitochondria to injured cells [ ]. For example, human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs) implanted in ischaemic myocardium in rodent models restored cardiomyocyte bioenergetics through mitochondrial transfer, as confirmed by the detection of donor mitochondrial DNA in recipient cells and an improvement in cardiac output [ ].
However, mitochondrial transplantation therapies are invasive procedures that can be costly and may require extended recovery periods. Researchers have developed a therapeutic product that enables mitochondrial therapy for the ischaemic heart through an oral delivery method. This approach involves engineering mitochondria to specifically target tissues that overexpress ROS, which are characteristic of ischaemic heart disease. These engineered mitochondria have demonstrated significant therapeutic effects in animal models of both acute and chronic ischaemic heart disease [ , ].
Collectively, mitochondrial transfer is an effective treatment for MI because it enhances energy production and reduces oxidative stress. By transferring mitochondria, ATP levels are restored, and ROS are balanced, thereby protecting cells and promoting healing. These processes help ensure cell survival and mitigate damage, making mitochondrial transfer a promising therapeutic strategy [ ].

## Ischaemic conditioning

## Ischaemic preconditioning
Cardioprotective interventions such as ischaemic preconditioning (IPC), ischemic postconditioning (IPost), and remote ischaemic conditioning (RIC) have consistently reduced infarct size in preclinical models of MIRI. IPC, first described in 1986, involves brief cycles of non-lethal ischaemia and reperfusion that activate endogenous signalling pathways and protective kinases [ ]. Early responses occur within minutes via bradykinin-mediated activation of G protein-coupled receptors, while late protection develops 12–24 h later through upregulation of endothelial antioxidant enzymes [ ]. Mechanistically, IPC influences mitochondrial function by partially uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation, preserving electron transport chain integrity, limiting cyt c release, and reducing ROS production [ , ]. The opening of mitochondrial ATP-sensitive potassium channels (KATP channels) contributes to this mild uncoupling and may engage the PI3K-GSK-3β signalling pathway to prevent mPTP opening, thereby decreasing ATP depletion and cell death [ , , ].
During ischaemia, impaired β-oxidation causes a rapid accumulation of long-chain acyl-CoA species within cardiomyocytes [ ]. These metabolites disproportionately depress intermediate respiratory states, exerting a direct regulatory effect on respiratory flux [ ]. Additionally, they competitively bind the adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT), impairing ADP/ATP exchange and consequently inhibiting oxygen-linked energy metabolism [ ]. Ischaemic preconditioning enhances mitochondrial fatty-acid import and use and helps maintain a balanced intramitochondrial acyl-CoA/CoA ratio [ , ]. Administering L-carnitine or propionyl-L-carnitine before ischaemia counteracts these metabolic disturbances by converting excess acyl-CoA into acylcarnitines, which buffer intracellular acyl-group load, and by promoting mitochondrial fatty-acid transport along with pyruvate dehydrogenase-dependent oxidative metabolism. Accordingly, experimental studies in isolated heart preparations and in vivo models show that pretreatment with these agents preserves mitochondrial respiration, accelerates ATP restoration, improves contractile recovery, and reduces infarct size in a dose-dependent manner [ – ]. However, it should be noted that most evidence comes from animal and isolated-heart studies, and clinical validation remains limited.

## Ischaemic postconditioning
IPost refers to the process of repeated ischaemic and brief reflow immediately at the beginning of reperfusion, followed by continuous reflow, to enhance the myocardial tolerance to ischaemia. It is typically performed within a short time (1 min) of the start of blood reperfusion, following a blood occlusion and reflux for four cycles. It protects the heart against IRI by activating the prosurvival kinase PI3-Akt and its downstream targets, eNOS and p70S6K. Reducing superoxide anion generation, as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tissue necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), intracellular and mitochondrial Ca2+ loading, and the opening of mPTP are also potential protective mechanisms [ , ]. The myocardial protection intensity of IPost is not inferior to that of IPC; however, some difficulties remain in its clinical implementation. IPost can better solve the problem of the timing of myocardial ischaemia but still cannot avoid the traumatic injury of the myocardium [ ].
IPost can be applied in AMI patients during PPCI by serially inflating and deflating (for 30–60 s) the coronary angioplasty balloon immediately after opening the infarct-related coronary artery to interrupt reperfusion. This manoeuvre has been shown to reduce MI size in AMI patients in small clinical studies [ ]. Although PPost and IPost are widely used in clinical practice and have a broad range of application prospects, they still cannot address all clinical problems in the pathogenesis and treatment of MIRI. Further in-depth studies are needed in the future to improve clinical efficacy continually.

## Remote ischaemic conditioning
RIC, which involves transient cycles of limb ischaemia and reperfusion in the upper extremity, has demonstrated protective effects on distant organs in both preclinical and clinical studies [ ]. Limb RIC has the advantage over IPost in that it can be applied non-invasively by simply inflating and deflating a pneumatic cuff placed on either the arm or leg, inducing three-minute cycles of brief ischaemia and reperfusion. In elderly patients with acute ischaemic stroke complicating AMI, RIC is a safe and effective therapy to reduce 3-month adverse clinical outcomes if initiated within 24 h of onset and performed twice daily for two weeks [ ]. Again, some small proof-of-concept studies reported limitations of MI size in AMI patients treated with either thrombolysis or PPCI [ , ]. However, limb RIC failed to improve clinical outcomes (death and HF hospitalisation at one year) in the 5400 patient CONDI-2/ERIC-PPCI trial [ ]. Although Many experiments demonstrate a significant and highly reproducible beneficial effect of RIC, this effect was highly heterogeneous.
The exact mechanism of RIC is unknown, but both humoral factors and upstream nervous system activations are implicated. Studies have shown that the accumulation of EVs in the injured myocardium can mediate infarct size reduction through RIC. The underlying mechanism involves the modulation of EV miRNA content that targets the mTOR pathway [ ]. Furthermore, repeated remote ischaemic conditioning (rRIC) prevented probable reductions in cardiomyocyte β-adrenergic and RAAS responsiveness and subsequently improved left ventricular function [ ].

## Conclusion
Various complex metabolic changes influence MIRI. These include an imbalance between FAO and glucose oxidation, the generation of ROS induced by succinate, and mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from the opening of the mPTP. Therapeutic approaches targeting these pathways, such as metabolic modulators, antioxidants, and mitochondrial transplantation, show significant promise in preclinical studies. Pharmacological agents, such as SGLT2 inhibitors and TMZ, illustrate the potential to improve ATP efficiency and reduce oxidative stress. Although ischaemic conditioning strategies face limitations in clinical practicality, they provide valuable insights into mechanisms of endogenous cardioprotection. Understanding metabolic remodelling during IRI not only clarifies pathophysiological mechanisms but also paves the way for innovative therapies aimed at enhancing myocardial salvage and reducing complications after AMI.
Energy metabolism plays a central role in the pathogenesis and progression of AMI and its associated IRI. The intricate imbalance between fatty acid and glucose oxidation, excessive succinate-driven ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and Ca2+ overload collectively drive cardiomyocyte death and adverse cardiac remodelling. Recent advances in pharmacological modulation of metabolism, mitochondrial-targeted therapies, and ischaemic conditioning strategies have provided compelling preclinical evidence for cardioprotection. Agents such as SGLT2 inhibitors, trimetazidine, and malonyl-CoA decarboxylase inhibitors exemplify the therapeutic potential of metabolic reprogramming, while antioxidant approaches, mPTP inhibition, mitochondrial transplantation, and conditioning paradigms highlight additional avenues to preserve energy homeostasis and limit tissue injury. Despite encouraging progress, translation into routine clinical practice remains challenging. The heterogeneous outcomes of clinical trials emphasise the need for more precise patient stratification, deeper mechanistic understanding, and optimisation of therapeutic timing and delivery. Looking forward, integrating multiomics technologies with advanced preclinical models may accelerate the identification of novel metabolic targets and facilitate the design of personalised interventions. Ultimately, combining metabolic modulation with established reperfusion therapies holds promise to enhance myocardial salvage, reduce complications, and improve long-term outcomes for patients with AMI.
