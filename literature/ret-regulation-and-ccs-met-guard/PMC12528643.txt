The 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Male Genitourinary System (2022) categorizes SCSTs into five major subtypes: Leydig cell tumors, Sertoli cell tumors [including large cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumors (LCCSCT) and Sertoli cell tumors not otherwise specified (SCT-NOS)], granulosa cell tumors, fibroma thecoma family of tumors, and mixed or unclassified sex cord-stromal tumors [ , ]. Notably, molecular profiling has revealed distinguishing genetic features between SCT-NOS and LCCSCT: the former harbors point mutations in exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene, leading to dysregulation of the β-catenin pathway, whereas the latter is typically associated with loss-of-function mutations in PRKAR1A [ – ].
Over the past few years, inflammatory and nested testicular sex cord tumor (IN-SCT), a novel type of testicular sex cord tumor, has been increasingly recognized and reported [ , ]. Clinically, patients typically present with painless testicular swelling, and imaging studies often lack specific features to guide diagnosis. Histologically, IN-SCT is characterized by distinctive nests of tumor cells accompanied by prominent inflammatory infiltration. Since its initial description, IN-SCT has posed substantial diagnostic challenges due to its histopathological overlap with other testicular neoplasms, such as seminoma or lymphoma. The identification of EWSR1::ATF1 gene fusion through molecular analysis has emerged as a crucial diagnostic marker, helping to distinguish IN-SCT from its histological mimics [ ].
Here, we report a rare case of IN-SCT in a 36-year-old male, detailing the clinical presentation, pathological findings, immunohistochemical features, and molecular results. Additionally, we review the relevant literature to systematically summarize the diagnostic pitfalls, therapeutic strategies, and prognostic implications associated with IN-SCT, aiming to provide valuable insights for pathologists and clinicians managing similar cases.

## Case presentation
A 36-year-old man was admitted to a local hospital in June 2024 with painless swelling of the left testis. Imaging findings revealed a well-defined left testicular lesion measuring 6.0 × 5.0 × 3.3 cm. Ultrasound showed a heterogeneous echotexture, primarily featuring an irregular dumbbell-shaped echogenic focus with mild hypervascularity on Doppler imaging (Fig.  A). A 1.5 × 1.2 cm hypoechoic area with posterior acoustic enhancement, indicative of liquefactive necrosis, was observed within the lesion (Fig.  B). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) confirmed the sonographic findings, displaying corresponding morphological features and enhancement patterns (Fig.  C). Serum tumor markers (AFP, HCG) were normal, but CA125 was elevated (> 1000 U/mL). The patient underwent radical left orchiectomy, and the initial postoperative diagnosis suggested seminoma at an outside institution. Subsequently, an external consultation in Shanghai raised suspicion of ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), prompting a referral to our institution for further evaluation.

 

Microscopically, the tumor exhibited ill-defined boundaries with the surrounding tissue and infiltrated irregularly into adjacent structures. Histologically, neoplastic cells were arranged in solid sheets, nested structures, and a reticular growth pattern (Fig.  A). The stroma exhibited dense inflammatory infiltrates consisting of neutrophils, eosinophils, and lymphocytes (Fig.  A, B). As shown in Fig.  C and D, the neoplastic cells were medium-sized and epithelioid, exhibiting round to oval nuclei with moderate nuclear pleomorphism. The cytoplasm demonstrated moderate to abundant volume, displaying regional heterogeneity characterized by alternating zones of finely granular eosinophilic staining and optically clear areas with prominent vacuolization. Notably, the vacuolated regions of the cytoplasm frequently exhibited an intricate, anastomosing network of eosinophilic strands, consistent with the classical “spider-web” pattern (Fig.  E, F). Nuclear features included well-demarcated nuclear membranes, evenly distributed chromatin, and 1–2 prominent nucleoli (Fig.  F). Mitotic figures were readily observed, averaging 5 per 2 mm2 (Fig.  G). Additionally, localized areas of geographic necrosis were identified (Fig.  H).

 

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed weak expression of inhibin-α and diffuse expression of broad-spectrum cytokeratin (CK), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and CD30 (Fig.  ). β-catenin was confined to the cytoplasm with no nuclear accumulation, and Wilms Tumor 1 (WT-1) exhibited diffuse nuclear staining in tumor cells. In contrast, the tumor cells were non-reactive for steroidogenic factor-1 (SF-1), placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP), sal-like protein 4 (SALL4), octamer-binding transcription factor 3/4 (OCT3/4), HCG, CD117, leukocyte common antigen (LCA), CD2, CD5, CD7, and T-cell intracellular antigen-1 (TIA-1). Molecular analysis using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) identified EWSR1 gene rearrangement (Fig.  ). We counted 100 tumor cells and found that 45% of them exhibited EWSR1 gene rearrangements. Based on the combined immunohistochemical and molecular findings, the final diagnosis was IN-SCT.

 

 

A regimen of 4 cycles of TP chemotherapy was delivered, comprising cisplatin (45 mg/m²) and albumin-bound paclitaxel (260 mg/m²). Due to thrombocytopenia, the patient was subsequently switched to 2 cycles of DP chemotherapy (cisplatin 45 mg/m² + docetaxel 75 mg/m²). By March 2025, 10 months post-surgery, pelvic CT imaging after chemotherapy showed no residual or recurrent tumor in the surgical area, with the left testis absent (Fig.  D).

## Discussion

## Differential diagnosis and distinctive features of IN-SCT
IN-SCT presents significant diagnostic challenges due to histological overlap with other SCSTs, particularly SCT-NOS and LCCSCT. SCT-NOS typically occurs in middle-aged adults [ , ] and exhibits tubular, nested, or solid growth patterns, without significant stromal inflammation [ ]. It is frequently associated with CTNNB1 mutations, resulting in nuclear β-catenin accumulation and cyclin D1 overexpression via Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation [ , , ]. LCCSCT, by contrast, often affects younger individuals, displays stromal calcification [ ], and is associated with PRKAR1A mutations [ , ], particularly in the setting of Carney complex [ , ].
IN-SCT must also be distinguished from other testicular neoplasms. Seminoma commonly presents as painless unilateral testicular masses and may be associated with retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, mimicking the clinical features of IN-SCT. However, unlike IN-SCT, seminoma expresses PLAP, SALL4, OCT3/4, and CD117 [ ]. LCTs, the most frequent subtype of SCSTs [ ], are typically inhibin-positive and lack stromal inflammation, with histological features such as eosinophilic cytoplasm and occasional Reinke crystals [ ]. They may harbor FOXL2 or LHCGR mutations, particularly in cases with malignant potential [ , ].
Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), particularly the ALK-negative subtype, is another potential mimic. Histologically, it is characterized by large pleomorphic cells, including hallmark cells with distinctive horseshoe- or kidney-shaped nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm [ , ]. The absence of sex cord-stromal markers such as inhibin and SF-1, together with LCA negativity and variable expression of T-cell antigens, supports its hematopoietic lineage [ ].
Although IN-SCT and clear cell sarcoma (CCS) share EWSR1 rearrangements, the two entities differ markedly in histological and immunophenotypic profiles [ – ]. CCS is typically found in soft tissues and demonstrates melanocytic differentiation with MITF activation, but lacks expression of sex cord markers [ , ]. This distinction underscores the necessity of integrating histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features to ensure accurate classification.

## Clinical summary and literature insights of IN-SCT
IN-SCT is emerging as a distinct subtype of testicular sex cord-stromal tumors, with increasing recognition over recent years. While individual case reports remain limited, a consistent clinicopathological pattern has been observed. As shown in Table , the median age at diagnosis is 34 years (range, 20–60), with all patients presenting with painless testicular masses and no endocrine-related symptoms. Tumor sizes range from 0.9 to 6.0 cm (median, 3.5 cm), consistent with other SCSTs. Histological analysis reveals a predominance of nested and solid sheet-like growth patterns, with tumor cells exhibiting eosinophilic cytoplasm, uniformly shaped nuclei ranging from round to oval, clear nucleoli, and minimal mitotic activity (median: 1/10 HPF, range: 0–8). The stroma is marked by prominent inflammatory cell infiltration, predominantly composed of lymphocytes and eosinophils. Additionally, 13 out of 15 cases demonstrate geographic necrosis, a distinctive feature not observed in SCT-NOS or LCCSCT [ , ].
Clinically, IN-SCT displays an aggressive course. Among the 12 cases with follow-up, 91.7% developed metastases, most commonly involving the lungs and retroperitoneum, but also affecting the contralateral testis and bone (Table  ). The progression-free survival ranged from 2 to 96 months, with a median of 23 months. Three patients died from disease progression during follow-up.
Immunohistochemically, IN-SCT consistently expresses SCST markers such as inhibin and SF-1, along with diffuse CD30 positivity (10/12) and cytoplasmic β-catenin localization (13/13), setting it apart from other SCSTs (Table ) [ ] (Table ). Notably, molecular analysis has identified EWSR1::ATF1 gene fusions in the majority of tested cases (11/14), confirmed through FISH or NGS, with our case also exhibiting an EWSR1 break (Table ). 

 

 
Taken together, the combination of inflammatory histology, distinctive immunophenotype, and recurrent molecular alterations supports the classification of IN-SCT as a separate pathological entity. Recognizing this subtype is vital for avoiding diagnostic pitfalls and assessing its clinical aggressiveness.

## Treatment and prognosis
Due to the rarity of IN-SCT, no standardized treatment protocol exists. Radical orchiectomy remains the primary intervention (14/15 cases), with a subset of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy (3/15) or radiotherapy (5/15). In our case, six cycles of cisplatin-based chemotherapy achieved clinical remission, suggesting potential benefit in advanced or metastatic settings.
Given the frequent presence of inflammatory infiltrates in IN-SCT, including CD8 + T lymphocytes, there may be potential for responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [ ]. Moreover, the discovery of the EWSR1::ATF1 fusion raises the possibility of targeted therapeutic strategies as a future direction. These hypotheses, however, require further validation in larger patient cohorts.

## Conclusion
IN-SCT is a rare and aggressive variant of sex cord-stromal tumors, distinguished by a triad of prominent inflammatory stroma, EWSR1::ATF1 fusion, and infiltrative growth. These features set it apart from SCT-NOS and LCCSCT, supporting its recognition as a distinct pathological entity. While surgical resection remains the mainstay treatment, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy and targeted molecular therapies deserves further exploration. Accurate diagnosis relies on integrating histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular findings. Ongoing case accumulation and research are crucial to defining optimal management strategies and improving patient outcomes.
