## Abstract
Spinal cord injury (SCI) repair is severely restricted by a hostile post-injury microenvironment that drives endogenous neural stem cells (eNSCs) toward astroglial scar formation rather than neuronal regeneration. Here, we developed a dual-functional “molecule–carrier–scaffold” strategy that combines engineered exosomes with a sustained-release hydrogel to simultaneously remodel the inflammatory niche and redirect eNSCs fate. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes were engineered to display a neurotrophic factor cocktail (GDNF/NT3/IGF1; termed EXOGNI) and then incorporated into an injectable, in situ photocrosslinkable hydrogel composed of ionic liquid-modified cellulose nanocrystal-reinforced methacrylated silk fibroin (CNCs/SilMA). The resulting EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA scaffold exhibited a biomimetic porous architecture, spinal cord-compatible mechanical properties, and sustained local release of exosomes during the acute–subacute repair window.
In a mouse complete spinal cord transection model, a single implantation of EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA attenuated acute neuroinflammation and promoted endogenous repair. Lineage tracing showed that the majority of lesion-region Tuj1+ cells were derived from the traced endogenous lineage, and EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA significantly reduced astrocytic differentiation while increasing neuronal and oligodendroglial lineage outputs. Mechanistically, EXOGNI acted through a dual route: uptake by macrophages/microglia contributed to inflammatory niche modulation, while direct uptake by eNSCs supported fate instruction. Inhibitor studies further indicated that PI3K–AKT and Wnt/Ca2+–CaMKII signaling functioned as parallel pro-differentiation modules, with reduced sustained β-catenin signaling associated with neuronal lineage commitment. These cellular and molecular changes were accompanied by improved motor/sensory recovery and electrophysiological conduction. Collectively, this engineered exosome-loaded hydrogel provides a combinatorial biomaterial platform that reshapes the inhibitory SCI niche and harnesses endogenous neural stem cell-based repair.
Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Engineered exosomes, Sustained-release hydrogel scaffold, Endogenous neural stem cells, Cell fate redirection, Neural regeneration

## Introduction
Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating disorder of the central nervous system that leads to irreversible neurological dysfunction below the level of injury, severely impairing motor, sensory, and autonomic functions [ ]. Following SCI, the excessive release of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth-inhibitory molecules, together with insufficient neurotrophic support and glial scar formation, generates a hostile microenvironment characterized by inflammation, nutritional deficiency, and limited regenerative potential [ ]. Given the highly complex pathophysiology of SCI, single-modality therapies are insufficient to achieve meaningful neuronal regeneration and functional recovery. Instead, combinatorial treatments should be applied to this complex, multifaceted disease process [ , ]. However, standalone exosome therapies are limited by rapid clearance, poor lesion retention, and unstable local bioavailability, whereas hydrogel-only scaffolds often lack durable bioactivity and precise spatiotemporal cue presentation, thereby restricting functional repair. These complementary limitations support the development of integrated carrier–scaffold systems for sustained local delivery of bioactive cues [ , ].
Although the adult spinal cord harbors endogenous neural stem cells (eNSCs), which become activated and migrate toward the lesion site after injury, their neuronal differentiation is strongly inhibited [ ]. Instead, most activated eNSCs differentiate into reactive astrocytes that contribute to glial scar formation, further blocking axonal regrowth and neural circuit remodeling [ , ]. Thus, overcoming the inhibitory niche, directing eNSCs differentiation toward neurons, and promoting functional neural circuit remodeling remain urgent challenges in SCI repair.
Compared with exosomes derived from neural stem cells, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs)-derived exosomes are widely used in tissue regeneration research for SCI primarily due to their ease of obtaining and culture [ ]. Exosomes derived from hUCMSCs have shown therapeutic promise in modulating the post-injury microenvironment by suppressing inflammation, regulating macrophage polarization, and stabilizing the blood–spinal cord barrier [ , , ]. Despite their anti-inflammatory benefits, hUCMSCs-derived exosomes alone often show limited ability to robustly drive endogenous neuroregeneration after severe SCI. In contrast, exogenous supplementation with growth factors such as glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), neurotrophin-3 (NT3), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) can promote axonal regeneration and regulate neural stem cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation via distinct signaling pathways [ , , , ]. Importantly, we selected GDNF/NT3/IGF1 because endogenous neurogenesis after SCI requires coordinated cues spanning neuronal survival, neurogenic engagement, and maturation, rather than a single trophic signal. NT3–based biomaterial delivery has been shown to elicit robust endogenous neurogenesis and functional recovery in severe SCI models [ , ], whereas IGF-1 provides pro-survival and pro-differentiation support for neural stem/progenitor cells through PI3K–AKT associated mechanisms [ ]. In parallel, GDNF is strongly linked to neuronal survival and axonogenesis and has been leveraged in SCI biomaterial platforms to promote neurogenesis and improve functional outcomes [ ]. These complementary roles provide the rationale for our engineered exosome-based tripartite “multi-ligand niche cue” design.
To address these challenges, recent efforts have focused on engineering exosomes to enhance their therapeutic potential. Anchoring growth factors onto the surface of exosomes derived from hUCMSCs improves their stability and biological activity. CD63, CD9, and CD81, highly conserved tetraspanins involved in exosome biogenesis, can be exploited for this purpose [ , ]. By expressing GDNF–CD81, NT3–CD9, and IGF1–CD63 fusion proteins in hUCMSCs, the corresponding growth factors can be enriched on exosomal membranes, generating growth factor-functionalized exosomes (EXOGDNF&NT3&IGF1, or EXOGNI). This design enables simultaneous regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment and the differentiation fate of neural stem cells.
In addition to exosome modification, biomaterial scaffolds play a crucial role in supporting neural repair. Silk fibroin, a natural protein with excellent biocompatibility, can be methacrylated to form photo-crosslinkable hydrogel (SilMA) that provide a permissive niche for neural regeneration [ ]. SilMA hydrogel exhibit excellent mechanical strength and cell adhesion properties, and encapsulating neural progenitor cells (NPCs) within SilMA/RGD hydrogel has been shown to enhance neuronal differentiation and axonal outgrowth [ ]. Moreover, cellulose nanocrystals (CNC), which are biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic, can further improve scaffold performance [ ]. Rod-like CNC possess a larger specific surface area, which facilitates increased drug loading capacity and further prolongs the release duration [ ]. While pristine rod-like CNC display poor dispersibility, surface modification improves their solubility, introduces positive charges to enhance exosome loading, and enables prolonged release of therapeutic agents.
In this study, we developed a multifunctional hydrogel scaffold that integrates growth factor-functionalized exosomes derived from hUCMSCs with an ion liquid surface-modified cellulose nanocrystal-reinforced silk fibroin methacrylated hydrogel (CNCs/SilMA). This hydrogel scaffold is designed to achieve dual regulation of the post-SCI microenvironment by suppressing inflammation and promoting the directed differentiation of eNSCs. This work, which integrates multiple therapeutic strategies targeting distinct pathological processes into a biomaterial structure, has been demonstrated to be effective in a mouse transection injury model, thereby offering a novel therapeutic strategy for spinal cord injury repair.

## Results
2.1.

## EXOGNI promotes neural stem cell proliferation and directs differentiation towards neurons and oligodendrocytes
Flow cytometry was performed to confirm the surface marker profile of hUCMSCs. The cells were negative for hematopoietic markers CD34 (1.22%) and CD45 (0.35%), while showing high positivity for mesenchymal stem cell markers CD73 (88.63%), CD90 (98.73%), and CD105 (99.08%), confirming their MSCs identity ( ). After induced differentiation culture of hUCMSCs, the results of Alizarin Red staining, Oil Red O staining, and Alcian Blue staining showed that the osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation induction cultures successfully induced hUCMSCs into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes ( ).
 A illustrates the schematic diagram of the design and isolation of growth factor-modified exosomes derived from hUCMSCs (EXOGNI). To verify successful genetic engineering of hUCMSCs, RNA was extracted from lentivirus-infected hUCMSCs (hUCMSCsGNI), reverse transcribed, and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Fusion gene expression of CD81–GDNF, CD63–IGF1, and CD9–NT3 was specifically detected in hUCMSCsGNI, but absent in wild-type hUCMSCs or cells transduced with negative control virus (hUCMSCsneg), indicating stable expression of the engineered constructs ( ).
 Exosomes were then isolated from the culture supernatants of hUCMSCs (EXO), hUCMSCsneg (EXOneg), and hUCMSCsGNI (EXOGNI) and characterized by NTA, TEM, and Western blot. NTA indicated similar particle size distributions, with peak diameters of ∼138.7 nm (EXO), 143.5 nm (EXOneg), and 128.2 nm (EXOGNI) ( B). Under identical preparation conditions, growth factor loading levels and particle characteristics showed reproducibility of engineered exosomes across independent batches ( ). TEM results showed that the morphology of exosomes from all three groups was clearly discernible, displaying a bilayer membrane structure ( C). These results suggest functional modification of exosomes did not alter their size or morphology. Western blot confirmed the presence of exosomal markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 in all groups. In EXOGNI, additional CD9 and CD81 fusion bands were detected (35–40 kDa), while the CD63 fusion band was less distinct due to post-translational modifications such as glycosylation ( D). To investigate whether growth factors GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 could be loaded into EXOGNI, Western blot was used to detect the relative levels of growth factors GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 in the three groups of exosomes. The results showed that compared to EXO and EXOneg, growth factors GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 were abundantly packaged into EXOGNI. Simultaneously, GDNF fusion bands, NT3 fusion bands, and IGF1 fusion bands were detected in EXOGNI, with molecular weights between 35 and 40 kDa ( E–J). Similarly, ELISA also confirmed that engineered EXOGNI have higher levels of growth factors GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 ( ). In addition, the fusion protein in engineered EXOGNI degrades more slowly compared to growth factors solely, indicating that EXOGNI have a protective effect on the stability of growth factors GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 ( ).
Given that exosomes derived from hUCMSCs exert anti-inflammatory effects primarily by suppressing pro-inflammatory responses and modulating macrophage polarization, we next examined whether genetic modification influenced these properties. RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce M1 polarization. Compared with untreated controls, LPS markedly increased iNOS expression. Co-treatment with EXO, EXOneg, or EXOGNI significantly reduced the expression of iNOS protein, with no significant differences observed among the three exosome groups ( ). Similarly, qRT-PCR revealed that LPS-induced upregulation of iNOS, TNFα, and IL-6 was significantly attenuated by EXOGNI. Meanwhile, there were no significant differences in the mRNA expression of iNOS, TNFα, and IL-6 among the three exosome groups ( ), confirming preserved anti-inflammatory activity. Following LPS stimulation of macrophages, we found that all three exosome formulations (EXO, EXOneg, and EXOGNI) exerted anti-inflammatory activity primarily through inhibition of p-p65, indicating suppression of NF-κB pathway activation ( ). To evaluate M2 polarization, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with IL-4. IL-4 significantly increased expression of the M2 marker CD206, which was further enhanced by co-treatment with EXO, EXOneg, or EXOGNI ( ). qRT-PCR analysis showed that IL-4 induction elevated Arg1 and IL-10 mRNA levels. EXOGNI further increased Arg1 and IL-10 expression, again with no significant differences among the three exosome groups ( ).
NSCs were extracted from the cerebral cortex of mouse embryos and cultured in suspension. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of Nestin and the transcription factor SOX2 in the NSC neurospheres, validating their identity ( ). NSCs were treated with three different types of exosomes for 24 h or 7 days, respectively, and EDU staining was performed to assess cell proliferation. The results showed that after 24 h of treatment with EXOGNI, the proportion of EDU-positive cells was significantly higher compared to the Control, EXO, and EXOneg groups ( ). However, after 7 days of treatment, no significant differences were observed among the Control, EXO, EXOneg, and EXOGNI groups ( ), indicating that EXOGNI promotes the proliferation of NSCs only at the early stage.
NSCs possess the potential to differentiate into astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, but after SCI, they primarily differentiate into astrocytes, with limited differentiation into mature neurons and oligodendrocytes. We assessed the differentiation of NSCs after 7 days of co-culture with different exosomes using qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence. Analysis of neuronal marker expression showed that EXOGNI treatment significantly increased the mRNA levels of the immature and mature neuronal markers Tuj1 and MAP2, respectively, while reducing the expression of the astrocytic marker GFAP, compared to the EXO and EXOneg groups ( ). The proportions of Tuj1+ and MAP2+ cells were significantly higher in the EXOGNI group than in the EXO and EXOneg groups, which themselves showed an increase over the Control group ( K, L, N, P). Conversely, the proportion of GFAP+ astrocytes was significantly lower in the EXOGNI group ( K, L, O). Staining for Olig2, a marker for oligodendrocyte precursor cells, revealed a significant increase only in the EXOGNI group, with no differences observed among the Control, EXO, and EXOneg groups ( M–Q).
Collectively, these results demonstrate that EXOGNI not only successfully encapsulate GDNF, NT3, and IGF1, but also retain the inherent immunomodulatory functions of exosomes derived from hUCMSCs, including suppression of pro-inflammatory responses and promotion of macrophage M2 polarization. Moreover, EXOGNI can influence the differentiation fate of NSCs, promoting their differentiation into mature neurons and oligodendrocytes while inhibiting astrocytic differentiation.

2.2.

## Preparation and characterization of CNCs/SilMA hydrogel
To enhance the gelation properties of silk fibroin (SF), we first modified it with glycidyl methacrylate ( A), successfully obtaining silk fibroin methacryloyl (SilMA). The introduction of methacryloyl groups not only preserved the original protein backbone structure but also endowed SilMA with photocrosslinking ability, forming a molecular foundation for constructing controlled microstructures. Concurrently, to improve its dispersion within the hydrogel matrix, cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) were surface-modified with an ionic liquid, obtaining modified CNCs ( B). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra confirmed the successful incorporation of methacrylate by showing characteristic peaks for C=C double bonds (δ = 6.2-6.0 and 5.8-5.6 ppm) ( C). Following modification with ionic liquid, the surface charge of CNCs shifted from negative to positive, as evidenced by zeta potential analysis ( D). Molecular docking and theoretical calculations revealed a strong adsorption energy (Eads = −54.115 kcal/mol) between the ionic liquid and CNC, mediated by two hydrogen bonds (2.7 Å and 3.3 Å), which surpasses the CNC-water interaction energy. This confirms effective surface modification and predicts significantly improved interfacial compatibility within the composite hydrogel ( E). Further validation was provided by FTIR spectroscopy, showing a new C-H stretching vibration peak ( F).
 Based on prior optimization of SilMA concentration (10%, 15%, 20% w/v), the 10% formulation was selected for composite fabrication due to its largest pore size, suitable elastic modulus, and highest swelling ratio [ ]. CNCs/SilMA hydrogel were prepared at varying CNCs-to-SilMA volume ratios (1:20, 1:10, 1:5), all of which formed stable hydrogel within 30 s under UV light ( G– ). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a significant dependence of the hydrogel's microarchitecture on the CNCs-to-SilMA ratio. Pure SilMA hydrogel exhibited a rough, irregular pore structure with pore diameters in the range of 110–120 μm. Upon incorporation of CNCs at a 1:20 ratio, the pore morphology became more regular, adopting circular or elliptical shapes, and the pore size increased to 180–190 μm. The optimal microstructure was achieved at a 1:10 CNC/SilMA ratio, which produced a highly uniform, honeycomb-like porous network with pore sizes of 180–200 μm. Further increasing the CNC content to a 1:5 ratio led to visible phase separation, a reduction in pore size (120–130 μm), and increased structural heterogeneity ( H and I). The effect of different CNCs/SilMA ratios on the mechanical properties of the hydrogel was systematically studied through compression testing. Results showed that the introduction of CNCs significantly modulated the compressive mechanical behavior of the hydrogel, and the reinforcing effect exhibited clear concentration dependence. The pure SilMA hydrogel showed low mechanical strength, with a compressive stress of only 32 kPa at 50% strain and a compressive modulus of 45 kPa, corresponding to its SEM-observed smaller and irregular pore structure. In contrast, as CNCs content increased, the mechanical properties of the hydrogel significantly improved. The CNCs/SilMA (1/10) hydrogel exhibited optimal mechanical properties, with a compressive stress of ∼80 kPa at 50% strain and a compressive modulus of 76 kPa ( ). However, when the CNCs content was further increased, the mechanical properties of the hydrogel decreased instead, with compressive stress dropping to 45 kPa and compressive modulus decreasing to 70 kPa. This phenomenon aligned with the SEM-observed reduction in pore size and increased structural inhomogeneity, possibly due to the increased system viscosity hindering effective crosslinking of SilMA and potentially causing local CNCs aggregation, forming mechanical weak points. Cyclic compression tests showed that the hydrogel demonstrated good fatigue resistance, with minimal stress change after 20 cycles at 30% strain ( ). Furthermore, rheological tests demonstrated that the elastic modulus (G′) of the CNCs/SilMA (1/10) hydrogel consistently exceeded its viscous modulus (G″) over a broad range, confirming its solid-like behavior. The measured elastic modulus was approximately 850 Pa ( J), closely matching that of natural spinal cord tissue. Angular frequency sweeps within the linear viscoelastic region further revealed that the elastic modulus remained consistently higher than the viscous modulus, indicating predominantly elastic behavior ( K). In summary, the CNCs/SilMA (1/10) hydrogel achieves an optimal balance between mechanical properties and microstructure, demonstrating its promising potential for applications in spinal cord tissue engineering. To verify the injectability of the CNCs/SilMA (1/10) precursor solution before photocrosslinking (in situ photocrosslinking after injection), a quantitative syringe extrusion test was performed using a 1 mL syringe and measured the force required to extrude the precursor solution. The measured extrusion force was 3.8 N, indicating that the precursor can be readily injected and delivered to the spinal cord injury site prior to photocrosslinking.
To investigate whether the hydrogel possessed good biocompatibility, neural stem cells were co-cultured with the hydrogel for 1, 3, and 5 days, followed by live/dead cell staining. Results showed that cell viability in the Control group, SilMA group, and CNCs/SilMA group all exceeded 85%, with no significant difference among the three groups ( ). To directly visualize NSCs distribution within the hydrogel, the resulting of 3D confocal imaging showed that EGFP+ NSCs were present within the interior of the hydrogel (not restricted to the surface), supporting the scaffold's ability to accommodate cells in a true 3D manner ( ). More importantly, to address CNS-specific toxicity, we performed histological analyses of brain safety ( ). The results showed that EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA does not induce detectable brain gliosis, neuronal loss, or ectopic NSC proliferation compared with controls. Together, the in vitro cytocompatibility and new in vivo CNS safety results support that the final composite does not cause observable CNS toxicity. Subcutaneous implantation in mice for one week showed no significant infiltration of CD68+ macrophages, indicating good biocompatibility in vivo ( ). The in vivo degradation profile of the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel was monitored subcutaneously in mice. The implant cross-sectional area transiently increased to 101.3% at week 1 due to swelling, followed by consistent degradation, retaining only 12.28% of the original size by week 5 ( L and M), confirming its progressive resorption.
Finally, we evaluated the hydrogel's capability as a sustained-release platform for exosomes in vivo. Following complete spinal cord transection, four hydrogel groups were implanted: SilMA, CNCs/SilMA, DiR-EXOGNI@SilMA, and DiR-EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA. In vivo imaging revealed that the DiR-EXOGNI@SilMA hydrogel released over 50% of exosomes by day 7, and nearly all were released by day 12. However, the DiR-EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel still had nearly 40% of exosomes unreleased at day 12, with sustained release lasting up to 19 days ( N and O). Therefore, the CNCs/SilMA hydrogel can provide a stable and effective platform for the sustained release of functionally modified exosomes.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel mediates dual modulation of inflammation and neural stem cells fate
A co‐culture system of hydrogel and microglia (BV2) was established by using transwell chambers, where BV2 cells were cultured in the lower layer, and EXOGNI loaded CNCs/SilMA hydrogel was placed on the upper layer to simulate the exosome release process. The BV2 inflammatory model has been established, and LPS could promote the up-regulation of the M1 phenotypic marker iNOS and the down-regulation of the M2 phenotypic marker Arg1 in BV2 cells. The immunofluorescence results showed that both EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel and EXO@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel could inhibit iNOS expression and promote ARG-1 expression, and there was no significant difference between the two groups ( A–D). These findings proved that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel can inhibit the inflammatory response and promote M2 polarization of BV2 cells.
 Consistent with the direct exosome treatment results, NSCs exposed to the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of EDU-positive cells after 24 h compared to other groups ( ), while no significant differences were observed among four groups after 7 days of treatment ( ).
Next, we co-cultured the primary NSCs with the hydrogel using a transwell system to evaluate whether EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA offers greater advantages in promoting NSCs differentiation. After two weeks of co-culture, the effects on the differentiation fate of NSCs were analyzed via immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that, compared to the Control and CNCs/SilMA groups, the proportion of Tuj1-and MAP2-positive cells increased in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group. Furthermore, compared to the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group, the proportion of Tuj1-and MAP2-positive cells was significantly higher in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group ( E, F, H, J). GFAP staining results revealed that, compared to the Control and CNCs/SilMA groups, the proportion of GFAP-positive cells decreased in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group. Moreover, compared to the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group, the proportion of GFAP-positive cells further decreased in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group ( E, F, I). Olig2 staining results indicated no significant difference in the proportion of Olig2-positive cells among the Control, CNCs/SilMA, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups. However, compared to these three groups, the proportion of Olig2-positive cells was significantly higher in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group ( G–K). Morphometric validation using Sholl analysis demonstrated that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited enhanced neurogenesis, characterized by more complex dendritic arborization and significantly increased axonal length compared to the other three groups ( L and M). Taken together, these results demonstrate that EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA can regulate the differentiation fate of NSCs by promoting their differentiation into mature neurons and oligodendrocytes while inhibiting their differentiation into astrocytes.

## Mechanism of EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel regulates neural differentiation
To further elucidate the mechanism by which EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA promotes the differentiation of NSCs, we performed transcriptome analysis of differentiated cells via RNA sequencing. Principal component analysis of biological replicates demonstrated a good dispersion, suggesting the dependability of experimental data ( A). Volcano plot analysis revealed the distribution of differentially expressed genes, with 787 genes upregulated and 808 genes downregulated ( B). Heatmap results demonstrated that functionally modified exosomes efficiently drive NSCs toward neuronal differentiation by synergistically activating a comprehensive network encompassing neuronal fate determination, axonogenesis, and synaptogenesis ( C). The significant upregulation of key differentiation marker genes confirmed the initiation of neurogenesis: increased expression of the immature neuronal marker Dcx and the GABAergic neuron determinant Dlx1 indicated an irreversible exit from the stem cell state and commitment to neuronal lineage differentiation. Concurrently, core pathways regulating axonal growth and guidance were specifically activated: coordinated upregulation of the Netrin pathway regulator Draxin, Wnt ligand Wnt5a, and axonal branching-related protein Cspg5 molecularly explained the observed robust axonal growth phenotype. Notably, the synergistic expression of postsynaptic functional component genes marked neuronal functional maturation: simultaneous upregulation of key glutamatergic synaptic components Gria1 (AMPA receptor), Grm5 (metabotropic glutamate receptor), and Grik3 (kainate receptor) indicated that the differentiated neurons had acquired preliminary electrophysiological excitability and neurotransmission capabilities.
 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that the significantly enriched pathways included the PI3K−AKT signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, axon guidance, calcium signaling pathway, cell cycle, Rap1 signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells. These pathways collectively form a core network regulating neural stem cell differentiation ( D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further supported these findings, showing that upregulated genes were primarily involved in biological processes such as passive transmembrane transporter activity, substrate-specific channel activity, ion gated channel activity, tubulin binding, microtubule motor activity, signal transducer activity downstream of receptor, calcium ion transmembrane transporter activity, neurotransmitter receptor activity, postsynaptic neurotransmitter receptor activity, and glutamate receptor activity ( E). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed stronger enrichment scores in the functionally modified exosomes group within the axon guidance, central nervous system neuron development and Calcium signaling pathways compared to control ( F–H), reinforcing the synergistic regulatory effects of the combined treatment on neuronal differentiation.
Western blot results highly consistent with the transcriptional profile showed synergistically activation of the PI3K-AKT (upregulated P-PI3K and P-AKT) and the Wnt-Ca2+ signaling pathway (upregulated P-CaMKII), collectively promoting neuronal survival, axonal growth, and synaptogenesis. In contrast, the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway was selectively inhibited (downregulated total β-catenin, upregulated p-β-catenin) ( I–N). Subsequently, we performed the inhibitor experiments and additionally evaluated whether blocking these pathways affects NSCs differentiation. LY294002 treatment significantly reduced p-PI3K and p-AKT levels. Importantly, in our system, LY294002 did not alter p-CaMKII or p-β-catenin levels. Conversely, KN93 treatment significantly reduced p-CaMKII and p-β-catenin, while p-PI3K and p-AKT remained largely unchanged ( ). In addition, immunofluorescence staining of NSCs differentiation showed that LY294002 alone, KN93 alone, and combined LY294002 + KN93 each inhibited neuronal differentiation of NSCs and promoted differentiation toward astrocytic lineage ( ).
Collectively, these reciprocal inhibitor experiments indicate that PI3K–AKT activation and Wnt/Ca2+–CaMKII activation operate primarily as parallel signaling modules, rather than constituting a strict upstream–downstream hierarchical relationship. This mechanism not only initiates neurogenesis but also supports the complete neuronal maturation process, including axonal growth and synaptic formation. ( O).

2.5.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel promotes the recovery of motor and sensory function after SCI
During spinal cord injury treatment with the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel, the hindlimb motor function of mice was assessed using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS). BMS scoring results showed that on day 1 post-injury, the BMS scores of all experimental animals across groups dropped to 0, confirming the successful establishment of the complete spinal cord transection injury model. From 2 weeks post-injury, the BMS scores of the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group and the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group began to be higher than those of the SCI group. From week 4 post-injury onwards, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group showed significantly higher scores compared with both the EXO@CNCs/SilMA and SCI groups ( A). At week 8, the mean BMS score of the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group was 2.906, significantly higher than that of the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group (2 score) and the SCI group (1 score) ( B). Notably, 57% of mice in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group achieved a BMS score of 3, compared to only 12.5% in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group ( C). Meanwhile, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group achieved the highest BMS scores among all groups, with statistically significant differences compared with the other treatment groups, including the free EXOGNI + CNCs/SilMA, EXOGDNF@CNCs/SilMA, EXONT3@CNCs/SilMA, EXOIGF1@CNCs/SilMA groups and (GNI + EXO)@CNCs/SilMA ( ).
 The activity of hindlimb joints during movement was recorded for mice in each group at 8 weeks post-injury. As shown, mice in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited plantar stepping with extensive joint movement in their hindlimbs during locomotion. In contrast, mice in the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups showed spastic hindlimbs with only slight ankle movement. Mice in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited extensive ankle movement but still lacked plantar stepping ( D). Immunofluorescence analysis of tibialis anterior muscles at week 8 revealed a significant increase in muscle fiber cross-sectional area in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group compared with the SCI and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups ( E and F).
At 8 weeks post-injury, neuroelectrophysiological assessments of motor evoked potentials (MEP) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SEP) were performed to assess the recovery of nerve conduction function. MEP results showed a significant increase in amplitude in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group (41.60 ± 2.504 μV) compared with the SCI group (12.25 ± 3.858 μV). Importantly, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group (55.50 ± 3.215 μV) exhibited further enhancement compared with the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group ( G and H). Similarly, SEP results showed that compared to the SCI group amplitude (7.167 ± 2.651 μV) and the CNCs/SilMA group amplitude (12.00 ± 1.39 μV), the amplitude in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group (24.17 ± 2.007 μV) showed significant improvement ( I and J).
The hot plate test was performed at 4 and 8 weeks post-injury to evaluate the recovery of sensory function in mice from each group. Results showed that at 4 weeks post-spinal cord injury, compared to the SCI group, the response time to thermal stimulation was shortened in both the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group and the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group, with no significant difference between the two treatment groups ( K). At 8 weeks post-injury, there was no significant difference among the SCI, CNCs/SilMA, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups, while the response time to thermal stimulation in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group was significant shortened ( L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel effectively promotes motor and sensory functional recovery after SCI, accompanied by improved muscle preservation and enhanced neural conduction.
Furthermore, the biosafety of the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel for spinal cord injury repair was evaluated through histopathology and biochemical analyses. HE staining revealed no morphological abnormalities in the liver, kidney, heart, lung and spleen, indicating that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel did not induce organ toxicity ( ). Additionally, to further detect the potential of the hydrogel to induce inflammatory responses, CD68-positive macrophages and CD3-positive T cells in liver and kidney tissues were examined. Results showed no changes in either cell type after treatment with the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel ( ). Consistently, liver function markers such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), as well as kidney function markers such as blood urea (UREA), creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA), were used to detect hydrogel toxicity. After treatment with the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel, neither liver nor kidney function markers were elevated ( ). The above experimental results indicate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel does not cause any detectable toxicity or inflammatory response during spinal cord injury repair.

2.6.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel modulates the inflammatory response in the acute phase of SCI
After complete spinal cord transection injury in mice, EXO@CNCs/SilMA or EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel were implanted into the lesion site. At 7 days post-injury, exosomes released from both hydrogel were internalized by CD68-positive macrophages and Iba1-positive microglia ( ). After spinal cord injury, the blood-spinal cord barrier is disrupted, and a large number of CD68-positive macrophages from the peripheral blood are recruited to the injury site and activated. Compared with the SCI group, the CNCs/SilMA group moderately decreased the fluorescence intensity of CD68-positive macrophages at the rostral, caudal, and central regions of the lesion. Both EXO@CNCs/SilMA and EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA groups further significantly reduced the fluorescence intensity of CD68-positive macrophages at the rostral and caudal regions, again with no significant difference between these two groups ( A, E). Compared with the SCI group, the CNCs/SilMA group moderately reduced the fluorescence intensity of Iba1-positive microglia at both the rostral and caudal ends of the lesion. Notably, both the EXO@CNCs/SilMA and EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA groups further significantly reduced the fluorescence intensity of Iba1-positive microglia at these regions, with no significant difference observed between the two groups ( B–F).
 To further examine inflammatory cell polarization, immunostaining for the M1 marker iNOS and the M2 marker CD206 was performed. The ratio of CD206 to iNOS fluorescence intensity was calculated as an indicator of inflammatory cell polarization. At all examined regions (rostral, caudal, and center), there was no significant difference between the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups. In contrast, both the EXO@CNCs/SilMA and EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA groups significantly increased the CD206/iNOS ratio compared with the SCI group, with no difference between the two groups ( C, D, G). These findings indicate that CNCs/SilMA can alleviate acute neuroinflammation following SCI but lack the ability to modulate inflammatory cell polarization. By contrast, both EXO@CNCs/SilMA and EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA not only suppress the acute inflammatory response but also promote the polarization of inflammatory cells toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype.

2.7.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel promotes neural stem cell migration and neural regeneration while reducing glial scar formation after SCI
Extracellular vesicles targeting NSCs are crucial for regulating their fate. Immunofluorescence staining proves that engineered exosomes can be internalized by NSCs ( ). To investigate the effect of EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA on NSCs migration following SCI, Nestin staining was performed. Results showed that at 7 days and 8 weeks post-injury, the area of Nestin-positive cells at the rostral, caudal, and central regions of the lesion was significantly increased in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group compared with the SCI, CNCs/SilMA, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups ( A–D). The above results indicate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel promotes the migration of NSCs after SCI, thereby laying the foundation for neural regeneration within the lesion site.
 At 8 weeks post-injury, GFAP was used to label the astrocytic scar in the injury region. Compared with the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups, the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group showed a significant reduction in GFAP-positive areas at the rostral and caudal ends of the lesion. Importantly, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited a further significant decrease in GFAP-positive area compared with the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group ( E and F). Neural regeneration within the injury site was further evaluated using Tuj1 and NF200 immunostaining at 8 weeks post-injury. As shown in the results, compared to the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups, the area of Tuj1-and NF200-positive cells within the injury region was significantly increased in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group. Consistent with the behavioral results, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group showed the largest Tuj1+ and NF200+ positive areas in the lesion region, again with statistically significant differences versus the (GNI + EXO)@CNCs/SilMA group ( ). Moreover, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group showed a further significant increase compared with the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group ( G, H, J, K). In order to verify neural functional connectivity, the results of 5-HT immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA treatment group enhanced axonal/serotonergic fiber presence associated with behavioral improvement ( ). Compared to the SCI group, CNCs/SilMA group, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA group, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group significantly increased the area of MBP-positive cells ( I–L). Furthermore, TEM was performed to quantify myelin ultrastructure and g-ratio. After SCI, severe demyelination was observed, with disordered concentric lamellae, frequent myelin breaks/dissolution, uneven thickness (g-ratio = 0.49), and poor axonal wrapping. In the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group, myelin sheaths exhibited regular concentric layers, uniform thickness (g-ratio = 0.74), and tight axonal association ( ). These TEM data directly complement the MBP+ area, confirming that our system restores myelin quality, not just quantity.

2.8.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel enhances migration and proliferation of eNSCs after SCI
To explore the effect of the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel on the migration and proliferation of eNSCs, we crossed Nestin-CreERT2 mice with Rosa26-tdTomato reporter mice to enable lineage tracing of eNSCs. Mice were sacrificed on day 14 post-injury to observe the migration of eNSCs in each group. Results showed that at both the lesion border and center, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of tdTomato-positive cells among the SCI, CNCs/SilMA, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups. In contrast, compared to the three groups, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel significantly increased the number of tdTomato-positive cells in these regions ( A and B). Therefore, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel can promote the migration of eNSCs from the rostral and caudal ends toward the lesion site after SCI.
 The effect of the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel on the proliferation of eNSCs was also investigated by detecting tdTomato and EDU co-localization staining. Results showed that at 2 weeks post-SCI, no significant differences were observed in the proportion of tdTomato and EDU co-localized cells among the SCI, CNCs/SilMA, and EXO@CNCs/SilMA groups, whereas the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel significantly increased the proportion of tdTomato and EDU co-localized cells ( C–E). However, at 3 weeks post-SCI, no significant differences were found in the proportion of tdTomato and EDU co-localized cells among the four groups. These results indicate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel promotes the proliferation of eNSCs during the early stages after SCI ( D–F).

2.9.

## EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel directs the differentiation fate of eNSCs toward functional neuronal and oligodendrocytic lineages
At 6 weeks post-injury, co-localization staining with GFAP was performed to observe the proportion of eNSCs differentiating into astrocytes in each group. Results showed that no GFAP-positive cells were observed in the lesion center across all groups. At the lesion border, the proportion of tdTomato/GFAP double-positive cells was significantly reduced in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group compared with the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups, and further reduced in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group compared with the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group ( A, E). The above results indicate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel inhibits the differentiation of eNSCs into astrocytes after SCI. Co-localization staining with Tuj1 (immature neuron marker) and MAP2 (mature neuron marker) was performed separately to observe the proportion of eNSCs differentiating into neurons. Results showed that in the SCI and CNCs/SilMA groups, 15.04 ± 1.10% and 16.36 ± 0.73% of tdTomato-positive cells differentiated into immature neurons, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups. This proportion increased to 34.35 ± 2.16% in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group and further to 64.69 ± 0.80% in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group ( B, F). A similar trend was observed for mature neuronal differentiation. The percentage of tdTomato/MAP2 double-positive cells remained low and comparable in the SCI (4.73 ± 1.76%) and CNCs/SilMA (5.81 ± 1.15%) groups. The EXO@CNCs/SilMA treatment significantly increased this proportion to 26.84 ± 1.06%, which was further elevated to 48.89 ± 2.01% in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group ( C, G). These results demonstrate that the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel most effectively directs eNSCs toward both immature and mature neuronal lineages. Co-localization staining with MBP was performed to observe the proportion of eNSCs differentiating into oligodendrocytes. The EXO@CNCs/SilMA group exhibited an increase in tdTomato/MBP double-positive cells, although the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group showed a significant increase in tdTomato/MBP double-positive cells compared with all other groups ( D, H).
 To further identify neuronal subtypes, tdTomato-positive cells were co-stained with choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), serotonin (5-HT), vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (vGLUT1), and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) at the lesion center. The proportions of tdTomato-positive cells expressing these markers were 22.15 ± 1.47% (ChAT), 9.17 ± 1.16% (5-HT), 11.47 ± 1.02% (vGLUT1), and 8.38 ± 0.25% (GABA). These findings indicate that EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA hydrogel promotes eNSCs differentiation into multiple neuronal subtypes, with ChAT-positive neurons representing the predominant population within the lesion center ( I, J).

## Discussion and conclusion
The failure of functional recovery after SCI is largely attributed to a multifaceted inhibitory microenvironment, characterized by persistent inflammation, a deficiency in neurotrophic support, and the formation of a glial scar [ ]. This hostile milieu not only impedes axonal regeneration but also critically misdirects the fate of eNSCs, steering them predominantly toward astrocytic differentiation rather than functional neuronal lineages [ , ]. Although single-barrier-focused individual treatment has shown promise in preclinical spinal cord injury animal models, it leads to only limited and incomplete functional recovery, hindering its translation to clinical trials [ ]. Recently, combination strategies that target various pathological processes have proven more effective than individual treatments alone [ ]. Therefore, an ideal therapeutic strategy must simultaneously remodel this inhibitory niche and direct eNSCs differentiation toward functional lineages. In this study, we developed an integrated therapeutic platform that combines growth factor-engineered hUCMSCs-derived exosomes (EXOGNI) with a biomimetic sustained-release hydrogel scaffold (CNCs/SilMA). This system provides dual regulation of the inflammatory niche and eNSCs differentiation, ultimately facilitating robust neural regeneration and significant functional recovery.
The therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes in SCI has been widely recognized, primarily for their immunomodulatory and paracrine functions [ ]. We selected hUCMSCs as the source of exosomes due to their clinical translational potential, featuring non-invasive acquisition and ease of standardized expansion [ ]. However, the inherent capacity of native hUCMSCs-derived exosomes to drive substantial neurogenesis and circuit recmodeling remains limited [ ]. To address this issue, we engineered hUCMSCs to carry these key neurotrophic factors. Larger payloads, such as proteins, are typically loaded endogenously during exosome biogenesis within the producer cells [ , ]. This is commonly achieved by genetically engineering the producer cells to overexpress the target protein fused to an exosomal sorting protein, thereby facilitating the endogenous sorting of the cargo. This strategy can direct the molecules to either the surface or the lumen of the exosomes [ ]. For instance, fusion of the membrane protein CD9 with the RNA-binding protein HuR, which has high affinity for miR-155, leads to the high enrichment of miR-155 into exosomes during their biogenesis [ ]. Alternatively, when the exosomal membrane protein CD63 is fused to GFP and the Cas9 protein is fused to a GFP nanobody, the high-affinity interaction between GFP and its nanobody results in the specific capture and efficient loading of Cas9 into exosomes, rather than random encapsulation [ ]. To date, only a limited number of proteins have been characterized for loading target proteins into exosomes, most of which belong to the tetraspanin family, such as CD9, CD63, and CD81 [ ].
In this study, we engineered hUCMSCs to stably express fusion proteins that anchor key neurotrophic factors—GDNF, NT3, and IGF1—to the exosomal surface via tetraspanins (CD81, CD9, CD63). Crucially, EXOGNI retained the innate immunomodulatory functions of native exosomes, effectively suppressing pro-inflammatory factors (iNOS, TNFα, IL-6) and promoting M2 macrophage markers (CD206, Arg1, IL-10) in vitro. Meanwhile, EXOGNI can promote the early proliferation of NSCs and regulate their differentiation towards neurons and oligodendrocytes, while inhibiting their differentiation into astrocytes. Thus, EXOGNI achieves a dual therapeutic objective by modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and enhancing the potential for neural regeneration.
To address the rapid clearance of free exosomes, we developed a sustained-release delivery system using an injectable and photocrosslinkable composite hydrogel. Silk fibroin methacrylate (SilMA) was selected as the base material due to its photocurable properties, which enable in situ gelation, and its three-dimensional porous structure that facilitates nutrient exchange [ , ]. To further optimize performance, ionic liquid-surface-modified cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) were incorporated, forming a nano-reinforcing network with SilMA through hydrogen bonding interactions. Molecular docking and theoretical calculations revealed stable hydrogen bonding between CNCs and SilMA, providing a fundamental structural advantage.
These intermolecular interactions directly regulate the formation of the hydrogel's microstructure. At the optimal CNCs/SilMA ratio (1:10), the hydroxyl groups on the CNCs surface form hydrogen bonds with the β-sheet domains of SilMA. These interactions not only serve as additional physical crosslinks but also introduce steric hindrance that moderates the photocrosslinking kinetics. The delayed gelation allows for more orderly phase separation and solvent evaporation, ultimately resulting in a uniform honeycomb-like macroporous structure (180–200 μm), which is markedly superior to the irregular architecture of pure SilMA. At this ratio, the CNCs concentration approaches the percolation threshold, forming a continuous three-dimensional network skeleton that not only increases crosslinking density but also optimizes pore size distribution through nanoconfinement effects. Conversely, excessive CNCs content increases system viscosity, hindering the diffusion of SilMA molecular chains and leading to nanoparticle aggregation, which disrupts network continuity and results in a heterogeneous structure with reduced pore size. Therefore, precise tuning of the CNCs/SilMA ratio is critical to achieving an ideal biomimetic microstructure that closely resembles the native extracellular matrix.
This optimized microstructure mimics the native spinal cord extracellular matrix and, combined with a matched elastic modulus, provides an ideal physical niche for cell infiltration and axonal guidance. Previous studies have shown that using imidazolium-based ionic liquids as plasticizers to treat CNC can disrupt its agglomeration structure, thereby maintaining better dispersibility in corresponding solvent systems [ ]. In this study, the ion liquid-modified CNCs exhibited a positive surface charge, which significantly prolonged the sustained release of EXOGNI from the hydrogel, achieving a release duration of up to 19 days in vivo. This release window effectively covers the subacute phase of SCI, a critical period during which neural regenerative programs are continuously initiated. By sustaining growth factor presentation throughout this key timeframe, the system provides a favorable temporal niche to support and guide ongoing regenerative processes following SCI. Furthermore, the scaffold exhibited excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, establishing a safety foundation for clinical translation.
The core therapeutic achievement of our EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA system lies in its ability to systematically overcome the differentiation barrier of eNSCs. Both in vitro and in vivo lineage tracing experiments provided compelling evidence. The composite hydrogel not only enhanced the migration and early proliferation of eNSCs towards the lesion core but also orchestrated their differentiation away from scar-forming astrocytes and towards functional neurons and oligodendrocytes. In the co-culture model of hydrogel and neural stem cells, EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA treatment significantly increased the proportion of Tuj1+ immature neurons to 51.04% (control: 23.49%), MAP2+ mature neurons to 50.85% (control: 9.80%) and Olig2+ oligodendrocytes to 16.70% (control: 4.43%), while simultaneously suppressing the GFAP+ astrocyte differentiation rate to 24.57% (control: 44.98%). This fate redirection effect was validated in vivo using Nestin-CreERT2; Rosa26-tdTomato neural stem cells lineage tracing mouse model. The number of tdTomato+ eNSCs in the lesion center increased over 2-fold, with a remarkable 64.69% and 48.89% differentiating into Tuj1+ and MAP2+ neurons, respectively. The generation of diverse neuronal subtypes, including ChAT+ motor neurons (22.15%), 5-HT+ serotonergic neurons, vGLUT1+ glutamatergic neurons, and GABAergic neurons, underscores the capacity of this strategy to reconstruct a heterogeneous and potentially functional neuronal network.
The mechanism underlying this directed neurogenesis involves a dual-track regulation through the remodeling of the inflammatory microenvironment and the activation of pro-regenerative signalling pathways. On one hand, EXOGNI released sustainedly from the hydrogel inhibited M1 polarization and promoted an M2 phenotype (significantly increased CD206/iNOS ratio in vivo), reducing the inhibition of neural differentiation by inflammatory factors. This aligns with researchers' findings—hUCMSCs-derived exosomes have been shown to repair SCI by suppressing inflammation and regulating microglial/macrophage polarization [ , , ]. On the other hand, the synergistic action of GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 activated the PI3K-AKT and Wnt-Ca2+ pathway, directly regulating the differentiation fate of eNSCs towards functional neuronal lineages. Previous studies have demonstrated that glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) exerts its neuroregulatory functions by binding to the GFRα1/RET receptor complex. This binding effectively activates the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade to promote neuronal regeneration and survival [ ]. Additionally, it induces Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to the phosphorylation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII), which further promotes the migration of neuronal progenitor cells [ ]. The NT3-modified collagen scaffold promotes the formation of functional neural networks from TrkC-modified iPSC-derived neural stem cells through the PI3K-AKT and Wnt-Ca2+ signaling pathways [ ]. Concurrently, IGF1 synergistically reinforces the activation of both PI3K-AKT and Ca2+ pathways via IGF1R-mediated signaling, a mechanism proven to regulate the growth, maturation, and differentiation of neural-related cells including neurons and oligodendrocytes [ , ]. Notably, the selective inhibition of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway observed in this study is closely linked to the activation of the non-canonical Wnt-Ca2+ pathway. Consistent with previous findings, the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway maintains stem cell self-renewal and pluripotency through β-catenin/TCF/LEF-mediated transcriptional regulation [ , , ], and its suppression by the Wnt-Ca2+ pathway relieves the inhibition of spontaneous differentiation, facilitating the lineage commitment of eNSCs [ , ]. This sophisticated signaling modulation, combined with the hydrogel's physical properties mimicking natural spinal cord tissue, collectively created a permissive niche that supported not only neurogenesis but also extensive axonal regeneration (evidenced by increased NF200+ fibers) and remyelination (increased MBP+ area).
This multifaceted repair at the cellular and molecular levels translated into significant functional improvement. The BMS score of the mice treated with EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA reached 2.9 (control: 1.0), with 57% of mice exhibiting plantar stepping (only 12.5% in the EXO@CNCs/SilMA group). The enhanced amplitudes of both motor and sensory evoked potentials confirmed the re-establishment of electrophysiological conduction across the lesion. The recovery of sensory function, evidenced by the shortened response latency in the hot plate test, further highlights the comprehensive nature of the neurological repair. Importantly, the entire therapeutic system demonstrated an excellent safety profile, with no signs of systemic toxicity or off-target inflammation, highlighting its clinical translational potential due to its safety and endogenous repair orientation.
Despite these promising results, several limitations should be noted. First, in the EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA group, ∼78% of Tuj1+ cells in the lesion area co-localized with tdTomato, whereas ∼22% were tdTomato−. These Tuj1+tdTomato− cells may arise from (i) pre-existing/surviving neurons near the lesion border, (ii) progenitor populations not captured by the Nestin lineage-tracing window (e.g., OPC/NG2 glia), (iii) glia-to-neuron conversion reported in certain injury contexts, and/or (iv) incomplete labeling inherent to inducible CreER systems. Future studies will use complementary lineage-tracing strategies (e.g., FoxJ1-CreERT2) and additional marker-based fate mapping to define their origin. Second, although we demonstrated the benefit of the tri-factor design, the individual contributions of GDNF, NT3, and IGF1 still require further dissection. Third, efficacy was validated in a complete transection model, and performance in more clinically relevant contusion/compression models remains to be tested. Fourth, definitive causal attribution of functional recovery to eNSCs-derived neurons will require lineage-specific loss-of-function studies, such as Nestin-lineage ablation (e.g., Nestin-CreERT2 × Rosa26-iDTR) or selective blockade of eNSCs neurogenesis. Finally, clinical translation will require validation in large-animal models and deeper investigation of the electrophysiological properties and synaptic integration of newly formed neurons.
In conclusion, we have developed a novel “molecule-carrier-scaffold” therapeutic paradigm that integrates tri-growth-factor-functionalized exosomes (EXOGNI) with a biomimetic, sustained-release CNCs/SilMA hydrogel. This EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA system effectively breaks the inhibitory cycle of the post-SCI microenvironment by simultaneously attenuating neuroinflammation, providing sustained neurotrophic support, and presenting a permissive physical niche. Through precise activation of the PI3K-AKT and Wnt-Ca2+ signaling pathways, the system successfully reprograms the fate of endogenous neural stem cells, driving their differentiation into functional neuronal subtypes and oligodendrocytes while inhibiting astrocytic commitment. These reparative changes at the molecular and cellular levels translated into significant motor and sensory recovery, electrophysiological restoration, and enhanced neural circuit remodeling. Our strategy thus provides a safe, efficacious, and translatable combinatorial framework for spinal cord repair, highlighting the promise of engineered exosome-biomaterial integration for CNS regenerative medicine.

## Materials and methods
4.1.

## Cell isolation and culture
Human Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hUCMSCs, passage 5), RAW 264.7 cell lines (CL-0190) and BV2 cell lines (CL-0493) were used in this study. hUCMSCs were provided by Tianjin Changhe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. hUCMSCs were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium α (MEM α, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Gibco). RAW 264.7 and BV2 cell lines were bought from Procell (Wuhan, China) and were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS.
Mouse Primary Neural Stem Cells (NSCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mouse (purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) with gestational day 14. Briefly, cerebral cortex was carefully isolated from each embryo and minced into ∼1 mm3 pieces. After being centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was digested with DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and papain (Worthington Biochem, Lakewood, NJ, USA) to obtain single-cell suspension. The cells were grown in a proliferation medium composed of Neurobasal, 1% PS, 1% GlutaMAX™, 2% B27 supplement without vitamin A, and 20 ng/mL each of bFGF and EGF (all from Gibco). Prior to plating, culture plates were coated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min and rinsed three times with PBS. Neurospheres were then dissociated into single cells using Accutase (Gibco), followed by filtration through a 40 μm strainer. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in differentiation medium (Neurobasal, 1% PS, 1% GlutaMAX™, 1% FBS, 2% B27 supplement without vitamin A), counted, and seeded onto the coated plates. All cells were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

4.2.

## Plasmid construction and lentivirus production
The fusion gene CD81-GDNF-P2A-CD9-NT3-E2A-CD63-IGF1 was synthesized and PCR-amplified using primers listed in , generating a 4211 bp fragment ( ). The amplified product was cloned into the GV707 plasmid vector ( ) at the BamHI/XhoI sites, followed by verification of the recombinant plasmid ( ). For packaging of overexpression lentiviral particles, a three-plasmid co-transfection system was employed, consisting of the target gene-containing vector plasmid, and the Helper 1.0 and Helper 2.0 packaging plasmids. The viral supernatant was concentrated and purified through ultracentrifugation-based concentration, ultimately yielding a high-titer lentiviral stock solution.

4.3.

## Lentiviral transduction of hUCMSCs for gene overexpression
To determine the optimal transduction conditions, hUCMSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 103 cells per well. After 12 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh hUCMSCs medium containing the lentivirus at different multiplicities of infection (MOI: 1, 10, 20, and 50) along with transduction enhancer reagent. Following 24 h of transduction, the supernatant was changed to fresh medium and cells were cultured for an additional 72 h. At 96 h post-transduction, fluorescence microscopy was used to assess the intensity of green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression. The optimal transduction conditions, defined as those yielding approximately 80% transduction efficiency with good cell viability, were identified as an MOI of 20 with HiTransG P.

4.4.

## Exosome isolation and identification
The hUCMSCs culture medium was replaced with exosome-depleted serum medium. After 48 h of continued culture, the cell culture supernatant was collected. The supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 1000×g for 15 min, subsequent centrifugation at 2000×g for 20 min, and ultimately 10,000×g for 30 min. After filtering through a 0.22 μm sterile membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), the resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged for 70 min at 100,000×g (Optima XPN-100, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA). A second ultracentrifugation step was performed on the pellet at the same parameters. The final pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS to obtain purified exosomes for subsequent experiments. The morphology of exosome was examined by TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and their size and distribution were assessed using NTA (ZetaView PMX110, Particle Metrix, Germany). Exosome-specific markers were verified by Western blot analysis. The exosome dosing is 5 μg/mL (in vitro anti-inflammatory) and 10 μg/mL (in vitro differentiation).

4.5.

## Western blotting
Protein samples were denatured at 99 °C for 10 min and then transferred to an activated PVDF membrane at 280 mA constant current. Following membrane transfer, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and subsequently incubated with diluted primary antibodies, including CD63 (1:1000, ab134045, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), CD81 (1:1000, ab109201, Abcam), CD9 (1:1000, ab92726, Abcam), IGF1 (1:1000, AF-291-SP, R&D, Minneapolis, MN, USA), NT3 (1:1000, AF-267-SP, R&D), GDNF (1:1000, AF-212-SP, R&D), TSG101 (1:1000, ab125011, Abcam), HSP70 (1:1000, ab181606, Abcam), CD206 (1:1000, ab64693, Abcam), iNOS (1:1000, ab283655, Abcam), P-PI3K (1:1000, 4228, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), PI3K (1:1000, 4292, Cell Signaling Technology), P-AKT (1:2000, 4060, Cell Signaling Technology), AKT (1:1000, 4691, Cell Signaling Technology), p-β-catenin (1:1000, 4176, Cell Signaling Technology), β-catenin (1:1000, 8480, Cell Signaling Technology), P-CamKⅡ (1:1000, 12716, Cell Signaling Technology), CamKⅡ (1:1000, 4436, Cell Signaling Technology), HRP-Beta Actin (1:5000, 60008-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), GAPDH (1:50000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). Next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies, including Anti-Rabbit (1:3000, SA00001-2, Proteintech), Anti-Goat (1:5000, SA00001-3, Proteintech). Finally the membrane was exposed for imaging using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

4.6.

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (R1200, Solarbio, Beijing, China). mRNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Primescript RT reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the LightCycler 96 system and TB Green Preix Ex Taq Ⅱ (RR820B, Takara). Primer sequences used in these experiments are listed in of the Supporting Information.

4.7.

## RNA-seq
TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA) was used to extract total RNA from neural stem cells, following the manufacturer's instructions. Novogene (Beijing, China) conducted RNA-sequencing for the obtained RNA samples. The NEBNext Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina was used to generate sequencing libraries. An index of the reference genome was built using Hisat2 (v2.0.5) and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 (v2.0.5). Following the alignment, feature counts (v1.5.0-p3) were used to analyze differential gene expression, utilizing the BAM files obtained from each alignment.

4.8.

## Detection of neural stem cell proliferation capacity
According to manufacturer's protocol, proliferation capacity of neural stem cells were detected by EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C0075S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Staining was observed and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

4.9.

## Synthesis of silk fibroin and silk fibroin methacrylated
Silk fibroin (SF) was dissolved in 9.3 M LiBr solution, heated to 60 °C, and stirred for 1 h until fully dissolved. A defined amount of glycidyl methacrylate was added, and the reaction proceeded at 60 °C for 3 h to introduce methacrylate groups onto the silk fibroin molecular chains. The reaction solution was transferred to dialysis tubing and dialyzed against distilled water for 4 days until the conductivity of the dialysate dropped below 10 μS. The dialyzed solution was frozen at −80 °C and then lyophilized for 48 h to obtain silk fibroin methacrylated (SilMA) as a freeze-dried powder.

4.10.

## Modification of cellulose nanocrystals
0.05 g of cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) was dispersed in 20 mL deionized water and sonicated for 15 min 0.1 g of 1-Hexadecyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride was added, and the reaction proceeded at room temperature for 6 h to ensure thorough interaction. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 r/min. The precipitate was collected and freeze-dried for 48 h to obtain ionic liquid-modified cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs).

4.11.

## Preparation and characterization of CNCs/SilMA
Hydrogel preparation: Sterile 10% SilMA and 8% CNCs were mixed at different volume ratios (1:20, 1:10, 1:5), followed by the addition of exosomes. Finally, the photoinitiator LAP at a concentration of 0.25% was added to the mixed solution, and the solution was irradiated with UV light (405 nm) for 30 s to induce photocrosslinking and form the hydrogel. Zeta potential was measured on a Zetasizer Nano ZS90. In the Zeta potential test, the pH of the system was set to 7.5 to better simulate the acid-base environment in the body. SEM images were obtained by using a HITACHI Regulus 8100 SEM. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Advance III HD600 NMR spectrometer (Billerica, MA, USA). FTIR spectra were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 (Waltham, MA, USA).

4.12.

## Mechanical property testing of hydrogel
Compression performance was tested using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu, AGS-X, 100 N, Japan). Hydrogel samples were cylindrical with dimensions of 15 mm in length and 10 mm in diameter. Compression tests were performed at a speed of 20 mm/min. To assess the fatigue resistance of the hydrogel, cyclic compression tests were conducted at 10-50% strain, and 20 cycles of compression testing were performed at 30% strain.

4.13.

## Rheological property testing of hydrogel
The viscoelastic properties of the gels were characterized using an Anton Paar MCR-302 rotational rheometer. The linear viscoelastic region of the material was determined by strain sweep mode: a strain amplitude of 0.01%-1000% was applied, and the change curves of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded at a constant frequency of 5 rad/s. The critical strain value where G′ and G″ remained stable was selected as the upper limit of the linear region for subsequent tests. Frequency sweeps were performed at 1% strain amplitude: the sweep range covered 0.1 rad/s to 100 rad/s, and the change curves of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded to evaluate the relaxation behavior and viscoelastic balance characteristics of the gel network structure.

4.14.

## Live/dead cell staining
Neural stem cell proliferation medium containing SilMA or CNCs/SilMA hydrogel was added to wells and incubated for 20 min to equilibrate the hydrogel. 100 μL of Calcein AM/PI detection working solution (C2015, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added to plates and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Images were observed and captured under a fluorescence microscope.

4.15.

## Mouse spinal cord transection model
C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Jinan, Shandong, China; Approval number: KYLL2024403; Animal use permit number: SYXK: 20230003). Mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation. The T8 spinous process was located. An incision was made along the dorsal midline and lamina were carefully dissected. The spinal cord at the T8 segment was fully exposed and a 2 mm incision perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the spinal cord was made, ensuring complete transection of bilateral and ventral spinal cord tissue. After transection, the severed spinal cord tissue was carefully removed using fine forceps. According to the experimental group assignment, the corresponding hydrogel was injected into the lesion site and crosslinked using UV light irradiation. The hydrogel (40 μg exosomes loaded into 10 μL hydrogel per animal) was used to fill the transection gap by a single implantation immediately after injury, and no additional repeated dosing or subsequent intervention was performed thereafter. After treatment, muscles, fascia, and skin were sutured layer by layer. Postoperatively, mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain an appropriate temperature. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily, and the wound and perineal areas were disinfected. Antibiotics were administered daily to prevent urinary tract infections.
The intraoperative and postoperative criteria for complete transection include: (a) Intraoperative visual confirmation under a surgical microscope to verify that the spinal cord was fully severed across the entire dorsoventral axis and that no tissue bridges remained; (b) Observation of a clear gap and retraction of the rostral/caudal stumps after transection, confirming anatomical discontinuity; (c) Post hoc histological verification on representative sections to exclude residual spared fibers at the lesion center, using immunostaining for axonal markers (e.g., NF200).

4.16.

## In vivo imaging for monitoring sustained exosome release from hydrogel scaffolds
EXOGNI obtained by ultracentrifugation was resuspended in PBS solution. DiR dye (D12731, Thermo) was added to achieve a final concentration of 1 μg/mL. After thorough mixing and incubation, the solution was centrifuged at 100,000×g for 70 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in SilMA solution or CNCs/SilMA solution to obtain DiR-labeled EXOGNI@SilMA and DiR-labeled EXOGNI@CNCs/SilMA solutions, respectively. A mouse T8 spinal cord transection model was established as described previously. After thorough hemostasis, hydrogel were injected and cured in situ by UV light irradiation for 30 s. At designated time points, in vivo imaging was performed. Mice were anesthetized, and images were acquired using excitation light at 745 nm and emission light at 800 nm. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using IVIS Spectrum software.

4.17.

## Transcardial perfusion and tissue cryosectioning
Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the heart was exposed after opening the chest cavity. The pre-cooled PBS solution was then perfused through the heart until clear fluid flowed out and the liver color paled. Subsequently, pre-cooled 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) was trans-cardiac perfused. After perfusion, the spinal cord was exposed by laminectomy along both sides of the spine from caudal to rostral. The spinal cord was carefully removed and fixed by 4% PFA for 24 h. Tissues were dehydrated in a sucrose gradient: sequentially placed in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions until they sank. To prepare the tissue for further analysis, the dehydrated spinal cords were embedded in OCT mounting media and sectioned at 8 μm thickness using a cryotome (CM3050S, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

4.18.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Tissue immunofluorescence staining: Before staining, sections were thawed at room temperature for 20 min and hydrated in PBS for 5 min. Permeabilization/blocking solution (20% goat serum and 0.25% Triton X-100) was added, and sections were incubated in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 1 h. After three PBS washes (5 min each), primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution [CD68 (1:500, ab283654, Abcam), Iba1 (1:200, ab178846, Abcam), iNOS (1:1000, ab283655, Abcam), CD206 (1:1000, ab64693, Abcam), Nestin (1:100, ab221660, Abcam), Tuj1 (1:1000, ab18207, Abcam), NF200 (1:2000, ab8135, Abcam), GFAP (1:1000, ab7260, Abcam), Laminin (1:100, L9393, Sigma-Aldrich), MBP (1:50, 78896S, Cell Signaling Technology), GABA (1:500, A2052, Sigma-Aldrich), vGLUT1 (1:1000, 135302, Synaptic Systems, Gottingen, Germany), ChAT (1:500, AB144P, Sigma-Aldrich), 5-HT (1:5000, 20080, Immunostar, Hudson, WI, USA), MAP2 (1:2000, NB300-213, NovuBio, Centennial, USA)] were applied evenly onto the tissue sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or 555 (1:500, Invitrogen) were applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, mounting medium containing DAPI was applied, coverslips were gently placed, and slides were air-dried in a ventilated area.
Cell immunofluorescence staining: Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and incubated with permeabilization/blocking solution for 1 h. Primary antibodies diluted in PBS [Tuj1 (1:1000, ab78078, Abcam), GFAP (1:2500, ab7260, Abcam), MAP2 (1:5000, NB300-213, NovuBio), Olig2 (1:100, ab109186, Abcam)] were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After three PBST washes, DAPI (10 μg/mL) was added and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

4.19.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining
Mouse liver/kidney tissues were sectioned at 8 μm thickness using a microtome. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded ethanol and rinsed in deionized water. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained were performed on the tissue for 5 min and followed by eosin staining for 3 min. Finally, the tissue was sealed with neutral resin and directly observed under the microscope NanoZoomer S60 (Hamamatsu, Japan).

4.20.

## Behavioral detection
Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) Scoring: To assess the recovery of hindlimb motor function, BMS scoring was performed before injury and on days 1, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, and 56 after SCI. BMS scores range from 0 to 9, representing varying degrees of hindlimb motor function. Scoring is based on multiple parameters of hindlimb movement, including joint movement, paw position, stepping pattern, coordination, trunk stability, and tail position.
Mouse Sensory Evaluation: The hot plate test was carried to evaluate sensory functional recovery. A glass enclosure surrounded the hot plate to facilitate observation. After the hot plate temperature stabilized at 55 °C, mice from different groups were randomly tested. The timer was stopped immediately upon observing nociceptive responses (leg withdrawal, jumping, or paw licking), and the reaction time was recorded.
Neuroelectrophysiological Testing: At 8 weeks post-SCI, motor evoked potentials (MEP) and sensory evoked potentials (SEP) were monitored using a BL-420N biological signal acquisition system to assess the recovery of neural conduction function. The sciatic nerve and sensorimotor cortex were surgically exposed. Square-wave pulse stimulation (intensity 5 mA, pulse width 0.2 ms, frequency 10 Hz) was applied to record SEP and MEP. The amplitudes of MEP and SEP for each mouse were calculated and statistically analyzed.

4.21.

## Identification and induction of neural stem cell lineage tracing mice
Nestin-CreERT2 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Rosa26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice were purchased from Beijing Vitalstar Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Nestin-CreERT2 mice were crossed with Rosa26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice. The offspring pups were promptly genotyped using a mouse tail genotype rapid identification kit (D7283M, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primer sequences for Nestin-CreERT2 and Rosa26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice are listed in , respectively. A 20 mg/mL Tamoxifen solution was prepared in corn oil and stored protected from light. Mouse body weight was measured, and the required volume of Tamoxifen solution (75 mg/kg) was calculated. Tamoxifen was administered via intraperitoneal injection daily for 5 consecutive days. After a 7-day clearance period, recombination was induced.

4.22.

## Statistical analysis
All data in this study were presented as means ± standard errors of the means (SEM). Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical analysis of BMS scores and sustained release kinetics of hydrogel were performed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2).
