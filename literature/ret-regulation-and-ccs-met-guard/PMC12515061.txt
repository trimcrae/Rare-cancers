## ABSTRACT

EWSR1::CREM fusion positive intrabdominal sarcomas defines a rare emerging group of aggressive mesenchymal neoplasms with a predilection for the celomic cavity and often manifesting perplexing immunophenotypic profile. There is no specific standard of care therapeutic option though anecdotal reports response to pazopanib have been reported. We report herein a case of a 62‐year‐old lady who presented with lower abdominal mass and pain. Right hemicolectomy performed showed a malignant epithelioid neoplasm. By immunohistochemistry, the tumor cells were positive for CD117, keratins, and mutation analysis was positive for KIT p.V5301 mutation, initially prompting a diagnosis of epithelioid gastrointestinal stromal tumor. However, the patient developed omental relapses while on imatinib and progressive disease persisted despite change to sunitinib. A re‐review of the histopathology of both the hemicolectomy and relapsed omental sites showed marked cellular atypia, increased cellularity and mitosis in metastatic omental sites. By immunohistochemistry, the tumors in addition to CD117 positivity were also immunoreactive to keratins, synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD56, and MUC4. Next generation sequencing performed on both the primary and relapsed sites were positive for EWSR1::CREM gene fusion in addition to the KIT. P.V5301 mutation. The tumor was then reclassified as EWSR1::CREM fusion positive intrabdominal unclassified epithelioid sarcoma with concomitant KIT mutation of unknown significance. The patient was treated with cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiation therapy with partial response, but treatment was stopped because of complications and lack of tolerance. Pazopanib as a single agent was then initiated, and patients have continued to respond for the past 7 months. We highlight the significant immunophenotypic heterogeneity in these tumors and perform an extensive review of the literature of this specific entity and broader group of EWSR1::CREM/FUS fusion positive tumors ranging from quasi‐benign to malignant and finally we underscore the therapeutic utility of pazopanib as a single agent in this rare group of aggressive sarcomas.

## Introduction
The widespread use of targeted RNA next generation sequencing (NGS) in diagnostic soft tissue surgical pathology practice has led to a dramatic increase in the numbers and variants of gene fusions detected in neoplasms and several new molecularly defined entities are emerging. CREM, CREB1 and ATF1 belong to CREB family of basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors, which play key roles in neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, glucose homeostasis, spermatogenesis, and cytokine regulation [ , , , , ]. Fusions involving genes encoding the CREB family of transcription factors have been implicated in oncogenesis with EWSR1::ATF1 and EWSR1::CREB1 being most widely recognized [ , , , , , , , ]. The spectrum of tumors harboring these fusions range from benign to malignant and includes: angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma (AFH), myxoid AFH, soft tissue and gastrointestinal clear cell sarcoma, hyalinizing clear cell carcinoma of salivary gland, metastatic pheochromocytoma, subset of mesotheliomas and intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumor [ , , ]. Recent literature suggests there is an emerging group of unclassified EWSR1::CREM gene fusion positive tumors with disparate immunophenotypic profile but with striking predilection for celomic cavities such as the abdominal and thoracic cavity [ , ]. Within the CREB fusion family, neoplasms harboring EWSR1‐CREM/FUS‐CREM fusions are rare and poorly characterized [ ]. Soft tissue sarcomas harboring EWSR1::CREM fusion are challenging to treat.
Indeed, recent reports have shown clinical benefit with the use of Pazopanib, a multi‐tyrosine kinase inhibitor in these tumors [ ]. Given the striking phenotypic variation coupled with an aggressive clinical course in some of these intra‐abdominal malignant epithelioid neoplasms, it is important to diagnose them correctly for appropriate treatment.
Herein, we report a unique case of an aggressive unclassified EWSR1::CREM gene fusion‐positive intra‐abdominal epithelioid sarcoma with concomitant KIT p.V5301 mutation and morphologic and immunohistochemical features resembling gastrointestinal tumor (GIST) that achieved a durable response to pazopanib after failed multiple targeted therapies.

## Material and Methods
2.1.

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemical stains using the following commercially available antibodies were performed using the Leica Bond III autostainers (Leica Biosystems): AE1/E3 (Dako, AE1&AE3, 1:1200), CK7 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, OV‐TL 12/30, 1:200), CK20 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, SA35‐03, 1:500), CDX2 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, EPR2764Y, 1:100), GATA3 (Abcam, L50‐823, 1:2000), CD117 (Abcam, YR145, 1:400), DOG‐1 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, SP31, 1:100), ER (Abcam, E115, 1:1000), CEA (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 1106, 1:1000), OSCAR (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Z2539MP, 1:200), MUC4 (Abcam, EPR27199‐56, 1:500), CD56 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 56C04, 1:200), PAX8 (Abcam, EPR23508‐20, 1:2000), TTF‐1 (Abcam, SP141, 1:100), p40 (Abcam, EPR17863‐47, 1:1000), WT1 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, SC06‐41, 1:500), SDHB (Abcam, EPR10880, 1:2000), EMA (Thermo Fischer Scientific, E29, 1:200), calretinin (Thermo Fischer Scientific, SP13, 1:100), p63 (Biocare, 4A4, 1:300), CD34 (Cell Marque, QBEnd/10, 1:400), smooth muscle actin (Dako, 1A4, 1:600), desmin (Dako, D33, 1:200), myogenin (eBioscience, F5D, 1:100), MyoD1 (Abcam, EPR6653‐131, 1:500), calponin (Dako, CALP, 1:600), S100 (Dako, R poly, 1:2000), SOX10 (BioCare, BC34, 1:200), HMB‐45 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, ARC0685, 1:100), Ki‐67 (Dako, MIB‐1, 1:400), INI1 (Santa. Cruz, A‐5, 1:200). All protocols were developed by and performed at the OSU Wexner Medical Center Clinical Laboratory, 410 West 10th Avenue, Doan Hall 310, Columbus, OH 43210. All positive and negative controls showed appropriate staining.

2.2.

## 
RNA Sequencing
RNA was extraction from formalin fixed paraffin embedded sections using PureLink FFPE Total RNA Isolation Kit (ThermoFisher), reverse transcribed, library constructed with KAPA Stranded RNA‐Seq Kit with RiboErase reagents (Roche Genomics, Indianapolis, IN) and assessed by a probe hybridization‐based next‐generation sequencing (NGS) by NextSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA). A total of 190 genes associated with gene fusions in solid tumors were assessed.

2.3.

## Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization
After review of material to be tested by a molecular pathologist to select an area with representative tumor cells, formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from this case were evaluated by interphase fluorescence in situ hybridization The LSI EWSR1 (22q12) Dual Color, Break Apart Rearrangement Probe consisted of two fluorophore‐labeled probes that hybridize to chr 22q12 flanking the EWSR1 translocation breakpoint, including a 3′ telomeric SpectrumGreen probe and a 5′ centromeric SpectrumOrange probe (Abbott Molecular).
Up to 100 interphase tumor cells from different fields were scored by a technologist using computer assisted technology (BioView Image Analysis System), with results confirmed by the interpreting pathologist. Cells were scored as negative if orange and green signals were adjacent or fused/yellow or had single green signal(s) without corresponding orange signal(s). Cells were scored as positive if at least one set of orange and green signals were two or more signal diameters apart or had orange signal(s) without corresponding green signal(s). A case was considered positive for a EWSR1 rearrangement when more than 15% of tumor cells were positive.

## Results
3.1.

## Case Report
A 62‐year‐old woman with prior history of basal cell carcinoma presented with sudden onset left lower quadrant abdominal pain. CT abdomen revealed a 4.4‐cm soft tissue mass in the cecal region. A biopsy was performed (at the referral institution, not available for review) and showed a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm exhibiting epithelioid cytomorphology with focal rhabdoid/plasmacytoid features. By immunohistochemistry, the tumor cells were positive for CD117 (cKIT), keratins (including AE1/3 and OSCAR), focally positive for D2‐40, synaptophysin, MUM1 (focal) with retention of INI1 but negative for DOG1, WT1, CK7, CK20, Estrogen Receptor (ER), PAX8, Thyroid Transcription Factor‐1 (TTF‐1), p40, SOX10, Desmin, GATA3, CD34, Calretinin, Epithelial Membrane Antigen (EMA), Myogenin and MyoD1. These constellations of findings were presumed to be consistent with grade 2 gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). This triggered a right hemicolectomy at a referring institution, and the resected mass was morphologically similar to the previous biopsy and was positive for CD117 (cKIT), and negative for CK7, CK20, ER, SOX10, Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA), Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), Desmin, CDX2, GATA3, and HMB45 (Figure  ). She was treated with adjuvant imatinib (400 mg daily) for nodal metastasis and monitored with repeat imaging. Two years later, disease progression/recurrence was noted on imaging which revealed a new 4.9 × 2.4 cm mass between the psoas and iliacus muscles as well as multiple enlarged lymph nodes and omental caking concerning for peritoneal sarcomatosis (Figure  ). A repeat biopsy performed was consistent with GIST and a hotspot GIST panel reported mutation in KIT, 1588 G‐A exon 10 with amino acid change V5301 in 52.8% of cells (interpreted as variant of uncertain significance). This led to the switch to sunitinib (37.5 mg daily) as well as prompting a second opinion to review pathologic diagnosis at our institution (Figure  ). Slides from initial hemicolectomy were reviewed in tandem with the prior omental resection and showed features of a malignant epithelioid neoplasm with positive reactivity for CD117 and negative expression for DOG1, CK7, CK20, ER, SOX10, CEA, SMA, Desmin, CDX2, GATA3, and HMB45. Additional ancillary studies performed at our institution showed the tumor cells, by immunohistochemistry were positive for keratins including AE1/3 and CAM5.2, CD117 (cKIT), CD56, SOX11, MUC4, synaptophysin, chromogranin (spotty) and negative for DOG1, CD34, SMA, Desmin and WT1. SDHB was retained and p53 was wild type.
 Pathological examination of the relapsed omental resection showed a similar epithelioid phenotype as the prior hemicolectomy specimen but with increased cellularity, marked cytologic atypia, and brisk mitotic activity (Figure  ). DNA‐based NGS on the omental relapse confirmed the KIT p.V530I variant, interpreted by our lab as a germline VUS, with no pathogenic variants seen (other findings: MSI stable, total mutation burden < 2 mutations/mega base with no pathogenic copy number calls). However, targeted probe‐based RNA sequencing revealed an EWSR1::CREM fusion involving fusion of exon 15 of EWSR1 (chr22:2969532) to exon 6 of CREM (chr10:35477128) which was the predominant transcript (Figure  ) [ ]. This finding was validated by demonstrating the presence of EWSR1 gene rearrangement by fluorescence in situ hybridization (Figure  ). Taken together, these findings were considered prototypic of a malignant unclassified epithelioid sarcoma harboring EWSR1::CREM gene fusion, a recent/emerging entity distinctly different from GIST, warranting a change in therapy to doxorubicin (60 mg/m2), gemcitabine (1500 mg/m2) and docetaxel (50 mg/m2). She experienced partial responses on doxorubicin and on second‐line treatment with gemcitabine + docetaxel (Q2 week schedule) with initial stabilization followed by partial response to treatment (Figure  ). Treatment was held for a while due to complications (grade 3 hyponatremia, decreased performance status, and pleural effusions).
 She also received pelvic radiation to a larger right pelvic mass with continued response with combination gemcitabine‐docetaxel chemotherapy. Single‐agent Pazopanib (400 mg oral daily) started three months after radiation, which demonstrated a continued decrease in tumor size, excellent performance status (ECOG 0) and good overall wellbeing. She has been on Pazopanib for seven months and is currently doing well on a daily 400 mg maintenance dose of Pazopanib (Figure  ).

4.

## Discussion
We present an unusual case of aggressive intra‐abdominal sarcoma with EWSR1::CREM gene fusion as the oncogenic driver. Given its histopathologic mimicry of epithelioid GIST and the finding of a KIT p.V5301 VUS, an initial diagnosis of GIST was rendered. Treatment with imatinib was unsuccessful with the patient developing diffuse intraabdominal sarcomatosis, failing several additional therapies. After the case was reclassified as a EWSR1::CREM sarcoma the patient achieved a durable response to single agent‐Pazopanib.
The emerging paradigm of soft tissue and bone sarcomas with novel gene fusion continues to challenge optimal treatment recommendations for these rare entities. Indeed, the current WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumor identified more than 150 types/sub‐types and unfortunately large rigorous phase 3 clinical studies are not available on many of these entities because on their rarity. Notable is the marked genomic redundancy and promiscuity with attendant differences in molecular phenotypic and prognostic implications. Specifically, subsets of clear cell sarcoma, angiomatoid fibrous histiocytomas (AFH), and clear cell carcinomas of the head and neck are all known to have EWSR1‐CREM/FUS‐CREM fusions but with remarkable phenotypic differences, and attendant prognostic and therapeutic implications [ ]. EWSR1–CREM has been noted in intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumor as well as myxoid AFH [ , ]. Cases of hyalinizing clear cell carcinoma of salivary gland harboring this fusion, as an alternative to the more common EWSR1–ATF1 fusions have also been detailed [ ]. The phenotypic diversity of neoplasms associated with this fusion was highlighted by a study describing two unclassified malignant neoplasms, one of which was an aggressive intra‐abdominal pediatric tumor characterized by proliferation of swirling spindle cells positive for cytokeratin and CD34 while the other was a chest wall lesion in an adult exhibiting a MUC4‐positive sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma‐like histology [ ].
A recent report described two CREM‐rearranged intra‐abdominal (one renal and one mesenteric) malignant epithelioid neoplasms with perplexing immunophenotypes. One demonstrated expression of ALK with keratin while the other expressed keratin with neuroendocrine markers. In both, diagnosis was only made after NGS testing uncovered the characteristic gene fusions [ ].

EWSR1/FUS‐CREM‐positive intra‐abdominal epithelioid sarcoma is a newly emerging entity with only 17 cases reported in the English literature. Of these, EWSR1‐CREM fusion was noted in seven, while FUS‐CREM fusion was described in five. Omentum/mesentery has been the commonest site, followed by stomach, kidney, pelvic organs, and adrenal [ , , ]. Table  summarizes the previously reported cases of EWSR1/FUS::CREM fusion malignant epithelioid neoplasm. These neoplasms are characterized by a predilection for peritoneal recurrences and nodal and distant metastasis, underscoring their malignant potential [ , , , , ]. The monotonous epithelioid morphology is offset by considerable histopathologic and immunophenotypic diversity, resulting in diagnostic pitfalls. Notably, a majority express keratin and EMA, though ALK, MUC4, synaptophysin, and chromogranin‐A positivity has also been reported [ , , , , ]. Due to these confounding immunophenotypic profiles, these tumors may be misdiagnosed as other entities, including gastrointestinal stromal tumor, sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma (SEFs), ALK‐rearranged renal cell carcinomas (RCCs), and neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) [ , ].
 The rarity of EWSR1‐ CREM fusion sarcomas poses a challenge in treatment though recently Pazopanib showed some benefit in two patients with undifferentiated sarcoma [ , ]. Further, pazopanib was shown to be effective in a patient with metastatic unclassified round cell sarcoma of bone harboring EWSR1::NFATC2 gene fusion after tumor progressed on multiple cytotoxic chemotherapy regimen [ ]. Pazopanib has been FDA approved for the treatment of advanced soft tissue sarcomas (STSs) in patients who have previously received chemotherapy [ ]. Pazopanib exerts its antiangiogenic effects by inhibiting the intracellular tyrosine kinase of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) [ ]. Despite these promising results, the precise mechanism underlying the action of these inhibitors in sarcomas with ESWR1 fusions is unclear [ ].
In summary, our case highlights the value of NGS for precise genotyping of these challenging lesions with therapeutic implications. Patient's optimal treatment was unfortunately delayed because of misdiagnosis. Unique to our case, and not previously reported among this emerging group of tumors is a phenotypically resembling epithelioid GIST but tumor was resistant to imatinib and sunitinib. The reporting of a KIT p.V5301 VUS in this tumor NGS study further complicated the diagnosis/therapy selection (most genomic laboratories now regard this variant as a relatively common polymorphism unrelated to GIST pathogenesis but the genomic laboratory in this case highlighted it on their report).
We expand on the immunohistochemical and molecular genetic landscape of the under‐recognized EWSR1/FUS::CREM‐positive intra‐abdominal malignant epithelioid neoplasms/sarcomas, underscoring a durable response to Pazopanib as a single agent, like other precedent cases. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to evaluate the therapeutic role of Pazopanib in the routine treatment of this rare but aggressive group of intra‐abdominal sarcoma containing the EWSR1::CREB gene fusion.
