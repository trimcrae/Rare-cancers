## Abstract
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly lethal hematologic malignancy, and reliable prognostic biomarkers remain urgently needed. We investigated the expression and clinical relevance of the actin cytoskeleton regulator N-WASP in AML. Analysis of TCGA data revealed significantly reduced N-WASP expression in AML compared with normal tissues, and low expression was associated with adverse clinical features, including FAB-M1/M4 subtypes, DNMT3A mutations, and cytogenetic abnormalities such as t(8;21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and del(7q). Patients with low N-WASP expression exhibited shorter overall survival, and multivariate Cox regression identified N-WASP reduction as an independent risk factor, alongside advanced age and FLT3, DNMT3A and TP53 mutations. Co-expression and enrichment analysis highlighted FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR and BRAF as N-WASP-associated regulators converging on mTOR signaling. Experimental validation via RT-qPCR and western blotting in AML bone marrow samples and cell lines confirmed the downregulation of N-WASP and key co-expressed genes. Collectively, these results demonstrate that N-WASP is suppressed in AML and is closely associated with disease risk and clinical outcome. N-WASP may serve as a novel prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target warranting further investigation.

## Introduction
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous malignancy of the hematopoietic system, characterized by blocked differentiation, uncontrolled proliferation, and accumulation of immature myeloid precursors in the bone marrow, ultimately leading to clinical manifestations such as anemia, bleeding, and infections . AML can occur at any age but is most frequently diagnosed in older adults, with an incidence in China of approximately 4 cases per 100,000 people annually , , a rate influenced by ethnic, socioeconomic, and geographical factors , . The disease often progresses rapidly, with a global incidence of about 1.3 per 100,000, and is associated with high mortality. Except for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), more than half of patients across various AML subtypes experience relapse or death, resulting in a five-year survival rate of 40–60%. First-line treatment typically involves intensive chemotherapy, such as the “7 + 3” regimen (cytarabine plus an anthracycline), yet challenges, including drug resistance, relapse, and minimal residual disease, persist. Even with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, long-term disease-free survival is achieved in only 30–40% of adults and 60–70% of children . Therefore, identifying novel prognostic markers and therapeutic targets is urgently needed to improve outcomes in AML.
The actin cytoskeleton plays a fundamental role in cellular structure, signaling, and motility, and its deregulation is increasingly implicated in tumorigenesis. Actin-regulating proteins, in particular, are critical for maintaining cytoskeletal dynamics and mediating cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Neural Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein (N-WASP) , , a member of the WASP family, is a key actin-nucleation-promoting factor that links upstream GTPase signaling (e.g., Cdc42, RAC1) to the activation of the ARP2/3 complex, thereby driving actin polymerization and cytoskeletal remodeling . In solid tumors, N-WASP is frequently overexpressed; for example, in colorectal cancer, elevated N-WASP promotes ARP2/3-mediated actin assembly, enhancing cell migration and invasion . Similarly, in hepatocellular carcinoma, high N-WASP expression correlates with poor prognosis , whereas in non-small cell lung cancer, suppression of the Cdc42/N-WASP pathway inhibits invadopodia formation and metastasis . However, N-WASP can also act as a tumor suppressor in certain contexts, such as in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, where its loss accelerates tumor formation by dysregulating ERK/FOXO1 signaling and reducing TXNIP expression , . Despite these advances, the role of N-WASP in hematological malignancies—especially in AML—remains largely unexplored. Notably, recent evidence suggests that RAC1-dependent hyperphosphorylation of N-WASP contributes to midostaurin resistance in FLT3-ITD-positive AML by enhancing actin polymerization, stromal adhesion, and survival, pointing to a potential link between cytoskeletal regulation and treatment failure .
In this study, we analyzed N-WASP expression and its co-expression network using TCGA data and validated its clinical relevance in AML. We further investigated the functional impact of N-WASP on leukemic proliferation and signaling pathways. Our findings aim to clarify the dual role of N-WASP in AML pathogenesis and therapy resistance, providing a rationale for targeting the actin cytoskeleton as a novel therapeutic strategy.

## Methods

## Patient data
The TCGA is a world-renowned database that maps the genomic variation of all human cancers (including AML) through genomic analysis technology to understand the mechanism of cancer occurrence and development. Our data were sourced from an public online database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) which features RNA-seq detection data for AML. Samples that had previously been diagnosed with other malignancies were excluded, resulting in a total of 138 AML samples with available expression data. This dataset included mRNA expression data, clinical and laboratory parameter data, as well as survival data for AML patients.
In addition, clinical information and relevant clinical data, including clinical manifestations and hematological indicators, were collected from newly diagnosed AML patients and control samples at the Department of Pediatrics and the Department of Hematology in the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University, between 01/2021 and 12/2023. This dataset featured 30 newly diagnosed AML patients with remaining bone marrow blood samples. In addition, 20 control samples were collected from volunteers and hospitalized patients with non-hematological malignancies and non-severe infections during the same period. Informed consent was obtained from the patients, their families, and volunteers in writing. All minor participants have obtained their guardian’s consent in writing. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. Ethical Approval Number: KLLY-2020-188.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Using gene expression values from patient samples in the TCGA database, we calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficient to assess the correlation between N-WASP and selected other genes. Genes with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R) > 0.3 were defined as co-expressed regulatory genes of N-WASP. Once we had identified the co-expressed regulatory genes of N-WASP, we then performed gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) analyses . This involved identifying the GO and KEGG that these co-expressed genes are associated with.
Based on the co-expression and regulatory relationships between N-WASP and the co-expressed mRNA, as well as the pathway regulatory relationships involving mRNA, we then constructed an co-expression gene network of N-WASP. This network revealed how N-WASP indirectly regulates relevant signaling pathway. Significant GO and KEGG analyses were identified by Fisher’s exact test and the χ2 test. Significance thresholds were defined based on P-values (P < 0.001) and False Discovery Rate (FDR < 0.001).
The mRNA expression levels of N-WASP were then analyzed by the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/detail.php), followed by a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of N-WASP and its key co-expressed genes mainly based on the main Go, KEGG and correlation coefficient between N-WASP and ComRNA, including FNIP1 (folliculin interacting proteins 1), CLIP1 (CAP-gly domain containing linker protein 1), RICTOR (rapamycin insensitive companion of TOR), and BRAF (v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B). Next, we used https://kmplot.com/analysis/ (Kaplan Meier Plotter) on-line analysis to generate Kaplan-Meier survival curves to compare overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS).

## RT-qPCR
Next, we conducted RT-qPCR (quantitative real-time PCR) analysis to investigate mRNA expression levels. RNA was extracted from both patient and AML cell lines (Human myeloid leukemia cell lines: THP-1, MV4-11, OCI-AML2, MOLM14, HL60, and 293 T were donated by Peking First Hospital and West China Hospital of Sichuan University and are maintained long-term by the Institute of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology at the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University.) samples using the RNAiso Plus method (Japanese TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Subsequently, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the TaKaRa Primeseript RT reagent Kit (Japanese TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd). The mRNA levels of N-WASP and its co-expressed genes, BRAF, CLIP1, FNIP1, and RICTOR, were then determined with the Takara SYBR real-time PCR reagent kit (Japanese TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The relative mRNA expression levels of each gene were represented by the 2−ΔΔCt method, where ΔCt of the target gene was defined by Ct(target gene) mean −Ct(GAPDH) mean. ΔΔCt was calculated as ΔCt of the experimental group −ΔCt of the control group . The primers used to amplify the target genes were obtained from Shanghai Generay Biotech Co., Ltd, with GAPDH serving as the reference gene. The primers used in this study are shown in Table  .
 

## Western blotting
Next, we lysed bone marrow cells from AML patients and control groups from collected samples using RIPA buffer (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd). to extract total protein. A BCA protein concentration assay kit was used for protein quantification (Shanghai Generay Biotech Co., Ltd). Subsequently, proteins were separated by electrophoresis and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Wuhan Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd). Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1.5 housr, followed by three washes with TBST. The membrane was then washed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, the membranes were washed three times in TBST at room temperature and incubated with a secondary antibody for 1.5 h. The exposure solution (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd) was prepared, and the PVDF membrane was placed in the exposure solution before exposure, and analyzed by Image J software. The relative expression levels of the target protein were represented by comparing the grayscale values of each protein to those of a reference (GAPDH). GAPDH antibody (dilution ratio: 1:5000) was purchased from Hangzhou Hua’an Biotechnology Co., Ltd, and N-WASP antibody (dilution ratio: 1:1000) was purchased from Abcam in the United Kingdom.

## Statistical analysis
SPSS 29.0 software was used for statistical analysis, while graphs were generated by GraphPad Prism 7.0 and SCImago Graphica 1.0.53 software. Categorical data were analyzed by the χ² test. Continuous data that confirmed to a normal distribution are described by mean and standard deviation ( ±s). When variances were homogeneous, the independent sample t-test was used for comparison between two groups; two or more groups of single factors were tested by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In case of heterogeneous variances, we applied Welch’s correction. Non-normally distributed data are described as a median (Q1, Q3), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison between two groups. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to the normal distribution, and the Levene test was applied to test the homogeneity of variance.
For survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed, and differences were assessed using the Log-Rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to analyze factors associated with prognosis. The cutoff values used to categorize continuous variables into two groups in Cox regression analysis are defined based on clinical criteria widely accepted for patients at this hospital and the upper limits of normal established by the laboratory; these values are relevant to prognosis and treatment stratification , . Correlations were evaluated by Pearson’s correlation analysis, with correlation coefficient values ranging from − 1 to 1, where positive values indicated a positive correlation and negative values indicated a negative correlation. All P-values were based on bilateral tests, and the statistical significance was P < 0.05.

## Results

## N-WASP expression levels were low in patients with acute myeloid leukemia and associated with poor prognosis
To investigate the transcriptional expression profile of the N-WASP gene in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), we first analyzed its mRNA expression using the GEPIA database, which incorporates data from the TCGA and GTEx projects. Our analysis revealed that N-WASP expression was significantly lower in AML samples compared to normal controls (Fig.  A). We further conducted a pan-cancer analysis by downloading N-WASP expression data for 31 different human cancers from GEPIA (Fig.  B). In contrast to its reported upregulation in certain solid tumors (such as breast cancer and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma), N-WASP expression was generally reduced in AML. This pattern aligns with observations in other malignancies like cervical cancer and clear cell renal cell carcinoma. The pan-cancer expression profile thus highlights that N-WASP exhibits cancer type-specific expression patterns, with its downregulation in AML potentially indicating a distinct role in leukemogenesis. Consequently, these findings suggest that N-WASP may play a key and context-dependent role in the progression of AML.
 TCGA online survival analysis showed that patients in the low expression group had a poorer overall survival (OS) compared than patients in the high expression group (P < 0.05). We did not find any statistically significant differences in terms of event-free survival (EFS) with the levels of N-WASP (P > 0.05) (Fig.  C–F). Therefore, we found that N-WASP was expressed at low levels and associated with an unfavorable prognosis in AML.

## N-WASP expression and its association with specific clinical characteristics provided by the TCGA database
Since N-WASP expression was significantly correlated with the prognosis of AML patients, we next investigated the association between N-WASP expression and the specific clinical features of patients with AML in the TCGA database (Table  ). FAB typing, fusion gene and chromosome karyotype detection play a key role in the early diagnosis, treatment and prognosis assessment of AML. Then We identified a significant difference between N-WASP expression and the distribution of FAB subtypes. Notably, patients with low levels of N-WASP expression were more frequently associated with the FAB-M1 and FAB-M4 subtypes (P = 0.018 and 0.041, respectively) and less frequently with the FAB-M7 subtype (P = 1.000). Furthermore, the high N-WASP expression group exhibited a higher prevalence of DNMT3A mutations (P = 0.011). However, N-WASP expression was not statistically different from mutations in other genes.
 In addition, compared with the high expression group of N-WASP, the low expression group was significantly different from t(8; 21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1 (P = 0.002) and del(7q)/7q- (P = 0.035) chromosome abnormalities/fusion gene differences were statistically significant. There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of other chromosome abnormalities/fusion genes, including t(15; 17)/PML::RARA and BCR::ABL1 (P > 0.05). However, this difference was not statistically significant between the low and high N-WASP expression groups with regards to gender, age, white blood cell count (WBC), peripheral blood (PB), bone marrow (BM) blast percentage, cytogenetic risk classification (CRC), transplant status, or other factors.
In summary, these data suggest that low N-WASP expression is a feature of high-risk AML and is more commonly observed in patients with the FAB-M1 and FAB-M4 subtypes, along with t(8; 21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and del(7q)/7q- patients with chromosomal abnormalities/fusion genes. Conversely, high N-WASP expression is associated with DNMT3A gene mutation.

## Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis identified N-WASP as an independent prognostic factor for AML
To understanding the prognostic value of N-WASP expression under the influence of various clinical characteristics, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses based on the following bivariate variables: N-WASP expression levels (low vs. high), gender (male vs. female), age (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years), white blood cells (WBC) count (< 50 × 109/L vs. ≥ 50 × 109/L), anemia (severe < 90 g/L vs. mild ≥ 90 g/L), blood platelet (PLT) count (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 × 109/L), peripheral blood blasts (< 50 vs. ≥ 50), bone marrow blasts (< 50 vs. ≥ 50), cytogenetic risk classification (non-high risk vs. high risk), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (yes vs. no), molecular factors such as FLT3 mutations (mutant ‘+’ vs. wild-type ‘-’), relapse (yes vs. no), fusion genes such as PML::RARA (yes vs. no), t(8; 21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1, and other chromosome abnormalities/fusion genes (yes vs. no) (Table  ).
 Univariate Cox Regression Analysis demonstrated that low levels of N-WASP expression, older age, the absence of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and gene mutations (FLT3, DNMT3A, TET2, and TP53) are all independent risk factors affecting OS in AML patients. Low levels of N-WASP expression had an adverse impact on OS (HR = 0.576, 95% CI 0.357–0.929; P = 0.024), while older age (≥ 60 years) was also unfavorable for OS (HR = 3.493, 95% CI 2.227–5.477; P = 0.000). HSCT had a favorable effect on OS (HR = 0.480, 95% CI 0.300–0.768.300.768; P = 0.002). In addition, mutations in FLT3 (HR = 1.694, 95% CI 1.059–2.712; P = 0.028), DNMT3A (HR = 2.042, 95% CI 1.244–3.350; P = 0.005), TET2 (HR = 2.041, 95% CI 1.014–4.108; P = 0.046), and TP53 (HR = 4.098, 95% CI 1.739–9.656; P = 0.001) were all associated with an increased hazard of death, indicating adverse effects on OS. Gender, age, PLT, and other clinical and molecular factors did exert significant effects on OS. That variables were first identified in univariate analysis and their independent prognostic value was subsequently confirmed through multivariate analysis.
Multivariate Cox regression analysis included the following covariates: low levels of N-WASP expression, older age (≥ 60 years), the absence of HSCT, and gene mutations (FLT3, DNMT3A, TET2, and TP53). Our analysis indicated that low levels of N-WASP expression (HR = 0.347, 95% CI 0.198–0.610; P < 0.001), older age (HR = 3.379, 95% CI 1.981–5.764; P < 0.001), FLT3 (HR = 1.865, 95% CI 1.120–3.103;P = 0.017), DNMT3A (HR = 2.125, 95% CI 1.172–3.853; P = 0.013), and TP53 gene mutation (HR = 3.425, 95% CI 1.350–8.690; P = 0.010) were all independent risk factors for OS. Thus, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified low levels of N-WASP expression, older age, and gene mutations (FLT3, DNMT3A, and TP53) as independent risk factors for OS, thus rendering these as valuable prognostic markers and guiding factors for the treatment of AML patients.

## Molecular mechanisms mediated byN-WASPin AML
To further investigate the mechanistic role of N-WASP in AML, we performed co-expression network analysis based on RNA sequencing data from 138 AML patients in a database. Based on Pearson’s correlation (Pearson’s R > 0.3) between N-WASP expression and genes, we identified a total of 475 genes that were co-expressed with N-WASP (P < 0.01). Of these, 416 genes exhibited a positive correlation with N-WASP expression, while 59 genes exhibited a negative correlation. In addition, we performed a heatmap analysis based on Pearson correlations between N-WASP and key co-expressed genes (Fig.  A). Subsequently, we conducted GO functional enrichment analysis of N-WASP co-expressed regulatory genes to further explore the biological pathways involving N-WASP in AML. GO functional analysis identified several key processes, including protein ubiquitination, transcriptional regulation, gene expression regulation, apoptosis, and cell cycle involvement (Fig.  B).
 Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis of N-WASP co-expressed regulatory genes identified significant enrichment in cancer-related pathways, such as the mTOR, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, JAK-STAT, and AMPK signaling pathway, with 264 co-expressed genes involved. The complete enrichment analysis also included the AML and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) pathway (FDR < 0.01), suggesting a strong association between N-WASP and leukemia, potentially via these functional pathways (Fig.  C).
Next, we constructed a network to elucidate the regulatory relationships of N-WASP with co-expressed mRNAs and their involvement in regulating major signaling pathways, either directly or indirectly. Network analysis confirmed that TELO2 (Pearson R = −0.3995), FNIP1 (Pearson R = 0.6646), CLIP1 (Pearson R = 0.4406), and RICTOR (Pearson R = 0.5906) are genes that are directly regulated by N-WASP, which in turn can modulate the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig.  D).
In addition, BRAF (Pearson R = 0.5884) was identified as a target of multiple genes (YWHAB, YWHAE, PRKCB, PRKCG, RAP1A, and PRKCA) and directly linked to the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig.  ).
 

## N-WASPand its co-expressed genes (FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, andBRAF) in AML patients/cell lines: prognosis and expression
Next, we conducted further correlation analysis between N-WASP and its co-expressed genes, FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF, using the TCGA database. Our results showed that N-WASP was positively correlated with FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF (all with P-values < 0.05) (Fig.  A–D). To better understand the significance of N-WASP co-expressed genes with regard to the prognosis of AML patients, we divided these patients into two groups based on the quartile of gene expression (a low expression group [n = 35] and a high expression group [n = 103]) and generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves for overall survival (OS). Analysis indicated that patients in the low expression group had a lower OS when compared to those with higher expression levels of FNIP1 (P = 0.016), CLIP1 (P = 0.969), RICTOR (P = 0.000), and BRAF (P = 0.032) (Fig.  E–H). Therefore, we were able to confirm that N-WASP co-expressed genes (FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF) are also associated with the prognosis of AML, though their correlation with N-WASP suggests these relationships may not be independent.
 Similarly, we analyzed the expression levels of N-WASP and its co-expressed genes FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF in 30 types of human cancer by downloading data from the GEPIA database. Using GraphPad Prism software, we conducted pan-cancer analysis to observe the difference of gene expression in different tumor types, which revealed the dual roles of these genes in both cancer inhibition and promotion (Fig.  A–D). Furthermore, we validated the mRNA expression levels of these genes in AML patients using RT-qPCR and found that N-WASP, along with its co-expressed genes FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF, were all expressed at significantly lower levels in AML patients (Fig.  E–I). The mRNA expression levels of N-WASP were notably lower than those in the control group [(0.4983 ± 0.1251) vs. (0.9380 ± 0.1538)] (P = 0.032). Similarly, the mRNA expression levels of FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF were all significantly reduced when compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Subsequently, we used Western Blotting to confirm the protein expression level of N-WASP in AML patients, which was significantly decreased when compared to the control group [0.4849 (0.3486, 0.5878)] vs. [0.8679 (0.7023, 1.1233)] (P = 0.010) (Fig.  J,K).
 We further used RT-qPCR to detect the mRNA expression level of N-WASP in AML cell lines (THP-1, MV4-11, Molm14, OCI, HL60), which was significantly decreased when compared to the control 293 T cell lines in THP-1 cell lines [0.2473 (0.2188, 0.2925) vs. 0.9300 (0.7367, 1.5008)] (P = 0.021, Fig.  L). The mRNA expression levels of N-WASP in MV4-11 cells were significantly lower than those in control 293 T cells [0.7981 (0.6255, 0.9451) vs. 0.9300 (0.7367,1.500)] (P = 0.034, Fig.  L). Our results showed that N-WASP is expressed at low levels at both patient and cellular levels.

## Survival validation of N-WASP co-expressed genes across cancer types
To further evaluate whether N-WASP co-expressed genes are functionally relevant to tumor progression rather than merely differentially expressed, we performed Kaplan–Meier survival analyses using the GEPIA database across multiple cancer types (Supplementary Fig. 1).
Specifically, patients within each cancer type were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression levels of FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF. The results demonstrated that these genes could significantly stratify patient prognosis within individual cancer types, indicating their potential functional relevance. For FNIP1, high expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in KICH, OV, and THCA, whereas it correlated with improved OS in KIRC, suggesting context-dependent roles across tumor types. For CLIP1, elevated expression was associated with worse OS in LGG. Similarly, RICTOR exhibited dual prognostic roles: high expression predicted poor prognosis in LIHC but was associated with better survival outcomes in KIRC and LGG. In addition, BRAF high expression was linked to unfavorable prognosis in PRAD but favorable survival in KIRC. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that N-WASP co-expressed genes not only show differential expression patterns but also provide consistent prognostic stratification within specific cancer types. Importantly, the direction of their prognostic effects varies across tumor contexts, supporting the notion that these genes may exert bidirectional roles in tumor progression and suppression.

## Discussion
AML is an aggressive hematologic malignancy driven by the malignant transformation of hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by high relapse rates and poor overall survival , . Despite advances in molecularly targeted therapies, such as FLT3 and BCL-2 inhibitors, treatment resistance and disease recurrence remain major clinical challenges , . There is a persistent need to identify reliable biomarkers for prognosis prediction and novel therapeutic targets. The actin cytoskeleton regulator N-WASP has been implicated in solid tumor progression, but its role in hematologic malignancies—particularly in AML—has been largely unexplored. In this study, we provide the first comprehensive analysis of N-WASP expression and its clinical and functional significance in AML.
We found that N-WASP is significantly downregulated in AML patients compared to normal controls, a pattern that contrasts with its frequent overexpression in certain solid tumors such as colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma. Pan-cancer analysis further highlighted the tissue-specific duality of N-WASP expression, suggesting it may play distinct roles in different cancer types. Critically, low N-WASP expression was associated with unfavorable overall survival in AML, supporting its potential role as a prognostic biomarker. These findings were consistent across database analyses and validated in patient-derived samples using RT-qPCR and Western blotting. At the cellular level, N-WASP mRNA was also significantly reduced in multiple AML cell lines compared to control cells, reinforcing the clinical relevance of its downregulation.
Notably, low N-WASP expression was associated with specific disease features, including specific French-American-British (FAB) subtypes (M1 and M4), DNMT3A mutations, and adverse cytogenetic abnormalities such as t(8;21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and del(7q). That low N-WASP expression shows a subtype-specific pattern, being more frequent in M1 and M4 cases within our cohort. That while DNMT3A mutations are common in AML and clonal hematopoiesis, their prognostic impact is context-dependent, often influenced by co-mutations. In our multivariate analysis, DNMT3A mutation emerged as an independent risk factor for overall survival, which is consistent with some studies, particularly in older adults or when co-occurring with other lesions like FLT3-ITD. CBF-AML, including t(8;21), is indeed associated with a favorable prognosis. Our finding that this translocation was more frequent in the low N-WASP group is an interesting association that may point to a distinct biological role of N-WASP within this generally favorable genetic subset. We now propose that the downregulation of N-WASP might identify a biological subgroup within CBF-AML or could be related to specific aspects of its leukemogenesis, without implying it converts CBF-AML into a high-risk category. The prognostic value of N-WASP appears to be independent of this cytogenetic marker in our multivariate model.
To explore the functional context of N-WASP in AML, we constructed a co-expression network based on TCGA data. We identified 475 genes significantly correlated with N-WASP expression, many of which are involved in critical processes such as protein ubiquitination, transcriptional regulation, and apoptosis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in cancer-related signaling cascades, including mTOR, PI3K-Akt, JAK-STAT, and MAPK – . Among the most strongly correlated genes were FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, and BRAF—each linked to key regulatory hubs in these pathways. These genes were consistently downregulated in AML patients and also correlated with survival, suggesting that N-WASP may influence AML progression through these molecular interactions.
For instance, FNIP1 is a modulator of AMPK-mTOR signaling and cellular metabolism; CLIP1 is involved in microtubule dynamics and has been reported as a fusion partner in kinase-driven solid tumors; RICTOR is a core component of mTORC2 with context-dependent oncogenic or tumor-suppressive roles; and BRAF is a known effector in the MAPK pathway. The downregulation of these genes in AML hints at a coordinated disruption of cytoskeletal and metabolic signaling networks, which may contribute to disease pathogenesis or represent a vulnerability that could be therapeutically exploited.
In particular, FNIP1, named Folliculin Interacting Protein 1, was initially identified due to its association with the tumor suppressor folliculin (FLCN) . FLCN/FNIP, as a multifunctional complex, plays a key role in the regulation of mTORC1 and AMPK activity in metabolic processes. FNIP1 can be phosphorylated by AMPK, which acts as a negative regulator in the mTOR nutrition-sensing pathway , . FNIP1/2 may also regulate the mTOR pathway by modulating Hsp90 activity . Recent studies have suggested that the regulatory role of FNIP1 on AMPK may be cell-type-dependent . FNIP1 is also known to play a key role in regulating fat cell metabolism and glucose homeostasis independently of the AMPK/mTOR pathway in certain scenarios. It has also been reported that oxidative stress can promote the degradation of FNIP1, and that FNIP1 can facilitate the differentiation and maturation of B-lymphocytes .
CLIP1, called as CAP-Gly domain-containing linker protein 1, belongs to the family of cell-skeletal proteins and is characterized by conserved proline-rich domains. CLIP1 interacts with microtubules and plays a crucial role in intracellular vesicle transport . Several studies have shown that in lung cancer, CLIP1 is a known fusion partner of other receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) including ALK, ROS1, and RET, as well as LTK, thus making it a key driver of oncogenesis. In other words, CLIP1-LTK fusion genes are associated with the occurrence and development of lung cancer. ALK inhibitors induced apoptosis of tumor cells and inhibited the activity of CLIP1-LTK kinase , .
RICTOR is an mTORC2-specific accessory factor and an upstream kinase for various AGC kinases such as AKT. RICTOR plays a critical role in mTORC1 substrate specificity, stability, and regulation. Interestingly, the association of RICTOR with different complexes has both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive implications. In liver cancer, its interaction with ILK promotes carcinogenesis, favoring processes such as invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis, and cancer cell survival. On the other hand, other studies have suggested that in renal cell carcinoma, RICTOR’s complex formation with PCD4 reduces cancer cell metastatic capability and is associated with a better prognosis . RICTOR has also been implicated in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, metabolism, and drug resistance. Moreover, studies have found that patients with RICTOR amplification do not respond well to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Therefore, RICTOR amplification might be associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes and resistance to TKIs. Further findings suggest that RICTOR lead to tumor resistance to RTK-TKI via an Akt-dependent pathway .
BRAF is a member of the raf/mil protein family that encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase. This protein plays an important role in the passage of the MAP kinase/ERK signaling pathway and is involved in differentiation, cell division, and secretion. Mutations in the BRAF gene are common in a variety of cancers, including pediatric gliomas, lung cancer, and malignant melanoma. BRAF activates MAPK pathways with or without RAS, thereby promoting tumor development – . That while activating BRAF mutations are indeed oncogenic drivers, the role of BRAF expression levels in cancer is context-dependent and can vary across tumor types. In AML, specific genetic alterations or regulatory mechanisms might lead to a scenario where reduced BRAF expression is a feature of aggressive disease. This could be due to the complex regulatory networks involving BRAF, where its function might be modulated by interactions with other pathways (like mTOR signaling identified in our study) rather than solely by its mutation status. Furthermore, the prognostic significance of gene expression levels does not always align directly with mutation-driven oncogenic activity, as expression can be influenced by epigenetic modifications, transcriptional repression, or feedback loops within the leukemic microenvironment. Our data suggest that in the specific context of AML, decreased BRAF expression, potentially as part of a coordinated downregulation of a signaling module involving N-WASP, is associated with adverse outcomes. This highlights the need for further functional studies to dissect the precise role of BRAF in AML pathogenesis.
Interestingly, the downregulation of N-WASP and its co-expressed genes in AML contrasts with the resistance mechanism reported by Garitano-Trojaola et al., in which RAC1-dependent hyperactivation of N-WASP contributed to midostaurin resistance in FLT3-ITD AML . This suggests that the role of N-WASP may be highly context-dependent, varying according to genetic background, disease stage, or microenvironmental influences. In some settings, N-WASP may act as a tumor suppressor whose loss promotes leukemogenesis, whereas in others—such as FLT3-ITD-positive cells under inhibitor pressure—its hyperactivity may support survival and adhesion. This duality underscores the importance of patient stratification when considering N-WASP or cytoskeleton-targeted strategies.
Interestingly, a recent study identified EG-011, a first-in-class small-molecule activator of WASP, which demonstrated potent antitumor activity in preclinical models of hematologic cancers, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, by promoting actin polymerization and impairing leukemic cell viability and migration . This finding provides a compelling proof-of-concept that pharmacological activation of WASP family proteins, including N-WASP, could represent a novel therapeutic strategy. In the context of our findings, where N-WASP is downregulated in AML, exploring whether such activators can restore functional N-WASP activity or modulate its downstream pathways (e.g., mTOR signaling) in AML models warrants future investigation.
Our co-expression and enrichment analyses indicate that N-WASP and its co-expressed genes (FNIP1, CLIP1, RICTOR, BRAF) converge on key signaling pathways, particularly the mTOR pathway. We propose a model where the coordinated downregulation of this gene module (including N-WASP) may lead to dysregulation of mTOR signaling, which is a central regulator of cell growth, metabolism, and survival. Specifically, FNIP1 is a negative regulator of mTORC1, RICTOR is a core component of mTORC2, and BRAF is an upstream activator of the MAPK pathway which cross-talks with mTOR – . The downregulation of these regulators could disrupt normal mTOR pathway homeostasis, potentially contributing to the altered survival and proliferation of AML cells. While our current data establish a strong clinical and expression-level association, we acknowledge that this proposed mechanism requires direct functional validation.
Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical sample size was limited, and all data were derived from a single cohort. Future multicenter studies with larger patient numbers are needed to validate the prognostic utility of N-WASP and further establish its independence through multivariate analyses incorporating N-WASP. Second, while bioinformatic analyses strongly suggest involvement of mTOR, PI3K-Akt, and JAK-STAT pathways, functional validation using in vitroor in vivomodels—such as through N-WASP overexpression or knockdown—is necessary to establish causality. Third, the relationship between N-WASP and the bone marrow microenvironment, particularly mesenchymal stromal cells, merits deeper investigation given emerging evidence of cytoskeleton-mediated resistance mechanisms. Finally, while our bioinformatic and expression analyses establish a strong clinical association, they do not demonstrate causality. The proposed model where N-WASP downregulation contributes to AML pathogenesis through dysregulation of pathways like mTOR requires direct functional validation. Future studies should employ gain- and loss-of-function experiments in AML cell lines and primary samples to elucidate the precise mechanistic role of N-WASP. Specifically, we plan to investigate the impact of N-WASP modulation on AML cell proliferation, clonogenic potential, differentiation, and pathway activation (e.g., mTOR, PI3K-Akt). Furthermore, the relationship between N-WASP expression, specific genetic subtypes like CBF-AML with t(8;21), and the bone marrow microenvironment merits deeper investigation to understand the context-dependent roles of this cytoskeletal regulator. These functional studies are essential to move beyond correlation and establish causality, potentially paving the way for therapeutic strategies targeting the N-WASP network.

## Conclusion
Our findings reveal that N-WASP is downregulated in AML and associated with high-risk clinical and molecular features. Its expression level serves as an independent prognostic factor, and its co-expression network highlights potential interactions with mTOR and other signaling pathways. These insights position N-WASP as a candidate biomarker and a potential target for further mechanistic and therapeutic exploration in AML.
