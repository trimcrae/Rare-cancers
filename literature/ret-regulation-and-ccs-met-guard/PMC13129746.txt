## Abstract

## Background/Aims
Lenvatinib resistance remains a critical barrier in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) therapy. However, the underlying mechanisms and strategies for reversing resistance remain incompletely understood.

## Methods
Integrated transcriptomics of lenvatinib-resistant patient tumors and an acquired-resistance murine model identified a novel macrophage subpopulation. Functional validation employed CRISPR-SAM screening, conditioned medium (CM) assays, subcutaneous/orthotopic xenografts, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), and patient-derived xenografts (PDXs). Mechanistic studies included ChIP-qPCR, co-immunoprecipitation, and pharmacologic targeting. Clinical relevance was assessed in a retrospective cohort.

## Results
Resistant HCC exhibited significant enrichment of a COLEC12high TAM subset, which correlated with poor survival and treatment response. These TAMs secreted neuregulin-1 (NRG1), activating HER2/HER3-AKT signaling in tumor cells to drive cancer stemness and lenvatinib resistance. Mechanistically, in TAMs COLEC12 sequestered STAT1 in the cytoplasm, preventing its phosphorylation, and thereby derepressing STAT3-mediated NRG1 transcription. Depletion of NRG1 reversed the stemness phenotypes and resensitized tumors to lenvatinib both in vitro and in vivo. Clinically, high NRG1 expression predicted an inferior lenvatinib response and shorter survival. Crucially, the bispecific anti-HER2/HER3 antibody zenocutuzumab restored lenvatinib efficacy in PDOs, PDXs, and murine models.

## Conclusions
Our work establishes the COLEC12high TAM/NRG1 axis as a master regulator of therapeutic resistance and identifies NRG1 as a predictive biomarker, providing a clinically actionable strategy to overcome lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

## INTRODUCTION
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, with systemic therapies central to advanced disease management [ , ]. Lenvatinib, a multityrosine kinase inhibitor of VEGFRs, FGFRs, PDGFRα, RET, and KIT, is a standard first-line treatment that is noninferior to sorafenib [ ]. Despite initial efficacy, rapid intrinsic and acquired resistance limits benefit, with most patients progressing within months [ ]. Tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms, such as kinase rewiring (e.g., EGFR hyperactivation) and oncogenic mutations [ - ], have been studied, but the immunosuppressive and plasticity-inducing tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), plays a critical but incompletely understood role in therapeutic resistance.
TAMs are a dominant, heterogeneous TME component with remarkable plasticity [ - ]. They promote progression, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy, including sorafenib [ , ]. Spatial and single-cell analyses revealed distinct TAM states and spatial patterns linked to tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis [ ] Notably, specific subsets, such as TREM2+ TAMs, occupy immunosuppressive niches that exclude CD8+ T cells and are strongly associated with immunotherapy resistance and metastasis [ , ]. These findings highlight how TAM subsets and spatial dynamics shape resistance. However, the specific TAM subsets responsible for lenvatinib resistance and their molecular mediators have not yet been fully characterized.
To explore macrophage-derived mediators potentially involved in lenvatinib resistance, we focused on Collectin Subfamily Member 12 (COLEC12), a pattern recognition receptor involved in innate immunity and phagocytosis that is expressed on specific macrophage subsets [ ]. However, its functional role in cancer, and particularly in mediating therapeutic resistance, is undefined. Similarly, Neuregulin 1 (NRG1) is a potent ligand for ERBB2 (HER2) and ERBB3 (HER3) receptors [ ]. NRG1 binding induces HER3–HER2 heterodimerization, activating the PI3K-AKT and MAPK cascades that are central to survival, proliferation, and stemness [ - ]. While NRG1 gene fusions are oncogenic drivers in other cancers (e.g., lung and pancreas) and are targeted by agents such as bispecific anti-HER2/HER3 antibody zenocutuzumab (MCLA-128) [ , ], the significance of NRG1 in HCC and its contribution to TKI resistance are unknown.
Here, using integrated transcriptomic analysis of clinical samples and an acquired resistance model, we identified a COLEC12high TAM subset enriched in lenvatinib-resistant HCC. These TAMs are the main source of secreted NRG1, which activates tumor cell-intrinsic HER2/HER3 signaling to drive stemness and lenvatinib resistance. COLEC12 promotes STAT3-dependent NRG1 transcription by sequestering STAT1 and inhibiting its phosphorylation. We further validated NRG1 as a predictive biomarker for lenvatinib response and survival, and showed that zenocutuzumab, which targets the NRG1–HER2/HER3 axis, overcomes resistance in preclinical patients, including patient-derived systems. This study identifies the COLEC12high TAM/NRG1 axis as a potential driver of lenvatinib resistance and proposes a dual therapeutic strategy targeting this circuit.

## METHODS

## Ethics statement, patients and tissues
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) (Approval No.: G2023‑184‑01). This retrospective study involved fifty HCC tissue samples collected from patients who received preoperative lenvatinib treatment at the SYSUCC between 2018 and 2023. Treatment response—classified into sensitivity and resistance groups—was evaluated based on RECIST v1.1 criteria. Sample processing followed standard pathological procedures, and all human specimens were anonymized in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, as revised).

## Animal experiments
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Ethics Approval ID: L102012024050F). BALB/C nude mice (male, 4–5 weeks old, ~30 g) were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under standard conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 20–22°C, 40–70% humidity).

## Statistics and reproducibility
Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 8.0 with two-sided tests and significance at P<0.05. Survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test for parametric data and Mann–Whitney U for nonparametric data; comparisons among >2 groups used one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was assessed using Levene’s test. The quantitative data are presented as the mean±standard error of the mean. The numbers of biological replicates, animals, or independent experiments are given in the figure legends. Immunoblotting (IB), immunohistochemical (IHC), and immunofluorescent (IF) images are representative of ≥3 independent replicates with similar results.

## RESULTS

## COLEC12high TAMs promote lenvatinib resistance in patients with HCC
To identify the key TME component mediating lenvatinib resistance in HCC, we integrated transcriptomic and CIBERSORT analyses of data from lenvatinib-treated patients (n=6; 3 resistant, 3 sensitive). Macrophages were the most markedly increased immune subset in resistant tumors ( ). Using a MycOETrp53KO spontaneous HCC model with chronic lenvatinib exposure, we profiled macrophages from resistant tumors and patient samples, and identified a conserved 58-gene resistance-associated macrophage signature ( , ). A CRISPR-SAM gainof-function screen of THP-1-derived macrophages revealed that COLEC12 overexpression strongly increased HCC cell survival under lenvatinib treatment ( , ; ).
 Clinically, COLEC12 expression was restricted to macrophages in single-cell datasets (GSE151530 and GSE149614) and colocalized with CD68 in HCC tissues ( ; , ). COLEC12 expression correlated with CD68 expression in the TCGA-LIHC ( ), and tumors enriched with COLEC12high TAMs had shorter PFS and OS and lower objective response rates to lenvatinib ( ; , ).
Collectively, these data reveal that COLEC12high macrophages constitute an enriched TME subpopulation associated with lenvatinib resistance.

## COLEC12high TAMs drive lenvatinib resistance through paracrine-mediated stemness reprogramming
Based on the results of transwell coculture experiments showing physical separation of macrophages and HCC cells, we hypothesized that a paracrine mechanism is involved. Huh7 and Hep3B cells were exposed to conditioned medium (CM) from COLEC12-overexpressing (COLEC12OE) macrophages, including cells derived from THP-1 cells, monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) generated from patient blood, and TAMs isolated from HCC resections, with each population verified by flow cytometry (CD45+CD11b+CD68+; ; , , ). COLEC12OE CM increased cell viability and clonogenicity, reduced apoptosis and increased the IC50 of lenvatinib ( ; – , ).
 This paracrine effect was further validated in vivo. Subcutaneous xenografts exposed to COLEC12OE CM showed accelerated growth, reduced lenvatinib sensitivity, and increased tumor volume/weight. In contrast, orthotopic models similarly failed to respond to systemic lenvatinib, as reflected by increased liver-to-body weight ratios ( ).
Mechanistically, COLEC12OE CM enriched hematopoietic stem cell differentiation pathways and upregulated stemness markers (SOX2/OCT4/CD44/CD133) at the mRNA and protein levels in vitro and in xenografts ( ; ). Sphere and limiting dilution assays demonstrated enhanced self-renewal and stem cell frequency ( ; , ), and CM-preconditioned cells initiated tumors more efficiently at lower inocula in vivo ( ).
Collectively, COLEC12high macrophages drive lenvatinib resistance in HCC through paracrine secretion activating cancer stemness reprogramming.

## NRG1 mediates COLEC12high TAM-induced stemness and therapeutic resistance
We integrated COLEC12OE macrophage transcriptomic data with the secretome dataset from The Human Protein Atlas (THPA) to identify potential mediators of COLEC12high macrophage–induced stemness in HCC. These findings highlight NRG1 as a key candidate related to stemness and therapeutic resistance [ , ]. Validation confirmed that NRG1 upregulation at the transcriptional, protein, and secretion levels in COLEC12high macrophages ( ). Consistently, single-cell RNA-seq datasets (GSE151530 and GSE149614) revealed that NRG1 expression is predominantly confined to macrophage/monocyte clusters ( ).
 To functionally validate NRG1, we treated HCC cells in parallel with rNRG1 and COLEC12OE-CM. At an optimal dose ( ), rNRG1 increased proliferation ( ), upregulated stemness markers (SOX2/OCT4/CD44/CD133; ), promoted sphere formation ( ) and reduced lenvatinib sensitivity (colony formation; ), with effects comparable to those seen with COLEC12OE CM.
NRG1 knockdown in COLEC12OE macrophages reversed resistance, restoring IC50, normalizing proliferation, reducing colony formation, and increasing apoptosis ( ; – ). In subcutaneous and orthotopic models, COLEC12OE CM promoted tumor growth, liver burden, and lenvatinib resistance, all of which were abolished by silencing NRG1, establishing secreted NRG1 as the main resistance driver ( ).
Mechanistically, NRG1 was required for stemness: its knockdown blocked stemness marker induction, impaired sphere formation, and reduced the tumor-initiating cell frequency both in vitro and in vivo ( , ; – ).
Collectively, these data demonstrate that NRG1 is the master effector through which COLEC12high macrophages orchestrate stemness-driven therapeutic resistance.

## COLEC12 drives NRG1 transcription via STAT1 sequestration-mediated STAT3 activation
Our data revealed that COLEC12 overexpression upregulated NRG1 mRNA, suggesting transcriptional regulation. Since COLEC12 lacks intrinsic transcriptional activity, we screened NRG1 regulators across hTFtarget, KnockTF, and FIMO-JASPAR, identifying STAT3, JUN, RELA, and KLF4; siRNA in COLEC12OE macrophages showed only STAT3 knockdown reduced NRG1, pinpointing STAT3 as the key effector, with JASPAR predicting a STAT3 site near the NRG1 TSS ( ). ChIP–qPCR confirmed STAT3 occupancy at this site, and luciferase assays demonstrated that STAT3 activated wild-type but not mutant promoters ( ).
 To investigate its regulation, IP–MS of COLEC12-Flag complexes revealed STAT1 as a major binding partner ( ). As phosphorylated STAT1 inhibits STAT3 via sequestration [ ], we hypothesized that COLEC12 binding blocks STAT1 phosphorylation, alleviating STAT3 repression. Co-IP confirmed endogenous STAT1–COLEC12 interaction across THP-1–derived macrophages, MDMs, and TAMs, immunofluorescence showed cytoplasmic co-localization, and docking predicted direct binding of COLEC12’s intracellular domain to STAT1 SH2 ( ).
Docking predicted direct binding between the COLEC12 intracellular domain and STAT1, and COLEC12 lacking this domain failed to interact with STAT1 ( ). Full-length COLEC12 reduced STAT1 phosphorylation while increasing STAT3 phosphorylation in THP-1, MDM, or TAM macrophages whereas the mutant had no effect ( ). In addition, the IF results revealed that full-length COLEC12 increased the nuclear localization of pSTAT3 ( ).
Thus, COLEC12 promotes STAT3-driven NRG1 transcription by binding and inhibiting STAT1 phosphorylation.

## NRG1 activates cancer cell-intrinsic HER2/HER3 signaling to drive lenvatinib resistance and stemness in HCC
Previous studies have established that NRG1-driven oncogenesis occurs mainly through HER2/HER3 activation in tumor cells [ ]. Immunoblots revealed that compared with vector CM, COLEC12OE CM increased HER3, HER2, and AKT phosphorylation, and these effects were attenuated by NRG1 knockdown and by zenocutuzumab (a clinically validated HER2/HER3 bispecific antibody) in Huh7 and Hep3B cells exposed to CM ( ; ) [ ]. Treatment with zenocutuzumab markedly lowered the lenvatinib IC50 in HCC cells exposed to COLEC12OE CM and restored drug sensitivity, as further supported by enhanced growth inhibition in proliferation assays ( ; , ). COLEC12OE CM also enhanced colony formation and tumorsphere growth, both of which were abolished by zenocutuzumab, indicating effective suppression of COLEC12–NRG1–driven stemness ( ; , ).
 Zenocutuzumab suppressed COLEC12OE CM–driven tumor growth and the combination of zenocutuzumab and lenvatinib produced the most potent suppression in both subcutaneous and orthotopic Huh7 HCC models ( ; ). Furthermore, CDX-3379, an anti-HER3 antibody [ ], enhanced lenvatinib sensitivity in the immunocompetent C57BL/6 Hepa1-6 subcutaneous HCC model, and the combination therapy of CDX-3379 and lenvatinib yielded the strongest tumor control, characterized by increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration and reduced TAM abundance. Consistent with the proposed mechanism, IHC showed that the combination therapies across all models significantly reduced p-HER3, p-HER2, and p-AKT ( ; – ; , ). In patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), the lenvatinib–zenocutuzumab combination achieved the strongest growth inhibition compared with monotherapies ( ; ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that COLEC12high macrophage-derived NRG1 drives HER2/HER3 signaling to promote lenvatinib resistance and cancer stemness in HCC, suggesting a combinatorial strategy to overcome this microenvironment-mediated resistance mechanism.

## NRG1 serves as a biomarker and therapeutic target for lenvatinib resistance in HCC
To assess the role of NRG1 in lenvatinib resistance, we analyzed residual tumors from 50 SYSUCC HCC patients treated preoperatively with lenvatinib. IHC stratified patients into high-NRG1 and low-NRG1 groups ( ). MRI and waterfall plots showed significantly less tumor regression in NRG1-high patients under RECIST v1.1 ( ). The objective response rate was higher in NRG1-low patients (55.2% vs. 14.3%; P=0.003), and ROC analysis indicated that NRG1 predicted the lenvatinib response (AUC=0.704; ). Elevated NRG1 by IHC or serum ELISA correlated with shorter PFS and OS ( , ). Thus, NRG1 in tumors and circulation serves as a biomarker of poor response and prognosis in lenvatinib-treated HCC. Mechanistically, COLEC12high macrophages secrete NRG1 to activate HER2/HER3 signaling and cancer stemness, and COLEC12 increases STAT3-dependent NRG1 transcription by binding STAT1, blocking its phosphorylation, and relieving STAT1-mediated suppression of STAT3 ( ).
 

## DISCUSSION
This study suggests a previously underappreciated, TME-driven mechanism of lenvatinib resistance in HCC, centered on a specific COLEC12high TAM subpopulation that orchestrates therapeutic resistance through paracrine NRG1-HER2/HER3 signaling. Our integrated approach combining clinical cohorts, murine resistance models, and functional validation demonstrates that COLEC12high TAMs are transcriptionally reprogrammed to secrete NRG1, activating cancer stemness pathways in tumor cells and conferring therapeutic resistance. Crucially, we identified NRG1 as a clinically actionable biomarker and validated the pharmacological blockade of the NRG1-HER2/HER3 axis as an effective strategy to overcome this microenvironment-mediated resistance.
Several key findings distinguish our work from prior studies on TKI resistance. First, in addition to the reported role of TAMs in sorafenib resistance (e.g., HGF/MET and CXCR2–ERK) [ , ], our discovery of the COLEC12high TAM subset specifically driving lenvatinib resistance reveals previously unrecognized functional heterogeneity within macrophage populations. The conserved 58-gene signature identified through cross-species integration provides a molecular fingerprint for resistance-associated TAMs. Second, our mechanistic analysis revealed a noncanonical signaling axis in which COLEC12, traditionally recognized as a pattern recognition receptor in innate immunity [ , , ], functionally repurposes its intracellular domain to sequester STAT1, relieving the repression of STAT3 and enabling the transcription of NRG1—which is distinct from cytokine-driven STAT3 activation in TAMs [ , ]. Third, we established that TAM-derived (rather than tumor cell-autonomous) NRG1 is the dominant driver of stemness reprogramming in this context, resolving conflicting reports on the sources of NRG1 in HCC.
Given compensatory ERBB signaling in HCC, prior work emphasized EGFR-mediated lenvatinib resistance (PAK2–ERK5; STAT3–ABCB1) [ , ], we identified a distinct HER2/HER3-dependent escape pathway. NRG1 elevation in resistant tumors is correlated with poor response and survival, which is consistent with the established role of ERBB signaling in HCC progression [ - ]. Our data demonstrate that pharmacologic HER2/HER3 blockade with zenocutuzumab abrogates AKT activation and resensitizes tumors to lenvatinib. This finding suggests that combinatorial ERBB targeting—potentially dual inhibition of EGFR and HER2/HER3—may be needed to overcome adaptive resistance, especially in NRG1-high contexts.
The clinical relevance of this axis is supported by several findings: COLEC12high TAM infiltration is correlated with poor survival and reduced lenvatinib response; NRG1 expression predicts therapeutic resistance and worse survival; and the bispecific HER2/HER3 antibody zenocutuzumab reverses resistance in preclinical models, including patient-derived models. Together, these data suggest that combining lenvatinib with HER2/HER3 inhibitors could restore efficacy in patients with high-NRG1 HCC. The proven clinical activity of zenocutuzumab in NRG1 fusion–driven cancers further supports its potential for repurposing in this setting.
Our findings align with emerging recognition that TME-mediated resistance operates independently of tumor cell-intrinsicadaptations [ , ]. While prior studies focused on TAMs in immunotherapy resistance [ ], our work expands their role to targeted kinase inhibitor failure. The spatial organization of COLEC12high TAMs within macrophage-enriched niches may create localized “resistant microenvironments” in which high NRG1 concentrations drive stemness reprogramming—a hypothesis that warrants future investigation using spatial transcriptomics.
Our small cohort limits power and subgroup generalizability. The Hepa1-6 syngeneic model complements nude/Huh7 cells but reflects murine, not human, immunity; thus orthotopic and humanized models are needed. Although CDX-3379 potentiated lenvatinib with immune modulation, single-model inferences warrant caution. The drivers of lenvatinib-induced COLEC12high expression remain unknown, and mediators other than NRG1 may contribute. Prospective validation of serum NRG1 levels and in vivo investigation of the COLEC12–NRG1 axis in orthotopic/humanized HCC are warranted.
Clinically, NRG1 (tissue or serum) could stratify lenvatinib responsiveness; trials combining lenvatinib with HER2/HER3 inhibitors (e.g., zenocutuzumab) should prioritize for NRG1-high HCC; and targeting COLEC12high TAMs (e.g., COLEC12-directed or polarization strategies) may extinguish resistance at its source. Future work should test TAM plasticity during therapy and prospectively validate NRG1 as a predictive biomarker.
In conclusion, the COLEC12high TAM/NRG1 axis may act as a central regulator of lenvatinib resistance in HCC. By linking macrophage-specific COLEC12 to STAT1 sequestration, STAT3-dependent NRG1 transcription, and tumor stemness, we provide mechanistic hypotheses and potential therapeutic strategies to overcome microenvironment-mediated resistance.
