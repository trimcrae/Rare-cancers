Herein, we investigated the influences of transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) derived from P. gingivalis on osteogenesis and developed a nanotherapeutic agent to counteract their negative effects. High-throughput tsRNA sequencing and functional analyses identified tRF-Pro-GGG-1 as a key P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNA for impairing osteogenesis. Mechanistically, tRF-Pro-GGG-1 directly bound purine-rich element binding protein B (PurB), and inhibited the PurB-mediated transcription of Collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), as shown by ChIP-seq and luciferase assays. To counteract the compromised osteogenesis induced by P. gingivalis-derived OMVs, we engineered a tetrahedral framework nucleic acid (tFNA) functionalized with si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 (si-tRF-tFNA). This nanotherapeutic agent effectively abrogated OMV-induced osteogenic inhibition by activating PurB and promoted bone regeneration in vitro and in vivo. These findings reveal a novel bacterial RNA-driven pathogenic pathway and provide insights for the development of precise therapeutics for periodontal bone regeneration.

## Introduction
Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by bacterial dysbiosis that affects approximately 61.6% of the global adult population and the primary cause of edentulism [ , ]. A hallmark of this condition is progressive alveolar bone loss, which arises from dysregulated interactions between host immune responses and oral microbial communities. Emerging evidence underscores a significant bidirectional association between periodontitis and systemic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, and coronary syndrome [ , ]. These comorbidities collectively impose a significant burden on global healthcare systems, contributing to considerable social and economic challenges. Conventional therapeutic interventions, including scaling and root planning, primarily aim to mitigate bacterial biofilm accumulation. While such strategies are effective in suppressing inflammatory progression, they exhibit limited efficacy in promoting the functional regeneration of lost periodontal bone tissues, highlighting a critical unmet clinical need.
Polymicrobial communities are recognized as aetiologic agents in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. These communities exhibit the capacity to dysregulate host immune defenses and perpetuate inflammatory cascades through a self-reinforcing feedback mechanism [ ]. Within these microbial communities, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) functions as a keystone pathogen, orchestrating both the local progression of periodontitis and associated systemic conditions, which is achieved through its disruption of microbial homeostasis, subversion of host immune responses, and acceleration of tissue degradation via a plethora of virulence factors [ ]. Emerging evidence underscores the significant role of P. gingivalis outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) in increasing its pathogenicity. These nanosized vesicles encapsulate bacterial nucleic acids (e.g., RNA and DNA) and deliver membrane-associated components such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and gingipains [ – ]. Notably, OMVs exhibit greater pathogenic potency than their parent bacteria do, as they deliver concentrated virulence factors to host tissues. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that periodontal pathogen-derived OMVs exacerbate osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption [ ], amplify inflammatory responses, and impair the mineralization capacity of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) [ , ]. While recent research attributes OMV pathogenicity primarily to their delivery of membrane components and enzymes (e.g., LPS and gingipains) [ , ], the functional role of OMV cargoes in periodontal bone regeneration remains insufficiently characterized, necessitating further mechanistic investigation.
In addition to membrane-associated components, OMVs derived from periodontal pathogens, such as Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, P. gingivalis, and Treponema denticola, have been reported to transfer small RNAs (sRNAs) into eukaryotic cells [ , ]. These bacterial sRNAs, which typically span 50–400 nucleotides in length, are transcribed from intergenic regions of microbial genomes and are increasingly recognized as critical mediators of host-oral microbial interactions [ ]. A prominent subclass of noncoding RNAs, transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), represents the most abundant group of small regulatory RNAs [ ]. tsRNAs are generated through the enzymatic cleavage of precursors or mature tRNAs, with classification into tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs) and tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) based on the cleavage sites and sequence length [ ]. These sRNAs have been increasingly recognized as key regulators of evolutionarily conserved biological pathways, spanning gene transcription and protein synthesis. Emerging studies suggest that the OMV-mediated transfer of tsRNAs may modulate host cell gene expression through interactions with RNA-binding proteins [ , ]. Nevertheless, the functional roles of P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNAs in the modulation of periodontal osteogenic regeneration remain to be elucidated.
Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs), which have emerged as a novel category of DNA-based nanomaterials, are composed of four single-stranded DNA strands arranged in a tetrahedral structural configuration [ ]. Owing to their unique structure, tFNAs have the capacity for increased cellular uptake efficiency, robust intracellular delivery, and superior tissue penetrability and have been widely used to deliver sRNAs to treat various diseases [ – ]. In the present study, we explored the underlying mechanism by which P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNAs regulate periodontal osteogenic regeneration and then chemically loaded specific inhibitors of key tsRNAs on tFNAs to counteract the P. gingivalis OMV-mediated negative effects on periodontal bone regeneration. Our study reveals the intricate crosstalk between microbial virulence factors and host cellular responses while establishing a platform for the development of precision targeted therapeutic interventions.

## Materials and methods

## Bacterial cultures
P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 obtained from the American Type Culture Collection was initially grown on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar plates (Hopebiol, China) supplemented with 5% sterile defibrinated sheep blood (Hongquan Biotechnology, China), 5 µg/mL hemin (Hopebiol), and 1 µg/mL vitamin K1 (Hopebiol). The cultures were maintained in anaerobic chambers (80% N2, 10% H2, 10% CO2) at 37 °C for 8–10 days until black-colored colonies formed. Individual colonies were then resuspended in BHI broth (Hopebiol) enriched with 5 µg/mL hemin and 1 µg/mL vitamin K1. The P. gingivalis suspension in BHI broth was cultured anaerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h. OMVs were subsequently isolated when P. gingivalis reached the logarithmic growth phase.

## Isolation and identification of P. gingivalis OMVs
The procedures for isolating and purifying P. gingivalis OMVs were performed according to previously described methods with modifications [ , ]. First, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore syringe filter (Millipore, USA) to remove the residual bacteria. Then, the sterile-filtered supernatant was concentrated using a 100-kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore) at 4,000 rpm for 20 min. Subsequently, the obtained supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was subsequently discarded, whereas the crude P. gingivalis OMVs were resuspended in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) (Gibco, USA). The crude P. gingivalis OMVs were subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. Finally, the purified P. gingivalis OMVs were resuspended, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until further use. Freshly isolated P. gingivalis OMV samples were added to a 400-mesh copper grid and incubated for 2–3 min, followed by negative staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid at room temperature for 2 min to identify the morphology and size of the isolated P. gingivalis OMVs. The structural morphology of the P. gingivalis OMVs was then observed using transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi HT7800, Japan). The samples diluted in PBS at a ratio of 1:10,000 were analyzed using a Flow NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM, China) to determine the concentration and particle size distribution of the P. gingivalis OMVs. To assess the purity of the P. gingivalis OMVs, the samples was treated with 10% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China). After incubation on ice for 30 min, the particle concentration was analyzed by Flow NanoAnalyzer.

## Establishment of a murine periodontitis model and P. gingivalis OMV injections
For these experiments, six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Animal Center of Air Force Medical University. All the animal experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Air Force Medical University (AFMU-20240016). A ligation-induced murine periodontitis model was established as previously described [ ]. Briefly, a sterile 4 − 0 silk suture was used to surgically ligate the interdental space between the maxillary first and second molars to facilitate microbial colonization and periodontal inflammation. Following the completion of the ligation, the tongue was gently exteriorized and maintained within a warmer environment until the mice regained full consciousness. Postoperative care included the provision of a normal diet and water ad libitum throughout the recovery period. After the establishment of the murine periodontitis model, each mouse was injected separately with either 10 µL of PBS or 10 µL of P. gingivalis OMVs at a dose of 5 µg/µL into the gingival sulcus between the maxillary first and second molars every other day [ ]. The silk ligature was ensured to be in place every day. At 10 days postsurgery, all the mice were euthanized via an anesthetic overdose, and the maxilla tissues were harvested and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for further investigation (Coolaber, China).

## Microcomputed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis
The maxillary bones were subjected to imaging using a high-resolution micro-CT scanner (AX2000 CT, Always Imaging, China). Three-dimensional (3D) volumetric reconstructions were generated from the acquired tomographic data and analyzed quantitatively with VG Studio MAX 3.5.1 software (Volume Graphics, Germany). Morphometric parameters, including the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), were systematically calculated to evaluate the trabecular microarchitecture of alveolar bone between the maxillary first and second molars. Additionally, periodontal bone loss was objectively quantified by measuring the distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) of the maxillary first molars in the sagittal section view of the micro-CT scanning images.

## Immunofluorescence analysis
The fixed maxilla samples were decalcified in a 10% EDTA solution (Proandy, China) for 2 weeks, and the decalcification solution was changed every 2–3 days. The decalcified tissues were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 2-µm-thick slices using a microtome (Leica, Germany). For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer at 95 °C for 15 min. After being blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following osteogenic markers: Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2; 1:150, AF5186, Affinity Biosciences, China), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; 1:200, DF6225, Affinity Biosciences), or Collagen type 1 alpha 1 (COL1A1; 1:200, AF7001, Affinity Biosciences). The sections were subsequently incubated with corresponding Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Abcam, UK) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; SL7100, Coolaber), and the sections were mounted with anti-fade medium. Images of immunofluorescence staining were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Olympus FV3000, Japan). A quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

## Isolation, culture and identification of PDLSCs
PDLSCs were harvested as previously described [ ]. The cells were isolated from clinically healthy premolars or third molars extracted for orthodontic treatment from donors aged 12–30 years under protocols approved by the Ethics Committees of the School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University. The periodontal ligaments were carefully scraped from the middle third of the tooth roots and mechanically minced into 1 mm³ fragments. Enzymatic digestion was performed using 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Sigma, USA) dissolved in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco) under constant agitation at 37 °C for 45 min. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by the addition of α-MEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, USA). After centrifugation (800 rpm, 5 min), the tissue pellets were resuspended in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS; Gibco) and maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). The culture medium was changed every 3 days. Subculture was conducted at 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-0.25% trypsin-ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Gibco), with the cells passaged at a 1:3 ratio. PDLSCs at passages 3–5 were utilized for subsequent experiments following the validation of the stem cell identity via flow cytometry (CD31⁻/CD34⁻/CD45⁻ and CD90⁺/CD105⁺/CD146⁺ immunophenotypes) and functional differentiation assays. The osteogenic differentiation capacity was confirmed by Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining after 21 days of induction in osteogenic medium, whereas the adipogenic potential was assessed via Oil Red O staining following an equivalent duration of adipogenic culture.

## Cellular uptake of P. gingivalis OMVs by PDLSCs
P. gingivalis OMVs were labelled with PKH26 fluorescent dye (CS4108, G-clone, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 µL of P. gingivalis OMVs (1 µg/mL) was incubated with 500 µL of diluent C containing 2 µL of PKH26 dye at room temperature for 3 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 1 mL of FBS, followed by ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 1 h, 4 °C) to remove the unbound dye. PDLSCs cultured on glass-bottom confocal dishes were incubated with PKH26-labelled OMVs for 1, 4, and 8 h to assess P. gingivalis OMV internalization. Cytoskeletal labelling was achieved by an incubation with fluorescein phalloidin (1:1000 dilution; HY-K0902, MCE, USA) for 30 min, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 3 min. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using CLSM.

## ALP staining and quantification
PDLSCs were induced to undergo osteogenic differentiation by replacing complete α-MEM with osteogenic differentiation medium (Oricell, China) supplemented with different concentrations of P. gingivalis OMVs (1, 3, and 5 µg/mL). After 7 days of osteogenic induction, PDLSCs were fixed with 4% PFA. ALP staining was conducted using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Colour Development Kit (Beyotime) for 30 min according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Images were obtained using a light microscope (Nikon, Japan). For the quantitative analysis of ALP enzymatic activity, culture supernatants from PDLSCs were collected, and ALP activity was subsequently determined using an ALP assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

## ARS staining and quantification
Following 14 days of osteogenic induction, PDLSCs were fixed with 4% PFA. The cells were subsequently stained with ARS staining solution (Oricell) for 5 min and then rinsed with deionized distilled water (ddH2O) to remove the unbound dye. Stained calcium deposits were visualized using a light microscope. For the quantitative analysis, calcium deposits were solubilized with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC; Merck, Germany), and the optical density (OD) value of the CPC-treated solution was subsequently measured at 562 nm to quantify the degree of ARS-stained calcium deposition.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
Following 7 days of osteogenic induction, total RNA was extracted from PDLSCs using AG RNAex Pro RNA (AGbio, China). Then, reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the Hifair® III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was utilized as the housekeeping gene for data normalization, with the subsequent quantification of osteogenesis-related gene markers: Runx2, ALP, COL1A1, and osteocalcin (OCN). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay was conducted using qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer information for each gene is listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## Western blot (WB) analysis
Following 7 days of osteogenic induction, PDLSCs were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA; Beyotime) buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime) to inhibit protease activity. The total protein concentration was measured via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer to a final concentration of 1×, denatured at 100 °C for 7 min, and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; Epizyme, China). The separated proteins were electrophoretically transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-GAPDH (1:20000, CL594-60004, Proteintech, China), anti-Runx2 (1:1000), anti-ALP (1:1000), and anti-COL1A1 (1:1000). After three 10-min washes with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:50,000; Yeasen) for 70 min at room temperature. Following additional TBST washes, the protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence (ECL) super kit (ABclonal, China) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad). Relative band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

## High-throughput sequencing of tsRNAs
The tsRNA sequencing workflow was initiated with total RNA extraction from P. gingivalis OMVs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Total RNA samples then underwent a pretreatment to eliminate interference from RNA modifications during small RNA library construction, including (1) deacylation of 3′-aminoacyl (charged) to generate 3′-OH for 3′ adapter ligation; (2) removal of 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (cP) groups to expose 3′-OH; (3) phosphorylation of 5′-hydroxyl (5′-OH) groups to 5′-phosphate (5′-P) for 5′-adapter ligation; and (4) demethylation of m1A and m3C modifications to increase the reverse transcription efficiency. Pretreated RNA was ligated with 3′ and 5′ adapters using a NEBNext® Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (New England BioLabs, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis and PCR amplification. After the library quality was assessed using tools such as an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), the qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform with NextSeq 500/550 V2 kits (Illumina, USA) at Aksomics (China), generating single-end 50 bp reads optimized for small RNA analysis. The raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control with FastQC, and trimmed reads (passing the Illumina quality filter and trimming 5′ and 3′ adaptor bases with cut adapt) were aligned to mature tRNA and precursor tRNA sequences in the tRNA database using Novo Align software (Novocraft Technologies, Malaysia).

## Quantification of tsRNA expression by poly(A)-tailing qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from P. gingivalis OMVs and P. gingivalis OMVs-treated PDLSCs using TRIzol reagent to validate the expression of tsRNAs by qRT-PCR. A polyadenylation reaction was performed using the miRNA RT SuperMix (Tailing A) kit (TSK3001, Tsingke, China) to facilitate the reverse transcription of tsRNAs. Briefly, tsRNAs were enzymatically polyadenylated at their 3’ terminus using poly(A) polymerase to append poly(A) tails, followed by cDNA synthesis with reverse transcriptase. qPCR amplification was conducted using the miRNA Universal SYBR qPCR Mix (TSE2001, Tsingke) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. U6 was employed as the reference control. The information for the top 7 candidate tsRNAs, including their IDs, sequences, cleavage subtypes, and lengths, is systematically presented in Supplementary Table S2. The primer information for U6 and each tsRNA is listed in Supplementary Table S3.

## Cell transfections
P. gingivalis OMVs-treated PDLSCs were transfected with a tiRNA-Asn-GTT-2 inhibitor (si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-2), tiRNA-Pro-CGG-1 inhibitor (si-tiRNA-Pro-CGG-1), tRF-Gly-GCC-1-M3 inhibitor (si-tRF-Gly-GCC-1-M3), tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 inhibitor (si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1), or tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibitor (si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1), alongside a negative control inhibitor (Tsingke), using TSnanofect V2 (Tsingke) to explore the influences of candidate tsRNAs on the expression of osteogenesis-related genes in PDLSCs. The roles of tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 and tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in modulating osteogenic differentiation were further validated by transfecting PDLSCs with the tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 mimic (OE-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1), tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 inhibitor (si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1), tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimic (OE-tRF-Pro-GGG-1), tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibitor (si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1), or their negative controls (Tsingke), employing the same transfection protocol. The osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs was assessed via ALP staining and quantification, ARS staining and quantification, and WB analysis. The sequences of the tsRNA inhibitors and mimics are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

## Cytoplasmic and nuclear fraction isolation
Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated from P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs using a Minute™ Cytoplasmic and Nuclear Extraction Kit (SC-003, Invent, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to analyze the subcellular expression of tRF-Pro-GGG-1. Total RNA was extracted from both fractions, and the relative abundance of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments was quantified via qRT-PCR. Briefly, the expression levels of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 within each fraction were normalized to whole-cell lysate values, with whole-cell expression set to 100%. U6 was used as the nuclear marker, while β-actin was used as the cytoplasmic marker to validate the integrity of the subcellular fractionation.

## RNA-fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH)
An RNA-FISH assay was performed using an RNA FISH kit (GenePharma, China) to verify the subcellular localization of tRF-Pro-GGG-1. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, 5 × 104 PDLSCs were seeded on glass-bottom confocal dishes. After 24 h of treatment with P. gingivalis OMVs, the cells were fixed and permeabilized, and hybridization was then conducted using a Cy3-labelled tRF-Pro-GGG-1-specific probe (GenePharma), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. The fluorescence signals were visualized and captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

## RNA pulldown-mass spectra (MS) and protein-binding screen
RNA pulldown-MS was conducted using an RNA pulldown kit (BW2507; BaiweiBio, China) to investigate RNA-protein interactions. Briefly, biotin-labelled tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and NC probes were transcribed in vitro according to the manufacturer’s protocol. These probes were then incubated with PDLSC lysates to facilitate the formation of RNA-protein complexes. The complexes were then enriched with streptavidin magnetic beads and washed repeatedly to remove unbound proteins. Following separation by SDS-PAGE and visualization through silver staining, the proteins bound to tRF-Pro-GGG-1 were eluted and subjected to identification using MS. Candidate proteins that interact with tRF-Pro-GGG-1 by stringently filtering the MS data from the RNA pulldown (RPD) group. First, the proteins codetected in the NC group were excluded to eliminate nonspecific interactions. Next, the top 10 protein entries ranked by both − 10LgP (confidence score) and Coverage (the protein coverage ratio) were selected. Proteins designated “TOP is false” (indicating ambiguous identification) and “Unique = 1” (detected via only a single unique peptide) were also excluded to further increase the specificity. The resulting proteins represented the most promising candidate proteins binding to tRF-Pro-GGG-1.

## RNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR (RIP-qPCR)
RIP was performed using a RIP Kit (BW2506, BaiweiBio) to investigate RNA-protein interactions. Briefly, 2 × 107 PDLSCs were lysed in RIP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and RNase inhibitors. After DNA depletion and equilibration of the Protein A/G magnetic beads, the lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-PurB antibody (18128-1-AP, Proteintech) or anti-IgG isotype control antibody (negative control). The immunocomplexes were precipitated, and RNA was isolated using phenol-chloroform-isooctanol, reverse transcribed into cDNA, and quantified via qRT-PCR. The fold enrichment of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in immunoprecipitated samples was normalized to the input RNA levels.

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
The direct interaction in vitro between the purified recombinant human purine-rich element binding protein B (PurB) protein (Ag12705, Proteintech) and tRF-Pro-GGG-1 was analyzed using an EMSA. Briefly, a biotin-labeled RNA probe was synthesized based on the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 sequence. Protein-RNA binding reactions were set up in a 10 µL volume containing 2 µL of 5× EMSA/Gel-Shift Binding Buffer (GS005, Beyotime), 1 µL of biotin-labeled probe, and 2 µg of purified protein. To confirm binding specificity, competition assays were performed by including a 10, 50, or 100-fold molar excess of unlabeled identical RNA probe (competitor) in the reaction mixture prior to the addition of the labeled probe. All reaction mixtures were incubated at 25 °C for 50 min after a 10-minute pre-incubation period. Subsequently, 1.2 µL of 10× EMSA/Gel-Shift Loading Buffer (GS006, Beyotime) was added. The complexes were resolved on a pre-run 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel at 80 V. The resolved complexes were then transferred onto a positively charged nylon membrane (RPN303B, Solarbio) at 200 mA for 60 min. After UV cross-linking, the membrane was blocked and incubated with HRP-conjugated Streptavidin (1:3000 dilution, SA00001-0, Proteintech). Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and captured on X-ray film.

## Exploration of the biological functions of purb
P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting PurB (si-PurB) (Tsingke) using TSnanofect V2 to investigate the functions of PurB in the context of the P. gingivalis OMV-mediated effects. The sequence of si-PurB is listed in Table S3. Following 7 days of osteogenic induction, the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs was assessed by qRT-PCR and ALP staining. In a complementary rescue experiment to further investigate the role of PurB in the tRF-Pro-GGG-1-mediated decrease in the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs, P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs were cotransfected with tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimic and si-PurB. The osteogenic differentiation potential was subsequently evaluated via qRT-PCR, ALP staining, and ARS staining.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq) and DNA-binding screening
A ChIP assay was performed using a ChIP Kit (BW2501, BaiweiBio) to detect the potential DNA sequences that bind to PurB. Briefly, 2 × 107 PDLSCs were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min to stabilize protein-DNA interactions. The chromatin was then fragmented into 200–600 bp small DNA fragments via sonication and immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with either an anti-PurB antibody or an anti-IgG isotype control. The immunoprecipitated complexes were washed stringently to eliminate nonspecific binding, and protein-DNA crosslinks were reversed to elute the enriched DNA fragments. The purified DNA was processed for library preparation, including end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification, followed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) on the Illumina Genome Analyzer platform. The raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality filtering to remove low-confidence bases, followed by alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38). Peaks representing PurB-binding regions were identified using MACS2 (q value < 0.05) and compared with IgG controls. Genomic annotations of peak loci, including distances to transcriptional start sites (TSSs), were determined using ChIPseeker. The nearest genes to the peaks were designated promising PurB targets, with nonfunctional loci (e.g., uncharacterized open reading frames, pseudogenes, miRNA-like fragments, or long noncoding RNAs) excluded. Eight candidate genes were prioritized for validation. We assessed the influence of PurB on the expression of the eight candidate genes by knocking down PurB (si-PurB) in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs. The expression of the eight candidate genes was analyzed using qRT-PCR. PDLSCs treated with P. gingivalis OMVs were transfected with si-PurB or the scrambled control (si-NC) to assess PurB-mediated transcriptional regulation. The expression of the candidate genes was determined via qRT-PCR. In addition, the promoter regions of the candidate genes were identified using the NCBI database, with a focus on sequences 2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site. The binding sites between PurB and the promoters of the candidate genes were subsequently predicted using JASPAR to calculate binding affinity scores.
Genes exhibiting > 2-fold changes in expression (p < 0.05) in the si-PurB group versus the si-NC group were classified as high-confidence PurB-regulated targets. These genes also required the concurrent fulfilment of > 10 predicted binding scores of PurB-binding sites and positive strands within their promoter (the stand was “+”). The primer sequences of the eight candidate genes are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The wild-type (WT) sequence of the COL1A1 promoter (containing the predicted PurB-binding site) (named the COL1A1 promoter) and the corresponding mutant sequence (MUT) (with the PurB-binding site mutated, named the COL1A1 promoter ΔPurB) were separately cloned and inserted into the dual-luciferase reporter vector pGL3 (Tsingke) to construct the recombinant plasmids COL1A1 and COL1A1 ΔPurB. PDLSCs were cotransfected with either the PurB overexpression plasmid or the empty vector control (pcDNA3.1) using Hieff Trans™ Liposomal Transfection Reagent (40802ES01, Yeasen) to assess the PurB-mediated transcriptional regulation of COL1A1. OE-tsRNA or si-tsRNA was cotransfected into PDLSCs to investigate the influence of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 on the transcriptional activation of the COL1A1 gene by PurB in PDLSCs. Relative luciferase activities were quantified using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (11402ES60, Yeasen).

## Synthesis and characterization of si-tRF-tFNA
A one-pot assembly method was employed to construct the tFNA-based scaffold. Specifically, three single-stranded DNAs (S1, S2, and S3) with designed sticky ends and one single-stranded DNA (S4) without sticky ends were mixed at an equimolar ratio (1:1:1:1) in TM buffer. The mixture was subjected to an annealing process consisting of heating at 95 °C for 10 min followed by cooling at 4 °C for 20 min to facilitate the formation of the tFNA-based scaffold. Subsequently, si-tRF was added to the above mixture at a molar ratio of 1:3 (S1:si-tRF). Their detailed sequences presented in Supplementary Table S5. The resulting solution underwent a stepwise annealing process at 35 °C, 33 °C, 31 °C, 29 °C, 27 °C, and 25 °C, each for 10 min, to form si-tRF-tFNA. The successful construction of si-tRF-tFNA was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and capillary electrophoresis. TEM, atomic force microscopy (AFM), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) were employed to characterize the morphology, particle size, and zeta potential of the nanostructures.

## Enzymatic and serum stability of si-tRF-tFNA
For the serum stability assay, various concentrations (0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%; v/v) of FBS were added to solutions containing either free si-tRF or si-tRF-tFNA. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 or 24 h. Stability was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis. For the enzymatic stability assay, different concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 U/mL) of RNase A (Sangon, Shanghai, China) were added to the si-tRF and si-tRF-tFNA solutions and incubated at 37 °C for 2 or 24 h. The integrity of the samples was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis.

## In vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging
To directly assess the local biodistribution and clearance of si-tRF-tFNA, in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed. Cy5-labeled si-tRF-tFNA was locally injected into the gingival sulcus between the maxillary first and second molars in a murine periodontitis model. For in vivo imaging, mice were anaesthetised with 2% isoflurane and placed in the supine position. Whole-body fluorescence images were acquired at 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection using an in vivo imaging system (AniView100DXA, Biolight Biotechnology, China). For ex vivo imaging, the maxillae along with major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested and arranged on a black background at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection.

## Statistical analysis
The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Intergroup differences were analyzed using Student’s t tests for comparisons between two groups. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05: * < 0.05; ** < 0.01; *** < 0.001; and ns, not significant.

## Results

## P. gingivalis OMVs accelerate bone destruction in vivo and inhibit osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs in vitro
We first isolated P. gingivalis OMVs through ultracentrifugation, and characterized the morphology and size of the P. gingivalis OMVs by TEM and nanoflow cytometry, respectively. The representative TEM images showed that P. gingivalis OMVs exhibited a bilayer-enclosed spherical morphology with nanoscale dimensions (Fig.  A). Nanoflow cytometry indicated a heterogeneous size distribution spanning 40–150 nm, with a mean diameter of 62.2 nm (Fig.  B). To validate the membrane integrity and purity of the isolated OMVs, we treated OMVs with Triton X-100. The nanoparticle concentration was dramatically reduced upon Triton X-100 treatment (Fig. S1), verifying that most of the isolated nanoparticles are indeed lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles, rather than detergent-resistant contaminants like protein aggregates or non-vesicular structures. To analyze the influence of P. gingivalis OMVs on the development of periodontitis, a ligature-induced periodontitis murine model was established. P. gingivalis OMVs were subsequently injected into the interdental space between the maxillary first and second molars every other day (Fig. S2A). As shown by H&E staining (Fig. S2B), Masson’s trichrome staining Fig. S2C) and micro-CT analysis (Fig. S2D and E), P. gingivalis OMVs treatment significantly accelerated periodontitis-induced bone resorption. Representative images of immunofluorescence staining showed that the expression of osteogenesis-related markers, including Runx2 (Fig. S2F), ALP (Fig. S2G), and COL1A1 (Fig. S2H), was decreased in the periodontal tissues following the P. gingivalis OMV injection. Consistently, the quantification of the immunofluorescence staining revealed significantly lower expression of Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1 in the OMVs group than in the control group (Fig. S2I). Taken together, these results indicated that the P. gingivalis OMV injection accelerated alveolar bone loss and significantly suppressed osteogenic activity during the development of periodontitis.
To interrogate whether P. gingivalis OMVs could compromise the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs, PDLSCs were isolated from human periodontal ligament tissues using previously published protocols [ ]. The isolated PDLSCs exhibited a fibroblast-like morphology (Fig. S3A) and robust proliferative viability (Fig. S3B). Flow cytometry revealed the negligible expression of hematopoietic lineage markers, including CD31, CD34, and CD45, while showing marked positivity for mesenchymal surface markers such as CD90, CD105, and CD146 (Fig. S3C). Furthermore, the multilineage differentiation potential of PDLSCs was confirmed by ARS Staining (Fig. S3D) and Oil Red O staining (Fig. S3E). Collectively, these findings confirm that the isolated PDLSCs possess defining characteristics that are consistent with those of mesenchymal stem cells. PDLSCs were treated with 1, 5, or 10 µg/mL P. gingivalis OMVs, and cell proliferation was quantified using a CCK-8 assay to investigate the effects of P. gingivalis OMVs on the proliferation of PDLSCs. P. gingivalis OMVs (1 and 5 µg/mL) significantly promoted the proliferation of PDLSCs, whereas 10 µg/mL P. gingivalis OMVs had an inhibitory effect (Fig. S4). Subsequent osteogenic differentiation assays were conducted using subtoxic P. gingivalis OMV concentrations (1, 3, and 5 µg/mL) to mitigate potential cytotoxic outcomes. The internalization of PKH26-labelled P. gingivalis OMVs by PDLSCs was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. C). After 7 days of osteogenic induction, different concentrations of P. gingivalis OMVs (1, 3, and 5 µg/mL) significantly inhibited ALP activity in PDLSCs (Fig. D). The calcium deposition capacity of PDLSCs was also significantly decreased following the P. gingivalis OMV treatment, as evidenced by ARS staining and quantification (Fig. E). Furthermore, different concentrations of P. gingivalis OMVs (1, 3, and 5 µg/mL) also significantly decreased the relative expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes (Runx2, ALP, COL1A1, and OCN) (Fig. F) and the relative expression levels of osteogenesis-related proteins (Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1) in PDLSCs (Fig. G). Taken together, these findings indicate that P. gingivalis OMVs inhibit the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs. The minimally effective OMV concentration (1 µg/mL) was selected for subsequent investigations to elucidate the underlying mechanism of the inhibitory effects of P. gingivalis OMVs on the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs.

 

## P. gingivalis OMVs inhibit osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs by transferring tRF-Pro-GGG-1
Emerging evidence indicates that tsRNAs, produced through the enzymatic cleavage of tRNAs, participate in host-oral microbial interactions [ ]. We therefore next examined whether P. gingivalis OMVs could deliver tsRNAs to inhibit the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs. High-throughput sequencing was conducted to obtain the expression profiles of the tsRNAs within P. gingivalis OMVs (Fig. A). This sequencing identified 191 novel tsRNAs within P. gingivalis OMVs, none of which had been previously reported in established tsRNA databases. The 191 novel tsRNAs identified can be categorized into four distinct types, as shown in the pie charts: 2.62% tRF-2, 27.75% tRF-3 (subdivided into the tRF-3a and tRF-3b subtypes), 59.69% tRF-5 (subdivided into the tRF-5a, tRF-5b, and tRF-5c subtypes), and 9.95% tiRNA-5 (Fig. B). The expression of the top 7 candidate tsRNAs in P. gingivalis OMVs, which exhibit specific cleavage types and a minimum length of 15 nt, was further confirmed via qRT-PCR (Fig. C). To investigate whether P. gingivalis OMVs transferred these tsRNAs to PDLSCs, the expression of these candidate tsRNAs in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs was quantified via qRT-PCR (Fig. D). Following an incubation with 1 µg/mL P. gingivalis OMV for 7 days, the relative expression of the top 6 candidate tsRNAs increased significantly, except for tRF-fMet-CAT-2. Five tsRNAs, namely, tiRNA-Asn-GTT-2, tiRNA-Pro-CGG-1, tRF-Gly-GCC-1-M3, tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1, and tRF-Pro-GGG-1, exhibited statistically significant differential expression (p < 0.05) and a fold change in expression greater than 1.5 in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs (Fig. E). These findings indicate that P. gingivalis OMVs transfer tsRNA to PDLSCs, and the influences of five candidate tsRNAs on osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs were further investigated.
PDLSCs were transfected with tsRNA-specific inhibitors, and the effects on the expression of osteogenesis-related genes were preliminarily evaluated by qRT-PCR. Among the five si-tsRNA tested, only si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 and si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 significantly increased the expression of osteogenic differentiation-related genes (Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1) (Fig.  F). To confirm the effects of tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 and tRF-Pro-GGG-1 on the osteogenic differentiation potential, cells were transfected with either OE-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1/tRF-Pro-GGG-1 (for overexpression) or si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1/tRF-Pro-GGG-1 (for silencing). Paradoxically, both si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 and OE-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 increased calcium deposition (Fig.  H) but decreased the expression of Runx2 in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs (Fig.  I). While si-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 increased ALP activity (Fig.  G), OE-tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 suppressed ALP expression (Fig.  I), revealing the contradictory effects of tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 on the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. The knockdown and overexpression efficiency of the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibitors and mimics was confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. S5). OE-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 significantly decreased the osteogenic differentiation potential, as evidenced by decreased ALP activity (Fig.  J), diminished calcium deposition (Fig.  K), and downregulated expression levels of osteogenesis-related proteins (Fig.  L). Conversely, si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 restored ALP activity (Fig.  J), calcium deposition (Fig.  K), and the expression levels of osteogenesis-related proteins (Fig.  L) in PDLSCs. These findings identify tRF-Pro-GGG-1 as a key tsRNA that negatively regulates the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs.

 

## P. gingivalis OMV-derived tRF-Pro-GGG-1 regulates the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs by binding to PurB

tRF-Pro-GGG-1, generated via the 5′ terminal cleavage of tRNA-Pro harboring a GGG anticodon, has been shown to exhibit diverse biological activities by binding to proteins in a manner contingent upon its subcellular localization (Fig. A). We elucidated how tRF-Pro-GGG-1 influences the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs by first determining the localization of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs using qRT-PCR and RNA-FISH. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed successful subcellular fractionation using the nuclear marker U6 and the cytoplasmic marker β-actin, and significant enrichment of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in both the nuclear and the cytoplasmic fractions was detected in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs (Fig. B). The representative RNA-FISH images also revealed the nuclear and cytoplasmic expression of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs (Fig. C). We performed RNA pulldown-MS assays to gain additional insights into whether tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibited the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs by binding to proteins. Silver staining revealed different protein profiles among the input group, RNA pulldown group (named RPD), and negative control group (named NC) (Fig. D), indicating that these proteins could be subjected to an MS analysis. After nonspecific interactions were eliminated, the top 10 protein entries from the MS data were filtered by removing ambiguously identified proteins (“TOP is false”) or those detected by a single unique peptide (“Unique = 1”). The resulting PurB and dermcidin (DCD) proteins were identified as potential candidates (Fig. E). Considering that DCD is an antimicrobial peptide that is expressed mainly in sweat glands [ ], while PurB is a sequence-specific ssDNA-binding protein implicated in transcriptional regulation [ , ], PurB but not DCD was selected for further investigation. The binding of PurB to tRF-Pro-GGG-1 was backward verified by RIP-qPCR, as evidenced by the increased enrichment of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 immunoprecipitated by an anti-PurB antibody (Fig. F). To provide evidence for direct interaction of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and PurB in vitro, we performed EMSA using recombinant human PurB protein and synthesized a biotin-labeled tRF-Pro-GGG-1 probe. A clear shift band appeared after incubation with GST-PurB, indicating direct binding between tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and PurB. The shift band was significantly reduced by adding an excess of unlabeled competitive probe, confirming sequence-specific binding (Fig. G). The EMSA results are consistent with RNA pulldown-MS and RIP-qPCR data, together confirming that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 directly and specifically binds to PurB.
Moreover, PDLSCs were transfected with si-PurB, and the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs was assessed by ALP staining and qRT-PCR to further investigate the effects of PurB on the osteogenic differentiation potential of P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs. The knockdown efficiency of si-PurB was confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. S5). Both ALP activity (Fig.  H) and the expression of osteogenesis-related genes (Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1) (Fig.  I) were increased following transfection with si-PurB, which indicated that the P. gingivalis OMV-mediated decrease in the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs depended on PurB binding. A rescue experiment was conducted by cotransfecting cells with OE-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and si-PurB to further investigate the role of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 binding to PurB in the compromised osteogenic differentiation potential of tRF-Pro-GGG-1. Although OE-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, si-PurB treatment resulted in significantly increased expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes (Fig.  J) and increased ALP activity (Fig.  K) and calcium deposition (Fig.  L), indicating that si-PurB could rescue the osteogenic differentiation potential that was compromised by tRF-Pro-GGG-1. Overall, we verified that P. gingivalis OMV-derived tRF-Pro-GGG-1 regulates the osteogenic differentiation potential of P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs by binding to PurB.

 

## tRF-Pro-GGG-1 impairs the transcriptional activation of COL1A1 by PurB
PurB, a DNA-binding protein, exerts cell type-specific effects on gene expression, functioning as a transcriptional activator [ ] or repressor [ ]. We hypothesized that suppressed the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs by modulating the PurB-mediated transcriptional regulation of downstream targets. ChIP-seq was performed to identify PurB-binding genomic loci (Fig. A). Following the exclusion of nonfunctional binding sites, eight candidate genes were subjected to further analysis, and their information is listed in Fig. B. The transcription factor-binding sites were subsequently predicted by JASPAR, which indicated that COL1A1, rearranged during transfection (RET), and semaphorin 6 A (SEMA6A) had the highest binding affinity scores, and their stand of binding sites was “+”, which meant that PurB bound to the positive strand of DNA (Fig. C). The influence of PurB on the expression of these eight candidate genes was further analyzed by qRT-PCR. Notably, si-PurB significantly upregulated the expression of embigin (EMB), COL1A1, fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2), and SEMA6A, resulting in greater than 2-fold increases in their expression levels (Fig. D). Given the established role of COL1A1 in osteogenesis [ ], we further hypothesized that PurB may influence osteogenic differentiation by regulating COL1A1 gene transcription. The influence of PurB overexpression on COL1A1 transcription in PDLSCs was quantified via a dual luciferase reporter gene assay (Fig. E). The PurB transfection specifically increased the transcriptional activity of the COL1A1 promoter, However, mutant COL1A1 lacking the PUR binding site (COL1A1 promoter ΔPurB) abrogated the PurB transfection-enhanced transcriptional activity, indicating that PurB activated COL1A1 transcription by directly binding to the promoter sequence of the COL1A1 gene (Fig. F). To investigate whether tRF-Pro-GGG-1 activated COL1A1 genes by PurB, PDLSCs were co-transfected with either tsRNA mimics/inhibitors and pcDNA3.1-PurB. The relative luciferase activities indicated that although tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimics alone do not affect the transcriptional activity of COL1A1, co-transfection of tsRNA mimics with PurB significantly reduces COL1A1 transcriptional activity. In contrast, the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibitor relieved this inhibitory effect and enabled the normal transcriptional activation of COL1A1 via PurB-mediated regulation (Fig. G). Collectively, these findings suggest that the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 binding converts the regulatory function of PurB from an activator to a repressor (Fig. H), providing mechanistic evidence for its role in modulating the osteogenic differentiation potential of P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs.

 

## A tFNA-based nanocomplex (si-tRF-tFNA) is engineered for the efficient and stable delivery of si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1
Building on previous evidence indicating that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 is a critical suppressor of osteogenic differentiation, we postulated that the targeted inhibition of this tRNA-derived fragment could counteract its regulatory effects and enhance the osteogenic capacity of P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs. Owing to their inherent biocompatibility, structural stability, cellular internalization efficiency, and editable functionality, tFNAs have been widely used as alternative vehicles for the delivery of functional nucleic acids to treat various types of diseases [ , , ]. Compared with traditional carriers such as liposomes, tFNAs offer more uniform and efficient delivery of siRNA into cells [ ]. Therefore, in this study, tFNA was employed as a nanocarrier to deliver si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 for enhanced cellular uptake and to restore the P. gingivalis OMV-mediated decrease in the osteogenic differential potential of PDLSCs. As illustrated in Fig. A, three single-stranded DNAs with sticky ends (S1, S2, and S3) and a fourth strand (S4) were assembled via an annealing process to form the tFNA-based scaffold. Subsequently, the si-tRF molecules were anchored to the three vertices of the tFNA-based scaffold through complementary sticky-end hybridization, yielding the si-tRF-tFNA nanocomplex. The successful assembly of si-tRF-tFNA was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis and capillary electrophoresis (Fig. B and C). Morphological characterization revealed a triangular-like nanostructure with an average size of approximately 20 nm and a negative surface charge (Fig. D-H). Both its serum stability and enzymatic stability against RNase A were assessed to further evaluate its stability under complex physiological conditions. As shown in Fig. I and J, free si-tRF underwent rapid degradation into smaller fragments after 2 h of incubation in either serum or RNase A solution. In contrast, when loaded onto the tFNA-based scaffold, the degradation of si-tRF was both concentration and time dependent. Although partial detachment and degradation were observed, a significant proportion of si-tRF remained stably associated with the scaffold even after 24 h of incubation with the highest concentrations of serum or RNase A. These results indicate that the incorporation of the tFNA scaffold substantially increases the stability of si-tRF under biological conditions.

 

## si-tRF-tFNA significantly promotes osteogenesis of PDLSCs by increasing the transcriptional activity of PurB towards COL1A1 genes
Initial investigations focused on assessing si-tRF-tFNA internalization in PDLSCs. Notably, the si-tRF-tFNA complexes demonstrated an intrinsic self-delivery capability, bypassing the requirement for transfection reagents typically associated with single-stranded nucleic acid delivery (Fig.  A). ALP activity was analyzed after 7 days of osteogenic induction to evaluate the effects of si-tRF-tFNA on osteogenic differentiation. While both tFNA and si-tRF-tFNA increased ALP activity compared with that of the OMV-treated controls, a significantly higher level of ALP activity was observed in the si-tRF-tFNA group (Fig.  B). Similarly, ARS staining and quantification revealed maximal calcium deposition in si-tRF-tFNA-treated PDLSCs (Fig.  C). Furthermore, si-tRF-tFNA also significantly increased the expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes (Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1) in the si-tRF-tFNA group (Fig.  D), collectively indicating its pro-osteogenic efficacy. We investigated whether the si-tRF-tFNA-enhanced osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs was attributed to the increased transcriptional activity of PurB towards the COL1A1 gene by deleting the PurB binding site in COL1A1 (COL1A1 promoter ΔPurB), after which the influences of tFNA and si-tRF-tFNA on the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs were determined (Fig.  E). The results of the ALP activity assay and qRT-PCR indicated that deleting the PurB binding site in COL1A1 abolished the si-tRF-tFNA-induced increases in ALP activity (Fig.  F), the calcium deposition (Fig.  G), and the expression of osteogenesis-related genes (Fig.  H) in P. gingivalis OMV-treated PDLSCs compared with that in tFNA-treated PDLSCs. These results indicated that si-tRF-tFNA promoted the osteogenesis of PDLSCs by increasing the transcriptional activity of PurB towards COL1A1 genes.

 

## si-tRF-tFNA significantly promotes periodontal bone regeneration
The therapeutic performance was explored by coinjecting si-tRF-tFNA with P. gingivalis OMVs every other day in a ligature-induced periodontitis model (Fig.  A). Local injection of si-tRF-tFNA exhibited no significant impact on hepatic and renal function (Fig. S6A) or complete blood count (Fig. S6B). Furthermore, H&E staining of major organs revealed no detectable toxicity (Fig. S6C), collectively attesting to its favorable in vivo biocompatibility. To visualize the in vivo biodistribution of si-tRF-tFNA, we performed in vivo fluorescence imaging following the periodontal injection of Cy5-labeled si-tRF-tFNA into ligation sites. Strong fluorescence signals were observed at the injection site at early time points (2 h, 6 h, and 12 h), which gradually diminished over time. Notably, at 24 h post-injection, only a weak residual fluorescence signal was detectable in the kidneys. At 48 h, the signal was nearly absent in all major organs, confirming that si-tRF-tFNA was effectively cleared without long-term accumulation (Fig.  B). To provide evidence of tissue-specific targeting and degradation, we also conducted ex vivo imaging of the harvested maxillae and major organs. Strong fluorescence signals were detected at the maxillae up to 48 h post-injection, indicating that si-tRF-tFNA was retained locally for an extended period to ensure sustained functional activity. Meanwhile, significant fluorescence was observed in the liver and kidneys at early time points (6–24 h), which progressively declined by 48 h, suggesting that si-tRF-tFNA is primarily metabolized and cleared through the liver and kidneys (Fig.  C).
At 10 days postsurgery, the restoration of periodontal tissue, residual periodontal bone and osteogenic activities were analyzed via H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, micro-CT analysis and immunofluorescence staining, respectively. Representative images of H&E staining showed that treatment with si-tRF-tFNA markedly reduced P. gingivalis OMV-induced alveolar bone resorption (Fig.  D). Representative images of Masson’s trichrome staining showed that compared with the OMV and tFNA groups, the periodontal ligament of the si-tRF-tFNA group displayed significantly greater density and improved organization of collagen and elastic fibers, with an architecture approximating a normal anatomical arrangement (Fig.  E). Compared with those in the tFNA or OMV group, representative micro-CT images revealed greater preservation of the periodontal bone architecture in the si-tRF-tFNA group (Fig.  F). The quantitative analysis of micro-CT scans also confirmed that si-tRF-tFNA significantly promoted periodontal bone regeneration, as evidenced by the highest trabecular number (Tb.N) and lowest Tb.Sp and CEJ-ABC among the tested groups (Fig.  G). Representative images of immunofluorescence staining revealed increased expression levels of osteogenesis-related markers, including Runx2 (Fig.  H), ALP (Fig.  I), and COL1A1 (Fig.  J), within periodontal tissues following the si-tRF-tFNA injection. Consistent with these observations, the quantitative analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in the expression of Runx2, ALP, and COL1A1 in the si-tRF-tFNA-treated group compared with the OMV and tFNA-treated groups (Fig.  K). Taken together, these findings indicate that tFNA-mediated delivery of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 inhibitors effectively rescues P. gingivalis OMVs from impaired osteogenesis and accelerates periodontal regeneration. This strategy highlights a translatable therapeutic platform for addressing periodontitis-associated bone loss.

 

## Discussion
P. gingivalis has been recognized as a keystone periodontal pathogen involved in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, which induces polymicrobial dysbiosis, subverts the immune response, and accelerates bone loss by delivering a plethora of virulence factors [ – ]. P. gingivalis OMVs concentrate these virulence factors, which further exaggerate the damage to periodontal tissues and contribute to systematic disorders, including cardiovascular injury [ ], Alzheimer’s disease [ ] and kidney diseases [ ]. Although the pathogenicity of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as LPS, gingipains, and fimbriae, in P. gingivalis OMVs has been widely recognized [ – ], the influences of OMV cargoes on the pathogenesis of periodontitis remain insufficiently characterized. This study redefines the current understanding of P. gingivalis OMV pathogenicity by demonstrating that P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNAs functionally impair osteogenesis and establishes novel targeted strategies to counteract this inhibition and restore periodontal bone regeneration. Our findings showed that P. gingivalis OMVs suppressed osteogenesis in vivo and inhibited the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs via the transfer of tsRNAs, especially tRF-Pro-GGG-1, in vitro. Mechanistically, we identified that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 directly binds to PurB, a transcriptional regulator, and impairs the transcription of the COL1A1 gene, thereby inhibiting the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs. Finally, we developed a tFNA-based delivery system for si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1, which effectively rescues osteogenesis in vitro and in vivo. Our findings not only reveal the mechanism by which a novel P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNA promotes periodontitis but also establish a targeted therapeutic strategy for periodontal bone regeneration.
Accumulating evidence has indicated that OMVs derived from periodontal pathogens exacerbate the pathogenesis of periodontitis via the delivery of various virulence factors [ , , ]. For example, P. gingivalis OMVs amplify proinflammatory responses, inhibit the mineralization process [ ], and induce apoptosis in PDLSCs [ ]. By delivering LPS and gingipain, P. gingivalis OMVs activate Toll-like receptor 2 to promote osteoclast differentiation [ , ] and disrupt epithelial cell adhesion through gingipain-mediated proteolysis [ ]. Consistent with these studies [ , ], our data indicate that P. gingivalis OMVs significantly exacerbate alveolar bone loss and suppress osteogenesis both in vivo and in vitro. In addition to classical PAMPs expressed on the membrane of P. gingivalis OMVs, P. gingivalis OMVs also encapsulate pathogen-derived RNAs or DNA to influence bacteria-to-human interactions [ , , ]. Notably, we identified tRF-Pro-GGG-1, a previously uncharacterized tsRNA, as the key mediator through which P. gingivalis OMVs impair the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs. Functional validation confirmed that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimic significantly decreased the osteogenic differentiation potential, whereas inhibiting tRF-Pro-GGG-1 rescued the expression of osteogenic markers, as evidenced by increased ALP activity, increased calcium deposition, and the upregulated expression of osteogenesis-related genes/proteins. These findings not only reveal a novel mechanism by which P. gingivalis OMVs exert pathogenic effects but also broaden our understanding of the functions of tsRNA-mediated bacteria-host crosstalk in periodontal disease.
In this study, both overexpression and inhibition of tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 influenced the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs but in seemingly contradictory directions, hinting at the complexity of its regulatory mechanisms. First, non-coding RNAs, including certain tsRNAs, may exhibit concentration-dependent bidirectional regulatory functions, analogous to hormesis. Previous research has established that miRNAs as a system mediates “regulatory hormesis”, manifested in biphasic behavioral and physiological phenotypes [ ]. Second, a single tsRNA may engage in multiple, potentially antagonistic, signaling pathways by targeting different RNA-binding proteins or mRNAs. Furthermore, post-transcriptional modifications of tsRNAs can affect their stability, subcellular localization, and choice of interacting proteins [ ]. Differences in the modification states between the synthetic mimic and endogenous molecules may lead to functional discrepancies. The complex regulatory pattern revealed by tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 underscores the multi-faceted roles of bacterial tsRNAs involved in in host-microbe crosstalk, presenting an intriguing direction for future research.
tsRNAs are evolutionarily ancient and highly conserved classes of noncoding RNAs that play pivotal roles in the regulation of gene expression through interactions with proteins or mRNAs [ , ]. Functionally, tsRNAs can exhibit microRNA (miRNA)-like activity by forming ribonucleoprotein complexes with Argonaute (AGO) proteins, thereby suppressing target gene expression [ , , ]. Alternatively, tsRNAs can inhibit translation initiation through interactions with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), such as Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) [ , , ], or by binding directly to translation initiation factors (e.g., eIF4G) or components of the multisynthetase complex [ , ]. Through an RNA pulldown analysis, we identified a specific interaction between tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and the RNA/DNA binding protein PurB. PurB interacts with both single-stranded DNA and RNA, modulating gene transcription and mRNA translation [ ]. This protein forms homodimers, heterodimers with PurA, or heterotrimers with PurA and YBX1 within the nucleus [ , ]. Notably, PurA and YBX2 (functionally analogous to YBX1) were also detected in our RNA pulldown analysis of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 interactors, along with eIF4G, although PurA, YBX2, and eIF4G did not meet the predefined enrichment criteria for significant interactors. In addition, the AGO proteins were not detected, indicating that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mainly regulates transcription rather than translation. The interaction of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and PurB was further confirmed by RIP-qPCR and EMSA, which revealed that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 was significantly enriched with an anti-PurB antibody. The functional analysis confirmed that si-PurB rescued the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs that was compromised by tRF-Pro-GGG-1 or P. gingivalis OMVs, demonstrating that PurB is the main target of P. gingivalis OMV-derived tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in regulating the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs. PurB is known to interact with mRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) [ ], or circular RNAs (circRNAs) [ ] to regulate downstream targets, but its interaction with tsRNAs has not been previously documented. Hence, our findings elucidate a novel mechanism through which a P. gingivalis OMV-derived tsRNA influences host gene expression.
PurB is a transcriptional coactivator that binds single-stranded purine-rich elements within promoter regions, thereby contributing to transcriptional regulation. PurB plays diverse roles in many physiological and pathological contexts, such as cancer and myogenesis [ , ]. PurB reportedly modulates the expression of multiple genes, such as DNA binding protein 2 (DDB2) [ ], ADGRB1 [ ], and FLK1 [ ]. However, the influence of PurB on osteogenesis has not been recognized. In the present study, we showed that PurB binds to the promoter of COL1A1 and influences its transcription by ChIP-seq and dual-luciferase reporter gene assays. Although tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimics alone do not affect the transcriptional activity of COL1A1, co-transfection of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 mimics with PurB significantly reduces COL1A1 transcriptional activity, providing direct functional evidence that the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 binding inhibited the osteogenesis by binding to PurB rather than nonspecific silencing. When the tRF-Pro-GGG-1 was absent, PurB binds to the promoter of COL1A1 and activates its transcription, indicating that tRF-Pro-GGG-1 binding converts PurB from an activator to a repressor. This perfectly explains the contradiction that PurB knockdown enhances osteogenesis in OMV-treated PDLSCs, while functions as transcriptional activator of COL1A1 when tRF-Pro-GGG-1 is absent. Overall, this work reveals a previously unrecognized role for interaction of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 and PurB in regulating osteogenic differentiation. In the future, we will use ChIP-qPCR to investigate histone modifications at the COL1A1 promoter, and provide more detailed epigenetic mechanism (e.g., recruitment of repressive complexes) behind this functional switch.
Given the critical role of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in suppressing osteogenesis based on the aforementioned findings, we explored the development of therapeutics targeting this specific tsRNA. In this study, we selected tFNA as the delivery platform for si-tRF-Pro-GGG-1 based on several outstanding properties that align with the requirements for effective nucleic acid delivery. Compared to traditional delivery systems (e.g., lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, or viral vectors), tFNAs possess multiple comparative advantages. First, tFNAs exhibit superior structural stability and programmability due to their precise, base-pair-directed self-assembly, which yields a highly uniform nanostructure with enhanced resistance to nuclease degradation. For example, during aerosolization, tFNAs maintained more stable particle size, zeta potential, and morphology before and after nebulization than lipid nanoparticles [ , ]. Second, tFNAs demonstrate an inherent self-delivery capability independent of transfection reagents, primarily attributed to their suitable nanoscale size, spatial structure, and interactions with cell membrane receptors, enabling efficient cellular internalization [ ]. Third, tFNAs demonstrate favorable biocompatibility and safety profiles. Composed of natural DNA, they are biodegradable and exhibit low immunogenicity [ , ]. Fourth, tFNAs offer enhanced tissue permeability and targeting potential. Their small size and rigid structure promote deeper tissue penetration compared to larger lipid-based vesicles, a critical feature for targeting localized diseases such as periodontitis [ , , ]. Additionally, their vertices can be functionalized with targeting ligands (e.g., peptides, aptamers) for active targeting, though this strategy was not employed in the present work [ ]. Finally, tFNAs provide versatile and stable drug-loading capacity. They can encapsulate diverse cargoes (e.g., nucleic acids, small molecules, proteins, and inorganic nanoparticles) via intercalation, embedding, or surface adsorption [ ]. Therefore, to efficiently, stably, and safely deliver degradation-prone siRNA to PDLSCs within the complex periodontitis microenvironment, thereby countering the inhibitory effects of bacterial OMVs, tFNAs integrate the key advantages of high stability, efficient self-delivery, excellent biocompatibility, and superior tissue penetrability, making them a more ideal delivery platform than traditional carriers. By directly loading nonnucleic acid therapeutics (e.g., low-molecule weight compounds, natural products) or nucleic acid drugs (e.g., siRNAs, miRNAs) onto the delivery platform, researchers have successfully fabricated functionalized tFNA nanostructures, facilitating the incorporation of various drug classes onto tFNA platforms [ ]. tFNA-based therapeutics have exhibited robust therapeutic potential in diverse diseases. For example, a bioswitchable tFNA-based delivery system loaded with miR-30a-5p was developed to achieve the noninvasive treatment of uveal melanoma, effectively targeting the miR-30a-5p/E2F7 axis [ ]. A liver-targeted tFNA-siCcr2 delivery system exhibiting potent CCR2 inhibition significantly attenuated liver fibrosis by normalizing immune cell profiles and establishing an antifibrotic niche [ ]. In the present study, we engineered a tFNA-based bioswitchable delivery system capable of delivering an inhibitor targeting tRF-Pro-GGG-1, named si-tRF-tFNA. The si-tRF-tFNA system has a self-delivery capability and biocompatibility and overcomes key limitations of nucleic acid delivery [ ]. The si-tRF band was barely visible in the lane corresponding to the final si-tRF-tFNA complex, indicating that nearly all si-tRF molecules were incorporated into the tFNA scaffold. This suggests that the loading efficiency of si-tRF onto tFNAs was close to 100%. Ribonuclease H (RNase H) is an endogenous enzyme that specifically recognizes and cleaves the RNA strand within DNA/RNA hybrid duplexes, thereby mediating the intracellular release of RNA from the tFNA scaffold. Previous studies have demonstrated that RNase H can effectively trigger the release of RNA from DNA-based nanostructures in vitro [ ]. The data showed that when different concentrations of RNase H were incubated with tFNA–RNA complexes at 37 °C for 1 h, RNA unloading occurred at enzyme activities of 50 U/mL and above, and the release efficiency increased with higher enzyme activity, reaching approximately 80% at activities over 100 U/mL. These findings suggest that intracellular RNase H may facilitate RNA release from the tFNA scaffold in our system, mimicking the physiological degradation and release process. Encouragingly, si-tRF-tFNA restored the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs that was suppressed by P. gingivalis OMVs. Furthermore, in a ligature-induced periodontitis murine model, the local injection of si-tRF-tFNA effectively neutralized the osteogenic-inhibitory effects of P. gingivalis OMVs and significantly promoted the regeneration and repair of periodontal bone tissue. Concurrently, we further validated the mechanism of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 in vitro. Following the deletion of the PurB binding site in COL1A1, si-tRF-tFNA exhibited effects comparable to those of standard tFNA, failing to exert its significant rescue effect. This result further confirms the aforementioned molecular mechanism.

## Conclusion and limitations
In conclusion, our study revealed that P. gingivalis OMVs inhibit osteogenesis by transferring tRF-Pro-GGG-1, which suppresses the transcription of COL1A1 by directly interacting with PurB. By integrating siRNAs targeting tRF-Pro-GGG-1 into tFNA, we propose that si-tRF-tFNA is a promising therapeutic candidate for periodontitis. These findings suggested the pathogenic roles of tsRNA transferred by OMVs in microbial pathogenesis and highlighted the potential of RNA-targeted nanotherapeutics in periodontitis treatment. Despite these findings, several limitations need further exploration. First, in addition to tRF-Pro-GGG-1, the mechanistic roles of other tsRNAs warrant systematic investigation. The paradoxical role of tiRNA-Asn-GTT-1 in osteogenic modulation requires further resolution. The impact of tsRNA modifications on gene regulation remains unexplored. In addition, a rescue experiment with an RNA-binding deficient PurB mutant would be ideal for establishing functional specificity of tsRNA. Moreover, the biogenesis, processing, and regulatory mechanisms of tRF-Pro-GGG-1 are incompletely characterized. Potential cotargets in addition to PurB and crosstalk with signaling pathways need to be elucidated. Finally, P. gingivalis OMVs may not fully recapitulate the human periodontal microenvironment, and the clinical viability of si-tRF-tFNA delivery systems, such as manufacturing costs and in vivo stability, remains unvalidated. Future research will focus on optimizing the si-tRF-tFNA delivery system, evaluating its long-term safety and efficacy, and exploring its potential significance in treating a broader range of diseases.
