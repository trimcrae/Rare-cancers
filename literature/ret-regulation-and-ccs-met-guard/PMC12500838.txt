Nearly 40–50% of patients with sepsis develop reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, ventricular dilation, and impaired systolic function, with some also exhibiting right ventricular dysfunction(Lanspa et al. ). A deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SICM, coupled with the exploration of targeted therapeutic strategies, could yield more effective treatments and improve patient survival(Beesley et al. ). This review summarizes recent advances in the mechanisms of mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk in SICM and provides updates on therapies targeting these pathways, aiming to offer new perspectives for treating sepsis-induced cardiac injury.

## Mitochondria–nucleus communication
Mitochondria serve not only as the central hub for cellular energy metabolism but also achieve precise functional regulation through highly coordinated interactions with the nucleus. As nuclear DNA encodes most mitochondrial proteins, optimal mitochondrial function critically depends on nuclear transcriptional regulation. Reciprocally, mitochondria initiate retrograde signaling to communicate their functional state to the nucleus, thereby modulating cellular stress adaptation and metabolic reprogramming. This bidirectional communication mechanism primarily comprises two signaling pathways: anterograde signaling and retrograde signaling(Quiros et al. ). Anterograde signaling denotes nuclear-to-mitochondria communication through transcriptional control of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes. This pathway orchestrates mitochondrial biogenesis, dynamics (fusion/fission), energy metabolism, and stress adaptation responses(Sengupta & Levy ). Retrograde signaling, conversely, denotes the process through which mitochondria—when functionally impaired or under stress—transmit their status to the nucleus via diverse signaling modalities to regulate nuclear gene expression(Butow & Avadhani ). Anterograde and retrograde signaling pathways work in close collaboration to collectively maintain homeostasis between mitochondria and the nucleus.

## Mitochondria–nucleus anterograde signaling
Mitochondria-nuclear anterograde signaling is a fundamental mechanism by which the nucleus regulates mitochondrial biogenesis, dynamic remodeling, and functional coordination, relying on the modulation of nuclear-encoded transcription factors, protein import machinery, and metabolic cues.
Under normal physiological conditions, nuclear respiratory factors 1 (NRF1) and 2 (NRF2) play a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis within the nucleus. They act in concert with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-α (PGC-1α), which enhances the transcription of genes involved in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) replication, the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system, and mitochondrial cristae structure, thereby maintaining normal mitochondrial function(Scarpulla ). Specifically, PGC-1α binds to NRF1, upregulating the expression of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM). TFAM, a key mtDNA-binding protein, not only maintains mitochondrial genome stability but also directly participates in mtDNA replication and transcription, thus safeguarding the integrity of the mitochondrial genetic system(Kelly & Scarpulla ). Furthermore, a complex post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism governs the translation of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins. Specifically, the RAS response element binding protein 1 (RREB1) binds to the 3' untranslated region (3'UTR) of mRNAs encoding subunits of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II (succinate dehydrogenase). This interaction modulates mRNA translation efficiency, thereby influencing complex II assembly and function(G. Han et al. , ).
In addition to transcriptional regulation, mitochondrial biogenesis is also intricately controlled at the post-transcriptional level by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and non-coding RNAs. At the RBP level, studies have shown that polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1) can bind to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of specific mRNAs and regulate their fate—for instance, by preventing UPF1 binding and thereby inhibiting nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), leading to mRNA stabilization(Fritz et al. ). It is therefore plausible to hypothesize that under certain pathological conditions, PTBP1 may similarly bind to the 3′UTR of PGC-1α mRNA, modulating its translational efficiency and subsequently influencing mitochondrial biogenesis. Within the non-coding RNA network, miR-181c has been demonstrated to translocate into the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes, where it binds to the 3′UTR of mitochondrial-encoded cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (mt-COX1) mRNA, inhibiting its translation. This disrupts the assembly of complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), leading to impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). This mechanism is closely associated with metabolic reprogramming, ROS accumulation, and the progression of heart failure(Das et al. , ). Moreover, miR-34a has been reported to directly target and suppress SIRT1 mRNA expression, and its upregulation has been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction in various metabolic diseases and cellular stress models(A. J. Wang et al. ). If the long non-coding RNA GAS5 functions as a ‘molecular sponge’ to sequester miR-34a, it may upregulate SIRT1 expression, thereby enhancing SIRT1-mediated deacetylation and activation of PGC-1α, which in turn promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic adaptation(Q. J. Wu et al. ). Collectively, these mechanisms constitute a multilayered post-transcriptional regulatory network that precisely coordinates mitochondrial biogenesis and functional maintenance. This network may serve as a critical adaptive regulatory node for cellular responses under stress conditions.
Although mitochondria possess their ownDNA, the overwhelming majority of mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded. These proteins are synthesized in the cytosol and subsequently imported into mitochondria via a highly specialized translocation machinery. This essential process is collectively termed the mitochondrial protein import system(Endo et al. ). For proteins destined for the mitochondrial matrix and specific regions of the inner membrane, the mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) represents the most classical import signal. The mitochondrial targeting signal (MTS), typically an N-terminal amphipathic α-helix, is specifically recognized and bound by the translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) complex—the primary entry gate for mitochondrial protein import. Following recognition, precursor proteins are translocated across the inner membrane into the matrix via inner membrane translocase complexes. Within the matrix, the MTS is cleaved by mitochondrial processing peptidases, and the imported proteins subsequently undergo proper folding, assembly, and functional maturation with the assistance of matrix chaperones, such as Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), and specific assembly factors(Wiedemann et al. ). This classical import pathway constitutes a key mechanism through which anterograde signaling regulates essential mitochondrial functions. A representative regulatory molecule imported via this pathway is the nuclear-encoded mitochondrial Lon protease (LonP1). LonP1 harbors a canonical MTS at its N-terminus, directing its mitochondrial recognition and import into the matrix. Once localized within the matrix, LonP1 exerts precise control over mtDNA stability, transcription, and replication by selectively degrading transcription factor A mitochondrial (TFAM), thereby maintaining mitochondrial functional homeostasis(Zanini et al. ).
In addition to the classical N-terminal MTS-dependent pathway, the targeting and functional integration of nuclear-encoded proteins into mitochondria also involve multiple non-canonical mechanisms, ensuring that diverse regulatory signals are accurately delivered to specific mitochondrial compartments. Many proteins localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane or involved in regulating mitochondrial dynamics lack cleavable N-terminal MTSs. Instead, they utilize internal signal sequences or specific structural domains to directly interact with receptors on the mitochondrial surface. For instance, the nuclear-encoded GTPase dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) exists in an autoinhibited conformation in the cytoplasm. Upon activation, its internal domains become exposed and are specifically recognized by receptor complexes on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby facilitating its precise recruitment and functional engagement at the mitochondrial surface(S. Hao et al. ). Furthermore, proteins localized to the mitochondrial intermembrane space (IMS), particularly those characterized by high disulfide bond content such as Tim8 and Tim13, primarily utilize the mitochondrial intermembrane space import and assembly (MIA) pathway for their import and maturation. The core components of this pathway are the oxidoreductase Mia40 and the sulfhydryl oxidase Erv1. After initial translocation through the TOM complex, cysteine residues within the precursor proteins form transient disulfide bonds with Mia40. Subsequent oxidative folding and stable localization are completed with the assistance of Erv1. This process is independent of N-terminal MTSs and does not involve the TIM translocase complexes(Chacinska et al. ). Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins also represent a crucial regulatory mechanism. Modifications such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation can dynamically modulate the interactions between precursor proteins and mitochondrial outer membrane import receptors (e.g., Tom20, Tom70) or cytosolic chaperones (e.g., Hsp70, Hsp90). These PTMs influence the efficiency, specificity, and timing of mitochondrial import, providing a sophisticated layer of regulation for the delivery of nuclear-encoded directives(Schmidt et al. ).
Metabolic signals serve as a pivotal component of cellular anterograde communication. The intracellular level of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) directly regulates mitochondrial function(Ying ). NAD⁺ activates the deacetylase SIRT1, which specifically deacetylates lysine residues Lys13 and Lys14 of PGC-1α, relieving its autoinhibited conformation(Gomes et al. ). Activated PGC-1α subsequently recruits the ERRα/PPARγ complex to cooperatively upregulate the expression of mitochondrial biogenesis genes such as NRF1, TFAM, and oxidative phosphorylation-related genes like COX5B(Ramjiawan et al. ). Conversely, a reduction in NAD⁺ levels suppresses SIRT1 activity, leading to downregulation of NRF1 expression(Y. Wang et al. ). Moreover, decreased NAD⁺ availability induces a “pseudohypoxic” state under normoxic conditions, characterized by aberrant accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), which further impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)(Ryan & Hoogenraad ). Beyond NAD⁺, fatty acid-derived metabolites including ketone bodies and free fatty acids further regulate mitochondrial function by modulating the activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) family transcription factors(Arima ). PPARα predominantly governs the expression of fatty acid oxidation genes including CPT1A and ACADL, and synergizes with PGC-1α to enhance β-oxidation capacity(Liao et al. ). On the other hand, PPARγ activates the glucose transporter GLUT4 and thermogenic gene UCP1; activation of PPARγ has been shown to improve mitochondrial membrane potential(Phu et al. ).
In summary, mitochondria-nuclear anterograde signaling is a central mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial functional homeostasis, relying on multilayered and coordinated regulation. The transcriptional network centered on PGC-1α orchestrates mitochondrial biogenesis and gene expression. Nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins are selectively imported via both classical (presequence-dependent) and non-classical pathways. Concurrently, metabolic signals integrate cellular status and transmit retrograde feedback to the nucleus, further fine-tuning mitochondrial function. Elucidating the dynamic remodeling patterns of anterograde signaling across different tissues and stress conditions, particularly the regulatory thresholds of key nodes, will provide novel strategies for targeted interventions in mitochondrial dysfunction-related diseases(Fig.  ). 

## Mitochondria–nucleus retrograde signaling
Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial retrograde signaling coordinates nuclear gene expression through molecular mediators such as ROS, calcium signaling, and mtDNA, as well as a series of adaptive mechanisms, including energy-sensing pathways, mechanotransduction, the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), and epigenetic modifications. These processes collectively ensure mitochondrial functional integrity and cellular metabolic homeostasis.

## Retrograde signaling mediators
Mitochondria-derived ROS are not only metabolic byproducts but also serve as critical signaling molecules. The mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I and III are the primary sites of ROS generation. Complex I, particularly under reverse electron transport conditions, tends to produce substantial amounts of superoxide anions, whereas complex III continuously releases ROS into both the mitochondrial matrix and cytosol through intermediates of the Q cycle(Robb et al. ). At physiological levels, reactive oxygen species (ROS) serve as signaling mediators, activating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and activator protein-1 (AP-1). This induces expression of antioxidant enzymes including superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), establishing a negative feedback loop that maintains redox homeostasis(Rhoads et al. ). Additionally, reactive oxygen species (ROS) oxidize critical cysteine residues on nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). This modification promotes Nrf2 dissociation from its inhibitor Keap1, facilitating nuclear translocation where Nrf2 binds antioxidant response elements (AREs) and upregulates antioxidant gene expression(Kasai et al. ). However, excessive ROS accumulation induces oxidative mtDNA damage, representing a key mechanism for inflammation amplification and organ injury in pathological states(Quan et al. ). Specifically, superoxide anions directly attack guanine bases within mtDNA, generating mutagenic lesions such as 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxo-dG). This results in replication errors and double-strand breaks, with 8-oxo-dG levels reported to increase by approximately 3.5-fold in septic myocardium(Halczuk et al. ) Moreover, elevated ROS levels inhibit the activity of the essential DNA repair enzyme 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1), impairing the base excision repair (BER) pathway and leading to persistent accumulation of damaged mtDNA(Y. K. Lee et al. ). Concurrently, mitochondrial damage can trigger retrograde signaling to activate the ATM/ATR pathways in the nucleus, initiating DNA damage response (DDR) programs in an attempt to preserve genomic stability(Liang et al. ).Calcium ions (Ca2⁺), serving as ubiquitous intracellular second messengers, not only participate in the regulation of mitochondrial metabolism but also mediate the retrograde regulation of nuclear gene expression in response to mitochondrial functional status by modulating the activity of nuclear transcription factors(Campanella & Kannan ). Mitochondria rapidly uptake and release Ca2⁺ through mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), specialized structures formed with the sarcoplasmic reticulum. In response to physiological stimuli, this process generates localized calcium microdomains (or calcium transients), thereby activating a cascade of Ca2⁺-dependent signaling pathways(Jin et al. ). Ca2⁺ signaling modulates the activity of the nuclear transcription factor cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), inducing the expression of genes involved in metabolism, stress responses, and cell survival(H. Wang et al. ). Furthermore, studies have revealed that Ca2⁺ signaling influences the localization and activity of epigenetic modifiers, such as histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby further regulating chromatin conformation and gene expression states (Schlumm et al. ). However, under pathological conditions, calcium overload mediated by the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) complex triggers the aberrant opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). This leads to the release of the pro-apoptotic factor cytochrome c, subsequently amplifying cardiomyocyte injury(Bertero et al. ).
Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is strictly segregated within the mitochondrial matrix. During cellular stress or damage, however, mtDNA fragments escape into the cytosol via BAX/BAK-mediated outer membrane pores or mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) formation. Cytosolic mtDNA is detected by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), activating the cGAS-STING pathway. This triggers production of type I interferons (IFN-α/β) and other immune mediators, enhancing antiviral defense responses(Kim et al. ).
mtDNA damage profoundly disrupts cellular metabolism and stress homeostasis. As mtDNA encodes core subunits of respiratory chain complexes I, III, IV, and V, its oxidative damage reduces oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) efficiency(Nissanka & Moraes ). To counter this energy crisis, cells initiate compensatory metabolic reprogramming, such as aerobic glycolysis(Epstein et al. ). Concurrently, respiratory chain dysfunction leads to succinate accumulation, which stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), further suppressing OXPHOS gene expression(Bao et al. ). Furthermore, mtDNA damage compromises critical stress responses. Specifically, it induces mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse and disrupts the PINK1/Parkin pathway, thereby impairing mitophagy. This cascade leads to accumulation of damaged mitochondria and pathological ROS overproduction(Orekhov et al. ). On the other hand, mtDNA damage can attenuate the UPRmᵗ by inhibiting the nuclear translocation and activation of activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5). This compromises proteostasis maintenance and exacerbates mitochondrial functional imbalance(L. Yang et al. ).
Beyond its role in immune responses, mtDNA release also influences nuclear gene expression by activating retrograde signaling pathways. mtDNA release activates IRF3 and NF-κB via the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby regulating the expression of genes governing inflammation, immune responses, and cell survival(Kong et al. ). Additionally, studies indicate that mtDNA release can modulate cellular metabolic status, promoting the induction of autophagy(Sprenger et al. ). Therefore, the role of mtDNA in nuclear retrograde signaling is not only critically linked to immune responses but also plays a significant role in regulating gene expression and cellular metabolism.
Under physiological conditions, reverse electron transfer (RET) represents a tightly regulated bioenergetic process. During RET, when the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) is elevated (> 140 mV) and the NAD⁺/NADH ratio is low, electrons flow in reverse from the reduced ubiquinol pool (QH₂) back through complex I(Wright et al. ). This RET-induced electron flux leads to the controlled generation of superoxide (O₂•⁻) at the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) site of complex I. This constitutes a portion of the basal mitochondrial ROS production, typically accounting for 0.1–0.5% of total oxygen consumption(Filippo Scialò et al. , ). Moreover, this low-level ROS generation serves as a redox signaling mechanism, regulating nuclear transcriptional responses involved in cellular metabolism and stress adaptation(F. Scialò et al. , ). Under mild hypoxia or metabolic stress, RET-derived ROS can stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), thereby coordinating programs of metabolic reprogramming, angiogenesis, and mitochondrial biogenesis(Sena & Chandel ). Furthermore, RET is implicated in mitochondria-nucleus feedback loops that fine-tune gene expression patterns essential for maintaining mtDNA integrity, antioxidant defenses, and energy homeostasis, underscoring its role in inter-organellar communication(F. L. Shi et al. ).

## Retrograde signaling response mechanisms
A dynamic balance exists between nuclear transcriptional activity and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Cells maintain energy homeostasis and mitochondrial function through finely tuned energy-sensing mechanisms. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a principal cellular energy sensor, continuously monitors fluctuations in the ATP/AMP ratio. Under conditions of relative energy balance, AMPK remains moderately activated, regulating basal metabolism to support mitochondrial functional homeostasis(Carling et al. ). When ATP levels decline, AMPK is activated by energy stress, promoting the expression and activity of PGC-1α, thereby initiating the mitochondrial biogenesis program and enhancing both oxidative phosphorylation capacity and antioxidant defenses(Jäger et al. ). Upon mitochondrial stress, AMPK activation facilitates the nuclear translocation of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein H1 (hnRNPH1), which cooperates with transcription factor NRF1 in the nucleus to significantly enhance transcription of the mitochondrial fission protein DRP1(Zhao et al. ). Additionally, AMPK phosphorylates nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), promoting intracellular NAD⁺ synthesis and thereby augmenting the activity of the deacetylase SIRT1. Activated SIRT1 deacetylates PGC-1α at lysine residues Lys13 and Lys14, relieving its autoinhibited conformation and upregulating genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis(Cantó & Auwerx ). SIRT1 also enhances the activity of the upstream kinase LKB1 by deacetylating it at Lys48, which in turn facilitates phosphorylation and activation of AMPK at Thr172(Lan et al. ). Moreover, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase β (CaMKKβ) can independently activate AMPK in response to calcium signaling. AMPK activation further increases NAD⁺ levels, amplifying SIRT1 deacetylase activity and establishing a SIRT1–AMPK positive feedback regulatory loop. This cooperative mechanism significantly enhances mitochondrial adaptive responses, including promotion of mitochondrial biogenesis, dynamic remodeling, and antioxidant defense, thereby preserving energy homeostasis and cellular integrity under metabolic stress(Cantó et al. ). This nuclear response to changes in mitochondrial energy status exemplifies the cell’s highly adaptive capacity to metabolic stress and represents a key pathway for sustaining energy balance and cell survival.
The UPRmt is a critical retrograde signaling pathway that plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis and ensuring normal mitochondrial function. When protein-folding homeostasis is disrupted or misfolded proteins accumulate within mitochondria, the UPRmt is activated as a protective mechanism(Inigo & Chandra ). Activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5), a central regulator of the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), undergoes stress-induced activation and nuclear translocation. Within the nucleus, ATF5 induces expression of UPRmt target genes including mitochondrial chaperone HSP60 and protease ClpP(Suárez-Rivero et al. ). HSP60 facilitates the proper refolding of misfolded proteins, while ClpP degrades irreparably damaged proteins, thereby restoring mitochondrial protein homeostasis and mitigating stress-induced injury(Deng & Haynes ).
Mechanical forces also significantly influence retrograde signaling through interactions between mitochondria and the cytoskeleton. Specifically, mechanical stress exerted by the extracellular matrix can modulate intracellular mitochondrial dynamics. Such stress inhibits the activity of the key fission protein Drp1, which is responsible for excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, thus reducing mitochondrial division and promoting the formation of mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum (ER) contact sites. These contact points not only help stabilize mitochondrial function but also markedly enhance the efficiency of calcium ion transfer from mitochondria to the nucleus(Oliver-De La Cruz & Roca-Cusachs ). As a crucial second messenger, calcium ion fluctuations in the nucleus further activate key transcription factors such as calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) and cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB). Activation of this signaling cascade ultimately regulates the expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis, including TFAM, thereby promoting mitochondrial function and biogenesis (Adlimoghaddam & Albensi ).
Epigenetic regulation, through DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA expression, is a central mechanism in retrograde signaling that modulates nuclear gene expression. Metabolites of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, such as succinate and acetyl-CoA, translocate into the nucleus where they can directly regulate epigenetic modifications. Succinate inhibits the histone demethylase KDM5A, leading to elevated levels of H3K4me3 and consequently promoting gene transcription(Xiao et al. ). Acetyl-CoA, serving as a substrate for histone acetyltransferases (HATs), enhances H3K27ac modification, thereby facilitating the expression of NRF1, a key gene involved in mitochondrial biogenesis(W. Li et al. ). These metabolites not only convey cellular energy status but also dynamically reprogram nuclear transcriptional programs. Furthermore, the deacetylase SIRT1 orchestrates the coordination of mitochondrial function and inflammatory responses by deacetylating histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9ac) and the transcription factor forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) (Y. Yang et al. ). SIRT1 is recruited to the promoter regions of pro-inflammatory genes, where it specifically deacetylates H3K9ac, promoting heterochromatin formation and thereby suppressing the transcriptional activity of NF-κB target genes(T. F. Liu & McCall ). Under conditions of energy or oxidative stress, SIRT1 deacetylates FOXO1 at lysine residues 242 and 245 (Lys242/Lys245). This modification enhances FOXO1's DNA-binding capacity and promotes its nuclear translocation, subsequently activating the expression of antioxidant and anti-apoptotic genes, such as SOD2, BCL2, and Catalase(Y. F. Xu et al. ). This SIRT1-mediated mechanism effectively scavenges mitochondrial ROS, inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and maintains cellular homeostasis.
Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondria establish a complex retrograde signaling network through the engagement of diverse molecular mediators and the activation of intricate response mechanisms. These signals dynamically coordinate nuclear gene expression, thereby maintaining mitochondrial function, cellular metabolic balance, energy homeostasis, and the capacity to respond to environmental stressors. This highly sophisticated and interconnected signaling network exemplifies the remarkable cellular ability to monitor mitochondrial functional status in real-time and exert dynamic regulation over nuclear transcriptional programs. This underscores that mitochondria are far more than mere energy factories; they serve as central information-processing hubs within the cell, whose status directly impacts global gene expression. Deeper investigation into the communication mechanisms between these signaling pathways requires further exploration to identify dominant signaling pathways and their underlying regulatory switches within specific physiological or pathological contexts. Such research will provide crucial insights for understanding disease mechanisms and developing targeted intervention strategies.

## Role of mitochondria–nucleus crosstalk in sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy
Mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk plays a pivotal role in SICM. The anterograde signaling pathway from the nucleus to mitochondria, mediated by the PGC-1α/NRF1/NRF2 axis, is suppressed by inflammatory factors. This suppression leads to reduced mitochondrial biogenesis and impaired energy metabolism(Ping et al. ). Conversely, mitochondria release excessive ROS and mtDNA as retrograde signaling mediators. These mediators activate nuclear pathways, including NF-κB and cGAS-STING, thereby triggering a cytokine storm and cellular apoptosis(Oduro et al. ). Ultimately, this cascade results in imbalanced mitochondrial fission–fusion dynamics, calcium overload, and metabolic reprogramming, culminating in impaired myocardial contractile function. These mechanisms represent both a key pathogenic driver of the high mortality associated with sepsis and a potential therapeutic target (Fig.  ). 

## Dysregulation of anterograde signaling pathways
The mitochondrial-nuclear anterograde signaling pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of SICM, primarily mediated by the PGC-1α/NRF1/NRF2 axis, SIRT3-dependent deacetylation pathways, and TFAM-mediated mtDNA maintenance (Table  ).
 
PGC-1α is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, primarily functioning by activating nuclear respiratory factors 1 and 2 (NRF1 and NRF2) to promote the expression of mitochondrial genes. During sepsis, proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 suppress the expression of PGC-1α, ultimately leading to reduced mitochondrial biogenesis(Durand et al. ). Cardiac-specific PGC-1α knockout mouse models further confirm that loss of PGC-1α directly results in impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and cardiac dysfunction(Kärkkäinen et al. ). Recent studies demonstrate that 5′tiRNA-33-CysACA-1 is significantly upregulated in septic cardiomyopathy, where it impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics by reducing PGC-1α mRNA stability, thereby suppressing mitochondrial biogenesis(Yuan et al. ). Furthermore, NRF1 and NRF2 are essential for maintaining oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) efficiency by regulating transcription of mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) genes. In septic cardiomyopathy patients’ myocardial tissue, reduced NRF1/NRF2 activity strongly correlates with impaired function of mitochondrial complexes I and II(Matkovich et al. ).In recent years, pharmacological strategies targeting the PGC-1α pathway have made promising progress. Resveratrol enhances PGC-1α activity via Nrf2-dependent antioxidant signaling and concurrently suppresses NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses, significantly reducing LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis in sepsis(E. Hao et al. ). Notably, synthetic AMP 19–2.5 demonstrates therapeutic efficacy in septic cardiomyopathy by restoring ETC function and reducing ROS through PPARγ/PGC-1α/β upregulation(Martin et al. ). Another study demonstrated that Panaxadiol saponins (PDS) improve mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation by activating the NRF2 signaling pathway and promoting PGC-1α expression, thereby alleviating LPS-induced myocardial injury(Z. Yang et al. , ).Collectively, these findings indicate that the PGC-1α/NRF axis not only serves as a central regulatory pathway for mitochondrial biogenesis but also represents a promising therapeutic target for myocardial protection in sepsis.
During septic cardiomyopathy (SICM), impaired SIRT3 activity leads to mitochondrial dysfunction. The SIRT3-mediated deacetylation pathway plays a pivotal role in disease progression. On one hand, reduced SIRT3 activity results in hyperacetylation of key metabolic enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, disrupting ATP synthesis and energy metabolism, which directly contributes to impaired myocardial contractility. In sepsis models, SIRT3 knockout mice exhibit exacerbated mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, ROS accumulation, and myocardial tissue injury (Y. Xu et al. ). On the other hand, SIRT3 inactivation suppresses AMPK activity, impairing mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy. This leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, excessive ROS production, aggravated oxidative stress, and the occurrence of ferroptosis, collectively promoting cardiomyocyte death and worsening cardiac function(Xin & Lu ). Notably, the ANXA1 peptide has been shown to significantly upregulate SIRT3 expression, activating the AMPK–mTOR pathway to promote protective mitophagy and enhance p53 deacetylation, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and improving both cardiac function and mitochondrial homeostasis(Qin et al. ). Emodin, a natural compound, has been identified as a SIRT3 activator that restores its activity and reverses sepsis-induced hyperacetylation of key metabolic enzymes and lactate accumulation, exerting a cardioprotective effect(Y. Xu et al. ). Moreover, KOENTGES et al. demonstrated that inhibition of calpain with MDL28170 prevents SIRT3 inactivation-induced cleavage of ATP synthase subunit ATP5A1, partially restoring ATP production and myocardial contractility(Koentges et al. ). Taken together, SIRT3 represents a promising therapeutic target in septic cardiomyopathy, and strategies aimed at restoring or enhancing its activity are currently supported by experimental evidence.
Transcription factor A, mitochondrial (TFAM), a nuclear-encoded protein responsible for packaging mtDNA, plays a crucial role in maintaining mtDNA stability and replication. During sepsis, oxidative stress leads to mtDNA damage and downregulation of TFAM expression, further exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunctio(Cimolai et al. ). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been shown to inhibit TFAM transcription by inducing hypermethylation of its promoter region, whereas histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors can reverse this epigenetic repression, restoring TFAM expression and mtDNA integrity(X. Shi et al. ). Studies have demonstrated that melatonin significantly enhances mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in septic myocardium by upregulating TFAM expression and promoting mtDNA repair(Pan et al. ). In addition, the traditional Chinese medicine formula Po-Ge-Jiu-Xin Decoction activates PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, which facilitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria, maintains energy metabolic homeostasis, and promotes TFAM functional recovery(Z. Wang et al. ). These findings suggest that targeting TFAM not only helps repair mtDNA damage but also confers cardioprotection through mitophagy and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms.
A deeper understanding of mitochondria-to-nucleus anterograde signaling pathways provides essential mechanistic insights into SICM and offers a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted therapies. Future research should focus on elucidating the crosstalk between signaling networks and addressing challenges in clinical translation, aiming to advance these promising therapeutic targets from bench to bedside to ultimately improve patient outcomes.

## Disruption of retrograde signaling pathways
Dysregulation of mitochondrial-nuclear retrograde signaling is central to the pathogenesis of septic cardiomyopathy. This process involves the release of molecular mediators such as ROS, mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs), and calcium signals, which trigger nuclear responses through epigenetic modifications, the UPRmt, and mitochondrial-derived peptide signaling pathways. These responses collectively activate pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic pathways, ultimately leading to cardiomyocyte dysfunction and injury (Table  ).
 

## Mediator-Driven Retrograde Signaling
Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) function as both damaging agents and signaling molecules in sepsis. Excessive mtROS upregulate proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) by inducing oxidative modifications in nuclear transcription factors NF-κB and AP-1.In LPS-induced sepsis models, ROS generated by mitochondrial complex III activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby exacerbating pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes(Dai et al. ). Studies have shown that α-ketoglutarate (AKG) alleviates cardiomyocyte apoptosis by promoting mitochondrial autophagy and fission, thereby eliminating ROS-overproducing mitochondria and restoring ATP production(W. Wu et al. ). The dual role of ROS signaling is further reflected in its interaction with the AMPK pathway. During sepsis, ROS inhibit AMPK activity, aggravating mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction. Metformin activates AMPK to enhance the expression of the antioxidant enzyme SOD2, significantly improving cardiac function(Gao et al. ). Additionally, SIRT3 overexpression has been reported to reverse ROS-induced damage in H9C2 cardiomyocytes by enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant defense(Cheung et al. ). These findings suggest that targeting the dynamic balance between ROS production and clearance may offer therapeutic benefits in septic cardiomyopathy.
Mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) are molecules released by mitochondria under stress or injury that can trigger host immune response(Galizzi & Di Carlo ). mtDAMPs activate nuclear inflammatory pathways primarily through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the cGAS-STING signaling axis(Q. Yang et al. , ). Studies using cGAS knockout mice have confirmed that inhibition of this pathway effectively blocks the cGAS-STING–dependent inflammatory cascade triggered by mtDNA release, thereby alleviating cardiac inflammation and myocardial injury in sepsis(H. Liu et al. ). Elevated circulating mtDNA levels in septic patients can also activate the TLR9-MyD88-NF-κB pathway, leading to cardiomyocyte apoptosis(Yao et al. ). Additionally, extracellular histones disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential and induce cytochrome c release, thereby directly activating caspase-3–dependent apoptotic pathways(Waterhouse et al. ). Recent evidence has highlighted the critical role of the PGAM5-VDAC1 axis in regulating mitochondrial membrane permeability and mtDAMPs release. Targeting this axis with puerarin significantly mitigated septic myocardial injury(Zhuang et al. ). Therapeutic strategies targeting mtDAMPs also include small-molecule inhibitors. TAK-242, a TLR4 inhibitor, markedly improved cardiac function by blocking mtDAMPs signaling(J. Yang et al. ). Furthermore, in LPS and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic cardiomyopathy models, the novel Gasdermin D (GSDMD) inhibitor GI-Y2 reduced myocardial dysfunction by suppressing GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and mitochondrial injury(Mei et al. ).
During sepsis, elevated cytosolic calcium levels lead to calcium influx into mitochondria, activating calcineurin. Activated calcineurin dephosphorylates nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), promoting its translocation into the nucleus and inducing the transcription of genes involved in cardiac hypertrophy, inflammation, and apoptosis(Chaklader & Rothermel ). Yang et al. found that oxidative modification of ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2) leads to sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium leakage, further impairing myocardial contractility(J. Yang et al. ). Treatment with the RyR2 stabilizer JTV-519 significantly improved cardiac function in sepsis by preventing calcium leakage. The relationship between calcium signaling and mitochondrial dynamics has also attracted considerable attention. Excessive activation of mitochondrial Drp1 promotes abnormal opening of the IP3R calcium release channel, exacerbating calcium overload. Panaxadiol saponins (PDS) alleviate mitochondrial fragmentation by inhibiting Drp1 phosphorylation, thereby maintaining calcium homeostasis and improving myocardial contractility(Z. Yang et al. , ). Recent studies have demonstrated that the specific Drp1 inhibitor MDIVI-1 improves calcium overload and contractile dysfunction in LPS-induced septic cardiomyocytes by blocking the Drp1/Fis1 interaction and preventing mitochondrial over-fragmentation and mPTP opening(Zhu et al. ). Conversely, overexpression of Mfn2 enhances mitochondrial fusion, reduces calcium leakage, restores excitation–contraction coupling, and attenuates necrotic apoptosis in cardiomyocytes(Song et al. ). These findings suggest that regulation of calcium homeostasis contributes to the stabilization of mitochondrial structure and function during septic cardiomyopathy.

## Response mechanism–mediated retrograde signaling
Mitochondrial damage signals can also reprogram nuclear gene expression via epigenetic mechanisms, profoundly affecting cellular functional status. During sepsis, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive accumulation of ROS, mtDNA, and metabolic intermediates. These retrograde signals can directly or indirectly modulate histone modifications and DNA methylation patterns in the nucleus, triggering nuclear epigenetic reprogramming(Wagner et al. ). For instance, succinate accumulation from the TCA cycle inhibits histone demethylase KDM5A, resulting in elevated H3K4me3 levels and activation of genes involved in inflammation and metabolic reprogramming(Soloveychik et al. ). In addition, acetyl-CoA, as a substrate for histone acetyltransferases (HATs), promotes H3K27ac modification and enhances the transcriptional activity of key mitochondrial biogenesis genes such as NRF1 and TFAM, constituting a compensatory response to the cellular energy crisis(Bradshaw ). Mitochondrial ROS can also oxidatively modify critical cysteine residues of histone deacetylase SIRT1, suppressing its deacetylase activity and leading to aberrant elevation of H3K9ac. This not only disrupts heterochromatin structure but also relieves repression at pro-inflammatory gene promoters, amplifying NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses(Kalous et al. ). Conversely, SIRT1 activation can suppress inflammatory gene expression and enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2 and catalase by deacetylating H3K9ac and the transcription factor FOXO1, reflecting the bidirectional regulatory role of epigenetic modifications(W. Zhang et al. ). Moreover, mitochondrial damage triggers the release of mtDNA fragments, activating the cGAS-STING pathway and upregulating the expression and activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), resulting in hypermethylation at promoter regions of genes such as PGC-1α and PINK1, thereby impairing mitochondrial biogenesis and autophagy and contributing to a vicious cycle(Bam et al. ; Kim et al. ). Resveratrol has been shown to activate SIRT1, enhance mitochondrial metabolism, and inhibit HAT p300 activity, thereby suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory genes(Kuno et al. ). Furthermore, histone deacetylase inhibitors (e.g., vorinostat) and DNA demethylating agents (e.g., 5-azacytidine) have been demonstrated to reverse epigenetic abnormalities caused by mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis models, providing experimental support for epigenetic-based interventions in septic cardiomyopathy (SICM) (J. Yang et al. ). While epigenetic therapies have been extensively explored in oncology, their application in sepsis-induced myocardial injury remains limited. Unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying nuclear epigenetic reprogramming mediated by mitochondrial damage holds promise for offering novel therapeutic targets and strategies for myocardial protection in sepsis.
Aberrant activation of the UPRmt is closely associated with dysregulated retrograde signaling. During sepsis, inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress impair mitochondrial proteostasis, triggering misfolded protein accumulation and consequent UPRmt activation. Moreover, mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy) has been reported to coordinate UPRmt activation, thereby alleviating LPS-induced septic cardiomyopathy(Y. Wang et al. ). Chen et al. demonstrated that Salvianolic acid B (Sal B) enhances the ATF5-mediated UPRmt pathway, facilitating the clearance of misfolded proteins and improving LPS-induced mitochondrial membrane potential loss and cardiomyocyte apoptosis(Chen et al. ). The regulation of UPRmt involves mitophagy, post-translational modification, and transcriptional control of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins. The functional integrity of UPRmt is critical for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and cardiomyocyte survival. Thus, targeting UPRmt modulation may offer a novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of septic cardiomyopathy.
In recent years, mitochondrial-derived peptides (MDPs), a class of small bioactive peptides encoded by mtDNA, have emerged as novel mitochondrial-to-nuclear retrograde signaling molecules and have garnered increasing attention for their protective roles in various diseases. Major MDPs include Humanin, mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA-c (MOTS-c), and small Humanin-like peptides 1 to 6 (SHLP1–6) (Kal et al. ). These peptides are upregulated or released under cellular stress and can exert multiple biological effects—including metabolic regulation, antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and anti-apoptosis—by directly translocating into the nucleus or activating intracellular signaling pathways, thereby exhibiting promising therapeutic potential. Studies have shown that in models of lung ischemia–reperfusion injury and cardiopulmonary bypass-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome, MOTS-c activates antioxidant response element (ARE) transcription and inhibits pro-inflammatory gene expression via the ROS-CK2A-MYH9 mitochondrial-to-nuclear signaling axis, thus attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation and protecting the pulmonary vascular endothelial barrier(X. Li et al. ). Furthermore, in aging-related cardiovascular diseases, Humanin promotes nuclear translocation of the transcription factor Nrf2 to induce antioxidant gene expression, while suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways such as JNK/p38 MAPK, thereby reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and tissue fibrosis. It has demonstrated significant cardioprotective effects in diseases such as atherosclerosis and myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury(Cai et al. ). Although MDPs have shown substantial protective roles in several disease models, their regulatory mechanisms in septic cardiomyopathy remain largely unexplored, highlighting a promising direction for future research.
Dysregulation of mitochondrial-nuclear retrograde signaling plays a central role in SICM, where it collectively drives cardiomyocyte dysfunction through molecular mediators and nuclear response mechanisms. Current research indicates that reversing this dysregulated retrograde signaling represents a crucial therapeutic strategy for SICM. However, complex interplay exists between these pathways, exemplified by the ROS-mtDAMPs-calcium signaling cascade and epigenetic regulation of the UPRmᵗ. Future studies should focus on integrating multi-target regulatory networks to advance the clinical translation of related intervention strategies.
However, sepsis represents a dynamically evolving pathological process, during which transcriptional programs undergo continuous remodeling as the disease progresses. In the early (compensatory) phase of sepsis, cells activate anterograde signaling pathways—such as the PGC-1α/NRF1/NRF2 axis—to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant capacity, thereby maintaining energy homeostasis(Sygitowicz & Sitkiewicz ). As inflammatory responses persist and metabolic stress intensifies, however, retrograde signaling mediators—including ROS and mtDNA—gradually accumulate. These mediators drive the activation of pro-inflammatory and apoptotic pathways within the nucleus, notably NF-κB and cGAS-STING, establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory cascade. This process progressively shifts the pathology toward the decompensated phase(Y. Han et al. , ). Recent spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing studies reveal that during the transition from the compensatory to the decompensated phase in septic cardiomyopathy, cardiomyocytes undergo programmed transcriptional reprogramming. This shift is characterized by the upregulation of energy metabolism genes being superseded by the activation of inflammation and cell death genes(K. Zhang et al. ). This dynamic transcriptional remodeling reflects the stage-specific regulation of mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk, representing a key mechanistic driver in the progression from functional adaptation to organ failure in sepsis-induced cardiac injury. Consequently, phenotype-specific diagnosis and personalized therapeutic strategies tailored to the distinct transcriptional signatures of different sepsis phases may emerge as a critical direction for the precision medicine of SICM in the future.

## Imbalance in specific inter-organellar signaling
Recent research has revealed that beyond mitochondrial-nuclear axis communication, the synergistic dysregulation of sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) stress and Golgi stress also contributes to SICM. These processes collectively establish a critical pathological foundation for dysfunctional inter-organellar signaling and multi-organ dysfunction(Khan et al. ; Meng et al. ). Within cardiomyocytes, the SR and mitochondria communicate via specialized membrane contact sites known as mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), facilitating bidirectional exchange of calcium signaling, lipid metabolism, and stress signals(Ding et al. ). Under septic conditions, oxidatively modified ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2) causes abnormal calcium leakage from the SR stores. The resulting cytosolic calcium overload not only directly activates calcium-dependent apoptotic pathways but also induces mitochondrial calcium overload via MAMs. This triggers aberrant opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), exacerbating cardiomyocyte apoptosis and functional impairment(Sepúlveda et al. ). Concurrently, ER stress sensor IRE1α activates its RNase domain to mediate non-canonical splicing of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA, producing the transcriptionally active isoform XBP1s. This spliced variant upregulates genes critical for protein folding and lipid metabolism. Notably, in some cell types, it interacts with the metabolic regulator PGC-1α, influencing mitochondrial bioenergetic reprogramming and mitochondria-nucleus feedback regulation. However, the specific role of this mechanism in cardiomyocytes requires further validation(J. Lee et al. ). Furthermore, studies indicate that FAM134B acts as a receptor for ER-phagy. Its retrotranslocation-homology domain (RHD) mediates the binding of ER membrane fragments to LC3, initiating ER quality control degradation. Although direct evidence linking Golgi stress to the FAM134B-RHD3 axis influencing the transport and activation of the mitophagy receptor optineurin (OPTN) is currently lacking, the potential crosstalk between ER-phagy and mitophagy in organellar homeostasis warrants focused investigation(He et al. ). Collectively, these findings suggest the existence of a dynamically coupled multi-organellar communication network involving the "SR-Golgi-mitochondria-nucleus." When sepsis disrupts the regulation of this network, cardiomyocytes may enter a vicious cycle of calcium dyshomeostasis, impaired energy metabolism, and mitochondrial functional failure, ultimately exacerbating myocardial injury and organ dysfunction. Future research must leverage spatial multi-omics technologies—such as spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics—to systematically elucidate the mechanisms of dynamic remodeling at inter-organellar contact sites and identify key signaling pathways during sepsis progression. This approach is essential for discovering novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

## Conclusion and future perspectives
Sepsis, a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units, is characterized by multiple organ dysfunction. Among the affected organs, the heart is particularly susceptible to inflammatory and metabolic disturbances, often resulting in cardiac dysfunction manifested by reduced cardiac output, impaired myocardial contractility, and arrhythmias. In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted the pivotal role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the onset and progression of septic cardiomyopathy (SICM). This is not only due to mitochondria being the central hub of cellular energy metabolism, but also because of their dynamic bidirectional communication with the nucleus, which is essential for cellular stress responses and homeostatic regulation. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of mitochondria–nucleus interactions is of great significance for understanding the pathophysiology of SICM and identifying potential therapeutic targets. The pathological mechanisms of mitochondria–nucleus crosstalk in SICM involve a complex imbalance in bidirectional signaling. Disruption of anterograde signaling impairs mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic capacity, while dysregulation of retrograde signaling triggers a vicious cycle of inflammation and oxidative stress. The interplay of these mechanisms ultimately leads to myocardial contractile dysfunction, contributing to the high mortality associated with septic cardiomyopathy.
Despite significant advances in basic and translational research on mitochondria–nucleus communication, the clinical application of targeted interventions for SICM remains challenging. Firstly, the pathophysiological process of SICM is highly dynamic and heterogeneous. Most existing studies focus on single time points or specific disease stages, lacking a systematic understanding of the temporal dynamics of mitochondria–nucleus signaling throughout disease progression. Moreover, substantial inter-individual variability among septic patients leads to inconsistent therapeutic responses, highlighting the urgent need for precise molecular subtyping and real-time monitoring approaches to enable personalized treatment strategies. Secondly, the majority of current therapeutic interventions are based on in vitro experiments or animal models, and the gap between these models and the human pathological context has not been fully bridged. Technical bottlenecks remain in achieving effective drug targeting, membrane permeability, and mitochondria-specific delivery. Furthermore, the metabolic stability and biosafety of such therapies in the complex inflammatory microenvironment of sepsis require further validation. The regulatory network of mitochondria–nucleus crosstalk is highly intricate, with extensive feedback and crosstalk between anterograde and retrograde pathways. Targeting a single signaling axis may be insufficient or even detrimental due to unintended side effects. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop multitargeted combination strategies that can precisely modulate the dynamic balance between forward and backward signaling, thereby restoring the coordinated function of mitochondria and the nucleus. Lastly, there is a lack of effective early diagnostic biomarkers and dynamic monitoring indicators for SICM in clinical practice, which hampers timely intervention and diminishes treatment efficacy. Future research should integrate single-cell multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics technologies to identify SICM-specific molecular markers and dynamic signaling pathways, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for early diagnosis and personalized therapy.
In summary, the clinical translation of mitochondria–nucleus signaling pathways in SICM faces multiple challenges, including mechanistic complexity, individual variability, limited therapeutic strategies, and inadequate monitoring tools. Future efforts should focus on advancing precision subtyping, targeted delivery systems, multitargeted interventions, and dynamic monitoring technologies to enable effective transition from bench to bedside. Despite the current gaps in our understanding of mitochondria–nucleus interactions, ongoing research continues to uncover their therapeutic potential in SICM, offering promising avenues for both theoretical exploration and clinical application.
