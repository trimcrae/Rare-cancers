## ABSTRACT
We report the case of a 51-year-old female with an unusual presentation of a cutaneous granular cell tumor (GCT) on the lower back. The lesion was asymptomatic, well-defined, and firm, with an initial clinical differential of dermatofibroma, pilomatricoma, or leiomyoma. Histopathological examination revealed a dermal tumor composed of large polygonal cells with granular eosinophilic cytoplasm and uniform nuclei, without atypia or necrosis. Immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis with strong S100 and CD68 positivity. Due to tumor extension tumor extension to the deep resection margin, close follow-up was advised. Accurate diagnosis of cutaneous GCTs relies on histopathology and immunohistochemistry, as clinical and dermoscopic findings are often nonspecific. Complete surgical excision is the primary treatment, with a generally favorable prognosis. Awareness of malignant potential and proper surgical management is crucial for optimal outcomes.

## INTRODUCTION
Granular cell tumors (GCTs) are rare, predominantly benign neoplasms originating from Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system.[ ] Although GCTs can occur anywhere in the body, they are of rare occurrence in skin, with <1% occurring in the skin.[ ] Cutaneous GCTs most frequently appear in middle-aged adults and have a slight female predilection.[ ] It usually manifests as small (0.5–3 cm), firm, slow-growing nodule and is most commonly found on the head-and-neck region, though it can appear in other areas.[ , , ] Here, we report a rare case of a cutaneous GCT presenting as a lower back swelling, an unusual location, along with a brief literature review highlighting the diagnostic challenges, molecular pathogenesis, treatment paradigms, and prognostic factors.

## CASE REPORT
A 51-year-old female presented with a gradually increasing swelling over her lower back for the past 6 months. The swelling was asymptomatic, with no associated pain, itching, or skin changes. There was no history of preceding trauma, insect bite, or local infection. The patient denied systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or night sweats. She had no personal or family history of similar lesions, malignancies, or neurocutaneous syndromes. On local examination, a well-defined, firm, nontender papulonodular swelling measuring approximately 4 cm × 3 cm was noted over the lower back. The overlying skin was unremarkable, with no signs of ulceration, erythema, punctum, or discharge. There was no evidence of regional lymphadenopathy. With the clinical differentials of dermatofibroma or pilomatricoma or leiomyoma, an excisional biopsy of the lesion was performed. Gross examination revealed a well-circumscribed, tan-colored lesion measuring 3.5 cm × 3 cm × 2.5 cm, located within the dermis. The cut surface was homogenous without areas of hemorrhage or necrosis. Microscopic evaluation showed a well-defined dermal tumor composed of large polygonal cells with abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm, fairly monomorphic round-to-oval nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli. No evidence of nuclear atypia, increased mitotic activity, or necrosis was observed. The tumor extended to the deep resection margin, but the lateral margins were free of tumor involvement [ - ]. Immunohistochemistry for S100 protein demonstrated diffuse cytoplasmic and nuclear positivity in the tumor cells, confirming the diagnosis of cutaneous GCT [ ]. The tumor cells were also positive for CD68 [ ]. There were no complaints at 3 weeks’ and 6 months’ follow-up post-surgery [ ].
 

## DISCUSSION
Accurate diagnosis of cutaneous GCT relies on a combination of clinical suspicion, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry. Clinical diagnosis can be challenging due to its nonspecific presentation and can be misdiagnosed as other benign entities, as in the present case. Tumors typically present as small, firm, and often painless papules or nodules, usually <3 cm in diameter.[ ] The overlying skin may appear normal, but sometimes, it can be slightly raised, discolored, or even ulcerated.[ ] Dermoscopy may aid in evaluation, but definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology can sometimes be utilized, but excisional biopsy remains the gold standard. FNA smears typically show sheets and clusters of large oval to polygonal cells with abundant granular cytoplasm, slightly eccentric nucleus and finely dispersed nuclear chromatin.[ ]
Histopathologically, GCTs are composed of large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm, a feature attributed to the presence of lysosomal aggregates.[ ] These granules are typically periodic acid-Schiff positive and diastase-resistant, indicating the presence of complex carbohydrates. The tumor cells exhibit fairly uniform, round-to-oval nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli, and mitotic figures are generally absent or rare. While most cases are benign, malignant variants (<2%) can demonstrate nuclear pleomorphism, spindling of tumor cells, necrosis, increased mitotic activity (>2 mitoses per 10 HPF), and Ki-67 labeling index >10%.[ , ] However, vascular invasion and metastasis remain the only definitive indicators of malignancy.[ , ] Immunohistochemistry plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis of cutaneous GCTs and distinguishing it from other tumors with similar morphology [ ].[ , , , , ] Strong, diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic S100 positivity confirms the Schwannian lineage of GCTs. Other positive markers include SOX10, CD68, and NSE, while cytokeratins and melanocytic markers (HMB-45, Melan-A) are negative. A recent study by Van Winden et al. indicated that Melan-A expression can noted in cases of cutaneous GCTs, potentially leading to diagnostic confusion with malignant melanoma. However, HMB45 and PRAME showed no staining in any of the GCTs examined in their study, indicating that HMB45 is a better marker in differentiating melanoma versus cutaneous GCTs.[ ]
 Complete surgical excision is the primary treatment for cutaneous GCTs, with a favorable prognosis. Wide local excision is typically recommended to ensure complete removal of the tumor and to reduce the risk of local recurrence.[ ] In cases where complete excision is not feasible, close follow-up or adjuvant therapies, including radiation therapy, may be considered.[ , ] Although malignant transformation is rare, patients should be monitored for local recurrence, particularly in cases with deep tissue involvement, rapid growth, ulceration, or histological features suggestive of malignancy. Given that the tumor in this case involved the deep resection margin and adequate margin clearance was not achieved, close clinical follow-up and potential reexcision is being planned.

## CONCLUSION
GCT should be considered in the differential diagnosis of firm, painless dermal swellings. Given its variable clinical presentation and histologic overlap with other soft tissue neoplasms, histopathology and immunohistochemistry remain the gold standard for diagnosis. Complete surgical excision with negative margins is the cornerstone of treatment to prevent recurrence. Despite its generally favorable prognosis, awareness of malignant potential and appropriate surgical management are essential to ensure optimal outcomes.
