## Introduction
Lung cancer is a significant global health challenge. Despite the broad application of the personalized and targeted therapies for lung cancer treatment, it remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide . The nucleosome is the fundamental structural unit of chromatin. Adjacent nucleosomes coil and fold to form highly condensed chromatin structures that store genetic material within the nucleus and loss of this compact structure results in the exposure of DNA sequence, a phenomenon known as chromatin remodeling . Chromatin remodeling is one of the important epigenetic processes in cancer, mainly orchestrated by alterations in genes encoding subunits of the SWI/SNF complex.
Studies have shown that about 20% of human cancer patients harbor mutations in SWI/SNF complex subunits , . SMARCA4 (BRG1) and SMARCA2 (BRM) are two mutually exclusive catalytic subunits within the SWI/SNF complex responsible for utilizing ATP hydrolysis to provide energy for chromatin remodeling. In lung cancer, SMARCA4 is one of the most common mutated subunits of the SWI/SNF complex and associated with poor clinical outcomes, and lung cancers with SMARCA4 deficiency were defined as a separate subtype clinically – . Notably, SMARCA4 mutations are often accompanied by mutations in other key tumor oncogenes or suppressor genes, including Kras, LKB1 and p53, with p53 being the most frequent co-mutated . Here we mainly introduce the composition of the SWI/SNF complex, structure and roles of SMARCA4/2, as well as clinical characteristics, diagnosis and current treatment of SMARCA4 mutant lung cancer.

## The SWI/SNF complex and structure of the SMARCA4 protein
Chromatin remodeling is one of the crucial epigenetic processes in cancer. Currently, there are two major classes of complexes involved in chromatin remodeling, namely histone modification complexes and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes. Based on phylogenetic and functional analyses, these chromatin remodeling complexes are further classified into four major families: SWI/SNF, imitation switch (ISWI), chromodomain helicase DNA-binding (CHD) and INO80 . Among these, the SWI/SNF family is the most extensively studied complex. Studies showed that it exhibits potent tumor-suppressive functions, and mutations of genes encoding the complex are prominent features of cancer initiation and progression, with mutations in one or more subunits exerting carcinogenic effects – .
The SWI/SNF complex utilizes energy derived from ATP hydrolysis through mutually exclusive catalytic subunits, SMARCA4 and SMARCA2. This energy is used to manipulate the spatial relationship between histones and DNA, disrupting their tight associations and leading to chromatin relaxation. Consequently, DNA becomes more accessible, facilitating interactions with proteins and RNA, which could either promote or inhibit gene expression, thereby regulating a range of essential cellular processes such as transcription and senescence , , . The SWI/SNF complex is composed of up to 15 subunits encoded by over 29 genes , . Based on their specific components, the SWI/SNF complex could be categorized into three different complexes (Fig. ): the BRG1/BRM-associated factor complex (BAF), the polybromo-associated BAF complex (PBAF) and the non-canonical complex (ncBAF) . The subunits assemble in a highly structured manner to create various families of complexes comprising both shared and distinct subunits. It typically includes SMARCC1 (BAF155), SMARCC2 (BAF170) and one of the two mutually exclusive catalytic subunits SMARCA4 or SMARCA2. SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 contributed to maintaining the normal activity of SWI/SNF through ATP hydrolysis, while BAF155 and BAF170 could regulate the assembly of SWI/SNF subunits – . 
SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 are multi-domain proteins that composed of DNA and protein interaction motifs, with up to 76% sequence similarity and complementary functions, but still exhibit different antagonistic activities (Figs.  and ) – . Both protein contain six highly conserved domains (Figs.  and ): proline-rich (PRR) domain, QLQ domain, small helicase/SANT-associated (HSA) domain, DNA-dependent ATPase domain, retinoblastoma (RB)-binding domain and Bromo domain .

 

The PRR domain and the QLQ domain, located at the N terminus of SMARCA4, are frequently associated with protein interactions, as the N terminal deletions lose the ability of binding to several key BAF proteins – . The PRR domain also plays an essential role in mediating interactions between histone acetyltransferase protein CBP and p53, contributing to p53 destabilization and inactivation . The HSA domain could bind to actin and its associated proteins to regulate the activity of chromatin-remodeling ATPase . It was found the HSA domain interacts with BCL7 proteins to maintain the normal chromatin remodeling activity . Additionally, the HSA domain is also involved in the interaction with BAF250a/ARID1A and Ku70, which mediates the glucocorticoid receptor-dependent transcriptional activation , . The highly conserved DNA-dependent ATPase domain is essential for chromatin remodeling, normal mitogenesis and transcription . The RB-binding domain interacts with retinoblastoma proteins to induce cell cycle arrest . The Bromo domain, located at the C terminus of SMARCA4, has the ability to interact with acetylated histones , , . Apart from the above domains, The SnAC domain acts as a histone anchor, is involved in SWI/SNF remodeling nucleosomes and required for the ATPase domain to gain traction and generate traction to move DNA inside the nucleosome , . It is also required for the normal mobilizing activities of both ATPase and nuclear bodies . Rather than solely depending on the ATPase domain for their functions, SMARCA4/2 may require domains both within and outside the ATPase domain for chromatin remodeling. Additionally, these domains interact with each other functionally, especially the effects of the HSA and SnAC domain on the ATPase domain. Actually, our understanding of the mechanisms and functions of these domains remains incomplete, and further studies are needed to achieve comprehensive understanding the functional complexity of the SMARCA4/2 proteins. 

## Impact and mechanism of SMARCA4/2 in lung cancer
Studies have demonstrated that SMARCA4/2 play essential roles in a range of cellular processes such as differentiation, proliferation, senescence and apoptosis , – . SMARCA4 mutations have been found in types of cancers, and restoring the expression of SMARCA4 could promote cancer cell differentiation and inhibit tumor progression – . It is widely acknowledged that SMARCA4 acts as a tumor suppressor, especially in lung cancers. However, the oncogenic or suppressive roles of SMARCA4 in other types of cancers are not consistent – . SMARCA2, also identified as a tumor suppressor gene, exhibits a significantly capability to inhibition cancer cell viability . Generally, SMARCA4 loss is mostly attributed to mutations, rather than hyper-methylation or gene silencing, while rarely mutations of SMARCA2 have been detected across multiple cancer cell lines or primary lung tumors . Post-transcriptional regulation and epigenetic silencing, such as promoter hypermethylation, primarily contribute to the SMARCA2 loss , . Studies have demonstrated that epigenetic silencing of SMARCA2 is associated with increased risk of lung cancers and worse prognosis , , , , , . Furthermore, SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 have complementary functions both in normal and cancer cells – . The proliferation of tumors with SMARCA4 inactivation relies heavily on SMARCA2. SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 are intricately coordinated and balanced, and their dysregulation contributes significantly to tumorigenesis. However, simultaneous deletions of SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 are also observed in NSCLC, paradoxically, they are correlating with unfavorable outcomes , , . Due to the intimate and complicated relationship between these two proteins, more studies are needed to further elucidate the roles of SMARCA4/2 in cancers.
It has been reported that SMARCA4/2 participate in regulating the expression of key genes involved in lung cancer development – . The absence of SMARCA4 and/or SMARCA2 leads to downregulation of genes that play roles in inhibiting cancer development and upregulation of genes associated with tumor progression , . Studies have demonstrated that SMARCA4 silencing promotes the proliferation and invasion of NSCLC through the alteration of nucleosome positioning at a wide range of cancer-associated genes . Loss of SMARCA4 could disrupt the direct eviction of poly-comb recessive complex from chromatin, resulting in subsequent altered expression levels of genes involved in tumorigenesis – . Loss of SMARCA4/2 has been further shown to be able to promote the progression of lung cancers through epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and increased oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) , . DNA replication and repair abnormalities are important causes of early tumorigenesis, and increased heterochromatin formation and replication fork stalling occur in SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma cells . Studies have also demonstrated that SMARCA4 could regulate tumor progression by inducing genomic instability resulted from the abnormality of DNA methylation and mismatch repair – . Additionally, SMARCA4 is also involved in the development of lung cancer through the regulation of the non-coding genome through enhancer remodeling, microRNA expression, and chromatin accessibility . Recent studies demonstrated that SMARCA4 loss leads to widespread disruption of enhancer activity, particularly at loci controlling immune-related genes such as STING1 and IL1β, resulting in impaired immune cell infiltration and resistance to immunotherapy . In small cell lung cancer (SCLC), SMARCA4 also governs transcriptional state plasticity by directly binding enhancers of neuroendocrine lineage-defining genes (ASCL1, NEUROD1) and regulating REST splicing via SRRM4, thereby orchestrating transitions between neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine phenotypes . Beyond enhancers, SMARCA4 activates tumor-suppressive microRNAs such as miR-222 through direct engagement with their distal regulatory elements and cooperatively upregulates miRNA biogenesis factors (DROSHA, DICER, DGCR8) in response to DNA damage , . Acute depletion of SMARCA4 globally reduces chromatin accessibility, RNA polymerase II occupancy, and nascent transcription at both coding and non-coding loci, highlighting its primary role as a transcriptional activator . Collectively, these findings reveal that SMARCA4 orchestrates a multilayered regulatory network across non-coding genomic regions, linking chromatin structure to immune evasion, cell fate plasticity, and genome stability in cancer. Future studies are needed to further elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of SMARCA4-mediated genomic regulation across different biological contexts.

## Co-mutations and mutually exclusive mutations associated with SMARCA4 mutations
SMARCA4 mutations are mutually exclusive with some common genes targeted for therapy, such as EGFR or ALK (Table  ) , – . This may imply SMARCA4 mutant cancer cells with EGFR mutations are difficult to survive, or they possibly share important signaling pathways and drug resistance during tumor progressions. A recent study has found that osimertinib-resistant lung cancers depend on the ATPase activity of SMARCA4/2, and inhibition of SMARCA4/2 ATPase can reverse osimertinib resistance . It is also widely reported that SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers are often accompanied with p53 and Kras mutations , , , – . It is reported that SMARCA4 inactivation together with loss of p53 and activation of Kras resulted in robust development of lung cancers as compared to p53 loss and activation of Kras . Mechanistically, SMARCA4 mutant cells exhibit enhanced oxygen consumption and increased respiratory capacity, suggesting a reliance on oxidative phosphorylation(OXPHOS) for energy production which possibly provide the basis for targeting SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers with OXPHOS inhibitors. Furthermore, in Kras mutant lung cancers, SMARCA4 and KEAP1 mutations are associated with metastatic disease and poor OS . It was also found that co-mutations in KEAP1, SMARCA4 and CDKN2A could serve as independent determinants of poor clinical outcomes with KRAS G12C inhibitor monotherapy in lung cancer cells . Overall, studies on the relationship between SMARCA4 mutations and these co-mutations, as well as mutually exclusive mutations have been relatively limited. More effort needs to be done to achieve comprehensive understanding of the SMARCA4 mutant cancers.

 

## The cellular origin of SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer
Recent work has elucidated that the cellular origin of SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinomas plays a critical role in determining tumor behavior and progression. Through the use of lineage-specific genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), it was found that SMARCA4 loss in club cells (CCSP⁺) promotes aggressive, high-grade, and metastatic tumors, whereas SMARCA4 inactivation in the more commonly implicated alveolar type II (AT2, SPC⁺) epithelial cells largely restricts tumor growth or produces low-grade lesions . These findings indicate that club cells—or potentially bronchioalveolar stem cells capable of adopting a club cell fate—serve as a permissive cell-of-origin for transformation in the context of SMARCA4 deficiency. Mechanistically, loss of SMARCA4 impairs SWI/SNF complex function and disrupts lineage-specific transcription factor networks, including NKX2-1 and GATA6, leading to epigenetic reprogramming toward poorly differentiated and metastatic cell states . This lineage dependency underscores the importance of cell-of-origin in shaping tumor phenotypes and suggests therapeutic opportunities targeting lineage plasticity, SWI/SNF synthetic lethality (e.g., SMARCA2 inhibition), or metabolic vulnerabilities in SMARCA4-mutant LUAD. Future studies are needed to validate these mechanisms in human tumors and explore combinatorial strategies to counteract the dedifferentiated state imposed by SMARCA4 loss.

## SMARCA4 mutations in lung cancer and clinic pathological features
Since the first report of SMARCA4-deficient thoracic sarcoma in 2015, SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer has attracted increasing attention. According to genomic abnormalities, SMARCA4 mutations are divided into two categories: type I includes truncations, fusions and homozygous deletions, primarily associated with the loss of SMARCA4 expression, while type II predominantly comprises missense mutations, typically retains SMARCA4 in tumor tissues , . In NSCLC patients, the mutation rate of SMARCA4 is around 10% in NSCLC, and more than half are the missense mutations . It was reported the SMARCA4 missense mutations occurred frequently at evolutionarily conserved motifs—such as the Walker A (binding), Walker B (hydrolysis), DNA-binding grooves, and brace helices—including hotspot residues like G784E, K785R, E882K, and R1192C (Fig.  ) , , . Despite retaining the ability to incorporate into SWI/SNF complexes, these variants exhibit impaired chromatin remodeling activity. Furthermore, although mutations lead to distinct chromatin binding kinetics: ATP-cleft mutations (e.g., G784E) delay chromatin release, while DNA-groove mutations (e.g., D881G) weaken DNA engagement, both converging on loss of enhancer accessibility, altered super-enhancer function, and aberrant transcriptional upregulation of oncogenes such as MYC . Moreover, these missense variants fail to displace Polycomb Repressive Complexes (PRC1/2), leading to their accumulation at gene promoters and CpG islands, thereby reinforcing transcriptional repression of developmental regulators . Both types of SMARCA4 mutations are associated with poor clinical prognosis, with type I mutations usually having a worse prognosis .
Based on the clinical and molecular characteristics, SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer was classified as thoracic SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated tumors (SMARCA4-dUT) and SMARCA4-deficient non-small cell lung cancer (SMARCA4-dNSCLC). SMARCA4-dUT is prevalent in middle-aged men with smoking, often accompanied with metastasis and poor prognosis – . SMARCA4-dNSCLC predominantly occurs in elderly male smokers and more than half of the SMARCA4-dNSCLC patients are classified as adenocarcinoma, followed by squamous cell carcinoma , . Histologically, SMARCA4-dUT often consists of diffusely arranged epithelioid cells of varying sizes, with monomorphic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, poor differentiation , . SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibits a broad spectrum of cytomorphological features, including well-defined cell boundaries, linear or papillary arrangement, binucleated or multinucleated tendency, predominantly high grade nuclei – .
The co-deficiency of SMARCA2 and SMARCA4 is a fundamental feature of SMARCA4-dUT, in which SMARCA4 deletion is accompanied by diffuse SOX2 positivity in poorly differentiated tumor tissues, where tissues retaining SMARCA4 are often SOX2 negative , . Moreover, SMARCA4-dUT is also characterized by positive SALL4 and CD34 , , . Cytokeratin 7 (CK7) expression is the main feature of SMARCA4-dNSCLC in which approximately 80% is CK7 positive , . TTF-1 and hepatocyte paraffin 1 (HepPar-1) are mostly positive and negative, respectively in conventional lung cancer tissues, while HepPar-1 is often positively expressed in SMARCA4-dNSCLC, and stem cell markers (SOX2 and CD34), CK20, CK5/6 and TTF-1 are mostly negative , – especially in poorly differentiated SMARCA4-deficient tumor tissues . In addition, SMARCA4-dNSCLC expresses at least one epithelial marker, while cytokeratin markers tend to be either weak or absent in SMARCA4-dUT , . Moreover, SMARCA4-dUT are virtually negative in all lung adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma markers . Taken together, SMARCA4-dUT and SMARCA4-dNSCLC have distinctive histological and cell markers, which could be utilized for future clinical diagnosis and treatment.

## Diagnosis of lung cancer with SMARCA4 gene mutations
With the development of next-generation sequencing(NGS) technologies, a growing number of lung cancer patients have been identified with SMARCA4 mutations, which account for approximately 5%−10% of all lung cancer cases , , , , . In fact, SMARCA4 alterations may be more than currently detected, as some mutations may be missed due to technical limitation or sufficient sequencing depth . Patients with SMARCA4 mutant lung cancer are typically diagnosed at advanced stages, characterized by large, highly invasive primary tumors, and a high prevalence of distant metastasis. Most of these patients could not benefit from current therapies and face a poor prognosis, with a median survival time of less than 12 months , , , , . These challenges underscore the urgent need for early and accurate detection of SMARCA4 mutant lung cancer to improve patient outcomes. SMARCA4 deficiency should be considered when there are varying degrees of epithelial differentiation, severe inflammatory stroma and necrosis in NSCLC cancers .
In 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed that immunohistochemistry (IHC) can be used for the diagnosis of lung cancer with SMARCA4 deficiency. For poorly differentiated NSCLC, IHC testing for SMARCA4 is essential and holds significant importance in confirming diagnosis, classification, as well as selecting appropriate treatment strategies , . IHC is particularly valuable for middle-aged men and above with large thoracic masses and unclear contours . Additionally, IHC for SMARCA4 is advisable in cases lacking squamous differentiation and driver mutations, as these features may indicate underlying SMARCA4 deficiency . For these patients, early IHC testing is necessary. Actually, IHC remains the primary means of diagnosis for SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer , , . A negative IHC result for SMARCA4 is generally sufficient to confirm the diagnosis of SMARCA4 deficiency . Despite its utility, IHC has limitations and cannot detect all the types of SMARCA4 mutations. For example, type II SMARCA4 mutations, which often retain protein expression, may not be identified through IHC . In this circumstance, genetic sequencing could serve as a supplement. While genetic sequencing of SMARCA4 can identify mutations, it is not mandatory for diagnosis, particularly when IHC results are conclusive. Beyond IHC and genetic sequencing, emerging evidence suggests that18F-FDG PET/CT imaging may be used in the early diagnosis of SMARCA4-deficient lung cancers . Combining IHC, genetic sequencing and advanced imaging techniques could improve the accuracy and timeliness of SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer diagnosis. Efforts should be made to develop biomarker or screening tools for early detection of SMARCA4 mutations, particularly in high-risk population such as middle-aged male smokers. Early diagnosis could enable timely intervention and patient outcomes.

## Present and potential therapeutic strategies
SMARCA4 mutations are rarely accompanied with targetable mutations such as EGFR. Consequently, although much progress has been made in the personalized treatment of lung cancer, there are currently no standard effective treatment methods for patients with SMARCA4 mutations. A small subset of patients with SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer may benefit from surgical treatment, while more patients have a higher risk of recurrence, sometimes progressing rapidly after surgery . Therefore, the treatment of SMARCA4-mutant lung cancer remains a challenge.

## SMARCA2 inhibition
Previous studies have demonstrated that SMARCA2 could compensate for the functional defects of SMARCA4, making it an attractive therapeutic target for SMARCA4-deficient cancers – . Practically, selective inhibition of SMARCA2 has been proved to be an effective strategy for treating SMARCA4-dNSCLC, especially in case with homozygous SMARCA4 missense mutations , – . Recently, it highlighted that ferroptosis induction could enhance the efficiency of SMARCA2 antagonists in SMARCA4 mutant cancer cells . Further studies have found that the ATPase domain of SMARCA2 could serve as a synthetic lethal target for SWI/SNF mutant tumors , . SMARCA4/2 ATPase inhibitors were able to effectively inhibit the proliferation of SMARCA4 mutant tumors . However, present challenges remain as stable growth inhibition of SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers cells often requires near complete depletion of SMARCA2 .
To date, it was reported that proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) targeting SMARCA2 could be a potential option due to its high inhibition efficiency (Table  ) . Farnaby et al. developed ACBI1, a PROTAC degraders of the SMARCA2 and SMARCA4, that has potent anti-proliferative effects on SMARCA4 mutant NSCLC cells . They further achieved an orally bioavailable VHL-recruiting PROTAC through structural optimization, named ACBI2, which could selectively degrade the SMARCA2 protein to inhibit the growth of SMARCA4-deficient cancers with little drug resistance effect . Recently, selective SMARCA2 targeting PROTAC drugs such SMD-3040, A947, SMD-3236 and YD23 have also been developed. With continuous optimisation, these inhibitors have shown great selectivity and potency to specifically degrade SMARCA2 without affecting other members of the SWI/SNF complex. They all demonstrate efficient growth inhibition on SMARCA4 mutant cancers – . The development of these inhibitors provides new therapeutic options for patients with SMARCA4-deficient cancers, particularly those resistant to conventional therapies. Furthermore, combining SMARCA2 inhibition with conventional therapy for lung cancer is able to have better anti-tumor effect in SMARCA4 mutant cancers. For example, targeting SMARCA2 demonstrates synergistic efficacy when combined with inhibition of OXPHOS pathways and BCL-2 anti-apoptotic gene family in SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers . It is worth mentioning that A947 exhibits synergistic effect with MCL1 inhibitors in SMARCA4 mutant cancers . SMARCA2 targeting was also able to increase the sensitivity of SMARCA4 mutant NSCLC cells to radiotherapy . The synergistic application of YD23 and TEAD inhibitors markedly curtail the proliferation of SMARCA4 mutant cells . Despite much progress has been made in the development of SMARCA2 inhibitors, there are still no clinically relevant targeted drugs. It is reassuring that there is an ongoing multi-center Phase 1 study of the SMARCA2 degrader PRT3789 as monotherapy and in combination with docetaxel, evaluating participants with advanced or metastatic solid tumors with loss of SMARCA4 due to truncating mutation and/or deletion (Home | ClinicalTrials.gov) . This study may provide a basis for the clinical application of SMARCA2 inhibitors, and we look forward to an encouraging outcome.

 

## Cancer vulnerability
In addition to SMARCA2 inhibition, numerous studies have been conducted to explore the vulnerability of SMARCA4-deficient cancers. It has been reported that the cell cycle-regulating kinase AURKA is required in SMARCA4-dNSCLC cells, where AURKA inhibition could induce mitotic arrest and cell death . In SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer models, PARP and ATR inhibitors synergize by increasing heterochromatin and ATR dependency, exacerbating DNA damage and enhancing susceptibility to ATR inhibition . Recent studies have founded that OXPHOS inhibitors in combination with chemotherapeutic agents exert significant anti-tumor effects in SMARCA4-dNSCLC cells . Moreover, inhibitors targeting OXPHOS or glutamine metabolism showed strong antitumor activity in cancers with concomitant loss of SMARCA4/2136. With the deep understanding about chromatin remodeling in tumors, epigenetic-based therapeutic strategies have shown great potential implications. SMARCA4-mutant cancer cells show a strong dependency on the histone demethylases, and the KDM6 inhibitor GSK-J4 significant inhibits the proliferation of SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer cells . Bromodomain inhibitors also demonstrate strong anti-tumor activity against SMARCA4-dNSCLC cells . SMARCA4-dNSCLC cells were found to be sensitive to dual inhibition of EZH2 and TopoII . In conclusion, AURKA, EZH2, Topoisomerase 2 A, OXPHOS, Glutaminase, ATR and CDK4/6 inhibitors may serve as potential options for lung cancer patients harboring SMARCA4 alterations (Fig.  ; Table  ) , , , , , . 

 

## Chemotherapy
Platinum-based chemotherapy is the first-line treatment for patients with advanced lung cancer lacking targetable mutations – . SMARCA4-dUT patients hardly benefit from chemotherapy, and a small part of SMARCA4-dNSCLC patients could have extended survival time after chemotherapy, while SMARCA4-deficient lung cancers are more likely to develop resistance to conventional chemotherapy , . Decreased Ca2+ flux from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the mitochondria may be responsible for inhibiting chemotherapy-induced apoptosis , . Theoretically, promoting this Ca2+ flux is a promising approach to enhance the chemotherapy effect in SMARCA4-deficient lung cancers. It has also been reported that the histone deacetylase inhibitors could enhance the sensitivity of SMARCA4-deficient cancers to chemotherapy . A recent retrospective study demonstrated that the combination of ICIs and chemotherapy is more effective than chemotherapy alone in patients with advanced SMARCA4-deficient thoracic tumors . However, whether the combination of inhibitors and chemotherapeutic agent further enhances treatment efficacy remains unclear. The hypothesis may be validated in future prospective studies.

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)
Since the advent of immunotherapy in 2015, ICIs has been an important application for lung cancer treatment . Tumors lacking SMARCA4 typically have high mutational burdens, prompting studies on the efficacy of immunotherapy. Encouragingly, studies have shown that patients with SMARCA4-dUT or SMARCA4-dNSCLC exhibit desirable response to immunotherapy, in which the T-cell and mature B-cell related genes are up-regulated and possibly perform certain functions , , – . Even male patients with stage IV metastatic disease might have a longer median survival time after receiving immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy . Patients with type I or type II mutations could both benefit from ICIs, with type I mutation patients having better response, although there are no significant differences in terms of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) . The expression level of PD-L1 is a predictive biomarker for ICIs therapy, while studies showed the response to ICIs in SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer patients was not associated with PD-L1 expression levels , . For example, SMARCA4-dUT patients with high tumor mutation burden (TMB), regardless of PD-L1 levels, experience significantly shrinkage of both primary and metastatic lesions after treatment with ICIs, with a better prognosis , – . Inconsistently, patients with SMARCA4 mutations were reported to benefit less from ICIs, and homozygous SMARCA4 truncating mutation lung cancer patients even have worse prognosis after treatment of ICIs , . STK11/KEAP1 mutations, KRAS mutations, as well as the NRF2 signaling pathway were also reported be involved in the immunotherapy resistance for the SMARCA4 mutation lung cancer patients , – . Furthermore, SMARCA4-dNSCLC tumors were found to be highly infiltrated with FOXP3+ cells and neutrophils, which may be responsible for the limited efficacy to anti-PD-1 inhibitors , – . These findings are corroborated by pan-cancer bioinformatic analyses showing a negative correlation between SMARCA4 expression and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in multiple tumor types, alongside altered macrophage polarization and dendritic cell (DC) signatures . Recent studies utilizing an integrated experimental platform comprising humanized mouse models, syngeneic tumor grafts, and conditional knockout mouse models have systematically elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which SMARCA4 deficiency reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment through epigenetic reprogramming . SMARCA4 loss silences STING1, IL1β, and type I interferon pathways via enhancer reprogramming, impairing the recruitment and activation of cross-presenting conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s, characterized by the surface markers CD11b + CD11c + CD103+) and diminishing T cell priming and infiltration . Additionally, SMARCA4 deficiency significantly reduced M2 macrophages while preserving M1 macrophages . Notably, it was reported that lung cancer patients with SMARCA4 mutations showed significantly higher ER stress score (ERSS), which exhibited significantly higher infiltration of M1 macrophages, activated DCs, and lower infiltration of CD8 + T cells and B cells, indicating an activated tumor antigen-presenting but suppressive immune response status . In summary, the efficacy of immunotherapy remains contentious, and it is critical to identify patients who could benefit from immunotherapy. Future studies should investigate whether these tumor exhibit unique immune profiles or biomarker that could predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

## Discussion
In conclusion, SMARCA4, one of the catalytic ATPase subunits of the SWI/SNF complex, is recurrently mutated in lung cancer and defines a distinct molecular subtype. SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer is frequently associated with malignant cancer phenotypes, including poor differentiation, rapid progression, and resistance to conventional therapies, making it increasing attention in both research and clinical settings. Compared to conventional lung cancer, the occurrence rate of targetable drive mutations is lower in SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer, which limits the availability of standard targeted therapies for these patients. Clinically, SMARCA4 mutations are mainly diagnosed through IHC, which detects the loss of SMARCA4 protein expression , , . Unfortunately, lung cancer patients with SMARCA4 deficiency are often diagnosed at advanced stages. Moreover, our understanding of the cytological characteristics of SMARCA4 mutations is limited. Thus, it is important to explore the cytomorphology of SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers, facilitating its early diagnosis. Advanced genomic and proteomic techniques could be employed to identify specific biomarkers associated with SMARCA4 mutations. In addition, SMARCA4 mutations are rarely accompanied with targeted gene mutations such as EGFR, and limited studies have been conducted on exclusive alterations and co-mutations in SMARCA4 , – . Consequently, it is important to fully elucidate the molecular mechanism and achieve a comprehensive comprehension of SMARCA4 mutant lung cancers. Preclinical studies using animal models carrying SMARCA4 mutations can help to better understand the role of these mutations in tumorigenesis, progression and metastasis. These models can also serve as valuable tools for testing novel therapeutic strategies and identifying potential vulnerabilities in SMARCA4-deficient tumors.
It is currently believed that SMARCA4 mutations act mainly by destabilizing the genome, contributing to the onset and progression of lung cancers. Although the protein sequence of SMARCA2 is quite similar to that of SMARCA4, mutations in SMARCA2 are rarely seen in lung cancer and loss of functional SMARCA2 primarily occurs through epigenetic silencing mechanisms . Furthermore, SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer cells are highly reliant on SMARCA2 – . This dependency has been widely demonstrated in preclinical studies, where selective inhibition of SMARCA2 exhibits a synthetic lethal effect in SMARCA4-deficient non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells. Several small molecular inhibitors have been developed, with the PROTAC drugs targeting SMARCA2 being particularly promising. However, none of these inhibitors have been approved for clinical use, mainly due to concerns about dose-limiting toxicity and the lack of well-defined biomarkers to identify potential beneficiary populations. In the future, more studies are expected to identify subgroups of lung cancer patients with SMARCA4 mutations that could benefit from these inhibitors. Clinical trials should be designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these inhibitors in SMARCA4-mutant cancers, with a focus on optimizing dosing regimens and minimizing off-target effects. Additionally, real-world data collection could provide valuable insights into the natural history of SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer and the impact of current treatment. By establishing large-scale patient cohorts and biobanks, it is possible to systematically analyze the co-occurrence patterns of SMARCA4 mutations with other genetic alterations, such as TP53, STK11, KEAP1, and others, as well as the impact of these alterations on treatment response and prognosis. To advance personalized medicine, the development of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) or xenografts (PDXs) from SMARCA4-deficient tumors could facilitate drug screening and biomarker discovery. These models can recapitulate the heterogeneity of human tumors and provide a platform for testing novel therapeutic combinations. Understanding the mechanisms of therapy resistance in SMARCA4-deficient cancers is critical for developing second-line therapies or strategies to overcome resistance, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
