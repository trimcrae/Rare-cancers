## Abstract

## Background
Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma (MTC) is closely associated with mutations in the RET proto-oncogene, placing the activated RET protein at the center of MTC pathogenesis. Existing therapeutic solutions, primarily tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as selpercatinib, vandetanib, and cabozantinib, have shown moderate efficacy but are accompanied by increased risks of side effects and resistance. This study unveils a promising avenue using nonactin, a compound historically recognized for its antibacterial properties, targeting the G-quadruplex interactions within the RET proto-oncogene.

## Method
In this research, high-throughput screening was conducted using a luciferase reporter-based cellular assay. The MTC TT cell line was treated with nonactin for 24 and 48 h. Immunoblotting and RT-PCR were employed to measure the protein and RNA levels of RET and its downstream stream proteins. Binding to the G-Quadruplex was assessed using melting curves and Circular Dichroism. The cell cycle was analyzed using FACS, and caspase activity was measured to indicate the activation of apoptosis.

## Results
Nonactin was identified to significantly reduce luciferase activity driven by the RET promoter. A deeper exploration revealed nonactin's remarkable selectivity against tumor cell lines harboring RET mutations, effectively inducing apoptosis. Nonactin was also found to bind to the G-quadruplex region on RET.

## Conclusion
The findings highlight the compound's therapeutic potential, emphasizing its mechanism of inducing apoptosis in active mutant RET cell lines by interacting with G-quadruplex structures. This novel insight opens avenues for a potentially effective treatment for MTC, potentially bypassing the challenges associated with current TKIs.

## Introduction
Among the endocrine malignancies, thyroid carcinoma stands as a predominant type [ ]. Histologically, it presents in three primary forms: differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), accounting for approximately 90% of thyroid malignancies; anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC), which is relatively rare; And medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), representing about 3–4% of all thyroid cancer, yet accounts for 15–20% thyroid cancer-related deaths [ – ]. MTC can be broadly categorized based on its causes: most commonly as hereditary or the more aggressive sporadic MTC [ , ]. The growth of tumors in both sporadic and hereditary MTC is primarily driven by somatic mutations and germline mutations in the RET “REarranged during Transfection” proto-oncogene, respectively [ ].
The RET gene, located on chromosome 10q11.2, encodes a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor. When mutated in MTC, it constantly activates downstream signals like MEK/ERK pathway and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, promoting cell proliferation, invasion, and survival [ , ]. Therefore, RET has become a vital target in MTC treatment, particularly using tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy. Numerous studies highlight the importance of targeting mutated RET [ – ]. Drugs like cabozantinib and vandetanib have shown some effectiveness in managing metastatic MTC, and recently, the FDA approved selpercatinib (Loxo 292) for various RET-related cancers [ – ]. However, resistance to TKI treatments remains a significant challenge, especially in advanced MTC.
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors frequently induce adverse events in a significant proportion of patients, including hypertension, gastrointestinal disturbances, thyroid dysfunctions, fatigue, and weight reduction [ , ]. These side effects might necessitate lowering the dose, pausing treatment, or even stopping it entirely [ , ]. Given that the current medications often fail to produce the desired results, there's a pressing need for new treatments that can bypass resistance and lessen the side effects associated with existing TKIs. Yet, due to the rising costs associated with developing new drugs, alternative treatments for MTC that are resistant to treatment remain unavailable. As a solution, we've investigated drug repurposing as a potential method for finding other MTC treatments.
Research on the transcriptional regulation of the RET proto-oncogene in a human MTC cell line indicates that the adjacent upstream region of the human RET gene lacks a TATA box and is predominantly G-rich [ , ]. Many TATA-less mammalian genes, such as Hmga2, EGF-R, c-Myc, and others, have similar G-rich sections in their proximal promoter areas [ ]. These G-rich regions are thought to be structurally flexible, adopting various non-B-DNA formations like single-strands, hairpins, or slipped helices under physiological conditions [ , ]. Notably, in the presence of monovalent cations like Na+ and K+, G-rich sequences can form diverse G-quadruplex structures made up of two or more G-tetrads [ ]. Structural studies have shown these G-quadruplexes to be varied and stable under physiological conditions [ , ]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests the in vivo presence of G-quadruplexes [ , ].
To identify potential therapeutic agents for MTC, we investigated RET transcription inhibitors using a luciferase reporter-based cellular assay. Our high-throughput screening for these inhibitors employed a HEK293 cell line designed to express the firefly luciferase gene under RET promoter control, a methodology detailed in published research [ , ]. These previous investigations confirmed the efficiency of this cell-based approach for pinpointing RET transcription inhibitors. For this study, we employed the 96-well format of the luciferase reporter assay to evaluate a drug collection from the NCI Diversity set, which included various types of drugs. As highlighted in our report, we found that nonactin reduced the luciferase activity driven by the RET promoter.
Nonactin is a naturally occurring macrotetrolide isolated from Streptomyces griseus, which is composed of nonactinic acid, known for its antibiotic [ ]. Its distinct structure comprises tetra-furan rings, which allow for the creation of multiple synthetic analogs with varying pharmacokinetic properties, including alternative routes of administration [ , ]. Nonactin functions at sub-lethal concentrations as an antibiotic, acting as a potassium ion transporter, often referred to as a monovalent cation ionophore [ ]. This interferes with cell membrane ion channels by forming complexes with potassium cations, which then transport across the lipophilic membrane of the bacteria's mitochondria. This process creates pores, altering the K+ permeability and disrupting the electrochemical gradient vital for energy production and metabolite transport, leading to pronounced bacterial cell depolarization and death. The antibacterial spectrum of nonactin is narrow, as it primarily targets gram-positive bacteria, especially S. aureus and E. faecalis, which have developed resistance against vancomycin. Nonactin has demonstrated antitumor properties against mammalian cell lines in laboratory conditions and has shown efficacy in preclinical models, including mouse studies, where related compounds reduced tumor growth by approximately 40–50% over a treatment period, with no significant toxicity observed [ , ].
In our ongoing research, we have discovered that nonactin possesses strong selectivity against tumor cell lines with RET mutations, inducing apoptosis in in vitro models. Additionally, we shed light on the specific mechanism through which nonactin induces apoptosis in cell lines with active mutant RET.

## Method and materials

## Chemical
Nonactin was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, United States) (#N2286). Nonactin was dissolved in DMSO at a final concentration of 10 mg/ml.

## Materials
The RET-WT oligonucleotide labeled with 5’-FAM (5’- AGCGGGTAGGGGCGGGGCGGGGCGGGGGCGG -3’) and the RET-MT1 oligo (5’- AGCGGGTAGGAGCGGAGCGGGGCGGGGGCGG-3’) were obtained from Sigma Genosys (Woodlands, TX, USA).

## Cell culture and media
The cell lines TT, TPC1, and Nthy-ori 3–1 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The MTC-derived cell lines TT was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Cellgro; Corning) enriched with 15% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). The Nthy-ori 3–1 line, an immortalized normal human thyroid epithelial cell was grown in RPMI-1640 and Ham's F12 medium, supplemented with 9% FBS. TPC1 was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 9% FBS. Meanwhile, the isogenic cell lines HEK293-WT and HEK293-MT1, which have a luciferase reporter gene integrated, were also utilized. The genes in HEK293-WT and HEK293-MT1 were under the influence of either the wild-type or G4-mutated RET promoter sequence, as previously described in our studies [ , ]. Both HEK293-WT and HEK293-MT1 were cultivated in DMEM supplemented with 9% FBS.
All cell lines were maintained under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Post-acquisition from the cell bank, the lines were utilized within a 7-month timeframe. Regular screenings for mycoplasma contamination were conducted, and the authenticity of the cell lines was confirmed using STR profiling.

## Luciferase assay for cell-based screening
The isogenic HEK293-WT and HEK293-MT1 cells were plated in a 96-well format, with a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well. Subsequently, cells were treated with nonactin and were incubated for up to 24 h. For the lysis of cells, 25 μL of passive lysis buffer (#E6110 Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was utilized. The expression levels of luciferase were then measured using the Steady-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the provided manufacturer's guidelines. A DMSO-only treatment served as the negative control.

## Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy analysis
The RET-WT oligonucleotide, at a concentration of 5 μM, underwent G-quadruplex structuring in a Tris–HCl buffer solution (20 mM, pH 7.6) combined with 25 mM KCl. This process involved denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by a gradual return to room temperature. CD spectra relating to temperature and wavelength were captured on a Jasco-810 spectrophotometer (Easton, MD, USA) with a 1 mm path-length quartz cell. The instrument settings were a scanning speed of 100 nm/min, a response duration of 1 s, and a wavelength interval of 230–330 nm. This procedure, increasing the temperature from 20 °C to 90 °C, follows a previously outlined method [ ]. The Tm was calculated by observing the molar ellipticity at 262 nm as the temperature rose from 20 °C to 90 °C, increasing at a rate of 1 °C per minute.

## Reverse transcription PCR analysis of the RET mRNA in MTC cells
Total RNA was extracted from the TT and TPC1 cells utilizing the RNeasy MiniQIAcube kit (QIAGEN, Redwood city, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was subjected to reverse-transcription using the oligo(dT) 18 primer with QuantiTect reverse-transcription kit (QIAGEN) to generate single-stranded cDNA. The primers used for RT-PCR were as follows: RET forward: (5’-GCAGCATTGTTGGGGGACA-3’) and RET reverse (5’ CACCGGAAGAGGAGTAGCTG-30); Rpl9 forward (5’ CTGAAGGGACGCACAGTTAT-3’) and Rpl9 reverse (5’- ACGGTAGCCAGTTCCTTTCT-3’); RET/PTC1 forward (5’—GTCGGGGGGCATTGTCATCT −3’) and RET/PTC1 reverse (5’ – AAGTTCTTCCGAGGGAATTC – 3’). The PCR reactions involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 33, 23 and 34 cycles for RET, Rpl9 and RET/PTC1, respectively, at 95 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s on a GeneAmp PCR system 9600 (Perkinelmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR products were analyzed on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

## Western blot
Cells were lysed to obtain whole-cell protein lysates utilizing a 1% CHAPS lysis buffer, supplemented with 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 0.15 M NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, and the Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). These proteins were then resolved on either 4–12% or 4–20% gradient polyacrylamide SDS-PAGE, from Thermo Fisher, which was described earlier [ ]. The primary antibodies were used as follow: anti-RET (#3220), mTOR (#2983), pmTOR (#5536), Cyclin E1 (ab119719), H2AX (#9718), Anti-PARP1 (ab191217), Rb (#9309), p-Rb (#8516S), pAkt (#9271), and Akt (#9272) from Cell Signaling, Beverly, MA, USA; anti-cyclin E (sc-247), E2F1 (sc-56661), cyclin D (sc-20044), and BCL-2 (sc-509), from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Additionally, anti-actin (ab8227) was purchased from Abcam.
As secondary antibodies, both mouse and rabbit IgG linked with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) from BioRad were used. The detection phase involved the use of an enhanced chemiluminescence kit, provided by Santa Cruz Biotechnology.

## Flow cytometric analysis
For cell cycle analysis, TT cells were processed as previously described [ , ]. First, cells underwent trypsinization and were subsequently rinsed using cold 1X PBS before fixation in 70% ethanol, stored at -20 °C for a duration of up to 24 h. Post-fixation, the cell samples were again rinsed using chilled 1X PBS. This was followed by an incubation step with RNaseA (200 μg/ml) combined with propidium iodide PI (25 μg/ml) at 37 °C, lasting for approximately 30 min. Finally, the DNA fluorescence patterns were evaluated using a Coulter Epics Profile flow cytometer. A cell-cycle distribution histogram was subsequently produced for every sample.

## Cell viability assay
MTC, TPC1 and N-thy-Ori-3–1 cells were plated at 7,500 cells per well in a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. Following this, they were exposed to various concentrations of nonactin for up to 72 h. To evaluate cell viability, a solution of 0.33 mg/mL MTS dye mixed with 200 µg/mL phenazine methosulfate (PMS) was used. Absorbance readings were taken at 590 nm using the Synergy HT Multi-detection Microplate Reader from BioTek (Winooski, VT, USA).

## Assessment of caspase-3 activity
Caspase-3 activity in TT cells was evaluated utilizing the ApoAlert Caspase Fluorescent Assay kit (Clonetech Laboratory, Mountain View, CA, USA), adhering to the provided instructions. In essence, TT cells were first rinsed with PBS and subsequently lysed with the designated cell lysis buffer. These lysates were combined with the caspase-3 specific substrate, DEVD, tagged with the fluorophore 7-amino-4-trifluromethyl coumarin (AFC), all in the presence of the caspase-3 assay buffer. The proteolytic action of caspase-3 results in the liberation of AFC, leading to a spike in fluorescence. This fluorescence was captured using the Synergy HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader from BioTek (Winooski, VT, USA), setting the excitation at 395 nm and emission at 509 nm.

## Statistical analysis
The results from a minimum of three independent experiments are presented as the mean ± SEM. To compare means across multiple treatment conditions and identify significant variances, we conducted statistical evaluations employing either one-way ANOVA with subsequent Tukey’s post hoc assessment or two-way ANOVA complemented by Bonferroni post hoc analysis. Where pertinent, the student’s t-test was utilized to differentiate significant variances between two distinct treatment sets. A p-value less than 0.05 was regarded as indicative of significant differences between groups. The data analyses were executed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

## Results

## In vitrointeraction between nonactin and the RET G-quadruplex
The effect of nonactin on RET gene transcription was explored using a luciferase reporter assay with HEK 293-RET cells, a method previously described [ , ]. The reporter cell line, 293-RET, was created by stably introducing the luciferase reporter gene controlled by the RET wild-type (WT) promoter into HEK 293 cells (Fig.  A). When HEK 293-RET cells were treated with varying concentrations of nonactin for 24 h, there was a notable decrease in basic luciferase expression, as shown in Fig.  B. Another cell line HEK 293 RET-MT1 was constructed and used to determine whether nonactin reduces RET gene expression by stabilizing its G-quadruplex. In this line, the luciferase reporter gene expression is governed by G4 knockout (KO) mutant RET promoter regions (Fig.  A), as described in reference [ ].
 It has been reported that the G-quadruplex structure plays a role in the downregulation of the RET gene transcription, and this effect can be enhanced by stabilizing this structure using small molecules [ , ]. To further our research, we wanted to investigate the interaction between nonactin and the G-quadruplex structures in vitro. To confirm the stabilization of the RET G-quadruplex by nonactin, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopic analysis was conducted for the RET-WT sequence both without and with nonactin. Initially, we assessed whether the binding of nonactin impacts the structural configuration of the RET G-quadruplex by observing the CD spectra without and with increasing amounts of nonactin (Fig.  C). CD spectroscopy is commonly used to determine the strand orientation of a G-quadruplex structure, especially to differentiate between parallel and antiparallel G-quadruplex configurations. The parallel structures typically exhibit positive peaks at ~ 260 nm, while the antiparallel structures show positive peaks near ~ 295 nm. As illustrated in Fig.  C, the titration of RET-WT with nonactin did not result in any noticeable shift in the positive or negative peaks at approximately 262 nm and 240 nm in the CD spectra when 25 mM K+ was present. This indicates that there was no alteration in the folding pattern.
To understand whether the binding of nonactin offers stability to the G-quadruplex structure, we observed the CD spectra without and with nonactin (5 equivalents) at varying temperatures. As illustrated in Fig.  D, the intensity of the peak at 262 nm gradually diminished as the temperature ranged from 20 °C to 90 °C, with a 50% reduction seen at approximately 72 °C. This temperature is associated with its melting temperature (Tm) (Fig.  D, E). However, in the presence of nonactin (5 Equivalents), the Tm rose to approximately 80.5 °C (Fig.  E), which implies that the RET G-quadruplex is indeed stabilized by nonactin. This outcome compellingly indicates that the formation of G-quadruplex structures within the G-rich region plays a pivotal role in the transcriptional suppressive action of nonactin on the RET promoter.

## Selective suppression of RET in TT cells by nonactin
After our in vitro findings which indicated that nonactin may interact with the G-quadruplex structure present in the RET promoter region, we next investigated the impact of nonactin on RET expression using an in vitro cell-based analysis. In this study, TT MTC cells harboring RETC634W were because RET proto-oncogene expression is controlled by the G-quadruplex structure [ , ].
As shown in Fig.  A, after treating TT cells with concentrations of 0.12, 0.25, and 0.5 µM of nonactin for 24 h, there was a reduction in RET protein levels by 20%, 50%, and 70%, respectively, when compared to the control. Moreover, a pronounced decline in RET protein levels was observed in TT cells incubated with nonactin for 48 h compared to those treated for 24 h (Fig.  A). Next, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels for the RET gene in MTC TT cells following treatment with nonactin. Figure  B illustrates that nonactin reduced the RET mRNA expression in TT cells in a dose-dependent manner after treatments for 24 and 48 h.
 To understand whether the transcriptional suppression effect of nonactin in MTC TT cells is specific to the RET promoter region, we examined the papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) TPC1 cell line. In TPC1 cells, the tyrosine kinase domain of RET combines with the 5′-region of the CCD6 gene due to chromosomal rearrangement, leading to a hybrid RET/PTC1 fusion protein [ – ]. Therefore, the transcriptional activation of the RET/PTC1 gene in TPC1 cells is under the control of the CCD6 gene promoter region. However, even when exposed to a varying concentration of nonactin for 48 h, there was no considerable reduction in the expression of RET/PTC1 mRNA and protein in TPC1 cells. This suggests that nonactin's inhibitory action can be associated with the RET promoter region. We have also utilized MZ-CRC 1 that harbors M918T mutation. The effect of nonactin was observed in MZCRC-1 cells, although it was less pronounced compared to TT cells. This may be attributed to the critical role of G-quadruplex structures in mediating nonactin's effect (Fig. S1).

## Impact of nonactin on the RET signaling pathway
Several studies have highlighted that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is a pivotal signaling route activated by oncogenic RET. This activation subsequently increases the survival of cancer cells through the phosphorylation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) protein [ , – ]. Therefore, we assessed the influence of nonactin on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in TT cells. As depicted in Fig.  A, nonactin diminished the levels of pmTOR and pAKT, following a 24-h treatment period. This indicates that the decline in the expression of downstream targets such as pmTOR and pAKT due to nonactin can be attributed to its impact on RET expression in TT cells.
 Following our previous observations, we extended our study to examine the effects of nonactin on the TPC1 cell line. Notably, in this context, nonactin did not reduce the levels of pmTOR and pAKT after 24-h of treatment with nonactin (Fig.  B). From these results, it becomes evident that the decrease observed in TT cells is primarily linked to the suppression of oncogenic RET activity by nonactin. Therefore, the changes in pmTOR and pAKT levels are more likely attributed to nonactin's impact on RET signaling rather than a direct interaction with these proteins.
To ensure that the effects of nonactin were specific to TT cells and not normal noncancerous human cells, we included primary human thyroid (N-Thyi-Ori 3–1) as a control cell line. We used the MTT assay to measure growth inhibition, treating various cell lines with different concentrations of nonactin for 72-h. The results in Fig.  C show that TT cells had a clear decrease in growth with increasing nonactin doses, reaching an IC50 value of 0.12 µM. We also tested nonactin on TPC1 and N-Thy-Ori 3–1 cells. The growth of those cell lines was less affected by nonactin. To attain comparable growth inhibition in non-TT cells, a concentration of nonactin 3–5 times greater than that used for TT cells was needed.

## Nonactin's effect on cell cycle progression in TT cells
The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is recognized for promoting cancer cell proliferation by driving the transition from the G1 to S phase in cell cycle progression. This transition is facilitated through the activation of the cell cycle regulator protein, such as cyclin D1 [ , ]. Upon evaluating the influence of nonactin on TT cells, Fig.  A reveals that nonactin led to a decreased expression of cyclin D1 protein, as determined by western blotting after a 24-h treatment with 0.25 µM.
 To examine a deeper understanding of the role of the reduced levels of cyclin D1 in modulating cell-cycle progression, flow cytometry analyses were completed in TT cells with or without nonactin (0.25 µM) after a 24-h treatment. As illustrated in Fig.  B, treating TT cells with nonactin resulted in a notable accumulation of cells in the G1 phase within the 24-h period. Subsequent observations at 24 h post nonactin treatment exhibited a 15–17% elevation in the proportion of cells in the G1 phase compared to the control group. This was paralleled by a diminished count of cells in the S and G2 phases. Confirming this cell cycle arrest, an evaluation of crucial cell cycle regulatory proteins—such as Cyclin D, Cyclin E, E2F, p-Rb, and Rb—was conducted in TT cells following a 24-h treatment with nonactin. The results demonstrated a reduction in the levels of these proteins, as evidenced by western blot (Fig.  A). The impact of nonactin on the TPC1 cell cycle was minimal and not significant, reinforcing the crucial role of G-quadruplex structures in mediating nonactin's effect (Fig. S2).

## Nonactin's induces apoptosis in TT cells
The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is also involved in modulating BCL2 family proteins, which are considered crucial targets for anti-apoptotic mechanism [ , ]. When the PI3K/AKT pathway is activated, it results in elevated expression of BCL2 and BCLxL, and a reduction in the expression of Bad and Bax. This pathway's modulation extends to influencing apoptosis further, as evidenced by past research showing that the activated PI3K/AKT pathway enhances the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2, subsequently inhibiting the activation of caspase 3 [ ]. In our study, we found that nonactin decreased anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 expression in TT cells after 24-h treatment (Fig.  A). This change was associated with a tenfold increase in caspase-3 activity compared to controls (Fig.  B). It is important to highlight that after a 24-h treatment with nonactin, there was an observed increase in H2AX and cleaved PARP proteins, indicating the induction of DNA damage in TT cells (Fig.  A).
 

## Discussion
Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is intricately associated with germline mutations in the RET proto-oncogene, placing the activated RET protein at the epicenter of MTC pathology [ – ]. This unique vulnerability of MTC offers an attractive therapeutic target, and extensive research efforts have attempted to exploit this weakness [ – ].
Among the multifaceted therapeutic modalities tested against RET-driven MTC, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, particularly selpercatinib, vandetanib and cabozantinib, have emerged as frontrunners [ – ]. Both of these agents, by virtue of their broad-spectrum action against VEGFR, EGFR, MET, and RET, have gained regulatory approval for advanced MTC cases. Yet, their long-term use is shadowed by two primary concerns: the inevitable rise of drug resistance and the onset of debilitating side effects [ – ].
In the therapeutic landscape, the use of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that specifically target RET expression presents a promising avenue of intervention. Promising results were reported when the TT cell line was transfected with RET siRNA, demonstrating both in vitro anti-proliferative effects and in vivo tumor growth inhibition [ , ]. However, the road to the clinical application of siRNAs is fraught with challenges. Their intrinsic physicochemical properties, namely their large size and negative charge, hinder cellular uptake. Additionally, their extracellular stability is compromised due to susceptibility to enzymatic degradation [ , ].
Recognizing these challenges, our study pivots an innovative approach: targeting the RET proto-oncogene transcription using nonactin. We believe that harnessing small molecule inhibitors could offer enhanced stability, better cellular penetration, and fewer off-target effects.
The stabilization of the formation of the G-quadruplex structure in the RET gene promoter region induces a transcriptional inhibitory effect. To harness this property, we aimed to stabilize this secondary structure using nonactin, consequently suppressing RET gene expression. Through rigorous in vitro biochemical and cell-based assays, nonactin emerged as a promising molecule capable of binding to and stabilizing the RET G-quadruplex in the TT MTC cell line. Notably, the stabilization of the G-quadruplex by nonactin led to a marked inhibition of the RET promoter activity. This inhibitory effect was evidenced by a decrease in luciferase expression, which is under the regulatory control of the RET promoter region in the HEK 293 RET WT cell line (Fig.  A).
In this study, we explored how nonactin curtails the proliferation of TT cells by suppressing RET expression. Oncogenic RET activation drives cell proliferation and survival by initiating various intracellular signaling cascades. In a precedent study, Drosten et al. detailed the downstream signaling pathways tied to RET that are critical for tumor progression and growth in TT cells [ , ]. By utilizing an adenoviral vector that expressed a truncated dominant-negative RET protein, lacking the intracellular tyrosine kinase domain, they halted RET phosphorylation and activation in TT cells. This intervention led to the suppression of the Raf/MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, signifying their central role in RET-driven transformation [ , ]. Echoing their findings, our research indicates that by inhibiting RET expression in TT cells with nonactin, the phosphorylation of AKT, and mTOR is reduced (Fig.  A). Consequently, there was a marked reduction in the expression of cyclin D1, Cyclin E, and BCL2, all of which are orchestrated by the Raf/MEK/ERK and PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathways (Fig.  A and A). Additionally, our observations reveal a halt in the cell cycle at the G1 phase.
The critical aspect of our investigation was to elucidate whether the formation of the G-quadruplex structure on the proximal promoter region of the RET gene is essential for mediating nonactin's inhibitory effects. To delve deeper into this matter, we incorporated HEK 293 RET MT1 and the papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) TPC1 cells into our experimental framework. While both MTC and PTC are characterized by the constitutive activation of the RET protein in a ligand-independent manner, the underlying genetic triggers differ [ , , – ]. In PTC, the gain-of-function mutation arises from a chromosomal rearrangement; specifically, the fusion of the RET kinase domain coding region with the 5′ terminal of the coiled-coil domain-containing gene 6 (CCD6) located at chromosome 10q11.2. This rearrangement produces a truncated fusion protein, RET/PTC1. Here, the dimerization domain of the CCD6 gene facilitates the formation of RET homodimers, setting the stage for tyrosine residue phosphorylation and subsequent activation of downstream signaling pathways [ ]. A pivotal distinction lies in the fact that due to the chromosomal inversion; the RET/PTC gene is regulated by the CCD6 promoter region. This promoter lacks the GC box region, rendering it incapable of forming the G-quadruplex structure [ ]. This absence means that nonactin is unable to bind to a G-quadruplex since it simply not presents in this RET/PTC. Our experimental results corroborated this hypothesis.
In the presence of nonactin, the RET/PTC1 mRNA and protein levels remained stable, underscoring the compound's specificity for MTC. This specificity arises from the G-quadruplex forming sequence found exclusively in the RET promoter. Furthermore, the impact of nonactin on the downstream functions of TPC1 was minimal, in stark contrast to its pronounced anti-proliferative effects in the TT MTC cell line (Figs.  and ).
Nonactin is known to interact with specific cations such as potassium and sodium, subsequently promoting apoptosis. Within the Wnt signaling pathway, β-catenin plays a crucial role, in influencing cell growth, survival, and several other cellular processes [ , ]. Many tumors, including approximately 10% of specific colon cancers and 20% of liver cancers, exhibit mutations in β-catenin [ , ]. It has been reported that nonactin triggers apoptosis in cells bearing mutated β-catenin, but not in its wild type. However, in medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), the function of β-catenin differs from its role in colon cancer and is comparatively less dominant [ ]. In our research, after 24 h of exposure to nonactin, we observed a pronounced elevation in caspase 3/7 activity in MTC cells, signaling the initiation of apoptosis (Fig.  B). This apoptotic effect is largely attributed to the binding and inhibition of the RET proto-oncogene, rather than the action on β-catenin.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our findings collectively present compelling evidence that nonactin displays significant antitumor activity against MTC, chiefly through the downregulation of RET expression. Moreover, our results validate the notion that specific G-quadruplex structures can manifest within the RET promoter region. This suggests a mechanism where RET gene transcription could be potentially regulated via ligand-mediated stabilization of the G-quadruplex. This finding reinforces the novel concept that G-quadruplex structures might play crucial roles in vivo, opening doors to new approaches in rational drug design.
