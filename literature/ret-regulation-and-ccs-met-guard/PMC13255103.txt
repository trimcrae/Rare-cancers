## Abstract
SMARCA4 is an ATPase subunit in the switch/sucrose non-fermentable complex (SWI/SNF), a chromatin-remodeling complex. SMARCA4 provides energy for the SWI/SNF complex to regulate gene expression through chromatin structural rearrangement and transcription regulation. In SMARCA4-deficient cancers, its paralog SMARCA2 is sometimes upregulated and can serve as a pharmacological target. Of the 20–25% of cancers identified to have a mutation in a gene encoding a protein for the SWI/SNF complex, 15% of these alterations include SMARCA4 alterations. Therefore, SMARCA4-directed therapies are under investigation in clinical trials and are a promising new strategy for treating patients with a SMARCA4-mutated cancer. Currently, all published clinical trials of SMARCA4-directed therapies are for orally bioavailable small-molecule inhibitors. Clinical trials without results include small-molecule inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and a bifunctional MabPair product. The aim of this literature review was to summarize the current SMARCA4-directed therapies in oncology clinical trials.
Keywords: SMARCA4, clinical trials, cancer, review article

## INTRODUCTION
SMARCA4, also known as transcriptional activator BRG1, codes for one of two ATPase subunits in the switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI/SNF) complex, a chromatin-remodeling complex.[ ] The SWI/SNF complex plays a key role in regulating gene expression through chromatin structural rearrangement and transcription regulation ( ). Approximately 20–25% of cancers are found to have a loss-of-function mutation in the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, an epigenetic regulator, making it a desirable target for cancer therapy.[ , ] There are four main SWI/SNF subunits that are commonly mutated, including SMARCA4, ARID1A, SMARCB1, and SS18.[ ] SMARCA4 mutations and directed therapies will be discussed in this review.
 SMARC4 is a part of the SMARC family, including SMARCA2 (BRM), SMARCB1, SMARCC1, SMARCC2, SMARCD1, SMARCD2, SMARCD3, and SMARCE1.[ ] SMARCA4 is located on chromosome 19p.13.2, whereas the other members of the SMARC family are located at different chromosomal loci.[ , ] SMARCA4 encodes a tumor suppressor protein that has multiple anti-tumorigenic activities. Within the SWI/SNF complex, SMARCA4 directly modulates the architecture of chromatin by using ATP to provide energy for how tightly the DNA is coiled around a histone protein that forms chromatin. As such, regulating the SWI/SNF complex activity influences which genes are transcribed versus not transcribed. This allows SMARCA4 to have a variety of functions, including regulating the transcriptional activity of genes involved in DNA replication, DNA repair, and the modulation of cell growth, division, and differentiation ( ).[ ] Additionally, embryologically, SMARCA4 plays a key role in the growth of smooth muscle of the heart and gastrointestinal tract, is required for the transcription activation in the Sonic hedgehog pathway, can bind to BRCA1, and regulates the expression of CD44 (a transmembrane, tumorigenic, glycoprotein).[ ]
SMARCA4 is one of the more frequently mutated chromatin remodeling ATPases in cancer. Of the 20–25% of cancers identified to have a SWI/SNF alteration, 15% of these alterations include SMARCA4 alterations.[ ] Approximately 75–100% of small cell carcinomas of the ovary, and 10% of non–small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) exhibit SMARCA4 alterations.[ , ] SMARCA4 alterations have also been observed in common solid tumors such as colon adenocarcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, and invasive breast ductal carcinoma, and, in rare cancers, such as rhabdoid tumors, thoracic sarcomas, uterine sarcoma, and undifferentiated tumors.[ , , ] When SMARCA4 is a germline mutation, such as in rhabdoid tumor predisposition syndrome 2 or Coffin-Siris Syndrome, patients carry a higher risk of developing secondary primary tumors.[ ] Genetic alterations include class 1 mutations (truncating mutations, fusions, and homozygous deletions) and class 2 mutations (missense mutations). SMARCA4 class 1 mutations result in loss-of-function, whereas class 2 mutations impair ATPase activity required for chromatin remodeling and may exert dominant-negative effects, rather than gain-of-function effects, which are rare.[ ] In SMARCA4-deficient cancers, its paralog SMARCA2 is sometimes upregulated or may serve as the remaining catalytic ATPase subunit of the SWI/SNF complex, making tumor cells reliant on SMARCA2-mediated chromatin remodeling for survival. Because this dependency is not present in normal cells that retain a functional SMARCA4, SMARCA2 is an attractive therapeutic target.[ , ]
Patients with a SMARCA4 alteration tend to have a poorer prognosis. In SMARCA4-deficient NSCLC, approximately 83% of patients are diagnosed with a stage IV cancer at the time of discovery and have a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 30 days.[ ] This is consistent with findings that SMARCA4-biallelic–mutated tumors have a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) and higher fraction of genome alterations (FGA) (TMB = 10.5, FGA = 0.53; p < 0.001) compared with the monoallelic (TMB = 6.1, FGA = 0.3; p < 0.01) and wild type (TMB = 4.4, FGA = 0.47; p < 0.01).[ ] Additionally, in most small cell carcinomas of the ovary, there is the presence of a SMARCA4-biallelic–mutation, which results in increased expression of enhancer of zeste homolog-2 (EZH2).[ ] When EZH2 is expressed in the greater than 29th percentile, the median PFS is significantly less when compared with the tumors with a less than 29th percentile expression of EZH2 (median PFS of 24 vs 90 months, p = 0.025).[ ]
SMARCA4 alterations have potential predictive value. In a retrospective study of 2689 patients diagnosed with NSCLC and treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, patients with a SMARCA4 mutation had a significantly shorter median overall survival (OS) of 15.6 months compared with the patients with the wild-type SMARCA4 who had a median OS of 25 months (hazard ratio = 0.64, 95% CI, 0.53–0.78, p < 0.001).[ ] All other SWI/SNF genomic alterations did not exhibit a significant difference in median OS compared with their wild-type counterparts. This finding challenged a previous retrospective study that found SMARCA4 alterations in NSCLC treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors had an improved outcome (p = 0.01).[ ] Furthermore, this study found that class 1 SMARCA4 alterations had a higher overall response rate when treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors than class 2 alterations or wild-type tumors (p = 0.027); however, there was no difference in PFS (p = 0.74) or OS (p = 0.35).[ ]
Multiple preclinical studies targeting SMARCA4 directly or indirectly have shown promising antitumor activity. A preclinical study of the selective SMARCA2 degrader, YD23, demonstrated inhibition of tumor growth in cell lines of SMARCA4-mutant lung cancer.[ ] Another preclinical study, which studied the selective BRM inhibitor, LY4050784, in BRG1-mutant xenografts, demonstrated significant antitumor activity across all dose levels with minimal adverse effects.[ ] LY4050784 also had a greater than 30-fold selectivity for BRM over BRG1.[ ] A preclinical study investigated the effects of PRT3789, a selective SMARCA2 degrader, and found robust inhibition of cell proliferation in subcutaneous cell-line–derived xenograft models of SMARCA4-deleted NSCLC in a concentration-dependent manner.[ ] Additionally, the SWI/SNF complex subunits were disrupted. There was no effect on cancer growth in the SMARCA4 wild-type cancers. Another SMARCA2 inhibitor, PRT7732, with more than 10,000 times selectivity for SMARCA2 over SMARCA4, was studied in a SMARCA4-deficient lung cancer xenograft murine model.[ ] This preclinical study showed robust response rates 24 hours after dosing.[ ]
Given the central role of SMARCA4 in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation, loss of its function results in widespread epigenetic dysregulation, altered gene expression, and changes in tumor-immune interactions.[ ] These disruptions create multiple biologically relevant vulnerabilities that can be therapeutically exploited.[ , , ] Emerging strategies targeting SMARCA4-deficient tumors have focused on both direct and indirect modulation of the SWI/SNF complex and its downstream effects.[ , , ] These approaches include paralog dependency targeting through SMARCA2 degradation, inhibition of the BAF chromatin-remodeling complex, epigenetic therapies such as EZH2 inhibition, and immune checkpoint blockade.[ ] Collectively, these therapies represent a mechanistically driven approach to targeting the consequences of SWI/SNF deficiency and are currently being investigated across preclinical[ , , ] and clinical studies[ , , , , , ] (summarized in and ).
 SMARCA4 and respective molecular counterparts, such as SMARCA2, are clinically relevant markers for risk-stratifying patients and engineering target-specific treatment. In this clinical review, we summarize emerging therapeutic strategies targeting these vulnerabilities in the currently available clinical trials of SMARCA4-directed treatment with published clinical trial results[ , , ] ( ) and without published clinical trial results[ , , , , , ] ( ). A literature search was conducted between November 1 and November 30, 2025, using the Medical Subject Headings term “SMARCA4 protein, human.” Relevant studies were identified from peer-reviewed publications, publicly available abstracts presented at major oncology conferences (European Society for Medical Oncology, American Society of Clinical Oncology, American Association for Cancer Research), and clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Supplementary searches were also performed using Google Scholar to identify additional relevant publications and conference materials. Articles describing SMARCA4 biology, therapeutic targeting strategies, and clinical trials were included in the review.

## CLINICAL TRIALS WITH PUBLISHED RESULTS

## PRT3789
PRT3789[16] (Prelude Therapeutics Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) is a SMARCA2 degrader delivered as a weekly intravenous infusion. The study design included an escalation study with backfill cohorts added at active doses. Eligible patients included those with a solid tumor and any SMARCA4 mutation (loss-of-function or missense). Forty patients were enrolled in the trial as of March 7, 2024 (NSCLC, n =18; pancreatic, n = 5; breast, n = 3; esophageal, n = 2; other, n = 12). Of the patient population, 55% had a loss-of-function mutation. Initial dosing started at 24 mg and was increased through 6 levels to a maximum of 212 mg. There were no reported dose-limiting toxicities or study drug-related serious adverse events reported. The most common adverse events reported included nausea (25%), constipation (17.5%), dyspnea (17.5%), decreased appetite (15%), fatigue (15%), and anemia (12.5%). The SMARCA2 levels had an inverse relationship to the drug dose; there were minimal effects on SMARCA4 levels. The clinical activity of PRT3789 included partial responses and prolonged stable disease in patients with esophageal cancer and NSCLC. Further development of PRT3789 will be paused, per a Prelude press release on August 14, 2025, due to underwhelming phase 1 results. Instead, the focus will shift to PRT7732, a promising oral bioavailable SMARCA2 degrader.[ ]

## FHD-286
FHD-286[ ] (Foghorn Therapeutics Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) is an orally administered compound that selectively inhibits the ATPase components of the BAF complex (SMARCA4/BRG1 and SMARCA2/BRM). In the first-in-human, dose-escalation phase 1 trial, two dosing regimens were investigated. In the daily dosing regimen, the initial dose was 2.5 mg daily and increased incrementally to 10 mg daily. In the intermittent dosing regimen, the medication was dosed 1-week-on/1-week-off at either 10 or 15 mg. The eligible patient population included metastatic uveal melanoma. This study did not use a predictive biomarker test, and the enrolled patients were not selected based on the presence of a SMARCA4 alteration. As of December 31, 2022, a total of 52 patients had received at least one dose of FHD-286. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 83% of patients and included dysgeusia (49%), dry mouth (25%), fatigue (31%), aspartate aminotransferase elevation (29%), nausea/vomiting (29%), and rash (25%). Adverse events graded 3 or higher occurred in 25% of patients and included anemia, asthenia, alkaline phosphatase elevation, hypokalemia, muscular weakness, and rash. At the 7.5-mg daily dose level, dose-limiting toxicities were observed in 3 patients, including 1 with grade 3 keratitis and 2 with a grade 3 rash. The adverse reactions improved with dose interruption. There were no reported treatment-related deaths. A partial response was seen in 1 patient assigned to the 10 mg daily dosing, and prolonged stable disease and reductions in tumor burden were observed across dose levels. This medication’s pharmacokinetics revealed accumulation with daily dosing and have a direct relationship with increasing dose level.

## Tazemetostat
Tazemetostat (Ipsen Manufacturing, Dublin, Ireland) is a selective EZH2 inhibitor approved for the treatment of epithelioid sarcoma and follicular lymphoma. EZH2 is the catalytic subunit of the polycomb repressive complex (PRC2), and its primary mechanism is to methylate histone 3 lysine 27, which silences tumor suppressor genes.[ ] The SWI/SNF complex (which includes SMARCA4, SMARCA2, SMARCB1, ARID1A, etc.) normally opposes the PRC2 complex whose catalytic subunit is EZH2.[ ] When a tumor loses a SWI/SNF subunit, such as SMARCA4, the balance breaks. PRC2 becomes overactive, leading to excessive methylation of histone 3 lysine 27, resulting in silencing of tumor suppressor genes.[ ] In SMARCA4-deficient tumors, the tumor cells become dependent on EZH2 activity. Tazemetostat inhibits EZH2, reducing the abnormal PRC2 activity and potentially restoring the expression of genes the tumor has silenced.[ ]
A phase 2 trial (MATCH APEC 1621C)[ ] investigated tazemetostat in pediatric patients with an EZH2 gain-of-function or loss-of-function mutation of the SWI/SNF complex subunits SMARCB1 and/or SMARCA4. The trial enrolled 20 patients between October 2017 and July 2020. The median age of study participants was 5 years. The two most common cancer diagnoses were atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (n = 8) and malignant rhabdoid tumor (n = 4). Actionable alterations included SMARCB1 loss (n = 16), EZH2 mutation (n = 3), and SMARCA4 loss (n = 1). Additionally, eligibility criteria included ages 12 months to 21 years and a Karnofsky score of at least 50%. Exclusion criteria included uncontrolled infection, cytopenias, a prior history of malignancy, a prior history of T-lymphoblastic lymphomas or acute lymphoblastic leukemia, a history of solid organ transplantation, or known use of strong CYP45A-inducing agents. Tazemetostat was administered orally at the recommended pediatric dose of 1200 mg/m2 twice a day, with each cycle lasting 28 days. Of the 20 patients enrolled, grade 3 or higher serious adverse events included anemia (n = 2), thrombocytopenia (n = 1), intracranial hemorrhage (n = 1), lung infection (n = 1), and alkaline phosphatase increase (n = 1). Of note, one patient experienced four grade 3 adverse reactions within the first cycle, including dyspnea, abdominal pain, increased alanine aminotransferase, and decreased platelet count. Three patients were reported to have a serum bromide elevation because tazemetostat is formulated as a bromide salt. No patients developed a secondary malignancy during the trial or follow-up period. The primary end point of the study revealed an overall response rate was 5% (90% CI, 1–20), which did not reach the goal primary efficacy endpoint. One patient with a SMARCA4-deficient non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis had a confirmed partial response with a 91% two-dimensional decrease in size. Prolonged stable disease was found in 4 of 16 patients (25%), all of whom had a SMARCB1 loss. This resulted in the Food and Drug Administration–approved indication for tazemetostat in epithelioid sarcoma with a SMARCB1 loss in January 2020. One patient with epithelioid sarcoma completed 26 cycles, and all other patients came off tazemetostat within 2 cycles, with a median of 2 cycles completed. Secondary endpoints demonstrated a 6-month PFS rate of 35% (95% CI, 15.7–55.6) and a 6-month OS rate of 45% (95% CI, 23.1–64.7).

## CLINICAL TRIALS WITHOUT PUBLISHED RESULTS

## LY4050784 (FHD-909)
LY4050784 (FHD909)[ ] (Foghorn Therapeutics) is an oral selective SMARCA2 inhibitor currently in phase 1a/b, open-label, first-in-human trial. In the phase 1a dose-escalation, eligibility criteria include any patient with an advanced solid tumor and an SMARCA4 alteration. In a preclinical study, LY4050784 was administered to SMARCA4-mutant lung cancer xenografts, which revealed significant antitumor efficacy.[ ] The response rate to LY4050784 significantly reduced the tumor volume (p ≤ 0.001) at all dose levels; however, the greatest decrease in volume was observed at the highest dose of 60 mg/kg.[ ] Additionally, all doses were well-tolerated.
In FHD909, LY4050784 will be administered orally at incrementally increasing dose levels to determine the maximum tolerated dose. Phase 1b dose expansion, part A, will include patients with NSCLC who have a loss-of-function SMARCA4 alteration or loss of protein expression and who have received 2 prior lines of standard-of-care therapies. Part B will include all other types of advanced solid tumors with a SMARCA4 alteration. The main inclusion criteria include patients aged younger than 18, with measurable locally advanced or metastatic disease, SMARCA4 alteration, who have received all standard-of-care therapies deemed appropriate, and have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status of 0–1. The main exclusion criteria include known loss-of-function SMARCA2 alteration or malignancy, and previous use of SMARCA2 (BRM) inhibitors/degraders. This trial is currently in progress with pending results.

## PRT7732
PRT7732[ ] (Prelude Therapeutics) is an oral SMARCA2 degrader currently in an open-label, first-in-human, phase 1 study. Cancers with a SMARCA4 deletion rely on its paralog, SMARCA2, for survival, making SMARCA2 an available target.[ ] The main inclusion criteria include patients 18 years and older, diagnosed with an advanced, recurrent, or metastatic solid tumor malignancy with any SMARCA4 mutation who have progressed or are ineligible for standard of care therapy, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status of 0–1, and adequate organ function (hematology, renal, and hepatic). The main exclusion criteria include patients with a known concomitant SMARCA2 mutation or who have previously received SMARCA2/SMARCA4-targeted therapy. This trial is currently enrolling patients in the seventh dose escalation cohort of 125 mg once daily; results are pending.[ ]

## Tiragolumab and Atezolizumab
SWI/SNF-deficient tumors demonstrate increased immune infiltration and upregulation of immune checkpoint ligands, including programmed death-ligand-1 (PD-L1), poliovirus receptor, and Nectin-2.[ ] These features suggest a potentially enhanced responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade, including therapies such as tiragolumab and atezolizumab.[ ]
Tiragolumab[ ] (Genentech, Oceanside, CA, USA) is a human monoclonal antibody that targets the T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domains. Tiragolumab blocks the interaction between the T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domains on T cells and its ligands, poliovirus receptor, and Nectin-2 expressed on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells (APC), thereby enhancing antitumor immune activity.[ ] Tiragolumab is currently under investigation from preclinical studies to phase III. Tiragolumab works synergistically with other immunotherapies and is often combined a PD-L1 inhibitor such as atezolizumab. Thus far, clinical phase I–III studies have shown promising results in patients diagnosed with NSCLC and esophageal cancer.[ ]
Atezolizumab[ ] (Genentech), sold under the brand name Tecentriq, is a Food and Drug Administration–approved human monoclonal antibody that targets PD-L1, blocking its interaction with PD-1 and B7-1 receptors, thereby restoring antitumor T-cell activity. Atezolizumab is approved for use in NSCLC, small cell lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, bladder cancer, melanoma, and alveolar soft part sarcoma.
PEPN2121 is a phase ½ study of tiragolumab and atezolizumab in patients with relapsed or refractory SMARCB1- or SMARCA4-deficient tumors (most often seen in malignant rhabdoid tumor, atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor, poorly differentiated chordoma, epithelioid sarcoma, and renal medullary carcinoma).[ , ] Tumors with defective SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, of which the SMARCB1/A4 are subunits, exhibit increased immune cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression. It is hypothesized that when tiragolumab and atezolizumab are used in tandem in SMARCB1- and/or SMARCA4-deficient tumors, prolonged duration of response to immunotherapy should be observed. In part A of the study, patients will receive tiragolumab monotherapy, administered based on weight and age (300 mg if < 15 kg; 420 mg if > 15 kg to ≤ 40 kg; 600 mg if > 40 kg or ≥ 18 years). In part B of the study, patients will be treated with atezolizumab and tiragolumab. Both atezolizumab and tiragolumab will be administered on day 1 of a 21-day cycle, repeating up to 5 years. The dosing of atezolizumab will be dosed at 15 mg/kg (max dose of 1200 mg) if < 18 years or 1200 mg if ≥ 18 years.

## QL1706
QL1706[ ] (Qilu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) is a novel bifunctional MabPair product against PD-1 and CTLA-4, produced in a fixed ratio of 2:1. In this phase II, open-label trial, QL1706 and platinum-based chemotherapy are administered to patients with a SMARCA4-deficient, locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC. The main inclusion criteria include patients aged older than 18, life expectancy greater than 3 months, stage IIIb-IV NSCLC with SMARCA4/BRG1 loss, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status of 0–1. The main exclusion criteria include histology positive for small cells, recurrence within 6 months of completing prior adjuvant therapy, and the use of Chinese herbal medicines with antitumor activity within 2 weeks of the study. Participants receive QL1706 at a dose of 5 mg/kg, intravenously on day 1 of a 21-day cycle for 4–6 cycles. If there is no evidence of disease progression, then QL1706 can be continued as maintenance therapy at a dose of 5 mg/kg on day 1 of a 21-day cycle. The maximum treatment duration will be 2 years.

## DISCUSSION
To date, three trials targeting SMARCA4-mutated cancers have reported results, and four are in progress with results not yet published ( and ). Preliminary antitumor activity has been observed in NSCLC, esophageal cancer, uveal melanoma, and non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis. Treatment with PRT3789 achieved partial responses and prolonged stable disease in patients with esophageal cancer and NSCLC.[ ] The phase 1 trial of FHD-286 in uveal melanoma patients reported one partial response and an overall reduction in tumor burden across all dose levels.[ ] The phase 2 trial of tazemetostat reported a partial response in a patient with confirmed SMARCA4-deficient, non-Langerhans histiocytosis.[ ]
Overall, these early-phase studies demonstrate emerging activity across multiple therapeutic classes targeting SMARCA4-deficient tumors.[ , , , , , , , ] Therapeutic strategies targeting SWI/SNF-deficient tumors are increasingly guided by the distinct biological vulnerabilities created by loss of chromatin-remodeling function.[ , , ] These include paralog dependency, epigenetic dysregulation, and alteration in the tumor immune microenvironment.[ , , ] The clinical studies described above reflect these mechanistic approaches, with emerging activity observed across SMARCA2-targeting agents,[ , , , , , , , ] EZH2 inhibitors,[ , ] and immune checkpoint strategies.[ , , , ] Collectively, these approaches highlight a shift toward mechanism-driven therapeutic development, in which treatment strategies are tailored to the underlying molecular consequence of SWI/SNF loss. Ongoing clinical trials will be critical in defining the efficacy of these approaches and identifying the optimal therapeutic combinations for this biologically distinct subset of cancers.
Dose-limiting toxicities noted in the above trials include grade 3 keratitis, rash, anemia, intracranial hemorrhage, lung infection, and increased alkaline phosphatase. There were no reported dose-limiting toxicities for PRT3789. There is some overlap in side effects between the SMARCA2 degrader and selective inhibitor of the BAF complex, including nausea. However, each medication had a unique side-effect profile, which most likely reflects the various mechanisms of action among these SMARCA4-targeted therapies. Tazemetostat, uniquely, caused liver enzyme elevation, abdominal pain, and thrombocytopenia. In contrast, FDH-286 reported dysgeusia, dry mouth, and rash. Both tazemetostat and PRT3789 have been reported to cause anemia; however, the severity of anemia was much worse for patients who received tazemetostat.
Currently under investigation are several other potential selective biomarkers for treatment with a SMARCA4-directed treatment, including KEAP1, STK11, KRAS, and PDL-1 expression.[ , ] In a genomic study of 131,668 patients with SMARCA4 variants, co-mutations in KEAP1 and STK11 often coexist. Genetic alterations in SMARCA4, KEAP1, and STK11 can most likely be ascribed to the fact that these three genes are found on the same loss-of-heterozygosity segment.[ ] Of note, SMARCA4 alterations were found to be mutually exclusive with alterations in genes that encode other BAF subunits like ARID1A, ARID1B, ARID2, PBRM1, SMARCB1, and SMARCD1.[ ] Additionally, SMARCA4 mutations were also found to be mutually exclusive with the following actionable driver genes in NSCLC: EGFR, ALK, MET, ROS1, and RET, with EGFR alterations exhibiting the strongest mutual exclusivity.[ ] Regarding KRAS mutations, approximately 15–17% of SMARCA4-deleted NSCLC also have a KRASG12C mutation.[ ] In terms of PD-L1 expression and SMARCA4 mutations, previous research suggests loss of function of the SWI/SNF complex can be associated with increased PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden.[ ] This suggests a possible biomarker that could help determine the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in patients with high PD-L1 expression.[ ] However, the evidence is limited by retrospective studies and needs further elucidation.
Strategies to combine SMARCA4-directed treatments with other agents hold potential for improved efficacy. An abstract presented at the American Association for Cancer Research in 2023 demonstrated the efficacy of combining PRT3789 with MRTX849 (KRASG12C inhibitor), gemcitabine, PRT2527 (CDK9 inhibitor), and PRT3645 (CDK4/6 inhibitor) in NSCLC and urinary bladder cancer cell line–derived xenografts with low expression of SMARCA4. All combinations revealed a significant decrease in tumor growth and an increase in tumor regression in SMARCA4-mutated NSCLC, suggesting a synergistic effect when combined with other small-molecule inhibitors or chemotherapy.[ ] The greatest mean tumor growth inhibition was achieved with the combination of PRT3789 + PRT3645 in SMARCA4-deleted NSCLC cell line–derived xenografts at 93% (p < 0.001), followed by the PRT3789 + gemcitabine combination with a mean tumor growth inhibition of 89% in SMARCA4-deleted NSCLC cell lined–derived xenografts (p < 0.01). The combination of PRT3789 with MRTX849 in both SMARCA4/KRASG12C mutant NSCLC and urothelial cancer cell line–derived xenografts also showed a significant decrease in cell viability and mean tumor volume over a period of 21 days. These results support that PRT3789, a SMARCA2 degrader, has synergistic effects with other small-molecule inhibitors that target the MAPK pathway, chemotherapy, CDK4/6, and CDK9 inhibitors.
Although the SMARCA4-directed trials with results summarized in this article are small-molecule inhibitors, there is potential to target SMARCA4 with other technologies, including monoclonal antibodies, bispecific antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, and CAR-T cell therapies. A preclinical study investigating a bispecific antibody targeting PD1 × IL-2 was presented at the 2024 European Society for Medical Oncology annual meeting. Preliminary promising results were reported in SMARCA4-mutated NSCLC in NOD-scid gamma mice models.[ ] With intraperitoneal administration, there were increased levels of IL-2, which led to persistent activation of STAT5 in CD8+T cells, overall resulting in enhanced antitumor immunity and suppressed tumor progression through reversing CD8+T cell exhaustion.[ ]

## CONCLUSION
In summary, multiple trials with published results and ongoing trials for SMARCA4-directed therapies hold promise for achieving clinical benefit across a variety of common and uncommon solid tumor cancers. Strategies targeting paralog dependency, including SMARCA2 degraders and BAF complex inhibitors, represent a promising therapeutic approach for tumors harboring SMARCA4 alterations.[ , , ] These agents are not only orally bioavailable and generally well tolerated but have also shown synergistic effects in preclinical models when combined with other targeted therapies, chemotherapy, and CDK inhibitors. Additionally, there is preliminary evidence that SMARCA4-deficient lung cancers derive enhanced benefit from immunotherapy compared with SMARCA4-proficient tumors.[ ] All in all, these findings support a mechanism-driven framework for treating SWI/SNF-deficient cancers, with ongoing and future studies needed to define optimal therapeutic combinations and improve outcomes in this biologically distinct patient population.
