## Abstract
This study investigates the transcriptome-level alterations that influence production traits and early fattening stage myogenesis in Hanwoo cattle, specifically focusing on the highly prized Longissimus dorsi (LD) and Psoas major (PM) skeletal muscles, which hold significant commercial value. We conducted RNA sequencing analysis on LD and PM muscles from 14 Hanwoo steers (n = 7, each group) at the age of 10 months, all fed the same diet. Our results unveiled a total of 374 and 206 up-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in LD and PM muscles, respectively, with statistical significance (p < 0.05) and a log2fold change ≥ 1. Genes governing muscle development processes, such as PAX3, MYL3, COL11A1, and MYL6B, were found to be expressed at higher levels in both tissues. Conversely, genes regulating lipid metabolism, including FABP3, FABP4, LEP, ADIPOQ, and PLIN1, exhibited higher expression in the PM muscle. Functional enrichment analysis revealed a tissue-specific response, as PM muscle showed increased lipid metabolism allied pathways, including the PPAR signaling pathway and regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, while LD was characterized by growth and proliferative processes. Our findings validate the presence of a muscle-dependent transcription and co-expression pattern that elucidates the transcriptional landscape of bovine skeletal muscle.
Keywords: Hanwoo cattle, skeletal muscle transcriptome, myogenesis, gene co-expression, longissimus dorsi, psoas major

## Introduction
The livestock sector plays a significant role in South Korea, contributing nearly 40% to total agricultural production. , Within this sector, beef production ranks second, accounting for 30% of meat production. As the Korean economy has grown, per capita beef consumption has risen correspondingly, reaching 11.6 kg annually in 2015, with this upward trend anticipated to continue. Hanwoo beef holds a premium status in the Korean meat market due to its exceptional taste, texture, and superior nutritional value.
Hanwoo cattle carcasses are typically segmented into ten distinct muscle cuts, including loin, tenderloin, rib, brisket, topside, shank, striploin, neck, blade, and rump, each with unique nutritional compositions and physico-chemical properties that influence their flavor profiles. Consequently, consumer preferences vary across these individual muscle cuts. Recent trends in the global beef industry have seen a shift toward marketing individual muscle cuts, emphasizing the importance of understanding and catering to these varying consumer preferences in the distribution and marketing of beef products. In this regard, comprehending the genetic relationships or distinctions among the individual muscles of Hanwoo beef holds significant importance.
In Hanwoo beef, the longissimus dorsi (LD) and psoas major (PM) skeletal muscles, commonly referred to as top fillet and under fillet in the meat industry, stand out as highly coveted cuts of the carcass, possessing substantial commercial and culinary significance. The LD muscle is located along the lateral wall of the thorax in the region of the thoracic vertebrae, while the PM muscle is an elongated muscle running along the posterior aspect of the spine, spanning from the hip bone to the thirteenth rib and positioned just behind the kidney. The LD and PM skeletal muscles in cattle exhibit differences in terms of function, size, muscle fiber composition, and potentially intramuscular fat content. These differences reflect their distinct roles in posture and locomotion and are influenced by genetics, hormonal regulation, and nutrition. Moreover, the characteristics of these muscles impact meat quality, affecting factors, such as color, water retention, marbling, and overall texture. , 
Understanding the genetic aspects that govern the variance between these two different skeletal muscles is crucial for breeding and genetic selection in the livestock industry to produce beef with desired characteristics, including marbling, tenderness, and flavor. Furthermore, it offers insights into the genetic mechanisms that govern muscle development (myogenesis) and how variations observed at the proteomic and transcriptomic levels can influence production traits. Myogenesis is primarily regulated by myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs), including MYOD, MYOG, MRF4/MYF6, and MYF5, which oversee myoblast proliferation, migration, and fusion. In addition to these factors, numerous other genes play crucial roles in regulating skeletal myogenesis, growth, and differentiation. However, many uncertainties remain about the precise molecular mechanisms underlying muscle differentiation. Transcriptomic analysis has been valuable in describing variations in gene expression among muscles from different breeds, showcasing distinct traits across various muscle categories.
Several research efforts have previously been undertaken to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in bovine skeletal muscle associated with various traits. For instance, Du et al. investigated transcriptomic differences related to water-holding capacity (WHC) in the LD muscles of 49 Chinese Simmental beef cattle, revealing seven candidate genes (HSPA12A, HSPA13, PPARγ, MYL2, MYPN, TPI, and ATP2A1) that influence WHC in muscle tissue. Another study examined gene expression changes in skeletal muscle in response to daily weight gain in Hanwoo cattle, highlighting the dysregulation of genes associated with muscle development, extracellular matrix organization, and calcium signaling pathways in hypertrophied muscle tissue. Additionally, Komolka et al. studied the gene expression profiles in the LD muscle of Japanese Black and Holstein cattle with different genetic predispositions for intramuscular fat content, identifying genes linked to carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and molecular transport, thus providing insights into the molecular basis of intramuscular fat deposition. 
Therefore, we conducted an RNA-seq study to compare the genetic profiles of the LD and PM muscles in Hanwoo steers. The aim was to gain insights into the genetic mechanisms responsible for the differences between these two skeletal muscle types. We collected samples from 14 steers at 10 months of age, grouped based on the specific muscle being analyzed (LD and PM). Genes related to adipokines, lipid metabolism, and muscle development were identified. Functional enrichment and pathway analyses were performed on the DEGs to identify significantly enriched gene ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with different muscles. Additionally, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed to identify co-gene expression and hub genes based on their connectivity across the skeletal muscle system.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics statement, animal resources, slaughtering, and sample preparation
The research procedures detailed in this paper received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee at the National Institute of Animal Science (NIAS) in the Republic of Korea (Approval No. NIAS-2022133). Tissue samples from Hanwoo cattle for this study were collected and organized by the Animal Genomics and Bioinformatics Division of NIAS. Furthermore, all experiments conducted in this manuscript adhere to the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Regarding the slaughter process, at the slaughterhouse of the NIAS, castles were gently stunned using a captive bolt gun and then slaughtered following the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) guidelines for the slaughter of Animals.
In this investigation, a cohort of 14 Hanwoo steers with 10 months of age at the early fattening stage was selected. These cattle were uniformly fed the same diet. Following the slaughter of the cattle, LD and PM muscle samples from specific regions were harvested. The collection of these samples followed stringent sterile surgical protocols, involving the application of 70% ethanol to disinfect the collection site and the use of a sterile scalpel to make precise incisions. To maintain the integrity of the samples, they were promptly placed in liquid nitrogen for preservation and subsequently stored at −80 °C.

## mRNA isolation, and transcriptomic mRNA sequencing, quality control, and alignment
Total RNA was extracted from each homogenized tissue sample (LD and PM) using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and integrity of the RNA were evaluated using a nano photometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA) and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay kit with the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) values exceeding 8 were chosen for library preparation. The library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol, which involved random fragmentation, adapter ligation, and tagmentation. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Adapter-ligated fragments underwent PCR amplification, followed by gel purification. Cluster generation was achieved using the TruSeq PE cluster kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. For sequencing, the library was loaded into the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System, which generated paired-end reads. Base calling from the raw images produced by the Illumina sequencer was performed using real-time analysis (RTA) software. Subsequently, the BCL image files generated were converted to FASTQ raw readings using the Illumina BCL to fastq program, and these were employed for post-analysis.
For the data analysis in this study, we started with FASTQ files as our input. We initially evaluated the quality of the raw RNA-seq data using FastQC v0.11. To ensure data integrity, we employed Trimmomatic v0.39 for several preprocessing steps, including the removal of N bases, trimming of adapter sequences from both the 5′ and 3′ ends, and the exclusion of reads with a quality score below Q20. This quality control process was applied to generate clean paired-end reads. The resultant filtered sequences were then annotated to the Bos taurus reference genome as background (version ARS-UCD 1.2) using the Hisat2 v2.2.1 mapping tool, along with the corresponding index file. Following alignment, we utilized the mapped reads to create a count matrix using FeatureCounts from the Subread package (v2.0.1). We generated a principal component analysis (PCA) plot to visualize the data’s inherent structure and variability. Finally, we analyzed DEGs in the R environment (v3.4.4) using the respective read counts matrix file to identify DEGs.

## Identification of differential gene expression (DEGs)
In this study, we conducted differential expression (DE) analysis between the groups for the LD muscle PM muscle tissues. To identify DEGs, we applied a significance threshold, requiring a p < 0.05 and a log2fold change ≥ 1. The DEGs obtained were categorized into two groups: up-regulated in the LD group compared to PM tissue and upregulated in PM tissue compared to LD tissue, based on their log2fold change values. To visually represent the genetic distinctions between these two groups, we created a heatmap using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12) in R, which can be accessed at https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/pheatmap.pdf. Additionally, we generated a PCA plot and a volcano plot to further illustrate the data using ggplot2 (v3.3.3) and the VolcaNoseR web tool (https://github.com/JoachimGoedhart/VolcaNoseR/issues), respectively.

## GO and KEGG enrichment, visualization of network, and associated expression profile
We utilized the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) v6.7 tool to perform functional analysis on a network of enriched biological process terms derived from a list of DEGs. This analysis aimed to identify Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways exhibiting significant enrichment scores. Specifically, we focused on the upregulated DEGs associated with LD and PM muscle. The reference genome used for this investigation was that of the cow (Bos taurus). To establish statistical significance, we adopted a significance threshold of p < 0.05. For visualizing and constructing the enriched pathways network, we utilized the ClueGO plugin in Cytoscape (v3.2.1).

## Protein-protein interaction network and co-expression analysis
We employed the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING, v10.0) online database to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network involving DEGs from both LD and PM muscle tissue. To discern meaningful interactions, we set an interaction score cutoff of 0.4. Subsequently, we utilized the Cytoscape program to visualize this PPI network. In our search to identify key genes within the PPI analysis, we harnessed the CytoHubba plugin within Cytoscape. We extracted the top 20 genes based on node-connect degree. The MCODE plugin within Cytoscape was used to get co-expression modules within the network. The MCODE parameters we utilized included a degree cutoff of 2, a node score cutoff of 0.2, a K-score of 2, and a maximum depth of 100.

## Quantitative reverse‑transcription‑PCR (qRT‑PCR)
We conducted qRT-PCR on the five randomly selected upregulated DEGs from both tissues to validate the findings obtained from RNA-seq. Primer design for qRT-PCR was accomplished using the Primer3 tool (Version 0.4.0), and the list of primers used in this study is provided in . The mRNA levels of the DEGs were normalized using the housekeeping gene 18S rRNA. Relative gene expression values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔct technique to compare the expression levels between the two tissues. In addition, the expression levels of regulated genes were compared between the RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data.
 

## Statistical analysis
qRT‑PCR experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard error of measurement (SEM). GraphPad Prism 6 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the gene expression data with a pairwise t-test. *** signifies p < 0.001 and * displays p-value <0.05 as statistical significance.

## Results

## RNA-seq data quality check, mapping to reference genomes, PCA analysis
The total mRNA of LD and PM muscles from Hanwoo cattle were obtained to evaluate the distinct and specific pattern of gene expression that emerges as a consequence of either modified or unchanged biological processes in both tissues. In this study, we began by ensuring the quality of our raw sequencing data by rigorously filtering the raw reads resulting in more than 6,278,808,420 reads for each sample. Each sample yielded raw read quality score (Q20 and Q30) percentages >97 and 94%, respectively, and a CG content percentage of 46–52% ( ). These processed reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Bos taurus). We observed an overall mapping rate of 94.46–97.48% across samples from all tissue groups, with 79–90% of the reads being uniquely mapped. In addition, PCA analysis distinctly segregates the LD muscle tissue samples from the PM samples. Specifically, the results indicate that the first principal component (PC1) accounts for 27% of the variability, while PC2 accounts for 17% of the variability in the DEGs across all 14 samples ( ).

Compared to PM muscle tissue, we identified the expression of 374 bovine genes to be upregulated in LD muscle when compared to PM and 206 genes to be upregulated in PM muscle tissue when compared to LD muscle. In this plot, the top five significantly expressed genes are distinctly marked. The top five significant upregulated genes were ZIC1, ZIC3, PADI2, IGFN1, and HOXC8 in LD tissue and IRX5, DPYS, SIM1, TBX1, DCSTAMP in PM tissue based on p-value.
 In addition, we have specifically focused on the expression profiles of genes related to lipid metabolism, those involved in muscle development, and genes associated with adipokine when examining the differentially expressed genes in the two distinct skeletal muscle tissues. In contrast, we did not observe any significant upregulation of genes associated with lipid metabolism in the LD tissue when compared to the PM tissue. Conversely, muscle development-related genes like FMOD, MYH2, TPM1, MYL6B, and COL11A1 were found to be upregulated by more than 1-fold in LD muscle tissue. Notably, LEP, ADIPOQ, APLN, TNF, and IL10 genes displayed significant upregulation in PM tissue. On the contrary, these adipokine genes were identified as downregulated in LD tissue when compared to PM tissue.

## Quantitative real-time PCR validation (qRT-PCR)
To assess the reliability of the RNA-seq data, a subset of five up-regulated DEGs was randomly selected for validation using qRT-PCR ( ). The qRT-PCR results demonstrated that the expression levels of up-regulated genes in LD (ZIC1, ZIC3, ZIC4, TNMD, and SPHKAP) and PM (CLRN2, DPYS, IRX5, HOXC11, and MARCHF10) sample were significantly changed in each tissue, which corroborated the findings from the RNA-seq analysis ( ). This consistency implies that all examined genes exhibited significant differential expression between the muscle groups.
 

## Discussion
In the context of genetic diversity, variations during embryonic myogenesis contribute significantly, underscoring the importance of understanding the genetic mechanisms governing muscle development (myogenesis) and how transcriptomic differences impact production traits. In this context, our study explored the transcriptomic diversity between two distinct skeletal muscles, LD and PM, which exhibit varying quality traits. To achieve this, we conducted RNA sequencing analysis on LD and PM skeletal muscles from 10-month-old Hanwoo cattle at the early fattening stage, all of which were raised under identical dietary and environmental conditions.
We have identified significant differences in the expression of several DEGs between the LD and PM muscles. Notably, some of the upregulated DEGs in the LD muscle, including ZIC1 (Zic family member 1), ZIC3 (Zic family member 3), ZIC4 (Zic family member 4), TNMD (tenomodulin), HOXC8 (homeobox C8), and PAX1 (paired box 1), as well as in the PM muscle, such as SIM1 (SIM bHLH transcription factor 1), TBX1 (T-box transcription factor 1), HOXC11 (homeobox C11), and PVALB (parvalbumin), have been previously implicated in adipocyte differentiation, energy homeostasis, muscle growth, and metabolism. , In a prior study, SIM1 and PVALB were found to be upregulated in pig LD tissue, contrasting with our findings, possibly due to species differences. However, these two genes were suggested as candidates responsible for driving expression differences and influencing muscle development disparities between purebred and Duroc-crossbred pigs. For PM tissue, the most upregulated gene was CLRN2, which encodes a tetraspanin-like protein, clarin-2. This protein, part of the clarin family, is involved in hearing in adults and is an important target for developing biological treatments for hearing loss. Although the role of CLRN2 in muscle development has not been previously reported, its potential significance as a muscle-specific biomarker should not be overlooked.
Additionally, the gene TBX1, highly expressed in PM muscle, was previously found to be upregulated in fast muscle-derived myoblasts and linked to muscle fiber types and oxidative metabolism modulation in myotubes through gain- and loss-of-function experiments. , This supports the idea that the upregulation of TBX1, a transcription factor in the PM muscle, is associated with the rapid maturation of its myotomes during myogenesis. This provides valuable insights into muscle physiology and suggests potential breeding strategies for improving meat quality. Consequently, the upregulation of TBX1 expression provides partial insight into the higher intramuscular fat content observed in PM muscle.
In the LD muscle, we observed the upregulation of the Zic gene family, including ZIC1, ZIC3, and ZIC4. This gene family encodes a conserved group of zinc finger proteins that play crucial roles in neural development and the formation of the vertebrate embryo’s axial skeleton. , Specifically, ZIC1 and ZIC3 have been associated with the early control of myogenesis and myostatin signaling. , , Our study also identified the upregulation of HOXC11 in the PM muscle and the upregulation of ZIC4 and HOXC8 in the LD muscle. This is consistent with findings by de Las Heras-Saldana et al., suggesting these genes play a role in determining muscle fate differentiation in cattle. Previous research indicates that the HOXC8 protein may be necessary for the full activation of muscle-specific gene expression in a specific subset of muscles. , Additionally, we observed the upregulation of PAX1, a PAX family member, in LD muscle. PAX1 is essential for vertebral column development and is implicated in the early stages of tissue differentiation. In LD tissue, we noted the upregulation of two genes associated with the extracellular matrix (ECM): tenomodulin (TNMD), a type II transmembrane glycoprotein essential for mobility, and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), an ECM glycoprotein crucial for collagen assembly and ECM stability. This suggests a role in ECM remodeling in LD skeletal muscle, with variations in gene expression patterns influencing myogenesis. , 
We also observed distinct expression patterns of genes associated with lipid metabolism, muscle development processes, and adipokine genes between the two muscle types. Notably, several well-known regulatory genes of lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle, including fatty acid binding protein 3 (FABP3), fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4), perilipin 1 (PLIN1), leptin (LEP), and adiponectin (ADIPOQ), exhibited higher expression levels in the PM muscle compared to the LD muscle. This upregulation suggests that these genes may play a role in promoting various aspects of lipid metabolism and the transcription of genes involved in fatty acid transport and metabolism within the PM muscle.
Similarly, genes, such as acyl-CoA synthetase medium-chain family member 1 (ACSM1), mal T cell differentiation protein 2 (MAL2), and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) were significantly upregulated in PM tissue. These genes are well-described as being involved in fatty acid synthesis and elongation, directly correlating to intramuscular fat percentage in bovine skeletal muscle. , Another key gene in the fatty acid synthesis pathway, fatty acid synthase (FASN), , showed higher expression in PM than in LD. Moreover, the including thyroid hormone-responsive (THRSP), cell death inducing DFFA like effector c(CIDEC), ADIPOQ, FABP4, S100 calcium binding protein G (S100G), adipogenin (ADIG), and glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase, mitochondrial (GPAM) are involved in TAG synthesis and storage in cattle muscle. , The elevated expression of several DEGs linked to fatty acid and TAG synthesis may regulate intramuscular adipocytes in PM tissue. This hypothesis is supported by studies demonstrating that TAG stored in intramuscular adipocytes directly influences intramuscular fat percentage. , Consequently, this molecular-level analysis helps elucidate the genetic basis for the higher intramuscular fat content observed in PM muscle. 
Furthermore, significant upregulation, by more than 1.5-fold, of several genes associated with muscle development processes was observed in the PM muscle, including PAX3 (paired box 3) and MYL3 (myosin light chain 3). PAX3 is crucial for guiding muscle progenitor cells to differentiate into skeletal muscle fibers during embryogenesis. The upregulation of MYL3 in our study aligns with previous research, showing consistent elevation in MYL3 expression specifically within Type I muscle fibers, implying an augmented presence of myofibers in PM muscle. The upregulation of MYL3 in our study aligns with previous research, showing consistent elevation in MYL3 expression specifically within Type I muscle fibers, implying an augmented presence of myofibers in PM muscle. , Additionally, higher expression of the myosin heavy chain (MHC) isoforms in both tissues was noted, with MHC3 highly expressed in PM tissue and MHC2 showing higher expression in LD muscle. Studies suggest that organisms produce numerous muscle MHC isoforms, which are expressed in temporally and spatially regulated patterns. This indicates that different MHC isoforms possess distinct characteristics essential for defining specific muscle properties. 
In PM tissue, regulators of G protein signaling 20 (RGS20), thymocyte selection associated high mobility group box (TOX), and fibromodulin (FMOD) were highly expressed. FMOD is implicated in the differentiation of muscle satellite cells and ECM assembly, playing a regulatory role in initiating myoblast differentiation. 
RGS20 inhibits signal transduction by enhancing the GTPase activity of G protein alpha subunits, related to muscle regeneration processes. TOX protein, characterized by an HMG box DNA binding domain, serves as a transcriptional regulator primarily involved in T-cell development. However, the roles of RGS20 and TOX in skeletal muscle differentiation, development, or regeneration are not well understood.
In contrast, LD muscle tissue exhibited increased expression of MYL6B (myosin light chain 6B) and COL11A1 (collagen type XI alpha 1 chain) genes. MYL6B is essential for non-muscle Myosin II (NMII), contributing to tissue architecture maintenance, while COL11A1 produces type XI collagen, providing structural support to muscles. Additionally, the upregulation of tropomyosin 1 (TPM1) suggests its role in stabilizing thin filaments and facilitating actin-myosin interaction, indicating the LD muscle’s endurance and power. Collagen, a major component of intramuscular connective tissue, provides structural support for muscle and adipose tissue. Our study also found increased expression of adipokine genes in PM tissue, suggesting an increase in adipose tissue and overall body fat content. Adipokines play significant roles in modulating fat distribution, adipocyte function, and glucose and lipid metabolism. 
To interpret the DEGs upregulated in each skeletal muscle tissue, we conducted a functional analysis. In the PM muscle, several lipid metabolism pathways were enriched, most of which are involved in muscle growth processes. These pathways include the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, glycerol-phospholipid metabolism, and the PPAR signaling pathway, which are characteristic of a highly proliferative developmental stage. , These pathways align with GO-BP related to metabolic processes, such as glycerol-3-phosphate metabolism, lipid catabolism, brown fat cell differentiation, and fatty acid beta-oxidation. The upregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism and fat biosynthesis aligns with the increased activity of lipid metabolism in the PM muscle. Our results are consistent with previous studies observing similar trends in pig skeletal muscle. , 
LD muscle exhibited enriched pathways related to signaling or metabolic processes, including the calcium signaling pathway, nitrogen metabolism, motor proteins, and glutathione metabolism, suggesting more active cellular growth. The enrichment of the calcium signaling pathway in both muscles is likely due to its essential role in myogenesis, muscle growth, and muscle regeneration during muscle evolution. , Overall, the decreased expression of these lipid metabolism-associated genes trend across LD muscle development suggests a differential evolution of LD and PM. Differences in transcriptional regulation underpin much biological variation. 
Additionally, we constructed a PPI network to gain insights into the interaction of DEGs in different muscle types. Among the 20 genes identified, the top hub gene was interleukin 10 (IL10), which exhibited the highest fold change and degree. IL10, an adipokine, plays a significant role in energy homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and the anti-inflammatory response. Other upregulated genes by more than 1.5-fold included LEP, CD3E (CD3 epsilon subunit of T-cell receptor complex), FLT3 (tyrosine kinase 3), and ADIPOQ. ADIPOQ and LEP are well-known key regulators of lipid metabolism, correlated with intramuscular fat content during early fattening. , , 
ADIPOQ has been described as an important candidate marker gene for fat deposition. Although the functions of the CD3E and FLT3 in LD or PM skeletal muscles are not well-documented, CD3E is implicated in skeletal muscle regeneration and is associated with the skeletal muscle immune microenvironment. , In prior research, Ge et al. demonstrated that the knockdown of tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (FLT3L) or its receptor FLT3 suppressed myoblast differentiation in mouse muscle cells. 
This study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small, and the investigation focused exclusively on two skeletal muscles. While this approach provides the advantage of capturing transcriptional relationships or distinctions within a single muscle, it may not fully generalize across different muscle types or species due to the limited scope of the muscles examined. Previous studies analyzing the transcriptomic profiles of muscle tissue in cattle have primarily focused on a single type of tissue, often only examining the LD. , , None of these studies have reported findings in Korean Hanwoo cattle. The PM, which has important stabilizing roles due to its intimate anatomical attachments to the diaphragm and the pelvic floor, is equally as significant as the LD. This study benefits from being able to compare the transcriptomic profiles between these two different skeletal muscles in Hanwoo cattle, both of which hold substantial commercial and culinary significance. In cattle, muscle development is crucial for increasing body weight and lean tissue. By examining both the LD and PM in Hanwoo cattle, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of muscle development, addressing the limitations of previous research that focused solely on a single muscle type. A large-scale study is needed to examine different muscle types to describe the transcriptional landscape of bovine skeletal muscle comprehensively.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our findings provide strong evidence for a muscle-dependent transcription pattern between LD and PM muscles in Hanwoo cattle. Through comparative transcriptome analysis, we have identified 206 upregulated genes in LD and 374 upregulated genes in PM muscles. This study sheds light on crucial gene expression profiles associated with lipid metabolism, muscle development processes, and adipokines in both LD and PM skeletal muscles. Our functional analysis has unveiled key lipid metabolism-related pathways in PM muscles and signaling/metabolism pathways in LD muscles. Furthermore, the differentially expressed hub genes (IL10, LEP, CD3E, FLT3, and ADIPOQ) identified through the PPI network may serve as valuable candidates for future research on segmented muscle cuts in Hanwoo cattle.
