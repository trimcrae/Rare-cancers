## Abstract
Neurotrophic factors (NTFs) are secreted proteins that are crucial in neuronal growth, survival, and function. Individuals with neurodegenerative diseases, characterized by neuronal loss and various functional disorders, have been reported to exhibit altered levels of NTFs. This suggests that modulating NTF levels may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to alter the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. Although numerous efforts have been made to deliver NTFs to target regions, their clinical application remains challenging due to their inability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and the adverse side effects observed in clinical trials. Consequently, various delivery methods have been explored to overcome these limitations. In this review, we discuss recent therapeutic approaches utilizing NTFs and their signaling pathways as interventions against neurodegenerative diseases.
Keywords: Neurotrophic factors, Neurodegenerative disease, NTFs delivery system, Neuroprotection

## INTRODUCTION
Neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ , ], Parkinson’s disease (PD) [ ], Huntington’s disease (HD) [ ], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [ ], and spinocerebellar ataxia type 2 (SCA2) [ ], are characterized by neuronal degeneration mainly in the central nervous system (CNS), mostly accompanied by abnormal accumulation of certain proteins in the area [ ], resulting in functional loss, such as cognitive impairment and/or decline in motor ability [ ]. Neurodegeneration is often associated with ageing and dementia, which has become a significant social burden worldwide due to the increasingly ageing global population [ ]. The number of dementia patients is predicted to reach 152 million by 2050 [ ]. However, the development of medicines had limited success to date, as the majority of medications only delay, rather than reverse, the progression of these diseases [ , ].
Many efforts have emerged for alternative therapeutic approaches to fulfill the unmet needs for treating neurodegenerative diseases. As a neuronal defense mechanism, neurotrophic factors (NTFs) have been identified as candidates, as NTFs perform crucial roles in neuronal homeostasis and survival [ ]. Numerous studies have reported that NTF levels in patients or animal models of neurodegenerative diseases are reduced or disrupted compared to healthy control populations [ ], and these disrupted NTF levels also result in deleterious progression in neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. These previous findings suggest that supporting the neuronal defense mechanism by introducing NTFs into diseased area became an important part of efforts to rectify the progression of neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. Several efforts exist that utilize NTFs as a therapeutic tool [ ]. However, while NTFs offer great potential, their application remains challenging, and efforts have not always been successful in clinical trials due to limitations such as the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and side effects, as well as the short half-life of NTFs [ ].
To overcome these limitations in implementing clinical applications and beyond, the utilization of various NTFs, delivery mechanisms [e.g., utilization of adeno-associated virus (AAV) and stem cells], and relevant pathways [e.g., activation of ras homolog enriched in brain (Rheb) and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)] have been explored as part of efforts to establish treatments for neurodegenerative diseases [ , ]. However, limitations in clinical application persist, highlighting the importance of continued research as a promising strategy moving forward. This review provides an overview of the current state of research on various therapeutic approaches aimed at halting the progression of neurodegenerative diseases, focusing on the use of NTFs and/or their associated signaling pathways.

## MAJOR NEUROTROPHIC FACTORS AND THEIR LINK TO THE Rheb–mTOR PATHWAY
NTFs are secreted proteins that are crucial for supporting neuronal growth, survival, and functionality [ ]. Moreover, NTFs can interact with certain receptors on the cell surface to activate intracellular signaling cascades [ , ]. This mechanism supports the various roles of NTFs in relation to neurons [ ]. Notably, NTFs are involved in neuronal proliferation, differentiation, survival during neuronal development, and are also important for the protrusion of the neurites [ ]. In addition, NTFs have roles in the synaptic aspect, including the formation and maintenance of the synapse, as well as synaptic plasticity, which is important for learning and memory [ ]. NTFs also participate in the repair process, promoting regeneration after injury and modulating inflammation and glial responses [ ].
NTFs are categorized mainly into three groups: the neurotrophin family, the glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) family, neurokines, and other NTFs ( ). Meanwhile, the Rheb–mTOR pathway is also involved in the expression of multiple NTFs as a key upstream mechanism [ , ].
 

## Neurotrophins
The neurotrophin family, a well-characterized group among NTFs, includes the nerve growth factor (NGF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), and neurotrophin-4/5 (NT-4/5), and plays essential roles in neuronal development, survival, plasticity, and maintenance in both the CNS and peripheral nervous system (PNS) [ , , , ]. NGF promotes the survival of sympathetic, sensory, and basal forebrain cholinergic neurons, which are important in memory functions [ , ]. BDNF is key for synaptic plasticity and survival of cortical and dopaminergic neurons and is highly expressed in the hippocampus, cortex, and cerebellum [ , ]. NT-3 aids spinal cord development and proprioceptive neuron survival [ ], while NT-4/5, similar to BDNF, primarily supports the PNS [ ].
Neurotrophins promote signals via the high-affinity tropomyosin receptor kinase (Trk) receptors, with NGF targeting TrkA, BDNF and NT-4/5 targeting TrkB, and NT-3 targeting TrkC [ ]. Additionally, neurotrophins bind to the p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) with lower affinity, which modulates Trk signaling and may trigger apoptosis under certain conditions [ , , - ]. Overall, neurotrophins promote neuronal survival, differentiation, axon/dendrite growth, synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter regulation, and neural repair [ , , ].

## Glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor family
The GDNF family, which comprises GDNF, neurturin, artemin, and persephin [ ], is well known for supporting the development, survival, and function of dopaminergic and motor neurons, making the GDNF family highly relevant in PD and ALS research [ ]. GDNF is the most studied NTF protein in the context of PD and is essential for dopaminergic neuron survival in the substantia nigra, while also supporting motor and peripheral neurons [ ]. Neurturin shares features with GDNF and aids parasympathetic, sensory, and enteric neurons, while artemin supports sensory, sympathetic, and motor neurons, also aiding in pain and nerve regeneration [ ]. Persephin represents the least explored family member, although persephin is known to support motor and gamma-aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) neurons [ ].
Each GDNF family member binds a specific GDNF family receptor-α (GFRα) co-receptor: GFRα1 for GDNF, GFRα2 for neurturin, GFRα3 for artemin, and GFRα4 for persephin [ ]—and signals via the REarranged during Transfection (RET) tyrosine kinase receptor [ ]. This signaling activates intracellular pathways including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) (survival), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) (growth), and phospholipase C gamma (PLCγ) (plasticity) [ ]. Collectively, these factors promote neuronal survival, axonal regeneration, synaptic plasticity, neuromuscular maintenance, and pain modulation.

## Neurokines
Neurokines are cytokines, mainly from the interleukin-6 (IL-6) family, and influence neuronal survival, differentiation, inflammation, and synaptic plasticity [ ]. This group of neurokines includes the ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF), leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), IL-6, interleukin-11 (IL-11), cardiotrophin-1 (CT-1), oncostatin M (OSM), cardiotrophin-like cytokine (CLC), and neuropoietin [ ]. CNTF supports motor neuron survival and glial differentiation [ , ], while LIF promotes neuronal and astrocytic development and regulates neural stem cells (NSCs) [ ]. IL-6 and IL-11 mediate neuroinflammation and neuroprotection [ ]. CT-1 and CLC support motor and sensory/autonomic neurons [ ], OSM modulates inflammation and astrocyte activity [ ], and neuropoietin aids oligodendrocyte precursor differentiation [ ].
Neurokines act through CNTF receptor α (CNTFRα), LIF receptor β (LIFRβ), and glycoprotein 130 (gp130) receptors [ , ], contributing to neuronal survival, glial development, synaptic modulation, and injury repair in the CNS.

## Other neurotrophic factors
In addition to the main NTF families, further factors also support neural health: Insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1, IGF-2) act via IGF-1 receptor (IGF1R) to regulate neurogenesis, survival, and function [ ]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), acting through VEGF receptors (VEGFR-1/2), contributes to angiogenesis and neuroprotection in the CNS [ ]. Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) promote stem cell proliferation and neural development via the FGF receptors (FGFRs) [ ]. Meanwhile, additional factors, such as ephrins [ ] and neuregulins [ ], aid in axonal guidance, synaptogenesis, and plasticity.

## Rheb–mTOR pathway
Rheb is a small guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) and known to be an activator of the mTOR signaling pathway [ ]. Rheb directly activates mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), which promotes protein synthesis and neuronal growth, inhibits autophagy, and regulates cell survival signaling [ ]. The activation of the Rheb–mTOR pathway by NTFs is widely recognized as a fundamental nutrient signaling pathway [ , ]. Moreover, this pathway plays a pivotal role in promoting the expression of NTFs by enhancing transcription, mRNA translation, and cellular metabolism [ , ]. Specifically, Rheb-mTOR pathway activation via AAV-Rheb (S16H) enhances translation of BDNF mRNA [ ], cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element-binding protein (CREB)-mediated transcription [ ], transcription and secretion of GDNF [ ], and expression of CNTF [ ]. The upregulation of BDNF and GDNF via Rheb–mTORC1 activation are involved in the interplay between neurons and astrocytes that further feedbacks via production of CNTF and GDNF from astrocytes to aid neuroprotection ( ) [ ]. In addition, there is evidence that mTOR pathway activation induces upregulation of VEGF-A mRNA [ ], IGF-1 [ ], and NGF [ ], but they are not yet confirmed in the neurons.
 

## NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES AND DISRUPTION OF NEUROTROPHIC FACTORS

## Alzheimer's disease
AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia, accompanied by symptoms including memory loss, impaired reasoning and judgment, language difficulties, changes in behavior, and personality [ ]. Age, genetics (e.g., APOE ε4 allele), cardiovascular health, inflammation, and oxidative stress have been reported as risk factors [ , ]. In the progression of AD, extracellular protein amyloid-beta (Aβ) accumulates to form Aβ plaques, disrupting neuronal communication and triggering inflammation [ ]. In addition, hyperphosphorylated tau protein leads to microtubule destabilization, accumulates into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), and induces neuronal death [ ]. The synaptic and neuronal loss is distinct, especially in the hippocampus and cortex [ ]. Meanwhile, NTFs are disrupted in AD patients and are expected to contribute to the progression of the disease.
BDNF mRNA expression is decreased in the hippocampal and temporal cortical neurons in AD brains [ ], which are critical regions for synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. Meanwhile, TrkB receptors, which bind BDNF with high affinity, are also either downregulated or exhibit impaired function in AD [ ]. Furthermore, BDNF transcription and signaling are negatively affected by Aβ oligomers and tau pathology [ , ]. The Val66Met polymorphism in BDNF leads to reduced activity-dependent BDNF secretion and is linked to higher AD risk and earlier cognitive decline [ ]. Thus, disrupted BDNF signaling contributes to accelerated neuronal atrophy, dendritic degeneration, impaired synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation (LTP), diminished memory formation, and reduced neuronal resistance to oxidative stress and neurotoxins in AD [ ].
NGF signaling is disrupted in both preclinical and clinical stages of AD due to the impaired maturation of precursor of nerve growth factor (proNGF) and increased degradation of mature NGF in the frontal cortex [ ]. Additionally, defects in the retrograde axonal transport of mature NGF exacerbate the imbalance between proNGF and mature NGF levels [ ]. The expression of TrkA, a high-affinity receptor for NGF, is also reduced in cholinergic neurons [ ]. This shift promotes proapoptotic signaling through the proNGF–p75NTR pathway, contributing to the degeneration and loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons. These changes result in impaired attention, learning, and memory [ ], suggesting a strong link between NGF dysfunction, cognitive decline, and cholinergic system deficits in AD.
Moreover, the IGF-1 levels are decreased in AD in both the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [ ] and serum [ ]. Additionally, IGF1Rs in neurons are either downregulated or exhibit impaired function [ ]. AD neurons also tend to develop resistance to IGF1R and insulin signaling [ ], which disrupts downstream PI3K/Akt pathways [ ]. Subsequently, IGF-1 dysfunction in AD contributes to reduced Aβ clearance due to impaired autophagy and phagocytosis, weakened neuroprotective and antiapoptotic mechanisms, diminished glucose metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired synaptic plasticity, particularly in hippocampal neurons—ultimately leading to cognitive decline [ ].
Although direct evidence of CNTF disruption in AD patients remains limited, prior studies have shown that lower CNTF gene expression is associated with poorer cognitive function in diabetic individuals [ ], suggesting a possible role in dementia, including AD. This role may involve reduced expression of CNTF receptor components such as CNTFRα, LIFR, and gp130, leading to impaired downstream signaling. Meanwhile, CNTF deficiency can compromise astrocyte-mediated neuronal support and reduce cholinergic neuron survival, contributing to memory impairments. Additionally, CNTF deficiency weakens the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway, which is essential for neuronal maintenance and repair [ ].
Rheb expression is reduced in the prefrontal cortex of post-mortem AD brains and shows an inverse correlation with beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) [ ], a key enzyme involved in Aβ production, suggesting a potential link to Aβ pathology. Rheb also exhibits neuroprotective effects in hippocampal neurons, protecting these neurons from thrombin-induced cell death, which is relevant to AD-like neurodegeneration [ ]. Thus, Rheb downregulation leads to decreased mTORC1 activation, a pathway essential for regulating protein synthesis, cellular metabolism, neuronal survival, and synaptic integrity. Notably, mTOR signaling dysregulation is considered a contributing factor in the pathogenesis of AD [ ]. As a result, reduced Rheb impairs BDNF-mediated neurotrophic signaling and increases neuronal susceptibility to oxidative and metabolic stress.

## Parkinson’s disease
PD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder primarily affecting motor function, caused by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta of the brain, accompanied by symptoms including motor (bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, postural instability) and nonmotor (depression, cognitive impairment, sleep disorders, autonomic dysfunction) disorders [ ]. Age, genetics [e.g., synuclein alpha (SNCA), leucine rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)], and environmental toxins (e.g., pesticides) have been identified as risk factors of PD. In the progression of PD, intracellular protein α-synuclein aggregates into Lewy bodies, which cause neuronal dysfunction and cell death [ ]. In addition, neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction also contribute to the progression of PD [ ]. NTFs are disrupted in PD patients and are expected to contribute to the progression of the disease.
The GDNF protein levels are reduced in the substantia nigra pars compacta in PD [ ]. Meanwhile, impaired retrograde transport of GDNF from the striatum to dopaminergic neuron cell bodies contributes to neuronal degeneration [ ]. Additionally, decreased expression of the GFRα1 and RET receptors further disrupts GDNF signaling [ ]. This loss of neuroprotective support accelerates neuronal degeneration, impairs neuronal repair mechanisms, and hampers dopamine synthesis [ ]. As a result, dopaminergic neurons become increasingly susceptible to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.
BDNF levels are also diminished in dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta of PD patients [ , ]. Additionally, the expression of TrkB, a high-affinity receptor of BDNF, is reduced in this region [ ]. Factors such as α-synuclein accumulation, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation have been shown to suppress BDNF transcription [ ]. This decline in BDNF signaling may worsen α-synuclein-induced toxicity, disrupt synaptic plasticity, and compromise the survival of dopaminergic neurons, ultimately contributing to the impairments in motor learning and cognitive functions associated with PD.
Moreover, CNTF expression is reduced in the substantia nigra pars compacta of PD patients [ ]. Astrocyte dysfunction, a common feature in PD, further contributes to decreased CNTF secretion [ ]. Inflammatory cytokines may also interfere with CNTF-induced JAK/STAT signaling [ ]. These findings suggest that disruption of the CNTF pathway leads to a loss of neurotrophic support for dopaminergic neurons, diminished resilience to excitotoxic and inflammatory insults, increased stress sensitivity, and impaired glial–neuronal communication—factors essential for neuroprotection and neural repair.
Conversely, IGF-1 levels are elevated in both the serum and CSF of PD patients [ - ], which may reflect a compensatory or “fight-to-injury” response [ ]. Notably, one study reported that IGF-1 levels are more strongly associated with the progression of nonmotor symptoms than motor symptoms in PD [ ]. However, IGF1R expression is decreased in regions, including the frontal white matter and amygdala, and insulin/IGF-1 signaling is disrupted due to persistent inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. This impairment also affects the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [ ]. Thus, IGF-1 pathway disruption leads to mitochondrial instability, increased oxidative stress, impaired neuronal energy metabolism and survival, and reduced autophagic clearance of toxic proteins, such as α-synuclein, ultimately compromising the viability of dopaminergic neurons.

## Huntington’s disease
HD is a rare, inherited neurodegenerative disorder that is characterized by progressive motor dysfunction, cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms [ ]. HD is an autosomal dominantly inherited disorder caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the huntingtin (HTT) gene, which leads to the production of a toxic, mutant form of the HTT protein (mHTT) [ ]. In HD, toxic mutant huntingtin protein aggregates predominantly affect the striatum and cortex [ ]. Meanwhile, NTFs are disrupted in HD patients and are expected to contribute to the progression of the disease.
In HD, BDNF levels and transcription are reduced in the cortex [ , ], caudate [ ], putamen [ ], and serum [ ]. The mHTT protein suppresses BDNF gene transcription by interfering with key transcription factors such as CREB and repressor element 1 silencing transcription factor (REST)/neuron-restrictive silencer factor (NRSF) [ ]. Additionally, mHTT disrupts microtubule dynamics, impairing axonal transport of BDNF from the cortex to the striatum [ ]. TrkB, the high-affinity receptor for BDNF, is also downregulated in striatal neurons [ ]. Together, these disruptions lead to a loss of BDNF-mediated trophic support, resulting in cortical and striatal neuron degeneration, impaired synaptic plasticity, deficits in learning and motor control, and accelerated cognitive and behavioral deterioration.
IGF-1 levels are also reduced in both serum [ - ] and plasma [ ]. However, IGF-1 treatment has been shown to protect striatal neurons from mHTT-induced cell death through Akt signaling cascade [ ]. Additionally, chronic neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in HD may further impair IGF-1 signaling [ ]. Therefore, diminished IGF-1 leads to reduced Akt-mediated antiapoptotic signaling, contributing to increased neuronal loss, impaired energy metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, decreased neuronal growth, and weakened neurogenesis and repair mechanisms.
Furthermore, mTOR activity is decreased in the striatal neurons of both HD patients and animal models [ ]. Moreover, mHTT has been shown to disrupt the Rheb–mTORC1 signaling pathway, although this finding was reported in a cardiac model [ ]. This disruption resulted in impaired autophagy and the accumulation of mHTT, leading to increased cellular stress, disturbed protein homeostasis, and neurodegeneration. Additionally, the breakdown in mTOR–BDNF signaling was shown to compromise neuronal survival mechanisms further [ ].

## Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
ALS is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that primarily affects motor neurons, the nerve cells responsible for controlling voluntary muscles. ALS is characterized by the loss of upper and lower motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord, as well as progressive muscle weakness, spasticity, and paralysis, along with difficulties in speaking, swallowing, and breathing [ ]. ALS is mainly sporadic and 5~10% are familial, often linked to gene mutations [e.g., superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), TAR DNA binding protein (TARDBP), fused in sarcoma (FUS), chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9orf72)] [ ], involved in oxidative stress, glutamate excitotoxicity, protein aggregation [e.g., transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43), FUS, and SOD1], mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation [ ]. NTFs are disrupted in ALS patients and are expected to contribute to the progression of the disease.
CNTF expression is diminished in the spinal cords of individuals with ALS [ , ]. Since astrocytes are a major source of CNTF, the dysfunction of astrocytes in ALS likely contributes to reduced CNTF secretion. Additionally, CNTF signaling through the JAK/STAT pathway is impaired, weakening the neuroprotective functions provided by this pathway. This suppression of the CNTF pathway may lead to a loss of trophic support for spinal motor neurons, hastened neuronal degeneration and muscle wasting, and impaired compensatory mechanisms during the later stages of the disease [ ].
The GDNF levels are also reduced in the serum of ALS patients [ ]. Moreover, loss of astrocyte-mediated GDNF signaling exacerbates disease progression in ALS models [ ]. The disruption of GDNF signaling has been shown to promote decreased motor neuron survival and maintenance, increased sensitivity to oxidative stress and excitotoxic insults, and deterioration of neuromuscular junctions.
Findings relating to the BDNF levels in the serum of ALS patients have been inconsistently reported: some studies show increased levels, while others report reductions. However, specific ALS subgroups, such as C9orf72 mutation carriers [ ] or individuals with depressive symptoms and lower cognitive performance [ ], exhibit notably lower BDNF levels. The TrkB receptor, a high-affinity receptor of BDNF, is often downregulated or functionally compromised in ALS. In addition, the truncated TrkB isoform, TrkB.T1, is upregulated and acts as a dominant-negative regulator, inhibiting full TrkB signaling [ ]. These abnormalities in BDNF signaling contribute to decreased synaptic plasticity, reduced motor neuron resilience, impaired axonal regeneration, and heightened neuronal vulnerability to stress.
IGF-1 expression is reported to be decreased in the serum of ALS patients [ ], while CSF levels have also exhibited a decline in some studies [ ], although findings are not entirely consistent [ ]. Inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and motor neuron damage suppress IGF1R signaling, a dysfunction that extends to downstream effectors, such as the Akt and mTOR pathways. Moreover, reduced IGF-1 activity leads to impaired protein synthesis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal loss, as well as decreased autophagy, resulting in the accumulation of toxic proteins such as TDP-43 and SOD1, along with accelerated axonal degeneration and muscle denervation.
VEGF levels are reduced in motor neurons within the CSF [ , ] and spinal cord [ ] of ALS patients. This decrease is particularly evident in motor neurons and astrocytes, where VEGFR-2 signaling is also impaired, reducing neurovascular support. Lower VEGF expression contributes to compromised blood–spinal cord barrier (BSCB) integrity, leading to hypoxia and insufficient nutrient delivery to motor neurons. In addition, reduced VEGF expression weakens angiogenesis, neuroprotection, and glial support, further exacerbating motor neuron vulnerability.

## Spinocerebellar ataxia type 2
SCA2 is a rare, inherited neurodegenerative disorder that belongs to the group of spinocerebellar ataxias. The cerebellum and brainstem are primarily impacted, accompanied by symptoms including ataxia (impaired balance and coordination), slow saccadic eye movements, dysarthria, muscle stiffness, tremors, and, in later stages, Parkinsonism, dementia, and neuropathy [ ]. SCA2 has a strong genetic background (autosomal dominant) that is caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the ataxin 2 (ATXN2) gene on chromosome 12, leading to the production of an abnormally long polyglutamine tract in the ataxin-2 protein, which becomes toxic to neurons [ ]. Notably, more repeats are known to cause earlier onset and more severe disease [ ]. During the progression of SCA2, Purkinje cells in the cerebellum undergo progressive degeneration, and the brainstem, spinal cord, and cerebral cortex are also involved [ ]. Motor neurons are also affected in the later stage of the disease [ ]. While direct evidence of NTF disruption has not been presented in SCA2 patients, evidence has been presented in animal models of SCA2. Therefore, NTFs are potentially downregulated and are expected to contribute to the progression of the disease in a clinical context.
The BDNF levels were reduced in the Purkinje cells and other cerebellar neurons in a SCA2 mouse model [ ]. The mutant ATXN2 protein is known to disrupt transcriptional regulation and RNA processing [ ], contributing to decreased BDNF expression. Additionally, anterograde transport of BDNF to its target neurons, such as Purkinje cells, may be impaired. This potential decline in BDNF compromises synaptic plasticity and neuroprotection in cerebellar neurons, heightens the vulnerability of Purkinje and brainstem neurons to stress and degeneration, and worsens motor coordination deficits associated with cerebellar atrophy.
Similarly, GDNF expression is diminished in the cerebellum of the SCA2 mouse model [ ], while reductions were also observed in the spinal cord and brainstem—regions heavily influential in motor function. Astrocyte dysfunction, which impairs glial-derived GDNF production, further limits the availability of GDNF. This leads to weakened support for motor neurons due to impaired GFRα1/RET signaling, particularly in the spinal cord [ ], and contributes to instability at neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) and muscle denervation. These effects may promote motor neuron loss and muscle weakness, resembling the ALS-like symptoms occasionally observed in SCA2.
Additionally, CNTF levels were decreased in the cerebellum of the same SCA2 model [ ]. This reduction likely impairs survival signaling through the CNTF receptor complex [ ], particularly affecting lower motor neurons and brainstem nuclei, and resulting in increased motor neuron degeneration, worsening muscle weakness, and further breakdown of glial–neuronal interactions, all of which may accelerate disease progression.
As listed above, numerous reports have demonstrated that the expression of NTFs and their signaling pathways is disrupted in the major neurodegenerative diseases ( ), suggesting that the sustained expression levels of NTFs are critically involved in neuronal survival, protection, regeneration, repair, synaptic plasticity, inflammation, and glial modulation; therefore, disruption of NTFs may cause critical failure of neurons, disease progression, and clinical disorders ( ).
 

## NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES AND THERAPEUTIC APPROACHES OF NEUROTROPHIC FACTORS

## Therapeutic potential
The progression of neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, PD, HD, ALS, and SCA2, is often accompanied by the progressive loss of specific neuronal populations. In addition, NTF expression levels are disrupted, and their functions are compromised in neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, it is interesting that introducing NTFs into the affected area as a beneficial therapeutic approach to restore neuronal homeostasis and even enhances the clearance of pathological proteins that accumulate during the progression of the disease. Moreover, many studies have reported that the delivery of NTFs not previously known to be involved in the specific disease also exhibits neuroprotection and restores neuronal functionality in the diseased area.

## Therapeutic strategies and delivery methods
Numerous strategies have been developed to deliver NTFs to affected regions in patients with neurodegenerative diseases. Among these, direct infusion of recombinant NTFs has been widely used in early preclinical and clinical trials [ , - ]. Delivery methods include intracerebroventricular (ICV) infusion, peripheral injection, intranasal administration, or direct injection into the targeted brain regions. These approaches bypass issues related to gene expression by supplying active protein directly. However, due to the inability of most NTFs to cross the BBB, directly delivering NFTs often requires invasive stereotaxic procedures. Additionally, the short half-life of NTFs necessitates repeated administrations, and the risk of off-target effects may lead to undesirable side effects, limiting the clinical utility of NTFs [ ].
Therefore, viral vector-based gene therapy has been explored to overcome these limitations [ ]. Viral vector-based gene therapy employs vectors such as AAV or the lentivirus to deliver NTF-related genes into specific brain regions, allowing localized, long-term expression after a single injection. Cell-type specificity can also be achieved by using targeted promoters, reducing the likelihood of off-target effects. Nevertheless, challenges remain in gene therapy, such as poor dose control, potential overexpression, and safety risks. Specifically for vectors, AAV has a limited loading capacity, and lentiviral vectors carry a risk of insertional mutagenesis [ ].
Cell-based therapies represent another promising approach [ ]. Engineered cells, including NSCs, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), NTF-secreting cells, and encapsulated cell systems (e.g., NT-501 for CNTF delivery), can be transplanted to provide sustained NTF production. Meanwhile, in addition to trophic support, these cells may offer immunomodulatory and regenerative benefits, whereby the multipotency of these cells allows for the potential replacement of lost neurons or glial cells; many cell types also exhibit low immunogenicity. However, challenges include difficulties in sourcing, scaling, and standardizing cells, as well as ensuring cell survival post-transplantation. Tumorigenicity and other safety concerns also remain.
Alternatively, small molecules, drugs, and peptide-based mimetics can activate neurotrophic signaling pathways without the need for protein infusion. These include VEGFR agonists (e.g., VEGF-A/B/C), BDNF mimetics, such as 7,8-DHF, CNTF analogues such as Axokine, and various expression enhancers (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake Inhibitors (SSRI)s, histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, CREB modulators). These compounds are often designed to cross the BBB and can be formulated for improved biocompatibility and lower toxicity. Moreover, the advantages of these compounds include noninvasive delivery, easier dose regulation, and suitability for long-term, large-scale application. Nonetheless, similar to direct protein infusion, small molecules typically have short half-lives and require repeated administration to maintain therapeutic levels.
Overall, while diverse delivery strategies have been explored in both animal models and clinical studies, each comes with specific benefits and limitations; examples of these approaches will be presented in the following sections.

## Alzheimer’s disease
Since AD is characterized by neuronal loss, synaptic degeneration, and impaired neural plasticity, NTFs are being investigated as therapeutic agents to enhance neuronal survival, mitigate degeneration, and restore cognitive function, particularly targeting vulnerable brain regions, such as the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, parietal cortex, and basal forebrain.
Multiple delivery strategies for BDNF have been investigated, including direct infusion, viral vectors (e.g., AAVs), stem cell-based methods, and small molecules that either activate TrkB receptors or enhance endogenous BDNF expression. In animal models of AD, direct BDNF infusion into regions, such as the entorhinal cortex [ ] or hippocampus [ ], has reduced neuronal loss and improved cognitive function. Intranasal administration, which bypasses the BBB, has also promoted memory improvement in AD11 mice [ ]. When administered intravenously with a BBB-modulating agent (Ac-CDTPPVC-NH2, or ADTC5), BDNF significantly enhanced cognition in amyloid precursor protein (APP)/presenilin 1 (PS1) mice compared to BDNF alone [ ]. AAV-mediated BDNF expression in the ventricular system [ ] or hippocampus [ ] reduced neuronal degeneration and behavioral deficits in transgenic AD mice. BDNF also prevented Aβ-induced neurotoxicity in vitro [ ]. Other strategies include BDNF delivery via a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-derived membrane-penetrating peptide [ ] or genetically engineered astrocyte-expressing BDNF in a 5xFAD model [ ], both of which improved AD-like symptoms and cognition. Additionally, small molecules, such as 7,8-dihydrocannabinol (7,8-DHC) [ ], 7,8-dihydroxyflavone (7,8-DHF) [ ], neuropep-1 [ ], and peptide 021 [ ], as well as the viral delivery of CREB-binding protein, have all been shown to elevate BDNF levels and yield similar benefits. Clinical trials are currently underway for AAV-BDNF (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05040217) and a BDNF mimetic NP03 (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05423522), although the results of these trials have yet to be released. While human data remain limited, extensive preclinical studies support the therapeutic potential of BDNF.
NGF-based therapies have also been tested in AD models. For example, AAV-mediated NGF expression targeted to basal forebrain cholinergic neurons in a fimbria–fornix lesion model promoted neuroprotection and functional recovery [ ]. Liposomal nanoparticle delivery successfully crossed the BBB in vitro and reduced soluble and insoluble Aβ while increasing synaptic markers in vivo in APP/PS1 mice [ ]. Small molecules, such as BNN27 [ ] and J147 [ ], have further enhanced cognition in AD models. Clinical trials involving direct intraventricular infusion of NGF in AD patients have presented some cognitive benefit [ , ] but also led to adverse effects [ ], highlighting the limitations of this approach. AAV-mediated NGF delivery has produced mixed outcomes; some studies showed benefit [ , ], while others reported no improvement despite successful transduction [ - ], suggesting that refinement in delivery technique is needed. Encapsulated NGF-secreting cell implants demonstrated successful delivery without side effects, but did not yield further functional benefits. While NGF has shown promise in preclinical models, safety and efficacy concerns are currently limiting clinical applications.
Low serum IGF-1 levels have been linked to brain atrophy and increased AD risk [ ], suggesting that IGF-1 supplementation could be beneficial. However, paradoxically, higher IGF1R activity has also been associated with increased dementia prevalence [ ], complicating the therapeutic use of IGF-1. In AD animal models, intracerebroventricular IGF-1 administration in a streptozotocin-induced model reduced inflammation and Aβ accumulation, improving spatial memory [ ]. Subcutaneous injection of IGF-1-loaded microspheres in APP/ presenilin 2 (PS2) mice similarly improved cognition and reduced Aβ burden [ ]. However, some studies reported no effect on Aβ clearance [ ] or cognitive improvement [ ]. Notably, an IGF1R inhibitor reduced neuroinflammation in another AD model [ ]. Clinical trials remain sparse, and the inconsistent preclinical findings make IGF-1 a more complex and less validated therapeutic candidate.
Although CNTF is not directly implicated in AD pathology, the expression of CNTF has been inversely correlated with cognitive function in diabetic patients [ ], suggesting relevance in dementia. CNTF is known to exhibit neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties. Meanwhile, delivery approaches include encapsulated cell therapy and CNTF-mimetic peptides. In preclinical models, encapsulated CNTF-secreting cell transplantation into Tg2576 AD mice preserved cognition and prevented neuronal and synaptic degeneration [ ]. Active CNTF fragments (peptides 6 and 6A) have also promoted neurogenesis in APP transgenic mice [ ]. However, clinical research on CNTF in AD remains lacking, while limited animal studies suggest that more extensive investigations are required before CNTF can be validated as a therapeutic option.
Rheb expression is reduced in the prefrontal cortex of AD patients and negatively correlates with BACE1, which is involved in Aβ production [ ]. Notably, disruption of downstream mTOR signaling, where Rheb plays a key role, is considered a risk factor in AD [ ]. As Rheb and the subsequent downstream mTOR signaling are involved in AD, the benefits of both have been investigated in relation to AD [ , ]. Interestingly, hippocampal delivery of AAV-Rheb (S16H) in 5xFAD mice enhanced the expression of BDNF, CNTF, TrkB, and CNTFRα, while also suppressing Aβ production and improving LTP and cognitive outcomes [ ]. Another study showed that activating the Rheb–mTOR pathway in Aβ-injected rats reversed memory deficits [ ]. However, clinical studies on Rheb-based therapies remain limited, and further validation in animal models is necessary before advancing to human trials.
There are many promising results from animal model studies utilizing NTFs on AD pathology; however, caution remains required when applying the results to clinical aspects, as some cases have shown that NTFs were well-delivered but failed to exhibit beneficial effects. Further refinement is essential.

## Parkinson’s disease
In PD, NTFs have been studied for their potential to protect, restore, or regenerate dopaminergic neurons, to slow disease progression or reverse neuronal loss—primarily targeting the dopaminergic pathways in the substantia nigra and striatum of the midbrain.
Preclinical studies have demonstrated that inducing GDNF expression through AAV [ , ] or lentiviral vectors [ ] in PD rodent models leads to behavioral improvements and neuroprotection. Moreover, intracerebral delivery of recombinant GDNF in 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-lesioned rhesus monkeys alleviated core PD symptoms [ ]. Additionally, delivering GDNF via macrophage/microglia-based stem cells in MPTP mice preserved nigral dopaminergic neurons and their striatal projections [ ]. A modified GDNF protein fused to destabilizing domains enhanced motor function in the 6-hydroxydopamine hydrobromide (6-OHDA) mouse model [ ]. However, clinical trials involving ICV-mediated GDNF infusion did not yield functional improvements [ ]. In contrast, AAV-GDNF delivery in PD patients resulted in increased GDNF expression, enhanced tyrosine hydroxylase activity, elevated catecholamine levels, and improved PET imaging signals, indicating regenerative potential [ ]. Together, these findings suggest that gene or cell-based delivery of GDNF may offer more therapeutic benefit than direct protein infusion.
Though not directly implicated in PD pathology, neurturin shows similar therapeutic potential. In rodent models, AAV-mediated neurturin delivery (e.g., CERE-120 or AAV2) into the striatum conferred dopaminergic neuron protection in 6-OHDA-lesioned rats [ , ]. NSCs engineered to secrete neurturin also exhibited neuroprotective effects in similar models [ ]. Clinically, AAV-based delivery (primarily CERE-120) represents the main method of application [ - ]. However, while some human studies showed modest benefit [ ], others failed to demonstrate significant improvements [ , ], suggesting that delivery methods require further optimization for clinical efficacy.
Several approaches have been applied to enhance BDNF signaling in PD models, including viral delivery, mimetics, and stem cell therapy. Meanwhile, AAV-BDNF injection into the lateral ventricles of MPTP-treated mice has been shown to mitigate both motor and cognitive deficits [ ]. Lentiviral-mediated BDNF overexpression in MPTP mice activated the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, promoting autophagy, reducing α-synuclein aggregation, and decreasing neuronal damage [ ]. Exosomes derived from human umbilical cord MSCs (hUC-MSCs) carrying BDNF protected neurons from ferroptosis in both in vitro and in vivo PD models [ ]. Small molecules, such as the TrkB agonist CF3CN [ ] and the natural compound sericin [ ], also activated BDNF signaling and improved motor outcomes. However, clinical trials specifically targeting BDNF in PD are limited, despite strong preclinical support.
While direct application of CNTF in PD models is sparse, activation of astrocytic CNTF production via transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channel has been shown to enhance neuroprotection in PD rat models [ , ]. Nonetheless, despite the potential of CNTF, this protein has yet to be evaluated in clinical PD trials, and more research is needed to validate its therapeutic relevance and optimize its delivery.
IGF-1 has been delivered through direct infusion, viral vectors, and cell transplantation in PD models. In MPTP-injected mice, IGF-1 infusion into the lateral ventricle reduced autophagy and protected against motor dysfunction and dopaminergic neuronal loss [ ]. Moreover, adenoviral-mediated IGF-1 delivery into the caudate-putamen (CPu) improved cognitive performance and increased tyrosine hydroxylase expression in the 6-OHDA model [ ]. However, lentiviral IGF-1 delivery into the striatum did not yield neuroprotection or behavioral improvements [ ]. Transplantation of human neural progenitor cells secreting IGF-1 protected substantia nigra neurons and alleviated behavioral symptoms [ ]. Although no clinical trials have been conducted in PD patients, animal studies suggest IGF-1 has therapeutic potential, pending refinement in delivery methods.
While Rheb has yet to be studied clinically in PD patients, the therapeutic potential of Rheb is supported by preclinical research. AAV-mediated delivery of Rheb into the substantia nigra activates mTOR signaling, providing neuroprotective effects [ , ]. Constitutively active Rheb (AAV1-Rheb (S16H)) has been shown to counteract PD-like pathology induced by 6-OHDA [ ] or prothrombin kringle-2 (pKr-2) [ ]. These results highlight Rheb as a promising candidate for future clinical trials targeting mTOR-related neurodegeneration in PD.
Overall, preclinical evidence supports the potential of several NTFs in PD, but clinical translation remains limited, and delivery methods need further optimization.

## Huntington’s disease
Since HD involves disruptions in neurotrophic signaling, synaptic integrity, and cellular metabolism, NTFs have been applied to address these deficits, primarily targeting neurons in the striatum and cortex [ - ].
Various strategies to enhance BDNF signaling in HD include long-term expression via viral vectors, epigenetic regulators, and BDNF-secreting cell transplants to provide localized trophic support. Recombinant BDNF delivered subcutaneously [ ], AAV-BDNF injected into the striatum [ ], cortical transplantation of BDNF-expressing NSCs [ ], and transplantations of MSCs [ ], striatal progenitor cells [ ], or dopamine D1 receptor (D1R)/dopamine D2 receptor (D2R) neurons [ ] all produced neuroprotective effects and improved disease phenotypes in HD rodent models (R6/2, YAC128, and QA-lesioned). Meanwhile, striatal neurons were preserved, and motor deficits were reduced in double-transgenic models (YAC128 [ ], R6/2 [ ]) overexpressing BDNF. Pridopidine, a selective sigma-1 receptor (S1R) agonist developed for HD, has been shown to restore BDNF and TrkB trafficking in HD neurons [ ]. While clinical data remain limited, studies using BDNF-secreting MSCs reported no adverse effects [ , ]. Despite the lack of robust clinical evidence, animal studies strongly support BDNF-based approaches as promising treatments for HD.
Although CNTF has not been shown to change with HD progression, CNTF supports striatal neuron survival [ ], astrocyte differentiation, and glial regulation, making CNTF a potential therapeutic candidate. Investigated delivery methods include gene therapy via viral vectors and encapsulated cell systems (e.g., NT-501). In animal studies, CNTF expression via viral vectors reduced striatal damage in QA-lesioned models [ ], and transplantation of CNTF-secreting stem cells or encapsulated cells protected striatal neurons in rodent [ , - ] and non-human primate models [ ]. Although clinical trials for CNTF in HD have yet to be performed, a delivery platform has been designed for future use [ ], and preclinical data on CNTF support the continued development of its delivery.
While there is no direct evidence of decreased GDNF in HD, GDNF plays a key role in the survival and function of striatal medium spiny neurons and dopaminergic circuits. Delivery options include viral vectors, plasmid-liposome infusion, and cell-based transplantation. In rodent HD models, AAV-GDNF delivered into the striatum provided neuronal protection [ , , ], with one study also reporting reduced mHTT inclusions in N171-82Q mice [ ]. Furthermore, GDNF-expressing neural progenitor cell transplantation improved motor behavior in the same model [ ]. A GDNF plasmid–liposome complex delivered with focused ultrasound enhanced gene transfer and neuronal survival [ ]. Although clinical trials are lacking, these findings suggest GDNF has therapeutic value in HD.
Given reports of insulin and IGF-1 resistance in HD, direct infusion of recombinant protein has been explored. Intranasal administration of recombinant IGF-1 in YAC128 mice improved motor performance and corrected metabolic imbalances [ ]. Peripheral IGF-1 infusion in R6/2 mice offered protection against diabetes-like features, though the infusion method did not significantly improve motor outcomes [ ]. Despite the absence of clinical trials, these research results suggest that IGF-1, even when delivered systemically or intranasally, may offer therapeutic benefit.
Although FGF-2 is not directly altered in HD, the role of FGF-2 in neurogenesis and cell proliferation makes this protein a candidate for therapeutic exploration. Subcutaneous FGF-2 administration in R6/2 mice promoted enhanced cell proliferation, motor function, and lifespan [ ]. However, no clinical trials on FGF-2 administration have been conducted, and additional research is necessary to evaluate the therapeutic potential of FGF-2 in HD.
Interestingly, Rheb has not specifically been directly implicated in HD pathology, but as a key activator of mTOR—which is downregulated in HD brains [ ]—Rheb presents a promising therapeutic target. Gene therapy using constitutively active Rheb (S16H) via AAV delivery improved striatal function and reversed disease phenotypes in the N171-82Q mouse model [ ]. However, despite these promising findings, clinical validation remains required.
Overall, BDNF, GDNF, and CNTF represent the most promising NTFs in HD, with solid preclinical support, although clinical translation remains a major challenge across all candidates.

## Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
NTFs have been explored in ALS research for their ability to protect motor neurons, support axonal regeneration, and slow disease progression, primarily targeting the motor cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord.
Therapeutic strategies involving CNTF aim to deliver it to regions affected by motor neuron degeneration. While CNTF has not been directly evaluated in standard ALS models, studies using the progressive motor neuronopathy (PMN) mouse model—characterized by lower motor neuron loss—showed that intraperitoneal injection of CNTF-expressing embryonic stem cells reduced disease progression and extended survival [ ]. Clinically, subcutaneous rhCNTF injections did not improve symptoms or survival in ALS patients and instead caused side effects, including anorexia, weight loss, and coughing [ , ]. Another approach included using encapsulated baby hamster kidney cells engineered to release CNTF into CSF via intrathecal implantation, which showed safe delivery without reported side effects [ ]. However, the therapeutic effects of CNTF were not assessed. Thus, the short half-life and inability of CNTF to cross the BBB remain key obstacles, emphasizing the need for optimized delivery strategies.
GDNF delivery is considered beneficial for ALS but faces challenges when injected intrathecally, such as limited tissue diffusion and a short half-life. In animal models, expressing GDNF in skeletal muscles of SOD1G93A mice slowed disease progression and improved locomotion [ ]. Meanwhile, intramuscular [ ] or intravenous [ , ] injection of AAV-GDNF in SOD1G93A rats improved motor neuron survival and delayed disease progression. Transplants of GDNF-secreting neural progenitor cells [ - ] and human MSCs (hMSCs) [ ] also showed therapeutic effects in ALS models. Clinically, human neural progenitor cells engineered to produce GDNF (CNS10-NPC-GDNF) were transplanted into the lumbar spinal cords of ALS patients. Although the procedure safely resulted in continuous GDNF production, no changes in disease symptoms were reported [ ]. While animal studies are promising, clinical applications may require dose optimization and strategies to improve CNS distribution.
Although direct intrathecal or intramuscular injection of BDNF is possible, the administration of BDNF is limited by poor BBB permeability and a short half-life. Gene therapy using AAV vectors or cell transplantation with BDNF-secreting MSCs or neural progenitors offers sustained delivery at the site of need. TrkB receptor agonists, such as 7,8-DHF, provide an alternative approach, as these agonists can cross the BBB and activate BDNF signaling. Notably, viral delivery of BDNF prevented motor neuron death in axotomy ALS models [ ], and transplantation of BDNF-expressing neural progenitor cells protected motor neurons in SOD1G93A mice [ ]. BDNF-overexpressing hUC-MSC-derived motor neuron transplantation also enhanced motor function and extended survival [ ]. Additionally, 7,8-DHF improved motor function and supported lower motor neuron survival [ ]. Clinical trials involving intrathecal [ , ] or subcutaneous [ ] BDNF administration have been conducted, although only one study reported positive effects [ ]. Nonetheless, BDNF remains a promising target, but current challenges, such as reduced TrkB expression and delivery limitations, need to be addressed in future studies.
In preclinical ALS models, IGF-1 delivered via AAV vectors to cerebellar nuclei [ ], muscle [ ], or the ventricular system [ ] delayed disease progression. Genetic crossbreeding of SOD1G93A mice with transgenic mice overexpressing IGF-1 in muscle yielded mixed results, with some models showing delayed onset and progression [ ], whereas others reported no significant benefits [ ]. Clinical trials have also shown mixed outcomes: subcutaneous IGF-1 injections failed to improve symptoms [ , ], whereas intrathecal IGF-1 delivery showed significant benefits without serious side effects [ ]. The use of IGF-1 remains promising, but further investigations are needed to overcome limitations related to BBB penetration, short half-life, and reduced receptor sensitivity in ALS patients.
Therapeutic VEGF delivery options include recombinant proteins, gene therapy, and cell transplantation. In ALS models, VEGF expression induced by viral vectors into the intrathecal space [ ], ventricles [ ], spinal cord [ ], or muscles [ ] improved motor neuron survival and delayed disease progression. The transplantation of VEGF-expressing hMSCs extended lifespan and preserved motor function in SOD1G93A rats [ ]. Moreover, double-transgenic mice overexpressing VEGF showed reduced motor neuron loss and extended survival [ ]. Clinically, intracerebroventricular VEGF delivery was well tolerated for up to six years [ ], although no functional benefits were observed. Transplants of MSCs [ ] and NSCs [ ] have promoted some clinical improvements. However, despite the potential of VEGF therapy, this treatment method carries risks related to unwanted angiogenesis, including blood pressure changes, retinal effects, and tumor vascularization—necessitating careful dose regulation.
Although Rheb has not been directly studied in ALS, Rheb plays a central role in activating mTOR signaling, which is impaired in ALS. Meanwhile, mTOR inhibition accelerates motor neuron degeneration and reduces lifespan in ALS models [ ], whereas the activation of mTOR provides neuroprotection [ ]. Enhancing Rheb activity could help restore mTOR function, potentially supporting motor neuron survival, axon regeneration, and autophagy regulation.
While clinical trials involving NTFs in ALS have been limited and often yield inconsistent results due to challenges in dosing, delivery, and side effects, a wealth of animal model data supports the therapeutic potential of targeting neurotrophic pathways. Continued research and refinement in delivery technologies and treatment strategies are essential to translate these findings into effective clinical therapies.
Clinical trials on ALS with NTFs also exhibited limited trials and mixed results due to the variable roles of NTFs and optimization issues due to the dose and side effects. However, numerous animal studies have demonstrated the benefits of utilizing NTFs for treating ALS, which makes it worthwhile to continue efforts on the therapeutic approaches.

## Spinocerebellar ataxia type 2
NTFs have been investigated to rescue mainly Purkinje cells in the cerebellum and also, in some cases, motor neurons. Due to the rarity of SCA2, relatively few efforts have been made in animal model studies and therapeutic approaches for SCA2 remain limited in clinical studies.
Therapeutic strategies for SCA2 should prioritize the targeted delivery of BDNF and GDNF into the cerebellum, where neuronal vulnerability is prominent. Transplantation of neural progenitor cells or MSCs engineered to secrete BDNF or GDNF may offer sustained paracrine neurotrophic support. In a preclinical SCA2 mouse model, administration of hMSCs led to the rescue of Purkinje neurons, elevated cerebellar levels of both BDNF and GDNF, and a concurrent reduction in neuroinflammation, ultimately preserving locomotor function [ ]. Similar to BDNF and GDNF, CNTF levels are also diminished in the cerebellum of SCA2 models [ ]. In the same animal study using hMSCs, cerebellar CNTF levels were restored, supporting CNTF’s therapeutic influence in SCA2 [ ].
While Rheb has not been directly linked to SCA2 pathogenesis, Rheb remains a promising therapeutic target due to its role in activating the mTORC1 pathway. Thus, delivering AAV-Rheb into cerebellar Purkinje neurons or deep nuclei may stimulate mTORC1 activity, especially through the suppression of the tuberous sclerosis protein (TSC) 1/2 complex by IGF-1 or CNTF, thereby enhancing Rheb signaling. A recent study using a genetic SCA2 model demonstrated that AAV-mediated expression of a constitutively active Rheb mutant, Rheb (S16H), successfully reactivated mTORC1 signaling, preserved Purkinje cell integrity, and improved motor coordination [ ].
While clinical research on SCA2 is limited, such preclinical findings provide a strong foundation for future therapeutic development.

## CURRENT OBSTACLES AND PROSPECTS IN NTF-BASED THERAPIES FOR NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES
No clinical study has yet been approved for clinical applications and achieved commercialization, as prior studies have not proven sufficient to restore neuronal degeneration and/or improve cognitive or motor functionality in patients. These data may be attributed to the following factors.
1) The NTF doses are not high enough. As most NTFs have very short half-lives, even if they are introduced to the right region, they are quickly degraded without promoting any significant disease-modifying effects. Indirect application may also cause this issue. 2) The introduced region is not accurate or appropriate. The direct introduction of NTFs to the diseased region has failed in some cases due to difficulties with stereotaxic surgery on human patients. Hence, it has been suggested that the application of real-time magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can improve the accuracy of the surgery. Indirect applications (e.g., peritoneal, intraventricular injection) have also exhibited issues with delivery accuracy and may encounter difficulties crossing the BBB if injected peripherally. 3) The continuity of the supply of NTFs is not enough. Most of the earlier studies utilized the delivery of recombinant NTFs, which is typically a one-time application of NTFs and does not last long. Therefore, multiple invasive surgeries are required to sustain NTFs for a long period, which is not favorable in clinical practices.
Overcoming those factors will guarantee the effective application of NTFs to modify the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, efforts are already underway to bypass the limitations in animal models, as introduced throughout this review ( ); thus, we need to pay attention to and learn from any successful cases. For instance, the application of viral injections (e.g., AAV, lentivirus) as a gene therapy and the transplantations of NTF-expressing cells (e.g., encapsulated stem cells) will provide area-specific and continuous expression of target NTFs, thereby minimizing the requirements for invasive stereotaxic surgeries on patients. Application of certain drugs, processed to pass through the BBB, will make peripheral injections a more valid option when targeting the CNS. However, these positive results from animal studies have not been well translated into clinical trials; hence, these delivery methods require further refinement. Of course, a caveat of successful results from animal studies is that the various adverse side effects reported in clinical studies cannot be simply translated and monitored in animal models; therefore, this raises the necessity of conducting safety tests on the application of NTFs.
 Additionally, many NTFs exist in both precursor (pro-form) and mature forms, and these two distinct forms have opposite effects on cellular signaling, which is a crucial aspect to consider when evaluating the therapeutic efficiency of neurotrophic factors in neurodegenerative diseases [ - ]. Specifically, proBDNF preferentially binds to the p75NTR/sortilin receptor complex to initiate pro-apoptotic signaling cascades, whereas mature BDNF activates TrkB receptors to promote neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity [ , ]. Similarly, proNGF functions as a high-affinity ligand for the p75NTR/sortilin complex, triggering neuronal apoptosis through JNK3 and caspase activation, while mature NGF binds to TrkA receptors to mediate neuroprotective effects [ , ]. Importantly, transgenic mouse studies have demonstrated that elevated proNGF/NGF ratios serve as upstream drivers of cholinergic neurodegeneration and amyloid β pathology in AD models [ ]. Consistent with these preclinical findings, post-mortem analysis of human AD brains revealed increased proNGF accumulation concurrent with reduced TrkA expression, indicating a pathological shift toward pro-apoptotic signaling [ , , ]. The therapeutic implications of these opposing effects necessitate sophisticated treatment strategies that target total neurotrophin levels and the pro/mature neurotrophin balance through multiple mechanisms. These approaches include modulation of processing enzymes such as furin and plasmin that convert precursor forms to mature neurotrophins, development of selective antagonists targeting p75NTR/sortilin receptor complexes, and establishment of preferential delivery systems for mature neurotrophins to restore physiological signaling balance [ , ]. Recent clinical trials have begun to validate this approach, with the p75NTR modulator LM11A-31 demonstrating safety and biomarker efficacy in patients with mild-to-moderate AD [ ].

## CONCLUSION
In summary, NTFs play crucial roles in neuronal growth, proliferation, functionality, and survival; therefore, altered expression, protein levels, and activities in specific areas of the CNS or PNS may impact neuronal homeostasis and lead to deleterious consequences ( ). Each neurodegenerative disease has a distinct profile of NTFs that are affected by the progression of the disease, either directly by pathological protein oligomers or indirectly through altered signaling pathways. This suggests that introducing the impacted NTFs into the region can be potentially utilized as a therapeutic approach to these diseases. Numerous studies have demonstrated that supplementing NTFs in the impacted area successfully improved neuronal survival and eventually restored cognitive and/or motor functions in the animal models of these neurodegenerative diseases by utilizing various delivery methods, including direct infusion of the recombinant NTFs, gene therapy with delivery of AAV or lentivirus, transplantation of NTF-expressing cells, and small molecules that mimic NTFs. However, many hurdles exist that must be overcome in relation to the actual application of those delivery methods in clinical trials. Delivery efficiency, adverse side effects, and dose optimization may require further refinements. A major issue identified in the clinical studies was that the delivery of the NTFs into the region of interest was successful in most cases, but did not significantly restore or protect the neurons or cognitive and motor functions. As multiple NTFs are impacted in each neurodegenerative disease, it raises the idea that the combined introduction of multiple NTFs or other signaling enhancers through combinations of various AAVs as cocktail, with stem cells, can supplement multiple NTFs, as well as combinations of agonists/enhancers, or combinations of any delivery methods that can promote synergistic improvements on rectifying the progression of neurodegenerative diseases.
