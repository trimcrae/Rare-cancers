## Abstract

## Purpose:
Doxorubicin is standard chemotherapy for metastatic soft tissue sarcomas (STS) but also enhances innate/adaptive immune responses by inducing immunogenic cell death. Most STS are immune “cold” tumors that do not respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) blocking PD-1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4. We hypothesized that concurrent doxorubicin would improve tumor immunogenicity and boost the efficacy of ICI in STS.

## Patients and Methods:
We conducted a single-arm, phase 2 trial of doxorubicin plus zalifrelimab (anti–cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 antibody) and balstilimab (anti–PD-1 antibody) for patients with advanced/metastatic STS without prior doxorubicin or ICI (NCT04028063). The study was a Simon minimax two-stage design to accrue 28 patients evaluable for primary endpoint of progression-free survival rate at 6 months (PFS6mo) by RECIST 1.1. The study aimed to improve PFS6mo by 20% over a historic null rate of 43.4% with doxorubicin monotherapy. Secondary endpoints included the objective response rate, disease control rate, overall survival, duration of response, and adverse events (AE).

## Results:
The PFS6mo for 28 evaluable patients was 46.4% [95% confidence interval (CI), 27.5–66.1] and not superior to the null rate, with a median PFS of 25.3 weeks (95% CI, 24.0–42). The best objective response rate was 33.3% (95% CI, 17.3–52.8) with a disease control rate of 80.0% (95% CI, 61.4–92.3), including STS types unlikely to respond to doxorubicin or ICI alone. Grade 3/4 treatment-related AE occurred in 45% of patients, with immune-mediated AE requiring immunosuppression in 9%.

## Conclusions:
Although the study did not meet the predefined endpoint for PFS improvement, promising signals of efficacy warrant future investigation including response/resistance biomarkers to inform patient selection.

## Translational Relevance
Durable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) can occur in some patients with soft tissue sarcoma; however, most patients do not benefit from therapy. Evidence suggests that most sarcomas are not sufficiently immunogenic to trigger robust immune responses. Cytotoxic chemotherapy, including doxorubicin, can induce novel neoantigen release from necrotic cells and stimulate immunogenic cell death pathways, leading to improved innate and adaptive immune responses. We conducted a phase 2 trial of doxorubicin plus dual CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade with zalifrelimab and balstilimab in patients with advanced/metastatic soft tissue sarcoma. We observed meaningful and durable efficacy in patients with sarcoma types historically unlikely to respond to doxorubicin or immunotherapy alone, including patients with low tumor mutational burden and lacking tumor PD-L1 expression. Although the study did not meet the primary endpoint, our findings support the importance of including CTLA-4 blockade in future explorations of combination chemotherapy/immunotherapy studies for immune- cold sarcomas.

## Introduction
Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) are a heterogeneous group of bone and soft tissue neoplasms of mesenchymal origin that comprise about 1% of adult solid cancers ( ). Despite approval of targeted therapies for some STS types, doxorubicin as monotherapy or in combination with other cytotoxic agents has remained the standard of care for most metastatic or unresectable STS since the 1970s ( ). With median overall survival (OS) rates of metastatic STS ranging from 12 to 18 months, novel therapies to improve outcomes are urgently needed. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) against PD-1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) have dramatically impacted outcomes in many solid cancers; however, among patients with common adult STS types, responses are seen in fewer than 20% of patients ( ). Transcriptomic analysis has suggested that 20% of adult STS have high expression of immune-related genes (immune-hot) with the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), whereas the remaining 80% exhibit low immune-related gene expression (immune cold), suggesting relative resistance to immune kill, potentially through lack of adequate tumor neoantigens, faulty antigen presentation, and/or a suppressive tumor microenvironment ( ). Furthermore, sarcomas with an immune-hot signature and TLS tend to have better outcomes with PD-1 blockade ( , ), suggesting that tumor-specific T cell populations already exist in these sarcomas to be activated with immunotherapy. Thus, therapies that can generate novel neoantigens, boost tumor immunogenicity and antigen presentation, and repolarize suppressive microenvironment conditions may be needed to improve the generation of tumor-specific T cells and response to ICI in immune-cold STS.
Doxorubicin, a prototype inducer of immunogenic cell death, causes release of damage-associated molecular patterns from dying tumor cells that enhance innate immune cell recognition and recruitment in preclinical studies ( , ). As a topoisomerase-2 inhibitor, doxorubicin also forms DNA adducts that act as neoantigens, triggering type I IFN production and boosting immune responses ( – ). In addition to preclinical evidence, low-dose doxorubicin priming before nivolumab therapy increased IFN production, CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and tumor responses in patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) in the TONIC trial ( ). To date, two single-arm phase 2 studies have combined doxorubicin with pembrolizumab in patients with STS, demonstrating an acceptable safety and toxicity profile and some evidence of improved clinical outcomes ( , ). In the Pollack study, objective responses occurred in 19% of patients, including durable responses in those with undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) and dedifferentiated liposarcomas (dLPS), with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 8.1 months. In the Livingston study, responses occurred in 36.7% of patients, including those with UPS, dLPS, and leiomyosarcomas (LMS); however, the median PFS was less at 5.7 months.
CTLA-4 is increasingly implicated as a critical target to enhance antigen presentation and T-cell priming in immune-cold tumors. Preclinical studies from our laboratory using the murine fibrosarcoma model MCA-205 demonstrated suppression of tumor growth and improved survival with doxorubicin plus anti–PD-1/anti–CTLA-4 antibodies compared with doxorubicin alone, with increased infiltration of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL), remodeling of T-cell clonotypes, and increased RNA expression of T-cell activation gene signatures ( ). Based on our preclinical modeling, and superior outcomes observed with ipilimumab/nivolumab compared with nivolumab monotherapy in a large trial of multiple sarcoma types ( ), our central hypothesis was that doxorubicin would increase tumor immunogenicity and synergize with CTLA-4 blockade, improving cross-talk between antigen-presenting cells and T cells and boosting T-cell priming and expansion. Subsequently, PD-1 inhibition would enhance cytotoxicity and decrease exhaustion of newly generated tumor-specific T cells, collectively promoting antitumor responses ( ).
Herein, we report the clinical results of a phase 2 trial combining doxorubicin with zalifrelimab, an anti–CTLA-4 inhibitor, with balstilimab, an anti–PD-1 inhibitor, for patients with advanced/metastatic STS (NCT04028063).

## Patients and Methods

## Study design and treatment plan
This study was an open-label, nonrandomized, single-institution, investigator-initiated, single-arm phase 2 trial using a Simon minimax two-stage design including a safety lead-in (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Treatment cycles were administered every 21 days (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Following screening and eligibility confirmation, stage 1 patients received zalifrelimab and balstilimab alone at cycle 1 (immune priming cycle). We reasoned that by first blocking immune suppression, we would ensure that antigen-presenting cells and cytotoxic T cells were primed for maximal recognition and response to damage-associated molecular patterns and neoantigens released by doxorubicin treatment. Thus, doxorubicin 75-mg/m2 i.v. push was initiated at cycle 2 and continued for up to six doses. Zalifrelimab 1 mg/kg i.v. was administered every other cycle (every 6 weeks) up to four infusions as limited by toxicity. Balstilimab 300-mg i.v. flat dose was administered every cycle (every 3 weeks) and continued as maintenance therapy after completion of doxorubicin and zalifrelimab infusions until progression or prohibitive toxicity for total treatment duration of up to 2 years. Supportive medications in stage 1 patients included the cardioprotectant dexrazoxane, administered in a 10:1 ratio, anti-emetics, including fosaprepitant, palonosetron, and low-dose dexamethasone 4 mg i.v. on day 1 only, and pegfilgrastim for all doxorubicin-containing cycles. Doxorubicin dose reductions were permitted as per standard of care, whereas no dose reductions were permitted for zalifrelimab/balstilimab. Zalifrelimab/balstilimab doses were omitted or delayed for grade ≥2 immune-mediated toxicities excluding rash, fatigue, or asymptomatic laboratory abnormalities other than creatinine or liver function test elevations and permanently discontinued for grade ≥3 immune-mediated toxicities. Baseline scans for tumor responses were performed before starting doxorubicin (cycle 2) to address potential pseudoprogression that could occur early with ICI alone during the first cycle. Subsequent scans for response assessment were performed every 6 weeks up to cycle 10 (primary endpoint) and then every 9 weeks thereafter.
During stage 1, several patients did not reach cycle 2 with doxorubicin due to rapid progression of sarcoma during screening and the immune priming cycle. Additionally, emerging studies in other cancer types, including TNBC, gastric/esophageal cancer, head and neck cancer, and non–small cell and small cell lung cancer, showed superior effects of chemotherapy with PD-1 blockade but with all drugs administered simultaneously ( ). Thus, for stage 2 patients, the protocol was amended to omit the zalifrelimab/balstilimab priming cycle, with all patients beginning doxorubicin, zalifrelimab, and balstilimab concurrently at cycle 1 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Additionally, instead of limiting zalifrelimab to only four infusions, stage 2 patients were permitted to continue zalifrelimab along with balstilimab as maintenance after completion of doxorubicin if no prohibitive toxicity occurred. For stage 2, the baseline scan was completed within 28 days of initiating study therapy. Subsequent scans for response assessment were performed every 6 weeks up to cycle 9 (primary endpoint) and then every 9 weeks thereafter. To account for pseudoprogression or Choi responses to therapy, stage 1 and stage 2 patients were allowed to continue treatment after radiographic progression if the patient was deemed safe for continued treatment by the treating investigator. Full details of the treatment plan can be found in the study protocol (Supplementary Data).
Safety assessments included routine complete blood counts with differentials and complete metabolic panels, thyroid assessment, and additional laboratories as clinically indicated to assess for immune-mediated toxicities. Management of immune-mediated toxicities included corticosteroids and anti–TNF-α blockade with infliximab for colitis following standard guidelines. All patients had baseline echocardiograms to assess cardiac function before doxorubicin, before the third (stage 1) or fourth (stage 2) cycle of doxorubicin, and after the final dose of doxorubicin. All patients had collection of correlative samples, including mandatory tumor biopsies at baseline and cycle 2 within 3 to 7 days after treatment, an optional biopsy at progression, and peripheral blood samples obtained at baseline, cycle 2 day 1, cycle 2 day 5, cycle 4 day 1, and end of treatment.
The study was conducted in accordance with the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in this study. Of note, following completion of the study reported here, the trial was amended to enroll an additional cohort of patients to receive doxorubicin with botensilimab, an Fc-enhanced next-generation CTLA-4 inhibitor, instead of zalifrelimab, with or without balstilimab. This cohort continues to accrue and will be reported separately.

## Eligibility criteria
Enrolled patients were at least 18 years of age with locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic STS types appropriate for doxorubicin, with diagnosis confirmed by bone and soft tissue pathologists. Patients were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1 with adequate organ function and measurable disease by RECIST 1.1 with a tumor site amenable to biopsy. Patients were restricted to no more than one prior line of therapy for metastatic disease and no prior anthracyclines or ICI. Patients were excluded for comorbid malignancies requiring treatment, immunodeficiency or autoimmune disorders requiring immunosuppressants, chronic infections, history of noninfectious pneumonitis requiring steroids, bleeding disorders, or if pregnant or breastfeeding. Washout periods of 2 weeks were required for chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or radiation and 4 weeks for antibodies or investigational agents. Full eligibility criteria are available in Supplementary Data (protocol).

## Study outcomes and assessments
The primary objective of the study was to assess the efficacy of combination doxorubicin/zalifrelimab/balstilimab, with the primary study endpoint of 6-month progression-free rate (PFS6mo) assessed by RECIST 1.1. Secondary endpoints included the (i) objective response rate (ORR), (ii) disease control rate (DCR) encompassing complete response (CR), partial response (PR), and stable disease (SD), (iii) PFS, and (iv) duration of response (DoR), all determined by RECIST 1.1. Patients were followed for response until starting a new therapy, death, loss to follow-up, or withdrawal of consent. Additional secondary endpoints included OS and adverse events (AE) defined by the NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.03. Exploratory endpoints include assessment of changes with treatment within the tumor-infiltrating and circulating immune microenvironment using RNA sequencing of study biopsies and multiplex flow cytometry analysis of TILs expanded from biopsies and circulating peripheral blood mononuclear cells. The results of correlative studies will be reported at a future date.

## Statistical analysis
The data cut date was January 20, 2025. The safety-evaluable population included all patients who received at least one complete dose of any of the study drugs, and the efficacy-evaluable population included all patients who received at least one dose of all study drugs and had at least one posttreatment imaging assessment. Patients who received all study drugs but were withdrawn from the study before reaching the primary endpoint timepoint of 6 months for any reason other than radiographic progression, clinical progression, or death were replaced for per-protocol analysis. For determination of the primary endpoint, the hypothesis was that the proportion of patients who were progression-free at 6 months with treatment would be superior to the null PFS6mo rate of 43.4%. The null rate was derived by back-calculating from the median PFS previously reported in two large studies of doxorubicin monotherapy and taking the average of the two values ( , ). We proposed an increase of 20% in PFS6mo with combination therapy. Using a Simon minimax two-stage design with a 0.1 type I error rate and 80% power, up to 28 evaluable patients were required. The first Simon stage would enroll up to 16 evaluable patients, and the trial would be terminated if after 6 months of follow-up, six or fewer patients were progression-free (Supplementary Fig. S1A). If the trial continued, rejection of the null hypothesis would require at least 15 of the 28 evaluable patients to be progression-free at 6 months. PASS 14 Power Analysis and Sample Size Software (2015) was used for the sample size calculation (NCSS, LLC, ncss.com/software/pass.)
Early-stopping rules for toxicity were implemented for the safety lead-in phase of stage 1 of the trial, in which if ≥ two patients experienced prohibitive toxicities within the first 9 weeks of study therapy, the study would be halted. These were defined as (i) grade 2 or greater cardiac toxicity, (ii) any grade 3/4 toxicity that did not resolve to grade 2 or less within 3 weeks despite maximal medical management, including corticosteroids for immune-mediated toxicities, or (iii) grade 5 treatment-related toxicity.
Analysis of objective responses by RECIST 1.1 were tabulated, summarized, and reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). DoR was reported as median with range. OS and PFS were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with SE according to Greenwood’s variance and the log–log transform method. Median OS and PFS were also derived and reported. AE were summarized by type, grade, and attribution to either doxorubicin, zalifrelimab/balstilimab, or combination therapy, with attention to immune-mediated toxicities requiring immunosuppression. Baseline clinical/demographic characteristics were summarized by the number and percent of patients in each category, and continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Post hoc analysis comparing clinical outcomes between stage 1 and stage 2 patients was performed using exact binomial test or Fisher exact tests with P values descriptive in intent. Analysis was performed using R software (RRID: SCR_001905) and GraphPad Prism software version 10.1.1 (RRID: SCR_002798).

## Data availability
Data that were generated in this study but are not included in the article files are not publicly available due to the ongoing nature of the clinical trial and correlative analysis; however, data may be available from the corresponding author upon written request and clarification from the requesting party about how the data will be utilized.

## Results

## Patient characteristics
Between February 12, 2020 and December 22, 2022, 37 patients signed consent and were assessed for eligibility, with two screen failures and two patients excluded from subsequent analyses due to nonconforming treatment ( ). Patient 003 did not receive an entire dose of study drugs at cycle 1 due to development of chest pain during infusion requiring emergent evaluation. This was later determined to be disease burden–related coronary ischemia and unrelated to study drugs. Patient 032 received therapy based on an erroneous weight and was underdosed per protocol and thus was not included in the safety or efficacy analysis. Thirty-three patients received complete doses of at least one study drug and were evaluable for safety, 30 received all study drugs and were efficacy evaluable with at least one posttreatment scan, and 28 were evaluable for the primary endpoint of PFS6mo.
 Clinical and demographic characteristics of enrolled patients (N = 35) are summarized in , and the generalizability to the broader sarcoma population is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Most patients were male (54.1%) and White (88.6%), with a median age of 59 (range, 27–80). Most patients had metastatic disease (82.9%) and were untreated (68.6%), with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 (42.9%) or 1 (57.1%). Nine of the 11 patients who had received prior treatment had received gemcitabine/docetaxel. One patient with clear cell sarcoma–like tumor of the gastrointestinal tract had received crizotinib/pazopanib, and one patient with epithelioid sarcoma had received tazemetostat. STS types enrolled included LPS (n = 9), LMS (n = 9), UPS/myxofibrosarcoma (n = 5), angiosarcoma (n = 4), and other types (n = 8).
 

## Efficacy in the overall study population
Analysis of the safety lead-in patients demonstrated no prohibitive toxicity rates; thus, the study continued to enroll as designed. Interim analysis after stage 1 accrual met criteria for advancing to stage 2 and completing enrollment for 28 evaluable patients. However, for all patients evaluable for the primary endpoint of PFS6mo (n = 28), the rate was 46.4% (95% CI, 27.5–66.1), which was numerically superior but not statistically significantly different from the null PFS6mo of 43.4% for doxorubicin monotherapy ( ). The median PFS was 25.3 weeks (95% CI, 24.0–42.0). The ORR was 33.3% (95% CI, 17.3–52.8) for the 30 patients evaluable for efficacy, with one CR and nine PR, and the DCR was 80% (95% CI, 61.4–92.3; ). With the limitations of cross-trial comparisons, these findings were numerically better than historic outcomes with doxorubicin in recent clinical trials (ORR, 14%–20%; DCR, 41%–76%; refs. – ) and PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade (ORR, 12%–16%; refs. , ). For all patients, the median OS was 114.3 weeks [95% CI, 86.9–not applicable (NA)] with a median follow-up time of 190.3 weeks (95% CI, 135–NA; ).
 Overall, the median DoR was 38.7 weeks (90% CI, 12.6–106.4). Responses occurred in patients with sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma/low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma hybrid (SEF/LGFS, n = 1, CR) and angiosarcomas, including one cardiac and two radiation-associated breast tumors (n = 3, PR), and one patient each achieved PR with intimal sarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), soft tissue LMS (stLMS), endometrial stromal sarcoma (ESS), UPS, and dLPS. Response duration tended to follow one of two patterns. Several patients who achieved initial PR or SD but then progressed tended to do so shortly after discontinuation of doxorubicin while on single-agent balstilimab or balstilimab/zalifrelimab maintenance, including stage 1 patients 008, 009, 015, and 020 and stage 2 patients 026 and 028 ( and ). In contrast, other patients achieved early responses, which were durable. These included five patients who maintained responses for well more than 1 year, three of which were ongoing at the last follow-up ( and ). For example, patient 002 (intimal sarcoma) achieved a CR in target lesions with residual scarring at nontarget sites, completed the entire 2 years of study treatment, and was without active disease at the last follow-up (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Patient 012 (resected cardiac angiosarcoma) achieved a PR with liver, lung, and bilateral adrenal metastases but discontinued study therapy due to immune-mediated colitis shortly after completing six cycles of doxorubicin. The patient has been off study therapy for more than 3 years without disease progression (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Finally, patient 034, the sole responder in stage 2, had metastatic SEF/LGFS with bone and lung metastases, all of which resolved completely on therapy (Supplementary Fig. S2C). The patient discontinued study therapy due to immune-mediated gastroparesis after 19 months of treatment and remains on follow-up. For patients 007 (MPNST) and 016 (breast angiosarcoma), both achieved responses that enabled consolidation surgery and long-term disease control with adjuvant PD-1 blockade administered off trial.
 We were intrigued to see responses in STS types historically unlikely to respond to doxorubicin monotherapy or immunotherapy alone, including MPNST, high-grade ESS, and LMS. Given these observations, we reviewed available tumor genetic sequencing results obtained for clinical care. Fifteen of the 30 efficacy-evaluable patients had sequencing results available for review (Supplementary Table S2). As expected, most patients with sequencing data showed low tumor mutation burden (TMB) and microsatellite stability. Importantly, patients with low TMB/microsatellite stability status could still achieve responses, including patients 007 (MPNST), 012 (cardiac angiosarcoma), 017 (stLMS), 018 (ESS), 021 (UPS), and 034 (SEF/LGFS). Only two patients had intermediate/high TMB, including patient 020 with radiation-associated breast angiosarcoma who had 11 mutations/megabase and achieved a brief PR before progression and patient 030 with stLMS who had high TMB and high microsatellite instability (MSI) but only achieved SD and progressed prior to 6 months on treatment. Of the efficacy-evaluable patients, five were PD-L1–positive by IHC, eight were PD-L1–negative, and 17 did not have testing available. PD-L1–positive patients included patients 007, 017, and 021 who achieved durable PR but also patients 004 and 008 with dLPS, both of whom achieved SD but progressed before the 6-month timepoint. Patients 012 and 020 were both PD-L1–negative but achieved PR. Overall, this suggests that doxorubicin plus PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade may facilitate responses in immune-cold sarcomas with low TMB and absent PD-L1 expression.

## 
Post hoc comparison of stage 1 versus stage 2 patients
Despite meeting criteria at the interim analysis to advance to stage 2, the final PFS6mo for the study was lower than we anticipated, raising the question of whether changes made to treatment for stage 2 impacted efficacy. Alternatively, it was possible that a greater proportion of patients with immune-hot sarcomas were enrolled on the study in stage 1, which included three patients with angiosarcoma and two patients with UPS and UPS-like intimal sarcoma, which are known to potentially respond to immunotherapy and doxorubicin ( – ). In contrast, stage 2 patients included predominantly immune-cold subtypes, including uterine and stLMS, CCS, and dLPS, with only one patient each with angiosarcoma and UPS.
To explore this, we conducted an exploratory, hypothesis-generating, post hoc analysis comparing stage 1 with stage 2 patients. Because this was a nonrandomized study, we were not able to determine whether responses were being driven by immune-hot patients likely to respond to immunotherapy alone rather than synergy from the combination. However, stage 1 patients had imaging at screening for eligibility and then at the start of doxorubicin at cycle 2, which could be compared to determine impact from the immunotherapy priming cycle. We hypothesized that patients who had a decrease in tumor target lesions during the immunotherapy priming cycle would be more likely to achieve PR, driven by underlying immunogenicity rather than an effect of combination chemotherapy/immunotherapy. We found that four of the nine PR patients had a decrease in tumor dimensions during cycle 1, one had no change, and four had increased tumor dimensions (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In patients achieving SD/PD as best response, four of seven showed increased tumor growth, including both patients with PD. There was no significant difference in the mean percent change from screening to baseline between PR or SD/PD patients (PR 2.1% increase vs. SD/PD 8.0% increase, P = 0.55, by an unpaired t test). Thus, an increase in tumor dimensions during immunotherapy did not appear to correlate with ultimate response to treatment.
We then compared clinical outcomes between stage 1 and stage 2 patients. The PFS6mo for stage 1 patients was 56.2% (95% CI, 29.9–80.2) compared with 33.3% (95% CI, 9.9–65.1) for stage 2 patients (descriptive P value = 0.28). The median PFS for stage 1 patients was 31.7 weeks (95% CI, 24–NA) compared with 25.0 weeks (95% CI, 8.6–NA) for stage 2 patients (P = 0.39; Supplementary Fig. S3B). The median OS was not significantly different between stage 1 and stage 2 patients [stage 1, 117.6 weeks (95% CI, 86.9–NA) vs. stage 2, 102.3 weeks (49.3–NA), P = 0.69; Supplementary Fig. S3C]. Stage 1 patients had markedly better response rates at 56.2% (95% CI, 29.9–80.2) compared with stage 2 patients [7.1% (95% CI, 0–33.9), P = 0.007; Supplementary Fig. S3D]. The DCR was also higher in stage 1 at 93.8% (95% CI, 69.8–99.8) compared with stage 2 at 64.3% (95% CI, 35.1–87.2), P = 0.07. Although these comparisons are not sufficiently powered for definitive conclusions, the favorable outcomes observed in stage 1 warrant in-depth exploration of correlative samples to determine whether superior immune activation in response to doxorubicin is observed with sequential versus concurrent use of immunotherapy.

## Safety and AE
Overall, 33 patients were included in the safety evaluation, and all experienced at least one treatment-emergent AE that was deemed at least possibly related to doxorubicin, zalifrelimab/balstilimab, or the combination [treatment-related AE (TRAE); ]. The most common reported TRAE were fatigue (66.7%), nausea/vomiting/dyspepsia (51.5%), mucositis (36.4%), and diarrhea (33.3%). Fifteen patients (45%) experienced a grade 3 or higher TRAE. Most grade 3 or higher TRAE were hematologic and related to doxorubicin therapy, including thrombocytopenia (18.2%), neutropenia (15.2%), anemia (12.1%), and febrile neutropenia (6.1%). Two patients demonstrated a decrease in cardiac ejection fraction with treatment, one grade 1 and one grade 2.
 The most frequent immune-mediated AE related to zalifrelimab/balstilimab included rash (21.2%), dry skin/pruritus (15.1%), hypothyroidism (18.1%), and thyroiditis/decreased thyroid-stimulating hormone (18.1%). Two patients developed pleural effusions attributed to either zalifrelimab/balstilimab or combination doxorubicin/zalifrelimab/balstilimab. One patient (009) had transient worsening chest pain associated with the effusion that resolved with supportive care and did not require thoracentesis. The second patient (010) had a known preexisting disease-related exudative effusion that worsened during treatment, requiring discontinuation of study therapy for palliative radiation. Significant immune-mediated AE requiring corticosteroids and/or infliximab therapy occurred in three patients (9%), including patient 012 who developed grade 3 colitis, patient 017 with grade 3 colitis and grade 3 pancreatitis, and patient 027 with new type 1 diabetes mellitus who presented in ketoacidosis along with grade 3 thyroiditis, grade 4 pancreatitis, and grade 3 hypoxemia. Previous work has suggested a positive correlation between immune-mediated AE and response in sarcomas ( ), and although our numbers are small, two of the three patients with serious immune-mediated AE achieved PR (012, 017). None of these events occurred within the first 9 weeks of study therapy, the period designated for prohibitive toxicities by our early-stopping toxicity rules. Thus, combination doxorubicin did not seem to increase the frequency or alter the timeline for the emergence of immune-mediated AE compared with historic experience with ipilimumab/nivolumab ( , ). Among five stage 2 patients who received more than four zalifrelimab infusions during the post-doxorubicin maintenance period (median, 5 infusions; range, 5–13), only one patient (034) discontinued therapy for late immune-mediated toxicity after 13 zalifrelimab infusions. Thus, continuation of CTLA-4 blockade past the initial four infusions did not appear to lead to higher rates of immune-mediated toxicity. All serious events resolved with treatment apart from type 1 diabetes; however, patients who developed hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency required maintenance with levothyroxine or prednisone, respectively. In summary, the toxicity profile was manageable and as expected without new safety signals identified with combination doxorubicin plus zalifrelimab/balstilimab.

## Discussion
ICI blocking PD-1 and CTLA-4 can produce durable responses and meaningful PFS in some STS, most notably in alveolar soft part sarcoma, leading to approval of atezolizumab ( ). Responses can also occur in cutaneous angiosarcoma, UPS, and dLPS; however, most patients still will not be cured of their sarcomas ( ). In an effort to improve activity of ICI for more patients with sarcoma, various trials exploring combinations with targeted therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors and cytotoxic chemotherapies have been conducted. Despite this, most are small, nonrandomized, and enroll numerous sarcoma types, making signals of synergy difficult to identify. In addition to doxorubicin/pembrolizumab combinations ( , ), dual PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade has been studied with trabectedin ( ), and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition with trabectedin ( – ), gemcitabine with or without docetaxel ( , ), doxorubicin/dacarbazine ( ), doxorubicin/ifosfamide ( ), and eribulin ( ). Taken together across trials, outcomes with chemotherapy plus checkpoint blockade have trended toward improvement compared with historic outcomes with either agent alone, raising hope for a strategy to induce responses in immune-cold sarcomas.
To our knowledge, our study of doxorubicin plus zalifrelimab/balstilimab is the first to integrate CTLA-4 inhibition with a doxorubicin backbone and target three points of resistance to immune responses in early treatment lines of metastatic STS: lack of tumor immunogenicity, weak antigen presentation and T-cell priming, and suppression of tumor-specific T cells. Combination CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade improves clinical efficacy over PD-1 monotherapy alone in other cancers, including melanoma, non–small cell, and renal cell carcinoma ( ), and was superior compared with PD-1 monotherapy in sarcomas ( ). Additionally, promising activity has been observed in a variety of immune-cold or ICI-refractory solid tumors, including sarcomas, in a phase 1 trial of botensilimab, a novel CTLA-4 inhibitor with enhanced affinity for Fcγ receptors, with balstilimab ( , , ). In our study, we observed responses and prolonged SD with combination therapy in sarcoma types that would not be expected to have long-term benefit from doxorubicin or PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy, such as SEF, MPNST, ESS, cardiac angiosarcoma, and LMS. Additionally, for patients with available clinical sequencing, no responders had high TMB or MSI, and responses could occur in patients with low TMB and absent tumor PD-L1 expression. These outcomes support that combination doxorubicin/zalifrelimab/balstilimab therapy is promoting and maintaining immune responses in at least some patients with immune-cold sarcomas. The contribution of CTLA-4 blockade is being further explored within our ongoing expansion cohort combining botensilimab with doxorubicin, in which the enhanced affinity of the Fc portion of the antibody may further improve early immune cell activation and T-regulatory cell (Treg) depletion in immune-cold tumors ( ).
Despite a favorable response rate of 33%, which is higher than historic results from doxorubicin or ICI alone in sarcomas, the study did not significantly improve PFS6mo above historic outcomes with doxorubicin. However, we observed markedly better clinical outcomes in stage 1 compared with stage 2, including PFS6mo (56.2% vs. 33.3%), ORR (56.2% vs. 7.1%), and DCR (93.8% vs. 64.3%). Although we cannot exclude patient selection as a contributor given the low number of patients, we speculate that our decision to remove the immune priming cycle in stage 2 of the study may have driven these inferior outcomes. Given the lymphotoxicity of doxorubicin, and our use of low-dose dexamethasone for prevention of chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting, we postulate that antigen presentation and T-cell priming that should occur with initial exposure to CTLA-4 blockade was suppressed in stage 2 at a critical timepoint, preventing generation and expansion of tumor-specific T cells. In contrast, stage 1 patients would have had 3 weeks to generate novel T-cell populations and more resilient memory cells that could persist following myelosuppression with doxorubicin. Evidence supporting that the stage 2 chemotherapy may have been detrimental to an immune response is suggested by outcomes from patient 030, who had stLMS with high TMB and MSI, yet only achieved brief SD with concurrent treatment. In other solid tumors, the results of clinical trials with concurrent chemotherapy and checkpoint inhibitors have had mixed outcomes; however, most do not include CTLA-4 inhibition [reviewed in ( )]. Very few clinical trials have explored sequential immunotherapy followed by chemotherapy versus concurrent administration. In the phase 2 GeparNuevo trial for TNBC, patients were randomized to receive a single dose of PD-L1 inhibitor durvalumab or placebo 2 weeks before neoadjuvant chemotherapy with nab-paclitaxel followed by epirubicin/cyclophosphamide ( ). Patients receiving durvalumab demonstrated a higher rate of pathologic CR (61.0% vs. 41.4%, OR, 2.22, P = 0.035), particularly PD-L1–positive tumors. For our study, ongoing analysis of TIL from stage 1 and stage 2 patient biopsies will be critical to confirm differences in early T-cell infiltration and activation resulting from sequential versus concurrent zalifrelimab/balstilimab with doxorubicin. An ongoing randomized trial of doxorubicin/pembrolizumab versus doxorubicin followed by pembrolizumab at progression for UPS and related poorly differentiated STS is eagerly anticipated to establish synergy with the approach and will compare concurrent versus sequential use of both agents (NCT06422806).
In addition to identifying the optimal sequence of immunotherapy and chemotherapy, further exploration of the optimal immune priming dose of doxorubicin is needed. In the TONIC trial for breast cancer, an extremely low dose of doxorubicin (15 mg flat dose × 2) was sufficient for immune priming based on gene expression and T-cell infiltration ( ). Lower dose, metronomic administration of doxorubicin is well supported in preclinical studies to support the induction of immunogenic cell death, and several studies have shown comparable efficacy in breast cancer compared with standard dosing ( – ). Interestingly, in 1987 Borden and colleagues ( ) reported similar efficacy in STS with lower-dose weekly doxorubicin (15 mg/m2) compared with standard dosing (70 mg/m2) every 3 weeks, challenging the assumption that higher doses of doxorubicin are required for clinical benefit. More recent sarcoma studies have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of lower-dose weekly doxorubicin in combination with other agents, including gemcitabine/docetaxel with nivolumab ( ) and lurbinectedin ( ). Prolonged treatment with doxorubicin past the standard anthracycline cumulative dose limitations of 450 mg/m2 have been increasingly explored with the use of dexrazoxane for cardioprotection, which has correlated with improved PFS in sarcomas ( , ). Given our observations that some patients progressed following discontinuation of doxorubicin, a lower dose or metronomic doxorubicin schedule could permit a longer duration of chemotherapy within accepted anthracycline dose limits with an improved toxicity profile, potentially prolonging PFS and optimizing immune-stimulatory effects.
Finally, identifying better biomarkers remains a priority for selecting patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Italiano and colleagues ( ) have reported that apart from TMB and PD-L1 expression, the presence of TLS in sarcomas with a low burden of tumor-infiltrating Tregs predicts that patients with sarcoma are likely to respond to pembrolizumab therapy. Interestingly, Tregs are among the cells with the highest expression of CTLA-4, and depletion of Tregs through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity has been identified as a critical mechanism of CTLA-4 inhibitor activity ( ). Whereas our results suggest that doxorubicin plus CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade can generate responses in immune-cold STS that likely lack TLS at baseline, investigating the balance of cytotoxic and activated TIL phenotypes in comparison with Tregs is a priority for our correlative studies. We also suspect that nonresponding patients could have fundamental alterations in antigen-presentation machinery, such as loss of MHC class I expression, and/or primary resistance to immune-stimulatory effects of doxorubicin, potentially through deficits in type I IFN signaling pathways ( ). Confirmation of these resistance mechanisms as potential biomarkers will also inform patient selection for a future randomized clinical trial.
Our study has several important limitations. Apart from the single-arm, nonrandomized design, the study was exploratory in intent, with the unconventional alteration of treatment at stage 2 limiting validity of the primary endpoint determination. Comparisons with historic doxorubicin outcomes are inherently biased, particularly because our patient population included heterogeneous STS types with different biology and historic sensitivity to doxorubicin and a mix of untreated and second-line patients. Patients with rapid progression of disease on immunotherapy alone before receiving chemotherapy or who developed early toxicity were replaced for evaluation of the primary endpoint, perhaps selecting for patients with more indolent disease. Thus, trends observed in outcomes are descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Despite these limitations, we have demonstrated preliminary evidence of significant, meaningful, and durable clinical activity in a subset of patients unlikely to respond to doxorubicin or PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade alone, including patients with low TMB and negative PD-L1 expression, suggesting that doxorubicin can improve responses to ICI in immune-cold STS. Additionally, the regimen was safe with acceptable toxicity, without new safety signals from the combination. With more extensive study of biomarkers of response and resistance and further modeling of optimal sequencing of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, our findings warrant future investigation of combination doxorubicin with dual PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade through a randomized clinical trial.
