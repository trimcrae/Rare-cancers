## Abstract
Cutaneous melanocytic tumor with CRTC1::TRIM11 fusion (CMCT) is a recently described entity with only 13 cases reported in the literature. Histopathologically, the neoplasm consists of atypical epithelioid to spindled cells that form a well‐circumscribed nodule usually confined to the dermis and subcutis with cytological features including large vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry shows variable expressivity of melanocytic markers. Currently, there are limited data regarding long‐term outcomes of this newly described entity. Most cases have done well, but there is one case reported with an adverse event. Hence, further studies are needed to accurately classify this tumor. Definitive diagnosis is made by laboratory evidence of CMCT. Herein, we report the first case of CMCT with epidermal involvement in the youngest patient known to be affected to date.

## INTRODUCTION
Next‐generation sequencing of both DNA and RNA can provide important insights into the etiology, prognosis, and treatment decisions of melanocytic tumors. Recently, five cases of primary, intradermal, non‐pigmented tumors immunophenotypically expressing melanocytic markers associated with a novel CRTC1::TRIM11 fusion were reported. Eight additional cases have been reported since this initial case series. A single case showed metastatic activity, suggesting a subset of these tumors may have malignant potential. The term “cutaneous melanocytic tumor or melanocytoma with CRTC1::TRIM11 fusion” (CMCT) has been suggested to reflect the biologic uncertainty of these neoplasms. Histopathologically, CMCT typically presents as a dermal/subcutaneous nodule composed of atypical epithelioid and spindled cells. The differential diagnosis often includes an atypical cellular blue nevus, clear cell sarcoma (CCS) of the soft parts, and primary dermal or metastatic malignant melanoma. Most cases of CMCT have occurred in adults, with one case presenting in an 11‐year‐old child.
Herein, we present a case of CMCT occurring in a 5‐year‐old child, the youngest patient to date. Furthermore, the lesion presented clinically as an exophytic nodule with prominent epidermal hyperplasia and epidermal involvement, mimicking changes of a spitzoid malignant melanoma. It also uniquely had nests of cells involving both the epidermis and dermis, making it the first reported example of a compound CMCT.

## MOLECULAR METHODS
The tumor was sequenced using previously described pipelines for combined DNA targeted sequencing and RNA whole‐transcriptome sequencing. , In brief, tumor tissue was sequenced from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded sections cut at 5 μm. Library preparation and sequencing to a depth of ×500 were performed using Illumina HiSeq 4000. DNA sequencing was performed using the Tempus xT (v4) sequencing panel and accompanying bioinformatics pipeline. This validated next‐generation sequencing assay covers 648 cancer‐associated genes. Single‐nucleotide variants and small insertion and deletions (indels) were called down to a variant allele frequency of 5%. DNA reads were aligned to GRCh37 as the reference genome and single‐nucleotide polymorphism and indel variant calls were made using Freebayes and Pindel. Somatic variants were filtered and annotated by Tempus bioinformatics pipelines with the incorporation of manual review of RefSeq, 1000 Genomes, and COSMIC datasets in assessing variants of unknown significance for potential pathogenicity. Copy number variants were assessed algorithmically because of lack of matched normal samples through comparison of allele‐specific deletions and amplifications in tumor tissue. Whole‐transcriptome RNA sequencing was performed with gene fusions detected in an unbiased manner using a validated fusion calling pipeline as described previously.

## CASE REPORT
An otherwise healthy 5‐year‐old girl presented to the Pediatric Dermatology Clinic with a nodule on the right upper arm. The lesion had arisen 1 year prior and initially grew slowly until it measured 6 × 5 mm. Two months prior to presentation to our clinic, the patient was presented to an outside clinic where the lesion was biopsied via shave technique. At that time, the lesion was superficially transected and interpreted as a benign Spitz nevus by an outside dermatopathologist with recommendation for no further intervention other than clinical monitoring. However, over the subsequent 2 months, the lesion showed concerning features including rapid growth and bleeding, which led the patient's parents to seek a second opinion. Upon presentation to our clinic, the neoplasm was a 12 × 10‐mm pink‐red, rubbery, friable tumor (Figure  ). The original biopsy slides were reviewed and interpreted as spitzoid malignant melanoma.
 Given the concerning clinical and pathologic features, excision of the lesion was performed promptly with 5‐mm safety margins. The lesion showed a markedly hyperplastic epidermis. Nests of epithelioid and spindle‐shaped cells with vesicular nuclei, prominent central nucleoli, and amphophilic to clear appearing cytoplasm were seen, involving both the epidermis and extending deeply into the dermis (Figures  , , ). The nests were arranged in a back‐to‐back pattern in the dermis separated by thin fibrous bands of collagen (Figure  ). The tumor formed an expansile proliferation at the base, which abutted the subcutaneous tissue in a pattern like that often seen with cellular blue nevus. The mitotic count was 10/mm2, including some atypical forms. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed expression of SOX10, S100, and NKI/C3. Melan‐A, CD10, and ALK were negative. IHC for p16 showed near‐complete loss of staining.
 Considering the prominent epidermal hyperplasia, compound architecture, symmetric silhouette, epithelioid and spindled cytomorphology, and young age of the patient, a spitzoid malignant melanoma was initially favored. To further characterize this tumor and assess for possible genomic fusions and other prognostic markers, molecular testing was performed. RNA whole‐transcriptome analysis detected a CMCT, which was then confirmed at the DNA level. The fusion comprised an in‐frame chromosomal rearrangement in exon 1 of CRTC1 and exons 2–6 in TRIM11 [t(19;1)(p13.11;q42.13)] (Figure  ). The findings were independently validated by the Comprehensive Oncologic Molecular Pathology and Sequencing Service clinical genomics program of the Laboratory of Pathology, National Cancer Institute (Bethesda, MD) using the TruSeq RNA exome fusion panel (Illumina, Inc.). RNA expression analysis also showed decreased expression of CDKN2A, although no variants or deletions of this gene were detected. This was consistent with the near‐complete loss of p16 seen on IHC staining. Several variants of uncertain significance were detected on DNA variant analysis. A Tempus Tumor Origin Assay was found to be indeterminate. The patient subsequently underwent complete re‐excision with 1‐cm margins, which showed no residual tumor. positron emission tomography‐computed tomography scan showed no evidence of metastasis. The patient now has remained disease free for 4 months. She will return to the dermatology clinic every 3 months for close surveillance.

## DISCUSSION
To date, only 14 cases (including this one) of CMCT have been reported in the literature. The median age of affected patients is 33 years (range 5–82). Table  summarizes patient and tumor characteristics of prior cases. Nine (64%) have occurred in females. There is no site‐specific predilection as the upper and lower extremities, trunk, and head and neck have all been affected. Clinically, CMCT typically presents as a slow‐growing, asymptomatic intradermal or subcutaneous nodule with a median size of 0.9 cm (range 0.4–5.1 cm). Suggested clinical diagnoses have included dermatofibroma, epidermal cyst, Spitz nevus, melanoma, and pyogenic granuloma. Except for one case with metastasis 13 years after diagnosis, none have recurred following excision after a median follow‐up of 12 months (range 1 month to 13 years). , , , , 

 Histopathologically, CMCT is typically a well‐circumscribed, unencapsulated dermal tumor with nodular to multinodular borders. Our case is the first example of a compound CMCT showing considerable epidermal involvement. The symmetric silhouette with prominent epidermal hyperplasia with intraepidermal involvement highly mimicked a spitzoid malignant melanoma, exhibiting features including large vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli and abundant cytoplasm, which was quite pale in this case. Hence, as is the case with CCS of the soft parts, rare compound variants may exist. Features that made this case unusual for a diagnosis of a spitzoid malignant melanoma included the high degree of nuclear atypia, paler rather than eosinophilic cytoplasm, and high mitotic count of 10/mm2. Furthermore, Spitz tumors with ALK fusion show distinctive histopathologic features including exophytic surface, wedge‐shaped, infiltrative lower border, and large, fusiform melanocytes arranged in elongated, vertically oriented nests. Negative ALK IHC excluded this diagnosis. This case supports the notion that molecular analysis is warranted in the assessment of unusual melanocytic neoplasms that do not clearly fit well into recognized categories, or potentially any case in which a diagnosis of spitzoid malignant melanoma of childhood is being considered.
Previously described features of CMCT include central necrosis, multinucleated giant cells, polypoid silhouette, and perinodular fibrosis. Interestingly, the only other reported pediatric case also had a degree of nuclear atypia and high mitotic activity (12/10 HPF). Perineural and lymphovascular invasion have not been described in these tumors. IHC consistently shows positivity for melanocytic markers SOX10 and MITF, although S100, Melan‐A, and HMB‐45 are variably expressed (Table  ). 

It has been previously proposed that based on overlapping features, CMCT may be CCS with a novel fusion. CCS is a soft tissue tumor that typically occurs in the distal extremities of young adults with a slight female predominance. It has a predilection for fascia, tendons, and aponeuroses. Rarely, it can arise in or spread to cutaneous tissue, usually the dermis. Distinction is crucial as this neoplasm portends high rates of recurrence, metastasis, and tumor‐related death. Like CMCT, pathologic findings include nests and fascicles of epithelioid or spindled tumor cells containing prominent nucleoli and pale to eosinophilic cytoplasm. There may be focal invasion into the subcutis, and the epidermis may be involved. CCS also expresses S100, Melan‐A, and HMB‐45. Most CCSs harbor a EWSR1::ATF1 fusion [t(12;22)(q13;q12)], and less commonly EWSR1::CREB1 fusion [t(2:22)(q34;q12)]. , , Neither of these fusions were detected in our case despite evaluating a 1711 gene targeted tumor and normal DNA sequencing panel with tumor RNA sequencing. Rarely, CREM, a member of the CREB family of transcription factors, can function as a substitute for more prevalent fusion partners within the same family. 

Metastatic melanoma and primary dermal melanoma (PDM) are diagnostic considerations that should be excluded. Systemic work‐up and imaging aid in assessing for regional or distant involvement. As with CCS, PDM may prove difficult to distinguish from CMCT histopathologically; however, melanomas frequently possess other genetic alterations such as activating variant of BRAF and NRAS in contrast to the CMCT. Furthermore, apart from BRAF and NRAS variants, melanoma is expected to show multiple copy number aberrations compared to CMCT.
Disruption of both CRTC1 and TRIM11 may contribute to oncogenesis. CRTC1, or cAMP response element‐binding protein (CREB) regulated transcription co‐activator‐1, is a transcription factor known to play both physiologic and pathologic roles in the human body. Genetic alterations of CRTC1 have been implicated in tumorigenesis of mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MAML2 fusion) as well as cancers of the colon, lung, and esophagus. , , , 
TRIM11 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase and genetic alterations of this gene have been linked to breast cancer, lymphoma, gastric cancer, lung cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. , , , , 

In conclusion, we present the first case of CMCT with epidermal involvement and the youngest patient to be diagnosed with this tumor to date. Because of the few existing reports in the literature, additional investigations are necessary to determine the precise classification, pathogenesis, clinical behavior, prognosis, and optimal management for this entity.
