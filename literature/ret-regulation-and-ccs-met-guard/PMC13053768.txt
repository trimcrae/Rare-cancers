## Summary
The core pathological mechanisms underlying cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure are closely linked to disturbances in energy metabolism. As pivotal metabolic intermediates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, ketone bodies and succinate engage in a dynamic functional interplay that exerts a critical regulatory influence on the progression of cardiovascular diseases. For the first time, this review systematically proposes the conceptual framework of the “ketone body-succinate metabolic axis,” integrating recent advances in understanding their roles within cardiovascular system, and comprehensively elucidating the molecular mechanisms, cellular functions, and clinical relevance of this axis in cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure. Ketone bodies not only function as efficient alternative energy substrates under pathological conditions but also confer cardioprotective effects, including anti-inflammation and antioxidation actions. These benefits are mediated through multiple mechanisms, such as histone β-hydroxybutyrylation, suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and activation of the GPR109A receptor. In contrast, succinate accumulates aberrantly under pathological conditions like ischemia and hypoxia, thereby promoting myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, and hypertrophy. These deleterious effects are driven by activation of the succinate receptor SUCNR1, stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), induction of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, and regulation of protein succinylation. Together, ketone bodies and succinate constitute a tightly interconnected “yin-yang balance” regulatory network, characterized by shared metabolic nodes within the TCA cycle and antagonistic modulation of downstream signaling pathways, including inflammation and oxidative stress. Disruption of this dynamic balance represents a key mechanistic driver of disease progression from cardiac hypertrophy to heart failure. Furthermore, this review examines the regulatory influence of ketogenic diets and epigenetic modifications on the ketone body-succinate metabolic axis, and discusses the therapeutic potential and challenges of targeted interventions, such as ketone ester supplementation, SUCNR1 antagonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. Collectively, these insights provide a novel conceptual framework and promising research direction for the development of precise metabolic therapies for cardiovascular diseases.

## Introduction
Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, among which cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure represent key pathological processes and final end-stage manifestations. In recent years, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that disturbances in cardiac energy metabolism constitute critical pathogenic drivers of the onset and progression of myocardial dysfunction. , As a highly energy-demanding “metabolic omnivore,” the heart relies on flexible utilization of multiple substrates, including fatty acids, glucose, and ketone bodies, to sustain normal contractile performance and structural stability. Disruption of this metabolic flexibility precipitates profound metabolic remodeling, ultimately leading to cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis, fibrosis, and pump failure. Within the intricate metabolic network of the heart, ketone bodies and succinate have emerged as two pivotal metabolic intermediates that have attracted increasing attention for their functional roles. Traditionally, ketone bodies (mainly β-hydroxybutyrate) were regarded as alternative energy substrates generated under conditions such as fasting or diabetes. However, recent landmark studies have revealed that ketone bodies exert broad signaling functions beyond energy provision including inhibition of histone deacetylases and modulation of inflammasome activity, thereby conferring anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cardioprotective effects. , In contrast, succinate, a central metabolite of the TCA cycle, is no longer merely an intermediate in energy metabolism. Its pathological accumulation under stress conditions such as ischemia and hypoxia has been shown to function as a potent pro-inflammatory and pro-hypertrophic signal. Mechanistically, succinate mediates these effects through activation of the G protein-coupled receptor SUCNR1, stabilization of the HIF-1α, and induction of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts. , 
Notably, the metabolic pathways of ketone bodies and succinate converge within the TCA cycle and exhibit pronounced functional antagonism across multiple cardiovascular pathological signaling pathways. These observations suggest that ketone bodies and succinate do not operate in isolation but instead constitute an intrinsically interconnected metabolic functional axis. Although the concept of a “ketone body-succinate axis” has not yet been explicitly or systematically defined, a substantial body of converging evidence strongly supports the notion that, in the context of cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, these two metabolites form a dynamic “yin-yang” balance network. By sharing common upstream metabolic drivers (such as mitochondrial dysfunction and shifts in substrate utilization) and regulating common downstream pathological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and fibrosis, this network plays a decisive role in determining cardiac fate. Therefore, this review aims to systematically synthesize and integrate, for the first time, the latest advances in understanding the roles of ketone bodies and succinate within the cardiovascular system, and to innovatively propose the conceptual framework of the “ketone body-succinate metabolic axis.” We comprehensively examine the molecular mechanisms, cellular functions, and clinical relevance of this metabolic axis in the context of cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, drawing upon evidence from both experimental studies and clinical observations. In addition, this review explores how environmental factors, such as the ketogenic dietary interventions, and epigenetic mechanisms modulate this metabolic axis, and critically discuss the therapeutic potential and existing challenges of emerging strategies targeting this pathway. We anticipate that this integrated perspective will provide novel insights into the complexity of cardiac metabolic disorders and pave the way for the development of more precise and effective metabolic intervention strategies.

## Ketone bodies: From alternative fuel to protective signaling molecules

## Generation, classification, and metabolism of ketone bodies
Ketone bodies are a class of water-soluble energy metabolites primarily generated in hepatic mitochondria from fatty acids through β-oxidation. They mainly comprise β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB), acetoacetate (AcAc), and acetone. Ketogenesis is initiated when excessive acetyl-CoA is produced as a consequence of enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation. Two molecules of acetyl-CoA first condense to form acetoacetyl-CoA, which Subsequently combines with an additional acetyl-CoA molecule under the catalysis of the rate-limiting enzyme mitochondrial 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) to generate 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA)., HMG-CoA is then cleaved to produce ketone bodies, primarily AcAc. Notably, the liver lacks succinyl-CoA:3-oxoacid CoA-transferase (SCOT), the essential enzyme for ketone body oxidation, thereby rendering it a “pure producer” rather than a consumer of ketone bodies. AcAc can be reversibly reduced to β-HB, the predominant circulating ketone body, or undergo spontaneous decarboxylation to form acetone. Through this process, fatty acids that cannot be fully oxidized in the liver are converted into a transferable “energy currency” that can be efficiently utilized by peripheral tissues. This metabolic adaptation is essential for maintaining systemic energy homeostasis and supporting vital physiological functions during conditions such as prolonged fasting, exercise, and metabolic stress.
In the heart, ketone body metabolism is a tightly coordinated and highly regulated process. Circulating ketone bodies, predominantly β-HB,are transported into cardiomyocytes primarily via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1). Cytosolic β-HB subsequently enters the mitochondria, where it is oxidized back to AcAc by β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1). The most critical and rate-determining step in myocardial ketone body utilization is catalyzed by SCOT. This enzyme uses succinyl-CoA as a high-energy CoA donor to activate AcAc, converting it to acetoacetyl-CoA. Importantly, this reaction bypasses the conventional ATP-dependent thiokinase step, thereby enabling acetoacetyl-CoA to enter the TCA cycle efficiently for oxidative ATP production. , Owing to this energetic advantage, ketone bodies represent a more oxygen- and ATP-efficient fuel source compared with long-chain fatty acids. Under pathological conditions such as heart failure, both cardiac uptake and oxidation ketone bodies are markedly increased, reflecting an adaptive metabolic reprogramming whereby the failing heart preferentially selects more energetically efficient substrates to sustain contractile function. 
The oxidative utilization of ketone bodies in the myocardium, together with the hepatic ketogenesis, constitutes an integrated and closed A defining feature of this cycle is the tissue-specific functional divergence of key enzymes, particularly BDH1. In the relatively reducing mitochondrial environment of the liver, BDH1 catalyzes the terminal step of ketone body production. In contrast, within the more oxidizing mitochondrial milieu of the heart, BDH1functions in the opposite direction to initiate ketone body utilization. Accordingly, hepatic BDH1 activity governs the systemic availability of circulating ketone bodies, whereas myocardial BDH1 activity determines the local capacity for ketone body oxidation. The coordinated regulation of BDH1 across these tissues ensures the efficiency and dynamic balance of the ketone body metabolic cycle ( ). 

## Multiple mechanisms of ketone bodies as signaling molecules

## Epigenetic regulators
In 2016, mass spectrometry-based proteomics in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mouse models first identified β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) as a novel histone post-translational modification. This seminal study further demonstrated that histone β-HB is closely associated with transcriptional activity, implicating this modification. Subsequent investigations have progressively elucidated the enzymatic regulatory machinery governing histone β-hydroxybutyrylation. The histone acetyltransferase p300 has been identified as a principal “writer” enzyme capable of catalyzing histone β-hydroxybutyrylation in vitro and in vivo, thereby activatingp53-mediated transcriptional responses. In terms of modification removal, both class I histone deacetylases (HDAC1 and HDAC2) and class III deacetylases (SIRT1 and SIRT2) have been characterized as effective “eraser” enzymes, operating through zinc-dependent and NAD+-dependent mechanisms, respectively. , Notably, distinct HDAC subtypes exhibit differential substrate specificity toward β-hydroxybutyrylation. For example, SIRT3 displays relatively weak de-β-hydroxybutyrylation activity and shows a preference for non-physiological S-enantiomers. Collectively, these findings delineate a dedicated and finely tuned regulatory network for β-hydroxybutyrylation, providing an important mechanistic framework for understanding how metabolic intermediates such as ketone bodies directly modulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms.

## Inflammation inhibitors
Suppression of inflammation represents one of the most prominent non-metabolic functions of β-HB. Accumulating evidence indicates that ketone bodies produced by the liver can markedly reduce the generation of inflammation-related mediators. In a landmark study, Dixit and colleagues demonstrated that β-HB directly binds to the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby preventing its interaction with the adapter protein ASC. This interference inhibits caspase-1 activation and ultimately suppresses the maturation and secretion of potent pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18. Consistent with this mechanism, supplementation with β-HB or induction of a ketogenic metabolic state has been shown to significantly attenuate tissue inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue injury in multiple disease models, such as atherosclerosis and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury. , These findings firmly establish β-HB as an endogenous anti-inflammatory metabolite with broad relevance to cardiovascular pathology.

## G protein-coupled receptor agonists
β-HB functions as an endogenous agonist of the G protein-coupled receptor GPR109A, also known as hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCAR2). In immune cells,including macrophages and neutrophils, activation of GPR109A triggers an inhibitory signaling cascade mediated by Gi/o proteins, resulting to reduced intracellular cyclic AMP (Camp) levels. This which in turn signaling axis suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines while promoting the release of anti-inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10). , In adipose tissue, it inhibits the hormone-sensitive lipase activity, thereby reducing lipolysis and limiting excessive free fatty acid release. Through this receptor-mediated mechanism, β-HB subtly links the metabolic state of peripheral tissues, reflected by circulating ketone body levels, to systemic immune and inflammatory responses, thereby establishing an integrated metabolic-immune regulatory axis.

## NAD(H) homeostasis regulator: A central hub linking ketone body metabolism to cardioprotection
Ketone body metabolism serves as a critical regulator of the NAD+/NADH redox balance in cardiomyocytes. During mitochondrial oxidation of β-HB, BDH1 catalyzes the conversion of β-HB to acetoacetate, directly increasing the NADH/NAD+ ratio. This redox shift not only exerts feedback control over TCA cycle flux but also systemically expands the total intracellular NAD+ pool. Elevated NAD+ availability confers substantial cardioprotective benefits. As a central cofactor integrating metabolic reactions and signaling pathways, increased NAD+ levels significantly improve myocardial energetic performance and cardiac function, while alleviating energy deficiency in heart failure. Collectively, these findings indicate that ketone bodies extend beyond their role as alternative energy substrates and exert multifaceted cardioprotective effects through modulation of NAD(H) homeostasis, positioning NAD(H) as a pivotal metabolic and signaling nexus in the heart.

## Pleiotropic effects of ketone bodies in different cardiac cell types ( )
The cardioprotective effects of ketone bodies arise from their precise and multifaceted actions across distinct cardiac cell types. In cardiomyocytes, the biological functions of ketone bodies extend far beyond their traditionally recognized role as alternative energy substrates. During metabolic remodeling in heart failure, cardiomyocytes exhibit diminished fatty acid oxidation capacity and impaired glucose utilization efficiency. Under these conditions, ketone bodies, serving as highly efficient, water-soluble fuels with a higher phosphate/oxygen (P/O) ratio than fatty acids, can effectively replenish the acetyl-CoA pool, optimize TCA cycle flux, and thereby help the failing heart overcome an energetic deficit or “energy crisis”. Beyond their metabolic contribution, β-HB functions as a potent endogenous inhibitor of class I and IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs). HDAC inhibition by β-HB markedly increases histone acetylation at residues such as H3K9 and H3K14, resulting in chromatin relaxation and enhanced transcription of antioxidant and anti-apoptotic genes, including Foxo3a. , , In addition, ketone bodies attenuate cardiomyocyte hypertrophy through suppression of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. Ketone-derived metabolites further protect cardiomyocytes against oxidative stress-induced cell death. Moreover, in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes subjected to ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, elevated β-HB levels not only reduced cell death but also promote autophagic activity, preserve mitochondrial integrity, function, and membrane potential, and attenuate ROS accumulation. 
Ketone bodies enhance endothelial function through multiple, context-dependent mechanisms. In vascular endothelial cells, β-HB inhibits HDAC activity, activates endogenous antioxidant pathways, and attenuates mitochondrial oxidative stress. In addition,β-HB has been shown to upregulate the expression of carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1A (CPT1A) through histone lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb), thereby promoting post- myocardial infarction angiogenesis and preserving endothelial homeostasis under hypoxic conditions. More interestingly, β-HB can bind to heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNP A1), enhancing its interaction with Oct4 Mrna. This interaction stabilizes Oct4 transcripts and sustains Oct4 expression, which in turn increases Lamin B1 levels and confers resistance to DNA damage-induced endothelial cell senescence. Importantly, the protective effect of ketone bodies on endothelial cells appears to be highly concentration dependent. Zou et al. reported that 5 mM β-HB reduced endothelial angiotensin-converting enzyme (eACE) activity, resulting in pulmonary hemorrhage, interstitial and alveolar neutrophil infiltration, and acute pulmonary endothelial dysfunction. In contrast, a lower concentration of 1 mM β-HB exerted a protective effect by inhibiting endothelial cell senescence. These findings underscore the existence of a narrow therapeutic window for ketone body-mediated endothelial protection.
Ketone bodies directly attenuate myocardial fibrosis by interfering with the activation and function of phenotype of cardiac fibroblasts. Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) is the most potent profibrotic cytokine driving the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. As discussed above,β-HB exerts epigenetic regulatory effects through inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby suppressing the transcriptional activity of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. This interference results in reduced expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and effectively blocks fibroblast-to-myofibroblast activation. Activated myofibroblasts represent the primary source of extracellular matrix components, including type I and type III collagens. Consistently, treatment with butyrate markedly downregulates both mRNA and protein levels of Col1a1 and Col3a1, leading to decreased collagen deposition within the myocardial interstitium and consequent improvement in myocardial compliance.
In infiltrating cardiac immune cells, ketone bodies function as potent “immunometabolic regulators” that profoundly shape the cardiac inflammatory microenvironment and influence the progression of pathological remodeling by finely tuning both innate and adaptive immune responses. Chronic elevation of circulating β-HB levels has been shown to alleviate transverse aortic constriction TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and systolic/diastolic dysfunction. These beneficial effects are accompanied by a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and significant suppression of macrophage recruitment and infiltration into the myocardium. Mechanistically, in vitro studies have demonstrated that β-HB directly enhances signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) phosphorylation in interleukin-4 (IL-4)-stimulated macrophages, leading to upregulation of M2 macrophage-specific gene programs. These findings indicate that β-HB actively promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory and pro-repair M2 phenotype via a STAT6-dependent signaling pathway. Such a polarization shift facilitates the clearance of necrotic debris and supports tissue repair, thereby mitigating maladaptive cardiac remodeling under conditions of pressure overload.
Beyond its effects on macrophages, ketone bodies also exert significant regulatory effects on adaptive immune cells, particularly CD4+T lymphocytes. Notably, ketone bodies have been reported to suppress the differentiation of naive CD4+T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs). More importantly, conditioned media derived from ketone body-treated Tregs can synergistically activate cardiac fibroblasts, resulting in enhanced fibroblast proliferation and increased collagen synthesis, thereby directly promoting cardiac fibrosis and pathological remodeling. This observation reveals an apparent “ketone body paradox”: despite their intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties, ketone bodies may indirectly elicit pro-fibrotic effects through specific immune cell states, particularly Treg-mediated fibroblast activation. Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity and context dependency of ketone body-mediated immune regulation in the heart, suggesting that ultimate impact on cardiac remodeling is determined by the net balance of effects across distinct immune cell subsets.

## The association between ketone bodies and myocardial hypertrophy as well as heart failure
In cardiometabolic research, ketone bodies are among the most extensively investigated metabolites related to cellular energy metabolism. In cardiovascular diseases such as myocardial hypertrophy, heart failure, and cardiac arrhythmia, ketone bodies have been increasingly recognized as critical metabolic regulators rather than merely alternative energy substrates. Dysregulation of ketone body metabolism is tightly linked to impaired myocardial energy supply, heightened oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which are key drivers of the progression of myocardial hypertrophy and HF, irrespective of ejection fraction phenotype. In the pathological process of heart failure, remodeling of ketone body metabolism represents far more than a passive biomarker change. Rather, it reflects profound dysregulation of myocardial energy metabolism and interconnected molecular signaling networks. Clinical studies have consistently shown that circulating ketone body (β-HB), are elevated in patients with heart failure and are independently associated with disease severity and adverse prognosis. , 
Mechanistically, β-HB exerts pleiotropic cardioprotective effects that extend beyond its role as an energy substrate. As an endogenous inhibitor of class IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs), β-HB suppresses HDAC activity, reduces histone deacetylation, and enhances histone acetylation, thereby promoting chromatin relaxation and upregulating transcriptional programs involved in antioxidant defense and cell survival, including Forkhead box O3a (Foxo3a)–dependent pathways. In parallel, β-HB directly binds to and inhibits the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome, preventing caspase-1 activation and markedly reducing the maturation and release of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18, ultimately attenuating myocardial inflammatory infiltration. Furthermore, β-HB has been shown to modulate mitochondrial protein acetylation status, thereby enhancing the activity of multiple mitochondrial enzymes. This coordinated regulation improves mitochondrial antioxidant capacity and preserves mitochondrial functional homeostasis, collectively slowing the progression of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in murine models. 
With disease progression, Rame et al. analyzed myocardial tissue from patients with advanced heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and observed a striking metabolic dissociation: despite elevated plasma β-HB levels and upregulated SCOT, myocardial β-HB and its key intermediate succinyl-CoA were markedly reduced. This phenomenon-characterized by elevated circulating β-HB but diminished myocardial ketone body content in the presence of increased SCOT expression-suggests thatimpaired myocardial ketone body utilization limits the cardioprotective potential of sustained hyperketonemia. Consequently, ketone bodies may transition from adaptive protective signals during the compensatory phase to heart failure to passive biomarkers of metabolic derangement in advanced disease, culminating in the paradoxical of circulating hyperketonemia and myocardial ketone utilization disorders”. Several mechanisms may underlie this metabolic uncoupling. Progressive deterioration of the cardiomyocyte milieu-including acidosis and excessive oxidative stress-may suppress the activity of key ketone-oxidizing enzymes, such as β-HBBDH1. In addition, severe mitochondrial dysfunction may constrain effective flux through TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), rendering ketone body oxidation inefficient despite adequate substrate availability. Under these conditions, persistently elevated circulating ketone bodies may no longer confer cardioprotection and may even exert adverse effects through as-yet-undefined mechanisms, such as aberrant protein modifications at supraphysiological concentrations. Notably, clinical studies have demonstrated that elevated plasma ketone body levels are associated with increased all-cause mortality and heart failure incidence in the elderly populations. Consistently, Melenovsky et al. examined 867 patients with advanced HFrEF and reported a strong correlation between plasma β-HB levels and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Mechanistic investigations further indicate that neurohormonal stress is amplified in heart failure, leading to reduced ketone body clearance and enhanced hepatic ketogenesis. This compensatory rise in circulating ketone bodies is thought to promote myocardial ketone oxidation, particularly in earlier disease stages. Importantly, the core value of ketone bodies lies in their ability to provide an additional energy source for the failing heart. Ketone bodies are transported into cardiomyocytes primarily via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and are subsequently converted by BDH1 and SCOT into acetyl-CoA, which directly enters the TCA cycle. This metabolic pathway bypasses key rate-limiting enzymes of glucose oxidation and therefore does not interfere with glucose utilization. Moreover, elevated ketone body availability has been shown to significantly suppress fatty acid oxidation rates in HFrEF hearts, potentially through feedback inhibition of CPT1, the rate-limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid uptake. These properties render ketone bodies an energetically efficient and metabolically favorable substrate under conditions of cardiac energy stress. Accordingly, this adaptive remodeling of ketone body metabolism is widely regarded as a cardioprotective response to metabolic stress. Supporting this concept, experimental studies have demonstrated that mice with impaired cardiac ketone oxidation develop severe heart failure following injury, whereas exogenous ketone body supplementation improves cardiac function in models of HFpEF. By supplying high concentrations of exogenous ketone bodies, the stagnant TCA cycle may be driven forward through substrate abundance, while downstream protective signaling pathways are robustly activated, thereby partially bypassing dysfunctional endogenous metabolic regulation.
In recent years, accumulating clinical evidence has further substantiated the close association between ketone body metabolism and the progression of heart failure. Using proteomic profiling, Aubert et al. demonstrated that in myocardial tissue from patients with early-stage heart failure (NYHA class II), expression of BDH1, a key enzyme mediating ketone body oxidation, was significantly upregulated compared with that in non-failing controls. Concurrently, circulating levels of succinate and hydroxybutyryl carnitine, metabolites derived from ketone body metabolism, were elevated, suggesting that the failing heart activates a compensatory ketone body utilization program during the early stage of heart failure to offset impaired energy supply. From a therapeutic perspective, emerging translational and clinical studies provide direct evidence supporting the functional benefits of exogenous ketone body supplementation. A preclinical translational study published in 2024 reported that 24 patients with HFrEF who received 2-week supplementation of ketone esters (predominantly 3-hydroxybutyrate, 3-OHB) exhibited significant improvements in hemodynamic parameters at rest and during exercise. This intervention was associated with a reduction in N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels and an increase in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was increased (37% vs. 34%), indicating that exogenous ketone bodies can directly enhance myocardial energy metabolism and cardiac performance in HFrEF patients. 
Similarly, in patients with HFpEF, Gopalasingam et al. conducted a randomized crossover study involving 24 individuals with concomitant HFpEF and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Each participant underwent two 14-day treatment periods with either ketone ester beverages or placebo, separated by a 2-week washout interval. Right heart catheterization performed at the end of each treatment period revealed that ketone ester administration increased cardiac output by 0.2 L/min (95% CI, 0.1–0.3) within 4 h and reduced resting pulmonary capillary wedge pressure by 1 mmHg (95% CI, −2 to 0), reflecting improved cardiac output, reduced filling pressures, and decreased ventricular stiffness. Despite these encouraging findings, an apparent paradox remains. Despite these encouraging findings, an apparent paradox remains. While experimental and clinical studies indicate that ketone bodies can exert cardioprotective effects in HF, elevated endogenous circulating ketone body levels are frequently associated with adverse outcomes in advanced HF and even in populations without overt cardiovascular disease. Experimental evidence from Takahara and colleagues demonstrated that long-term exogenous ketone supplementation attenuated the decline of cardiac function in murine HF models. In contrast, epidemiological studies in diverse ethnic populations without established cardiovascular diseases have linked higher circulating total ketone body levels to increased risks of cardiovascular events, all-cause mortality, and incident heart failure. Collectively, this indicates that ensuring appropriate ketone body levels is crucial, which is not only essential for the normal function of cells but also extremely important for preventing potential diseases related to metabolic abnormalities.
It is important to note that ketone body metabolic remodeling and its functional implications differ substantially between HFrEF and HFpEF. In HFrEF, which is characterized by impaired systolic function and severe mitochondrial dysfunction, the heart exhibits an increased reliance on ketone bodies as alternative energy substrates to compensate for diminished fatty acid oxidation and glucose utilization. Clinical studies consistently demonstrate that circulating β-HB levels are elevated in patients with advanced HFrEF and are closely associated with disease severity and adverse clinical outcomes. In contrast, HFpEF is predominantly characterized by diastolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and relatively preserved mitochondrial oxidative capacity. In this context, ketone bodies may exert cardioprotective effects primarily through anti-inflammatory and antioxidative signaling mechanisms rather than serving as major energy substrates. Preclinical studies in HFpEF models have shown that β-HB supplementation improves diastolic function by targeting mitochondrial inflammatory pathways and attenuating oxidative stress. Collectively, these distinctions underscore the necessity of phenotype-specific strategies for modulating ketone body metabolism in heart failure, highlighting that therapeutic interventions should be tailored to the unique metabolic and pathophysiological features of HFrEF and HFpEF.

## Succinate: From metabolic intermediate to pathogenic signaling molecule

## Metabolism, accumulation of succinate and origin as a “danger signal”
Succinate is a key four-carbon dicarboxylic acid intermediate of the TCA cycle and has long been regarded as a central hub of cellular energy metabolism. ## Characteristics of succinate as a metabolic intermediate
Under physiological conditions, succinate serves as a central metabolite within the cardiomyocytes energy metabolism network, with its turnover tightly integrated into mitochondrial functional. Succinate is generated in the mitochondrial matrix by succinyl-CoA synthetase, which catalyzes the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate in a reaction coupled to substrate-level phosphorylation of guanosine triphosphate (GTP). GTP can subsequently be converted to ATP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase, making this step the only reaction within the TCA cycle that directly yields a high-energy phosphate bond. The newly formed succinate is then oxidized to fumarate by succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), also known as mitochondrial respiratory chain Complex II. During this process, which subsequently donates electrons to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q) via iron-sulfur clusters, thereby feeding into the electron transport chain (ETC) and driving oxidative phosphorylation. As such, succinate metabolism represents a critical interface between the TCA cycle and the ETC and exhibits several defining characteristics. First, succinate functions as a bidirectional hub linking the TCA cycle and electron transport chain. Unlike other TCA cycle that participate exclusively in intracycle reactions, succinate oxidation is catalyzed by SDH, which simultaneously serves as a component of the ETC(Complex II). This dual identity makes succinate the only metabolite that directly couples carbon metabolism with mitochondrial energy production. Through this mechanism, succinate oxidation sustains TCA cycle flux to preserve intermediate pool homeostasis while concurrently supplying electrons for oxidative phosphorylation, thereby enabling efficient coupling of carbon skeleton oxidation and ATP generation. Consistent with this concept, studies have demonstrated that SDH-mediated succinate oxidation is a central determinant of coordinated TCA cycle activity and electron transport efficiency in cardiomyocytes, and that SDH dysfunction can simultaneously impair carbon metabolism and energy production disorders. Second, succinate metabolism confers flexible energy regulation under stress conditions. During succinate oxidation, SDH transfers electrons to the ubiquinone pool in the inner mitochondrial membrane without passing through Complex I. This unique feature provides cardiomyocytes with adaptive flexibility during fluctuations in energy demand or mitochondrial dysfunction. For instance, when Complex I activity is compromised by ischemia or toxin injury, succinate oxidation can sustain ubiquinone reduction and ATP synthesis via a functional “bypass” pathway, thereby preserving basic energy supply for myocardial contraction. In hypoxia models of isolated rat cardiomyocytes, exogenous succinate supplementation has been shown to attenuate ATP depletion and delay cellular disfunction through activation of this bypass mechanism. Third, succinate participates in multi-pathway metabolic coordination through intermediate exchange. Beyond its role in ATP production, succinate metabolism contributes to the regulation of metabolic network balance. Its precursor, succinyl-CoA, serves as a branching point for pathways such as heme biosynthesis and ketone body metabolism, while fluctuations in succinate levels can feedbackregulation on upstream TCA cycle enzymes, including isocitrate dehydrogenase. Through these mechanisms, succinate facilitates coordinated optimization of carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolism, which is particularly critical for high-energy-consuming cardiomyocytes. Notably, work by Cheng and colleagues demonstrated that succinate accumulation caused resulting from SDH dysfunction disrupts this integrated metabolic balance, induces a pathological shift from fatty acid oxidation toward glycolysis, and ultimately promotes the development of cardiomyopathy. Notably, under pathological conditions such as myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, succinate metabolism undergoes profound reprogramming. During the early stage of ischemia, reduced oxygen concentration leads to impairment of the electron transport chain, suppression of SDH activity, and, in some contexts, reversal of SDH function. Under these conditions, SDH, which normally catalyzes succinate oxidation, may operate in reverse, utilizing reducing equivalents from the ubiquinone pool to convert fumarate into succinate. Concurrently, ischemia promotes the metabolic precursors derived from purine nucleotide degradation and reversal of the malate-aspartate shuttle, collectively driving a marked intracellular accumulation of succinate in cardiomyocytes. However, alternative evidence suggests that in intact cardiomyocytes and in vivo models of myocardial ischemia, succinate accumulation predominantly originates from continued flux through the conventional TCA cycle rather than from SDH-mediated reverse catalysis. This mechanistic discrepancy remains an area of active investigation and requires further experimental. Additional studies have demonstrated that SDH inhibition is closely linked to oxidative damage and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload during ischemia-reperfusion injury. ROS generated upon reperfusion can directly damage the structural integrity of SDH, while intracellular Ca2+ overload further exacerbates enzyme dysfunction by disrupting the conformation of its catalytic center. Together, these processes establish a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of succinate accumulation - mitochondrial damage. When succinate levels exceed physiological thresholds, its functional role shifts from that of a metabolic intermediate to that of a potent signaling molecule. Excess succinate participates in the regulation of pathological processes such as myocardial remodeling and inflammatory response through multiple mechanisms, including stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), activating succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1), and induction of protein succinylation modification. 

## Multiple mechanisms of succinate as a signaling molecule

## G protein-coupled receptor signaling: A key mediator of intercellular communication
Succinate acts as an endogenous and specific ligand for the G protein-coupled receptor SUCNR1 (also known as GPR91), which is abundantly expressed in cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts. Upon binding of succinate, SUCNR1 preferentially activates two principal intracellular signaling pathways, namely Gq and Gi/o. Activation of the Gq pathway leads to recruitment and stimulation of phospholipase C-β (PLC-β), resulting in the generation of inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). These second messengers induce intracellular Ca2+ mobilization and subsequent activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) signaling cascade, thereby regulating cellular proliferation and differentiation. In parallel, activation of the Gi/o pathway suppresses adenylate cyclase (AC) activity, leading to reduced intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels. This reduction in cAMP signaling alleviates inhibitory constraints on the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, thereby promoting the transcription of pro-inflammatory factors and fibrosis-related genes. Through these receptor-mediated signaling mechanisms, SUCNR1 directly couples intracellular succinate metabolic accumulation to cardiac tissue remodeling, establishing an integrated regulatory axis of metabolic changes-receptor activation-pathological response. This axis plays a pivotal role in the progression of cardiovascular pathologies, including myocardial hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis.

## Hypoxia-inducible factor stabilization: A metabolic regulator of hypoxic responses
Stabilizing HIF-1αrepresents one of the most emblematic signaling functions of succinate and serves as a canonical example of metabolite regulation of hypoxic responses. Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is hydroxylated by HIF prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), which targets it for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation via the proteasome. When succinate accumulates to supraphysiological levels, its structural similarity to 2-oxoglutarate, the obligate co-substrate of PHDs, enables succinate to competitively inhibit PHD activity, thereby preventing HIF-1α hydroxylation. As a result, stabilized HIF-1α escapes proteasomal degradation, translocates into the nucleus, heterodimerizes with HIF-1β, and activates the transcription of a broad array of downstream target genes. These include genes involved in metabolic remodeling, such as glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), hexokinase 2 (HK2),which facilitate a shift from oxidative phosphorylation toward glycolysis, angiogenesis-related genes, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promote neovascularization, and genes associated with inflammation and fibrosis, such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs),which accelerate pathological myocardial remodeling. 

## Reactive oxygen species generation trigger: An amplifier of oxidative stress
Succinate represents a central mitochondrial source of ROS during I/R injury, and succinate-driven ROS generation is distinguished by its highly explosive nature. During ischemia, succinate accumulates extensively within cardiomyocytes. Upon reperfusion, the rapid restoration of oxygen availability triggers accelerated oxidation of accumulated succinate is rapidly oxidized by SDH, which drives electrons backward from Complex II to Complex I through a process known as reverse electron transport (RET). ROS generation via RET occurs with substantially greater efficiency than that produced during forward electron transport, resulting in the abrupt production of large quantities of superoxide anions (O2−) within a short time frame. This burst of ROS overwhelms the intrinsic antioxidant defense systems of cardiomyocytes. Excessive ROS not only directly damages mitochondrial DNA and membrane proteins but also activates oxidative stress-responsive signaling pathways, including JNK/p38. Activation of these pathways amplifies apoptotic and inflammatory signaling cascades, thereby exacerbating cardiomyocyte injury and tissue damage during I/R injury.

## Potent promoter of inflammatory responses: A metabolic switch for innate immunity
Succinate promotes inflammatory responses through multiple converging mechanisms. When succinate accumulates intracellularly, it not only synergistically enhances the expression of inflammatory mediators via SUCNR1 and HIF-1α dependent signaling but also directly triggers the assembly and activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome through two principal pathways. First, succinate-driven RET generates excessive mitochondrial ROS, which function as danger-associated signal capable of oxidizing critical cysteine residues of the NLRP3 protein. This redox modification induces conformational changes that facilitate the recruitment of apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) and caspase-1, thereby promoting inflammasome complex assembly. Second, succinate activates phospholipase C-β (PLC-β) via SUCNR1, leading to intracellular Ca2+ release, and Ca2+ overload further accelerates NLRP3 inflammasome oligomerization and activation. Once activated, the NLRP3 inflammasome cleaves pro-interleukin-1β (pro-IL-1β) into its mature, bioactive form IL-1β. This potent pro-inflammatory cytokine not only directly exacerbates cardiomyocyte injury but also promotes the recruitment and infiltration of immune cells, including neutrophils and monocytes, into myocardial tissue, thereby amplifying the local inflammatory cascade.
Macrophage phenotype switching is a critical regulatory node in inflammatory responses, and succinate serves as a central metabolic signal driving macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory state. Upon activation by stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), macrophages undergo metabolic reprogramming characterized by a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, accompanied by a marked accumulation of intracellular succinate. Elevated succinate enhances mitochondrial oxidation through SDH and increases mitochondrial membrane potential, together driving robust mitochondrial ROS production. SDH- dependent succinate oxidation and the resulting ROS act as key signals that induce a pro-inflammatory transcriptional program, thereby promoting macrophage differentiation toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of succinate oxidation (e.g., dimethyl malonate) or attenuation of mitochondrial ROS generation (e.g., through expressing alternative oxidase AOX) suppresses the pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization and even favor anti-inflammatory phenotypes. 

## Protein succinylation modification: Direct coupling of metabolism and function
Lysine succinylation (Ksucc) was first reported in 2011 and, relative to methylation and acetylation, can induce more pronounced alterations in protein charge, conformation, and function. Succinate is not only a metabolic intermediate and signaling molecule but also serves as a succinyl-group donor for protein lysine succinylation (Ksu). This emerging post-translational modification (PTM) can directly “label” target proteins, thereby dynamically coupling intracellular succinate availability to protein functions. In this way, succinylation provides a direct molecular bridge through which metabolic status is translated into changes in cellular physiology and pathology, with important roles in regulating metabolic enzyme activity, mitochondrial homeostasis, and disease-related signaling pathways. Succinylation is widely observed on key enzymes of the TCA cycle. For instance, during myocardial ischemia, succinate accumulation can drive excessive succinylation of lysine residues on IDH, directly inhibiting its catalytic activity. This inhibition promotes accumulation of upstream TCA cycle intermediates and further exacerbating metabolic derangements. In a developmental context, Fukushima et al. perfused hearts from neonatal rabbits of different ages to investigate the contribution of novel PTMs to the metabolic transition from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation. They reported that the succinylation levels of pyruvate dehydrogenase (a rate-limiting enzyme for glucose oxidation) and long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidase) increased with age, and that succinylation was associated with enhanced activity of both enzymes. 
Beyond metabolic enzymes, succinylation can also modulate transcriptional regulation by directly affecting transcription factors and chromatin structure, thereby influencing the expression of disease-related genes. Early studies showed that histone succinylation promotes transcription at the nucleosome core. Histone succinylation is enriched at gene promoters, and promoter-associated histone succinylation correlates positively with transcriptional activity. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing further revealed a distinctive promoter-proximal localization pattern of chromatin succinylation, supporting its role in transcriptional control and association with gene expression programs. Importantly, subsequent work indicated that chromatin succinylation can mechanistically link TCA cycle defects to epigenetic regulation of transcription. By weakening histone-DNA interactions, reducing nucleosomes and chromosomes stability, and facilitating chromatin relaxation, histone succinylation enhances accessibility for transcription factors binding and thereby promotes gene transcription. 

## Pathogenic effects of succinate in different cardiac cell types ( )

## Effects of succinate on cardiac cardiomyocytes
Cardiomyocytes are highly efficient energy-producing cells that rely predominantly on fatty acid oxidation to meet approximately 70% of their ATP demand, with the remaining energy supplied by glucose metabolism and other substrate pathways. Accumulating evidence indicates that excessive succinate accumulation suppresses the activity of enzymes involved in fatty acid oxidation, thereby impairing normal β-oxidation and disrupting fatty acid-dependent energy supply. Notably, studies by Cheng and colleagues demonstrated that dysfunction of SDH inthe inner mitochondrial membrane leads to succinate accumulation in cardiomyocytes, which in turn inhibits the transcription of genes associated with fatty acid oxidation and reduces overall fatty acid metabolic capacity. To compensate for the resulting energy deficit, cardiomyocytes undergo metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glycolytic flux, leading to excessive production. Lactic acid accumulation induces intracellular acidification, which adversely affects the activity of multiple intracellular enzymes and further compromises cardiomyocyte function. This sustained imbalance in myocardial energy metabolism ultimately limits ATP availability required to support normal contractile performance, thereby contributing to myocardial contractile dysfunction, reduced cardiac output, and the progression to heart failure. 
Beyond disturbances in energy metabolism, succinate accumulation triggers a series of molecular events that culminate in epigenetic reprogramming. Pathologically elevated succinate levels in cardiomyocytes disrupt DNA methylation dynamics. Mechanistically, succinate inhibits the activity of the α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase family, which is essential for active DNA demethylation. Findings from Cheng and colleagues demonstrated that SDH deficiency induces global genomic hypermethylation within the cardiomyocyte nucleus. This aberrant DNA methylation landscape profoundly alters gene expression, particularly genes involved in related to cardiomyocyte energy metabolism, structure integrity, and contractile function. 
Notably, genes associated with fatty acid oxidation are especially affected, increased methylation inhibits their transcription, leading to reduced expression of fatty acid oxidation-related proteins and further impairing of fatty acid metabolic capacity. Importantly, pharmacological intervention with DNA methylation inhibitors in cellular and animal models partially reverses succinate-induced DNA methylation inhibitors in cellular and animal models partially reverses succinate-induced DNA hypermethylation and restores, at least in part, normal gene expression patterns and cardiomyocyte function. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of succinate-driven epigenetic alterations in the initiation and progression of myocardial diseases.

## Effects of succinate on cardiac endothelial cells
Accumulating evidence indicates that succinate markedly exacerbates endothelial dysfunction through multiple interrelated mechanisms. One such mechanism involves the promotion of mitochondrial ROS production via translocation of cytoplasmic Drp1 to the outer mitochondrial membrane, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation and oxidative stress. In addition, oxidized succinate can further amplify ROS generation through reverse electron transport. , Excessive ROS impairs the vasodilatory effects of nitric oxide on endothelial cells and disrupts NO bioavailability by inhibiting its synthesis, thereby aggravating endothelial dysfunction.
Beyond ROS-mediated effects, succinate activates the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) through interaction with SUCNR1. SUCNR1 activation elevates angiotensin II levels, promoting vasoconstriction and suppressing endothelial NO production, which further contributes to endothelial dysfunction. Moreover, engagement of succinate with GPR91 on platelets enhances thromboxane A2 production, leading to platelet activation. Platelet activation plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis (AS). As platelet adhesion to the endothelium induces endothelial injury, triggers pro-inflammatory responses, and increases the risk of thrombotic events. In addition, succinate directly promotes the endothelial inflammatory by activating intracellular signaling pathways, notably the NF-κB pathway. The subsequent infiltration of inflammatory cells further amplifies vascular injury through the release of proteases and reactive oxygen species, ultimately compromising vascular endothelial integrity and disrupting the structure and functional stability of the vascular wall.

## Effects of succinate on smooth muscle cells
Emerging evidence indicates that succinate accumulation during the progression of AS promotes smooth muscle cell (SMC) proliferation and intimal migration. The pathological activation of SMCs by succinate can be attributed principal signaling pathways. First, succinate stimulates activation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), leading to the release of angiotensin II. Angiotensin II promotes fibrosis, SMC growth, and hypertrophy via activation of the SMAD signaling pathway. Additionally, angiotensin II enhances the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, thereby driving the phenotypic switch of SMCs from the contractile to asynthetic state. Once transformed, SMCs secrete inflammatory mediators and extracellular matrix (ECM) components, collectively accelerating vascular remodeling and AS progression. Second, elevated succinate levels promote the stabilization and transcriptional activity of HIF-1α, in part through the modulation of Fe2+-dependent enzymatic activity. Increased HIF-1α signaling stimulates SMC proliferation by inducing the expression of growth factors and promoting mitochondrial fission, thereby supporting the metabolic and structural demands of SMC expansion. Third, succinate activates the NF-κB signaling pathway via engagement of NF-κB ligands. This activation enhances the synthesis of cytoskeletal proteins in SMCs and drives their phenotypic differentiation, proliferation, and migration through the NF-κB/mTOR pathway.

## Effects of succinate on cardiac immune cells
Succinate also promotes the progression of AS by driving macrophages polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Elevated intracellular succinate levels stabilize HIF-1α, which subsequently induces the production of IL-1β. In addition, succinate-induced ROS generation and engagement of succinate receptor 1 (GPR91/SUCNR1) synergistically enhance the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-18, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and TNF-β, thereby reinforcing macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory stat. Beyond macrophages, succinate can stimulate dendritic cells to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines through activation of GPR91 expressed on their surface. Cytokines released by dendritic cells, such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, further contribute to AS progression and help sustain the pro-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions. Recent studies s have also highlighted the critical roles of lymphocytes, including B cells and T cells, in AS pathogenesis. However, the potential contribution of succinate to AS development through modulation of B cell and T cell function remains poorly defined. Elucidating the immunomodulatory effects of succinate on adaptive immune responses may therefore represent a promising avenue for future research in AS.

## The association between succinate and cardiovascular diseases
In cardiometabolic research, succinate is one of the most intensively investigated metabolites of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. In particular, for diseases such as aortic dissection, heart failure, and ischemic cardiomyopathy, succinate has been identified as both a clinically relevant risk marker and a key pathogenic mediator. , , Accumulating evidence further indicates that aberrant succinate accumulation is tightly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, which are key drivers of the development of cardiovascular diseases. 
Elevated succinate levels resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction are closely implicated in the pathogenesis of atrial fibrillation. Through activation of its specific receptor SUCNR1, succinate s concurrently engages multiple downstream signaling pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB pathways, thereby promoting the initiation and maintenance of atrial fibrillation. Acting as a pro-inflammatory signal, succinate induces IL-1β production in an HIF-1α. IL-1β not only intensifies myocardial inflammatory infiltration but also activates MAPK family members such as p38 MAPK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Activated MAPKs further promotes the phosphorylation and degradation of inhibitor of κB (IκB), relieving NF-κB inhibition and establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies inflammatory signaling. Concurrently, MAPK signaling mediates the transcription of myocardial hypertrophy-related genes, including ANP and BNP). These coordinated pathological processes drive cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and adverse cardiac remodeling, ultimately exacerbating pathological myocardial hypertrophy. Consistent with this mechanism, clinical studies and animal experiments have demonstrated that succinate promotes myocardial hypertrophy under pressure overload conditions through activation of the SUCNR1-MAPK signaling axis. Mechanistic investigations further revealed that succinate induces cardiac hypertrophy in a GPR91-dependent manner extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) phosphorylation, upregulating calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase IIδ (CaMKIIδ), and promoting cytoplasmic translocation of histone deacetylase 5 (HDAC5), all of which are hallmark hypertrophic signaling events. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of SUCNR1 effectively blocks succinate-stimulated MAPK activation and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Beyond hypertrophic remodeling, succinate accumulation contributes to heart failure through multiple complementary mechanisms. Succinate promotes myocardial fibrosis by stimulating cardiac fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, thereby impairing diastolic function. It also disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism and ATP production, adversely affecting atrial remodeling, and perturbs intracellular calcium homeostasis, leading to excitation-contraction coupling dysfunction and worsening hemodynamic disorders in heart failure. Collectively, these results indicate that pathological succinate accumulation is a critical driver of myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure, and the SUCNR1-MAPK signaling pathway serving as a central mediator of succinate’s pro-hypertrophic effect. 
Clinical evidence indicates that succinate levels are associated with both the incidence and severity of chronic heart failure (CHF). Experimental studies further demonstrated that succinate accumulation promotes myocardial interstitial and perivascular fibrosis, increase myocardial stiffness and ultimately leads to diastolic dysfunction. Consistent with these findings, elevated serum succinate levels have been reported in patients with cardiac hypertrophy secondary to both acute and chronic ischemic diseases. Beyond heart failure and hypertrophy, succinate has emerged as a potential diagnostic marker for aortic dissection. Untargeted metabolomic analyses have revealed that plasma succinate concentrations are significantly higher in patients with acute aortic dissection (AAD) than in healthy controls as well as in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients, and pulmonary embolism (PE) patients. Importantly, after adjustment for confounding variables such as age, gender, and hypertension, succinate remains an independent predictor of AAD, highlighting its potential clinical value in differential diagnosis and risk stratification. 
As discussed above, cellular energy metabolism and succinate homeostasis are tightly interconnected through the TCAcycle. Within this framework, ketone body metabolism represents a central component of myocardial energy metabolism and forms a complex interactive network with succinate through shared metabolic intermediates as well as functionally antagonistic signaling pathways. Therefore, clarifying the specific mechanisms by which energy metabolism disorders represented by ketone bodies contribute to abnormal succinate accumulation is crucial for exploring the therapeutic value of targeting this metabolic axis in cardiovascular diseases.

## Integrated mechanisms and disease roles of the ketone body-succinate axis

## Interactive network of metabolism and signaling

## Mitochondria: A shared hub for ketone body-succinate metabolic crosstalk and a central regulator of cardiovascular disease
Mitochondria are not only the cellular “powerhouses” but also a central hub integrating metabolism, signal transduction, and cell-fate determination in cardiovascular diseases. , , During the progression of myocardial injury and heart failure, structural and functional mitochondrial abnormalities represent one of the core mechanisms driving energy deficiency, oxidative stress, and cardiomyocyte death. , , The pivotal role of mitochondria in cardiac energy metabolism is further underscored by their function as the principal intracellular site for multiple cardiovascular disease-related metabolic pathways. Ketone bodies, primarily β-HB, are transported into cardiomyocyte mitochondria via MCT1. Through sequential catalysis by BDH1 and succinyl-CoA: SCOT, ketone bodies are converted into acetyl-CoA and subsequently enter the TCA cycle for oxidative energy production. Owing to their higher energetic efficiency compared with fatty acids, ketone bodies constitute an important component of adaptive metabolic remodeling in the failing heart. Succinate is a key intermediate of the TCA cycle, generated from succinyl-CoA by succinyl-CoA synthetase and oxidized to fumarate by SDH. Notably, SDH also functions as mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II, directly coupling metabolic flux with electron transport and serving as a critical node of oxidative phosphorylation. Under pathological conditions, mitochondrial dysfunction provides the structural basis linking disturbances in ketone body metabolism with succinate dysregulation. In the early stages of cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation is accompanied by a compensatory increase in ketone body utilization. As disease progresses, reductions in mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory chain activity suppress SDH function, leading to pathological succinate accumulation. Accumulated succinate promotes excessive ROS generation through reverse electron transport, whereas ketone bodies enhance antioxidant defenses by activating SIRT3 and inhibiting HDACs. Thus, within the same organelle, succinate and ketone bodies form a dynamic redox antagonism. Beyond serving as a site of metabolic reactions, mitochondria also act as generators of metabolic signals. Consequently, mitochondria constitute both the physical and functional platform for the metabolic interplay between ketone bodies and succinate, and mitochondrial dysfunction represents the structural basis for imbalance within this metabolic axis. A deeper understanding of the central role of mitochondria in metabolic integration will facilitate a more systematic elucidation of how ketone bodies and succinate interact during cardiovascular disease progression, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for therapeutic strategies targeting this metabolic axis ( ). 

## Interactions between ketone bodies and succinate
As closely interconnected metabolic nodes in the TCA cycle, ketone bodies and succinate display distinct yet concurrent metabolic features in heart failure, characterized by increased ketone body utilization and pathological succinate accumulation, respectively. Growing evidence suggests that they form a functionally antagonistic metabolic regulatory network in cardiomyocytes. Specifically, β-HB exerts cardioprotective effects through epigenetic regulation and inhibition of inflammasome activation, , whereas succinate promotes pathological cardiac remodeling via SUCNR1-dependent signaling and stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors n. Importantly, the interaction between ketone bodies and succinate represents an integrated metabolic regulatory axis that extends beyond their isolated biological roles. This interaction does not arise from direct molecular binding but is instead supported by substantial evidence demonstrating that ketone bodies and succinate exert opposing regulatory effects on shared downstream pathways within the a common metabolic milieu. Conceptually, the altered metabolic state of the failing heart serves as a unifying upstream driver that simultaneously reshapes ketone body and succinate homeostasis. Through their antagonistic signaling actions, these metabolites collectively determine the ultimate cardiac phenotype, thereby highlighting the ketone body-succinate axis as a critical determinant of metabolic adaptation versus maladaptation in heart failure.
First, at the metabolic level, the two are tightly interconnected through the TCA cycle. In heart failure, enhanced ketone body oxidation represents an adaptive response aimed at supplying a highly efficient energy substrate to the failing myocardium. In principle, increased ketone body derived acetyl-CoA replenishes the TCA cycle and may help overcome metabolic bottlenecks caused by impaired SDH activity, thereby potentially mitigating pathological succinate accumulation. Supporting this concept, Bertoni et al. demonstrated that a ketogenic diet increases SDH activity in aging cardiomyocytes. Given that SDH is the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for succinate oxidation, we reasonably hypothesize that a ketogenic diet may regulate succinate generation. Recent studies have further uncovered a molecular link between succinate metabolism and ketone body catabolism involving succinyl-CoA ligase ADP-forming subunit β (SUCLA2), a TCA cycle enzyme that catalyzes the reversible conversion between succinate and succinyl-CoA. In hepatocellular carcinoma cells, phosphorylated SUCLA2 interacts with OXCT1, a key enzyme in in ketone body degradation. Through this interaction, SUCLA2-derived succinyl-CoA not only provides metabolic substrates for OXCT1 but also induces succinylation of OXCT1 at lysine 421, thereby enhancing its enzymatic activity, promoting ketone body catabolism, and increasing ATP production to support tumor cell proliferation and growth. This study highlights SUCLA2 as a metabolic integrator linking succinate metabolism to ketone body utilization and establishes an integrated regulatory axis that coordinates cellular metabolic flux and functional outcomes. Although these observations were derived from oncological models, both SUCLA2 and OXCT1 are highly expressed in cardiomyocytes. This expression pattern strongly suggests that a conserved “succinate metabolism—SUCLA2-OXCT1-ketone body catabolism” axis may also operate in the heart, potentially constituting a core molecular mechanism underlying the metabolic coupling between succinate and ketone bodies in cardiac physiology and disease.
Secondly, at the level of cellular signaling, more direct evidence supports a functional antagonism between succinate and ketone bodies. Succinate has been identified as a potent activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome through engagement of SUCNR1 and stabilization of HIF-1α. In contrast, the ketone body metabolite β-HB has been consistently demonstrated across multiple studies to act as an endogenous inhibitor of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Thus, within the inflammatory microenvironment of the myocardium, functions as a pro-inflammatory signal that amplifies inflammasome activation, whereas ketone bodies counterbalance this effect by suppressing inflammatory signaling. A similar antagonistic relationship is observed in the regulation of oxidative stress. Succinate accumulation triggers for mitochondrial ROS production, particularly through reverse electron transport at respiratory chain complex I. Conversely, ketone bodies enhance cellular antioxidant capacity by activating cytoprotective pathways, including Nrf2. This coordinated “push-pull” regulation at shared inflammatory and redox signaling nodes provides compelling evidence for functional crosstalk within the ketone body-succinate axis and underscores its role as a critical determinant of inflammatory and oxidative balance in cardiovascular disease.
Notably, although both ketone bodies and succinate play pivotal roles in metabolic remodeling during heart failure, the majority of existing studies have examined their alterations and functions in isolation. A largely underexplored yet fundamental question is whether these two metabolites, both positioned at the core of the TCA cycle, are intrinsically and functionally linked within the pathological milieu of heart failure? Emerging evidence supports the concept that ketone bodies and succinate may together constitute a coordinated “metabolic axis” that shapes cardiac fate through the integration of metabolic coupling and signaling antagonism. Mechanistically, several lines of evidence suggest a direct molecular connection between ketone body metabolism and succinate homeostasis. β-HB has been shown to improve mitochondrial metabolic function by upregulating SIRT3 expression and activity, leading to deacetylation and activation of multiple mitochondrial metabolic enzymes. Importantly, the SDHA has been identified as a critical substrate of SIRT3, and SIRT3-mediated deacetylation of SDHA is required to maintain enzymatic activity. Based on these findings, it is reasonable to hypothesize that elevated intracellular β-HB enhances SIRT3 deacetylase activity, thereby promoting SDHA, increasing SDH activity, and ultimately accelerating succinate oxidation while limiting its pathological accumulation. This regulatory mechanism based on the SIRT3 signaling axis may constitute an important molecular bridge connecting ketone body metabolism and succinate homeostasis.
Based on the above evidence, we propose a putative interaction cascade, elevated β-HB enhanced SIRT3 activity, leading to increased deacetylation of SDHA, activation of SDH,accelerated succinate oxidative clearance of succinate, and subsequent suppression of succinate-mediated SUCNR1 inflammatory signaling. This mechanism directly links the “protective signal” conveyed by ketone bodies with the “damaging signal” driven by succinate, providing a plausible explanation for the dual ability of ketone bodies to ameliorate both metabolic dysfunction and inflammation in heart failure.
Conversely, impaired ketone body oxidation may exacerbate succinate dysregulation through multiple interconnected pathways. First, disruption of ketone body metabolism may directly perturb the dynamic balance between succinate production and clearance by altering the activity of SCOT, a key enzyme linking ketone body utilization to the TCA cycle. , Second, defects in ketone body metabolism are associated with increased mitochondrial ROS generation, which in turn can inhibit SDH activity, thereby establishing a vicious cycle characterized by “ketone body metabolic disorder—exacerbated oxidative stress—suppressed SDH suppression - succinate accumulation”. 
Beyond the specific regulation of SDHA, β-HB may exert broader effects on succinate metabolism by reshaping the mitochondrial post-translational modification landscape. β-HB is known to induce extensive lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) of mitochondrial proteins, potentially modulating the activity of multiple metabolic enzyme complexes, including SDH, and thereby contributing to global dysregulation of the succinate metabolic network. Supporting this concept, treatment with sodium β-hydroxybutyrate (Na-β-HB) has been shown to significantly increase the Kbhb modification at lysine 81 of the succinyl-CoA ligase subunit, resulting in enhanced enzymatic activity. Moreover, a ketogenic diet markedly increased β-HB levels, Kbhb modification and enzymatic activity of the succinyl-CoA ligase, and ATP production, while concomitantly reducing β-amyloid plaque burden and microgliosis in murine models. In parallel, emerging evidence indicates that metabolites such as succinate and short-chain fatty acids can act as systemic signaling molecules, transmitting metabolic information via the autonomic nervous system, crossing the blood-brain barrier, and contributing to cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease models. These findings raise the intriguing hypothesis that ketogenic diet, induced Kbhb modification of the succinyl-CoA ligase subunit may suppress succinate accumulation and thereby ameliorate cognitive dysfunction in neurodegenerative disease. These observations were derived primarily from studies of the nervous system, the Kbhb modification machinery is also present in cardiac tissue, suggesting that this epigenetic regulatory mechanism may represent a conserved and broadly applicable mode of metabolic regulation.
It should be emphasized that current investigations of ketone bodies and succinate in the cardiovascular system remain largely conducted in an “isolated” manner. The pathogenic roles of succinate in cardiovascular disease have been extensively characterized, and the cardioprotective effects of ketone bodies have likewise been well documented. However, direct in vivo and cellular experimental evidence demonstrating how these two metabolites form an integrated regulatory axis through metabolic coupling and signaling antagonism within the pathological microenvironment is still lacking. Accordingly, future studies should focus on elucidating whether the SUCLA2-OXCT1 interaction operates in cardiomyocytes under pathological conditions such as heart failure and ischemic cardiomyopathy, and whether β-HB directly regulates succinate metabolism via SIRT3-dependent mechanisms. Clarifying these issues will be essential for validating the ketone body-succinate metabolic axis and for determining its mechanistic and therapeutic relevance in cardiovascular disease. Notably, accurate characterization of this metabolic axis is highly dependent on the integration of multi-level measurement strategies. At the in vivo level, plasma metabolomic analyses enable quantification of circulating β-HB and succinate concentrations; However, such systemic measurements provide limited information regarding cardiac-specific metabolic remodeling. At the tissue level, targeted mass spectrometry of cardiac samples allows precise determination of myocardial metabolite abundance, while the incorporation of ˆ13C isotope tracing approaches enables resolution of dynamic metabolic fluxes within the tricarboxylic acid cycle. At the live-cell level, recently developed fluorescent probes and genetically encoded sensors permit real-time monitoring of mitochondrial metabolites, offering unprecedented temporal and spatial resolution of intracellular metabolic dynamics. Future studies should therefore integrate these complementary methodologies to construct a comprehensive, spatiotemporally resolved map of the ketone body-succinate metabolic axis in the heart. In parallel, large-scale cohort studies are required to determine whether circulating β-HB and succinate levels exhibit a consistent inverse correlation, and to assess whether this relationship remains independent of other metabolic indicators. Furthermore, translational investigations should evaluate whether combined therapeutic strategies—such as ketone ester supplementation in conjunction with SUCNR1 antagonism—can synergistically improve cardiac function by simultaneously enhancing ketone body-mediated cardioprotection and suppressing succinate-driven pathological signaling. Collectively, these approaches may open new avenues for metabolically targeted interventions in cardiovascular disease.

## Role of the ketone body-succinate axis in the progression from myocardial hypertrophy to heart failure
During the progression of cardiac diseases from compensatory myocardial hypertrophy to decompensated heart failure, dynamic alterations in the ketone body-succinate axis play a critical regulatory role. Dysregulation of this metabolic axis not only contributes to the initiation of myocardial hypertrophy but also critically influences the transition toward overt heart failure. In the compensatory phase of myocardial hypertrophy, the heart preserves functional stability through adaptive metabolic remodeling. During this stage, cardiomyocyte uptake and utilization of ketone bodies are moderately increased, and the plasma β-HB levels are maintained within an optimal range. Through mechanisms including inhibition of HDAC activity and suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, β-HB exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, thereby delaying pathological remodeling. Although succinate levels are already elevated in the early stage of cardiovascular diseases, its pro-hypertrophic and pro-inflammatory actions may be partially counterbalanced by ketone body mediated cardioprotection, allowing the ketone body-succinate axis to remain in a relatively balanced state. As the disease advances to the decompensated stage, this metabolic equilibrium collapses. Persistent pathological stress leads to profound mitochondrial dysfunction and marked suppression of SDH activity, resulting in pathological succinate accumulation. Excess succinate, in turn, drives myocardial fibrosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress through activation of SUCNR1 receptor and stabilization of HIF-1α, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing vicious cycle. Notably, although cardiac uptake of ketone bodies is further increased at this stage, ketone body metabolism becomes impaired. Reduced activity of BDH1 leads to relative intracellular accumulation of β-HB. Abnormally elevated β-HB may paradoxically exacerbate SDH dysfunction by inhibiting SIRT3 activity, further promoting succinate accumulation and metabolic derailment. This shift in metabolic characteristics represents a critical inflection point marking the transition from compensatory adaptation to decompensation failure. At the molecular level, the ketone body-succinate axis influences disease progression through multiple pathways. At the transcriptional level, β-HB modulates the expression of metabolic and stress-responsive genes via histone β-hydroxybutyrylation, whereas succinate regulates hypoxia-responsive genes through HIF-1α. At the signaling level, ketone bodies and succinate exhibit functional antagonism within key pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB signaling. Collectively, these multilayered interactions determine cardiomyocytes fate and govern both the tempo and severity of disease progression toward heart failure.

## Potential and prospects of the ketone body-succinate axis as a therapeutic target
In recent years, advances in the understanding of metabolic mechanisms underlying heart failure have increasingly highlighted the therapeutic potential of the ketone body-succinate axis as a key hub linking cardiac energy metabolism with myocardial remodeling. Maintenance of a dynamic balance within this metabolic axis is crucial for preserving cardiac function, whereas its disruption directly contributes to the initiation and progression of heart failure. With respect to therapeutic strategy development, accumulating preclinical evidence supports the feasibility of targeting the ketone body-succinate axis in heart failure. Exogenous ketone body supplementation, including ketone salts and ketone esters, has been shown to confer cardioprotective effects through dual and complementary mechanisms. First, ketone bodies serve as highly efficient energy substrate, improving myocardial energetic status. Experimental studies demonstrate that a moderate elevation in plasma β-HB levels significantly enhances myocardial ATP production efficiency and mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Second, beyond their metabolic role, ketone bodies function as signaling molecules capable of suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inhibiting histone deacetylase activity, thereby attenuating myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, and adverse remodeling. Importantly, these beneficial effects exhibit a clear dose-dependent profile. Excessive elevation of plasma β-HB (BHBA), particularly beyond approximately 2.4 mM, may exert deleterious effects, potentially through inhibition of SIRT3 activity and disruption of mitochondrial metabolic regulation, suggesting that precise dose regulation is the key to achieving therapeutic benefits.
Substantial progress has also been achieved in therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating pathological succinate accumulation. An increasing body of evidence indicates that pharmacological inhibition of the succinate receptor SUCNR1 represents a promising approach for myocardial protection. For example, in a Trichinella spiralis-induced cardiac fibrosis model, metabolomic analyses revealed marked elevations in myocardial succinate levels, whereas genetic deficiency of SUCNR1 significantly attenuated myocardial fibrosis, supporting a causal role for succinate-SUCNR1 signaling in fibrotic remodeling. In parallel, modulation of SDH activity to enhance succinate oxidation has emerged as another potential therapeutic potential, especially in heart failure related to ischemic cardiomyopathy. 
Considering the complex and bidirectional interactions within the ketone body-succinate axis, combinatorial intervention strategies may offer synergistic therapeutic benefits. In theory, an optimal theoretically optimal plan should include moderate ketone body supplementation to maintain energy metabolism balance, combined with SUCNR1 signal blockage to inhibit pathological remodeling, and supplemented with antioxidant therapy to reduce oxidative stress damage. This multi-target approach may more effectively restore the dynamic balance of the metabolic axis, but its specific implementation plan still needs further optimization. Moreover, patient stratification based on metabolic phenotype, such as high succinate type, impaired ketone body utilization, or mixed metabolic profiles, may facilitate the development of more precise and individualized therapeutic regimens. Although preclinical findings are encouraging, the clinical translation of therapies targeting the ketone body-succinate axis faces several substantial challenges. Key unresolved issues include identification of the optimal treatment time window, comprehensive assessment of long-term safety, potential interactions with established heart failure pharmacotherapies, and the establishment of robust biomarkers to guide individualized treatment. Future research should focus on developing more specific SUCNR1 modulators, exploring precise administration schemes for ketone body supplementation, establishing a heart failure classification system based on metabolic phenotypes, and verifying their effectiveness and safety in human heart failure through well-designed clinical trials.
In conclusion, modulation of the ketone body-succinate axis represents a novel therapeutic paradigm for the prevention and reversal of heart failure. Precise intervention within this interconnected metabolic network has the potential to disrupt the vicious cycle of metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and adverse remodeling that drives HF progression, thereby improving cardiac function at a fundamental metabolic level. As mechanistic insights into this axis continue to expand, metabolic regulation is expected to emerge as an integral component of comprehensive heart failure management, opening new avenues for addressing this major global health problem. Nevertheless, successful clinical translation will require further in-depth investigation into underlying mechanisms, optimization of dosing and administration strategies, and the development of individualized treatment approaches tailored to patient-specific metabolic phenotypes.

## Targeted intervention strategies: From diet to drugs
After elucidating the central role of the ketone body-succinate axis in myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure, a key question emerges: can this metabolic axis be therapeutically modulated through external interventions to confer cardioprotection?? Encouragingly, accumulating evidence indicates that a range of dietary and pharmaceutical strategies can profoundly influence the balance of this axis. These findings not only provides new perspective for understanding the underlying mechanisms of existing therapies but also points out the direction for the development of a new generation of metabolic therapies ( ). 

## Dietary intervention: A pilot strategy to reshape metabolic homeostasis
Among environmental modulators, diet represents most direct and powerful intervention affecting systemic metabolism. In this context, the ketogenic diet, as an extreme form of metabolic manipulation, induces a profound shift in whole-body energy substrate utilization from glucose toward ketone bodies. This metabolic reprogramming makes the ketogenic diet an informative experimental and clinical model for investigating the regulation and pathophysiological significance of the ketone body-succinate axis.
Studies have demonstrated that the ketogenic diet can markedly elevate plasma β-HB concentration in the short term. This abrupt metabolic shift implies dual effects on r the heart. On the one hand, increased ketone availability enables the myocardium to utilize ketone bodies as highly efficient energy substrates and bioactive signaling molecules. On the other hand, we hypothesize that this sustained elevation of β-HB may enhance SDH activity and accelerate oxidative succinate clearance via the previously described SIRT3-SDHA molecular bridge, thereby synergistically restoring the balance of the ketone body-succinate axis by simultaneously strengthening protective signaling and attenuating deleterious succinate-mediated effects. However, the metabolic consequences of a ketogenic diet are systemic and inherently complex. Over the long term, its effects may extend beyond simple regulation of the metabolic axis, involving broader alterations in systemic immunity, endocrine signaling, and gut microbiota composition. Consequently, in the metabolic management of heart failure, the concept of “precision nutrition”-namely, moderate and phased dietary modulation tailored to individual metabolic phenotypes (such as impaired ketone body availability or succinate-dominant profiles)-may represent a more feasible and safer strategy than prolonged maintenance of an extreme ketogenic state. In this context, exogenous ketone salt or ketone ester supplementation has emerged as an attractive alternative, as these approaches allow rapid and controllable elevation of circulating ketone body levels without fundamentally altering overall dietary structure, thereby conferring greater potential for clinical application.

## Pharmaceutical regulation: Pharmacological rebalancing of the metabolic axis
The druggability of the ketone body-succinate axis offers a compelling opportunity for its therapeutic exploitation in heart failure. A wide spectrum of pharmacological agents, ranging from established heart failure therapies to emerging metabolic modulators, has been shown to influence distinct nodes of this axis, with their clinical benefits being at least partially attributable to restoration of metabolic homeostasis. Elucidating the axis-specific mechanisms through which these agents exert their effects will be critical for advancing precision-based metabolic therapies in heart failure.

## Empowering the protective arm: Ketone body-oriented therapies
SGLT2 inhibitors are now widely recognized as metabolic regulators in the treatment of heart failure, with their cardioprotective effects largely attributed to distinct metabolic reprogramming actions. In addition, pimozide has been reported to improve diabetic phenotypes by increasing circulating ketone body levels through inhibition of SCOT, the rate-limiting enzyme for ketone body oxidation in skeletal muscle. Collectively, these agents can induce a moderate elevation in circulating ketone bodies, resulting in increased plasma β-HB concentration. 
Elevated β-HB not only optimizes myocardial energy supply as a highly efficient oxidative substrate but also directly attenuates myocardial inflammation and fibrosis through its signaling functions, including inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome and epigenetic regulation. An important mechanistic question therefore arises, could ketosis enhance SDH activity through the SIRT3-SDHA molecular bridge described in this review, thereby accelerating the oxidative clearance and indirectly alleviating its pathological accumulation? Although this hypothesis awaits direct experimental validation, it offers a compelling theoretical framework to explain how SGLT2 inhibitors may disrupt the vicious cycle of heart failure by modulating the entire ketone body-succinate axis rather than acting on a single molecular target. Ketone ester supplements: Precision amplifiers of the protective as exogenous ketone body precursors, ketone esters can achieve rapid and precise regulation of plasma β-HB levels. They represent the most direct intervention strategy: enhancing the signal output of the protective arm. In a lipopolysaccharide-induced sepsis model, exogenous ketone ester supplementation not only protected mice from sepsis-associated cardiac dysfunction but also attenuated inflammatory responses in the kidney and liver. Beyond providing metabolic support during acute energy stress, but also the activated ketone body signaling pathways (e.g., HDAC inhibition, NLRP3 inhibition) can directly counteract succinate-driven pathological processes. Importantly, the therapeutic value lies in proving that simply strengthening the protective arm is sufficient to produce therapeutic effects and providing an ideal tool for dose exploration.

## Inhibiting the destructive arm: Antagonizing succinate signaling
SUCNR1 antagonists: Precision snipers of the destructive as tools directly targeting succinate signaling, SUCNR1 antagonists have shown clear effects in blocking fibrosis and inhibiting pathological remodeling in preclinical models. The development of this drug class is particularly important in validating a key therapeutic principle: even without altering absolute succinate levels, selective blockade of its downstream signaling is sufficient to elicit significant cardioprotective effects. These findings establish the succinate-SUCNR1 signaling pathway, representing the destructive arm of the ketone body-succinate axis, as an independent and actionable therapeutic target in heart failure.
Metformin, a beneficial metabolic paradox as a classic insulin sensitizer, exerts a unique impact on succinate metabolism. Sun et al. found that age-induced succinate accumulation can promote cardiac fibrosis, and metformin targeting succinate accumulation can inhibit mouse fibroblast activation and apoptosis resistance, which is a potential strategy for controlling age-related cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction ( ). 

## Creating a balanced environment: Indirect regulation by traditional drugs

## β-blockers
“Throttle valves” for metabolic demand by reducing heart rate and myocardial contractility, β-blockers directly decrease the overall energy demand of the heart. This “energy-saving” effect may indirectly stabilize the ketone body-succinate axis. By reducing energy stress, it lowers the driving pressure for mitochondria to produce ROS and accumulate succinate, while creating a more favorable environment for the utilization of efficient fuels such as ketone bodies, which can be regarded as providing a stable “macroenvironment” for the restoration of the metabolic axis. Previous studies have investigated the effects of norepinephrine on human ketone body production and lipolysis under different adrenergic receptor blockade conditions. These studies demonstrated that β-receptor blockade abolishes the ketogenic and lipolytic effects of norepinephrine, suggesting that selective β1-receptor blockers may influence ketone body metabolism by modulating adrenergic signaling. In patients with heart failure, long-term β-blockers therapy has been associated with a more stable ketone body metabolic profile, which may partially contribute to their well-established cardioprotective effects.

## Future directions and challenges

## Future directions
Traditional heart failure therapies have predominantly focused on neurohormonal modulation, whereas the introduction of the ketone body-succinate axis expands therapeutic targets from simple “signal pathway blockade” to “metabolic network reconstruction.” This axis defines an antagonistic relationship between “protective metabolites (ketone bodies)” and “deleterious metabolites (succinate),” thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the development of bidirectional, precision-based therapeutic strategies. For example, patients with a “high-succinate” metabolic phenotype may preferentially benefit from SUCNR1 antagonists combined with antioxidant therapy, whereas those with impaired ketone body utilization may be supplemented with ketone esters. Such metabolic phenotype-guided, individualized interventions have the potential to overcome the limitations of the current “one-size-fits-all” treatment paradigm and improve clinical outcomes. From a drug development perspective, this axis not only offers novel mechanistic insights into the cardioprotective effects of existing agents, such as SGLT2 inhibitors and metformin, but also highlights promising directions for the development of next-generation therapies, including highly selective SUCNR1 antagonists, modulators targeting SDHA acetylation, and controllable ketone body donors. In addition, combined strategies integrating dietary interventions with pharmacological modulation provide a new framework for exploring synergistic effects between non-pharmacological and drug-based therapies, with the potential to further enrich comprehensive HF management.

## Challenges
Lack of heart-specific validation of core mechanisms. Current evidence supporting the interactions within the ketone body-succinate axis—such as SUCLA2-OXCT1 interactions and the SIRT3–SDHA regulatory pathway—largely originates from tumor cell models or systemic animal studies, with limited validation in cardiomyocyte-specific contexts. As cardiomyocytes are highly energy-demanding cells with unique metabolic characteristics, it remains unclear whether ketone body and succinate metabolism in the heart fully conforms to these established mechanisms. Key questions, including the binding efficiency of SUCLA2 and OXCT1 in cardiomyocytes and the dose-dependent effects of β-HB on SIRT3 activity, have yet to be confirmed using cardiomyocyte-specific knockout or overexpression models, thereby constraining precise functional interpretation of the axis.
Insufficient clinical evidence linking the axis to heart failure. Despite promising preclinical findings, direct clinical evidence linking the ketone body-succinate axis to human HF remains limited. On the one hand, large-scale clinical studies have not yet established clear associations between the β-HB/succinate ratio and HF severity, therapeutic responsiveness, or prognosis, and the sensitivity and specificity of this ratio as a biomarker require further validation. On the other hand, most clinical interventions targeting this axis, such as ketone ester supplementation or SUCNR1 antagonism, remain at an early exploratory stage, lacking long-term safety and efficacy data in HF populations. Moreover, differential benefits among HF subtypes (HFrEF versus HFpEF) have not been clearly defined, further limiting translational applicability.
Limited evidence regarding safety and optimization of intervention strategies. Existing intervention strategies require substantial optimization. The long-term safety of ketogenic diets—particularly their effects on glycemic control, lipid metabolism, and hepatic and renal function—has not been adequately evaluated in HF patients and may pose risks of metabolic imbalance due to interindividual variability. For exogenous ketone supplementation, optimal dosing regimens, timing, and duration of administration remain undefined, and potential adverse effects associated with excessive ketone levels, such as metabolic acidosis or suppression of SIRT3 activity, warrant careful assessment. Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying potential synergistic effects, drug-drug interactions, and long-term risks of combination strategies (e.g., ketone esters plus SUCNR1 antagonists) have not been systematically investigated in preclinical studies, limiting their clinical feasibility.
Inherent methodological limitations in current research. Most existing studies rely on conventional metabolomics, in vitro cell models, and systemic animal models, which are insufficient to precisely capture dynamic, localized metabolic changes within myocardial tissue. For example, real-time monitoring of intracellular ketone body and succinate fluctuations across different stages of HF, their subcellular localization, and the temporal sequence of their interactions remains technically challenging. In addition, a lack of single-cell-level analyses hampers understanding of axis-specific functions across distinct cardiac cell populations, including cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells, resulting in an incomplete view of the integrated regulatory network governing the ketone body-succinate axis.

## Conclusion
The core pathological basis of myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure lies in disruption of the integrated “metabolism-signaling” network, within which dynamic regulation of the ketone body-succinate metabolic axis represents a critical hub in the disease process. This review is the first to systematically propose and delineate the conceptual framework of this integrated metabolic axis. Succinate, acting as a “pathological signal amplifier” of the TCA cycle, promotes inflammation, fibrosis, and myocardial remodeling through SUCNR1 receptor, stabilization of HIF-1α, and induction of mitochondrial reverse electron transport. Correspondingly, circulating succinate levels have emerged as independent prognostic indicators in cardiovascular conditions such as aortic dissection and heart failure. In contrast, ketone bodies function as “protective metabolic modulators” under pathological conditions. They enhance myocardial energy supply by supplementing the acetyl-CoA pool, suppress the NLRP3 inflammasome, and may accelerate succinate clearance by through activation of the SIRT3-SDHA pathway, thereby forming a core defense mechanism that is functionally antagonistic to succinate. Importantly, the between ketone bodies and succinate does not rely on direct molecular binding but is mediated through the high myocardial expression of shared metabolic intermediates (succinyl-CoA), key enzymes (SUCLA2-OXCT1), and reciprocal regulation of common signaling nodes, including NLRP3, ROS, and MAPK pathways. This conceptual advance provides a novel perspective for understanding metabolic remodeling in heart failure. Although direct clinical studies validating this axis are currently lacking, evidence from individual interventions, such as ketogenic diet, ketone ester supplementation, SGLT2 inhibitors, and SUCNR1 antagonists has indirectly supported the feasibility of the combined strategy that simultaneously “enhancing the ketone body protective arm and suppresses the destructive succinate arm,” thereby laying a foundation for future translational research. Future investigations should focus on three key directions. First, cardiomyocyte-specific models should be employed to verify the SUCLA2-OXCT1 interaction and SIRT3-mediated SDHA, in order to define the heart-specific role of this metabolic axis. Second, multicenter cohort studies are needed to establish the β-HB/succinate ratio as a biomarker for patient stratification and prognosis evaluation in heart failure. Third, small-sample clinical studies should be conducted to assess the safety of the combined ketone ester and SUCNR1 antagonist therapy and to evaluate its potential for improving cardiac function. In summary, the proposal and systematic analysis of the ketone body-succinate metabolic axis transcend the limitations of traditional isolated studies of individual on metabolic molecules, introduce a new paradigm for precision metabolic therapy in heart failure, and are expected to facilitate the translation of metabolic regulation from basic research to clinical application, ultimately opening new avenues for improving outcomes in patients with cardiovascular diseases.
