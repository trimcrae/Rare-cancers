## INTRODUCTION
DNA consists of two antiparallel complementary strands coiled around each other in a right-handed helix ( ). This structure introduces a unique challenge: Accessing the genetic information encoded in the DNA requires continuous duplex melting, which, in turn, generates overwound and underwound DNA regions, commonly referred to as positive (+) and negative (−) supercoiling, respectively. The “twin-supercoiled-domain” model illustrates this phenomenon, wherein elongating RNA polymerases generate (+) supercoiling ahead and (−) supercoiling behind of the transcription bubble ( ). The importance of resolving this torsional stress is underscored by the existence of DNA topoisomerases across all life forms ( ).
In mammalian cells, transcription-derived DNA supercoiling is mainly relieved by the action of DNA topoisomerases I (TOP1) and II (TOP2A and TOP2B) through controlled cut-and-reseal mechanisms in which the enzyme covalently associates to DNA in a cleavage-complex intermediate ( ). TOP1 interacts physically with RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) but only becomes fully activated during transcription elongation, enabling local removal of DNA supercoiling directly associated with ongoing transcription ( ). Whereas TOP2A and TOP2B share similar catalytic and structural properties, they are not functionally redundant and display specific expression patterns and different yet partially overlapping functions ( , ). TOP2B is expressed throughout the cell cycle and has been mainly associated with transcription and, more recently, with genome architecture ( – ). On the other hand, TOP2A is highly expressed during mitosis and has been traditionally linked to chromosome segregation, although this general view is challenged by recent reports suggesting additional relevant functions during transcription ( , ). Consistently, both TOP2A and TOP2B are enriched at transcriptional regulatory elements such as enhancers and promoters, highlighting their broader roles in transcriptional regulation ( , ).
Chromatin contacts between regulatory elements are mediated, or at least stimulated, by cohesin, a ring-shaped complex that, by holding DNA segments together, establishes genome organization into loops ( , ). This is a dynamic process in which, from its loading site, cohesin initiates and gradually enlarges the chromatin loop (“loop extrusion”) until it becomes arrested at specific boundaries. Thus, contacts between functional elements can be regulated by cohesin loading sites and the position of barriers or hurdles that limit its extrusion. Notably, enhancers can recruit cohesin ( , ), while the presence of RNA Pol II, both at enhancers and promoters seems to counteract the loop extrusion process ( , ), thereby providing an interesting framework for transcriptional control. Emerging evidence also suggests that transcription-derived DNA supercoiling may favor chromatin interactions, not only by facilitating DNA element proximity, as shown in vitro and through molecular simulations, ( , ) but also by “pushing” the cohesin ring to promote loop extrusion ( , ). Moreover, TOP2 enzymes have been recently shown to regulate (+) supercoiling and contacts between active regions and lamina-associated domains ( ). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms governing DNA supercoiling regulation during chromatin contact formation remain incompletely understood.
The estrogen response, which functions by extensive estrogen receptor α (ERα)–mediated chromatin looping that brings enhancers and target genes together for coordinated regulation, ( – ) constitutes an example of the intricate interplay between chromatin architecture and transcriptional control. ERα activity depends on the presence of estrogen hormones, which mediate its homodimerization and subsequent binding to more than 10,000 different sites across the genome ( , ). These ERα-binding sites are predominantly located at enhancers, whose transcription is also stimulated in response to estrogen treatment and strongly correlates with that of their target genes ( – ). The rapid and robust induction of estrogen-responsive genes thus serves as an excellent model to study the potential role of topoisomerases in coordinating the dynamic interplay between transcription-derived DNA supercoiling, three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture, and transcriptional regulation.
Here, we systematically examined the roles of TOP2A and TOP2B during the ERα-dependent transcriptional response. Unexpectedly, we found that TOP2 paralogs have contrasting functions in estrogen-mediated transcriptional regulation. Our results indicate that TOP2A facilitates the estrogen response. In contrast, we found that, upon estrogen stimulation, TOP2B activity is specifically down-regulated at ERα target regions. Inhibition of TOP2B, which is accompanied by an accumulation of (−) supercoiling and increased regulatory chromatin interactions, promotes RNA Pol II recruitment to estrogen-responsive genes. This inactivation mechanism is controlled by ER, TOP2A and, most interestingly, by the TOP2-interacting SUMO-ligase zinc finger protein associated with TDP2 and TOP2 (ZATT)/ZNF451, which can directly inhibit TOP2 activity in vitro. Thus, we propose a mechanism of topological regulation by which ZATT-mediated inhibition of TOP2B allows the accumulation of (−) DNA supercoiling, favoring regulatory chromatin contacts and fully activating the estrogen-dependent transcriptional response.

## RESULTS

## TOP2B activity is locally down-regulated during the estrogen response
To investigate direct roles of TOP2A and TOP2B in estrogen-induced transcription, we first analyzed changes in RNA Pol II recruitment by chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) in MCF7 cells following a short 45-min treatment with 17β-estradiol (E2). This defined 359 E2-responsive protein-coding genes and 264 E2-responsive enhancers [log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1; P < 0.05] under our experimental conditions (fig. S1, A and B). These E2-responsive regions showed strong estrogen-induced transcriptional up-regulation in previous nascent RNA studies by global run-on sequencing (GRO-seq) ( ), supporting the use of RNA Pol II recruitment as a proxy for transcriptional activation in this context (fig. S1C). Then, we monitored E2-induced changes in the ChIP-seq distribution of TOP2A and TOP2B at these regions when compared to the same number of randomly selected nonresponsive elements. We found a strong and specific recruitment of TOP2A to estrogen-responsive genes ( ) and enhancers ( ) but not to nonresponsive regions, as quantified by E2 versus vehicle fold-change ratios ( , right panels). In contrast, we observed that TOP2B binding to estrogen-responsive elements was largely unchanged after E2 stimulation ( ), with only a slight redistribution from nonresponsive to responsive genes ( , right panel) (fig. S1D for antibody specificity). These data suggest an important redistribution of TOP2A chromatin association during the estrogen response, while TOP2B exhibits more modest changes.
 We next explored whether the catalytic activities of TOP2A and TOP2B were regulated in response to estrogen. To address this, we performed In vivo Complex of Enzyme (ICE) assays to measure the accumulation of TOP2-DNA cross-links [or TOP2 cleavage complexes (TOP2cc)] as a defining intermediate of the TOP2 catalytic cycle. Thus, the accumulation of ICE signal upon a brief incubation with the TOP2 poison etoposide serves as a proxy for global TOP2 activity. We did not find, however, notable differences in etoposide-induced accumulation of total TOP2Acc or TOP2Bcc upon E2 stimulation (fig. S1E). To investigate local changes, we developed a genome-wide adapted version of the ICE protocol that relies on paralog-specific TOP2cc IP (fig. S1F for antibody specificity), followed by tagmentation-based DNA sequencing (ICE-seq). Consistently, the genome-wide ICE-seq distribution notably overlapped with that of the corresponding ChIP-seq signal (fig. S1G). Moreover, ICE-seq was strongly dependent on etoposide treatment (fig. S1H), validating the specificity of our experimental setup.
We observed that E2 treatment specifically increased TOP2A ICE-seq signal at estrogen-responsive genes ( ). This was, however, not the case for responsive enhancers, in which, despite a minor qualitative increase in the meta-profile ( , left), the FC in E2 versus vehicle TOP2A ICE-seq was largely unchanged by estrogen treatment ( , right). In contrast, TOP2B ICE-seq signal displayed a marked reduction at estrogen-induced genes ( ), mostly concentrated at promoter regions, and also at responsive enhancers ( ), in both cases being quantitatively different when compared to nonresponsive elements ( , right). A genome browser view of the GREB1 regulatory region exemplified this unexpected reduction in TOP2B ICE-seq signal ( ), particularly at the enhancer and promoter (highlighted regions), which was further confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis ( ). The activity of TOP1, another major transcription-associated topoisomerase, markedly increased at responsive elements following E2 treatment, as measured by camptothecin-induced TOP1 ICE-seq (fig. S1I). These results suggest that, unlike TOP2A and TOP1, TOP2B catalytic activity—although not its binding—is locally down-regulated in response to estrogen.
To gain finer temporal resolution over these changes, we measured TOP2A and TOP2B activity after 20 min of E2 treatment. The activity of both topoisomerases behaved similarly at this early time point, with increased activity at genes and a decreased activity at enhancers (fig. S1J). These results indicate that the opposing behavior of TOP2 paralogs at 45 min of E2 treatment is preceded by a reduction in total TOP2 activity at enhancer regions and is therefore unlikely to result from competitive or compensatory mechanisms. This is unexpected given the high topological stress associated with acute transcriptional activation and points to the existence of a regulatory mechanism acting directly on TOP2 enzymes to preferentially inhibit TOP2B catalytic activity. Together, the findings suggest a precise, locus-dependent and time-resolved mechanism of TOP2B down-regulation, initiating at enhancers and subsequently extending into target genes.

## TOP2 paralogs differentially affect the estrogen response
Given the differential behavior of TOP2A and TOP2B upon E2 exposure, we next investigated whether this reflected distinct functions in the estrogen-dependent transcriptional response. We performed RNA Pol II ChIP-seq experiments in cells depleted for either TOP2A or TOP2B (knockdown specificity in fig. S2A) under both E2-deprived and -induced conditions. Overall, RNA pol II occupancy across transcribed genes was not strongly affected by depletion of either paralog, although we observed a modest trend toward reduced occupancy upon TOP2A depletion and increased occupancy upon TOP2B depletion (fig. S2B). When the estrogen response was analyzed, we observed that TOP2A depletion specifically reduced E2-induced RNA Pol II recruitment to estrogen-responsive genes ( ). In contrast, TOP2B depletion produced the opposite effect, substantially increasing estrogen-induced RNA Pol II recruitment ( ). These contrasting effects were, on average, particularly evident at the promoters of estrogen-responsive genes ( ) but could also be observed in the gene body ( ). Representative examples of two genes showing this differential behavior, RET and GREB1, are shown in . At responsive enhancer regions, the depletion of both paralogs caused a mild decrease in RNA Pol II recruitment, only reaching statistical significance in the case of TOP2A (fig. S2C). Only minimal changes in cell cycle distribution were observed (fig. S2D), ruling out major cell-phase–related indirect effects.
 These differential effects of TOP2 paralog depletion on estrogen-induced RNA Pol II recruitment to responsive regions were also observed under conditions of catalytic inhibition by short-term treatments with merbarone and ICRF-187 (fig. S2E), which, consistent with previous reports, ( , ) preferentially affected TOP2A and TOP2B, respectively (fig. S2F). In addition to confirming the results obtained with TOP2 paralog depletion, these findings demonstrate a direct involvement of their catalytic functions, in agreement with the observed opposing changes in activity. Reverse transcription qPCR analysis confirmed that both TOP2A depletion and merbarone treatment impaired E2-induced mRNA accumulation of the model genes GREB1, RET, and TFF1. Conversely, preferential inhibition of TOP2B by ICRF-187 treatment consistently enhanced their mRNA induction, while the effect of TOP2B depletion was more variable, causing either no reduction or a moderate increase, depending on the gene (fig. S2, G and H). We conclude that the two TOP2 paralogs play contrasting roles during the estrogen transcriptional response, with TOP2A facilitating and TOP2B restricting RNA pol II recruitment to E2-induced genes.
TOP2B has been previously proposed to trigger estrogen-mediated transcription through the stabilization of cleavage complexes and the subsequent generation of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) at responsive gene promoters ( ). However, at these early time points after estrogen treatment, we found no evidence for etoposide-independent accumulation, neither of TOP2B ICE-qPCR signal (fig. S2I), which, as a matter of fact, showed lower levels consistent with reduced TOP2B activity, nor of the well-established marker of DNA damage, γH2AX (fig. S2J), at the GREB1 locus. We therefore conclude that early estrogen-mediated transcriptional stimulation occurs without the stabilization of TOP2Bcc or the induction of DSBs. The discrepancy with previously proposed models could be explained if DSBs represent an accidental consequence rather than the cause of transcriptional up-regulation ( ), in line with additional reports indicating that estrogen-induced DSBs are dependent on DNA replication ( , ). Overall, our results suggest that TOP2B limits the estrogen response at the level of RNA Pol II recruitment, which is consistent with the observed physiological down-regulation of its activity.

## TOP2B removes estrogen-induced (−) supercoiling
To explore the molecular mechanism by which TOP2 enzymes, and TOP2B in particular, regulate estrogen signaling, we decided to measure DNA supercoiling levels in our experimental system. Psoralen intercalation and UVA-induced cross-linking (psoralen photobinding) is useful for this, as the molecule has a preference for (−) supercoiled DNA ( , ) and can be labeled [e.g., biotin-psoralen (BP)] for visualization and quantification purposes ( ). However, psoralen intercalation is also influenced by DNA sequence and chromatin structure ( ), making traditional psoralen photobinding measurements difficult to interpret. We therefore decided to try to improve the BP photobinding approach to be able to specifically discern the topological component of psoralen intercalation. In our chromatin BP (ChBP) photobinding method ( ), chromatin is prefixed, allowing for the inclusion of a critical control step: in vitro treatment with recombinant Escherichia coli Top1 (EcTop1) before intercalation and cross-linking of BP. Because bacterial Top1 specifically removes (−) supercoiling, this enables clear distinction of topologically relevant psoralen intercalation. Detection of intercalated BP with fluorescently labeled streptavidin was substantially reduced following EcTop1 treatment in the RPE-1 cell line. This was observed using both slot-blot analysis (fig. S3A) and flow cytometry–based detection [ChBP fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)] (fig. S3B). This demonstrates that the fixed chromatin retained its topological state and determines the baseline of supercoiling-independent BP incorporation. Furthermore, transcription inhibition with triptolide treatment specifically reduced the supercoiling (i.e., removed by EcTop1 treatment) component of BP intercalation (fig. S3C), indicating that ChBP represents a method for streamlined and reliable measurements of transcription-induced (−) supercoiling.
 Using ChBP FACS experiments in MCF7 cells, we observed that estrogen treatment led to a marked increase in BP intercalation, which was largely dependent on Top1 treatment ( ), suggesting a strong accumulation of (−) supercoiling during the estrogen response. To dissect whether this accumulation of (−) supercoiling was specifically associated with estrogen-responsive regions, we adapted the ChBP technique for DNA library preparation and sequencing [chromatin BP sequencing (ChBPseq)] ( ). This was achieved by leveraging psoralen-mediated protection from exonuclease digestion ( ) and subsequent multiple-displacement amplification of the protected single-stranded DNA stretches using phi29pol and random hexamers ( ). A first experiment in MCF7 cells showed how ChBPseq signal was reduced following Top1 treatment (fig. S3D). As a specific measurement of (−) supercoiling, we calculated the difference in BP intercalation signal (ΔChBPseq) between untreated samples (i.e., total BP intercalation) and EcTop1-treated samples (i.e., baseline, supercoiling-independent BP intercalation). ΔChBPseq signal, as previous measurements of (−) supercoiling ( , , ), specifically accumulated upstream of transcription start sites (TSSs) and gradually decreased along the gene body ( ). We then quantified ΔChBPseq signal during the estrogen response and observed an accumulation specifically upstream of the TSS of estrogen-responsive genes when compared to nonresponsive genes ( ). Last, we used ChBP FACS analysis to determine how TOP2A and TOP2B affected the accumulation of (−) supercoiling during the estrogen response ( ). TOP2B depletion, on its own, increased BP incorporation, which was markedly enhanced when combined with E2 treatment. Similar changes were also observed, although to a milder degree, when TOP2A was depleted ( ). These differential effects on BP incorporation were also observed by short-term treatments with the TOP2B and TOP2A preferential catalytic inhibitors ICRF-187 and merbarone (fig. S3E), supporting a direct and functional effect. Collectively, these results demonstrate that estrogen signaling induces an accumulation of (−) supercoiling that would be otherwise locally resolved by TOP2B, with a smaller contribution from TOP2A.

## TOP2B modulates ERα-mediated contacts between regulatory regions
It is well established that the estrogen response involves an ERα-mediated rewiring of long-range regulatory chromatin contacts ( – ). Because DNA supercoiling has been proposed to favor these interactions, either through the formation of superhelical structures ( , ) or by driving cohesin movement ( , ), and TOP2B binding is tightly associated with 3D genome organization ( – ), we decided to analyze whether TOP2B depletion may affect the regulatory chromatin contacts taking place in response to estrogen. We therefore performed chromatin conformation capture (3C) qPCR experiments and analyzed the well-characterized ERα-mediated chromatin contacts taking place at the GREB1 and RET loci ( , ). We found that known regulatory contacts between the enhancer and either the promoter or the gene body of both genes were increased by TOP2B depletion, irrespective of estrogen treatment ( and fig. S4A). This suggests that, under basal conditions, TOP2B prevents ERα-mediated regulatory contacts that are induced by estrogen treatment, at least in these model genes.
 To extend these observations to a genome-wide level, we decided to use, as an indicator of changes in 3D chromatin organization, the redistribution of cohesin that normally ( ), and specifically during the estrogen response, ( , ) mediates and/or stabilizes long-range contacts. We therefore performed RAD21 ChIP-seq experiments under untreated versus E2-treated conditions and focused our analysis on estrogen-induced ERα-mediated chromatin contacts of enhancer regions previously identified by ERα chromatin interaction analysis by paired-end tag sequencing (ChIA-PET) experiments ( ). These included 1526 ERα-mediated chromatin contacts that substantially overlapped with our defined set of E2-responsive regions (fig. S4B). Consistent with previous reports ( , ), we found that E2 treatment globally increased RAD21 binding at E2-mediated enhancer contacts, both globally ( ) and particularly at the GREB1 locus as an illustrative example ( ). Notably, we observed that TOP2B depletion further increased estrogen-induced cohesin binding at these regions ( ). Furthermore, TOP2B depletion, on its own, resulted in an increased cohesin occupancy at the enhancer contact point even under noninduced conditions ( ). We therefore conclude that TOP2B reduces cohesin occupancy at ERα regulatory contact regions, which is compatible with it exerting a negative regulation of chromatin contacts. In agreement with this, we found that TOP2B ICE-seq signal was reduced at ERα-mediated contact sites upon estrogen treatment ( ), indicating a local down-regulation of TOP2B activity specifically at these regions. Collectively, these findings suggest that TOP2B modulates cohesin-mediated regulatory chromatin contacts taking place during the estrogen response and are compatible with this regulation being exerted by controlling the levels of (−) DNA supercoiling. Local down-regulation of TOP2B activity would thus generate a topological environment that facilitates regulatory chromatin interactions, providing a molecular explanation for its function in estrogen-dependent transcriptional signaling.

## Down-regulation of TOP2B activity depends on ERα and TOP2A
We next sought to uncover the molecular mechanism(s) by which the activity of TOP2B was down-regulated in response to estrogen. Given that this down-regulation specifically occurred at estrogen-induced loci, we wondered whether TOP2B inactivation required ERα recruitment. As mentioned above, ERα-binding sites are predominantly located at enhancers ( ), so virtually all estrogen-induced enhancers show ERα recruitment under our experimental conditions. However, we could classify estrogen-induced protein-coding genes depending on whether their promoters displayed ERα recruitment or not. We found that TOP2B activity was specifically down-regulated at promoters bound by ERα ( ). Consistent with this, and as an illustrative example, TOP2B in vivo complex of enzyme and immunoprecipitation (ICEIP) qPCR analysis at the estrogen-responsive gene P2RY2 revealed that TOP2B activity was down-regulated only at the enhancer, which shows ERα binding, but not at the promoter, which does not ( ). In contrast, as shown above, both the enhancer and promoter regions of the GREB1 locus, which display strong ERα recruitment, exhibited down-regulation of TOP2B activity ( ). To establish cause-effect relationships, we next treated cells with fulvestrant, a competitive ERα antagonist that triggers its degradation ( ), and analyzed TOP2B activity regulation in response to estrogen. Despite incomplete ERα degradation (fig. S5A), we observed that fulvestrant pretreatment partially suppressed E2-induced TOP2B down-regulation ( ), further supporting the notion that ERα recruitment is important for TOP2B inactivation. To explore whether this regulatory mechanism would be reflected in expression patterns, we analyzed TOP2B levels in relation to ER status across different cellular and tissue contexts. ER-negative cancer cell lines expressed lower levels of TOP2B than their ER-positive counterparts (fig. S5B) ( – ). In agreement, we observed a modest positive correlation between ERα gene (ESR1) and TOP2B expression in ER-positive, but not ER-negative, breast cancers [correlation coefficient (r) = 0.25 versus –0.08] (fig. S5C). This correlation was even stronger (r = 0.53) in noncancer breast tissue (fig. S5D), further supporting a physiological link between ERα signaling and TOP2B regulation.
 As described above, TOP2A is also recruited to estrogen-responsive regions upon E2 treatment. TOP2A recruitment to promoters strongly correlated with ERα recruitment, even more than with changes in RNA Pol II density (fig. S5E), suggesting that it does not merely depend on increased transcription. We thus wondered whether TOP2A was also involved in the estrogen-induced down-regulation of TOP2B activity. We observed a global association of TOP2A recruitment with the down-regulation of TOP2B ICE-seq signal ( ) and the presence of ERα ( ) at TSSs. To test the causal relationship, we performed TOP2B ICEIP qPCR analysis at the GREB1 locus upon small interfering RNA (siRNA)–mediated knockdown of TOP2A, which, notably, completely suppressed down-regulation of TOP2B activity in response to estrogen ( ). However, TOP2A inhibition by merbarone treatment did not significantly affect the estrogen-induced decrease in TOP2B activity ( ), arguing that TOP2B inactivation depends on TOP2A but not on its catalytic activity. Furthermore, we also found that TOP2A depletion completely suppressed the increase in the enhancer-promoter chromatin contact taking place at the GREB1 locus upon E2 stimulation ( ). We conclude that recruitment of ERα to its responsive elements, together with a noncatalytic function of TOP2A, triggers a local down-regulation of TOP2B activity that facilitates regulatory chromatin contacts.
To investigate how ERα, along with TOP2A, inactivates TOP2B, we next performed coIP experiments. Pulling down endogenous ERα coimmunoprecipitated not only TOP2B, as previously reported ( ), but also TOP2A ( ). Interaction between the three factors was, as a matter of fact, confirmed in reciprocal pull-down experiments ( ). These results, together with their respective ChIP-seq profiles at estrogen-responsive elements, suggest that ERα can simultaneously interact with TOP2A and TOP2B paralogs on chromatin. Consistent with this, in situ proximity ligation assays (PLA) indicated an association between TOP2A and TOP2B, which was increased upon E2 stimulation ( and fig. S5F). This suggests direct molecular interaction as possible molecular mechanism of ERα- and TOP2A-dependent down-regulation of TOP2B activity.

## ZATT inhibits TOP2B activity to facilitate the estrogen response
We noticed that part of the fraction of TOP2B that was associated with ERα showed an upward mobility shift ( ), with the appearance of a second band suggestive of possible posttranslational modifications. We observed that this slower-migrating band was immunoreactive against anti-SUMO2/3 antibodies ( ), indicating that a substantial fraction of ERα-associated TOP2B was SUMOylated. On the basis of this, we turned our attention toward ZATT, a major TOP2B SUMO ligase ( ). We found that ZATT interacted with ERα, as proven by reciprocal endogenous coIP experiments ( ), and also with TOP2 paralogs, as previously reported in other contexts ( ). Furthermore, depletion of ZATT reduced the SUMOylation levels of ERα-bound TOP2B ( ). Together, these results indicate that ZATT associates with the ERα-TOP2A-TOP2B hub, within which it SUMOylates TOP2B.
 We then tested whether ZATT could be directly responsible for the down-regulation of TOP2B activity that takes place in response to estrogen and started by performing TOP2B ICEIP qPCR analysis at responsive elements. ZATT depletion abolished the down-regulation of TOP2B activity observed upon E2 stimulation ( ), indicating that ZATT is necessary for the negative regulation of TOP2B activity in the context of ERα signaling. Because ZATT interacts with, and SUMOylates, TOP2B, we next wondered whether it could directly inhibit TOP2B activity. To test this, we performed in vitro plasmid relaxation assays using (−) supercoiled pBR322 plasmid. We found that the addition of recombinant ZATT protein notably reduced the plasmid relaxation activity of TOP2B ( ), indicating that ZATT has the intrinsic capacity to inhibit TOP2B. To test the role of TOP2B SUMOylation, we repeated the assay in the presence of a complete SUMOylation system. However, although TOP2B was efficiently SUMOylated under these conditions (fig. S6A), the extent of its inhibition by ZATT remained unchanged (fig. S6B). We then examined whether ZATT also affects TOP2A activity. Consistent with previous reports ( ), we observed that TOP2A displayed milder (−) supercoiling relaxation activity when compared to TOP2B (fig. S6C). Recombinant ZATT also reduced the capacity of TOP2A to relax (−) supercoiled pBR322 plasmid (fig. S6C). However, when the assay was performed under conditions allowing complete plasmid relaxation, ZATT selectively inhibited TOP2B, with no detectable effect on TOP2A (fig. S6D). Collectively, these results indicate that ZATT has the intrinsic capacity to inhibit TOP2 enzymes, with a preference for TOP2B, suggesting that it may be a direct regulator of its function during the estrogen response.
To test this possibility, we next characterized ZATT binding dynamics and its impact on the estrogen-dependent transcriptional response. First, we performed ZATT ChIP-seq experiments and studied its genome-wide distribution before and after E2 treatment. Globally, ZATT was mostly located at promoters yet exhibiting certain enrichment at enhancer regions ( ). We observed that, upon E2 stimulation, ZATT was significantly recruited to estrogen-induced genes and enhancers ( , and fig. S6E) and that this was specific when compared to nonresponsive elements (see quantification of the E2 versus vehicle fold-change ratios in the right panels). This is in line with an inhibitory function of ZATT on TOP2B activity. Decreased TOP2B ICE-seq signal strongly correlated with ZATT recruitment at ERα-mediated enhancer contacts, even more than with ERα or TOP2A (fig. S6F). In contrast, increased TOP2A ICE-seq signal positively correlated ZATT (and ERα) (fig. S6F), indicating that the inhibitory effect of ZATT on TOP2A activity observed in vitro either did not occur in cells or was overridden by the topological demand caused by acute increased transcription. When classifying estrogen-induced protein-coding genes depending on whether they displayed or not an ERα-binding site at their promoter, we observed that ZATT was preferentially recruited to those promoters bound by ERα ( ) and that the level of ZATT and ERα recruitment strongly correlated (fig. S6F). This could be observed at the GREB1 locus as an illustrative example ( ), where ZATT ChIP-qPCR analysis further validated the observed recruitment (fig. S6, G and H). Overall, these results agree with the down-regulation of TOP2B being directly mediated by ZATT ( ) and particularly occurring at ERα-bound sites ( ).
Last, to address whether ZATT-mediated down-regulation of TOP2B activity facilitated the estrogen response, we performed RNA Pol II ChIP-seq experiments in ZATT-depleted cells. We observed a significant and specific reduction of E2-induced RNA Pol II recruitment to estrogen-induced genes and enhancers upon ZATT depletion ( ; see also an illustrative example in fig. S6I). Together, the results presented here are consistent with a model in which local ZATT-mediated down-regulation of TOP2B activity facilitates the estrogen-induced transcriptional response through an accumulation of (−) supercoiling and the stimulation of contacts between distal chromatin regulatory regions ( ).
 

## DISCUSSION
DNA supercoiling has been traditionally considered an undesired by-product of transcription that is resolved by DNA topoisomerases to generally favor gene expression. However, this view is changing in recent years, and topoisomerases are being assigned relevant regulatory functions ( , , ). In these cases, topoisomerases are normally believed to act as activators of transcription, while their mechanism of action remains poorly characterized. Studying TOP2 functions during the estrogen-induced transcriptional response, we uncovered a regulatory role of TOP2B that operates by removing DNA supercoiling and restraining long-range chromatin contacts.
In our model ( ), TOP2B activity at estrogen-responsive elements would remove the topological stress generated under basal transcription conditions. Upon exposure to estrogen, ERα, TOP2A, and ZATT are recruited to these regions, leading to a down-regulation of TOP2B activity that results in an accumulation of (−) DNA supercoiling that would favor regulatory chromatin interactions to stimulate the estrogen response. In this scenario, regulatory regions would have an intrinsic tendency to interact, which would be normally kept in check by continuous TOP2B-mediated removal of (−) supercoiling. The feedback loop of more (−) supercoiling, leading to more transcription that generates more (−) supercoiling and so on, would allow a rapid response upon TOP2B inhibition. (−) supercoiled DNA can provide a particular topological context in which distal chromatin contacts are favored while maintaining permissive conditions for transcription, i.e., by facilitating promoter melting and the advance of RNA Pol II and/or nucleosome remodeling ( , – ).
Our results argue that physiological down-regulation of TOP2B activity at estrogen-responsive elements depends on ERα, TOP2A, and ZATT. It is difficult to causally link TOP2B inactivation with ERα because, being the molecular trigger of the entire pathway, all downstream events are expected to be affected. We, however, believe that the correlation of its recruitment with the reduction in TOP2B activity, together with the cooccupancy and physical interactions with TOP2A, TOP2B, and ZATT, strongly suggests that ERα is indeed directly involved in TOP2B down-regulation and the topological control of the estrogen response. Regarding TOP2A, our findings suggest both, a catalytic-independent mechanism of stimulating the estrogen response by repressing TOP2B activity and a more traditional function in the removal of associated topological stress ( , , ). This would be consistent with the increased TOP2A activity observed upon estrogen stimulation and how (−) supercoiling is mildly accumulated upon its depletion. Last, our results suggest that ZATT is likely the effector that directly inhibits TOP2B activity. We show that ZATT has the in vitro ability to inhibit TOP2 relaxation activity in a SUMOylation-independent manner. We cannot, however, rule out the possibility that, in a cellular context, ZATT-mediated SUMOylation, either of TOP2B or some accessory factor, may enhance or mediate TOP2B inhibition. The specific interplay between TOP2 paralogs and ZATT in transcriptional regulation is an interesting matter for future investigation.
Our model links DNA supercoiling with 3D genome architecture, a phenomenon long-studied in bacteria ( , ) that is currently emerging in eukaryotes ( , , ). How can transcription-derived DNA supercoiling facilitate regulatory chromatin contacts? On the one hand, DNA supercoiling, in the form of plectonemic structures, may simply favor a 3D conformation of the chromatin fiber that physically reduces the distance between regulatory elements, as previously shown in vitro ( ). On the other hand, a nonmutually exclusive possibility would be that DNA supercoiling “pushes” the cohesin ring along the chromatin fiber, thereby facilitating DNA loop extrusion ( , ). This is in line with recent reports highlighting the relevant interplay between transcription and cohesin dynamics to shape genome organization ( , – ) and enhancer function in particular ( , , , ). In this sense, we observed that cohesin is accumulated at ERα-mediated contact points upon TOP2B depletion, suggesting that these supercoiling-mediated contacts are associated with the cohesin complex, either as de novo loading to stabilize interactions or as part of a supercoiling-driven loop extrusion mechanism. ERα binding, either on its own or in conjunction with RNA Pol II ( , ), could also help to shape regulatory contacts by representing barriers for the spreading of supercoiling and/or cohesin movement. The combination of increased supercoiling levels with the appearance or strengthening of boundaries would constitute an ideal scenario for the rewiring of long-range chromatin interactions.
The physiological modulation of DNA supercoiling levels would therefore represent an unexplored transcriptional regulatory layer. This is not incompatible with other functions assigned to TOP2 enzymes in transcriptional regulation, such as, for example, the reported repressive function of TOP2A in the control of promoter-proximal pausing during the immediate early response. ( ). The specific genomic and regulatory contexts of individual gene expression programs are likely to determine the precise contributions and interplay of TOP2 enzymes. Regardless of the specific molecular mechanisms involved, it is becoming increasingly clear that topoisomerases not only act as facilitators of transcription. In this sense, it would be worth investigating whether previously reported repressive transcriptional roles for TOP2B ( , ) operate through similar (−) supercoiling removal mechanisms and whether this activity contributes to the regulation of chromatin contacts. Extending the present work to the androgen response would be also particularly informative, given the substantial evidence implicating TOP2B and ZATT in that context ( , ). More broadly, it is also conceivable that other DNA-based processes, such as DNA replication, could also rely to some degree on an integrated control of DNA supercoiling and chromatin organization, highlighting the potential relevance of DNA topoisomerases as master regulators of genome dynamics. Our results may also be of clinical relevance, given the combined treatment with hormonal and TOP2 poisons in ER-positive breast and ovarian cancers.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Cell culture and treatments
MCF7 cells (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, 86012803) were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) with Earle’s salts supplemented with penicillin (50 U/ml), streptomycin (50 μg/ml), 2 mM l-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich), and nonessential amino acids. hTERT–RPE-1 cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL-4000) were culture in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium F12 supplemented with penicillin (50 U/ml), streptomycin (50 μg/ml), 2 mM l-glutamine, and 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich). Cell maintenance was conducted in HEPA class-100 incubators (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C with 5% CO2. Regular mycoplasma testing was performed using the MycoAlert PLUS Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza). Escherichia coli strains were grown in LB medium with appropriate antibiotics at 37°C.
For experiments involving estrogen treatment, cells were deprived of hormones before estrogen induction. Sixteen hours after seeding cells in culture plates, the medium was replaced with MEM without phenol-red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15218384) supplemented with 5% charcoal-stripped FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, F6765). Cells were incubated for 48 hours under these conditions before conducting the experiments. Transcription inhibition was achieved by pretreating cells with 10 μM triptolide (Sigma-Aldrich, T3652) or 0.5 μM flavopiridol (Sigma-Aldrich, F3055) for the indicated time. To inhibit topoisomerase activity, cells were preincubated with 100 μM merbarone (Sigma-Aldrich, M2070) or 100 μM ICRF-187 (Sigma-Aldrich, D1446) for the indicated time. For ICE and ICE-seq experiments, cells were treated with 100 μM VP16 for 30 min before cell lysis (Sigma-Aldrich, E1383). For ERα degradation, MCF7 cells were treated with 100 nM fulvestrant (Sigma-Aldrich, I4409) for the indicated time.
Protein knockdowns were performed by transfecting cells with siRNAs 72 hour before conducting experiments. MCF7 were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12343563) following the standard protocol for a reverse transfection.

## In situ PLA
The in situ PLA experiments were performed following the manufacturer’s protocol available from MilliporeSigma. The following Duolink in situ PLA probes and reagents were used: anti-mouse MINUS (DUO92004), anti-rabbit PLUS (DUO92002), and Duolink Detection Reagents (DUO92008).
A total of 40,000 cells was seeded in 24-well plates and, after treatment, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)–phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 min. After three PBS washes, cells were subjected to a 30-min incubation at 37°C in a humidity chamber with a blocking solution, followed by a 90-min incubation with the respective primary antibodies at 37°C. Coverslips were then washed twice with buffer A and incubated with PLA probes for 1 hour at 37°C. After a single wash with buffer A, the ligation reaction was performed according to the standard protocol for 30 min at 37°C, followed by incubation with polymerase for rolling circle amplification during 90 min at 37°C. After two consecutive 10-min washes with buffer B, cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted with VECTASHIELD mounting medium (VectorLabs). Images were captured using a Leica DM-6000B wide-field fluorescence microscope.
Foci located within cell nuclei were counted using Metamorph as follows: Nuclei were selected on the basis of DAPI staining and size (min width = 35 μm, max width = 150 μm, and background level > 300 gray levels). Foci were then counted using “granularity” function with standard algorithms (min width = 0 μm, max width = 6 μm, and background level > 200 gray levels).

## Chromatin conformation capture
3C experiments were conducted, adapting the protocol described in ( ). At least 5 × 106 cells were fixed for 10 min at room temperature using 1% formaldehyde in prewarmed nonsupplemented MEM without phenol-red. Cross-linking was quenched by incubating the samples in 150 mM glycine for 5 min at room temperature, followed by 15 min at 4°C. Next, cells were scraped into 2.5 ml of ice-cold PBS supplemented with the cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693116001) and centrifuged at ×800g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml of cold hypotonic lysis buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 10 mM NaCl, and 0.5% NP-40], supplemented with the cOmplete cocktail, and incubated for 15 min on ice. After incubation, cells were homogenized and lysed on ice using a cell douncer, with a pestle size suitable for nuclear conservation. Lysates were transferred to low-binding protein tubes and centrifuged for 5 min at ×2500g and 4°C. The resulting pellets were washed twice with the appropriate restriction enzyme ice-cold buffer and resuspended in 360 μl of ice-cold buffer. Undigested input samples were used to verify digestion efficiency. Following the addition of SDS to a final concentration of 0.1%, nuclei were incubated at 65°C for 10 min. After immediate incubation on ice, 1% Triton X-100 was added to quench the SDS. At this point, 200 U of the restriction enzyme [BtgI, New England Biolabs (NEB)] was added, and samples were incubated overnight at 37°C with interval shaking.
Digested samples were incubated for 20 min at 65°C to inactivate residual enzymatic activity. A total of 10 μl aliquots was used as digestion controls for comparison with undigested samples. The 3C protocol was optimized to achieve >85% digestion efficiency in all experiments. Ligation was performed by adding 720 μl of the ligation mix [400-U T4 DNA ligase, 1.7× T4 DNA ligase buffer, 1.7% Triton X-100, and molecular-grade bovine serum albumin (BSA; 200 μg/ml)] and incubating the samples for 4 hours at 16°C with interval shaking. To reverse the cross-linking, Proteinase K was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml, and samples were incubated for 2 hours at 65°C. A second round of Proteinase K addition was performed before an overnight incubation at 65°C. Samples were then purified by phenol-chloroform and ethanol precipitation in 300 mM AcNa and 70% EtOH. A second purification step with G-50 Sephadex beads was performed to clean the samples from salts and contaminants before ribonuclease A (RNase A; Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0531) treatment (30 min at 37°C; 40 μg/ml). Double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) was quantified using Qubit, and 50 ng of DNA was loaded per qPCR plate well. Internal primers amplifying undigested regions were used for loading adjustments.
For normalization purposes, a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone (RP11-884L15 for GREB1 and RP11-123K13 for RET) containing regions of interest was digested and ligated following the same procedure. This allowed normalization of the data to the intrinsic frequency of ligation for each pair of regions. BACs were purified from 500 ml of overnight cultures. A total of 20 μg of DNA was digested, ligated, and ligation frequencies determined as previously described for test samples. Interaction frequencies were expressed as the ratio between the ligation products obtained in experiments and the ligation products obtained from the BAC.

## ChBP
For BP detection by flow cytometry, 1 million MCF7 cells were collected in 15-ml conical tubes. Nuclei were extracted in 1 ml of extraction buffer [0.1% NP-40, 5 M NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4)] for 5 min on ice. The samples were fixed by adding PFA-PBS to a final concentration of 1% for 20 min on ice and then washed twice with 1× PBS–1% BSA at ×1.6g at 4°C for 5 min. Fixed nuclei were incubated in 300 μl of 1× rCutSmart Buffer in PBS–0.1% NP-40 at 37°C, 300 rpm, for 30 min, with 25 U of Escherichia coli Topoisomerase I (NEB, M0301) added to control samples. After incubation, samples were washed twice in PBS containing 1% BSA.
For BP incorporation, samples were incubated for 5 min in 1 ml of PBS containing 10 μM BP (EZ-Link Psoralen-PEG3-Biotin, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 29986) and 0.01% digitonin in four-well plates and then irradiated with UV (360 nm) for 20 min for BP-DNA cross-linking. Samples were washed twice in PBS–1% BSA and incubated in 100 μl of staining buffer [0.1% NP-40, 0.5 mM EDTA (pH 8), 4% BSA, 6.5 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, 2.7 mM KCl, and 137 mM NaCl] with Streptavidin Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, S21374) diluted 1:50 for 30 min on ice. Excess antibody was removed by centrifuging twice in PBS–0.1% NP-40. Samples were resuspended in 250 μl of FACS buffer (0.1% BSA, 3 mM EDTA, and 1× PBS) and analyzed using a FACSCanto II, exciting with a red laser (633 nm), and collecting the fluorescence with a 660/20 filter.

## Immunoprecipitation
For coIP experiments, cells were seeded in 15-cm plates at 80% confluence. Following indicated treatments, plates were washed with cold PBS, and cells were scraped and centrifuged (×300g for 5 min at 4°C). The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 600 μl of ice-cold lysis buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.5% Triton-X 100, 2 mM MgCl2, 2.5-kU benzonase, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and PMSF] and incubated on ice for 30 min. Simultaneously, 4 μg of IP antibody were incubated with Dynabeads (12.5 μl of Protein A and 12.5 μl of Protein G) at room temperature for 40 min in IP buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)]. After lysis, cell extracts were centrifuged (13000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C), and 0.5 mg of total protein in lysis buffer was diluted in IP buffer (to reduce Triton-X 100 concentration below 0.2%) before incubation in rotation with antibody-bound Dynabeads (2 hours at 4°C). Beads were washed three times with IP buffer and boiled for 10 min in 2× Laemmli buffer before conducting Western blot analysis.

## Western blotting
Protein extracts were prepared by boiling cells in lysis buffer [125 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 20% (w/v) glycerol, and 4% SDS] for 10 min. Lysates were then mixed with Laemmli buffer and subjected to an additional boiling step. Approximately 15 to 20 μg of protein were loaded onto either 10% in-house acrylamide gels or 4 to 20% Mini-PROTEAN Tris-Glycine Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad). Samples were then transferred to an Immobilon-FL Transfer PVDF Membrane (Millipore) for 2 hours at 4°C and 70 V.
To block nonspecific binding, membranes were incubated in 5% BSA– tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. This was followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C in 5% BSA-TBS with 0.1% Tween 20. Afterward, membranes were washed three times in TBS with 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated with the corresponding IRDye-conjugated secondary antibody in 5% BSA-TBS with 0.1% Tween 20. After three washes with buffer, membranes were dried and subjected to analysis using the Odyssey CLx system and ImageStudio Odyssey CLx software (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE), following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## TOP2 plasmid relaxation assay
Relaxation experiments were performed using the Inspiralis kit (HTRB201). Briefly, 6 nM of negatively supercoiled pBR322 plasmid was incubated with recombinant human TOP2A or TOP2B enzymes at 37°C in a 30-μl reaction [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 125 mM NaCl, BSA (100 mg/ml), and 5 mM DTT] in the absence or presence of human recombinant ZATT. Incubation times and recombinant protein concentrations are indicated in figure legends. Reactions were terminated by the addition of 30 μl of STOP solution [40% (w/v) sucrose, 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 1 mM EDTA, and Bromophenol Blue (0.5 mg/ml)], purified, and cleaned by adding an equal volume of chloroform/isoamyl alcohol [24:1 (v:v)]. The aqueous solution was resolved in a 1× tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE) and 1% (w/v) agarose gel for 2 hours at 85 V. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide (1 μg/ml in 1× TAE) and visualized using a transilluminator.

## In vitro SUMOylation assay
In vitro TOP2B SUMOylation assay reactions (20 μl) were conducted in a buffer containing 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 50 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, 1 mM DTT, 200 μM adenosine 5′-triphosphate, and 4 mM MgCl2. The reaction mixture consisted of 75 nM TOP2B (Inspiralis, HTB201), 3 μM SUMO2 (SignalChem, S294-31H-200), 1 μM UBC9, 100 nM SUMO E1 mix (SAE1/UBA2, Enzo, BML-UW9330-0025), and 10 nM ZATT. Reactions were incubated at 30°C for 90 min and assessed by Western blot using anti-TOP2B antibodies. A total of 3 μl of the SUMOylation reaction mixture was added to the plasmid relaxation assay.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation
To prepare chromatin, 10-cm culture plates at 80% confluency were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, 252549) in cell medium for 10 min at 37°C. The cross-linking reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine (Sigma-Aldrich, 357002). Subsequently, culture plates were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and cells were collected in ice-cold PBS supplemented with 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, 04693116001) and 1 mM PMSF using cell scrapers. Cells were then centrifuged (×300g for 5 min at 4°C) and lysed in two steps. In the first step, cells were incubated for 10 min in 1 ml of Lysis Buffer A [5 mM Pipes (pH 8), 85 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail] for nuclei isolation. Next, Lysis Buffer B [50 mM tris HCl (pH 8.1), 1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail] was added to the pelleted nuclei (4000 rpm for 5 min) for nuclear lysis. Sonication was performed using Bioruptor (Diagenode, UCD-200) at high intensity, with 25 cycles (3-s sonication on/off). Chromatin was then clarified by centrifugation (×17,000g for 10 min at 4°C).
For assessing chromatin fragmentation, 5% of the chromatin volume was incubated in Lysis Buffer B with Proteinase K (0.25 mg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10181030) for 16 hours at 65°C to reverse the cross-linking. The DNA was subsequently purified using phenol:chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation (70%) and quantified by NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fragment size distributions were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to obtain a smear ranging between 300 and 600 base pairs (bp).
For IP, 30 μg of chromatin and the corresponding amount of antibody were incubated overnight in IP buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and 1 mM PMSF] at 4°C. Samples, in lysis buffer B, were diluted at least five times in IP buffer to reduce SDS concentration below 0.2% for antibody incubation. Subsequently, 25 μl of BSA preblocked Protein A and Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added and incubated with chromatin (4 hours at 4°C). Beads were then sequentially washed with rotation using IP buffer, IP buffer with increased salt (500 mM NaCl), LiCl buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% NaDOC, and 1 mM EDTA], and Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer. To elute the DNA, beads were incubated at 50°C in 100 μl of elution buffer (1% SDS and 100 mM NaHCO3). Cross-linking was reversed by incubating samples with 200 mM NaCl and 100 μg of Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (65°C, overnight). The DNA was purified using QIAGEN PCR purification columns (QIAGEN, 28106) after treatment with RNase A (0.5 mg/ml). Last, samples were subjected to qPCR using the indicated primers.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing
ChIP-seq protocol followed the previously described ChIP method with small variations. For spike-in normalization, mouse chromatin from mouse embryonic fibroblasts was added to the samples before IP at a 1:20 ratio. Upon LiCl washing of chromatin-bound Dynabeads, samples were subjected to the ChIPmentation protocol as described in ( ). Briefly, beads were washed twice with 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8). Then, samples were transferred to new tubes for incubation with Tn5 enzyme in buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 50% (v/v) N,N′-dimethylformamide, and 25 mM MgCl2] at 37°C for 10 min to incorporate adaptors into chromatin fragments. The Tn5 enzyme used was provided by the Proteomic Service of Centro Andaluz de Biología del Desarrollo. Following tagmentation, samples were immediately placed on ice and washed again with LiCl buffer and TE buffer before transferring to new tubes.
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) libraries were prepared after quantifying and assessing the quality of the DNA fragments by qPCR. Illumina-indexed primers, which partially align with the Tn5-introduced adapters, were used in qPCR to validate Tn5 reactions and quantify DNA amount. Optimal Cqs (quantification cycle) were determined for the actual library preparation based on the qPCR results, and the libraries were then amplified for N cycles, where N corresponds to the starting point of the exponential curve amplified in the qPCR. qPCR reactions were performed using an in-house SYBR Green master mix under the same conditions as PCR used for final library preparation. Libraries were generated using NEBNext High-Fidelity Polymerase (NEB, M0541). Subsequently, size selection of DNA fragments ranging from 300 to 500 bp was performed using Sera-Mag Select Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 09-981-123). A 0.7× volume of beads mixture was used to remove fragments larger than 500 bp, followed by the addition of an additional 0.15× volume to isolate fragments larger than 200 bp. Primers were added at a final concentration of 0.2 μM, and PCR amplification was performed according to the following protocol: 1× (72°C for 5 min), 1× (98°C for 30 s), N× (98°C for 10 s–63°C for 30 s–72°C for 30 s), and 1× (72°C for 5 min). NGS libraries were sequenced at the Genomic Unit of CABIMER (Seville, Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas) using a NextSeq 500 High-Output flowcell in a single-end configuration with 75-bp read length. Before sequencing, libraries were quantified using Qubit and analyzed for size profiles using Bioanalyzer.

## ICE assay
Cells, treated and untreated with etoposide (VP16, Sigma-Aldrich, E1383) or camptothecin (Sigma-Aldrich C9911), were lysed immediately in a denaturing cell lysis solution consisting of 1% (v/v) N-Lauroylsarcosine sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich, L7414) and 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. Cell plates were scraped, and lysates were collected into polypropylene tubes. Samples were homogenized using a latex-free syringe with a 25-gauge needle, and genomic DNA precipitation was performed through a CsCl density gradient.
For DNA precipitation, CsCl (AppliChem-Panreac, A1098) was added to lysates to achieve a final concentration of 0.67 g/ml. Lysates were ultracentrifuged (57000 rpm for 20 hours at 25°C) using 3.3-ml polyallomer OptiSeal tubes (Beckman Coulter) in a TLN100 rotor (Beckman Coulter). After centrifugation, DNA pellets were washed with 1 ml of 70% EtOH and resuspended in molecular-grade H2O containing 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. Samples were incubated at 4°C for 30 min to ensure complete rehydration of DNA, followed by a 5-min heating step at 65°C in a water bath. DNA quantification was performed using a NanoDrop 1000. To remove nucleic acids, samples were incubated with 0.2 μl of benzonase (Millipore, 70746) for 30 min on ice. Then, DNA and RNA-free samples were diluted in TE buffer before transferring them to a nitrocellulose membrane (Odyssey Nitrocellulose Membrane, LI-COR Biosciences) using the Bio-Dot SF microfiltration apparatus (Bio-Rad). Immunodetection was performed following the same protocol as described for Western blot experiments.

## ICEIP and ICE-seq
To map topoisomerase cleavage complexes, material isolated by ICE assay was subjected to IP. In this process, 40 μg of ICE-isolated DNA was digested using dsDNA Fragmentase (NEB, M0348). Samples were incubated with 10 μl of the enzyme in 1× dsDNA Fragmentase buffer and 10 mM MgCl2 according to the standard protocol (30 min at 37°C). Enzymatic reactions were stopped by adding 50 mM EDTA. The size of the resulting DNA fragments was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis to obtain a DNA smear ranging from 200 to 500 bp. Subsequently, samples were diluted in IP buffer, and the IP was performed as in ChIP protocol with minor modifications. Incubations with 2 μg of antitopoisomerase antibodies were carried out in the presence of molecular-grade BSA (0.5 mg/ml). DNA was eluted from beads in 100 μl of elution buffer (30°C for 30 min) and treated with 100 μg of Proteinase K (37°C for 2 hours). Samples were then purified using Sera-Mag SpeedBeads magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 1:1 ratio following standard procedures before qPCR analysis.
The ICE-seq protocol included additional variations to described ICEIP. First, isolated topoisomerase cleavage complexes bound to antibody-coated dynabeads were subjected to the ChIPmentation protocol, as described for ChIP-seq. Second, immunoprecipitated and purified DNA was subjected to a second step of Tn5 tagmentation before library preparation.

## ChBPseq
To map BP-binding sites, cells grown in 10-cm culture plates at 80% confluency were subjected to preextraction in CSK buffer [0.5% Triton-X 100, 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, and 300 mM sucrose] for 1 min on ice. Samples were then washed in cold PBS and fixed in 1% PFA-PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed plates were incubated in 2 ml of 1× rCutSmart Buffer at 37°C, 20 rpm, for 30 min, with 30 U of Escherichia coli Topoisomerase I (NEB, M0301) added to control samples. After incubation, samples were washed in PBS and incubated with 10 μM BP in PBS–0.01% digitonin (5 ml). Plates were then irradiated with UV (360 nm) for 20 min to achieve BP-DNA cross-linking. Cells were lysed in 1 ml PK buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS] with 100 μg of Proteinase K overnight at 65°C.
Upon purification using phenol:chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation (70%), 10 μg of DNA were digested with micrococcal nuclease (NEB, M0247) to achieve a smear between 10 and 1 kb. Samples were then purified with Sera-Mag SpeedBeads magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 1:1 ratio according to standard procedures. Post–DNA size determination, the DNA was incubated with 50 U of Lambda Exonuclease (NEB, M0262) in enzyme buffer at 37°C, 300 rpm, overnight, followed by enzyme inactivation at 80°C for 20 min. Samples were place on ice upon enzyme inactivation.
To prepare NGS libraries, samples were amplified using EquiPhi29 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A39390) and exo-resistant random primers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SO181). Optimal amplification time was determined via qPCR, setting the time at the initial point of exponential amplification. qPCR reactions used an in-house SYBR Green master mix under the same conditions as the final amplification. Amplifications by EquiPhi29 were performed following the standard protocol using 1 ng of DNA as starting material and eliminating DNA denaturation step. Enzyme was inactivated at 65°C for 10 min.
Upon amplification, purified samples underwent tagmentation with Tn5 as described in the ChIP-seq protocol (see Materials and Methods) and were purified again with Sera-Mag SpeedBeads magnetic beads in a 1:1 ratio following standard procedures. Final libraries were prepared as previously described for ChIP-seq.

## mRNA quantification
To prepare RNA extracts, cells seeded in six-well plates were collected by scraping in RLT buffer from the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74106) supplemented with 1% 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M6250). RNA extraction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To eliminate DNA contamination, 500 ng of RNA were treated with RQ1 RNase-Free DNase (Promega, M6101). The resulting RNA was retrotranscribed using random hexamer primers and Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EP0752) following the standard protocol for GC-rich RNA templates. qPCR was performed using the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System with SYBR Green. The qPCR program was as follows: 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 1 s and 60°C for 20 s, ending with a melt curve step of 15 s at 95°C, 1 min at 65°C, and 15 s at 95°C.

## High-throughput sequencing analysis
Sequenced reads obtained from NGS were demultiplexed and quality filtered using FastQC. For ChIP-seq and ChBPseq experiments, Bowtie 1.2 ( ) was used for read alignment against either human (hg19) or mouse (mm9) genome. The option “-m 1” was used to retain only uniquely mapped reads. For ICE-seq, “Rsubread” package was used to align ICE-seq reads (type = 1, TH1 = 2), as it resulted in higher mapping rate.
Visualization: In all cases, signal tracks were generated using bamCoverage (deepTools) with a bin size of 50 bp, a read extension of 300 bp, and reads per genomic content (RPGC) normalization. For replicates, mean signal was computed. Signal tracks were visualized using the UCSC Genome Browser, with mean values and a smoothing window between 6 and 12 pixels. Profiles and heatmaps were generated using SeqPlots, where profiles represent the mean signal in 100-bp bins. ΔChBPseq profiles were generated by subtracting EcTop1-treated from the nontreated samples.
ChIP-seq experiments incorporated additional scaling with mouse chromatin spike-in. Following the calibrated ChIP-seq methodology developed by Hu et al. ( ), RPGC-normalized tracks were subsequently scaled by the occupancy ratio (OR), which was calculated using the bin approach described by Minchell et al. ( ). Briefly, from raw signal tracks, enrichment (IP/input) was calculated and averaged for all the positive (i.e., counts in both IP and input sample) 50-bp bins. OR was then obtained by dividing average enrichment in human and mouse readsOR=average (Eh)/average (Em)where Eh = enrichment at each bin of the human experimental genome; Em = enrichment in each bin of the reference mouse genome; beingE=CIP/CINwhere CIP = counts in the IP sample at each genome bin; CIN = counts in the input sample at each genome bin.
Quantification: In ChIP-seq experiments, BedCoverage (Samtools v1.6.) was used for quantification at regions of interest using BAM files. Counts per million (CPM) values were scaled by OR to allow intersample comparisons.
For nonspiked datasets (ICE-seq and ChBPseq), quantification was performed by differential binding analysis through the integration of R libraries, including “csaw,” “edgeR,” “rtracklayer,” and “statmod.” BAM files were used as input with the specific parameters: window width = 150, spacing = 50, and minq = 40. “normFactors,” “asDGEList,” “estimateDisp,” and “glmQLFit” functions were used for normalization and differential binding analysis using CPM values. For single replicate experiments (TOP2B ICE-seq at 20 min and ChBPseq samples), dispersion was manually set to 0.05 and model fitting using “glmFit” algorithm instead of glmQLFit. ΔChBPseq quantifications were generated by subtracting EcTop1-treated from the nontreated samples. If normality in the distribution at region types (e.g., promoters, enhancers, genes, …) was observed, or could be assumed, then iterative Grubbs’ method (α = 0.2) was used to remove individual outlier values.
For correlation analysis, the corresponding regions were systematically sorted and grouped into nonoverlapping sets containing of 5 to 10 regions. This strategy aimed to mitigate the potential impact of outliers on the analysis. Subsequently, values were correlated using the Spearman method in R.
MACS2 ( ) was used for calling broad peaks from ChIP-seq experiments. For annotation, called peaks were extended 1.5 kb bidirectionally. Regulatory regions, namely, enhancers, promoters, and insulators, were defined as follows. For promoters, the whole set of transcripts associated with Ensembl-annotated genes were considered. Then, promoters were defined as ±1 kb from the TSSs. Enhancers were defined as histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) peaks not overlapping with a promoter and insulators as CTCF peaks not overlapping with promoters and enhancers.
Annotated E2-responsive enhancers from GRO-seq data (GSE43836) were used. The enhancer coordinates annotated in the hg18 genome assembly in this study were converted to the hg19 assembly using the UCSC LiftOver tool. Protein-coding genes were obtained from annotated transcripts in the Ensembl database (hg19). Induced and noninduced enhancers were classified on the basis of RNA Pol II ChIP-seq data from four independent experiments (10 nM E2 for 45 min). This classification was performed using bedCoverage analysis with Samtools, as described above, and R. Induced genes and enhancers were defined as those exhibiting a log2FC greater than 1 and a P value less than 0.05 (based on a t test with the greater alternative hypothesis). E2 nonresponsive genes and enhancers were randomly selected to match the same number as induced transcripts, considering transcripts with log2FC values between −0.1 and 0.1, P values greater than 0.05, and RNA Pol II values greater than 1 × 10–3. To identify ER-mediated contacts colocalizing with enhancers, the ChIA-PET dataset was intersected with ER peaks annotated at enhancers (peaks overlapped with H3K27ac peaks, but not Ensembl-annotated genes). Quality control parameters for high-throughput sequencing datasets according to ENCODE guidelines are included in table S1.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Non–high-throughput sequencing data presented in this study originate from independent biological replicates; individual dots in each graph representing each replicate. Detailed information regarding data collection, quantification, and statistical methods are provided within the corresponding sections or figure legends. Statistical analyses [unpaired t tests and one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)] were performed using GraphPad Prism v9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and R v3.2.0.
