## Abstract
Since the early 2000s, omics approaches to study skeletal muscle hypertrophy consequent to loading (e.g., resistance exercise) have expanded dramatically. Beginning with genomics and transcriptomics, there are now omics datasets from hypertrophying skeletal muscle spanning methylomics, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics, with further integration of single cell/nucleus-specific omics, among others. The purpose of this review is to explore the history of leveraging omics to enable understanding and discovery with respect to loading-induced hypertrophy in adult skeletal muscle. We elaborate on key historical and contemporary studies and findings, highlight specific examples where omics discoveries led to a mechanistic understanding of skeletal muscle growth, and provide background on established and emerging omic technologies. We focus on findings from human skeletal muscle tissue but also provide context and support from the rodent literature, including insights from gain- and loss-of-function experiments. Moving forward, the computational integration of omics datasets will provide unprecedented information and exciting new directions for studying how resistance exercise mediates skeletal muscle health. This information will help inform how to target key factors influencing muscle mass with a deep, comprehensive, and integrated multilayered understanding of their molecular regulation.
Keywords: bioinformatics, phosphoproteomics, proteomics, RNA-sequencing, single-cell RNA-sequencing

## INTRODUCTION
In 1962, Jonas Bergström revolutionized the study of human skeletal muscle by reintroducing a modified needle biopsy technique, first described by Duchenne in the mid-nineteenth century. This “Bergström” biopsy technique allowed for serial sampling of muscle tissue from humans ( , ). The reintroduction of this technique paved the way for biochemical analyses (e.g., histochemistry, enzyme activity, substrate utilization, etc.) of skeletal muscle from humans in response to acute and chronic exercise training ( - ). The expanded use of biochemical techniques in the late 1960s and early 1970s was the forerunner for more advanced methods and technologies capable of capturing the molecular underpinnings of exercise’s effect on skeletal muscle. Foundational findings from this era, along with advancements in both complexity and efficiency of molecular biology techniques, ushered in the modern era of omics-focused adult skeletal muscle loading-induced hypertrophy research.
Major breakthroughs in molecular biology occurred during the 1970s and 1980s. These advancements include Paul Berg’s research on recombinant DNA technology ( ), Gilbert and Sanger’s methods for sequencing DNA ( , ), and Kary Mullis developing polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to amplify single DNA fragments ( - ). All of these discoveries ultimately earned Nobel Prizes in chemistry or physiology and medicine. The early adoption and use of such tools in exercise research was spearheaded, most notably, by the celebrated exercise physiologist Dr. Frank Booth ( - ). In 1974 Jaenisch and Mintz ( ) developed the first transgenic mouse model, which ultimately led to the creation of “knockout” mice—another Nobel Prize-winning advancement ( ). The adoption of gene knockouts in a mammalian system revolutionized the field of molecular biology by allowing for mechanistic loss-of-function studies in vivo. These advances, when applied to skeletal muscle, provide the tools to understand the genes and proteins that are necessary and/or sufficient to control muscle mass regulation and metabolism at the cellular and molecular levels. The coalescence of exercise research and molecular biology has inevitably led to a new age of “omics” investigations. Studies in this new omics era aim to map the intricate and coordinated biological pathways that are responsible for tissue-specific responses and adaptations to exercise ( - ). In particular, omics capabilities to identify key pathways during acute and chronic exercise promises to unravel the integrative cellular and molecular mechanisms regulating skeletal muscle mass.
Resistance exercise (RE) is a potent loading stimulus on skeletal muscle that increases muscle mass and function. Strong and powerful skeletal muscle leads to lowered risk of morbidities, diseases, and all-cause mortality ( - ). The outward increase in muscle mass subsequent to RE training (RET) is perhaps one of the most readily recognizable adaptations to exercise. Aside from becoming visibly larger and accordingly stronger, enhanced skeletal muscle mass and function are vital to other aspects of physiology such as immunity and infection ( - ), inflammation ( , ), mobility and independence ( - ), adiposity ( - ), and athletic performance ( - ). The benefits of RE have been described since the age of antiquity ( - ). Up to the mid-20th century, the benefits of RE were primarily derived from anecdotal and observational evidence from practitioners that included physicians, educators, coaches, and hobbyists ( ). However, in 1945 an Army physician named Thomas L. DeLorme III conducted the first in a series of controlled RE studies to observe the rehabilitation effects of RET on quadriceps muscle size and function in servicemen after thigh and knee injuries during World War II ( , ). Through his findings and experimentation, he concluded that personal standardization of RE intensity [i.e., percentage of individual one-repetition maximum (1RM)] and increases in volume throughout training led to increases in skeletal muscle mass and function ( - ). This gave rise to the term “progressive resistance exercise” training (a term originated by DeLorme’s wife, Eleanor) and helped lay the groundwork for future RE research ( ). Expanding on DeLorme’s findings, seminal papers from Goldspink ( ) and Goldberg ( , ) were among the first to document the biological correlates thought to control loading-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy in preclinical animal models. These studies laid a firm foundation for the molecular-oriented RE research that is implemented today. Contemporary research now focuses on examining the multiomic responses and adaptations to RE in skeletal muscle tissue.

## THE FOUNDATIONS OF OMICS IN STUDYING SKELETAL MUSCLE ADAPTATION TO RESISTANCE EXERCISE
The first omics techniques to become prevalent in the field of exercise research were genomics and transcriptomics. Sequencing of the human and mouse genomes was not completed until 2001 ( , ), but pioneers in exercise genetics like Claude Bouchard had already begun the process of determining the heritability of exercise adaptations in the 1990s. In the seminal HERITAGE study ( ), his group estimated that heritability accounted for 47% of the changes in maximal aerobic capacity with endurance-type exercise training ( ). This DNA-centric approach in humans led to studies in the early 2000s where gene polymorphisms were linked to RE-oriented outcomes such as muscle mass and strength ( - ). Information on specific genetic polymorphisms in human genes (e.g., myostatin, angiotensin-converting enzyme, interleukin receptor 15 alpha, steroid sulfatase) were then used to make predictions about RE trainability ( - ). One example of a deliberate effort to identify genes associated with acute RE and chronic RET (10 wk) in young, untrained humans was the Molecular Epidemiology of Resistance Exercise Training study, or MERET ( , ). Subjects in the MERET study, who had a G allele of a steroid sulfatase polymorphism, had associations with increased plasma levels of steroid precursors for testosterone and estrogen ( ). MERET also identified genetic variations in the 3’ untranslated region of the interleukin 15 receptor subunit alpha as being strongly associated with skeletal muscle hypertrophy after RET ( ). HERITAGE and MERET would lay the groundwork for modern large-scale exercise studies, such as MoTrPAC—the Molecular Transducers of Physical Activity Consortium—supported by the common fund of the National Institutes of Health ( ). This multi-million-dollar multicenter effort is evaluating the effects of exercise upstream (e.g., epigenetics) and downstream (e.g., mRNA and protein) of the genome using integration of large, diverse human cohorts with state-of-the-art omics platforms. To date, MoTrPAC has completed its preclinical investigation that examined time course, sex, and aging effects of endurance exercise in rats through a multiomic lens ( - ). The clinical investigation of MoTrPAC is ongoing but will examine similar time course, sex, aging, and training status effects of exercise in ~2,600 participants ranging from adolescents (10–17 yr) to aging adults (>60 yr) ( ). This effort may represent a leap forward in understanding what mediates exercise adaptation; importantly, MoTrPAC includes acute RE and chronic RET for the adult cohorts.
At the turn of the 21st century, Dr. Charlotte Peterson’s group was the first to utilize a microarray platform in human skeletal muscle samples after a bout of RE to understand the transcriptomic response to exercise. These early arrays provided limited coverage of the genome, only measuring the transcript levels of ~500 genes, but was nevertheless a large advance from performing assays for individual genes via targeted methods like quantitative PCR. This study highlighted, for the first time, a blunted molecular response to acute REin elderly individuals as compared with their younger counterparts. Molecular markers of stress, damage, and transcription factors in muscle differed between old and young after a bout of RE ( ). This study predated the publication of the human and mouse genomes ( , ) that mapped the ~20,000 genes that we can now measure the transcripts for simultaneously using current technology. Since Dr. Peterson’s foundational work, other “omes” have become prevalent in muscle research related to RE. Epigenomics is a way to assess the regulation of the genome by chemical modifications at specific CpG sites as well as histone proteins. Proteomics is a technique allowing for the global study of quantity, interactions, function, composition, and structure of proteins and activities at the cellular level. Phosphoproteomics is one technique used to define posttranslational reversible modifications to proteins at their phosphorylation sites that affect their structure and ultimately function. Other “omes” of relevance to RE research include the metabolome and secretome. These omes allow for analysis of the molecules produced by a tissue that subsequently serve various biological functions as they relate to metabolism in skeletal muscle, as well as systemically. Omic techniques have deepened our understanding of the molecular responses and adaptations to mechanical loading and resistance exercise leading to the identification of novel molecular regulators of skeletal muscle hypertrophy.
In this review, we will provide a historical perspective of how omics has extended our insight into hypertrophic adaptations of skeletal muscle to mechanical loading, as highlighted in the timeline overview in . We will focus primarily on work performed in human skeletal muscle but will highlight key findings from animal models of muscle hypertrophy as supporting evidence.
 

## Genomics and Skeletal Muscle Mass
Genomics is the study of an organism’s DNA, termed the “genome,” to identify and characterize all the genes within the system and ultimately how they interact. Genomics holds promise for identifying DNA variants altering muscle hypertrophic phenotypes. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) identifies variants within the genome associated with several phenotypic outcomes. The cost to sequence the first human and mouse genomes by the Human Genome Project was approximately $3 billion at the time of completion in 2001. In 2024, sequencing an entire genome can cost as little as $200. The advancements in genome sequencing technologies, along with the decreased cost, have enabled the integration of GWAS with exercise studies.
A salient example of genomics applied to human muscle research is from Cordero and colleagues ( ), who undertook a massive GWAS of 85,000+ middle-aged men and women from the UK Biobank (38–49 yr old) . This undertaking identified 182 chromosome loci and 38 gene candidates that were associated with appendicular lean mass (ALM) ( ). Information on what regulates lean mass across individuals is a first step toward understanding the genetic underpinnings of hypertrophic adaptability to RET. Interestingly, it was determined that the heritability of ALM, as well as leg and arm lean mass, was the same between women and men. A similar GWAS examined grip strength in over 195,000 individuals and identified 16 genome regions associated with muscle strength ( ). A meta-analysis of 53 human GWASs determined five gene loci (HSD17B11, VCAN, ADAMTSL, IRS1, and FTO) that were associated with lean body mass (independent of biological sex). Of those genes, only FTO had previously been identified using candidate gene approaches ( ). Another GWAS meta-analysis of 45 studies identified 47 candidate genes for muscle growth from mouse studies of hypertrophy ( ). Although these specific candidates did not always change gene expression levels or phosphorylation state of their protein product in the minutes and hours after acute RE in human studies, some (e.g., AKT1, IGF1, and MSTN) were altered with large effect sizes during 1–14 days of plantaris overload via synergist ablation in mice ( ). Mutations causing loss-of-function of myostatin (MSTN) in humans result in hypermuscularity, making it an attractive target for muscle wasting conditions, as well as athletic performance enhancement ( ). Indeed, repressing MSTN levels in postdevelopmental skeletal muscle can increase muscle mass ( - ). As another example, the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism is linked to skeletal muscle power and strength ( , ), and its abundance has been experimentally corroborated with respect to muscle performance, mass, metabolism, fiber type, contractile speed, and trainability ( - ).
GWAS approaches have been extended into exercise research. A recent study used GWAS techniques to determine interindividual differences in muscle growth of the upper leg after 12 wk of chronic RET. This study identified 11 single nucleotide polymorphisms that explained 47% of the variance of muscle growth responses ( ). Most genes are diploid, but ribosomal DNA (rDNA) copy number can vary widely across individuals ( ). Since ribosome biogenesis capacity relates to muscle hypertrophic potential via control of translational capacity ( - ), it was hypothesized that ribosome gene dosage influences the ribosome biogenesis response to acute RE in human skeletal muscle ( ). Indeed, ribosome biogenesis after acute RE in humans correlated with rDNA copy number ( ). Although rDNA copy number relates to body mass in humans ( ), it might not associate with long-term hypertrophic adaptations with chronic RET ( ). More work is needed to corroborate the relationship between rDNA copy number and muscle plasticity to exercise in a larger cohort. GWAS represents a first but important step toward understanding the role of candidate genes during contraction-induced hypertrophy. Nevertheless, these analyses provide a foundation for in-depth study of potentially important genes regulating skeletal muscle hypertrophy.

## Epigenomics and Loading-Induced Muscle Hypertrophy
Epigenomics assesses the regulation of the genome by chemical modifications (i.e., methylation) at cytosine-guanine (CpG) sites within a gene, as well as the accessibility of the chromatin, which is tightly bound around histone proteins. In general, when a promoter region and/or first exon CpG site in a gene becomes hypermethylated, its transcriptional activity will become lessened, whereas hypomethylation has the opposite effect ( , ). The assessment of CpG methylation most commonly occurs using methylation arrays, whereas other techniques such as reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) and whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) are also used. Modifications to histone proteins influence its accessibility for transcriptional machinery via methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, ADP ribosylation, citrullination, RNA polymerases, transcription factor (TF) binding, and/or architectural proteins, to name a few. These various modifications can organize and permit access to DNA, which ultimately regulates gene expression ( - ). Assessment of chromatin accessibility can be made using ATAC-seq, DNase-seq, MNase-seq, NOMe-seq, or ChIP-on-chip that generally entail fragmenting DNA, constructing a library, and sequencing the fragments ( , ).
The first study examining the epigenomic landscape before and after RET was published in 2014 by Rowlands et al. ( ). In this study, obese, type II diabetic Polynesian women and men underwent a 16-wk whole body RET program. A moderate-coverage genome-wide methylation array revealed changes to 500 CpG sites with over 400 reducing their methylation pattern (i.e., hypomethylation) after RET ( ). These findings were the first to suggest that RET induces preferential DNA hypomethylation in skeletal muscle. DNA methylation using improved CpG coverage revealed a more balanced CpG methylation pattern (9,153 hypo vs. 8,212 hyper) after initial RET in healthy, human muscle samples; however, this profile was drastically altered after an unloading and reloading phase (18,816 hypo vs. 8,339 hyper) ( ). Hypomethylated CpG regions of hypertrophy-related genes after acute RE corresponded to greater gene expression after reloading, ultimately leading to augmented muscle growth compared with initial RET. These seminal findings were the first to highlight DNA methylation after acute RE and the substantial carry-over effects of acute methylation changes after RE to chronic gene expression with RET ( ).
Time course studies of acute RE and other exercise modalities have begun to clarify in greater detail the early epigenetic changes to DNA and its control on gene expression after exercise at later time points ( - ). Recently, our group published a time course of human muscle biopsies after a single bout ofRE in young healthy untrained individuals; a biopsy time course of unexercised controls was also included to account for the effects of circadian rhythm, the biopsy itself, and feeding. We found that changes in the methylome 30 min after acute RE significantly related to changes in gene expression 3 h postexercise, but not at later time points out to 24 h ( ). Methylation control of gene expression was implicated in the downregulation of HOX genes and upregulation of MYC with acute RE ( ). HOX genes are crucial for development ( ), are regulated by DNA methylation ( ), and are hotspots for DNA methylation during aging ( ). In addition, HOX genes are differentially regulated by aging and physical activity status at the methylation and mRNA levels in humans ( ), but essentially nothing is known about their mechanistic role in hypertrophic adaptation to RET. MYC, a Yamanaka factor, is implicated in epigenetic reprogramming ( , ) and has been postulated to be a key TF coordinating responses and adaptations after RE and RET ( , , ). Our evidence suggested that MYC may be controlled by DNA methylation in response to a single bout of RE and that the pulsatile induction of MYC protein in skeletal muscle (mimicking the transient exercise response) is sufficient for muscle hypertrophy in mice—perhaps via control of ribosome biogenesis ( ). We also demonstrated distinct methylation patterns in isolated myonuclei and interstitial nuclei after acute (3 days) of mechanical overload (MOV)-induced hypertrophy of the fast-twitch plantaris muscle via synergist ablation of gastrocnemius and soleus muscles. This myonucleus-specific analysis revealed epigenetic changes with MOV linked to muscle hypertrophic processes including mTOR-related factors and autophagy genes including Rheb, Rictor, and Hdac1/2, as well as Myc ( ). A combination of myonucleus-specific DNA methylation with transcriptomics during acute MOV in female mice further exposed coordinated epigenetic-transcriptomic downregulation of oxidative metabolism genes that corresponded to lower mitochondrial respiration ( ). This downregulation likely relates to enhanced “aerobic glycolysis,” or a “Warburg effect” ( , ). Such a shift in metabolism may support the biosynthesis of carbon sources for anabolism during rapid muscle hypertrophy, similar to how cancer cells reprogram metabolism during growth ( ); in other words, evidence that “cell growth is cell growth” no matter the cell type ( ). Short-term surgical MOV in rodents and acute RE in humans are admittedly not the same, but the transcriptomic response to both stimuli are similar in magnitude and have shared gene expression ( ).
Studies on changes to DNA methylation in skeletal muscle with RE led to the hypothesis that persistent epigenetic changes after training could constitute a “muscle memory” of prior adaptation. This muscle “epimemory” could facilitate future growth after a period of inactivity. The Sharples laboratory first showed that a period of RET, inactivity, and then retraining resulted in further CpG hypomethylation in mixed muscle tissue after reloading compared with naïve RET (~19,000 CpGs vs. ~9,000 CpGs, respectively) ( , ). A subset of genes (e.g., UBR5, RPL35a, HEG1, PLA2G16, SETD3) had augmented hypomethylation and gene expression that was associated with accelerated muscle hypertrophy after retraining versus the initial RET ( , ). These same researchers went on to show how UBR5 can mechanistically control muscle mass in preclinical models ( , ). The discovery of UBR5s role in muscle is an example of how human RET methylome findings can lead to potentially meaningful clinical targets for intervention.
Animal studies have further expanded on the concept of an intrinsic epigenetic memory specifically in skeletal muscle fibers as a result of exercise training. Using progressive weighted wheel running (PoWeR) as an analog for concurrent training (strength + endurance exercise) ( ), previously trained mice exhibited greater hypertrophy of whole muscle (+10%–20%) and muscle fibers (+10%) upon retraining compared with naïve trained mice, mirroring observations in humans ( ). This augmented growth with prior training was attributed, at least in part, to the long-term changes to DNA methylation specifically within myonuclei for genes associated with muscle growth and adaptation (e.g., Pkd2l1, Gdf10, Pitx1, Sun2, Eif1a, and Ski). Evidence for a long-term “epigenetic memory” after training showed a tendency toward promoter hypermethylation in myonuclei and promoter hypomethylation in interstitial nuclei of skeletal muscle, suggesting reciprocal regulation between nuclear populations ( ). Factors such as skeletal muscle fiber type proportion ( , ), biological sex ( - ), training status ( , ), and loading stimulus ( ), to name a few, may also influence the magnitude and direction of DNA methylome responses to RE and RET; however, more studies are needed to fully understand their influence.
Chronological aging has a pronounced effect on the epigenetic profile of skeletal muscle DNA ( , , , , ). In human muscle tissue, RET is sufficient to partially reverse aging-induced changes to the DNA methylome ( , ). High levels of physical fitness are also associated with younger-appearing muscle DNA methylome profiles ( , ). In mice, late-life PoWeR training changes CpG methylation to coincide with the upregulation of skeletal muscle proteins involved in ribosomal, mitoribosomal, mitochondrial complex I, and skeletal muscle health (e.g., LACTB, MIB1, and UBR4) ( ). Additional studies using methylation aging “clocks” to assess DNA methylation age (DNAmAGE) or “biological age” with late-life PoWeR training in mice reported a deceleration in DNAmAGE of 8 wk, representing ~8% of a typical mouse lifespan ( , ). PoWeR is an analog for concurrent training, but its hypertrophic effects on skeletal muscle resemble findings observed in RET models ( ), resulting in a favorable muscle phenotype in aged muscle concomitant with a remodeled myonuclear epigenome ( ). The studies to date suggest that RET remodels the muscle DNA methylome down to the myonuclear level, but more studies are required in humans.
In humans, chronic RET may lead to improved skeletal muscle mitochondrial respiration and remodeling dependent on individual training status ( ). In mice, upregulation of mitochondrial and mitoribosomal proteins in skeletal muscle after PoWeR training highlights the benefits of muscle loading on mitochondrial health ( ). Given the importance of mitochondria to the energetics of muscle cell growth, initial investigations to understand epigenetic changes within the mitochondrial genome during hypertrophy have begun to emerge. mtDNA lacks histones and the chromatin structure of nuclear DNA, so the most studied epigenetic modification in mtDNA is CpG methylation ( , ). Epigenetic alterations to the “mitoepigenome” likely influence mtDNA transcription and replication ( , ); however, the magnitude this regulation exerts on mitochondrial function, if at all, during muscle hypertrophy and aging remains unclear ( ). A study of untrained older men (~65 yr) examined changes in the mitoepigenome after 6 wk of RET ( ). Of the 254 CpGs sites evaluated, 159 (63%) reduced their methylation status after RET, suggesting a more “youthful” signature in older men ( ). Data from aged mouse muscle, however, observed no significant changes in mtDNA methylation with either 8 wk of PoWeR training or aging ( ). Smaller alterations in mtDNA methylation, relative to nuclear DNA methylation, may have a greater impact on gene expression ( , ); however, optimization of mtDNA methylation techniques is required to fully expose cytosine regions during conversion, via relaxation of secondary coiled structures, to accurately assess methylation values ( ). There is still a debate on whether mtDNA methylation exists in a meaningful capacity to influence mitochondrial transcription ( , ), so more research is needed to understand mtDNA methylation in general, but also in the context of skeletal muscle hypertrophy.
Resistance exercise training studies assessing histone modifications and chromatin accessibility changes are limited. A few studies used chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), which identifies genome-wide binding sites for TFs and other proteins, in response to skeletal muscle loading in rodents and humans ( , ). In human skeletal muscle, acute RE and chronic RET facilitate gene activation by regulating histone turnover and acetylation, allowing for more transcriptionally active chromatin ( ). The loss of total histone variant H3.3 levels at active loci after acute RE suggests that nucleosome disassembly occurs in parallel with increased gene expression, possibly facilitating transcriptional activation ( ). Increased H3 acetylation relative to total H3 content indicates reduced nucleosome formation, maintenance of the loci in a transcriptionally active state, and enhanced gene expression with chronic RET ( ). In mice, electrical stimulation of skeletal muscle led to a global accumulation of H3S10 histone phosphorylation, which is crucial to muscle remodeling as an early indicator for genes involved in muscle adaptation and protein synthesis ( ). These findings were corroborated with human muscle samples after a bout of high-intensity RE; that is, similar signaling pathway activation in parallel with H3S10 phosphorylation ( ). Histone modifications have been studied in slow- and fast-twitch muscle fibers at baseline and with overload in rats ( ). Histone modifications in slow-twitch fibers induced by overload were not necessarily specific to slow fiber types and were independent of gene expression, whereas, in fast fibers, augmented gene expression correlated with increased transcription associated with histone modifications ( ). Greater coordination of H3K4me3 and H3 acetylation and transcription was observed for fast fiber-type genes compared with slow fiber-type genes; however, muscular activity likely regulates histone modifications in both fiber types ( ). Studies of histone modification with muscle loading and hypertrophy are sparse, but the limited data suggest that gene expression is influenced by RE or RET via changes in the histone epigenome, similar to how DNA methylation regulates transcriptional activity.
The current body of evidence highlights the role of DNA methylation, histone modifications, and other epigenetic modifications in skeletal muscle during hypertrophy. Epigenetic memory, mediated through persistent DNA methylation profiles after RET has ceased, may enhance future muscle plasticity and adaptation to hypertrophic stimuli. Emerging data suggest that RET is a potent tool to mitigate epigenetic aging, supporting the use of exercise to promote skeletal muscle health throughout the lifespan. Significant gaps still remain, particularly in understanding the influence of histone modifications, the mitoepigenome, as well as biological sex and age on the skeletal muscle nuclear epigenome.

## microRNAs and Loading-Induced Muscle Hypertrophy
MicroRNA (miRNA) are small noncoding RNAs regulating translational inhibition during loading in skeletal muscle ( - ). These single-stranded RNAs consist of ~18–25 nucleotides that bind directly to 3’ UTR regions of mRNA. This binding of miRNA to mRNA results in transcript instability and/or disrupted translation of the affected strand ( ). miRNAs can be considered epigenetic modifiers since they affect mRNA levels without influencing the genetic code. Furthermore, histone and DNA methylation modifying enzymes are targeted by miRNAs leading to regulation of their expression ( )—further influencing epigenetics. The expression of miRNAs themselves may be influenced by the epigenetic landscape. DNA methylation affects the expression of these small noncoding transcripts, in part because many reside in elements of protein-coding genes that are also controlled by DNA methylation ( - ). Furthermore, mature miRNAs may enter the myonucleus during mechanical loading, possibly to influence transcription in a fashion consistent with muscle metabolic changes—again reinforcing their role as epigenetic modifiers ( ). Specific to striated skeletal muscle, there is a class of highly conserved muscle-enriched miRNAs (myomiRs) that are mechanistically implicated in the regulation of muscle mass ( , ). Sempere et al. were the first to describe miR-1, miR-133a/b, and miR-206, which would later be designated as the first myomiRs ( , ). Additional miRNAs have been designated to the myomiR family and include miR-208a/b, miR-499, and miR-486 ( , ).
McCarthy and Esser ( ) were the first to characterize the responses of specific myomiRs (miR-1 and miR-133a) during muscle remodeling utilizing a 7-day synergist ablation model in mice. They observed a 50% decrease in the abundance of mature miR-1 and miR-133a ( ). These myomiRs can target molecular machinery by binding to mRNAs in the IGF-1/Akt signaling pathways that are implicated in the hypertrophic response to RE ( ), thus serving as a molecular “brake” on growth. Indeed, recent evidence confirmed that the induction of miR-1 during muscle mechanical loading blunts the hypertrophic response ( ). These observations laid the groundwork for human studies exploring global miRNA responses to RE in skeletal muscle. In young men, altered expression of ~1/3 of the miRNAs measured by low-coverage arrays, including miR-133a, was observed in response to acute RE ( ). Interestingly, correlations between median miRNA absolute fold-change and lean tissue mass (LTM) show an inverse relationship to miRNA scores and LTM at rest; however, in response to acute RE this relationship becomes positive ( ). The expression of miRNAs both basally and in response to RE may therefore be linked to skeletal muscle mass ( ). In response to 3 days of MOV in mice, 48 miRNAs in isolated myonuclei were differentially abundant compared with a sham control group ( ). Mature miRNA are processed in the cytoplasm but are reportedly transported into nuclei for reasons that are not entirely clear. In silico analysis identified ~14,000 potential gene targets for differentially expressed miRNAs in myonuclei and, of these, over 1,600 were differentially expressed at the mRNA level after MOV ( ). The evidence is clear from humans and rodents that miRNAs in young skeletal muscle are altered by mechanical loading.
Aging affects the miRNA profile of skeletal muscle and may contribute to detrimental effects on muscle size and function ( - ). Targeting miRNAs associated with sarcopenia, particularly certain myomiRs since they are enriched in skeletal muscle and may have less off-target effects in other tissues, is a potential avenue for identifying therapeutic strategies to slow skeletal muscle loss with age ( ). Kim et al. ( ) performed genome-wide miRNA analysis on young and old mouse muscle to determine changes with age. Aging led to 34 differentially expressed miRNAs, including upregulation of miR-206 that may have contributed to the downregulation of genes involved in skeletal muscle health ( ). Further integration of genome-wide miRNA and mRNA expression profiles revealed miRNA influence on essential processes for skeletal muscle function related to transcriptional regulation, metabolic health, and kinase activity ( ). In humans, RE or RET has minimal effects on the miRNA dysregulation associated with aging in skeletal muscle, but some evidence suggests potential therapeutic targets for further investigation ( , , ).
Muscle miRNAs also have roles related to satellite cell biology ( ), skeletal muscle “memory” ( ), and metabolism ( , ) with RE and RET. Global miRNA profiling revealed that the myomiR miR-206 is the most enriched miRNA in activated myogenic progenitor cells in culture ( , ). Delivery of miR-206 from satellite cells by extracellular vesicles to other cell types in the muscle environment facilitates murine loading-induced hypertrophy via extracellular matrix (ECM) regulation ( - ). Building on the finding that miR-1 is reduced in murine skeletal muscle with acute MOV ( , ), another example that leveraged miRNA profiling and in silico miRNA target analyses exposed how miR-1 is released from muscle in extracellular vesicles after RE. These miR-1 enriched EVs influence adipose cell metabolism in mice and humans ( , ). Finally, miRNA profiling showed that miR-1 remains low in murine skeletal muscle after months of detraining, potentially serving as another muscle “epimemory” mechanism ( ).
Integrated omic technologies applied to mice and humans will be essential to our understanding of the consequences of global miRNA expression on other omes (e.g., transcriptome and proteome) during muscle hypertrophy ( , - ). Analysis of miRNA, in particular myomiRs, has provided information on the molecular mechanism governing skeletal muscle hypertrophy in response to RE. Research on miRNAs to date has implicated them in the regulation of known hypertrophic pathways and epigenetic mechanisms in skeletal muscle.

## Transcriptomics and Loading-Induced Muscle Hypertrophy
Transcriptomics is the genome-wide analysis of gene expression using techniques such as microarrays or RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). With transcriptomics, researchers can simultaneously assess expression levels of large gene sets (microarray) or the majority of the genome (RNA-seq) using similar amounts of isolated RNA required for smaller studies of gene expression with qPCR. Unlike microarrays, RNA-seq does not require prior knowledge of the RNA sequence of interest and, therefore, can detect single nucleotide and splicing variants, posttranscriptional modifications, silencing RNAs or miRNAs, noncoding RNAs, exon-intron boundaries, and pre-mRNA ( ). Third-generation “long-read” sequencing is now becoming widely adopted and is even better than next-generation sequencing for assessing epitranscriptomics and splicing, although its usage in skeletal muscle has been limited to date. Transcriptomic techniques in studies of skeletal muscle hypertrophy have been used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between conditions (e.g., exercise, sex, age). These studies provide a basis to explore gain- or loss-of-function studies in genetically modified animal models, while also highlighting potential therapeutic targets to benefit skeletal muscle health. The DEGs generated from transcriptomic analysis are also used for gene ontology (GO) and pathway analysis to determine molecular function and pathway enrichment, for example, before and after RE training. Such analyses help define the magnitude of contribution from certain gene classes to adaptation and the coordinated nature of exercise-responsive signaling pathways at the mRNA level.
Shortly after Dr. Charlotte Peterson’s group performed the first RE transcriptome analysis using microarray, Dr. Esser and colleagues ( ) leveraged broader microarray techniques to determine the transcriptomic response to a hypertrophic stimulus (in this case electrical stimulation) in rodent muscle. Using an array capable of detecting ~7,000 genes, they identified 18 DEGs at 1 h into recovery after induced muscle contractions and around 70 DEGs at 6 h. These genes related to transcriptional and translational regulation of cell cycle genes implicated in muscle growth—among these were the transcription factors Fos and Myc ( ). A follow-up study using similar microarray techniques after acute RE in the skeletal muscle of men found similar changes in stress response and growth markers with eccentric contractions ( ). Then, in 2013, McCarthy and coworkers ( ) defined the transcriptional time course of MOV-induced hypertrophy in mice over 14 days. This work provided crucial insights on the genes and pathways that contribute to early, intermediate, and later-stage hypertrophic adaptation. Data from both animal models and humans highlight the conserved transcripts involved in the hypertrophic response to RE and RET between species, while noting potential interspecies differences ( , - ).
The acute transcriptome response to muscle loading is influenced by many factors, including biological sex ( - ), training status ( , ), trainability (presumably due to genetics and/or environment) ( ), skeletal muscle fiber type ( , ), and age ( , , , ). In young healthy men, the acute transcriptome response to RE, relative to endurance exercise, is enriched for transcriptional regulation, cytokine activity, cell adhesion, kinase activity, and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway, indicating exercise mode-specific transcriptional circuitry ( ). Our recent 24-h transcriptional time course after acute RE in skeletal muscle of men and women showed that global protein-coding gene expression peaked 8 h post-RE ( ). This finding contrasts with other studies that report 2–4 h as the peak mRNA response timepoint ( , , - ) and has implications for biopsy timing after exercise to capture specific hypertrophic processes. The activation of stress-related genes early after acute RE transitioned later to transcriptional and translational processes implicated in ribosome assembly and protein synthesis. Ribosomal biogenesis-related mRNAs dominate the transcriptomic landscape between 8 and 24 h after acute RE ( ). Enhanced ribosomal efficiency and/or capacity after acute RE may partially drive the initial phases of skeletal muscle hypertrophy and be mediated by MYC ( , , - ). In the myonuclei of short-term mechanically overloaded plantaris muscles of mice, similar upregulation of protein synthesis and ribosomal biogenesis genes is observed; in vivo overexpression experiments suggest these genes were in part driven by MYC ( ). ECM genes, specifically within muscle fibers, are also upregulated during early phase MOV. ECM remodeling is a central component of skeletal muscle hypertrophic adaptation and the maintenance of a healthy muscle fiber niche ( , ). ECM-related genes are upregulated after acute RE in young men, but this response is blunted in older men, likely resulting in disrupted muscle adaptation ( ). The ECM acts as a muscle scaffolding, providing enzymatic, biochemical, and mechanical support that permits loading-induced hypertrophy ( , ). Dysregulated ECM gene expression contributes to muscle myopathies and altered muscle regeneration with aging ( , , ). Thus, RET may serve as a therapy for conditions where muscle ECM is aberrant.
Transcriptomics is beginning to decode hypertrophic response heterogeneity to RET ( , , ). So-called “extreme responders” with respect to hypertrophy had over 8,000 DEGs compared with “nonresponders” after a 16-wk RET program in young women and men ( ). Upregulation of genes related to transcriptional control, histone modifications, satellite cell activity, muscle regeneration, ribosome biogenesis, and anabolic pathways in extreme responders occurred concomitant with increased myofiber size and muscle function compared with nonresponders ( , , ). Non-responders have heightened expression of genes related to inflammation and damage, potentially suppressing muscle adaptations to RE ( ). Array-based RNA profiling techniques were recently used in human muscle to identify novel molecular regulators of muscle hypertrophy by leveraging information from disparate loading conditions in large cohorts ( ). One hundred forty-one genes correlated to muscle hypertrophy across multiple cohorts, but dynamic 3’ and 5’ untranslated region (UTR) regulation emerged as a previously unnoticed aspect of muscle remodeling ( ). Evolving transcriptomic techniques and analysis approaches promise to identify the causes of interindividual variability, enabling more dependable detection of molecular regulators of muscle hypertrophy. Defining the regulators ofRE sensitivity may also facilitate the development of strategies and therapies for improving muscle health in various conditions, which could foster personalized medicine and exercise prescription approaches.
Most RE and RET studies in humans use homogenized muscle tissue samples for transcriptomic analysis; however, skeletal muscle is a heterogeneous tissue comprised of muscle fiber types with differing structural and metabolic qualities. These fiber types often adapt differently to hypertrophic stimuli, with “fast-twitch” fibers tending to be the most responsive ( - ). To identify fiber type-specific genes controlling loading-induced hypertrophy, Raue et al. ( ) analyzed skeletal muscle biopsy samples from young and old women and men using microarrays to determine muscle fiber type-specific gene expression after the first and last sessions of a 12-wk RET program. Comparing to bulk tissue analysis, fiber-type specific analysis highlighted that fastfibers drove the gene expression profile after acute RE and chronic RET ( ). A provocative target that emerged from this analysis was the TWEAK receptor TNFRSF12A, or FN14, which is enriched in fast fibers during muscle hypertrophy ( , ). FN14 has continued to appear prominently in contemporary human RE RNA-seq datasets ( , ), but its mechanistic function in RE-induced muscle growth is unclear ( ). FN14 induction during muscle adaptation could be related to noncanonical NF-κB pathway activation ( ), and inhibition of FN14 in human myotubes increases C/EPβ and MuRF ( ). Mechanistic work in rodents suggests Fn14 knockout in muscle fibers improves endurance exercise capacity and inhibits neurogenic muscle atrophy ( ), but ablation in satellite cells attenuates muscle regeneration ( ). Given these contrasting findings, more gain and loss of function preclinical studies are needed to clarify the role of Fn14 in hypertrophic muscle adaptation ( , ). Collectively, additional fiber type-specific transcriptome studies with RE are warranted since conditions such as aging (preferential fast-twitch atrophy) and unloading/inactivity (preferential slow-twitch atrophy), where RET is a common countermeasure, have different etiology. Deeper understanding of fiber type-specific transcriptomic responses to RE and RET may help inform effective therapies.
Supervised resistance training studies in humans are difficult to execute and often relatively short in duration (≤16 wk). Cross-sectional muscle transcriptome information from highly trained compared to sedentary individuals can therefore be informative for exposing the effects of long-term muscle hypertrophy training. Using microarrays, chronically resistance-trained men (>8 yr of RET) had a modestly different gene expression profile relative to sedentary as well as long-term endurance-trained individuals ( ). Specifically, endurance exercise training led to upregulation of mitochondrial and oxidative metabolism genes, whereas RET led to upregulation of tissue growth and protein synthesis genes, likely contributing to enhanced body mass and muscle force production in the latter group ( , ). A more recent and comprehensive transcriptome analysis using highly trained male and female strength and endurance athletes (>15 yr of training) indicated that endurance training has a more pronounced effect than RET on the gene expression landscape in resting muscle ( ). As such, effort should be directed toward understanding the acute response to RE in the untrained versus trained state, as the acute response to RE over 24 h can result in >2,000 DEGs ( ). Indeed, some evidence suggests that the transcriptome response to acute RE changes with training over time in rats and may become blunted ( ). It is also conceivable that gene expression peaks earlier after a bout of exercise when trained ( ), but this possibility has not been explored in detail after RE.
Transcriptomic investigations of loading-induced hypertrophy suggest that biological sex differences significantly influence RE responses in skeletal muscle ( - , , ). In 2002, Roth and colleagues ( ) used microarray technology to explore how biological sex affects muscle gene expression after a 9-wk RET program. Biological sex influenced the expression of ~210 genes, with 175 of those being more highly expressed in males as opposed to females after RET ( ). Biological sex also appears to influence the molecular response timeline to acute RE. Preliminary evidence from a small cohort suggests the transcriptional response to acute RE is more sustained in males than in females after 24 h ( ). This result is thought-provoking since males and females tend to accrue the same amount of muscle mass relative to body weight and initial muscle mass with chronic RET ( , ), so further validation should be performed in a larger cohort. Both sexes exhibit transcriptional regulation of some similar biological processes, but females may uniquely upregulate genes related to blood coagulation, insulin receptor binding, transforming growth factor-β signaling, and transcriptional activators ( ). A recent study showed that sex hormones (e.g., testosterone and estradiol) profoundly shape the molecular signature of skeletal muscle at baseline and after 3 mo of RET ( ). These data suggest that sex hormones, rather than sex chromosomes, predominantly regulate protein synthesis and molecular responses to RET ( ). Interestingly, young males exhibit a significantly larger transcriptomic response after RET compared with females, but this trend reversed with aging ( ). Despite these insights, research on sex-specific responses and adaptations, as they pertain to omics technologies, remain limited. Addressing this gap in knowledge will be critical for advancing our understanding of the role of sex and aging on skeletal muscle hypertrophy.
Muscle loses plasticity throughout the lifespan but still demonstrates transcriptome responses and adaptations to RE and RET later in life ( , , , ). Building on the seminal work of Jozsi et al. ( ), an investigation in 2007 comprehensively profiled the aging transcriptome response to RET in humans ( ). This study revealed differential transcription regulation at baseline between young and old individuals; however, after 6 mo of RET, older individuals reversed their molecular skeletal muscle aging profile resembling their younger counterparts, coinciding with increased whole muscle function ( ). These data comport with recent work in mice showing that hypertrophic exercise training shares transcriptomic similarities to Yamanaka factor-induced epigenetic reprogramming of skeletal muscle toward a more youthful profile ( ). Generally, a blunted response to acute RE and chronic RET is reported in older individuals; however, a generous overlap of genes is shared between young and old in the trained state ( ). Aging and training led to a unique profile of 569 genes specific to older individuals after 12 wk of RET in hypertrophic pathways related to Wnt, mTOR, IGF-1, PI3K-AKT, and AMPK signaling, among others ( ). Genes related to ECM organization, which play vital roles in skeletal muscle growth, function, and repair, are upregulated with RET in aging individuals ( , ). A robust ECM remodeling response at the transcriptome level is indicative of healthy skeletal muscle ( ). Although RET is the most effective countermeasure against aging-mediated muscle loss, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) elicits a more pronounced change in muscle gene expression than RET ( ). This observation could mean that intense training should be adopted in older adults and/or a concurrent HIIT and RET approach may be most effective for driving muscle growth ( )—specifically to target the fast-twitch muscle fibers that are most impaired by aging. To this point, a concurrent endurance/resistance training approach in aged mice elicited transcriptome commonalities with the training response in younger mice in the oxidative/slow-twitch enriched soleus muscle resulting in muscle hypertrophy, but the transcriptome response in the fast-twitch plantaris muscle was blunted concomitant with less hypertrophy ( ). These data suggest higher-intensity exercise may be required to maximize hypertrophic effectiveness, but so long as it is balanced by adequate rest in aged muscle ( ).
Transcriptomics has emerged as a powerful and accessible tool to unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying skeletal muscle biology, offering insights into gene regulation, pathway activation, and variability within and between study populations in response to loading-induced hypertrophy. Early studies laid the foundation for understanding conserved and species-specific responses and adaptions, whereas recent advancements through RNA-seq have refined our ability to identify critical regulators of skeletal muscle hypertrophy. Enough studies have now been published so that large-scale meta-analyses of human RE and RET transcriptomes can provide detailed information on the key mRNAs associated with adaptation across ages, time points, biological sex, and conditions ( , - ). From this meta-analytical work, NR4A3 was identified as one of the most exercise-responsive genes across modes and conditions that regulates skeletal muscle metabolism ( ) and had been mechanistically shown to control muscle mass ( ). Despite the significant progress made in the early part of the twenty-first century, knowledge gaps remain particularly as they pertain to sex- and age-specific transcriptomic responses and adaptations. Allocating resources toward addressing these gaps is essential for developing targeted therapeutics to optimize muscle health, mitigate age-related sarcopenia, and potentially lead to personalized training strategies to maximize muscle hypertrophy and/or prevent atrophy. Continued advancement in transcriptomic techniques/technologies promises to enhance our understanding of skeletal muscle biology during hypertrophy.

## Proteomics and Loading-Induced Hypertrophy
Quantification of targeted proteins in skeletal muscle and exercise research has been enabled by classic techniques such Western blotting or “immunoblotting.” This technique involves extraction and denaturation of proteins from muscle, separation of proteins by gel electrophoresis, electrotransfer of proteins from the gel to a membrane, and finally, detection and visualization of target proteins using immunochemistry. Developed in the late 1970s ( , ), Western blotting is accessible and affordable for most laboratories but only allows for quantification of limited protein targets. Proteomics provides large-scale, nontargeted analysis of protein abundance. This approach generates information on responses and adaptations to various stimuli with data-driven approaches aiding in hypothesis development. In brief, proteomics workflows start with protein digestion into peptides and then separation by reverse-phase liquid chromatography so that peptide abundance, synthesis rates, and/or phosphorylation state are determined by tandem mass spectrometry, which is then analyzed using computational data processing pipelines ( ).
Few studies have utilized proteomics platforms with RE and muscle hypertrophy in humans or animal models. However, some initial findings suggest that increased protein abundance with RE training is associated with increased protein turnover (synthesis + degradation) rates, but that these responses may be protein-specific ( ). Interestingly, Emanuelsson et al. ( ) observed minimal remodeling of the resting skeletal muscle proteome in men with 15+ years of RET compared with healthy controls, even with enhanced muscle mass and function due to RET. Proteomic responses to RET in humans may also be mode-specific (e.g., concentric vs. eccentric) ( , ). Recent proteomic analyses of human muscle identified changes in protein abundance involved in steroid metabolism, protein folding, ribosomal health, and ECM organization following 4 wk of RET ( ). Notably, upregulation of KLHL41 was identified and led to the discovery that β2-adrenergic receptors may act as a mimetic to induce skeletal muscle hypertrophy in the absence of RE. These findings collectively underscore the vast potential of proteomics to identify therapeutic targets for muscle health and possible pharmaceutical agents to alleviate atrophy ( ).
In the context of aging, skeletal muscle mass loss occurs due to chronic elevation in protein breakdown that exceeds protein synthesis rates. Worth noting, however, is that there is a growing body of evidence that protein synthesis in skeletal muscle is higher in aged than young skeletal muscle ( , ) due to hyperactive mTORC1 ( ). This is perhaps why mTORC1 inhibition can benefit aging skeletal muscle when sedentary ( ). Aging also disrupts ECM proteostasis in skeletal muscle, characterized by impaired collagen turnover ( ). These new perspectives may shift our mechanistic understanding of how RET reduces muscle mass loss with aging. Nevertheless, it is known that RET promotes skeletal muscle hypertrophy in older adults, but these adaptations are blunted compared with young, healthy individuals ( , , - ). With aging in human skeletal muscle, RE induces changes in mitochondrial and glucose metabolism proteins, even in the absence of skeletal muscle growth, thereby improving the overall health of the tissue ( , , ). A complimentary proteomic finding was reported in the muscles of aged rats after RET ( ). Using exploratory proteomics, 8 wk of unilateral RET reportedly had minimal effect on the myofibrillar and nonmyofibrillar proteome in middle-aged humans, with noncontractile related proteins predominantly altered with aging and RET ( ). By contrast, using aged female mice, our laboratory leveraged hypertrophic PoWeR exercise to determine that late-life training results in increased abundance of ~900 proteins linked to the ribosome, mitoribosome, mitochondrial complex I, translational regulation, and skeletal muscle health ( ). Many of these proteome changes were also found in the proteome of aged humans after HIIT and RET ( ). Several proteins after PoWeR correlated with increased in vivo muscle function, exposing new potential regulators of skeletal muscle function with exercise. Comparison of our aged PoWeR proteome to the proteome of younger weighted wheel running-trained mice ( ) revealed ~25% similarity as well as distinct differences in the proteome after training that are worth further investigation.
Proteomics will continue to provide novel insights on the molecular underpinnings of skeletal muscle hypertrophy throughout the lifespan. By identifying protein-level changes that are not readily apparent through transcriptional or targeted protein analyses, proteomics has highlighted the complexity of muscle adaptation and helped identify potential therapeutic and pharmacological targets for improved muscle health. Integrating the transcriptome with the proteome across recovery time points after acute RE and with chronic RET will reveal information on how well transcription matches with translation; this is an area of controversy and interest in the context of skeletal muscle exercise research ( , , ). Furthermore, proteomics at the fiber type-specific level will provide us with the tools to elucidate molecular mechanisms that drive beneficial remodeling of skeletal muscle with RET ( ), especially as it pertains to aged muscle. As technology advances, proteomics applications will refine our understanding of exercise-induced responses and adaptations in skeletal muscle.

## Phosphoproteomics and Loading-Induced Hypertrophy
The assessment of posttranslational phosphorylation of proteins, known as the “phosphoproteome,” provides critical information on the activation of signaling molecules and molecular machinery through the addition or removal of phosphate groups ( ). Although phosphoproteomics studies are limited with respect to loading-induced muscle hypertrophy, initial progress with this approach has led to the mapping of novel signaling factors that are associated with skeletal muscle growth.
An early study in rats used targeted protein phosphorylation to determine changes in phosphorylation state of intracellular signaling proteins (i.e., S6K1 and ERK2) involved in muscle hypertrophy ( ). Phosphorylation state of S6K1 and ERK2 were altered up to ~12 h postexercise and were influenced by rest periods between consecutive RE bouts in rats ( ). On this foundation, Potts et al. ( ) identified ~6,000 unique phosphorylation sites (phosphosites) in mouse muscle, with 663 sites being modified mainly by mTOR, MAPKs, and CAMKs in response to MOV. These findings underscore the importance of mTOR and kinase activity in response to hypertrophic stimuli ( ). Phosphoproteomics after acute resistance-like contractile activity in rats identified STIM1 (stromal interaction molecule 1) as an AMPK substrate that may fine-tune myofiber calcium levels to affect intracellular signaling and fatigue, which may have consequences for RE adaptation ( ). Solagna et al. ( ) in 2020 used phosphoproteomics to identify that phosphorylation of MRTF-B is required for its nuclear translocation, which drives the expression of the Fos transcription factor after acute RE in mice. They also determined that histone phosphorylation of SRF, which is activated by MRTF-B, is essential to protein synthesis after acute RE ( ). A phosphoproteomic screen of murine skeletal muscle after resistance-like contractions revealed that phosphorylation of TRIM28 in muscle fibers is sufficient to induce hypertrophy in adult mice ( ). Proteomic responses to divergent exercise modalities in humans revealed 1,957 distinct phosphosites associated with a single bout of RE, compared with only 420 that were shared between acute endurance, sprint, and resistance exercise ( ). This analysis also identified that a phosphosite of the previously uncharacterized protein C18ORF25, an AMPK substrate, changes with all modes of exercise. It was determined that phosphorylation of this protein regulates skeletal muscle function and exercise capacity using a loss-of-function mouse model, likely via disturbed calcium handling ( ). Precisely how C18ORF25 mechanistically controls muscle mass is still to be determined. Distinct as well as shared phosphoproteomic signatures and posttranslational modifications with RE versus AE likely contribute substantially to the unique health benefits or RE observed in skeletal muscle. Phosphoproteomics research into loading-induced muscle growth is in its early stages. Still, this technology shows immense promise in unraveling how protein activation and deactivation through posttranslational modifications shape the structural and functional landscape of skeletal muscle.

## MUSCLE HYPERTROPHY FROM A SINGLE CELL OMICS PERSPECTIVE
Transcriptomics at single cell and nucleus resolution in skeletal muscle only began to emerge within the last decade. These approaches provide high-resolution insights into cellular and myonuclear heterogeneity and complexity that traditional bulk analysis lacks, revealing diverse cell types, dynamic cellular states, and rare cell populations ( ). Platforms such as the 10X Chromium Controller have enabled widespread adoption of single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) as well as single nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq). Given how relatively new this technology is to the skeletal muscle field, examples of using it to study loading-induced hypertrophy are limited. Nevertheless, since skeletal muscle contains a variety of mononuclear cell types (e.g., satellite cells, fibrogenic cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, etc.) as well as multinucleated muscle fibers, the insights gathered from single-cell omics during hypertrophy thus far has been novel and valuable. Single cell and nucleus omics technologies allow for granular identification of molecular triggers for cell state transitions and fate changes, as well as cross talk between and among cell types and nuclei in the myofiber syncytium ( ).
The first study to utilize scRNA-seq during muscle hypertrophy was published in 2021. After MOV in adult mice, scRNA-seq (to target interstitial cell transcription) revealed a communication network connecting satellite cells to numerous other cell types in muscle ( ). scRNA-seq during loading-induced muscle hypertrophy combined with recombination-independent satellite cell-specific lineage tracing in mice went on to define a subpopulation of satellite cells characterized by low levels of Notch1, Nrf2, and Gclm that fuse directly to muscle fibers without first proliferating ( ). Using our PoWeR approach in mice, snRNA-seq (to target muscle fiber transcription) revealed how satellite cells positively influence resident myonuclear transcriptional profiles following a bout of high-volume endurance/resistance exercise in trained mice ( , ). This study also showed how satellite cells in trained muscle can activate after exercise, perhaps to communicate within the muscle niche, but may no longer fuse appreciably to muscle fibers upon continued nondamaging exercise ( ). Satellite cells affecting resident myonuclear transcription were recently corroborated and expanded on using the surgical mechanical overload model in mice combined with satellite cell-specific recombination-independent lineage tracing ( ). snRNA-seq also revealed that satellite cell fusion may deliver unique transcripts such as H19, Igf2, and ribosomal genes to growing muscle fibers to support hypertrophy ( , ). Currently, scRNA-seq studies involving RE in human muscle are even more sparse than preclinical studies. One investigation revealed the heterogeneity of macrophages following acute RE ( ). More advanced single cell and nucleus transcriptional analyses combined with methylome ( , ), ATAC-Seq (chromatin accessibility) ( ), and proteomics ( , ) at single cell and nucleus resolution will continue to provide unprecedented information on the contributions of supporting cell types as well as myonuclei to hypertrophic muscle adaptations when combined with human RE and RET interventions.

## ADDITIONAL OMES IN SKELETAL MUSCLE HYPERTROPHY RESEARCH
Emerging multiomic technologies will expand our understanding of the vast biological responses and adaptations that occur during skeletal muscle hypertrophy. Metabolomics is a tool to assess the production of metabolites within a biological tissue as a result of a stimulus. After RET there are changes in the muscle metabolome that are much less pronounced than after endurance exercise training ( ). The authors of this study concluded that total energy expenditure during an exercise bout drives metabolite responses and adaptations, and this accounted for the differences between modalities. Gehlert et al. ( ) investigated the metabolomic response to acute RE and chronic RET in muscle of untrained men. Acute RE altered 33 metabolites associated with protein breakdown, increased protein turnover, NAD and ATP metabolism, and growth, whereas chronic RET altered 46 metabolites compared with the untrained state that was related to growth and lipid metabolism ( ). Interestingly, only five of the metabolites that were altered after acute RE remained after 13 RET sessions, indicating training coordinates other metabolic changes not related to the acute exercise response ( ). Pataky et al. ( ) observed exercise mode-dependent changes in lipid-related metabolites, where after 12 wk of RET, metabolites related to the lipidome were predominantly upregulated compared with the divergent adaptations in HIIT. Sex-specific differences after RET were observed for abundance of the TCA cycle metabolites ketoglutarate (females increased) and cis-aconitate (males increased), highlighting sex-specific metabolite responses may differ, but ultimately result in similar phenotypic changes in skeletal muscle (i.e., size and function) ( ).
The release of cross talk proteins (myokines) from muscle after exercise is well established and adds to the complexity of muscle tissue as an endocrine organ ( - ), but studies of RE are sparse. Limited data from studies evaluating the muscle secretome reveal that RET elicited secretion of a select set of myokines from skeletal muscle ( ). A recent study used proteomics of skeletal muscle interstitial fluid after exhaustive kicking exercise to identify the derivative of the bioactive molecule cathelicidin-related antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) as a potential hypertrophic regulator ( ). Other emerging omes that will expand our knowledge of skeletal muscle adaptations to hypertrophic stimuli include the microbiome ( , ), UFmylation ( ), RNA methylation ( , ), spatial transcriptomics and proteomics ( - ), ubiquitinomics ( , ), and ribosome methylation sequencing (RiboMeth-seq) ( ). As a timely example, a recent study used RiboMeth-seq to characterize ribosomal RNA methylation of muscle fiber ribosomes during murine MOV ( ). Modification to the RNA epitranscriptome could represent a new form of ribosome specialization and/or transcriptional regulation during muscle hypertrophy ( ) that could be corroborated using an approach such as long-read RNA-seq ( ) in human muscle. Ubiquitinomics can be implemented to better characterize posttranslational ubiquitination events by specific ligases that promote protein degradation and the influence of these highly complex interactions during skeletal muscle hypertrophy ( ). Integration of emerging technologies into skeletal muscle hypertrophy research will expand our understanding of the intricate intercellular and interorgan responses and adaptations that work collaboratively to induce muscle growth.

## INTEGRATION OF OMICS ON A LARGE SCALE TO UNDERSTAND MUSCLE HYPERTROPHY
As the breadth of omics research in skeletal muscle grows, it will become increasingly important to make concerted efforts at computationally integrating omics datasets to infer regulation and mechanism of action. Information on which molecular layer is most influential during hypertrophic muscle adaptation can help guide where therapies for muscle mass loss should be targeted. Initial efforts at integrating multiple omics datasets were done via meta-analysis comparing methylome and transcriptome responses after RE and RET by the Sharples’ group in 2018 ( ). This analysis helped identify novel genes related to skeletal muscle hypertrophy, anabolism, and epigenetic memory, further solidifying the rationale for integrating multiple omics datasets using bioinformatics to explore the regulation of acute and chronic responses to RE and RET ( ). We have made initial efforts at bioinformatically integrating the epigenome, transcriptome, and/or proteome to infer control of transcription and translation during muscle hypertrophy ( , ). Dr. Yuan Wen’s laboratory at the University of Kentucky adapted an approach called binding and expression target analysis (BETA) to infer control of transcription by DNA methylation during short-term MOV ( ). BETA integrates methylomics with other omic datasets and uses methylation distance from transcriptional start sites of differentially regulated genes, then applies a regulatory score to determine significance. This approach revealed how the muscle methylome at 30 min significantly influences the upregulation of genes in the transcriptome 3 h after a bout of RE in humans ( ). We also used BETA with late-life PoWeR in mice to infer epigenetic regulation of protein translation/abundance. We found that DNA methylation status influenced upregulated proteins related to skeletal muscle metabolic and mitochondrial quality, as well as ribosomal and mitoribosomal proteins ( ). Recently, Dr. Marcas Bamman’s group ( ) performed integration of the skeletal muscle methylome with the skeletal muscle and serum extracellular vesicle (EV) transcriptomes to determine sex-specific differences in untrained females and males after acute concurrent training. Integration of the skeletal muscle methylome and transcriptome revealed enrichments of ECM receptor interactions in females and enriched complement and coagulation cascade in males ( ). Integration of the skeletal muscle methylome and EV transcriptome revealed enriched genes in females related to cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and complement and coagulation cascade and in males related to retinol metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis, with methylomic regulation of EVs favoring upregulated genes in both sexes ( ). Furthermore, this paper integrated the muscle transcriptome to the serum metabolome and revealed 23 differentially enriched pathways related to protein biosynthesis, ATP-binding cassette transporters, and bile secretion among others ( ). Although these studies provide resources and exploratory findings, much larger collaborative studies like MoTrPAC have published animal studies exploring and analyzing numerous omic responses and adaptations to endurance exercise in several tissues (i.e., muscle, adipose, blood, brain, reproductive organs, pancreas, etc.) ( - , ). These initial studies did not implement an RE model but highlight the power and potential of multiomic integration for future RE studies, including the human clinical arm of MoTrPAC that will explore multiomic responses in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and blood with both acute and chronic RE training (12 wk and >12 mo) ( ). The emergence of data mining, network-based integration, machine learning, and artificial intelligence ( , ) applied to muscle hypertrophy omics datasets hold promise for revealing relationships and potential mechanisms that customary comparative analysis procedures may miss. Integration of large, exploratory multiomic datasets will be foundational to guide future hypothesis-driven as well as mechanistic preclinical investigations of skeletal muscle hypertrophy.

## CONCLUSIONS
Multiomic research in resistance exercise studies is still in its early stages, but the technological and computational tools that continue to be refined will provide an impactful analysis of molecular regulators of muscle mass. A schematic based on examples from the literature and our discussion is presented in . RE and RET studies have already shed light on the molecular cues for skeletal muscle growth at numerous omic layers in humans. For instance: GWAS identified polymorphisms such as ACTN3 R577X associated with hypertrophic adaptability and muscle performance ( ); epigenomics revealed how training and retraining alters UBR5 to control muscle mass ( - ); small RNA profiling led to discoveries on how miR-1 can affect lipolysis after RE ( , ); transcriptomics honed in on MYC and NR4A3 as highly RE-responsive genes that regulate muscle mass ( , , ); proteomics showed how KLHL41 may simulate RET via β-adrenergic signaling ( ); phosphoproteomics uncovered the role of C18ORF25 in fast-twitch calcium handling ( , ) and TRIM28 during myofiber growth ( , , ); and single cell RNA-seq provided an initial glimpse into macrophage diversity following a bout of RE ( ). Future research should aim to more widely adopt these techniques, as well as seek to integrate these omic layers in combination with machine learning and artificial intelligence computational approaches to unleash the potential of omics for understanding muscle mass regulation by exercise.
