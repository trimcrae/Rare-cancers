## Background
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a global health challenge, leading to chronic cognitive and motor deficits. The limited neuroregenerative capacity of adult mammals hampers the development of effective therapies. In contrast, zebrafish exhibit remarkable brain regeneration, making them an ideal model for studying molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying TBI.

## Methods
This study established a telencephalic stab wound injury model in zebrafish to mimic TBI. Transcriptomic analyses were performed at control, 1-day post-lesion (dpl), 4 dpl, and 7 dpl to identify dynamic transcriptional changes during brain regeneration. Key signaling pathways were identified, and functional inhibition experiments were conducted to evaluate the role of specific pathways in the regenerative process.

## Results
Transcriptomic analysis revealed temporal transcriptional changes underlying brain regeneration, from early injury responses to proliferation and tissue remodeling. Key signaling pathways, including Insulin, FoxO, MAPK, mTOR, and NOD-like receptor signaling, were implicated in neural progenitor activation, proliferation, migration, and maintenance. The MAPK pathway, particularly p38α MAPK (MAPK14a), was identified as a critical regulator of the regenerative response. Functional inhibition of MAPK14a disrupted progenitor activation, impaired proliferation, and diminished neuronal and oligodendrocyte recovery, and led to increased cell death in neurogenic zones. Additionally, MAPK14a was found to influence Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways further highlighted its integrative role in coordinating regeneration.

## Conclusions
This study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms of zebrafish brain regeneration, highlighting MAPK14a as a central regulator of neural repair. These findings may inform strategies for enhancing brain repair in mammals.

## Background
Regeneration of lost or damaged tissue is a fundamental biological process, observed across various organisms with remarkable diversity in capability and mechanism. While regenerative potential in mammals is generally limited, certain species like zebrafish (Danio rerio) exhibit an extraordinary ability to regenerate multiple organ systems, including the central nervous system (CNS) [ – ]. This capacity makes zebrafish a powerful model for understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying regeneration and for developing regenerative therapies applicable to humans [ – ].
Among the organs capable of regeneration, the zebrafish brain stands out for its robust and widespread adult neurogenesis, with 16 distinct proliferative zones distributed along the rostro-caudal axis of the brain. These zones include ventricularly positioned radial glial cells (RGCs), functional orthologs of mammalian ependymal cells, and neuroepithelial-like progenitors, which play central roles in regeneration [ – ]. These progenitors respond to injury by proliferating, differentiating, and integrating into the brain’s existing circuitry, facilitated by a permissive environment devoid of scar formation [ ]. Notably, regeneration in the zebrafish brain occurs in response to injuries such as stab wounds or localized ablations, providing an opportunity to investigate early transcriptional responses that prime regeneration [ , ]. Previous studies have identified several key signaling pathways that regulate progenitor cell maintenance and neurogenesis during CNS regeneration in zebrafish. Pathways such as Sonic Hedgehog (Shh), bone morphogenetic proteins (Bmp/TGF-β), Wnt/β-catenin, AhR, Notch, and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) are central to both constitutive and injury-induced neurogenesis [ , – ]. These signaling molecules, essential during CNS development, are reactivated during regeneration. Additionally, inflammation caused by traumatic brain injury or neurodegeneration triggers specific neurogenic programs that work alongside these pathways to compensate for neuronal loss [ , ]. A number of parallel studies in zebrafish and mice further emphasize the roles of pathways such as Insulin, BDNF, and Steroids in regulating neurogenesis by controlling progenitor cell quiescence, proliferation, and differentiation [ – ]. Despite the wealth of studies on neurogenesis and CNS repair in zebrafish, the molecular events during the initial stages of brain regeneration remain poorly understood.
To address this, we have established and validated a telencephalic stab wound injury model to study traumatic brain injury in zebrafish [ ]. This model allows for the study of transcriptional and cellular responses to traumatic brain injury, with the contralateral, non-injured hemisphere serving as a control. Through RNA sequencing at four time points, namely control, 1-day post-lesion (dpl), 4 dpl, and 7 dpl, we sought to uncover the transcriptional dynamics governing brain regeneration. This analysis revealed several novel pathways and transcriptional players, with a significant enrichment of MAPK signaling pathways. Among these, the p38 MAPK signaling cascade emerges as a central mediator of brain regeneration in zebrafish.
The p38 MAPK pathway is well-established as a regulator of neural stem cell (NSC) maintenance and migration [ – ]. It plays critical roles in neuronal function by modulating protein translation, regulating ion channels, and influencing neurotransmitter receptor activity [ ]. Evidence from mammalian studies highlights its dual nature: while p38 MAPK activation promotes NSC proliferation and neurogenesis, hyperactivation of this pathway has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, and Huntington’s [ – ]. Induction of p38α MAPK in the mouse subventricular zone (SVZ) increases progenitor proliferation and neurogenesis, whereas its deletion results in diminished NPC proliferation [ ]. However, its aberrant overactivation can be neurotoxic, as seen in cadmium-induced neurotoxicity [ ].
In this study, we highlight for the first time the central role of p38α MAPK in facilitating brain regeneration in zebrafish. By integrating transcriptomic analyses with the well-characterized telencephalic stab wound model, our work sheds light on the molecular underpinnings of zebrafish brain regeneration, offering novel insights into mechanisms that may inform regenerative medicine strategies in the future.

## Results

## Establishment of telencephalic stab wound injury model to study brain regeneration in zebrafish
To investigate brain regeneration dynamics in zebrafish, we developed a robust and well-characterized telencephalic stab wound injury model (Fig. A). Using a fine 30G needle, we precisely targeted the right hemisphere of the dorsolateral telencephalon, injuring both the neurogenic niche and surrounding parenchyma. The left hemisphere served as a contralateral control for comparative analysis of injury-induced regenerative responses. 
Histological assessments were performed at multiple time points post-lesion, including 4 dpl, 7 dpl, 14 dpl, and 30 dpl. At 4 dpl, the injury was clearly evident at the poke site as accumulation of cells was observed, with progressive reduction in lesion size observed at subsequent time points. Notably, by 14 dpl, the poke site had closed and tissue integrity had significantly improved indicating active regeneration processes. By 30 dpl, the brain exhibited near-complete regeneration with only minor residual evidence of the initial injury as shown in Fig. C. Also, PCNA expression (Fig. B; Figure A) was significantly upregulated during the regenerative process, confirming active cell proliferation in the injured brain. This further validated the ongoing cellular responses required for neurogenesis and tissue repair.
To further characterize the regenerative response, we conducted molecular analyses, including qPCR and Western blotting, to assess the expression of key markers involved in neurogenesis (Fig. D–E). At 7 dpl, qPCR analysis of mRNA levels for tbr2 and neurod1 (Fig. E) confirmed upregulation of genes associated with neural progenitor cells and neurogenesis. Additionally, analysis of sox2, olig2, and gfap mRNA levels (Fig. D) revealed significant increases, suggesting that radial glial cells (RGCs) and other progenitors were activated during the regenerative process. These results corroborated the histological findings, reinforcing the regenerative capacity of the zebrafish brain after injury.
Additionally, immunostaining with cell-specific markers was performed to further characterize the regenerative response following injury (Fig. A). BrdU incorporation, administered four hours before sacrifice, was used to label proliferating cells. At 4 days post-lesion (dpl), a marked increase in BrdU-positive cells was observed in the injured telencephalic hemisphere, particularly in the neurogenic niches and surrounding parenchyma, compared to the uninjured control brain (Fig. A, compare panels A and B; quantified in Fig. B). This proliferative response further intensified by 7 dpl, with a greater number of BrdU-positive cells in the injured parenchyma, indicating sustained cell division in response to injury. By 14 dpl, the number of BrdU-positive cells declined relative to earlier time points but remained elevated compared to the uninjured control, suggesting tapering yet persistent regenerative activity (Figure B). Immunostaining for Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), a marker for radial glial cells (RGCs), revealed enhanced expression in the injured hemisphere at 7 dpl, particularly at the injury site and neurogenic zones. By 14 dpl, GFAP expression was observed throughout the injured telencephalon, indicating widespread activation of progenitor cells (Fig. A, panel F; Figure D), in contrast to the limited expression in the uninjured brain (Fig. A, panel E; quantified in Fig. D). Similarly, NeuroD1, a transcription factor involved in neuronal differentiation, was upregulated in the injured telencephalon at 7 dpl, with a further increase by 14 dpl, consistent with ongoing neurogenesis (Fig. A, panel D; Figure C), as compared to the uninjured control (Fig. A, panel C; quantified in Fig. C). Collectively, these results indicate a coordinated regenerative response in the zebrafish brain following injury, characterized by increased cell proliferation, activation of radial glial progenitors, and upregulation of neurogenic differentiation markers, highlighting the robust neurogenic potential of the adult zebrafish telencephalon. 
In summary, our results underscore the efficacy and reliability of the telencephalic stab wound injury model for studying traumatic brain injury (TBI) in the zebrafish telencephalon, highlighting both the histological and molecular events that support robust brain regeneration.

## Transcriptome profiling and functional annotation of the zebrafish telencephalon during different stages of brain regeneration
The molecular mechanisms underlying early brain regeneration remain poorly understood, particularly at the level of differential gene expression. To address this gap, we aimed to identify and compare the transcriptomes of the zebrafish telencephalon at key time points post-injury, namely 1day post-lesion (1 dpl), 4 dpl, and 7 dpl. The telencephalon has been shown to initiate a strong proliferative response starting at 3dpl, primarily within the ventricular zone of the injured hemisphere [ – ]. By selecting early time points such as 1 dpl, followed by 4 dpl and 7 dpl, we aimed to capture the full spectrum of the wound healing process, from the initial injury response to the proliferative phase.
Several genes were found to be dysregulated at different time points compared to the uninjured control. A substantial number of genes were differentially expressed at each time point (e.g., 986 upregulated at 1 dpl, peaking at 2551 upregulated genes at 4 dpl, and tapering to 1351 upregulated at 7 dpl), corresponding to distinct phases of regeneration: early injury, peak proliferation, and tissue remodeling respectively (Fig. A–C). The complete list of dysregulated genes at different time points is present in Additional file . The PCA plot effectively illustrates that gene expression profiles following injury at various time intervals (1dpl, 4dpl, 7dpl) are not only different from one another but also distinctly separated from control samples, indicating significant transcriptomic alterations and possible biological processes that vary by stage. Each category creates a well-defined cluster, suggesting strong experimental consistency and reproducibility within each group (Figure ). Volcano plots representing the log2 fold change and level of significance of the DEGs have also been shown in A-C). These plots highlight the extent of gene expression alterations at each time point, revealing distinct patterns of gene regulation at each phase of regeneration. To elucidate the biological relevance of the DEGs, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways at each time point. GSEA revealed distinct pathways associated with each stage of the regenerative response, including those involved in cellular proliferation, cell death, signaling pathways, metabolic changes, and neurogenesis. The list of all processes is present in Additional file . Additionally, pathway analysis highlighted key molecular signaling cascades, including MAPK, FoxO, PPAR, calcium signaling among others, that may play important roles in regulating the regenerative response during early stages of brain injury. 
In zebrafish, acute inflammation appears to be essential for facilitating recovery after injury, supporting neural progenitor proliferation, and promoting subsequent neurogenesis through the activation of injury-induced molecular programs [ , ]. Our study identifies various inflammatory response genes that were found to be dysregulated during regeneration at 1 dpl, 4 dpl and 7 dpl (Figure ). This was validated using qRT-PCR of selected genes (hmox1a, il6 and il1b) (Figure C-E). Several genes were upregulated upon injury-induced regeneration (Figure A). These represent the immediate activation of the immune response to injury and include typical pro-inflammatory mediators, interleukin-1 beta (il1b), associated with recruitment of leucocytes and regeneration response and interleukin-6 (il6), associated with neuroprotection and nerve regeneration [ – ]. Additionally, stress-response gene, hmox1a was also upregulated indicating protective responses to limit oxidative damage [ ]. Another gene upregulated was pparg, which is known to exert neuroprotective effects in mammals and enhances microglial activation which is necessary for initiation of regenerative response through acute inflammation [ ].
In contrast, several genes were downregulated during regeneration (Figure B). A nuclear transport receptor gene, nr4a1, which is known to inhibit inflammatory response was downregulated suggesting a microenvironment promoting activation of transient inflammation for successful regeneration [ ]. Similarly, park7 which is a stress sensor and acts as anti-oxidant that modulates oxidative stress when transiently downregulated, might promote regenerative pathways [ ]. Another gene cxcr4b, which regulates macrophage infiltration at the injury site, was also downregulated suggesting its role in regulating acute or transient inflammation during initial phase of regeneration. Taken together our data suggests that inflammation, during zebrafish brain regeneration is a tightly regulated and balanced process with both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory genes playing significant role, wherein after initiating the regenerative response, inflammation gets resolved quickly.

## Early response genes are enriched at 1 dpl
At 1 dpl, GSEA revealed enrichment of pathways related to the early response to injury, including cell cycle regulation, neurogenesis, gliogenesis, angiogenesis, and differentiation. Genes such as angptl4, e2f7, and vegfbb, known to promote angiogenesis, were upregulated, aligning with reports of rapid blood vessel formation following injury in zebrafish [ – ]. This suggests that angiogenesis begins as early as 1 dpl in brain regeneration. Simultaneously, genes like stat3, ncam3, and fosb, associated with synaptic signaling, gliogenesis, and axon development, were downregulated [ – ]. Additional pathways, such as FoxO signaling, ErbB signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation, were also enriched. Processes related to nitrogen metabolism, protein export, and cell death reflect the cellular response to injury and the initiation of repair mechanisms. Enrichment of processes related to wound healing, MAPK, PPAR, and insulin signaling pathways (Fig. D–E) highlighted the initiation of repair mechanisms and the restructuring of the CNS architecture.

## 4 dpl corresponds to the beginning of proliferative phase of neurogenesis
At 4 dpl, GSEA highlighted pathways associated with cell proliferation and metabolic activity, including VEGF signaling, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the TCA cycle. Enriched terms also included cell cycle regulation, gliogenesis, CNS development, and organ growth, reflecting the proliferative expansion of progenitor cells. Since proliferative activity dominated the transcriptome, a significant upregulation of genes involved in cell cycle progression was seen, such as bub1 (involved in spindle assembly checkpoint [ ]), sass6 (critical for centriole duplication [ ]), and stil (required for dopaminergic neural proliferation and regeneration [ ]). Other upregulated genes included spdl1 (associated with cell cycle regulation [ ]), rad54b (DNA damage response [ ]), and cenpf (promotes proliferation in carcinomas [ ]). Processes such as G1/S transition, endothelial cell proliferation, and synapse organization were prominently enriched (Fig. F). Notably, pathways such as MAPK, PPAR, FoxO, and ErbB signaling remained active, emphasizing their sustained importance in driving the proliferative response and neural repair (Fig. G). These findings highlight the transition from injury repair to the expansion of neural progenitor populations.

## 7 dpl reflects a transition to remodelling associated genes and pathways
By 7 dpl, GSEA indicated a shift toward pathways involved in tissue remodeling and differentiation. Notably, cxcl18b (inflammatory chemokine [ ]), c6 (complement component [ ]), and rig1/ddx58 (involved in innate immunity during cerebral ischemia [ ]) were enriched, indicating an active inflammatory response. Additionally, nr1d1, crucial for excitatory neuron migration and synaptic network formation, was significantly upregulated [ ]). Calcium signaling, a key regulator of neural proliferation and differentiation, was enriched alongside processes such as neuron projection morphogenesis and glial cell development (Fig. H–I). Enriched terms additionally included gliogenesis, CNS development, organ growth, and cell-cell signaling. The pentose phosphate pathway and MAPK signaling persisted, highlighting their roles in metabolic support and signaling during the later stages of regeneration. Processes related to cell cycle regulation and metabolism remained prominent, reflecting ongoing tissue remodeling and integration of new neurons into the existing circuitry.
Several pathways, including MAPK, PPAR, and insulin signaling, were enriched across all time points, suggesting their central roles in orchestrating the regenerative response. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death, was notably enriched throughout regeneration but with downregulation of ferroptosis-promoting genes like ncoa4 and tfr1, supporting its inhibition as a pro-neurogenic mechanism [ , ]. Enrichment of apoptotic processes at 4 dpl and ferroptosis/necroptosis pathways at 7 dpl highlighted the delicate balance of cell death and survival mechanisms during regeneration. These pathways likely balance cell death and survival to promote effective tissue repair.
To validate the transcriptomic data, we performed qPCR for selected genes. Downregulation of ncam3 and fosb, along with upregulation of rad54b and rig1, was consistent with RNA sequencing results (Fig. J), further confirming the reliability of our analyses. Hence, our results provide a comprehensive overview of the transcriptional dynamics underlying early zebrafish brain regeneration. The data reveal distinct molecular signatures at 1 dpl, 4 dpl, and 7 dpl, corresponding to key stages of the regenerative process. Early injury responses at 1 dpl are marked by pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and initial repair mechanisms, transitioning to robust proliferation at 4 dpl, and tissue remodeling at 7 dpl. Key signaling pathways, including MAPK, FoxO, PPAR, and ErbB, were enriched across multiple stages, highlighting their central roles in orchestrating the regenerative response.

## Weighted co-expression network construction
Building on previous timepoint-specific enrichment analyses, we performed WGCNA to identify co-expressed gene modules across 1, 4, and 7 dpl samples. Using a 0.4 merge threshold (Fig. A), the dynamic tree-cut method identified a total of 20 modules (Fig. B, Additional File ), with cyan (6,363 genes) and brown4 (150 genes) being largest and smallest respectively. Notably, the genes within each module exhibited high correlation with each other. The network heatmap of the top 1000 genes is shown in Additional Figure A. The number of genes in each module is illustrated in Additional Figure B. Module-trait correlation analysis revealed distinct temporal associations: greenyellow, cornflowerblue, lightskyblue4, blue1, and coral3 modules demonstrated a strong correlation with 1-day post-lesion (1 dpl). In contrast, the midnightblue, blueviolet, deeppink1, cyan, and coral4 modules were significantly correlated with 4-days post-lesion (4 dpl). Additionally, lightblue4, deeppink, honeydew1, blue4, and chocolate3 showed a strong correlation with 7-days post-lesion (7 dpl), as depicted in Additional Figure C. Enrichment analyses of the twenty modules (Additional File ) revealed stage-specific biological processes (Fig. C) and KEGG pathways (Fig. D). At 1 dpl, cornflowerblue and greenyellow modules showed enrichment for transcriptional regulation and inflammatory responses, while blue1 featured Wnt signaling and extracellular matrix organization. The 4 dpl-associated cyan module (largest module) coordinated diverse repair processes including DNA damage response, synaptic reorganization, and key signaling pathways (MAPK/mTOR). At 7 dpl, lightblue4 and honeydew1 modules exhibited combined immune (Toll-like receptor signaling) and neurogenic activities. KEGG analysis confirmed these patterns: cornflowerblue (1 dpl) showed metabolic reprogramming pathways, cyan (4 dpl) dominated by proliferation/survival signals, and 7 dpl modules enriched for neuro-immune crosstalk. These results demonstrate distinct molecular transitions during regeneration, consistent with previous findings. 

## Identification of key molecular players with high degree of connectivity across the different stages of regeneration
Regeneration is a highly coordinated process requiring the activation of interconnected molecular networks. Identifying central genes, or hub genes, within these networks provides critical insights into the regulatory mechanisms driving this process. Such hub genes often serve as key orchestrators, integrating signals from multiple pathways to mediate cellular responses like proliferation, migration, and tissue remodeling. To unravel these central players and their potential roles during zebrafish brain regeneration, we analyzed protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks across all time points. By identifying genes with high connectivity within these networks, we aimed to uncover key molecular nodes that underpin the regenerative process and may serve as targets for further functional studies. To achieve this, the DEGs enriched in 1 dpl, 4 dpl and 7 dpl respectively were converted into PPI networks using STRING. These networks were then used as an input in Cytoscape. Subsequently, hub genes in networks were detected by the degree algorithm in the CytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape. 
The hub genes at 1 dpl included plk1 (a key regulator of mitotic progression), aurka (involved in centrosome maturation and spindle assembly), cdc6 (essential for initiating DNA replication), aspm (associated with mitotic spindle function and neural progenitor proliferation), dlgap5 (linked to cell cycle regulation), ttk (a mitotic checkpoint kinase), bub1 (crucial for chromosome alignment and segregation), mcm4 (a component of the DNA replication licensing complex), and prc1b (implicated in cytokinesis).
At 4 dpl, the identified hub genes included cdca8 (involved in chromosomal passenger complex function during mitosis), plk1, exo1 (a DNA exonuclease involved in DNA repair and recombination), cdc20 (a key activator of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome), ttk, gapdhs (associated with glycolysis and metabolic regulation), aspm, bub1, and ndufab1a (a mitochondrial protein contributing to energy metabolism).
At 7 dpl, hub genes were aspm, nuf2 (a kinetochore-associated protein required for chromosome segregation), usp18 (a deubiquitinating enzyme implicated in immune regulation), aurka, fancm (involved in DNA repair through the Fanconi anemia pathway), plk4 (essential for centriole duplication), prc1b, mcm4, and dhx58 (a helicase involved in antiviral responses). These hub genes highlight their dynamic involvement in processes such as cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, immune response, and energy metabolism, as shown in (Additional Figure A-C).
The hub genes found to be common across all the time points were enriched in protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ER), protein export, metabolic pathways, cell cycle, phagosome, endocytosis, motor proteins and MAPK signaling as depicted in the chord plot (Additional Figure D). All these are pathways fundamental to cell proliferation, survival, immune response, and cellular signaling, underlining their importance in driving the regenerative process.
Proteins are the primary carriers of biological activity. To understand how proteins encoded by these DEGs interact, we combined DEGs from all the time points for PPI analysis, where strong interactions among these DEGs were observed. Furthermore, we also analyzed the functions and networks of these DEGs through Cytoscape plugin ClueGO and Cluepedia (Additional Figure ). The result suggested that these genes were closely linked in cell cycle, neuroactive ligand- receptor interaction, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, calcium signaling, PPAR signaling, MAPK signaling, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), apoptosis, phagosome, ferroptosis and few others. The identification of these pathways points to the crucial roles they play in cellular homeostasis and regeneration. For example, calcium signaling and PPAR signaling are vital for cellular responses to environmental cues and metabolic regulation, which are particularly relevant during tissue repair. Similarly, MAPK signaling and apoptosis are critical in regulating cell survival and proliferation, key processes for regeneration.
To dig further in the signaling pathways, gene expression patterns across the three time points (1 dpl, 4 dpl, and 7 dpl) were analyzed, providing insights into the dynamic regulatory processes underlying cellular regeneration (Fig. ). In the cell cycle pathway, distinct temporal patterns of gene expression were observed. On 1 dpl, genes such as mcm2, mcm4, e2f1, skp2, esco2, cdc6, cdc14aa, and cdt1 were highly expressed, reflecting their roles in initiating DNA replication and cell cycle progression. Specifically, e2f1 functions as a transcription factor, regulating genes required for S-phase entry, while mcm2 and mcm4 are critical for the formation of the minichromosome maintenance complex, enabling DNA replication. In contrast, 4 dpl saw the upregulation of genes such as aurkb, cdc20, dbf4, bub1bb, sgo1, and mtbp, which are involved in mitosis and spindle assembly, with aurkb promoting chromatid alignment and cdc20 activating the anaphase-promoting complex. Notably, mad2l2 and tfdp1b exhibited high expression only on 7 dpl, indicating their roles in the mitotic checkpoint and transcriptional regulation, respectively. Genes such as ttk, bub1, and plk1 were expressed on both 1 dpl and 4 dpl, highlighting their involvement in cell cycle progression and checkpoint control. Further, mcm4 and mcm5 showed expression on both 1 dpl and 7 dpl, linking their function in DNA replication initiation with the later stages of cell cycle progression. On 4 dpl and 7 dpl, ccna2 was expressed, linking it to the regulation of G1/S phase transition. Interestingly, knl1 and mcm6 exhibited consistent expression across all time points, emphasizing their stable roles in chromosome alignment and replication, while cdkn1d, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, remained low throughout the time course, suggesting limited involvement in cell cycle regulation during regeneration. These findings, when viewed in the context of the three distinct time points, i.e. 1-day post-lesion (1 dpl), 4-days post lesion (4 dpl), and 7-days post lesion (7 dpl), highlight the dynamic shifts in gene expression that correspond to different stages of regeneration. On 1 dpl, the high expression of genes involved in DNA replication and cell cycle initiation aligns with the immediate response to injury, where cellular repair mechanisms are rapidly activated. On 4 dpl, the upregulation of mitotic genes points to the early proliferation phase, where cells are transitioning from repair to active division. By 7 dpl, the expression of genes involved in the mitotic checkpoint and transcriptional regulation suggests a late proliferation phase, emphasizing the completion of cell division and the onset of tissue regeneration. These temporal expression patterns provide valuable insight into the progression of cellular regeneration and highlight the importance of precise temporal regulation in these processes. 
In MAPK signaling, most of the gene expression occurred on 4-days post lesion (dpl), with genes such as cacna1sb, rps6kal, rasgrp2, cacna2d4a, cacna1ab, cacna1fa, and erbb3b showing high expression. These genes are essential for cellular responses to extracellular stimuli, with rasgrp2 activating the RAS/MAPK pathway and cacna1sb and cacna1ab regulating calcium influx, which further activates MAPK signaling. On 1 dpl, fgf17 showed high expression, suggesting its role in early regenerative signaling, while cacng5a and flt3 exhibited low expression, which may indicate their limited involvement at this stage. On 7 dpl, fas was highly expressed, potentially linking it to cellular stress responses via MAPK activation, while hspa1b remained low, reflecting the downregulation of heat shock protein responses. Several genes, including nr4a1, hsp70.1, hsp70l, and hsp70.3, showed low expression across all time points, indicating their minimal role in the recovery phase of regeneration. Furthermore, il1b demonstrated high expression on both 1 dpl and 4 dpl, linking it to inflammation and cellular signaling during the regenerative process, while ret was low on 1 dpl and 7 dpl, which may suggest its limited role in these specific time points. These findings highlight the dynamic regulation of MAPK signaling, which is critical for coordinating cellular responses to injury, inflammation, and stress during the regenerative process.
In PPAR signaling, genes involved in lipid metabolism were highly expressed on 1-day post lesion (dpl), including cpt1b, me1, slc27a2b, and rxrga. These genes are critical for fatty acid transport and metabolism, with cpt1b playing a central role in fatty acid oxidation, and rxrga regulating gene expression in response to lipid signals. Additionally, angptl4 and fabp4a were upregulated on both 1 dpl and 7 dpl, indicating their involvement in lipid metabolism and energy regulation during later stages of regeneration. The gene pparg, which regulates adipogenesis and lipid homeostasis, was highly expressed on 1 dpl and 4 dpl, underlining its role in metabolic control during early and mid-regeneration. On 4 dpl, slc27a6 showed high expression, supporting lipid transport, while scd, involved in the synthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, was expressed on both 4 dpl and 7 dpl. Conversely, genes such as pck1, cyp27a1.2, cpt1cb, and acsl5 showed low expression on 4 dpl, potentially reflecting a shift in metabolic pathways during this phase. The relevance of the PPAR signaling pathway lies in its pivotal role in regulating lipid metabolism, which is crucial for energy balance during the regeneration process. As cellular energy demands increase during tissue repair, the upregulation of these genes suggests that lipid metabolism is a key aspect of the regeneration process.
Similar to the MAPK signaling pathway, the calcium signaling pathway also shows that many genes are involved on 4-days post lesion (dpl). This includes high levels of expression for genes such as cacna1sb, ptger1a, atp2a1, cacna1ab, cacna1fa, and erbb3b. These genes are involved in regulating calcium ion influx and cellular signaling, with ptger1a mediating calcium release through prostaglandin signaling, and atp2a1 responsible for calcium pumping in and out of the cell. In contrast, genes such as grpr, vegfaa, kdrl, fgf7, mast1, and ltb4r2a exhibited low expression on 4 dpl, indicating their reduced involvement in calcium signaling during this time. Genes such as camk1ga, tnnc1a, and calml4b, which are involved in calcium/calmodulin-dependent signaling, showed expression on both 4 dpl and 7 dpl, highlighting their contribution to calcium-dependent cellular processes in later stages. Additionally, trdn and mcoln3a exhibited high expression, potentially playing a role in regulating calcium signaling and muscle contraction. On the other hand, phkg1a and cxcr4b displayed low expression on both 1 dpl and 4 dpl, suggesting minimal involvement in calcium signaling during the early and mid-regeneration stages. The gene ret, similar to its pattern in MAPK signaling, showed low expression on both 1 dpl and 7 dpl, further emphasizing its limited role. Furthermore, fgf17, involved in signaling pathways during neuronal regeneration, exhibited high expression on 1 dpl, while cxcr4a remained low. The genes drd5a and slc25a4 showed low expression only on 7 dpl, suggesting their role in later stages of regeneration. Lastly, agtr1b and mst1ra showed high and low expression, respectively, across all three time points, suggesting their regulation across the entire regenerative process. The calcium signaling pathway is particularly relevant here as calcium ions play a critical role in various cellular processes, including signal transduction, muscle contraction, and enzyme activation, all of which are crucial during cellular regeneration and tissue repair.
The role of the aforementioned genes in their respective biological and signaling pathways was visualized in KEGG pathways and is present in Additional Figure -A&B, providing a comprehensive view of their interconnectivity and functional relevance in cellular regeneration. These findings demonstrate a dynamic shift in gene expression over time, providing critical insights into the cellular mechanisms that drive regeneration following injury.

## Activation dynamics of proliferative genes across different time points orchestrate the regeneration process
So far, we presented a global view of the regenerative process using GSEA-based GO and KEGG pathway analyses, highlighting the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions involved at each stage of zebrafish brain regeneration. While these analyses provided a broad understanding of the enriched pathways and processes, they do not reveal the specific gene-level dynamics that orchestrate these changes. To address this gap and uncover the precise molecular mechanisms driving regeneration, we focused on identifying the activation patterns of key proliferative and regeneration-associated genes across different time points. This detailed analysis is essential for understanding how transcriptional regulation translates into the cellular activities required for neurogenesis, axonal growth, and neuronal differentiation. Our gene-level investigation revealed that biological processes such as glial cell development, synaptic signaling, synapse organization, neuron projection development, and neuron projection morphogenesis were consistently enriched at all time points (Fig. A). These findings emphasize the intricate and continuous remodeling of the nervous system during regeneration. Furthermore, cellular components such as neuron projections, somatodendritic compartments, and neuronal cell bodies, alongside molecular functions like axon guidance receptor activity and neuropeptide hormone activity, highlight the complexity of processes enabling regeneration. Regeneration-specific processes such as tissue regeneration and developmental growth were evident throughout, but certain processes, such as dendrite morphogenesis, were uniquely enriched during the differentiation stage. Insulin-like growth factors (IGF-I and IGF-II), which are known to regulate brain growth, development, and neuronal density [ , ], were particularly prominent during this phase. 
The activation of cell cycle-related terms across all stages was expected, given the need for rapid proliferation in response to injury. To investigate further, we analyzed specific genes involved in cell cycle regulation and their temporal expression patterns (Fig. B). Aurora kinase A (aurka) and Aurora kinase B (aurkb), critical for centrosome maturation, spindle formation, and axonal outgrowth, showed significant upregulation at 4 dpl [ – ]. This aligns with the peak proliferative phase of brain regeneration. Similarly, rgcc, which balances neural stem cell renewal and differentiation [ ], exhibited high expression at 7 dpl, reflecting its role in promoting neuronal differentiation. The gene wdr76, a tumor suppressor known for degrading RAS and inhibiting cancer progression [ , ], peaked at 4 dpl and declined by 7 dpl, suggesting its involvement in regulating proliferation during regeneration. Other key cell cycle regulators, such as dsn1, chaf1a, stil, sass6, spdl1, and wdhd1, were enriched at 4 dpl, indicative of their role in supporting the rapid cell division required during the proliferative phase [ , , – ]). The MCM (Minichromosome Maintenance) proteins, vital for DNA replication during the cell cycle [ ], showed sustained expression at all time points, underscoring their role in facilitating continuous cell proliferation necessary for tissue repair and regrowth. The increased presence of mcm2, mcm4, and mcm6 at all the stages was essential for the rapid cell proliferation necessary for tissue repair and regrowth, underscoring their pivotal role in the regenerative processes of brain.
By linking these gene-specific findings to the broader pathways described earlier, our data provides critical insights into the molecular underpinnings of zebrafish brain regeneration. The activation of proliferative genes at specific time points reveals the tightly coordinated regulation required to drive neurogenesis, axonal growth, and differentiation. This granular analysis complements the broader pathway insights and shows the regulatory events driving the regenerative process.

## p38α MAPK is upregulated during brain regeneration in zebrafish
To further dissect the molecular mechanisms driving brain regeneration in zebrafish, we conducted a comparative pathway analysis across all three regeneration stages (1 dpl, 4 dpl, and 7 dpl). The analysis revealed distinct pathway enrichment patterns specific to each time point, highlighting the dynamic nature of the regenerative process. At 1 dpl, pathways such as PPAR signaling and adipocytokine signaling were predominantly enriched, reflecting early metabolic and inflammatory responses to injury. In contrast, pathways like VEGF signaling, polycomb repressive complex regulation, mRNA surveillance, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling were specifically enriched at 4 dpl, corresponding to the proliferative phase where cellular reorganization and gene regulation are critical. By 7 dpl, pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and necroptosis were uniquely observed, indicative of tissue remodeling and regulated cell death during the differentiation phase.
Interestingly, certain pathways were enriched across two regeneration stages or consistently present at all three time points, reflecting their broader relevance to brain regeneration. For instance, FoxO signaling and ErbB signaling were shared between 1 dpl and 4 dpl, aligning with their roles in early cellular stress responses and proliferation. Similarly, pathways like NOD-like receptor signaling, mitophagy, autophagy, calcium signaling, and apelin signaling were enriched during the transition from 4 dpl to 7 dpl, highlighting their importance in cellular maintenance, repair, and differentiation during later stages of regeneration.
Remarkably, some pathways, including MAPK signaling, insulin signaling, ferroptosis, oxidative phosphorylation, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, were consistently enriched across all three time points (Fig. C). Among these, the MAPK pathway emerged as a particularly robust signaling cascade, underscoring its indispensable role in orchestrating the cellular and molecular responses during zebrafish brain regeneration. These findings not only reinforce the temporal complexity of the regenerative process but also highlight the significance of the MAPK pathway as a central regulator across all stages of brain regeneration.
Building on the above findings, we examined the specific role of the extracellular regulated kinase/microtubule-associated protein kinase (ERK/MAPK) signaling pathway during zebrafish brain regeneration. This pathway is essential for transmitting signals that induce changes in metabolic pathways and gene expression, ultimately affecting cellular behaviours such as proliferation, differentiation, and survival. ERK/MAPK signaling has been shown to be critical for nervous system development, guiding neural progenitor cells derived from the embryonic mesoderm through various stages of neurodevelopment [ ].
In our analysis, the MAPK pathway was consistently enriched as both a molecular process and a KEGG pathway (Fig. A& 6C) across all three stages of regeneration, emphasizing its overarching role in orchestrating restorative neurogenesis. A closer inspection of the MAPK cascade revealed dynamic regulation of several key genes. Upregulated genes included rps6kal, cacna1ab, erbb3b, rasgrp2, ins, fgf17, cacna2d4a, fas, and ilb, each of which plays a crucial role in cellular communication and regeneration. For instance, RPS6KAL (ribosomal protein S6 kinase alpha-like) is involved in mRNA translation and cell growth [ ], while CACNA1B (calcium voltage-gated channel subunit alpha1 B) and CACNA2D4A (calcium channel subunit alpha2delta) are essential for calcium signaling, which is vital for neurogenesis and synaptic activity during repair [ , ]. ERBB3B (a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor family) and RASGRP2 (RAS guanyl-releasing protein 2) facilitate vascular development and growth factor signaling, promoting cellular proliferation and differentiation [ – ]. The insulin gene ins and fibroblast growth factor fgf17 contribute to neuronal survival and growth and have been shown to be quintessential for brain regeneration in zebrafish [ , , ], while FAS and IL-1β (ilb) are involved in immune responses and inflammation [ , ], processes that are critical during the early wound-healing stage.
Conversely, downregulated genes such as dusp1, dusp2, nr4a1, vegfaa, kdr, kdrl, hsp70, ret, and flt3 highlight a shift in signaling priorities across the stages. DUSP1 and DUSP2 (dual-specificity phosphatases) act as negative regulators of MAPK signaling by dephosphorylating active MAPKs [ , ], suggesting a tightly regulated balance of pathway activity during regeneration. The nuclear receptor gene nr4a1, which is involved in stress responses and apoptosis [ ], showed decreased expression, reflecting the need to suppress apoptotic signals in favor of regenerative growth. VEGFAA (vascular endothelial growth factor A) and its receptors KDR and KDRL (kinase insert domain receptor-like) play key roles in angiogenesis, which is vital during the early stages of regeneration but decreases as vascular remodeling stabilizes [ , ]. Similarly, hsp70, known for its role in stress response and protein stabilization [ ], showed declining expression, indicating reduced cellular stress as regeneration progresses. Downregulation of genes such as ret and flt3, both involved in neuronal development and signaling [ , ], suggests a fine-tuning of proliferative and differentiation signals as the brain transitions through the phases of regeneration. These dynamic changes visualized in the heatmap (Fig. D) show the regulated orchestration of the MAPK cascade facilitating a temporal and spatial balance of cellular processes essential for successful brain regeneration in zebrafish [ – ]. Focusing further, we examined the role of MAPK14a (p38α MAPK), a prominent isozyme within the p38 MAPK family known to influence proliferation, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis of neural stem cells. Our results revealed significant phosphorylation of MAPK14a at 1 dpl and 4 dpl, aligning with the early wound-healing and proliferative stages of brain regeneration. Interestingly, its activity returned to baseline levels by 14 dpl (Fig. E&G). This dynamic pattern highlights MAPK14a’s critical role in facilitating cellular responses necessary for the early stages of brain regeneration in zebrafish. Further, we observed that the downstream substrates of p38α MAPK were also upregulated during brain regeneration [ ]. An increase in the levels of phosphorylated MK2, also known as MAPKAPK2 (MAP kinase-activated protein kinase 2) was observed at 1 dpl, 4 dpl and 7 dpl compared to the uninjured control brain and the phosphorylation decreased at 14 dpl (Fig. F&H). Additionally, phosphorylated MK2 is known to activate Hsp27 (Heat shock protein 27) by phosphorylating it [ ]. Our results show a significant increase in the levels of phosphorylated Hsp27 at 1 dpl and 4 dpl which then decreases at 7 dpl although still more than the control (Fig. F&J). Transcription factors are another group of substrates that are activated by p38α MAPK, one of them being c-Jun, which is a key component of the AP-1 (activator protein-1) complex known to regulate gene expression, proliferation, and cell survival [ , ]. Our immunoblot results show that c-Jun is upregulated at 1 dpl, followed by a decrease at 4 dpl and then again increased at 7 dpl compared to the uninjured non-regenerating brain (Fig. F&I). This result is in sync with the analysis of gene expression levels of c-Jun in our transcriptomic data. These results further emphasize a crucial role of p38α MAPK pathway during the early wound healing and proliferative stage of brain regeneration in zebrafish.

## Inhibition of MAPK14a leads to an impairment in progenitor response
Having established that MAPK14a (p38α MAPK) is activated during brain regeneration and potentially plays a significant role in this process, we next sought to confirm its functional involvement by inhibiting MAPK14a activity. To this end, we used BIRB796, an inhibitor of p38 MAPK, to assess the spatiotemporal effects of this inhibition localized to the site of injury in the telencephalon (Fig. A). The efficacy of BIRB796 in suppressing MAPK14a activity was validated through immunoblotting, which showed a dose-dependent reduction in the phosphorylation levels of p38α MAPK (Fig. B–C). Further, BIRB796-mediated p38α MAPK inhibition also led to a decrease in the levels of downstream substrates such as phosphorylated MK2, phosphorylated Hsp27 and c-Jun in the regenerating brain at 4 dpl (Fig. D–H). 
To investigate the consequences of MAPK14a inhibition on cell proliferation, we performed immunofluorescence staining for the proliferative marker PCNA. A significant reduction in PCNA+ cells was observed in BIRB796-treated zebrafish compared to the DMSO control, indicating diminished proliferation in the injured telencephalon (compare Fig. I panel A2 to A2’ & 7J). Furthermore, glial progenitor cell activation appeared to be impaired, as evidenced by reduced numbers of GFAP+ and S100B+ cells in the injured hemisphere of MAPK14a-inhibited fish (compare Fig. I panel C2 to C2’; D2 to D2’ & 7 L-M). These findings suggest that MAPK14a is essential for the activation and proliferation of glial progenitor cells, and its inhibition disrupts this response, resulting in an incomplete regenerative process.
Consistent with this, neuronal recovery was also compromised in the absence of MAPK14a activity. Immunostaining for the neuronal marker HuC/D revealed a reduced neuronal population in the injured hemisphere upon MAPK14a inhibition (compare Fig. I panel B2 to B2’& 7K). This suggests that the regenerative capacity of the zebrafish brain is significantly impaired when MAPK14a is inhibited, resulting in an inability to mount an effective regenerative response.
We also investigated the effects of MAPK14a inhibition on the oligodendrocyte progenitor population by immunostaining for Olig2, a marker of oligodendrocyte precursor cells. We observed a reduction in the number of Olig2+ progenitor cells in the injured hemisphere of the MAPK14a-inhibited zebrafish (Additional Figure A). This suggests that MAPK14a plays a role in maintaining the oligodendrocyte progenitor pool, which is critical for the regeneration of myelinating cells in the injured brain.
To further assess the potential effects of MAPK14a inhibition on cell survival, we performed TUNEL staining, which detects apoptotic cell death. Slightly increased cell death was specifically observed in the in the posterior zone of the dorsal telencephalic area (Dp), equivalent to the SGZ of the mammalian hippocampus, in the injured hemisphere (Additional Figure B). This suggests that the inhibition of MAPK14a may lead to increased progenitor cell death in specific regions, further impairing the regenerative process.
Further, to understand the broader role of p38α MAPK pathway during brain regeneration, we investigated its regulatory effects on other signaling pathways that play a role in zebrafish brain regeneration. Wnt/β-catenin pathway is known to play a crucial role in zebrafish brain during the early wound healing phase of regeneration [ ]. Our immunoblotting results also showed an upregulation of β-catenin during brain regeneration (Additional Figure A). Our results show that BIRB796-mediated inhibition of p38α MAPK activity also reduced β-catenin levels (Additional Figure B), consistent with prior reports that p38 MAPK is known to regulate the levels of β-catenin by modulating GSK3β levels [ ]. Also, PI3K/AKT pathway has been shown to be crucial for zebrafish brain development and neuronal survival [ ]. Our results show that levels of phosphorylated AKT increase during zebrafish brain regeneration while total AKT levels do not change (Additional Figure C). Interestingly, upon p38α MAPK activity inhibition, the phosphorylated AKT levels were reduced in regenerating brain compared to the DMSO treated regenerating brain at 4 dpl (Additional Figure D), suggesting that MAPK14a may act upstream or in concert with PI3K/AKT to promote neurogenesis. These findings reveal a broader signaling interplay, where MAPK14a influences multiple regenerative pathways including Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT.
In conclusion, these results underscore the importance of MAPK14a in the activation and proliferation of neural progenitor cells during brain regeneration. The inhibition of MAPK14a disrupts the regenerative response by reducing glial progenitor proliferation, diminishing neuronal recovery, and reducing the oligodendrocyte progenitor population. These findings highlight the indispensable role of MAPK14a in zebrafish brain regeneration. Future studies will aim to delineate the precise transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms governed by MAPK14a, which may reveal therapeutic targets for enhancing brain repair following injury.

## Discussion
The zebrafish brain regeneration model provides an invaluable opportunity to explore the molecular and cellular processes that enable effective repair and recovery after injury. While the zebrafish telencephalon has been extensively studied for its neurogenic niches, comparative transcriptomic studies focusing on the early stages of regeneration are limited. Our study contributes to this knowledge gap by offering a comprehensive comparative transcriptomic analysis of the early stages of telencephalon regeneration covering both the wound-healing and early and late proliferative phases. By profiling the entire zebrafish brain, we provide a holistic view of the transcriptional changes occurring in response to injury, enabling the identification of injury-specific systemic molecular adaptations that occur throughout the brain. This approach not only highlights critical pathways but also uncovers core regeneration genes that govern this complex process.
A variety of injury paradigms have been established to induce acute damage in different regions of the adult zebrafish central nervous system (CNS), with a particular focus on the telencephalon, which has been extensively studied in the context of regeneration. In the present study, we employed a stab wound injury that targets the medial region of the telencephalon. This type of injury damages both the dorsal part of the ventricular zone (VZ), which contains ependymoglial cells with stem cell properties, and the brain parenchyma, which houses postmitotic neurons and glial progenitors. This injury model provides valuable insight into the regenerative response of progenitor cells in the adult zebrafish telencephalon, and how they contribute to recovery after injury.
Our study examines the transcriptome of regenerating zebrafish brains at three critical stages to capture the dynamic cellular and molecular events underlying regeneration. The first time point, 1 day post-lesion (1 dpl), corresponds to the early wound-healing phase, characterized by rapid inflammatory responses, glial activation, and clearance of cellular debris. The second time point, 4 days post-lesion (4 dpl), marks the initiation of proliferation, with activation of neural stem/progenitor cells and early cell cycle gene expression. By 7 days post-lesion (7 dpl), the regenerative response reaches its peak, with maximal proliferative activity, differentiation of progenitors, and tissue remodeling. The selection of these time points allows us to comprehensively capture the transition from injury response to active regeneration, providing a high-resolution view of the molecular events driving repair. The novelty of this work lies in its expansive coverage of the proliferative phase, encompassing both its initiation and the peak stages.
Among the three time points analyzed, 4 dpl exhibited the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). This peak was followed by 7 dpl and then 1 dpl, highlighting the temporal orchestration of transcriptional changes during regeneration. This temporal pattern of gene expression highlights the coordinated progression of regenerative events across different stages. One of the key insights from this study is the identification of critical biological pathways and genes involved in injury-specific processes. At 1 dpl, we observed the activation of inflammation-related pathways such as PPAR and NOD-like receptor signaling, which are crucial for resolving the initial injury response. The early stages also see an enrichment in insulin and calcium signaling pathways, both of which play roles in cellular stress responses and tissue repair. The high expression of genes involved in DNA replication and cell cycle initiation at this time point, such as mcm2, mcm4, and e2f1, aligns with the necessity for initiating the regenerative process by ensuring the rapid proliferation of progenitor cells. By 4 dpl, pathways associated with cell cycle regulation, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis became prominent, reflecting the onset of cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration. The upregulation of mitotic genes, such as aurkb, cdc20, and dbf4, suggests a critical switch towards active cell division and the formation of new tissue structures. This finding is further supported by the identification of hub genes like plk1, which play essential roles in maintaining cell cycle progression and genomic stability during this phase. By 7 dpl, as proliferation peaks, genes related to differentiation, such as mad2l2 and tfdp1b, marks the shift towards tissue remodeling and maturation. This phase also saw the enrichment of differentiation-related pathways like ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and ferroptosis, indicating a transition from cellular proliferation to the functional integration of newly generated cells into the tissue.
The specific genes and pathways activated at each time point provide critical insights into how cellular responses are coordinated across the early, mid, and late stages of regeneration. These findings show the temporal orchestration of key regenerative pathways, with each phase characterized by distinct yet interconnected signaling mechanisms. The progression from inflammation and DNA replication at 1 dpl, to proliferation and tissue formation at 4 dpl, and finally to differentiation and maturation at 7 dpl, point to the complexity and precision of the regenerative response in zebrafish brain tissue. The interconnectedness of these pathways, such as MAPK, insulin signaling, and calcium signaling reflects the complexity of the regenerative response, where multiple signaling networks work in concert to regulate progenitor activation, proliferation, and survival. The simultaneous presence of multiple signals can lead to synergistic effects, promoting cell proliferation or neurogenesis depending on the stage of the regenerative process. Moreover, many of the pathways involved in regeneration appear to be repurposed from developmental processes, highlighting how the same signaling networks that pattern the brain during development are re-engaged to facilitate repair and recovery after injury.
Our findings strongly implicate the MAPK signaling pathway, particularly MAPK14a (p38α MAPK), as a central regulator of the regenerative process. The activity of MAPK14a was markedly elevated during the early injury response, progressively returning to basal levels as proliferation peaked at 7 dpl and differentiation ensued by 14 dpl. This aligns with the established role of p38 MAPKs, which are activated in response to cellular stress and inflammatory signals, key events following traumatic brain injury [ , ]. Based on our transcriptomic analysis findings, we conducted further analysis of the transcriptomic data from the traumatic brain injury mouse model in the hippocampus at 3 days post-injury (3 dpi) [ ]. The findings revealed that MAPK emerged as significantly enriched in both GO biological Processes and the KEGG pathway, as illustrated in Additional Figure S (Additional File ). This observation is particularly noteworthy, as our study centers around the MAPK signaling cascade, reinforcing its potential role as a key regulatory pathway in the context of traumatic brain injury. Recent research indicates that p38α promotes the proliferation and migration of adult neural progenitor cells (NPCs) within mammalian neurogenic regions such as the SVZ and SGZ, essential for brain regeneration [ , ]. Importantly, the forced expression of p38α has been shown to restore NPC proliferation that declines with aging, partially by influencing Wnt signaling pathways. Furthermore, raising intracellular levels of p38α improves NPC migration without impacting their survival or differentiation, indicating a possible strategy to enhance the recruitment of NPCs to sites of injury [ ]. These insights, combined with our findings from zebrafish models, emphasize MAPK14 as a valuable therapeutic target for boosting the body’s natural repair processes in cases of brain injury.
Supporting this view, mammalian studies have also linked the p38 MAPK axis to improved neuroprotection and repair. For instance, activation of the p38MAPK-PGC-1α-PPARγ pathway promotes M2-type microglial polarization and neurotrophic factor release, aiding recovery after traumatic brain injury [ ]. Conversely, controlled inhibition of p38 activity has been reported to limit excessive neuroinflammation and reduce tissue damage in rodent injury models [ , ]. Together, these findings highlight the cell- and context-dependent roles of MAPK14, suggesting that targeted modulation, rather than broad inhibition, may be key to harnessing its regenerative potential across species.
Our findings firmly establish MAPK14a (p38α MAPK) as a central regulator of zebrafish brain regeneration, operating at multiple levels of the regenerative response. Beyond confirming its activation post-injury, functional inhibition of MAPK14a via BIRB796 revealed a profound impairment of regenerative capacity, marked by diminished proliferation of glial and neural progenitor populations. The observed reduction in PCNA+, GFAP+, S100B+, and Olig2+ cells underscores MAPK14a’s role in supporting both neurogenesis and gliogenesis, likely by maintaining progenitor cell competence and survival. The concurrent loss of HuC/D+ neurons in the injured hemisphere upon MAPK14a inhibition suggests that MAPK14a activity is not only necessary for progenitor expansion but also for successful neuronal differentiation and integration. Furthermore, our data highlight a broader regulatory function for MAPK14a through its influence on key regenerative signaling pathways. The downregulation of β-catenin and phosphorylated AKT upon MAPK14a inhibition suggests that MAPK14a may function upstream of Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT signaling, either directly or via modulatory intermediates such as GSK3β. This positions MAPK14a as a nodal regulator coordinating multiple pro-regenerative pathways, thus ensuring effective wound healing and tissue restoration. These findings are consistent with prior studies in mammals, where MAPK14a has been shown to regulate neural precursor cell proliferation and adult neurogenesis [ ]. In zebrafish, MAPK14a appears to not only control the activation and proliferation of neural stem cells but also contribute to the survival of progenitor cells, ensuring the integrity of the neurogenic niche during regeneration.
In addition to MAPK signaling, our transcriptomic analyses revealed the enrichment of PPAR and FoxO pathways at distinct regenerative stages. While the precise mechanistic interplay between these cascades remains to be clarified, their temporal co-activation alongside MAPK suggests potential crosstalk in coordinating metabolic regulation, stress responses, and progenitor proliferation. We speculate that these pathways may act in parallel yet convergent manners to fine-tune regenerative outcomes, an area that warrants future dedicated investigation.
This study offers significant insights, but it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations. First, as with all mechanical lesion models, the stab wound injury involves a degree of physical manipulation, which may introduce minor differences across individual animals. However, the protocol is well-established and consistently yields reproducible regenerative outcomes [ , , ]. Second, while BIRB796 was used to inhibit MAPK14a activity, this pharmacological agent targets all p38 MAPK isoforms (α/β/γ). Thus, potential contributions from other isoforms cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the results. Future studies could employ isoform-specific inhibitors or utilize genetic approaches such as CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockouts or morpholino-mediated knockdowns of MAPK14a to confirm its specific role in regulating neural regeneration. Finally, although regenerative outcomes differ between zebrafish and mammals, the zebrafish model remains a powerful platform to identify conserved pathways and principles that could guide approaches to improve repair in the mammalian brain. However, given the evolutionary conservation of these pathways, future investigations are required to delineate the precise molecular mechanisms by which MAPK14a mediates these effects and to identify its downstream effectors in the context of zebrafish brain regeneration. Interestingly, while the activation of MAPK14a aligns with established roles in regulating neurogenesis and progenitor cell proliferation, the identification of other pathways, such as GnRH, NOD-like receptor, ErbB, and FoxO signaling, provides novel insights into previously uncharacterized mechanisms that may contribute to brain regeneration. The activation of these pathways, particularly during the proliferative phase, opens new avenues for exploration and suggests a more complex interplay between different signaling cascades than previously understood. Future research utilizing transgenic models to manipulate MAPK14a and its downstream effectors will provide deeper mechanistic insights into how these pathways govern regeneration.

## Conclusions
Our study emphasizes the complex interplay between localized injury-induced signals and broader, systemic brain-wide adaptations in zebrafish brain regeneration. The temporal and spatial regulation of various signaling pathways provides new insights into the mechanisms driving the restorative neurogenesis underlying brain regeneration. By adopting a whole-brain transcriptomic approach, we have highlighted the importance of considering the overall brain response to injury, rather than focusing solely on neurogenic niches. Future research exploring the relationship between regional and global brain responses could uncover novel therapeutic targets, advancing our understanding of restorative neurogenesis and potentially guiding strategies for enhancing recovery after brain injury.

## Methods

## Ethics statement
The animal studies were conducted in compliance with the guidelines and established protocols of the Institute Animal Ethical Committee of IISER Mohali (IISERM/SAFE/PRT/2024/031). All the procedures were performed under anaesthesia and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

## Zebrafish maintenance and induction of stab wound injury
Wildtype zebrafish were maintained at a temperature of 25–28 °C and physiological pH (7.0–7.5), with a 14 hr photoperiod and 10 hr dark cycle. The fish used in the experiments were 6–8 months old.

## Telencephalic stab-wound injury
For induction of stab wound injury, the fishes were first anaesthetized using Tricaine (MS222, Sigma Aldrich) to induce deep sleep, followed by insertion of a 30G needle perpendicularly to the skull under a stereo microscope as previously described [ , – ]. The needle was positioned to injure the right hemisphere of the telencephalon, with the needle being no more than 2 mm inside the skull. Thereafter, the fishes were released back into the system water for recovery. The fish were sacrificed at different timepoints, and the brains were harvested for further studies.

## Lysate preparation and immunoblotting
For immunoblotting, harvested zebrafish brains were lysed in commercial RIPA buffer (G-Biosciences, Cat #786–489) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat #PPC1010) and 1 mM DTT. The protein lysates were quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat #23225) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and equal amounts of protein were mixed with 4X Laemmli buffer, followed by boiling at 95 °C for 5 minutes. The samples were resolved on an 8–12% SDS-PAGE gel at 90 V for 120 minutes.
Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA dissolved in 1X TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 hour at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Primary antibody incubations were performed overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing with TBST. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. The blots were developed using the Thermo Scientific SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Cat #34580), and signal detection was performed on a GE LAS500 chemiluminescent imager.

## Primary antibodies used
Rabbit monoclonal Phospho p38 MAPK (1:1000; 4511S; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit polyclonal total p38 MAPK (1:1000; 9212S; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit monoclonal Phospho HSP27 (1:1000; 9709S; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse monoclonal Total HSP27 (1:500; CPTC-HSPB1-2; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), rabbit monoclonal Phospho MK2 (1:1000; 3007S; Cell Signaling Technology), Total MK2 (1:1000; EB11929; Everest Biotech), rabbit monoclonal c-Jun (1:1000; 9165S; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse monoclonal β-catenin (1:1000; 610153; BD Biosciences), Phospho AKT (1:1000; 4058S; Cell Signaling Technology), Total AKT (1:1000; 2920S; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse monoclonal β-actin (1:1000; sc47778; Santa Cruz).

## Histological assessment

## BrdU (5-bromo-2’−deoxyuridine) labelling
To label proliferating cells, zebrafish were immersed in 5 mM BrdU solution for 4 hrs prior to sacrifice at the desired time points. After performing the telencephalic stab wound injury and BrdU treatment, zebrafish were euthanized at the indicated time points following ethical guidelines.

## Tissue fixation, paraffin block preparation and sectioning
The brains were carefully harvested, washed thoroughly in 1X PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C. Fixed tissues were subjected to a series of graded ethanol washes (80, 95, and 100%) to dehydrate the samples, followed by xylene washes to clear the tissue. The brains were then embedded in paraffin wax, and 5 μm sections were obtained using a rotary microtome (Medimeas). The sections were mounted on glass slides and dried overnight at room temperature to ensure proper adhesion.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin staining
For histological assessment, slides were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100, 95, 80, and 70%) and finally in distilled water. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin for nuclear visualization, followed by counterstaining with Eosin to highlight the cytoplasmic architecture. After staining, the slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol washes, cleared with xylene, and mounted using DPX mounting medium. The prepared slides were examined under a brightfield microscope to assess histological changes and cellular architecture at different time points post-injury.

## Immunofluorescence staining
The slides were first dewaxed using brief incubation at 60 °C followed by overnight xylene treatment to remove paraffin. After dewaxing, the slides were rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100, 95, 80, and 70%) and then washed thoroughly in 1X PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the slides in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 minutes, followed by cooling to room temperature. The slides were then blocked using 1X normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Primary antibody incubations were carried out overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The slides were subsequently washed in 1X PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies and incubated with fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies at room temperature for an hour in the dark.

## Counterstaining with DAPI and imaging
To visualize nuclei, the slides were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 minutes and washed briefly with PBS. Finally, the slides were mounted using Mowiol mounting medium to preserve fluorescence and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope at 63X magnification. High-resolution images were acquired and analyzed for detailed cellular and subcellular localization of the targets of interest.

## Primary antibodies used
Mouse monoclonal Huc/D (1:300; A21271; Invitrogen), mouse monoclonal BrdU (1:50; DSHB-S1-862; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), mouse monoclonal PCNA (1:150; sc25280; Santa Cruz), rabbit polyclonal S100β (1;200; ab41548; Abcam), rabbit polyclonal Olig2 (1:150; AB9610; Millipore), mouse monoclonal GFAP (1:500; ab154474; Abcam).

## RNA isolation
Freshly harvested zebrafish brains were immediately washed in ice-cold 1X PBS to remove debris and contaminants, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further processing. For each timepoint, five brains were pooled together to form one biological replicate, with three such biological replicates collected per timepoint. This pooling strategy minimizes variability and ensures sufficient RNA yield, while three replicates provide statistical robustness. Similar designs have been widely used in zebrafish transcriptomic studies [ – ].
RNA extraction was performed using the QIAGEN RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Cat #74804) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, brain tissues were homogenized in QIAzol Lysis Reagent using a mechanical homogenizer. Following homogenization, chloroform was added to the lysate, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 minutes at 4 °C to separate the phases. The upper aqueous phase, containing RNA, was carefully transferred to a fresh tube. Ethanol was then added to the aqueous phase to precipitate the RNA, which was subsequently bound to the spin column provided in the kit. To ensure DNA-free RNA, on-column DNase treatment was performed using the RNase-Free DNase Set (Cat #79254). The RNA was washed using the kit’s wash buffers to remove impurities and eluted in RNase-free water. The quality and quantity of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop UV spectrophotometer. Approximately 1–2 μg of high-quality RNA per biological replicate was sent to MedGenome Labs, Bangalore for RNA sequencing.

## RNA sample QC, library construction and RNA sequencing
The samples (n = 3; pooled 5 brains per biological replicate per time point) quantification was performed using Qubit RNA BR Assay (Invitrogen, Cat# Q10211). The purity and integrity were assessed via QIAxpert and Agilent TapeStation using RNA ScreenTapes (Agilent, Cat# 5067–5576). Samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 8 were taken forward for RNA sequencing. RNA samples were diluted to ensure that all samples have 100ng RNA. rRNA was depleted using QIAGEN QIAseq FastSelect −rRNA Fish Kit (Cat 333252) by following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (Cat. E7760L) using the manufacturer’s protocol. NEBNext® Multiplex Oligos for Illumina® (96 Unique Dual Index Primer Pairs Set 3) were used for multiplexing. A paired-end, strand-specific sequencing platform was used on an Illumina Novaseq6000 (Illumina, CA, United States) with the read length 150 bp.

## Transcriptomic data analysis
Raw data were cleaned using Trim Galore (v0.6.4) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) and were checked for quality using FastQC (v0.11.8) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). [ ]. STAR aligner (v2.7.9a) was deployed to align the reads to the zebrafish reference genome GRCz11 (danRer11) once they had passed the quality control. After mapping, transcripts were counted with the featureCounts (v2.0.3) tool. Normalization of the read counts and differential expression analysis were performed by using DESeq2 package (v1.42.0) of Bioconductor package in R. Wald test was performed using DESeq2 for different timepoints with respect to uninjured control to find the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with log fold change cutoff set to ± 1 and padjust-value < 0.05. The package clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) of R BioConductor was used for Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The enriched gene ontologies and KEGG pathways were analyzed across all three comparisons for different time points. To conceptualize the protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks of the statistically significant DEGs across all time points, the PPI data from STRING (Version 12) was imported into the Cytoscape program (v3.9.1). Significant nodes and hubs within the PPI network were identified using the CytoHubba plugin (Version 0.1). Additionally, ClueGO (v2.5.10) and CluePedia (v1.5.10) were applied to the DEGs from all-time points to construct networks and perform functional enrichment analysis. ClueGO integrates KEGG pathways and GO terms to generate an organized, pathway-centric network based on the DEG dataset.

## Weighted gene co-expression network analysis
A scale-free co-expression network was constructed using the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) package in R [ ]. WGCNA was applied to a filtered and transformed expression matrix derived from the zebrafish brain regeneration dataset. The raw counts were transformed using the variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) from the R package DESeq2 [ ]. The genes with missing values and those that exhibited zero variance across samples were filtered out for subsequent analysis. After filtering, 29,831 genes were included in the WGCNA. Next, the average linkage method was performed for all pairwise genes to construct a co-expression similarity matrix. The co-expression similarity matrix was then transformed into the adjacency matrix by choosing the power of β = 6 as the soft-thresholding parameter to ensure an unsigned scale-free network. Then, we created a topological matrix using the topological overlap measure (TOM) [ ]. To classify genes with similar expression patterns into gene modules, the dynamic hybrid cut method according to TOM-based dissimilarity was performed with the following major parameters: minModuleSize of 30 and deepSplit of 2. A cut-line of 0.4 was applied to the module dendrogram to define gene clusters, and closely related modules were subsequently merged based on the dissimilarity of their module eigengenes (MEs). MEs were defined as the first principal components of each module and represent the characteristic expression pattern of the majority of genes that are expressed within that module. At last, the genes in each module underwent enrichment analysis using the DAVID bioinformatics tool [ ].

## Quantitative RT-PCR

## cDNA synthesis
cDNA was synthesized from 1 μG total RNA using Takara PrimeScript 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat 6110A) using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

## qRT-PCR
The qPCR was carried out using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (BioRad, Cat 1725124) in a BioRad CFX96 Real Time PCR machine. Primer sequences for target genes are provided in Table (Additional Information). The rpl13a gene was used as an internal control for normalization of gene expression data. All qPCR reactions were carried out in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The amplification protocol included an initial denaturation step, followed by amplification with a specified annealing temperature, and a final melt curve analysis to verify product specificity. The relative gene expression was quantified using the ΔΔCt method, and fold changes in gene expression were calculated relative to the control group.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed in at least biological triplicates (n = 3) otherwise specified; For RNA sequencing and qRT-PCR, five brains were pooled to generate one biological replicate. For all other experiments, a single brain was used as one biological replicate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparison between two groups, a Student’s t-test was used. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05. Further statistical analysis mentioned below was done for comparison in the RNA sequencing analysis and WGCNA.

## Normalization and transformation
Raw count data were normalized using the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) implemented in DESeq2.
This transformation stabilizes the variance across genes with different expression levels and makes the data approximately homoscedastic, which is important for correlation-based analyses like WGCNA.

## Gene filtering
Genes with missing values in any sample were removed.
Genes with zero variance across all samples were excluded, as they do not contribute to correlation structures.
No additional filtering based on expression thresholds (e.g., mean expression or variance quantile cutoffs) was applied, because the VST transformation already suppresses technical noise from lowly expressed genes.
After applying these filters, 29,831 genes remained and were used as input for network construction.

## Multiple testing correction
In downstream analyses (e.g., correlations of module eigengenes with sample traits, or testing gene significance within modules), p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR).
This ensured that significant associations reported between modules and biological traits were robust and not inflated due to multiple hypothesis testing.
