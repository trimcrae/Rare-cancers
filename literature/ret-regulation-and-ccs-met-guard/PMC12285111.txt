## Background
Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the MEN1 gene located on the long arm of chromosome 11.
Case presentation.
A 32-year-old male was admitted to the Division of Endocrinology with a three-year history of recurrent episodes of altered consciousness and abnormal mental behavior. Laboratory tests and imaging studies revealed primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), multiple pancreatic adenomas, and a pituitary adenoma. Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous germline mutation in exon 2 of the MEN1 gene (NM_130799; c.416A > T, p.(His139Leu)), confirming a diagnosis of Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1).

## Conclusions
MEN1 patients should be evaluated for clinical manifestations of neuroglycopenia caused by pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs). These neuroglycopenic symptoms (altered consciousness and abnormal mental behavior) should be distinguished from the neuropsychiatric and cognitive symptoms associated with PHPT. Furthermore, our report describes the identification of the first mutation associated with MEN1 as NM_130799.2: c.416A > T, p.(His139Leu). It provides valuable insight into the clinical presentation of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1.

## Introduction
Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1) is a rare autosomal dominant tumor syndrome characterized by a predisposition to endocrine tumors, primarily affecting the parathyroid glands, pancreatic tissues, and anterior pituitary gland, along with non-endocrine tumor types[ ]. 70% to 95% of patients with the disease have mutations in the MEN1 gene, which encodes the protein menin, a 610-amino acid protein[ , ]. According to the 2012 Clinical Practice Guidelines for MEN1, diagnosis is based on clinical, familial, or genetic factors. Patients with one or more of the three primary manifestations of MEN1 can be diagnosed[ ]. A MEN1 diagnosis can be confirmed if a patient exhibits a MEN1 manifestation along with a first-degree family history of MEN1, or if the MEN1 gene mutation is detected[ ]. To date, over 1300 different variants of the MEN1 gene have been described in more than 400 families across various ethnic backgrounds[ ].
This paper presents a complex case of MEN1 associated with neurogenic hypoglycemia. The presence of multiple pancreatic adenomas raises concerns regarding the MEN1 diagnosis. Notably, neurogenic hypoglycemia as the primary symptom has not been previously reported in MEN1 patients. We hope that this case will serve as a reminder to colleagues that when examining MEN1 patients, they should be vigilant in distinguishing neurogenic hypoglycemia from PHPT's neuropsychiatric and cognitive symptoms.

## Case presentation
A 32-year-old male was admitted to our hospital in January 2019 due to recurrent episodes of altered consciousness and abnormal mental behavior over the past 3 years. His medical history revealed similar incidents in 2016, characterized by blurred consciousness and difficulty waking up in the morning without identifiable triggers. These episodes typically lasted 2–3 h and resolved spontaneously. There were no accompanying symptoms such as limb twitching, pallor, hunger, sweating, palpitations, or other discomfort. Furthermore, he had no personal or family history of epilepsy or psychiatric disorders.
These symptoms recur 2–3 times annually, accompanied by heightened fatigue and personality changes. He sought medical attention at a local hospital in December 2018. Laboratory tests showed elevated serum calcium levels (2.80 mmol/L, normal range 2.2–2.7 mmol/L) and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels (125.90 pg/mL, normal range 15–65 pg/mL), while the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results were normal. The sestamibi scintigraphy revealed localized radioactive uptake in the parathyroid regions of both lobes of the thyroid gland, leading to a diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT). Treatment with subcutaneous injections of salmon calcitonin (50 IU/day for 10 days) provided no significant relief, and attacks increased to twice weekly. What is worse, the patient's condition progressed from simple confusion to abnormal mental behavior, including spontaneous lying down, loss of self-control, and agitation such as throwing objects. Family members noted that eating certain foods (such as rice and rice porridge) temporarily alleviated his symptoms.
On January 18th, the patient was admitted to our hospital. On admission, he was conscious with normal vital signs: body temperature 36.5 °C, pulse rate 78 beats/min, and blood pressure 119/79 mmHg. Physical examination revealed a moist face without headache, rash, subcutaneous hemorrhage, nodules, visual changes, hirsutism, moon face, central obesity, or purple striae. There was no palpable neck mass or renal tenderness.
Laboratory tests showed elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels at 144.70 pg/ml, and slightly elevated serum calcium at 2.71 mmol/L, possibly due to his prior use of salmon calcitonin. Other results included serum phosphorus 1.11 mmol/L (normal range 0.78–2.7 mmol/L), urinary phosphorus 26.25 mmol per 24 h (normal range 9.7–42.0 mmol per 24 h), urinary calcium 8.85 mmol per 24 h (normal range 2.7–7.5 mmol per 24 h), ALP 85.5 U/L (normal range 45–125 U/L for males), 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] 8.90 ng/ml (normal range 20–32 ng/mL), β-C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (β-CTX) 1.21 ng/ml (normal range 0.100–0.612 ng/L for males), and procollagen type I propeptide (P1NP) 149.20 ug/L (normal range 20–76 ug/L for males). Additional tests included an insulin release test, a C-peptide release test, and a starvation test (Table  ). During the patient’s first admission, the OGTT results showed normal fasting blood glucose levels and 30, 60, 120, and 180 min blood glucose levels. Unfortunately, during the second admission, although the fasting glucose was normal, the OGTT revealed elevated blood glucose levels at 30, 60, 120, and 180 min (normal range: 30/60-min glucose < 11.1 mmol/L, 120-min glucose < 7.8 mmol/L, 180-min glucose < 6.1 mmol/L)) simultaneously. Additionally, insulin levels were elevated at 30, 60, 120, and 180 min following the insulin release test. Furthermore, insulin levels were greater than 10 times those of fasting insulin at 30, 60, and 120 min, indicating insulin resistance. Meanwhile, 960 min after the starvation test, the patient's blood glucose level had dropped to 2.01 mmol/L. This level of blood glucose is lower than the normal level, indicating hypoglycemia. Additionally, the patient's previous altered consciousness and abnormal behavior returned. Despite an insulin release index of 0.255, the corrected index was 149.51 uU/mg, significantly higher than 85 uU/mg. Together with these changes in insulin and C-peptide, we concluded that this hypoglycemia might be related to excessive insulin secretion, suggesting the possibility of insulinomas or other disorders associated with abnormal insulin secretion.
 
As the patient had previously been diagnosed with PHPT and had normal calcium levels upon admission, a 99mTc-MIBI thyroid scan was performed to reassess his condition. According to the parathyroid results (Fig.  A), there was an abnormal focal uptake in the mid-posterior portion of the left thyroid lobe, which indicates the presence of a parathyroid adenoma. According to these results, as well as previous evaluations, the patient has been diagnosed with PHPT. 
Further imaging of the pancreas with non-contrast and contrast-enhanced CT scanning and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a blood-rich nodule measuring 17 × 14 mm at the anterior border of the pancreatic body. The contrast-enhanced CT scan showed significant enhancement of this nodule (Fig.  B). MRI showed multiple nodules and masses with abundant blood supply in the pancreatic body, suggesting multiple neuroendocrine tumors (Fig.  C). Therefore, multiple pancreatic adenomas were diagnosed in the patient.
According to our findings, the patient presented with neuroglucocorticoid symptoms (altered consciousness and abnormal psychiatric behavior) because of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. Furthermore, this explains why previous hypocalcemic therapy did not relieve the symptoms.
Furthermore, all laboratory tests for serum thyrotropin (TSH), prolactin (PRL), luteinizing hormone (LH), growth hormone (GH), adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) returned within normal ranges. A pituitary MRI showed that the anterior pituitary signal was uneven, with a slightly shorter/longer T1 signal, as well as an uneven dynamic enhancement, suggesting the presence of a pituitary microadenoma(Fig.  D-E). This led to a diagnosis of non-functioning pituitary adenoma. Further investigations included cortisol rhythm and thyroid function tests, as well as tumor markers, gastrin, and CEA, detailed in Table  .
As the disease presents with all three hallmark tumors (parathyroid, pancreatic, and pituitary) at an early age without a family history, genetic testing was recommended[ ]. We utilized targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) to efficiently detect both common and novel mutations across the MEN1, RET, and CDKN1B genes in a single assay[ ]. Because NGS can simultaneously analyze multiple MEN1-associated genes and detect low-frequency mosaicism, it was selected over Sanger sequencing[ ]. Genomic DNA was extracted from proband's blood samples and fragmented for use in sequencing libraries. A custom-designed capture probe was used to enrich specific regions of the MEN1, RET, and CDKN1B genes, including the coding exons and adjacent 10-bp intronic sequences. A high-throughput platform was used to sequence 7015 base pairs with an average depth of 413.02X. A total of 98.39% of the targeted area was covered by depths greater than 30X, ensuring data reliability for downstream analysis.
Variant calling and annotation were performed using standard bioinformatics pipelines. Filters were applied to the variants in order to eliminate those with low sequencing depth or poor quality. Mutations were predicted using computer simulation tools such as SIFT and PolyPhen. The c.416A > T, p.(His139Leu) mutation in the MEN1 gene was a de novo mutation and was predicted to be harmful (REVEL: 0.872). At position 139 of the menin polypeptide chain, a position that is highly conserved in all vertebrates, histidine (His) is replaced by leucine (Leu) in exon 2 of the MEN1 gene (Fig.  G).
DNA sequencing of the proband's parents was performed to further understand how the mutation was inherited. MEN1 mutations were not detected in the proband, confirming the origin of the mutation as being de novo (Fig.  G).
Based on the available evidence, the identified mutation was classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines as follows[ ]:PM1: Non-truncating non-synonymous variant located in a mutational hot spot and/or critical and well-established functional domain.
PM2: Low frequency in gnomAD population databases.
PM5: Different amino acid changes as a known pathogenic variant.
PM6: De novo in a patient with phenotype consistency.
PP2: Missense variant in a gene with a low rate of benign missense mutations for which missense mutation is a common disease mechanism.
PP3: Computational prediction tools unanimously support a deleterious effect on the gene.
PP4: The patient’s phenotype or family history is highly specific for a disease with a single genetic etiology.

Based on the clinical features (PHPT, multiple pancreatic adenomas, and pituitary microadenoma) and the de novo mutation, the identified mutation was classified according to the ACMG guidelines as pathogenic (class V; PM1, PM2, PM5, PM6, PP2, PP3, and PP4), supporting its role in MEN1 pathogenesis.
The patient initially presented with recurrent episodes of altered consciousness and psychiatric symptoms due to neuroglycopenia. The concurrent presence of parathyroid adenoma, pancreatic adenoma, and pituitary microadenoma aligns with MEN1 clinical profile. Genetic testing confirmed MEN1 diagnosis.
MEN1 was definitively diagnosed following comprehensive laboratory, imaging, and genetic testing. It was noted that mild hypoglycemia effects can be reversible, while sustained or severe cases may cause irreversible neuronal damage and structural brain injury[ ]. Considering the patient's neuroglycopenic symptoms and disease risks, a pancreatectomy was recommended, along with regular follow-up.
On February 25, 2019, the patient accepted our advice and underwent laparoscopic resection of the pancreatic body and tail under general anesthesia at a local hospital. The patient underwent a laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy. Preoperative localization confirmed tumors in the pancreatic body (17 mm)and tail (45 mm). Intraoperative ultrasound and frozen sections ensured the complete resection. Perioperative precautions included glucose monitoring, intravenous glucose supplementation to prevent intraoperative hypoglycemia, and close monitoring for postoperative pancreatic fistulas (POPF) or bleeding[ ]. The surgical approach was selected to ensure complete tumor resection while preserving pancreatic function.
Postoperative pathology confirmed neuroendocrine tumors measuring 2 cm and 4.5 cm in diameter, with 1–2 mitoses per 10 high-power fields and a Ki-67 index exceeding 2%, consistent with a well-differentiated grade 2 neuroendocrine tumors (NETs). Immunohistochemistry revealed positive staining for Synaptophysin (Syn) and Chromogranin A (CgA), and negative staining for CD10, with a Ki-67 index of 2–4% (Fig.  F)[ ]. Table outlines postoperative follow-up details. Regarding the follow-up of PHPT, we recommended early surgical intervention for parathyroid adenoma after evaluating potential contraindications. However, the patient was working abroad at the time and could not undergo surgery.

## Discussion
Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1) is a rare and highly penetrative autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline mutations in the MEN1 gene[ ]. Inactivation of the remaining healthy gene copy due to somatic mutations can promote hyperplasia and tumor formation in various endocrine and non-endocrine tissues[ ]. Commonly affected organs include the parathyroid glands, pancreas, and pituitary gland, with primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) being the most frequent initial clinical presentation[ , ]. Approximately 80% of PHPT patients initially present with subtle neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognitive disorders, musculoskeletal pain, gastrointestinal dysregulation, or osteoporosis before classic symptoms emerge[ ]. In this case, PHPT could not explain the patient's recurrent consciousness disturbances and abnormal behavior. It may be a mistake to attribute these abnormal mental symptoms to hypercalcemia and overlook the fact that insulinomas may be causing neurogenic hypoglycemia. The misdiagnosis led to the patient's condition deteriorating, causing confusion, uncontrolled behavior, and agitation. This oversight highlights the importance of considering alternative diagnoses when evaluating MEN1 patients with atypical symptoms.
Another significant manifestation of MEN1 is pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) [ ]. Individuals with MEN1 have an 80% lifetime risk of developing pNETs[ ]. The most prevalent types are non-functioning pNETs (NF-pNETs), which account for 85–90% of cases and do not produce excess hormones or lead to clinical syndromes[ ]. However, in 10 to 15% of MEN1 cases, insulinomas produce excessive insulin, leading to symptomatic hypoglycemia[ , ]. In this case, upon receiving calcium-lowering treatment, the patient's symptoms of impaired consciousness progressed from simple confusion to unpredictable abnormal behavior, and the frequency of the episodes increased to twice weekly. We concluded that these symptoms were caused by neurogenic hypoglycemia due to pNETs and not by PHPT. Fortunately, his neurogenic hypoglycemia symptoms significantly improved following the pancreatectomy, and his insulin levels returned to normal.
Symptoms of hypoglycemia can be autonomic as well as neuroglycopenic[ ]. Autonomic symptoms associated with hypoglycemia, such as palpitations, sweating, and tremors, are mediated by the sympathetic nervous system when blood glucose levels fall below approximately 3.3 mmol/L[ – ]. In contrast, neuroglycopenic symptoms, such as cognitive impairment, behavioral changes, psychomotor abnormalities, seizures, and comas, are caused by direct glucose deprivation of the central nervous system when blood glucose levels are below 2.8 mmol/L[ ]. An analysis of hypoglycemia-related neuroglycopenic symptoms and autonomic symptoms is shown in Table  . Severe hypoglycemia can result in neurological deficits, cardiovascular complications, and even mortality[ ]. Cardiovascular effects may arise from the stress response and potential arrhythmias triggered by hypoglycemic episodes[ ]. There have been reports that severe hypoglycemic episodes may result in sudden death[ ]. It is important to note that neuroglycopenic symptoms can appear without aut onomic warning signs, especially when hypoglycemia is recurrent or chronic, increasing the risk of unrecognized severe hypoglycemia[ ].
 
MEN1 is generally characterized by neuroglycopenia caused by insulinoma-driven hyperinsulinemia, whereas autonomic features may be absent from hypoglycemia-associated autonomic failure (HAAF). The absence of sweating/palpitations in this patient supports this theory. This case illustrates the importance of distinguishing neuropsychiatric symptoms caused by PHPT from neuroglycopenic symptoms caused by insulinomas. Even though the patient did not present classic Whipple's triad symptoms of hypoglycemia, their recurrent episodes of altered consciousness and abnormal behavior on an empty stomach were initially overlooked. The patient's blood glucose level and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) were normal upon admission, leading to the overlooking of the patient's neuroglycopenic symptoms and delaying the recognition of the initial MEN1 symptom[ ]. In MEN1 patients with recurrent neuroglycopenic episodes, even without classic Whipple's triad, insulinoma should be considered, especially if symptoms worsen despite calcium lowering. To prevent irreversible neuronal injury, comprehensive endocrine evaluation and imaging(contrast-enhanced MRI) are essential to detect pNETs early.
Clinical symptoms in patients with the MEN1 gene mutations typically manifest after the age of 21, with clinical penetrance exceeding 90% by age 50[ ]. About 90% of familial MEN1 cases and 27% of sporadic cases are attributed to the MEN1 gene mutations. Somatic mutations are present in up to 50% of pNETs[ , ]. Given the presence of three primary MEN1-associated endocrine tumors (parathyroid adenoma, pancreatic tumor, and pituitary adenoma (Fig.  A-D), genetic sequencing of the MEN1 gene was performed (Fig.  E). Genetic sequencing in this patient revealed a novel missense mutation [p.(His139Leu)] in exon 2 of the MEN1 gene (NM_130799), indicating a de novo mutation, as both parents tested negative. The mutation, a histidine to leucine substitution at position 139 of the menin protein, has never been documented in major databases such as GnomAD(https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org) or VarSome(https://varsome.com). This case report highlights a patient with MEN1 associated with a novel heterozygous missense mutation in the MEN1 gene (c.416A > T). Disease progression over time is documented in Table  , showing predominantly pancreatic and parathyroid involvement due to the new MEN1 gene mutation, with other glands less affected.
Lemos and Thakker reviewed the MEN1 gene mutations found in the first ten years after its discovery in 2008[ ]. In 2016, Concolino et al. reported 75 newly identified the MEN1 gene mutations in patients with MEN1 and other hereditary endocrine disorders. They also recorded 576 new mutations at that time[ ]. In 2019, the UMD-MEN1 database added 181 newly identified germline mutations to the list[ ]. In 2021, Thakker et al. analyzed heterozygous MEN1 germline mutations. They found that frameshift mutations made up 42.2% of cases, missense mutations 25.5%, nonsense mutations 14%, splice-site mutations 10.5%, in-frame deletions or insertions 5.5%, and gross deletions 2.5%[ ]. These 757 unique MEN1 germline mutations cover the entire coding region, with no hotspots identified[ ]. Over 1,300 mutations in the MEN1 gene have been identified so far. However, the functional impact of missense mutations and in-frame insertions or deletions involving one or more amino acids remains unclear. These mutations do not always show menin inactivation, and it is hard to tell if they are benign or pathogenic without more research[ ]. Currently, the ClinVar database shows that the MEN1 gene contains 127 mutations caused by missense variants, indicating both pathogenic and likely pathogenic genes (Fig.  ). In this report, a Chinese patient with MEN1 was identified with a novel heterozygous MEN1 missense mutation [p.(His139Leu)]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of this specific mutation. 
By querying the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), we found that other missense mutations of the same amino acid, including H139Q, H139P, H139R, H139Y, H139D, affect this residue. All of these mutations affected histidine residue 139 of the MEN1 protein. As part of the menin protein, this residue plays a key role in regulating gene expression through interactions with transcription factors and cofactors. There has been extensive research on the interaction between menin, MLL, and JunD, which is crucial for their function as tumor suppressors. Studies have shown that the binding of menin to MLL is a key factor in the development of MLL-related leukemia. The H139D mutation disrupts the binding between menin and MLL, affecting MLL-driven gene expression and chromatin remodeling, thus leading to tumorigenesis[ ]. The H139D mutation has also been reported to affect the interaction of menin with JunD, a component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex. This interaction is important for the regulation of genes involved in cell proliferation and differentiation. Disruption of the interaction may lead to increased cell proliferation and decreased apoptosis, promoting tumor development[ ]. The H139L mutation replaces histidine (His) with leucine (Leu). Leucine is a nonpolar amino acid with a hydrophobic side chain. This mutation alters the local structure and may affect the menin protein folding and stability. The introduction of leucine disrupts the binding of menin to MLL and JunD, especially binding that depends on polar interactions. This results in significant alterations in the regulation of gene expression and affects the tumor suppressor function of menin.
Compared with all previously reported mutations at the same His139 residue (H139Q, H139P, H139R, H139Y, H139D), which have been classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in ClinVar. Notably, substitutions for His139 (e.g., H139D) disrupt menin’s binding to MLL and JunD, compromising transcriptional regulation and promoting tumorigenesis[ ]. The novel heterozygous missense mutation c.416A > T (p.His139Leu) identified in this patient presents with similar functional disruptions, particularly impairing menin's interaction with MLL and JunD. This suggests that the novel H139L mutation may share comparable tumorigenic potential, which aligns with the patient's clinical presentation of early-onset and multifocal endocrine neoplasms. Further validation via protein-binding assays is required to validate the functional impact of p.His139Leu.
Due to its many symptoms and overlaps, this case illustrates how difficult it is to diagnose MEN1. For proper treatment, early detection and genetic testing are essential. This is especially true for new mutations such as those found in this patient. Due to the patient's international work, it was difficult to monitor and manage their condition. Such complex diseases are difficult to treat, even when follow-up and long-term care are recommended.

## Conclusion
The article discusses a case of MEN1 with neurogenic hypoglycemia. Neuroglycopenic symptoms due to insulinoma may be the first and predominant manifestation of MEN1, as illustrated by this case. We also found a novel mutation (c.416A > T) in the MEN1 gene, which has not been reported before. The proband's parents tested negative for MEN1, suggesting that the MEN1 in the proband is caused by a de novo mutation. This case shows the importance of identifying neurogenic hypoglycemia as a possible sign of MEN1 for accurate diagnosis and treatment.
We share important insights into the diagnosis and treatment of MEN1. The article highlights the need to understand different clinical symptoms to improve early diagnosis and treatment.
