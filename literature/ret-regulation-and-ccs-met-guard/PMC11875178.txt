## Introduction
With a deeper understanding of the complexities of nucleic acid structures, our focus has increasingly shifted from the well-known information containing DNA double helices to non-canonical regulatory nucleic acid motifs ( ). The latter class predominantly includes G-quadruplexes (G4s), C-tetraplexes, and stem-loop structures. The prior motifs emerge from the repetitive and periodic occurrence of guanine (“G”) and cytosine (“C”) nucleotides, giving rise to complementary sequences that can form G4s and i-motifs, respectively. , 
The mere presence of a putative tetraplex-forming sequence does not guarantee the endogenous formation of these structures, however. The folding of nucleic acid strands into guanine-tetrads or intercalated motifs (i-motifs) is a complex process governed by various extracellular, intracellular, nuclear, and cytoplasmic factors. Recently, a new oligodeoxynucleotide structure known as the AC (adenine and cytosine)-motif has also been discovered, which has been shown to regulate the promoter region of the CDKL3 gene. These findings highlight that nucleotide repeats can form stable secondary structures under physiological conditions, leading to a diverse array of regulatory motifs within the genome. The widespread presence of polypurine and polypyrimidine tracts, across the genome, underscores their potential critical biological functions. The ongoing debate among molecular biologists concerns whether these structures act as crucial regulatory switches or are mere transient artifacts. In this review, we concentrate on one of the less-explored secondary structures, i-motifs, and discuss their regulatory roles within promoter regions and potential mechanisms involved in their regulation.

## Structure and stability of i-motifs
The hemiprotonated cytosine-cytosine motif, originally believed to be a duplex structure, was later reinterpreted as a single-stranded structure following NMR spectroscopy, which revealed its true form as a polycytidine-tetrad. The intercalation of C-C+ base pairs has led to numerous observations of i-motif formation at acidic to slightly acidic pH levels, with the greatest stability observed between pH 4.0 and pH 5.0. This stability was further validated in 2018 using an i-motif-specific single-chain variable fragment called iMab. The binding of this i-motif-specific antibody fragment to the human telomere (hTelo) significantly decreased as the pH increased, with negligible binding at pH 9.0, indicating a gradual unfolding of human telomeric i-motifs (hTeloiMs) with increasing pH. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopic analysis of a C-rich 21-mer oligonucleotide sequence revealed the dynamic nature of i-motif folding across different pH levels. It was observed that i-motif formation occurs at pH 5.0, while at pH 8.0, the structure transitions to a random coil. Additionally, CD assays of the promoter regions I and II of the ACC1 gene (acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase 1) showed that the melting temperature (Tm) values for i-motif structures decrease progressively as the pH rises from 4.5 to 6.5, confirming that i-motif stability is inversely correlated with pH. 
These findings raised questions about the in vivo feasibility of i-motif formation, prompting a closer examination of various intracellular factors contributing to i-motif stability. Notably, i-motif formation has been detected in the nuclei of cancer cell lines under physiological conditions using the iMab binding. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that the promoter regions of specific oncogenes, such as HIF-1α and PDGF-A, can form stable i-motifs at pH 7.0. Furthermore, a 1.8-Å resolution crystal structure of promoter region i-motif from HRAS oncogene throws light on the folding pattern of i-motif structures. It shows that both canonical and non-canonical base pairings are responsible for the structural stabilization of i-motifs, thus providing molecular insights into these lesser known secondary structures. 
A critical structural aspect influencing the intracellular stability of i-motifs is the length of their loops. i-Motifs can be categorized into class I and class II structures based on loop length. Class I structures have shorter loops of 2–4 nt, while class II structures have loops of up to 8 nt. It has been proposed that the interactions within the longer loops make class II i-motifs more stable at higher pH levels compared to class I structures. For example, the P1 promoter of BCL2 preferentially forms a stable i-motif with a folding pattern of 8:5:7, demonstrating that class II structures can enhance i-motif stability. This increased stability is attributed to the formation of capping structures created by the longer sequences. However, the same is far from being established as a rule, since a regression model attributes i-motif stability to longer C-tracts and shorter loop lengths. It might also be reasonable to assume that certain factors demonstrate a sequence- and location-specific contribution to i-motif stability. As a result of their dynamic structure, the physiological relevance of i-motifs has been subjected to speculations and further investigations. Despite minimal fluctuations in intracellular pH values, i-motif structures in the nucleus exhibit dynamic stabilization and destabilization. Therefore, the formation of i-motifs cannot be solely attributed to pH changes; several other factors may also contribute to the folding and unfolding of these structures, some of which are shown in . Significant variables influencing this non-canonical formation include temperature, molecular crowding, and protein interactions. 

## Temperature
Nucleic acids tend to form i-motifs at neutral to slightly alkaline pH levels, especially when the temperature is lowered to 4°C. This observation could provide insights into the role of i-motifs in promoting nuclear dormancy. However, given the extremely low temperatures required for stabilization, which result in heightened structural stability, the biological significance of temperature-dependent i-motif stabilization remains uncertain.

## Molecular crowding
This second factor influencing i-motif stabilization has gained increasing significance due to its relevance in intracellular environments. Notably, pH levels between 6.0 and 8.0 favored polyethylene glycol (PEG)-induced molecular crowding to stabilize intercalated structures, resulting in higher Tm than controls. Additionally, it has been shown that single-walled carbon nanotubes can promote i-motif formation under physiological conditions and at pH levels as high as 8.0. They simulate molecular crowding by binding to the DNA major groove, inhibiting the association of DNA duplexes. , Moreover, molecular crowding is known to inhibit DNA cleavage, thereby preventing the relaxation of negative superhelicity. This is significant because negative superhelicity facilitates i-motif folding, as observed in genes like BCL2 and c-MYC. , Additionally, molecular crowding can hinder the progression of cells into the M phase of the cell cycle, a stage where both i-motifs and G4s are minimally present allowing DNA duplexes to predominate. Building on this, it has been experimentally demonstrated that neighboring duplex and tetraplex nucleic acid structures can readily impact the thermal stability of these i-motifs. , Thus, molecular crowding plays a crucial role in understanding i-motif dynamics.

## Protein interactions
The interactions between proteins, particularly transcription factors and i-motif structures, do not have a uniform effect and are vastly dynamic in nature. While certain ligands and transcription factors can stabilize i-motifs, others may destabilize them and certain others act as readers. Identifying these secondary structures dictates their folding and/or unfolding and subsequent transcriptional activation or repression of specific genes. , Notably, proteins such as poly(C) binding proteins, including heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) K and hnRNP LL, have been reported to interact with i-motifs across multiple gene promoters. A more detailed discussion of some of these protein-i-motif interactions can be found in, ‘ ' section of this review.

## i-Motifs and their physiological significance
Since i-motifs are increasingly being explored as molecular switches for transcriptional regulation, their role in disease is an area of significant interest. Recently, the first genome-wide mapping of i-motifs using iMab-based CUT&Tag has revealed that these structures are predominantly present in the open chromatin regions of the genome. Similar findings and conclusions have emerged throughout i-motif research, reinforcing our speculations regarding their pathological significance. However, it must be noted that a vast majority of our physiological understanding of intranuclear i-motifs is based on iMab-based studies, wherein we consider that iMab binds specifically to i-motif structures and does not lead to any structural disruptions. However, these findings are controversial, and a set of biophysical analyses shows that iMab has the potential to unfold i-Motif structures and can also bind to C-rich sequences that do not have the potential to fold into i-motifs. 
This review focuses on i-motifs in the cell cycle and some key pathophysiological conditions, such as cancer, aging, and metabolic disorders, which are interconnected in a causal-response relationship. Understanding the role of these i-motifs in disease-related genes and pathways could be crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. enlists some of the documented genes known to contain i-motif structures with regulatory potential. The transcriptional significance of all the i-motif structures is not yet documented. 

## i-Motifs in cell-cycle progression
The physiological folding of nucleic acid strands into quadruplexes is closely linked to cell-cycle progression, with i-motifs being the most prevalent during the transcriptionally active G1 phase. As cells transit into S phase, there is a shift from the formation of i-motifs toward the formation of G4s ( ). , Such phase-dependent transitions indicate the significance of DNA-protein interplay and cell-cycle-associated pH changes in the stabilization of these quadruplex structures. 
In situ monitoring of cellular pH revealed a complex curve for both cytosolic and nuclear pH values. These data suggest that the pH of cells during the cell cycle generally falls within a narrow range of 6.9–7.1. Cells entering the G1 phase experience a transient drop in pH followed by a rapid increase to around 7.4, indicating that only a small fraction of the genome containing potential i-motif sequences demonstrates folded i-motif structures at any given point in the cell cycle. Similar pH values across different phases do not necessarily reflect the same prevalence of i-motifs within the genome. Studies employing in-cell NMR have proposed a dual origin of i-motif cell-cycle dependency to explain this phenomenon, and it is primarily attributed to cell-cycle-dependent chromatin states, which significantly affects DNA folding. 
Therefore, it is reasonable to correlate i-motifs with other cell-cycle-dependent factors, especially DNA-binding proteins. These proteins can induce negative superhelicity in the DNA duplex, which remains a driving force for i-motif formation. It is also important to note that the formation of C-quadruplex and G4s is not strictly independent—the stabilization of one can inhibit the formation of the other. For example, research using ellipticine analogs has shown that stabilizing G4s- can decrease the number of i-motifs in the nucleus, and conversely, stabilization of i-motifs can reduce the number of G4s. Thus, it can be inferred that cell-cycle-dependent proteins that stabilize i-motifs may function to destabilize the complementary G4s and vice versa. Examining transcription factors in this context could shed light on the mechanisms that regulate gene expression and may also impact the cell cycle.

## Cancer
Cancer is a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors, and the onset is dependent on the malfunction of multiple pathways, primarily the ones involved in the regulation of cell cycle, cell death, immune responses, and DNA damage repair. As previously noted, a correlation has been established between the formation of DNA tetrads and cell-cycle progression. The highest frequency of i-motifs has been detected during the G1/S phase transition and that gives us the rationale to investigate their role in transcriptional regulation. These intercalated motifs like G4s, located in oncogenic promoters, can function as both transcriptional activators and repressors. For instance, studies have shown that treatment of the anti-apoptotic BCL2 promoter with anti-i-motif stabilizer (IMC-48) led to an increase in the gene expression, whereas treatment with IMC-76, which is known to inhibit tetrad formation, led to significant cell death and tumor reduction. This suggests that that the formation of a cytosine tetrad can lead to the transcriptional upregulation of BCL2. In contrast, the c-MYC gene, a key regulator of cellular metabolism and proliferation, shows i-motif to be a transcriptional repressor. This opposing regulatory effect of i-motifs in promoter regions underscores the necessity of exploring their pH-independent formation and stabilization, emphasizing the significance of other factors, particularly protein-nucleic acid interactions. Given that hypoxia and the changes in pH gradient are well established cancer hallmarks, it would be interesting to examine their role in the structural organization of oncogenes and associated i-motifs. In this context, the presence of secondary structures in the promoter regions of proto-oncogenes and oncogenes has drawn significant attention over the years. Considering this, these secondary structures could be considered to be an intriguing target for therapeutic intervention. While G4s have been extensively studied, considerably less is known about i-motifs and their regulatory roles.
The presence of i-motifs in the promoters of oncogenes and telomeres links them to two major hallmarks of cancer: cell proliferation and replicative immortality, respectively. , However, little has been explored to elucidate the role of i-motifs as tumor suppressor elements. Reports indicate the presence of i-motif-forming elements in the promoter regions of genes such as DAP, RAD17, Rb, and SMARCA4, but their role in tumor gene regulation has not been validated yet. Considering the uniform changes in nuclear and cytosolic pH levels, it may be worthwhile to investigate whether the stabilization of i-motifs in the promoter regions of oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes produces opposing effects. Another possibility is that cancer-associated physiological changes and signaling pathways exert contrasting influences on the i-motifs of these two sets of genes, potentially mediated by different proteins. Either conclusion could pave the way for novel precision medicines aimed at achieving the desired modulation of gene expression.

## Aging
The relationship between aging and pH, particularly in the context of hypoxia, establishes a fundamental connection. Yet another well-accepted theory of aging is the telomere shortening theory, which in turn paves the way for i-motifs in gerontology. Telomeres play a crucial role in cellular responses to stress and growth, and this response is influenced by the number of prior cell divisions. As telomeres shorten with each cell division, they offer a biological explanation for cellular senescence. Due to their repetitive sequences, hTelos are prime sites for the formation of i-motif structures, which have been shown to play a role in telomeric protection. Evidence from studies on Arabidopsis thaliana suggests that epigenetic modifications, such as methylation, influence telomeric stability, and i-motifs containing methylated cytosines have been shown to be more resistant to thermal unfolding. Telomerase enables the elongation of telomeres with each cell division, and its activity is preserved in germline cells. This makes germ cells valuable for studying the impact of i-motifs on telomerase activity. While there is no definitive documentation regarding the effects of hTelo i-motif formation on telomerase activity and telomeric extension, their protective role suggests that C-tetraplexes could represent a significant aspect of gerontology. Furthermore, it may also indicate differences in the prevalence and distribution of i-motifs between germline and somatic cells. A comparative study of these differences could shed light on variations in genomic morphology and aging in a tissue-specific context. Aging is associated with all stages and events of the life cycle. Therefore, i-motifs may be connected to aging, either directly or indirectly, through various pathways such as hypoxia, cancer, metabolic anomalies, and other cellular or physiological conditions.

## Metabolic disorders
Metabolic reprogramming is central to almost every studied physiological anomaly ranging from degenerative disorders to cancer. An important gene whose activity is associated with a large number of complex human diseases is the ACC1. ACC1 expression is regulated by three promoters, two of which house i-motif-forming sequences, namely PI and PII. It has been seen that these sequences are responsible for negative regulation of transcription and that mutating the cytosine from these sequences to thymine resulted in a greater expression of this gene. 
Two most common metabolic disorders associated with the presence of i-motif structures in the promoter region of a relevant gene include non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and diabetes mellitus. Apoptosis acts as a driving factor for NAFLD. Thus, anti-apoptotic factors like BCL2 can act as potential therapeutic targets. The importance of i-motifs in transcription regulation of BCL2 is discussed in detail in the section on . Additionally, ACC1 gene is associated with diabetes and conditions that lead to diabetes, namely obesity. It was suggested that the unwinding of secondary structures upstream of the insulin gene can result in increased transcription. , The role of i-motifs in metabolic pathways has yet to be established. However, once done, these structures have the potential to serve as novel therapeutic targets.

## Mechanism of action
The potential therapeutic implications of i-motif formation in oncogenic promoters necessitate a thorough understanding of the mechanisms through which these structures perform their regulatory functions. i-Motif formation can be attributed to negative supercoiling induced by the transcription machinery, alongside other intranuclear factors. Thus, i-motif-induced transcription regulation and transcription-induced i-motif formation follows a cyclical mechanism. Currently, our understanding of cytosine tetraplexes is limited to their role in the transcriptional modulation of specific genes. In this review, we exploit this existing knowledge to provide a layout of the mechanisms through which i-motif-induced transcriptional modulation occurs. The possible mechanisms of transcriptional regulation by i-motifs are summarized in and . 

## Transcriptional activation
i-Motifs often serve as sites for protein binding as evidenced by many groups. hnRNP LL binds to the i-motif within the BCL2 promoter sequence. These i-motifs may also act as reference points to facilitate DNA-protein interactions located both upstream and downstream of the i-motif-binding region. The former mechanism of interaction has been more thoroughly explored, while the latter has yet to be investigated. BCL2 is one of the most extensively studied examples in which i-motifs serve as transcription activators ( A). Structurally, the C-rich region of the BCL2 promoter forms an i-motif-like structure, which is in dynamic equilibrium with a flexible hairpin structure. Selective binding with structure-specific ligands can shift this equilibrium toward one of the two configurations. Equilibrium leaning toward the hairpin structure represses transcription, and shift toward the i-motif enhances transcription. The transcription factor hnRNP LL that binds to BCL2 features four RNA recognition motifs (RRM1–4), with RRM1 and RRM2 displaying strong binding affinity for the i-motif of BCL2 promoter. These RRM domains attach to the lateral loops that form when the i-motif is fully folded. Absence of these i-motif hinders the binding of transcription factors, thereby preventing transcriptional activation. Thermodynamic interactions between RRM1, RRM2, and the lateral loops drive the unfolding of the BCL2 promoter i-motif into a more stable structure, which then remains in a binary complex with hnRNP LL, facilitating transcriptional activation. , To further demonstrate the effect of i-motifs on BCL2 transcription, the two contrasting molecules IMC48 and IMC76 were used. IMC48, known to stabilize i-motif structure, supports preferential binding, which results in transcriptional activation, while IMC76 favors the flexible hairpin leading to transcriptional repression. , The i-motif-mediated transcription of BCL2 can be described as a winding-binding-unwinding mechanism. Targeting these structural regulatory switches can offer newer strategies to modulate apoptotic pathways for patient benefit. Importantly, it also must be noted that the stabilization of the G4 in the BCL2 promoter can lead to transcriptional repression of the genes. While the exact mechanisms driving i-motif formation are still in their infancy, they are likely to be attributed to negative superhelicity resulting from the unwinding of the DNA duplex and to molecular crowding. 
A similar mechanism is observed in the case of KRAS, where hnRNP K functions as the i-motif-binding protein. , Among the three potential i-motif sequences in the KRAS promoter, the mid C-sequence is known to form the most stable structure and exists in dynamic equilibrium with a hairpin loop. However, unlike in BCL2, this hairpin corresponds to the central loop of the i-motif and can both stabilize and promote the formation of the i-motif by acting as a capping structure. hnRNP K recognizes the mid-CT (cytosine and thymine) i-motif, along with i-motif-hairpin hybrid structures, leading to transcription initiation. Due to the presence of three closely spaced i-motif-forming sequences, the transcriptional regulation of KRAS appears more complex and is therefore less well understood. The coexistence of these three sequences facilitates the formation of composite intramolecular species, which adds to the complexity of the regulatory system. 
A third example is from the promoter region of the lepidopteran gene BmPOUM2 ( B). Studied in Bombyx mori, this gene gives us a glimpse into i-motif-mediated transcription activation in invertebrates, wherein i-motif formation is initiated in the C-rich region only after the transcription machinery has been assembled. Negative supercoiling leads to the formation of these i-motifs, as discussed in the section on . This i-motif subsequently binds to a transcription factor or DNA-binding protein, activating the transcription machinery and driving the initiation and elongation processes. Once the transcription is completed, the DNA returns to its canonical duplex structure. Unlike BCL2, transcription in this case is not initiated by the prior unwinding of the i-motif, necessarily because BmILF is not known to possess any RRM domains. For BmPOUM2, the stabilizing factor for the i-motif is BmILF2, a zinc-finger protein that shows 53% similarity to the human ILF2 in terms of amino acid residues. 
In conclusion, i-motif-mediated activation mechanisms rely largely on the recognition of lateral loops of i-motifs by specific transcription factors, followed by conformational changes.

## Negative regulation of transcription
Negative regulatory activity of these motifs came to light when it was seen that i-motifs located in the upstream region of the genes of c-MYC and ACC1 could show transcriptional repression. , , Therefore, we aim to discuss these two genes to further illustrate the role of i-tetrads as transcriptional repressors.
ACC, which is essential for fatty acid biosynthesis, can exist in two isoforms in humans: ACC1 and ACC2. ACC1 is primarily found in the liver and adipose tissues, while ACC2 is predominantly expressed in oxidative tissues such as cardiac and skeletal muscles. As a key gene in cellular metabolism, ACC1 is often dysregulated in various diseases, including cancer and cardiovascular disorders. Studies indicate that upregulated/downregulated levels of ACC1 expression promotes cancer cell proliferation through metabolic reprogramming. Therapeutically targeting ACC1 expression could, therefore, serve as a central strategy in treating related disease conditions. 
Mutational studies of the i-motif sequences in promoters PI and PII of ACC1 have demonstrated that both G4s and i-motifs formed in these regions contribute to the repression of gene transcription. It was concluded that G4s and i-motifs collaborate in regulating transcription for ACC1. Furthermore, this repression occurs regardless of the strand the i-motif or G4 is formed on. Based on these results, it can be hypothesized that the presence of these non-canonical structures in the promoters acts to restrict/hinder the binding of transcription initiation factors to the promoters either through steric hindrance or by sequestering the unique binding sequence.
c-MYC gene, another target of i-motif-mediated repression, is a proto-oncogene whose upregulation can disrupt metabolic pathways and facilitate the entry of cells into the cell cycle. Similar to KRAS, c-MYC is also regulated by the transcription factor hnRNP K. , 
c-MYC connects two key hallmarks of cancer—metabolic reprogramming and cellular proliferation—acting as the “master regulator of tumorigenesis.” As illustrated in , the NHEIII1 region, located approximately −142 to −115 bp upstream of promoter P1, plays a significant role in regulating c-MYC transcription. Stabilization of this i-motif upstream of the c-MYC gene results in reduced transcription levels since i-motif unfolding is essential for the formation of a stable binary complex between hnRNP K and a 5CT element. However, we note that i-motif formation is also essential to the transcriptional modulation of this gene. The NHEIII1 region of c-MYC can accommodate more than one i-motif. The i-motifs that formed in this area are highly dynamic and contribute to the stabilization of the structure. 
The binding of hnRNP K to the NHEIII1 region requires the formation of an i-motif by the 4CT sequence, which occurs through two lateral loops of the 4CT i-motif. Torsional stress provided by another transcription factor, SP1, makes the fifth CT accessible, allowing the i-motif to unfold. Upon this unfolding, the third KH domain of hnRNP K binds to the 5CT element, leading to complete transcriptional activation. Consequently, stabilization of the i-motif structure or the inability of the 4CT i-motif to unfold results in c-MYC transcriptional inactivation. 

## i-Motif-induced gene regulation mediated by hairpin construction
i-Motifs are often found in equilibrium with hairpin structures. The influence of these hairpin structures on i-motif-mediated transcription regulation is discussed in,‘ ' section of this review, featuring examples from two well-studied genes. The mode of action associated with hairpin loops can be described in one of the following ways.

## Facilitative and cooperative mode of action
The facilitative and cooperative mode of action is observed in the mid-CT sequence of the KRAS promoter region, where the central loop of the i-motif can also fold into a hairpin loop. This hairpin can either promote i-motif folding or stabilize the i-motif by acting as a capping sequence. In KRAS, the formation of the i-motif leads to transcription activation once it is recognized and bound by hnRNP K. 

## Folding-unfolding mode of action
The folding-unfolding mode of action phenomenon is evident in the i-motif of the BCL2 promoter, which exists in equilibrium with a flexible hairpin. Observations indicate that shifting the equilibrium toward the hairpin results in transcriptional suppression. Conversely, when the i-motif is stabilized, transcriptional activation occurs, as the tetrad structure is recognized by the transcription factor hnRNP LL. It is important to note that the prevention of the i-motif formation by a hairpin loop can lead to either transcriptional activation or repression, emphasizing the gene-specific regulatory roles of these secondary structures.

## The combined effect of G4s and i-motifs also plays a role in regulating transcription
Genetic regulation through C-tetraplexes cannot be adequately examined without discussing the additional secondary structures formed near the i-motif sequences on either DNA strand. Transcription regulation of a gene is best understood through a combined analysis of G4s and i-motifs. Though the focus of this article is primarily on the latter structure, it is also essential to recognize that none of these mechanisms can be considered standalone regulatory modes of transcription.

## Epigenetic basis of i-motif-mediated transcription regulation
The impact of epigenetic modifications on i-motif stability has been studied using hTelo i-motif since telomeric location-specific cytosine methylation is known to control telomeric length and integrity in mammalian cells. However, some contrasting studies have shown that cytosine methylation, along with various other modifications, does not affect i-motif formation but affects its stability. , Some of the major epigenetically controlled modifications include modified cytosine residues include 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine, and 5-carboxylcytosine, all of which serve as significant epigenetic regulators. Additionally, epigenetic modifications like hydration dynamics affected the stabilization and destabilization of these i-motifs. The dual interaction of water molecules with these i-motif structures was seen to contribute to its stabilization. However, when tested for thermal stability, modifications of 5mC did not show significant changes in melting temperature. This can be instrumental in i-motif-based physiological therapies, medications, and treatments.

## i-Motifs and their role in replication
The role of i-motifs during the S phase of the cell cycle has been discussed in relation to the activities of replication protein A (RPA) and helicase proteins. Studies have shown that the intracellular presence of i-motifs is highest during the G1-S phase transition. Consequently, their involvement in the initiation and progression of DNA replication is now being considered. Since i-motifs are single-stranded tetraplex structures, the involvement of single-stranded DNA-binding proteins (SSBs) in their formation, stabilization, and destabilization is essential. SSBs are critical for DNA replication, repair, and recombination. The eukaryotic SSBs are known as RPA, which stabilizes ssDNA and is integral to the replication machinery. Moreover, RPA interacts with various components involved in nucleic acid metabolism. While the interactions of RPA with G4s have been studied in greater depth, its relationship with i-motifs is less well understood. RPA participates in the unfolding and linearization of G4s, with the accessibility of these guanine structures being the rate-limiting factor. , Although knowledge of the interactions involving i-motifs is limited, it has been demonstrated through fluorescence resonance energy transfer assays that RPA also induces the linearization of i-motifs, and this unfolding remains stable. 
Another S phase protein that has been studied in relation to i-motifs is helicase. Traditionally, helicases function by using energy to unwind the double-stranded DNA helix, and they are also known to influence specific DNA-protein interactions. While helicases are widely recognized for their interactions within the DNA double helix, evidence shows that at acidic pH, BLM, DHX36, RecQ, and Rep helicases exhibit a greater affinity for i-motifs compared to other DNA structures such as G4, forked DNA, and duplex DNA. Efficient unwinding of i-motifs by helicases was observed at low pH in an ATP-dependent manner, whereas their ability to unwind G4 and DNA duplexes was comparatively less effective. This increased efficiency in the linearization of i-motifs is attributed to the stronger affinity of helicases for these structures, and both factors are pH dependent.
Research on the effects of i-motif formation on replication pathways indicates that i-motifs can stall replication. For example, in the case of i-motif formation in the HIF-1α promoter, full-length replication products were not observed. Additionally, PEG-induced molecular crowding was found to inhibit replication, likely due to the stabilization of i-motifs caused by this crowding effect. Therefore, it can be concluded that the formation of i-motifs is inversely proportional to the rate of replication. 

## RNA i-motifs
To investigate the physiological significance of nucleic acid structures, one cannot overlook the regulatory checkpoint at the translational stage. Gene expression at the proteomic level is fundamental to every known physiological phenotype. Self-associated DNA and RNA structures have been shown to exhibit a 29°C difference in their Tm, indicating their varying stability. Studies have previously shown that oligoribonucleotide sequences are incapable of forming stable i-motif structures. This finding aligns with earlier studies suggesting that poly[r(C)] structures are less stable than their deoxyribo counterparts and that oligoribonucleotides are less likely to form secondary structures. , , The free energy of the RNA i-motifs is seen to be half that of the value for DNA i-motifs, and the transitional pH for RNA i-motifs has been shown to be lower than that for the DNA i-motifs. , CD spectroscopic analysis of oligoribonucleotide sequences showed that a C3U3 i-motif unfolded at a pH of 5.5. Moreover, C2U3 did not display an i-motif peak at acidic pH, while C2T3 was able to form a tetraplex structure. However, the study also suggests that higher-order cytosine repeats in RNA (C5 and beyond) could form i-motifs with physiological significance. 
Furthermore, it has been proposed that i-motif formation may have been central to the primordial RNA world hypothesis, where these secondary structures could stabilize RNA polymers, with a potential role in ligand binding as well. The hypothesis surrounding primordial i-motifs is consistent with their structural stability at lower pH levels, and it is reasonable to assume that their role in RNA-ligand binding could be analogous to that observed in DNA. Within the cellular context, these ribonucleic structures may also be linked to the termination of translation due to their non-linear characteristics. Alongside various hypotheses, the in vivo occurrence of RNA i-motifs remains a subject of debate.

## i-Motifs as therapeutic targets
pH variations are intricately wired with an array of pathophysiological conditions, and i-motif structures being responsive to intracellular pH (pHi) changes are garnering attention for their ability to act as markers for pHi variations. i-Motifs are also found to impact neighboring DNA secondary structures, including neighboring i-motifs and hairpin loops. Owing to these aspects of i-motif dynamics, i-motifs can frequently be used as diagnostic markers. Additionally, regulating gene expression by modulation of i-motif dynamics using small-molecule ligands can help us propose desirable physiological manifestations. For instance, using ellipticine analogs, proteins, synthetic intercalating agents, or large biomolecular ligands that bind to DNA structures can alter or disrupt the i-motif structure and thus bring a desired pathophysiological manifestation. , , Additionally, i-motifs ideally might not work on the basis of an all-or-none phenomenon. It is only logical to believe that a multitude of intermediate structures can also have regulatory roles based on their half-life. These aspects can help establish i-motifs as therapeutic targets and diagnostic tools.

## Conclusion
This review explores the biological relevance of i-motifs, with a particular focus on their mechanisms of transcriptional regulation. Given their prevalence in telomeres and in the promoter regions of genes associated with various physiological conditions in humans, understanding i-motifs as regulatory switches is essential. Based on our current knowledge on i-motifs one can infer the following:(1)Stability studies indicate that i-motifs exist as transient structures within cells, suggesting that their regulatory significance arises from both their formation and their disruption or destabilization. Induced negative superhelicity and the hydration shell can cause the duplex to unwind, leading to the formation of single-stranded i-motifs or hybrid i-motif conformations. This unwinding facilitates the recognition and binding of essential transcription factors.
(2)The modulation of gene expression by i-motifs is best understood as a sequential process of winding and unwinding.
(3)Furthermore, by examining each mechanism in isolation, one may overlook the dynamic nature of our interphase genome. It is reasonable to assert that gene regulation involving i-motif dynamics is a complex interplay of multiple mechanisms.
(4)Additionally, the occurrence of G4s and C-quadruplexes is complementary and largely interdependent, indicating that gene regulation functions as a cross-talk between these non-canonical structures and hairpin loops, as discussed in the section “ .”
(5)Epigenetic modifications are also crucial for i-motif-induced transcription regulation by impacting the stability of i-motif structures and regulating the binding of transcription factors.

Each mechanism described in this review presents a simplified, focused perspective on the multitude of interactions and pathways co-occurring in an interdependent manner. Ultimately, the goal is to regulate the transcription of specific genes under particular physiological conditions. While we have begun to study i-motifs as regulatory switches, especially in relation to oncogenes and proto-oncogenes, a more nuanced understanding of the winding-binding-unwinding phenomenon of i-motifs could advance precision medicine and help overcome challenges such as drug resistance and cellular toxicity.
