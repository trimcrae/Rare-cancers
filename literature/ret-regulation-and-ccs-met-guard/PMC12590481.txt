## Summary
Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) transmit visual information from the eye to the brain, but do not regenerate after damage. Understanding RGC development aids regeneration. In this study, we investigated the regulatory network of two transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) superfamily proteins, growth and differentiation factors (GDF) 11 and 15, which exhibit opposing effects on RGC differentiation. To explore their roles in retinal development, we studied GDF11, GDF15, and Tgfβr2 using cre-driven conditional knockout mice. Gdf11 deletion promoted RGC differentiation, an effect lost when Gdf15 was also knocked out, suggesting that GDF15 counteracts the differentiation-promoting effects of GDF11. Tgfβr2 knockout increased RGCs but reduced photoreceptors, indicating a broader influence on retinal cell fate. Transcriptomic analysis revealed altered RGC subtype markers and genes involved in axon formation and guidance. These findings highlight a GDF-TGFβ regulatory network in RGC specification and provide insights into potential therapeutic targets for retinal regeneration.

## Introduction
Retinal ganglion cell (RGC) differentiation is a fundamental process in the development of the vertebrate visual system, as RGCs are the primary neurons responsible for transmitting visual information from the retina to the brain. Understanding how transcription factors, signaling pathways, and epigenetic mechanisms guide retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) toward an RGC fate is therefore essential. These insights not only deepen our knowledge of neural development but also hold major implications for regenerative medicine and neuroprotection. In neurodegenerative diseases such as glaucoma and optic neuropathies, progressive RGC loss leads to irreversible vision impairment, making strategies to preserve or restore RGCs especially critical. In the mouse retina, at least 46 RGC subtypes have been identified. Although most RGCs degenerate following axonal injury, certain subtypes—particularly intrinsically photosensitive RGCs (ipRGCs) and alpha-RGCs—exhibit relative resilience in the optic nerve crush (ONC) model. , However, the molecular basis of this resilience remains poorly understood. Ongoing research seeks to identify the regulatory networks and therapeutic targets that could enable RGC protection, regeneration, or replacement, ultimately advancing strategies for vision restoration.
Among key regulators of RGC fate are members of the transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) superfamily. Growth and differentiation factor 11 (GDF-11), also known as bone morphogenetic protein 11 (BMP-11), suppresses RGC differentiation by inducing RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST) and repressing Atoh7 expression in vivo. In addition to this study, our prior work further established that GDF-11 and GDF-15 have opposing roles in RGC fate determination. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) further revealed that GDF-11 promotes photoreceptor development and maturation, whereas GDF-15 drives RGC maturation by shifting cells into later developmental stages. Despite these findings, how the balance between GDF-11 and GDF-15 is maintained during RGC development remains unclear. To address this question, we generated GDF-11/GDF-15 double knockout (dKO) mice to dissect their relative contributions to RGC fate.
Mechanistically, Smad2 acts as a key downstream effector of GDF-11 signaling, with its deletion markedly enhancing the expression of RGC markers. Transforming growth factor β receptor 2 (Tgfβr2), a major receptor upstream of Smad2, , is known to regulate photoreceptor , , and amacrine cell development, yet its role in RGC differentiation has not been defined. Since both GDF-11 and GDF-15 signal through the TGFβ pathway, we hypothesized that Tgfβr2 may be a critical regulator of RGC fate specification.
Here, we demonstrate that GDF-15 knockout abolishes the effects of GDF-11 knockout on RGC differentiation, highlighting their interdependent functions. Using both in vivo analysis and scRNA-seq, we provide the first evidence that Tgfβr2 influences the formation of retinal cells. Specifically, Tgfβr2 knockout promotes RGC differentiation by upregulating genes associated with eye development, while simultaneously impairing photoreceptor differentiation through enhanced apoptotic pathways and reduced photoreceptor-specific gene expression. Notably, Tgfβr2 loss also alters the transcriptional profiles of multiple RGC subtypes, including genes involved in axon guidance. Together, these findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for Tgfβr2 in retinal development and establish the GDF-TGFβR2-Smad axis as a key signaling pathway in RGC fate specification, offering potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

## Results

## Effects of GDF-15 and GDF-11 on RGC differentiation
Our prior study demonstrated that GDF-11 and GDF-15 opposingly regulate RGC differentiation. Although GDF-15 can abolish the GDF-11-induced suppression of RGC gene expression in the RPC culture, which GDF is the key regulator for retinal development remains unknown. Following previous studies of late RGC formation by probing RGC and photoreceptor markers from postnatal day 0 (P0) pups, Chx10-Cre GDF-11
fL/fL and GDF-15 mice were utilized the P0 retinal sections collected from single knockout (GDF-11, GDF-15) or double knockout (GDF-11/GDF-15) mice for analysis. Consistent with our previous report, we observed an increased expression of RGC markers Brn3a, Rbpms, and Atoh7 ( A–1C) and no change of recoverin expression ( D) in GDF-11 KO mice. Our previous study showed that RGC formation is reduced in the retina of GDF-15 KO mice. Our data showed that GDF-11 deletion fails to upregulate the expression of Brn3a and Rbpms ( E and 1F) but remains elevating Atoh7 expression ( G) in the GDF-15 KO mice. By performing cell counting on the cryosection of retinas from P0 pups, we confirmed the gene expression data that the GDF-11 deletion-induced Brn3a+ cells were diminished in GDF-15 KO mice ( I). Photoreceptor progenitor marker recoverin expression ( H) and recoverin+ cells ( J) remained unaffected under these conditions. These findings suggest a complex interplay between GDF-11 and GDF-15 in RGC differentiation, with Atoh7 expression being independently influenced. 

## TGFβR2 mediates GDF-11-induced Smad2 activation and RGC formation
GDF-11 and GDF-15 are TGF superfamily proteins that regulate RGC fate by differently activating Smad2 signaling in distinct ways. Tgfβr2 might serve as one of the upstream receptors of Smad2 signaling. Tgfβr2 expression peaked at E12, but it dropped and stabilized from E14 to P5 in the developing retinas ( A), but its specific impact on early retinal development remains unclear. We generated Tgfβr2 KO mice by crossing with Chx10-Cre to further address this question. We first verified the reduction of Tgfβr2 gene ( B) and protein ( C) expression in P0 Tgfβr2 KO mice. To understand whether TGFβR2 is a receptor of GDF-11 for activating its downstream signaling during early retinal development, the E14.5 retinal tissues isolated from either WT or Tgfβr2 KO and its littermate mice were treated with GDF-11 for 1 h ( D–2G). We observed that GDF-11 induces phosphorylation of Tgfβr2 and Smad2 ( D) in WT mice, and GDF-11-induced Smad2 phosphorylation was attenuated in Tgfβr2 KO retinas ( E). Consistent with previous studies, , GDF-11 suppressed Brn3a expression in Tgfβr2 WT retinas, whereas this effect was absent in Tgfβr2 KO retinas ( F), suggesting that TGFβR2 is a receptor of GDF-11 in regulating RGC differentiation. In contrast, treatment with GDF-15 led to increased Brn3a expression in both Tgfβr2 WT and KO retinas, indicating that GDF-15 may promote RGC differentiation via a signaling mechanism that is independent of TGFβR2 ( G). 

## TGFβR2 KO promotes RGC but suppresses photoreceptor differentiation
To continue investigating the role of TGFβR2 in late RGC formation as we did with GDFs, we examined the gene expression and cell formation in P0 mice. We hypothesized that TGFβR2 regulates retinal development during early stages and modulates cell apoptosis at later stages. Therefore, we conducted our experiments using P0 mice. We found an increase in Brn3a expression ( H), with no significant changes in Sox4, Sox11, or Notch1 levels ( I–2K) in Tgfβr2 KO mice. In addition the expression of RE1 silencing transcription factor (REST), a known suppressor of RGC differentiation, was reduced in Tgfβr2 KO mice ( L). Our previous study showed that GDF-11 induces REST expression via activating Smad2 signaling. Likely, the reduced REST in Tgfβr2 KO mice is due to the inhibition of Smad2 activation. Regarding photoreceptor development, Tgfβr2 KO reduced recoverin expression ( M), suggesting that TGFβR2 signaling plays a critical role in early photoreceptor development. Cleaved caspase-3 staining was performed to determine whether apoptosis is involved in TGFβR2 KO-mediated RGC number. The green spots were counted as apoptotic cells in whole retinal sections. The results indicated a reduced number of cleaved caspase-3+ cells in Tgfβr2 KO mice ( N), suggesting that decreased cell death contributes to the RGC number changes observed following Tgfβr2 deletion. Immunostaining data confirmed our gene expression data, showing an increase in Brn3a+ cells and a decrease in recoverin+ and Otx2+ cells in Tgfβr2 KO mice ( O). These findings indicate that TGFβR2 opposingly regulates RGC and photoreceptor formation.

## TGFβR2 regulates retinal cell differentiation and gene expression
To investigate the role of Tgfβr2 in retinal development, we analyzed retinas from Tgfβr2 KO mice and their littermate controls at postnatal day 0 (P0). Data from four mice were integrated and the major cell classes were identified by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering in Seurat. , scRNA-seq revealed 12 distinct cell clusters, corresponding to retinal progenitor cells (RPCs, clusters 0, 1, 7, and 8), photoreceptor precursors (clusters 2 and 4), amacrine cells (cluster 3, 6, and 9), RGCs/ACs (cluster 5 and 10), and neuronal progenitor cells (cluster 11) ( A and 3B). Cell fractions reveal an overall ratio in each cell type across these two groups. Consistent with the immunostaining data, the proportion of RGCs/ACs increased, while the proportion of photoreceptor precursors decreased in Tgfβr2 KO retinas ( C). We also observed that expression of proliferative markers such as Mki67 and Pcna were higher (log2FC 0.209 and 0.265, respectively) in Tgfβr2 KO than in control ( ). Further analysis of gene expression revealed distinct patterns between different retinal cell types. Pro- and anti-proliferative gene expression levels increased in Tgfβr2 KO retinas, with a more pronounced pro-proliferative effect observed in RGC/AC populations ( D). GO enrichment analysis showed that the expression of pro-proliferative genes is relatively higher in RGCs, which likely contributes to the observed increase in overall RGC numbers ( ). Key transcription factors critical for RGC fate, including Atoh7, Sox4, and Sox11, , , exhibited altered expression in the absence of Tgfβr2. While Atoh7 expression levels remained unchanged, the fraction of Atoh7-positive cells increased ( E and 3F). Similarly, Sox4 and Sox11 showed a significant increase in Tgfβr2 KO retinas (p < 0.0001, G and 3H). 

## TGFβR2 KO delays photoreceptor precursor development and maturation
Photoreceptor precursors (clusters 2 and 4 in A) were assessed further to investigate the effects of Tgfβr2 KO in photoreceptor differentiation. Our results showed that Tgfβr2 KO slightly increased the cell number in cluster 2 ( A). Previous studies showed that photoreceptor precursors differentiated across over two transitional stages, which are characterized by the expression of Atoh7 and Otx2. , The results showed that both the cell fraction and the number of Atoh7-positive cells were slightly increased (p < 0.0001), whereas the expression of Otx2 did not change in Tgfβr2 KO ( B). In contrast, Tgfβr2 KO declined the cell number in cluster 4 ( C). We found that photoreceptor precursor markers, including Crx and Nrl were expressed in this cluster and both Crx and Nrl expression were decreased in Tgfβr2 KO (p < 0.0001, D). Differential expression of heatmap was assessed to investigate the gene regulation between Tgfβr2 KO and control ( E). For example, Tgfβr2 KO increased the expression of genes related to neuron apoptotic process, like Cdk5r1. Conversely, it decreased the expression of genes associated with photoreceptor differentiation, including Rp1 and Nrl. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the photoreceptor precursor lineages in Tgfβr2 KO showed that the upregulated genes were involved in not only neurogenesis but also the apoptotic process ( F). Most intriguingly, GO in Tgfβr2 KO showed that the downregulated genes strongly related to photoreceptor development and differentiation ( G). Next, the cells in clusters 2 and 4 were aligned and re-clustered by pseudotime. Pseudotime at 0 was assigned based on the constant expression of Mki67 ( H and 4I). The UMAP with pseudotime using trajectory analysis revealed a significant distinction between cluster 2 (immature cells) and 4 (mature cells) ( J). As the trajectory analysis has shown, the cell distribution in Tgfβr2 KO appeared at the early, least mature stage, whereas most of the cells in control appeared at the later, more mature stage ( K). These results support our in vivo findings, suggesting that Tgfβr2 KO impeded the photoreceptor precursor development and maturation. 

## TGFβR2 KO promotes the expression of several genes related to RGC subtypes and axon guidance cues/receptors
To understand whether deletion of Tgfβr2 affected RGC subtype differentiation in P0 retinas, we reclustered RGC/AC populations (clusters 5 and 10) into 11 subclusters ( A). Interestingly, Tgfβr2 KO differently regulates these subclusters. Tgfβr2 KO increases the fractions of clusters 0, 5, and 10, while reducing the fractions of clusters 1, 3, 6, and 8 ( B). We next examined the specific gene expression within these subclusters. We found that cluster 0 significantly expressed the cell proliferation marker Ccnd1, while cluster 3 was identified as amacrine by the expression of Tfap2b ( C). RGC markers were identified in clusters 1, 2, and 4–10, expressing Isl1, Gap43, Nefl, Pou4f1, Pou4f2, and Ebf1 ( C). Several RGC subtype markers were observed, including Opn4 in cluster 2, Cartpt in cluster 1, Satb1 primarily in cluster 1, and Foxp2 primarily in cluster 5 ( C). Additionally, some Ccnd1+ cells co-expressed pan-RGC or specific RGC subtype markers, which may reflect a transitional proliferative state during RGC differentiation ( D). 
We selected several RGC subtype markers for further analysis, including intrinsically photosensitive RGC (ipRGCs; Opn4), Cartpt+ RGCs, ON-OFF direction-selective RGC (ON-OFF DS RGCs; Satb1), and F-RGCs (Foxp1/Foxp2). Data showed that Tgfβr2 KO increased the fraction of these RGC subtypes, particularly Foxp1 and Foxp2 ( A). The primary cluster (cluster 2) expressing Opn4 showed no difference between control and Tgfβr2 KO ( B and 5C), suggesting that other Opn4+ cells outside cluster 2 contribute to the difference. However, the differences in fraction and function among RGC subtypes in adult mice should be validated in future studies. Notably, Tgfβr2 KO also upregulated the fractions of RGCs expressing axon guidance receptors, such as Robo1, Epha4, Ephb2, and Nrp1 ( B), suggesting Tgfβr2 plays a key role in axon guidance during development. 
We next compared the RGC subclusters that were upregulated (clusters 5/10) or downregulated (clusters 1/6/8) by Tgfβr2 deletion or showing proliferative marker (cluster 0). Since Tfap2b was highly expressed in cluster 3, which is considered as amacrine, and cluster 2, 4, 7, and 9 took similar expressions between control and Tgfβr2 KO, these clusters will be excluded in the following analysis. Compared to clusters 1/6,/8, clusters 5/10 were enriched in subtype markers, including Foxp1 and Foxp2 ( C). Conversely, compared to clusters 5/10, clusters 1/6/8 highly expressed Robo1 (a receptor of RGC axon guidance cue, Slit protein), and Sema3a (a chemorepellent for RGC axon guidance). We also compared cluster 5/10 to cluster 0. Data showed that Gap43 and Sncg were expressed in both groups with similar expression levels, suggesting Gap43 and Sncg are RGC markers expressed in proliferative and mature stages ( D). Tgfβr2 KO increases both gene expressions, confirming the role of Tgfβr2 in RGC fate ( D). Finally, GO analysis revealed that clusters 5/10 exhibited upregulation of genes associated with eye development compared to clusters 1/6/8 ( E), suggesting that Tgfβr2 KO promotes eye development. Furthermore, clusters 5/10 showed increased expression of genes related to axon guidance and neuron projection guidance relative to clusters 0 ( F), suggesting that the RGC axon guidance process primarily occurs in non-proliferative RGCs. Pseudotime trajectory analysis shows that RGCs started differentiating from cluster 0, with amacrine cells observed in cluster 3. The other trajectories were directed toward different RGC subtypes ( G–6I). The cluster 0 was slightly increased in the Tgfβr2 KO eyes compared to the control group ( J).

## Discussion
This study uncovers a central role of the GDF-TGFβR2-Smad2 signaling axis in coordinating retinal cell fate, highlighting how distinct ligands within the same superfamily exert opposing yet complementary effects on RGC development. We demonstrate that GDF-11 acts as a suppressor of RGC differentiation through an Atoh7-dependent, TGFβR2-Smad2 pathway, while GDF-15 promotes RGC formation independently of Atoh7, likely through SoxC transcription factors ( ). The striking observation that GDF-15 deletion eliminates the RGC increase seen in GDF-11 knockout mice highlights the linkage of these two ligands and identifies SoxC TFs as a critical downstream point of convergence. This interplay provides a conceptual framework for understanding how lineage-specific transcriptional networks are integrated during retinal neurogenesis. Our findings also refine the understanding of GDF-11’s role in photoreceptor development. While exogenous GDF-11 promotes photoreceptor differentiation in embryonic explants, genetic deletion of GDF-11 did not diminish photoreceptor numbers in vivo, suggesting redundancy or compensation by other TGFβ superfamily members. By contrast, TGFβR2 deletion produced profound changes in retinal composition, including enhanced RGC formation, expansion of specific RGC subtypes (ipRGCs, ON-OFF direction-selective RGCs, and Cartpt+ RGCs) gene expression, and impaired photoreceptor development. These results position TGFβR2 as the principal receptor mediating GDF-11’s effects on RGCs and photoreceptors, thereby establishing its role as a pivotal determinant of lineage balance in the developing retina. 
TGFβ signaling, mediated by TGFβR2, has been implicated in numerous retinal cell development, including photoreceptor , , and amacrine cells. However, its specific contribution to RGC lineage commitment and maturation remains poorly defined. Our previous work showed that GDF-11 induces Smad2 signaling. Moreover, our current work indicates that GDF-11 fails to suppress RGC differentiation in the TGFβR2 KO retinas, suggesting that TGFβR2 is crucial in regulating RGC differentiation and is a receptor of GDF-11. Unlike GDF-11, GDF-15 does not induce Smad2 signaling and is identified as a key appetite-regulating hormone involved in body-weight regulation through the GFRAL/RET receptor. , Our previous work demonstrated that the RET gene is highly expressed in RGCs at E14, suggesting that RET may be a receptor of GDF-15 and plays a potential role in early RGC development.
Beyond cell fate specification, our data reveal broader functions of TGFβR2 in neural circuit assembly. Axonal guidance is critical during RGC development, relying on interactions between axonal receptors and environmental ligands. , , Transcriptomic profiling showed upregulation of axon guidance receptors such as Robo1, Epha4, Ephb2, and Nrp1 following Tgfβr2 deletion, implicating this receptor in directing axon trajectory and synaptic targeting. These alterations suggest that TGFβR2 integrates fate specification with wiring decisions, coupling cell identity to circuit formation. Moreover, changes in amacrine cell transcriptional programs, including reduced Robo1 expression, point to additional roles for TGFβR2 in interneuron diversification through modulation of Slit-Robo signaling. At the molecular level, our results suggest that TGFβR2 exerts dual signaling roles: canonical Smad2-dependent suppression of Atoh7 and noncanonical regulation of alternative pathways, as reflected in the increased expression of Sox4/11 and Foxp1/2. The enrichment of Foxp family members in Tgfβr2 knockouts is particularly notable, as these transcription factors are linked to F-RGC subtype specification and direction-selective circuit formation. This raises the possibility that TGFβR2 controls not only the overall size of the RGC pool but also the relative representation of functionally specialized subtypes. As a future direction, performing immunohistochemistry using classic subtype markers will be important for quantifying cell type abundance and validating that the observed subtype differences are biologically meaningful.
TGFβR2 has been identified as a target of several miRNAs with well-characterized roles in retinal biology. For example, during the differentiation of human parthenogenetic embryonic stem cells into retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, downregulation of miR-302 leads to the upregulation of Tgfβr2, suggesting that this receptor may participate in maintaining pluripotency-associated pathways. In addition, bioinformatic analyses predict that Tgfβr2 is targeted by miR-130a-3p, miR-181a/b-5p, miR-93-5p, and miR-9-5p, which are strongly enriched in developmental signaling networks. These miRNAs are known to regulate processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, neuronal survival, and angiogenesis, all of which are essential for normal retinal development and function. Thus, incorporating miRNA sequencing into future analyses could uncover additional layers of post-transcriptional regulation of Tgfβr2 and identify molecular players that fine-tune RGC differentiation and subtype specification. Such insights would not only deepen our mechanistic understanding but may also inform strategies to manipulate RGC fate decisions in vitro and optimize cell replacement therapies.
Taken together, these findings outline two distinct but intersecting mechanisms for RGC formation: GDF-11/TGFβR2/Smad2, which restricts differentiation to ensure appropriate lineage allocation, and GDF-15/RET/SoxC, which promotes maturation and expansion of RGCs. The interplay between these pathways ensures robustness in RGC production while permitting subtype diversification and circuit assembly. By identifying TGFβR2 as a central node in this regulatory network, our work highlights a receptor that simultaneously governs cell fate, proliferative dynamics, subtype specification, and axonal guidance. The implications of these findings extend beyond developmental biology. In regenerative contexts, manipulating TGFβR2 signaling may provide a means to bias human pluripotent stem cells toward RGCs or photoreceptors, thereby enhancing the efficiency of cell replacement therapies. Moreover, the link between TGFβR2 and axon guidance receptors suggests that targeting this pathway could influence not only the generation of RGCs but also their ability to integrate into host circuitry, a major barrier in optic nerve regeneration strategies. By uncovering how GDF ligands shape RGC fate through distinct receptor pathways, this work offers a mechanistic foundation for developing therapeutic approaches that combine neuroprotection, regeneration, and circuit repair in optic neuropathies and other neurodegenerative diseases.

## Limitations of the study
This study provides a valuable foundation for understanding retinal cell fate specification and highlights potential therapeutic avenues for retinal cell replacement. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although TGFβR2 expression decreased at E14.5, it remained detectable throughout retinal development. Since RGC differentiation spans E12 to P0, and immature RGCs and differentiating photoreceptors are still present at P0, our analysis is most suitable for investigating mid-to-late stages of development, cell maturation, and vision formation. , , , Inclusion of additional developmental time points, such as E14.5 and P5, would yield a more comprehensive view of both early and late retinal processes. Moreover, our use of the Chx10-Cre driver introduces limitations, as its mosaic expression leads to incomplete recombination across retinal progenitors. This heterogeneity may complicate the interpretation of gene function and phenotypic outcomes. , , In addition, our conclusions regarding receptor–ligand specificity rely primarily on loss-of-function genetics and transcriptomics, which, while informative, do not establish direct biophysical interactions between GDF ligands and candidate receptors such as TGFβR2 or RET. Future receptor-ligand binding assays and conditional receptor knockouts will be necessary to validate these relationships. Similarly, although scRNA-seq revealed subtype changes in RGCs, their biological significance remains to be confirmed at the protein and functional levels through immunohistochemistry, subtype-specific markers, and circuit-mapping approaches. Furthermore, our findings are derived from murine models, and the degree to which these mechanisms are conserved in primate or human retinal development remains uncertain. Finally, while we identified correlations between TGFβR2 deletion and axon guidance gene expression, direct causal links between receptor signaling and axonal pathfinding were not established. Addressing these limitations will be essential for translating our mechanistic insights into clinically relevant strategies for retinal repair.

## Method details

## Tissue treatments
Mouse recombinant GDF-11 and GDF-15 were purchased from R&D systems. Primary retinal tissues were isolated from E14.5 mouse eyes, following a previous study. Timed-pregnant mice were euthanized and the retinas from E14.5 mouse pups were swiftly dissected. Retinal tissues were cultured in retinal progenitor cell (RPC) media prepared with DMEM/F12 medium containing glucose (0.6%), GS21 (1:100), Sato supplement (1:100), insulin (5 μg/mL), epidermal growth factor (20 ng/mL), fibroblast growth factor (20 ng/mL), penicillin/streptomycin (1%). Each well contained 3–4 retinal tissues and was cultured with 500 μL of RPC medium in a humidified atmosphere incubator at 5% CO2, 37°C. E14.5 retinal tissues were treated with GDFs for 5 days for RGC marker detection. To study Smad signaling pathways, E14.5 retinal tissues were treated with GDF-11 for 1 h for p-Smad2 and p-Tgfβr2 detection, or 5 days for Brn3a and Recoverin measurement.

## Western blotting
Protein samples were collected in Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) and heated to 95°C for 10 min. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE (Invitrogen) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), using a semi-dry blotter (Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-p-Tgfβr2, Tgfβr2, p-Smad2, Smad2 (1:1000), GAPDH (1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using the Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad). Chemiluminescence was detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system. Western blots were performed in triplicate for densitometry quantification and statistical comparison. ## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from retinal tissue according to the manufacturer’s protocol (RNeasy plus micro kit, Qiagen). RNA (5 μg) was reverse transcribed using iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). For qRT-PCR, the TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Taqman primers for mouse GAPDH, Atoh7, Rbpms, Brn3a (Pou4f1), Rest, Sox4, Sox11, and Recoverin were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA USA). GADPH was used as an internal control. All qRT-PCR data was analyzed by 2ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times for statistical comparison.

## Immunostaining
Eye globes were fixed with 4% PFA for an hour, followed by sequential overnight immersion in 15% and 30% sucrose. The eyes were frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, sectioned at 10 μm thickness, blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS) for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-Brn3a antibody (1:200), rabbit anti-Recoverin (1:500), mouse anti-Otx2 (1:200), rabbit anti-cleaved Caspase-3 (1:500) and guinea pig anti-Rbpms (1:500) in a humidity chamber. Goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 antibody, donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 55, goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and DAPI were used for fluorescence and nuclei detection, respectively. All images were taken using fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss) or confocal microscopy (LSM 880, Zeiss). Three independent replications were conducted in the study. To prevent sampling bias, all the microscope counts were performed blind to the genotype. Cell numbers in retinal sections were quantified and normalized to the linear micrometer length of the retinal cell layer. The central section containing the optic nerve head was used as a standard for slide selection during cell counting.

## Cell dissociation for 10X genomics
Retinas from P0 mice were isolated and dissociated using papain (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. After mixing with Low-ovomucoid buffer (1.5% BSA, 1.5% trypsin inhibitor dissolved in DPBS, pH 7.4), retinas were centrifuged at 400xg for 5 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Falcon, 352340) to remove cell clumps. Cell pellets were labeled with Cell Multiplexing Oligo (10X Genomics). 10,000 cells were then input into the 10X library construction. GEM generation, reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and library construction steps were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions (Chromium Single Cell 3′v1/v2/v3 platform, 10X Genomics). Libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) with paired-end 150 bp reads.

## scRNA-sequencing analysis

## Data processing
We applied fastp with the default parameters to filter the adaptor sequence and remove the low-quality reads to achieve clean data. Then the feature-barcode matrices were obtained by aligning reads to the mm10 genome using CellRanger v7.2.0. Seurat analysis was performed in R using Seurat (v5.1.0). The data was normalized and passed quality control to remove dead cells. All datasets were integrated and scaled. Cell clustering was visualized by UMAP dimensional reduction and cell types were identified by different markers. The two clusters of photoreceptors (clusters 2 and 4) were subset for the following analysis. Similarly, the RGC/AC cluster was subset and re-clustered. The datasets generated during this study are available on GEO: GSE277570. Materials are available upon reasonable request.

## Pseudotime analyses
We applied the single-cell Trajectory analysis utilizing Monocle 3 (v0.2.2.0) with default parameters. The photoreceptors were performed a completely “unsupervised” analysis for its machine learning approach. Batch effects were removed by aligning across samples.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Protein intensities on Western blot were assessed using ImageJ. Average intensity per pixel values were recorded by selecting a rectangular area along each band, with the background subtracted. qRT-PCR data were analyzed by the 2ΔΔCt method, and gene expression levels were represented as bar charts. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindMarkers function on log2-normalized and scaled data, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied, with all other parameters set to their default. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism for either group or column comparison. Results were shown as the Means ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. “N” numbers in all experiments refer to the number of independent replications. Comparisons between the two groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. For multiple groups, Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
