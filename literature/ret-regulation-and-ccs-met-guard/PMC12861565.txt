## Abstract
Intracranial mesenchymal tumors (IMTs) with FET::CREB fusion are newly recognized molecular entities, provisionally classified into subgroups A and B. Although Group B has been partially characterized, the clinicopathological and molecular heterogeneity of Group A remains poorly defined. This study aimed to conduct an integrated analysis of 6 newly diagnosed and 20 previously reported IMTs with FET::CREB fusion. Notably, Group A was further stratified into two distinct entities A1 and A2 based on unsupervised methylation profiling. Compared to Group A1, Group A2 demonstrated significantly shorter progression‐free survival (PFS), a higher proportion of male patients, and less frequent occurrence of myxoid‐rich stroma. Amplification of 10p15.3 was frequently observed in Group A2. Furthermore, GLUT‐1 could serve as a potential diagnostic indicator in IMTs with FET::CREB fusion. Overall, we identified a new subgroup of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion with poor PFS and distinct clinicopathological and molecular features, offering actionable insights to refine therapeutic strategies and improve risk stratification in this emerging diagnostic category.
Keywords: DNA methylation, FET::CREB fusion, intracranial mesenchymal tumors

Six new cases of intracranial mesenchymal tumors with FET::CREB fusion, alongside 20 previously reported cases underwent DNA methylation profiling uncovered a novel epigenetic subgroup distinguished by inferior PFS and unique clinicopathological, molecular, and epigenetic hallmarks. GLUT‐1 could serve as a potential diagnostic indicator in IMTs with FET::CREB fusion.

## INTRODUCTION
Intracranial mesenchymal tumors (IMTs) with FET::CREB fusion are rare tumors of the central nervous system (CNS), previously described as angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma (AFH) or intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumors (IMMTs). , , IMTs with FET::CREB fusion share molecular homology with extracranial neoplasms driven by recurrent fusions between FET family RNA‐binding proteins (EWSR1 or FUS) and CREB‐family transcription factors (CREM, CREB1, or ATF1). These tumors include AFH, primary pulmonary myxoid sarcoma, gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors, clear‐cell sarcoma (CCS), and hyalinizing clear‐cell carcinoma of the salivary gland. , , , , , , , , These pathognomonic fusions constitute the primary diagnostic criterion for IMTs with FET::CREB fusion.
The histopathological features of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion are diverse, encompassing a dense lymphoplasmacytic cuffing at the tumor periphery, myxoid‐rich stroma, collagenous stroma, pseudoangiomatous spaces, and meningothelial‐like whorls. Immunophenotypically, tumors consistently express Vimentin, CD99, EMA, Desmin, and CD68. Negative expression of HMB45, MelanA, GFAP, Olig2, STAT6, SSTR2, CD34, and cytokeratin AE1/AE3 could distinguish IMTs with FET::CREB fusion from CCS, glioblastoma, meningioma, and metastatic carcinoma.
Studies utilizing methylation‐based clustering analysis have demonstrated that IMTs with FET::CREB fusion can be stratified into two provisional molecular subtypes: Group A (associated with improved clinical outcomes) and Group B (exhibiting aggressive behavior). As this tumor represents a newly classified category by the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of reported cases remains limited. The molecular stratification, the key clinicopathological features, including histomorphological diversity, immunophenotypic patterns, and molecular heterogeneity remains insufficiently characterized. To address these gaps and identify diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, this study aimed to perform comprehensive DNA methylation profiling of 6 newly diagnosed and 20 previously published cases of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Samples and clinical data collection
Tumor samples and clinical data of six patients harboring IMTs with FET: CREB fusion were obtained from the Institute of Pathology and Southwest Cancer Center, Southwest Hospital, Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University), Chongqing, China; Department of Pathology, Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital, Chongqing, China; Department of Pathology, The Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, Qingdao, China; Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC, Anhui, China; Department of Pathology, Guangdong 999 Brain Hospital, Guangdong, China; and Department of Pathology, Henan Provincial People's Hospital, Henan, China. All sections were examined by two neuropathologists (Xiao‐Hong Yao and Tao Luo). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution (approval number: KY2024050).

2.2.

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemistry was performed using formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the following antibodies: CD99 (clone MX111, undiluted; Maixin, Fuzhou, China), Desmin (clone MX046, undiluted; Maixin), D2‐40 (clone D2‐40, undiluted; Maixin), CD34 (clone MX123, 1:150 dilution; Maixin), CD68 (clone KPI, undiluted; Maixin), Vimentin (clone UMAB159, undiluted; ZSGB, Beijing, China), EMA (clone GP14, 1:150 dilution; ZSGB), S100 (clone 15E2E2 + 4C4.9, 1:200 dilution; ZSGB), GFAP (clone UMAB129, undiluted; ZSGB), MUC4 (clone 8G7, undiluted; ZSGB), GLUT‐1 (clone ZA‐0471, undiluted; ZSGB), STAT6 (clone EP325, undiluted; ZSGB), SSTR2 (clone EP149, undiluted; ZSGB), HMB45 (clone HMB45, undiluted; ZSGB), MelanA (clone A103, undiluted; ZSGB), Myogenin (clone EP162, undiluted; ZSGB), MyoD1 (clone EP212, undiluted; ZSGB), and Ki‐67 (clone MIB‐1, undiluted; ZSGB). All antibodies were applied using a fully automatic immunohistochemical staining system from Bench Mark ULTRA.

2.3.

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis
FISH analysis was performed using 4‐μm paraffin sections to detect EWSR1 gene breakage. Processing, repair, digestion, denaturation, hybridization, and rinsing were performed. The EWSR1 probe (Anbiping, Guangzhou, China) was used, with red fluorescence at the 5′ end and green fluorescence at the 3′ end of the EWSR1 gene. In the nucleus of the wild‐type EWSR1 probe, two fused signals were observed, and 100 cells were counted. The presence of one red signal, one green signal, and one fused signal in the nucleus, comprising approximately 15% of the total signal, indicated EWSR1 breakage.

2.4.

## 
DNA next‐generation sequencing (NGS)
For the formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded samples, 10‐μm tumor slices were used to extract DNA using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Libraries were prepared following the previously reported methods. Hybridization‐based target enrichment was performed using the GeneseeqOne™ pan‐cancer gene panel (494 cancer‐relevant genes, Geneseeq Technology Inc.) in conjunction with the xGen Lockdown Hybridization and Wash Reagents Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies). Libraries enriched for the target were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina) using 2 × 150 bp paired‐end reads. Single nucleotide polymorphisms and indels were identified using VarScan2 and the Haplotype Caller/Unified GenoTyper in GATK. Gene fusions were identified using FACTERA, and copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed using ADTEx.

2.5.

## 
DNA methylation profiling
Genomic DNA was extracted from tissue samples using the Tiangen DNA Extraction Kit. Bisulfite conversion was subsequently performed using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (D5001, Zymo Research), preserving methylated cytosines while converting unmethylated cytosines to uracil. Converted DNA was profiled using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Raw methylation data files (.idat) were analyzed and compared against the following reference datasets: GSE109381, GSE140686, GSE85218, GSE152653, GSE136361, GSE172081, GSE138221, and GSE190362. Raw signal intensities were extracted from the .idat files using the minfi package (version 1.48.0) within Bioconductor (version 3.18) under R version 4.4.3. Data normalization was performed via the preprocess Quantile function. Methylation probes localized to the XY chromosomes or overlapping single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites were excluded, as were non‐informative probes specific to the 450 K array. Beta values were then calculated. For cluster analysis, the 20,000 CpG sites exhibiting the highest methylation variability (measured by standard deviation) were selected.
2.5.1.

## t‐SNE

Analysis of the methylation chip data was performed using the t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE) method (perplexity = 20, max_iter = 2500, other parameters default) with the Rtsne package (version 0.17). For samples of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion, only change the perplexity = 3/5 (keeping all other parameters unchanged).

2.5.2.

## UMAP
The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method (method = naïve, n_neighbors = 20, other parameters default) with UMAP package (version 0.2.10.0). For samples of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion, only change the perplexity = 3/5 (keeping all other parameters unchanged).

2.5.3.

## Reference samples
Reference samples were selected based on their histological and molecular profiles, which were highly analogous to those observed in IMTs with FET::CREB fusion. These tumors include: atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor, subclass SHH (ATRT_SHH), atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor, subclass MYC (ATRT_MYC), atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor, subclass TYR (ATRT_TYR), hemangioblastoma (HMB), low‐grade glioma, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (LGG_DNT), meningioma (MNG), schwannoma (SCHW), solitary fibrous tumor/hemangiopericytoma (SFT/HPC), CNS Ewing sarcoma family tumor with CIC alteration (EFT_CIC), chordoid glioma of the third ventricle (CHGL), angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma (AFH), Clear Cell Sarcoma (CCS) and Chordoma (CHORDM)

2.5.4.

## Unsupervised hierarchical clustering
Unsupervised hierarchical clustering (column_km = 3, other parameters remain default) of 26 cases in IMTs with FET: CREB fusion was performed using the Complex Heatmap package (version 2.18.0).

2.5.5.

## Correlation analysis of methylation patterns between samples
Methylation raw data were processed using the ChAMP package (version 2.34.0) for quality control, normalization, and batch correction. The 500 most differentially methylated sites were then selected for inter‐sample correlation analysis. A correlation heatmap was generated using the ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.18.0).

2.5.6.

## Copy number variation analysis
CNV analysis was performed using the conumee package (version 1.26.0). Seven normal human brain tissue samples were included as reference controls for data standardization. Then GISTIC2.0 (reference genome: hg19, other parameters default) was employed to identify statistically significant peak regions of genomic amplification and deletion. Subsequently, the maftools package (version 2.18.0) was utilized to visualize the somatic alteration landscape.

2.5.7.

## Fraction of genome altered
CINmetrics package (version 0.1.0) was used to analyze CNV data; any segment with a log2 ratio exceeding ±0.2 was defined as a segmental alteration. The proportion covered by the changes of each chromosome segment in the sample was calculated as the Fraction of Genome Altered of chromosome (FGA_chr). The sum of the genome change scores of the 22 chromosomes (not counting the X and Y chromosomes) was determined as the sample FGA. To eliminate confounding effects of unbalanced translocations on FGA, the modified genome change score (FGA_modified) was obtained by excluding the genome change scores of the two chromosomes where the sample fusion gene was located. For instance, in a case harboring an EWSR1::CREM gene fusion, where EWSR1 is located on chromosome 22 and CREM on chromosome 10, the FGA_modified was calculated as the sum of the fraction of FGA_chr across the remaining 20 chromosomes, excluding chromosomes 10 and 22. The fraction of the genome altered ranges from 0 (no segment) to 1 (all segment). Prior to heatmap generation using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.20.0), FGA values were Min‐Max normalized.

2.5.8.

## Prediction of cellular composition in tumors
MethylCIBERSORT was used to estimate tumor cells and various tumor microenvironment (TME) cell components in each sample, using a previously generated custom CNS tumor signature matrix at the National Cancer Institute as a reference. The Wilcoxon test with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction for p‐values was used to analyze differences in cellular components among groups, and results were visualized using the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1).

2.5.9.

## Random forest (RF) modeling
The brain tumor reference data contain 2801 samples and 91 sample types ; the sarcoma reference data contain 1077 samples and 65 sample types. The data was randomly divided into a training set and an independent test set at a ratio of 80% to 20%. Methylation chip analysis was performed under R 4.3.1 version, using the minfi package (version 1.48.0) in Bioconductor (version 3.18) to obtain the raw signal strength from the methylation chip raw file IDAT. The combineArrays function was used to perform combined analysis on EPIC V1 and 450 K microarray data; the preprocess Quantile function was applied to homogenize the data. The methylation probes on the XY chromosome and covering SNP sites were removed. Furthermore, non‐informative features on the 450 K array were also excluded. The RF classifier was implemented using the randomForest package (parameters: ntree = 10,000, mtry = sqrt (n), others default). Initially, an RF model was trained to identify the top 10,000 most discriminative probes, which were then used to rebuild an optimized RF model. The nested cross‐validation strategy was employed to prevent data leakage. This consisted of two layers of loops. Outer loop: three‐fold cross‐validation was applied, which was a process of evaluating the performance of a machine learning model by dividing the data into three equal parts, two for training and one for testing. Inner loop: a further three‐fold cross‐validation was applied to the training subsets for hyperparameter tuning. To enhance the precision of the model's predictions, Ridge Regression was applied for post‐RF calibration via the glmnet package. The regularization parameter λ was optimized across a logarithmic search space (lambda.min.ratio = 10−6), with other parameters kept at default values, yielding the final RF model.

2.6.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and SPSS Statistics 27. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to analyze the progression‐free survival (PFS, the time from diagnosis to tumor progression or death) and overall survival (OS, the time from diagnosis to death from any cause). Prognostic factors were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models implemented with the survival (version 3.6‐4) and survminer (version 0.4.9) packages in R. Initial screening was performed via univariable analysis (significance threshold: p < 0.05), followed by backward stepwise multivariable regression using the same significance level for variable retention. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyze the ages of the three groups. Fisher's exact test was used to compare sex distribution and presence of myxoid‐rich stroma among the three groups using pairwise comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare FGA scores between the three epigenetic groups pairwise. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## 
DNA methylation cluster analysis of IMTs with FET::CREB Fusion
The t‐SNE (Figure  ) and UMAP (Figure  ) analysis of DNA methylation data confirmed that the six newly diagnosed cases clustered with previously reported cases of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion (n = 20). Among the 26 cases analyzed, 25 formed a cluster clearly distinct from potential diagnostic mimics, while one case (Case 4) exhibited proximity to AFH in the epigenetic landscape. Previous studies have demonstrated that DNA methylation profiles are determined by two fundamental factors: cellular origin and driver genetic alterations. Notably, AFH and IMTs with FET::CREB fusion share highly similar driver mutations. This molecular similarity likely explains why Case 4 exhibited both spatial proximity to AFH clusters in t‐SNE visualization (Figure  ) and low classification scores in our sarcoma RF model (Table  ). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of DNA methylation data revealed that the 26 cases could be classified into two distinct subgroups: Group A (n = 21) and Group B (n = 5), which were consistent with previous reports. However, further unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis showed that Group A could be subdivided into two subgroups: Group A1 (n = 14) and Group A2 (n = 7) (Figure  ), among the six cases in this study, Cases 2, 4, 5, and 6 were classified as Group A1, Case 3 was classified as Group A2, and Case 1 was classified as Group B.
 Both t‐SNE (Figure  ) and UMAP (Figure  ) analysis of DNA methylation data, which were performed across multiple perplexity parameters, consistently corroborated the clustering patterns identified through unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Figure  ). Complementary methylation correlation analysis, as visualized in the heatmap (Figure  ), demonstrated significant intergroup differences, thereby providing independent validation of the distinct molecular subgroups initially revealed by unsupervised approaches.
Kaplan–Meier analysis of 24 evaluable cases (with two exclusions due to missing follow‐up: one from the 6‐case cohort and one from the 20‐case cohort) demonstrated significantly shorter PFS in Group B compared to Group A (median PFS: 6 vs. 49 months; HR = 5.42; 95% CI: 1.41‐20.92; p = 0.007), consistent with established literature (Figure  ). Group A2 showed significantly shorter PFS than Group A1 (median: 12 vs. 49 months; HR = 4.61; 95% CI: 0.98‐21.74; p = 0.043) (Figure  ). No significant difference was observed between Group A2 and Group B (median: 12 vs. 6 months; HR = 1.86; 95% CI: 0.53‐9.22; p = 0.311) (Figure  ). These findings suggest that Group A1 represents a distinct favorable prognostic subset and Group A2 and Group B share comparable survival outcomes.

3.2.

## Clinical characteristics of epigenetic subgroups
The clinical characterization of the 26‐case cohort revealed that the mean age of onset in Group A2 was 25 years (range: 10–70) and in Group A1 was 21.2 years (range: 13–39), the mean age at onset in Group B was 13 years (range: 4–32 years). Notably, Group A2 exhibited a higher proportion of male patients, whereas female patients were more frequent in Group A1 (p = 0.006, Table  ). The tumors of Group A2 were frequently observed in the cerebral convexity, cerebellopontine angle, and tentorium. The tumors of Group A1 were more commonly associated with lesions in the cerebral convexity and lateral ventricle. The tumors of Group B were more frequently observed in patients with cerebral convexity (Table  ). The most prevalent symptom was headaches in six cases of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion (Table  ). All six patients underwent gross total resection, and two patients received radiotherapy. Clinical follow‐up of the five patients revealed no recurrence or metastasis (Table  ). The radiological features of four cases (Cases 1, 2, 4, and 5; Figure  ) were remarkably consistent, showing a clear tumor border, cystic and solid lesions, substantial peritumoral edema, and pronounced contrast enhancement in the tumor region. A dural tail sign was observed in Case 4 (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Histologic characteristics of epigenetic subgroups
In this study, IMTs with FET::CREB fusion exhibited a variety of histological features (Table  ). Specifically, all six tumors exhibited clear boundaries from the surrounding normal brain tissue with no invasive manifestations. Histopathological analysis of six cases revealed distinct morphological features, including (1) stromal heterogeneity with myxoid‐rich (4/6 cases; Figure  , a, b, e) and collagenous (5/6; Figure  , c, f) patterns, as well as case‐specific pseudoangiomatous spaces and calcifications (Case 3; Figure  , g, h); (2) inflammatory features comprising hemosiderin deposition (3/6; Figure  , d), hemorrhage (3/6; Figure  , d) and variable lymphoplasmacytic infiltration (focal in 3/6 cases, Figure  , l; peripheral cuffing in 2/6 cases‐diagnostic indicator, Figure  , j; perivascular in Case 1, Figure  , k); (3) cellular diversity characterized by epithelioid/rhabdoid (5/6; Figure  , a), round/oval (4/6; Figure  , b, i), and spindle/stellate (3/6; Figure  , e, f) phenotypes (Table  ). Comparative analysis showed significantly higher prevalence of myxoid‐rich stroma in Group A1 (12/14 cases, 85.7%) compared to Group B (1/5, 20.0%; p = 0.017, Table  ), with intermediate frequency in Group A2 (3/7, 42.9%), while Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated no association between myxoid stroma abundance and PFS in the 24‐case cohort with follow‐up information (log‐rank p = 0.59, Figure  ). The predominant cell morphology observed in Group A1 was spindle/stellate cells (8/14, 57.1%), followed by epithelioid/rhabdoid cells (4/14, 28.6%) and round/oval cells (2/14, 14.3%) (Table  ). The predominant cell morphology observed in Group A2 was epithelioid/rhabdoid cells (4/7, 57.1%), followed by spindle/stellate cells (3/7, 42.9%) (Table  ). The predominant cell morphology in Group B was round/oval cells (3/5, 60.0%) and epithelioid/rhabdoid cells (2/5, 40.0%). Meanwhile, the predominant spindle/stellate cell morphology was not observed in Group B. The predominant cell morphology of round/oval cells was not observed in Group A2 (Table  ).
 Comprehensive methylation profiling of tumor and microenvironment components in 26 cases showed Group A2 exhibited significantly higher tumor cell (Tc, p = 0.001) but lower monocyte (Mono, p = 0.001)   and Treg cell (p = 0.025) fractions versus Group A1, which was featured by a Tc‐dominated/immune‐depleted profile closely resembling Group B (p >0.05), while Group B uniquely showed elevated endothelial cell (Ec) versus both Group A1 (p = 0.0005) and A2 (p = 0.021) (Figure  ). The results suggest A2‐A1 divergence in tumor/immune ecology and A2‐B shared microenvironment except vascular features.

3.4.

## Molecular characteristics of epigenetic subgroups
NGS revealed EWSR1::CREM fusion in Cases 1–3, EWSR1::ATF1 fusion in Cases 4–5, and EWSR1::CREB1 fusion in Case 6 (Table  ). Furthermore, none of the genes previously associated with poor prognosis in CNS tumors were detected in this cohort of six cases (e.g., TP53, TERT, and CDKN2A/B). Though no significant differences were observed in gene fusions among the three groups, EWSR1::CREB1 and EWSR1::ATF1 fusions were frequently observed in Group A1, EWSR1::ATF1 in Group A2, and EWSR1::CREM in Group B. Moreover, FUS::CREM fusion was only detected in Group B, whereas EWSR1::CREB1 fusion was not observed in Group B, which may be attributed to the small sample size (Table  ).
The chromosomal profiles of the three groups were analyzed. The amplification of 17q21.31 and deletion of 16q23.3 were frequently observed in Groups A1 and A2. Notably, 17q21.31 amplification was rarely observed in Group B, whereas 15q11.2 amplification was commonly observed in Groups A1 and B, but not in Group A2 (Figure  ). Furthermore, 10p15.3 amplification was frequently observed in Group A2. Chromosome deletions were more common in Group B, with the 11q12.1 deletion being the most frequent site, which may serve as an indicator for differentiating between Groups A and B (Figure  ).
 Chromosomal Fraction of Genome Altered (FGA) quantifies the proportion of a specific chromosome affected by copy number alterations (CNAs) or structural variants (SVs), which are associated with prognosis in various tumor types. FGA analysis across the three groups (A1, A2, B) revealed that Group B demonstrated significantly higher FGA scores than Group A1 (p = 0.02, Figure  ), consistent with its enrichment for unbalanced translocations (EWSR1::CREM/FUS::CREM) versus the balanced translocations (EWSR1::CREB1/ATF1) predominant in Group A1. To account for confounding by unbalanced translocations, we calculated modified FGA scores excluding affected loci (chr22/EWSR1, chr 10/CREM, chr 16/FUS). While these regions showed markedly elevated FGA in the heatmap (Figure  ), modified scores showed no intergroup significance (Figure  ). Survival analysis of 24 cases demonstrated that mean‐based FGA stratification significantly predicted PFS (p = 0.036, Figure  ), though this association was lost with median‐based (p = 0.45, Figure  ) or modified‐FGA approaches (mean: p = 0.40, Figure S3D; median: p = 0.45, Figure  ). While univariable analysis linked both FGA scores to PFS (p = 0.02, Table  ), multivariable adjustment nullified this association (Table  ), suggesting the prognostic value of FGA may be context‐dependent. Collectively, these data indicate that although FGA is confounded by translocation type, it may reflect underlying genomic instability patterns relevant to clinical outcomes, warranting validation in expanded cohorts.

3.5.

## Immunohistochemical and FISH characteristics of epigenetic subgroups
Vimentin was uniformly expressed in all tumors. CD99 positivity was observed in 5/6 cases. EMA was variably stained with diffuse expression in 3/6 cases and focal expression in 1/6 cases. Desmin, D2‐40, and S‐100 exhibited similar expression patterns to EMA. CD68 was diffusely expressed in 2/6 cases. The tumors were negative for STAT6, SSTR2, CD34, HMB45, MelanA, Myogenin, and MyoD1 (Figure  ; Table  ). Previous literature identified MUC4 as a promising immunohistochemical marker for pathological diagnosis , its diagnostic utility in our cohort was limited: diffuse staining was observed in merely 1/6 cases, and focal expression occurred in another 1/6 cases. The mean Ki‐67 labeling index was 6% (range: 1%–10%), indicating a low‐grade tumor profile (Table  ). Distinct GLUT‐1 expression profiles differentiated FET::CREB‐fused IMTs from histologic mimics, with diffuse positivity in 5 cases (5/6, 83.3%) and focal staining in 1 case (1/6, 16.7%). This pattern contrasted markedly with intracranial solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) (focal in 2/18, 11.1%) and meningiomas (focal in 4/21, 19.05%), where diffuse expression was never observed (Table  ; Figure  ). While requiring validation in larger cohorts, our data indicate GLUT‐1 immunohistochemistry could complement molecular testing in the differential diagnosis of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion. The molecular identity of the FET::CREB‐fused IMT cohort was uniformly confirmed through EWSR1 break‐apart FISH (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
The findings indicated that these 6 cases, along with 20 previously reported cases, could be classified into Group A and B through unsupervised hierarchical clustering of DNA methylation data, aligning with previous literature. Group A was further subdivided into two subgroups, A1 and A2, which exhibited distinct clinical, pathological, and molecular characteristics.
Group A2 tumors were more prevalent in males, whereas Group A1 tumors were more frequently observed in females. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a notable disparity emerged in PFS: Group A2 had a significantly inferior PFS to Group A1. However, no significant difference was observed between Groups A2 and B. A comparison of the histological features revealed that the presence of myxoid‐rich stroma significantly distinguished Group A1 (85.7% positive) from Group B (20.0%, p = 0.017), but not Group A2 from B, implying that the myxoid‐rich stroma may serve as a potential histological diagnostic indicator for Group A1. Our results demonstrated that the cellular morphology dominated by spindle/stellate cells was more common in Group A1, the cellular morphology dominated by epithelioid/rhabdoid cells was common in Group A2, and the cellular morphology dominated by round/oval cells was common in Group B.
DNA methylation‐based analysis revealed distinct TME compositions across groups, with Group A1 exhibiting significantly lower tumor cell and endothelial cell fractions compared to Groups A2 and B. Conversely, Group A1 exhibited an increase in the abundance of specific immune cell populations, notably NK cells, monocytes, and Treg cells. These findings align with established roles of NK cells, monocytes, and Tregs in inhibiting tumor progression. , The marked TME disparities, particularly the tumor cell‐low/immune‐rich profile in Group A1 versus the tumor cell‐high/immune‐depleted A2 phenotype, provide a mechanistic basis for their divergent clinical outcomes. Gene fusion type was analyzed in all three groups. EWSR1::CREB1 and EWSR1::ATF1 fusions were observed with high frequency in Group A1, while EWSR1::ATF1 fusion was frequently observed in Group A2, and EWSR1::CREM fusion was frequently observed in Group B. As indicated by the extant literature, patients exhibiting the presence of EWSR1::ATF1 fusion have been documented to demonstrate a suboptimal prognosis. 
EWSR1::ATF1 fusion has been observed in 57.1% of cases of Group A2, which may be considered a contributing factor to the shorter PFS observed in Group A2. In this study, we observed the chromosomal characteristics of the epigenetic subgroups. The amplification of 10p15.3 was frequently observed in Group A2. The amplifications of 17q21.31 and 15q11.2 were frequently observed in Group A1. The deletion of 11q12.1 was frequently observed in Group B.
A correlation has been identified between elevated FGA scores and poor prognosis across various tumor types, and higher FGA scores are associated with poorer treatment outcomes. In this study, the modified FGA scores failed to attain statistical significance across the three groups, potentially due to confounding effects from unbalanced chromosomal translocations and limited sample size. However, univariate Cox regression analysis confirmed FGA was associated with PFS. These findings suggest that FGA may serve as a PFS biomarker for IMTs with FET::CREB fusion; however, further validation in larger cohorts is required.
Immunohistochemical analysis revealed six cases of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion positive for CD99, Vimentin, EMA, Desmin, and D2‐40, consistent with previous studies. , Although CD68 and MUC4 have been reported to be expressed in most cases, their expression was only observed in 2/6 cases in the present study. This suggests that MUC4 and CD68 need to be further confirmed as diagnostic markers. The previous literature has reported four cases of diffuse GLUT1 expression in IMTs with FET::CREB fusion. , Likewise, our six cases showed GLUT‐1 expression, ranging from diffuse (5/6 cases) to focal (1/6 cases). Meningioma and SFT, which are considered differential diagnoses of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion, are mostly observed negative for GLUT‐1, with occasional focal positivity. Therefore, GLUT‐1 may be an immunohistochemical biomarker for the differential diagnosis of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion.
In summary, our study defined three biologically and clinically distinct subgroups through DNA methylation profiling, validated the epigenetic feature of the aggressive A2 subgroup, and proposed GLUT‐1 as a potential immunohistochemical marker to complement molecular diagnostics.
Although this study revealed the molecular heterogeneity of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion, some limitations warrant consideration. First, the restricted sample size (n = 5) in comparative Group B constrained the statistical power for subgroup analysis. Second, the multicenter retrospective design may introduce potential selection bias. Finally, incomplete clinical documentation regarding therapeutic interventions, particularly adjuvant therapy protocols, may limit the applicability of our results. Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter collaborations to validate our findings in expanded cohort studies. Overall, our study underscores the molecular heterogeneity of IMTs with FET::CREB fusion, providing insights for more precise classification.
