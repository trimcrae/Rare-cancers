## Abstract

## Introduction
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) rarely exhibits the TFCP2::FUS gene fusion and is even more rarely present as multiple cutaneous lesions.

## Case Presentation
We describe the case of a 48-year-old man who presented with multiple cutaneous masses eroding through the skin as well as visceral metastases following two previous resections for cutaneous neoplasms. Histopathology showed spindle morphology and positive staining for keratins, desmin, MYOD1, and myogenin. Genetic sequencing showed a noncanonical TFCP2::FUS fusion that, in combination with the immunohistochemistry, was diagnostic of RMS. He was treated with vincristine, dactinomycin, and cyclophosphamide, followed by cabozantinib and pembrolizumab, and finally pembrolizumab and concurrent radiation therapy to the targetable lesions. He had a continual, rapid progression of disease and expired 2 months following radiation.

## Conclusion
This case is notable because it presents a rare example of an aggressive, TFCP2::FUS gene fusion-positive RMS presenting with multiple cutaneous metastases, and highlights that this entity is highly resistant to multiple chemotherapies, immunotherapy, and radiation therapy.

## Introduction
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a type of soft tissue sarcoma that is derived from mesenchymal cells that differentiate into skeletal tissue [ ]. It is most commonly diagnosed in children, with only 1% of cases found in adults [ ]. There are four subtypes of RMS, which include embryonal, alveolar, pleomorphic, and spindle cell/sclerosing [ ]. Embryonal RMS is the most common and has the best prognosis, while alveolar and pleomorphic RMSs are associated with the poorest outcomes [ ]. The most common subtypes of RMS are rarely present in the skin and are treated with a multimodal approach that includes surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, as microinvasion and metastases are common [ ]. RMS has been associated with various gene fusions, the most common being the PAX3::FOXO1 fusion, which is present in approximately 55% of alveolar RMS, a subtype that comprises 20% of RMS cases overall [ ]. Conversely, TFCP2 fusions are exceedingly rare and occur in the uncommon spindle/epithelioid subtype of RMS that shows a predilection for intraosseous sites. The largest series to date reported 53 cases [ ], accounting for less than 1% of all RMS. The TFCP2 mutation is especially aggressive and is found fused with either the EWSR1 gene (37% of reported cases) or the FUS gene (63% of cases), resulting in spindle cell or epithelioid variants [ ]. We describe a rare case of an aggressive metastatic cutaneous RMS with the noncanonical TFCP2::FUS gene fusion. This adds to only 6 previously reported cases of patients presenting with cutaneous FUS::TFCP2 fusion-positive RMS [ – ].

## Case Report
A 48-year-old man presented to our sarcoma medical oncology clinic with multiple painful ulcerated skin nodules and plaques on his back, left axilla, and right axilla. Eighteen months earlier, he underwent an excision of the back lesion at an outside institution, which showed a poorly differentiated sarcoma measuring 9.5 cm with negative margins. One year after excision of the back lesion, and 5 months earlier, the back lesion started to regrow concurrently with the appearance of a nodule in the right axilla. He underwent an excisional biopsy of the right axilla nodule at an outside institution, which showed a high-grade spindle cell neoplasm with rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation, measuring 15.4 cm with negative margins and no lymphovascular invasion. New pulmonary nodules were also noted, but the patient declined further treatment. Two weeks before the current presentation, the patient presented to his local emergency department for shortness of breath. CT angiography showed diffuse metastatic lesions in both lungs, and CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed multiple low-density lesions in the liver and a 1.1 cm × 0.8 cm hypodense lesion in the main portal vein. The patient presented to our clinic to discuss further treatment options. At the initial visit, he had an indurated ulcerated plaque with heme crust involving his back measuring approximately 10 cm in longest diameter (shown in a). He also had an indurated translucent nodule involving his right axilla (shown in b) and a firm nodule involving his left axilla (shown in c).
 The pathology slides from the prior back and right axilla resections were obtained and reviewed at an intradepartmental meeting and by the hospital’s surgical pathology department. The prior punch biopsy of the original back lesion showed a malignant spindle cell neoplasm involving the dermis without involvement of the epidermis. The tumor was composed of atypical spindle cells arranged in a fascicular architecture that dissected the dermal collagen. The tumor cells had an eosinophilic cytoplasm with pleomorphic and hyperchromatic nuclei, and mitoses, including atypical forms, were easily identified. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were diffusely positive for pankeratin AE1/AE3 and positive for E-cadherin and GATA3, with 20% of the tumor cells staining for Ki-67. The tumor cells were negative for CK7 and CK20, HMB45 and Melan-A, CD45, MOC31, TTF1, NKX 3.1, chromogranin and synaptophysin, and EMA CK5/6.
The subsequent excision of the 9.5 cm back lesion showed a malignant spindle cell neoplasm involving the dermis and subcutis, with extensively ulcerated overlying epidermis that was not connected to the tumor or with cytologic atypia. The tumor had variable cellularity and atypia with large areas of atypical spindle cells arranged in fascicles. There was extensive tumor necrosis and brisk mitotic activity, including atypical forms. The margins were free of tumor, although the reported closest margins were <1 mm at multiple locations. The tumor cells stained diffusely positive for pankeratin AE1/AE3, SMA, and desmin with focal CD34 positivity, while remaining negative for CK5/6, p40, and S100 protein.
Based on this immunohistochemical profile showing positive staining for both epithelial markers (pankeratin AE1/AE3 and E-cadherin) and muscle cell markers (SMA and desmin), the patient was initially diagnosed with a poorly differentiated carcinosarcoma with rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation versus RMS. However, this initial diagnostic approach had significant limitations. The diagnosis appeared to rely heavily on keratin positivity without adequate integration of morphologic features and clinical context, as keratin expression is a well-recognized feature of many soft tissue tumors and is not exclusively indicative of epithelial differentiation or carcinoma. The fascicular spindle cell architecture was more consistent with sarcoma morphology from the outset, and true carcinosarcomas are exceedingly rare in soft tissue locations. Furthermore, no RMS-specific immunohistochemical markers (MYOD1 or myogenin) were performed at this time, which represented a significant diagnostic gap given that RMS was included in the differential diagnosis.
The subsequent excision of the 15.4 cm right axilla lesion provided crucial additional diagnostic information, showing a poorly differentiated epithelioid and spindle cell neoplasm with extensive necrosis and prominent perivascular crowding (shown in a). The tumor cells were arranged in fascicles or large nests and clusters (shown in b), with significant cytologic atypia and brisk mitotic activity, including atypical forms. Importantly, the immunohistochemical profile of the axillary lesion demonstrated diffuse positivity for pankeratin AE1/AE3 (shown in c), desmin (shown in d), MYOD1 (shown in e, f), and focal positivity for myogenin (shown in g), while remaining negative for p40, p63, EMA, SOX10, S100 protein, SMA, CD31, SS18-SSX, and SSX-C terminus antibody. H3K27Me3 expression was retained. The critical addition of MYOD1 and myogenin staining, which are specific for skeletal muscle differentiation, helped provide further evidence for RMS diagnosis.
 Molecular testing on the axillary lesion was performed using FoundationOne® CDx and RNA sequencing, revealing 11 mutations per megabase, equivocal microsatellite status, MET amplification, CDK6 amplification, and a noncanonical TFCP2::FUS fusion. This rare rearrangement involves TFCP2 exon 1 fused to FUS exon 7 [TFCP2(NM_005653)–FUS(NM_004960), T1;F7], according to the GRCh37 reference sequence. The FoundationOne® report noted that RMS typically has a tumor mutational burden of 2.5 mutations/megabase, and higher mutational burdens, such as observed in this case, are often found in undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas. PD-L1 immunohistochemistry analysis using Dako 22C3 pharm™ yielded a Tumor Proportion Score of 1%. The identification of the gene fusion was particularly significant, as this rare fusion has been previously reported in cases of spindle cell RMS [ – ], providing molecular confirmation of the diagnosis.
The patient was initially treated with a combination of the chemotherapy drugs vincristine, dactinomycin, and cyclophosphamide (VAC), which is the standard of care for metastatic RMS [ ]. Following three cycles of VAC, a CT abdomen pelvis showed disease progression with new pleural disease at the left lung base and increased burden of pulmonary metastatic disease, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, right anterior chest wall masses, right axillary lymphadenopathy, descending colon serosal mass, and hepatic hypodensities.
Given disease progression following three cycles of VAC, a new regimen of pembrolizumab 200 mg IV every 3 weeks and cabozantinib 60 mg PO daily was started. Pembrolizumab is an immune checkpoint inhibitor that targets PD-1 and has been approved for patients with a high tumor mutational burden (>10 mutations/megabase) who have progressed following prior treatment [ ]. Pembrolizumab was selected given the tumor’s 1% PD-L1 status, 11 mutations/megabase, and progression following VAC. Cabozantinib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets multiple receptors and was selected for its activity against MET [ ], which was amplified in this patient’s tumor.
Approximately 1 month into treatment, the patient experienced hand itching and foot blistering consistent with painful hand and foot syndrome, a known side effect of cabozantinib [ ]. Cabozantinib was stopped, and the patient continued on the single-agent pembrolizumab with subsequent visual progression of disease, notably on his back and left axilla. The back lesion grew to approximately 15 cm in greatest dimension and became nodular and vegetating (shown in a, b), and the left axilla lesion became more nodular with crust (shown in c).
 After extensive counseling, the patient agreed to undergo concurrent radiation therapy targeting his back and left axilla lesions. Unfortunately, all radiated lesions showed further visual progression, and the back lesion grew to approximately 20 cm. The patient chose to pursue fully palliative options and expired 2 months afterward.

## Discussion
We describe a rare case of RMS presenting as multifocal cutaneous masses with the noncanonical TFCP2::FUS gene fusion involving TFCP2 exon 1 fused to FUS exon 7. This breakpoint differs from the canonical TFCP2::FUS (T1;F6) fusion previously reported, potentially altering the functional domains of the chimeric transcription factor. This case is notable because the tumor not only contains a fusion of FUS and TFCP2, reported in only 37 cases of RMS in the largest published series [ ], but also for its cutaneous presentation, reported in only 6 cases of FUS::TFCP2 fusion-positive RMS [ – ]. Furthermore, the extensive burden of his cutaneous masses has not previously been reported in FUS::TFCP2 fusion-positive RMS. Lastly, this case underscores the diagnostic pitfall of overinterpreting keratin positivity in soft tissue tumors and highlights that TFCP2 fusion RMS is keratin-positive by definition.
The largest published series only reports 37 cases of FUS::TFCP2 fusion-positive RMS [ ], making it a very rare mutation. Of these cases, twenty-five (67%) arose in the craniofacial bones, eight (22%) in other bones, and four (11%) in soft tissues. The majority were female (62%), and the median age at diagnosis was 30 ± 16. Tumors stained positive for keratins, desmin, MYOD1, myogenin, and ALK, and had spindle or epithelioid morphology. Median time to recurrence was 2.1 months following surgery, 1.6 months following chemotherapy (often VAC initially), with a median survival of 24.7 months. This small series reflects the novelty of the RMS FUS::TFCP2 gene fusion and its very aggressive nature.
In our case, the comprehensive evaluation incorporating morphology from both resections, an expanded immunohistochemical profile including RMS-specific markers, and confirmatory molecular genetics led to the final diagnosis of high-grade epithelioid and spindle cell RMS. The morphology consistently showed a poorly differentiated epithelioid and spindle cell neoplasm arranged in fascicles, characteristic of the spindle cell variant of RMS. The immunophenotype demonstrated diffuse positivity for pankeratin AE1/AE3, desmin, and crucially, MYOD1 with focal myogenin positivity, which is specific for skeletal muscle and helped diagnose RMS. While MYOD1 and myogenin can be positive in sarcomatoid carcinoma or transdifferentiated melanoma with rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, the lack of in situ epithelial dysplasia in the excisional specimen was inconsistent with sarcomatoid carcinoma. The molecular profile with the rare noncanonical TFCP2::FUS fusion provided definitive confirmation, as the canonical fusion has been specifically reported in spindle cell RMS cases [ – ]. This case illustrates the importance of integrated diagnosis, the potential pitfalls of over-relying on keratin expression in soft tissue tumors, and the critical value of lineage-specific immunohistochemical markers and molecular testing in achieving accurate diagnosis of challenging sarcoma cases.
Furthermore, there are only 6 reported cases of the RMS FUS::TFCP2 gene fusion that are presented as cutaneous masses [ – ]. Patients were aged 35–67, and lesions occurred on the scalp and thoracic regions. All cases showed similar staining as noted in our immunohistochemical workup. Given the uncommon cutaneous presentation, several of the cases were originally misdiagnosed as other tumor types, delaying treatment, and were only definitively diagnosed as RMS following genetic testing showing the FUS::TFCP2 gene fusion. Therapies included resection, radiation, and/or chemotherapy, with variability in response and disease progression. Some cases metastasized within several months of treatment. These cases reflect the difficulty of diagnosing cutaneous RMS and importance of genetic testing to confirm the diagnosis.
While our case is similar to those previously reported, it is unique given the rare, noncanonical TFCP2::FUS (T1;F7) gene fusion, cutaneous presentation, and extensive burden of the cutaneous masses. Regarding the extent of these masses, the patient first experienced the solitary lesion on his back, which progressed to his bilateral axilla and internal organs. This is likely due to the 1.5-year delay in starting adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation following his original back lesion resection, which had <1-mm margins in multiple areas. His tumor was also very aggressive, with continued disease progression, resistance to all therapies, and mortality within 24 months of initial lesion resection.
Regarding treatment, he was started on a combination of pembrolizumab and cabozantinib to target the tumor’s molecular profile following disease progression with three cycles of VAC. Several clinical trials have demonstrated a clinical benefit and tolerable side effect profile with this combination. It has been studied in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (objective response rate [ORR]: 65.8%, confidence interval [CI]: 49.9–78.8) [ ], metastatic urothelial cell carcinoma (ORR: 46%, CI: 31–62) [ ], metastatic gastric/gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma (progression-free survival at 6 months: 22.2%, CI: 9–39) [ ], and recurrent head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (ORR 52%) [ ]. However, there are currently no clinical trials assessing pembrolizumab and cabozantinib combination therapy for RMS, and there is also no standard of care for recurrent disease. Further research is needed to assess the efficacy of pembrolizumab and cabozantinib for treating RMS, and recommendations for further lines of therapy when the disease reoccurs. A limitation of this case report is the reliance on outside surgical resections and pathological sectioning, and the delay in diagnosis and treatment course, which may have influenced the aggressiveness of his tumor.

## Conclusion
Overall, this case adds to the very limited literature describing the FUS and TFCP2 gene fusion in RMS and adds to only 6 prior reported cutaneous cases of this tumor mutation. Furthermore, the patient had the noncanonical fusion of TFCP2::FUS, and especially high burden of cutaneous masses, which had not previously been described. This case highlights the importance of early detection, diagnosis, and aggressive treatment.
