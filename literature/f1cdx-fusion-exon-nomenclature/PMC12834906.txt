## Abstract

## Background
Salivary gland cancers (SGCs) are rare, heterogeneous malignancies with limited systemic therapies. This study aimed to define a histology-driven molecular profiling (MP) algorithm for SGCs to optimize testing strategies and support therapeutic decision-making.

## Materials and methods
We retrospectively analyzed all SGC patients who underwent MP (2016-2023) for gene rearrangements and pathogenic/likely pathogenic DNA variants (P-LPV). We considered adenoid cystic carcinomas (AdCCs) versus non-AdCCs, the latter as low aggression (LA) or high aggression (HA). MP included DNA/RNA next-generation sequencing (NGS). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) ± fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) were used for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, and IHC for androgen receptor (AR) status. FISH, NGS and/or IHC were used for neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) detection.

## Results
Among a total of 253 SGCs, AdCCs (132/253, 52%) had MYB/MYBL1 fusions in 47% of cases, all lacking P-LPV. Among HA non-AdCCs (84/253, 33%), P-LPV were found in 55% of cases, including TP53 (34%), PIK3CA (18%), HRAS (14%) and PTEN (5%), in the absence of gene fusions. We found P-LPV in 50% of LA non-AdCCs (37/253, 15%), with ETV6–NTRK3 fusion in one secretory carcinoma. HER2 analysis identified HER2 positivity in 23% of cases (all HA non-AdCC), with 36% HER2-low and 41% HER2-negative. We found less P-LPV in HER2-positive (17.6%) versus HER2-low (67%) or HER2-negative (64%), regardless of AR status. MP-guided therapy was delivered in 4% of cases. Based on our results, we propose a stepwise, histology-driven MP algorithm for SGCs. In AdCCs, we recommend initial FISH for MYB/MYBL1 fusions: fusion-positive cases typically lack other P-LPV, sparing further MP. In fusion-negative cases, DNA NGS can be considered. In HA non-AdCCs, HER2 and AR should be tested upfront, proceeding with DNA NGS in HER2-low/negative tumors. In LA non-AdCCs, we recommend ETV6–NTRK3 FISH followed by DNA/RNA NGS in fusion-negative cases.

## Conclusions
Our data support the clinical implementation of a tailored, histology-driven MP algorithm, potentially optimizing the genomic testing resources in SGCs.

Key words: salivary gland cancer (SGC), molecular profiling, next-generation sequencing (NGS), HER2, androgen receptor (AR), targeted therapy

## Highlights
•MP in SGCs is valuable due to limited systemic treatment options.
•In AdCC, test MYB/MYBL1 fusion first; if negative, consider DNA NGS.
•In HA non-AdCC, test HER2 and AR first; if HER2-low or -negative, regardless of AR status, DNA NGS is recommended.
•In LA non-AdCC, test ETV6–NTRK3 fusion first; if negative, consider DNA/RNA NGS.
•In SGCs, MP should be histology driven, not ‘one-size-fits-all’, to improve precision and potential cost-effectiveness.

## Introduction
Salivary gland cancers (SGCs) are rare, heterogeneous neoplasms that arise from either major or minor salivary glands, accounting for <5% of all head and neck cancers. , , , The 2022 World Health Organization classification identifies 21 SGC histological subtypes, each characterized by distinct morphological, immunohistochemical and genetic features, and unique clinical behaviors. To complement the traditional histology-based distinction of SGCs, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Guidelines have proposed a biology-driven classification into two broad categories: low aggression (LA) and high aggression (HA). This trans-histology, biology-based approach better captures the intricate nature of SGCs.
Despite advances in understanding the clinical/biological heterogeneity of SGCs, these insights have not yet been fully integrated into therapeutic approaches. While surgery remains the treatment cornerstone, often complemented by adjuvant radiotherapy (RT), systemic oncologic treatments still rely on a limited array of compounds applied through a non-personalized, ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach, often leading to suboptimal outcomes. , This underscores the need for refined, personalized therapeutic strategies informed by molecular profiling (MP), which is increasingly employed across various solid tumors to identify actionable targets. 
Molecular alterations in SGCs are neither uniformly distributed across histological subtypes, nor frequently actionable. , Therefore, as with immunohistochemistry (IHC) markers [e.g. androgen receptor (AR) , and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), mostly applied in adenocarcinoma (ADC) and salivary duct carcinoma (SDC)], the pursuit of molecular alterations in SGCs should also be informed, appropriate and histology driven, especially considering the higher costs and limited accessibility of MP. Thus, it is imperative to define specific scenarios warranting MP application in the context of SGCs.
This study aims to address this unmet clinical need by analyzing clinical and molecular data on SGCs from our tertiary referral center. The goal is to design a tailored, histology-driven MP algorithm for SGCs, optimizing the application of MP and rationalizing resource use to better guide therapeutic intervention in a context with limited treatment opportunities.

## Materials and methods
We retrospectively analyzed a real-world, single-institution cohort of patients affected by SGCs who underwent MP analysis in our tertiary cancer center (National Cancer Institute of Milan) from 2016 to 2023, discussed within the Institutional Molecular Tumor Board (MTB) since 2020. The primary objective was to design a histology-driven SGCs MP algorithm proposal.
The study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Institute of Milan (trial n. 74/23). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. For cases where consent could not be obtained (e.g. deceased patients or non-retrievable contacts), an exemption was granted by the Ethics Committee, in accordance with applicable regulations.
Our results followed the ASCO’s SGCs classification, except for adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC): these were all classified as ‘LA’, as our sample population predominantly consisted of second-opinion histological reviews (largely tumor biopsies), which were sent back to the originating centers or lacked sufficient tissue for analysis. Therefore, we could not determine the potential presence of a solid component >30%, required to classify AdCC as ‘HA’.
Centralized histopathological review was carried out in all cases.

## Clinical data collection
The following clinical features were collected for each subject: sex, age, TNM (tumor–node–metastasis) stage according to the VIII edition, primary tumor site, primary curative treatment (surgery, post-operative RT ± chemotherapy), number of systemic treatments administered before MP, number and type of MP-based targeted treatments, SGC histological subtypes, aggression grade (LA versus HA ), date of diagnosis, date of recurrent/metastatic (R/M) disease, date of MP, patient status (alive/dead) at last follow-up and date of last follow-up (or death).

## Molecular analysis
MP was carried out in a non-paired fashion on either primary tumor (T), lymph nodal (N) or distant metastases (M) samples, as available. MP was not performed at initial diagnosis and was generally requested at the onset of non-curable disease (locally advanced or R/M) to guide therapeutic decision making. The timing of MP was not dictated by institutional protocols and was left to clinician discretion, typically when an active treatment strategy was needed, or after exhaustion of standard options.
Manual macrodissection of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections was employed to isolate neoplastic cells for DNA and RNA extraction. Maxwell® Rapid Sample Concentrator (RSC) DNA or RNA FFPE Kits (Promega, Madison, WI) were used for DNA/RNA automatic extraction, followed by quantification with Qubit™ DNA or RNA high-sensitive assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA).
From 2018, the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay (OCA) plus RNA panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and the FusionPlex©Lung panel (Archer Dx, Boulder, CO were employed for next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based fusion detection.
DNA NGS was primarily conducted using the Ion AmpliSeq™ Cancer Hotspot Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), targeting mutations in 50 tumor-associated genes. From 2019, comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) was carried out with the OCA Plus DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), targeting mutations in 500 tumor-associated genes and providing copy number variation (CNV) including HER2 amplifications, tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI) and the assessment of genomic instability by genomic instability metric (GIM) ≥16. CNVs were reported as ‘gain’ for threshold values copy number ≥ 5, whereas ‘loss’ included either mono- or bi-allelic gene deletions, according to default manufacturer’s thresholds for each gene. Sequencing was performed on the Ion GeneStudio S5 Prime (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), with data analysis conducted using Torrent Suite Software™ and Ion Reporter™ software, as previously described. CGP was also carried out with the external tests Foundation One CDx Assay (324 genes) (Foundation Medicine, Cambridge, MA) and Oncotype MAP™ Pan-Cancer Tissue Test (257 genes) (Exact Sciences Corporation, Madison, WI). Variants were clinically classified into five categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of unknown significance (VUS), likely benign and benign. In our results, only pathogenic and likely pathogenic DNA variants (P-LPV) were included, whereas VUS, benign and likely benign variants were excluded. We defined as ‘actionable’ those alterations with either a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)/European Medicines Agency (EMA)-approved treatment or a treatment with published clinical evidence in other cancer types. When applicable, European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) score was reported. 
IHC was performed on an automated immunostainer (BenchMark Ultra Ventana, Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, AZ) using androgen receptor (AR) 441 [Agilent Technologies (Dako), Glostrup, Denmark], polyclonal HER2 A0485 (Dako) and pan-tropomyosin receptor kinase [Roche Diagnostics (Ventana), Basel, Switzerland] antibodies. AR positivity was scored as ‘high’ (strong staining in ≥70% of nuclei), ‘low’ (weak or moderate staining in 10%-69% of nuclei) and negative (negative staining in <10% of nuclei ). HER2 staining was interpreted based on the Dako HercepTest scoring guidelines. 
Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was carried out on FFPE sections using the Her2/neu/CEP 17SG probe 30-171060 (Abbot Molecular, Abbott Park, IL) for HER2 amplification; RP11-121M4, RP11-285I14, RP11-846G5, RP11-75N23 and RP11-418C2 for ETV6–neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK)3 rearrangement; and RP11-105M6, RP11-104D9, RP11-355C15 and RP11-54D21 probes for MYB–NFIB rearrangement, as previously described. , , 

## Statistical analysis
Standard descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinical and biological patient characteristics. Median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

## Results

## Patients’ characteristics
Overall, 288 SGC patients underwent MP from 2016 to 2023: 253 patients were finally included for the analyses ( A-C, and , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033), after excluding 6 patients with non-confirmed SGC diagnosis and 29 with NGS failure. Most included patients were female (53%) with a median age of 50 years ( ). Most cases (41.8%) were diagnosed at stage IV (TNM VIII edition) and the most common primary tumor subsite was the parotid gland (43.5%), followed by head and neck minor salivary glands (28.8%) and submandibular gland (16.6%). Most patients received primary surgery + post-operative RT and underwent MP after a median of 0-1 treatment lines. Nonetheless, a minority of patients (n = 11, 4%) received MP-based treatment ( ) by standard practice, compassionate-use access, off-label or within clinical trials ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033). In detail, in the overall sample (n = 253), the median time from diagnosis to MP was 1398 days [95% confidence interval (CI) 1108-1705 days], with 605 days (95% CI 517-732 days) from diagnosis to R/M disease and 772 days (95% CI 606-945 days) from R/M disease to MP. In patients receiving MP-based treatment (n = 11), the median time from diagnosis to MP was 1000 days (95% CI 444-2643 days), with 626 days (95% CI 61-4413 days) from diagnosis to R/M disease and 238 days (95% CI 80-1396 days) from R/M disease to MP ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
 AdCC was the most prevalent histological subtype (132/253, 52%) ( ). Among the non-AdCCs, 84/253 were HA (33%) and 37/253 were LA (15%). Among the 84 HA, the most frequent subtypes were SDC (n = 35, 42%), poorly differentiated carcinoma (PDC) (n = 19, 23%) and ADC not otherwise specified (NOS) (n = 10, 12%). The 37 LA non-AdCCs included predominantly acinic cell carcinoma (AcC, n = 13, 35%) and myoepithelial carcinoma (MECA, n = 10, 27%).
 For details covering all the histological subtypes, see .
At a median follow-up of 8.37 years (95% CI 6.52-10.6 years), overall survival (OS) from recurrence/metastasis was 33.81 months (95% CI 29.72-41.28 months), while OS from MP was 7.71 months (95% CI 4.15-10.18 months).

## Molecular analysis
For a comprehensive portrait of the gene variants detected by DNA and/or RNA NGS (n = 253), see and , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033.

## AdCC: gene rearrangements
Overall, 30 of 132 AdCCs were characterized for MYB–NFIB by FISH (n = 9) and for MYB/MYBL1–NFIB fusions by RNA NGS (n = 21) ( A). Almost half of them (14/30, 47%) showed the translocation, with similar frequency between T (50%), N and M (45%) samples.
In detail, FISH for MYB revealed MYB–NFIB fusion in 7/9 (78%) AdCCs. One FISH-negative case exhibited MYB amplification. RNA NGS showed gene fusions in 7/21 (33%) AdCCs, specifically MYB–NFIB (5/21, 24%) and MYBL1–NFIB (2/21, 9%) rearrangements.
Finally, NTRK expression, as surrogate of NTRK gene fusion, was investigated by IHC in 14 cases who underwent DNA-only NGS, all resulting negative.

## AdCC: DNA alterations
DNA NGS analysis was carried out in 126 of 132 AdCCs and the majority (113/126 = 90%) was characterized using panels sequencing ≤50 genes, while 13 cases (10%) underwent CGP. Most AdCCs (81/126, 64%) were wild-type and 17 (13%) showed VUS variants only.
P-LPV were identified in 29 (23%) AdCCs, with no differences between T (21%) and N/M (23%) samples. The most frequently mutated genes were NOTCH1 (9/126, 7%), TP53 and PIK3CA (4/126, 3%). Among the group of 27 AdCCs with available DNA NGS which were investigated for MYB fusions by RNA NGS, no P-LPV was detected in the 14 AdCCs exhibiting MYB or MYBL1 gene fusions ( A), whereas 38% (5/13) of fusion-negative cases showed P-LPV.
In the 13 cases analyzed by CGP, additional mutations were found, including BCOR (15%) and KDM6A (8%), both coding for chromatin modifiers ( A).
Concerning CNV, CGP described both gene losses (KMD6A, ARID1A or MTAP) and gains (KDR or MDM2) ( A).
None of the AdCCs analyzed by CGP exhibited a high TMB [TMB ≥10 mutations/megabase (mut/Mb)], with all values <5 mut/Mb, except one case (TMB = 9 mut/Mb).
Genomic instability, assessed by GIM analysis, was available for four cases, all showing low GIM values (<16).
Finally, MSI analysis was conducted by NGS (13 cases) or PCR (40 cases): one AdCC case was MSI-low (MSI-L), while no MSI-high (MSI-H) cases were observed.
Overall, RNA and DNA data in AdCC highlighted a low frequency of P-LPV (23%), including only PIK3CA (3%) as actionable DNA alteration, and the lack of mutations in MYB-translocated cases, with no high TMB/positive GIM or MSI-H ( ).
 

## HA and LA non-AdCC: gene rearrangements
RNA NGS was carried out in 19/84 (23%) of HA non-AdCCs and in 10/37 (27%) of LA non-AdCCs. Among the 19 HA non-AdCCs, no gene fusions were observed ( B), while NGS revealed 1 (1/10, 10%) ETV6–NTRK3 fusion in LA non-AdCC ( C). Specifically, this rearrangement occurred in a secretory carcinoma (SC) case, confirming the presence of the ETV6–NTRK3 fusion previously detected by FISH for the diagnosis of SC. This was in line with IHC NTRK nuclear expression observed in 60% of tumor cells for this case. NTRK expression was also investigated by IHC in seven HA and three LA cases, all resulting negative at RNA NGS.

## HA non-AdCC: DNA alterations
NGS was carried out in 83 out of 84 HA non-AdCCs, mainly using a panel sequencing ≤50 genes (63/83, 76%). Overall P-LPV were detected in half of the cases (55%), mainly involving TP53 (34%), PIK3CA (18%), HRAS (14%) and PTEN (5%) genes ( B). These variants were similarly distributed across T (65%), N (87%) and M (60%) samples.
While TP53 mutations were shared by all histological subtypes, SDC (32%) and ADC NOS (20%) were characterized by HRAS mutations. PIK3CA mutations occurred in 26% SDC, 16% PDC and 11% mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC). Notably, HRAS mutations co-occurred with PIK3CA or AKT1 mutations in all but one SDC case; moreover, PIK3CA and PTEN mutations co-occurred in one PDC and one ADC NOS case. Further actionable alterations were sporadically found in ADC NOS (10% RET, HER2, BRAF, FGFR3), SDC (6% AKT1; 3% HER2, BRAF, HER3) and carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma (CXPA) (11% HER4, ARID1A). CGP revealed further variants in genes such as KMT2C (30%), CDK12 (10%) or ATR (10%), most often found in SDC. Regarding CNV, CGP also suggested both gene loss (MTAP, CDKN2A, ARID1A) and gain (FGFR4, EGFR, MET, CDK12 in SDC; FGFR1 in ADC NOS, respectively) ( B).
Moreover, CGP showed a TMB <5 mut/Mb in 16/17 (94%) HA cases, as only one SDC (1/9 = 11%; i.e. 1/17 = 6% among total HA non-AdCC) had a high TMB (10.42 mut/Mb).
The GIM value was available in 12 cases (6 SDC, 2 ADC NOS, 2 MEC and 2 CXPA) and 2 were positive for genomic instability (GIM value ≥16), including 1/6 (17%) SDCs and 1/2 (50%) CXPAs.
MSI analysis was carried out by NGS (18 cases) or PCR (8 cases), revealing MSI-L in 1 (4%) ADC NOS.
Overall, NGS data showed the absence of gene fusions, with actionable DNA alterations in a significant fraction of HA cases ( ).

## LA non-AdCC: DNA alterations
NGS was carried out in 34 out of 37 LA cases (28/34 with panels ≤50 genes), revealing P-LPV in 17 cases (50%), primarily involving TP53 (12%), PTEN (9%) and PIK3CA, CDKN2A and HRAS (6%). A similar mutation frequency was observed in T (43%) and M (60%) samples, although TP53 variants were exclusively found in T samples. No N samples were profiled. In detail, AcC showed mutations in PTEN (17%) and FBXW7 (8%); MECA in PIK3CA (20%), PTEN (10%) and ATM (10%); and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) in SMAD4 (25%) and CDKN2A (25%). Notably, the two epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMCs) cases were both characterized by HRAS mutations. For all variants detected among the different histological subtypes, see C.
CGP revealed ARID1A and KDM5C variants in AcC (1/9, 11%) and clear-cell carcinoma (CcC) (1/3, 33%), respectively ( C). Concerning CNV, FGFR4 gene gain and MTAP loss were exclusively observed in MECA and AcC, respectively, whereas ARID1A, CDKN2A, PTEN and BAP1 loss were common among different subtypes ( C).
No LA cases showed high TMB ≥10 mut/Mb (mean: 4.3 mut/Mb; range: 0-7.58); GIM analysis was always characterized by low values.
MSI analysis was carried out by NGS (9 cases) or PCR (12 cases), with 1 (4%) CcC case being MSI-L.
Overall, although few cases have been analyzed, DNA and RNA data showed DNA alterations in 50% of LA cases and the presence of NTRK3 fusion in a subject with SC ( ).

## HER2 expression and gene amplification analysis in HA and LA non-AdCC
In 77 non-AdCCs, including 64 HA and 13 LA, HER2 expression was assessed by IHC, showing 3+ (19.5%), 2+ (15.5%) and 1+ (26%) scores. SDC displayed the highest frequency of HER2 expression (25/30, 83%) and of 3+ score (12/30, 40%) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
HER2 gene amplification was detected by FISH/NGS (CGP) in 11 of 24 (46%) cases, including 9 SDC, 1 PDC and 1 carcinosarcoma (CS). Only 1 of 8 (12%) cases with score 2+ showed HER2 gene amplification. As expected, nine cases with a 3+ IHC score tested by FISH/NGS revealed HER2 gene amplification ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
Combining IHC and FISH/NGS results among 74 cases (61 HA and 13 LA), we identified 17 (23%) HER2-positive (i.e. 3+ score or presence of HER2 gene FISH/NGS amplification), 27 (36%) HER2-low (i.e. 1+ or 2+ score in absence of HER2 FISH/NGS amplification) and 30 (41%) HER2-negative (i.e. 0 score) cases ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033). The HER2-positive cases were exclusively HA, mainly represented by SDC (13/17, 76%). By contrast, HER2-low and HER2-negative subgroups included both HA and LA cases ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
Considering HA and LA non-AdCCs, P-LPV were observed in 17.6% of HER2-positive (3/17), 67% of HER2-low (18/27) and 64% of HER2-negative (18/28), as 2 of the 30 HER2-negative HA and LA non-AdCC underwent only RNA NGS, while the remaining 28 were analyzed both with DNA + RNA NGS ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033). Considering only actionable targets (i.e. HRAS, PIK3CA, AKT1, PTEN, BRAF, RET and ERBB2 mutations, NTRK3 translocation, FGFR1/4 and MET amplification, loss of MTAP and ARID1A, high TMB >10 mut/Mb), only one PTEN and two ERBB2 variants were found among HER2-positive cases. By contrast, several actionable variants were found in both HER2-low and HER2 negative HA cases ( A). Actionable targets were less frequent among HER2-low LA, being mostly represented by loss of ARID1A in AcC and BCC ( C). Moreover, ETV6–NTRK3 fusion was documented in a single SC case ( C) among HER2-negative LA cases. When considering HA non-AdCCs alone (i.e. excluding LA non-AdCCs), the difference in the prevalence of P-LPV across HER2-positive, HER2-low, and HER2-negative subgroups became more pronounced (17.6% versus 71% versus 69%, respectively), as detailed in .
 

## AR expression in HA and LA non-AdCC
Overall, AR expression was evaluated by IHC in 81 HA and LA non-AdCCs and scored as high (41%), low (5%) and negative (54%). The AR-high cases were exclusively HA, of which 79% (26/33) were SDC ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
Considering the three HER2 subgroups, AR-high expression was most common among the HER2-positive (80%, mostly SDC), decreasing from HER2-low (44%) to HER2-negative (14%) cases (P = 0.00015, , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033).
The HER2-positive/AR-high subgroup revealed one PTEN and one ERBB2 mutation. Conversely, within the HER2-low and HER2-negative subgroups, we did not observe significant differences in total or actionable alterations based on different AR expression levels, and only one ERBB2 mutation was detected in one AR-negative case ( B).

## Discussion
In this study, we analyzed the molecular data from SGCs evaluated at our tertiary referral center (2016-2023) to develop a histology-driven MP algorithm extending beyond AdCC to HA and LA non-AdCC subtypes, aiming for a more personalized treatment strategy ( A-C).
 The first step for AdCCs MP should be FISH analysis for MYB– and MYBL1–NFIB fusions, present in >50% of cases. , In our sample, MYB– and MYBL1–NFIB fusions were balanced between T (50%) and N/M samples (45%), suggesting biological stability across different stages of disease progression. We carried out FISH exclusively for MYB, not for MYBL1. RNA NGS identified two additional fusion cases not detected by FISH. Overall, RNA NGS outperforms FISH in detecting MYBL1 fusions (∼5%). Nonetheless, due to NGS higher costs, we recommend FISH for MYB– and MYBL1–NFIB fusions as an initial approach. AdCC cases with MYB– or MYBL1–NFIB fusions do not harbor actionable mutations, as well described in our cohort and as reported in the ASCO Guideline , discouraging further sequencing. However, in cases lacking MYB/MYBL1 fusion, further DNA NGS testing can be considered, as these tumors may harbor actionable mutations ( A). In this regard, the generally low mutational burden typical of AdCC should be kept in mind. In our cohort, the broad application of an NGS panel targeting up to 50 genes revealed that the majority of AdCCs were wild-type; the small AdCC subset with mutations showed few actionable alterations, such as PIK3CA, currently lacking studies on targeted inhibitors, and NOTCH1, where all previous attempts at targeted inhibitions have failed. Conversely, when CGP was applied, more genetic alterations were detected, including actionable loss of MTAP and ARID1A and mutations in genes related to chromatin modification such as BCOR and KDM6A, although the chromatin remodeling inhibitor vorinostat has been tested in AdCC without satisfactory results. , 
Moving to HA non-AdCCs, if the pathologist suspects a diagnosis of SDC or ADC NOS ( ), HER2 and AR status should be tested (ESCAT score: II-B). 
Starting from HER2 status, this should be assessed first using IHC/FISH, guiding the subsequent therapeutic strategy: HER2-positive cases benefit from HER2-targeted trastuzumab combined with standard-of-care chemotherapy. Despite evidence of activity, , , , other anti-HER2 agents beyond trastuzumab, available in other cancer settings, are currently not accessible for SGCs. Upon progression to HER2-targeted therapy, DNA NGS testing may be pursued for further potential treatment options. From a cost perspective, upfront IHC and FISH offer a clear advantage over NGS. To illustrate the cost-effectiveness of this approach, we considered the reimbursement rates within our Regional Healthcare System as representative estimates: ∼€63 for IHC, €300 for FISH and €1550-€2070 for NGS.
HER2-low cases, mostly including HA non-AdCCs (21/27, 77%) such as SDC (10/27, 37%), ADC NOS (4/27, 15%), PDC (5/27, 18%) and CXPA (2/27, 7%), represent one-third of our cohort (36%), consistent with recent data. Initial evidence suggests that HER2-low patients may also benefit from anti-HER2 therapy, as seen in HER2-low and ultra-low breast cancer, although with a lower drug activity as compared with the HER2-positive counterpart. , Given the current lack of approved HER2-targeted agents, chemotherapy remains the first option for these patients. Notably, HER2-low and HER2-negative tumors appeared biologically similar ( A), exhibiting a significant mutational burden and potential actionable targets: this justifies DNA NGS as a priority ( B). Although our patient sample was not adequately descriptive ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033), DNA NGS may also identify ERBB2-activating mutations in HER2-negative cases. , 
Interestingly, the co-occurrence of multiple molecular alterations in some HER2-low/negative cases (i.e. PIK3CA with HRAS or PTEN mutations) may influence response to single-agent therapies, (i.e. primary/secondary resistance ), and provide a potential rationale for treatment sequencing or combinations.
Moving to AR status, AR inhibition is a further option in AR-overexpressing tumors: these represented 51% of HA cases in our series with a decreasing gradient of AR expression from HER2-positive (80%, mostly SDC) to HER2-low (44%) to HER2-negative (14%) ( and , respectively, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033). AR inhibition should be exploited, showing efficacy in HER2-positive, but also in HER2-low or -negative cases.
For LA non-AdCCs, if the pathologist suspects a diagnosis of SC, FISH ETV6–NTRK3 fusion testing should be carried out (ESCAT score: I-C). If NTRK fusion is detected, further genomic profiling is discouraged. If no NTRK fusion is found, DNA and RNA NGS should be carried out to search for actionable alterations ( C). RNA NGS may identify less common NTRK fusions, as well as other diagnostic translocations (e.g. CRTC1/3-MAML2 in MEC; EWSR1-ATF1/CREM in CcC, NR4A3 in AcC). In our LA non-AdCC sample, DNA NGS revealed actionable mutations, such as HRAS in EMCs, PIK3CA in MECA and loss of ARID1A in AcC and BCC. In our retrospective sample, HER2 was non-systematically evaluated in LA non-AdCCs, with HER2-low expression observed in some cases of AcC (3/13, 23%), BCC (2/13, 15%) and PA (1/13, 8%) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.106033). However, since HER2 is not a therapeutic target typically found in LA non-AdCCs, we did not include it among the recommended evaluations for this setting ( C).
While CGP allows for TMB assessment, relevant for potential immunotherapy eligibility (TMB >10 mut/Mb, seen in one SDC case among our cohort), both AdCCs and non-AdCCs generally show limited benefit from immunotherapy, , with slightly better responses observed in HA non-AdCC. Moreover, as SGCs are typically mismatch-repair proficient, NGS is not recommended for MSI detection, with IHC being a more cost-effective and accessible alternative. In our cohort, CGP identified two HA cases with a positive GIM value, indicative of homologous recombination deficiency, prompting its prospective assessment as a potential biomarker predictive of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor response, as seen in ovarian cancer. While CGP offers broader insights than limited-panel sequencing (≤50 genes), its main advantage in the context of SGC lies in its ability to detect a wider spectrum of CNVs, rather than mutations identifiable with smaller panels. Hence, we endorse the development of customized panels focusing on mutations and CNVs relevant to SGCs ( A-C), minimizing unnecessary costs from profiling mutations unlikely to be present. As for the time being, when choosing how to prioritize CGP, we recommend a stepwise approach based on the expected mutational burden: firstly, in HA non-AdCCs that are HER2-low/negative; then, in LA non-AdCCs without NTRK fusion and in HA HER2-positive non-AdCCs upon resistance to anti-HER2 treatment; and, finally, if access to advanced profiling techniques is feasible and sustainable, CGP may also be considered for AdCCs lacking MYB– or MYBL1–NFIB fusion.
It is worth considering that the proposed algorithm ( ) is substantially structured around a histology-driven diagnostic framework. However, in the real-world decisions regarding MP in SGCs, the pragmatic clinical context must also be evaluated. In MTB practice, MP is typically considered for patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease not amenable to curative treatment, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0-2, and a life expectancy ≥3 months.
A key strength of our study is the cohort size, notable given SGCs’ rarity. Centralized histopathological review ensured diagnostic consistency, enhancing data reliability. However, limitations must also be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential biases, as molecular testing methods varied across cases, leading to data heterogeneity. HA AdCC cases (>30% solid component) could not be identified, limiting conclusions for this subset. Also, our SGCs classification does not reflect the potential for biological evolution over time, including high-grade transformation. In this respect, we highlight the importance of carrying out tumor re-biopsy when feasible, particularly after targeted therapy or upon disease progression, to refine treatment. Finally, our cohort included few patients (11/253, 4%) successfully treated as per MP results, likely due to delayed profiling—coherent with the short median OS after MP (7 months). This aligns with the few treatment lines (0-1) received at the time of MP, as our sample included a high proportion of AdCCs (often managed with surveillance) and HA non-AdCCs, which have poor prognosis. Additionally, treatment beyond progression is common in SGCs, due to limited alternatives.
In conclusion, integrating our findings with available literature, , , we propose a stepwise, potentially cost-effective, histology-driven approach to MP in SGCs. This strategy prioritizes patients most likely to benefit from MP, particularly those with HA non-AdCC, and especially within the HER2-low/negative subset ( B). Future research should focus on further refining biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies and exploring novel targeted therapies for these rare malignancies.
