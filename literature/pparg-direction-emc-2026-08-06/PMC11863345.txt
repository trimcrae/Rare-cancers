## Highlights
•Gene expression profiles of RNA-seq-based exercise responses (GEPREP) stands as the inaugural comprehensive data atlas of RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) based exercise-responsive gene expression profiles, encompassing multiple species, tissues, and exercise modalities.
•GEPREP provides meticulous meta-data annotations for each profile, ensuring inclusion of demographic details, exercise interventions, health statuses, and library generation specifics.
•GEPREP presents a uniformly processed data framework with batch effect correction, optimized for cross-study analyses, and is accompanied by an intuitive user interface designed for ease of use.

## Background
Physical activity can regulate and affect gene expression in multiple tissues and cells. Recently, with the development of next-generation sequencing, a large number of RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)-based gene expression profiles about physical activity have been shared in public resources; however, they are poorly curated and underutilized. To tackle this problem, we developed a data atlas of such data through comprehensive data collection, curation, and organization.

## Methods
The data atlas, termed gene expression profiles of RNA-seq-based exercise responses (GEPREP), was built on a comprehensive collection of high-quality RNA-seq data on exercise responses. The metadata of each sample were manually curated. Data were uniformly processed and batch effects corrected. All the information was well organized in an easy-to-use website for free search, visualization, and download.

## Results
GEPREP now includes 69 RNA-seq datasets of pre- and post-exercise, comprising 26 human datasets (1120 samples) and 43 mouse datasets (1006 samples). Specifically, there were 977 (87.2 %) human samples of skeletal muscle and 143 (12.8 %) human samples of blood. There were also samples across 9 mice tissues with skeletal muscle (359, 35.7 %) and brain (280, 27.8 %) accounting for the main fractions. Metadata—including subject, exercise interventions, sampling sites, and post-processing methods—are also included. The metadata and gene expression profiles are freely accessible at http://www.geprep.org.cn/.

## Conclusion
GEPREP is a comprehensive data atlas of RNA-seq-based gene expression profiles responding to exercise. With its reliable annotations and user-friendly interfaces, it has the potential to deepen our understanding of exercise physiology.

## Introduction
Regular exercise has long been considered a crucial factor for disease prevention and health improvement. , The global transcription response of multiple tissues and organs is the key step in achieving these effects and has itself been widely studied in different populations and sports types. , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , With the rapid development and cost reduction of next-generation sequencing technology, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has become an increasingly attractive tool for transcription studies. Compared with other high-throughput gene expression profiling technologies such as microarray, RNA-seq allows unbiased measurement of gene expression levels throughout the genome, without prior knowledge of gene sequences. This approach avoids the need for complicated experimental design and provides the ability to detect unannotated transcripts and genes. , 
Recent years have witnessed a surge of studies of global transcription responses to exercises by RNA-seq. These studies were carried out both in animal models , , , , and human populations , , , , examining acute exercises , , , , and long-term training. , , , , The studies focused on numerous tissues and organs, including skeletal muscle, , , , , , brain, , , , blood, , , liver, , , immune cells, neurocytes, and so on. However, these datasets are still largely under-utilized. This is primarily due to the large variation of individuals involved, types of sports, experimental designs, and bioinformatics pipelines among different studies. Inadequate samples and sequencing depths were also not sufficient for deep analysis, with the exception of gene expression profiling and differential expression analysis. People usually consider the small fraction of genes on specific pathways or with functions they are concerned about, leaving the large majority of the remaining data unmined. Third, data were commonly deposited in universal sequencing data archives, such as Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), European Nucleotide Archive (ENA, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena), and Genome Sequence Archive (GSA, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa), , mixed with numerous other types of data and with few preprocessed results and cataloged metadata, making it hard to search, download, and use.
The number of published RNA-seq-based studies of gene expression profiles response to exercise has grown at an unprecedented speed. For example, among the 42 such studies , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , using mice as experimental objects, 42.86 % (18 studies , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ) were generated over a span of only 2 years (2021–2022). The proportion is 25.81 % (8 studies , , , , , , , ) out of 31 human-based studies. , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , The expansion of this kind of study indicates its potential impact on the depiction of global transcription response to exercise and related physiological topics. Therefore, it is essential to facilitate studies of sports sciences to thoroughly collect, annotate, analyze, and access RNA-seq data on exercise responses.
Here we present GEPREP, a comprehensive atlas of gene expression profiles of exercise responses derived by RNA-seq. To construct the database, we manually searched and validated 69 publicly available exercise-related RNA-seq datasets (from 73 studies) conducted on humans (31 studies , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ) and mice (42 studies , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ), involving both acute exercise , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , and long-term training, , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , both resistance training , , , , , , , , and aerobic exercise, , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , together with sedentary controls (inactivity). Other exercise forms, such as combined exercise , , , , , and high-intensity interval training, , , are also included. All RNA-seq data were carefully quality controlled and processed using a uniform pipeline for alleviating the deviations introduced by the bioinformatics processing as much as possible. Batch effects introduced by data sources were also removed by appropriate bioinformatic tools. For all samples, metadata such as the species, the tissue or organ, the type of exercise (e.g., acute or long-term, resistance training or aerobic exercise, or sedentary control) were manually collected and validated from the associated publications, as were the details of the exercise intervention, including training time, intensity, frequency, exercise protocol, demographic information (e.g., age and sex), health status, and information about other forms of interventions (e.g., diet). The processed transcription data, together with the annotated metadata of the samples, were organized into a website (http://www.geprep.org.cn/) whose functionalities include browsing, searching, downloading, and visualization. Detailed information on raw datasets and samples was offered through external links.
Overall, GEPREP presents a comprehensive compilation of published gene expression profiles about exercise responses in multiple species and organs with reliable annotations and user-friendly interfaces. In addition to data collection, GEPREP is developed to benefit further downstream analysis. As far as we know, before GEPREP only a few studies had attempted to collect such gene expression profiles and perform meta-analysis. , , , , However, those studies mainly focused on the expression levels as measured by microarray, and information on the samples was not carefully gathered and/or contained some but not all of the data of interest to the authors. To address these issues, GEPREP concentrated on gene expression as profiled by RNA-seq, including as much data as possible with detailed sample information, and processing data within a controlled bioinformatics pipeline to make the results directly comparable. GEPREP also offers advanced data browse, search, and visualization, together with multiple gene expression measurements available for download. We anticipate this database will empower future studies on exercise physiology and deepen our understanding of how the gene regulatory networks affect exercise responses.

## Methods
2.1

## Data collection and database contents
To collect the gene expression data, we referred to a series of standardized procedures for consistent and reliable data collection from biological databases. We first searched the GEO with the following keywords: “exercise” together with “RNA sequence” or its synonyms “transcript” and “transcriptome” (up to 12/31/2022); we then restricted its dataset type to high throughput sequence (expression profiling, non-coding RNA profiling, third party reanalysis, or other) and retrieved 219 candidate studies with at least 1 RNA-seq library. We did our main search with the above terms, then filled in the gaps in subsequent searches. These candidate studies were manually validated by their correlation with the exercise responses, the quality and innovation of the publication, and the availability of the raw RNA-seq data. microRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) and electrical pulse stimulation (EPS) interventions in vitro were not included. Simultaneously, the studies with significantly incomplete experimental information were excluded. For example, it is not clear what type of exercise patterns the samples in GSE130722 and GSE206505 belonging to, which results in a dimness of the grouping information for each sample. The datasets GSE172162 and GSE208615 were off the recruitment due to the obvious loss of information on exercise type and protocol.
The total number of studies was reduced to 73 (with 2372 biosamples). General information, such as the accession number and tissue/organ for each sample, was directly extracted from its record in GEO. Other metadata attributes, such as age and sex, type of exercise intervention, and health status, were manually extracted from their publications. The collected metadata attributes were listed in , with details presented in and corresponding publication information shown in . 
Unfortunately, the data collected so far do not include any form of resistance training in mice. To review our data, we re-searched the mouse datasets with an emphasis on resistance exercise patterns. Only a few studies on the simulation of resistance motility by muscle mechanical overload were found in refs. , , To be specific, this mock approach is characterized by induction of plantar muscle hypertrophy through the removal of a small fraction of soleus and gastrocnemius muscle. Their corresponding RNA-seq data (GSE195707, GSE135903, GSE162827 and GSE159082 ) were not eligible for inclusion.
To make the collected RNA-seq data comparable between different studies, we processed them using a uniform bioinformatics pipeline with exactly the same parameter combination, genome reference, and gene annotation for each species and library type ( ). The quality statistics of each library (both single-end and pair-end) were calculated by FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and then manually validated. Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were filtered using Fastp. The rRNA sequences were removed by aligning the reads against the rRNA sequences (downloaded from Ensembl (https://www.ensembl.org/index.html), Version hg19 (GRCh37) for humans and mm10 (GRCm38) for mice). Valid reads were then aligned against the corresponding reference sequences (hg19 for humans or mm10 for mouse) using HiSAT2 (Version 2.2.1). If the library type was not specified in the article, the first 1-million reads or read pairs were aligned in the strand-specific and unspecific ways, respectively. The library type was then determined by comparing the read counts on the sense and anti-sense strands. Ultimately, the library type was integrated into the metadata ( ), and if there was a conflict, the above judgment results prevailed to prevent the possible mistake of the record. Reads that failed to align, those with low mapping quality (MAPQ < 10), and those with more than 1 mapping position were removed. Gene expressions were then quantified against the latest gene annotations in GENCODE (https://www.gencodegenes.org/) on the corresponding reference genome versions (v19 for human and vM25 for mouse) using StringTie (Version 2.2.1; https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/). The expression levels measured by both fragment per kilobase per million (FPKM) and transcript per million (TPM) were kept. The raw read counts mapped to each gene were then calculated by htseq-count (Version 0.11.3.). Downloads for all 3 kinds of gene expression measurements were offered to facilitate the downstream visualization and differential gene expression analysis by other bioinformatic packages, such as DESeq2 or edgeR. 
After calculating the gene expression levels, we adopted a further filter according to the expression profiles of housekeeping genes. Only samples with more than 95 % of the housekeeping genes expressed (with TPM > 1) were retained. Studies with more than 1 sample retained after the filtering were kept and reported in the final database. The number of studies decreased to 69 (26 for human and 43 for mouse) with 2126 samples (1120 for human and 1006 for mouse).
In order to facilitate the direct comparison of expression levels from different studies, we adopted a pre-computed batch effect correction scheme using ComBat for FPKM and TPM data and ComBat-Seq for counts data following their default configuration, which was achieved by taking the dataset ID, the library construction strategy (polyA+ or Ribo-zero), the strand specificity, sequencing strategy (single-ended or paired), and sequencing platform ID as batch effects (only samples with identical values of all these attributes were considered as the same batch). In the metadata, the corresponding attributes mentioned above are named “datasets”, “extracted molecule”, “library prepared method”, “layout”, and “platform ID”. The data after correction were also offered for browsing, downloading, and visualization.

2.2

## System design and implementation
The gene expression profiles collected above, together with corresponding metadata, were all organized into a website called GEPREP. The website was developed and maintained on the Django (v4.2.5) web framework (https://www.djangoproject.com/) running on an Apache HTTP server (https://httpd.apache.org/). The front interface uses Bootstrap (v3.3.7) framework (https://getbootstrap.com/docs/3.3/) and jQuery (https://jquery.com/) for display and interaction. The backend uses SQLite (https://www.sqlite.org/index.html) for data storage and several Python (https://www.python.org) modules for data processing. The current version of GEPREP supports most of the standard web browsers, including IE, Chrome, Safari, Firefox, etc.

2.3

## Data availability
GEPREP is publicly accessible for worldwide users without any registration or login through the URL: http://www.geprep.org.cn/. All the gene expression levels and metadata are free for download. Large, intermediated data (including sequences, alignments, and transcription assemblies) are offered for download upon request. Please contact us as indicated on the “Help” page on the web site.

## Results
The current release of GEPREP (v1.2212.B) contained 69 datasets of gene expression profiles before and after exercise intervention in human and mice as measured by RNA-seq. There were 26 datasets on humans, with 1120 samples, of which 977 (87.2%) were of skeletal muscle and 143 (12.8%) of blood ( A). There were also 43 mouse-derived datasets, including 1006 samples from 9 tissues, of which the skeletal muscle (359, 35.7%) and brain (280, 27.8%) tissues occupy the largest fractions ( A). For humans, there were 4 main categories of exercise intervention (acute resistance exercise, acute aerobic exercise, long-term resistance training, and long-term aerobic training) integrated together to form various combinations of exercise and high-intensity interval training. For mice, only 2 types of aerobic exercise were revealed ( B). Sedentary control samples were categorized into “inactivity” in both species. On average, 10,768 expressed protein-coding genes (with TPM > 1) are detected in each human sample, with 12,210 in each mouse sample. An average number of 424 (accounting for 3.39% of genes involved) human and 327 (accounting for 2.34% of genes involved) mouse protein-coding genes were identified as differentially expressed between samples with any type of exercise interventions and those sedentary controls measured in the same tissue and study, with DESeq2 and a p value threshold 10−5 ( C). 
3.1

## Database features
3.1.1

## User-friendly web interface
We have built an easy-to-use, interactive website as the front end of the GEPREP ( A), which offers browsing and downloads based on both the genes and samples ( B). The site consists of 4 main levels (pages): the overview page (also serves as the home page), the sample browsing page, the gene expression profile page, and the visualization page ( A). The human and mouse data were separately organized in exactly the same way, and the species can be switched in the overview page. The main feature of the overview page is the experimental matrix ( C), which serves as both a summary of samples in tissues and exercise types and as a quick entry for the full experimental profile. The full metadata of the samples are listed on the sample browsing page. On this page, we added a series of filters as checklists for the sample selection based on these metadata ( B). Samples can also be selected manually. Information about each study or sample in the GEO database was provided by external links. Once the samples of interest are picked, the user comes to the gene expression profile page, where gene expression levels are shown in the fashion of raw read counts, TPM, and FPKM ( D); users can switch between the 3 fashions. Downloads of the expression matrixes are also provided here. Summaries of the expression profiles of selected genes are provided on the visualization page in the fashion of μ ± σ for each exercise type, together with the Kruskal-Willis test results for significance of expression differences among exercise types ( E). Visualization and comparison of 1 or a small batch of genes can also be achieved by boxplots against the exercise types ( F). 

3.1.2

## Advanced searching
There are 2 dimensions of searching in GEPREP, searching the samples ( B) and searching the genes ( D). The searches of genes are simply offered by accepting a list of ensemble gene IDs or gene names (no more than 3000) in the boxes of the overview and sample browsing pages. If left blank, the 3000 genes with highest average expression levels are selected automatically. The searches of samples are mainly on the sample browsing page ( B), where metadata of samples are presented, and orders of samples can be changed according to each attribute of the metadata. Users can select the samples of interest by using the checklists above the metadata, in which the metadata attributes are grouped and listed as drop-down lists according to their values. Selections are offered by checking for 1 or more values. If more than 1 value is selected, samples matching any 1 of them are filtered out. If filters are applied to multiple attributes, those selected by all of them are finally reported.

3.1.3

## Data download and update
The full sample metadata matrix ( ) and gene expression profiles containing 2126 samples together with those filtered out by the user in the sample and gene expression browsing pages can be downloaded at any time through the download option on the pages. Three gene expression measurements, the raw read counts, the TPMs, and the FPKMs are offered. All downloaded tables are tab-delimited text files. Customized tables can also be exported in the required format. Users are free to browse, search, and integrate them into their own analysis pipelines. Due to limited bandwidth, large intermediate data, such as the raw sequences, the aligned binary alignment/map format (BAM) files, the signal tracks, the transcript assemblies, and transcript-level expression quantification results are not directly downloadable from the website but are available on request. We have also started a long-term updating plan of the database to add the newly generated data every 1 or 2 years. Users are also encouraged to post their own data by contacting us at any time; the data will be added as soon as possible.

3.2

## Uniqueness of GEPREP compared to other similar databases
To the best of our knowledge, there are currently only 4 databases that target gene expression profiling before and after exercise intervention: GeneXX, SGDB, metamex, and extrameta. GEPREP is significantly different from them on the following major points: (a) GEPREP is the first such database that is devoted to the gene expression profiles derived by RNA-seq, while the other ones were either totally based on microarray data (SGDB) or mixed between the two (GeneXX, metamex, and extrameta), with RNA-seq data occupying no more than 15% in the first version of published articles. (b) In the databases RNA-seq data involved (GEPREP, GeneXX, metamex, and extrameta), GEPREP is the only 1 that re-processes the data using a uniform bioinformatic pipeline with careful quality checks and batch effect treatment. The extrameta didn't have a clear declaration on re-analysis of the raw data. GeneXX and metamex re-analyzed the raw data in different approaches with regard to distinct dataset types, leaving untreated the batch effects introduced by differences of bioinformatics. (c) GEPREP includes more tissues and exercise types. In total, between humans and mice, there are 10 tissue types involving all 4 major types of exercise intervention together with other exercise forms and sedentary controls. The SGDB, GeneXX, and metamex are concentrated on skeletal muscle only, while extrameta contains skeletal muscle and blood data only. Furthermore, the first version of each of the other 4 databases are based on human samples only. (d) The metadata for each sample are carefully curated and offered for search and download in GEPREP, including detailed demographic information, health status, and exercise intervention. In the other 4 databases, no more than a concise overview is provided. (e) We offered a user-friendly front end for GEPREP, where users with little experience in bioinformatics can easily browse, search, and export the data and do basic comparisons. Moreover, for users who want to do further analysis, we offered 3 commonly used gene expression measurement forms. For advanced users, we also provided the on-request download service for large and intermediate results, including sequences, alignments, and gene assemblies. (f) GEPREP plans to continuously maintain and update data, while no update has been made available for the others (with the exception of metamex) since their release. The metamex has an updated version that includes both human and mouse data, and it is the most comprehensive database available so far. However, the proportion of RNA-seq is still low (33.6 %, containing a miRNA-seq data), and its published illustration is absent. (g) GEPREP can target gene sets of interest, whereas the extrameta and metamex can only explore transcriptomic changes of a single gene at a time.

3.3

## Application: Detection of genes in bone marrow that responded to long-term aerobic exercise
Here, as an example, we show an application of GEPREP from one of our recent publications on how “exercise promotes bone marrow microenvironment”. We wanted to explore the effect of long-term aerobic exercise on bone marrow in diet-induced obese male mice. We searched GEPREP's mouse page by checking long-term training in classification, aerobic exercise in exercise type, and bone marrow in tissue. We found 2 eligible samples from 1 study ( A). We then manually validated the correlation to the purpose and data quality and selected GSE205676 for further exploration ( B). The expression profiles measured by raw read counts were downloaded ( B) and differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2. The downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched at the extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction pathway, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway, etc. ( C), indicating exercise is beneficial to the bone marrow microenvironment partially through these trajectories, which is consistent with our previously published literature. 

3.4

## Application: Meta-analysis of human skeletal muscle transcriptome in response to different types and durations of exercise
To systematically analyze the transcription responses of human skeletal muscle to exercise, we first searched the GEPREP database. We discovered 50 comparisons between pre- and post-exercise, involving common types of exercise such as resistance exercise, aerobic training, high-intensity interval training, and their combinations, together with both acute and long-term exercise durations ( A). There were 671 samples from 249 individuals included in this meta-analysis. 
We first sought to detect genes potentially regulated by all exercise types and durations. To achieve this, we first applied the DESeq2 package to get the log2 fold change (log2 FC) values of the gene expression levels between pre- and post-exercise for each comparison, together with their p values. The p values were then aggregated by Fisher's method. Genes with aggregated p values (after false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment) less than 10−10 were determined to be exercise responders. In total, there were 2554 genes distinguished through the above pipeline ( ). The top over-expressed genes after exercise (with largest average log2 FC values, B) were all previously reported exercise responders, such as Early Growth Response 1 (EGR1), Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 4 Group A Member 3 (NR4A1), and Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma Coactivator 1-Alpha (PPARGC1A). , Hierarchical clustering of the comparisons based on the log2 FC values of genes showed a clear separation between different combinations of exercise types and durations ( ). These observations indicated the validity of these detection schemes.
Moreover, the clustering hierarchy ( ) showed that gene expression change profiles after exercise were preferentially clustered by exercise duration, and subsequently by exercise type. This observation indicated that exercise duration and type might play distinct roles in the reprograming of human skeletal muscle transcriptomes.
To quantitatively assess the roles of exercise type and duration in the human muscle transcription response, we concentrated on the 42 comparisons with resistance or aerobic exercises involved. A series of gene-wise two-way analyses of variance using exercise types and durations as effects were performed, with the log2 FC values as dependent variables. Genes were classified as follows, according to the significances of the F tests, coefficients of main effects, and their interaction term ( C and ): uniformly responded to all exercise types and durations (“uniform”), specifically responded to different exercise types (“type specific”), specifically responded to different exercise durations (“duration specific”), independently responded to both types and durations (“independent”), and interactively responded to different exercise types and durations (“interactive”). As expected, a vast majority of genes (1105) were duration responders (classified into “duration specific”, “independent”, or “interactive”). The number of type responders (559) was much smaller. More interestingly, the number of genes responding to both exercise types and durations (354, classified into “independent” or “interactive”) was even smaller than expected when assuming the independence of the effects of types and durations (the expected number was 471.52 and p = 4.66  × 10−13, χ2 test; only those genes not classified as “uniform” were considered). When we annotated the functions of the 119 genes classified as “interactive” by Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, no term was enriched out under the threshold of q < 0.01 after FDR adjustment. Duration responders and type responders were also enriched into distinct terms ( D and E). These observations indicated that the genes’ transcription responses to exercise were largely independent and distinct between exercise types and durations.
In conclusion, by meta-analyzing the human skeletal muscle transcriptome data in GEPREP, a set of potentially exercise-regulated genes was determined with confidence. Moreover, current data supports the conclusion that the effects of exercise duration and type are largely independent when it comes to reshaping the human skeletal muscle transcriptome, and that exercise duration plays a much larger role in this process.

## Discussion
In this study, we developed GEPREP, a comprehensive database focused on gene expression profiling based on RNA-seq before and after exercise intervention. To the best of our knowledge, GEPREP contains the most comprehensive data collection of exercise-related RNA-seq data, together with the most detailed metadata. The samples are processed by a fine-tuned and uniform bioinformatics pipeline. The user-friendly website makes for ease of browsing, searching, visualization, and data download.
GEPREP offers 2 unique capabilities for sports scientists. On the one hand, it offers the opportunity to search for candidate effector genes of exercise intervention from the historical data, avoiding a large number of unnecessary experiments. On the other hand, it makes it possible to study the relationship between the effects of exercise intervention on the gene expression regulations and covariates such as age, sex, diet, and health status, using worldwide virtual cohorts.
To make GEPREP more comprehensive and useful, we plan to incorporate the following features into our next update, alongside the addition of recently published data. First and most importantly, we will feature more flexible batch effect correction options to facilitate users’ variable requirements. Secondly, we plan to provide more commonly used bioinformatic tools (such as the differential expression analysis based on DESeq2 or edgeR, the GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and the gene set enrichment analysis) as services on the website. Last but not least, we will add more abundant visualization options, such as plots of sample distances based on dimension reduction results from principal component analysis (PCA) or t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE), sample and gene clustering heatmaps, as well as Bland-Altman (MA) plots and volcano plots showing the results of differential gene expression analysis.

5

## Conclusion
The GEPREP data atlas described in this study is a comprehensive database of RNA-seq-derived gene expression profiles on exercise responses in model organisms with carefully annotated metadata and an easy-to-use front end. It serves as a rich and open data source and will eventually ease the research of sports science with respect to exercise-related gene expression regulations.
