## Abstract

## Background
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), with their regenerative, anti‑inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties, represent a promising therapeutic strategy for degenerative retinal diseases. Current treatments fail to address core pathologies-such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis-in conditions including diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, retinitis pigmentosa, and glaucoma, underscoring the need for regenerative approaches.

## Methods
This review summarizes the signaling pathways through which MSCs and their exosomes promote tissue repair and neuroprotection in ocular disorders. It evaluates published preclinical and clinical data to outline the current therapeutic applications, efficacy, and safety profiles of MSC-based therapies in ophthalmology.

## Results
MSC-based interventions show potential in alleviating key pathogenic processes in retinal degeneration. Clinical trials indicate their ability to reduce inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptotic cell death, thereby supporting retinal cell survival and function. These benefits are primarily mediated through paracrine signaling via extracellular vesicles such as exosomes.

## Conclusion
MSCs constitute a significant advance toward retinal repair and functional restoration. However, translation of these findings is hindered by the lack of standardized and regulated treatment protocols. Future efforts should focus on establishing optimized delivery methods, safety standards, and a deeper understanding of molecular mechanisms to bridge the gap between promising experimental outcomes and reliable clinical application.

Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Exosomes, Retinal degenerative diseases, Signaling pathways

## Introduction
Degenerative diseases of the eye, including diabetic retinopathy (DR), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinitis pigmentosa (RP), and glaucoma, are the leading cause of irreversible vision loss globally. These diseases are caused by altered function, reduced numbers, and abnormalities in intercellular junctions, as well as the distribution of photoreceptor cells and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells ophthalmic diseases that cause visual dysfunction. The conditions are characterized by mechanisms, such as inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and damage to retinal neurons or supporting cells caused by nematodes. The human eye has limited endogenous regenerative capacity, and current treatments, such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy for age-related retinopathy or intraocular pressure control for glaucoma, often relieve symptoms but fail to halt disease progression or restore lost vision [ , ]. Therefore, novel therapeutic strategies targeting tissue repair and functional restoration are urgently needed. 
The paracrine neuroprotective factors and immunomodulatory properties of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), as well as their pluripotent differentiation potential, have made them ideal candidates for regenerative therapies [ ]. Preclinical studies have shown that MSCs can promote retinal cell survival, modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, and differentiate into photoreceptor-like cells under specific conditions [ , ]. In addition, the low immunogenicity of MSCs and their ease of isolation from various tissues (e.g., bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cords) enhance the feasibility of transformation [ ].
Despite these advances, critical challenges remain, including the optimal routes of administration (intravitreal versus subretinal), long-term safety issues (e.g., aberrant differentiation and tumorigenicity), and standardized protocols for cell preparation. This review synthesizes the current evidence on MSC-based therapies for ocular degenerative diseases and highlights future directions by analyzing their therapeutic mechanisms, summarizing the results and limitations of current clinical trials, and bridging the gap between laboratory studies and clinical implementation. The information is expected to provide a theoretical basis for optimizing the clinical use of MSCs to advance the development of new therapeutic regimens for degenerative ophthalmic diseases.

## Characteristics and functions of MSCs
MSCs are fibroblast-like cells with regenerative capacity. They were first discovered and isolated from bone marrow by Alexander Friedenstein in 1976, who named these cells fibroblast colony-forming units [ ]. Arnold Caplan officially named them MSCs in 1991 [ ].
MSCs are classified based on their tissue origins into bone marrow MSCs (BM-MSCs), adipose-derived MSCs (ADMSCs), umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UCMSCs), dental pulp mesenchymal stromal cells, placental matrix-derived MSCs, synovial membrane-derived MSCs, and peripheral blood-derived MSCs [ ]. MSC function varies with the tissue source.
MSCs can influence critical processes, such as proliferation, differentiation, cellular metabolism, inflammatory responses, cellular senescence, cellular stress, and cell migration by transferring extracellular vesicles (EVs) to other cells [ ]. EVs, also known as exosomes(Exos), are a class of vesicles ranging from about 30 to 100 nm in diameter. EVs encapsulate small fractions of material, such as proteins, genetic material (DNA and RNA and their derivatives), and cytokines [ , ]. EVs are thought to act as paracrine signaling mediators between MSCs and the cells they target. Exosomes with BM, AD, and UC origins are morphologically and structurally similar. All of them participate in migration, proliferation, adhesion, and apoptosis function [ ]; however, their therapeutic expression varies depending on their origin.

## MSCs and the use of MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-exos) in ocular degenerative diseases
The retina consists of 10 neural layers and six distinct cell types, including photoreceptor cells, horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, and Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), with each layer interconnected via synapses. Each cell type is involved in detecting changes in light and movement [ ]. The retinal microvasculature comprises a neurovascular unit (NVU), whose structure and function resemble the blood-brain barrier. An NVU consists of a structural and functional complex of neuronal cells, including RGCs, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, and horizontal cells, as well as non-neuronal glial cells (Müller cells, astrocytes, and microglia) and vascular cells (endothelial cells and pericytes) [ ]. Retinal pericytes, endothelial cells, and perivascular glial cells collectively form the retinal microcirculatory system. This system participates in nutrient extraction from the blood, the removal of metabolic waste, the maintenance of the blood-brain barrier, and the dynamic regulation of blood flow in response to changes in neural activity [ ].

## Retinal neurovascular unit injury
DR is now widely recognized as a neurovascular disorder caused by the disruption of the NVU. This disruption can lead to vision-threatening clinical manifestations [ ]. Its pathogenesis is mainly characterized by an inflammatory response, and the release of oxidative cytokines leads to retinal cell loss, vascular dysfunction, and neovascularization [ ].
Endothelial cells release vasoactive factors, such as the prostacyclin prostaglandin I(2), reactive oxygen species (ROS), endothelin-1, nitric oxide, and angiotensin II, which increase oxidative stress and impair endothelial repair. Oxidative stress is a pathological state of ROS production. The overproduction of ROS and increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin 1β (IL-1β), play a key role in the pathogenesis of DR. The signaling pathways associated with elevated ROS levels include nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), transforming growth factor-β, matrix metalloproteinases, and nuclear factor erythroid-associated factor 2 (Nrf2) [ ]. Endothelial injury is the main cause of diabetic vasculopathy. MSC-exos can regulate changes in the above cell proliferation regulators, antioxidant regulators, inflammatory factors, and vascular endothelial growth factor, and thus inhibit retinal injuries, such as retinal inflammation, neovascularization, microvascular injury, and vascular leakage induced by high glucose concentrations, through multiple signaling pathways (Fig. ).
 Retinal ischemia and hypoxia upregulate VEGF expression by activating hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1). Elevated VEGF levels promote retinal neovascularization in patients with DR [ ]. MSC-exos can reduce retinal damage by activating the HIF-1α/enhancer of zeste homolog 2/Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator (PGC)-1α signaling pathway to decrease hyperglycemia-induced retinal damage [ ]. VEGF is an important paracrine factor in MSCs. It decreases retinal damage by activating the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI-3 K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin/nitric oxide synthase and p38/MAPK signaling pathways to promote the survival and migration of pancreatic islet microvascular endothelial cells [ ]. In addition, BM-MSCs release factors that activate the VEGF/AKT signaling pathway, promote proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and increase human umbilical vein endothelial cell migration and tube-forming ability [ ].
MiR-126 of Human Umbilical Cord MSC-derived exosomes(hucMSC-exos) was shown to reduce the expression of high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1) and its downstream target proteins NLRP3 inflammasome, NF-κB p65, and cysteine in high-glucose-treated retinal tissues and endothelial cells. It also decreased the production of several inflammatory factors, thereby reducing the inflammatory response in diabetic retinal disease [ ]. MSC-exos can also slow the inflammatory response in diabetic retinal disease through other miRNAs, such as miR-17-3p 5 [ ], miR192 6 [ ], and miR-133b-3p [ ]. MSC-exos can also be used to treat diabetic retinal diseases by carrying microRNA. These miRNAs can also attenuate the inflammatory response and oxidative stress damage in DR and protect the retina.
The Keap1-Nrf2-antioxidant response elements signaling pathway plays a central role in combating oxidative stress in humans, protects cells from damage or delays the progression of related diseases and is a major defense mechanism against inflammation. MSCs have been shown to phosphorylate Nrf2 through the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and activate the transcription of downstream genes that can resist exogenous and endogenous oxidative stress [ ]. Another study found that inhibiting neddylation by MSC-exo through miR-143-3p activated the Nrf2 signaling pathway, reduced ROS, and decreased retinal inflammation, thereby reducing DR [ ] (Fig. d).
 When hucMSC-exos were injected into the vitreous cavity, miR-18b was found to act on mitogen-activated protein kinase 1(MAP3K1), which inhibited NF-κB p65 phosphorylation to attenuate the inflammatory response in DR [ ]. The study demonstrated that STAT5 signaling pathway activation by MSC-exos through miR-222 could significantly inhibit neovascularization [ ]. MSC-exos inhibited microglial activation in a diabetic rat model study and decreased levels of inflammatory factors, including NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β, thereby alleviating DR progression [ ].
Microglia, perivascular macrophages, monocyte-derived macrophages, and/or vitreous hyaloid cells were observed to be abundant at the retinal surface in proliferative DR [ ]. MSC-exos could also delay the inflammatory response in the diabetic retina by secreting microglial cytokines such as arginine-1 and CD206. BMSC-exos can activate the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-κB signaling pathway and inhibit the expression of TLR4 via miR-486-3p to decrease oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in retinal cells and promote the proliferation of (high-glucose) HG-treated Müller cells, thereby decreasing the release of inflammatory factors [ ] (Fig. a, b). MSCs delayed the inflammatory response in the retina of diabetic model rats by converting inflammatory cytokines, such as inducible nitric oxide synthase and CD16, to anti-inflammatory factors such as arginine-1 and CD206 in microglia by inhibiting the HMGB1/TLR4 signaling pathway [ ] (Fig. a).
The direct phosphorylation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (AMPK) regulates mitochondrial division and activates proteins upstream of autophagy and mitochondrial autophagy, promoting the degradation of damaged mitochondria [ ]. Bone marrow-derived MSCs were reported to attenuate oxidative stress and apoptosis in endothelial cells through the sirtuin 1/AMPK/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) pathway [ ]. HucMSCs promoted cell viability, wound healing, migration, and angiogenesis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells through the MAPK/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway, and significantly attenuated HG-induced cellular senescence [ ].
In the iron regulatory element (IRE)/thioredoxin-interacting protein signaling axis, activated IRE1 recognizes X-box binding protein 1 mRNAs and induces their degradation, which promotes cell death through caspase-2 expression (Fig. c). Increased thioredoxin-interacting protein expression activates NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, promoting inflammation and apoptosis [ ]. MSC-exos carrying lncRNA SNHG7 inhibited endothelial-mesenchymal transition and neovascularization in human retinal microvascular endothelial cells through the miR-34a-5p/X-box binding protein 1 axis, alleviating diabetes-induced retinal neuronal degeneration and inhibiting microangiopathy, thereby enhancing the viability of HG-stimulated neuronal cells and inhibiting their apoptosis [ ]. ROS can inhibit mitochondrial death in DR by suppressing the FOXO3-pink1/parkin signaling pathway (Fig. c). A previous study found that overexpressing Sirt3 triggered mitochondrial autophagy to inhibit apoptosis through the FOXO3a/parkin pathway, thus protecting against RP [ ].
A study by Sun et al. found that neuronal precursor cells expressed developmentally downregulated 4, activating the degrading phosphatase and tensin homolog/AKT signaling pathway, causing Nrf2 to bind to antioxidant response elements and activate hemeoxygenase-1 (HO-1) to reduce retinal oxidative damage, thereby exerting a therapeutic effect on the retina. An intravitreal injection of MSC-sEV delayed retinal apoptosis, decreased oxidization levels, and enhanced the proliferation of RPE cells cultured under HG conditions in vitro [ ].

## Neuronal degeneration
Glaucoma is the result of the progressive, irreversible loss of retinal RGCs and axons, which causes optic nerve atrophy and visual field defects. Ischemia-reperfusion injury is one of the major pathogenic mechanisms of glaucoma. Osteoblastin promotes the proliferation and activation of retinal microglia and contributes to glaucoma, ultimately leading to the loss of RGCs and visual dysfunction [ ]. Many recent studies have focused on the role of glial cells and the immune system in the development of glaucomatous optic neuropathy [ ], with microglia activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines being the main factors in retinal cell death in glaucoma. Microglia overexpression may also be an important degenerative factor in increased cell death [ ].
Glaucoma is mainly treated by lowering intraocular pressure; however, RGC degeneration and vision loss may still progress. Regarding optic neuroprotection, cell transplantation provides a new therapeutic strategy for glaucoma. MSC therapy mainly involves cell transplantation to replace dead and lost RGCs. A previous study [ ] found that MSCs had a neuroprotective effect in acute glaucoma-induced murine retinal damage. Exos secreted by MSCs were shown to promote the survival of RGCs and the regeneration of their axons [ ]. TNF-α was also found to activate gingival MSC-derived exosomes in retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury through the maternally expressed gene 3/miR-21a-5p axis, exerting neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. Another study demonstrated that MSC-exos reduced neuronal cell death after hypoxic and ischemic injury both in vitro and in vivo, stimulated axon growth, and exerted anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, suggesting potential therapeutic roles in retinal diseases [ ]. The inner layer of the retina of mice treated with ADSC-exos for 2 weeks was higher than that of the control group. The relative protein expression of TLR4, p-NFκB, p65, p-p38 MAPK, and p-ERK was decreased, suggesting that ADSC-exos decreased the retinal thickness of the inner layer of the retina by inhibiting TLR4 and the TLR4/MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway [ ]. Activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway could reduce microglia activation and thus protect against retinal damage caused by hypermetropia.
RP is a common inherited disease of the retina [ ], caused by abnormalities in the RPE, photoreceptor cells and function. Clinical manifestations usually begin with night blindness, followed by the gradual loss of peripheral vision until the patient becomes blind [ ]. Optic nerve cells have a limited ability to repair and regenerate, and effective treatments are lacking. Thus, the self-renewing, highly proliferative, multidirectional differentiation, and induced differentiation potential of MSCs offer hope for treating retinal degenerative diseases.
BM-MSC-exos could activate retinal leucine zipper and protein kinase C gene expression via miR-183/96/182, which are key regulators of post-mitotic photoreceptor cells [ ]. MiR-204 carrying adeno-associated virus transplanted into the retina of transgenic RHO-P347S RP model mice inhibited the expression of downstream genes implicated in neutrophil chemotaxis, immune response regulation, and cell death. The study also found that miR-204 could slow the progression of RP by inhibiting photoreceptor apoptosis and microglia activation [ ]. MiR-146a in MSC-exos, which is a target of the Nr4a3 gene, could exert anti-inflammatory effects through the miR-146a-Nr4a3 signaling axis and delay retinal degeneration in rd10 mice [ ].
A clinical study involving intravitreal injections of autologous BM-MSCs into three eyes of three patients with advanced RP [ ] with a 1-year follow-up found that all patients showed improvement in light perception after 2 weeks that lasted for 3 months. However, adverse effects, such as traction retinal detachment, ischemia reperfusion injuries neovascularization, and cataract formation, were also observed. Another clinical study treated 14 patients with advanced RP with intravitreal injections of BM-MSCs [ ]. Improvements in visual acuity were seen within 12 months, which returned to baseline levels at 12 months, along with the stabilization of the visual field and central retinal thickness, thus controlling disease progression. The findings suggest that intravitreal injections of BM-MSCs are safe and have fair short-term efficacy. The study also tested three different doses of BM-MSCs, the lowest dose showing the best safety and short-term efficacy. A study by Oner et al. treated eleven patients with advanced RP with ADMSCs. Of them, three had brighter color and light perception than preoperatively, and one had improved visual acuity. Seven patients showed no significant change in visual acuity. One patient developed a complication of choroidal neovascularization at the transplantation site within 6 months and was treated with anti-VEGF, and five patients developed a complication of the anterior retina and were treated with vitrectomy and silicone oil filling [ ]. These findings indicate that the safety of subretinal ADMSC injections needs to be further explored.

## Glial cell pathology
AMD is a common age-related chronic degenerative fundus disease. It is further categorized into dry AMD and wet AMD. Dry AMD mainly manifests as PRE pigmentation and geographic atrophy. AMD mainly presents with RPE pigmentation, vitreous wart formation, and digitiform atrophy. The pathogenesis of AMD starts with RPE cell damage, which, in turn, leads to degenerative changes in photoreceptor cells and progressive vision loss [ ]. The most effective clinical treatment for AMD is vitreous cavity injections of anti-VEGF drugs; however, some patients have poor results or even more severe visual impairment after receiving these treatments. MSC-exos containing microRNA-22-3p were found to protect RGCs by inhibiting the expression of MAP3k12, ERK, p38, and caspase-3 protein involved in the MAPK signaling pathway [ ]. This study demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of MSC-exos on choroidal neovascularization, suggesting a new direction for treating AMD.
Excessive ROS and a weakened antioxidant system result in oxidative stress, which leads to damage and apoptosis in RPE cells, photoreceptor cells, and the choroidal capillary layer. In AMD, excessive ROS production promotes mitochondrial autophagy in the RPE by activating the p62/Nrf2 pathway. Increased ROS production, the excessive accumulation of nonfunctional mitochondria, and oxidative stress-triggered mitochondrial autophagy in the RPE were demonstrated to be critical for AMD progression [ ]. The expression of mitochondrial autophagy-related proteins was increased in a double knockout mouse model of PGC-1α and Nrf2, lysosomes accumulated in RPE cells, and the double knockout promoted the degeneration of the RPE and led to visual dysfunction similar to that seen in mice with AMD [ ].
MSC-exos were shown to mitigate RPE cell damage by regulating the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway [ ]. In an in vitro study by Tang et al., MSC-exos attenuated human RPE cell injury, inhibited lactate dehydrogenase activity, decreased ROS levels, and upregulated superoxide dismutase activity. In in vivo studies, MSC-exos protected the RPE layer, the outer segment/inner segment layer of the photoreceptor, and the outer nuclear layer from sodium iodate-induced damage [ ].
The inflammatory response is another major pathological AMD process. MSCs inhibit NLRP3 inflammatory vesicle expression activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, while sodium iodate-induced RPE cell death [ ]. The dynamic homeostasis of retinal photoreceptor cells can be maintained by exosomes from MSCs containing miR-124-3p. Photoreceptor cells gradually degrade and undergo apoptosis in the process of maintaining the dynamic homeostasis of retinal photoreceptor cells. In this case, exosomes can no longer regulate the immune response, leading to the gradual death of retinal cells [ ].
The effect of BSMDs in treating AMD is believed to be through the secretion of cytokines and neurotrophic factors while also affecting the recipient cells and altering the neurodegenerative process through immune modulation [ ]. Clinical phase 2 results of ADMSC transplantation in eight patients with dry AMD and macular dystrophy showed no complications and improved visual acuity in all patients, indicating that using MSCs is a safe and effective rationale for treating this condition [ ].
Stargardt disease is a hereditary macular degeneration disease that occurs in children or adolescents and often presents with the progressive loss of central vision. The pathogenesis of Stargardt disease involves the accumulation of lipofuscin, which progressively leads to the loss of central vision and the degeneration of RPE cells. The disease is usually inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and is associated with ABCA4 gene mutations [ ]. A study identified the RDH8 gene in Stargardt disease using gene sequencing methods and found an association with retinal photoreceptor cell degeneration. Thus, this method could be used to make a genetic diagnosis [ ]. Another study of Stargardt disease treated patients with autologous BM-MSCs, which were administered by injections into the retrobulbar area, subbulbar area, vitreous cavity, or subretinally, and intravitreously. The patients were followed for 1 year. The results showed that 85.0% of the macular dystrophy symptoms improved or remained stable,and the vitreous cavity injection method was more effective than the other methods [ ]. These findings indicate that MSC therapy provides a new strategy and hope for treating inherited retinal diseases.

## Composite injury
Insufficient blood supply to the retina during retinal ischemic injury causes hypoxia and metabolic disorders, resulting in reduced retinal cell function and decreased visual acuity. Systemic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and leukemia, and other ocular diseases, such as retinal vascular occlusion, ocular ischemia syndromes, uveitis, and AMD, can also lead to ischemic necrosis of the retina.
Early studies showed that microglia and macrophages can exhibit pro-inflammatory (M1) or reparative (M2) phenotypes and functionally switch between the two phenotypes depending on the local environment [ ]. M1 macrophages produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators and have been associated with neuronal degeneration and neural network dysfunction. In contrast, M2 phagocytes are thought to suppress inflammation and promote tissue remodeling by altering gene expression and neuroprotective factor secretion. Microglia activation after retinal ischemia triggers cytokine and TLR responses in a mouse model of IR within 2 hours of ischemia [ ]. During the acute phase of injury, activated microglia and inflammatory vesicles release pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-1β, which can lead to tissue damage.
Several studies have investigated the effect of using MSC-exos in retinal ischemic injury (Fig. ). RGCs are output neurons that transmit visual information from the retina to the brain. The degeneration of RGC cytosol and axons can lead to irreversible vision loss. A study by Guo et al., has found that the calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II-cAMP response element binding protein (CaMKII-CREB) signaling pathway is impaired after RGC injury [ ]. Another study demonstrated that after injecting BM-MSCs into the vitreous cavity in a mouse model of retinal ischemia and hypoxia, the MSC exosomes activated the downstream binding protein CREB pathway by activating the calcium channel of the CAMKII pathway, promoting the activation of related proteins to reduce the direct effects of retinal ischemia. BMMSC-exos could significantly reduce retinal thinning and neovascularization [ ]. A 2016 study injected adipose-derived and human umbilical cord-derived MSC-exos into the vitreous cavity of mice with retinal injury and ischemia and found that they could alleviate disease progression. In vivo, MSC-exos reduced the damage, inhibited apoptosis, and suppressed inflammatory responses, thereby obtaining better visual function [ ]. In addition, the expression of monocyte chemotactic protein (MCP)-1 was downregulated in the retina after injecting MSC-exos, ameliorating retinal injury. MCP-1 is associated with oxidative stress, and oxidative damage increases MCP-1 expression in retinal cells, which is promoted by activating the NF-ĸB signaling pathway in vascular endothelial cells [ ].
 MSCs can reduce retinal inflammation and improve visual function by modulating T-regulatory cells (Tregs) to promote the expression of transcription factors [ ]. The study findings confirmed that induced MSCs (iMSCs) can inhibit the T-cell population effect and that iMSCs can enhance the differentiation of T cells to Foxp3 Tregs in vitro. They can also reduce inflammation and decrease ischemia-reperfusion by activating Treg injury-induced retinal degeneration in vivo, thereby improving retinal immune function.
The signaling pathways and physiological functions of TNF receptor-associated factor are involved in the onset and progression of ischemia-reperfusion injury, and TNF receptor-associated factor is involved in retinal ischemia reperfusion injury, which activates both classical and nonclassical NF-κB pathways (Fig. ). The NF-κB pathway is an important regulator of many pathological changes in ischemic injury, including inflammation and apoptosis [ ]. Hypoxia was shown to induce the necrotic apoptosis of microglia in a mouse model of retinopathy [ ]. The study identified a subpopulation of microglia that highly expresses necroptosis-associated genes, including receptor-interacting protein 3 (RIP3). When activated by hypoxia, these microglia undergo necroptosis through a RIP1/RIP3-mediated pathway, which also induces the release of fibroblast FGF2 from the microglia. The microglia-specific deletion of RIP3 significantly attenuates hypoxia-induced retinal neovascularization, suggesting that this necrotic apoptotic pathway is related to retinal neovascularization. A study by Yu et al. investigated the neuroprotective role of exosomes derived from MSCs in retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury. Human gingival MSC-exos were injected into the vitreous of mice, and miR-21-5p was found to protect against ischemia-reperfusion injury-induced neuronal death and inflammation [ ]. Gingival MSC exosomes transfected with siRNA-maternally expressed gene 3 were stimulated with TNF-α (TG-exos) or left unstimulated.The results showed that an injection of TG-exos into retinal ischemic mice significantly reduced inflammation and cell loss compared with an injection of unstimulated TG-exos. Thus, miR-21-5p was identified as a key factor in the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of TG-exos [ ].
 MSCs were integrated with the HO-1 gene to obtain MSCs overexpressing HO-1. MSC-HO-1 had a protective effect on H2O2-treated RGC, and overexpressing HO-1 in MSCs increased the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs up to 2-fold compared to MSCs alone [ ]. These findings suggest that overexpressing HO-1 enhances the therapeutic potential of MSCs for retinal injury.
A study by Wen et al. found that an intravitreal injection of human Wharton’s jelly MSCs (hWJMSCs) into an experimental model of optic nerve ischemia was associated with a significant risk of severe retinal inflammation, which can disrupt the retinal structure, visual function, and retinal function [ ]. This intravitreal MSC delivery modality is unsafe as an alternative retinal and optic nerve disease therapy. However, conditioned culture medium (CM) effectively inhibited macrophage infiltration into the optic nerve and microglia activation at the optic nerve. HWJMSC-derived CM treatment provided neuroprotection in an experimental optic nerve ischemia model by significantly inhibiting RGC apoptosis and optic nerve inflammation. It also preserved visual function and RGC density. Therefore, the authors concluded that hWJMSC-derived CM provides neuroprotection after optic nerve necrosis through anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects. However, several recent studies reported the favorable therapeutic efficacy and safety of MSCs [ , ].
In a phase 1 clinical trial using MSCs to treat traumatic optic neuropathy [ ], 20 patients were consecutively treated with optic canal decompression combined with the topical implantation of MSCs and evaluated at postoperative days 1, 7, 1, 3, and 6. No complications were reported, and the follow-up showed that visual acuity was improved. Thus, MSC transplantation was found to be effective and safe in patients with traumatic optic neuropathy.
In experiments comparing fresh MSCs with MSCs thawed from cryopreservation [ ], MSCs were found to retain > 95% viability after thawing, remained responsive to inflammatory signals, and were able to inhibit activated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. When kept viable throughout cryopreservation, which is an easy preservation method, MSCs retained therapeutic efficacy in both in vitro potency assays and in vivo models of ischemia/reperfusion. Therefore, many recent studies related to using MSC-exos for retinal ischemic injury have shown promising results and could be applied to other ischemic retinal diseases, such as retinopathy of prematurity, ocular ischemia syndromes, and retinal vein and artery occlusions.

## Other retinal diseases
A macular hole (MH) represents a complete rupture of retinal tissue at the fovea centralis, impairing central vision and causing visual distortion [ ]. Traditional MH treatments include vitrectomy, membrane peeling, and pneumonopexy. However, alternative techniques, such as autologous retinal grafts, have emerged for larger or chronic holes [ ]. In a rabbit RH model, implanted hAD-MSCs achieved normal mean central thicknesses of reconstructed tissue by day 12 post-transplantation. Additionally, donor-derived opsin-positive photoreceptor-like cells and protein kinase C-positive bipolar-like cells were sporadically observed in the transplant group. The healing process of MHs in rabbits was significantly accelerated, improving visual recovery [ ]. In a study of intravitreal injections of MSCs or MSC-exos in patients with idiopathic MH, both treatments promoted functional and anatomical recovery of MHs. MSC-exo therapy was reported to improve postoperative visual outcomes in patients with refractory MH, although fibrotic membranes were observed on the retinal surface in one MSC-treated patient. One patient receiving a higher dose of MSC-exos exhibited an inflammatory response, necessitating further experimental studies to refine safety assessments [ ]. The use of MSCs as adjunctive therapy for refractory and advanced MHs is promising; however, additional evidence regarding their safety is needed. Autologous MSCs harvested from the same patient reduced rejection rates and immune responses. [ ]. Therefore, long-term clinical trials featuring control groups are needed to compare the efficacy and safety of MSC applications versus vitrectomy and other adjunctive techniques.

## Treatment of progressive ocular disorders with MSCs
In recent years, the use of MSCs and their derivatives in treating retinal diseases has made significant progress. MSCs from different tissue sources exhibit heterogeneous therapeutic effects. BM-MSCs were the earliest and most widely used, demonstrating significant neuroprotective effects and showing efficacy in treating RP [ , ] and glaucoma [ ]. ADMSCs are easily obtainable and have the advantage of outstanding vascular repair capabilities, making them suitable for use in diabetic retinopathy [ ] and AMD. As UCMSCs have the advantages of rapid proliferation and strong immune regulatory capabilities, they are commonly used to treat autoimmune diseases [ ].
The therapeutic effects of MSCs vary depending on the route of administration. Intravitreal injection allows direct action on the retina and is suitable for most diseases; however, repeated injections are required due to the short retention time of cells [ , , ]. Subretinal injections target MSCs to the RPE layer, although this procedure is technically challenging and has moderate efficacy [ ]. Choroidal space injections avoid vitreous opacity but require imaging guidance [ ], and intravenous systemic injection is a non-invasive procedure but has minimal efficacy [ ].
MSC exosome engineering can enhance functionality and intelligence. MSCs genetically modified to overexpress therapeutic genes, such as Brain derived neurotrophic factor, can promote RGC survival, and VEGF siRNA can inhibit abnormal vascular proliferation in AMD. Similarly, MSCs engineered with exosomes, such as MIR-21-5P, can enhance the therapeutic effects, in preventing cell apoptosis in the treatment of retinal ischemia. Microcapsules containing MSC-exos in the subvitreous cavity were reported to release exosomes over a period of more than one month during degradation, effectively alleviating retinal ischemia-induced damage [ ]. Combination therapy with MSCs and anti-VEGF agents significantly increases the injection frequency for wet AMD, but demonstrates a synergistic effect.

## Conclusion
The existing findings suggest that MSCs have great potential for treating ocular degenerative diseases and various refractory diseases due to their advantages of immunomodulation, tissue repair, and anti-neovascularization. However, while existing preclinical studies have demonstrated positive therapeutic effects, challenges remain in utilizing MSCs for treatment.
First, MSCs are stem cells with differentiation ability, which can be uncertain, high preparation costs, and it is difficult to assess their efficacy. Second, the clinical transformation and application of MSCs are further complicated. The safety and effectiveness of MSC treatment are mostly based on preclinical studies in animals and in vitro molecular cellular experiments, and the problems of immune rejection and the abnormal proliferation and fibrosis of human and animal MSCs have yet to be solved. Clinical studies conducted in ocular degenerative diseases have also demonstrated less efficacy than those in animal studies, and the complications and long-term effects are difficult to evaluate. In addition, although MSCs have achieved good results in treating certain diseases, their mechanism of action has not yet been fully clarified. Additionally, MSCs are influenced by the surrounding microenvironment and secrete different cytokines. Thus, there is no standardized therapeutic regimen for the clinical application of MSCs.
MSCs and MSC-exos have important advantages in protecting RGCs, photoreceptors, and RPE cells, which can alleviate diseases such as AMD and PR. In addition, MSC-exos can regulate the retinal microenvironment and reduce the inflammatory response by carrying miRNAs, lncRNAs, and other genetic material, thereby alleviating ischemia and hypoxia and providing new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for diabetic retinal diseases, retinal ischemic injury, retinal vein occlusion, and other diseases.
Although MSC therapy offers hope for retinal diseases, its clinical translation faces critical challenges. Future development hinges on advancing treatment standardization by establishing unified protocols for cell preparation, dosing, and delivery. Long-term safety assessments require the continuous monitoring of patient registry systems to identify potential risks, such as abnormal differentiation and immune rejection. Regulatory frameworks must clarify approval pathways for such therapies while encouraging rigorously designed clinical trials. Research priorities should shift toward engineered modifications (e.g., gene modification and targeted exosomes) and multidisciplinary integration (e.g., combining biomaterials and existing therapies), ultimately transitioning from experimental treatments to standardized therapies while ensuring patient safety.
