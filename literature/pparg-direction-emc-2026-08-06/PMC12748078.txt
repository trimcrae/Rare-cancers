## Background
Doxorubicin, a broad‐spectrum chemotherapy drug, is often associated with dosage‐dependent cardiotoxicity, which results in its limited clinical application. A therapeutic dose of doxorubicin can activate cardiac ubiquitin‐proteasome system, whereas the role and potential mechanisms of this process in doxorubicin‐induced cardiomyopathy (DIC) remain unclear. Herein, we assessed the potential role and therapeutic value of RNF2 (ring finger protein 2) on doxorubicin‐induced cardiac damage.

## Methods
Cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2 knockout or overexpression mice received doxorubicin intraperitoneal injection to establish the DIC model. The effects of RNF2 and its downstream mediators were explored through RNA sequencing, immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry analysis, and protein pulldown analysis.

## Results
The expression of RNF2 was significantly increased in doxorubicin‐treated murine myocardium and neonatal rat ventricular myocytes. Cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2 overexpression resulted in exaggerated DIC accompanied by increased cardiac dysfunction, fibrosis and apoptosis. Cardiac damage was mitigated in α‐myosin heavy chain promoter‐driven heterozygous‐Cre+/RNF2‐floxed mice. Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are crucial in DIC and are promoted by RNF2 overexpression and impeded by RNF2 knockout. Mechanistically, RNF2 directly interacted with mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase, followed by the ubiquitination and accelerated degradation of mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase to decrease hydrogen sulfide accumulation, thereby contributing to more severe oxidative damage and mitochondrial morphofunctional defects. Moreover, mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase overexpression or hydrogen sulfide supplementation in cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2‐overexpressing mice restored cardiac performance after doxorubicin challenge.

## Conclusions
Our findings reveal the role of RNF2 in oxidative stress and mitochondrial homeostasis and the progression of DIC, suggesting that targeting RNF2 may be a potential therapeutic benefit for DIC.

## 
RNF2 Deletion Ameliorates Cardiac Remodeling and Dysfunction in the Acute DIC Model
To further explore the effect of RNF2 in DIC, we generated cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2‐cKO mice by mating RNF2flox/flox mice with α‐myosin heavy chain promoter‐driven heterozygous‐Cre mice, and successful deletion of RNF2 in heart homogenates was verified by immunoblotting, subsequently the acute DIC model was established (Figure  through ). The results suggested that cardiac function under baseline conditions did not differ between mice with or without RNF2 deficiency (Figure  through ). However, after doxorubicin treatment, the RNF2‐cKO strain had a greater heart weight to tibia length and greater left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening (Figure  through ), which indicated that cardiac function was ameliorated in part by RNF2 deficiency. Cardiomyocyte atrophy, programmed cell death, and interstitial fibrosis constitute the central pathogenic axis driving doxorubicin‐induced myocardial remodeling. As assessed by Masson and TUNEL staining respectively, RNF2‐cKO mice were relatively resistant to doxorubicin‐induced fibrosis and apoptosis (Figure  through ). This decreased atrial natriuretic peptide (Anp) and B‐type natriuretic peptide (Bnp) mRNA expression and serum CK‐MB, LDH, and cTNT levels compared with those in their RNF2fl/fl counterparts were consistent with improved cardiac function in RNF2‐cKO mice (Figure  through ). Overall, these studies suggested that deficiency of RNF2 may provide an effective strategy to protect against acute doxorubicin overload‐induced heart failure and cardiac remodeling.
 We next overexpressed RNF2 in cardiomyocytes specifically by delivering cardiotropic recombinant AAV‐RNF2, with the AAV‐vector as a control (AAV‐NC) before doxorubicin treatment (Figure  ). Western blotting demonstrated that AAV‐RNF2 successfully elevated RNF2 expression in the hearts (Figure  and ), allowing us to explore the direct roles of RNF2 on cardiac function. As depicted in Figure  through , doxorubicin caused myocardial injury and deterioration of cardiac function, as characterized by an obvious reduction in the ratio of heart weight to tibia length, fractional shortening, and left ventricular ejection fraction measured by echocardiography. Interestingly, these aberrant alterations were further exacerbated by cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2 overexpression (Figure  through ). Similarly, compared with mice infected with the AAV‐NC control, mice infected with AAV‐RNF2 presented more severe cardiac fibrosis in response to doxorubicin consumption, as evidenced by increased left ventricular collagen deposition (Figure  and ). Moreover, TUNEL staining revealed that AAV‐RNF2 injection significantly enhanced apoptosis in doxorubicin‐treated mice (Figure  and ). In addition, RNF2 overexpression further promoted doxorubicin‐induced mRNA expression of 2 classic biomarkers of heart failure, Anp and Bnp (Figure  and ). Consistently, the serum CK‐MB, LDH, and cTNT levels, biomarkers of heart injury, were markedly higher in the RNF2‐overexpressing mice than in the AAV‐NC mice following doxorubicin injection (Figure  through ). However, no significant difference was observed under PBS conditions (Figure  through ), suggesting that RNF2 sensitively responds to doxorubicin stress instead of maintaining physiological homeostasis. These results collectively suggested that RNF2 promoted doxorubicin‐induced cardiac injury and exacerbated DIC progression.

## Cardiomyocyte‐Specific RNF2 Knockout Ameliorates Chronic Cardiotoxicity Induced by Doxorubicin

To investigate RNF2's role in chronic doxorubicin‐induced cardiac damage, we established a long‐term murine model by administering low‐dose doxorubicin (4 mg/kg per week) intravenously to RNF2fl/fl and RNF2 cKO mice weekly over 4 weeks. Cardiac function was assessed 1 week after the final doxorubicin dose (Figure  ). In line with the acute DIC model, relative to RNF2fl/fl mice, heart weight to tibia length ratio, left ventricular ejection fraction, and fractional shortening were significantly increased in RNF2 cKO mice (Figure  through ). In addition, myocardial fibrosis and injury were repressed in doxorubicin‐injected RNF2 cKO mice (Figure  through ). In addition, the number of TUNEL‐positive cells was markedly elevated in RNF2fl/fl mice compared with RNF2 cKO mice after doxorubicin treatment (Figure  and ). These findings demonstrate that RNF2 knockout confers protection against chronic DIC progression.
 

## 
RNF2 Promotes Oxidative Damage Induced by Doxorubicin Insult
To investigate how RNF2 exacerbates the progression of DIC in vivo, we applied comprehensive RNA‐seq analysis on the left ventricular tissues of AAV‐NC‐ and AAV‐RNF2‐infected mice subjected to doxorubicin challenge. The transcriptome of the hearts of the RNF2‐overexpressing mice was distinct from that of the control mice, as determined by principal component analysis (Figure  ). A total of 579 upregulated and 705 downregulated expressed genes differentially were identified between the 2 groups (Figure  ). Gene Ontology analysis revealed that these differentially expressed genes were enriched in oxidative stress and mitochondrial processes (Figure  ). Thus, we assessed oxidative stress levels via dihydroethidium staining in the myocardium. doxorubicin injection resulted in an overwhelming generation of ROS, and RNF2 elevation was upregulated, whereas RNF2 deficiency impeded cardiac ROS accumulation (Figure  and ; Figure  through ). Moreover, these results were verified by DCFH‐DA staining followed by flow cytometry analysis of NRVMs (Figure  through ). To further elucidate the regulatory role of RNF2 on oxidative stress, we assessed the levels of classic redox homeostasis regulators in the heart. Consistently, the AAV‐RNF2 group presented the lower levels of glutathione and SOD activity and a greater level of malondialdehyde than the AAV‐NC group did after doxorubicin treatment (Figure  through ), which was in line with the results showing that RNF2 knockout had the opposite influence (Figure  through ). To determine whether RNF2 modulates DIC through promoting oxidative stress, we manipulated the expression of RNF2 in NRVMs by infecting them with adenoviruses encoding the full‐length RNF2 transcript (Ad‐RNF2) or the control Ad‐NC, followed by doxorubicin treatment. N‐acetyl‐l‐cysteine was then used to decrease the ROS levels. In line with the in vivo data, RNF2 overexpression further exacerbated doxorubicin‐induced cytotoxicity in NRVMs, as evidenced by increased LDH release, decreased cell viability, and increased Anp and Bnp mRNA expression, which was notably abrogated by N‐acetyl‐l‐cysteine protection (Figure  through ). Collectively, these data suggested that RNF2 promoted doxorubicin‐induced cardiac dysfunction by promoting oxidative damage.
 Gene Ontology analysis revealed that mitochondrial processes were also enriched, in addition to oxidative stress (Figure  ). In addition, ROS production induced by doxorubicin can disturb mitochondrial function by decreasing the mitochondrial membrane potential and impairing DNA integrity. Therefore, we first assessed the mitochondrial ultrastructure in the myocardium by transmission electron microscopy. As expected, we found that RNF2 overexpression increased, whereas RNF2 ablation decreased, the number of abnormal mitochondria, which were characterized by marked vacuolation and swollen and lysed cristae, in doxorubicin‐treated mice compared with the corresponding controls (Figure  through ; Figure  and ). Consistent with the in vivo results, the doxorubicin‐induced impairment in mitochondrial structure was more severe in NRVMs overexpressing RNF2 but was significantly repaired in RNF2‐knockdown clones compared with that observed in control clones (Figure  and ). Then, extracellular flux analysis was used to monitor the mitochondrial respiratory capacity in real time. Overexpression of RNF2 significantly downregulated the basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates after doxorubicin treatment but had little effect on the PBS‐treated NRVMs (Figure  through ). Conversely, compared with the corresponding control, RNF2 knockdown restored the mitochondrial respiratory capacity after doxorubicin administration (Figure  through ). Overall, these results demonstrated that cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2 overexpression exacerbated doxorubicin‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction.
 

## Identification of MPST as an RNF2 Binding Partner in the Myocardium
Given the pivotal functions of oxidative stress and mitochondrial defects in the progression of doxorubicin overload‐induced cardiotoxicity, we next investigated the mechanisms through which RNF2 drives oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. Immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry analysis was subsequently conducted to identify the potential partners of RNF2 in doxorubicin‐treated NRVMs. We identified 129 protein candidates, and MPST was identified as the second highest on the basis of the integrated score ranking (Figure  ). We next confirmed the endogenous binding of RNF2 with endogenous MPST with a coimmunoprecipitation assay in NRVMs and mouse heart tissues (Figure  and ). The specific interaction between RNF2 and MPST was further verified with exogenously transduced RNF2 and MPST in human embryonic kidney 293T cells (Figure  ). Additionally, RNF2 directly bound to MPST by in vitro pulldown assays with purified recombinant proteins (Figure  ). Taken together, our data confirmed that RNF2 specifically interacted with MPST, which is a key enzyme related to H2S production.
 

## 
RNF2 Promotes MPST Degradation and Suppresses H2S Release
As a ubiquitinase, RNF2 might mediate the posttranslational modification of MPST. To investigate whether RNF2 ubiquitinates and destabilizes MPST, we next assessed the polyubiquitination level of MPST by a ubiquitination assay. The results revealed that the presence of RNF2 promoted MPST ubiquitination in vivo in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure  ). To further explore whether the ubiquitination driven by RNF2 regulates the stability of the MPST protein, we evaluated MPST protein levels in the presence of cycloheximide, an inhibitor of protein translation. Ad‐RNF2‐infected NRVMs significantly decreased the half‐life of the MPST protein compared with that in the control cells, which indicated that the overexpression of RNF2 led to a prominent increase in the instability of the endogenous MPST protein in NRVMs (Figure  and ). Furthermore, we incubated NRVMs with MG132, a potent 26S proteasome inhibitor, or chloroquine, an inhibitor of lysosome‐dependent protein degradation, and evaluated the MPST protein levels. RNF2 overexpression‐induced impairment of MPST protein levels was clearly detected in the DMSO or chloroquine incubation group, but there was no dramatic difference in the MG132 treatment group (Figure  ), suggesting that proteasome‐dependent degradation of MPST plays a critical role. Moreover, in human embryonic kidney 293T cells, overexpression of RNF2 (RNFwild‐type) contributed to MPST downregulation, but that of the enzyme‐dead mutant (RNFI53S) did not (Figure  and ). To further identify specific ubiquitination sites on MPST targeted by RNF2, we first analyzed the human MPST sequence through the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org), identifying 10 candidate lysine residues (K40, K156, K164, K170, K224, K236, K237, K242, K266, K295). Using site‐directed mutagenesis, we substituted these lysines with arginine (K→R) and systematically assessed RNF2‐mediated ubiquitination. Critically, the K224R and K295R mutants showed complete resistance to RNF2‐induced ubiquitination (Figure  through ). Consistently, RNF2 overexpression failed to accelerate the degradation of these mutants compared with wild‐type MPST (Figure  through ). These findings definitively identify K224 and K295 as the primary ubiquitination sites through which RNF2 regulates MPST stability. To verify these results in vivo, we evaluated the protein expression of MPST in doxorubicin‐treated hearts. In line with the in vitro data, MPST was downregulated in the hearts of the mice injected with AAV‐RNF2, whereas RNF2 silencing restored MPST protein levels without affecting MPST mRNA levels compared with that of the respective controls (Figure  through ; Figure  and ). MPST is a crucial enzyme for H2S generation in the cardiovascular system, and increasing evidence shows that H2S confers protection against DIC. , , , Hence, we subsequently evaluated H2S production in plasma and cardiac tissues. In parallel with the findings in Zhang's report, our results revealed that doxorubicin contributed to a decrease in plasma and cardiac H2S levels, which further promoted the progression of myocardial injury, and RNF2‐overexpressing mice presented a further reduction in H2S content (Figure  and ). Conversely, RNF2 cKO induced H2S elevation in both plasma and cardiac tissues (Figure  and ). Taken together, these findings suggested that RNF2 ubiquitinated and destabilized MPST, thereby negatively modulating MPST abundance and ultimately the release of H2S.
 

## 
RNF2 Targets the MPST‐H2S Axis to Exacerbate Heart Failure in DIC

We next explored whether the MPST‐H2S axis is required for the cardiac damage and structural remodeling mediated by RNF2. To this end, we delivered AAV‐RNF2 combined with AAV‐MPST, which is controlled by the cTNT promoter to achieve cardiomyocyte‐specific overexpression; we also used H2S donor 5a to ensure the slow release of H2S and mimic biological H2S generation. Mice were then subjected to doxorubicin treatment. The efficiency of AAV‐mediated overexpression of RNF2 was validated by immunoblotting (Figure  and ). In line with our previous results (Figure  through ), RNF2 overexpression exacerbated cardiac dysfunction in doxorubicin‐treated mice, as indicated by heart weight to tibia length ratio and transthoracic echocardiography measurements, whereas MPST overexpression or H2S supplementation mitigated these changes (Figure  through ). Additionally, the RNF2‐induced increase in fibrosis and apoptosis was reversed by MPST or H2S elevation (Figure  through ). This improved cardiac function in MPST‐overexpressing or H2S‐treated donor 5a mice was consistent with decreased Bnp mRNA expression (Figure  ) and serum CK‐MB, LDH, and cTNT levels (Figure  through ), compared with those in the AAV‐RNF2‐treated group. Together, these observations confirmed that the MPST‐H2S axis was essential for RNF2 function.
 

## 
MPST‐H2S Axis Restoration Rescues RNF2 Overexpression‐Induced Oxidative Stress and Mitochondrial Dysfunction
H2S is essential for sustaining the equilibrium of antioxidant levels in the body. By directly scavenging ROS and reactive nitrogen species, decreasing glutathione disulfide, and improving glutathione levels, appropriate H2S levels protect cardiomyocytes from oxidative stress. , Hence, we next explored whether the MPST‐H2S axis is necessary for RNF2‐mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in doxorubicin‐treated hearts. As expected, compared with the AAV‐NC control mice, the AAV‐RNF2‐treated mice presented more severe oxidative damage in response to doxorubicin consumption, as evidenced by the results of dihydroethidium staining and assessment of cardiac glutathione and malondialdehyde concentrations and SOD activity, which were significantly restored by MPST overexpression or H2S supplementation (Figure  through ); moreover, these results were confirmed in NRVMs (Figure  and ). We further investigated whether RNF2 increased mitochondrial damage in doxorubicin‐treated hearts by targeting the MPST‐H2S axis. RNF2 overexpression induced destruction of the mitochondrial structure, as evidenced by transmission electron microscopy analysis, which revealed significant loss and swelling of the mitochondrial cristae in both the myocardium and NRVMs, which was ameliorated in part by the injection of MPST or H2S supplementation (Figure  through ). Moreover, Seahorse respiration assays further revealed that MPST‐H2S axis activation reversed RNF2 overexpression‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure  through ). These results demonstrated that the MPST‐H2S axis induced cardioprotection benefits in RNF2‐overexpressing mice after doxorubicin administration, to some extent, through improvements in oxidative stress and mitochondrial homeostasis.
 Notably, neither exogenous MPST administration nor H2S supplementation fully rescued the cardiac dysfunction in AAV‐RNF2‐overexpressing mice following doxorubicin challenge, with persistent functional deficits relative to AAV‐NC controls. These data indicate the existence of noncanonical, RNF2‐regulated cytoprotective pathways beyond the MPST/H2S axis that critically maintain myocardial homeostasis under chemotoxic stress. Next, we used cardiomyocyte‐specific MPST knockdown via AAV9 delivering short hairpin RNA targeting MPST in RNF2 cKO mice. We found that MPST knockdown partially attenuated the cardioprotective effects of RNF2 deletion in doxorubicin‐treated mice. However, compared with RNF2fl/fl controls treated with AAV9‐short hairpin NC (negative control), RNF2 cKO mice with MPST knockdown still retained significant protection against doxorubicin‐induced cardiac injury (Figures  and ), which further confirmed that RNF2's role in DIC extends beyond the MPST/H2S axis. To identify additional pathways, we reanalyzed our mass spectrometry data to identify RNF2‐interacting proteins with potential roles in regulating oxidative stress or mitochondrial homeostasis. As summarized in Figure  , although MPST was the most significantly altered target, we further validated protein levels of other candidates. Notably, RNF2 cKO mice exhibited elevated protein levels of TOMM40 (a mitochondrial translocase subunit) and NDUFA4/NDUFA8 (core subunits of mitochondrial Complex I), whereas PRMT1 and NAMPT levels remained unchanged (Figure  and ), suggesting that RNF2 promotes ubiquitination and degradation of TOMM40 and NDUFA4/NDUFA8, contributing to the pathogenesis of DIC. Besides, we found that the mRNA levels of Tomm40 and Ndufa4/Ndufa8 in RNF2 cKO mice were also increased (Figure  ). As evidenced by Figure  and , H2S serves as a critical activator of both PGC‐1α (peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator 1‐alpha. the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis) and NRF2 (the central transcriptional mediator of antioxidant responses). , Thus, we propose that RNF2 deletion enhances MPST stability and H2S levels, further contributes to upregulation of PGC‐1α and RNF2, and ultimately drives Tomm40, Ndufa4/Ndufa8, and antioxidant gene networks transcription. This dual regulatory model explains both the protein stabilization and transcriptional amplification observed in RNF2 cKO mice, collectively mitigating doxorubicin‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress.

## 
RNF2 Inhibition Exacerbates Doxorubicin‐Induced Cytotoxicity in 4T1 Cells
To verify therapeutic benefits of RNF2 inhibition in doxorubicin‐induced cardiac damage and enhance the clinical benefits of our study, we further explored whether RNF2 knockdown could mediate the anticancer efficacy of doxorubicin. As doxorubicin is broadly used for the treatment of breast cancer, we infected 4T1 murine breast cancer cells with Ad‐short hairpin NC or Ad‐short hairpin RNF2 and then treated them with doxorubicin for analysis. In parallel with our results in cardiomyocytes, RNF2 knockdown dramatically increased MPST protein levels (Figure  and ) and further enhanced H2S synthesis (Figure  and ) compared with those in the control. Conversely, compared with control cells, 4T1 cells with RNF2 knockdown were more sensitive to doxorubicin‐induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by decreased cell viability (Figure  ). Cancer metastasis and progression are highly related with tumor cell migration and invasion, and we subsequently demonstrated that RNF2 knockdown further reduced cell migration in doxorubicin‐treated 4T1 cells, as assessed by cell culture wound closure and Transwell assays (Figure  through and ). Moreover, an immunofluorescence staining assay for Ki67 revealed that impeding RNF2 in vitro also inhibited 4T1 cell proliferation (Figure  ). Thus, the results demonstrated that RNF2 knockdown could ameliorate doxorubicin‐induced cytotoxicity in cardiomyocytes and potentiate the antitumor efficacy of doxorubicin in 4T1 cells (Figure  ).
 

## DISCUSSION
The cardiotoxicity of the effective chemotherapy drug doxorubicin hinders its clinical application, and pharmaceutical strategies for mitigating its cardiac side effects are still lacking. Over the past few decades, molecular targets of DIC have been the focus of numerous studies. Previous reports revealed that E3 ubiquitin ligases drive doxorubicin‐induced cardiac injury. , , Furthermore, a significant amount of evidence points to a close connection between DIC and ubiquitination, a form of posttranslational modification mediated by E3 ubiquitin ligases, which warrants more research to find potential therapeutic targets. , , Here, we report that the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF2 was increased in murine hearts after doxorubicin treatment. RNF2 cKO improved doxorubicin‐induced heart dysfunction, cardiac fibrosis, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis, whereas cardiomyocyte‐specific RNF2 overexpression exacerbated these pathological changes in mice. Mechanistically, RNF2 triggered ROS production and mitochondrial dysfunction by binding to MPST, provoking its ubiquitination and degradation, and ultimately repressing H2S release. In addition, we confirmed that MPST overexpression or H2S supplementation effectively rescued cardiac injury by restoring oxidative damage and mitochondrial defects. Hence, the findings of this study provide insights into the pathogenesis of DIC and RNF2 could act as a promising therapeutic option for doxorubicin‐induced cardiotoxicity.
The ubiquitylating enzyme family has key roles in multiple areas of cell biology, such as cardiovascular diseases, oncology, and metabolism, among which the biological function of RNF2 has rarely been reported. In recent years, the high expression of RNF2 has been demonstrated in multiple cancers, , and RNF2 knockdown to inhibit the occurrence and progression of cancer, like breast cancer, , , chondrosarcoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, ovarian cancer, gastric cancer, and pancreatic cancer, , has attracted widespread attention. Moreover, it has been reported that RNF2 inhibition improves the radiosensitivity and chemosensitivity in patients with lung cancer, esophageal cancer, and ovarian cancer. In particular, recent reports revealed that RNF2 ablation exerted crucial effects against breast cancer progression, , and our present study further demonstrated that RNF2 inhibition enhanced the antitumor efficacy of doxorubicin in murine breast cancer 4T1 cells. Consequently, this study broadens the understanding of the clinical implications of pharmacological RNF2 inhibition, such as its effects on enhancing the therapeutic effects of doxorubicin and reducing the toxicity associated with doxorubicin, in breast cancer therapy.
RNF2, as one of the primary constituents of PRC1 (polycomb repressive complex 1), catalyzes mono ubiquitination of histone H2A at lysine 119 and maintenance of compact chromatin to suppress the transcription of genes, such as E‐cadherin. However, only a few studies have investigated the function of RNF2 in mediating the ubiquitination of nonhistones. Here, for the first time, we identified MPST, a key H2S synthetase, as a ubiquitinating substrate of RNF2, and demonstrated that RNF2 promoted accelerated degradation of MPST at lysine K224 and lysine K295. H2S is endogenously synthesized from l‐cysteine by several enzymes, including cysteine aminotransferase, cystathionine‐β‐synthase, cystathionine‐γ‐lyase, and MPST, among which MPST is a crucial H2S‐generating enzyme in the cardiovascular system. In this study, we revealed that the MPST‐H2S axis was a downstream mediator contributing to RNF2‐mediated DIC. There is mounting evidence that H2S, which is endogenously produced at very low levels in mammalian cells, performs a number of biological functions, including the removal of oxidative species, reactions with the metal centers of iron‐heme proteins, and modifications of protein cysteine residues like persulfidation, and acts as a key drug target for managing oxidative insults and mitochondrial function. The function of H2S in cardiovascular disorders, such as myocardial ischemia/reperfusion damage, pulmonary arterial hypertension, heart failure, and DIC, has been thoroughly investigated in recent research. , For example, Zhang et al. demonstrated that H2S impeded ferroptosis through Keap1 S‐sulfhydration and Nrf2 activation to ameliorate DIC. In line with the findings of this study, we discovered that H2S downregulation contributed to RNF2‐mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in doxorubicin‐treated cardiomyocytes. Interestingly, the physiological functions of H2S depend on its exposure concentration. As previously mentioned, H2S can exert cardioprotective effects at a relatively low level; however, H2S is considered a toxic gas that reduces cell viability with excess accumulation. More recently, Yu et al. found that one of the most widely used H2S donors, 5‐(4‐hydroxyphenyl)‐3H‐1,2‐dithiole‐3‐thione, effectively inhibits the cell metastasis, proliferation, and viability of triple‐negative breast cancer cells, including malondialdehyde‐MB‐231 and 4T1 cells, both in vitro and in vivo. Consistent with these findings, our work indicated that RNF2 knockdown increased MPST and H2S levels and suppressed migration and invasion in doxorubicin‐treated 4T1 cells, although the potential mechanisms should be further investigated in future studies. Our present study provided further insights by investigating the opposite effects of RNF2 on doxorubicin‐treated cardiomyocytes and tumor cells.

## CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, this study revealed that the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF2 plays a crucial contributor of doxorubicin cardiotoxicity, driving cardiomyocyte oxidative injury and mitochondrial failure through ubiquitin‐proteasomal degradation of MPST and consequent suppression of cardioprotective H2S signaling. Our findings not only reinforce the biological function of RNF2 in heart diseases but also provide fresh insights into potential therapeutic targets for DIC.
