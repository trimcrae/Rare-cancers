## Summary
Brown adipocytes are unique in that they expend energy and produce heat to maintain euthermia through expression of uncoupling protein-1 (UCP1). Given their propensity to stimulate weight loss and promote resistance to obesity, they are a compelling interventional target for obesity-related disorders. Here, we tested whether an optogenetic approach could be used to activate UCP1-dependent thermogenesis in brown adipocytes. We generated brown adipocytes expressing a bacterial-derived photoactivatable adenylyl cyclase (bPAC) that, upon blue light stimulation, increases UCP1 expression, fuel uptake and thermogenesis. This unique system allows for precise, chemical free, temporal control of UCP1-dependent thermogenesis, which can aid in our understanding of brown adipocyte biology and development of therapies that target obesity-related disorders.

## Introduction
Periods of prolonged overeating result in excess lipid storage in white adipocytes, which can lead to inflammation, cellular stress, insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Conversely, brown adipose tissue (BAT) activity correlates positively with energy expenditure and reduced risk for metabolic syndrome and cardiometabolic diseases, making it an appealing therapeutic target tissue. , , , Brown adipocytes are rich in specialized mitochondria that expend energy to generate heat and maintain core body temperature. They are primarily activated in response to norepinephrine secreted from sympathetic nerve terminals that engage β-adrenergic surface receptors. Receptor activation triggers a signaling cascade leading to nutrient combustion that starts with cellular uptake of glucose and free fatty acids from the circulation and lipolysis of lipids derived from inner multilocular lipid droplets. , This process provides free fatty acids for β-oxidation that drive the electron transport chain to produce a proton-gradient within the inner mitochondrial membrane. Brown adipocytes are unique in that β-adrenergic activation also leads to the expression of uncoupling protein-1 (UCP1), an inner mitochondrial membrane protein that dissipates the mitochondrial electrochemical gradient from ATP synthesis by releasing protons back into the mitochondrial matrix, resulting in the release of energy through heat production. Unfortunately, modifying obesity and diabetes in humans through drug-based stimulation of β-adrenergic receptors has been met with limited success as it is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, prompting the development of alternative brown adipocyte activation strategies. , , , 
Although thermogenic adipose tissue has intrinsic anti-obesity properties, we lack a complete understanding of how UCP1-dependent thermogenesis is regulated. Levels of UCP1 regulation include its transcriptional activation, mRNA and protein turnover and its functional activation within the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it is inherently inactive in the absence of any accompanying stimulus. , To activate transcription, β-adrenergic receptors, coupled to Gs alpha subunits, activate membrane-associated adenylyl cyclases that lead to a rise in cAMP, a central second messenger regulating brown adipocyte function. Increased cAMP activates cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA) leading to the induction of lipolysis and liberation of free fatty acids, which are necessary for functional activation of UCP1-dependent proton leak. PKA also stimulates phosphorylation of CREB and activation of a set of thermogenic transcription factors that increase mitochondrial biogenesis, electron transport chain activity and induce Ucp1 transcription. 
A number of small molecule compounds have been employed to activate brown adipocytes directly through stimulation of β-adrenergic receptors (e.g., norepinephrine, isoproterenol, CL316,243, mirabegron) or adenylyl cyclase (forskolin). However, many of these compounds have off target effects and are not easily reversible. To circumvent off target effects, Gs-coupled designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) have been designed to be activated by highly specific synthetic ligands such as clozapine-N-oxide (CNO). When Gs-coupled DREADDs are conditionally expressed in adipose tissue of obese mice treated with CNO, metabolic improvements are observed including increased energy expenditure, normalized glucose homeostasis and decreased body weight and fat mass. , However, whether high affinity DREADD ligands such as CNO or Compound 21 (C21) are truly inert has been called into question as their chronic use has been linked to off target behavioral effects. 
An alternative approach to chemical modulation of cell signaling is optogenetics, a state-of-the-art technique that uses light-sensitive proteins to control specific cellular processes in genetically modified cells and can be used for precise temporal, spatial, reversible control of signaling. Optogenetic stimulation of the blue light-activated channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in sympathetic nerves has been shown to increase brown adipose tissue thermogenesis and lower blood glucose levels. As a more direct approach, to determine if brown adipocytes could be genetically modified for light inducible activation of UCP1-dependent thermogenesis, we took advantage of a bacterial photoactivatable adenylyl cyclase (bPAC), previously cloned from the soil bacterium Beggiatoa for chemical free stimulation of adenylyl cyclase. Upon stimulation with blue light, bPAC has been shown to increase cAMP levels and alter cellular physiology in cells from numerous species including Escherichia coli, Drosophila melanogaster (nervous system), Xenopus laevis (oocytes), Mus musculus (sperm), Rattus norvegicus (pyramidal neurons) and Homo sapiens (HEK-293T cells). , , Here, we demonstrate that blue light stimulation of bPAC in brown adipocytes promotes fuel uptake (glucose and fatty acids) and upregulation and activation of UCP1, concomitant with heat production. Overall, this novel system allows for precise, temporal control of UCP1-dependent thermogenesis in brown adipocytes and could be exploited therapeutically to reverse obesity-related disorders.

## Results

## Blue light stimulation of bPAC increases cAMP signaling in brown preadipocytes
Lentiviral transduction of a bPAC-mRFP1 expression construct with hygromycin selection was used to generate stably expressing, immortalized brown preadipocytes derived from the ThermoMouse ( A). , Because initial transduction of bPAC-mRFP1 in preadipocytes was very low (<3%), cells were purified by flow sorting for mRFP1 expression to increase purity to greater than 90% ( B). Purified brown preadipocytes selected with hygromycin were transiently transfected with the pGloSensor-20F cAMP plasmid to monitor intracellular changes in cAMP concentration in real-time via luminescence. Blue light activation of brown preadipocytes was conducted using an in house manufactured microplate photo irradiation system (MPS) configured with blue LEDs (466 nm). Significant increases in cAMP were observed after short blue light pulses that were equivalent to that observed with forskolin, a stimulator of endogenous adenylyl cyclase ( C). The specificity of our approach was confirmed by the absence of cGMP production after blue light stimulation compared to known stimulators of cGMP (ANP, IBMX) ( D). In addition, brown preadipocytes treated with blue light showed a dose dependent increase in phosphorylation of the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and expression of the immediate-early CREB target gene Nr4a3 ( E and 1F), demonstrating that activation of bPAC stimulates cAMP signaling. 
 Transduced preadipocytes represent a mixed population of transgenic and non-transgenic cells that could phenotypically drift over multiple passages. To generate brown preadipocyte clonal cell lines that maintain bPAC expression and differentiate into brown adipocytes, we performed single cell fluorescence-activated cell sorting for mRFP1 on the mixed, hygromycin selected preadipocyte population. Expanded preadipocyte clones Td1, Td3 and Td6 were selected for further analysis based on high bPAC expression when compared to the parental mixed culture ( G). These clones differentiated robustly into mature adipocytes as determined by lipid staining and expressed bPAC at levels similar to the original mixed preadipocyte population, with varying levels of Ucp1 expression ( H–1J). Clonal line Td1 was chosen for the majority of experiments moving forward because it consistently displayed high levels of bPAC and Ucp1 expression.

## Blue light stimulation of bPAC increases expression of Ucp1 and mitochondrial genes in brown adipocytes
To test if bPAC activation promotes Ucp1 transcription, we tested different temporal patterns of light treatments ( A). Differentiated brown adipocytes (clone Td1) were treated with transient or continuous blue light for 1 to 6h at time zero and harvested at 6 h. The length of the initial light pulse was proportional to the increase in Ucp1 expression, similar to results obtained with transient or continuous forskolin treatment ( B and 2C). Lowering blue light intensity resulted in a plateaued effect on Ucp1 transcription, demonstrating that light intensity can have a profound effect on bPAC activation ( B). Oscillating blue light pulses between 10 and 500 ms every second for 4 h were also tested. Similar to transient light pulsing, Ucp1 expression increased in a dose dependent manner, illustrating that continuous, uninterrupted light exposure maximizes Ucp1 expression ( D). Stimulation of bPAC negative brown adipocytes confirmed that blue light by itself does not stimulate Ucp1 expression ( E). We next tested bPAC induced expression of hallmark genes involved in mitochondrial biology, including biogenesis (Pgc1α, Tfam), ATP synthesis (Atp5g1, Atp5g3), electron transport (Ndufs4, Ndufb8/complex I, Sdhb/complex II, Uqcr2/complex III and Cox4i2/complex IV) and mitophagy (Bnip3, Bnip3l, Pink1, and Mff). These genes are necessary for increasing mitochondrial content, function and turnover in activated brown adipocytes and 4 h continuous blue light stimulation largely increased their expression (1.5–2.5-fold) even though these genes are not known to be direct transcriptional targets of CREB signaling ( F). Thus, light-induced stimulation of cAMP signaling increases expression of a program of genes that are necessary for proper mitochondrial function in brown adipocytes.
 Brown adipocytes in cell culture lose expression of UCP1 owing to absence of stimuli that activates downstream cAMP signaling, similar to those in mice housed at thermoneutrality. bPAC expressing brown adipocytes showed peak Ucp1 expression at 14 days after initiation of differentiation, which rapidly decreased thereafter ( G). To determine if activation of bPAC could lead to extended maintenance of Ucp1 expression, brown adipocytes were pulsed once per day with continuous blue light for 8 days, starting at day 10 and samples were harvested 24 h after the last light pulse ( G). 30–60 min of continuous blue light per day was sufficient to either maintain or increase Ucp1 transcript through day 18 compared to the previous peak (day 14). UCP1 protein levels were also higher in brown adipocytes after 8 days of 30–60 min blue light per day compared to cells receiving no light ( H). Td6 adipocytes failed to express UCP1 protein, consistent with qPCR analysis of Ucp1 transcript ( J). These data reveal that intermittent, optogenetic stimulation of cAMP signaling (30–60 min/day) is sufficient to prolong Ucp1 expression in brown adipocytes.

## Blue light stimulation of bPAC in brown adipocytes increases fuel uptake and thermogenesis in a UCP1-dependent manner
Upon activation, brown adipocytes increase uptake of fatty acids and glucose to be used as fuel for thermogenesis. Stimulation of Td1-bPAC+ brown adipocytes with 30 min of continuous blue light resulted in increased fatty acid and insulin-independent glucose uptake ( A and 3B). The addition of insulin in combination with blue light led to lower levels of glucose uptake compared to blue light stimulation alone, possibly because of negative crosstalk between insulin-independent and-dependent glucose uptake pathways. To determine if blue light stimulation of bPAC results in increased thermogenesis because of UCP1 activation, we used highly sensitive calScreener-based microcalorimetry to measure heat production. Brown adipocytes were pulsed with 30 min of blue light, placed in the calScreener and heat accumulation was measured over time ( C). bPAC+/UCP1+ brown adipocytes (clone Td1) responded robustly to blue light with a 4-fold greater accumulation of total heat compared to dark controls. No significant increases were observed in either the parental bPAC negative or UCP1 negative (clone Td6) adipocytes pulsed with blue light, suggesting that UCP1 may be the primary driver of bPAC stimulated heat production. To confirm the UCP1-dependent nature of the light inducible bPAC in brown adipocytes, Td1 clonal preadipocytes were transduced with short hairpin RNA (shRNA) that targets Ucp1 for degradation. Preadipocytes transduced with Scramble-EGFP:T2A:Bsd control or Ucp1-shRNA-EGFP:T2A:Bsd lentivirus were selected for blasticidin (Bsd) resistance over 7 days. The resulting preadipocytes stably express both the newly introduced viral constructs (EGFP), maintained expression of mRFP1-bPAC and differentiated into mature lipid containing adipocytes ( D). The Ucp1-shRNA lentivirus reduced Ucp1 expression in mature adipocytes by greater than 80% compared to scramble control cells and this was associated with an absence of detectable light-induced heat production ( E–3G). Overall, these results demonstrate that blue light stimulation of bPAC is able to increase UCP1-dependent thermogenesis in brown adipocytes.
 

## Discussion
Energy intake that chronically exceeds energy expenditure is a primary driver of increased adiposity, obesity and adipose tissue dysfunction. Although the balance between energy intake and expenditure can be shifted favorably in the obese through diet and exercise, the success rate of lifestyle interventions is generally modest and transient. Brown adipocytes become metabolically activated in response to cold-stimulated release of norepinephrine through the sympathetic nervous system, where they expend energy stored in glucose and lipids to generate heat. In brown adipose tissue, UCP1 is a key mechanistic component for increasing energy expenditure and heat production and its upregulation is associated with reduced obesity and improved insulin sensitivity. To take advantage of brown adipocytes for therapeutics, new models that circumvent the cardiovascular risks associated with β-adrenergic agonist-based activation of brown adipocytes are needed. Here, we established an optogenetic model to activate UCP1-dependent thermogenesis in which a light inducible adenylyl cyclase, originally cloned and genetically modified from the bacterium Beggiatoa, was used to increase intracellular levels of cAMP, UCP1 expression, fuel uptake and UCP1-dependent heat production in brown adipocytes. The kinetics associated with this optogenetic model suggest that 30 min of light-induced activation per day is sufficient to increase UCP1 expression and thermogenesis in cultured brown adipocytes. Of interest, a recent study demonstrated that transfer of mice from thermoneutrality to 18C for ≤30 min per day was sufficient to induce structural changes in brown adipose tissue with significant increases in UCP1 expression over a period of 4 weeks. 
Before this study, it was not clear if photoactivatable adenylyl cyclases isolated from other organisms such as bacteria or algae were powerful enough to stimulate cAMP production to levels that increase or maintain thermogenesis in brown adipocytes. Indeed, the discovery of new PACs may offer enhanced stimulation of thermogenesis in brown adipocytes with less light intensity or shorter treatment times than that of bPAC. For example, a plasma membrane-anchored PAC (PACmn) was recently engineered from bPAC that displays a 4000-fold increase in cAMP after 2s of blue light stimulation, greatly exceeding forskolin activation of endogenous cyclases. 
Our optogenetics approach also shows that UCP1 is the primary driver of heat production in Td1-bPAC+ brown adipocytes, as UCP1 deficient brown adipocytes lacked significant increases in heat production when stimulated with blue light. At this time, we are unable to conclude if blue-light activation of bPAC in other adipocyte models could additionally co-activate UCP1-independent pathways of thermogenesis, such as Ca2+ or creatine futile cycling. , For example, it was recently shown in thermogenic adipocytes that ADRA1A–Gαq signaling stimulates creatine futile signaling in a coordinated fashion with ADRB1-Gαs production of cAMP, in part through promoting creatine futile cycling gene expression. It is also noteworthy that we did not observe blue light mediated thermogenesis in bPAC negative brown adipocytes as several studies have shown that beige and brown adipocytes have a natural light sensing mechanism via the blue-light-responsive opsin, OPN3, which is necessary for enhanced thermogenic capacity and cold tolerance in mice. , Whether this particular mechanism could be harnessed to substantially increase thermogenesis to levels necessary for weight loss in the absence of cold is unknown.
A fundamental concern for therapeutic development has been the selective modulation of UCP1 expression in brown adipocytes without adversely affecting regulation of other tissues. For example, because β3-adrenergic receptor agonists are associated with cardiovascular risks, localized cell-based therapies, such as optogenetics, could limit off target effects and provide an effective and safer alternative to drugs for stimulation of energy expenditure in brown adipocytes. A strategy to overexpress bPAC in transplanted brown adipocytes for stimulation of UCP1-dependent thermogenesis may have merit as CRISPR based overexpression of UCP1 in human white adipocytes improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity and increased energy expenditure when transplanted into mice. One advantage of using precise optogenetic control of thermogenesis is that energy expenditure can be modulated bidirectionally to prevent side effects, such as excess heat production that could be detrimental to adipocyte function or proper maintenance of euthermia. Recently, implantation of a wireless optogenetic device adjacent to subcutaneous adipose tissue in ChR2 transgenic mice was used to activate the non-canonical Ca2+ cycling thermogenesis program and protect mice from obesity. In the future, combining multiple optogenetic activators of different thermogenic pathways may further enhance adipocyte energy expenditure in a precise manner for reversal of obesity and its related comorbidities.

## Limitations of the study
To ensure consistency and prevent phenotypic drift of mixed cultures of transgenic cells, we generated clonal preadipocyte cell lines overexpressing bPAC for this study. It’s important to note that different clonal populations of bPAC+ adipocytes may yield vastly different results because of the integration of viral DNA into diverse genomic locations. Adipocytes derived from the Td6 clonal cell line may represent one such example. In addition, higher intensities of blue light may positively or negatively affect the potency or time course of brown adipocyte activation. We were limited in investigating this potential phenomenon as the circuitry from the light board increased the temperature of the culture medium at higher light intensities. Using photostable culture media in the future could mitigate any negative impact on the cultured cells exposed to high intensity blue light. Lastly, it is still uncertain whether microcalorimetry experiments are sensitive enough to detect UCP1-independent mechanisms of heat production. It’s possible that these mechanisms are active but below the level of instrument detection.

## Method details

## Plasmid construction
To develop brown adipocytes capable of light induced activation, we utilized a variant of wild-type bPAC that contains a serine to alanine mutation at position 27 (bPAC-S27A). This variant has peak blue light absorption at 456 nm and reduced basal dark activity for tighter light inducible control compared to wild-type bPAC. , The parental bPAC plasmid pAAV-hSyn1-bPAC-cMyc-S27A-2A-tDimer was a gift from Thomas Oertner (Addgene plasmid # 85398; http://n2t.net/addgene:85398; RRID:Addgene_85398). pCD-EF1α-MCS-T2A (PGK-Puro) (SBI, CD822A-1) was modified via In-Fusion© cloning to pCD-EF1α-MCS-T2A (PGK-Hygro) (Takara Bio USA, Inc., 102518). The 2kb bPAC fragment was amplified with Q5 DNA polymerase (NEB, M0492S), gel purified and In-Fusion cloned into pCD-EF1α-MCS-T2A (PGK-Hygro) at Not1 and packaged into 3rd generation lentivirus.

## Cell culture and generation of clonal cell lines
ThermoMouse brown preadipocytes were expanded in basal medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (ATCC, 30-2020), 1X GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050061) and 1X Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco, 15240062). Brown preadipocytes were transduced with in house generated lentivirus containing the EF1α-bPAC-T2A-mRFP1-PGK-Hygro plasmid. Virus was removed 24 hours after transduction and cells were allowed to expand for an additional 24 hours. Preadipocytes were dissociated from cell culture plates using TrypLE (Gibco, 12563029), pelleted (300 xg) and resuspended in autoMacs Running Buffer (Miltenyi, 130-091-221) prior to flow sorting of the bulk population by mRFP1 (561nm/594nm) on a BD Biosystems FACSAria. Transduced cells were subsequently expanded and selected with and without 200 ng/ml hygromycin B (InvivoGen, ant-hg-1) for up to 7 days. Single cells from the hygromycin B selected, transduced bPAC+ preadipocytes were sorted by mRFP1+ expression into 96 well plates using a BD Biosystems FACsAria. Single cells were expanded into colonies and passed into increasing larger well/plate sizes while selected on hygromycin B (200 ng/ml). Samples from clones that continued to proliferate and maintain mRFP1 expression as determined by immunofluorescence microscopy on the EVOS M5000 Imaging System (ThermoFisher Scientific, AMF5000) were lysed and tested by qPCR for expression of bPAC. shRNA-scramble and shRNA-Ucp1 virus was custom generated using the plasmids described in the by VectorBuilder. Brown preadipocytes were transduced with shRNA lentivirus for 24 hours and cells were allowed to expand for an additional 24 hours prior to 7 days of blasticidin selection (2ug/ml). EGFP expression in stably transduced cells was verified by immunofluorescence microscopy.
For differentiation into brown adipocytes, cells were grown to 100% confluence in Geltrex coated Krystal™ 24 Well Glass Bottom Microplates (Southern Labware, 324041) and treated with brown adipocyte induction medium for 48 hours, consisting of basal medium supplemented with insulin (170nM), T3 (2nM), rosiglitazone (1uM), IMBX (0.5mM), dexamethasone (5uM), indomethacin (125uM) and ascorbic acid (50ug/ml). Following induction, medium was replaced with growth medium supplemented with insulin (170nM), T3 (2nM), rosiglitazone (1uM) and ascorbic acid (50ug/ml) for 72 hours. Cells were then maintained in basal medium until experiments were performed as described in the text.

## BODIPY staining
Culture medium was replaced with BODIPY diluted 1:2000 in fresh culture medium for 30 minutes at 37°C. Following 30 minutes, culture medium with BODIPY was removed and cells were gently washed once with PBS. PBS was removed and replaced with fresh cell culture medium prior to imaging on an EVOS Fluorescence Microscope using the 482 nm channel.

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
RNA was extracted from monolayer cultures with the RNeasy MicroKit (Qiagen, 74004). cDNA synthesis was performed using qScript cDNA SuperMix (Quanta Biosciences, 95048-100). qPCR was carried out using AzuraView GreenFast qPCR Blue Mix LR (Azura Genomics, AZ-2320) and amplified on a CFX384 real-time PCR machine (BioRad) using primers containing sequences listed in . Fold changes (relative mRNA) were calculated using the ΔΔCT method, normalized to endogenous Actb levels and displayed relative to vehicle treated or day 0 controls. Specificities of primer sets were determined by melt curve analysis on qPCR-generated amplicons. Average values (±SD) of three replicates are shown for each graph.

## Western blot
Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing Halt Protease & Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78440) and centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 5 minutes to pellet cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and cell lysates were denatured at 95C for 5 minutes. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225) following manufacturer’s instructions to normalize gel loading. Protein was separated on 4-20% Criterion TGX precast gels (BioRad, 5671094) and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4C in 1X Detector Block Solution (KPL, 72-01-03). Primary antibodies against CREB (Cell Signaling Technology, 9197), phospho-CREB (Ser133) (Cell Signaling Technology, 9198), UCP1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 72298) and β-tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology, 2146) were diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 in TBS with Tween 20 and 0.2X Detector Block Solution and incubated overnight at 4C. Membranes were washed 3 times with TBS with Tween 20 (5 minutes per wash). Following washes, HRP secondaries diluted at a ratio of 1:1200 were incubated for 2 hours at room temperature and washed 3 times with TBS with Tween 20 (5 minutes per wash). Membranes were developed with Clarity Western ECL Substrate (BioRad, 1705061) and detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System.

## Multiplate photoirradiation system (MPS) and light pulsing
Blue light pulsing was conducted using an in-house manufactured 24-well microplate photo irradiation system (MPS) configured with blue LEDs (466nm) with a 5-volt, 40-amp, AC power supply as described in. Cells were pulsed with blue light (466nm) in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator set at 37C with light intensities between 0.4 to 1 mW/cm2 for up to 6 hours as described in the text.

## cAMP, fatty acid and glucose uptake assays
The luciferase-based GloSensor cAMP Assay (Promega, E1290) was used to monitor intracellular cAMP in brown preadipocytes (clonal line Td1). bPAC+ brown preadipocytes were transfected in basal medium with the pGloSensor™-20F cAMP Plasmid (Promega, E1171) in tissue culture grade 96 well Krystal microplates (Porvair Sciences, 214006) using the TransIT-X2 Transfection Reagent (Mirus, MIR 6004). 48 hours after transfection, cells were incubated with GloSensor cAMP Reagent for 2 hours. 10 minutes after forskolin (5um) or blue light treatments, relative luciferase units (RLU) were measured using the GloMax Explorer Microplate Reader & Luminometer at 0.3-1.0 second integrations (Promega).
The Glucose Uptake-Glo™ Assay (Promega, J1341) was used to measure glucose uptake in mature brown adipocytes (clonal line Td1). 24 hours prior to the assay, basal medium was replaced with serum free DMEM containing 0.5% BSA, 10mM HEPES and 1X GlutaMAX for 24 hours. On the day of the assay, medium was replaced with 100ul of serum and glucose free DMEM for 1 hour at 37C and pulsed with blue light and/or treated with a range of insulin concentrations for 30 minutes. Glucose uptake analysis was performed by following the Glucose Uptake-Glo Assay Kit manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed for luminescence at 0.3-1.0 second integrations using the GloMax Explorer Microplate Reader & Luminometer (Promega).
The QBT Fatty Acid Uptake Assay (Molecular Devices, R8132) was used to measure fatty acid uptake in mature brown adipocytes (clonal line Td1). 24 hours prior to the assay, basal medium was replaced with 100ul of serum free DMEM containing 10mM HEPES and 1X GlutaMAX. Brown adipocytes were treated with 30 minutes of blue light and/or forskolin (5 um) at 37C. Medium was removed and 100ul of 1X Loading Buffer containing 0.2% fatty acid free BSA was added per well. Plates were transferred immediately to a FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices) pre-set to 37C for readings in bottom mode with excitation and emission parameters set at 485/515 nm.

## Microcalorimetry
Total metabolic activity was measured on the calScreenerTM (Symcel SverigeAB, Spånga, Sweden), an advanced isothermal microcalorimetry based instrument for accurate and sensitive measures of heat production in real-time. Brown preadipocytes were expanded and differentiated in 200ul of medium in tissue culture treated calWell insert plates (Symcel, 1900402) coated with Geltrex (ThermoFisher Scientific, A1413201). Immediately after blue light stimulation, calWell inserts containing brown adipocytes were transferred to screw-capped titanium vials. The calPlate containing 48 calWell inserts was inserted into two temperature equilibrium zones for a total of 30 minutes prior to entering the final measurement position. Data was collected after an additional 15-30 minutes stabilization time to allow for frictional heat loss due to final sample positioning. Data was continuously collected with the calView software and normalized to internal reference wells containing medium only.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
P values were calculated in Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) using a two-tailed, unpaired Student t test with P < 0.05 considered significant. One-way ordinary ANOVA was used to analyze the difference between the means when analyzing two or more groups. In the case of multiple comparisons, Tukey correction was used to determine P values after one-way ANOVA analysis. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Error bars represent the mean ± SD (standard deviation) of 3 replicates unless otherwise noted in the figure legends with ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P<0.001 and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
