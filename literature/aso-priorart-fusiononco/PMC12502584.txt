## Abstract
Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are emerging as important regulators of gene expression in both normal biological systems and disease. Among them, metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) has attracted considerable attention due to its high nuclear expression and evolutionary conservation. It was first identified as a biomarker for lung cancer metastasis, but since then, its involvement has been reported in a wide range of cancers. MALAT1 influences several key cancer-related processes, including cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, apoptosis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and the behavior of cancer stem cells. In this review, we take a closer look at how MALAT1 functions through different mechanisms, such as regulating RNA splicing, altering chromatin states, acting as a sponge for microRNAs, or modifying protein interactions, to drive these changes. We also discuss its growing role in shaping the immune landscape of tumors, particularly its involvement in immune evasion, inflammatory signaling, and regulation of immunogenic cell death. In addition, MALAT1 contributes to resistance against therapy, rewiring of cellular metabolism, and communication between tumor and stromal cells via exosomes. While many studies describe MALAT1 as an oncogene, others suggest it may also play a suppressive role depending on the cancer type and biological context. Here, we aim to offer a comprehensive overview of MALAT1’s diverse actions and evaluate its potential as a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target in cancer.
Keywords: Long noncoding RNA, MALAT1, MicroRNA, Drug resistance, Cancer immunology, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Biomarker

## Background
Early research on the molecular origins of human disease was focused mainly on protein-coding genes and their modifications. Recent advancements in human transcriptomics technology have revealed that while 75% of the human genome is transcribed, less than 2% of the genome encodes for proteins [ ]. These findings demonstrated that the human genome contains regions that are transcribed into non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) with vital roles in the regulation of gene transcription and protein translation. NcRNAs are generally categorized based on their length, with a threshold of 200 nucleotides that separates long from short ncRNAs [ , ]. As one of the most abundant types of ncRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have a significant role in numerous aspects of molecular biology, including epigenetic modifications, X-chromosome inactivation, imprinting, splicing, and telomerase activity regulation [ – ]. Many lncRNAs are linked to chromatin modifiers, enhancer activity, and nuclear architecture, highlighting their role in spatial gene regulation. In the cytoplasm, they also influence translation, metabolism, and signaling pathways [ ]. This expanding understanding of lncRNA diversity has reshaped the central dogma of molecular biology, emphasizing that RNA molecules, far beyond their messenger roles, carry out regulatory, structural, and catalytic functions across the genome.
LncRNAs exert regulatory effects through both cis and trans mechanisms [ ]. As cis acting molecules, lncRNAs usually influence the activity of genes that are in proximity to their own genomic location, most commonly through altering chromatin accessibility or recruiting transcriptional regulators. Acting in trans, lncRNAs influence genes or regulatory elements that are distant in the genome, also relatively commonly acting as molecular scaffolds or guides for protein complexes associated with gene expression or RNA processing [ ]. Their context-dependent functions make lncRNAs important in development and disease, adding a distinct regulatory layer beyond protein-coding genes. However, their specific roles in driving disease phenotypes remain debated, as many associations lack rigorous causal validation, particularly in human in vivo models.
Currently, the oncogenic or tumor-suppressive influence of lncRNAs in human cancers has received a lot of interest in the scientific community [ ]. HOX Transcript Antisense RNA (HOTAIR), Metastasis Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1 (MALAT1), Maternally Expressed 3 (MEG3), H19, Growth Arrest Specific 5 (GAS5), and Nuclear Enriched Abundant Transcript 1 (NEAT1) are some of the most significant lncRNAs, with ≥ 16 recorded cancer types (Fig. ) [ ].
 MALAT1, also known as nuclear-enriched abundant transcript 2 (NEAT2), is an 8.5 kb nuclear lncRNA, first identified in 2003 by Ji et al. in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells [ ]. MALAT1 is located at chromosome 11q13.1 [molecular location: Chromosome 11, NC_000011.10 (65,497,738–−65,506,516)] and is characterized as a single-exon gene. Unlike most of the identified lncRNAs, MALAT1 is highly conserved, which further illustrates its regulatory role in the evolution and normal development [ ]. It is suggested that MALAT1 is retained in the nucleus and localizes to nuclear bodies, i.e., nuclear speckles, to regulate gene expression by modulating the function of speckles [ – ]. MALAT1 is transcribed by RNA polymerase II and is among the most abundantly expressed lncRNAs in the human genome. Its promoter shows open chromatin features and is transcriptionally active across various tissues. The transcript lacks canonical introns and is processed through an unusual 3′ end cleavage by RNase P and RNase Z, which generates two separate RNA molecules: the long nuclear-retained MALAT1 and a smaller cytoplasmic tRNA-like fragment known as mascRNA (Fig. ) [ – ]. MALAT1 exemplifies an unusual yet refined mode of lncRNA biology. Despite lacking introns in its canonical form, it gives rise to both a nuclear-retained long transcript and a smaller cytoplasmic RNA, suggesting bifunctional potential. The 3′ end of MALAT1 forms a unique triple-helix by pairing a U-rich tract with an A-rich region, giving the RNA exceptional stability and nuclear retention while protecting it from exonucleolytic degradation [ , ]. This triple-helix region serves as both a structural motif and a functional hub, integrating epitranscriptomic signals with nuclear speckle organization. Its stability and nuclear retention are actively regulated by dynamic RNA modifications such as m6A, which shape MALAT1's folding and its scaffolding functions [ ]. Additionally, the natural antisense transcript TALAM1, transcribed from the same locus, works cooperatively with MALAT1 to enhance its expression via a feed-forward mechanism [ ]. Its remarkable conservation across species and high expression in diverse tissues emphasize its evolutionary and regulatory importance [ – ]. In nuclear speckles, MALAT1 interacts with splicing regulators such as SR proteins and hnRNPs, supporting its role in alternative splicing. It also associates with chromatin and guides transcriptional complexes to specific loci, influencing gene expression [ ]. Its presence at active promoters, CTCF sites, and involvement in chromatin looping further suggest a role in coordinating the spatial organization of gene expression [ ].
 The unique characteristics of MALAT1 have drawn attention for further evaluation of its physiological function and its potential as a next-generation anti-cancer therapeutic target [ , ]. Numerous studies have shown that by associating molecular signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad, WNT/β-catenin, mTOR, PI3K/AKT, MALAT1 exerts its roles in cell proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, metastasis, and apoptosis [ – ]. Nonetheless, the degree to which MALAT1 acts as a central driver versus a contextual amplifier of these pathways is still under debate and likely varies by tumor type and cellular state. Importantly, the human MALAT1 gene produces dozens of transcript isoforms, with recent annotations listing over 60 variants [ ]. While MALAT1 is predominantly a single-exon lncRNA, its isoforms vary in length, 3'-end processing and perhaps exon structure. This variation is not random; different cell types and conditions preferentially express specific MALAT1 variants, suggesting compartmentalized function in different biological contexts, such as development or tumor progression [ , ]. Thus, MALAT1 should be considered a family of related RNAs, not a single molecule, which has important implications for functional studies and therapeutic targeting.
In this review of the most recent findings in the literature, we provide a comprehensive analysis of MALAT1 function in human cancers, focusing on its role as both an oncogene and, in certain contexts, a tumor suppressor. While several studies have revealed its involvement in cancer proliferation, metastasis, metabolism, and immune evasion, the context-dependent nature of MALAT1 function, especially its dual role in tumor progression and immune regulation, remains poorly understood. Several conflicting findings, particularly between in vitro and in vivo results, underscore the need for careful re-evaluation of mechanistic claims surrounding MALAT1, as well as greater use of genetic knockout and lineage-tracing models. We summarize the contributions of MALAT1 to the major hallmarks of cancer, its molecular mechanisms and pathways of action, and its controversial and less-explored immunological contributions. We also elaborate some new and interesting therapeutic possibilities targeting MALAT1, including its epigenetic, metabolic, and exosomal effects, alongside discussing its potential for clinical application as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. This review is intended to provide more clarity of the complex biology of MALAT1, and we hope to encourage further exploration of its unexploited contributions to cancer progression and treatment resistance.

## Multiple cellular functions of MALAT1 in human cancers
MALAT1 plays a dual role as an oncogene/tumor suppressor in human tumors via regulating the expression or activity of downstream targets and by being involved in the hallmarks of cancer, including cell proliferation, cell motility, sustained angiogenesis, and evading apoptosis (Table ) (Fig. ).
 

## Cell proliferation
Sustained cell proliferation allows the tumor to grow more aggressively and rapidly, making it a key factor in cancer progression. In osteosarcoma tissues and cell lines, overexpression of MALAT1 increases cell proliferation by inhibiting miR-205 and activating the SMAD4 function [ ]. Similarly, inhibition of miR-590-3p by MALAT1 leads to enhanced proliferation and reduced apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells [ ]. Upregulation of MALAT1 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the PI3K/AKT pathway resulting in increased cell proliferation of the epithelial ovarian cancer cells [ ]. A recent study revealed that MALAT1 levels were negatively correlated with miR-374b-5p and positively correlated with SRSF7 in NSCLC tissues. According to the findings, elevated expression of MALAT1 promotes cell proliferation via the miR-374b-5p/SRSF7 axis [ ]. Furthermore, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, MALAT1 targets miR-101 and facilitates cell proliferation and invasion [ ]. An interesting study uncovered a novel MALAT1/miR-499-5p/Meis1 axis that regulates proliferation via suppression of Meis1, a known tumor suppressor. MALAT1 knockdown upregulated miR-499-5p, leading to reduced Meis1 expression and enhanced cell growth. This regulatory loop was shown to affect Wnt and mTOR signaling, emphasizing the multifaceted and context-specific role of MALAT1 in proliferation control [ ]. Importantly, large-scale transcriptome-wide CRISPR-Cas13 loss-of-function screens recently confirmed MALAT1 as one of only 46 universally essential lncRNAs across five diverse human cancer cell lines, including breast and lung models. MALAT1 knockdown led to G2/M cell cycle arrest and increased apoptosis, validating its role in sustaining proliferation and survival. Taken together, these studies affirmed that MALAT1 acts independently of neighboring protein coding genes, confirming its independent regulatory function [ ].
However, whether these effects are uniformly pro-oncogenic across tumor types or modulated by MALAT1 isoform diversity, subcellular compartmentalization, or interacting RNA-binding proteins remains largely untested in vivo. Most proliferation-related studies rely on overexpression or knockdown in vitro systems, and future CRISPR-based or inducible knockout models are needed to establish sufficiency and causality.

## Cell motility
A major cause of cancer complications and high mortality is the ability of tumor cells to invade neighboring tissues and metastasize to distant organs. MALAT1 is involved in the invasion and metastasis of numerous commonly observed cancers. In colorectal cancer cells, miR-106b-5p acts as a tumor suppressor, and its decreased expression is associated with poor survival in patients with colorectal cancer. MALAT1 targets miR-106b-5p to increase the invasion and metastasis of colorectal cancer cells by SLAIN2-enhanced microtubules mobility [ ]. miRNA-613 by modulating the SOX9 pathway hampered the expansion of HCC stem cells. MALAT1 upregulated the HCC invasion by inhibiting miRNA-613 through peripheral vascular infiltration [ ]. Furthermore, Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a highly metastatic subtype of breast cancer that is associated with aggressive clinical behavior and a poor prognosis. MALAT1 promotes the invasion of TNBC cells through sponging miR-129-5p [ ]. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is an RNA post-transcriptional modification. Recent findings demonstrated that, MALAT1 harboring m6A-motifs is related to the metastatic activity of MALAT1. This motif helps the MALAT1 to recruit YTH-domain-containing protein 1 (YTHDC1). The binding of YTHDC1 to m6A enables MALAT1 to regulate oncogene expression [ ].
Beyond canonical regulators of invasion, MALAT1 also orchestrates the spatial hierarchy of cell behavior during collective migration. Using single-cell nano biosensors, a recent study demonstrated that MALAT1 is dynamically upregulated in leader cells at the invasive front of cancer spheroids. Its knockdown prevented leader cell formation and abrogated collective invasion, highlighting MALAT1’s crucial role in coordinating invasion dynamics at the multicellular level [ ]. This adds a new layer to our understanding of MALAT1’s role in invasion beyond transcriptional control.
Elevated MALAT1 levels were linked to a poor outcome in individuals with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Experiments conducted both in vitro settings and in vivo showed that silencing MALAT1 significantly decreased the capacities of HNSCC cells to grow and spread to other parts of the body. MALAT1 inhibited the von Hippel–Lindau tumor suppressor (VHL) by activating the EZH2/STAT3/Akt axis through a specific mechanism. This activation resulted in the stability and activation of β-catenin and NF-κB, which play a crucial role in promoting HNSCC development and metastasis [ ].
Interestingly, a recent study uncovered its protective role against bone metastasis and osteoporosis. MALAT1 expression was found to be downregulated during osteoclastogenesis, and MALAT1-knockout mice exhibited increased osteoclast activity, resulting in both osteoporotic bone loss and enhanced bone metastasis of breast cancer and melanoma cells. Mechanistically, MALAT1 binds to Tead3, a transcription factor in pre-osteoclasts, and inhibits its interaction with Nfatc1, thereby suppressing the transcriptional program necessary for osteoclast differentiation. Notably, genetic re-expression of MALAT1 reversed the bone phenotypes, and single-cell RNA-seq of clinical samples revealed decreased MALAT1 in osteoporotic and metastatic bone lesions. These findings provide strong evidence that MALAT1 can act as a suppressor of bone metastasis and a regulator of bone homeostasis through the Tead3/Nfatc1 signaling axis, expanding its relevance beyond conventional tumor models [ ].
These findings underscore that MALAT1’s influence on invasion is highly context-dependent, shaped by both cell-intrinsic mechanisms and its genomic locus. Its pro- or anti-metastatic roles likely depend on specific epigenetic modifiers like EZH2 and external cues such as inflammation anf ECM remodeling. Clarifying these regulatory networks will be crucial to understanding MALAT1’s dual impact in cancer progression.

## Angiogenesis
Angiogenesis is a critical aspect of cancer cell growth, and MALAT1 plays a significant role in this process. In vitro analysis has demonstrated that the knockdown of MALAT1 in breast cancer cells downregulates the expression level of VEGF and inhibits tube formation [ ]. It has been shown that miR-140 may act as an oncomiR in several cancers. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma, MALAT1 promotes angiogenesis by sponging miR-140 and upregulating VEGFa [ ]. In osteosarcoma, MALAT1 regulates the miR-150-5p/VEGFa axis to increase tumor angiogenesis [ ]. Moreover, the basic FGF2 is a growth factor and signaling protein that induces angiogenesis in vivo [ ]. In hypoxic conditions of human neuroblastoma cells, overexpression of MALAT1 enhances FGF2 expression and promotes tumor angiogenesis [ ]. Key Notch signaling pathway ligand Jagged-1 (JAG1) contributes to vascular budding and has been found to be a poor prognostic factor for TNBC. JAG1 from TNBC cells induced angiogenesis in human vascular endothelial cells (HUVEC), according to research done after the cells were exposed to conditioned media from TNBC cells. Exosomes produced from TNBC-conditioned media were subsequently analyzed, and the results indicated that JAG1 stimulated MDA-MB-231 cell exosome production via the ALIX-RAB11A/RAB35 pathway. Additionally, it was found that exosomes from TNBC cells that overexpressed JAG1 had increased amounts of MALAT1, which promoted angiogenesis in HUVEC by sponging miR-140-5p [ ]. Inhibiting MALAT1 reduced the growth of multiple myeloma cells and promoted cell death, while also suppressing the formation of new blood vessels by endothelial cells. MALAT1 was shown to directly interact with microRNA-15a/16. In vitro, the effects of MALAT1 deletion on multiple myeloma cells were partially reversed by suppressing microRNA-15a/16. This reversal was also observed in vivo regarding tumor development and angiogenesis. Subsequent research found that MALAT1 functioned as a competitive endogenous RNA for microRNA-15a/16 in order to regulate the production of VEGFA [ ].
Altogether, these findings highlight the oncogenic role of MALAT1 in tumor vascularization through modulation of multiple angiogenic mediators. Therefore, MALAT1 exerts a pro-tumorigenic effect by enhancing angiogenesis.

## Apoptosis
Growing evidence supports the role of MALAT1 in the deregulation of programmed cell death in cancer cells. MALAT1 decreases retinoblastoma apoptosis by inhibiting miR-598-3p to activate PI3K/AKT pathway [ ].
In general, MALAT1's cellular functions in cancer hallmarks, proliferation, motility, angiogenesis, and apoptosis, involve a multifaceted regulatory role through both intracellular signaling and tumor microenvironment interaction. Mechanistically, MALAT1 acts by virtue of an extensive array of processes such as miRNA sponging, m6A modification, and regulation of major pathways like PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin. Whereas most past research identifies MALAT1 as a driver oncogene, mainly in cell proliferation and metastasis support, recent evidence reveals a more complex picture. MALAT1 may exert tumor-suppressing effects under some conditions, such as inhibiting TEAD/YAP signaling in breast cancer or the regulation of osteoclast differentiation in the bone microenvironment. These conflicting findings leave open the question of whether MALAT1's function is context-dependent, isoform expression, tissue-specific, or determined by subcellular localization. Notably, most recent research was conducted in knockdown models and in vitro assays, and additional in vivo and systems-level studies are needed to establish the conditions under which MALAT1 promotes or suppresses cancer formation. Identification of these regulatory contingencies will be critical to establishing MALAT1's worth as a biomarker or therapeutic target in different cancers. Future studies that employ lineage tracing, conditional deletion, and single-cell spatial transcriptomics are essential to resolve these controversies and clarify whether MALAT1 is a functional driver or an epiphenomenon in specific tumor ecosystems.

## Signaling pathways associated with MALAT1 in cancer
Due to the critical importance of cell signaling pathways in the initiation, development, and metastasis of cancer, lncRNAs participating in these pathways have the potential to influence all facets of carcinogenesis (Fig. ) [ ]. MALAT1 increases cell proliferation by regulating the Hippo-YAP pathway in pancreatic cancer. In other words, overexpression of MALAT1 induces the expression of YAP1 and inhibits the expression of LATS1, hence regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion [ ]. In colorectal cancer, high expression of MALAT1 promotes the growth of the cancer cells via inhibiting β-catenin degradation. Mechanistically, MALAT1 suppresses β-catenin degradation through upregulating H3K27 trimethylation and repressing GSK-3β expression [ ]. In addition, MALAT1 influences EMT by regulating the nuclear translocation of NF-κB in oral squamous cell carcinoma [ ]. Akt/mTOR pathway is a central regulator of various cancers. In NSCLC, MALAT1 promotes tumor cell migration and invasion by targeting miR-206 and activating Akt/mTOR signaling [ ]. Accumulating data showed that overexpressed methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) in prostate cancer mediated m6A modification of MALAT1 and increased proliferation and invasion by activating PI3K/AKT signaling [ ]. In a related mechanism observed in osteosarcoma, METTL3 was shown to catalyze m6A methylation of MALAT1, which enhanced its stability and promoted cancer cell proliferation and migration. This study highlights the emerging epitranscriptomic regulation of MALAT1 as an additional layer of oncogenic control in solid tumors [ ]. In prostate cancer, MALAT1 expression is transcriptionally activated by STAT3, forming part of a broader oncogenic cascade. Once upregulated, MALAT1 functions as a competing endogenous RNA to sponge miR-30a-5p, leading to increased downstream activation of cell proliferation and migration pathways. Concurrently, MYC reinforces this effect by directly repressing miR-30a-5p transcription, establishing a dual mechanism that amplifies STAT3-driven tumor-promoting signaling [ ]. MALAT1 increases mTOR/PKM2 pathway protein expressions by sponging miR-101-3p, promoting breast cancer cell progression [ ]. Consistently, a study on breast cancer patients and cell lines demonstrated that MALAT1 silencing significantly reduced PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activity at both transcript and protein levels, leading to impaired cancer cell proliferation and invasion, further supporting MALAT1's oncogenic role in modulating this axis [ ]. It also has been shown that MALAT1 promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma via activation of the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway [ ]. Another study demonstrated that MALAT1 increases cell proliferation by activating the Notch-1 signaling pathway in chondrosarcoma cells [ ]. The TGF-β signaling pathway is also a strong promoter of EMT, and MALAT1 plays a crucial role in regulating TGF-β1-induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition by downregulating miR-145 [ ].
 These findings underscore MALAT1’s ability to hijack multiple oncogenic pathways, including Hippo, Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT, ERK/MAPK, Notch, NF-κB, and TGF-β, to sustain proliferation, support immune evasion, and enhance cancer cell plasticity. This multifaceted control positions MALAT1 as a central regulator of tumor-promoting signaling networks across diverse cancer types.

## Detailed molecular mechanisms of MALAT1 in various cancers

## Solid tumor malignancies

## Lung cancer
MALAT1 is described as a prognostic marker of lung cancer metastasis in the early stages of diseases [ , ]. Several studies have reported the high expression of MALAT1 in patients with NSCLC and that MALAT1 expression is correlated with the severity of pathophysiological symptoms in the patients [ ]. Indeed, MALAT1 can be considered a candidate blood-based biomarker for the diagnosis of NSCLC [ ]. Furthermore, MALAT1 had a high expression level in NSCLC patients that exhibited recurrence in comparison with those that did not. This study also revealed that MALAT1 overexpression is associated with the female sex, Tumor-Node-Metastasis advanced stage, and vessel invasion [ ]. An increased level of MALAT1 promotes proliferation and invasion via sponging miR-202 [ ]. SOX9 has been implicated as an oncogene in various types of cancer. There is evidence that SOX9 protein has higher expression in NSCLC tissues, especially in those with overexpression of MALAT1 mRNA [ ]. Although the direct link between MALAT1 and SOX9 remains unclear, this correlation points toward a potential transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulatory axis that could be explored in future studies. MALAT1, with stabilizing FOXP3 can increase GINS1, which promotes cell proliferation in NSCLC [ ]. Also, genetic variation in MALAT1 is related to the risk of lung cancer [ ]. Nevertheless, further studies are required to shed light on the molecular mechanisms of the correlation between MALAT1 and SOX9.
A recent finding suggests the possible role of MALAT1 in regulating UBE2C, a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme that is higher expressed during the tumorigenesis and progression of several malignant tumors, including lung cancer. Mechanistically, MALAT1 functions as a ceRNA, targeting miR-491-5p and upregulating UBE2C [ ]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) are a heterogeneous group of immune cells from the myeloid lineage which play a role in tumor development and progression by possessing strong immunosuppressive activities. A recent study demonstrated that MALAT1 is downregulated in patients with lung cancer and that there was a moderately negative correlation between the expression of MALAT1 and the proportion of MDSCs [ ]. This suggests a possible dual role for MALAT1 in modulating immune responses during lung tumor progression, although further functional validation is necessary. Recent data show that polymorphisms in MALAT1 can affect its oncogenicity. A study on 444 lung cancer cases and 460 healthy controls demonstrated that carrying rs3200401 is related to promoted susceptibility to non-small-cell lung cancer and lung squamous cell carcinoma [ ].
In contrast to the predominantly oncogenic function of MALAT1 in NSCLC, a recent study describes a potentially tumor-suppressive haplotype, GGGT, within the MALAT1 locus. This genetic variant was associated with reduced MALAT1 expression and correlated with decreased tumor burden in brain metastases and lymph node involvement. Mechanistically, MALAT1 was shown to regulate the miR-328/KATNB1 axis, and its downregulation allowed miR-328 to repress KATNB1, a microtubule-severing protein linked to tumor aggressiveness [ ].
These insights point to MALAT1 as a dynamic regulator in lung cancer, capable of engaging distinct gene networks tied to proliferation, metastasis, and immune modulation. Its function appears tightly linked to genetic background and tumor subtype, reflecting the broader complexity of lncRNA signaling in pulmonary malignancies. Mapping the specific molecular contexts in which MALAT1 switches from promoting to restraining tumor behavior remains a critical goal for understanding its therapeutic relevance.

## Breast cancer
Breast cancer is the most common form of malignancy among women [ ]. Circulating MALAT1 expression is increased in patients with breast cancer compared to the healthy control groups [ ]. Moreover, dysregulation of MALAT1 is positively correlated with lymph node status, estrogen receptor (ER), tumor stage, and histological grade [ ]. Postoperative fever occurred in 45% of breast cancer cases, and it has been proposed that it is an indicator of a poor prognosis of the disease. A recent study showed that the expression level of MALAT1 in the fever group was significantly higher than those in the control group [ ]. A large number of lncRNAs are associated with multiple cancer types containing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). It is suggested that polymorphisms in MALAT1 may play an important function in breast cancer development. A study on three tagging SNPs, including rs3200401, rs619586, and rs7927113 indicated that the MALAT1 expression with AG, GG, and AG + GG genotypes of rs619586 was remarkably lower than that with genotype AA [ ]. Moreover, patients with genotype CT of rs3200401 had a lesser risk of developing breast cancer in the codominant and over-dominant models [ ]. Epigenetically, MALAT1 is implicated in transcriptional control of oncogenic programs. MALAT1 facilitates a pro-metastatic phenotype in breast cancer cells by altering the H3K4 epigenotype in the promoter of EEF1A1 [ ]. MALAT1 represents distinct expression levels in breast cancer subtypes with the highest expression level in patients with luminal tumors while the lowest level in ER-negative patient samples [ ]. Supporting this subtype-specific pattern, a recent qPCR-based study revealed that MALAT1 expression is significantly downregulated in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines compared to both normal breast epithelial cells (MCF-10A) and luminal breast cancer lines [ ]. Interestingly, while the major MALAT1 transcript was decreased in TNBC, several minority splice variants were elevated, suggesting an isoform-specific regulatory shift. These findings emphasize a functional heterogeneity of MALAT1 isoforms and raise questions about context-specific tumor-suppressive or oncogenic roles.
Nischarin is a tumor suppressor protein that reduces tumor growth and metastasis in breast cancer [ ]. MALAT1 governs a tumor-suppressive response in breast cancer cells in a Nischarin-dependent context. In other words, MALAT1 knockout had a restricted effect on cell function when Nischarin expression was lowered, while in cells with high expression levels of Nischarin, MALAT1 knockout resulted in considerable enhancements of cell proliferation and migration [ ]. Environmental stressors like hypoxia may also shape MALAT1 function. MALAT1 activation by HIF-1α and HIF-2α leads to sponge of miR-3064-5p, resulting in tumor growth and migration in breast cancer cells [ ]. MALAT1 also contributes to hypoxia adaptation in TNBC through epitranscriptomic regulation. Under hypoxic conditions, MALAT1 enhances WTAP expression, a component of the m6A methylation writer complex. WTAP upregulation, in turn, promotes the transcriptional activity of HIF1α and HIF1β, thereby strengthening the hypoxia response. Functionally, this axis increases EMT-related proteins such as N-cadherin and Vimentin and enhances migratory capacity [ ]. These findings position the MALAT1–WTAP–HIF1 signaling circuit as a potential therapeutic target in aggressive TNBC phenotypes.
Beyond canonical transcriptional roles, MALAT1 also impacts RNA processing and mutational plasticity. A recent study demonstrated that MALAT1 binds and sequesters APOBEC3B, limiting its RNA and DNA editing activity. This regulatory mechanism alters the APOBEC3A/APOBEC3B balance and suggests a feedback loop that may influence mutational burden and therapeutic response in breast cancer cells [ ]. Recent studies have revealed that MALAT1 may also regulate gene expression by modulating chromatin accessibility at distal regulatory elements. Specifically, MALAT1 was shown to control the expression of NR4A1 in breast cancer cells by altering the accessibility of a downstream enhancer-like element. This effect was validated in MCF7 cells but was absent in PANC1 cells, highlighting a cell-type-specific epigenetic function of MALAT1 [ ].
Sialylation is a pattern that has been seen in different cancers. ST8SIA is an enzyme family related to breast cancer. MALAT1, by sponging miR-26a/26b decreases the level of ST8SIA modulate cancer tumorigenesis [ ]. MALAT1 overexpression is also related to elevated levels of monocyte-myeloid-derived suppressor cell (Mo-MDSC) and circulating tumor cell (CTC) count in TNBC [ ]. The mechanism underlying MALAT1-mediated increase of Mo-MDSCs remains unclear and requires further investigation.
In the immune context, MALAT1 also appears to regulate inflammatory cytokines that influence therapeutic resistance. Up-regulated levels of TNF-α and IL-10 at tumor sites are associated with resistance to immunotherapy. miR-17-5p is a tumor suppressor that is related to the downregulated level of TNF-α and IL-10. It has been revealed that knocking down MALAT1 can induce miR-17-5p and reduce TNF-α and IL-10 [ ]. Circ_0076611 is a circular RNA, produced by back-splicing of VEGFA exon 7 which is regulated by MALAT1. Circ_0076611 interacts with proliferation-related mRNAs like MYC and VEGFA, promoting the proliferation and migration of TNBC cells and tissues [ ]. However, some studies claim that MALAT1 acts as a tumor suppressor gene (TSG). A significant study using breast cancer models provided strong functional evidence for this role by employing an RNA-specific inactivation strategy that avoided disrupting nearby genomic elements [ ]. In this study, inactivation of MALAT1 in both human breast cancer cells and mouse models resulted in a substantial increase in lung metastases, while re-expression of MALAT1 effectively reversed this phenotype. Mechanistically, MALAT1 was shown to suppress metastasis by binding and sequestering TEAD transcription factors, thereby inhibiting YAP/TEAD-driven transcription of pro-metastatic genes such as CTGF, CYR61, VEGFA, and ITGB4. These findings suggest that MALAT1 exerts its anti-metastatic effects through interference with the YAP–TEAD transcriptional program, rather than through indirect chromatin alterations. Notably, this study challenges earlier interpretations by highlighting MALAT1 as an active regulator of metastatic progression rather than a passive marker [ ]. Importantly, this work also demonstrates that MALAT1’s tumor-suppressive function can be mechanistically dominant under specific transcriptional contexts, offering a direct counterpoint to earlier studies linking its overexpression to metastasis. Such polarity underscores the risk of overgeneralizing MALAT1’s role without accounting for subtype-specific regulatory networks.
According to another study on breast cancer tissues, there is a positive correlation between the expression of MALAT1 and PTEN. High levels of MALAT1 are associated with increased patient survival. The study suggests that PTEN increases MALAT1 expression by binding and retaining miR-17, miR-20a, and miR-106b. Additionally, MALAT1 helps suppress the spread of breast cancer cells by downregulating pro-metastatic molecules such as EpCAM and ITGB4, thereby inhibiting cell migration and invasion [ ].
Emerging evidence suggests that individual MALAT1 isoforms have distinct roles in cancer biology. In breast cancer, a major alternatively spliced MALAT1 variant (Δsv-MALAT1) has been identified, whose expression pattern differs markedly from full-length MALAT1. Notably, low levels of Δsv-MALAT1 are linked to altered splicing regulation, activation of oncogenic pathways such as PI3K–AKT and YAP, and poorer patient outcomes [ ].
The role of MALAT1 in breast cancer appears shaped by a combination of subtype-specific expression, isoform diversity, and signaling interplay. While much of the literature supports its oncogenic impact across transcriptional, immune, and epigenetic layers, several studies underscore context-dependent tumor-suppressive roles. This duality is not merely theoretical but substantiated by model-dependent outcomes, transcriptomic divergence, and differences in MALAT1-interacting partners across subtypes. Understanding how these opposing functions emerge across different molecular landscapes will be essential for future therapeutic exploration.

## Ovarian cancer
Ovarian cancer remains a leading cause of gynecologic cancer death worldwide. In this context, MALAT1 has emerged as a critical regulator of ovarian tumor biology by regulating cellular behavior and the molecular context. Elevated MALAT1 expression is consistently seen in ovarian cancer tissues and cell lines, and associated with advanced stage, recurrence, and poor overall survival [ ]. Its significance becomes even clearer when examining how ovarian cancer metastasizes—through direct seeding in the peritoneal cavity, a process that requires cancer cells to resist detachment-induced cell death, or anoikis. MALAT1 expression rises sharply in ovarian cancer cells grown in suspension, closely modeling this metastatic behavior [ ]. Silencing MALAT1 impairs cell proliferation and invasion, reduces anchorage-independent growth, and makes cancer cells more susceptible to anoikis. The impact is not limited to cell survival. MALAT1 orchestrates alternative splicing events by regulating the splicing factor RBFOX2, which in turn controls the expression of anti-apoptotic and EMT-related genes. Specifically, MALAT1 knockdown or RBFOX2 suppression both drive the splicing of KIF1B toward its pro-apoptotic isoform, resulting in increased apoptosis and reduced tumor cell viability [ ]. The fine-tuned control of cell fate at this level is one reason MALAT1 stands out in ovarian cancer research. Beyond splicing, MALAT1 is a strong modulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a central axis in tumor growth and migration. Downregulation of MALAT1 leads to reduced expression of DVL2, β-catenin, and cyclin D1, and increases GSK-3β levels. This shift halts proliferation, slows migration and invasion, arrests the cell cycle, and triggers apoptosis—an effect that can be reversed by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [ ]. In short, MALAT1 enables ovarian cancer cells to thrive and spread by keeping the Wnt/β-catenin pathway active.
The methyltransferase METTL3 is upregulated in ovarian cancer and increases MALAT1 stability by promoting its m6A modification. The RNA-binding protein ELAVL1 further boosts MALAT1 expression [ ]. When either METTL3 or MALAT1 is silenced, ovarian cancer cell proliferation drops significantly, highlighting the importance of this epitranscriptomic axis in supporting malignancy [ ].
In summary, MALAT1 functions as a key regulator of ovarian cancer pathogenesis, integrating alternative splicing, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and m6A-dependent regulatory processes to promote tumor cell survival, metastasis, and proliferation. The multifaceted nature of its functions challenges the idea of MALAT1 as a poor-prognostic biomarker; MALAT1 represents a promising drug target in light of ovarian cancer's unique metastatic biology. The broad regulatory reach of MALAT1, touching everything from alternative splicing to cell signaling and epitranscriptomic modification, reveals why it is not only a marker of poor prognosis but also a compelling candidate for targeted therapy. The challenge now is to translate these mechanistic insights into therapeutic approaches that can disrupt MALAT1-driven networks and offer hope against this deadly disease.

## Cervical cancer
Cervical cancer is the second most common malignancy in women worldwide [ ]. MALAT1 expression is significantly higher in cervical cancer cells [ , ]. MALAT1 promotes tumor growth, vascular invasion, lymph node metastasis, and its expression is independently associated with poorer survival [ , ]. Furthermore, MALAT1 sponges miR‐429 in cervical cancer and leads to the downregulation of the tumor-suppressor gene [ ]. In vitro analysis showed an increased apoptosis rate of HeLa and Caski cancer cells in response to MALAT1 inhibition [ ]. 70% of cervical cancer cases are linked to human papillomaviruses (HPV), including HPV 16 and HPV 18 infections [ ]. MALAT1 in HPV16-positive cervical cancer can up-regulate Methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A) by sponging miR-485-5p, leading to increased proliferation of cancerous cells [ ]. A recent finding showed that MALAT1 transcription is upregulated through a positive feedback loop from HPV oncoproteins and IL-6/STAT3 inflammatory signaling pathways [ ]. It has also been reported that MALAT1 upregulates in high-risk HPV-positive tumor tissues in comparison with the control ones and can regulate growth factor GRB2 expression through competing with miR-124, increasing cell growth rate and invasion [ ]. It has been shown that rs3200401 in MALAT1 and rs4977574 in ANRIL may play a pivotal role in cervical cancer. The frequency of these polymorphisms is also different between the normal population and the patients with cervical cancer [ ].
Additionally, MALAT1 modulates pyroptotic cell death in cervical cancer [ ]. Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death with cell swelling, membrane rupture, and the release of pro-inflammatory intracellular contents, often triggered in response to infection or cellular stress and increasingly recognized for its role in cancer immunogenicity [ ]. A recent study revealed that MALAT1 suppresses pyroptosis in cervical cancer cells by sponging miR-124, thereby maintaining the expression of SIRT1, a key regulator that inhibits pyroptosis. The knockdown of MALAT1 in both patient-derived cervical cancer cells and mouse xenograft models resulted in gasdermin-dependent pyroptosis and decreased tumor viability, establishing a novel MALAT1/miR-124/SIRT1 regulation axis in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer [ ]. Although pyroptosis is not traditionally classified as immunological, it is an inflammatory cell death mode with immune stimulatory effects which indicate that MALAT1 may also have a function in immune evasion by blocking immunogenic cell death pathways.
The evidence suggests a regulatory function of MALAT1 in cervical cancer advancement—with focus on its interplay with viral oncogenes and immune-evasive mechanisms. MALAT1 produces its effect through influencing genetic susceptibility to the secondary effect of regulating pyroptosis and HPV-induced oncogenicity, thus incorporating host and viral signals into the tumor transcriptome. The mechanism by which MALAT1 facilitates the immune evasive strategies and induces survival path has been shown proposes it as a unique node for establishing novel therapeutics.

## Liver cancer
Hepatocellular carcinoma is the most common form of liver cancer in adults, which accounts for 75% of the cases. The expression level of circulating MALAT1 was significantly higher in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma compared with healthy controls [ , ]. FOXM1 is one of the promoters of metastasis in various types of tumors. It is noted that MALAT1 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression through sponging miR-125a-3p and modulating FOXM1 expression [ ]. A recent finding illustrated that MALAT1 functions as a ceRNA to modulate SMAD5 expression through binding to miR-142-3p, hence promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [ ]. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that MALAT1 mediates the expression of transcription factor BRF2, which is associated with recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma tumors, larger tumor size, and a poor prognosis. MALAT1 was shown to act as a ceRNA by sponging has-miR-1-3p, causing upregulation of BRF2. It was discovered that BRF2 silencing inhibited hepatocellular carcinoma progression via triggering the LKB1/AMPK signaling pathway. Furthermore, BRF2 overexpression restored the hepatocellular carcinoma cell viability-inhibiting effects of MALAT1 knockdown [ ]. In terms of signaling regulation, MALAT1 is linked to the Wnt pathway, which is a key driver of cancer stemness and metastasis in HCC. Targeting the MALAT1/Wnt signaling axis decreases the expression levels of β-catenin, vimentin, and Twist1 proteins, suppressing stemness and metastasis in hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. In addition to its transcriptional function, epitranscriptomic regulatory mechanisms of MALAT1 are emerging as a critical mechanism of drug resistance. Recently, evidence has demonstrated a connection between the 5-methylcytosine (m5C) methylation of MALAT1 and the resistance of HCC to sorafenib. Specifically, NSUN2 and ALYREF stabilize MALAT1 through m5C methylation, allowing it to bind ELAVL1 and increase SLC7A11 expression, which inhibits ferroptosis. Pharmacologic targeting of this MALAT1/ELAVL1/SLC7A11 axis resulted in sorafenib sensitivity in tumors in preclinical models, and may provide a strategy to overcome resistance [ ]. Beyond its functional role in HCC, MALAT1 is significantly elevated in the serum of HCC patients and shows strong diagnostic potential as a non-invasive biomarker [ ].
Cholangiocarcinoma is a much less common type of liver cancer that forms in the bile ducts. Interaction between MALAT1 and miR-204 leads to increased levels of cholangiocarcinoma proliferation, migration, and invasion via targeting CXCR4 [ ].
Overall, MALAT1 plays a central role in liver cancer progression, therapy resistance, and metastasis. Its diagnostic and therapeutic potential highlights the need for further studies to translate these insights into clinical applications.

## Colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer remains one of the most common malignancies around the globe, with a high mortality rate [ ]. It has been demonstrated that MALAT1 expression is related to the proliferation and migration of cancerous cells in colorectal cancer [ ]. MALAT1 expression is 2.26 times higher in colorectal cancer tissues compared to non-cancerous tissues and a determinant of poor prognosis [ ]. However, a study pointed out that MALAT1 expression was not associated with colorectal cancer outcomes. The mechanistic basis of MALAT1’s prognostic association in colorectal cancer remains unexplored in this study and warrants further investigation [ ]. The interaction of lncRNAs, including MALAT1 and RNA-binding proteins, has been studied in-depth, taking into account a range of aspects such as regulation of expression, subcellular localization, and the mediation of various functions [ ]. SFPQ and PTBP2 are two RNA-binding proteins that interact with MALAT1 in colorectal cancer cell. MALAT1 may bind to SFPQ, causing PTBP2 to be released from the SFPQ/PTBP2 complex. As a result of the increased SFPQ-detached PTBP2, cell proliferation and migration were promoted. MALAT1's regulatory effects were mediated in part by SFPQ [ ].
Differentiation antagonizing non-protein coding RNA (DANCR) is an anti-apoptotic lncRNA. It increases MALAT1 expression by controlling QK protein (an interacting RNA-binding protein) in colorectal cancer [ ]. Growing evidence has shown the impact of genetic variations on malignancies risk. Recently has been shown that MALAT1 genetic variant rs3200401 is correlated with colorectal cancer risk and can be used as a predisposing marker [ ].
Overall, MALAT1 emerges as a multifaceted lncRNA in colorectal cancer, operating through RNA–protein interactions, transcriptional networks, miRNA sponging, and genetic susceptibility. Though it primarily has a pro-oncogenic role, heterogeneity in clinical associations indicates an opportunity for further investigation regarding context-dependent roles and isoform variation.

## Gastric cancer
Gastric cancer is yet another commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide [ ]. MALAT1 has been shown to have a critical role in controlling cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis of gastric tumor cells [ ]. It has been reported that MALAT1 regulates chemoresistance by modulating autophagy-related pathways, particularly through targeting miR-23b-3p [ ]. Recently, a finding has demonstrated that MALAT1, by impairing autophagic flux and increasing IL-6 secretion, results in an elevated level of cancer-associated fibroblasts that can promote gastric cancer progression [ ]. MALAT1 promotes cell proliferation via upregulating expression levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-STAT3 [ ]. Another recent finding revealed that MALAT1 is overexpressed in gastric cancer tissues, which is increasing the disease progression via sponging miR-1297 and regulating HMGB2 expression [ ]. MALAT1 also promotes gastric cancer progression through regulating the expression levels of vasculogenic mimicry and angiogenesis-associated markers, including VE-cadherin, β-catenin, MMP-2, MMP-9, MT1-MMP, p-ERK, p-FAK, and p-paxillin [ ]. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of more than 119,000 cells across different gastric cancer TCGA molecular subtypes demonstrated the distinctions in cellular compositions and lncRNA-driven regulatory networks between subtypes [ ]. Importantly, the study identified MALAT1 as a subtype-specific regulator and prognostic marker within genomically stable (GS) tumors, where it forms a regulatory pair with CTNNB1 (β-catenin), implicating it in Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation [ ]. This pairing was unique to the chromosomal instability subtype, illustrating the role of MALAT1 in subtype-specific oncogenic signaling. Moreover, MALAT1 expression was significantly related to prognosis in GS-subtype tumors, further suggesting clinical relevance for this molecule as a stratification biomarker when considering gastric cancer heterogeneity. These findings illustrate how MALAT1 contributes not only to tumor-intrinsic biology but also reflects broader subtype-specific transcriptomic architectures.
In gastric cancer, MALAT1 functions at the interface of tumor cell biology and stromal remodeling, driving progression through autophagic suppression, angiogenic shift, and fibroblast reprogramming. These functions reveal MALAT1 to be much more than a simple oncogene, it is able to coordinate the adaptive reorganization of the tumor ecosystem in response to metabolic and immune stress. This broader ecological role raises new possibilities for targeting MALAT1 in therapies focused on disrupting tumor–microenvironment interactions.

## Melanoma
Melanoma is widely recognized as one of the most aggressive and challenging skin cancers to treat [ ]. Mechanistically, MALAT1 acts in part by functioning as a molecular sponge for a series of tumor-suppressive microRNAs to enhance melanoma cell growth, invasion, and metastasis [ ]. In cutaneous melanoma, MALAT1 has been shown to negatively regulate miR-34a by direct binding, resulting in enhanced c-Myc and MET expression, two critical drivers of melanoma progression [ ]. Furthermore, MALAT1 was found to be enriched in the Ago2 complex, and mutation of its miR-34a binding site abrogated this interaction, emphasizing the specificity of this regulatory mechanism [ ]. Another research reported that the expression of MALAT1 is upregulated in primary and metastatic melanoma tissues, and MALAT1 functions by downregulating miR-23a promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion of melanoma cells, and inhibiting MALAT1 or upregulating miR-23a increased apoptosis and reduced malignancy [ ]. In addition, MALAT1 promotes the progression of uveal melanoma, a distinct and highly metastatic ocular malignancy, through the silencing of miR-140 and modulation of downstream targets such as Slug and ADAM10 [ ]. Downregulation of MALAT1 in uveal melanoma led to increased miR-140 levels and significantly impaired cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Recent studies further reveal a MALAT1/miR-608/HOXC4 regulatory axis in uveal melanoma, in which MALAT1 upregulation leads to suppression of miR-608, increased HOXC4 expression, and enhanced tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo; conversely, MALAT1 knockdown restrains melanoma development by upregulating miR-608 and downregulating HOXC4 [ ].
At the signaling level, MALAT1 regulates key oncogenic pathways in melanoma, notably the MAPK pathway, which is central to tumorigenesis and therapeutic resistance. MALAT1 inhibition in melanoma cell lines resulted in reduced BRAF expression and impaired MAPK pathway activity, while also limiting colony formation and tumor growth, even in models resistant to MEK inhibitors. Notably, MALAT1 expression was correlated with MAPK pathway genes in patient samples, and MAPK pathway inhibition upregulated MALAT1 expression, indicating complex feedback mechanisms between MALAT1 and MAPK signaling [ ]. These findings show MALAT1's context-dependent, critical oncogenic functions in MAPK pathway-driven tumors.
A recent spatial transcriptomic study of melanoma showed that they enriched MALAT1 in regions of mitotically active tumor [ ]. MALAT1-high regions co-express key cell cycle regulators such as MKI67 and CDK2, and various metabolic effectors, like FABP5. The enriched expression profiles and identified key cell cycle regulators demonstrate MALAT1's ability to encode metabolic and proliferative signals to promote tumor growth in the unique microenvironment. The compartmentalized expression of MALAT1 suggests, therefore, that it establishes a nuclear architecture that acts as a scaffold to co-ordinate transcriptional programs in tumor cells that regulate nutrient acquisition, utilization, and cellular division [ ]. These results show a spatial-function dynamic of MALAT1-driven metabolic reprogramming that advances our understanding of context-dependent metabolic rewiring found in aggressive cancers.
Overall, the effects of MALAT1 in melanoma are driven by a complex web of miRNA sponging, transcriptional control, and modulation of cancer-related signaling. This multifaceted activity is seen in both cutaneous and uveal melanoma, positioning MALAT1 as a central driver of disease progression. These findings have not only established the potential of MALAT1 as a prognostic marker of interest but also a potential therapeutic target, particularly in cases when there is resistance to MAPK inhibitors. The various mechanisms that MALAT1 modulates microRNA interactions and essential pathways continue to show increasing relevance of MALAT1 in the evolving field of melanoma research and therapy.

## Hematological malignancies

## Leukemia
A recent study introduces MALAT1 as a promising therapeutic target for hindering oncogenic fusion proteins, which provoke specific subtypes of leukemogenesis. MALAT1 promotes PML-RARA, MLL-ENL, MLL-AF9, and other fusion proteins. Furthermore, MALAT1 controls the chimeric mRNA export process and regulates the myeloid progenitor cell differentiation [ ]. The increased expression trend of MALAT1 has been reported through a microarray-based gene expression profiling of patients with leukemia (AML, CML, ALL, CLL) compared to healthy people. Notably, the most significant difference in MALAT1 expression was detected in patients with CLL subtype [ , ]. Moreover, MALAT1 upregulation has been declared as a prognostic indicator for patients with the AML subtype and is correlated with poor survival [ ]. MALAT1 also regulates CXCR4 by sponging miR-146a in AML, promoting migration, proliferation, and reduced apoptosis [ ]. E‐cadherin is an important tumor suppressor. Recent evidence has clarified that MALAT1 can cause poor outcomes in patients with AML by suppression of E-cadherin. Mechanistically, this repression is mediated through H3K27 trimethylation driven by EZH2 and SUZ12 recruitment at the E-cadherin promoter [ ]. MALAT1 overexpression, as a critical miRNA sponge, correlates with the lower survival rate of patients with ALL subtypes and enhances the proliferation and inhibited apoptosis of ALL cells through regulating miR‐205 and suppressing PTK7 expression as a result [ ]. In AML, MALAT1 was shown to regulate leukemia stem cell progression via a newly characterized MALAT1/miR-582-5p/GALNT1/MUC1 axis that modulates the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Notably, MALAT1 acted both upstream and downstream of STAT3 in a feedforward regulatory loop, while also promoting aberrant O-glycosylation through GALNT1, a novel mechanistic insight into lncRNA-driven oncogenic glyco-signaling [ ]. MALAT1 binds to METTL14 and promotes m6A modification of the oncogenic transcription factor ZEB1. Knockdown of MALAT1 reduces proliferation, migration, and invasion while inducing apoptosis, and these effects can be partially reversed by ZEB1 overexpression [ ].
In T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL), a transcriptomic study combining RNA-seq profiling and clinical outcome analysis identified MALAT1 as one of several lncRNAs with prognostic value. MALAT1 was among the 23 validated targets that stratified patients by immunophenotypic and transcriptomic signatures. Although the mechanistic basis of MALAT1’s role in T-ALL remains to be elucidated, its inclusion in this robust prognostic panel, validated in a large, independent cohort, underscores its emerging relevance as a potential biomarker for leukemic progression and patient survival [ ].
Altogether, MALAT1 in leukemia appears to function as a multi-layered regulator, coordinating transcriptional silencing, RNA export, stemness, and oncogenic glycosylation. This dynamic and subtype-spanning involvement underscores MALAT1 not only as a hallmark of leukemic persistence but also as a molecular integrator of epigenetic and post-transcriptional leukemogenic programs.

## Lymphoma
The abundant expression of MALAT1 as well as its association with lower overall survival of patients has been reported by numerous investigations [ ]. Epigenetically, MALAT1 interacts with PRC2 components EZH2 and SUZ12, facilitating H3K27 trimethylation at the promoters of key tumor suppressors such as E-cadherin, p21, and p27. This silencing leads to the upregulation of β-catenin and c-Myc, which enhances cell proliferation, invasion, and survival in mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), NK, and T cell lymphomas [ , ]. Sponging hsa-miR-150-5p by MALAT1 can be related to poor prognosis in Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [ ]. MALAT1 expression is inversely correlated with overall survival and progression-free survival in primary gastrointestinal diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (PGI-DLBCL) [ ]. Cutaneous T-Cell Lymphoma (CTCL) is a rare non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In this context, silencing MALAT1 leads to EMT reversal, decreased cell proliferation, and inhibition of cancer stem-like properties. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated through the reactivation of miR-124, which is normally suppressed by MALAT1 [ ]. A recent study has demonstrated that p53 can serve as a transcription repressor of MALAT1 expression in Jurkat cells through binding to three suitable binding motifs on the promoter of MALAT1, suggesting that MALAT1 transcriptional repression by p53 is caused by oxidative stress [ ]. Notably, in hepatitis B virus–infected germinal center B-cell–like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, MALAT1 takes on a tumor-suppressive role. Suppression of MALAT1 by the viral HBX protein makes lymphoma cells less sensitive to ferroptosis, a cell death pathway that can restrict tumor growth. Restoring MALAT1 reverses this effect, re-sensitizing cells to ferroptosis and suggesting a protective function against disease progression in this specific context. This highlights the highly context-dependent impact of MALAT1 across different lymphoma subtypes [ ].
Taken together, these findings highlight MALAT1 as a context-dependent regulator in lymphoma, sometimes driving tumor progression, while in other settings, such as hepatitis B virus–infected germinal center B-cell–like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, acting as a tumor suppressor. Its impact is shaped by disease subtype, cellular context, and external stress, underscoring the need for tailored approaches if MALAT1 is to be targeted therapeutically in lymphoma.

## Myeloma
MALAT1 is upregulated in bone marrow mononuclear cells taken from newly diagnosed patients with multiple myeloma in different stages; showing a significant overexpression compared to the healthy group. Furthermore, patients with disease progression or a relapse indicated a significant enhancement in MALAT1 expression. Follow-up analyses verified a stepwise upregulation of MALAT1 from normal plasma cells to myeloma cells and further into extramedullary disease stages [ ]. On that note, MALAT1 overexpression is correlated with the poor prognosis of patients with multiple myeloma. Furthermore, anti-MALAT1 therapy promoted apoptosis in multiple myeloma [ ]. In the bone marrow stromal compartment, MALAT1 overexpression in mesenchymal stem cells transcriptionally activates LTBP3, a key regulator of bone remodeling and osteoclast inhibition, by recruiting transcription factor SP1 to its promoter [ ]. MALAT1 was shown to enhance CXCL12-induced proliferation, invasion, and homing of multiple myeloma cells by upregulating CXCR4 and adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VLA-4 [ ]. These findings extend the immunoregulatory role of MALAT1 to the chemokine-mediated guidance of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow microenvironment. However, the upstream regulation of CXCR4 by MALAT1 remains incompletely defined, leaving open questions about direct or indirect modes of transcriptional control.The interplay between MALAT1 and EZH2 epigenetically upregulates the activating transcription factors of NRF1 and NRF2 genes, proteasome subunit, as well as affecting the miRNome of the disease like tumor suppressor miR-29b [ , ]. MALAT1 and miR-188-5p levels are inversely correlated in Multiple myeloma. MALAT1 by regulating miR-188-5p promotes myeloma proliferation but decreases its apoptosis [ ]. MALAT1 serves as a central regulator of the myeloma microenvironment, orchestrating malignant plasma cell behavior through coordinated immune-niche interactions and epigenetic remodeling. Its multifaceted influence on bone remodeling, chemokine signaling, and microRNA suppression underscores MALAT1 as a mechanistically distinct lncRNA in hematologic malignancies and highlights its potential as a compelling therapeutic target.
Across both solid and hematological malignancies, MALAT1 emerges as a context-dependent regulator with deep functional plasticity. Rather than adhering to a unidimensional role, MALAT1 integrates epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional mechanisms to either support or suppress tumor progression depending on cellular identity, tissue microenvironment, and genetic background. Its oncogenic role, through miRNA sponging, chromatin remodeling, or immune evasion, is strongly prominent in lung, liver, and hematologic cancers. In some contexts, though, such as TNBC, bone metastasis, and certain breast cancer subtypes, MALAT1 is tumor-suppressive, often through isoform switching or TEAD/YAP sequestration. This dual role is further substantiated by genetic polymorphisms within the MALAT1 locus that alter its expression and impact cancer risk or therapeutic response. In addition, MALAT1's ability to control inflammation, stromal remodeling, and immune checkpoints extends its role beyond cancer cells, positioning it as a key orchestrator of the tumor microenvironment. The findings emphasize the need to explore the role of MALAT1 in precise molecular and cellular contexts. In order to leverage its complete utility as a diagnostic or therapeutic target will take careful developments of integrative models that include isoform utilization, subcellular localization, and dynamic interactions with MALAT1 and the regulatory networks that are specific to a lineage.

## Functions of MALAT1 during Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition
Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a pivotal and inevitable process that drives migration, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance [ ]. During EMT, epithelial cells lose their polarity and cell–cell adhesion properties and acquire mesenchymal features, including enhanced motility and resistance to apoptosis. This transition plays a crucial role not only in embryonic development and wound healing but also in the malignant progression of tumors. MALAT1 may play a crucial role in cancer progression by promoting EMT in various types of cancer. Revealing the promotive role of MALAT1 in EMT during cancer progression, several studies have also investigated the molecular mechanisms involved [ ]. This oncogenic lncRNA evokes EMT through regulating splicing, altering signaling pathways, affecting epithelial transition as well as participating in epigenetic regulation such as interacting with SUZ12 or EZH2 the PRC2 components [ ]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, high expression of MALAT1 induces EMT through de-inhibiting Capn4 by sponging miR-124 [ ]. Another study demonstrated that MALAT1 binding to miR-429 promotes the EMT of human lung adenocarcinoma cells through RhoA [ ]. Targeting the miR-200c/MALAT1 axis inhibited EMT-associated protein expression, including TGF-β, ZEB1, N-cadherin, and vimentin in endometrioid endometrial carcinoma cells [ ]. m6A methyltransferase METTL3 elevated MALAT1 expression in breast cancer, and MALAT1 promotes HMGA2 by sponging miR-26b. It has recently been shown that silencing METTL3 can downregulate MALAT1 and HMGA2, which inhibits EMT [ ]. A recent finding has revealed that MALAT1 suppression can inhibit laryngocarcinoma-related EMT by increasing miR-708-5p expression and decreasing BRD4 and YAP1 [ ]. In pancreatic cancer, MALAT1 was shown to promote EMT and metastasis by acting as a sponge for miR-141-5p, thereby rescuing TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 expression and reactivating TGF-β signaling. This MALAT1/miR-141-5p/TGFBR1/TGFBR2 axis plays a key role in enhancing cell migration and invasion, highlighting MALAT1 as a critical driver of TGF-β-induced EMT [ ].
This multifaceted control of EMT reflects a broader paradigm in which lncRNAs such as MALAT1 act as dynamic hubs at the intersection of gene regulation, signal transduction, and cellular plasticity. Understanding these complex interactions may offer a more integrated view of EMT and reveal therapeutic vulnerabilities not evident through protein-coding gene analyses alone.

## The impact of MALAT1 in the modulation of cancer stem cells and cancer progression
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a dynamic and highly plastic subpopulation of malignant cells that have self-renewal capacity as well as the ability for tumor initiation, immune evasion, and innate and acquired resistance to standard therapies. Unlike terminally differentiated cancer cells, CSCs exhibit quiescence or slow-cycling behavior, allowing them to evade immune recognition and survive under therapeutic pressure [ , ]. Notably, CSCs inhabit a unique tumor microenvironment called the CSC niche, where immune cells, stromal components, and extracellular matrix components work together to maintain stemness and create immune privilege. An increasing amount of data shows that immune evasion in CSCs operates through complex and multifactored avenues involving immune checkpoint overexpression, altered antigen presentation, immunomodulatory secretome changes, and epigenetic behavior. In this complex regulatory landscape, lncRNAs, particularly MALAT1, have emerged as pivotal regulators of CSC biology [ ]. Through modulating transcriptional programs, interacting with other regulatory RNAs, and preserving structural genome elements, MALAT1 orchestrates a wide range of CSC-associated phenotypes. These multifunctional roles place MALAT1 at the intersection of stemness, plasticity, and therapeutic resistance in aggressive cancers [ ].
The SOX family members have been demonstrated to play a key function in CSCs maintenance [ ]. MALAT1 may enhance the proportion of pancreatic CSCs, preserve self-renewal capacity, decrease chemosensitivity to antitumor medicines, and promote angiogenesis levels in pancreatic cancer cells in vitro. Besides, the knockdown of MALAT1 decreases the expression levels of some self-renewal-associated factors such as Sox2 [ ]. Elucidating the functions of OCT4 is a popular area of interest since it is one of the stem cell factors required for embryogenesis and pluripotency. Various studies indicated that OCT4 is highly expressed in CSCs [ ]. MALAT1 promotes stem cell-like features in colorectal cancer cells through modulating the miR-20b-5p/Oct4 axis [ ]. In support of MALAT1’s broader relevance in CRC stemness, CD95 has been shown to enhance tumorsphere formation, chemoresistance, and in vivo tumor growth via upregulating MALAT1, even in the absence of exogenous CD95L stimulation. Mechanistically, MALAT1 silencing abrogated CD95-induced stemness, underscoring a CD95/MALAT1 axis as a potential therapeutic vulnerability [ ]. Lastly, MALAT1 may increase the CSC characteristics of liver cancer cells by overexpressing YAP1 via miR-375 sponging. The MALAT1/miR-375/YAP1 axis could be a potential therapeutic target of liver cancer, notably for the elimination of liver CSCs [ ]
Overall, these findings highlight the unique role of MALAT1 as a master regulator of CSC plasticity regardless of tumor type. In engaging post-transcriptional, transcriptional, and epigenetic mechanisms simultaneously, MALAT1 directs the expression of pluripotency factors as well as ncRNA networks that sustain CSC identity. Although this information positions MALAT1 as a biomarker of CSC enrichment, this broad scope of regulation highlights MALAT1 as more than a biomarker; rather, it is a functional regulator of stemness, and aggressiveness in cancer. Targeting MALAT1 in CSC-enriched tumors presents a new avenue for overcoming drug resistance and preventing tumor recurrence. Future directions should include cell-of-origin, isoform-specific knockouts, as well as transcriptome-methylome approaches to map the role of MALAT1 in CSC hierarchies and their therapeutic vulnerabilities.

## Regulatory effects of MALAT1 on autophagy in malignant tumorigenesis
Autophagy is an intracellular recycling system and cellular self-degradation process, which can be stimulated by multiple forms of cellular stress, including DNA damage, abnormal protein aggregation, reactive oxygen species, and nutrient deprivation [ , ]. Autophagy plays a dual role in tumor-promoting and tumor-inhibiting in various cancers [ ]. Numerous studies disclosed the considerable roles of lncRNAs in the regulation of autophagy [ ]. Up-regulated MALAT1 acts as an endogenous sponge by directly binding to miR-101 leading to activating autophagy, thus promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells [ ]. Smad1 binds to ATG5 and promotes its expression. MALAT1 expression is positively correlated with smad1 in colorectal cancerous cells by sponging miR-26a-5p [ ]. It has been postulated that MALAT1 induces autophagy and promotes gastric cancer cell proliferation via binding to microRNA-204 [ ]. Human NSCLC A549 cells transfected with si-MALAT1 are shown to have increased apoptosis rate inhibited levels of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via inhibiting autophagy [ ]. Moreover, MALAT1 could promote pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell proliferation and metastasis by activating autophagy. To this end, MALAT1 directly interacts with RNA binding protein HuR and increases the post-transcriptional regulation of TIA1 [ ].
STX17 is a protein-coding gene involved in autophagy that mediates autophagosome maturation. In retinoblastoma, MALAT1 promotes autophagy via miR-124-mediated STX17 regulation [ ]. More recently, MALAT1 increases the expression level of HMGB1, a critical regulator of autophagy, in multiple myeloma to promote autophagy resulting in the prevention of apoptosis [ ].
Together, these findings highlight how MALAT1 helps cancer cells adapt and survive by fine-tuning autophagy. By regulating key miRNAs and interacting with autophagy-related genes, MALAT1 supports tumor growth, helps cells resist stress, and limits cell death, adding another layer to its role in cancer development. Further studies using autophagy flux assays, real-time imaging, and genetic interaction mapping would help confirm the extent to which MALAT1 modulates autophagy as a primary mechanism or as part of a broader cellular stress response.

## Multiple roles of exosomal MALAT1 in cancer
Exosomes are nano-sized biovesicles that contain constituents (protein, DNA, and RNA) and are released by cells into the extracellular space [ ]. Because of their membrane features, exosomes can protect their cargo from external destructive elements. It has been shown that exosomal ncRNAs such as miRNAs and lncRNAs participate in the regulation of tumorigenesis and can serve as an ideal diagnostic biomarker for cancer (Table ) (Fig. ) [ ]. Recent studies have identified MALAT1 as one of the most enriched lncRNAs in tumor-derived exosomes, with emerging evidence showing that exosomal MALAT1 can enhance angiogenesis, drug resistance, and immune evasion. These findings suggest that MALAT1, beyond its intracellular functions, may also exert critical extracellular roles, acting as a mobile regulator that reprograms the tumor microenvironment and facilitates systemic tumor adaptation [ ].
 MALAT1 is upregulated in metastatic epithelial ovarian cancer cells and enriched within their exosomes, which can efficiently transfer MALAT1 to HUVECs and promote the expression of multiple angiogenesis-related genes. Accordingly, exosomal MALAT1 drives angiogenesis, at least in part, by increasing the expression of VEGF-A, VEGF-D, ENA-78, PlGF, IL-8, angiogenin, bFGF, and leptin in recipient endothelial cells [ ]. Exosomal MALAT1 upregulates the oncogenic FUT4 expression indirectly by sponging miR-26a/26b, promoting colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation [ ]. A recent study demonstrated that MALAT1 is highly enriched in the exosomes of patients with NSCLC. Moreover, these findings suggested that MALAT1 promotes NSCLC cells tumorigenicity by serving as a sponge of miR-515-5p to regulate EEF2 [ ]. Another study has confirmed that MALAT1 expression increased in NSCLC tissues and derived exosomes. However, no significant differences between MALAT1 expression levels in cancerous tissues and their exosomes were observed [ ]. Some findings on RNA cargos in extracellular vesicles derived from blood serum in pancreas associated conditions indicated that MALAT1 is highly expressed in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm [ ]. A recent study demonstrated that NSUN2/YBX1-modified exosomal MALAT1 enhances osteoclastogenesis via the RANKL/AKT/MAPK axis in multiple myeloma, linking MALAT1 to tumor-induced bone destruction. While compelling, the specificity of MALAT1’s contribution relative to other exosomal RNAs warrants further mechanistic clarification [ ]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), exosomal MALAT1 promotes tumor growth by epigenetically enhancing glyoxalase 1 (GLO1) expression through the MALAT1/miR-206/KAT2B axis, linking lncRNA signaling to methylglyoxal detoxification and metabolic adaptation in cancer cells [ ]. Exosomal MALAT1 is also upregulated in breast cancer, which induces cell proliferation in vivo, and in vitro [ ].
Breast cancer-derived exosomes containing MALAT1 can augment metastasis and chemoresistance by inhibiting the VASP/Rap1 signaling axis through disrupting miR-1-3p [ ]. In TNBC, MALAT1 is abundantly expressed in tumor-derived extracellular vesicles and transferred to macrophages, where it promotes M2 polarization and immunosuppressive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, exosomal MALAT1 upregulates POSTN, which activates the Hippo/YAP signaling cascade, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis [ ]. Moreover, in gastric cancer, MALAT1 was found to be highly enriched in exosomes derived from M2 tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). These exosomes transfer MALAT1 to gastric cancer cells, where it promotes aerobic glycolysis and enhances proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance in a glycolysis-dependent manner. Mechanistically, MALAT1 interacts with δ-catenin, preventing its ubiquitination and degradation by β-TRCP, and sponges miR-217-5p to increase HIF-1α expression. The resulting activation of β-catenin and HIF-1α signaling pathways drives metabolic reprogramming and tumor progression. Notably, dual inhibition of MALAT1 in both macrophages and gastric cancer cells via exosome-mediated siRNA delivery significantly suppressed tumor growth and restored chemosensitivity in vivo [ ].
Spatial transcriptomic analysis in pancreatic cancer has identified MALAT1 enrichment in tumor regions with elevated repeat RNA expression, particularly HSATII, which was associated with pro-inflammatory and mesenchymal signatures [ ]. Notably, MALAT1 was also found within extracellular vesicles, indicating its potential role in mediating paracrine signaling. By participating in viral mimicry pathways through repeat RNA transfer, exosomal MALAT1 may influence stromal activation and immune modulation, positioning it as a key mediator of tumor–microenvironment communication and progression [ ].
Exosomal MALAT1 serves as a potent intercellular messenger that enhances tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance across diverse cancer types. By functioning as a cargo of communication between tumor cells and components of the microenvironment, including immune cells and stromal cells, it adds a critical layer to our understanding of how tumors evolve and adapt. Targeting exosomal MALAT1 or its transfer routes may represent a promising frontier for therapeutic intervention in advanced cancers.

## The potential role of MALAT1 in cancer metabolic reprogramming
Metabolism is described as the biological pathways through which living organisms make or utilize energy. Due to the fact that cancer is a metabolic disease, metabolic reprogramming is largely regarded as a new hallmark of cancer cells [ , ]. Cancer cells have distinct metabolic characteristics as compared to normal cells, including increased glucose and glutamine uptake, aerobic glycolysis, and aberrant lipid metabolism. Cancer metabolic reprogramming, which promotes increased cell proliferation, has been thoroughly modulated by oncogenes activation or loss of tumor suppressors [ ]. It has been widely reported that lncRNAs, including MALAT1, have the potential to effectively regulate cancer metabolism in different ways [ ]. Understanding how lncRNAs contribute to metabolic reprogramming and carcinogenesis may lead to the identification of novel cancer therapeutic options.
The glucose metabolism pattern is changed in cancer cells to support their rapid proliferation and invasion [ ]. MALAT1 has been found to play a function in regulating cancer metabolism at both the glycolysis and gluconeogenesis levels [ ]. MALAT1 is involved in hepatocellular carcinoma progression through increasing mTOR-mediated translation of TCF7L2, enhancing glycolysis, and inhibiting gluconeogenesis [ ]. Silencing MALAT1 decreased the expression of several metabolic enzymes, such as ME3, PDK1 and PDK3, and CHKA in prostate cancer cells. As a result, prostate cancer cells' metabolism shifted to a glycolytic state, as evidenced by increased lactate production, growth arrest, and cell death [ ]. Moreover, MALAT1 via MYBL2/mTOR axis control glycolysis and the Warburg effect in prostate cancer. So inhibiting MALAT/MYBL2/mTOR axis can be considered a therapeutic option [ ]. MALAT1 role has been confirmed in the biosynthesis of Sphingolipid and Ceramide in prostate cancer. MALAT1, via activation of Choline kinase alpha (CHKA), can accelerate Sphingolipid and Ceramide production. The data showed that MALAT1 silencing results in CHKA downregulation, which can be used as a treatment in patients with prostatic cancer [ ]. Changes in lipid metabolism are one of the most common metabolic abnormalities in cancer [ ]. Increased lipid production or uptake promotes cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth [ ]. A recent study on hepatocellular carcinoma indicated that silencing MALAT1 decreased the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism, including SCD and SREBF1, via pre-mRNA splicing or transcription. Additionally, MALAT1 depletion reduced glucose uptake, most likely by suppressing AMPK signaling [ ].
Alterations in mitochondrial bioenergetics and signaling are important for cancer [ ]. A study showed that MALAT1 was enriched in mitochondria of hepatoma cells. MALAT1 interacted with different genes, including D-loop, COX2, ND3, andCYTB in the mitochondria genome. It has been shown that MALAT1 knockdown was related to altered mitochondrial structure, low oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and activation of mitochondrial apoptosis, which finally changed tumor phenotype [ ]. Additionally, a recent study in colorectal cancer uncovered that MALAT1 indirectly promotes ferroptosis insensitivity by facilitating the function of NUDT16L1, a newly identified ferroptosis repressor [ ]. Mechanistically, MALAT1 participates in the stabilization of NAD-capped RNAs that support mitochondrial integrity, preventing mitochondrial DNA leakage and maintaining ferroptosis resistance [ ]. This axis not only advances tumor progression in vivo but also presents a novel mitochondrial link through which MALAT1 may regulate redox homeostasis and therapy resistance.
MALAT1 acts as a central modulator of cancer metabolism, influencing glycolysis, lipid biosynthesis, and mitochondrial function through diverse mechanisms involving transcriptional control, post-transcriptional regulation, and lncRNA-protein interactions. These metabolic roles of MALAT1 not only support tumor cell survival and progression but also offer novel intervention points, especially in tumors where conventional metabolic inhibitors have limited efficacy. Future studies should prioritize the use of isotope-labeled metabolic tracing, organoid models, and MALAT1 isoform-specific knockouts to resolve its precise contributions to metabolic rewiring. As metabolic reprogramming continues to be a defining feature of cancer biology, MALAT1 stands out as a multifaceted regulator worth therapeutic attention.

## MALAT1 in cancer immunology and immune evasion
The immune system plays a central role in eliminating tumor cells and maintaining tissue homeostasis through mechanisms including T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, immune surveillance, and immunogenic cell death [ ]. However, tumor cells often acquire adaptive strategies to escape immune elimination. Recent studies have demonstrated that the immune system not only responds locally within the tumor microenvironment but also relies on coordinated interactions across peripheral immune tissues such as the bone marrow, spleen, and lymph nodes. Tumor burden induces profound disruptions in hematopoiesis, alters the distribution of immune lineages, and leads to functional exhaustion of cytotoxic lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells. These systemic perturbations establish a suppressive immune landscape that impairs the generation of effective antitumor responses. Notably, successful immunotherapy requires de novo activation of naive T cells in peripheral compartments, emphasizing that the efficacy of immune-based treatments depends on the integrity of the systemic immune architecture. Therefore, a deeper understanding of peripheral immune dysfunction in cancer is critical for improving immunotherapy outcomes and designing strategies that restore immune competence beyond the tumor site.
LncRNA MALAT1 has been highlighted as a critical regulator of immune evasion, acting through multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms (Table ). These include not only the well-studied PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway, but also modulation of pyroptosis, immune checkpoint ligand expression, macrophage polarization, and suppression of T cell cytotoxicity via downstream targets such as SERPINs, miRNAs, and transcriptional regulators. MALAT1 thus emerges as a central orchestrator of immune escape, influencing both innate and adaptive immune responses in the tumor microenvironment. Below, we summarize the multifaceted roles of MALAT1 in modulating tumor immunology across various cancer types [ ].
The PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway has gained extensive attention among all immune checkpoints due to its demonstrated exceptional efficiency as a targeted immune therapy in a diverse variety of tumors [ ]. Nevertheless, the responsiveness to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy varies between cancers and individuals, and several pathways have been discovered to impair tumor immunity during carcinogenesis [ ]. This motivates scientists to investigate the fundamental mechanisms that modulate the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in tumor development. NcRNAs have recently been revealed to play a role in the regulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in tumorigenesis [ ]. It has been reported that MALAT1 promotes cell proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells by regulating the miR-200a-3p/ PD-L1 axis. Mechanistically, MALAT1 modulates PD-L1 expression via sponging miR-200a-3p [ ]. As discussed above, PD-L1 facilitates cancer metastasis by helping cancer cells evade immune surveillance. X-inactive specific transcript (XIST) by inhibiting MALAT1 regulates the expression of PD-L1 that can be a candidate for immunotherapy [ ]. TIGIT/CD155 and PD-1/PD-L1 axes are immune-modulatory receptors. It has been indicated that their level is correlated with MALAT1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. The study elucidated that knocking down MALAT1 can decrease TIGIT/CD155 and PD-1/PD-L1 levels [ ]. The findings in pancreatic cancer showed that MALAT1 increases PD-L1 expression via METTL3-related regulation. METTL3 can promote MALAT1 expression, and MALAT1 upregulates PD-L1 expression [ ]. It is noteworthy that MALAT1 promotes tumorigenesis and immune escape ability by sponging miR-195 and regulation of PD-L1 in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [ ].
Extending these observations to solid tumors, recent data in lung adenocarcinoma revealed that MALAT1 modulates immune evasion and radioresistance via the miR-140/PD-L1 axis [ ]. MALAT1 sponges miR-140, resulting in an increase in PD-L1 expression and lower sensitivity to irradiation. Knockdown of MALAT1 restored miR-140 levels, suppressed PD-L1 expression, increased apoptosis, and inhibited tumor growth in both cell culture and xenograft models[ ]. This further establishes MALAT1 as a critical regulator of the expression of immune checkpoints and therapeutic resistance suggesting that targeting the MALAT1/miR-140/PD-L1 axis may improve the efficacy of radiotherapy and, hence, antitumor immune responses in lung cancer.
Beyond checkpoint ligand regulation, MALAT1 directly influences cytotoxic T cell (CTL) activation and memory formation. A study identified a functional interaction between MALAT1 and the tumor-suppressor miRNA family miR-15/16, which plays a pivotal role in T cell expansion, activation, and long-term memory [ ]. Using CRISPR-mediated editing of the miR-15/16 binding site on MALAT1 in mice, researchers demonstrated that disruption of this axis reduces CD28 signaling, IL-2 expression, and memory T cell persistence following viral infections [ ]. MALAT1 sequesters miR-15/16 to buffer repression of their downstream targets, thereby enhancing T cell activation programs. This work uncovers a previously underappreciated, cell-intrinsic immunostimulatory function of MALAT1 in adaptive immunity, though paradoxically, such a mechanism may also be hijacked by tumors to enhance T cell exhaustion or immune evasion in chronic disease settings.
A groundbreaking study recently revealed a pivotal role for MALAT1 in suppressing pyroptosis and evading T cell-mediated killing of incipient metastatic cells, thereby linking immune evasion with metastatic reactivation [ ]. In genetically-engineered mouse models of both breast and other tumor types, the researchers established that MALAT1 is needed for both tumor initiation and metastatic outgrowth. Mechanistically, MALAT1 localizes nuclear speckles, which couple transcriptional regulation, alternative splicing, and maturation of mRNA, supporting its previously-characterized functions in the nucleus. Importantly, MALAT1 upregulates the expression of WNT ligands, inducing autocrine WNT signaling loops, which support the self-renewal of cells that are metastatic. Moreover, MALAT1 induces the expression of Serpin family protease inhibitors, particularly SERPINB6B, which acts to suppress gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated pyroptosis. Pyroptosis as mentioned before is a form of inflammatory, immunogenic cell death that is triggered by caspase-1 and cathepsin G, both of which are inhibited by SERPINB6B. As a result, incipient metastatic cells expressing high levels of MALAT1 are able to evade cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated killing, thereby maintaining metastatic dormancy or reactivating metastatic growth under immune-suppressive conditions [ ]. Strikingly, therapeutic inhibition of MALAT1 using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) significantly impaired metastasis in a SERPINB6B-dependent manner, revealing MALAT1 not only as a driver of metastatic immune escape but also as a clinically actionable therapeutic target. These findings underscore a multifaceted and previously underappreciated role for MALAT1 in orchestrating immune evasion, stem-like behavior, and escape from dormancy, positioning it at the nexus of tumor progression and immune regulation (244). It makes the MALAT1–SERPINB6B–pyroptosis axis a potential pathway for metastatic cells to inhibit immune surveillance that may have tremendous implications in treating dormant micrometastases.
Beyond its role in suppressing pyroptosis and evading T cell-mediated killing, MALAT1 also reprograms the inflammatory landscape of the tumor microenvironment to facilitate metastasis. A recent study utilizing CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) in both patient-derived lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cell lines and an autochthonous K-ras/p53 LUAD mouse model demonstrated that overexpression of MALAT1 is sufficient to drive metastatic progression [ ]. In this context, MALAT1 overexpression enhances tumor cell motility and induces paracrine secretion of the chemokine CCL2, which subsequently recruits pro-tumorigenic macrophages to the tumor microenvironment. Notably, macrophage depletion and Ccl2 blockade reversed the metastatic effects associated with MALAT1 overexpression [ ]. At the chromatin level, MALAT1 increases global chromatin accessibility, particularly at the Ccl2 locus, thereby enabling its transcriptional activation. These findings suggest that MALAT1 exerts a potent immunomodulatory effect by promoting innate immune cell recruitment and tumor-promoting inflammation through epigenetic reprogramming and chemokine induction [ ]. Taken together with its previously described role in suppressing CTL-induced pyroptosis, MALAT1 acts as a regulator of both adaptive and innate immune evasion, establishing a tumor microenvironment that supports cancer progression and metastatic spread. However, the mechanistic drivers that connect MALAT1 overexpression to global chromatin remodeling, especially at immune loci, like CCL2, are still not completely known. Integration with ATAC-seq and chromatin looping studies would help clarify this regulatory axis.
In gastric cancer, integrated profiling of TP53-associated lncRNAs revealed that elevated MALAT1 expression is significantly enriched in high-risk patient groups, characterized by increased infiltration of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages, upregulation of immune checkpoint pathways (including APC co-stimulation and CCR signaling), and widespread alterations in RNA methylation marks such as m6A (FTO, ZC3H13, YTHDC1, RBM15) and m5C (NOP2, TET1) [ ]. In addition, MALAT1 expression is associated with oxidative stress-related genes (e.g., MPO, MAPK14, HMOX1), indicating that MALAT1 may contribute to a tolerogenic tumor microenvironment. Taken together, these data demonstrate that MALAT1 is not only an immune modulator, but is a predictive biomarker of chemotherapy responsiveness and risk stratification in gastric cancer [ ]. Notably, MALAT1 also influences macrophage polarization, a key component of innate immunity [ ]. It promotes the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype by sequestering miR-30b, a microRNA that favors M2 anti-inflammatory differentiation. Knockdown of MALAT1 enhances M2 marker expression and impairs macrophage phagocytosis, antigen processing, and cytokine release, reinforcing its immunostimulatory role in macrophage biology [ ].
Additionally, MALAT1 exerts potent immunosuppressive effects on dendritic cells (DC) via exosome-mediated signaling. The lung carcinoma cells secrete exosomal MALAT1, which inhibits the phagocytosis, inflammatory cytokines production (IFN-γ, IL-12), expression of costimulatory molecules and initiates autophagy through stimulation of the AKT/mTOR pathway [ ]. All of these effects on DCs inhibit their ability to present antigens and activate T cells effectively, suppressing early anti-tumor immunity. Notably, silencing MALAT1 in tumor-derived exosomes restored DC immunostimulatory effects and improved T cell proliferation, emphasizing that MALAT1 alters the tumor immune microenvironment. As such, exosomal MALAT1 has been positioned as an important player in regulating DC-mediated immunity and a promising target for reversing tumor-induced immune tolerance [ ].
MALAT1 has a role in immune evasion of TNBC by suppressing both the innate and adaptive immune responses [ ]. In the MDA-MB-231 cell line, MALAT1 silencing resulted in a further upregulation of NK cell-activating ligands MICA/B and downregulation of immune checkpoint molecules PD-L1 and B7-H4, enhancing the cytotoxicity of NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells [ ]. Functionally, MALAT1 exerts its immunosuppressive effects through sponging miR-34a and miR-17-5p, thereby limiting the expression of immune-activating pathways. Reintroduction of miR-34a restored MICA/B levels, while miR-17-5p suppressed PD-L1 and B7-H4 [ ]. This defines a MALAT1–miR-34a/miR-17-5p axis that dampens antitumor immunity in TNBC by modulating ligand and checkpoint expression across both immune arms.
MALAT1 regulates an intricate combination of processes involved in immunoregulation that shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Suppressing pyroptosis and cytotoxic lymphocyte function, and alteration of both myeloid cells and checkpoint signaling, clearly establishes MALAT1 as a potent driver of immune evasion. Its role encompasses more than just a passive biomarker; it is the molecular rheostat regulating immune escape at different levels: genetic, epigenetic, and post-transcriptional. As cancer immunotherapy advances, targeting MALAT1 and its associated axes offers an exciting frontier for overcoming resistance, enhancing immune surveillance, and reawakening dormant antitumor responses. To this end, combinatorial strategies that couple MALAT1 inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade, macrophage reprogramming, or exosome interception may be especially promising, particularly in tumors with established immunotherapy resistance.

## Linking MALAT1 to drug resistance
Drug resistance is a major obstacle to achieving optimal treatment in cancer patient. The underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon remain to be fully elucidated. Several mechanisms can promote direct or indirect drug resistance in cancer cells, including drug inactivation, EMT, DNA damage repair, multidrug resistance, cell death inhibition, drug target alteration, and epigenetic change [ – ]. Considering the important regulatory roles of lncRNAs in biological processes, it is not surprising that their deregulation enables the tumor cells to become resistant to chemotherapy [ ]. Over the past decade, several works have been published regarding the mechanisms by which MALAT1 induces drug resistance in human cancer cells (Table ). However, the relative dominance of MALAT1 among other lncRNAs in driving these resistance phenotypes remains underexplored, especially in patient-derived models or large-scale transcriptomic analyses.

 Recent data have shown that MALAT1 via miR-27a-5p/PBOV1 axis can regulate resistance to gemcitabine in NSCLC cells. MALAT1, by sponging miR-27a-5p that targets PBOV1, increases gemcitabine-related resistance [ ]. In line with this, MALAT1 was also shown to mediate resistance to EGFR-TKIs in LUAD by acting as a ceRNA for miR-125 and upregulating Rab25, leading to enhanced proliferation, EMT, and drug resistance. This MALAT1/miR-125/Rab25 axis was validated through both luciferase reporter assays and functional studies in Erlotinib-resistant LUAD cells, emphasizing MALAT1’s role as a central driver of targeted therapy resistance in NSCLC [ ].
MALAT1 regulates oxaliplatin resistance by sponging miR-22-3p and increasing the expression level of ZFP91 in gastric cancer cells [ ]. A recent finding demonstrated that the high expression of MALAT1 is associated with a worse prognosis in patients with breast cancer. Further analyses revealed that the expression level of MALAT1 is higher in taxane-resistant and Adriamycin-resistant MCF-7 cells, and MALAT1 knockdown increases sensitivity to taxane and Adriamycin treatment. However, the mechanisms of how MALAT1 promotes drug resistance in breast cancer remain to be understood [ ]. Research on breast cancer subtypes demonstrated metastatic triple-negative breast cancer and trastuzumab-resistant HER2 + have the highest level of MALAT1 expression. The knockdown of MALAT1 in trastuzumab-resistant HER2 + cells enhances the sensitivity of the cells to trastuzumab treatment. This study also revealed MALAT1 is regulated by the transcription factor FOXO1 through the PI3/Akt pathway. Hence, targeting this pathway could inhibit the high expression of MALAT1 [ ]. Additionally, MALAT1 can enhance trastuzumab resistance via an m6A-dependent mechanism, where PAK5-mediated METTL14 phosphorylation stabilizes MALAT1, which in turn prevents HER2 degradation by interfering with USP8, thereby increasing nuclear HER2 accumulation and reinforcing resistance phenotypes [ ]. These findings suggest a broader opportunity to intervene upstream of MALAT1 through signaling pathway modulation, though the potential systemic effects of such interventions need to be carefully evaluated. In breast cancer, MALAT1 increases SHOC2 expression by sponging miR-497-5p. This action leads to paclitaxel resistance in breast cancer with a poorer prognosis [ ]. It has been shown that high MALAT1 expression is associated with 5-fluorouracil resistance in the early stage of colon cancer. Silencing of MALAT1 in colon cancer leads to more sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil [ ]. Furthermore, in acute myeloid leukemia cells, MALAT1 targets miR-96 and increases cytarabine chemosensitivity [ ].
Histone demethylase KDM4C promotes MALAT1 expression via MALAT1's promoter demethylation, which decreases miR-328-3p and finally results in cytarabine resistance in AML [ ]. Downregulation of miR-335-3p by MALAT1 and NEAT1 in the drug-resistant acute lymphoblastic leukemia cases illustrates the role of MALAT1 in the development of chemoresistance in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia [ ]. ADAM17 is a metalloproteinase that is overexpressed in numerous cancer cases. It has been proposed that MALAT1 and ADAM17 could regulate oxaliplatin-resistance in colorectal cancer cells. MALAT1 enhances the colorectal cancer cells' resistance response by targeting miR-324-3p and upregulating ADAM17. While MALAT1 reduces cell proliferation and migration, ADAM17 overexpression counteracts these effects in oxaliplatin-resistance colorectal cancer cells [ ]. Moreover, MALAT1 can be related to radiosensitivity in colorectal cancer. Accumulative data demonstrated that silencing of MALAT1 can promote HCT116 cell line radiosensitivity, which can be related to the glycosaminoglycan pathway [ ]. Doxorubicin resistance is common in hepatocellular carcinoma therapy. It has been reported that MALAT1 acts as a sponge of miR-3129-5p to upregulate RNA binding protein NOVA1, and mediate doxorubicin resistance of hepatocellular carcinoma cells [ ]. Several studies demonstrated the roles of MALAT1 in cisplatin-resistance of various cancer cells. In cervical cancer, MALAT1 induces cisplatin resistance via regulating BRWD1 and PI3K/AKT pathway [ ]. MALAT1 upregulates the ATG5, a key protein for autophagic vesicle formation, through sponging miR-30b, promoting autophagy-associated cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer cells [ ]. Triple-negative breast cancer patients, resistance to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) were examined for the transcriptional profile. MALAT1 was up-regulated in those patients. MALAT1 knockdown through CRISPR/Cas9 system reduced drug-resistant to paclitaxel and doxorubicin [ ]. Elevated MALAT1 by m6A methyltransferase METTL3, through the E2F1/AGR2 axis increase Adriamycin resistance in breast cancer cells [ ]. ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters play a pivotal role in the development of multidrug resistance in cancer cells [ ]. These membrane proteins utilize ATP hydrolysis to actively efflux a wide range of chemotherapeutic agents out of cells, thereby reducing intracellular drug accumulation and diminishing therapeutic efficacy. Among the most studied ABC transporters are P-glycoprotein (MDR1/ABCB1) and multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1/ABCC1), both of which have been implicated in resistance to various anticancer drugs [ ]. It has been demonstrated that overexpression of MALAT1 induces cisplatin-resistance through upregulating MRP1 and MDR1 by STAT3 activation in lung cancer cells [ ]. Additionally, MALAT1 also can activate both PI3K/Akt/mTOR and P-glycoprotein to develop cisplatin resistance in oral squamous carcinoma [ ].
In prostate cancer, gene fusions are defined by the presence of 5′ genomic regulatory elements, which are most frequently regulated by androgen, fused to members of the Ets family of transcription factors and NTRK1 [ ]. An interesting study discovered that MALAT1-fusions with MVP and NCBP3 are common in TRAMP tumor cells and mediate resistance to androgen receptor inhibition [ ].
A systems-level study using a dynamic Boolean network highlighted the centrality of the MALAT1–BMI1 axis in orchestrating chemoresistance in NSCLC through suppression of miR-145-5p [ ]. The model revealed key regulatory circuits involving MALAT1, BMI1, and downstream EMT drivers such as Sp1 and KLF4, offering mechanistic insight into how lncRNA-mediated ceRNA interactions contribute to therapeutic failure. While the in-silico predictions align with prior experimental findings, the study remains largely computational and requires experimental validation to confirm the biological significance of these newly proposed regulatory loops.
Another recent study demonstrated that MALAT1 expression is epigenetically regulated by the histone demethylase JMJD2C, which demethylates H3K9me3 and H3K36me3 at the MALAT1 promoter, leading to its upregulation in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer cells. MALAT1, in turn, activates the NF-κB/P-gp and P38 MAPK/ERCC1 pathways, contributing to chemoresistance. Interestingly, while low-dose metformin increased JMJD2C expression and had limited efficacy, its combination with JMJD2C knockdown synergistically suppressed MALAT1 and effectively reversed platinum resistance in vitro and in vivo [ ].
MALAT1 is increasingly recognized as a central mediator of drug resistance across cancer types, functioning through a diverse array of mechanisms, ranging from epigenetic reprogramming and miRNA sponging to autophagy activation and gene fusions. Its consistent involvement in chemoresistance, targeted therapy failure, and immunotherapy escape underscores MALAT1 as a powerful therapeutic target. Future work aimed at dissecting context-specific MALAT1 signaling circuits and integrating antisense-based inhibitors into clinical regimens may open new opportunities to overcome resistance and improve patient outcomes. Ultimately, patient stratification based on MALAT1 expression or associated pathway activity may be required to maximize clinical benefit from MALAT1-directed therapies.

## Therapeutic targeting and challenges of MALAT1 in cancer
Targeting lncRNAs has emerged as a compelling strategy in the era of RNA therapeutics, offering opportunities to manipulate oncogenic gene expression networks with unprecedented precision. Unlike protein-coding genes, lncRNAs exhibit tissue- and cancer-type-specific expression patterns, modular structures, and multifaceted regulatory functions in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments, rendering them attractive yet complex therapeutic targets. Their ability to orchestrate chromatin remodeling, transcriptional dynamics, and post-transcriptional events implicates them as critical regulators of tumor progression and immune evasion. However, the development of lncRNA-based therapies faces several challenges, including poor evolutionary conservation, limited structural annotation, and difficulties in effective delivery to tumor tissues, especially for nuclear-enriched targets [ ]. Our knowledge of the field of lncRNA biology is still rudimentary and much basic experimental work still needs to be done. For many lncRNAs, including MALAT1, it may be difficult to pinpoint exact biological functions, and knockout models frequently show subtle or even no phenotypes due to possible developmental compensation or redundancy [ ]. This functional ambiguity complicates rational therapeutic targeting and risk assessment in preclinical models [ ]. Despite these hurdles, rapid advancements in oligonucleotide chemistry, RNA-targeting CRISPR tools, and high-throughput screening platforms have significantly accelerated the discovery and functional validation of disease-driving lncRNAs. Among them, MALAT1 stands out as one of the most well-characterized oncogenic lncRNAs with demonstrated therapeutic potential across various tumor types. Namely, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), small molecules, and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) have been evaluated as potential therapeutic options (Table ) (Fig. ).
 SiRNAs can induce MALAT1 degradation by recruiting the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) and endonuclease Ago-2 [ ]. MALAT1 knockdown through siRNAs has been shown to induce cell cycle arrest and inhibition of cell growth in prostate cancer and inhibit cell migration in colorectal cancer [ , ]. Despite the effectiveness, the off-target effects of siRNAs as well as challenges with effective delivery, remain a limitation to be addressed [ ]. Oligonucleotides also have the potential to hybridize both on- and off-target, resulting in unwanted and unanticipated effects, and only a fraction of designed molecules efficiently silence their target. RNA structure, protein association, and strict requirements for RNase H activity all contribute to variability in efficacy [ , ]. Off-target toxicity is difficult to control, and sequence-dependent pro-inflammatory or immunogenic responses have been seen in clinical trials of other RNA therapeutics. Therefore, stringent experimental controls and thorough RNAseq or bioinformatics analysis are recommended, but these are rarely practical or sufficiently quantitative in most studies. Moreover, delivery vehicles for RNA therapeutics remain suboptimal, often leading to instability, inefficient intracellular uptake, and lack of organ or tumor selectivity, which collectively reduce the translational potential of these approaches [ , ]. Achieving safe and efficient in vivo delivery remains a crucial hurdle, and while chemical modifications, viral vectors, and nanoparticles are being explored, systemic delivery still results in non-specific tissue accumulation and related unwanted effects.
ASOs are short single-stranded oligonucleotides that bind to RNA via standard Watson–Crick base-pairing [ ]. The formed heteroduplex triggers RNA degradation through an RNase H-mediated pathway [ ]. ASOs have been utilized as a therapeutic choice in various disorders such as familial hypercholesterolemia and bone disease [ , ]. LNA-gapmeR ASOs are a therapeutic choice specifically targeting lncRNAs [ ]. The effectiveness of LNA-gapmeR as a superior anticancer option has been shown in vivo in multiple myeloma [ ]. In a TNBC mouse model lacking Tp53, administration of a MALAT1 ASO has been demonstrated to reduce MALAT1 RNA expression. This led to a slowdown in the growth of primary tumors, reduced cell proliferation, and increased apoptosis [ ]. Still, dose optimization and systemic toxicity remain under-addressed in most ASO studies, and longer-term safety data are lacking. The specific targeting of ASOs, as well as the poor cell membrane permeability, have imposed a challenge for the effective delivery of the drug [ , ]. In addition, oligonucleotide chemistry and sequence both contribute to adverse events, including fever, chills, or injection site reactions; preclinical testing in animal models is necessary to eliminate toxic sequences, but human responses remain difficult to predict [ ]. In lung cancer, a smart delivery system using gold nanoparticles co-functionalized with a TAT peptide dramatically enhanced nuclear uptake of MALAT1-targeting ASOs [ ]. This strategy not only stabilizes ASOs but also leads to potent suppression of MALAT1, effectively blocking migration and metastasis in vivo. The approach highlights how nanotechnology can unlock the therapeutic potential of lncRNA targeting [ ]. However, despite their promise, nanoparticle-based delivery systems still face important hurdles. In vivo, nanoparticles may suffer from nonspecific distribution, rapid clearance by the immune system, or accumulation in off-target organs like the liver and spleen, which limits their delivery efficiency and may result in unintended toxicity. Surface modifications and careful characterization are needed to improve their biodistribution and safety profile before clinical translation [ – ]. Additionally, similar to siRNAs, ASOs may execute off-target effects, and more research is warranted in the field. RNA therapeutics can also trigger innate immune responses, leading to cytokine release, flu-like symptoms, and other toxicities, which have caused termination of some RNA drug trials; chemical modifications are being developed to mitigate these risks, but thorough immune screening is still required before clinical application [ ]. Furthermore, small molecules can target highly conserved RNA motifs and disrupt the RNA spatial structures [ ]. MALAT1 RNA contains an unusual element for nuclear expression (EME), which folds into a triple-helical structure [ , ]. EME provides MALAT1 protection from degradation and it has been shown that targeting EME through small molecules can reduce the MALAT1 expression [ , ]. The potential for small molecules to be utilized alongside ASOs for dual anticancer therapy is currently being investigated [ ].
CRISPR and the associated proteins, the Cas family, have received extensive attention as a genome editing tool in recent years. CRISPR can target lncRNAs, and preclinical data support its effectiveness in silencing MALAT1 expression [ , , , ]. A recent study using CRISPR/Cas9 to deplete MALAT1 in prostate cancer, demonstrated that MALAT1 depletion led to defective homologous recombination, decreased BRCA1 and RAD51 protein levels, and increased formation of γH2AX foci [ ]. In functional assays, silencing of MALAT1 increased sensitivity to olaparib in both in vitro and in vivo studies, further supporting MALAT1 had a potential therapeutic synergy with PARP inhibitors [ ]. A second CRISPR/Cas9-based study in DU-145 cells further confirmed these findings. MALAT1 knockout increased apoptosis and significantly reduced proliferation and migration. MALAT1 was also identified as one of the most upregulated lncRNAs in prostate tumors through TCGA-based analysis, reinforcing its role as a diagnostic and therapeutic target [ ].
CRISPR has the potential to be utilized as a therapeutic choice and can inhibit MALAT1 and other lncRNAs by targeting their loci [ ]. However, CRISPR has significant drawbacks as it may cause deleterious effects to the neighboring genes [ , ]. Recently, a novel CRISPR-based system fused to G-quadruplex (G4)-binding proteins and ligands has enabled precise stabilization of promoter G4s, including those of MALAT1 [ ]. This approach selectively downregulates MALAT1 transcription by targeting its G4-forming sequences, using dCas9–SunTag–streptavidin to recruit biotinylated G4 ligands (Bio–PDC). Unlike conventional G4 ligands, this system avoids global G4 stabilization and minimizes off-target effects. Transcriptome and chromatin analyses confirmed efficient and selective silencing of MALAT1 without impacting other G4-driven genes such as MYC or KRAS. This CRISPR–G4 strategy introduces a new dimension in lncRNA-directed therapeutics, combining structural genomics with programmable editing [ ].
MALAT1 has emerged as a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in a wide spectrum of cancers (Table ). Elevated MALAT1 levels in tumor tissue or circulation are consistently associated with advanced disease, recurrence, and poor outcomes in cancers such as NSCLC, breast, ovarian, colorectal, gastric, hepatocellular carcinoma, melanoma, and various hematological malignancies [ , ]. Recent studies also support its utility as a non-invasive biomarker, for example in blood or saliva [ , ]. However, the clinical adoption of MALAT1 remains limited by context-dependent effects, isoform variability, and inconsistent findings in certain subtypes. Standardized quantification methods and isoform-specific assays, validated in large clinical cohorts, are needed to clarify MALAT1’s true value and enable its translation into personalized oncology. A recent clinical trial also validated the diagnostic performance of MALAT1 as a salivary biomarker in oral squamous cell carcinoma [ ]. In this clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05708209), saliva samples were collected in patients with OSCC and healthy controls and MALAT1 expression was significantly higher and miRNA-124 was reduced in patients with disease. Additionally, MALAT1 levels correlated with the patients' metastatic status. At a defined cut-off, this biomarker had excellent diagnostics performance overall, with a 95% sensitivity and 90% specificity for all patients with OSCC compared to controls [ ]. These clinical findings provide evidence that MALAT1 is a promising, non-invasive biomarker for use as part of the diagnostic process for cancer.

 

## Alternative treatments
A group of researchers has been focusing on alternative tools such as plant-based compounds and physical activity for regulating MALAT1 expression. Quercetin, a flavonoid compound, has been shown to effectively downregulate MALAT1 expression in vivo in a dose and time-dependent manner in prostate cancer [ ]. Another recent study found that methoxylated quercetin glycoside inhibits TNBC cell growth, colony formation, migration, and invasion. Methoxylated quercetin glycoside inhibited MALAT1 and induced TP53 and its downstream miRNAs; miR-155 and miR-146a affect several downstream signaling pathways, including the nitric oxide synthesizing machinery and natural killer cell cytotoxicity [ ].
An interesting study has targeted gastric cancerous cells with molecular hydrogen as a therapeutic option. It demonstrated that gas can promote proliferation and migration of gastric cancer by inhibiting MALAT1 and promoting miR-124-3p [ ].
It has been illustrated that resveratrol decreases the expression of MALAT1 in Parkinson's Disease [ ]. A recent work evaluated the utility of moderate to vigorous physical activity in downregulating MALAT1 expression in healthy subjects [ ]. The use of conventional therapies in combination with chemotherapeutic drugs has shown promise. Knockdown of MALAT1 in breast cancer cells treated with metformin showed an increase in Bax/Bcl2 ratio and decreased cyclin B1 expression [ ]. The high expression of antioxidant genes in response to exercise was proposed to be the mechanism behind the observation. Moreover, in hepatocellular carcinoma, resveratrol enhanced mitophagy and suppressed tumor progression by targeting the MALAT1/miR-143-3p/RRM2 axis, revealing a novel link between MALAT1 modulation and mitochondrial quality control under anticancer treatment [ ].
MALAT1 is a key factor in tumor progression, immune evasion, and treatment resistance and has become a promising target for therapy in cancers. While antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, small molecules, and CRISPR-based tools have demonstrated potential in preclinical models, their clinical translation remains limited by challenges in specificity, delivery, and safety. Meanwhile, complementary approaches, including natural compounds and lifestyle modifications, offer additional layers of MALAT1 modulation. Continued research into combinatorial therapies and delivery systems will be critical to realize the translational potential of MALAT1-targeted interventions and integrate them into next-generation precision oncology strategies.

## Concluding remarks and future directions
MALAT1 stands out among lncRNAs for its deep evolutionary conservation across vertebrates, showing high sequence homology. In the context of cancer, these evolutionarily preserved features make MALAT1 highly stable and easily upregulated, supporting tumor progression and metastasis through regulation of alternative splicing, nuclear speckle organization, and gene expression. Notably, MALAT1 alterations, including overexpression, mutations, and chromosomal rearrangements, are linked to aggressive cancers. Thus, its evolutionary conservation highlights both its biological importance and its potential as a therapeutic target, as cancer cells often hijack these ancient regulatory mechanisms for malignancy. The seemingly paradoxical ability of MALAT1 to act either as an oncogene or a tumor suppressor is mostly attributed to its highly context-dependent molecular interactions and functional plasticity. Major factors underlying this duality include isoform-specific expression, subcellular localization, and various genetic or epigenetic backgrounds. MALAT1 generates a plethora of transcript variants, which can have different RNA secondary structures and various abilities to bind RNA-binding proteins, transcription factors, and miRNAs. Thus, distinct isoforms or genetic haplotypes, e.g., the tumor suppressive GGGT haplotype in NSCLC, can significantly redistribute MALAT1's regulatory landscape. Furthermore, subcellular localization strongly dictates MALAT1's molecular interactions; nuclear retention predominantly facilitates chromatin remodeling and alternative splicing regulation, whereas aberrant cytoplasmic localization or nuclear compartmentalization patterns can dramatically alter its functional output. Microenvironmental contexts and cell lineage specificity, as in virus-infected lymphoma cells or in bone metastases, also control MALAT1's functions by dictating which transcriptional programs or signaling networks prevail. As an lncRNA, MALAT1 directly mediates molecular and cellular pathways and regulates cell growth, invasion, and migration in patients with cancer, and its critical role in modulating EMT, autophagy, and metabolic reprogramming is also addressed here. However, mechanistic dissection of these processes often relies on in vitro systems, and functional in vivo studies validating MALAT1’s role in each pathway are still limited. Determining the molecular pathways by which MALAT1 activates and deactivates EMT and autophagy is essential for developing new paths for lncRNA-directed cancer diagnostics, prognosis, and therapeutic approaches. Cancer metabolic dysregulation is important for cancer cells to function abnormally. LncRNAs, including MALAT1, have a broad and complicated effect on cancer metabolic transcription factors and pathways, either directly or indirectly, extending our knowledge of cancer metabolic reprogramming. Herein, we defined and discussed the possible role of MALAT1 in regulating cancer metabolic reprogramming.
MALAT1 is packaged into exosomes, which deliver it to recipient cells and can modulate gene expression linked to tumor progression. Establishing how exosomal MALAT1 differentially regulates immune evasion and angiogenesis across tumor subtypes remains a research priority. Standardized profiling of exosomal RNA in patient plasma is critical for biomarker validation. MALAT1’s impact on metastasis is highly context dependent. In MMTV–PyMT breast cancer models, MALAT1 depletion (via ASOs or promoter deletion) reduces lung metastases and promotes differentiation, while polyadenylation insertion paradoxically increases metastasis, implying experimental outcomes depend on whether the transcript or genomic locus is targeted. Transgenic rescue supports the importance of MALAT1 RNA, and mechanistically, MALAT1 binds and inhibits TEAD factors, repressing metastatic genes (e.g., ITGB4, VEGFA).
These findings highlight the need for complementary approaches (ASO knockdown, polyA insertion, promoter deletion, ChIRP-MS) to define MALAT1’s function. Distinguishing RNA-mediated effects from locus-dependent changes (such as effects on NEAT1 or chromatin) is a major technical challenge. Future studies must use allele-specific rescue, CRISPRi, and transcriptional interruption to separate MALAT1’s RNA and cis-regulatory functions, which is critical before clinical targeting to avoid off-target genomic or epigenetic effects. MALAT1 also acts as a nuclear ceRNA, sponging tumor-suppressive microRNAs, thereby derepressing oncogenes and immune regulators. This nuclear ceRNA function is supported by AGO2 pull-down and RISC enrichment studies, but whether it fully explains MALAT1’s oncogenic effects is debated, as protein- and chromatin-binding may have broader impacts. Separation-of-function approaches, combined with high-resolution localization and quantitative modeling, are needed to clarify the relative contributions of MALAT1’s mechanisms and assess therapeutic potential.
In the present review, we provided vital information about MALAT1-dependent aberrant signaling networks within cancer cells and its potential as a novel cancer therapy strategy. Furthermore, we elaborated on the role of MALAT1 in promoting drug resistance. On that note, we introduced MALAT1 as a potential drug target and discussed the advancements in pharmaceutical therapy for patients with cancer. Since MALAT1 regulates drug resistance in human cancers, it can be a prospective candidate to develop new targeted therapies. Still, the extent to which MALAT1-driven resistance overlaps with other resistance pathways, such as metabolic rewiring or epigenetic adaptation, requires systematic investigation. Therapeutically, it will be critical to define biomarkers of MALAT1 dependence and resistance mechanisms to stratify patients for MALAT1-targeted interventions. Combination therapies with epigenetic inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade may offer synergistic potential.
Of particular note, the ability of MALAT1 to regulate immune checkpoints and modulate tumor immune responses points to its significance in cancer immunotherapy. As such, targeting MALAT1 may also enhance antitumor immunity or overcome immune evasion mechanisms employed by tumors. Given its dual role in both immune stimulation and suppression, context-specific effects of MALAT1 must be carefully delineated to avoid unintended immune consequences. Importantly, recent evidence underscores MALAT1’s versatile roles in shaping the tumor immune landscape, from enhancing cytotoxic activity in CD8⁺ T cells to promoting immunosuppression through effects on M2 macrophages. This highlights MALAT1 as a critical regulator of cancer immunity with significant implications for therapy. Despite the substantial progress made, several key questions remain. What regulates the spatiotemporal expression of MALAT1 across cancer types and stages? How does MALAT1 selectively interact with proteins, RNAs, or chromatin to exert its effects? Moreover, it remains unknown whether MALAT1’s subcellular localization is dynamically regulated in response to oncogenic stress or treatment exposure. Addressing these gaps will not only improve our mechanistic understanding of lncRNA biology but also facilitate the development of MALAT1-based therapeutic platforms.
Nonetheless, the field faces pressing conceptual and methodological challenges. High-throughput approaches often fail to capture MALAT1’s cell type-specific functions, and knockout models show variable phenotypes depending on genetic background and stress conditions. Furthermore, the absence of functionally resolved isoforms and lack of tools to probe MALAT1’s modular RNA domains hinder mechanistic clarity. Despite recent insights, critical challenges persist in translating MALAT1 biology into clinical impact. This gap reflects not only technical limitations but also incomplete understanding of MALAT1’s structural and regulatory complexity. The complexity of its RNA–protein and RNA–chromatin interactions, often shaped by context-specific structural domains, remains incompletely understood. Moreover, the potential for off-target effects when modulating MALAT1 therapeutically underscores the need for highly selective delivery systems. Additionally, the selective packaging and functional consequences of exosomal MALAT1 require deeper investigation, especially in the tumor–immune interface. Addressing these questions will benefit from advanced molecular tools such as GRID-seq, RAP–MS, and single-cell spatial transcriptomics, which may help decode MALAT1’s dynamic localization, target specificity, and modular domains. A better understanding of these layers will be essential not only for harnessing MALAT1 as a therapeutic target but also for positioning it as a molecular entry point into the broader landscape of lncRNA-regulated oncogenic programs.
In summary, MALAT1 is not merely a passive biomarker but a functional driver of cancer biology with context-dependent roles in tumor progression, immune escape, metabolic rewiring, and therapy resistance. Future work must prioritize in vivo validation, isoform-specific functional mapping, and translational modeling to harness MALAT1’s full potential. By resolving its structural–functional relationships and contextual interactions, MALAT1 may serve as a cornerstone in the next generation of RNA-based cancer diagnostics and therapeutics.
