## Abstract
N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, primarily regulated by methyltransferase-like protein 3 (METTL3), plays a pivotal role in RNA metabolism and leukemogenesis. However, the post-translational mechanisms governing METTL3 stability and function remain incompletely understood. Given the widespread occurrence of O-GlcNAcylation on nuclear and cytosolic proteins, we hypothesized that METTL3 might undergo O-GlcNAcylation, thereby influencing its stability and oncogenic function in myeloid malignancies. In this study, we found that METTL3 is O-GlcNAcylated in both myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and its expression positively correlates with O-GlcNAcylation levels. Functional assays demonstrated that O-GlcNAcylation enhances METTL3 protein stability and promotes leukemic cell survival. Mechanistically, O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 stabilizes mRNA of serine- and arginine-rich splicing factor 1 (SRSF1), leading to increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein MCL-1. This, in turn, suppresses apoptosis and supports MDS/AML cell viability. Targeting the O-GlcNAcylated form of METTL3 using a competitive peptide significantly inhibited MDS/AML progression in preclinical models. In conclusion, our findings reveal a novel O-GlcNAcylation-dependent mechanism that regulates METTL3 stability and oncogenic activity through the m6A-SRSF1-MCL-1 axis, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy for MDS and AML.
Keywords: m6A modification, METTL3, O-GlcNAc, SRSF1, MCL-1, gene splicing, targeted therapy, IGF2BP2, MDS, AML

## Introduction
Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is myeloid neoplasms characterized by clonal proliferation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), recurrent genetic abnormalities, ineffective hematopoiesis, peripheral blood cytopenia, and a high risk of evolution to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). , Despite great progress achieved regarding the molecular basis of MDS/AML pathogenesis, the overall survival of adult MDS/AML patients has not significantly improved in the past several decades. Recent evidence has revealed that epigenetic factors establish a gene-specific epiregulome for normal development and maintenance of lineage-specific gene expression patterns during hematopoiesis. Among the various epigenetic modifications, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) has emerged as one of the most prevalent modifications on mRNA and holds significant functional importance. , 
The dynamics and plasticity of m6A-mediated regulation are tightly controlled by a network of modulators, with the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 being linked to both the initiation and maintenance of AML. , METTL3 undergoes a variety of post-translational modifications (PTMs), including acetylation, sumoylation, phosphorylation, and lactylation. , , , O-GlcNAcylation, as a unique glycosylation, predominantly influences protein function, stability, subcellular localization, and spatial conformation. It is characterized by the addition or removal of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) residues to specific serine or threonine residues on proteins, catalyzed by the enzymes O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and O-GlcNAcase (OGA), respectively. A recent study in hepatocellular carcinoma was the first to report O-GlcNAcylation of METTL3. However, whether METTL3 can be O-GlcNAcylated and, if so, how this modification regulates the fates of various mRNAs in MDS/AML remain unclear.
In our study, we discovered that METTL3 can be O-GlcNAcylated and that this modification is essential for enhancing the m6A stability of the SRSF1 mRNA, ultimately leading to increased expression of full-length myeloid cell leukemia 1 (MCL-1) (MCL-1L is full length) in an O-GlcNAcylation-dependent manner. These findings underscore the capacity of METTL3 O-GlcNAcylation to influence the regulation of key oncogenic genes in MDS/AML cells. Notably, our results suggest that therapeutic targeting of O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 could represent a promising strategy for treating MDS/AML.

## Results

## Correlation of METTL3 expression and O-GlcNAcylation levels in MDS/AML
To explore the relevance of m6A RNA methylation in myeloid malignancies, we first examined CD34+ bone marrow cells (BMCs) from MDS/AML patients. These cells exhibited significantly elevated global m6A levels compared to healthy donors (HD) ( A and 1B). To model disease initiation, we established the Mx1-Cre, LSL-NrasG12D mouse model to recapitulate MDS initiation ( C) and observed significantly increased m6A levels in cKit+ BMCs compared to controls ( D). As METTL3 is the core methyltransferase catalyzing m6A modification, we next assessed its expression. METTL3 was markedly upregulated in MDS/AML patient samples ( E). Consistent with our previous study, MDS/AML patients exhibited elevated OGT expression and increased O-GlcNAc levels ( F and 1G). METTL3 and OGT were consistently upregulated in clinical CD34+ BMCs, MDS/AML cell lines, and mononuclear BMCs of Mx1-Cre, LSL-NrasG12D mice, accompanied by elevated O-GlcNAc levels ( F–1J and A).
 To further explore the association between METTL3 and OGT, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of AML patients revealed co-upregulation of METTL3 and OGT in malignant cells, showing significant positive correlation ( K and B). This correlation extended to multiple cancer types in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data ( L, C, and S1D), with METTL3 protein levels positively correlating with O-GlcNAc in AML patients ( M). Notably, both genes were significantly elevated in AML versus controls ( E) and associated with poorer survival outcomes ( F). Thiamet G (TMG; an OGA inhibitor) or high glucose treatment elevated O-GlcNAc levels, increasing m6A in KG1a, SKM1, and M1 cells, while OGT small molecule inhibitor (OSMI-1; an OGT inhibitor) reduced both O-GlcNAc and m6A levels ( G and S1H). m6A was higher in mononuclear BMCs from Mx1-Cre, LSL-NrasG12D mice compared to Mx1-Cre mice ( I). Introducing MLL-AF9 into cKit+ BMCs from OGTfl/fl and Mx1-OGTfl/fl mice showed that OGT deficiency significantly lowered m6A levels in BM cKit+ cells ( J).

## The effect of O-GlcNAcylation on METTL3 expression and function
To investigate whether O-GlcNAcylation modulates METTL3 expression, in TMG-treated cKit+ BMCs from Mx1-Cre, LSL-NrasG12D mice and CD34+ BMCs from AML patients, increased O-GlcNAc levels were associated with elevated METTL3 expression, whereas in OSMI-1-treated cells, decreased O-GlcNAc levels resulted in reduced METTL3 expression, compared to cells from Mx1-Cre mice or HD ( A and 2B). Similar trends were observed in high glucose-, TMG- or OSMI-1-treated KG1a and M1 cells ( C and A). We next examined whether OGT directly interacts with METTL3. Co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) assays confirmed the physical association of OGT with METTL3 in KG1a cells ( B). Moreover, elevated O-GlcNAc levels increased METTL3 expression and its O-GlcNAcylation, while reduced O-GlcNAc levels decreased both ( D and C). Interestingly, although METTL3/METTL14/WTAP can form an m6A methyltransferase complex, METTL3 O-GlcNAcylation did not alter its interaction with METTL14 or WTAP ( E).
 Using in vitro glycosylation reactions and mass spectrometry analysis ( D), we found that O-GlcNAc can modify the METTL3 protein ( F), with modification sites identified at serine 184 and serine 243 ( E). These modification sites are conserved across most vertebrates ( F). Mutations (serine-to-alanine) at either or both of these sites led to a decrease in O-GlcNAc levels on METTL3, accompanied by decreased METTL3 expression in KG1a cells ( G and 2H). Suppressing O-GlcNAc modification promoted METTL3 degradation, with mutations at 184/243 accelerating its decay. Conversely, augmenting O-GlcNAc modification inhibited its degradation ( G and 2H). At a mechanistic level, previous studies have reported that METTL3 can interact with the deubiquitinase USP5 to regulate its protein stability. , In this study, we found that either single or double mutations markedly impaired the interaction between METTL3 and USP5, leading to an increased level of METTL3 ubiquitination ( I). These findings suggest that O-GlcNAcylation maintains METTL3 protein stability by interacting with USP5. Cells overexpressing O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 (wild-type METTL3 [METTL3-WT]) exhibited significantly lower apoptosis ( J) and promoted the cell cycle and proliferation compared to the METTL3 mutants (O-GlcNAcylation deficient) ( K and 2L), while myeloid differentiation markers CD11b and CD14 were upregulated in O-GlcNAcylation-deficient mutants ( M and 2N). This suggests that O-GlcNAcylation of METTL3 plays a crucial role in regulating the differentiation and clonality of MDS/AML cells.

## SRSF1 identified as the main target of O-GlcNAcylated METTL3
Considering the critical role of METTL3 as an m6A writer, we further investigated whether its O-GlcNAcylation affects its methyltransferase activity. Compared to METTL3-WT, cells expressing O-GlcNAcylation-deficient METTL3 mutants exhibited significantly reduced global m6A levels ( A and 3B).
 RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq) analysis of KG1a cells treated with TMG or OSMI-1 revealed an altered binding profile of METTL3 to mRNAs, particularly affecting target genes involved in RNA splicing (e.g., SRSF1, SRSF9, SF1, and PTBP1) ( C, A, and S3B). Gene Ontology enrichment and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analyses further indicated that these mRNAs are closely associated with spliceosome function ( D, C, and S3D).
Moreover, the increased mRNA expression levels of SRSF1, SRSF9, and SF1 in TMG-treated KG1a cells were confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) ( E). Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (m6A-seq) analysis demonstrated that the GGAC motif was the most enriched consensus motif in both control and treated samples, with m6A peaks predominantly located in coding sequences, especially near stop codons. TMG treatment increased m6A peak abundance, whereas OSMI-1 treatment reduced it ( E–3G).
Integrated analysis of RNA-seq, RIP-seq, and m6A-seq data identified 29 potential target genes, among which SRSF1 was the only splicing-related gene showing both stronger METTL3 binding capacity and significantly upregulated m6A modification ( H and F). Gene set enrichment analysis further revealed that these target genes are mainly enriched in the cell cycle and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways ( G–S4I). RIP-qPCR and methylated RIP (meRIP)-qPCR assays further validated that TMG treatment enhanced the interaction between METTL3 and SRSF1 mRNA, whereas the METTL3-184/243A mutant exhibited significantly reduced binding affinity to SRSF1 mRNA compared to METTL3-WT ( I and J). Additionally, TCGA database analysis showed a similar positive correlation between METTL3 and SRSF1 expression levels ( J). These consistent findings supported SRSF1 as an ideal target to explore the impact of METTL3 O-GlcNAcylation on m6A regulation.

## SRSF1 m6A modification needs reader IGF2BP2
METTL3, the primary m6A methyltransferase, adds m6A modifications to RNA, while reader proteins recognize these modifications to regulate RNA stability or degradation. Treatment with 2.5 and 5 μM STM2457, a highly potent and selective catalytic inhibitor of METTL3, in KG1a and SKM1 cells resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in SRSF1 mRNA levels ( A). To explore the downstream mechanisms, using the StarBase database, we identified insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding proteins (IGF2BPs) as m6A readers binding to SRSF1 mRNA. TCGA analysis revealed a positive correlation between IGF2BP2 and SRSF1, with no significant associations for IGF2BP1 or IGF2BP3 ( B, A, and S4B). Knockdown of IGF2BP2 significantly reduced SRSF1 expression at mRNA and protein levels, whereas knockdown of IGF2BP1 or IGF2BP3 had no effect ( C–4H and C). We next tested whether METTL3 influences the binding of IGF2BP2 to SRSF1 mRNA. In IGF2BP2-overexpressing cells, METTL3 knockdown significantly reduced the binding of IGF2BP2 to SRSF1 mRNA, as assessed by RIP-qPCR ( I). Next, we knocked down IGF2BP2 in KG1a cells overexpressing METTL3 and examined the expression of SRSF1 protein. The results showed that IGF2BP2 knockdown significantly abolished the METTL3-mediated upregulation of SRSF1 protein expression ( J). Furthermore, we knocked down METTL3 in KG1a cells overexpressing IGF2BP2 and assessed SRSF1 protein expression. As expected, METTL3 knockdown also abolished the IGF2BP2-mediated increase in SRSF1 protein expression ( K).
 

## O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 modulated SRSF1determines the splicing of MCL-1L
To assess the clinical relevance of SRSF1 in myeloid malignancies, we first analyzed the Blood Spot Database, which revealed that SRSF1 expression is significantly elevated in MDS/AML patients ( A and A). TMG treatment stabilized SRSF1 mRNA in KG1a cells, while OSMI-1 promoted its degradation ( B). Overexpression of METTL3-WT in KG1a/SKM1 cells resulted in an elevation of SRSF1 at both mRNA and protein levels, whereas O-GlcNAcylation-deficient METTL3 mutants did not ( C, B, and S5C). Interestingly, no O-GlcNAc modification was identified on SRSF1 protein ( D, D, and S5E). To understand the functional consequences of this regulatory axis, we performed RNA-seq in KG1a cells treated with TMG or DMSO. Global analysis revealed significant alterations in alternative splicing (AS), predominantly affecting cassette exon inclusion ( E). RNA-seq (TMG versus DMSO) identified differential AS, mainly cassette exons, with seven oncogene targets, including MCL-1 ( F and F). Validation further confirmed AS of MCL-1 among the top targets ( G and G).
 MCL-1 splicing variants encode the antiapoptotic protein MCL-1L and the proapoptotic protein MCL-1S, with MCL-1S lacking parts of the BH1 domain and all of the BH4 domain. Upregulated MCL-1 mRNA was observed in the TCGA database and confirmed in CD34+ BMCs from MDS/AML patients ( H, H, and S5I). METTL3-WT overexpression increased SRSF1, MCL-1L, and p-ERK expression, while METTL3-184/243A reversed these effects ( I). METTL3-WT cells exhibited elevated MCL-1L expression levels and a significant reduction in MCL-1S levels, whereas METTL3-184/243A cells showed no notable differences ( J and J). In KG1a cells overexpressing METTL3-WT, small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown of SRSF1 led to a decrease in MCL-1L expression while increasing MCL-1S expression ( K). This suggests that the splicing isoforms of MCL-1 is highly dependent on O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 regulated SRSF1 ( K).

## Targeting O-GlcNAc level reinforced STM2457 function
To better investigate the potential of therapeutic applications targeting O-GlcNAcylated METTL3, we treated CD34+ BMCs from MDS/AML patients and cell lines with OSMI-1, in combination with STM2457. Due to the cytotoxicity of OSMI-1, a low dose was selected for the combined treatment with STM2457 ( A). Compared to STM2457 alone, the combination of OSMI-1 and STM2457 significantly enhanced apoptosis and inhibited proliferation ( A, 6B, B, and S6C). Furthermore, OSMI-1 combined with STM2457 induced a significant G0-G1 phase arrest ( C and D). Myeloid differentiation markers CD11b and CD14 were upregulated in cells treated with both OSMI-1 and STM2457 ( D–6G). In a murine model where C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with M1-GFP cells to simulate AML, combined treatment with STM2457 and OSMI-1 was administered ( H). By day 35, the combined therapy resulted in a significant reduction in M1 cell counts in the BM, spleen, and peripheral blood, along with a noticeable decrease in spleen size ( I–6L and E). Furthermore, the combination of OSMI-1 and STM2457 significantly extended survival compared to treatment with OSMI-1, or STM2457 alone ( M).
 

## O-GlcNAcylated mimetic peptides impeded the functionality of METTL3
Designing specific peptides that competitively inhibit the O-GlcNAcylation of target proteins without disrupting overall cellular O-GlcNAcylation offers a promising alternative strategy. In this study, we overexpressed METTL3 fragments with O-GlcNAcylation sites at 184/243 or corresponding mutant sequences (amino acids [aa] 177–191 and aa 236–250) in KG1a cells ( A and A). Overexpression of 184S-FLAG and 243S-FLAG suppressed METTL3 O-GlcNAcylation, significantly inhibiting SRSF1 expression and ERK signaling ( B). m6A levels were also reduced in these cells ( C). Furthermore, combining 184S-FLAG or 243S-FLAG overexpression with STM2457 treatment enhanced apoptosis, suppressed proliferation, and induced G0-G1 phase arrest ( D–7F).
 We then synthesized four METTL3 peptides (aa 177–191 and aa 236–250) fused with the cell-penetrating peptide (CPP)-tat for efficient delivery into cells ( G). Treatment of KG1a cells with CPPtat1S and CPPtat2S showed a clear competitive inhibitory effect, suppressing METTL3 and SRSF1 expression ( H) and reducing the m6A levels of KG1a and SKM1 cells ( I). Moreover, combining CPPtat1S or CPPtat2S with STM2457 enhanced apoptosis, inhibited proliferation, and induced G0-G1 phase cell-cycle arrest in CD34+ BMCs from MDS/AML patients and KG1a cells ( J–7L and B–S7D). Simultaneously, CD34+ cells isolated from healthy donors showed no significant changes in apoptosis, proliferation, or cell-cycle progression following treatment with CPPtat1S or CPPtat2S combined with STM2457 ( E–S7G), indicating that the synthetic peptides exhibit no apparent toxicity toward normal CD34+ cells. Furthermore, we established a KG1a cell-derived xenograft model in NSG mice and administered CPPtat1S or CPPtat2S in combination with STM2457. At day 40, the mice were euthanized, and BM and peripheral blood cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis. The combination treatment significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation ( M and H). Concurrently, survival analysis in a separate cohort demonstrated that mice receiving the combination therapy exhibited markedly prolonged survival ( N).

## Discussion
Epigenetics involves modifications in gene expression that do not entail changes to the DNA sequence. These modifications encompass cytosine methylation, alterations to histone proteins, and m6A modifications to RNA, among others. m6A modification has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in influencing cell fate decisions during early hematopoiesis and maintaining the identity and symmetric commitment of HSCs. , m6A RNA marks could remodel the acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cells to macrophages, by decorating the 3′ UTRs of genes associated with ribosomal translational machinery. Highly expressed m6A reader IGF2BP2 in leukemia stem cells promotes the development/maintenance of AML by regulating glutamine metabolism. Demethylase ALKBH5-driven 5′ UTR m6A demethylation precisely adjusts the translation of SF3B1, a highly mutated splicing factor that governs the splicing of key DNA repair and epigenetic regulators crucial for leukemic transformation. Our data showed that METTL3 and responding m6A levels were increased in MDS/AML clonal cells, confirming the important role of dysregulated m6A modifications in the pathogenesis of hematological malignancies.
Recently, increasing evidence has highlighted the interplay of m6A RNA modification with certain PTMs. For instance, the lactylation of METTL3 is indispensable for its ability to capture target RNA, thereby promoting the immunosuppression of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells. The acetylation on METTL3 determines its subcellular localization between the nucleus and the cytosol. A more recent study has revealed the function of O-GlcNAc modifications on various components of m6A methylation machinery. For example, YTHDF1/3 act as a deterrent, preventing their interactions with translational components and inhibiting the delivery of methylated mRNA by YTHDF1/3 to granules. O-GlcNAcylation on YTHDF1 facilitates its cytosolic localization, and O-GlcNAcylation on YTHDC1 enhances homologous recombination-mediated DNA damage repair and promotes cell survival. These data suggest that different statuses of O-GlcNAcylation may result in the different pattern of m6A RNA modification. Here, we found that high O-GlcNAcylation was positively correlated with high m6A RNA modification, while suppressing O-GlcNAcylation resulted in lower m6A RNA modification in MDS/AML. Using glycoproteomics strategy, we found that METTL3 can be O-GlcNAcylated at sites serine 184 and serine 243. Mechanism study further revealed that O-GlcNAc modification influenced the stability of METTL3.
O-GlcNAcylation on METTL3 significantly influenced its targets of m6A modification within the cell. Through RIP-seq analysis, we identified a large number of splicing-related genes, including SRSF1, as METTL3 targets regulated by O-GlcNAcylation. However, subsequent m6A-seq analysis revealed that only SRSF1 exhibited significantly altered m6A modification levels. SRSF1, a known splicing factor, exerts its function by binding in proximity to the 5′ splice site, which typically enhances exon inclusion, while binding near the 3′ splice site facilitates either exon skipping or inclusion. Enrichment of SRSF1 was found in several hematological malignancies. It showed an increasing trend with the progression of myeloma and correlated with worse clinical features and poorer outcomes. , Moreover, SRSF1 levels were maintained in CD34+ chronic myeloid leukemia progenitors by cytokines despite effective BCR-ABL1 inhibition, and increased levels of SRSF1 promote impaired imatinib response. SRSF1 also serves as an anti-apoptotic factor and contributes to leukemogenesis in pediatric ALL patients by cooperating with protein arginine methyltransferase1. Our data suggested that the enhanced expression of SRSF1 can switch MCL-1 into a more oncogenic form, the full-length MCL-1, which is frequently observed in many hematological malignancies and is closely associated with poor prognosis and drug resistance. 
The potential of therapeutic application targeting METTL3 was reported using the small molecules STM2457, which can reduce AML growth and increase AML cell differentiation and apoptosis. Using STM2457 in combination with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 can reinvigorate cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and mediate tumor regression. Several natural compounds have also been designed to target METTL3 and exhibited stability and deeper penetration into the hydrophobic core of METTL3. Based on our findings, we discovered that attenuating O-GlcNAcylation can reinforce the inhibition effect of STM2457. Our designed specific peptide containing the O-GlcNAcylation site of METTL3 could effectively suppress the expression of METTL3 and subsequently reduce SRSF1 expression.
Overall, in this article, we have characterized the O-GlcNAcylation sites of METTL3 and the function of O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 in MDS/AML progression. Our findings show that O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 is essential for m6A modification of SRSF1 to promote oncogenic splice switching of MCL-1 in MDS/AML. Our data suggest that targeting the O-GlcNAcylated METTL3 is a promising alternative strategy for MDS/AML persistence and provides a rationale for future investigations.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
The human hematopoietic cell lines KG1a and SKM1 were cultured and maintained as detailed previously. The mouse-derived AML cell line M1 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Kibbutz Beit-Haemek, Israel), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA). Normal CD34+ cells were cultured in StemSpan SFEM medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) supplemented with the following cytokines (all purchased from STEMCELL Technologies): human interleukin-11 (IL-11) (catalog no. 78025, 100 ng/mL) or human IL-6 (catalog no.78148, 100 ng/mL) and human Flt3 ligand (catalog no. 78009, 100 ng/mL). Unless otherwise specified, the drug treatment conditions used in this study were as follows: 20 μM TMG and 20 μg/mL OSMI-1 for 48 h, 10 μM MG132 for 48 h, and 5 μM STM2457 for 72 h.

## Transfectant construction
The full-length METTL3 and OGT genes were cloned from cDNA of KG1a cells. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using fusion PCR. The WT and the site-specific mutant METTL3 were individually inserted into the pLVX-AcGFP-N1 plasmids (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The MLL-AF9 plasmid was generously provided by Professor Guangyao Kong (Xi’an Jiaotong University). Lentiviral vectors were constructed and packaged into HEK-293T cells using the packaging systems pMD2.G and psPAX2, along with polyethylenimine MAX (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA). Stable transfectants were established by infecting cells with the lentivirus and selecting them using puromycin.

## Patient samples
BM aspirates were collected from MDS/AML patients or HD at the Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital ( ). Mononuclear cells were separated by Ficoll-Hypaque gradient centrifugation. CD34+ cells were isolated by magnetic-activated cell sorting per the manufacturer’s protocol (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and cultured in medium from STEMCELL Technologies. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, adhering to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the research ethics committee of Northwest University.

## RIP assay and RIP-seq
RIP assay (RIPA) was performed using the P0102 kit (Geneseed, Guangzhou, China). Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer, and the resulting supernatant was incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads conjugated with anti-METTL3 antibodies (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) for 4 h. Subsequently, the immunoprecipitation was washed, treated with Proteinase K (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (ABclonal). The genes bound to METTL3 were determined by qRT-PCR. For RIP-seq, rRNA was removed using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), and a cDNA library was prepared with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Preparation Kit (New England Biolabs), followed by sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq platform, provided by Haplox-Bio Technology (Shenzhen, China).

## m6A-seq
Total RNA from TMG- or OSMI-1-treated KG1a cells was isolated, purified, and subjected to m6A RIP. Eluted m6A-containing fragments and untreated input fragments were converted to the final cDNA library using the deoxyuridine triphosphate method. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China).

## Mouse model
Animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines under the Animal Care and Use Committee at Northwest University. To establish an MDS murine model, Mx1-Cre, LSL-NrasG12D mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of poly(I:C) (250 μg; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) at 21 days of age. After developing MDS-like symptoms, the mice were euthanized, and BM aspirates were collected from femurs and tibias. Mononuclear cells were isolated using a mouse BM sample separation kit (TBD, Tianjin, China) and cultured in a complete mouse BM HSC medium (Procell Life Science&Technology, Wuhan, China). The Mx1-Cre, OGTfl/fl mice were constructed using a similar protocol. Mononuclear cells from the femurs of 6-week-old OGTfl/fl and Mx1-Cre, OGTfl/fl mice were isolated post-euthanasia and purified using cKit+ magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec).
In an in vivo murine AML model, C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were subjected to a sublethal irradiation dose of 4.5 Gy prior to cell injection, followed by intravenous injection of 5 × 106 M1-GFP cells via the tail vein. One week post-injection, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): PBS, OSMI-1, STM2457, and OSMI-1/STM2457. OSMI-1 (1 mg/kg, MedChemExpress) was intravenously administered, while STM2457 (50 mg/kg, MedChemExpress) was intraperitoneally given every other day for 3 weeks. On day 35, peripheral blood was collected to determine the proportion of M1-GFP cells by flow cytometry. The same batch of treated mice was used to record survival time.
For the NOD SCID gamma (NSG) mouse experiment, NSG mice were preconditioned with 2 Gy X-ray irradiation. Subsequently, 5 × 106 KG1a cells were injected via the tail vein. One week post-injection, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5 per group): PBS, STM2457, CPPtat1A + STM2457, CPPtat1S + STM2457, CPPtat2A + STM2457, and CPPtat2S + STM2457. Peptides (1.5 mg/kg) were administered via tail vein injection along with STM2457 (50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection, both every other day for 3 weeks. On day 40, peripheral blood was collected to assess the proportion of hCD45+ cells by flow cytometry. Survival time was recorded using the same cohort of treated mice.

## Western blotting analysis
Protein samples were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis, and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with a 3% (w/v) solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) in TBS-T (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 8.0) for 1 h. Following this, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, rinsed with TBS-T, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies (dilution: 1:5,000; Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China).

## Analysis of mRNA m6A methylation by dot-blotting assay
The dot-blotting method of Du et al. was followed, with minor modifications. Briefly, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (ABclonal), and its concentration was determined with a DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE). The RNA was denatured at 95°C for 5 min and immediately cooled on ice. Aliquots of RNA (200–800 ng) were then spotted onto positively charged nylon membranes (Beyotime) and air-dried for 5 min. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with an anti-m6A primary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. Signals were visualized using ECL, and equal RNA loading was verified by methylene blue staining.

## In vitro O-GlcNAcylation and analysis of O-GlcNAc sites on METTL3
The in vitro O-GlcNAcylation reaction was carried out following established procedures. Briefly, METTL3 gene fused with a 6× His-tag in the pET28a plasmid and OGT gene carrying a glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tag in the pEGX-4T1 plasmid were separately overexpressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using a commercial purification kit (Beyotime). The overexpressed proteins included 10 μg His-tagged METTL3, 20 μg GST-tagged OGT, and 2.5 mM UDP-GlcNAc (provided by Professor Junqiang Fang, Shandong University, China). These proteins were combined in a 100-μL reaction buffer containing 125 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.4. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 6 h. O-GlcNAc-modified METTL3 underwent SDS-PAGE separation and a series of treatments, including dehydration, reduction, alkylation, and trypsin digestion. The resulting peptides were separated using a PepMap C18 nanocolumn on the Thermo Fisher U3000 RSLCnano system (QE HF-X, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). The O-GlcNAcylation sites were analyzed using pFind software. 

## Half-life of METTL3 expression
Following various treatments, MDS/AML cells were exposed to 50 μM cycloheximide (MedChemExpress) to suppress protein synthesis and then collected at designated time intervals. Subsequently, cell was lysed by RIPA buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE. METTL3 expressions were analyzed by western blotting as described above.

## coIP
The coIP assay was carried out in accordance with established procedures. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with METTL3 or hemagglutinin (HA) antibodies, and subsequently exposed to protein A/G agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) for 12 h at 4°C. The agarose was thoroughly washed with PBS and collected through centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 5 min. The samples were boiled in 1× SDS loading buffer for 10 min and subjected to western blotting.

## Cell differentiation, cell proliferation, and cell cycle
For cell differentiation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by blocking with 3% BSA solution at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, cells were incubated with CD14-APC (ABclonal) and CD11b-FITC (ABclonal) antibodies at 37°C in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the cells were diluted with the appropriate buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.
For cell proliferation, cells were labeled using the 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) Alexa Fluor 647 kit (catalog no. KGA334-50, Keygen, Jiangsu, China) and analyzed by flow cytometry (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, CA).
For cell-cycle assessment, EdU-labeled cells were treated with the PI/RNase kit (Beyotime) for 30 min and then subjected to flow cytometry.

## Apoptosis analysis
The cell apoptosis assay was performed using the annexin V/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
HD and MDS/AML patients’ mononuclear cell lysates (10 μg/mL) in coating buffer were dispensed into each well of a microtiter plate and incubated at 4°C overnight. Afterward, the coating solution was aspirated, and the plate was washed three times with 200 μL 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS. Subsequently, the anti-OGT antibody, anti-METTL3 antibody, anti-O-GlcNAc antibody, and anti-m6A antibody were added and incubated overnight at 4°C. Following the incubation with the secondary antibody conjugated with HRP at 37°C for 1 h, 100 μL tetramethylbenzidine (Beyotime) was introduced, and the intensity of the chromogenic reaction was measured at 490 nm using a plate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

## Confocal microscopic analysis
KG1a and SKM1 cells were seeded onto confocal microscopy plates (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) and cultured overnight. The cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 (MedChemExpress) in PBS for 30 min. The cells were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 2 h and incubated with METTL3 primary antibody overnight. The next day, a secondary rabbit antibody was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei and cytoskeleton were stained with DAPI and phalloidin (Abcam), respectively, for 15 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscopy system (model TCS SP8, Leica, Mannheim, Germany).

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted utilizing TRIzol, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Amplification and detection procedures were conducted using the Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Cwbiotech) in conjunction with the specific primers ( ) on the Gentier 48R System (Tianlong Technology, Xi’an, China). The observed copy numbers of the target genes were normalized against the internal glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase and quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. 

## mRNA stability
KG1a cells were treated with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (Selleck, Houston, TX) for 0, 2, 4, and 8 h, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed to analyze mRNA levels. The half-life (t1/2) of target mRNA was calculated using the following equation, as previously described: t1/2 = In 2/kdecay. 

## meRIP quantitative real-time PCR analysis
The poly(A)-mRNA was initially purified using the Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), with 10% of the RNA reserved as the input control. Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were pre-washed and incubated with 5 μg anti-m6A antibody (Abcam) or rabbit IgG for 2 h at 4°C with rotation. Following bead washing, the antibody-conjugated beads were combined with the purified poly(A)-mRNA for overnight incubation at 4°C. The m6A RNA was subsequently eluted using elution buffer and recovered through ethanol precipitation at −80°C overnight. Further enrichment was determined via qPCR, and the corresponding m6A enrichment in each sample was calculated by normalization to the input. 

## Data analysis
The scRNA-seq data of BM samples from AML patients were obtained from the ABC portal database (dataset: phs000159). This dataset includes expression profiles of 23,839 genes across 87,538 filtered cells. Raw data were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 and further processed using Seurat (version 3.1.1), Harmony (version 0.1.0), Scmap (version 1.4.1), and InferCNV (version 1.2.1). These tools were applied for downstream analysis, including cell clustering and visualization, batch effect correction, cell-type annotation and classification, and copy-number variation analysis. The data analyses for RNA-seq, RIP-seq, and m6A-seq were performed following the methodology described in previous publication. , All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Data were presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using the software program GraphPad Prism version 7.0. Datasets for the two groups were compared by two-tailed Student’s t test. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
