## Background
Dysregulation or abnormality of the programmed cell death (PCD) pathway is closely related to the occurrence and development of many tumors, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Studying the abnormal characteristics of PCD pathway-related molecular markers can provide a basis for prognosis prediction and targeted drug design in AML patients.

## Methods
A total of 1394 genes representing 13 different PCD pathways were examined in AML patients and healthy donors. The upregulated genes were analyzed for their ability to predict overall survival (OS) individually, and these prognostic genes were subsequently combined to construct a PCD-related prognostic signature via an integrated approach consisting of 101 models based on ten machine learning algorithms. RNA transcriptome and clinical data from multiple AML cohorts (TCGA-AML, GSE106291, GSE146173 and Beat AML) were obtained to develop and validate the AML prognostic model.

## Results
A total of 214 upregulated PCD-related genes were identified in AML patients, 39 of which were proven to be prognostic genes in the training cohort. On the basis of the average C-index and number of model genes identified from the machine learning combinations, a PCD index was developed and validated for predicting AML OS. A prognostic nomogram was then generated and validated on the basis of the PCD index, age and ELN risk stratification in the Beat AML cohort and the GSE146173 cohort, revealing satisfactory predictive power (AUC values ≥ 0.7). With different mutation patterns, a higher PCD index was associated with a worse OS. The PCD index was significantly related to higher scores for immunosuppressive cells and mature leukemia cell subtypes. As the gene most closely related to the PCD index, the expression of SMAD3 was further validated in vitro. AML cells harboring KMT2A rearrangements were more sensitive to the SMAD3 inhibitor SIS3, and the expression of the autophagy-related molecular marker LC3 was increased in KMT2A-rearranged cell lines after SIS3 monotherapy and combined treatment.

## Conclusion
The PCD index and SMAD3 gene expression levels have potential prognostic value and can be used in targeted therapy for AML, and these findings can lead to the development of effective strategies for the combined treatment of high-risk AML patients.

## Introduction
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematopoietic tumor caused by the abnormal proliferation and differentiation of myeloid cells. AML has different subtypes due to its wide range of cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities. Chemotherapy as the standard treatment for AML [ , ], about 10–40% of newly diagnosed AML patients cannot achieve complete remission (CR) [ ], and approximately 50% of AML patients who achieve remission can relapse within 2–3 years after initial treatment [ ]. Moreover, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is also commonly used for treating AML, but many patients cannot tolerate transplantation because of the lack of suitable donors or treatment complications, and some patients still relapse after transplantation. In addition, although chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapy has made some progress in AML, such as CD33, CD123 and many new potential targets (CLL1, FLT3, NKG2D, Lewis Y, CD44v6, CD38, CD7, and LILRB4) [ – ], there are still challenges, including very limited clinical data and the lack of truly specific AML surface antigens to avoid the killing of normal hematopoietic cells.
In recent years, with increasing basic research, especially the use of large-scale genomic analysis, the development of AML diagnosis, risk stratification and targeted therapy has advanced. For example, FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (Flt3) mutations occur in approximately 30% of newly diagnosed AML patients (20-25% with FLT3-ITD and 5-10% with FLT3-TKD) [ ] and are associated with an increased risk of relapse and worse survival. Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 and 2 (IDH1 and IDH2) mutations occur in approximately 5-15% and 10-15% of newly diagnosed AMLs, respectively [ ], and can cause DNA and histone hypermethylation, leading to the blockade of cell differentiation and the development of AML. In addition, the increased expression of BCL-2 in AML can lead to decreased sensitivity to chemotherapy and an increased recurrence rate [ ]. Therefore, these genetic variants play key roles in diagnostic algorithms, risk stratification, and first-line therapy in AML patients and could lead to potential targets for drug development. For some high-risk relapsed/refractory (R/R) AML patients who are not eligible for allo-HSCT or intensive chemotherapy, targeted therapy can be administered to different molecularly defined subgroups. In recent years, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved gilteritinib, ivosidenib and enasidenib for the treatment of R/R AML patients with FLT3, IDH1 and IDH2 mutations, respectively, and these inhibitors have shown promising clinical efficacy as first-line treatments [ – ]. The combination of the selective BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax and azacitidine has also been proven to effectively improve the objective response rate (ORR), CR rate and median overall survival (OS) of R/R AML patients [ ]. However, the existing markers are far from meeting clinical needs, and properly evaluating and classifying AML patients to select the appropriate treatment plan is still a major problem. Therefore, further studies on potential biomarkers of AML are urgently needed to incorporate additional prognostic factors, such as the European Leukemia Network (ELN) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), into clinical guidelines to improve the risk stratification model [ ] and provide potential targets and a theoretical basis for drug design and clinical treatment.
Programmed cell death (PCD) refers to the autonomous and orderly death of cells under the control of genes. It is an important regulatory mechanism for the body to stabilize the internal environment and balance the number of cells in the physiological and pathological environment. It relies on specific molecular mechanisms and can be regulated by pharmacological or genetic interventions. The induction of PCD disorders or abnormalities is closely related to the occurrence of a variety of diseases, such as neurological, metabolic, autoimmune, and infectious diseases and cancer [ ], indicating broad application prospects in clinical practice. Studies have shown that different PCD lethal subprograms exist during cancer initiation, progression, and treatment response [ ]. In the early stage of the disease, cancer cells may have mutations that destroy the PCD pathway and produce anticancer treatment properties. Therefore, escape from PCD is an important marker of cancer occurrence and development. Through single or combined application of the PCD signaling pathway, multiple targeted drugs can be developed to increase the therapeutic response of specific cancer types. Focusing on the combination of different PCD pathways may be a new direction for cancer therapy in the future.
At present, there are at least 13 known types of PCD, including common types such as apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis. The typical process of apoptosis involves a series of hydrolysis, caspase activation and signal transduction processes; thus, this process is also called the caspase-dependent apoptosis pathway [ ]. Apoptosis is the most intensively studied type of PCD. AML cells frequently develop defects in apoptosis, leading to treatment resistance [ ]. Therefore, increasing the apoptotic sensitivity of leukemic cells is a valuable therapeutic strategy [ ]. Necroptosis is often triggered by Toll-like receptor activation, ROS accumulation in mitochondria, tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα), etc. PCD in necrosis is controlled by a unique caspase-independent signaling pathway and has necrosis-like morphological characteristics [ ]. Currently, several studies have demonstrated the impact of necroptosis on the occurrence, progression, and metastasis of various types of cancer [ , ]. In addition, the downregulation of key molecules mediating necroptosis is associated with poor clinical prognosis in AML [ , ]. Pyroptosis is often associated with innate immune responses, such as pathogen invasion, and its occurrence depends on the formation of plasma membrane pores by gasdermin family proteins [ , ]. Pyroptosis has a strong regulatory effect on the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of cancer cells and ultimately affects the prognosis of patients [ , ]. In addition, there is a strong correlation between pyroptosis and antileukemia therapy [ , ]. Ferroptosis is a type of PCD caused by iron-dependent oxidative damage without the action of caspases. It is characterized by disturbed intracellular iron ion flow and significantly increased ROS and lipid peroxide levels [ ]. Studies have shown that AML cells can exhibit increased ferroptosis susceptibility factors, such as reactive oxygen species production and active iron uptake [ , ], and abnormal expression of genes regulating ferroptosis, such as GPX4 [ , ], thus providing ideas for improving the sensitivity of leukemia treatment by regulating ferroptosis. In addition, a variety of new cell death pathways, including cuproptosis and disulfidsis, have been discovered in recent years. The relationships of these genes with AML have also been revealed. For example, controlling the distribution of copper in mitochondria can regulate the self-renewal of AML stem cells [ ], which provides ideas for the treatment of AML with copper ionophores and copper chelating agent-induced cuproptosis. The growth and proliferation of AML cells also depend on increased active glucose metabolism [ ], so inducing disulfidptosis by glucose starvation also has potential value in the treatment of AML.
Moreover, many PCD-related genes are associated with the treatment and prognosis of AML. For example, pyroptosis is strongly correlated with leukemia therapy, and small-molecule inhibitors of the serine dipeptidases DPP8 and DPP9 have been found to induce procaspase-1-dependent pyroptosis in human myeloid cells and primary AML samples [ ]. Decreased levels of the autophagy-related genes Beclin-1 and p62 are related to poor prognosis in AML patients, suggesting that these genes could be used as biomarkers for hematological tumors [ ]. The all-trans retinoic acid derivative 4-amino-2-trifluoromethyl-phenyl retinate can induce ferroptosis by activating the ROS/autophagy pathway, thus exerting a targeted anticancer effect on AML cells [ ]. The overexpression of the antiapoptotic gene BCL-2 is an important factor that causes resistance to intensive chemotherapy, and a combination of venetoclax and taselisib can induce BAX-dependent mitochondrial apoptosis in AML cells by targeting BCL-2 and PI3K, resulting in potent anti-AML activity and resistance to multiple forms of venetoclax resistance [ ]. Therefore, the study of the crosstalk between different PCD pathways and the abnormal characterization of their molecular markers in AML can provide some basis for prognosis prediction and targeted drug design, offering a new perspective for the diagnosis and treatment of AML in the future.
In this study, we obtained multiple AML cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Beat AML and subsequently identified 14 PCD index genes associated with AML prognosis and identified SMAD3 as a potential therapeutic target for KMT2A-rearranged AML, which provides a valuable basis for the prognosis and targeted therapy of AML.

## Materials and methods

## Data acquisition and processing
An overview of this study is presented in Fig.  .

 

All the data sources are detailed in Supplementary Table , including identifiers and data processing. The expressed and clinical profiles of AML patients were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (GSE13159, GSE106291, and GSE146173), Beat AML [ , ], and UCSC Xena (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). To provide more reliable expression analysis between AML patients and healthy controls (HCs), we downloaded a cohort from TCGA-TARGET-GTEx (Genotype-Tissue Expression Project) from the UCSC Xena project, which was processed together via the Toil method, normalized with RSEM TPM (transcripts per million) and log2 transformed. We first extracted the expression profiles of adult AML patients and HCs from this cohort to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), then, from GSE13159, used 74 HC samples, 73 of which were bone marrow (BM) samples, to further confirm the DEGs by intersection. The GDC TCGA-AML cohort was downloaded to scan PCD genes associated with AML prognosis and fit a predictive model (n = 150); then, we validated the predictive model in three other AML cohorts, GSE106291 (n = 250), GSE146173 (n = 246), and Beat AML (n = 440). The inclusion criteria for Beat AML patients were RNA-seq data available at initial diagnosis as well as complete survival information. A total of 1394 PCD-related genes (Table ), excluding duplicate genes, were collected according to previous studies; these genes exhibited 13 types of PCD patterns [ , ].

## Identification of PCD-related genes upregulated in AML
Overexpressed genes in tumors often play a cancer-promoting role. To determine which PCD-related genes were upregulated in AML, the transcriptional levels were compared between adult AML patients (n = 173) and healthy blood HCs (GTEx, n = 337) from the TCGA-TARGET-GTEx cohort. The values of log2-transformed fold change (log2FC) ranged from − 8.6 to 6.9, and upregulated expression genes (UEGs) were defined as those with log2FC > 2.0 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Table ). Moreover, the GSE13159 dataset, which included expression profiles of many kinds of hematological malignancies and HCs, was also downloaded to confirm the UEGs in AML patients (log2FC > 0 and adjusted P < 0.05), with log2FC values ranging from − 0.5 to 0.3 (Table ). The intersection of UEGs with PCD-related genes was deemed to lead to the upregulation of PCD-related genes in AML (n = 214). The UEGs were identified via the “limma” package [ ].

## Development of a PCD-related signature on the basis of integrative approaches
The PCD-related signature was generated for predicting OS in AML patients as follows: (1) Among the 214 overexpressed PCD genes, univariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic PCD-related genes for OS in the training cohort (TCGA-AML) (n = 39, P < 0.05). (2) On the basis of leave‒one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), a total of 101 algorithms were applied to these 39 prognostic genes to fit prognostic models for predicting OS in the training cohort. The integrative method consists of ten machine learning algorithms [ ], including Lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator), RSF (random survival forest), Ridge, stepwise Cox, Enet (elastic network), CoxBoost, plsRcox (partial least squares regression for Cox), SuperPC (supervised principal components), survivalSVM (survival support vector machine), and GBM (generalized boosted regression modeling). (3) All the models were validated in three independent AML datasets (Beat AML, GSE106291, and GSE146173). (4) We then calculated the C-index across all three validation sets, which reflected the discriminatory ability of each model. When the average C-index was greater and there were fewer PCD genes, the model was considered to be optimal. The PCD index was subsequently calculated via the optimal model.

## Validation of the PCD-related signature and nomogram construction
To validate the PCD-related signature in AML, both the training and validation sets were divided into high-PCD and low-PCD indices according to the median value of each cohort’s PCD index. Survival analyses were implemented to compare the differences between AML patients with high and low PCD indices. Multivariate Cox regression models were also used to confirm the independent prognostic indicators of the PCD index in AML patients.
The two AML cohorts (Beat AML and GSE146173) both included age and ELN risk stratification, which are major factors affecting AML prognosis. After combining the PCD index with these two variables, a prognostic nomogram was developed on the basis of the Beat AML cohort and validated in the GSE146173 cohort through the “rms” package. The performance of the nomogram was checked and visualized through calibration curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). To quantify the prediction accuracy of the nomogram, the areas under the time-dependent ROC curves (AUCs) were calculated and visualized via the R package “timeROC”. A model with an AUC value of 0.7–0.8 is considered acceptable, whereas a value greater than 0.8 is considered favorable. If the AUC values are greater than 0.7 in both the training and verification sets, the model represents satisfactory predictive power.

## Functional analysis and mutational spectrum
Enrichment analysis of upregulated PCD-related genes was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was subsequently performed to determine the main items for which the high PCD index was associated with two gene sets (c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1.symbols and h.all.v7.5.1.symbols [hallmarks]). The functional analyses were performed via the R package “clusterProfiler”. As recommended, |NES| (normalized enrichment score) > 1.0 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance in the GSEA.
The differences at the genomic level may help to further understand the biological characteristics of AML patients. In the Beat AML cohort, patients were divided into high- and low-PCD indices according to the median value of the PCD indices. The mutational profiles of 421 of the 440 AML patients were acquired for mutational analysis. Waterfall plots were created to present the occurrence of frequent gene mutations, and a forest plot with Fisher’s exact test was used to demonstrate the differences in mutated genes between high- and low-PCD indices via the “maftools” package.

## Immune patterns and leukemia cell types
The relationships between the PCD index and 14 PCD-related genes and the scores of other different cells were subsequently evaluated. On the basis of gene expression in the Beat AML cohort (n = 440), scores of different immune cells were calculated via the xCell and CIBERSORT algorithms, and six molecular signatures reflecting differential expression patterns of AML [ ] were downloaded to quantify the scores of AML individuals via the ssGSEA algorithm. Spearman’s tests were used to determine correlations, and the results were visualized via a heatmap. All the analyses were performed through the R packages xCell, CIBERSORT, GSVA, psych, scico, and pheatmap.

## Cell culture
AML line cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were cultured in 90% RPMI 1640 medium (C11875500BT, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ST30-3302, PAN, Germany), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in PBS (C0221A, Beyotime, China).

## Western blot
The cells were lysed to extract total protein via RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with PMSF (ST2573-5 g, Beyotime, China). The concentrations of the extracted proteins were quantified via a enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). The samples were denatured by boiling in a water bath at 95 °C for 5 min. Following incubation with primary antibodies (1:500 anti-SMAD3 antibody (9523, Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐P-SMAD3 antibody (9520T, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐LC3 I/II antibody (12741 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐Caspase-3 antibody (14220 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐Cleaved Caspase-3 antibody (9664 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐PARP antibody (9532 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐cleaved PARP antibody (5625 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐P-MLKL antibody (91689 S, Cell Signaling, USA), 1:500 anti‐cleaved GasderminD antibody (36425, Signaling, USA) and 1:10000 anti‐GAPDH antibody (EPR16891, Abcam, UK)), the membranes were incubated with 1:1000 HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody against rabbits (A0208, Beyotime, China) and mice (A0216, Beyotime, China). The bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (Odyssey) detection system. The protein expression levels detected by Western blotting were quantified via ImageJ software. All the experiments were performed three times.

## Cell viability assay
When the cells were in the logarithmic growth period, they were seeded in 96-well plates (TC-treated, 3599, Corning, USA) at a density of 8,000 cells per well. After treatment with SIS3 (0, 10 µM) (T3636, 521984-48-5, TargetMol, China), doxorubicin (70 nM) (T1456, 23214-92-8, Target Mol, China), or cytarabine (Ara-c, 1 µM) (T1272, 147-94-4, Target Mol, China) at 37 °C for 72 h, the cells were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (WK464, Dojindo, JAP) for 2 h at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured via a microplate reader at 450 nm, and the OD values are reported as the means ± SDs. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

## Statistical analysis
All the statistical analyses were performed via R software (version 4.2.2). Categorical variables were compared via Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test, while continuous variables were assessed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or T test. Correlations between continuous variables were tested via the Pearson or Spearman method. K‒M and Cox regression analyses were performed via the survival and survminer packages. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## Recognition of PCD genes overexpressed in AML
After duplicate genes were removed, a total of 214 PCD genes were overexpressed in AML (Fig.  A), including genes related to apoptosis (n = 89), autophagy (n = 68), ferroptosis (n = 36), lysosome-dependent cell death (n = 22), necroptosis (n = 12), cuproptosis (n = 7), disulfidptosis (n = 7), pyroptosis (n = 4), oxeiptosis (n = 2) and entotic cell death (n = 1) (Fig.  B). The heatmap confirmed that these 214 PCD genes were upregulated in AML (Fig.  C).

 

GO analysis revealed that these overexpressed genes were associated with various pathways associated with programmed cell death (Fig.  D-E), such as autophagy, apoptosis, necroptosis, and the lysosome. Moreover, KEGG analysis revealed that the enrichment of these genes was related to cell senescence; the cell cycle; and the PI3K-Akt, mTOR, p53, and AMPK signaling pathways. In addition, these genes are related to various cancers, including AML, colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma.

## Establishment of a PCD-related signature in AML
Through univariate Cox regression, 39 of 214 PCD-related genes were identified as prognostic genes in the training cohort (TCGA-AML) (Table ). Using these 39 PCD genes, a machine learning-based integrated approach was subsequently applied to determine the PCD index for predicting AML OS. Then, an integrated approach consisting of 101 models was fitted, and the C-index of each model is presented in Figure . Notably, the four models with the highest average C-index values were StepCox [forward], Enet [alpha = 0.1], Enet [alpha = 0.4] and CoxBoost + StepCox [forward]. There were 39, 30, 20 and 14 PCD genes included in these four models, respectively (Fig.  A). Finally, we developed a predictive model via the CoxBoost + StepCox [forward] method. To investigate the biological processes and functions associated with the PCD signature, GSEA was performed for all the training and validation cohorts, and the intersecting results were regarded as the enriched items. KEGG analyses (Fig.  B-C) revealed that the high PCD index was associated mainly with MAPK and Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and multiple immune or inflammatory pathways, such as those involving chemokines, B-cell receptors, and interactions between cytokines and their receptors. For the hallmark gene sets (Fig.  D-E), patients with a high PCD index were enriched in the apoptosis, hypoxia, interferon gamma or alpha response, and JAK-STAT3 (IL-6), p53 and KRAS signaling pathways but negatively related to the G2/M checkpoint, MYC and E2F targets.

## Relationships between the PCD index and clinical characteristics
In the Beat AML cohort, we explored the distribution of the PCD index in patients according to age, sex, de novo AML status, ELN risk stratification, survival status and response after induction therapy (Fig.  A-F). A higher PCD index was detected in patients with secondary AML (Fig.  C), intermediate and adverse risk according to ELN stratification (Fig.  D), death during follow-up (Fig.  E), and refractory AML after induction therapy (Fig.  F). Similarly, in the other two cohorts, GSE106291 and GSE146173, refractory AML was also significantly associated with a higher PCD index (Fig.  G-H).

 

## Validating the prognostic value of the PCD-related signature
With an increase in the PCD index, the mortality risk increased, and the survival time decreased (Fig.  A-D). All four AML cohorts (TCGA-AML, GSE106291, GSE146173 and Beat AML) were divided into high- and low-PCD-index groups on the basis of the median value of each cohort’s PCD index. A poor prognosis was observed in patients with a high PCD index across all four AML cohorts (Fig.  E-H). Multivariate Cox analysis also confirmed that the PCD index was an independent prognostic indicator in different AML cohorts (Fig.  I) (TCGA-AML, HR = 4.30, P < 0.001; GSE106291, HR = 1.44, P = 0.028; GSE146173, HR = 1.47, P = 0.023; and Beat AML, HR = 1.49, P = 0.003).

 

## Development of a nomogram for predicting AML survival
As shown in the multivariable analysis, the PCD index, ELN risk and age were confirmed to be independent risk factors for OS in both the Beat AML and GSE146173 cohorts (Fig.  I). These two cohorts had identical clinical variables. Hence, a nomogram was developed to predict OS in the Beat cohort (Fig.  A). The risk score of each patient was calculated via the prognostic nomogram. Survival analysis revealed an unfavorable OS in patients with high nomogram risk scores (Fig.  B). On the basis of the nomogram, some patients could be reclassified by risk stratification (Fig.  C). The calibration curves confirmed the favorable predictive ability of the prognostic nomogram (Fig.  D). Moreover, ROC curves revealed a favorable discriminatory ability for the prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in the Beat AML cohort (Fig.  E), with AUCs ranging from 0.818 to 0.855. In addition, DCA suggested that the nomogram model offered greater benefits than the other predictors did (Fig.  F). Notably, similar results were also verified in the GSE146173 cohort (Fig.  G-L), which suggested acceptable discrimination of the nomogram in the validation set, with AUCs ranging from 0.692 to 0.766.

 

## Mutational, immune patterns and leukemia cell types
Mutational analysis revealed a different pattern between patients with a low PCD index (Fig.  A) and those with a high PCD index (Fig.  B). Mutations in CEBPA (mainly frameshift mutations) and GATA2 (mainly missense mutations) frequently occurred in the low-PCD index subgroup, whereas patients with a high PCD index presented more mutations in RUNX1 (mainly frameshift and point mutations) and FLT3 (mainly frame insertions and point mutations), indicating that different mutation types in RUNX1 and GATA2 may be correlated with differential prognoses (Fig.  C).

 

We further elucidated the relationship between the PCD index and immune cell scores. According to the xCell algorithm (Fig.  A), the PCD index was positively associated with monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), T regulatory cells (Tregs), and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) but negatively related to megakaryocytic-erythroid progenitors (MEPs), multipotent blood progenitors (MPPs), granulocyte‒monocyte progenitors (GMPs), and common myeloid progenitors (CMPs). The CIBERSORT results (Fig.  B) also confirmed the correlation of the PCD index with monocytes, macrophages (M2), and Tregs.

 

Recently, six types of AML cells were generated via single-cell sequencing [ ], which can reveal differential expression patterns. We also evaluated the associations between the PCD index and different scores of six leukemia types in the Beat cohort (Fig.  C). The results demonstrated that the PCD index was positively related to the scores of conventional dendritic cell (cDC), monocyte-like and hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)-like subtypes but negatively associated with the scores of promono-, progenitor-like, and GMP-like subtypes. Therefore, a high PCD index is mostly associated with mature AML subtypes. Among the 14 signature genes, cluster analyses revealed that SMAD3 was most closely related to the PCD index, which suggests that SMAD3 plays an important role in the PCD-related signature.

## Verification of SMAD3 in AML cell lines
The results of the correlation analysis further revealed that 6 of the 14 signature genes, namely, SMAD3 (R = 0.544), CCDC6 (R = 0.339), ARL6IP5 (R = 0.227), RNF41 (R = 0.219), ACSF2 (R = 0.214), and PDLIM1 (R = 0.166), were positively associated with the PCD index (Fig.  A). Then, we checked the distribution of the PCD index in Beat AML patients with different cytogenetic backgrounds (excluding acute promyelocytic leukemia). Patients with RUNX1-RUNXT1 rearrangements had a lower PCD index, whereas those harboring KMT2A or GATA2-MECOM rearrangements had a greater PCD index (Fig.  B). Interestingly, the expression patterns of SMAD3 and CCDC6 exhibited trends similar to those of the PCD index across different cytogenetic backgrounds (Fig.  E and H). Both univariate (Figure ) and multivariate analyses (Table ) confirmed that high SMAD3 expression was associated with poor AML OS in different AML cohorts (excluding GSE106291).

 

On the basis of the above results, we then verified the expression of SMAD3 in vitro. A greater expression level of phosphorylated-SMAD3/SMAD3 was also confirmed in AML cell lines with a KMT2A rearrangement genetic background (Fig.  A-C). Consistent with these findings, cells harboring KMT2A rearrangements were more sensitive to a SMAD3 inhibitor (SIS3), especially when combined with Ara-C and doxorubicin in vitro (Fig.  D-F). In addition, the expression of the autophagy-related molecular marker LC3 was increased in KMT2A-rearranged cell lines treated with the SMAD3 inhibitor (SIS3) alone or in combination, suggesting that increased treatment sensitivity was related to the involvement of autophagy (Fig.  G-H).

 

## Discussion
In our study, we identified 214 PCD-related genes upregulated in AML and developed a PCD index to predict AML prognosis via an integrated approach. Combined with the PCD index, age, and ELN risk stratification, a prognostic nomogram was generated and validated, which showed satisfactory predictive power. Moreover, the PCD index, as well as SMAD3 expression, was significantly related to the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells and mature leukemia cell subtypes. When interpreting these results, more attention should be paid on the process of gene screening. Even so, the PCD index and SMAD3 gene expression levels have potential value in the prognostic evaluation and targeted therapy of AML.
Dysregulation or dysfunction of the cellular PCD pathway is closely related to the pathogenesis of many human diseases, including tumors [ ]. At present, more than a dozen cell death pathways have been identified, among which the dysregulation of various PCD pathways, such as the apoptosis, pyroptosis and autophagy pathways, may be involved in the initiation, progression and response to chemotherapy in AML [ , ]. However, these different types of PCD have not been systematically studied in AML patients. Here, we identified highly expressed PCD-related genes in AML and revealed that these overexpressed genes were enriched in many PCD pathways, such as the autophagy, apoptosis, necroptosis, and lysosome pathways, as well as in the cell senescence, cell cycle, and the PI3K-Akt, mTOR, P53 and AMPK signaling pathways. Previous studies have shown that the apoptosis-related genes MCL-1, BCL(x)L and BCL(w) drive the occurrence of leukemia [ – ]. The pyroptosis-related gene caspase-1 is highly expressed in AML and is associated with a lower OS rate [ , ]. Changes in the autophagy-related genes ATGs are also related to the occurrence and development of AML [ ]. Dramatic changes in the number and overall function of lysosomes, such as increased numbers, expansion of lysosomal compartments, and changes in sphingomyelin, are closely related to AML transformation [ , ] and may lead to AML chemotherapy resistance [ ]. In addition, the crosstalk between PI3K-Akt, mTOR, P53 and other signaling pathways can regulate some types of PCD. For example, P53 can inhibit the transcription of antiapoptotic genes such as BCL-xl and is often mutated in AML, which is related to poor prognosis [ ]. Targeting the PI3K/mTOR pathway further inhibits MCL-1 translation and expression through dephosphorylation of GSK3α/β [ ] and can also increase the anti-AML activity of BCL-2 inhibitors [ ]. Therefore, PCD-related features could help to predict OS and/or clinical outcome in AML patients.
In this study, we continued to analyze the ability of upregulated genes to predict AML OS through developing a PCD index on the basis of integrative approaches. The results revealed that a high PCD index was mainly associated with MAPK and TLR signal transduction as well as a variety of immune and inflammatory pathways. Moreover, patients with a high PCD index were also enriched in the pathways of apoptosis, hypoxia, interferon gamma or alpha response, JAK-STAT3 (IL-6), P53 and KRAS signal transduction. Studies have shown that damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) expressed by tumor cells can stimulate TLRs in immune cells, leading to chronic inflammation in the tumor microenvironment. These events are important factors in the occurrence and development of tumors [ ]. TLR2 signaling can activate MAPK in tumor cells and further produce IL-6 to promote the proliferation of tumor cells [ ]. In addition, the expression of TLRs in a variety of tumor cells is associated with disease progression, and the activation of TLR signaling has been confirmed to inhibit apoptosis and promote tumor growth and immune escape [ , ]. Furthermore, abnormal fusion gene expression is an important indicator in the process of diagnosing, treating and determining the prognosis of AML. Currently, a risk stratification system has been established for AML patients on the basis of karyotype and genetic abnormalities, revealing the relationships between different genotypes and AML prognosis. The present study revealed that the PCD indices of AML patients with different genotypes were inconsistent. A high PCD index was observed mainly in patients with intermediate and adverse risk, secondary AML and refractory AML after induction therapy. These findings suggest that the PCD index is strongly correlated with the current prognostic stratification and can distinguish primary/secondary disease and chemotherapy resistance/sensitivity groups. Moreover, a nomogram consisting of the PCD could help to reclassify AML patients according to risk and make clinical decisions. Therefore, the PCD has good value in evaluating AML prognosis and provides clues for the subsequent screening and identification of high-risk AML patients.
In addition, we elucidated the relationship between the PCD index and immune cell scores. Immune cell signature analysis revealed that the PCD index was positively correlated with monocyte, M2 macrophage, DC, MSC and Treg scores. Recent studies have shown that M2-type macrophages promote the survival and proliferation of AML stem cells and regulate their self-renewal as well as drug resistance by regulating the remodeling of the extracellular matrix and promoting the regeneration of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels, thus leading to AML drug resistance and relapse after remission [ ]. MSCs can induce immune tolerance by affecting the recruitment, maturation and function of DCs and weakening their antigen-presenting effects [ ]. Moreover, there are many Treg cells in the microenvironment. Maintaining autoimmune homeostasis and promoting immune tolerance, Tregs are important contributors to the formation of a tumor inhibitory microenvironment, which can suppress antitumor immune responses through a variety of mechanisms. For example, Tregs secrete inhibitory cytokines, including IL-10, TGF-β and IL-35; they can also kill effector cells through granzyme and perforin; moreover, Tregs inhibit the ability of T cells by inducing DC tolerance through stimulatory and inhibitory receptors (CTLA-4 or LAG3). Therefore, Tregs are considered the “culprits” that help cancer cells escape immune surveillance [ ]. So we speculate that AML patients with high PCD indices are often associated with poor prognosis, and the cell composition needs to be validated in the future.
Many key genes and their encoded core proteins are involved in the regulation of cell death via the PCD pathway. Therefore, targeting proteins related to PCD pathway is not only an effective anticancer treatment strategy but also an effective way to increase the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. PCD-related genes have become potential targets and research hotspots for tumor treatment. In our study, among the 14 key genes of the PCD-related signature, 6 genes, including SMAD3, CCDC6, ARL6IP5, RNF41, ACSF2 and PDLIM1, were positively correlated with the PCD index. Many of these 14 key genes have been shown to be related to PCD and treatment response in various cancers. SMAD3 is one of the major transcription factors involved in transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, and its function can be activated by phosphorylation [ ]. SMAD3 plays an important role in cancer progression [ , ]. The overexpression and activation of SMAD3 can promote tumor metastasis and confer a poor prognosis [ , ], which can also mediate acquired cancer resistance [ , ]. SMAD3 silencing can increase DNA damage during radiotherapy by interacting with the MRN complex, thereby sensitizing glioma cells to radiotherapy [ ]. In addition, SMAD3 may be involved in the regulation of apoptosis and autophagy in some cell lines [ , ]. The protein encoded by coiled-coil domain containing 6 (CCDC6, also known as H4/D10S170 or PTC1) is a ubiquitously expressed nuclear and cytoplasmic protein that is frequently rearranged in human malignancies [ ]. Normal CCDC6 is a substrate of ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) and is involved in ATM-mediated cellular responses, such as apoptosis, the DNA damage response, and DNA repair [ – ]. CCDC6 is related to drug resistance in lung cancer [ ], and knockdown of CCDC6 can increase the sensitivity of tumors to the oncolytic virus M1 and lead to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-induced apoptosis [ ]. ADP-ribosylation-like factor 6-interacting protein 5 (ARL6IP5) belongs to the PRAF3 family. Because it has highly functional isopentenylated receptor domain 1, it may play a role in protein transport [ ]. Studies have shown that siRNA ARL6IP5 expression reduces autophagy in cancer cells [ ]. In addition, ARL6IP5 has been widely studied in the field of cancer metastasis, but the specific mechanism is still unclear [ – ]. RNF41 (RING finger protein 41) or Nrdp1 (neuregulin receptor degradation protein-1) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. It mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of various protein targets, such as ErbB3 and BRUCE, in the proteasome or lysosomal pathway [ ]. RNF41 may be involved in the pathology of a variety of diseases, including intracerebral hemorrhage, cancer, and Parkinson’s disease, by regulating apoptosis and autophagy. Moreover, transient overexpression of RNF41 increases apoptosis by promoting the degradation of BRUCE and the activation of caspase 3, thereby reducing the resistance of human glioma cells to chemotherapy drugs [ ]. Acyl-CoA synthetase family member 2 (ACSF2) is the main member of the acyl-CoA synthetase (ACS) family [ ], and ACS-mediated coenzyme A production is the core of cellular lipid metabolism [ ]. Moreover, ACSF2-dependent lipid synthesis pathways can provide specific lipid precursors required for ferroptosis [ ]. Therefore, ACSF2 is also associated with ferroptosis [ ]. It has been proposed that ACSF2 is a driver of ferroptosis in ferroptosis-related cancer models and can be used as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer and ulcerative colitis (UC) [ , ]. PDZ and LIM domain protein 1 (PDLIM1), also known as CLP36, Elfin or CLIM1, is a cytoskeleton regulatory protein [ ] that regulates the organization of the actin cytoskeleton and is involved in cell morphological changes and migration [ ]. In human cancers, PDLIM1 has been shown to be a key regulator in breast cancer, promoting cell migration and metastasis [ ]. In addition, the inhibition of PDLIM1 can prevent the proliferation and cause the apoptosis of CML cells [ ]. Considering its tissue specificity, the role of PDLIM1 may differ across different cancers. PDLIM1 reduces the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition and metastatic ability of colorectal cancer cells by stabilizing α-actin at intercellular junctions [ , ]. PARP-1 is the main coordinator of parthanatos, a unique caspase-independent mode of programmed cell death [ ]. Its abnormal expression has been associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients with breast cancer and AML [ – ]. In addition, some studies have shown that, by inducing parthanatos during chemotherapy, PARP-1 can improve the prognosis of patients with leukemia [ ]. Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) acts as a malignant tumor switch in glioma. KEAP1 knockdown can promote resistance to ferroptosis by regulating SLC7A11, thereby accelerating proliferation and oncogenic transformation [ ].
Our study further revealed that a high PCD index was highly correlated with mature AML cell subtypes, and interestingly, the expression patterns of SMAD3 were basically consistent with the PCD index across different cytogenetic backgrounds. These findings imply that SMAD3 plays an important role in the PCD-related signature. Therefore, we used typical AML cell lines with different fusion genotypes for validation in vitro and reported that the p-SMAD3/SMAD3 expression level was the lowest in the leukemia cell line with the RUNX1-RUNXT1 fusion gene, whereas the p-SMAD3/SMAD3 expression level was greater in those with KMT2A rearrangement. Our cell viability assay further revealed that cells harboring KMT2A rearrangements were sensitive to a SAMD3 inhibitor (SIS3), especially during combination therapy in vitro. On this basis, we used western blotting to screen the expression levels of molecular markers of several types of PCD after drug administration to infer the relationship between PCD and treatment response. The results revealed that the expression of the autophagy-related molecular marker LC3 was increased in cell lines with KMT2A rearrangement when a SMAD3 inhibitor (SIS3) and drug combination treatment were used. The enhancement of the therapeutic sensitivity of cell lines with KMT2A rearrangement by SMAD3 inhibitors may be related to the involvement of autophagy. Previous studies have shown that the roles of autophagy in disease development and targeted therapy differ among different AML subtypes. Some leukemia types, such as FLT3-ITD mutants, can increase autophagy flux to support cell survival in AML, so the combination of autophagy inhibitors and FLT3 inhibitors can play a significant synergistic therapeutic effect [ ]. However, in other types of leukemia, such as PML-RARA and KMT2A rearrangement in AML, autophagy degradation of oncogenic proteins (PML-RARA and KMT2A fusion proteins) in AML can be promoted by inducing autophagy, thus playing a significant anti-leukemia effect [ ]. In addition, SAMD3 has been implicated in the regulation of autophagy [ ]. Therefore, we hypothesized that SAMD3 inhibitors may increase the sensitivity of cell lines with KMT2A rearrangements to drug treatment by regulating autophagy and increasing the degradation of the KMT2A fusion protein. Moreover, the expression of the apoptosis-related molecular markers cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP was also increased when the THP-1 (MLLT3-KMT2A) cell line was treated with the combination drug. This may be because autophagy can promote apoptosis in the late stage, thus further increasing sensitivity to treatment. However, the specific molecular regulatory mechanism of SMAD3 inhibitors to increase therapeutic sensitivity remains to be further studied and explored. As a result, SMAD3 may be an effective therapeutic target for combination treatment of high-risk AML patients.
In summary, our findings suggest that the PCD index is an independent prognostic indicator for AML patients and that SMAD3 could be an effective target for AML patients harboring KMT2A rearrangements.
