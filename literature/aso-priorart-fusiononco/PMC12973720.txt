## Summary
HO-1 (HMOX1) is a stress-inducible, rate-limiting heme catabolic enzyme aberrantly upregulated across hematologic malignancies and frequently associated with progression, relapse, and poor therapeutic response. This review integrates clinical and experimental evidence and frames HO-1 as a multidimensional resistance hub operating through three interconnected axes. (1) Enzymatic axis: heme degradation products (CO, biliverdin/bilirubin, and Fe2+) buffer therapy-induced oxidative stress, modulate apoptosis/autophagy, and tune ferroptosis susceptibility in a context- and dose-dependent manner. (2) Non-enzymatic axis: stress-induced truncation and nuclear translocation of HO-1 rewire transcriptional programs by stabilizing NRF2 and engaging epigenetic regulators, converting transient stress cues into durable resistance states. (3) Microenvironmental axis: HO-1 activity in stromal and immune compartments reshapes cytokine networks and suppresses immune recognition (e.g., HLA-C and CD48), reinforcing immune-evasive niches. We systematically summarize translational strategies at three levels: (1) agents directly targeting the HO-1 protein, (2) therapies targeting upstream regulatory pathways, and (3) approaches targeting downstream effectors and the HO-1-shaped immunosuppressive microenvironment.

## Introduction
Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), also known as heat shock protein 32, is encoded by the HMOX1 gene and serves as the rate-limiting enzyme in the heme degradation pathway, catalyzing the conversion of heme into carbon monoxide (CO), biliverdin/bilirubin, and ferrous iron (Fe2+). As a prototypical stress-inducible metabolic enzyme, HO-1 plays a critical role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis, buffering excess free heme and iron, suppressing inflammatory responses, and promoting tissue repair, and is therefore regarded as an important “cytoprotective gene” under diverse stress conditions. However, recent studies have shown that the function of HO-1 extends far beyond that of a simple “antioxidant enzyme.” On the one hand, full-length HO-1 localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria exerts its enzymatic activity and, through its metabolic products CO, biliverdin/bilirubin, and Fe2+, participates in the regulation of apoptosis, autophagy, iron homeostasis, and metabolic reprogramming. On the other hand, under conditions such as oxidative stress or inflammation, HO-1 has been reported to undergo proteolytic processing that removes its membrane-anchoring transmembrane segment, yielding a truncated, soluble form capable of nuclear translocation. In the nucleus, this truncated HO-1 is thought to exhibit little or no canonical heme-degrading activity; instead, it functions in a non-enzymatic manner by engaging protein-protein interaction networks to modulate the stability and activity of key transcription factors and to influence the composition and function of transcriptional complexes. Collectively, these non-canonical actions have been linked to genomic instability, transcriptional reprogramming, and the emergence of acquired drug resistance in cancer cells. In addition, HO-1 is highly expressed in multiple components of the immune microenvironment, including macrophages, dendritic cells(DCs), T cells, and bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), where it can, through CO signaling and epigenetic regulation, shape an immunosuppressive tumor niche and is increasingly recognized as a multifunctional “hub molecule” linking metabolism, inflammation, and immunity. 
Hematologic malignancies, including acute and chronic leukemias, multiple myeloma (MM), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), and various lymphomas, are characterized by pronounced heterogeneity and complex clonal evolution. Despite the rapid advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, conventional chemotherapy remains the first-line or backbone treatment for most hematologic malignancies, particularly acute leukemias and certain lymphoma subtypes. These agents exert their antitumor effects primarily by inducing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing DNA damage, disrupting mitochondrial function, and activating apoptotic pathways. , However, primary or acquired resistance markedly limits their long-term efficacy and is closely associated with disease relapse and poor prognosis. In recent years, the crucial role of HO-1, as a key component of the cellular antioxidant defense machinery, in mediating chemoresistance has attracted increasing attention. Studies have shown that chemotherapy-induced upregulation of HO-1 in cancer cells can attenuate ROS-mediated oxidative damage and counteract the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents, thereby promoting the development of acquired chemoresistance. Beyond metabolic reprogramming and stress responses within tumor cells themselves, the tumor microenvironment and aberrant activation of multiple adaptive stress pathways are also recognized as major drivers of resistance and relapse. Aberrant activation of HO-1 in bone marrow stromal cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and immune effector cells such as T cells and natural killer (NK) cells can remodel cytokine networks and dampen immune surveillance, creating an “HO-1-dependent” immunosuppressive microenvironment that represents an important extrinsic determinant of treatment resistance and relapse in hematologic malignancies.
Although several reviews have discussed the role of HO-1 in tumor drug resistance, most of them still regard HO-1 primarily as an “antioxidant/cytoprotective enzyme,” while relatively overlooking its non-enzymatic functions and its multilayered roles within the bone marrow microenvironment and the immune system. Compared with previous work, the present review seeks to provide a more integrated perspective in the following aspects.
(1)Conceptual framework: starting from three interconnected axes—enzymatic functions, non-enzymatic functions, and immune/microenvironmental regulation—we systematically delineate the multidimensional mechanisms by which HO-1 drives resistance in hematologic malignancies, and propose the conceptual framework of HO-1 as a “multi-dimensional resistance hub.”
(2)Mechanistic depth: beyond summarizing the classical nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)/HO-1 antioxidant defense axis and signaling pathways such as signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), we place particular emphasis on the roles of HO-1 in ferroptosis, autophagy, epigenetic reprogramming, and nuclear translocation as key mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance.
(3)Therapeutic targeting strategies: we comprehensively summarize HO-1-targeted strategies, including metalloporphyrins and HO-1/tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) hybrids that inhibit enzymatic activity, small molecules or protease inhibitors that block nuclear translocation, NRF2/PI3K/histone deacetylase (HDAC)-targeted agents that indirectly suppress HO-1 expression or signaling output, approaches that exploit the HO-1 buffering threshold to induce ferroptosis, and strategies aimed at remodeling the immune/microenvironment. These insights are intended to provide a theoretical basis for future individualized treatment and rational combination therapies.

## Expression characteristics of HO-1 and its prognostic significance in hematological malignancies
MDS are a group of clonal hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell disorders with an etiology that remains incompletely understood. They are mainly characterized by impaired differentiation and maturation of bone marrow hematopoietic cells, leading to varying degrees of multilineage cytopenia in the peripheral blood. Clinically, approximately one-third of MDS patients eventually progress to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), while the remaining patients typically develop high-risk MDS with poor prognosis. Although large-scale epidemiological data are still lacking, multiple studies suggest that HO-1 is highly expressed in MDS and may be closely associated with adverse outcomes. The expression level of HO-1 is significantly higher in patients with high-risk MDS compared to those with low-risk disease, and patients with high HO-1 expression are more likely to progress to AML, indicating a potential role of HO-1 in MDS disease progression.
Leukemia is a group of malignant tumors characterized by the abnormal clonal proliferation of leukemic cells in the bone marrow, which suppresses normal hematopoiesis and can further disseminate to peripheral blood and other tissues and organs. , The major subtypes include AML, chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). Although chemotherapy remains the mainstay of leukemia treatment, drug resistance continues to be a major factor limiting therapeutic efficacy and contributing to relapse. In AML, the expression level of HO-1 is closely associated with patient prognosis. Studies have shown that HO-1 expression is significantly higher in patients with poor prognosis compared to those in the intermediate- and favorable-risk groups, with expression levels 1.32-fold and 3.6-fold higher, respectively. Compared with AML patients with high HO-1 expression, those with low HO-1 expression exhibit significantly improved three-year overall survival (from 15% to 75%) and relapse-free survival (from 10% to 85%). Moreover, both CD34+CD38+ and CD34+CD38− leukemic cells from AML patients exhibit significantly higher HO-1 mRNA levels than normal CD34+ hematopoietic cells. In CML, HO-1 expression is also closely related to disease progression. During the complete molecular remission phase of CML, the mRNA level of HO-1 is as low as 0.0206 ± 0.0210, but it increases markedly to 3.852 ± 10.285 upon relapse. In the chronic phase, HO-1 expression is 0.0095 ± 0.0176, which rises to 0.0280 ± 0.0557 in the accelerated phase and significantly elevates to 0.2767 ± 0.4470 in the blast phase. These findings indicate that HO-1 may serve as an important biomarker for myeloid leukemias.
MM is a malignant tumor originating from plasma cells, accounting for 1%–2% of all cancers and 10%–15% of hematological malignancies. It is one of the most common hematologic malignancies, predominantly affecting the elderly, and currently remains incurable. Despite advances in medical technology, approximately 60% of patients eligible for autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) can survive for 10 years post-transplantation. However, data from the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) program indicate that, as of 2024, the 5-year relative survival (OS) rate for all MM patients is 62.4%. Only 10%–15% of patients can achieve a life expectancy comparable to that of the general population, while the majority eventually experience disease relapse and develop resistance to available therapies. Although direct evidence linking HO-1 to OS in MM remains limited, existing studies have shown that high HO-1 expression is closely associated with drug resistance and microenvironmental support in MM, indirectly suggesting a potential association with poor prognosis. , 
Lymphomas are a group of hematological malignancies with diverse origins and extremely high heterogeneity. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, they are broadly divided into Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) HL commonly occurs in young adults and the elderly, and its pathological hallmark is the presence of Hodgkin and Reed–Sternberg (R–S) cells. HL is mainly classified into four subtypes—nodular sclerosis, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, and nodular lymphocyte-predominant—and is characterized by a high cure rate and sensitivity to radiotherapy. In contrast, NHL encompasses multiple subtypes derived from B cells, T cells, or NK cells, with diverse clinical manifestations and the potential to involve multiple organ systems. Based on biological behavior, NHL can be classified into indolent and aggressive types, with diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) being the most common. Current treatment strategies for lymphoma are primarily based on chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with some patients also requiring combined immunotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. However, relapse and refractory disease remain major challenges limiting long-term survival. Previous studies have reported that HO-1 is markedly overexpressed in activated B cell-like DLBCL (ABC-DLBCL) and is closely associated with persistent nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling activation, a high Ki-67 proliferation index, and a tendency for extranodal infiltration, suggesting that HO-1 may serve as a molecular marker of poor prognosis in this subtype. 

## Molecular functions and regulatory mechanisms of HO-1 expression

## Mechanisms of transcriptional regulation of HO-1 expression and tumor type specificity

## Mechanisms regulating HO-1 transcription in oxidative stress and hypoxic microenvironments
Under normal physiological conditions, the expression level of HO-1 is relatively low in most tissues. However, under various pathophysiological stresses or stimuli—such as hypoxia, ultraviolet radiation, inflammatory mediators, heme, ischemia, and other harmful insults—HO-1 expression can be induced to protect cells from oxidative and inflammatory damage. , Oxidative stress exerts strong regulatory effects on HMOX1 transcription through multiple signaling pathways and transcription factors. Under oxidative stress conditions, heme bound within the heme-binding pocket of hemoproteins is released. Free heme binds to the heme-responsive CP motifs of the transcriptional repressor BTB and CNC homology 1 (BACH1), thereby inhibiting its DNA-binding activity and promoting its dissociation from antioxidant response elements (AREs)/Maf recognition elements (MAREs) within the distal enhancers of HMOX1. This relieves repression of HMOX1 and upregulates its transcription, thereby increasing HO-1 protein expression. In parallel, the accumulation of ROS oxidizes critical cysteine residues on KEAP1, attenuating Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)-Cullin 3 (CUL3)-mediated ubiquitination of NRF2. Stabilized NRF2 translocates into the nucleus, dimerizes with small Maf, and occupies ARE/MARE sites, further augmenting HMOX1 transcription. Together, BACH1 displacement from—and NRF2-small Maf engagement with—ARE/MARE elements cooperatively enhance HMOX1 expression. , , , 
FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) internal tandem duplication (ITD) is one of the most common mutation types in AML and is closely associated with poor prognosis. FLT3-ITD signaling can enhance the expression of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) via signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5), thereby promoting ROS generation. Previous studies have demonstrated that, in both patient samples and cell lines, FLT3-ITD can lead to elevated ROS levels and trigger antioxidant responses, activating the NRF2-HO-1 system and promoting HO-1 expression. Notably, the loss-of-function splicing variant Ikaros isoform 6 (IK6) of the transcription factor Ikaros (IKZF1) has also attracted attention in B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL). Studies have shown that HO-1 expression levels are significantly higher in IK6-positive B-ALL patients than in IK6-negative patients, with a strong positive correlation between the two. Mechanistically, IK6 can activate the STAT5 signaling pathway, in which HMOX1 is one of the downstream target genes. 
As “naturally hypoxic zones,” lymph nodes and bone marrow provide a favorable microenvironment for maintaining the homeostasis of immune cells and hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells Nakashima et al. reported that in HL, the side population (SP) cells with stem/progenitor-like properties exhibited significantly lower ROS levels compared to the major population (MP) cells. SP cells showed enriched expression of hypoxia-responsive genes, and experimental evidence confirmed that they were in a hypoxic state accompanied by high expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α). Further studies revealed that HIF-1α upregulates HO-1 expression, thereby reducing intracellular ROS and protecting stem/progenitor-like HL cells from oxidative stress-induced damage. Notably, inhibition or knockdown of HO-1 led to elevated ROS levels and significantly promoted the differentiation of SP cells into MP cells. Although the regulation of HO-1 by hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) has been widely accepted, Abe et al. found that in MM, HO-1 upregulation under hypoxic conditions was driven primarily by ROS rather than mediated by HIF. This suggests that, in hypoxic environments, the expression of genes related to cell survival may depend not only on HIF-mediated regulation but also on fine-tuning of intracellular ROS levels.

## Non-canonical signaling regulation of HO-1 and tumor subtype-specific expression profiles
In addition to the classical oxidative stress pathways, recent studies have shown that HO-1 expression is also subject to complex regulation by multiple molecules and signaling pathways. Rushworth et al. found that in AML cells, NF-κB (including the p50/NF-κB1 and p65 subunits) exerts a negative regulatory effect on HO-1 expression. Compared with non-malignant cells, inhibition of NF-κB markedly upregulated HO-1 expression in primary AML cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation and small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown experiments further confirmed that the p50 and p65 subunits of NF-κB play critical roles in the transcriptional regulation of HO-1. However, Huang et al. reported that in ABC-DLBCL, NF-κB promotes HO-1 expression. Constitutive activation of NF-κB led to high HO-1 expression, whereas treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor Bay11-7082 reduced HO-1 expression and increased apoptosis. We believe that the opposing regulatory effects of NF-κB on HO-1 expression in AML and ABC-DLBCL are primarily attributable to differences in baseline NF-κB activity. From the studies of Rushworth and Huang, it can be inferred that in AML, NF-κB generally acts as a stress-responsive factor with negative regulatory effects; its inhibition leads to compensatory HO-1 upregulation, enhancing antioxidant defense and chemoresistance under the influence of oxidative stress networks involving ROS or tumor necrosis factor (TNF). In contrast, in ABC-DLBCL, constitutive NF-κB activation (driven by aberrant B cell receptor (BCR) signaling) directly upregulates HO-1 overexpression, promoting anti-apoptotic activity and tumor aggressiveness, and reinforcing microenvironmental integration associated with inflammatory and proliferative markers such as Ki-67. This tumor-specific pattern reflects the bidirectional adaptability of HO-1 within different regulatory networks, suggesting that targeting strategies must take subtype differences into account.
In addition to NF-κB, growth factor independence 1 (GFI-1) has also been shown to play an important role in regulating HO-1 expression. Studies have demonstrated that in AML patients, low GFI-1 expression is often accompanied by high HO-1 expression, with the two showing a negative correlation. In vitro work by Cheng et al. shows that suppression of GFI-1 activates the PI3K/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, augments PI3K/Akt phosphorylation, and upregulates HO-1. In line with prior studies, activation of PI3K/Akt may promote NRF2 nuclear translocation/activation, thereby enhancing HMOX1 transcription and increasing HO-1 levels. Moreover, mitogen- and stress-activated protein kinase 1 (MSK1) and Brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1) are also important upstream regulators of HO-1. Fang et al. found that MSK1 promotes HO-1 expression through BRG1-mediated chromatin remodeling, whereas downregulation of BRG1 leads to decreased HO-1 expression. 
In summary, the expression of HO-1 is orchestrated by diverse molecular and signaling pathways, integrating multiple networks of oxidative stress responses and transcriptional regulation ( ).
 

## Enzymatic functions of HO-1 and the cytoprotective roles of its metabolic products
Heme oxygenases are a family of key metabolic enzymes encoded by the HMOX gene family, comprising two main functional isoforms: inducible HO-1 and constitutive HO-2. Although these isoforms are encoded by genes located on different chromosomes (22q12 and 16p13.3, respectively), they share high similarity in function, mechanism of action, required cofactors and substrates, and sensitivity to inhibition by metalloporphyrins. The primary function of HO-1 is to catalyze the degradation of heme into CO, biliverdin, and ferrous iron (Fe2+). Heme possesses pro-oxidant properties, as its Fe2+ moiety promotes free radical generation, while free Fe2+ itself is cytotoxic. Enhanced HO-1 activity can effectively remove excess heme, reduce intracellular ROS levels, and thereby mitigate oxidative stress-related cellular damage. Previous studies have shown that the antioxidant activity of HO-1 can protect against heme toxicity and inhibit tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-mediated apoptosis. 
The cytoprotective functions of HO-1 arise not only from its intrinsic enzymatic activity but also from its metabolic products. The three major products generated by its catalytic activity—Fe2+, CO, and biliverdin (the latter converted to bilirubin by biliverdin reductase)—can each exert protective effects through distinct mechanisms. CO can bind to cytochrome c oxidase and activate the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38-MAPK) signaling pathway, thereby enhancing cell survival in a manner synergistic with HO-1 itself. In addition, CO possesses immunomodulatory functions, capable of suppressing the activity of T cells and dendritic cells, thereby contributing to tumor cell evasion from immune surveillance Iron (Fe2+) released during heme/porphyrin degradation can expand the intracellular labile iron pool (LIP). Cells typically mitigate the associated pro-oxidant risk by upregulating iron-buffering systems such as ferritin and by promoting iron export; however, when buffering capacity is insufficient, excess labile iron can amplify oxidative stress and precipitate iron-dependent damage. , Bilirubin is a potent antioxidant molecule that can scavenge ROS at micromolar concentrations. Its mechanism of action, known as the “biliverdin-bilirubin cycle,” involves the reaction of bilirubin with ROS to generate biliverdin, which is then reduced back to bilirubin by biliverdin reductase, providing sustained antioxidant protection. Furthermore, bilirubin can exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the expression of E-selectin ( ). 
 

## Enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms of HO-1-mediated drug resistance in hematologic malignancies
HO-1, as a stress-inducible heme oxygenase, exerts its classical antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and iron-regulatory functions primarily through heme degradation and the actions of its metabolic products, CO, biliverdin/bilirubin, and Fe2+. On the other hand, accumulating evidence indicates that the cytoprotective effects of HO-1 cannot be fully explained by treating cells with CO, Fe2+, or bilirubin alone, especially in hematologic malignancies, where non-enzymatic functions of the HO-1 protein itself—such as its roles in signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and epigenetic reprogramming—are equally critical. In addition, high HO-1 expression in bone marrow stromal cells, macrophages, T cells, and other components of the immune microenvironment can remodel the cytokine network and reset immune response thresholds, thereby providing immune protection and a “sanctuary” niche for malignant clones.
Thus, the drug-resistant phenotype conferred by HO-1 in hematologic malignancies can be broadly summarized at three interconnected levels.
(1)Enzymatic functions dependent on heme degradation and its metabolic products, which buffer ROS and ferroptotic stress, inhibit apoptosis, and raise the threshold for drug tolerance;
(2)Non-enzymatic functions dominated by signaling, epigenetic, and cell-fate reprogramming, including cytokine network remodeling, epigenetic modification, autophagy regulation, and changes in subcellular localization;
(3)Regulation of the bone marrow microenvironment and immune system, whereby HO-1 shapes an immunosuppressive niche by acting on stromal cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), T cells, and NK cells.
In the following sections, we systematically dissect the mechanisms by which HO-1 mediates drug resistance in hematologic malignancies within this three-dimensional framework.

## Enzymatic HO-1 functions in redox homeostasis, ferroptosis balance, and stemness maintenance

## Antioxidant stress responses and classical anti-apoptotic pathways
In AML, cytarabine (Ara-C) and daunorubicin (DNR) are the core agents used in first-line induction chemotherapy. Studies have shown that, compared with drug-sensitive cells, Ara-C- and DNR-resistant AML cells exhibit significantly higher HO-1 mRNA and protein expression; likewise, in patients with relapsed or refractory AML, HO-1 mRNA levels are markedly elevated relative to newly diagnosed patients, those in complete remission, and healthy donors. Mechanistically, Ara-C and DNR exert their antileukemic effects in part by inducing intracellular ROS and activating mitochondrial apoptotic pathways. High HO-1 expression accelerates heme degradation and, through metabolic products such as biliverdin/bilirubin, scavenges ROS, thereby attenuating drug-induced oxidative stress. In parallel, HO-1 can modulate stress-responsive pathways such as c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/c-JUN, inhibit the cleavage of caspase-3 and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1), and consequently reduce the sensitivity of AML cells to Ara-C- and DNR-induced apoptosis. , 
In CML, the BCR-ABL fusion protein upregulates members of the NOX family and the cytosolic regulatory subunit p47ˆphox (NCF1), thereby enhancing ROS production and driving sustained HO-1 overexpression. , , Imatinib (IM), a first-line TKI for CML, nonetheless encounters primary or acquired resistance in approximately 40% of patients during the course of treatment. Analyses of clinical samples have shown that HO-1 is significantly overexpressed in CML and that its expression increases progressively with disease progression and relapse. HO-1 levels correlate positively with blast percentage and help distinguish different disease phases. In IM-resistant cells, HO-1 expression is further elevated and is closely associated with increased intracellular pH and activation of Na+/H+ exchanger 1 (NHE1). Activation of NHE1 promotes PKC-β phosphorylation and engages the p38 MAPK pathway, thereby facilitating NRF2 nuclear translocation and driving HO-1 upregulation in an NRF2-dependent manner. HO-1 overexpression similarly mitigates IM-induced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and suppresses caspase-3/PARP-1 cleavage, thereby enhancing cellular resistance to TKI therapy. Similar mechanisms have also been observed in lymphoid malignancies. In mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), high HO-1 expression markedly attenuates bortezomib (BTZ)-induced ROS accumulation, reduces apoptosis, enhances cell survival, and promotes the development of BTZ resistance. 
Taken together, these studies support the conclusion that the enzymatic activity-dependent functions of HO-1, by buffering chemotherapy-induced ROS bursts and suppressing stress-induced apoptosis, represent one of the fundamental mechanisms underlying chemoresistance in hematologic malignancies.

## Dual role of the NRF2-HO-1 axis in ferroptosis regulation
In recent years, the role and therapeutic potential of ferroptosis in hematologic malignancies have attracted increasing attention. Ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and is characterized by expansion of the LIP, accumulation of ROS, and oxidative damage to cellular membranes. At the molecular level, the glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-glutathione (GSH) axis is widely recognized as the canonical core pathway that restrains ferroptosis, whereas a series of iron-metabolism and iron-homeostasis regulators, including HO-1, constitute key upstream nodes that determine ferroptotic sensitivity. To meet the metabolic demands of rapid proliferation, tumor cells generally exhibit markedly increased iron uptake and utilization. Under physiological conditions, excess labile iron is largely sequestered within ferritin to prevent oxidative damage caused by iron overload; however, when nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy is activated, ferritin is degraded and stored iron is released into the intracellular LIP. The ensuing expansion of the LIP provides substrate for Fenton chemistry, promotes hydroxyl radical generation and lipid peroxidation, and ultimately triggers ferroptosis. 
Traditionally, the NRF2/HO-1 axis together with GPX4, solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), and related factors has been viewed as a “defensive barrier” against ferroptosis. , NRF2 upregulates SLC7A11 to enhance cystine uptake, thereby sustaining the GSH-GPX4 lipid peroxide-detoxifying capacity; in parallel, it induces iron-buffering/iron-homeostasis factors such as ferritin (FTH1/FTL) to sequester labile iron and reduce the intracellular labile iron pool (LIP), ultimately suppressing ferroptosis. , , Induction of HO-1, in turn, endows cells with enhanced antioxidant capacity; its heme-degrading activity generates biliverdin and bilirubin, which serve as potent antioxidants that neutralize ROS and lipid peroxides and thereby delay the onset of ferroptosis. Setiawan et al. reported that the Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor zanubrutinib, in combination with the B cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) inhibitor navitoclax, downregulates the NRF2-HO-1-GPX4 signaling axis and synergistically induces ferroptosis and apoptosis in double-hit lymphoma (DHL) cells, leading to effective suppression of tumor growth. In this setting, HO-1 functions as an anti-ferroptotic safeguard, and its downregulation constitutes an important molecular basis for the increased ferroptotic sensitivity of DHL cells.
Although most studies suggest that the NRF2–HO-1 axis primarily promotes treatment resistance in tumor cells by repressing ferroptosis, excessive activation of HO-1 can also exert the opposite effect. During HO-1-mediated heme degradation, Fe2+ is released; when present in excess, this labile iron can fuel lipid peroxidation, a pivotal step in ferroptosis. Lai et al. showed in THP-1, U-937, and SKM-1 cells that honokiol, a biphenolic natural compound, markedly upregulates HO-1 and induces a ferroptotic phenotype in AML cells without suppressing GPX4 expression. Consistently, Chen et al. demonstrated that mild activation of the NRF2/HO-1 axis in AML cells leads to limited heme degradation by HO-1, releasing small amounts of Fe2+ while bilirubin and biliverdin scavenge ROS, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and protecting cells. In contrast, high-dose treatment with the small-molecule compound Z-LIG induces robust activation of the NRF2/HO-1 axis and strong HO-1 upregulation, resulting in massive Fe2+ release and iron accumulation that exceed the cellular buffering capacity and in turn promote lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Hu et al. further reported that, in peripheral T cell lymphoma (PTCL), pharmacologic inhibition of prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS) enhances HO-1 expression and its heme-oxygenase activity, activates ferritinophagy, increases intracellular Fe2+, and ultimately triggers lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Several studies in multiple myeloma (MM) have likewise shown that modest activation of the NRF2-HO-1 axis helps to restrain ferroptosis, , , whereas strong activation can, via ROS-driven expansion of the LIP, promote ferroptosis, reflecting a complex and context-dependent regulatory pattern. 
To date, evidence for the dual roles of HO-1 in ferroptosis has largely come from in vitro cell-line studies and xenograft models and relies heavily on pharmacological tools such as honokiol, Z-LIG, BTK inhibitors combined with BCL-2 inhibitors, and PTGDS inhibitors; the specificity and potential off-target effects of these agents have not yet been fully excluded. Nevertheless, HO-1 appears to act as a dose- and context-dependent “double-edged sword” in the regulation of ferroptosis. Under conditions of relatively intact iron homeostasis, mild to moderate activation of the NRF2/HO-1 axis primarily clears heme and ROS, promotes ferritin-mediated iron sequestration and iron export, and cooperates with GPX4, SLC7A11, FTH1, and related factors to form an anti-ferroptotic defense system that supports tumor cell survival under therapeutic stress. By contrast, when HO-1 is persistently and strongly induced and iron export and storage mechanisms approach saturation, sustained Fe2+ release—together with ferritinophagy and related processes—drives uncontrolled elevation of the LIP and accumulation of lipid peroxides, shifting the NRF2/HO-1 axis from a “pro-survival” mode toward a “pro-ferroptotic” program.

## HO-1 and the maintenance of stemness in cancer stem cells and leukemia stem cells
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) and leukemia stem cells (LSCs) are considered the critical “seed” cell populations underlying relapse and treatment failure in hematologic malignancies. These cells are characterized by strong self-renewal capacity and marked resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. In several solid tumor models, including breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and melanoma, HO-1 upregulation has been implicated in the expansion and self-renewal of CSC populations. Among these, studies in breast and colorectal cancer have more systematically demonstrated that the enzymatic activity of HO-1 and its metabolic product CO are key mediators of stemness maintenance: on the one hand, HO-1 catalyzes heme degradation and scavenges ROS, thereby reducing oxidative damage within CSCs; on the other hand, CO can activate stemness-associated signaling pathways such as Notch1 or endothelin-converting enzyme-1/endothelin-1 (ECE-1/ET-1), promoting tumorsphere formation and the expression of stemness marker genes, and in turn driving the maintenance of the CSC phenotype and clonal expansion. , 
In hematologic malignancies, HO-1 is likewise thought to participate in the regulation of CSC/LSC survival and drug resistance. Herrmann et al. reported that, in samples from patients with AML, HO-1 expression was significantly higher in CD34+/CD38+ and CD34+/CD38- stem/progenitor subpopulations than in normal CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells, suggesting that HO-1 acts as an important survival factor involved in LSC maintenance and leukemia initiation. In MM, Abe et al. further showed that side population (SP) cells—believed to be enriched for myeloma stem-like cells/CSC-like populations—displayed HMOX1/HO-1 as one of the specifically upregulated genes, and that HO-1 maintains SP-cell survival and drug resistance under hypoxic and chemotherapeutic stress by efficiently scavenging ROS. However, these studies predominantly support a cytoprotective role of HO-1 at the level of “survival and resistance.” Whether HO-1 directly and functionally contributes to the maintenance of stemness in malignant hematopoietic cells remains to be clarified by further experimental evidence.

## HO-1-driven non-enzymatic signaling networks and epigenetic regulation
Compared with its classical “enzymatic” functions that depend on heme degradation and its metabolic products, the “non-enzymatic” activities of HO-1—in reshaping cytokine networks, modulating transcription, orchestrating epigenetic reprogramming, regulating autophagy, and altering subcellular localization—are increasingly recognized as major drivers of long-term drug resistance and clonal evolution in hematologic malignancies.

## Modulation of cytokine networks and survival signaling pathways
Cytokine-mediated anti-apoptotic signaling pathways play a pivotal role in the drug-resistance mechanisms of hematologic malignancies. In recent years, accumulating evidence has shown that HO-1 markedly promotes resistance to targeted agents and conventional chemotherapeutics by modulating multiple cytokines and their downstream pro-survival signals. In FLT3-ITD-positive AML, patients with high HO-1 expression exhibit stronger resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Using a mouse xenograft model, Zhang et al. further demonstrated that HO-1 enhances interleukin-4 (IL-4)-mediated signaling, particularly by promoting signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) phosphorylation and activating downstream anti-apoptotic pathways, thereby conferring TKI resistance on FLT3-ITD-mutant AML cells. 
In MM, growth arrest-specific 6 (Gas6) functions as a pleiotropic ligand that regulates multiple pro-survival pathways. Clinical data indicate that patients with high Gas6 expression respond poorly to bortezomib (BTZ)-based chemotherapy. Zhang et al. found that primary CD138+ myeloma cells exhibit high HO-1 expression, which correlates closely with Gas6 levels in bone marrow and plasma. In vitro, HO-1 activates the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway to upregulate Gas6 expression and secretion, while Gas6 in turn engages multiple pro-survival and anti-apoptotic signaling cascades, thereby enhancing the chemoresistance of MM cells. In addition, Wu et al. reported that interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the bone marrow microenvironment upregulates HO-1 expression in MM cells via the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)-STAT3 pathway. Elevated HO-1 further enhances IL-6 autocrine production through activation of the p38 MAPK pathway, forming a positive feedback loop that markedly increases MM cell survival and resistance to agents such as lenalidomide. 

## Epigenetic reprogramming and drug resistance
Epigenetic modifications play a critical role in the development of drug resistance. Increasing evidence indicates that, under conditions of sustained HO-1 overexpression, chromatin architecture and DNA methylation profiles are remodeled, converting transient oxidative and inflammatory stress signals into stable and long-lasting changes in transcriptional programs. In this way, tumor cells acquire resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies. This process can be regarded as one of the key downstream outputs of the non-enzymatic functions of HO-1.
The DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTis) decitabine and azacitidine (AZA) are first-line treatment options for patients with intermediate- and high-risk MDS. Our previous work showed that HO-1 is highly expressed in the MDS cell line SKM-1 and is closely associated with resistance to decitabine-induced apoptosis. Upregulation of HO-1 counteracts decitabine-mediated demethylation of the p15INK4B promoter, maintaining its hypermethylated state and thereby promoting cell proliferation while suppressing apoptosis. Analysis of clinical samples further revealed that HO-1 and enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) are concomitantly upregulated and positively correlated in high- and very-high-risk MDS. As the catalytic core subunit of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), EZH2 mediates trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) and silences multiple tumor-suppressor genes. Mechanistic studies suggest that HO-1 promotes EZH2 transcription via activation of the retinoblastoma protein (pRB)-E2F pathway, leading to downregulation of tumor suppressors, such as TP53 and p15INK4B, and that MDS patients with high HO-1 expression have an increased risk of transformation to AML. Wang et al. further demonstrated that HO-1 can enhance the expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1), maintain global DNA methylation, and oppose AZA-induced demethylation of the p15 and p16 promoters, thereby preventing apoptosis. 
Beyond DNA methylation, HO-1 also indirectly regulates chromatin accessibility and transcriptional programs by influencing a class of histone deacetylases (HDACs) that are highly sensitive to ROS. HDACs play a key role in redox-sensitive gene regulation, and their enzymatic activity is exquisitely dependent on intracellular ROS levels: HDAC proteins are enriched in cysteine residues that are prone to oxidation, and oxidative modification can impair their deacetylase function and alter the transcriptional status of downstream target genes. Acting as a major antioxidant enzyme, HO-1 lowers ROS levels and buffers oxidative stress, thereby indirectly preserving HDAC activity and enhancing the ability of tumor cells to resist apoptotic signals. Several studies have indicated that HO-1 upregulation contributes to drug resistance by activating HDACs. Wang et al. found that multiple HDACs are markedly upregulated in CML and B-ALL cells with acquired resistance and that their expression closely correlates with HO-1 levels. In CML models, HO-1 maintains HDAC activity by limiting ROS accumulation; in turn, HDACs suppress pro-apoptotic pathways and enhance anti-apoptotic capacity, thereby promoting the imatinib-resistant phenotype. In B-ALL, high HO-1 expression is frequently accompanied by increased expression of HDAC4, HDAC5, and Smad7, and Smad7 mRNA levels positively correlate with HDAC4/5. Further mechanistic studies show that HO-1 upregulates Smad7 and activates the phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) pathway, effectively inhibiting apoptosis in B-ALL cells. 
Notably, a complex positive feedback loop may exist between HDACs and HO-1. HDAC activity helps maintain NRF2 in a deacetylated state, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity and its ability to drive HMOX1 promoter activation. Consequently, histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) may not only induce tumor cell apoptosis but also weaken the HDAC-NRF2-HO-1 axis and further downregulate HO-1 expression, enhancing antitumor efficacy through dual mechanisms. , , , Recent pharmacologic studies further highlight that specific HDACis can modulate HO-1 expression and downstream JNK/activator protein 1 (AP-1) signaling, thereby linking epigenetic targeting to HO-1-dependent redox control in cancer cells. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for combining HDACis with HO-1-directed strategies, which will be discussed in subsequent sections.

## Autophagy-mediated metabolic adaptation and drug resistance
Autophagy is a lysosome-dependent process that degrades excess and damaged proteins and organelles and plays a critical role in the development of chemoresistance. Upregulation of HO-1 not only alleviates oxidative stress but also promotes metabolic adaptation by modulating autophagic activity, thereby helping tumor cells remove damage, maintain intracellular homeostasis, and delay or escape drug-induced cell death. In CML models, Cao et al. reported that elevated HO-1 expression inhibits the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, reducing the phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream effector ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta-1 (p70S6K) and thereby enhancing autophagic activity. This mechanism facilitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria and proteins during IM treatment, maintains energy supply and metabolic homeostasis, and ultimately increases CML cell resistance to IM. 
Taken together with the role of ferritinophagy in HO-1-mediated ferroptosis discussed previously, these findings suggest that autophagy within HO-1-centered resistance networks may act as a “protective renewal” mechanism under moderate stress, yet under extreme conditions can also contribute to iron dyshomeostasis and cell death. Thus, autophagy exhibits pronounced context dependence in the regulation of HO-1-driven therapeutic resistance.

## Reprogramming of subcellular localization and nuclear HO-1
Traditionally, HO-1 (approximately 32–33 kDa) has been regarded as a microsomal enzyme anchored to the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, where it exerts its function primarily by catalyzing heme degradation. However, under stress conditions such as hypoxia or increased ROS, or in certain pathological contexts, signal peptide peptidase (SPP) can cleave the N-terminal transmembrane segment of HO-1, releasing it from the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and generating an N-terminally truncated HO-1 isoform of approximately 28 kDa. This truncated form lacks membrane-anchoring capacity and can translocate to other subcellular compartments, particularly the nucleus. , Most studies to date indicate that nuclear HO-1 largely corresponds to this N-terminally truncated species, whereas the 33-kDa full-length HO-1 predominantly localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. In contrast to endoplasmic reticulum or mitochondria-localized HO-1, nuclear HO-1 essentially loses its heme oxygenase catalytic activity and instead functions as a protein-protein interaction mediator involved in signal transduction. 
Biswas et al. reported that nuclear HO-1 can directly bind NRF2, preventing its phosphorylation by glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) and subsequent β-transducin repeat-containing protein (β-TrCP)-mediated, Keap1-independent degradation. This interaction promotes NRF2 accumulation in the nucleus, enhances its transcriptional activity toward downstream antioxidant enzyme genes such as NAD(P)H dehydrogenase [quinone] 1 (NQO1) and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH), and increases cellular tolerance to oxidative stress, thereby providing adaptive protection for tumor cells under persistent oxidative pressure. 
Using K562 cells stably transfected with HO-1 constructs targeted to different subcellular compartments, Schaefer et al. found that highly active, ER-localized HO-1 does not markedly induce imatinib (IM) resistance, whereas nuclear HO-1 can render a subset of clones resistant to IM. Building on this, Tibullo et al. demonstrated in MM and CML models that treatment of MM cells with bortezomib (BTZ) and of CML cells with IM robustly induces HO-1 overexpression and nuclear translocation, with particularly prominent nuclear HO-1 accumulation in resistant cells. Detailed analyses revealed that nuclear HO-1 promotes the formation of micronuclei, nuclear bridges, and nuclear buds—hallmarks of genomic instability—and may enhance cellular adaptation to DNA damage and therapeutic stress by modulating DNA damage repair, cell-cycle regulation, and transcriptional programs. Notably, inhibition of HO-1 enzymatic activity alone has only a limited impact on the cytotoxic effects of BTZ and IM, whereas blocking HO-1 nuclear translocation significantly potentiates their antitumor activity. , 
Together, these findings indicate that HO-1 can drive therapeutic resistance in hematologic malignancies not only through its classical enzymatic functions but also, and perhaps more critically, through reprogramming of its subcellular localization. Upon translocation to the nucleus, HO-1 acts in a prototypical “non-enzymatic” manner as a regulatory hub integrating redox signaling, genomic stability, and adaptive stress responses.

## HMOX1 gene polymorphisms and regulation of HO-1 expression
HMOX1 gene polymorphisms are important genetic determinants of both basal and inducible HO-1 expression, most notably a promoter (GT)n repeat polymorphism and the T(−413)A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP). In the 5′ promoter region of the human HMOX1 gene, the number of (GT)n microsatellite repeats varies substantially among individuals and can markedly influence basal transcriptional activity as well as stimulus-induced upregulation of HMOX1. , According to the length of the (GT)n tract, alleles are commonly classified as short (S) when they contain <25 GT repeats and long (L) when they contain ≥25 repeats. , 
In vitro luciferase reporter assays have shown that, compared with L alleles, S alleles significantly enhance HO-1 transcriptional activity , , and lymphocytes from carriers of S alleles display greater resistance to apoptosis. An increasing number of studies suggest that HMOX1 promoter polymorphisms are associated with cancer susceptibility and prognosis across multiple tumor types. , For example, one study reported that the frequency of short (GT)n alleles is significantly higher in patients with ALL than in healthy controls, and that this allele may be linked to an increased risk of treatment failure. 
In the setting of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), Køllgaard et al. found that patients carrying short (GT)n alleles had a lower risk of developing acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) after transplantation but experienced higher relapse rates and shorter progression-free survival (PFS). The authors proposed that higher HO-1 expression associated with short (GT)n alleles may exert stronger anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects, thereby attenuating the graft-versus-tumor (GVT) response and increasing relapse risk. The T(−413)A SNP represents another polymorphism within the HMOX1 promoter. Cells harboring the A(−413)–(GT)30 allele exhibit significantly enhanced HO-1 bioactivity, and the A allele is closely associated with higher HMOX1 transcriptional levels. 

## HO-1-mediated immunosuppression and immune evasion
The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of infiltrating immune cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, extracellular matrix components, and a variety of soluble factors, critically supports tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis through complex cell-cell interactions within this niche. , Malignant cells have evolved multiple mechanisms to escape immune surveillance. In hematologic malignancies, HO-1 not only exerts its enzymatic and non-enzymatic functions within tumor cells but is also highly expressed in bone marrow stromal cells, macrophages, T cells, and NK cells. Through its metabolic products (such as CO and bilirubin) and its transcriptional and epigenetic regulatory effects, HO-1 collectively drives immunosuppression and immune evasion within the TME. , , 

## HO-1-mediated reprogramming of bone marrow stromal cells and formation of a drug-resistant microenvironment
Recent studies have shown that high HO-1 expression in BMSCs within the bone marrow microenvironment plays a critical role in the development of drug resistance in hematologic malignancies. It has been reported that HO-1 is not only highly expressed in hematologic tumor cells but is also markedly overexpressed in BMSCs from patients with CML and stage III MM. Upregulation of HO-1 in BMSCs promotes the secretion of multiple cytokines, particularly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and the chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 12 (CXCL12), also known as stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1). These factors activate the CXCL12/C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) axis and downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and JAK2/STAT3, leading to upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein B cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and downregulation of cleaved caspase-3. Consequently, tumor cells acquire an enhanced ability to resist chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. In addition, high HO-1 expression in BMSCs significantly increases the clonogenic capacity of MM cells and reduces their sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents. , 
Notably, VEGF is a key regulator of chemotherapy resistance mediated by the bone marrow microenvironment. In vitro studies by Yu et al. further demonstrated a co-expression relationship between VEGF and HO-1 in BMSCs: when HO-1 is upregulated, VEGF secretion is markedly increased, whereas HO-1 knockdown leads to reduced VEGF production. Moreover, exogenous recombinant VEGF protein was shown to enhance vincristine (VCR) resistance in B-ALL cell lines. At the mechanistic level, HO-1 promotes VEGF secretion by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby increasing the resistance of B-ALL cells to VCR. 

## HO-1 and immunosuppressive reprogramming of macrophages and TAMs
TAMs promote resistance to biologic therapies, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy in part by providing direct metabolic and nutritional support to cancer cells. As the dominant immune cell population within the tumor microenvironment, TAMs can acquire diverse phenotypic and functional states and are broadly polarized toward either a pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype. Nybakken et al. reported that CD163+ macrophages express HO-1. Compared with samples from patients with benign cytopenias, MDS bone marrow samples displayed significantly increased numbers of CD163+ macrophages together with higher HO-1 expression. Follow-up analyses showed that, in patients with MDS, high HO-1 expression was associated with shorter overall survival (OS), whereas in patients with benign cytopenias, HO-1 expression was not significantly correlated with OS. In addition, increased HO-1 expression in bone marrow macrophages appeared to exert a protective effect on MDS cells. Using a G7-OVA lymphoma model (ovalbumin-expressing lymphoma cells injected intradermally into mice with a specific HO-1 deficiency), Alaluf et al. further demonstrated that HO-1 not only regulates the transcription of immunosuppression-associated molecules in TAMs but also reshapes chromatin accessibility, thereby influencing the epigenetic regulation of key genes, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and arginase-1 (Arg-1). The presence of HO-1 maintains an immunosuppressive TAM phenotype, whereas HO-1 deficiency induces widespread reprogramming of both the transcriptome and chromatin landscape, driving TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor state. 
The mechanisms by which HO-1 promotes TAM reprogramming and macrophage polarization appear to depend largely on its metabolic product CO. Consonni et al. showed that treatment of macrophages with the HO-1 inhibitor zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPPIX) significantly downregulated M2 marker gene expression, whereas addition of an exogenous CO donor, such as carbon monoxide-releasing molecule-2 (CORM-2), reversed this effect. Multiple studies have demonstrated that CO, acting as a signaling molecule, can promote phosphorylation and activation of M2-associated transcription factors such as STAT3 and STAT6, thereby driving macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype, while concomitantly inhibiting the phosphorylation of M1-associated transcription factors such as STAT1 and downregulating M1-related genes. , M2-polarized TAMs suppress the proliferation of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment and impair their ability to produce cytotoxic effector molecules, including granzyme B and interferon-γ (IFN-γ). In this way, HO-1-dependent M2 reprogramming contributes to the establishment of a highly immunosuppressive niche that supports sustained tumor progression and promotes resistance to therapy. 

## HO-1-mediated regulation of T cell subset function
In T cells, HO-1 expression differs between CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and CD4+CD25− effector T cells (Teff). , In certain tumor types, elevated HO-1 expression has been reported to correlate with increased infiltration of factor forkhead box P3(Foxp3+) regulatory T cells (Tregs). Consistently, experimental studies have shown that HO-1 overexpression can render T cell models (e.g., Jurkat cells) more resistant to Fas-mediated apoptosis. In addition, the HO-1 metabolite CO may mediate its antiproliferative effects on CD4+ T cells by suppressing interleukin-2 (IL-2) production. 

## HO-1-mediated NK-cell immune evasion
NK cell-mediated innate immunity plays a crucial role in antitumor defense, particularly when adaptive immune function is impaired or exhausted, , or when tumor cells lack or downregulate major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules required for T cell priming and activation. In a series of related studies, Venstrom et al. showed that human leukocyte antigen C (HLA-C) can bind to killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor 2DS1 (KIR2DS1), thereby triggering NK-cell cytotoxic responses. Martínez-Sánchez et al. further reported that reduced HLA-C expression in patients with leukemia is closely associated with an increased risk of relapse. Building on these findings, Feng et al. demonstrated that, in patients with AML, HLA-C expression is negatively correlated with HO-1 levels. Silencing HLA-C markedly attenuated NK-cell–mediated cytotoxicity against AML cells. Mechanistically, HO-1 suppresses HLA-C expression in AML cells by activating the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/c-Jun signaling pathway, thereby diminishing NK-cell cytotoxicity. In a mouse model, transplantation of HO-1–silenced AML cells resulted in reduced leukemic infiltration, enhanced NK-cell cytotoxic function, improved survival, and these effects were significantly reversed by HLA-C overexpression. In addition, Zhang et al. found that HO-1 is broadly overexpressed in AML and is inversely correlated with the expression of CD48, a ligand for the activating NK-cell receptor 2B4 (CD244). Further mechanistic studies showed that excessive HO-1 expression induces upregulation of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), leading to deacetylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27) and transcriptional repression of CD48. The resulting decrease in CD48 expression ultimately weakens NK-cell antitumor activity. 
Overall, HO-1 exhibits two prominent modes of action within the immune microenvironment. On the one hand, its enzymatic activity and metabolic products—particularly CO—modulate polarization states and activation thresholds in TAMs and T cell subsets. On the other hand, HO-1 directly participates in immune evasion in a prototypical “non-enzymatic” manner by regulating the expression of key molecules such as HLA-C and CD48. Evidence from multiple animal models and in vitro studies is highly consistent in supporting the concept of an “HO-1-driven immunosuppressive niche” in hematologic malignancies ( ).
 

## Therapeutic strategies targeting HO-1 and advances in preclinical studies
In the preceding section, we systematically summarized the enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms by which HO-1 promotes resistance in hematologic malignancies. Collectively, these mechanisms provide multiple levels of therapeutic entry points. Some agents act directly on the HO-1 protein itself, inhibiting its enzymatic activity or blocking its nuclear translocation to attenuate its antioxidant and pro-survival functions. Others modulate upstream regulatory networks—such as NRF2, PI3K/BTB, and CNC homology 2 (BACH2), NF-κB, and epigenetic regulators—to indirectly downregulate HO-1 expression or dampen its downstream signaling output. In addition, an increasing number of studies target HO-1 downstream effectors and the immune microenvironment, aiming to disrupt HO-1-dependent survival pathways and immune-evasion circuits so as to potentiate the cytotoxic efficacy of conventional therapies.
In this section, we will systematically review available preclinical evidence and potential translational implications at three interconnected levels: (1) agents that directly target the HO-1 protein, (2) therapies directed against upstream regulatory pathways of HO-1, and (3) strategies aimed at HO-1 downstream effectors and the HO-1-shaped immune microenvironment.

## Direct targeting of HO-1

## Reversing drug resistance with HO-1 enzymatic inhibitors
Among the HO-1 inhibitors developed to date, metalloporphyrin compounds and imidazole derivatives are the most widely used. Metalloporphyrins, as the first generation of HO-1 inhibitors, share a structural similarity with heme and can competitively bind to the heme-binding site of HO-1, thereby effectively inhibiting its enzymatic activity. Zinc protoporphyrin (ZnPPIX) and tin protoporphyrin (SnPPIX) are the most representative and commonly used inhibitors in this class. Numerous studies have shown that ZnPPIX and SnPPIX can induce tumor cell apoptosis and effectively reverse drug resistance caused by excessive HO-1 activation. This effect has been demonstrated in various hematological malignancies, including MDS, CML, AML, ALL, MM and lymphoma. Furthermore, research indicates that metalloporphyrins, by inhibiting HO-1 activity, can reprogram TAMs to enhance immune responses against tumor cells, thereby improving the efficacy of immunotherapy. Specifically, ZnPPIX suppresses HO-1 enzymatic activity, restores the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and CXCL10, and inhibits the transcription of anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and CCL22. Additional evidence shows that this effect can be reversed by carbon monoxide-releasing molecules (e.g., CORM-2), suggesting that HO-1-derived CO may play a pivotal regulatory role in tumor immune evasion. However, it is important to note that these compounds are non-selective inhibitors of HO isoforms and may also affect other enzymatic systems.
To overcome acquired resistance to IM in patients with CML, Sorrenti et al. proposed and validated a dual-targeted therapeutic strategy. By combining the structural scaffold of IM with an imidazole-based HO-1 inhibitor, they developed a novel class of compounds referred to as HO-1/TKI hybrid molecules, designed to effectively counteract IM resistance. In IM-resistant K562R cells, these hybrids exhibited pronounced antiproliferative activity and efficiently blocked downstream signaling of the BCR-ABL kinase. At the same time, they markedly reduced HO-1 enzymatic activity and mRNA expression, induced an increase in ROS levels, and ultimately triggered apoptosis. Notably, these compounds displayed minimal cytotoxicity toward normal lymphocytes, indicating favorable selectivity and safety profiles. 

## Inhibition of HO-1 nuclear translocation and reversal of drug resistance
Increasing evidence indicates that nuclear localization of HO-1 plays a critical role in the mechanisms of drug resistance in tumor cells. Schaefer et al. reported that although HO-1 is abundantly expressed on the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, it fails to induce resistance to IM in K562 cells; however, translocation of HO-1 into the nucleus markedly promoted the acquisition of a resistant phenotype. Further studies by Tibullo et al. in MM and CML revealed that inhibiting HO-1 enzymatic activity alone is insufficient to reverse drug resistance. Since HO-1 nuclear translocation depends on proteolytic cleavage, they successfully blocked HO-1 transport into the nucleus using the cysteine protease inhibitor E64d, thereby significantly increasing the sensitivity of CML cells to IM and enhancing the cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects of BTZ in MM cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that targeting HO-1 nuclear translocation may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to overcome drug resistance in hematologic malignancies.

## Targeting upstream regulatory pathways of HO-1
As an enzymatically active functional protein, HO-1 expression can be induced through multiple signaling pathways. In addition to directly targeting HO-1 itself, increasing attention has been directed toward modulating its upstream signaling pathways to indirectly downregulate HO-1 expression, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of tumor cells to therapeutic agents.

## Targeting the NRF2/HO-1 axis and other upstream regulators
Unlike strategies that directly target the HO-1 protein, this class of approaches acts by blocking upstream signaling pathways such as NRF2, the PI3K/BACH2 axis, and NF-κB, thereby indirectly reducing HO-1 expression or weakening its antioxidant and pro-survival output and ultimately restoring tumor cell sensitivity to therapy. As described earlier, FLT3-ITD-mutated cells generate elevated levels of ROS, thereby activating the NRF2/HO-1 pathway and acquiring anti-apoptotic and drug-resistant phenotypes. Kanna et al. reported that the NRF2 inhibitor brusatol markedly downregulated HO-1 protein levels and suppressed AML cell proliferation; when combined with the FLT3 inhibitor quizartinib, it exhibited synergistic anti-leukemic effects both in vitro and in vivo, significantly prolonging survival and reducing tumor burden Ma et al. further investigated therapeutic strategies targeting upstream regulators of HO-1. Their study revealed that NHE1 induces HO-1 expression by increasing intracellular pH and activating the PKC-β/p38 MAPK/NRF2 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing anti-apoptotic capacity and promoting IM resistance. Based on this mechanism, they employed the NHE1 inhibitor cariporide or interventions targeting upstream nodes (e.g., PKC-β inhibitor LY333531, p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580, or NRF2 silencing), which significantly reduced HO-1 expression; when combined with IM, these interventions markedly enhanced apoptosis induction in resistant cells. Additionally, acute promyelocytic leukemia (M3-type AML), a curable subtype of AML, is almost universally characterized by the PML-RARA fusion and is highly sensitive to all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide (ATO). Valenzuela et al. found that ATRA suppresses NRF2 nuclear translocation, thereby reducing HO-1 expression and enhancing ATO-induced cytotoxicity. These findings suggest that targeting NRF2 to indirectly suppress HO-1 expression could represent a promising and translationally relevant therapeutic strategy.
In lymphoma, HO-1/HMOX1 expression is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, with PI3K/BACH2 and NF-κB being particularly prominent. HMOX1 is a downstream target gene of the transcription factor BACH2, and its expression is directly associated with BTZ efficacy. Chen et al. found that in BTZ-resistant MCL cells, BACH2 fails to translocate into the nucleus to repress anti-apoptotic and antioxidant genes, including HMOX1. PI3K-mediated phosphorylation of BACH2 causes its cytoplasmic retention; treatment with PI3K inhibitors (LY294002, CAL-101) or a nuclear export inhibitor (LMB) promotes BACH2 nuclear localization, thereby indirectly downregulating HO-1 and reversing drug resistance. In ABC-DLBCL, Huang et al. demonstrated that sustained NF-κB activation drives elevated HO-1 expression; combining the NF-κB inhibitor Bay11-7082 with HMOX1 siRNA markedly reduced HO-1 protein levels in OCI-ly10 cells and enhanced apoptosis. Overall, targeting these upstream regulatory nodes offers additional intervention points beyond direct HO-1 inhibition.

## HDAC inhibitors targeting HO-1-related epigenetic networks
As discussed earlier, activation of HDACs contributes to maintaining HO-1 transcriptional activity, whereas HDACi suppress this process, thereby disrupting HO-1-related signaling pathways, markedly inducing tumor cell apoptosis, and offering a novel therapeutic approach to overcome HO-1-mediated drug resistance. In recent years, the application of HDACi as a targeted strategy in MM has attracted considerable attention. Tang et al. demonstrated that the pan-HDAC inhibitor panobinostat, in combination with lenalidomide, suppressed the HO-1/IRF4/MYC axis, activated a caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathway, and overcame MM cell resistance to monotherapy. In a follow-up study, Tang et al. further revealed that the novel class I HDAC inhibitor BG45 downregulated HO-1 protein expression via inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, activated apoptotic signaling, induced cell-cycle arrest, and exhibited synergistic anti-myeloma effects when combined with lenalidomide. Moreover, Li et al. reported that the selective HDAC4/5 inhibitor LMK-235 induced MM cell apoptosis by activating the JNK/AP-1 signaling pathway through HO-1 suppression. In leukemia, Wang et al. investigated the role of HDACi in reversing drug resistance and found that the broad-spectrum HDAC inhibitor AR-42 counteracted HO-1 overexpression-induced HDAC upregulation, thereby synergistically promoting apoptosis. In ALL, the selective HDAC4/5 inhibitor LMK-235 reduced Smad7 and HO-1 expression by inhibiting HDAC activity and increasing histone acetylation levels, ultimately inducing apoptosis via suppression of p-AKT signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HDACi such as BG45, panobinostat, AR-42, and LMK-235 can target HO-1 through distinct signaling pathways, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy in hematological malignancies and providing a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance.

## Ferroptosis-inducing strategies targeting the NRF2/HO-1 axis
Ferroptosis is a form of non-apoptotic cell death dependent on Fe2+, in which iron-catalyzed Fenton reactions trigger lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, ultimately compromising cell viability. As a critical downstream effector in the antioxidant response of the NRF2 pathway, HO-1 exhibits a distinct dual role in ferroptosis regulation—both enhancing antioxidant defenses and promoting iron overload through the release of large amounts of Fe2+.
In AML, induction of ferroptosis has emerged as a promising targeted therapeutic strategy capable of overcoming chemoresistance. The natural biphenolic compound honokiol upregulates HO-1 expression, activating a noncanonical ferroptotic pathway that suppresses AML cell proliferation, induces cell-cycle arrest, and disrupts iron homeostasis—effects experimentally validated in THP-1, U-937, and SKM-1 cell lines. Similarly, Z-ligustilide (Z-LIG) exploits the dual effects of the NRF2/HO-1 pathway: mild activation protects cells and inhibits ferroptosis, whereas excessive activation triggers ROS-mediated iron accumulation, markedly suppressing tumor progression in xenograft models. 
In DHL and PTCL, targeting the NRF2/HO-1 pathway has likewise demonstrated potential for ferroptosis induction. Setiawan et al. reported that concurrent inhibition of BTK and BCL-2 synergistically induces ferroptosis in DHL. Both in vitro and in vivo, combined BTK/BCL-2 inhibition markedly downregulated the NRF2/HO-1 signaling axis, reducing cellular tolerance to lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, thereby triggering ferroptosis and effectively overcoming drug resistance. In PTCL, Hu et al. found that highly expressed PTGDS directly binds to HO-1 and negatively regulates its expression and activity. Treatment with the PTGDS inhibitor AT56 significantly enhanced HO-1-mediated heme degradation and ferritinophagy, resulting in increased intracellular labile iron, disruption of iron homeostasis, and subsequent ferroptosis, effectively suppressing tumor progression. 
In MM, targeting the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway to induce ferroptosis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Andrographolide activates the p38 MAPK signaling pathway, inhibits NRF2 nuclear translocation and HO-1 expression, disrupts the antioxidant defense in MM cells, and induces Fe2+ accumulation and elevated ROS levels. This leads to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, markedly suppressing MM cell proliferation. Functional validation via qPCR, western blot, and ferroptosis marker analysis confirmed that the p38/NRF2/HO-1 axis plays a key role, and andrographolide can reverse chemoresistance. Similarly, ethanol extract of Eclipta prostrata stabilizes Keap1 (a negative regulator of NRF2), thereby blocking NRF2 nuclear translocation and suppressing HMOX1 along with other antioxidant genes such as GPX4. This promotes ROS and lipid peroxidation accumulation, triggers ferroptosis, and significantly inhibits tumor growth in xenograft mouse models. In addition, the novel G9a inhibitor DCG066 exerts epigenetic modulation by inhibiting H3K9me2 methylation, disrupting the NRF2/HO-1 pathway, downregulating HO-1 expression, reducing antioxidant capacity, enhancing ROS production and mitochondrial damage, and inducing ferroptosis—effectively reversing resistance to conventional chemotherapy. In contrast, Huachansu (a traditional Chinese medicine preparation containing bufadienolides) activates the NRF2/HO-1 pathway, upregulates HO-1, and increases expression of iron transport proteins such as PRP and ZIP8. This results in excessive Fe2+ accumulation, triggers Fenton reaction-mediated ROS burst and lipid peroxidation, and induces iron overload-type ferroptosis. Both in vitro and in vivo, Huachansu showed efficacy comparable to BTZ, and its effect could be reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1, indicating a ferroptosis-dependent mechanism. 
Overall, the above strategies induce ferroptosis across different hematologic malignancies by modulating the NRF2/HO-1 axis or HO-1 itself. Moderate activation of HO-1 enhances antioxidant capacity and inhibits ferroptosis, whereas excessive activation or disruption of iron homeostasis leads to Fe2+ overload, increased Fenton reactions, ROS surges, and lipid peroxidation, ultimately resulting in ferroptotic cell death. However, clinical translation remains challenging, as interpatient variability in the HO-1 expression threshold, drug toxicity, and long-term efficacy require further investigation. Future studies should clarify the HO-1 threshold differences among tumor subtypes, evaluate drug safety and sustained efficacy, and explore combination regimens with conventional therapies and other ferroptosis inducers to advance the clinical application of this emerging targeted strategy.

## Targeting HO-1 downstream effectors and the immune microenvironment
In HO-1-mediated tumor drug resistance and immune evasion, its downstream effectors and signaling pathways play pivotal roles. Beyond degrading heme into CO, Fe2+, and biliverdin via its enzymatic activity to modulate oxidative stress and apoptosis, HO-1 also engages multiple signaling cascades that drive resistance in tumor cells. Targeting these downstream effectors not only circumvents the limitations posed by compensatory upregulation of HO-1 enzymatic activity but also enables precise dismantling of resistance mechanisms through synergistic blockade. Consequently, in addition to directly inhibiting HO-1 itself, growing research efforts are focusing on interventions against its downstream signaling molecules to achieve more precise and multilayered antitumor effects.
He et al. previously demonstrated that in MDS, overexpressed HO-1 promotes tumor cell survival and enhances therapeutic resistance by activating the pRB-E2F pathway, which upregulates EZH2 expression and strengthens EZH2-dependent epigenetic silencing and anti-apoptotic effects. Treatment with an EZH2 inhibitor, such as GSK126, markedly reduces EZH2 expression and decreases H3K27me3 deposition, thereby effectively reversing cellular drug resistance. 
Zhang et al. found that HO-1 can cooperate with the deacetylase Sirt1 to suppress CD48 expression, thereby impairing NK cell recognition and cytotoxicity against AML cells. This mechanism involves H3K27 deacetylation at the CD48 promoter region, leading to epigenetic transcriptional repression. Treatment with a Sirt1 inhibitor, such as selisistat, reduces Sirt1 activity, blocks H3K27 deacetylation, reverses HO-1-mediated CD48 suppression, and restores NK cell cytotoxic function. Further studies revealed that in FLT3-ITD–mutated AML, HO-1 is also a critical driver of gilteritinib resistance. HO-1 activates the STAT6 signaling pathway, thereby promoting leukemic cell survival and attenuating gilteritinib-induced apoptosis. Inhibition of this pathway with a STAT6 inhibitor, such as AS1517499, effectively downregulates anti-apoptotic gene expression and restores drug sensitivity to gilteritinib. 
Wang et al. found that high HO-1 expression in BMSCs from both B-ALL and CML patients is closely associated with the drug resistance of leukemic cells. In B-ALL, HO-1 activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to promote VEGF secretion by BMSCs, thereby enhancing tumor cell survival, inhibiting apoptosis, and inducing resistance to the commonly used chemotherapeutic agent VCR. In CML, upregulation of HO-1 stimulates BMSCs to secrete the chemokine CXCL12, which activates the PI3K/Akt pathway in leukemic cells via the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis. This, in turn, increases the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and suppresses caspase-3 activity, markedly enhancing resistance to IM. Further studies showed that the PI3K/Akt inhibitor LY294002 effectively blocks the activation of this pathway, attenuates the protective effects of BMSCs on B-ALL and CML cells, and restores their sensitivity to VCR and IM , ( ).
 

## Conclusion and perspectives
In recent years, the role of HO-1 in drug resistance in hematologic malignancies has attracted increasing attention. A growing body of evidence indicates that HMOX1/HO-1 is not merely a classical “antioxidant/cytoprotective enzyme,” but rather a multi-dimensional resistance hub that integrates enzymatic functions, non-enzymatic activities, and immune microenvironmental regulation. On the one hand, HO-1-derived metabolites buffer ROS and iron overload, thereby suppressing cell death; on the other hand, HO-1 drives clonal evolution and treatment failure through nuclear transcriptional regulation, epigenetic reprogramming, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Although the functional patterns of HO-1 vary considerably across disease entities and stages—and may even display a “dual” cytoprotective versus cytotoxic profile—systematic mapping of its multilayered networks provides a clear conceptual framework and several testable hypotheses for subsequent therapeutic intervention.
At the mechanistic level, most studies still attribute the pro-resistance effects of HO-1 primarily to its potent antioxidant capacity, by which it blunts chemotherapy-induced ROS bursts, preserves mitochondrial integrity, and inhibits caspase activation, thereby counteracting drug-induced cytotoxicity. , However, the catalytic products of HO-1—CO, Fe2+, and biliverdin/bilirubin—are themselves important signaling mediators and metabolic regulators, and the fine-tuned roles of CO in cell-cycle control, anti-apoptosis, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression remain incompletely understood. In parallel, the non-enzymatic functions of HO-1 have gained increasing recognition, including its protein-protein interactions with transcription factors such as NRF2, its participation in stress-responsive transcriptional reprogramming, and its regulation of DNA damage responses. , Future work should further dissect the HO-1 resistance network along enzymatic and non-enzymatic dimensions, distinguishing the relative contributions of its metabolic products from those of the HO-1 protein scaffold itself, in order to design more precisely targeted therapeutic strategies.
Reprogramming of HO-1 subcellular localization is one of the key entry points for understanding its atypical functions. Existing data suggest that N-terminally truncated, nucleus-translocated HO-1 no longer relies on classical enzymatic activity but instead stabilizes NRF2, modulates gene transcription, and promotes genomic instability, thereby conferring enhanced stress adaptation and acquired resistance in tumors such as CML and MM . How HO-1 truncation, nuclear translocation, and its precise interactions with chromatin regulators are orchestrated at the molecular level remains an important unresolved question. Building on clues from our previous work, nuclear HO-1 translocation may be linked to increased lactate metabolism, the formation of an acidic microenvironment, and T cell exhaustion in malignant cells, opening new directions for investigating HO-1 at the intersection of metabolism, epigenetics, and immunity. The development of selective inhibitors that target nuclear HO-1 or specific protein-interaction interfaces may provide a pharmacologic lever to selectively block its non-enzymatic resistance functions.
At the level of the tumor microenvironment and immune regulation, HO-1 is increasingly recognized as a critical node connecting myeloid cells, lymphocytes, and stromal elements. Current evidence indicates that HO-1 and its metabolite CO promote polarization of TAMs toward an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype, enhance the stability and apoptosis resistance of Tregs, inhibit the proliferation of effector T cells, and downregulate molecules such as HLA-C and CD48 that are essential for NK-cell-mediated immune surveillance. , , , Collectively, these findings outline a coherent picture in which HO-1 helps construct an immunosuppressive niche that provides an external protective shield for malignant clones. Future studies should more finely resolve the dose dependence and temporal dynamics of HO-1/CO signaling across different immune cell subsets, and explore the potential for synergy between the HO-1 regulatory axis and immunotherapeutic strategies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells, chimeric antigen receptor-modified NK (CAR-NK) cells, and bispecific antibodies. Such combinations may enable concurrent targeting of both tumor-intrinsic and microenvironment-dependent resistance mechanisms.
Iron homeostasis and ferroptotic pathways offer another attractive—but technically challenging—avenue for HO-1-based therapy. Current evidence suggests that low to moderate activation of the NRF2/HO-1 axis tends to suppress ferroptosis and promote tumor cell survival by clearing heme, limiting lipid peroxidation, and maintaining a balance between ferritin-mediated iron storage and iron export. By contrast, when HO-1 is massively induced or iron-export mechanisms are saturated, Fe2+ overload and Fenton reaction-driven lipid peroxidation can instead trigger lethal ferroptosis. , Defining, in a quantitative and context-specific manner, the threshold at which HO-1 shifts from an iron-protective to a pro-ferroptotic mode in different tumor types and therapeutic settings—and identifying a safe and effective clinical intervention window—will be essential for translating the HO-1–ferroptosis axis into a viable treatment strategy.
The reciprocal regulation between epigenetic modifiers and HO-1 is complex and not yet fully understood. Key enzymes such as EZH2 and DNMT1 are closely linked to HO-1 expression and cooperate in mediating tumor resistance, but the detailed architecture of their mutual regulatory networks remains obscure and warrants more in-depth investigation.
From a genetic standpoint, HMOX1 promoter (GT)n repeat length and the T(−413)A polymorphism have been shown to influence both basal and inducible HO-1 expression, and have been associated with chemotherapy resistance in childhood ALL as well as relapse risk after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. , These findings suggest that HMOX1 polymorphisms may serve as potential biomarkers for patient stratification and individualized therapy. Large, prospective cohorts are urgently needed to systematically evaluate the relationships among HMOX1 genetic variants, baseline and treatment-induced HO-1 dynamics, and long-term clinical outcomes, in order to determine whether these polymorphisms can be incorporated into risk-scoring systems and therapeutic decision-making algorithms.
On the translational front, several major challenges must be overcome before HO-1 can be fully exploited as a drug target in the clinic. First, HO-1 function is highly context-dependent: the same intervention may exert cytoprotective or cytotoxic/ferroptotic effects depending on tumor type, disease stage, and treatment combination, imposing stringent requirements on dose selection and treatment scheduling. Second, most current HO-1 inhibitors—particularly metalloporphyrins—lack sufficient selectivity for HO-1 over HO-2 and exhibit broad off-target effects. Achieving selective inhibition of HMOX1/HO-1 without compromising HO-2 function or disrupting physiological tissue protection remains a central challenge in medicinal chemistry. Third, NRF2, BACH1/BACH2 and other parallel antioxidant pathways have substantial compensatory potential. At the level of oxidative stress responses, HMOX1 is embedded within a broader NRF2-dependent antioxidant network, and it is therefore plausible that pharmacologic inhibition of HO-1 alone may be partially buffered by NRF2-driven induction of other antioxidant and detoxifying proteins, as suggested by several studies in solid tumors and selected hematologic models. By contrast, it remains largely unclear whether comparable compensatory mechanisms operate within the non-enzymatic module of HO-1, particularly nuclear HO-1-mediated transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming, due to a lack of systematic investigation. Overall, these considerations imply that single-node inhibition of HO-1 is likely to be attenuated by higher-order network remodeling, underscoring the need for future studies to prioritize pathway-level combinations and network-oriented interventions rather than HO-1 blockade in isolation.
In summary, HO-1 participates in a multilayered and multifaceted regulatory network that underlies therapeutic resistance in hematologic malignancies. This network encompasses not only enzymatic, metabolism- and redox-related functions but also non-enzymatic activities, including nuclear translocation, epigenetic reprogramming, and remodeling of the immune and microenvironmental landscape. Future research should further elucidate the fine mechanisms of HO-1-mediated resistance, with particular emphasis on the roles of its metabolic products, subcellular localization, microenvironmental effects, and epigenetic regulation. In parallel, the development of more selective agents targeting HO-1 and its associated signaling pathways may offer an important avenue for overcoming treatment resistance and improving clinical outcomes in hematologic malignancies.
