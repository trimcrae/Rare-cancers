## Abstract
Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been recognized as significant modulators of gene expression and are essential for various biological functions, even though they don’t appear to have the ability to encode proteins. Originally considered dark matter, lncRNAs have been recognized as being dysregulated and contributing to the onset, progression, and resistance to treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). AML is a prevalent type of leukemia characterized by the disruption of myeloid cell differentiation, leading to an increased number of immature myeloid progenitor cells. Currently, the need for novel biomarkers and treatment targets to enhance therapeutic alternatives has led to a focus on lncRNAs as possible indicators for prognostic, therapeutic, and diagnostic systems in various human cancers, including AML. Recent research has recognized a limited set of lncRNAs as possible prognostic biomarkers or diagnoses in AML. This review evaluates the key research that highlights the significance of lncRNAs in AML and discusses their roles and impacts on the disease. Furthermore, we intend to underscore the importance of lncRNAs as new and trustworthy markers for the diagnosis, prediction, drug resistance, and targets for treatment in AML.

## Introduction
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is classified as a form of blood cancer that is very aggressive and exhibits a variety of cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities. It is characterized by unregulated cell proliferation, impaired cell maturation, and the accretion of leukemic cells found in peripheral blood and bone marrow [ , ]. AML presents a mortality rate that surpasses 80%, with a significant impact on patients aged 60 and older [ , ]. Genetic mutations, epigenetic abnormalities, and molecular heterogeneity are critical factors in the initiation progression, and relapse of AML [ ]. Even though there are an increasing number of options for treating leukemia, including immunotherapy, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the disease’s prognosis is still determined due to the molecular heterogeneity of AML and the unpredictability of medication effectiveness and chemoresistance [ , ]. The clinical development of AML is impacted by the genetic composition and mutations discovered in leukemic blasts. Recent research has revealed a wide range of possible uses for noncoding RNA (ncRNA) transcripts in the context of AML. These transcripts regulate different cellular processes without encoding proteins [ ].
LncRNAs are characterized as RNA molecules that exceed 200 nucleotides in length, and they are not associated with protein-encoding [ ]. The importance of lncRNAs is becoming more apparent as they are essential for controlling gene expression, preserving homeostasis, and contributing to the onset of various diseases [ ]. Alternatively, numerous lncRNAs have been recognized as possible markers in a variety of cancer types and inhibitors of tumor growth that control significant apoptotic processes, emphasizing the diverse functions of these genetic constituents [ ]. An expanding compilation of lncRNAs playing a significant role in regulating hematopoietic gene expression complexes has been recognized. Currently, we are aware of multiple single lncRNAs that affect lineage determination; their dysregulation can result in hematopoietic malignancy throughout the hematopoietic hierarchy [ ]. Hence, investigations of the function of lncRNAs in AML pathogenesis emphasize their possible utility as biomarkers for both diagnosis and prognosis and the prospects for future treatment options [ ].
We sought to provide a thorough summary of the most recent developments regarding lncRNAs in AML in this review, focusing on their biological roles, underlying mechanisms, and upstream regulation. We will highlight their capabilities in diagnosis and prognosis, with particular attention to their potential therapeutic relevance in this disease.

## Genetic features of acute myeloid leukemia
AML is acknowledged as a disorder defined by remarkable genetic heterogeneity, both cellularly and molecularly, where chromosomal abnormalities, either quantitative values (hyperdiploid and hypodiploid) or structural modifications (amplifications, translocations, and insertions/deletions), are present. Epigenetic changes are also commonly associated with AML [ , ]. Various genetic abnormalities lead to distinct clinical pathways, which significantly influence the overall prognosis. AML is distinguished by various molecular characteristics, with a notable feature being its relatively low mutation load, particularly in the extensively researched coding genome [ ]. Epigenetic modifications are also essential for leukemogenesis, as evidenced by the common mutations in genes that act as epigenetic modifiers in AML [ ]. This condition is marked by alterations in transcription factors (AML1, ETS, PAX5, IKZF1, EBF1, ETV6, and STAT), suppressor genes (TP53, RB1, CDKN2A/CDKN2B, etc.), oncogenes (ABL1, ABL2, CSF1R, JAK2, PDGFRB, and CRLF2), B lymphoid cell differentiators (IKZF1, TCF3, EBF1, PAX5, and VPREB1), growth signaling pathways (NRAS, KRAS, PTPN11, FLT3), chromatin remodelers such as t(15;17) (PML-RARa), t(8;21) (AML1-ETO), and inv [ ] (CBFB/MYH11) are frequent, or epigenetic modifiers (DNMT3A, CREBBP, MLL2, NSD2, EP300, ARID1A, TET2, and CHD6). Additionally, mutations in various genes, such as FLT3, IDH2, NPM1, WT1, and CEBPA, and, more recently, TP53 have been recognized as factors for risk stratification. Alterations in the expression patterns of specific genes have also been identified as crucial in the diagnosis and treatment of AML [ – ]. A key characteristic of AML is the disruption in the differentiation process from myeloblasts to mature myeloid cells. The presence of immature myeloblasts indicates a blockage in this differentiation, a diagnostic hallmark of the disease [ ]. Advancements in high-throughput transcriptome analysis have enabled the identification of new biomarkers related with AML. This research goes beyond protein-coding genes and focuses on [ ].

## LncRNAs involved in myelopoiesis
Approximately 3% of the transcriptome of the human genome is widely recognized to be translated into biologically active proteins [ ]. Most genes that have been transcribed remain present at the RNA level. As a result, lncRNAs are arbitrarily defined as transcripts without coding potential [ , ]. Importantly, a single lncRNA can organize gene regulation across several levels through a variety of mechanisms of operation regarding its capacity to communicate with proteins, DNA, and RNA [ ]. One critical aspect of hematopoietic differentiation is the regulation of gene expression [ ].
Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are distinguished by their capability to undergo a series of differentiated cell identities, such as self-renewal, regulated expansion of progenitor cells, and precise maturation into mature blood cells [ ]. Recent extensive genome-wide investigations conducted on HSCs and lineage-primed multipotent progenitors have uncovered the manifestation of numerous lncRNAs alongside lineage-specific transcription factors (TFs) within these cells. Lineage-specific lncRNA molecules are recognized as significant hematopoiesis-related gene expression regulators (Fig.  ).
 Numerous lncRNAs have strong associations with TFs, particularly with a lineage, contributing significantly to the processes of hematopoietic differentiation and cell fate determination. This suggests lncRNAs may provide an additional level of regulatory mechanisms operating within the hierarchical structure of HSCs [ ]. Distinct lncRNA signatures were found to be associated with NPM1, FLT3‐ITD, RUNX1, IDH2, and CEBPα. Notably, these lncRNAs exhibited correlations with the expression of microRNAs (miRNAs) and messenger RNAs that showed significant enrichment in lymphocyte activation, inflammation, and apoptosis [ , ]. To evaluate functional lncRNAs in HSCs, recent studies have comprehensively identified lncRNAs specific to HSCs [ ]. Several lncRNAs play significant roles in regulating long-term HSCs (LT-HSCs) quiescence and self-renewal and have been implicated in the process of normal hematopoiesis. LT-HSCs express lncRNA H19 (H19) at high levels, and as LT-HSCs differentiate into multipotent progenitors and short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs), H19 expression gradually decreases. Preserving the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic cells during their early developmental stages is an important function of lncRNA metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) [ ]. One of the first myeloid lineage-specific lncRNAs found to be expressed from the human HOXA cluster was HOXA transcript antisense RNA, myeloid-specific 1 (HOTAIRM1). It has been proposed to participate in the myeloid differentiation lineage and has a role in controlling the advancement of granulocytes [ ]. Additionally, two lncRNAs associated with HOX genes, HOXBLINC and HOTTIP, play major roles in regulating HSC self-renewal and lineage dedication [ , ].

## LncRNAs as reliable indicators of prognosis and diagnosis
The increasing importance of the role of lncRNAs in cancer provides a chance to uncover new AML oncogenes [ ]. Furthermore, in addition to their involvement in the advancement of AML, lncRNAs can also function as significant prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers. The levels of lncRNA expression can anticipate the clinical characteristics and results of AML [ ]. AML research has typically focused on the protein arena. However, emerging studies recently placed increasing emphasis on non-coding transcripts involved in pathology. High-throughput analyses have demonstrated that the human genome is actively transcribed into a pool of several thousand lncRNAs, and they are not helpful in the translation [ ]. As a different molecular biology tool, RNA sequencing provides insight into numerous aspects of cancer biology, including insights into genomic aberrations, transcript isoforms, mutations, copy number variations, alternative splicing, and posttranscriptional modifications of expression profiles [ – ]. Recently introduced next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology—boasting high specificity and sensitivity—has opened up vast new horizons for understanding the genetic pathways altered in human cancers. It has revolutionized the ability to simultaneously screen multiple mutated genes across various cancers [ ]. An enhanced ability to identify key cancer-associated genetic alterations in solid tumors increases diagnostic specificity, prognostic value, and therapeutic targeting in the improvement of different patient outcomes [ ]. A plethora of research has validated the use of NGS for the examination of genetic variation and tumor mutation burden in solid tumors like colorectal, gastric, and breast cancers [ – ]. Advances in technology, including high-throughput experiments and CRISPR-based genetic screening, have enabled researchers to explain the functions of lncRNAs and compile them in specialized databases [ ]. The techniques presently employed for the identification and quantification of lncRNA biomarkers in biopsies primarily include RNA sequencing, qRT-PCR, and microarrays. Inconsistencies in detection methods, such as differences in lncRNA expression between sample types (e.g., bone marrow mononuclear cells versus plasma), genetic heterogeneity, disease progression, and individual biological variability [ ]. These factors can influence lncRNA expression, complicating the establishment of standardized diagnostic or prognostic thresholds. LncRNA expression may be affected by various patient-specific factors, such as genetic background, disease stage, and individual biological differences [ ]. These variations can impact the reliability of lncRNAs as universal biomarkers for AML. The expression levels of specific lncRNAs can differ among patients, complicating the establishment of standardized diagnostic or prognostic thresholds. To improve the reliability of lncRNA-based biomarkers in AML, the following strategies are recommended: (a) Developing and adhering to standardized protocols for sample collection, processing, and analysis can reduce technical variability. (b) Consensus on suitable internal reference genes for normalization in qRT-PCR assays is essential for accurate quantification. (c) Multicenter studies with diverse patient cohorts can account for biological variability and confirm the clinical utility of lncRNA biomarkers [ – ]. By addressing these challenges, the integration of lncRNA-based biomarkers into clinical practice for AML diagnosis and prognosis can be significantly improved.

## LncRNA PANDAR
Elevated expression of the promoter of CDKN1A antisense DNA damage-activated RNA (PANDAR) lncRNA is linked to a low complete remission (CR) rate and detrimental prognosis in AML patients. Yang et al. confirmed an upregulation of PANDAR lncRNA in 119 patients with recently diagnosed AML, and elevated PANDAR expression was related to a poor prognosis [ ]. PANDAR is expressed in high levels in patients with AML compared to healthy donors, and analysis of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve indicates that PANDAR may function as a valuable diagnostic biomarker for differentiating between AML patients and healthy individuals. Additionally, elevated levels of PANDAR were linked to increased AML blasts, advanced patient age, and unfavorable karyotypes; however, no correlation was found between common gene mutations. A study indicated that the expression of PANDAR at diagnosis is an autonomous risk factor that affects chemotherapy responses, with patients exhibiting high levels of expression being at a greater risk of being resistant to therapy [ ]. Furthermore, PANDAR expression has prognostic significance; patients with elevated PANDAR expression exhibit a reduced level of CR and shorter overall survival (OS) than patients with minimal PANDAR expression [ ].

## LncRNA HOTAIRM1
Notably, patients exhibiting elevated HOTAIRM1 levels typically have lower rates of OS and relapse-free survival [ ]. Díaz-Beyá et al. analyzed research on 241 AML patients with different cytogenetic subtypes to examine HOTAIRM1 expression. Their findings suggest a significant association between increased HOTAIRM1 expression and adverse prognostic outcomes, including shorter OS and heightened rates of relapse [ ]. However, there was no correlation between the levels of HOTAIRM1 expression and the likelihood of achieving CR or the evaluation of allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (alloHSCT) executed during the initial CR. These results were confirmed through examination of data from the Leukemia Gene Atlas database [ ]. Consequently, the evaluation of HOTAIRM1 expression levels provides critical prognostic information for patients with AML, facilitating more precise risk classification based on frequently identified molecular markers [ ].

## LncRNA MEG3
Numerous studies have shown that the promoter of lncRNA maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3) is hypermethylated, which is linked to a notable decrease in the OS of patients with AML. Benetatos et al. reported that the MEG3 lncRNA is hypermethylated in approximately 48% of AML cases, which notably negatively impacts the OS rates of these patients [ ]. Additionally, another investigation confirmed the link between MEG3 hypermethylation and longer disease-free survival (DFS) [ ].

## LncRNA IRAIN
Patients diagnosed with AML exhibit a notable reduction in the expression of IGF1R antisense imprinted nonprotein-coding RNA (IRAIN), suggesting its potential as a valuable prognostic biomarker [ ]. Studies suggest that patients who exhibited an inadequate response to chemotherapy and those who experienced relapse demonstrated a greater probability of exhibiting reduced initial IRAIN expression [ ]. Likewise, IRAIN expression is downregulated in leukemia cell lines. Thus, IRAIN might serve as a reliable marker for assessing an AML patient’s prognosis [ ]. Reduced IRAIN lncRNA has been associated with higher counts of blast and white blood cells, as well as high risk, poor prognosis, and decreased OS and reduced DFS [ ]. Currently, limited publisher reports suggest that lncRNAs can function as valuable tools for the early diagnosis and prognosis of AML. Consequently, further in-depth research is essential to identify additional novel lncRNAs and to explore their relationships with clinical features, mutations, and patient outcomes [ ].

## LncRNA HOTAIR
The HOTAIR (HOX Transcript Antisense RNA) is one of the well characterized oncogenic lncRNAs, that exhibits elevated expression levels at the time of diagnosis when compared to healthy individuals. Furthermore, its expression significantly decreases following treatment in contrast to untreated AML patients [ ]. The post-treatment expression level of HOTAIR was notably associated with poor OS, reduced relapse-free survival, and may serve as a predictor for susceptibility to relapse [ ].

## The roles of lncRNAs in AML

## Regulation and dysregulation of lncRNAs in AML
Several comprehensive expression studies in recent years have exhibited that lncRNAs have essential functions in the development of AML. These studies underscored the correlation between specific lncRNA profiles and genetically distinct subtypes of AML. The heterogeneity of AML is evident in the unique expression profiles of lncRNA noted in various subtypes. LncRNAs have a large impact on numerous essential signaling pathways during the progression of AML [ , ]. They can form signatures with independent prognostic abilities that can be used to subcategorize AML patients, just as they can in the case of lncRNA signatures that describe the biology of leukemic stem cells (LSCs) [ ]. LncRNAs in leukemia have been extensively studied, revealing their diverse functions as crucial regulators of proliferation, differentiation, resistance to therapy, biomarkers, and promising therapeutic targets [ ]. The disruption of lncRNA levels in leukemia can be attributed to various factors, including genetic alterations, chromosomal changes, and epigenetic modifications [ ]. In the context of AML, the process of differentiation is impeded by numerous mechanisms, such as chromosomal translocations and the resulting atypical expression of TFs that are essential in the event of differentiation processes [ ]. LncRNAs are essential in many different cellular functions, including affecting DNA repair pathways, maintaining genome integrity, and controlling the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes through epigenetic alterations [ , ]. Therefore, focusing on lncRNAs to impede the unregulated proliferation of AML cells represents a promising opportunity for treatment. Multiple investigations have shown that the expression of lncRNAs is often unique in AML cells, indicating that treatments focused on lncRNAs may provide an advantage over conventional chemotherapies that affect rapidly dividing cells indiscriminately [ ]. Currently, ex vivo studies account for the majority of lncRNA research in AML, and the precise mechanisms underlying lncRNA deregulation in AML are significantly less understood in addition to their biological functions [ ].

## LncRNA HOTAIRM1
HOTAIRM1 is a lncRNA that is predominantly expressed in myeloid cells; it is antisense transcribed to the HOXA cluster and is located between the HOXA1 and HOXA2 genes [ ]. Notably, a conventional lncRNA associated with the development of AML, HOTAIRM1, is an intragenic lncRNA that is expressed mainly at the end of granulocytic differentiation and controls the maturation and differentiation of myeloid cells in normal hematopoiesis. In normal circumstances, its expression appears to be limited to the lineage of myeloid cells [ ]. Studies using acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) human cell line NB4 have demonstrated that HOTAIRM1 controls the regulation of gene expression linked to myeloid differentiation, such as CD11b and CD18, and that the all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), which causes the differentiation of granulocytes, is impeded by silencing of this gene [ ]. The induced granulocytic differentiation of the NB4 cell line was hampered by knocking down HOTAIRM1, which resulted in a notably greater population of developing and immature cells transitioning from the cell cycle’s G1 to S phases [ ]. Patients with AML who had intermediate cytogenetic risk were found to have overexpressed HOTAIRM1, especially when NPM1 gene mutations were present [ ]. Researchers have reported that HOTAIRM1 provides structural support within the nucleus, facilitating the assembly of the murine double minute 2-early growth response protein 1 (MDM2-EGR1) complex’s assembly and elevating the degradation of the EGR1 transcription factor. Conversely, when situated in the cytoplasm, HOTAIRM1 functions as a storage site for miR-152-3p, consequently promoting unc-51-like kinase 3 (ULK3) expression and initiating autophagy [ ]. During the end-stage of granulocytic differentiation, HOTAIRM1 is mainly expressed. By targeting miR148b, HOTAIRM1 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) that may facilitate the growth of AML cells and inhibit apoptosis [ ]. Given that HOTAIRM1 is correlated with a shorter OS and has elevated expression levels in AML patients, it could be utilized as a useful molecular indicator associated with a bad prognosis [ ]. Based on the information provided, this lncRNA has significant potential as a target for the development and implementation of targeted therapy.

## LncRNA UCA1
The lncRNA urothelial carcinoma-associated 1 (UCA1) has been documented to regulate AML cell proliferation. In an in vitro study, UCA1 enhanced cell viability, migration, and invasion while also inhibiting apoptotic pathways, indicating its oncogenic functions in AML. Notably, a study reported that the lncRNA UCA1 is elevated in samples from AML patients and promotes the growth of AML cell lines. Suppressing UCA1 via short hairpin RNA transduction results in a notable reduce in cell proliferation and G1 cell cycle detain [ ]. Targeting miR-126 allows UCA1 to activate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling cascades. AML cells become more viable, migrate, and invade as a result of this stimulation [ ]. Selective interactions between UCA1 and various miRNAs, including miR-204, miR-96-5p, and miR-296-3p, have been discussed in numerous recent studies. The oncogenic possibility of UCA1 in AML is demonstrated through these interactions [ , ]. UCA1 is defined as an RNA scaffold that is essential in the proper development of erythrocytes and hematopoiesis [ ].

## LncRNA NEAT1
The lncRNA nuclear paraspeckle assembly transcript 1 (NEAT1), a prevalent and plentiful lncRNA, is known to affect the differentiation of AML cells [ ]. NEAT1 downregulation in AML is induced in cell culture studies and can be reversed with ATRA therapy [ ]. While myeloid differentiation is commonly believed to be regulated primarily by specific factors that are specific to myeloid cells, ATRA-responsiveness and essentiality for ATRA-induced myeloid differentiation have been reported for NEAT1 [ ]. Primary AML blasts and THP-1 cells exhibited increased apoptosis and reduced proliferation in response to NEAT1 overexpression. Recently, Zhao et al. reported that NEAT1 competitively binds to miR-23a-3p to control AML cell proliferation by regulating the structural maintenance of chromosome 1 alpha (SMC1A) expression, which in turn triggers cell cycle detain and apoptosis. The cell cycle is inhibited, and apoptosis is ultimately prompted by this regulatory mechanism, which also inhibits the proliferation of AML cells [ ]. A study has highlighted that the lncRNA NEAT1/miR-338-3p axis repressed the AML progression by modulating CREBRF. Additionally, the down-regulation of NEAT1 facilitated by the miR-194-5p/DNMT3A pathway, contributes to the advancement of AML, potentially offering new therapeutic targets for its treatment [ , ].

## LncRNA MEG3
Recent observations indicate that the expression of the lncRNA MEG3, which functions as a significantly increased tumor suppressor, occurs in AML [ ]. Elevated levels of MEG3 expression in adult patients with de novo AML were identified as a significant factor and autonomous prognostic factor for prolonged DFS and OS [ ]; in pediatric patients with newly diagnosed AML, increased expression of MEG3 was found to be linked with increased OS [ , ]. A correlation was observed between elevated MEG3 expression in individuals with AML following induction therapy and improved DFS and OS. On the basis of this discovery, MEG3 expression may serve as a functional biomarker to forecast the course of AML patients’ response to treatment [ ]. First-hand evidence in carcinoma cell lines demonstrated that MEG3 stimulates p53 transcription [ ]; nevertheless, a more recent study also revealed that MEG3 suppresses leukemogenesis by downregulating the DNMT3A gene, which is a p53-independent pathway. The involvement of the lncRNA MEG3 in the development of drug resistance in AML is attributed to the MEG3/miR-155 axis [ , ].

## LncRNA IRAIN
The lncRNA IRAIN is an ncRNA with antisense properties that originates from an insulin-like growth factor type I receptor (IGF1R) intron, an oncogene that stimulates AML cell proliferation [ ]. Leukemic cells exhibit high levels of activation of IRAIN, a receptor tyrosine kinase that is transcribed from IGF1R in the antisense direction. This activation enhances the cells’ ability to proliferate and develop resistance to treatment by initiating the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade mediated by the IGF1R receptor [ ]. AML patients with low IRAIN lncRNA expression have a poor prognosis, indicating that IRAIN may have a tumor-suppressor role. The migration of tumor cells is inhibited by the overexpression of IRAIN lncRNAs. According to Pashaiefar et al., a study involving 64 patients with de novo non-M3 AML revealed that reduced IRAIN expression was linked to a poor prognosis. This was evidenced by elevated white blood cell (WBC) and blast counts, as well as reduced OS and relapse-free survival rates. Additionally, patients who were resistant to chemotherapy and experienced relapse were more inclined to initially have lower levels of IRAIN expression [ ].

## LncRNA HOTAIR
HOTAIR is similarly elevated in multiple AML cell lines. The in vitro silencing of HOTAIR led to a decrease in cell proliferation, triggered apoptosis, and diminished the formation of colony units [ ]. HOTAIR functions as a multilayer regulator and acts as a microRNA sponge. It binds competitively to miR-193a, effectively sequestering it and thereby influencing the expression of c-KIT in AML cells [ ]. HOTAIR inhibits the expression of p15 in trans by inducing H3K27 trimethylation of its promoter, a process facilitated by PRC2 in AML [ ]. HOTAIR additionally attracts DNMT3B, leading to an increase in the methylation of the HOXA5 promoter. Silencing HOTAIR has been shown to trigger apoptosis and diminish the proliferation of AML cells in vivo [ ]. HOTAIR has also been shown to promote EZH2-dependent epigenetic silencing of the tumor suppressor gene p15, suggesting that certain lncRNAs may operate through various mechanisms to orchestrate complex gene regulation. This observation aligns with research conducted in different cellular environments, which illustrates how HOTAIR attracts the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) and the LSD1/coREST/REST complex to influence chromatin dynamics and facilitate epigenetic silencing[ ].

## LncRNA PVT1
The oncogenic activity of the lncRNA plasmacytoma variant translocation 1 (PVT1) is closely linked to cellular myelocytomatosis (c-Myc). Excessive expression of this gene can lead to abnormal growth and division of cancerous cells [ ]. PVT1 is indirectly regulated by c-Myc, which acts as a microRNA precursor and is also responsible for the upregulation of PVT1 [ ]. Garzon et al. developed a 48-lncRNA expression score that correlates with treatment response and survival outcomes in older AML patients with normal cytogenetics. Approximately 10% of patients diagnosed with AML demonstrate an increase in 8q24.21, causing the PVT1 lncRNA and the MYC gene to gain function. PVT1 is also found to be up-regulated in cytogenetically normal AML patients with mutated NPM1 [ , ]. The overexpression of PVT1 might trigger necrosis and apoptosis in AML cell lines [ ]. Furthermore, the concurrent amplification of PVT1 and MYC suggests the potential importance of exploring the correlation or potential causation between the copy number variations of lncRNAs and leukemogenesis [ ].

## LncRNA RUNXOR
The lncRNA RUNX1 overlapping RNA (RUNXOR) is derived from the transcription factor RUNX1 locus [ ]. Hematopoiesis depends on the transcription factor RUNX1, and AML is often linked to chromosomal translocations or mutations that disrupt the ability of RUNX1 to function. RUNX1-ETO, a chimeric protein, is created following the translocation t (8;21) and is found in 30–40% of AML cases [ ]. RUNXOR can utilize its 3′-terminal interaction with the promoter through fragments. RUNXOR, as an enhancer of RUNX1, is significantly engaged in the modulation of the expression of RUNX1 by facilitating the formation of intrachromosomal looping. Additionally, RUNXOR actively participates in long-range interactions between chromosomes, suggesting its potential involvement in chromosomal translocations and leukemia development [ ].

## LncRNA ANRIL
Antisense ncRNAs in the INK4 locus (ANRIL) have been demonstrated to exhibit increased expression levels in AML as well as in various other malignancies. Several mechanisms have been identified in the literature that contribute to the promotion of AML cell proliferation [ ]. The excessive expression of ANRIL inhibits the expression of adiponectin receptor 1 (AdipoR1), an essential controller of the metabolism of glucose, thereby promoting glucose uptake in AML cells. ANRIL has crucial functions in the survival of malignant cells and the progression of AML. Moreover, increased levels of ANRIL have been shown to improve the absorption of glucose in AML cells through suppression of AdipoR1 expression, an essential controller of glucose metabolism [ ]. Wang et al. documented that ANRIL acts as a sponge for miR-34, a tumor suppressor, disrupting miR-34’s regulation of histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1). This inhibition of apoptosis, combined with the activity of ANRIL, promotes the proliferation of cells within the HL-60 cell line [ ]. Tan et al. performed a study analyzing lncRNA expression in AML patients. They found that the levels of lncRNA ANRIL were elevated in AML patients in relation to healthy donors, demonstrating its potential to differentiate between these two groups. Additionally, a significant correlation was observed between lncRNA ANRIL and a reduced CR rate [ ].

## LncRNA H19
H19 is one of the few lncRNAs that has been studied in both malignant and normal hematopoiesis, highlighting its significant role in the blood system. In the context of hematopoiesis and AML, H19 serves as a scaffold for miR-19a-3p and miR-29a-3p, in addition to its known function as an epigenetic regulator [ ]. In AML patients, elevated H19 expression was linked to lower OS and a reduced CR rate. By controlling the expression of Wnt/β-catenin effectors, H19 knockdown in AML cell lines increased apoptosis and decreased proliferation [ , ].

## LncRNA CRNDE
Wang et al. used the U937 cell line and reported that the lncRNA CRNDE can control the growth and differentiation of AML cells [ – ]. Additionally, Lio et al. reported that CRNDE contributes to oncogenesis in KG-1a cells by enhancing cell proliferation through the modulation of miR-136-5p [ ].

## LncRNA CCAT1
The lncRNA colon cancer-associated transcript-1 (CCAT1) is an illustrated AML miRNA sponge that can manage the development and differentiation of AML cells. Furthermore, CCAT1 impedes the differentiation of monocytes while simultaneously enhancing the proliferation of AML-derived HL60 cells by decreasing miR-155 levels and subsequently increasing the expression of c-MYC in AML cells [ ].

## LncRNA CASC15
Compared with other cytogenetic profiles, cancer susceptibility candidate 15 (CASC15) is more highly expressed in AML patients. Increased expression of CASC15 led to the formation of bone marrow, which developed from a greater proportion of myeloid cells, decreased engraftment efficiency, and hindered colony formation. A research study conducted by Fernando et al. revealed that, in comparison with patients with other cytogenetic profiles, patients with abnormalities in RUNX1 had higher levels of CASC15 in AML [ ].

## LncRNA MORRBID
Certain lncRNAs are linked to leukemia recurrent causative mutations; the lncRNA myeloid RNA regulator of BIM-induced death (MORRBID) regulates AML proliferation through transcriptional regulation [ ]. Expression of the lncRNA MORRBID is linked to mutations in FLT3ITD and TET2 [ ]. MORRBID effectively prevents the apoptotic process in both human and murine AML cells by repressing BIM, resulting in uncontrolled cell growth. Consequently, loss of MORRBID leads to elevated BIM expression, resulting in enhanced cell death and extended survival of mice with MORBBID-depleted AML [ ].

## LncRNA CCDC26
Another lncRNA is coiled-coil domain-containing 26 (CCDC26). The expression level of CCDC26 is elevated at both the time of diagnosis and during relapse, and it correlates with factors such as age, anemia, risk stratification, and remission status. Patients exhibiting high levels of CCDC26 expression experienced a reduced OS, indicating its potential utility as a molecular biomarker for risk stratification in AML [ , ]. Through controlling the expression of the c-KIT tyrosine kinase receptor, CCDC26 plays an oncogenic role in AML, and overexpression of this lncRNA promotes increased cell proliferation [ ]. Leukemia cells may survive and proliferate in the absence of adequate growth factors if CCDC26 is inhibited. Some studies have proposed that AML cells may develop resistance to anticancer medications, such as ATRA, following CCDC26 knockdown because of the integration of retroviral DNA into the CCDC26 gene locus, indicating that this gene may be important in AML [ ]. Conversely, Hirano et al. reported that AML cell lines highly express CCDC26 [ ].

## LncRNA PANDAR
The upregulation of PANDAR has been detected in patients with AML. This upregulation is linked to a lower CR rate and reduced survival outcomes [ ]. In the context of AML, the excessive presence of PANDAR leads to the enhancement of cell survival mechanisms. Yang et al. discovered that PANDAR was upregulated in some AML subtypes [ ]. PANDAR plays a role in the DNA damage response, and p53 is required for its induction. Overexpression of PANDAR in AML increases cell survival by interacting with TFs to prevent them from binding to target gene promoters; thereby, PANDAR inhibits the expression of proapoptotic genes [ ].

## LncRNA TUG1
The lncRNA taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) is upregulated in AML and is linked to various clinical and molecular features. Hence, it has the potential to serve as a molecular indicator for an unfavorable prognosis. Furthermore, a positive correlation has been observed between elevated WBC count and elevated TUG1 expression [ ]. TUG1 is an oncogene that promotes cell viability and inhibits apoptosis by serving as a decoy for the miR-221-3p tumor suppressor [ ]. Luo et al. also reported that patients diagnosed with AML who presented elevated TUG1 expression presented decreased OS, reduced rates of CR, and a diminished overall response compared to patients with reduced TUG1 expression. AML cell lines exhibit a marked upregulation of TUG1 expression, and TUG1 overexpression in vitro has been shown to increase cell proliferation while reducing the rate of apoptosis. These findings indicate the potential oncogenic function of TUG1 in AML [ ]. Elevated levels of TUG1 are frequently observed in AML patients exhibiting a monosomal karyotype, FLT3-ITD mutation, and poor-risk patients and are associated with higher WBC count [ ].

## LncRNAs involved in AML resistance to treatment
Conventional research into the genes associated with resistance to therapy has focused primarily on protein-coding genes. However, current investigations are broadening this approach to include sections of the genome without coding. Chemoresistance is positively associated with lncRNA expression in different forms of leukemia, most notably AML [ ]. The evaluation of the expression of lncRNAs in LSCs associated with AML suggests that lncRNAs may have an impact on specific attributes of stem cells, including self-renewal and quiescence, and mechanisms of resistance in leukemia associated with lncRNAs can be categorized into multiple classifications: microRNA sponge, oncogenic signaling pathway activation, drug efflux mediated by multiple drug transporters, leukemia stem cell self-renewal, and inhibition of tumor suppressors leading to chemoresistance [ , ]. Numerous lncRNAs are recognized for their possible involvement in the development of therapy resistance in AML, which is attributed to their established roles in regulating gene expression and cellular pathways [ ]. To effectively combat chemoresistance in AML, a comprehensive comprehension of the fundamental mechanisms is essential. Therefore, exploring the role of lncRNAs in chemoresistance through additional investigations could be crucial in overcoming this challenge [ ]. LncRNAs can elicit chemoresistance through various mechanisms. For instance, they can enter cells through exosomes, which may lead to recipient cells developing chemoresistance [ ]. Numerous types of human cancers, including hematologic malignancies, lncRNAs represent significant targets for anticipating treatment responses and overcoming chemotherapy resistance. In numerous investigations have employed knockout techniques to reduce chemoresistance both in vivo and in vitro [ ].

## LncRNA TUG1
The TUG1 lncRNA plays a role in the aerobic glycolysis cascade and promotes tumor growth by triggering the AKT signaling cascade in AML cells [ ]. Recent research has also revealed that TUG1 enhances resistance to treatment with adriamycin (ADR) by facilitating the epigenetic inhibition of miR-34a. According to Li et al., in in vivo and in vitro systems, inhibiting TUG1 has been demonstrated to increase the sensitivity of ADR-resistant HL60 cells to ADR by inducing miR-34. TUG1 is also highly expressed in doxorubicin (Dox)-resistant leukogenic cells [ , ].

## LncRNA HOTAIRM1
In specific circumstances, an individual lncRNA has the potential to confer resistance to multiple drugs by impacting different cellular pathways, as evidenced by the lncRNA HOTAIRM1. HOTAIRM1 displays significant specificity towards myeloid cells, and the development of AML and myeloid cell differentiation are associated with its expression [ , ]. HOTAIRM1 exhibited increased expression levels in glucocorticoid-resistant AML cells as well as in AML patients characterized by a poor prognosis [ ]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that HOTAIRM1 enhances resistance to Ara-C in AML by affecting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Additionally, a separate investigation revealed that decreasing HOTAIRM1 levels led to a higher rate of apoptosis in AML cell lines treated with cytarabine (Ara-C), as well as the suppression of glycolysis and compromised function of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, as demonstrated by diminished expression of β-catenin and c-MYC [ ].

## LncRNA LINC00239
The long intergenic nonprotein-coding RNA 239 (LINC00239) was identified as one of the upregulated tumor promoters among AML patients. By stimulating the PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of the rapamycin (mTOR) axis, Linc00239 promotes chemoresistance, inhibits Dox-induced cellular apoptosis, and reduces the effectiveness of ADR in the KG-1 and HL-60 leukemic cell lines [ ].

## LncRNA UCA1
The lncRNA UCA1 has been recognized for its involvement in promoting chemotherapy resistance by sequestering miRNAs. Samples collected from AML patients after treatment with Ara-C and Dox UCA1 presented elevated expression levels. Furthermore, the overexpression of UCA1 resulted in the progression of Dox resistance in AML cell lines during in vitro experiments [ ]. UCA1 knockdown enhances chemosensitivity in AML by upregulating the expression of miRNA-125a, which subsequently leads to the inhibition of hexokinase-2 (HK-2) [ ]. Via the UCA1/miR-125a/HK2 axis, UCA1 inhibits glycolysis and aids in chemoresistance in AML. In ADR-resistant AML cells, the inhibition of UCA1 expression results in the recovery of responsiveness to chemotherapy [ ].

## LncRNA KNCQ1OT1
Silencing KCNQ1OT1 led to decrease in proliferation, migration, and invasion in ADR-resistant AML cells, while simultaneously promoting apoptosis in ADR-resistant HL-60 and K562 AML cell lines [ , ].

## LncRNA XIST
The lncRNA XIST, among the earliest lncRNAs to be characterized, has been discovered to additionally assist in the advancement of AML Dox resistance [ ]. XIST was discovered to function as a scaffold for miR-29a, resulting in the mitigation of the suppression of the oncogene MYC. By suppressing MYC and promoting apoptosis, small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated XIST knockdown increased Dox sensitivity in the AML cell line KG-1 [ ].
On the basis of current research, it appears that lncRNAs commit to the progression of chemoresistance in AML cells by influencing numerous cellular signaling cascades. These pathways may be aimed at avoiding or treating chemoresistant cancer patients [ ]. Researchers currently have access to high-throughput methods involving extensive genetic screening of lncRNAs. This method allows for the identification of genes associated with drug resistance and sensitivity. The increasing popularity of genome-wide screenings focused on lncRNAs suggests that numerous lncRNAs associated with therapy resistance will be identified in the coming years [ ]. The future sensitivity of drug-resistant or relapsed AML can be an effective approach for the modification of lncRNA expression [ ].

## LncRNA HOTAIR
High expression of HOTAIR is associated with resistance to antileukemic drugs (doxorubicin, imatinib), making this lncRNA a potential therapeutic target to limit drug resistance [ ]. HOTAIR was reported to suppress PTEN through an up-regulating DNMT3b-dependent way and confers doxorubicin resistance in AML [ ]. Through in vitro cytological studies, they found that LncRNA-HOTAIR is engaged in the multidrug resistance advancement of leukemia cells by regulating the expression of P21 and the AKT/Notch1 signaling pathway. Therefore, it can be speculated that the overexpression of HOTAIR is a molecular marker of chemoresistance in leukemia cells, and the downregulation of HOTAIR expression may be a way to overcome chemoresistance in leukemia cells [ ].

## LncRNA MALAT1
The lncRNA MALAT1 is essential in the development of therapeutic resistance in leukemia, as it has been shown to be involved in Ara-C resistance in AML [ – ]. Clinical research has demonstrated a negative correlation between MALAT1 expression and miR-96 levels in patients diagnosed with AML. According to Hu et al., miR-96 expression is reduced in the AML cell lines HL60 and THP-1, while MALAT1 expression is elevated. Additionally, these findings indicate that the suppression of MALAT1 enhances the effectiveness of Ara-C in AML cells. However, the mechanisms by which MALAT1 contributes to the development of resistance to Ara-C are yet to be elucidated [ , ].

## Perspectives for the future of lncRNA-based strategies to increase the prediction of treatment outcomes in acute myeloid leukemia
Although several prognostic factors, including leukemia type, age, WBC count, cytogenetics, and recurrent mutations, have been recognized, risk stratification and outcome prediction for leukemia patients are still not ideal and needs to be improved [ ]. Owing to the substantial importance of lncRNAs in tumorigenesis and the emergence of therapeutic resistance, lncRNA-targeting cancer therapies have been developed. LncRNAs have historically been regarded as "undruggable" targets; consequently, researchers have employed gene silencing techniques like siRNA and anti-sense oligonucleotides to prevent the expression of particular lncRNAs in experimental analysis [ ]. However, because of their unique expression profiles and range of functions, lncRNAs exhibit considerable potential as therapeutic cancer targets. Various approaches can be employed to regulate lncRNAs in cancer. These include the use of CRISPR/Cas9 technology for transcriptional inhibition or upregulation, the use of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) for posttranscriptional lncRNA degradation, the use of steric hindrance to disrupt lncRNA-protein interactions, and interference with the secondary structure of lncRNAs [ ]. The advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies has allowed for an extensive molecular characterisation of divers non-coding RNA expression profiles across various cancers. Additionally, technological advances for quantifying lowly expressed RNAs in the circulation have led to reliable identification of previously unknown and undetectable biomarkers in cancer patients. Utilizing these tools may enhance cost-effectiveness by enabling more precise diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies [ ]. Considering that lncRNAs have vital functions in determining the prognosis of leukemia patients and can be used as targets to overcome treatment resistance, a substantial amount of research has been conducted to investigate the possibility of incorporating lncRNAs into the current predictive framework for drug resistance [ ]. Several investigations have been carried out to examine the possibility of incorporating lncRNAs into the current predictive types for drug resistance. As a result, predictive scores for outcome prognosis have been established on the basis of the identified lncRNAs. These scores have proven effective in categorizing older AML patients with cytogenetically normal AML into subgroups according to DFS and OS durations [ ]. Wang et al. reported that 347 patients with newly diagnosed AML presented increased expression of the lncRNA KIAA0125 in their bone marrow. Patients with AML who underwent standard chemotherapy were stratified on the basis of their KIAA0125 expression levels. Individuals with high KIAA0125 expression presented a decreased rate of CR, as well as shorter OS and DFS, than did those with low expression of KIAA0125 [ ]. A well-known oncogene, HOX antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR) lncRNA is markedly elevated in AML patients compared with healthy individuals. Notably, posttreatment levels of HOTAIR expression are correlated with relapse-free survival, indicating a possible predictive value for relapse susceptibility [ ]. The suppression of HOXA-AS2 led to a decrease in adriamycin-resistant AML cells and triggered cell death, functioning as a sponge for miR-520c-3p. Bester et al. discovered that GAS6-AS2 plays a vital role in Ara-C resistance in AML. Its inhibition via antisense oligonucleotides enhanced K562 cell sensitivity to Ara-C by downregulating GAS6 and AXL [ ]. siRNAs provide targeted therapy for oncogenic lncRNAs with precise dosage control, low immunogenicity, and no risk for genome integration [ ]. By utilizing siRNA against UCA1, the studies demonstrated that knock-down of UCA1 contributes positively to the reversal of chemoresistance in AML cells, through suppressing glycolysis via the miR-125a/HK2 pathway [ ]. The knockdown of HOTAIR using siRNA was observed to block differentiation and keep AML blasts in an immature state [ ]. Inhibition of XIST through siRNA knockdown in the AML cell line KG-1 enhanced sensitivity to Dox by reducing MYC expression and promoting apoptosis [ ]. Recent advancements in genome editing techniques, particularly CRISPR/Cas9, are progressing rapidly and appear to be a highly effective method for knock-in or knock-out lncRNA candidates [ ]. Modified CRISPR systems have the potential to facilitate the conversion of cytidine into uridine in order to correct oncogenic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), knowing that several SNPs have been linked to possible predictive biomarkers for the risk of AML, including SNPs in ANRIL and MALAT1 lncRNA [ ]. A comprehensive comparison of lncRNAs in AML and other hematological malignancies could provide significant insights into their specificity and clinical applications. MALAT1 is upregulated in AML and has been implicated in the proliferation and therapeutic resistance of leukemic cells. Its overexpression is also noted in other hematologic malignancies, such as chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, suggesting its potential as a universal biomarker and therapeutic target [ ]. PVT1 is overexpressed in various cancers, including Burkitt lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, and T-cell leukemia, typically due to chromosomal translocations impacting the MYC gene. This increased expression suggests that PVT1 may contribute to the pathogenesis of various hematologic malignancies [ ]. ANRIL epigenetically silences the tumor suppressor gene p15INK4B, promoting leukemogenesis. Elevated levels of ANRIL expression correlate with poor prognosis in AML. In Other Hematologic Malignancies, ANRIL is also upregulated in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and MM, where it enhances cell survival through the modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [ ]. Beyond AML, H19 is also overexpressed in CML, myeloproliferative neoplasms, and adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, indicating a broader oncogenic function in hematologic malignancies [ ]. Functioning as a tumor suppressor, MEG3 is downregulated in AML, multiple myeloma (MM), and myelodysplastic syndromes. The reduction in its expression correlates with poor prognosis, highlighting its role in various hematologic malignancies [ ]. The overexpression of HOTAIR has been noted in AML, correlating with a negative prognosis. HOTAIR functions by epigenetically silencing tumor suppressor genes, which promoting leukemogenesis. Additionally, elevated HOTAIR levels have been reported in MM and CML, indicating its broad oncogenic role across hematologic cancers [ ]. NEAT1 plays a crucial role in leukemogenesis by regulating chromatin architecture and gene expression. It is significantly under expressed in AML cases with CEBPA double mutations, which impacts myeloid differentiation. In MM, NEAT1 is highly expressed and is associated with drug resistance. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), its overexpression facilitates cell proliferation [ ]. XIST plays a crucial role in the regulation of AML progression by functioning as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for microRNAs that target oncogenic transcripts. Additionally, XIST is upregulated in B-cell lymphoma and MM, where it affects cell proliferation and apoptosis [ ]. TUG1 promotes cell proliferation by functioning as a ceRNA for tumor-suppressing miRNAs. Its expression is frequently elevated AML, enhancing leukemic cell survival. In MM, TUG1 interacts with EZH2 to repress tumor suppressor genes. Additionally, it contributes to the advancement of resistance to bortezomib, an essential medication in the treatment of MM [ ]. UCA1 facilitates the proliferation of leukemic cells by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and functioning as a sponge for tumor-suppressing microRNAs. In CLL and MM, UCA1 plays a role in cell survival and drug resistance [ ]. Investigating the functions of lncRNAs across various hematologic malignancies enhances our comprehension of their contributions to cancer biology and supports the development of more precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. These findings suggest that the targeting of lncRNAs could be a promising approach for reducing AML cell chemoresistance. It is conceivable that the knockdown technique could be employed to suppress lncRNAs in LSCs. LncRNAs can be divided into two different subgroups on the basis of the literature: (1) oncogenes and (2) tumor suppressors. Because of the possibility of reducing tumor-suppressor lncRNAs or eliminating oncogene lncRNAs to overcome chemoresistance, this classification is significant [ ]. The concept that triggering differentiation and eliminating LSCs in AML can be achieved by manipulating lncRNAs through targeted treatment is an interesting prospect that could lead to novel therapeutic strategies in the field of AML. These discoveries offer scientists new molecular pathways to target therapeutic interventions [ ]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is still no authorized or clinically tested lncRNA-based therapy for AML, mainly because the study of lncRNAs has only started to progress in the last few years [ ]. Future development of specialized algorithms incorporating different prognostic factors and lncRNAs specific to each subtype of leukemia would be useful. The prognosis of patients and how they respond to treatment can be predicted via these algorithms [ ].
Targeted therapies have matured, yet many defective patients have poor prognosis, requiring new avenues of therapy to be explored [ ]. Regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats in clusters are characterized by their programmability, ease of use, quick execution, and efficient targeting, offering many advantages such as easy use, complete inactivation, high reproducibility and efficiency, and lower costs as compared with RNA interference and other gene-editing technologies include zinc finger nucleases and transcription activator-like effector nucleases [ ]. The multiplexed genome editing CRISPR screening platform has provided valuable insights into the molecular landscape of AML and has facilitated the identification of novel potential therapeutic targets. With that said, finding bioinformatics ones through which novel lncRNAs relating to AML through bioinformatics can be identified is undoubtedly a big move toward CRISPR-based genome editing for possible anti-leukemia interventions [ ].

## Limitations of targeting lncRNAs in acute myeloid leukemia
As prognostic or diagnostic biomarkers and molecular targets, lncRNAs in AML offer promising therapeutic opportunities, as demonstrated by the examples provided in this review. Nevertheless, there are a few drawbacks related to the focus on lncRNAs in a clinical context [ ]. The creation of patient-derived xenograft models and transgenic mouse models for AML has presented significant challenges. These challenges pose a significant impediment to the preclinical assessment of lncRNAs as putative therapeutic targets [ ]. Furthermore, given that these components are reliant on their nucleotide sequences, specificity can present difficulties, such as undesired on-target effects resulting from uptake within nontarget cells, as well as off-target effects that may arise from sequence homologies or excessive dosing that exceeds the anticipated endogenous levels [ ]. Specifically, we need to identify the lncRNAs and the corresponding components that interact with effector proteins and determine which of these lncRNAs are responsible for conveying specificity towards target molecules, which can be either DNA or RNA [ ]. It is crucial for diagnostic or prognostic assays that use lncRNAs to identify the splicing variants that are most relevant to their functional roles for efficient targeting since alternatively, spliced lncRNAs can display noticeably various cellular activities [ ]. This presents a barrier to the clinical application of lncRNAs, but new developments in RNA-seq data analysis and alternative splicing analysis based on RNA-seq could eventually offer solutions. Furthermore, the low conservation of lncRNAs across species presents a major obstacle to the creation and evaluation of efficacious lncRNA-targeted therapies for AML [ ]. A comprehensive examination of pharmacokinetics would involve discussing absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion challenges specific to RNA-based drugs, such as their rapid degradation by nucleases and the need for chemical modifications or delivery systems (e.g., lipid nanoparticles, conjugates) to enhance stability and bioavailability. In terms of pharmacodynamics, exploring the mechanisms by which RNA-based therapeutics modulate gene expression in AML cells—whether through RNA interference, antisense oligonucleotides, or RNA aptamers—would be crucial. This may encompass information regarding target engagement, duration of effect, and potential for resistance [ , ]. The challenges associated with delivery can be attributed to three primary factors: the inherent instability of unmodified, ‘naked’ RNA structures; the suboptimal efficiency of their intracellular delivery, necessitating the utilization of endosomal escape strategies; and the absence of suitable delivery vehicles that can effectively target the specific organ and cell types of interest [ ]. Furthermore, the mode of delivery is an additional topic worthy of investigation. The effective transport of oligonucleotides poses significant challenges owing to their inherent instability and negative charge, and RNAs are hydrophilic, which hinders their ability to permeate cell membranes. Addressing this issue could eliminate numerous challenges; nevertheless, even in contemporary times, there remains a pressing need for advanced delivery techniques [ ]. The use of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) is a potentially efficient drug delivery technique. This strategy has demonstrated efficacy in common with in vitro studies of AML; however, the practicality of using LNPs to deliver therapeutics targeted at lncRNAs in more clinically pertinent AML contexts has yet to be established. Furthermore, in addition to LNP-based therapies, small molecule screening techniques have been devised to discover compounds that interfere with lncRNA-protein interactions, thereby identifying possible therapeutic agents that target lncRNAs [ ]. Also, stability remains a significant challenge in clinical translation. In addition to chemical modifications, investigating the potential of carrier systems such as exosomes, nanoparticles, or peptide-based carriers may provide valuable insights [ ]. Numerous constituents exhibit sequences and chemical alterations that have the potential to induce immunogenicity. Additionally, concerns regarding tolerability also include the fact that different extracellular and intracellular receptors known as pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which can recognize both single-stranded (ss) and double-stranded (ds) RNA structures. This indicates that there may be significant challenges related to the immunogenicity of this treatment modality [ ]. Recent studies indicates that lncRNA expression profiles can differ markedly among patients, likely due to the biological complexity of AML and the absence of standardized sample collection and quantification techniques. This variability underscores the need for extensive, well-designed cohort studies and validation in separate groups to confirm the clinical relevance of lncRNA-based biomarkers [ ]. Targeting lncRNAs therapeutically presents challenges, particularly due to the incomplete understanding of their secondary structures and interactions, making it difficult to predict and control off-target effects. It is recommended that a thorough functional and molecular characterization of lncRNAs is essential to identify the most viable targets and to develop therapeutic tools that possess suitable efficacy and safety profiles [ ].
Regardless of the limitations and therapeutic potential, finding the best lncRNAs is a major concern [ ]. These constraints represent some of the primary obstacles that researchers must address to fully realize the benefits of lncRNA targeting in AML. They also emphasize how important it is to continue doing research in this area.

## Conclusion
The review provides a thorough overview of AML, highlighting the disease’s genetic complexity, molecular heterogeneity, and resistance mechanisms. This establishes a strong foundation for the discussion of lncRNAs in AML. This review aimed to determine the importance of lncRNAs as crucial genetic molecules that play pivotal roles in controlling various cellular procedures in AML cells, as well as their relevance in the initiation, progression, relapse, and resistance to regular chemotherapeutic factors in AML. The exploration of lncRNAs’ roles in regulating gene expression, cellular homeostasis, and hematopoietic malignancies is detailed and well-supported by references to current research. The focus on lncRNAs addresses a critical gap in AML management by proposing novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Additionally, we learn how this novel regulatory complexity, facilitated by lncRNAs, fits into the present protein-centric framework understanding of sickness and health in humans and helps to clarify the exceptional gene networks that will emerge. This is particularly relevant given the stagnation in survival rates and limitations of traditional treatments like chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The article underscores the complex regulatory roles of lncRNAs in AML, including their influence on lineage determination, progression, relapse, and chemoresistance. This approach enhances the understanding of lncRNAs as key molecular players. Furthermore, there is still much to learn about the characteristics of the connections between various molecules and lncRNAs in AML. To address these problems, techniques such as RNA immune precipitation (RIP) and capture hybridization analysis of RNA targets (CHART) must be used to determine the binding proteins or chromatin of lncRNAs. By addressing both the current state of lncRNA research and the challenges that remain (e.g., uncharacterized lncRNAs and unexplored mechanisms), the article provides a roadmap for future investigations. It aptly integrates the promise of precision medicine, emphasizing the potential of lncRNAs to revolutionize AML management. While the review broadly discusses lncRNAs’ roles, it would benefit from detailing specific lncRNAs (e.g., HOTAIR, NEAT1, or MALAT1) implicated in AML. Including examples with established clinical or preclinical relevance would strengthen the discussion. Although the article discusses lncRNAs’ regulatory functions, it could delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms, such as their interactions with chromatin, transcription factors, or miRNAs, and their impact on key AML pathways (e.g., FLT3-ITD or NPM1 mutations). In instances of lethal cancers such as AML, where rates of survival have stagnated and novel risk assessments and treatment objective techniques are necessary, comprehending the fundamental mechanisms of lncRNA functions and abnormalities will eventually result in a comprehensive understanding of the disease process and improved management of AML patients. The identification of highly promising lncRNA targets and the development of an optimal lncRNA therapeutic approach with appropriate effectiveness and safety characteristics offer significant opportunities for advancing precision medicine. There is substantial work that needs to be done before we can obtain a comprehensive understanding of the contribution of lncRNAs to AML biology. While the review touches on the therapeutic potential of lncRNAs, it could elaborate on specific RNA-targeted approaches, such as ASOs, siRNAs, or CRISPR-based gene-editing techniques. Additionally, discussing delivery challenges and safety concerns in RNA-based therapeutics would provide a more balanced perspective. The review could benefit from addressing the translational hurdles in applying lncRNA research to clinical practice, such as variability in expression profiles across patient populations, potential off-target effects, and the cost-effectiveness of lncRNA-based diagnostics or therapies. Uncharacterized lncRNAs: A significant portion of the lncRNA landscape in AML remains unexplored. Expanding the focus to include high-throughput screening and computational methods for identifying novel lncRNAs could accelerate discovery. Large-scale, multicenter clinical studies are essential to validate lncRNAs as reliable biomarkers for AML diagnosis and prognosis. Combining transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics data could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of lncRNAs in AML. Addressing the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of RNA-based therapeutics, along with overcoming challenges like immune activation and stability, will be critical for advancing clinical applications. Insights into the potential use of ASOs, CRISPR/Cas9, or RNA-targeted drugs to modulate lncRNA function in AML would be valuable. This review effectively underscores the critical role of lncRNAs in AML biology, offering a clear framework for their diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance. It highlights the potential of lncRNAs to transform AML management while acknowledging the challenges and gaps that remain. By integrating mechanistic insights with clinical implications, the article contributes significantly to the field and sets the stage for future research aimed at leveraging lncRNAs in precision oncology. However, incorporating more specific examples and addressing translational challenges could enhance its impact and applicability to clinical practice. Although many studies have identified potential lncRNAs in AML, many rely on small cohorts or preclinical models that may not be representative of broader patient populations, restricting their immediate clinical relevance. This research highlights the necessity for further large-scale, multi-center clinical validations to confirm the diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential of lncRNAs in AML. Additionally, there are still uncharacterized lncRNAs and unrecognized mechanisms implicated in AML, indicating that the utilization of lncRNAs as targets for diagnosis and treatment is still in its early stages. On the basis of prior research, we anticipate that lncRNAs will play a comprehensive role subsequently in the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of AML patients.
