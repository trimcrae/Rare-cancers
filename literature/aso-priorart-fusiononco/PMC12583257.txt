## Abstract
The phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) pathway and microRNA (miRNA) regulation have been implicated in the initiation and progression of acute leukemia. Despite significant progress in the understanding of leukemogenic mechanisms, current therapies remain limited by relapse, drug resistance, and poor long-term survival, underscoring the need for novel targeted strategies. This review provides the current evidence regarding the molecular interplay between PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and miRNA dysregulation in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). We highlight key oncogenic and tumor-suppressive miRNAs, discuss their regulatory impact on critical genes (PIK3CA, AKT1, PTEN, and FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 [FLT3]), and evaluate therapeutic approaches, including PI3K/AKT inhibitors and miRNA-based interventions. Furthermore, we identified existing controversies, methodological limitations, and unresolved research gaps. Finally, we emphasize the translational potential of miRNA-targeted therapies and pathway-specific inhibitors, providing insights into their integration into personalized treatment strategies for acute leukemia.
Keywords: MicroRNA, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, Leukemia, AML, ALL, CML, CLL

## Introduction
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short, noncoding RNAs (~ 22 nucleotides) that regulate cellular processes such as differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis by binding to the 3′ untranslated regions of target mRNAs, thereby inhibiting translation [ – ]. Dysregulated miRNA expression is linked to different cancers, including leukemia, where miRNAs often reside in genomic regions prone to alterations, such as loss of heterozygosity or amplification [ , ].
Leukemia encompasses hematologic malignancies characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of immature blood cells, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) [ , ]. AML, the most common adult leukemia (> 50 years), arises from hematopoietic progenitors [ , ]. ALL peaks in children (2–6 years) include the T- and B-lineage subtypes [ ]. CML, which accounts for approximately 15% of leukemia cases, stems from the breakpoint cluster region-Abelson (BCR–ABL) fusion [ , ]. CLL, which is prevalent in Western populations (approximately 25% of new cases), involves CD5 + B cell accumulation [ , ]. Despite therapeutic advances, leukemia remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ , ], with challenges such as drug resistance and relapse, underscoring the need for novel targets.
MiRNAs critically regulate leukemogenic pathways, contributing to disrupted apoptosis and cell cycle progression [ , – ]. Early evidence from CLL demonstrated that downregulation of tumor-suppressive miR-15a/16–1 [ – ], whereas subsequent studies revealed widespread miRNA dysregulation across subtypes [ ]. For instance, miR-149 targets PI3K/AKT genes, thereby increasing the risk of ALL [ ]. The phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) pathway, which is central to cell survival and proliferation, is frequently dysregulated in leukemia and interacts bidirectionally with miRNAs [ – ]. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing targeted therapies.
This review examined the role of miRNAs in leukemia progression via the PI3K/AKT pathway, focusing on AML, ALL, CML, and CLL, to highlight their diagnostic and therapeutic potential. Building on this foundation, the following sections discuss pathway involvement and specific miRNA interactions.

## Role of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and miRNAs in leukemia development

## Involvement of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in leukemia development
The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a pivotal regulator of cell cycle progression, proliferation, and survival, and its dysregulation is a hallmark of numerous cancers, including leukemia [ , ]. Hyperactivation of this pathway, driven by genetic or microenvironmental factors, promotes leukemogenesis across several subtypes, making it a compelling target for therapeutic intervention [ ]. Increased PI3K/AKT/mTOR activity in ALL leads to uncontrolled cell growth and contributes to chemoresistance. For instance, the glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) mediates resistance in ALL through the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β/Bim axis, where AKT phosphorylation inhibits pro-apoptotic Bim, sustaining leukemic cell survival [ , ]. In contrast, CML is driven by the BCR–ABL fusion protein resulting from the Philadelphia chromosome (t(9;22)), which constitutively activates PI3K/AKT/mTOR and enhances cell proliferation and genomic instability [ – ]. This fusion gene, formed by the translocation of ABL1 (chromosome 9) and BCR (chromosome 22), underscores the central role of this pathway in CML pathogenesis.
PI3K/AKT/mTOR hyperactivation in AML arises from multiple genetic alterations that significantly affect disease progression [ ]. Key mutations include:
FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) mutations: Internal tandem duplications (FLT3-ITD), present in ~ 25% of patients with AML with normal karyotypes, lead to persistent FLT3 receptor tyrosine kinase activation, driving PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and promoting cell survival [ ].
KIT mutations: Less prevalent than FLT3, KIT mutations activate PI3K/AKT via the adaptor protein GAB2, thereby contributing to AML progression [ ].
PTEN inactivation: As a negative regulator, PTEN mutations or deletions sustain pathway activity, although their precise role in AML requires further exploration [ ].
PIK3CA mutations: These affect the p110α catalytic subunit of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), enhancing pathway signaling, albeit less commonly [ ].
AKT1 mutations: The E17K mutation in the pleckstrin homology domain causes aberrant AKT1 membrane localization and amplifies downstream signaling [ ].
In addition to genetic mutations, autocrine/paracrine signaling loops, such as insulin-like growth factor (IGF)−1/insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R), further activate PI3K/AKT in AML. Studies demonstrate that neutralizing anti-IGF-1R antibodies inhibit IGF-1R and AKT phosphorylation in 70% of AML samples with active PI3K signaling, highlighting a non-mutational mechanism of pathway activation [ ]. These diverse mechanisms, illustrated in Fig.  , integrate upstream activators (e.g., growth factors and receptor tyrosine kinases) and downstream effectors (e.g., FOXO3a, p70S6K, and 4EBP1), driving leukemia initiation and therapy resistance [ , ]. For example, AKT-mediated phosphorylation of FOXO3a upregulates anti-apoptotic genes, whereas mTOR enhances protein synthesis via p70S6K and 4EBP1 phosphorylation, promoting cell growth [ , ].
 Numerous miRNAs function as potent tumor suppressors in this context. For example, the miR-15/16 cluster, which is frequently deleted in CLL, directly targets the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, thereby promoting programmed cell death. Similarly, miR-26a, miR-214, and miR-485-3p suppress oncogenic pathways. Conversely, several miRNAs act as oncogenes (oncomiRs). These include miR-155, which is often overexpressed and promotes cell proliferation, and miR-125b, which inhibits apoptosis. Other notable oncomiRs implicated in leukemia include miR-93, miR-127, and members of the let-7 family, the expression of which can be dysregulated. Furthermore, specific miRNAs, such as miR-128a/b, miR-185-5p, miR-196b, and miR-223, have been identified as key players, each modulating distinct aspects of leukemic cell biology.
The mechanistic action of these miRNAs is predominantly mediated by their targeting of crucial signaling pathways. A prime example is the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which is a central hub for pro-survival signals. MiRNAs can modulate this pathway by targeting their components, such as the upstream regulator PDK1 or the critical tumor suppressor PTEN, the loss of which causes constitutive AKT activation. Activated phospho-AKT (pAKT) drives cell growth and proliferation through downstream effectors, such as mTOR, and negates apoptosis by inhibiting pro-apoptotic factors, such as Bad, Bax, and BIM. The BCR–ABL1 oncoprotein, a hallmark of CML, is involved in the same downstream pathways. The net effect of miRNA-mediated regulation is the disruption of the delicate balance between pro-survival (e.g., Bcl-2) and pro-apoptotic (e.g., Bax, Bad) signals from the BCL-2 family, ultimately tipping the scales toward cell survival and expansion (Fig.  ) [ , ]. This intricate crosstalk between miRNA networks and signaling pathways underscores their potential as therapeutic targets and diagnostic markers of hematological malignancies.
The schematic in Fig.  builds upon the foundational PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway diagrams from Rascio et al. (2021) and Roshandel et al. (2021) [ , ], with original contributions integrating specific miRNA interactions (e.g., miR-93 and miR-223) based on our analysis of their regulatory roles in leukemia subtypes and oncogenic tumor suppressor miRNAs [ ].
The therapeutic targeting of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is promising across leukemia subtypes. PI3K inhibitors such as idelalisib and duvelisib induce apoptosis and reduce proliferation in CLL, yet resistance emerges via mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway mutations (e.g., BRAF and KRAS) or non-genetic adaptations, such as PI3Kγ upregulation or stromal support [ , , ]. AKT inhibitors such as MK-2206 demonstrate preclinical efficacy in AML by disrupting survival signals. Combining these inhibitors with chemotherapy or MAPK inhibitors can overcome drug resistance and enhance long-term outcomes [ ]. Building on these insights, the interplay between PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and miRNAs explored in Sect. " " further elucidates leukemogenic mechanisms and therapeutic opportunities. Dysregulation of the immune system plays a significant role in the pathophysiological processes of several malignancies [ ], including leukemia, by fostering chronic inflammation that enhances PI3K/AKT signaling and miRNA dysregulation. Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), a derivative of ATP, serves as a key mediator of intracellular signal transduction and plays a vital role in modulating inflammatory responses, encouraging the development of modulating agents [ ]. This cAMP axis interacts with the PI3K/AKT pathway and miRNAs (e.g., miR-142-3p), potentially suppressing proliferation and inducing apoptosis in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) through PKA activation [ ].

## Role of miRNAs in leukemia pathogenesis
MiRNAs are critical regulators of gene expression that influence cancer development by modulating proliferation, apoptosis, and stem cell maintenance [ , , ]. In leukemia, distinct miRNA expression patterns distinguish diseased from healthy tissues, offering insights into disease progression and therapeutic potential [ ]. This section explores the multifaceted roles of miRNAs in AML, ALL, CML, and CLL, emphasizing their interplay with key signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, as discussed in Sect. 2.1.

## AML
MiRNAs regulate hallmark features such as uncontrolled proliferation and impaired apoptosis in AML [ ]. Genetic mutations, such as the FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) gene, internal tandem duplications, altered miRNA-binding sites, and dysregulated gene expression drive leukemogenesis [ ]. For example, miR-488 suppresses cell cycle progression, indicating therapeutic potential [ ]. Similarly, miR-126, which is overexpressed in inv(16) AML with t(8;21) fusion genes, promotes disease in mouse models by enhancing leukemic cell survival. Genetic deletion of miR-126 reduces AML incidence and extends survival in CM knock-in mice, highlighting its role as a therapeutic target [ , ].
Furthermore, miRNAs exhibit context-dependent functions. For example, miR-155 functions as an oncogene by promoting tumor growth in certain AML contexts; however, it acts as a tumor suppressor when downregulated, where low expression exacerbates the disease via deregulated gene expression [ ]. Furthermore, the miR-125 family, located on chromosomes 19, 21, and 11, supports the self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) and contributes to the progression of myeloproliferative neoplasms to AML. As illustrated in Fig.  , these miRNAs influence AML prognosis, underscoring their diagnostic and therapeutic relevance [ ]. These findings pave the way for the exploration of miRNA-targeted therapies for AML, as discussed in Sect. " ".
 

## ALL
MiRNAs play pivotal roles in pathogenesis, diagnosis, and therapy in ALL. Variations in the 3' untranslated region of SETD8, a histone methylation regulator, particularly at the miR-502 binding site, increase childhood ALL risk by disrupting epigenetic control [ , ]. Aberrant miRNA expression further defines the ALL subtypes. Upregulated miRNAs such as miR-128a, miR-181b, miR-19b, miR-20a, miR-26a, miR-92, and miR-223 promote proliferation, whereas downregulated miRNAs such as miR-100, miR-143, miR-145, miR-196b, and let-7e act as tumor suppressors [ ]. Notably, miR-196b is overexpressed in T-ALL, whereas miR-100 is linked to the t(12, 21)(ETV6-RUNX1) translocation in B-ALL, enabling subtype-specific classification [ ].
Functionally, miRNAs such as miR-21 function as oncogenes by targeting tumor suppressors, whereas the miR-181 family can suppress tumor growth in specific contexts [ ]. In addition, miRNAs regulate lymphopoiesis, and their dysregulation leads to aberrant lymphoid development [ ]. Regarding prognosis, miRNAs, such as miR-125b, miR-24, miR-27a, miR-99/100, miR-124, miR-1225b, miR-128b, miR-142-3p, miR-155, and miR-335-3p, are associated with chemotherapy resistance and relapse [ , ]. Therapeutically, miRNA inhibitors (antagomiRs) targeting oncogenic miRNAs, such as miR-155, or mimics (agomiRs) that restore tumor suppressors show preclinical promise. Combining these with chemotherapy could enhance the efficacy and overcome resistance [ , ]. These insights are related to the role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in ALL, which is further explored in Sect. " ".

## CML
CML, which is driven by the BCR-ABL fusion gene from the Philadelphia chromosome, is a prime candidate for miRNA-based therapies. In silico studies identify miRNAs, such as CPA-miR8154 and osa-miR-1858a/b as potential regulators of BCR-ABL1’s 3'UTR, suggesting targeted intervention strategies [ ]. Dysregulated miRNA profiles in CML include upregulated oncogenic miRNAs (e.g., the miR-17–92 cluster, miR-155, and miR-221) and downregulated tumor suppressors (e.g., miR-150 and miR-199b) [ – ]. For example, miR-155 promotes CML by targeting apoptosis and DNA repair genes, whereas miR-150 suppresses MYB, a key BCR-ABL effector [ ].
In addition, miRNAs regulate hematopoiesis and signaling pathways such as MAPK, TGF-β, and p53, which are disrupted in CML [ , ]. These regulatory roles highlight the dual oncogenic and tumor-suppressive functions of miRNAs and offer opportunities for precise therapies that modulate BCR–ABL–driven signaling.

## CLL
The miRNAs in CLL are central to pathogenesis, with the deletion of the miR-15a/16–1 cluster at 13q14 leading to BCL2 overexpression and enhanced cell survival [ , ]. Oncogenic miRNAs, such as miR-155, miR-17–92, and miR-21, target tumor suppressors, such as SHIP1 and PTEN, activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [ ]. Conversely, tumor-suppressive miRNAs such as miR-34a, miR-29, miR-181a, and miR-223 are downregulated, with miR-34a targeting ZAP-70 to inhibit B cell receptor signaling [ ]. Notably, the role of miR-155 is context-dependent, promoting CLL; however, it functions as a tumor suppressor in AML when downregulated [ ]. Serum β2-microglobulin (β2M) serves as an independent prognostic marker in CLL and immunocytoma (IC), predicting progression-free survival in early-stage patients and guiding decisions for early, intensified treatments [ , ].
The expression patterns of miRNAs correlate with prognostic markers such as ZAP-70 and IgVH status, aiding in risk stratification [ ]. Upregulation of miR-21, miR-148a, and miR-222 is linked to fludarabine resistance, highlighting the role of miRNAs in the treatment response [ ]. Therapeutic strategies, including miRNA mimics, antagomiRs, and small-molecule inhibitors, offer the potential to restore tumor suppressor activity or inhibit oncogenic miRNAs, paving the way for combination therapies using PI3K/AKT inhibitors, as discussed in Sect. " ". As illustrated in Fig.  , several miRNAs have diverse effects on the prognosis of AML, indicating their multifaceted roles in disease development and progression.

## The intricate interplay of miRNAs and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in distinct leukemia subtypes
Current research continues to explore the complex interplay between specific miRNAs and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in leukemia subtypes, including AML, ALL, CML, and CLL [ – ]. This section examines these interactions, focusing on the bidirectional regulatory mechanisms that influence leukemogenesis. As shown in Fig.  , several miRNAs contribute to disease progression in each subtype, providing a framework for understanding these networks and guiding targeted therapies.
 The miRNA–PI3K/AKT/mTOR interaction forms feedback loops that either amplify oncogenic signals or dampen tumor suppression. The miRNAs often serve as upstream regulators, directly targeting pathway components. For example, miR-149 suppresses AKT1 in ALL [ , ]. Conversely, activated AKT phosphorylates transcription factors such as FOXO1/3, repress tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miR-22 in CLL, thereby sustaining pathway hyperactivity [ , ]. Similarly, mTOR regulates miRNA biogenesis via the Drosha/DGCR8 complex, altering the expression of miRNAs, such as miR-223 in CML [ , ]. In AML, FLT3-ITD mutations activate PI3K/AKT, leading to the epigenetic silencing of miR-7-5p and perpetuating proliferation [ ]. These loops underscore the role of the pathway in modulating miRNA levels and driving therapy resistance across subtypes, as recent reviews have highlighted the ongoing advancements in miRNA-based interventions [ , ].

## AML
AML exhibits aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, largely because of altered miRNA profiles influenced by genetic (e.g., FLT3 and NPM1 mutations) and epigenetic factors [ , ]. Recent studies have identified miRNAs as key regulators, offering diagnostic and therapeutic avenues [ ].
Downregulation of miR-7-5p inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis by targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR components [ ]. However, persistent pathway activation suppresses miR-7-5p expression via promoter hypermethylation, thus exacerbating chemoresistance [ ]. Similarly, overexpression of miR-93 targets DAB2, activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR and driving cell growth; its silencing reduces proliferation and supports hematopoietic differentiation [ – ].
Circular RNA circ_0009910 further activates PI3K/AKT by modulating B4GALT5 via miR-491-5p sponging, and the effects of miR-491-5p overexpression are reversed by B4GALT5 upregulation [ ]. In addition, miR-188-5p interacts with TET1 to influence PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and TET1 downregulation elevates miR-188-5p expression and enhances adriamycin resistance [ – ]. A meta-analysis revealed consistently downregulated miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-199a-3p and hsa-miR-29b-3p) linked to PI3K/AKT, warranting further research on innovative diagnostics and therapies [ ]. These mechanisms transition into similar patterns in ALL, where the lncRNA-miRNA axes dominate.

## ALL
The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in B-ALL drives the progression and treatment response [ , , ]. An intricate network involving lncRNAs DUXAP8, miR-29a, and PIK3CA modulates chemosensitivity, apoptosis, and proliferation [ ]. DUXAP8 inhibits miR-29a, activating oncogenic pathways such as WTAP/FAK, AKT/mTOR, and Wnt/β-catenin, promoting survival and resistance [ , , ]. Overexpressed in malignancies, DUXAP8 suggests personalized strategies targeting this axis to overcome resistance [ ].
Pathway contributors, such as BCL-2, PTEN, and AKT1/2 are implicated in ALL. Oncogenic miR-149 targets FOXM1, PARP-2, AKT1, caspase-2, and FASLG, regulating apoptosis and proliferation via PI3K/AKT; AKT feedback may upregulate sponging lncRNAs, reinforcing activity [ , , ]. In T-ALL, downregulation of miR-653-5p promotes apoptosis and autophagy by suppressing Reelin (RELN) and PI3K/AKT/mTOR. Circ-PRKDC sponges miR-653-5p, sustaining its activation, positioning miR-653-5p as a therapeutic candidate [ ]. CML interactions revealed BCR–ABL–mediated miRNA suppression.

## CML
PI3K/AKT/mTOR upregulation in CML highlights its potential as a therapeutic target. Downregulated miR-223, involved in myeloid differentiation, is suppressed by BCR–ABL [ , , ]. This reduction was partially reversed by pcDNA3.1-FLT3 modulation or miR-223 inhibitors, which decreased p-AKT/p-PI3K levels and inhibited xenograft growth and apoptosis [ ]. Silencing of miR-223 correlates with poor prognosis, emphasizing its role in pathway interplay [ ]. BCR–ABL-driven activation represses miR-223 transcription, forming a survival loop [ , , ].
Decreased miR-150-5p expression correlates with lncRNA HULC, is upregulated in CML, and regulates proliferation via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. HULC sponges miR-150-5p, promotes imatinib resistance, and inhibits apoptosis. HULC regulates CML cell proliferation via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, with elevated levels observed in CML and bone marrow mononuclear cells [ , ]. These findings parallel CLL strategies in which miRNA manipulation targets pathway hyperactivity.

## CLL
Targeting PI3K/AKT/mTOR induces apoptosis and inhibits proliferation in CLL, and miRNA modulation offers a targeted approach [ ]. Downregulation of miR-22, a tumor suppressor, modulates PTEN/AKT/FOXO1, thereby restoring its therapeutic value [ , , ]. AKT phosphorylation of FOXO1 inhibits miR-22, sustaining proliferation [ , ]. Dehkordi et al. demonstrated that the inhibition of miR-222 using a locked nucleic acid (LNA) antagomir in the B-CLL cell line (183-E95) reduced cell viability by up to 47% at 72 h post-transfection (p < 0.042), suggesting a novel therapeutic approach for B-CLL [ ].  Although Table  illustrates PI3K/AKT-associated miRNAs' therapeutic potential, clinical translation faces challenges, such as inefficient delivery, off-target effects, and in vivo instability [ , , ]. Innovations such as lipid nanoparticles or locked nucleic acids could bridge these gaps and accelerate miRNA therapies from preclinical to clinical stages [ ]. However, based on a research study of pregnant women undergoing elective caesarean sections, the prevalence of anemia was 10.85%, associated with the age group of 25–30 years, low education, urban residence, and diabetes, underscoring the need for integrated monitoring in patients with leukemia during pregnancy to mitigate renal and hematologic complications [ ].

## Clinical trials
Recent clinical trials have emphasized the translational capabilities of miRNA-and PI3K/AKT-targeted therapies for leukemia. For instance, miRNA mimics, such as MRX34 (NCT01829971), have exhibited preliminary effectiveness in hematologic malignancies but have faced safety concerns, whereas novel agents, such as miRisten (NCT07025564), are aimed at relapsed AML. PI3K inhibitors, such as idelalisib (NCT01539512), have led to improved outcomes in CLL; however, resistance remains a significant obstacle. Table summarizes significant ongoing and completed trials, emphasizing the potential of combination strategies to improve efficacy.

 Preclinical evidence further supports the therapeutic potential of LNA anti-miR-155 in Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM) and CLL. Overexpression of miR-155 in WM and CLL cell lines (e.g., BCWM.1 and MEC1) promotes pathogenesis; however, LNA anti-miR-155 (20 μM) reduced proliferation by up to 40% without affecting cell survival, upregulating target genes such as CEBPβ, SOCS1, and TP53DINP1, as confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) [ ].

## Study limitations
Despite its comprehensiveness, this narrative review has several limitations that should be recognized to provide context for its findings. First, a selection bias may have arisen from the emphasis on studies that concentrated on miRNA-PI3K/AKT/mTOR interactions, which may have led to the neglect of negative or contradictory results. Such bias, which is frequently observed in narrative reviews, originates from the lack of a systematic search protocol, as suggested by the PRISMA guidelines, which could have facilitated a wider inclusion of studies. Second, the lack of meta-analysis diminishes the statistical reliability of our conclusions. The diversity in study designs, including variations in experimental models (such as cell lines versus patient-derived xenografts) and differences in patient demographics (e.g., pediatric versus adult leukemia), has prevented quantitative synthesis, as indicated by recent methodological critiques. The presence of this heterogeneity, particularly in preclinical studies, may compromise the generalizability of the results to a range of leukemia subtypes and clinical scenarios.
However, clinical application of miRNA-based therapies has encountered considerable obstacles. MiRNAs demonstrate limited in vivo stability because of rapid degradation by nucleases and elimination by the immune system, which requires the development of sophisticated delivery systems [ , ]. For instance, ineffective delivery to leukemic cells, particularly within bone marrow niches, continues to pose a challenge, as studies have indicated inconsistent uptake in hematopoietic tissues. Off-target effects present further risks, as miRNAs might inadvertently silence genes that are not their intended targets, resulting in toxicity or modified cellular responses, as noted in initial phase trials. Furthermore, the ability of the immune system to clear miRNA therapeutics, such as those introduced via viral vectors, can lead to inflammatory responses, making their clinical application more challenging. Future studies must address these limitations by implementing systematic review protocols and enhanced bioinformatics to bolster the reliability and applicability of the outcomes.

## Future directions and conclusion
This review describes the dynamic interplay between miRNAs and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in leukemia, focusing on AML, ALL, CML, and CLL. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway emerges as a central driver of leukemogenesis, modulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and therapy resistance across subtypes [ , ]. As pivotal gene expression regulators, miRNAs intricately shape the activity of this pathway through bidirectional feedback loops, as detailed in Sect. " ". Key miRNAs, such as miR-7-5p and miR-93 in AML, miR-149 and miR-653-5p in ALL, miR-223 and miR-150 in CML, and miR-22 in CLL, target pathway components or are modulated by their effectors and influence disease progression and treatment outcomes [ , , , ]. These findings, which are summarized in Table  and illustrated in Fig.  , highlight the potential of miRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic targets.
Translating miRNA-PI3K/AKT insights into clinical applications is a priority; however, significant research gaps remain. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of miRNA-based therapies, such as antagomiRs (e.g., targeting miR-155) and mimics (e.g., restoring miR-29a); however, their clinical validation is limited [ , ]. Advanced delivery systems such as lipid nanoparticles and locked nucleic acids show promise for enhancing stability and specificity, as evidenced by recent trials on hematologic malignancies. However, challenges such as variable uptake in the bone marrow niches and immune clearance require further optimization [ ]. Combining miRNA therapies with PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors (e.g., idelalisib and MK-2206) could address resistance mechanisms, such as MAPK pathway activation, particularly in AML and CLL.
Future research should prioritize the translation of these insights into clinical applications. Although preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of miRNA-based therapies, such as antagomiRs (e.g., targeting miR-155) and miRNA mimics (e.g., restoring miR-29a), significant barriers remain. Achieving clinical feasibility requires addressing miRNA instability, inefficient delivery, and off-target effects [ , , ]. Advanced delivery systems, including lipid nanoparticles and chemically modified oligonucleotides (e.g., locked nucleic acids), demonstrate promise in enhancing stability and specificity, as evidenced by recent trials on hematologic malignancies [ , ]. Furthermore, integrating miRNA therapies with PI3K/AKT inhibitors, such as idelalisib or MK-2206, could overcome resistance mechanisms such as MAPK pathway activation, improving outcomes in resistant leukemias [ , ]. Exploring combination therapies, particularly in AML and CLL, where chemoresistance is prevalent, represents a critical direction for personalized medicine [ , , ].
In addition to therapeutic applications, miRNA signatures offer opportunities for precise diagnostics. For instance, downregulated miRNAs such as hsa-miR-199a-3p in AML and miR-100 in ALL correlate with prognosis, enabling risk stratification [ , ]. Large-scale genomic studies supported by machine learning could refine these signatures for routine clinical use, as recent analyses of childhood leukemia demonstrate [ – ]. Additionally, investigating lncRNA-miRNA interactions, such as the DUXAP8-miR-29a axis in ALL or HULC-miR-150 in CML, may uncover novel regulatory networks that enhance PI3K/AKT/mTOR targeting strategies [ , ]. These initiatives have the potential to transform diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks and provide optimism for enhanced survival rates and quality of life of patients with leukemia. Ongoing research is crucial for connecting the gap between the preclinical potential and clinical applications, thereby facilitating the development of innovative and personalized treatments.
