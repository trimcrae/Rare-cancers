## Abstract
Hematological malignancies, including leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma (MM), are cancers originating in the hematopoietic system, characterized by diverse pathogenesis and clinical features. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) such as microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs, play critical roles in regulating gene expression and influencing cell fate. Accumulating evidence indicates that ncRNAs are key modulators of programmed cell death (PCD) pathways, affecting tumor development, progression, and drug resistance in these malignancies. PCD, a precisely programmed and regulated cell death process, is vital for maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing the proliferation of dangerous cells. NcRNAs are implicated in the primary mechanisms underlying the development of drug resistance. The evasion of PCD is a hallmark of cancer, and ncRNAs can significantly impact these pathways, with dysregulation observed across leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma, influencing PCD pathways and clinical outcomes. Understanding the ncRNA-PCD interplay is crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes. This review explores the functions, regulatory mechanisms, and potential applications of ncRNAs in modulating PCD in hematological malignancies, particularly leukemias, providing insights for future anti-tumor therapies.

## Introduction
Blood-related cancers emerge within the hematopoietic system and can potentially infiltrate diverse physiological systems and organs. These malignancies predominantly encompass three primary categories: leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma (MM), characterized by significant heterogeneity in both pathogenetic mechanisms and clinical manifestations [ ]. Leukemia represents a hematopoietic disorder resulting from genetic mutations in progenitor cells, subsequently triggering abnormal white blood cell proliferation and accumulation across various organs [ ]. Multiple myeloma, a myeloid plasma cell neoplasm, demonstrates particularly unfavorable prognostic indicators [ , ]. Its progression typically initiates with malignant plasma cell hyperplasia, eventually advancing to an active stage accompanied by clinical complications [ ]. Lymphomas are conventionally classified into Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) [ ]. HL itself comprises classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL) [ ]. Its pathogenesis predominantly involves Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells and immune cell dysfunction [ ]. NHL originates from diverse lymphocyte lineages, including B cells, T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells [ ]. This category is further subdivided into multiple subtypes based on molecular mechanisms and specific lymphocyte variations [ ], with each subtype exhibiting unique clinical and biological characteristics. The World Health Organization’s hematopoietic tumor classification (WHO-HAEM5) has progressively integrated advanced molecular and histopathological insights, providing increasingly sophisticated diagnostic frameworks [ ]. Despite ongoing therapeutic optimizations enhancing patient outcomes, challenges with treatment refractoriness and relapse persist [ ]. Advancing precision medicine necessitates deeper comprehension of these malignancies’ fundamental pathophysiological processes. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), encompassing microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), emerge as critical regulators of gene expression and cellular fate. Emerging research demonstrates ncRNAs’ significant involvement in modulating programmed cell death (PCD) pathways within hematological malignancies, substantially influencing tumor development, progression, and potential drug resistance mechanisms [ , ].
PCD represents a meticulously orchestrated cellular demise mechanism governed by precise regulatory pathways. This process critically influences numerous physiological domains, including organismal development, tissue homeostasis maintenance, infectious defense, and immunological response modulation [ ]. PCD demonstrates significant correlations with tumor developmental and progressive mechanisms, distinguishing itself through molecular, biochemical, and functional characteristics [ , ]. Unlike accidental cellular termination, PCD can be therapeutically manipulated through pharmacological or genetic interventions, thereby potentially influencing cellular fate [ , ]. Its fundamental purpose involves eliminating potentially hazardous cellular entities, with tumor progression frequently characterized by PCD evasion mechanisms [ ]. Modern scientific investigations have unveiled various PCD mechanisms, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, PANoptosis, NETosis, lysosome-dependent cell death, cuproptosis, parthanatos, and ferroptosis [ – ]. In the context of hematological malignancies, research primarily focuses on understanding apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy, though many aspects remain unexplored. Both genetic and epigenetic factors can alter the molecular pathways associated with PCD in blood-related cancers, significantly impacting disease progression. A notable example involves receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), where its genetic ablation reduces blood cell death and facilitates AML development [ , ]. Further studies have demonstrated that RIPK3 deficiency transforms FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD)-induced bone marrow proliferation into AML by inhibiting cellular death and differentiation pathways in experimental settings [ ]. Abnormal expression of B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) in both AML and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) enables cancer cells to evade programmed death mechanisms [ , ]. Additionally, mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL), a crucial mediator of necroptosis, shows enhanced expression in patients with myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML). This increased expression correlates significantly with anemia severity and response to hypomethylating therapeutic agents [ ].
In the context of hematologic malignancies, ncRNAs emerge as crucial modulators, particularly in their interaction with cellular death pathways. These molecular entities have been identified as fundamental components in the emergence of therapeutic resistance mechanisms [ , ]. Their influence extends across both normal physiological functions and pathological conditions. Studies focusing on chemotherapy-resistant leukemia have revealed that ncRNAs significantly influence both the initiation and evolution of drug resistance patterns [ ]. Cancer cells’ ability to circumvent PCD represents a defining characteristic of malignancy, with ncRNAs serving as critical regulators in these survival pathways [ , ]. Within the spectrum of blood-related cancers, diverse mechanisms - both genetic and non-genetic - alter the expression patterns of PCD-associated molecules, thereby influencing tumor development. Specifically, aberrant ncRNA expression patterns have been documented across leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, with these alterations significantly impacting PCD pathways and, consequently, patient prognosis [ , ]. Deciphering the complex relationships between ncRNAs and PCD mechanisms in hematological cancers holds paramount importance for advancing therapeutic innovations and enhancing treatment efficacy. This comprehensive analysis examines the multifaceted roles, regulatory pathways, and potential therapeutic applications of ncRNAs in PCD modulation within blood-related malignancies, offering valuable perspectives for developing future anti-cancer strategies.

## Types of programmed cell death in hematological malignancies

## Apoptosis: the classic pathway
Apoptosis represents a distinctive form of programmed cellular demise, differentiating itself from alternative cell death mechanisms like necroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, netosis, and necrosis. Throughout this process, cells experience profound morphological transformations, including nuclear and mitochondrial contraction. Membrane blebbing progressively diminishes cellular dimensions and functional capacities, with apoptotic fragments rapidly eliminated through phagocytic mechanisms, thereby preventing substantial inflammatory responses. Two primary pathways can initiate apoptosis: an extrinsic death receptor pathway mediated by tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and first apoptosis signal death receptor superfamilies, and an intrinsic pathway regulated by the BCL-2 protein family. These mechanisms converge at the mitochondrial level via truncated BH3-interacting domain (tBID) protein, with this review emphasizing the intrinsic apoptotic pathway’s therapeutic targeting in hematological malignancies [ ].
Since the initial identification of the first cell death prevention protein, BCL-2 [ ], the intrinsic apoptotic pathway has become a focal point in cancer research. Apoptosis represents a critical barrier that nascent neoplastic cells must overcome during malignant transformation [ ]. The BCL-2 gene was initially characterized as a pivotal driver in follicular lymphoma (FL) pathogenesis, associated with the t(14;18)(q32;q21) chromosomal translocation - FL’s genetic signature [ ]. Subsequently, BCL-2 was discovered to contribute to pathophysiological mechanisms in CLL, MCL, and Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM). Its significance varies across other hematological malignancies, including MM [ ], diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [ ]. Uniquely, BCL-2 dysregulation does not enhance cellular proliferation but instead enables cell survival by preventing programmed death. Another critical prosurvival protein, MCL-1, initially recognized as a myeloid cell differentiation regulator, has emerged as a crucial survival factor in MM, CML, AML, ALL, and DLBCL [ , , ]. Similarly, B-cell lymphoma-extra-large (BCL-xL; Bcl2l1) plays an essential role in megakaryocyte survival, with its loss or inhibition potentially inducing thrombocytopenia [ ]. BCL-xL dysfunction may also contribute to pathogenesis in myeloproliferative disorders, including polycythemia vera [ , ].

## Necroptosis: regulated necrosis
Necroptosis, a specialized form of PCD, emerges as an alternative mechanism to apoptosis upon death domain receptor activation. In cancer cells where caspase-8 is inhibited, RIPK1 and RIPK3 collaborate to form a necrotic complex, triggering necroptosis [ , ]. Morphologically, necroptosis manifests through cellular and organelle swelling, membrane perforation, and subsequent cell rupture, which releases cellular contents and stimulates inflammatory and immune responses. The process primarily relies on MLKL phosphorylation by RIPK1 and RIPK3, with phosphorylated MLKL recruited to form necrosomes and initiate cell death [ – ].
RIPK3 activation occurs through multiple pathways. Fas-associated death domain proteins and toll-like receptors (TLR3/4) can trigger its activation, and the immune system leverages necroptosis to combat viral infections. For example, Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1) directly interacts with RIPK3 to induce activation. Aberrant RIPK3 expression in tumor cell lines suggests potential therapeutic implications. Potential mechanisms of RIPK3 silencing include BRAF mutations and DNA methylation. Demethylating drugs might restore RIPK3 and enhance drug-resistant cell susceptibility to necroptosis. Interestingly, RIPK3’s functional diversity is evident, as it doesn’t participate in MYC-driven lymphoma oncogenesis but plays a role in inflammatory responses and potentially colorectal cancer progression [ – ].
RIPK1 demonstrates a complex functional profile, simultaneously promoting cell survival through the NF-κB pathway and mediating cell death via interactions with RIPK3 and MLKL [ ]. Research across various hematological malignancies reveals nuanced roles: MDS and CMML patients with higher RIPK1 expression exhibit shorter survival [ ]. RIPK1 expression correlates with specific AML subtypes, particularly being upregulated in FLT3-ITD-positive AML [ ]. Pharmacological RIPK1 targeting can induce cell death and enhance chidamide sensitivity in FLT3-ITD-positive AML [ , ]. In DLBCL, RIPK1 is downregulated, and its overexpression effectively inhibits cell proliferation [ – ]. While necroptosis-based tumor therapy appears promising, critical questions remain about the selective targeting of tumor cells and potential systemic inflammatory responses.

## Pyroptosis: inflammatory cell death
Pyroptosis constitutes a paramount immunological defense strategy that unleashes inflammatory substances responding to cellular injury and pathogenic invasions. This programmed cell death mechanism involves proteolytic disassembly of gasdermin protein family members, encompassing GSDMA through GSDME. Distinctive cellular transformations include protein channel generation, cytoplasmic contents expulsion, cellular expansion, and membrane fragmentation [ – ].
Multiple intracellular signaling cascades can initiate pyroptosis. The conventional inflammasome pathway involves detecting pathogen-associated and danger-associated molecular patterns, which construct the NLRP3 inflammasome. This activation triggers proteolytic transformation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and gasdermin D, culminating in cell demise and subsequent inflammatory reactions [ , ]. The interleukin-1 cytokine family plays a pivotal role; IL-1β, a primary acute inflammatory mediator predominantly generated by macrophages [ , , ], facilitates Th17 cell differentiation. Conversely, IL-18 predominantly guides T helper cell development toward Th1 populations [ , ]. Through these regulatory mechanisms, GSDMD undergoes cleavage to generate N-terminal fragments, causing membrane perforation and inflammatory factor release [ , ]. Furthermore, caspase-3 can modulate GSDME fragmentation via apoptotic signaling pathways, resulting in pyroptosis [ , ].
NLRP3 emerges as a critical signaling molecule with substantial implications for malignancy development. Genetic variations have been associated with diverse cancer risks: Specific NLRP3 polymorphisms correlate with increased susceptibility to bladder, gastric, pancreatic, and lung cancers [ – ]. Hematological malignancies demonstrate particularly intriguing connections, with genetic polymorphisms in inflammatory cytokines linked to lymphoma susceptibility and CML characteristics [ – ]. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), NLRP3 inflammasome activation reveals complex tumorigenic effects; it can simultaneously promote tumor cell proliferation and drug resistance by upregulating oncogenic factors while suppressing tumor suppressor mechanisms [ – ].
Macrophage polarization becomes influenced by inflammasome components [ ]. Pyroptosis-associated molecules demonstrate noteworthy significance in hematological malignancies, with potential prognostic implications for survival and immune status [ – ]. Emerging therapeutic strategies explore targeting specific molecular interactions, such as PRMT5’s regulatory roles and BAFF-mediated activation mechanisms [ – ].

## Ferroptosis: the iron-dependent programmed cell death
Ferroptosis represents a recently discovered form of PCD characterized by iron-dependent oxidative processes [ ]. Unlike other cell death mechanisms, ferroptosis displays unique morphological features, including reduced cellular density, outer mitochondrial membrane disruption, diminished or absent internal mitochondrial cristae, and an intact nuclear structure. A critical hallmark of ferroptosis is the substantial accumulation of lipid peroxides [ ]. The regulation of lipid peroxidation involves complex mechanisms, with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serving as the primary cellular defense against excessive lipid peroxide buildup [ ]. The cystine/glutamate antiport system plays a pivotal role in cellular metabolism. When inhibited by agents like erastin, it prevents cystine cellular entry, consequently reducing cysteine availability for glutathione (GSH) synthesis. GSH is crucial for GPX4-mediated lipid peroxide hydrolysis. Disrupting GSH production or GPX4 functionality impedes lipid peroxide removal, ultimately triggering oxidative cellular damage and ferroptosis. Consequently, system xc-, GSH, and GPX4 represent critical intervention points for ferroptosis induction [ ].
Investigations across hematological malignancies reveal significant insights: GPX4 demonstrates elevated expression in: DLBCL, AML, and MM. GPX4 emerges as an independent prognostic indicator for DLBCL, with ferroptosis-related molecular prognostic models effectively predicting patient outcomes [ – ]. These findings underscore the remarkable heterogeneity of ferroptosis in hematological malignancies and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target. The complex interplay of molecular mechanisms suggests that ferroptosis represents a nuanced approach to understanding and potentially treating various blood-related cancers. By targeting specific molecular pathways, researchers may develop more precise and effective therapeutic strategies. The exploration of ferroptosis continues to reveal its intricate role in cellular metabolism, oxidative stress management, and potential cancer intervention, making it a promising field of ongoing research.

## Autophagy: the amazing self-degrading control system
The initiation of autophagy stems from diverse cellular stressors, including nutrient scarcity, growth factor absence, low oxygen environments, accumulation of reactive oxygen species, protein misfolding, organelle damage, and interactions with intracellular microorganisms [ , ]. Specific signaling cascades and broader molecular regulatory mechanisms are triggered to orchestrate autophagy’s various stages, typically functioning in synchronization with complementary cellular control systems like programmed cell death and protein degradation pathways [ , ]. Autophagy unfolds through a meticulously choreographed sequence of events. Initially, a segment of intracellular membrane segregates, forming a preliminary structure called a phagophore. Subsequently, the phagophore undergoes nucleation and expansion, ultimately sealing its edges to create a double-membrane vesicle known as an autophagosome, which encapsulates targeted cellular components. During maturation, the autophagosome merges with endosomes, transforming into an amphisome, which subsequently fuses with lysosomes. This fusion generates an autophagolysosome capable of enzymatically breaking down its internal contents, releasing metabolized materials into the cytoplasm for cellular energy production and to facilitate various biological processes [ , ].
Emerging scientific literature suggests that autophagy’s role in leukemia progression is intricately linked to specific oncogenic mechanisms [ ]. The RET proto-oncogene, a tyrosine kinase crucial in AML, demonstrates complex interactions with cellular degradation processes. Activated RET pathways can suppress autophagy, influencing the stability of leukemogenic factors such as mutated FLT3. Interestingly, RET pathway inhibition correlates with increased FLT3 degradation through autophagic mechanisms. Conversely, proteasome inhibitors can enhance the autophagic breakdown of FLT3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3/ITD) variants [ , ].
Disrupting the FLT3-ITD mutation in AML cells demonstrates that impeding autophagy-dependent proliferation both experimentally and in living systems can elevate baseline autophagic processes. This FLT3-ITD–associated autophagy critically depends on ATF4 transcriptional levels. In AML cells with nucleophosmin 1 (NPM) mutations, promyelocytic leukemia (PML) protein maintains cytoplasmic stability. Cytoplasmic PML triggers serine/threonine-protein kinases, phosphorylation, consequently promoting prosurvival autophagy [ , ].
Experimental investigations using MLL-ENL mouse models revealed that autophagy disruption through ATG7 elimination results in reduced leukemia-initiating cells (LICs) and enhanced survival. These conditions triggered increased reactive oxygen species generation, elevated mitochondrial activity, and enhanced cell death. Consequently, peripheral blood blast populations diminished due to heightened apoptosis [ ].
Studies of the MLL-ENL mouse model revealed that inhibiting autophagy through ATG7 or ATG5 elimination results in enhanced leukemia advancement in living organisms. These studies also showed that cells with disrupted autophagy displayed irregular mitochondrial behavior, particularly in growth and transformation processes [ ].
In contrast, autophagy suppression in the AML1-ETO AML mouse model inhibited leukemia-initiating cell multiplication. While MLL-Af9 (MA9) AML cells show increased autophagy compared to regular bone marrow, eliminating Rb1cc1 or ATG5 had minimal effects on MA9-AML cell viability or development both in vitro and in vivo. Evidence suggests autophagy’s critical role in disease initiation but not in its continuation [ – ].
HLX, a transcription factor overexpressed in AML, influences blood cell differentiation regulation. Studies indicate that increased HLX triggers AMPK activation and supports AML cell survival through autophagy mechanisms [ ]. It’s worth noting that due to AML’s diversity, autophagy can serve dual roles as either a cancer promoter or inhibitor, varying by AML subtype.
CML, accounting for 15% of adult leukemia cases, is defined by the t(22;9)(q34;q11) translocation and BCR-ABL fusion protein with enhanced tyrosine kinase activity. Evidence shows BCR-ABL1 triggers MAPK15-dependent autophagy, facilitating cellular transformation. However, alternative research indicates BCR-ABL suppresses ATF5-dependent autophagy in transformed cells [ ].
Under specific oxygen conditions, KML5 cells depend on autophagy mainly for differentiation rather than proliferation. BIM1, acting as a transcription factor, supports CML’s progression to acute phases [ , ]. Blocking BIM1 enhances CCNG2 expression while reducing tumor-suppressing autophagy responses. Current studies are investigating autophagy’s role in MDS development. Increased ATG2B and GSKIP expression correlates with genetic variations in myeloid cancers. U2AF35 (S35F) mutation carriers show elevated pre-mRNA ATG7 and reduced autophagy. High-risk MDS patients demonstrate decreased nuclear red blood cell LC3B and increased mitochondrial abnormalities, with LC3B levels linking to hemoglobin levels. Furthermore, MDS patients show reduced ATG3 expression compared to healthy individuals, while ATG3 overexpression in SKM-1 MDS cells increases caspase-dependent autophagy and cell death [ – ]. ## NcRNAs involved in programmed cell death
Comprehensive exploration of the human genome, encompassing approximately 3 billion base pair sequences, has unveiled a striking discrepancy between transcriptional activity and protein production. The Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) Project discovered that cytoplasmic primary transcripts represent up to 93% of genomic sequences, with an exceptionally small fraction—merely 1%—ultimately encoding functional proteins. Poly-adenylated (poly-A) tails serve as a distinctive characteristic of mature ribonucleic acid molecules, playing critical roles in RNA stabilization and nuclear export mechanisms. Interestingly, these poly-A tails are not exclusively associated with messenger RNAs, but are also prominently featured in numerous ncRNA variants, spanning both intermediary and mature developmental stages [ – ]. Cytosolic poly-A RNA populations constitute approximately 5–10% of the genome sequence [ ], which significantly overshadows the minimal 1% responsible for protein encoding. Consequently, the overwhelming majority of poly-A RNA molecules are categorized as ncRNAs. Historically, researchers have classified ncRNAs into two primary classifications based on molecular length; lncRNAs: Exceeding 200 nucleotides in length; Small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs): Less than 200 nucleotides, further subdivided into specialized categories including miRNAs, Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), and Small nuclear RNAs (snoRNAs) [ ]. These diverse ncRNA populations possess the potential to individually or collectively modulate cellular death processes through intricate molecular interactions (Fig. ).
 

## MiRNAs and their role in PCD
Small, non-coding ribonucleic acid segments known as miRNAs, typically measuring 21–23 nucleotides, serve as critical regulators of post-transcriptional gene expression mechanisms [ ]. These molecular entities profoundly influence cellular death processes, with the elimination of miRNA-processing enzymes like Dicer in neural stem and neural crest derivative cells resulting in widespread cellular demise. Additional miRNA processors, including Drosha, DGCR8, and XPO5, similarly exert substantial impacts on cell death pathways [ , – ].
BCL2 overexpression contributes to multidrug resistance in cancer, making BCL2-specific miRNAs, particularly the miR-181 family, promising therapeutic targets. Members of this family, including miR-181a, b, c, and d, regulate apoptosis by directly targeting BCL2 and related anti-apoptotic genes, thereby sensitizing resistant cancer cells to treatment across various malignancies such as MM and AML, as well as solid tumors [ ].
The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is initiated when membrane-bound death receptors (DRs) engage with their specific ligands. Of the six known DRs, researchers have primarily focused on Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor (TNFR) and Fas. Upon activation, these receptors’ intracellular domains recruit specific adaptor proteins, triggering a caspase cascade beginning with caspase-8. miRNAs demonstrate significant regulatory control over these death ligands. For example, miR-21 exhibits anti-apoptotic properties by targeting Fas ligand (FasL) in oxygen-deprived cardiomyocytes, neurons, and hepatocytes. Notably, Akt enhances miR-21 expression, establishing miR-21 as an intermediary in Akt-FasL signaling. Additionally, adaptor proteins serve as miRNA targets, exemplified by miR-27a’s interaction with Fas-associated protein with death domain (FADD) at its 3’-UTR. The miR-23a-27a-24 cluster can either autonomously trigger apoptosis or enhance TNFα-mediated cell death by downregulating FADD [ – ].
MiRNAs also influence downstream apoptotic signaling components. While its precise mechanisms remain unclear, miR-375 enhances TNFα-induced apoptosis, accompanied by decreased cIAP and cFLIP-L levels [ ]. Specific miRNAs serve as crucial necroptotic regulators. In cardiomyocyte progenitor cells (CMPCs), hydrogen-peroxide treatment significantly increases miR-155 expression, which targets RIPK1. Elevated miR-155 levels reduce CMPC necrosis with effectiveness comparable to the RIPK1 inhibitor necrostatin-1 [ ]. In contrast, miR-874 promotes necroptosis by targeting caspase-8, thereby removing its inhibitory effect on RIPK1/RIPK3 [ , ]. The transcription factor Foxo3a can suppress miR-874 expression, consequently reducing necroptotic cell death [ ]. These discoveries highlight miRNAs’ potential as vital regulators of programmed necrosis, suggesting therapeutic possibilities comparable to pharmaceutical interventions. This potential holds particular significance given the current limited availability of targeted molecular therapeutics in this field.

## LncRNAs in PCD regulation
LncRNAs represent an expansive category of ncRNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length. Analogous to miRNAs, these molecular entities orchestrate critical functions in biological processes, encompassing gene expression regulation, chromatin restructuring, and cellular signaling mechanisms [ , ]. Extensive research reveals lncRNAs’ multifaceted involvement in apoptotic modulation across diverse contexts. Numerous investigations have particularly focused on cancer cell lines, demonstrating how these cells strategically manipulate lncRNA expression to circumvent programmed cell death. The urothelial cancer-associated 1 (UCA1) lncRNA, prominently expressed in bladder cancer cells, elevates Wnt6 levels, thereby enhancing Akt-mediated BAX inhibition and establishing cisplatin-induced apoptosis resistance [ , ]. Similarly, T-ALL-R-LncR1, associated with T-cell ALL, demonstrates complex regulatory capabilities by suppressing caspase-3, inhibiting Par-4, and augmenting pro-apoptotic Smac protein expression [ , ].
Several cancer-specific lncRNAs inhibit apoptosis by targeting key regulators of programmed cell death pathways. For example, URHC suppresses the kinase ZAK, inactivating ERK/MAPK signaling to reduce apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, while HOXA-AS2 inhibits the extrinsic apoptotic pathway by repressing TRAIL and caspase activation in leukemia cells. SPRY4-IT1 modulates lipid metabolism through lipin 2, and its knockdown induces apoptosis via lipotoxicity and possibly MAPK pathway effects in melanoma. Additionally, PlncRNA-1 promotes prostate cancer cell survival by regulating androgen receptor and TGF-β1 signaling, and AFAP1-AS1 reduces apoptosis in esophageal cancer by preventing caspase-3 activation and enhancing VEGFA expression [ – ].
Conversely, certain lncRNAs actively promote apoptotic processes; AK139328, abundantly present in normal mouse liver, mediates apoptosis during liver ischemia-reperfusion injury. Reducing its expression diminishes caspase-3 activation and mitigates tissue damage. In vascular smooth muscle cells, the HIF 1 alpha-antisense RNA 1, regulated by Brahma-related gene 1, activates caspase-3 and contributes to thoracic aortic aneurysm pathogenesis [ , ]. Cancer cells additionally employ strategic mechanisms to downregulate pro-apoptotic lncRNAs. The Growth arrest-specific 5 (GAS5) lncRNA, a potent apoptosis promoter, experiences significant suppression in prostate cancer cells developing apoptosis resistance [ ]. Its apoptotic mechanism depends on caspase-8 activation and potentially involves p53 upregulation [ ]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the uc002mbe.2 lncRNA demonstrates markedly reduced expression compared to normal cells, yet can be dramatically induced (up to 300-fold) following Trichostatin A treatment, highlighting its pivotal role in anti-tumor mechanisms [ ]. Additional lncRNAs exhibit complex roles in regulating apoptosis, as seen with MEG3, which inhibits intrinsic apoptosis by reducing BAX and caspase-3 activity. Cancer cells exploit such mechanisms, exemplified by the hypoxia-induced downregulation of lncRNA-LET in gallbladder cancer, which fosters apoptosis resistance, while its overexpression reverses this effect by promoting cell death. The interplay between lncRNAs and oncogenes like Myc highlights their potential role in tumorigenesis and cancer cell survival, positioning Myc-regulated lncRNAs as promising therapeutic targets. Advances in nanoparticle-mediated RNA delivery are facilitating the development of novel treatments targeting these molecules [ – ].
Theoretically, lncRNAs and miRNAs could interact, introducing additional complexity to cell death regulation. A comprehensive transcriptome-wide bioinformatics analysis by Jalali et al. revealed extensive interactions between diverse lncRNA-miRNA pairs [ ]. Many lncRNAs possess distinctive miRNA recognition elements predominantly concentrated in their mid-to-3’ regions, rendering them potential targets for corresponding miRNAs. Conversely, some lncRNAs contain miRNA regulatory elements capable of targeting miRNAs themselves. This research provided structural insights into miRNA-lncRNA interactions, emphasizing bioinformatics’ potential in regulatory RNA investigations [ ]. LncRNA-miRNA interactions manifest through two primary mechanisms: Competitive Endogenous RNA (ceRNA) Sequestration: LncRNAs can act as molecular sponges, intercept miRNAs and preventing their interaction with target mRNAs [ ]. For instance, lncRNA FER1L4 and LncRNA RB1 collaboratively bind and sequester miR-106a-5p, preventing its targeting of PTEN [ ]. These ceRNAs likely operate through spatial occlusion, blocking miRNAs from accessing their intended targets [ ]. Some lncRNAs directly suppress miRNA expression. The lncRNA HULC exemplifies this mechanism by suppressing miR-372 in hepatocellular carcinoma, subsequently influencing CREB-mediated epigenetic modifications governing cell death and survival gene expression [ ]. The regulatory relationship between lncRNAs and miRNAs can be bidirectional, further complicating the regulatory network. The anti-apoptotic lncRNA PCGEM1 and pro-apoptotic miR-145 demonstrate this complexity by simultaneously antagonizing each other’s apoptotic regulation while mutually suppressing their respective expressions, creating a regulatory feedback loop [ ].
Protein factors significantly influence lncRNA-miRNA interactions, integrating them into an expansive cell death regulation network. The tumor suppressor p53 exemplifies this intricate regulation. It targets a pro-apoptotic lncRNA (loc285194) through a p53-response element, inducing its expression. A reciprocal repression exists between loc285194 and the anti-apoptotic miR-211, incorporating these regulatory ncRNAs into a sophisticated p53 signaling network [ ]. Intriguingly, protein-encoding mRNAs might also participate in this regulatory ecosystem by competing with other mRNAs or lncRNAs for miRNA binding, potentially functioning as additional ceRNAs. However, this mechanism remains speculative for death-related mRNAs and requires further investigation [ ].

## CircRNAs and their functions in PCD
CircRNAs represent a distinctive ncRNA subtype characterized by their unique covalently closed loop structure, fundamentally differentiating them from traditional linear RNA molecules. This circular configuration emerges through back-splicing, a molecular mechanism wherein a precursor mRNA’s 5’ and 3’ termini interconnect, generating a remarkably stable RNA molecule resistant to exonuclease degradation [ , ]. CircRNAs can originate from diverse genomic sources, encompassing protein-coding genes, introns, and even viral RNA genomes. Accumulating research suggests circRNAs significantly influence PCD in cancer cells, presenting promising avenues for future oncological investigations [ , ].
CircRNAs demonstrate substantial regulatory capabilities in cancer progression through multiple mechanisms; for instance, reduced circRNA_0000285 expression in cervical cancer cells correlates with decreased cell viability and increased apoptotic rates. This phenomenon is accompanied by diminished Bcl-2 protein levels and elevated Bax protein concentrations, offering novel insights into cervical cancer pathogenesis [ ]. In osteosarcoma, circ_0001649 overexpression triggers cell apoptosis by augmenting Apaf-1, cleaved-caspase-3, and cleaved-caspase-9 levels, potentially presenting innovative therapeutic strategies [ ]. CircRNAs also play critical roles in extrinsic apoptotic pathways. Research indicates circ_0007127 modulates K-562 cell apoptosis through the miR-513a-5p/caspase-8 axis, emerging as a potential molecular intervention target [ ]. Autophagy represents another significant domain of circRNA functional influence. Recent investigations reveal circRNAs predominantly regulate autophagy by functioning as miRNA sponges. CircCDYL exemplifies this mechanism by enhancing autophagy and promoting breast cancer progression via the miR-1275-ATG7/ULK1 pathway. This discovery positions circCDYL as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, offering personalized treatment insights [ ]. Another compelling example involves hsa_circ_0007813, which is upregulated in bladder cancer. This circRNA effectively sequesters miR-361-3p, thereby modulating IGF2R expression. Knockdown of hsa_circ_0007813 can inhibit autophagy and suppress IGF2R expression, demonstrating the intricate regulatory networks facilitated by circRNAs. The current research landscape suggests circRNAs play imperative roles in apoptotic and autophagic processes, presenting substantial clinical application potential. However, comprehensive understanding requires additional investigative efforts to fully elucidate their complex molecular interactions [ ]. CircATG7, a circRNA originating from the Atg7’s exon region, demonstrated increased levels in pancreatic cancer tissue samples and correlated with decreased patient survival rates. Research revealed that CircATG7 functions through dual mechanisms: as a miRNA sponge and by interacting with RNA-binding proteins to regulate autophagy. Specifically, it enhanced PC cell growth and movement by controlling autophagy through the miR-766-5p/ATG7 and HUR/ATG7 pathways. These findings suggest CircATG7’s potential as a therapeutic target for PC treatment. Similar autophagy-regulating circRNAs have been identified in other cancers: circSEPT9 in breast cancer, circMUC16 in ovarian cancer, and circPARD3 in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma [ – ].
Current literature contains limited research on circRNA’s role in necroptosis. Studies have shown elevated circCEP128 expression in bladder cancer cells and tissues. Its suppression activated RIPK3/MLKL-mediated necroptosis by enhancing RIPK3 phosphorylation, which restored cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer cells. These discoveries highlight circCEP128’s potential as both a diagnostic marker and treatment target [ ]. Additionally, circCNEACR has been found to regulate cardiac cell necroptosis by inhibiting FOXA2, thereby reducing RIPK3 expression (Table ) [ ]. While necroptosis research continues to expand, the relationship between circRNAs and tumor necroptosis remains largely unexplored, presenting opportunities for future cancer research. ## Mechanisms of ncRNA-mediated regulation of programmed cell death
miRNAs can interact with BAX; a pro-apoptotic protein typically located in the cytosol that undergoes conformational changes during apoptosis [ – ]. Ischemic preconditioning demonstrates that downregulating BAX and upregulating miR-1 and miR-21 can inhibit apoptosis and enhance cardiac tissue resistance [ ]. Innovative delivery mechanisms, such as viral vectors, enable targeted miRNA intervention. Adenovirus-mediated miR-22 overexpression can indirectly suppress BAX by interacting with CREB-binding protein [ ]. Some miRNAs, like miR-24, can prevent BAX translocation to the mitochondrial membrane, thereby inhibiting cytochrome-c release and apoptosis execution [ ]. Similar protective mechanisms have been observed in neurological contexts. For instance, miR-23a and miR-27a demonstrate rapid downregulation following traumatic brain injury, and their synthetic mimetics can effectively limit neuronal apoptosis and reduce cortical lesion volume [ ].
Bim, a characteristic BH3-only protein, not only undergoes direct miRNA regulation but also functions as an integrator of miRNA-mediated effects on other BCL2 family members through its BH3 domain interactions with BCL2, BCL2-XL, and MCL1. In mammalian primed pluripotent stem cells, miR-20, miR-92, and miR-302 regulate Bim to maintain cell survival by controlling apoptotic thresholds [ , ]. Previous studies have shown that miR-24 directly suppresses Bim by binding to its 3’-UTR. Following mouse acute myocardial infarction, miR-24 levels decrease in the left ventricular ischemic border zone. Local cardiac delivery of miR-24 mimics using lipofectamine-mediated transfection successfully reduced infarct size and improved cardiac function by targeting Bim [ ]. A fascinating observation reveals that Bim deletion can lead to an overcompensation of miR-24’s inhibitory effect on BAX translocation. This suggests that miR-24’s influence on BAX translocation operates indirectly, likely through Bim regulation, while also indicating possible alternative Bim-dependent apoptosis mechanisms independent of miR-24 [ ]. The therapeutic relevance of Bim-regulating miRNAs is demonstrated through the miR-17-92a cluster, which suppresses Bim via 3’-UTR binding. This cluster’s removal enhanced dexamethasone-induced apoptosis, while its increased expression supported estrogen’s anti-apoptotic effects in osteoblasts [ ]. While MOMP and cytochrome-c release have traditionally been considered points of no return in cell death, emerging evidence suggests potential intervention opportunities even after these events. Several miRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms target downstream components of the apoptotic pathway. For instance, apaf-1, crucial for apoptosome formation, is regulated by two miRNA clusters (miR-23a-27a-24 and miR-23b-27b-24). Neuronal-specific transgenic expression of miR-23b and miR-27b demonstrated protection against hypoxia-induced apoptosis. Additionally, miR-133, which targets caspase-9, shows protective effects in cardiac ischemia-reperfusion when upregulated through ischemic post-conditioning. Similarly, miR-378 targets caspase-3, with its overexpression enhancing cell survival in myocardial ischemia, while its inhibition worsens hypoxia-induced apoptosis [ – ].
p53 itself experiences sophisticated regulatory mechanisms across transcriptional, mRNA stability, translational, and post-translational levels [ , ]. While lncRNA interactions with p53 are currently limited to post-transcriptional modulation, these regulatory networks can significantly impact downstream apoptotic pathways [ ]. Several notable lncRNAs demonstrate intricate p53 regulation: Wrap53α, an antisense p53 transcript, stabilizes p53 through RNA-RNA interactions. Its knockdown inhibits p53-induced apoptosis, while overexpression potentiates it. The maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3) lncRNA activates p53 using specific secondary motifs, selectively facilitating p53-dependent apoptotic gene activation. LncRNA MALAT1 exhibits an inverse expression correlation with p53, though structural evidence remains inconclusive. ROR represents a unique p53-regulating lncRNA, forming a complex with heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein I to suppress p53. Interestingly, p53 induces ROR expression, creating a negative feedback loop that exemplifies the complex lncRNA-p53 interaction landscape [ – ]. ## Interplay between NcRNAs and programmed cell death pathways in specific hematological malignancies

## MiRNAs and LncRNAs in relation to PCDs and other related processes
Emerging research has unveiled the critical role of lncRNAs in hematopoietic lineage cell differentiation. HOTAIR, for instance, demonstrates significantly elevated expression in leukemic stem cells (LSCs) compared to normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. Utilizing shRNA-mediated HOTAIR silencing, researchers observed diminished colony formation in leukemia cell lines and primary AML blasts. Moreover, such interventions extended survival in leukemia-transplanted mice and reduced leukemic blast infiltration [ ]. Comprehensive analyses have revealed extensive lncRNA expression profiles across various hematological malignancies. Beyond modulating well-known oncogenes like BCL2, MYC, and KIT, these molecular regulators exhibit multifaceted mechanisms. The lnc-INSR potentially contributes to immune evasion by facilitating regulatory T cell differentiation [ ].
Another notable lncRNA, DANCR, emerged from RNA sequencing datasets of cytogenetically normal AML adults. This investigation uncovered a leukemic stem cell-specific signature encompassing 110 lncRNAs. Subsequent research validated DANCR’s upregulation in authenticated LSC-enriched populations. Critically, DANCR silencing induced cellular quiescence and diminished stem cell renewal, with its oncogenic potential confirmed through animal model studies [ ]. Significantly, lncRNAs demonstrate remarkable involvement in modulating crucial signaling pathways in hematological malignancies, including Wnt pathway, PI3K/AKT signaling, IGF1 mechanism, IL-11/STAT3 regulation, EGFR/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK cascade, and JAK/STAT signaling. Intriguingly, certain lncRNA expressions correlate with specific chromosomal rearrangements. For example, CCAT1 and PVT1 exhibit substantially higher expression levels in t(8;21) positive AML patients compared to their negative counterparts [ ].
While most human miRNAs reside within intronic regions of messenger RNA-coding genes, some are situated in non-coding regions or adjacent to 3’ untranslated regions of other genes [ ]. In hematological malignancies, altered miRNA expression patterns are frequently observed. Given that approximately half of human miRNAs exist in co-transcribed clusters [ ], researchers have identified multiple dysregulated miRNA panels in these conditions. Notably, many miRNAs are positioned within fragile sites or chromosomal breakpoint regions containing oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes [ ], with a significant portion located in areas associated with leukemia-related rearrangements [ ]. Studies comparing miRNA signatures among normal HSCs, LSCs, and LPs have revealed distinct profiles. For instance, miR-126 shows elevated expression in HSCs and LSCs compared to LPs, with its suppression reducing clonogenic ability and eliminating leukemic cells [ ]. Research by De Luca et al. highlighted the miR-128a/Lin28A axis’s role in blocking AML cell differentiation [ ], while miR-34a demonstrates tumor-suppressive properties in TIM3-positive LSCs [ ]. The intricate relationship between lncRNAs and miRNAs has garnered significant attention. For instance, ZFAS1 functions as a miR-150 sponge, with its suppression impeding AML progression. MEG3 interacts with miR-147, presenting potential therapeutic targets for CML blast crisis [ , ]. UCA1 mediates imatinib resistance by inhibiting miR-16 in CML cells. MEG3 influences drug resistance through miR-21 modulation. SMAD5-AS1 suppresses lymphoma cell proliferation by sponging miR-135b-5p and regulating APC expression. HOTAIRM1 targets miR-148b, contributing to AML progression [ – ].
The extensive dysregulation of lncRNAs and miRNAs in hematological malignancies has been well-documented through both in vitro and in vivo studies. HOTAIR exemplifies how lncRNAs influence hematopoietic malignancies through various mechanisms, including epigenetic regulation and miRNA level modulation. Given lncRNAs’ weak inter-species conservation, their regulatory effects require human-specific assessment for developing novel therapeutic approaches in hematological malignancies [ ]. Consequently, manipulating lncRNA expression has emerged as a potential strategy to mitigate resistance mechanisms in leukemic cells. Multiple lines of evidence suggest the existence of subtype-specific lncRNA signatures.
T-ALL-specific lncRNAs have been identified with some directly targeted by the Notch1/Rpbj κ activator complex [ ]. These findings not only illuminate lncRNAs’ specific roles in defining leukemia phenotypes but also demonstrate their functional interconnections with cancer-associated proteins and signaling pathways. LncRNAs have also shown promise as potential diagnostic markers. A microarray study discovered a collection of lncRNAs with distinctive expression patterns in B-ALL cytogenetic subtypes, termed B-ALL-associated long RNAs (BALR). These lncRNAs demonstrated capabilities in differentiating B-ALL subtypes, including prevalent aberrations like MLL rearrangement, E2A-PBX1 translocation, and TEL-AML1 fusion [ ]. Compelling correlations have emerged between lncRNA expression levels and patient survival. For instance, multiple myeloma patients with reduced PRAL levels experienced shorter disease-free and overall survival periods [ ]. A recent groundbreaking study identified a distinctive lncRNA signature capable of distinguishing progressive early-stage CLL requiring immediate treatment from indolent early Rai stage unmutated CLL. Predictive analyses suggest these lncRNAs can influence critical cellular processes and pathways that significantly impact CLL patient survival [ ]. Research has indicated that ZEB2‑AS1 lncRNA expression patterns could serve as indicators to enhance the precision of current risk assessment models and treatment response monitoring in AML patients [ ]. The relationship between ncRNA expression and common chromosomal abnormalities in hematological malignancies provides additional evidence of their fundamental importance and potential as diagnostic markers. This is supported by research demonstrating distinct miRNA patterns that differentiate between t(8;21), t(15;17), and other pediatric AML cases. These unique miRNA profiles may also indicate the developmental stage of leukemic cells, as exemplified by miR-23b, which shows increased expression during the maturation process from CD34 + cells to fully developed peripheral blood cells [ ].

## NcRNAs as therapeutic targets in hematological malignancies

## Targeting MiRNAs
MiRNA expression modulation in oncology encompasses sophisticated intervention strategies [ ]. Synthetically engineered antisense oligonucleotides, known as antagomiRs, present a promising approach for miRNA silencing [ ]. Pioneering research illuminated therapeutic potential across hematological malignancies. Dorrance et al. demonstrated that antagomiR-126 effectively reduced leukemia stem cell populations in experimental models [ ]. Subsequent investigations revealed compelling findings: Imatinib treatment triggered miR-21 upregulation in Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL cells. AntagomiR-21 administration produced remarkable outcomes, including enhanced cellular sensitivity, cancer cell apoptosis induction, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway inhibition [ ]. The CBFB-MYH11 gene fusion was observed to elevate miR-126 levels in AML, supporting leukemia-initiating cell survival. Genetic miR-126 elimination extended survival in murine experiments, with therapeutic anti-miR-126 combined with chemotherapy effectively suppressing AML cell progression [ ]. Additional studies validated antagomiR-196b and antagomiR-21 efficacy in MLL-AF9–initiated leukemia models, without observable alterations in murine physiological parameters [ ]. An in vitro examination of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma cell lines demonstrated that miR-155 inhibitor oligonucleotides attenuated cellular proliferation and reduced tumor volume in experimental models [ ].
CircRNAs exhibit intricate interactions with miRNAs, functioning as endogenous miRNA sponges by intercepting potential miRNA-target mRNA interactions [ ]. Recent research highlighted circRNAs’ potential in modulating hematologic malignancy cellular processes: Circ-0001947 suppressed AML cell proliferation by inhibiting miR-329-5p [ ]. Circ-KEL inhibited AML apoptosis by sponging miR-335-5p [ ]. Circ-0009910 prevented apoptosis by intercepting miR-34a-5p and miR-5195-3p in CML and AML [ , ]. Circ-0069767 diminished multiple myeloma cell invasiveness by targeting miR-636 and regulating K-RAS expression [ ].
Diminished miRNA expression in hematologic malignancies correlates with compromised therapeutic outcomes. Blood mononuclear cells from AML and CML patients exhibited substantially reduced miR-29b levels, associated with decreased treatment-free survival [ , ]. Garzon et al. revealed synthetic miR-29b oligonucleotides’ anti-tumor potential in AML treatment. Direct tumor injection in mouse models demonstrated reduced tumor growth. Laboratory investigations established connections between synthetic miR-29b transfection and anti-apoptotic MCL-1 downregulation [ , ]. Emerging research demonstrated that elevated ST8SIA4 gene expression correlates with MDR through enhanced PI3K/AKT pathway activation. Animal studies confirmed that miR-181c upregulation combined with adriamycin significantly reduced tumor growth in resistant models [ ].
Nanoparticle delivery systems have emerged as innovative solutions addressing synthetic miRNA challenges. Transferrin-conjugated nanoparticles delivering synthetic miR-29b achieved remarkable miRNA level increases and enhanced survival in leukemic mouse models [ ].

## LncRNAs as therapeutic molecules
LncRNAs present extraordinary therapeutic potential due to their distinctive molecular specificity, potentially minimizing unintended physiological consequences. Advancements in oligonucleotide-based therapeutic strategies now enable targeting previously inaccessible human transcriptome components that resist conventional small molecule or antibody-based interventions [ , ]. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) have particularly demonstrated promising clinical progression, with several receiving FDA approval [ ]. This innovative approach offers expanded therapeutic possibilities by leveraging lncRNAs’ functional complexity. Experimental investigations have provided compelling evidence supporting lncRNA-targeted therapeutic strategies. For instance, in vivo studies targeting lncRNA MALAT1 in a mouse mammary tumor virus-PyMT breast cancer model demonstrated significant therapeutic outcomes. Researchers observed enhanced cellular adhesion, improved cystic differentiation, and substantially reduced cellular migration following ASO-mediated MALAT1 knockdown [ ]. Similarly, targeting lncRNA Ube3a-ats through ASO administration in murine models revealed potential therapeutic interventions for Angelman syndrome [ ].
Despite these encouraging preliminary findings, substantial challenges persist in translating these approaches to human clinical applications. The primary obstacles include: Validating efficient molecular delivery mechanisms. Ensuring consistent, long-lasting therapeutic effects. Demonstrating robust safety profiles in human subjects. Nonetheless, the rapid evolutionary trajectory of nucleic acid-targeting therapeutic strategies, characterized by continuous improvements in toxicity profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters, suggests that systematic identification and comprehensive evaluation of lncRNA targets could potentially yield groundbreaking clinical interventions in the foreseeable future. The scientific community remains optimistic that ongoing research will progressively bridge the gap between preclinical promise and clinical implementation, ultimately transforming our understanding of targeted molecular therapeutics.

## CircRNAs and their therapeutic capacity
Innovative delivery strategies include packaging circRNA transcripts into viral vectors or oligonucleotides, targeted cell delivery to facilitate therapeutic interventions, and inhibiting specific circRNA expressions to enhance miRNA-mediated oncogene suppression (targeting XIAP, β-catenin, GSK3β, and YAP) [ – ]. The advent of CRISPR/Cas9 genetic engineering technology has revolutionized circRNA research. The CRISPR/Cas-assisted homologous recombination technique enables circRNA gene replacement with marker genes, effectively eliminating circRNAs without compromising existing genetic structures [ ]. The artificial circRNA sponge emerges as a sophisticated tool in circRNA-based anti-tumor therapies, with significant potential for future clinical applications. Its versatility in molecular interactions and targeted interventions positions it as a promising avenue for advanced therapeutic approaches. Key advantages include precise molecular targeting, multiplex interaction capabilities, and potential for personalized therapeutic strategies [ ]. The ongoing evolution of circRNA research continues to unveil complex regulatory mechanisms, offering hope for more effective and nuanced cancer treatment methodologies.
The successful implementation of artificial circRNAs and functional peptides necessitates effective cellular delivery mechanisms. Nanoparticle-based approaches have gained prominence, offering diverse administration routes like intravenous and tail vein injections, and representing cutting-edge cancer treatment technologies. In a notable advancement, researchers introduced the Micropoly-transfecter, a novel plasmid delivery system capable of intratumoral Circ-0001073 plasmid administration to impede tumor progression [ ]. Furthermore, exosomal circRNAs have demonstrated promising clinical potential, with studies revealing mechanisms such as circ-0051443’s ability to induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [ ]. ## NcRNAs as biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis

## MiRNAs in diagnosis/prognosis
Hematologic malignancies originating in bone marrow and lymph nodes prompted researchers to conduct comprehensive miRNA expression analyses across various biological samples, including peripheral blood, plasma, serum, and bone marrow aspirates. MiRNAs enter circulation through multiple mechanisms: direct release during apoptotic or necrotic events and active secretion by both malignant and normal cellular populations. When encapsulated within microvesicles or exosomes, miRNAs achieve remarkable stability, facilitating intercellular communication between cancer cells and surrounding non-malignant environments [ , ].
Consistent with observations in other oncological contexts, hematologic malignancies demonstrated significantly elevated exosome quantities compared to normal blood samples. These extracellular vesicles substantially influence critical pathological processes, including tumor survival, progression, and therapeutic resistance [ , ]. Notably, exosomal miRNAs exhibit reduced non-specific background compared to freely circulating extracellular miRNAs [ ].
Researchers successfully identified specific miRNA signatures discriminating between genetic subtypes, including T-ALL and B-ALL [ , ]. Certain miRNAs, i.e., miR-324-3p and miR-508-5p, were associated with early relapse prediction and potential drug resistance [ ]. In adult acute leukemia contexts, distinct miRNA expression patterns emerged. Upregulated miR-128a and miR-128b, combined with downregulated let-7b and miR-223, effectively distinguished ALL from AML [ ]. MiR-363 demonstrated prognostic significance in AML treatment strategies, with higher expression levels suggesting early allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, while lower levels correlated with improved overall survival [ ]. CML studies identified specific miRNA signatures, including miR-146a, miR-150, miR-486-5p, miR-126, and miR-122, potentially predictive of imatinib responsiveness [ – ]. Intriguingly, leukemia and lymphoma patients exhibited miRNA expression variations pre- and post-chemotherapy, offering potential therapeutic management insights. Methodological analysis revealed significant research limitations: approximately 70% of studies focused exclusively on malignant samples, with merely 30% exploring plasma or serum environments containing miRNAs from both cancerous and normal cellular sources. The prevailing hypothesis suggests exosomes primarily originate from viable cells, potentially minimizing background contamination from apoptotic and necrotic cellular components [ ]. Conclusively, researchers have systematically developed comprehensive exosomal miRNA panels facilitating early detection, subtype differentiation, and drug resistance prediction [ , , ].

## Diagnostic potential of LncRNAs
LncRNAs emerge as promising biological indicators due to their remarkable tissue and disease-specific expression patterns, which distinctly differ from protein-coding genes [ ]. This unique specificity and sensitivity enable lncRNAs to function as precise disease stage predictors through their differential expression relative to normal tissue. Contemporary biomarker screening techniques increasingly focus on patient extracellular fluids, offering non-invasive early diagnostic opportunities. Researchers have discovered that lncRNA level alterations associated with tumorigenesis can be detected in readily accessible bodily fluids like blood plasma and urine, significantly enhancing their biomarker potential [ ].
Prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3), a remarkable lncRNA, has emerged as a groundbreaking molecular diagnostic tool for prostate cancer. Surpassing traditional PSA serum measurements, PCA3 demonstrates exceptional diagnostic capabilities. This prostate-specific molecular marker is overexpressed in more than 90% of primary prostate tumors, effectively distinguishing malignant from benign tissues. Its unique characteristic of being detectable exclusively in urine samples and absent in other cancer types has led to FDA approval for molecular diagnostic screening [ – ].
Microarray investigations unveiled B-ALL-associated long RNAs (BALR), capable of predicting cytogenetic variations in B-ALL. These genetic variations encompass MLL rearrangements, E2A-PBX1 translocations, and TEL-AML1 fusions. Clinicopathological research indicated that elevated BALR-2 expression correlates with diminished patient survival and reduced responsiveness to prednisolone treatment [ ], suggesting potential disease stratification and treatment response prediction. Another notable example is the lncRNA PRAL, found to be significantly downregulated in primary MM cells, particularly those presenting del(17p) chromosomal deletion. Survival analyses revealed dramatically shortened disease-free and overall survival in MM patients with low PRAL expression [ ].
LncRNAs showcase considerable promise as biomarkers, characterized by exceptional specificity and convenient sampling methods. Their differential expression can be efficiently detected through established molecular biology techniques, including microarray assays, real-time quantitative PCR, and comprehensive RNA sequencing. Future clinical decision-making may benefit from integrating multiple lncRNA candidates with conventional biomarkers. The clinical application of these molecular markers is still in its infancy, with most disease-associated lncRNAs awaiting comprehensive validation.

## CircRNAs in diagnostic or prognostic modalities
The widespread and conserved expression patterns of circRNAs in hematopoietic cells, combined with their abundance and accessibility, position them as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for hematological malignancies. Among these, PVT1 has emerged as a significant participant in hematological malignancy pathogenesis. Notably elevated circPVT1 expression has been documented in various conditions, including ALL, pediatric B-precursor ALL, and AML cases exhibiting MYC amplifications through double minutes, homogeneously staining regions, or ring chromosomes (AML-Amp). Research has demonstrated that circPVT1 suppression inhibits cellular proliferation while promoting apoptosis in ALL. Furthermore, its overexpression in AML-Amp cases has proven valuable in distinguishing various AML karyotypes [ – ].
Reflecting the substantial heterogeneity of hematological malignancies and circRNA cell-type specificity, Hsa_circ_0012152 and Hsa_circ_0001857 accurately discriminate ALL from AML [ ]. For AML specifically, multiple circRNAs, such as circ-Foxo3 and circRNA annexin A2, have shown promise in distinguishing patients from healthy individuals [ , ]. Of particular interest, elevated circ-ANXA2 expression correlates with reduced PFS and OS in AML patients. Studies have revealed that patients achieving complete remission (CR) exhibited lower circ-ANAX2 levels compared to non-CR patients, corresponding with improved EFS and OS outcomes [ ]. Through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, circ-VIM expression has demonstrated capability in differentiating AML patients from healthy controls. High circ-VIM expression levels serve as an independent prognostic indicator for both OS and PFS in AML cases [ ].
Remarkably, 508 circRNAs exhibited dynamic expressions during all-trans retinoic acid treatment in NB4 cells, functioning independently from parental genes [ ]. In AML, circ_0004277’s expression dynamics provided insights into treatment efficacy: reduced levels post-chemotherapy and subsequent decreases during relapse after complete remission (CR) highlighted potential correlations between expression patterns and therapeutic outcomes [ ]. Consequently, circRNA expression’s dynamic nature during disease progression offers novel perspectives for evaluating therapeutic effectiveness and prognosis. Traditional diagnostic modalities often involve invasive procedures, whereas liquid biopsy emerges as a non-invasive, repeatable diagnostic alternative. Accumulating scientific evidence reveals circRNA enrichment within exosomes, which provide protective environments shielding internal circRNAs from external environmental influences [ ].
Specific examples illustrate exosomal circRNA’s clinical significance: Mc-COX2, a mitochondrial genome-derived circRNA, demonstrated significant plasma enrichment in CLL patients, correlating with diminished OS [ ]. Exosomal circMYC levels in bortezomib-resistant patients showed elevated connections with chromosomal deletions, specific translocations, and staging systems (Table ) [ ]. Despite high-throughput sequencing identifying numerous circRNAs with potential biomarker value, underlying targets and mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Emerging circRNA databases progressively facilitate target prediction and expression visualization. Overall, ncRNAs’ stability in tissue and biofluids, make them excellent candidates for biomarker discovery not only in common leukemic hematologic neoplasms but also in other lymphoma subtypes, such as MCL that is defined as Mantle cell lymphoma [ ], as well as B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma [ , ]. ## PCD-based targeted therapies against hematologic malignancies

## Apoptosis
Comprehensive preclinical investigations and clinical observations have consistently demonstrated that BCL-2 family member interventions represent a potent apoptosis-oriented anti-tumor methodology. Venetoclax, a BCL-2 inhibitor, extends its therapeutic applications beyond CLL and AML, demonstrating remarkable apoptosis-inducing capabilities in MM cellular environments [ ]. Intriguingly, hematological malignancy patients receiving venetoclax exhibit profound inter-patient variability. Specific molecular profiles significantly influence treatment outcomes; AML patients harboring isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) or IDH2 mutations demonstrated superior clinical responses compared to non-mutated counterparts. Polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4) inhibitor combined with venetoclax induced cellular destruction and chromosomal disruption in aggressive lymphoma subtypes [ – ]. Molecular expression proportions critically determine treatment responsiveness. Heightened BCL-2/MCL-1 and BCL-2/BCL-XL messenger RNA ratios are strongly associated with venetoclax susceptibility. The BCL-2 and BCL-XL inhibitor ABT-737 demonstrated remarkable efficacy in hyperdiploid multiple myeloma characterized by elevated BCL-2/MCL-1 ratios. Considering multiple myeloma cells’ complex apoptotic diversity, simultaneous targeting of BCL-2 and MCL-1 emerges as a potentially groundbreaking therapeutic approach. The X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP) represents a critical intervention mechanism, effectively preventing programmed cell death by binding and neutralizing essential enzymatic apoptotic pathways. Experimental research substantiates that XIAP suppression can substantially enhance chemotherapeutic sensitivity in treatment-resistant ALL cells, positioning XIAP as a promising molecular target [ ].

## Necroptosis
Research indicates that necroptosis triggers immune responses and inflammatory cascades, playing a significant role in tumor progression [ ]. The evasion of necroptotic mechanisms is a hallmark of malignancies, potentially leading to chemotherapy resistance and diminished patient recovery rates. Strategically targeting necroptosis emerges as a promising therapeutic approach for cancer treatment. Notably, leukemia cells demonstrate heightened susceptibility to necroptosis-inducing agents compared to other tumor types [ ]. Molecular apoptosis inhibitors, particularly smac mimetic compounds, demonstrate significant potential when strategically combined with complementary therapeutic interventions for targeting hematopoietic malignancies. The birinapant and emricasan partnership stands out particularly, revealing a distinctive mechanism for necroptosis induction through MYC expression suppression and facilitating RIPK1/RIPK3 complex formation [ ].
Complementary therapeutic compounds have also exhibited noteworthy necroptosis-triggering capabilities. Thymoquinone, for instance, demonstrates potential for initiating necroptosis in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma cellular environments. The tetrahydro benzimidazole derivative TMQ0153 demonstrates efficacy in generating necroptosis and reactive oxygen species accumulation within CML cells, particularly at elevated concentrations [ , ]. Additionally, trabectedin, a well-established anti-cancer therapeutic agent, can effectively provoke necroptosis in Hodgkin’s lymphoma cellular systems [ ].

## Pyroptosis
Novel therapeutic approaches for cancer treatment have emerged through small-molecule compounds targeting pyroptotic pathways. For example, Val-boroPro, a DPP8/9 serine dipeptidase inhibitor, facilitates pyroptosis in AML cells by targeting CARD8 (caspase recruitment domain family, member 8), enabling its interaction with caspase-1 and subsequent activation [ ]. Additional compounds targeting caspase-1 include curcumin and D089, a MYC G-quadruplex (G4) stabilizing molecule. These agents enhance DAMP signaling and inflammasome activation, ultimately triggering pyroptosis in multiple myeloma and leukemia cells [ , ]. The classical pyroptotic pathway can also be initiated through GSDMD targeting. Two distinct approaches have been identified: the combination of dasatinib and interferon α directly activates and upregulates GSDMD, while pyridoxine (vitamin B6) modulates GSDMD indirectly through BCL-2 targeting in ALL [ ]. Research has revealed that BCL-2 suppresses both pyroptosis and necroptosis by interacting with BH3-like domains present in GSDMD and MLKL, thereby reducing GSDMD cleavage and MLKL phosphorylation [ ].
Several compounds targeting the caspase-3/GSDME axis have been developed for cancer research. For instance, liproxstatin 1 demonstrates efficacy in promoting caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in leukemia cells, presenting a promising therapeutic avenue [ ]. Additionally, proteasomal inhibitors including MG132 and bortezomib have shown the ability to disrupt BCL-2/BAX interactions, leading to cytochrome c release and GSDME activation, ultimately inducing pyroptosis in multiple myeloma cells [ ]. This suggests that combining BCL-2 inhibition with PIs could enhance multiple myeloma cells’ susceptibility to pyroptosis-inducing treatments.

## Ferroptosis
Various pharmaceutical agents and compounds have demonstrated the ability to trigger ferroptotic cell death in hematologic malignancies. One primary strategy involves direct GPX4 inhibition [ ]. For instance, the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 effectively induces ferroptosis in ALL, either independently or in combination with the Smac mimetic BV6 [ ]. Additionally, research has shown that HDL-like nanoparticles (HDL NPs), functioning as low-cholesterol ligands, can initiate ferroptosis in cholesterol-dependent lymphoma cells by targeting SCARB1 and disrupting GPX4 function [ , ].
The system xc- plays a crucial role in maintaining glutathione homeostasis within cells. SLC7A11, a key subunit of this system, facilitates the transport of proteins involved in ferroptosis. Inhibitors targeting system xc-, such as erastin and sulfasalazine (SASP), have shown effectiveness in inducing ferroptosis in both lymphoma and AML cells by restricting GSH formation, emphasizing ferroptosis’s therapeutic potential in cancer treatment (Table ) [ , ].
 

## Drug resistance challenges and possible opportunities
Chemotherapy-induced apoptosis resistance poses significant challenges to targeted therapeutic approaches. Resistance to venetoclax, which has previously introduced, manifests through complex regulatory mechanisms. In DLBCL cell lines, resistant variants demonstrate increased AKT activation and elevated MCL-1 and BCL-XL expression, driving treatment resistance. AML exhibits similar resistance patterns, with RAS/MAPK pathway activation upregulating MCL-1 as a survival mechanism [ – ].
Combination therapy emerges as a promising strategy to circumvent treatment resistance. Preliminary research demonstrates synergistic potential between gilteritinib and venetoclax, particularly in FLT3 wild-type AML. A clinical trial (NCT03625505) has highlighted the efficacy of this combination in preclinical FLT3-mutated AML models. Similarly, omacetaxine and venetoclax exhibit significant synergistic effects in AML with RUNX1 mutations. Researchers emphasize that understanding tumor genetic heterogeneity is crucial for developing effective combination therapeutic approaches [ – ].
In retinoblastoma, GSDME expression levels correlate with chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity, suggesting pyroptosis as a potential therapeutic target [ ]. Despite encouraging results in PCD-targeted treatments, tumor heterogeneity and invasiveness continue to challenge therapeutic efficacy. Nanotherapy emerges as a promising approach, offering enhanced targeting and drug delivery capabilities. Recent literature has extensively explored nanomedicine’s role in cancer treatment, particularly in modulating ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis [ – ]. Researchers have comprehensively examined PCD-related nanotechnology’s potential in tumor therapy, highlighting its significance in improving immunotherapeutic approaches [ – ]. While PCD-related nanotherapy shows considerable promise in hematological malignancies, particularly in combination treatments, further research and systematic exploration are necessary to fully understand and implement these innovative therapeutic strategies.
Nanoparticulate iron-based therapeutic strategies leverage Fe2 + or Fe3 + ion release, initiating the Fenton reaction and subsequent ferroptosis mechanisms [ – ]. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma cellular contexts, iron oxide nanoparticles facilitate ferroptosis by elevating intracellular labile iron concentrations, increasing lipid peroxide levels, and suppressing glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4 MNPs) demonstrate additional capabilities, inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in T-cell ALL Jurkat cell lines [ , ]. Complementary nanoparticulate approaches utilizing zinc oxide and fisetin also effectively provoke apoptotic responses in lymphoma and leukemia cellular environments [ , ]. Nanoparticle platforms emerge as sophisticated drug delivery mechanisms, offering substantial advantages: minimized systemic toxicity, enhanced therapeutic efficacy, and synergistic intervention strategies. Experimental lymphoma xenograft models validate nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems’ potential, particularly for system xc- inhibitor administration, doxorubicin (DOX) targeting, and producing amplified apoptotic outcomes with reduced toxicity [ , ]. Specialized nanoparticulate formulations demonstrate remarkable therapeutic potential. Fe3O4@MTX magnetic nanoparticles loaded with methotrexate exhibit superior efficacy compared to conventional therapeutic approaches in primary central nervous system lymphoma, strategically modulating caspase-3 expression, BAX protein levels, and BCL-2 protein dynamics [ ]. Emerging research further explores nanomedical interventions: nanoparticle-delivered DOX triggering PANoptosis in melanoma [ ] and ultrasound nanomedicine utilizing engineered extracellular vesicles inducing immunogenic PANoptosis in triple-negative breast cancer models [ ].
Targeted nanoparticle delivery systems offer significant therapeutic advantages. For instance, PR@Ni3P-BTZ, coated with CD38-targeting peptide-modified red blood cell membrane nanovesicles, combines photothermal therapy with chemotherapy for multiple myeloma treatment. BCMA-BTZ-NPs specifically target BCMA-expressing MM cells, demonstrating superior efficacy in inducing mitochondrial depolarization and apoptosis compared to conventional approaches. Additionally, redox-responsive polymers like GEM-S-S-PEG enable precise drug release in tumor microenvironments [ – ].
Nanotherapy presents novel solutions for drug resistance. The As4S4/Imatinib nanocrystal system effectively reverses CML resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors through complementary pathway inhibition [ ]. As4S4/ZnS/Fe3O4 demonstrates multi-functional therapeutic capabilities in MM treatment [ ], while GNRa-CSP12 nanoparticles combat TKI resistance through ferroptosis induction [ ].
Multi-target nanomedicines like Nm@MSNs-DOX/SM showcase enhanced tumor-killing effects by simultaneously modulating BCL-2 family-mediated pathways and inflammation factors [ ].
While PCD-driven nanotherapy shows promising potential for personalized cancer treatment with good biocompatibility, concerns regarding nanocarrier clearance and metabolism necessitate further investigation. This comprehensive approach to nanotherapy demonstrates significant potential in cancer treatment, though continued research is essential to address safety and efficacy concerns. ## Conclusions and future prospects
PCD regulators play critical roles in physiological development and pathological conditions. NcRNAs, particularly miRNAs, significantly influence PCD expression and functionality. These molecular interactions impact various human disorders, including cardiac conditions, polycystic ovary syndrome, neurological diseases, immune disorders, endocrine pathologies, and multiple cancer types. PCD4 emerges as the most extensively miRNA-interacted family member. Multiple miRNAs, including miR-21, miR-208a-3p, miR-499-5p, miR-16, miR-155, miR-182, miR-141, miR-96, miR-499, miR-503, and miR-183, have been identified as critical modulators of PCD4 expression across diverse tissue types. Notably, the miR-21/PCD4 regulatory axis represents a significant pathway in numerous disease mechanisms. Despite extensive research on PCD-related miRNAs in cancer development, limited data exists regarding their direct impact on patient survival. Currently, only miR-183-5p, miR-21, miR-93, miR-26a-5p, miR-103, and miR-183 have confirmed associations with cancer prognosis. PCDs significantly influence carcinogenesis by modulating cancer cells’ responsiveness to pro-apoptotic stimuli. Consequently, PCD-related miRNAs represent promising therapeutic targets. Experimental knock-down studies in cellular and animal models have demonstrated encouraging outcomes, with miRNA down-regulation substantially reducing cancer cell viability and enhancing apoptotic responses. Additionally, these miRNAs potentially modulate cancer cells’ radio/chemoresistance, offering novel strategies to improve conventional anti-cancer therapies. LncRNAs exhibit more subtle interactions with PCD regulation, primarily functioning as molecular miRNA sponges. Specific lncRNA/miRNA/PCD axes like MALAT1/miR-200a-3p/PDCD4, ciRS-126/miR-21/PCD4, and SNHG14/PD-1-miR-5590 contribute to pathological mechanisms in human disorders. Comprehensive understanding of ncRNA roles in PCD regulation holds significant potential for developing targeted therapeutic approaches. While PCD5 and PCD7 interactions remain incompletely understood, theoretical applications span various human disorders involving cellular senescence and apoptotic processes. The intricate network of miRNA and lncRNA interactions suggests that multi-point targeting represents the most efficient strategy for modulating PCD expression and potentially developing innovative therapeutic interventions.
