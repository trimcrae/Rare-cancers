## Highlights
•PRCC-TFE3 induces oncogene-induced senescence in non-cancerous renal epithelial cells.
•A genome-wide CRISPR screen identified CCNC as essential for PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS.
•Cyclin C-CDK8/19 mediator kinase module drives PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcription.
•PRCC-TFE3 triggers OIS in non-cancerous cells but drives tumor growth in TFE3-RCC.
•CCNC loss or CDK8/19 inhibition suppresses tumor growth in TFE3-RCC mouse models.
Keywords: TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma, TFE3 fusion, Mediator complex, CCNC, Cyclin C, CDK8/19, CRISPR/Cas9 genome-wide screen

## Abstract
TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-RCC) is an aggressive kidney cancer driven by oncogenic TFE3 fusion transcription factors, yet the molecular machinery that enables these fusions to reprogram transcription and drive tumor growth remains poorly defined. Here, we identify the Cyclin C-CDK8/19 Mediator kinase module as an essential co-regulator of TFE3 fusion driven transcriptional programs and tumorigenesis. Inducible expression of PRCC-TFE3 in HK-2 cells, immortalized from normal renal epithelial cells, triggered a robust oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) phenotype. Using OIS as a functional readout, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screen and identified CCNC, encoding Cyclin C, as an essential gene required for PRCC-TFE3 activity. Genetic disruption of CCNC or pharmacologic inhibition of CDK8/19 abrogated PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS, establishing the Mediator kinase module as a critical cofactor for PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcription. Mechanistically, PRCC-TFE3 promoted nuclear accumulation of Cyclin C and their co-occupancy at genomic regions bound and transcriptionally activated by PRCC-TFE3. RNA sequencing revealed that PRCC-TFE3 induced transcriptional programs, including lysosomal, TFEB-associated, and metabolic pathways, were broadly suppressed by CDK8/19 inhibition. Importantly, while PRCC-TFE3 and Cyclin C-CDK8/19 drive OIS in non-cancerous renal epithelial cells, this same transcriptional axis exerts a context dependent pro-tumorigenic function in TFE3-RCC. In xenografts established from patient derived TFE3-RCC cell lines, genetic deletion of CCNC suppressed tumor growth, whereas in an orthotopic syngeneic TFE3-RCC mouse model, pharmacologic CDK8/19 inhibition significantly reduced tumor progression. These findings define the Mediator kinase module as a mechanistic and therapeutic vulnerability in PRCC-TFE3 driven TFE3-RCC, providing a rationale for mechanism based targeted therapy.

## Introduction
TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-RCC) represents a distinct and clinically important subset of RCC, formally recognized in the 2022 WHO classification [ ]. These tumors are characterized by X-chromosome rearrangements involving the TFE3 gene, leading to the formation of TFE3 fusion proteins with partner genes such as PRCC, ASPSCR1, SFPQ, and NONO [ , , ]. Although TFE3-RCC can arise across a wide age range, it disproportionately affects younger individuals and frequently exhibits aggressive clinical behavior and rapid progression [ , ]. While immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) plus tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) combinations appear to confer relatively higher activity in TFE3-RCC compared with other non-clear cell RCC subtypes, a substantial proportion of patients still experience inadequate or short-lived responses [ , ]. Thus, there remains an urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving TFE3-RCC pathogenesis and to identify therapeutically exploitable vulnerabilities.
TFE3 fusion proteins retain the basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper domain and function as constitutively active transcription factors [ , , ]. Accumulating evidence from our laboratory and others has demonstrated that forced expression of TFE3 fusion in murine renal tubular epithelial cells is sufficient to initiate TFE3-RCC like tumors, firmly establishing these chimeric transcription factors as oncogenic drivers [ , , ]. Transcriptomic studies have shown that TFE3 fusion aberrantly activates broad gene networks linked to lysosomal biogenesis, autophagy, mTORC1 signaling, hypoxia responses, oxidative stress pathways, and E2F-mediated cell-cycle programs [ , , , ]. However, because TFE3 fusion simultaneously engages numerous downstream pathways, directly targeting individual effector genes is unlikely to provide an effective or durable therapeutic approach.
These considerations prompted us to explore whether TFE3 fusion relies on discrete transcriptional co-regulators whose inhibition could more efficiently suppress its oncogenic activity. To identify such essential factors, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screen [ , ]. During this screening, induction of the PRCC-TFE3 fusion triggered a robust oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) program [ , ] in HK-2 human proximal tubular epithelial cells. By exploiting this PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS phenotype as a functional readout, we screened for genes whose disruption rescued senescence, thereby nominating factors required for TFE3 fusion transcriptional function.
This approach identified CCNC, which encodes Cyclin C, as one of the most significantly enriched hits. Hereafter, we use “CCNC” to refer to the gene and “Cyclin C” to refer to the protein. Cyclin C forms the Mediator kinase module together with CDK8/19, MED12, and MED13, constituting a regulatory subcomplex that modulates the activity of the Mediator core complex [ , ]. These findings raised the possibility that TFE3 fusion depends on the Mediator kinase module to establish and sustain its transcriptional program.
In this study, we systematically investigate the functional relationship between PRCC-TFE3 and the Cyclin C-CDK8/19 Mediator axis across multiple biological contexts, including OIS induction, transcriptional regulation, and tumorigenesis. Furthermore, recognizing the therapeutic potential of this dependency, we also evaluate the effects of selective CDK8/19 inhibition as a candidate strategy to target the transcriptional machinery supporting TFE3 fusion driven oncogenesis.

## Methods

## Cell lines and culture conditions
HK-2 cells expressing HA-tagged PRCC-TFE3 in a doxycycline-inducible manner have been described previously [ ]. HEK293 cells harboring doxycycline-inducible HA-PRCC-TFE3 were generated as previously described [ ].
The TFE3-RCC cell lineUOK124 was established from primary tumors of patients with TFE3-RCC treated at the Urologic Oncology Branch (UOB), National Cancer Institute (Bethesda, MD). UOK124 cell line carries PRCC-TFE3 fusion gene, as previously reported [ , , ].
All cell lines except HK-2 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS). Doxycycline-inducible HK-2 cells were maintained in Advanced DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 1.5% Tet-approved FBS, 1 × GlutaMAX, puromycin (0.8 μg/mL), blasticidin S (2 μg/mL), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.
Knockdown of PRCC-TFE3, p53, or p21 in PRCC-TFE3 inducible HK-2 cells was achieved using a miRE-based RNA interference system. Briefly, SGEP plasmid vectors (Addgene #111170) were cloned with shRNA-miRE sequences as previously described [ ]. Viral supernatants were used to infect TFE3-RCC cell lines, and infected cells were selected with puromycin (Promega). The target sequences used for miRE construction are listed below.
Control: 5’-TATTATTTTAATCACAAACCTA-3’;
shp53: 5’-TCCACTACAACTACATGTGTAA-3’;
shp21: 5’-CTCAGTTTGTGTGTCTTAATTA-3’.
shPRCC-TFE3: 5’- TATTATTTTAATCACAAACCTA-3’.
Knockout of CCNC in UOK124 cells was achieved using the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (a gift from Feng Zhang; Addgene plasmid #52961). The sgRNA target sequence used for this study is listed below.
gCCNC 1-F: 5’-CACCGATGCCAAAAACACACATGT-3’
gCCNC 1-R: 5’-AAACACATGTGTGTTTTTGGCATC-3’

## Cell proliferation assay
Cells were cultured in 24-well plates for the indicated durations. Resazurin solution (DPBS, pH 7.5; Resazurin Sodium Salt, Wako 191-07581) was added to the media to a final concentration of 0.03 mg/ml, followed by incubation for an additional 2–4 hours at 37°C in 5% CO₂. The cultured media was then collected, and fluorescence was quantified with excitation at 560 nm and emission at 590 nm.

## Western blotting
Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [ ]. The following primary antibodies were used: TFE3 (ZRB1272; Sigma-Aldrich), Rb (4H1; #9309; Cell Signaling Technology), phospho-Rb (Ser780; #8180; CST), phospho-Rb (Ser807/811; #8516; CST), p16 (#92803; CST), p21 (#2947; CST), p53 (sc-99; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), CDK2 (#2546; CST), CDK4 (#12790; CST), β-actin (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech), and Cyclin C (#68179; CST).

## RNA extraction and quantitative RT-qPCR
Total RNA was reverse-transcribed using ReverTra Ace with genomic DNA remover (Toyobo). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a LightCycler 96 system (Roche) with Thunderbird SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo). Gene expression levels were normalized to RPS18 as an internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

## Senescence-associated β-gal assay
Cells were fixed and stained for senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity using a commercially available SA-β-gal assay kit (Cell Signaling Technology), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## BrdU assay
Cells were pulse-labeled with bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) for 180 minutes, harvested, and fixed in 70% ethanol at -20°C. Fixed cells were then washed, DNA was denatured according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and samples were incubated with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-BrdU antibody (BioLegend). Nuclear DNA was counterstained with propidium iodide (PI; Dojindo), and BrdU incorporation and cell-cycle distribution were analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were fixed with 100% methanol for 10 minutes and washed three times with TBST. After fixation, cells were blocked for 30 minutes in TBST containing 0.1% BSA, 10% calf serum, and 3% normal goat serum. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Cyclin C (68179; Cell Signaling Technology) and HA (3F10; Roche) diluted in 0.1% TBST.
Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with TBST for 10 minutes each and incubated with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-rat and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific), diluted in TBST containing 0.1% BSA and 1.5% normal goat serum. After three additional washes with TBST, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 minutes. Fluorescence images were acquired using an A1 confocal microscope (Nikon).

## CRISPR genome-wide screening
Lentiviral Cas9 particles were generated by co-transfecting lentiCRISPRv2-hygro (a gift from Brett Stringer; Addgene plasmid #98291) [ ] together with packaging vectors into 293LTV cells. Viral supernatants were collected and used to transduce HK-2 cells expressing HA-tagged PRCC-TFE3 in a doxycycline inducible manner, which were subsequently selected with hygromycin B (500 μg/mL) for 7 days. Cas9 nuclease activity was validated by transduction with an sgRNA targeting eGFP (Supplementary Fig. 2A).
The Brunello human genome-wide knockout library, a gift from David Root and John Doench, (#73178 ; Addgene) comprises 76,441 sgRNAs targeting 19,114 protein-coding genes, along with 1,000 non-targeting control sgRNAs [ ]. The pooled plasmid library was prepared according to the Addgene protocol (https://www.addgene.org/pooled-library/broadgpp-human-knockout-brunello/), and library representation was confirmed by sequencing on a NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Genome-wide CRISPR screens were performed essentially as described previously [ ]. Briefly, 1.0 × 108 Cas9-expressing, PRCC-TFE3 inducible HK-2 cells were transduced with the lentiviral sgRNA pool at a transduction efficiency of approximately 30%, achieving an average library coverage exceeding 500 × on day 1. Twenty-four hours after transduction, cells were selected with puromycin (5 μg/mL) and maintained under selection for 7 days. At all times, at least 6 × 107 cells were cultured to preserve sgRNA representation. On day 8, cells were divided into two conditions: one cultured in medium supplemented with doxycycline (500ng/ml) to induce PRCC-TFE3 expression, and the other maintained in medium without doxycycline. Cells were harvested on day 18, and genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA Midiprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All screening experiments were performed in duplicate using independent biological replicates.
Library preparation for next-generation sequencing was carried out as described by Joung et al [ ]. Briefly, sgRNA cassettes were PCR-amplified from genomic DNA using KOD Plus Neo polymerase (TOYOBO). PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis, excised, and purified using a gel extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified libraries were quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina (Roche) and normalized to 1 nM. Libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq 500 platform. Sequencing reads were processed using Biopython to generate sgRNA count tables, which were subsequently analyzed using MAGeCK as described by Li et al [ ].
Knockout of CCNC and MYC in PRCC-TFE3-inducible HK-2 cells was achieved using the lentiGuide-Puro vector (a gift from Feng Zhang; Addgene plasmid #52963) carrying gene-specific sgRNA sequences. The target sequences used for sgRNA construction are listed below.

gCCNC 2-F: 5’- CACCGTGTTCTAATTACCATGCAAG-3’
gCCNC 2-R: 5’- AAACCTTGCATGGTAATTAGAACAC-3’
gMYC 1-F: 5’-CACCGAGAGTGCATCGACCCCTCGG-3’
gMYC 1-R: 5’-AAACCCGAGGGGTCGATGCACTCTC-3’
gMYC 2-F: 5’- CACCGCTGCGGGGAGGACTCCGTCG-3’

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
HK-2 cells were seeded onto 15-cm culture dishes at a density calibrated to achieve approximately 70% confluency at the time of harvest (2.0 × 106 cells per reaction). Twelve hours after plating, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing doxycycline (0.5 μg/mL). Cells were harvested 24 hours later for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays.
ChIP experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #9003S), as previously described [ ]. The following antibodies were used for immunoprecipitation: anti-Cyclin C (CST #68179, 1:100 dilution) and anti-HA (Roche clone 3F10, 1:100 dilution). Immunoprecipitation reactions were carried out by incubating samples with the respective antibodies for 12 hours at 4 °C with gentle rotation.
Recovered DNA was quantified by quantitative PCR using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO). Primer sequences used for qPCR analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

## RNA sequence
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA quality was assessed with a BioAnalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output v2.5 Kit, generating 75-nucleotide single-end reads.
Adapter sequences were trimmed from raw RNA-seq reads using Trim Galore (version 0.6.7) with default parameters (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). Duplicate reads were removed using HTStream (version 1.3.0) with default settings (https://s4hts.github.io/HTStream/). Trimmed, non-duplicated reads were aligned to the human reference transcriptome (GENCODE v43, GRCh38.p13) and quantified using Salmon (version 1.9.0). Salmon was run with the following parameters:cmd = salmon quant -i v43 -l IU -r [fastq file] –validateMappings -o [output file]. Transcript-level counts and TPM values were imported into R using the tximport package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from count data using the edgeR method implemented in the R package TCC. TPM values of DEGs were z-score normalized and subjected to k-means clustering using the R function kmeans. Heatmaps were generated using TreeView software (version 1.2.0).

## Gene set enrichment analysis
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software (Broad Institute, version 2.18.2). Genes were ranked based on differential expression statistics derived from RNA-seq analysis. Enrichment was assessed against curated gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), including Hallmark and curated pathway gene sets. Statistical significance was evaluated using permutation testing, and normalized enrichment scores (NES) and false discovery rate (FDR) values were calculated according to standard GSEA guidelines.

## Anchorage-independent tumor cell growth assay
Anchorage-independent tumor cell growth was evaluated as previously described [ ]. Briefly, UOK124 cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension, and 1 × 104 cells were mixed with DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS and 0.33% agar, with or without MSC2530818 at the concentration of 100nM. The cell-containing agar mixture was overlaid onto a base layer of the same medium containing 0.5% agar in six-well plates. Cells were cultured for 21 days, after which colonies were stained with 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-phenyl-2H-tetrazolium chloride (INT; Dojindo). Colony formation was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as the percentage of colonies relative to control conditions.

## LysoPrime Green assay
UOK124 cells, UOK124 cells treated with 100 nM MSC2530818, and TFE3-knockdown UOK124 cells (shTFE3) were stained with LysoPrime Green (Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with LysoPrime Green at 37°C for 30 min, washed with PBS, and resuspended in fresh medium. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed by flow cytometry, and data were processed using FlowJo software.

## Nude Mice tumorigenicity Assay
For tumorigenicity assays, six week old BALB/c-nu/nu mice were used. UOK124 wild type (WT) and CCNC knockout (KO) cells were cultured to approximately 80-90% confluence and detached using Accutase (Nacalai Tesque). Cells were resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and Matrigel at a concentration of 2 × 106 cells per 100 μL. After disinfection of the injection site with ethanol, 100 μL of the cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into both flanks of each mouse (right flank: WT cells; left flank: CCNC-KO cells). Tumor growth was monitored once or twice weekly, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula V = 0.5 × (L × W × H), where L, W, and H represent tumor length, width, and height, respectively. When tumors reached approximately 20 mm in maximal diameter, final measurements were recorded. Mice were then euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation, and tumors were excised and weighed.

## Immunohistochemistry
The resected tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned into 4-μm slices for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using standard procedures. For immunohistochemistry, antigen retrieval was conducted in an autoclave at 121°C for 15 minutes using 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 9.0). To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% H2O2 and incubated in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) containing 5% goat serum to block non-specific binding. The following primary antibody was used: rabbit monoclonal anti Cyclin C (#68179; CST; 1:200). The secondary antibodies used were horseradish peroxidase conjugated goat anti rabbit IgG (Nichirei, 424141) and goat anti mouse IgG (Nichirei, 414171), with visualization achieved using the Liquid DAB+ Substrate Chromogen System (DAKO, K3468). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

## In vivo efficacy study of SEL120, CDK8/19 inhibitor in PRCC-TFE3 fusion renal tumor bearing mice
We previously generated a mouse model in which a PRCC-TFE3 fusion transgene is expressed in renal epithelial cells, resulting in the development of multiple tumors and cystic lesions throughout the kidneys [ ]. Tumors derived from these mice were serially passaged by orthotopic implantation into the kidneys of recipient C57BL/6 mice. Tumor growth was monitored longitudinally by periodic ultrasound imaging. Four weeks after implantation, mice were randomized into treatment groups based on tumor volume (n = 10 per group). Animals were treated with the selective CDK8/19 inhibitor SEL120-34A (60 mg/kg) or vehicle control (10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween 80, and 45% saline) administered orally five times per week for 8 weeks. Body weight was measured regularly to monitor general health and treatment tolerability. Tumor response was assessed by biweekly ultrasound imaging, and tumor volumes were calculated and plotted over the course of the treatment period.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests or Welch’s t-tests for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Dunnett’s, Sidak’s, or Holm–Bonferroni post hoc tests as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 unless otherwise indicated.

## Results

## PRCC-TFE3 expression induces oncogene-induced senescence (OIS)
To elucidate the cellular consequences of PRCC-TFE3 expression, we established doxycycline inducible HK-2 and HEK293 cell models. Induction of PRCC-TFE3 by doxycycline treatment markedly suppressed cell proliferation in both cell lines ( A). Notably, this growth-suppressive effect was more pronounced in HK-2 cells, an immortalized human normal renal proximal tubule epithelial cell line, than in HEK293 human embryonic kidney cells. Accordingly, HK-2 cells were selected for subsequent analyses to investigate the oncogenic role of PRCC-TFE3 in a normal renal epithelial cell context. To further delineate the specific effects of wild type TFE3 (WT-TFE3) and PRCC-TFE3 in HK-2 cells, we compared the consequences of expressing WT-TFE3 or PRCC-TFE3. Although doxycycline treatment efficiently induced the expression of both WT-TFE3 and PRCC-TFE3 (Supplementary Fig. 1A), WT-TFE3 expression resulted in only a modest reduction in cell proliferation, whereas PRCC-TFE3 expression caused a pronounced growth suppression (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In HK-2 cells, doxycycline-induced expression of PRCC-TFE3 resulted in robust senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining after 5 days ( B). By contrast, induction of WT-TFE3 did not elicit SA-β-gal activity or senescence associated morphological changes (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Consistent with the induction of cellular senescence, PRCC-TFE3 expression in HK-2 cells was accompanied by a significant reduction in LaminB1 mRNA levels and increased expression of senescence associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, including IL1A, IL6, and VEGF ( C–D). Cell-cycle analysis using BrdU/PI double staining demonstrated a progressive shift in cell-cycle distribution following doxycycline induced PRCC-TFE3 expression. As the induction period advanced, BrdU positive S phase cells steadily decreased, accompanied by a marked accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase, indicating a time dependent suppression of cell-cycle progression ( E–F). Consistent with a senescence like arrest, PRCC-TFE3 induced hypo phosphorylation of Rb and upregulation of p16, p21, p27, and p53 whereas levels of CDK2 was reduced ( G). Knockdown of p21 or p53 led to a limited recovery of cell proliferation in PRCC-TFE3 expressing cells ( H). These findings indicate that PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS is not fully explained by the canonical p53-p21 pathway and may predominantly rely on additional, p53/p21 independent mechanisms. Together, these findings demonstrate that PRCC-TFE3 expression alone is sufficient to trigger OIS in non-cancerous HK-2 renal epithelial cells.
 

## CRISPR/Cas9 genome wide screening identifies CCNC as an essential regulator of PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS in HK-2 cells
To identify genes essential for PRCC-TFE3 function, we exploited the robust oncogene induced senescence (OIS) phenotype triggered by PRCC-TFE3 expression and performed a genome wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen in doxycycline inducible PRCC-TFE3 HK-2 cells ( A). Cells were transduced with a lentiviral sgRNA library targeting the entire genome, with four independent sgRNAs per gene, and cultured under non-induced (Dox-) or PRCC-TFE3 induced (Dox+) conditions. Because PRCC-TFE3 induction enforces OIS and limits cell proliferation, loss of genes essential for PRCC-TFE3 function was expected to provide a selective growth advantage under Dox(+) conditions. sgRNA abundance was quantified by next generation sequencing, and differential enrichment between Dox(+) and Dox(-) populations was analyzed to identify genes whose knockout alleviates PRCC-TFE3 dependent OIS. MAGeCK-based analysis revealed that multiple sgRNAs targeting genes involved in cell cycle regulation and protein homeostasis were significantly enriched among candidates required for PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS ( B, C). Notably, the top ranked cell proliferation related hits included MYC and CCNC, highlighting these genes as strong candidates for mediating the OIS response. To assess the impact of MYC and CCNC loss on PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS in HK-2 cells, we generated doxycycline-inducible PRCC-TFE3 HK-2 cell lines with CRISPR/Cas9 mediated knockout of either MYC or CCNC. Cell proliferation was then quantified using a resazurin assay. Notably, CCNC knockout robustly rescued the proliferation defect induced by PRCC-TFE3 expression ( D, E), indicating a functional interaction between PRCC-TFE3 driven transcriptional programs and the Cyclin C dependent regulatory machinery. CCNC encodes Cyclin C, a core component of the Mediator kinase module. Cyclin C assembles the Mediator kinase module together with CDK8 or CDK19, MED12, and MED13 [ , ]. Among these components, CDK8 and CDK19 are well established oncogenic kinases, frequently implicated in transcriptional addiction across multiple cancer types [ , , ]. Given that CCNC loss was sufficient to counteract PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS, we therefore focused on CCNC as a critical regulatory node required for the oncogenic transcriptional activity of PRCC-TFE3. The Mediator kinase module dynamically associates with the core Mediator complex and modulates its regulatory functions, including the transmission of enhancer derived transcriptional activation signals to promoter bound transcriptional machinery, as well as the fine tuning of transcription initiation and elongation [ ]. Notably, the screening also identified multiple components of the Mediator complex with high RRA scores, including kinase module subunits (MED12 and MED13) as well as additional Mediator subunits such as MED19, MED23, MED24, and MED25 ( F). Targeted knockout of these Mediator subunits similarly rescued cell proliferation in PRCC-TFE3-induced cells ( G). These data indicate that the Mediator kinase module is required to sustain PRCC-TFE3 associated OIS in HK-2 cells.
 

## CCNC deletion rescues cells from PRCC-TFE3 induced senescence
Given that our CRISPR/Cas9 screen identified CCNC as essential for PRCC-TFE3 induced senescence, we next performed further analyses using CCNC knockout cells. Loss of CCNC restored proliferation that was suppressed by PRCC-TFE3 expression in HK-2 cells ( A). Consistently, CCNC disruption markedly reduced SA-β-gal-positive cells ( B) and prevented PRCC-TFE3 dependent depletion of LaminB1 and induction of SASP genes ( C–D). Cell cycle analysis further showed that CCNC knockout largely reversed the PRCC-TFE3 induced cell cycle arrest, restoring S phase entry and reducing the accumulation of cells in G0/G1 ( E–F). These results demonstrate that CCNC is indispensable for PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS and that its loss is sufficient to restore proliferative capacity in HK-2 cells.
 

## CDK8/19 inhibition phenocopies CCNC loss and reverses PRCC-TFE3 induced senescence
Because Cyclin C functions as an essential regulatory subunit of the Mediator kinase module, we hypothesized kinase activity mediated by CDK8/19 is critical for the CCNC dependent PRCC-TFE3 induced senescence. We therefore examined whether pharmacological inhibition of CDK8/19 could similarly attenuate PRCC-TFE3 driven senescence. Treatment of PRCC-TFE3 inducible HK-2 cells with the selective CDK8/19 inhibitor MSC2530818 restored cell proliferation, reversing the growth suppression caused by PRCC-TFE3 induction ( A). In addition, MSC2530818 treatment markedly reduced senescence associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining, indicating attenuation of the PRCC-TFE3 induced senescence phenotype ( B). LaminB1 expression remained preserved,and induction of SASP genes was markedly reduced upon CDK8/19 inhibition ( C–D). MSC2530818 also reversed the G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest imposed by PRCC-TFE3 ( E). Thus, CDK8/19 kinase activity is similarly required for the maintenance of PRCC-TFE3 induced OIS.
 

## PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcription requires Mediator kinase module activity
The requirement of both CCNC and CDK8/19 activity in supporting PRCC-TFE3 function strongly suggests that PRCC-TFE3 cooperates with the Mediator kinase module. Consistent with this idea, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that induction of PRCC-TFE3 promoted the formation of nuclear Cyclin C puncta ( F). ChIP-qPCR revealed that, upon PRCC-TFE3 induction, Cyclin C is recruited to the same genomic loci bound by PRCC-TFE3 at target genes associated with cell cycle regulation ( G), supporting the notion that the Mediator kinase module directly facilitates PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcriptional activation. Consistently, expression of these five genes, which are co-occupied by PRCC-TFE3 and Cyclin C, was increased upon PRCC-TFE3 induction, as assessed by qPCR in doxycycline-treated HK-2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). To elucidate how the Mediator kinase module influences PRCC-TFE3 associated transcriptional programs, we performed RNAseq on PRCC-TFE3 inducible HK-2 cells treated with doxycycline and/or MSC2530818 for 48 h ( A). Principal component analysis based on the top 500 most variable genes revealed that doxycycline induced PRCC-TFE3 expression was associated with marked transcriptomic changes along PC1. Under MSC(-) conditions, PRCC-TFE3 induction resulted in clear separation from non-induced samples. In contrast, in the presence of MSC2530818, induced and non-induced samples clustered more closely along PC1, indicating that CDK8/19 inhibition attenuates the magnitude of PRCC-TFE3 associated transcriptomic changes. Notably, MSC2530818 treatment alone also altered global gene expression patterns, suggesting that CDK8/19 inhibition exerts broader transcriptional effects beyond PRCC-TFE3 dependent programs ( B, Supplementary Fig. 4A). Consistent with this observation, GSEA comparing MSC+/DOX- and MSC-/DOX- conditions revealed negative enrichment of transcription factor associated signatures, including HOXA9, CEBP, and SRF (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D), further indicating that CDK8/19 inhibition alters multiple transcriptional programs independent of PRCC-TFE3 induction. To assess the potential background effects of doxycycline, qPCR analysis of representative PRCC-TFE3 target genes was performed in parental HK-2 cells cultured with or without doxycycline, revealing minimal effects on their expression and suggesting that doxycycline alone is unlikely to account for the observed transcriptional changes (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Importantly, treatment of PRCC-TFE3 inducible HK-2 cells with the selective CDK8/19 inhibitor MSC2530818 modestly reduced doxycycline induced PRCC-TFE3 expression, while markedly suppressing expression of the PRCC-TFE3 target gene GPNMB (Supplementary Fig. 4F), indicating that CDK8/19 inhibition primarily attenuates PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcriptional activity. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that PRCC-TFE3 expression robustly induced TFEB target gene programs and activated lysosomal pathways ( C), consistent with the close relationship between TFE3 and TFEB as members of the MITF family of transcription factors. Conversely, treatment with the CDK8/19 inhibitor MSC2530818 markedly attenuated these PRCC-TFE3 enriched signatures, indicating that PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcriptional activation requires functional Mediator kinase activity ( D). Consistent with these findings, clustering analysis further demonstrated that genes upregulated upon PRCC-TFE3 induction were broadly downregulated by MSC2530818 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis for each RNA-seq cluster is presented in Supplementary Table 3. MSC2530818 treatment alone also altered transcriptional programs independent of PRCC–TFE3 induction, consistent with broader effects of CDK8/19 inhibition.
 Differential expression analysis revealed a broad set of genes that were strongly upregulated upon PRCC-TFE3 induction, including well-known TFE3 downstream targets (e.g., GPNMB, RRAGD, FNIP2). Notably, many of these PRCC-TFE3 responsive genes were substantially reduced following CDK8/19 inhibition with MSC2530818, indicating that their transcriptional activation depends on Mediator kinase module activity ( E). The intersection of genes upregulated by PRCC-TFE3 and those downregulated by MSC2530818 ( F) revealed a core set of 168 transcripts. Notably, this subset included multiple genes directly bound and transcriptionally activated by TFE3 [ ], as supported by our ChIP-qPCR results and heatmap visualization of TFE3 target genes ( G). RT-qPCR validation confirmed that MSC2530818 effectively suppressed the PRCC-TFE3 mediated upregulation of these targets ( H). Taken together, these findings indicate that PRCC-TFE3 associated transcriptional reprogramming is strongly supported by the Mediator kinase module.

## Mediator kinase activity supports oncogenic functions of TFE3 fusion in vitro and in vivo
In non cancerous renal epithelial cells, PRCC-TFE3 induces a robust oncogene induced senescence program in a manner that depends on the Mediator kinase module. Although the biological outcome of PRCC-TFE3 activity differs between normal and malignant settings, potentially reflecting differences in epigenetic state or transcriptional context, both senescence induction and tumorigenesis are fundamentally driven by the transcriptional activity of PRCC-TFE3. Importantly, forced expression of PRCC-TFE3 in mouse renal epithelial cells is sufficient to initiate TFE3-RCC [ ], indicating that this fusion protein can act as an oncogenic driver in a specific lineage of renal epithelial cells that is permissive for tumor development. These observations led us to hypothesize that, despite distinct cellular outcomes, the transcriptional dependency of PRCC-TFE3 may be conserved across contexts, and that Mediator kinase module activity could also be required to sustain PRCC-TFE3 driven oncogenic programs in established TFE3-RCC. To test this possibility, we next examined whether pharmacological inhibition of the Mediator kinase module alters the oncogenic activity of the PRCC-TFE3 fusion in TFE3-RCC cells. Treatment with the selective CDK8/19 inhibitor MSC2530818 significantly reduced the expression of PRCC-TFE3 target genes in patient derived TFE3-RCC cell line UOK124 cells harboring endogenous PRCC-TFE3 ( A). Consistent with this transcriptional suppression, inhibition of CDK8/19 in UOK124 cells also led to a marked decrease in lysosomal activity, as assessed by LysoPrime Green fluorescence, reducing it to a level comparable to that observed following TFE3 knockdown ( B, Supplementary Fig 5A). Importantly, MSC2530818 treatment did not reduce the expression of endogenous PRCC-TFE3 in UOK124 cells, as confirmed by western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C). Given that lysosomal hyperactivation is a well established downstream consequence of TFE3 fusion driven transcriptional programs, these findings indicate that this hallmark feature of TFE3-RCC depends on intact Mediator kinase module activity. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of CDK8/19 with MSC2530818 significantly reduced the number, but not the size, of colonies formed by UOK124 cells in an anchorage independent growth assay, indicating that Mediator kinase module activity is required for the tumorigenic potential of TFE3-RCC cells ( C). To directly assess the contribution of the Mediator kinase module to TFE3-RCC tumorigenesis, we next generated CCNC knockout UOK124 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 mediated genome editing ( D, top). Genetic ablation of CCNC led to a significant reduction in cell proliferation under two dimensional culture conditions ( D, bottom). Importantly, CCNC knockout dramatically suppressed tumor growth in a nude mouse xenograft model, as evidenced by significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights compared with control tumors ( E). Together, these findings provide compelling evidence that the Mediator kinase module plays a critical and indispensable role in sustaining the oncogenic growth of TFE3-RCC. These results offer the basis for the clinical application to advanced TFE3-RCC of targeting mediator kinase module strategy. Finally, to evaluate the translational relevance of targeting the Mediator kinase module in a setting that more closely mimics human disease, we examined the therapeutic efficacy of the CDK8/19 inhibitor SEL120, which is currently under clinical investigation for non RCC malignancies, using an orthotopic syngeneic mouse model of TFE3-RCC. This model was established by transplanting tumors derived from a genetically engineered mouse expressing PRCC-TFE3 [ ] into immunocompetent recipient mice. Treatment with SEL120 markedly suppressed tumor growth in vivo. Whereas tumors in vehicle treated mice exhibited a progressive increase in volume over time, SEL120 administration significantly attenuated tumor expansion throughout the observation period ( F). These results demonstrate that sustained CDK8/19 activity is required for PRCC-TFE3 driven tumor growth in vivo and provide strong evidence that the Mediator kinase module represents a therapeutically actionable vulnerability in TFE3-RCC. Collectively, our findings establish the Mediator kinase module as a critical cofactor supporting TFE3 fusion oncogenic function across cellular models and in vivo systems, as summarized schematically in G.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we identify the Cyclin C-CDK8/19 Mediator kinase module as a critical and therapeutically actionable co-regulator of TFE3 fusion function across senescence induction, transcriptional control, and tumorigenesis. Although TFE3 fusion proteins possess intrinsic transcriptional activity through their basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper domain [ , , ], our findings demonstrate that this activity is insufficient on its own to sustain the full oncogenic program. Instead, TFE3 fusion depends on the Mediator kinase module and, more broadly, is likely to engage Mediator dependent transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, including enhancer–promoter communication and chromatin organization, to establish and sustain its transcriptional program [ ]. A central observation is that PRCC-TFE3 induction in non-cancerous HK-2 renal epithelial cells triggers a robust oncogene induced senescence (OIS) program. This phenomenon parallels classical responses to potent oncogenes such as MYC or RAS and is also reminiscent of other fusion transcription factors, including EWSR1–FLI1, which can elicit senescence or growth arrest when expressed in non-transformed cells or in cellular contexts that do not correspond to the tumor cell of origin [ , , , ]. Together, these observations reflect the profound transcriptional and metabolic rewiring imposed by TFE3 fusion. The fact that CCNC knockout or CDK8/19 inhibition overrides this OIS response indicates that the ability of PRCC-TFE3 to reorganize chromatin, activate enhancer landscapes, and drive high level transcription is contingent on the Mediator kinase module. Importantly, loss of CCNC does not facilitate tumorigenesis; rather, CCNC deficiency results in marked suppression of tumor growth in both human and mouse TFE3 fusion RCC models. The induction of OIS observed in non-cancerous HK-2 renal epithelial cells should therefore be viewed as an immediate cellular response to forced PRCC-TFE3 expression, rather than as a barrier that must be bypassed during tumorigenesis. A plausible explanation for the divergent cellular outcomes of TFE3 fusion activity is that the transcriptional programs engaged by PRCC-TFE3 are shaped by the underlying epigenetic landscape. The Mediator complex, together with Cyclin C and CDK8/19, may regulate the accessibility of TFE3 fusion to specific genomic regions, including loci associated with senescence programs. Such accessibility is likely to differ among normal renal epithelial cells, tumor cells, and the putative cells of origin for TFE3-RCC. In this context, PRCC-TFE3 may preferentially activate senescence associated gene networks in HK-2 cells, whereas in TFE3-RCC cells, a distinct chromatin configuration permits engagement of transcriptional programs that support tumor growth and survival. Accordingly, the OIS phenotype serves as a sensitive functional readout for identifying transcriptional cofactors that are essential for TFE3 fusion activity. Importantly, these findings do not conflict with the absolute requirement for Cyclin C and the Mediator kinase module in sustaining TFE3 fusion driven tumor growth.
Mechanistically, our data reveal that PRCC-TFE3 and Cyclin C co-occupy genomic regions of canonical TFE3 fusion target genes, and that CDK8/19 inhibition attenuates the PRCC-TFE3 dependent transcriptional signature, including TFEB associated lysosomal programs, across HK-2 cells and TFE3 RCC cell lines. These results position the Mediator kinase module as a pivotal facilitator of TFE3 fusion driven transcription. While CDK8/19 inhibition may partially influence inducible transcription from the Tet system, the markedly greater suppression of downstream target gene expression supports a direct role of the Mediator kinase module in PRCC–TFE3 transcriptional function. Mediator is known to bridge enhancer-derived regulatory inputs to promoter-bound RNA Polymerase II and to contribute to chromatin looping, pause-release, and transcriptional elongation [ , , , , , ]. TFE3 fusion appears to exploit this machinery: the kinase module modulates Mediator’s ability to transmit long range enhancer signals and maintain an open chromatin architecture at TFE3 fusion target loci. In this sense, TFE3 fusion does not operate as a standalone transcription factor but instead functions as part of a larger chromatin-regulatory assembly in which the Mediator complex plays a decisive role.
The oncogenic dependency on this axis is further supported by our findings in cancerous contexts. In patient derived TFE3-RCC cell line UOK124, CDK8/19 inhibition suppresses PRCC-TFE3 target gene expression and reduces lysosomal activity, recapitulating the transcriptional vulnerabilities observed in HK-2 OIS assays. Genetic ablation of CCNC in UOK124 cells markedly impairs tumor formation in vivo, demonstrating that the Cyclin C Mediator axis is indispensable not only for transcriptional activity but also for tumor growth. Moreover, selective CDK8/19 inhibition significantly slows tumor growth in a orthotopic syngeneic mouse model of TFE3-RCC, providing in vivo evidence that pharmacologic disruption of this axis exerts antitumor effects.
These results collectively highlight the Mediator kinase module as a central regulator of TFE3 fusion oncogenic transcription and suggest that targeting Mediator dependent enhancer–promoter communication represents a conceptually new therapeutic strategy for TFE3-RCC. Although current immune checkpoint inhibitor and tyrosine kinase inhibitor combinations show clinical activity in TFE3-RCC, therapeutic resistance remains common [ , ], and no treatments specifically address the transcriptional addiction imposed by the fusion oncoprotein. Our findings raise the possibility that CDK8/19 inhibitors, or more selective strategies to disrupt the CCNC Mediator interface, could form the basis of therapeutic approaches against TFE3 fusion driven tumors, either alone or in combination with immune or targeted agents.
This study has limitations. The mechanistic dissection of chromatin architecture underlying PRCC-TFE3 Mediator cooperation (e.g., enhancer–promoter looping, chromatin accessibility dynamics) was not comprehensively addressed and will require high resolution approaches such as PLAC-seq or ATAC-seq in future work. Additionally, while CDK8/19 inhibition demonstrated clear therapeutic promise, the specificity of transcriptional repression, whether arising from direct effects on the chromatin engagement of TFE3 fusion proteins or from broader perturbation of Mediator dependent transcriptional regulation, remains to be fully resolved.
In summary, our findings establish the Mediator kinase module as an essential cofactor of TFE3 fusion and suggest that Mediator dependent transcriptional regulatory processes, including enhancer associated signaling and chromatin based regulation, play a central role in shaping the oncogenic transcriptional program of TFE3-RCC. By demonstrating that genetic or pharmacologic disruption of Cyclin C-CDK8/19 profoundly impairs TFE3 fusion activity across normal, cancerous, and in vivo settings, this work identifies the Mediator kinase axis as a tractable and biologically grounded therapeutic vulnerability in TFE3 fusion driven renal cell carcinoma.

## Conclusion
This study identifies the Cyclin C-CDK8/19 Mediator kinase module as a central regulator of PRCC-TFE3 driven transcription in both non-cancerous renal epithelium and TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma. By applying an unbiased genome-wide CRISPR screen using OIS as a functional readout, we discovered CCNC as an essential genetic dependency of PRCC-TFE3. Mechanistically, PRCC-TFE3 promotes nuclear accumulation of Cyclin C and engages the CDK8/19-Mediator complex to activate lysosomal, MITF family associated programs. Importantly, the PRCC-TFE3-CCNC-CDK8/19 axis enforces OIS in non-cancerous renal epithelial cells but drives tumor growth in TFE3-RCC, revealing a context dependent switch in TFE3 fusion oncogenic output. Consistent with this mechanism, genetic loss of CCNC or pharmacologic inhibition of CDK8/19 suppressed tumor growth in multiple in vivo models. Together, these findings establish the Mediator kinase module as both a mechanistic linchpin and a therapeutically actionable vulnerability in PRCC-TFE3 driven renal cancer, providing a strong rationale for mechanism based targeted therapy in TFE3-RCC.
