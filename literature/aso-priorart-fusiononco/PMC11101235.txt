## Abstract
Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) is a well-established oncogenic long non-coding RNA, the higher expression of which is strongly correlated with cancer events such as tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, drug resistance, and treatment outcome in solid cancers. Recently, a series of studies has highlighted its potential role in hematological malignancies in terms of these events. Similar to solid cancers, MALAT1 can regulate various target genes via sponging and epigenetic mechanisms, but the miRNAs sponged by MALAT1 differ from those identified in solid cancers. In this review, we systematically describe the role and underlying mechanisms of MALAT1 in multiple types of hematological malignancies, including regulation of cell proliferation, metastasis, stress response, and glycolysis. Clinically, MALAT1 expression is related to poor treatment outcome and drug resistance, therefore exhibiting potential prognostic value in multiple myeloma, lymphoma, and leukemia. Finally, we discuss the evaluation of MALAT1 as a novel therapeutic target against cancer in preclinical studies.
Keywords: Long non-coding RNA, MALAT1, Hematological malignancies, Tumorigenesis, Metastasis, Drug resistance, Prognostic biomarker

## Introduction
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), as key regulators of gene expression,[ ] have been shown to play crucial roles in cancer-related events such as tumorigenesis, progression, and treatment outcome. Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) has been widely investigated and is one of the most well-established lncRNAs to participate in cancer metastasis.[ ]
MALAT1 is located on chromosome 11q13 in humans and chromosome 19qA in mice;[ , ] it is transcribed via RNA polymerase II. RNases P and Z further process the nascent RNA transcript to produce two fragments: MALAT1-associated small cytoplasmic RNA (mascRNA), which is 61 nt long, and a larger fragment of 6.7 kb, which is recognized as mature MALAT1. Mature MALAT1 stays in the nucleus and localizes on nuclear speckles, while mascRNA transports to the cytoplasm, where its function is unclear.[ ] The 3′ end of mature MALAT1 is a highly conserved triple-helix structure, which protects it from exonucleases.[ ] Through a feedforward positive regulatory loop, the naturally occurring MALAT1 antisense transcript (TALAM1) also helps stabilize MALAT1.[ ] As a result, mature MALAT1 is highly stable in human B cells, with a half-life as long as 16.5 h.[ ]
MALAT1 can regulate biological functions through a variety of intermolecular interactions. First, MALAT1, localized within nuclear speckles, can regulate the transcription of many downstream genes primarily by acting as a molecular scaffold on interchromatin granule clusters.[ ] By acting as a cis/trans element or by attracting particular proteins to nuclear speckles, MALAT1 participates in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation of genes.[ ] Second, MALAT1 acts as a microRNA (miRNA) sponge by attaching to RNA response elements of miRNAs (e.g., miR-363-3p), which can suppress the expression of target RNAs involved in the pathogenesis of cancer.[ , ] Moreover, a series of studies has also highlighted the role of MALAT1 in regulating epigenetic change, as well as acting as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA).[ – ]
MALAT1 was first identified in patients with non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), revealing the strong correlation between its elevated expression level and high risk of metastasis and poor treatment outcome.[ ] To date, upregulation of MALAT1 has been found in various other solid-cancer types, including breast, ovarian, cervical, gastric, pancreatic, colorectal, and prostate cancers, osteosarcoma, and glioma. Its occurrence has been mostly correlated with poor prognosis.[ – ]MALAT1 participates in cancer pathogenesis and progress through its effects on multiple cancer-related biological events: (1) Cell proliferation. MALAT1 can inhibit miRNAs (e.g., miR-124) that regulate key elements in multiple pathways—including phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), Hippo, and β-catenin–Wingless/Integrated—thereby promoting tumor cell growth.[ – ] (2) Cancer metastasis. MALAT1 facilitates metastasis by regulating epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) via sequestration of miR-145-5p, leading to increased expression of neural precursor cell expressed developmentally downregulated 9 (NEDD9).[ ] Moreover, MALAT1 contributes to angiogenesis by affecting angiogenic factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).[ ] (3) Stress response of cancer cells. MALAT1 influences the stress response of cancer cells by modulating pathways involved in responding to stressors that can occur during tumor growth and metastasis, such as hypoxia, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage, and matrix detachment, thereby helping cancer cells evade apoptosis.[ , ]
In addition to solid cancers, increasing evidence in recent years has also established a potential role of MALAT1 in hematological malignancies, but a systematic review is lacking. Therefore, herein we present an updated, comprehensive overview that highlights various functions of MALAT1 and relavant clinical applications in hematological malignancies.

## Roles of MALAT1 in Hematological Malignancies

## Upregulation of MALAT1 expression in hematological malignancies
MALAT1 is upregulated in multiple hematological malignancies [Table  ]. In multiple myeloma (MM), MALAT1 overexpression has been reported both in clinical samples and cell lines.[ – ] For example, Cho et al[ ] demonstrated that MALAT1 was overexpressed in the bone marrow of newly diagnosed patients compared with both post-treatment patients and healthy controls, while Handa et al[ ] demonstrated that MALAT1 expression was significantly higher in MM than in monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance.
 Upregulation of MALAT1 has also been reported in various subtypes of lymphoma. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients, MALAT1 overexpression was observed in tumor tissue compared with normal lymph node tissue.[ ] In patients with primary gastrointestinal DLBCL (PGI-DLBCL), MALAT1 was overexpressed in tumor tissue compared with paired adjacent non-tumor tissue; further, it was notably more highly expressed in patients with the non–germinal center B-cell–like (non-GCB) phenotype, advanced stage, and high International Prognostic Index (IPI) score.[ ] In clinical samples of patients with natural-killer (NK)/T-cell lymphomas and in cell lines derived from patients with various types of these lymphomas, MALAT1 was distinctively upregulated compared with other lncRNAs in all disease subtypes.[ , ] In mantle cell lymphoma, MALAT1 was overrepresented in tumor tissue compared with normal B lymphocytes isolated from peripheral blood of healthy donors.[ ]
In leukemia, MALAT1 overexpression has been commonly identified in malignant cells.[ – ] For example, Ahmadi et al[ ] found that MALAT1 was not only upregulated in peripheral-blood samples from patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) compared with healthy individuals, but also correlated with cytogenetic abnormalities, including Del13q14 and Del11q22. Huang et al[ ] demonstrated a greater level of MALAT1 overexpression in bone marrow samples from patients with acute myeloblastic leukemia, subtype M5 (AML-M5) than from patients with other subtypes. Pouyanrad et al[ ] found MALAT1 to be upregulated in bone marrow samples from childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) patients compared with non-cancer controls. In the serum of patients with myelofibrosis, MALAT1 was also upregulated compared with healthy controls.[ ]
Several key molecules have been found to mediate upregulation of MALAT1 expression in hematological malignancies. For example, in MM, Family With Sequence Similarity 46 member C (FAM46C) is one of the most commonly mutated genes; inactivation thereof significantly upregulated MALAT1 in MM cell lines.[ ] In mycosis fungoides, chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 21 (CCL21) induced mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation, which enhanced MALAT1 expression.[ ] The mechanism underlying overexpression of MALAT1 in other subtypes of hematological malignancies waits to be revealed.

## Regulation of cell proliferation
Similar to solid tumors, MALAT1 can also act as a ceRNA in hematological malignancies and inhibit specific miRNAs involved in multiple cancer-related pathways, including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, Hippo, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1; Figure  ).
 In MM, MALAT1 was shown to promote cell proliferation by activating oncogenic pathways, as demonstrated by increases in phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt), p65–nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), ERK1/2, and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element-binding protein (CREB).[ ]In vitro, downregulation of MALAT1 via RNA interference can significantly inhibit proliferation of MM cells via cell cycle arrest at G1 phase.[ ]MALAT1 knockdown (KD) also inhibits tumor growth in vivo and can induce apoptosis via activation of cysteine–aspartic acid–specific protease/proteinases 3 and 9 (Caspase-3, -9), downregulation of B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), and upregulation of Bcl-2–like protein 4 (Bax).[ , ] Via sequence complementarity analysis and a luciferase reporter assay (LRA), Gu et al[ ] found that MALAT1 acts as a ceRNA for miR-509-5p, which targets the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) of forkhead box protein 1 (FOXP1) to suppress MM cell progression; increasing miR-509-5p can reverse MALAT1’s effect on MM progression. Sun et al[ ] reported that MALAT1 can also affect the proliferation and adhesion of myeloma cells by regulating miR-181a-5p and the Hippo–yes-associated protein (YAP) signaling pathway.
In lymphoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have recently drawn wide interest. Wang et al[ ] linked expression of MALAT1 to PD-L1, which enhances tumorigenesis and immune escape in cancers. According to their study, MALAT1 might sponge miR-195 to regulate expression of PD-L1, thereby regulating the proliferation of DLBCL.
MALAT1 also plays a regulatory role in cell proliferation in leukemia. For example, it can regulate proliferation and promote apoptosis in AML cells by sponging miR-96 and miR-146a[ , ] while affecting ALL cell proliferation and apoptosis by regulating the miR-205–protein tyrosine kinase 7 (PTK7) axis.[ ]

## Regulation of migration, invasion, and metastasis
EMT is an important dynamic process that contributes to metastasis and diffusion of tumor cells in cancer progression. In a DLBCL cell line, MALAT1 KD suppressed the EMT-like process via the Ras/ERK signaling pathway and inhibited cell migration.[ ] Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) is a rare non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by migration of T-lymphocytes to the skin, which MALAT1 can promote in vitro by activating mTOR signaling. Another study found that in CTCL, CCL21 regulated expression of MALAT1[ ] and that miR-124 might mediate MALAT1’s regulatory role in this cancer.[ ]
Unlike the cells of solid tumors and lymphomas, which grow in clumps, those of certain hematological malignancies (e.g., MM, leukemia) grow dispersed throughout bone marrow. MALAT1 can also promote the migratory ability of these cancer cell types and is associated with extramedullary invasion, a risk factor for inferior outcome. In MM, MALAT1 expression was markedly higher in extramedullary myeloma compared with corresponding intramedullary-myeloma cells.[ ] In a MM cell line, the migratory ability of cancer cells was increased by ectopic expression of MALAT1 and was decreased by transfection of anti-MALAT1 small interfering RNA (siRNA).[ , ] In another study, MALAT1 upregulation induced by inactivation of FAM46C dramatically increased the migratory ability of MM cells via PI3K/Rac1 signaling.[ ] In AML, MALAT1 might lead to transcriptional silencing of the E-cadherin gene CDH1 through trimethylation of histone H3-lysine 27 (H3K27) mediated by recruitment of enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) and suppressor of zeste 12 (SUZ12),[ ] thereby inducing metastasis and diffusion of tumor cells.[ ] In other studies, MALAT1 was found to promote the aggressiveness of AML by regulating m6A modification of zinc finger E-box–binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), which can also promote EMT,[ ] or to regulate migration by sponging miR-146a in order to regulate C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) expression in AML cells.[ ]

## Effects on stress response of cancer cells
During cancer development and progression, cancer cells are exposed to stress conditions such as hypoxia, DNA damage, and excessive signaling. These stressors induce apoptosis, DNA repair, inflammation, and autophagy in normal cells, but they further increase the survival advantages of cancer cells because dysfunction of these mechanisms commonly occurs in the tumorigenesis and development of various cancer types.[ ] Therefore, MALAT1 is considered to be involved in cancer cell stress response by regulating related signaling in cancers, including multiple types of hematological malignancies.[ , , , ]
Particularly in MM, MALAT1 plays a role in DNA repair mechanisms such as alternative non-homozygous end-joining (A-NHEJ) signaling.[ ]MALAT1 overexpression enhanced A-NHEJ repair by upregulating DNA ligase 3 (LIG3) proteins and poly (adenosine diphosphate [ADP]–ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1). Conversely, MALAT1 silencing via nanoconjugated antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) mediated decreases in PARP1 and A-NHEJ repair pathway activity, resulting in DNA damage. Defective DNA repair induced additional cleavage of PARP1 and Caspase-3, which contributed to cell apoptosis.[ ] A similar mechanism has also been reported in NSCLC.[ ]
MALAT1 can also regulate apoptosis by regulating proteasome machinery.[ ] Using locked nucleotide acid (LNA) gapmer ASO, Amodio et al[ ] found that MALAT1 antagonism induced the transcriptional activation of a negative regulator (i.e., Kelch-like epichlorohydrin-associated protein 1 [Keap1]), which downmodulated the two major transcriptional activators of proteasome subunit genes nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)–like 1 and 2 (NRF1, NRF2). Interestingly, NRF1 can in turn promote MALAT1 expression, establishing a positive feedback loop in the regulation of MALAT1 in proteasome machinery. Therefore, targeting MALAT1 resulted in reduced expression of antioxidant genes, followed by accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins and endoplasmic-reticulum (ER) stress-induced apoptosis. A similar mechanism has not yet been reported in solid cancers. Theoretically, targeting MALAT1 might reverse proteasome inhibitor resistance caused by compensatory hyperactivation of trypsine and caspase-like activities.[ ]

## Regulation of nuclear export of chimeric mRNAs
Oncogenic fusion genes, including promyelocytic leukemia protein–retinoic acid receptor alpha (PML–RARα), mixed-lineage leukemia (MLL) fusions, and AML1-ETO, are key drivers of leukemogenesis.[ ] Chen et al[ ] discovered that MALAT1 directly interacted with fusion proteins in nuclear speckles and influenced nucleocytoplasmic export of chimeric messenger RNAs (mRNA) and their protein production. In addition, MALAT1 functioned as a regulator that enhanced contacts between fusion proteins and m6A methyltransferases, which in turn regulated the process of exporting chimeric mRNAs by utilizing the m6A reader YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA-binding protein C1 (YTHDC1).[ ] Therefore, nuclear export of chimeric mRNAs depended on the MALAT1-triggered autoregulatory loop in leukemia. MALAT1 KD reduced the expression levels of fusion protein in AML cells and attenuated AML progression in a mouse model.[ ] A similar role of MALAT1 has not yet been reported in solid cancers.

## Regulation of glycolysis
Reprogrammed glucose metabolism of enhanced aerobic glycolysis is a hallmark of cancer. MALAT1 might contribute to tumorigenesis by regulating cancer glucose metabolism, enhancing glycolysis, and inhibiting gluconeogenesis in solid cancers.[ ] To this effect, Liu et al[ ] reported that via the miR-1271-5p/sex-determining region Y (SRY)–related high-mobility group box 13 (SOX13) axis, MALAT1 not only promoted cell viability and invasion in MM but also expedited glycolysis, as evaluated by glucose consumption, lactate production, adenosine triphosphate (ATP)/adenosine diphosphate (ADP) ratio, and detection of related enzymes.

## Structural changes
Based on putative structural changes to MALAT1 in a working model of non-cancerous cells,[ ] Wang et al[ ] found that the secondary structural model of MALAT1 became unstructured in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), which might increase the propensity for miRNA sponging and have profound effects on cancer cell function. By contrast, MALAT1 structure is largely maintained in HeLa cells, suggesting possible differences in underlying mechanisms between solid cancers and hematological malignancies.

## Clinical Applications of MALAT1 in Hematological Malignancies

## MALAT1 as a prognostic marker
Given that MALAT1 affects proliferation, metastasis, and multiple cell metabolic processes in hematological-cancer cells, its prognostic value has been evaluated in a series of clinical studies.
In MM, elevation of MALAT1 expression was significantly correlated with poor prognosis in terms of overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS).[ , ] In addition, MALAT1 expression was downregulated after treatment, and greater decreases in its expression levels predict significantly longer PFS.[ ] In general, most studies verified the correlation between MALAT1 overexpression and poor prognosis in MM, suggesting the prognostic value of MALAT1 in this cancer. However, some contradictory results were reported: Amodio et al[ ] analyzed two available clinically annotated datasets that included >550 patients but failed to find any significant correlation between MALAT1 expression and OS or time to relapse. Therefore, a meta-analysis, which included 26 studies involving 3501 MM patients, was conducted to clarify the potential of lncRNAs as prognostic biomarkers in newly diagnosed MM.[ ] In subgroup analysis, MALAT1 overexpression was associated with worse OS (hazard ratio [HR], 2.09; P = 0.014) and PFS (HR, 2.02; P = 0.001),[ ] thus establishing the potential of MALAT1 expression as a prognostic marker in MM.
The correlation between MALAT1 expression and patient outcome has also been documented in both B- and T-cell lymphomas. In PGI-DLBCL, elevated MALAT1 expression was correlated with inferior OS and PFS.[ ] In mantle cell lymphoma, MALAT1 upregulation was associated with higher IPI and reduced OS.[ ] In follicular lymphoma, high MALAT1 expression predicted shorter PFS.[ ] High MALAT1 expression is also significantly correlated with inferior OS in peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) and precursor T-lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL), whereas in extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type (ENKTL-NT), no association between MALAT1 upregulation and OS was observed.[ ]
MALAT1 can be released into the plasma as a circulating lncRNA. In CLL, MALAT1 expression in peripheral blood mirrored that in lymph nodes and was a prognostic factor for time to treatment.[ ]MALAT1 overexpression, as estimated using the plasma of patients with myelofibrosis, was associated with detrimental clinical variables, such as high leukocyte and Cluster of Differentiation 34–positive (CD34+) cell counts, severe grade of bone marrow fibrosis, and presence of splenomegaly; and was correlated with shorter OS.[ ] Therefore, non-invasively detecting MALAT1 expression is a promising way to predict prognosis.

## MALAT1 in drug resistance
MALAT1 overexpression can cause chemotherapeutic resistance in hematological malignancies through multiple underlying mechanisms, including regulation of the DNA damage repair pathway, drug efflux pump function, cell cycle, autophagy, and proteasome activity [Figure  ].
 

## Regulation of the DNA damage repair pathway
In MM, bortezomib and doxorubicin can significantly upregulate MALAT1. MALAT1 expression was markedly higher in extramedullary myeloma compared with corresponding intramedullary-myeloma cells, indicating the role of MALAT1 in cancer metastasis and resistance to chemotherapy.[ ] In MM, increasing MALAT1 expression enhanced the A-NHEJ pathway, which led to enhanced DNA repair ability, while anti-MALAT1 strategies, which decrease MALAT1 expression, could induce DNA damage and reduce the resistance of cancer cells to bortezomib, melphalan, and doxorubicin.[ ]

## Effects on drug efflux pump function
In a childhood ALL study, Pouyanrad et al[ ] found that MALAT1 was significantly upregulated in the drug-resistant group, which may be consistent with the non-coding variants induced drug tolerance.[ ] Mechanistically, MALAT1 functioned as a decoy lncRNA to suppress miR-335-3p, which led to overexpression of ATP-binding cassette sub-family A member 3 (ABCA3), a member of the ABC transporter family. The upregulated ABCA3 subsequently increased the efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs and lowered drug uptake, ultimately inducing consecutive occurrences of multidrug resistance (MDR).[ ]

## Regulation of cell cycle
MALAT1 expression was significantly increased in CML cell lines, inducing imatinib resistance. Conversely, knocking down MALAT1 strongly blocked CML cell cycle and inhibited cell proliferation by negating MALAT1’s sponging of miR-328, which attenuated the resistance of CML cells to imatinib.[ ] Similarly, MALAT1 inhibited miR-328-3p expression in AML, thereby inducing cytarabine resistance.[ ]

## Regulation of autophagy
Autophagy is an important cause of MDR in cancer.[ ]MALAT1 KD enhanced cytarabine sensitivity in AML cells by upregulating miR-96.[ ] Research shows that the underlying mechanism in solid cancers might be associated with the autophagy pathway, but further evidence is still required to validate this association.[ ]
Moreover, MALAT1 enhanced proteasome activity in MM, which might induce resistance to proteasome inhibitors.[ ] Other mechanisms, such as EMT and the regulation of apoptosis, also mediate chemotherapeutic resistance in solid cancers.[ ] Although the role of MALAT1 in regulating both these processes has been verified in hematological malignancies, direct evidence of MALAT1-induced drug resistance mediated by these mechanisms remains to be seen.

## MALAT1 as a therapeutic target
MALAT1’s prompting of tumorigenesis and drug resistance makes it a potential therapeutic target in hematological malignancies, through either a single agent or combined with chemotherapy to reverse drug resistance. In preclinical studies using both cell lines and mouse models, MALAT1 suppression has been shown to inhibit proliferation/migration and induce apoptosis of hematological-cancer cells. The most commonly used strategies are siRNA and ASO, the latter being more effective in reducing MM cell viability.[ ] Significant anti-tumor activity of ASO was observed in a humanized murine model of MM, with optimal tumor uptake and no evidence of systemic toxicity.[ ] In another study, ASO not only reduced the tumor burden in a disseminated-MM murine model but also extended lifespan significantly.[ ] These findings suggested that MALAT1 might serve as a potential target in hematological-cancer treatment. However, a clinical study of using siRNA or ASO to target MALAT1 is currently lacking. Toxicological and toxicokinetic studies in non-human primates and subsequent clinical trials are still required to evaluate the safety of these treatment strategies.

## Conclusion and Perspectives
MALAT1 was upregulated in multiple hematological malignancies, played an important role in pathogenesis, and correlated with inferior outcome via various mechanisms in cancer cells: (1) regulation of cell proliferation; (2) regulation of migration, invasion, and metastasis; (3) affecting stress response; (4) regulation of chimeric-mRNA nuclear export; (5) regulation of glycolysis; and (6) structural changes. Although MALAT1 has been shown to regulate several genes and signaling pathways, its full regulatory network waits to be fully elucidated. Moreover, whether MALAT1 is strictly overexpressed in cancer cells only or exhibits altered expression in the tumor microenvironment warrants deep investigation at single-cell resolution on the pan-cancer level with public resources.[ – ] While many studies have documented the value of MALAT1 as a prognostic biomarker in hematological malignancies, conflicting evidence also exists. Therefore, its clinical utility should be further validated in large-scale clinical trials. Targeting MALAT1 is an efficient way to attenuate tumorigenesis and improve survival in mouse models. Despite its potential role as a therapeutic target, however, much work remains to be done before MALAT1 can be clinical applied. Future studies should focus on developing more-efficient and -specific MALAT1-targeting therapies, and the safety and efficiency profiles thereof should be evaluated in clinical trials.
