## Abstract
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive blood cancer with frequently poor clinical outcomes. This heterogeneous malignancy encompasses genetically, molecularly, and even clinically different subgroups. This makes it difficult to develop therapeutic agents that are effective for all subtypes of the disease. Therefore, a selective, universal, and adaptable delivery platform capable of carrying various types of anti-neoplastic agents is an unmet requirement in this area. Two multifunctional fusion proteins were designed for the delivery of biotinylated cargoes to human myeloid leukemia cells by fusing an anti-IL-1RAP single-chain antibody with streptavidin (tetramer or monomer), a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP), and an endosomolytic peptide in a single biomacromolecule. The designed fusions were analyzed primarily in silico, and the biofunctionality of the selected fusion was fully characterized via several binding assays, hemolysis assay, confocal microscopy and cell cytotoxicity assay after production via the Escherichia coli (E. coli) system. The refolded protein exhibited desirable binding activity to leukemic cells, pure antigen and biotinylated BSA. Further analyses revealed efficient cellular uptake, endosomolytic activity, and nuclear penetration without any detectable cytotoxicity toward normal epithelial cells. The described platform seems to have great potential for targeted delivery of different therapeutics to malignant myeloid cells.

## Introduction
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most prevalent and fatal leukemia in adults , . The average 5-year survival rate in ≥ 20-year-old patients is approximately 25%, which is the worst among all leukemias . Among all types of cancers, AML has the worst prognosis in patients ≥ 65 years of age . Without clinical intervention, the disease can lead to death in several weeks .
The treatment schedule for AML generally consists of chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). The standard chemotherapy regimen for treating AML has remained nearly unchanged over the last 4 decades . This type of therapy is very intensive and, on occasion, is virtually ineffective (for example in poor-risks cytogenetics frequently seen in secondary AML) . Owing to their selectivity, each small-molecule inhibitor is beneficial only for a certain subtype or mutation. Monotherapy with these inhibitors generally induces only a temporal response , and resistant clones rapidly emerge. Moreover, these agents can never eliminate leukemia stem cells (LSCs), the main cause of relapse . HSCT, the most promising therapeutic procedure available, is a truly perilous process with several probable life-threatening complications, including increased risk for subsequent malignancies, the emergence of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), severe bacterial, fungal and viral infections, graft rejection, and relapse of leukemia .
In addition to HSCT, other immunotherapies have not yet been very useful for treating AML. Leukemic cells exhibit very low immunogenicity and are proficiently able to escape from the immune system via different deceptive and nullifying strategies , . Conventional therapies for AML are severely immunosuppressive , and the disease itself is an immunocompromised condition – . AML is a clonal malignancy that occurs in immunohematopoietic progenitor cells; consequently, undifferentiated leukemic leukocytes (myeloblasts) originating from these malignant ancestors are nonfunctional , . The immunosuppressive quality of the bone marrow niches of AML patients is one of the greatest impediments to developing operant immunotherapeutic modalities .
According to the aforementioned explanations, approaches that do not involve immune effector functions seem to be more fruitful. The therapeutic effects of gemtuzumab ozogamicin (Mylotarg™, Pfizer Inc., USA) or I-131-apamistamab (also called 131I-BC8 or Iomab-B™, Actinium Pharmaceuticals, Inc., USA), the only FDA-approved antibody-based therapies for AML, do not rely on immune effector functions. The drugs are simple delivery vehicles (humanized anti-CD33 or murine anti-CD45 antibodies) loaded with cytotoxic molecules (calicheamicin or radioactive iodine , respectively).
The major difficulty in targeted immunotherapy of myeloid leukemia is severe myeloablation or hematological toxicity arising from the shared expression of malignant cell surface antigens on normal HSCs (CD123, CD33, and CD44v6 as AML markers) or lymphoid organs (for example, CD25 as a CML marker) , .
One of the most promising candidate antigen for targeting myeloid leukemia is interleukin-1 receptor accessory protein (IL-1RAP), a member of IL-1 superfamily and an indispensable element in signaling of IL-1, IL-33 and IL-36 cytokines . Four different isoforms of IL-1RAP have been identified in human. The first isoform with 570 amino acid residues (also known as IL-1R3), is membrane bounding and have been shown to overexpress in several hematological and non-hematological malignancies. Isoform-2 and isoform-3 are shorter (356 and 346 amino acid residues respectively) and secretory. Isoform-4 is a brain-only membrane protein with 687 amino acid residues and does not involve in IL-1 family signaling .
Up-regulation of IL-1RAP has been shown in CD34+ cells from CML patients and also following the retroviral transduction of cord blood CD34+ cells with P210 BCR/ABL1 fusion construct, the hallmark of CML. The expression of IL-1RAP is directly associated with tyrosine kinase activity of the P210 oncoprotein, as cells transduced with inactive form of P210 do not up-regulate IL-1RAP and the expression of this biomarker is downregulated in CML cell line after treatment by tyrosine kinase inhibitor . IL-1RAP has superior distinguishing potential because normal CD34+/CD38− HSCs usually do not express it. In the hematopoietic system, only monocytes moderately express this protein . The presence or overexpression of this coreceptor on most malignant myeloid blasts and LSCs and its absence on normal HSCs , , make it an encouraging therapeutic target.
IL-1RAP chimeric antigen receptor T-cells and bispecific T-cell engager (anti IL-1RAP/CD3) targeting IL-1RAP showed very promising results in recent preclinical studies (xenograft models of AML) .
IL-1RAP has recently been utilized as a target for scheming therapeutic modality independent of immune effector cells. Two different ADC formats of IL-1RAP targeting antibody (ADV58) were produced via conjugation to monomethyl auristatin E (ADV58-MMAE) or deruxtecan (ADV58-DXd) and successfully applied against solid tumor cell lines and xenograft models .
Despite the advancements in the development of novel targeted delivery systems for myeloid leukemia in the last years (see and ), to our knowledge, no versatile delivery system has been reported targeting IL-1RAP. The aim of the present study is to create a potent delivery system for the selective transport of therapeutic cargoes by targeting membrane isoform of human IL-1RAP (hmIL-1RAP hereafter) on the surface of myeloid leukemia cells.
The entrance of membrane-impermeable therapeutic materials mostly depends on the endocytic pathway. To the best of our knowledge, there is no convincing evidence indicates that targeting hmIL-1RAP triggers endocytosis; thus, following binding, the therapeutic cargo may not necessarily or efficiently enter the target cell. To address this problem, we designed a bispecific delivery agent with a cell-penetrating property consisting of an anti- hmIL-1RAP single-chain variable fragment (scFv), a monomeric streptavidin (mSA), and a nine-residue TAT peptide from the HIV-1 transactivating protein (hereafter, TAT). The “biotin-binding site” of mSA could serve as a simple and efficient loading site for any biotinylated cargo (cytotoxic agents, nucleic acid complexes, proapoptotic mediators, etc.), and TAT, one of the most powerful and most studied cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), enables cellular internalization through the induction of macropinocytosis in mammalian cells – . We hypothesized that upon binding to the target cell via the scFv, TAT can persuade the entrance of the whole fusion and its cargo to the target cell.
Macropinocytosis is a highly regulated, energy dependent, actin-mediated, and signal-dependent cell internalization pathway in which bulky guzzles (extracellular fluid, particles and even cells, and pathogens) can enter living cells following the formation of apparently uncoated vesicles named macropinosomes . These vesicles have two different fates depending on the cell type: being sent back to the plasma membrane or being matured to late endosomes and endolysosomes . These organelles have an intraluminal pH of 6.0–4.9 and provide an appropriate environment for the activation and function of digestive enzymes .
Entrapment in endosomal compartments and subsequent enzymatic degradation by acid hydrolases is the major limiting bottleneck for the ultimate function of therapeutic cargos that pass the first impediment (internalization step) . To date, numerous natural and synthetic endosomolytic agents have been studied to overcome this obstacle , . Destabilization of the vesicle lipid bilayer by fusogenic peptides is a nearly safe and practical approach, especially for in vivo purposes , . Accordingly, we incorporated a newly discovered human-derived fusogenic peptide, S19 32, upstream of TAT to maximize the endosomolytic capability of our system. S19 is a beta-sheet forming peptide that has been suggested to induce TAT dimerization for endosomal escape . This peptide has also recently been applied for enhancement of siRNA delivery in two different protein-based carrier fusion proteins . The concept of the study is presented in Fig.  .
 We first performed an in silico analysis of the abovementioned fusion protein along with another similar construct (consisting of anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv, tetrameric core streptavidin (tSA), TAT, and terminal endosomal escape pentapeptide ). Then, we focused on the feasibility of bacterial production of these biomacromolecules. Finally, the physicochemical properties of the produced fusion protein were characterized in vitro.

## Results

## Design and in silico analyses of constructs
The sequence of the scFv used in this study was obtained from a whole antibody that evolved against a 12-amino acid sequence (351–362) located in juxtaposition with the transmembrane segment of hmIL-1RAP (Fig.  ) and is absent in soluble or secreted isoforms of human IL-1RAP (hsIL-1RAP). The amino acid sequences of the scFv domains and other proteins and peptides employed for the construction of the fusions are presented in Table  (also see the Supplementary Information).
 The two different antibody‒streptavidin (scFv‒SA) fusions were designed as shown in Fig.  a, b (the full sequences of the fusions (GenBank accession numbers: PQ417141 and PQ417142) are offered in the Supplementary Information). A simplified representation of the fusions (without terminal peptides) is shown in Fig.  c, d.
 In the next step, homology modeling was performed on the fusions to study their behavior in silico. The extracellular portion of hmIL-1RAP was also subjected to modeling because all existing crystallography-based 3D structures of hmIL-1RAP lack the 12-aa target peptide adjacent to the transmembrane domain. For each protein, three models were generated by GalaxyRefine, among which the best models (the first) were selected for further analyses. Figure  a&b shows the 3D structures of the fusions predicted by GalaxyWEB. The qualities of the modeled structures were validated by three different programs and found to be reliable (Supplementary Table ).
 To study the dynamic properties and stability of the fusion materials, molecular dynamic simulation (MDS) was performed for 100 ns. The root mean square deviations (RMSDs) of the Cα atoms of the entire fusion proteins were calculated and plotted to compare the overall spatial deviations of the proteins’ atomic positions from their initial conformations over the course of the simulation. The backbone RMSD plots of both designed structures of fusion-1 and 2 showed that the trajectories became stable after almost 35 ns of MD simulations (Fig.  c).
The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) plots of the fusions revealed lower fluctuations in fusion-1 on average (Fig.  d). Therefore, it seems to have greater conformational flexibility. In both fusions, the C-terminal sequences presented the greatest residue fluctuations. In particular, the C-terminus of mSA and the S19 peptide (residues 371–396 in fusion-2) are highly fluctuating region based on the simulation results. In addition, the TAT sequence possesses high fluctuation dissimilarity in its residues.
The radius of gyration (Rg) plot indicates the compactness of the modeled structures during simulation. The Rg plots of fusion-1 and 2 indicated that the structures reached stable and compact conformations after 15 ns and 50 ns of simulation, respectively (Fig.  e). The fusion-2 obviously exhibited lower gyration (~2.5 nm in comparison to 2.6–3 nm in fusion-1) and therefore more compactness after the ~50th ns. This observation is in accordance with the fact that tSA in fusion-1 needs tetramerization for complete folding. Both types of fusion reached the equilibrium state after 60 ns, indicating that additional simulation time was not needed.
To evaluate the interaction between the scFv portion of the designed fusion protein and the hmIL-1RAP molecules and the streptavidin: biotin interaction in the fusions, the 3D structures of the proteins were first subjected to energy minimization via the GROMACS package 2020.3 and then molecular docking via the HADDOCK tool. The data obtained from the docking of the complexes (Fig.  f&g) are presented in Table  .
 The complexes were analyzed to determine the interaction patterns of the proteins with hmIL-1RAP via LigPlot software. LigPlot analysis revealed eight H-bonds in both the fusion-1:hmIL-1RAP and fusion-2:hmIL-1RAP complexes (Fig.  h&i). In addition, the number of electrostatic bonds in the fusion-1:hmIL-1RAP was two. The criterion for hydrogen bonding was a 3.5 Å distance between the electron donor atom and the hydrogen of the electron acceptor atom. The data from LigPlot analysis of the fusions complexed with biotin are presented in Supplementary Fig. a&b.

## Bacterial production of the fusions

## Efforts to produce fusion-1 in active form were unsuccessful
Efforts were first focused on the production of tetrameric fusion products because of the higher binding affinity of tSA toward biotin and the theoretically greater avidity of fusion-1 toward target cells (multivalency).
We first tried to obtain active tetramers from the fusion-1 encoding construct. The construct was codon optimized for E. coli, artificially synthesized, and subcloned in a periplasmic expression vector, pET26b(+) immediately after and in frame with the pelB leader sequence (by Biomatik, Canada). Fusing with pelB facilitates the transport of the translation protein into the periplasmic space of the host via the Sec secretion system. The confirmed construct was transformed into the chemically competent BL21(DE3) strain.
The transformants were cultured and induced (0.1 mM IPTG, 30 °C, ON). Three fractions were analyzed by SDS‒PAGE and WB. The protein was not expressed under these conditions. Therefore, varying IPTG concentrations were tested (0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 mM). The turbidity of the culture was diminished or disappeared in higher induction strengths, implying strong toxicity to the host, possibly due to the high affinity of the fusion for intracellular biotin.
To overcome the toxicity, several conditions were tested, including the use of a more enriched (TB) or controlled glucose-releasing (EnBase) medium instead of LB, induction at higher OD600 values, or the use of the Bl21(DE3)pLysS strain (instead of Bl21(DE3)). However, no expression was detected in any of the mentioned conditions by WB. More experiments revealed that induction at a lower temperature (20 °C) can efficiently inhibit toxicity, and periplasmic expression was detected at this temperature by the addition of excessive amounts of biotin to the culture medium (Supplementary Fig. S2).
As expected, the fusions expressed under these conditions were saturated with free biotin and could not be purified by iminobiotin resin. Further analyses of the periplasm and cytoplasm of the induced cells revealed that the expressed proteins were mostly produced as IBs (another possible reason for the failures in purifications).
We next focused on refolding cytoplasmic IBs. When the pET28a vector was used, the fusion gene was highly expressed under the all tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3) but was expressed entirely in IB form. Frequent failures in in vitro refolding of IBs have led us to attempt to improve the in vivo folding of the protein. To this end, two strategies were employed: the coexpression of molecular chaperones and the use of a potent solubility partner, IF-2. We subcloned the construct in pET28a at the NdeI/XhoI sites to express the fusion with the N-terminal His-tag. We designed an experiment in which the SHuffle strain was transformed with chaperone plasmids from Takara to determine the optimal conditions. The results showed that two chaperone plasmids, pGTf-2 and pGTf-16, were equally effective at improving the solubility of the fusion protein in the SHuffle cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. S4). However, NiNTA purification of the induced SHuffle/pGTf-2 culture showed that the majority of the solubly produced protein was monomeric (in contrast to the WB results of small-scale experiments).
To maximize soluble expression and achieve functional fusion, we used the IF-2 solubility partner, which has been successfully employed for soluble expression of tSA in an active state and has been shown to be even more effective than the well-known MPB and NusA . IF-2 was PCR amplified from the E. coli genome and then introduced upstream of the N-terminal 6xHis-tag of the construct at the NcoI site (Supplementary Fig. S5a). The addition of IF-2 led to the complete folding of the IF-2-tagged fusion protein in the cytoplasm of BL21 (DE3) cells, as no IB was observed in the total cell fraction of the induced culture (Supplementary Fig. S5b&c). Further analysis of the expression samples via WB revealed a high-intensity band corresponding to the IF-2 fusion protein in BL21(DE3) cells (Supplementary Fig. S5d). However, the unsuccessful iminobiotin purification of the culture induced under these conditions indicated that the fusion was still completely blocked by intracellular biotin.
Overall, although the fusion protein was correctly folded into a tetramer in the E. coli cytoplasm, it was not able to bind to biotin and could not be used in future assays. In vitro refolding of purified 6xHis-tagged fusion products via different methods was also completely ineffective, and a tetrameric structure could not be formed under any of the tested conditions.

## Fusion-2 was efficiently produced in E. Coli and obtained in an active form
Similar to fusion-1, we first used the same periplasmic expression vector (pET26b(+)) for the production of fusion-2 (monomeric fusion). Unfortunately, the purification of the recombinant protein from the periplasm of the induced cultures was unsuccessful.
We subcloned the construct in pET28a (at the NcoI/XhoI restriction sites) in frame with a C-terminal 6xHis-tag. Despite the high expression level (as IBs), the protein could not be captured by NiNTA resin and did not show reactivity in WB with an anti-6xHis-tag antibody. We hypothesized that the problem is associated with the TAT peptide. At pH ≥ 7 (a suitable pH for the binding of a 6xHis-tag to NiNTA), histidine residues are negatively charged and can strongly bind to positively charged TAT peptides (instead of NiNTA). A high proportion of dilution-refolded protein samples from this construct were in the form of soluble aggregates (Supplementary Fig. S6), and the monomeric form was less than 17% of the preparation.
In the next step, we subcloned the fusion-2 encoding DNA in pET32a to overcome the aggregation (with the help of Trx-tag) and the purification problems (using an N-terminal 6xHis-tag instead of a TAT-neighboring C-terminal 6xHis-tag). With this system, the protein was expressed in both soluble and insoluble forms. Although the solubly expressed protein showed high biotin-binding activity in ELISA (Supplementary Fig. S7), a substantial proportion of the protein content in this preparation was in the truncated form (Supplementary Fig. S8).
On the basis of a previous study , we hypothesized that the upstream Trx may be the origin of this truncation. Hence, we deleted the upstream Trx via NdeI digestion. The resulting construct (6xHis-tag-scFv-mSA-S19-TAT, GenBank accession number: PQ417143) was named S19+ hereafter. A shorter fusion (named S19−) was also created as a control protein after the BamHI-assisted deletion of the S19-encoding fragment (Fig.  a&b).
 Both fusions (S19+ and S19−) were efficiently expressed in BL21(DE3). The IBs isolated from both constructs showed high purity (Fig.  c), and the NiNTA purification step did not improve their purity before and after refolding. Therefore, the fusions were finally obtained by dilution-refolding without a pre- or postrefolding purification step. The removal of Trx and the bioprocess shift toward IB formation dramatically reduced the truncation of the produced proteins (compare the purities of the fusion bands in Fig.  c with those in S8).
The produced proteins exhibited an apparent MW of ~45 kDa, which was confirmed by WB (Fig.  d). The refolding recovery rate of S19+ fusion was considerably greater than that of S19− (Supplementary Table S2). Substantial amounts of visible aggregates were observed during the refolding of S19− fusion (after the first dialysis step).

## Biophysical characterization of the produced fusions

## In silico analyses
The biophysical parameters of the produced fusions were computed via online tools. The results from the in silico predictions are presented in Supplementary Table S3. The proteins possessed identical isoelectric points (pIs), but the calculated pI values were different in different programs. The half-life in mammalian reticulocytes was estimated to be quite long for both fusions. The instability index for both fusions is < 40, so the fusions are classified as stable. The aliphatic index (defined as the relative volume occupied by aliphatic side chains) obtained from ProtParam indicated that the fusions are relatively thermotolerant. The negative GRAVY score shows that the fusions are hydrophilic. On the basis of the scaled solubility values, the fusions are less soluble than average soluble E. coli proteins, and S19+ has higher solubility than S19−, which is in agreement with the refolding results. As expected, the produced fusions were predicted to be probable antigens but not allergens.

## Binding activities
The binding activity of the produced fusions was evaluated via ELISA. The fusions exhibited comparable signals in cell-based ELISA using different leukemic cell lines (Fig.  a). The significantly weaker signal from KG-1 cells (low hmIL-1RAP expression) in the S19− fusion ELISA suggested that at least a proportion of the binding events were target–specific. However, the signal values were considerably greater than those obtained via ELISA with the same concentrations of anti-IL1RAP scFv (a molecule without mSA-S19-TAT) produced via a similar process (Fig.  b).
 In the absence of AML serum, all the proteins exhibited high binding activity. However, after exposure to AML serum, the binding activity of the control fusion (without the scFv moiety) was completely lost. In contrast, S19− and S19+ remained active (Fig.  c).
The biotin-binding ability of the fusion proteins was also demonstrated by the use of a biotin-coated plate. The produced fusions showed similar binding affinities toward biotinylated BSA coated on the wells (Fig.  d).
To investigate the ability of fusion proteins for simultaneous binding to the tumor antigen and the biotinylated molecule and also to confirm the specific binding of the fusion proteins to hmIL-1RAP, a different ELISA was planned and implemented in wells coated with purified antigen (hmIL-1RAP-specific 12-aa peptide). As explained in the Supplementary Information, thioredoxin (Trx), encoded by the pET32a vector, was employed as a solubility partner for bacterial expression of this short peptide in the cytosol of E. coli. The purified proteins, Trx-12aa and Trx (as a control), were used for coating of the wells. As presented in Fig.  e, both produced fusions (S19− and S19+) could significantly bind to Trx-12aa. The isotype control (Trx-mSA-S19-TAT) did not significantly bind to the Trx-12aa fusion protein.

## The presence of the S19 peptide in the designed vehicle significantly enhanced its membrane lytic activity
The ex vivo leakage assay is a simple method for studying membrane interaction-based (but not osmotic lysis-based) endosomal escape, and RBCs are a physiologically more relevant model than liposomes are. Hemolysis assay can be conducted at different pH values to evaluate the endosomolytic potential of peptides and particles. To assess the membrane lytic activity of the S19 peptide in our construct, we incubated the RBCs with fusions only at physiological pH because the S19 sequence had previously been shown to act in a pH-independent manner and does not need acidification of the endosome to trigger lysis of the vesicles. Considerable hemolysis occurred following 2 h of incubation of human erythrocytes with 250 µg/ml fusion protein. The membrane lytic activity of the S19+ fusion protein was 2-fold greater than that of the S19− fusion protein (Fig.  ).
 To confirm the cell internalization of our delivery system and assess the intracellular trafficking of the S19+ fusion protein, K-562 cells were treated with ~500 picomoles of fusions for 3 h, and a fluorescent anti-6xHis-tag Ab was used to probe the localization of the fusion protein inside the leukemic cells. As shown in Fig.  a, the fusion effectively introduced the cells and escaped from the endosomal vesicles.
 We then assessed the ability of our system to achieve the intracellular delivery of large macromolecules. To this end, K-562 cells were coincubated with S19+ and a fluorescently labeled anti-6xHis-tag Ab and then visualized by confocal microscopy in the living state. The results showed that S19+ was able to deliver 150 kDa cargo across the cell membrane and facilitate its endosomal release. Despite the diffusion of green fluorescence in the cytosol, more stained vesicles were observed than in the previous test (Fig.  b).

## The produced fusions showed no cytotoxicity toward normal human cells
Normal (HEK293T) and leukemic (K-562) cell lines were separately treated with different concentrations of both fusions. No reduction in cell viability/proliferation was detected in normal cells via the MTT assay. However, both fusions had moderate toxic effects on K-562 cells.

## Discussion
Tetrameric streptavidin (tSA) and its analogues are excellent adaptor proteins that possesses unique potential for versatile, fast, and multivalent linking of several functional domains in a single biomacromolecular assembly for different applications . The N- and C-termini of tSA can be fused with other peptides or proteins . Fusing with affinity ligands leads to tetravalent presentation and dramatic enhancement in the binding avidity of the ligand . These fusions can also bind to four similar or different biotinylated molecules with very high affinity . In addition to their inherent stability under harsh conditions and proteolytic degradation, these materials are excellent candidates for the targeted delivery of therapeutic payloads in vivo . In the past, scFv-tSA fusions have been employed in preclinical and clinical studies for pretargeted radioimmunotherapy of blood malignancies . The tSA molecule has also been used for the fabrication of multivalent CPPs to improve their cellular internalization . For example, bacterially produced TAT-tSA fusion protein was successfully applied to increase the transfection efficiency of biotinylated polyplexes . Multivalency is also one of the strategies for enhancing the endosomal release of CPPs .
The N- and C-termini of tSA (and mSA) in correctly folded molecules are near each other. This provides an opportunity for neighboring presentation of tumor-targeting agents and CPPs so that the attachment to cell surface receptors brings the CPP in close proximity to the plasma membrane. This can potentially enhance the effectiveness of cell penetration. Such a biomolecular design requires in silico assessments to investigate the probable interference and steric hindrance of both terminally fused domains. The bacterial expression of such a complex could also be challenging owing to its high molecular weight and multidomain entity. The use of other organisms as expression hosts is not without difficulty. For example, cytosolic expression in eukaryotic systems (mammalian cell lines or yeast) can impose severe toxicity on the host organism due to the depletion of intracellular biotin. Moreover, production in secretion mode is not appropriate because the high concentration of free biotin in culture media irreversibly blocks the tSA moiety. The production of avidin fusions in eukaryotic systems seems impossible under normal conditions . The production of active fusions requires the development of stably transfected cells and long-term adaptation in biotin-depleted culture medium, which is a timely and labor-intensive process.
In the present study, two different transducible scFv-SA fusions with cell-penetrating and endosomolytic potential were designed and analyzed in silico. Both fusions were found by model validation studies to be possible structures, and the performed assessments revealed their stability and binding capability. The protein domains did not interfere with each other in silico, and the binding affinity values for biotin and the leukemic marker were predicted to be high enough.
In contrast to better presentation in silico, the bacterial expression of fusion-2 was very challenging, and the efforts to produce the complex via different approaches were completely disappointing. The monomeric fusion, however, was efficiently produced by in vitro refolding of the IB produced in E. coli. The optimized procedure reported in this paper for bacterial production of the vehicle is efficient, cost-effective, and scalable.
Although the fusions were expressed at comparable levels, the overall yield of S19+ fusion was much greater. The presence of the S19 peptide reduced the aggregation propensity of the protein for unknown reasons. The production yield of the final mSA fusion reported here is very high. The maximum productivity of mSA obtained via in vitro refolding of IBs from E. coli has been reported to be approximately 3 mg of purified protein from a 1 L culture .
Although mSA has lower affinity and avidity, it offers simpler bioprocessing than tSA does and prevents the system from aggregating. Moreover, the mSA theoretically confers a lower possibility of spatial hindrance to protein domains and presents better tissue penetration due to its smaller size.
We chose the hmIL-1RAP molecule as a target because it is expressed on the surface of both malignant myeloid LSCs and primary blasts but not normal HSCs. The protein has been shown to be a strong discriminating biomarker for monitoring minimal residual disease (MRD) and predicting AML relapse . The antibody we used in the construction of our scFv fusion was developed against the membrane-anchored isoform of IL-RAP and therefore cannot be blocked by the high plasma levels of hsIL-1RAP that normally exist in healthy human blood circulation. hmIL-1RAP not only is an excellent target molecule for myeloid leukemia but also has been shown to be overexpressed (at the mRNA or protein level) or correlate with many other conditions and malignancies, including myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) , , hepatocellular carcinoma , the second most widespread bone tumor, Ewing sarcoma , the most prevalent and most progressive form of pancreatic cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) , , the most common type of malignant adult brain tumor, glioma , the difficult-to-treat and aggressive type of breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) , the most common form of lung cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) , , cervical cancer , and stomach adenocarcinoma .
To demonstrate the ability of S19− and S19+ molecules to bind to leukemic cells, we used a cell-based ELISA. Unexpectedly, high signal values were observed even in the KG-1 cell line, which has been shown to have weak expression of hmIL-1RAP. Indeed, no statistically significant difference in binding was detected between the cell lines in S19+ ELISA. On the basis of the lower signal values from ELISA with anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv in the same cell lines, we hypothesized that the signals from specific binding of scFv to hmIL-1RAP were buried in high signals generated from nonspecific binding events. To determine whether the obtained signals originated from only the specific binding of the scFv portion of our fusions to the tumor marker or other nonspecific interactions, we decided to perform another cell-based ELISA in the absence and presence of serum. TAT can theoretically bind to negatively charged cell membranes and membrane-associated proteoglycans (e.g., heparan sulfate proteoglycans) , . Serum proteins (especially albumin, which has a negative net charge of − 9 at physiological pH ) can theoretically attach to positively charged TAT sequences and prevent them from interacting with the cell membrane. On the other hand, the binding activity of the anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv is significantly inhibited in AML serum , possibly because hmIL-1RAP molecules are released from leukemic cells. Therefore, we decided to use human AML serum in the ELISA to determine whether the fusions can specifically bind to the target cells under such stringent conditions. In this situation, nonspecific binding from all the fusion sequences was eliminated because the control fusion (Trx-mSA-S19-TAT), which contains all the sequences except the scFv, completely lost its attachment and exhibited no statistically significant binding to the cells. Although the binding activity of the fusions decreased in the presence of AML serum, it was still significantly greater than that of the anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv (Fig.  c). These data showed that a substantial proportion of leukemic cell binding is receptor-specific and, more importantly, that the fusions remain active in the leukemic serum environment. However, it is not clear why signals from fusions were higher than those from the anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv. This may be attributed to better folding of the scFv moiety of the fusion agent or more exposed 6xHis-tag in the fusion agent than in the anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv molecule.
Based on confocal microscopy analysis, the produced fusion protein could effectively penetrate leukemic cells and escape from the endosome. Indeed, despite the presence of S19+ fusions in some vesicular compartments, the fusions were uniformly distributed in the cytosolic space of the cell (Fig.  a). The high fluorescence intensity of the leukemic cell nucleus demonstrates the efficient nuclear penetration of the vehicle, likely due to the presence of the TAT sequence, which is a well-known nuclear localization signal sequence. In contrast to S19+ fusion, control fusion proteins were mostly entrapped inside the endocytic vesicles, and their nuclear fluorescence was very weak.
The greater number of stained vesicles observed in the confocal analysis of living cells than in the analysis of fixed cells could have arisen from the lower concentration of fusion applied. The other explanation is that at the low pH of the endolysosome, the interaction between the S19+ protein and the fluorescent Ab was disrupted, and some Ab molecules remained entrapped in the vesicles. The lower fluorescence signal from the cell nuclei supports the latter assumption (see Fig.  b).
The lack of toxic effects on normal human cells is promising; however, there is no exact explanation for why fusions induce proliferation in HEK293 cells at relatively high concentrations (Fig.  ). It is also not exactly clear that why both fusions exhibit moderate toxicity (30–35% proliferation reduction) on K-562 cells. The observed toxicity could not be attributed to endosomal release because, based on the hemolysis and confocal microscopy data, the endosomolytic activity of the S19− fusion protein is much lower. One possible explanation is that these fusions spatially interfere with interleukin I (IL-1) signaling. Indeed, the binding of the fusion protein to the hmIL-1RAP coreceptor may hinder its binding to IL-1 receptor type-1 (IL-1R1) and/or IL-1β and prevent the formation of the IL-1 signaling complex. Consequently, the treated leukemic cells are deprived of the proliferative effect of this cytokine. Since the proliferation of normal HEK293 cells does not depend on IL-1β, these cells are not affected by the fusions. The observed toxicity could also be attributed to the membrane-disrupting properties of the TAT peptide. The fusions could strongly bind to hmIL-1RAP-overexpressing cells and allow the TAT sequence to be in proximity to the cell surface for some period of time. This could not occur in HEK293T cells because they do not overexpress the hmIL-1RAP. The evidence that supports both interpretations is that the control fusion (in which scFv has been substituted with Trx) under the same conditions showed no toxicity to K-562 cells (Supplementary Fig. S9).
 Emerging delivery technology for leukemia, and in particular myeloid malignancies, are mostly nanocarriers encapsulating chemotherapeutics, small molecule inhibitors, microRNAs (or other nucleic acids), or herbal medicines , , to change the administration route or improve the solubility, pharmacokinetics, and safety profile of their payloads . However, only a few formulations have been entered clinical trials or approved up to now (e.g., Marqibo®, DaunoXome®, Doxil®, and Vyxeos®). These examples are liposomal formulations of chemotherapeutic agents that present an easy manufacturing process, high capacity, and prolonged circulation lifespan that provide sustained release of encapsulated drugs, enhancing effectiveness and reducing the side effects of traditional chemotherapy . Other schemes employing inorganic nanoparticles (NPs), polymeric NPs, dendrimers, carbon-based nanomaterials, and functionalized multicomponent nanostructures, despite the great enhancement of bioavailability, biodistribution, and selectivity, are mostly at the experimental stage and seem to have a long way to translate into the clinic . Challenges associated with biodegradability, cost of synthesis, and difficulty in large-scale production in a consistent manner impede the clinical translation of these systems. Indeed, due to the multistep synthesis procedure, a tiny variation in each step can lead to dramatic alterations in the biophysical characteristics of the final product .
In comparison, the most prominent aspects of our proposed vehicle is its protein-only nature, which confers full biodegradability and enables easy, large-scale, reproducible, and economical manufacturing in prokaryotic expression systems. Moreover, its reasonably smaller size (less than 10 nm) allows efficient penetration to complicated sites such as the bone marrow microenvironment. The described platform could be applied for targeted carriage of very potent cytotoxic agents (such as drugs and toxins used in antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and immunotoxins (ITXs) respectively) that their delivery may be beyond the scope of encapsulating systems because of safety concerns. Our proposed fusion can potentially be used for re-targeting of oncolytic viruses toward leukemic cells to develop novel oncolytic virotherapy against leukemia. The resulting fusion (S19+) can also be a very suitable tool for straightforward decoration of biotinylated nanostructures to improve their cell specificity and uptake. Setting of tens or hundreds of S19+ molecules on a single NPs could dramatically augment its functions. Such an array has been reported for delivery of lipid-polymer hybrid NPs loaded with a heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) inhibitor to AML cells . The researcher coated the biotinylated NPs with a fusion protein consisting of anti-CD64 scFv and mSA, which led to 3.2-fold increase in the cell uptake compared with naked NPs.
The use of such a delivery system provides a unique avenue for the simultaneous administration of several different biotinylated therapeutic cargos (each targeting a specific antineoplastic pathway) to persistent quiescent LSCs (the main cause of relapse), particularly after chemotherapy-induced remission, to eradicate leukemia and achieve a permanent cure.
The specificity of the described platform could easily be altered toward other tumor-associated antigens by simply replacing the targeting moiety with other ligands.
Its efficient nuclear penetration capability makes the described fusion an appropriate candidate for ex vivo or in vivo delivery of locked nucleic acid (LNA) or peptide nucleic acid (PNA) for therapeutic or research purposes. For example, biotinylated PNA can be delivered to the nucleus of postmitotic cells for the purpose of transdifferentiation or quiescent human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells for genome editing or differentiation purposes.
Another potential application area of this platform is drug delivery to intracellular pathogens such as Leishmania and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
In conclusion, we reported here the design, production, and in vitro evaluation of a myeloid leukemia-directed protein-based multifunctional targeted delivery vehicle. We believe that the described carrier can pave the way toward targeted therapy of suspended tumors, especially AML and CML. In this study, our aim was to provide a proof-of-concept for the hypothesized functions of the protein-based carrier, without taking a mechanistic approach. The mechanism of action of each element in the vehicle has been well described in previous works. The potential shortcomings of our system are probable immunogenicity and in vivo blocking by high concentrations of biotin and hmIL-1RAP, which can be tested in animal studies and prevented by sensible strategies. We hope to conduct an in vivo study in the future to gain a better understanding of the safety profile of the proposed fusion protein as a delivery vehicle. This study will include investigating probable immunogenicity, toxicity, inflammatory responses, and off-target effects.

## Materials and methods

## Sequences
The sequence of the anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv was retrieved from a patent filed by Cellerant Therapeutics, Inc . For tetrameric fusion, the amino acid sequence of tSA (residues 13–133), and for monomeric fusion, the sequence of high-affinity engineered mSA was utilized. The TAT peptide was selected from the HIV-1 transactivator of transcription (residues 49–57). Pentapeptide GFWFG with its upstream linker was inspired by a previous study . The sequence of the S19 peptide was taken from human syncytin-1 (residues 322–340) as described previously .

## In silico evaluations

## Homology modeling and structural evaluation of the proteins
For hmIL-1RAP, the X-ray crystallographic structure (PDB ID: 4DEP, chain C) was retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB). The 3D structures of hmIL-1RAP and its fusions were generated by GalaxyWEB , . The best-ranked models were selected for further analyses. Model refinement was performed via the GalaxyRefine web server . The structural quality of the refined models was validated by ERRAT, verify 3D, and Protein Structure Analysis (ProSA) servers .

## Molecular dynamics study of modeled fusions
The stability and structural changes of the 3D structures of the fusions were studied by MDS for 100 ns via the GROMACS package 2020.3 with the AMBER force field (amber99sb-ildn) as described previously , . The MDS trajectory analysis, including RMSD, RMSF and Rg, was performed for the modeled proteins.

## Molecular docking study
The putative residues involved in tSA: biotin and mSA: biotin interactions were predicted by LigPlot (PDB ID NO: 4JNJ) software and dictated to HADDOCK to estimate the binding energy and proper mode of interactions. For the scFv: hmIL-1RAP interaction, the 35-aa residues (333–367) from hmIL-1RAP (HARSAKGEVAKAAKVKQKVPAPRYTVELACGFGAT ) were considered a putative binding site, and the scFv CDRs were predicted via the SAB Pred server. The best-ranked models of the complexes were subjected to PRODIGY and LigPlot for further analyses. The prediction of the free binding energy (∆G) and binding affinity (KD) of the complexes was implemented via the PRODIGY (PROtein binDIng enerGY prediction) web server (https://bianca.science.uu.nl/prodigy/cryst).
The interacting residues at the interfaces of the protein complexes were evaluated by LigPlot + v1.4.5 software considering several major molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions.

## Visualizations of in silico analyses
The results from the docking experiments and simulations were examined via VMD 1.9.3 software and visualized via PyMol.

## Biophysical and immunological predictions of final fusions
Solubility was predicted using Protein-Sol webserver . The biophysical parameters (length, pI, half-life, MW, instability and aliphatic index, and Grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY)) were computed by the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The pI was also calculated via SnapGene and Protein-Sol. To predict the antigenicity of the bacterially produced fusions, the webserver VaxiJen 2.0 (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) with a bacterial model at a threshold of 0.4 antigenicity was utilized. The allergenicity of the final fusions was predicted via the following online tools: AllerTOP v.2 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) and AllerCatPro 2.0 .

## Protein expression, inclusion body isolation, and refolding
Four Escherichia coli (E. coli) strains (BL21(DE3), BL21(DE3)pLysS, Origami B(DE3) and SHuffle® T7 Express), three expression vectors (pET26b(+), pET28a and pET32a) and different expression conditions were tested in this study, as mentioned in the section. The total cell fraction, soluble cytoplasmic fraction, periplasmic fraction, and high-purity IBs were isolated as described previously . For the preparation of the secreted fraction, the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation method was used (see Supplementary Information).
For the refolding of denatured IBs from the tetrameric fusion construct, several protocols inspired by different studies – were examined. The final monomeric fusions were recovered via pulse dilution refolding as described previously , followed by an intermediate dialysis step in 0.5 M urea, 25 mM imidazole, and 1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) and a final dialysis in the same buffer without any additive. ## Protein purification and concentration
All purifications were accomplished by a fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) system (BioRad) using iminobiotin (Sigma-Aldrich) or NiNTA (GE or Qiagen) affinity resins according to the protocols recommended by the manufacturers. To concentrate the purified proteins, an Amicon™ Ultra0.5 centrifugal filter with a 10 kDa cutoff (Merck, Germany) was used. For impure protein samples (cell lysate, for example), the Bradford assay was employed to estimate the concentrations. To determine the protein concentration of the pure samples, the Bradford assay and UV method were both performed in parallel.

## Cell culture
The normal epithelial cell line, human embryonic kidney (HEK293T), human chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cell line (K-562), and human AML cell lines (HL-60, KG-1, and THP-1) were obtained from the Iran National Cell Bank. All the cell lines were maintained in appropriate culture media supplemented with 10% FBS. HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s minimal essential medium (DMEM), and other lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

## Western blot (WB) analysis
WB was performed on the bacterial cell fractions, and the produced fusions according to the previously described method . Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated polyclonal anti-tSA antibody (Fitzgerald) and HRP-anti-6xHis-tag Ab were employed for the identification of fusion-1 and fusion-2 respectively.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for evaluation of the binding ability of the scFv portion of the fusion proteins
Two different formats of ELISA were implemented for binding analysis of the scFv portion of the final fusions; 1) Cell-based ELISA on different leukemia cell lines, which was accomplished as described previously . For ELISA with leukemia serum, a 59-year-old male AML patient with relapsed leukemia was selected as a volunteer before treatment initiation. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the “1964 Helsinki Declaration” and its later amendments and with the ethical standards of the Pasteur Institute of Iran research ethics committee (code of ethics: IR.PII.REC.1401.032). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient. 2) ELISA on wells coated with the purified fusion of the antigenic epitope (see the Supplementary Information for more information): a volume of 50 µl of pure antigenic epitope fusion or control protein (20 µg/ml diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) per well was used for coating. After overnight incubation in the refrigerator, the coating solution was aspirated, and the plate was washed with PBS and blocked with 2% BSA (200 µl/well) for ~4 h at 37°C. PBS (as a blank) or refolded fusions (at 50 µg/ml concentration) was added to the wells in quadruplicate (50 µl/well). After 2 h of incubation at 37°C, the plate was washed four times, and 50 µl/well of commercially available biotinylated IgG solution was added to each well. After 2 h of incubation at 37°C, the Ab solution was discarded, and the wells were thoroughly washed. For detection, HRP-conjugated streptavidin was added to the wells (50 µl/well), and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 45 min. The wells were washed four times with PBST (PBS plus 1% v/v Tween 20) and then with PBS before 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added. The reactions were stopped with sulfuric acid, and the plate was read at a wavelength of 450 nm via an Epoch microplate reader. The mean absorbance value of the blank wells was subtracted from the absorbance value of all the wells before the statistical analysis.

## ELISA for evaluation of the binding ability of the streptavidin portion of the fusion proteins
For evaluation of the biotin-binding activity of the produced fusions, biotin-coated plates were needed. To this end, bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) was conjugated with biotin (Sigma-Aldrich) via the zero-length crosslinker 1-ethyl-3-[3-dimethylaminopropyl] carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, CAS Number: 25952-53-8) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, CAS number: 6066-82-6). First, biotin, EDC, and NHS were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, CAS number: 67-68-5) and incubated for 2 h. The prepared biotin-NHS solution was slowly added to the BSA solution (dissolved in 0.1 M MES hydrate (CAS Number: 1266615-59-1)) to form carbodiimide bonds with the NH2 groups of BSA. The biotinylated BSA was coated on the wells (50 µl/well), and after washing, the wells were blocked (200 µl/well) for 2 h at RT. After washing, different concentrations (3–200 µg/ml, triplicate) of the fusion agents were added to the wells (50 µl/well). HRP-conjugated anti-His-tag antibody (1:1,000, 50 µl/well) was utilized for the detection of bound molecules. The process continued as described above.

## Immunofluorescence microscopy of fixed and living cells
To confirm cell internalization and visualize the intracellular localization of the fusion proteins, confocal microscopy was performed on fixed cells treated with the fusion proteins. To this end, K-562 cells with 95% viability were harvested and seeded on a 24-well plate at a density of 1.7 × 106 cells/well in 1 ml/well fresh medium (RPMI + 10% FBS). After the addition of ~0.5 nanomoles of anti-hmIL-1RAP scFv, S19−, or S19+ fusion agent, the plate was incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator and then overnight in the refrigerator. The treated cells were then transferred to 15 ml conical centrifuge tubes, washed with cold FACS buffer (4% v/v FBS in PBS) twice, and then fixed by resuspension in 1 ml of fixation buffer (4% v/v formaldehyde in PBS). After 15 min, the cells were washed as described above and permeabilized with 0.2% v/v Triton X-100 (10 min, at RT). The fixed permeabilized cells were blocked with FACS buffer (30 min, at RT) and then incubated with FITC-labeled anti-6xHis-tag monoclonal antibody solution (1:500 diluted in PBS) for 2 h at RT. The cells were washed and stained with DNA-binding fluorescent blue dye, Hoechst before imaging with a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal laser scanning microscope.
To investigate the ability of S19+ fusion to deliver macromolecular cargo to the cell cytosol, confocal microscopy was performed on living K-562 cells. The cells were harvested and resuspended in 1 ml/well of fresh medium (RPMI + 10% FBS). Then, 46 picomoles of the S19+ fusion protein was added to the cells. After 5 min of incubation at 37 °C, 5 µg of FITC-labeled anti-6xHis-tag monoclonal antibody was added, and the cells were transferred to a humidified incubator (37 °C for 2 h). The treated cells were thoroughly washed and resuspended in 1 ml of PBS containing Hoechst and Rhodamine B for staining of the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively. After complete washing, one drop of the cell suspension was placed on the slide for imaging.

## Hemolysis assay
Two-milliliter vein blood was collected from a healthy volunteer (one of the authors) in an EDTA-containing centrifuge tube. To confirm the fusogenic activity of the fusions (potential endosomolytic ability), red blood cells (RBCs) were separated and washed with PBS (800 × g, 5 min) two times. The fusion protein (250 µg/ml) was added to a V-shaped bottom 96-well plate (100 µl/well) in quadruplicate. Protein sample buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8) and Triton X-100 (1% v/v in dH2O) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. An equal volume (100 µl) of RBC suspension (2% v/v in PBS) was added to the wells, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C while shaking (50 rpm). After 2 h, the plate was centrifuged (800 × g for 10 min), and 150 µl volumes of the supernatants were carefully collected and transferred to a flat-bottom 96-well plate. The absorbance (Abs) of each well was measured spectrophotometrically (Epoch microplate reader) at a wavelength of 405 nm. The data are presented as percentages of the positive control:

## Cytotoxicity assay
A cell proliferation MTT assay was also conducted in quadruplicate to evaluate the potential cytotoxic effect of the produced fusions. HEK293T and K-562 cells were seeded in the wells of a 96-well plate. The cells were seeded at a density of ~5,000 cells/well in culture media supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h, the seeded cells were treated in quadruplicate with different concentrations of the fusions (2.5–80 µg/ml final concentration) serially diluted in the culture medium. Untreated wells and wells treated with 20% DMSO were considered negative and positive controls, respectively. We also included wells with only culture medium (without cells) to measure the background absorbance arising from the media.
At 48 h posttreatment, MTT reagent was added to all the wells at a final concentration of ~0.5 mg/ml, and the plate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The formazan crystals were solubilized by the addition of 100 µl solubilization solution (40% v/v DMF in 2% glacial acetic acid plus 16% w/v SDS, pH 4.7) . After 2 h, the absorbance of the wells was read at both 570 nm and 630 nm via an Epoch microplate reader. The Abs630 nm value of each well was subtracted from the Abs570 nm of the same well.
