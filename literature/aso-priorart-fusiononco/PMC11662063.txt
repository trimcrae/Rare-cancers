Preliminary research indicates that apoptosis evasion is a key factor in SS progression, primarily attributed to the overexpression of anti-apoptotic genes. However, the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon are still not fully understood. This study aims to investigate the factors responsible for apoptosis evasion, evaluate their potential as targets for anti-apoptotic interventions, and analyze their mechanisms in detail. Our findings reveal that tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) is upregulated in highly malignant SS. Through in vitro as well as in vivo functional analyses, we have demonstrated that, TYK2 significantly accelerates SS cells progression. Mechanistically, TYK2 activates STAT3, which promotes the expression of BCL2, an anti-apoptotic gene. Inhibition of STAT3 activation using specific inhibitors can disrupt the TYK2-enhanced expression of Bcl2, indicating that the TYK2/STAT3/Bcl2 axis is a key regulatory pathway mediating apoptosis evasion in SS. Furthermore, our investigation into the upstream regulation of TYK2 reveals that the fusion protein SS18-SSX enhances the transcriptional activity of TYK2 by binding to the promoter region of the TYK2 gene, thereby increasing its expression levels. Thus, the TYK2/STAT3/Bcl2 axis is a crucial mechanism through which SS18-SSX mediates apoptosis evasion in SS cells. In conclusion, our findings contribute to understanding how SS18-SSX-driven TYK2 expression mediates apoptosis evasion mechanisms and propose targeting TYK2 as a strategy to induce apoptosis in SS.

## Introduction
Synovial sarcomas (SS) are a subgroup of soft tissue sarcomas (STS), accounting for 5–10% of all STS cases (Gazendam et al. ; Riggi et al. ). They are considered high-grade sarcomas with a poor prognosis, with estimated 5 years and 10 years cancer-specific survival rates of 66% ± 1.6% and 56% ± 1.9% respectively (Sultan et al. ). SS primarily affects younger individuals, with half of all cases diagnosed before the age of 35, although it can affect patients ranging from 5 to over 80 years old (Vlenterie et al. ). SS is unique among STSs due to their ability to exhibit epithelial differentiation. Histologically, they are classified into three subtypes: biphasic (BSS), monophasic (MSS), and poorly differentiated SS (PDSS) (Stacchiotti and Van Tine ). The degree of differentiation is associated with the prognosis, with higher differentiation indicating a worse prognosis and greater malignancy. In the clinical management of SS, adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapies are commonly deemed efficacious due to the cells' general susceptibility to apoptosis prompted by these treatments (Eilber et al. ; Vlenterie et al. ). However, certain BSS cells may exhibit chemoresistance or experience disease advancement (Stacchiotti and Van Tine ). Presently, advancements in alternative therapeutic strategies for SS are advancing at a limited pace (Gronchi et al. ). Notably, targeted therapy utilizing tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like pazopanib has demonstrated promise in ameliorating the prognosis of SS patients (van der Graaf et al. ). Moreover, evidence suggests that combining CDK4/6 and HDAC inhibitors may enhance outcomes in cases of highly differentiated and aggressive SS (Jerby-Arnon et al. ). Conclusively, gene expression profiling in highly differentiated tumor cells can reveal candidate genes correlated with malignant potential, thereby facilitating the development of novel therapeutic approaches by targeting these genes.
Our research revealed that Tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), a member of the Janus kinase (JAKs) family, exhibited significantly elevated expression levels in BSS and MSS, while showing negligible expression in PDSS. Therefore, we propose that TYK2 is involved in the progression of SS. Follow-up studies confirmed that TYK2 facilitates malignant progression by enabling SS cells to evade apoptosis. Detailed mechanistic investigations determined that TYK2 circumvents apoptosis by activating STAT3 and enhancing the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2’s expression, a process distinct from the anti-apoptotic mechanisms found in other cancer forms (Grivennikov and Karin ; Leitner et al. ; Poli and Camporeale ; Yang et al. ). Based on these findings, we propose that inhibiting TYK2 may present a novel approach to trigger apoptosis and inhibit tumor growth in SS, thus offering potential new strategies for its therapy.
SS are characterized by a distinctive genomic feature: the pathognomonic chromosomal translocation t (X;18)(p11.2;q11.2) (Cyra et al. ; Jerby-Arnon et al. ). This translocation fuses the SS18 gene with one of the SSX family members, most commonly are SS18-SSX1 (SSI8::SSX1) and SS18-SSX2 (SSI8::SSX2), while less commonly is SS18-SSX4 (SSI8::SSX4). SS18-SSX1 fusion is accounting for approximately two-thirds of cases. SSX2 is the fusion partner in most of the remaining cases, while the SS18-SSX4 fusion occurs infrequently (Benabdallah et al. ; Hale et al. ; Isfort et al. ). Current research has suggested a potential link between the SS18-SSX fusion gene and apoptotic evasion in SS cells. Some studies have indicated that SS18-SSX may enhance Bcl2 expression (Jones et al. ). Despite progress in understanding SS18-SSX, many aspects of its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Nonetheless, preliminary evidence suggests that the modulation of Bcl2 expression by SS18-SSX may share mechanistic similarities with the regulation by TYK2, as revealed in our findings. Therefore, we hypothesize a potential association between SS18-SSX and TYK2. Our study is the first one to reveal the SS18-SSX can enhances the transcriptional activation of TYK2 through binding the promoter region of the TYK2 gene, then increasing TYK2’s expression levels. Consequently, the TYK2/STAT3/Bcl2 axis emerges as a key mechanism through which SS18-SSX mediates apoptosis evasion in SS cells. This discovery elucidates how SS18-SSX-driven TYK2 expression contributes to apoptosis evasion mechanisms, offering new scientific evidence for anti-tumor strategies that induce apoptosis in SS.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and cell lines
Human SW982 cell line was cultured with L-15 medium (sigma-Aldrich, USA), 37℃, in 100% air. Human HS-SY-II cell line was cultured in DMEM, 37℃, in 5% CO2. All medium were supplement with 10% fetal bovine serum and 200 µg/mL penicillin and streptomycin.

## Transfection by lentivirus and infection by siRNA
Lentiviral TYK2 shRNA, control shRNA (negative control), containing puro and GFP plasmids were utilized for in vitro human SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells experiments. Meanwhile we also used LvRNA containing puro plasmids to construct the TYK2 overexpression SW982 cells as well as HS-SY-II cells and the SS18-SSX1 overexpression HS-SY-II. After a 24 h transfection, cells were subjected to 0.5 µg/mL puromycin media for selection. These constructs with the most effective knockdown lentiviral shTYK2 construct, and TYK2 overexpression construct were screened and used in vitro.
shRNA targeting TYK2 Seq, shTYK2−1: 5’-GAGGCCATCATTCCGCACCAT −3’; shTYK2−2: 5’-TGGTATCACTCCTCCTTGCTT −3’.
siRNA targeting SS18-SSX (si-SS18-SSX, 5’-TGACCAGATCATGCCCAAGAA-3’) (Minami et al. ) is targeted to the co-fusion domain of SS18-SSX. 1 × 104 cells per well were infected with 100 pmol si-SS18-SSX using LipoRNAi™ transfection Reagent overnight, and change fresh medium the next day.
For the co-transfection cell lines, we first infected the cells with 100 pmol si-SS18-SSX for 6 h, then discarded the medium, and transfected the TYK2-LvRNA or SS18-OE plasmid for another 24 h. The plasmid transfection was using the Lipofectamine 3000.

## Lentiviral transduction
The full-length human SS18-SSX1 coding sequences tagged with 3 × Flag at the N-terminus were inserted into the pCDH-CMV-EF1-Puro plasmid. Then co-transfecting the HEK293T cells with our plasmid and the tool plasmid, psPAX2 and pMD2.G, to generated the lentiviruses. The virus was then transduced into HS-SY-II cells. Stable cell lines were then generated by selecting with puromycin, as described previously.

## Cells treatment with compounds
Stattic, also named as STAT3 inhibitor V (CAS 19983–44-9), which was prepared a 1 mM stock solution in DMSO first. 1 × 104 cells per well of TYK2 overexpression cells were first plated in 6-well plate over night and change fresh medium the next day. Then diluted the STAT3 inhibitor V by L-15 or DMEM (which contain 10% fetal bovine serum) to 1 µM, after treating the cells for another 48 h, harvested the cells’ protein and RNA.

## Western blot analysis
Once cells culture was finished, washed the cells with PBS, then used cell lysis buffer to collecte the cells and lysed for 15 min on the ice. Then, centrifuged the cell lysate at 4 °C at 12000 rpm for 20 min. Once centrifugation finished, collected the supernatant, and quantified the protein concentration using the BCA kit. Finally, 1 mg/mL protein samples were prepared using sample loading buffer.
When the protein samples preparing finished, subsequently separated using 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels. The electrophoresis conditions showed as below: first electrophoresis at 80 V for 30 min, then at 120 V until the loading buffer ran out of the gel. After electrophoresis, transferred the protein to a PVDF membrane at 240 mA for 100 min in the ice. After transfer, 5% non-fat milk TBS-T was used to block the membrane at room temperature for 2 h, then incubated with the anti-TYK2, anti-Bcl2, anti-SS18, anti-STAT3, anti-Caspase-3, anti-SS18-SSX, anti-PARP, anti-phosphor-STAT3 (P-STAT3), anti-Cleaved Caspase-3, anti-Bax, anti-Cleaved PARP and anti-β-tubulin antibody overnight at 4 °C. Next day, the member were visualized with goat anti-rabbit/mouse peroxidase-conjugated antibodies (1: 5000 diluted by 5% non-fat milk TBS-T) using the BiossECL Plus WB Substrate kit with Amersham™ ImageQuant™ 800 (AI800, GE, USA). We have repeated these WB assays at least three times, and used ImageJ software to analyzed the protein expression levels. The Phosphorylated proteins were run and detected on the same blot of unphosphorylated proteins by stripping.

## mRNA quantification by RT-qPCR
Cells were first plated 1 × 104 cells per well into 6-well plates, once the cells confluence reached to 80%, isolated the cells RNA using the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit, the tissue RNA was isolated by the RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit, then measured the concentration of RNA by the Nanodrop One (Thermofisher Scientific, USA), then reverse transcribed 1 µg RNA into cDNA, and performed Quantitative PCR in a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) by specific primers for hu-TYK2 (5’-GACAGTCCATGAGAAGTACCAAGG-3’, 5’-GTGGGGTCTCTAGACAGGAGTAAG-3’), hu-SS18-SSX1 (5'-CCAGCAGAGGCCTTATGGAT-3’, 5'-GGTGCAGTTGTTTCCCATCG-3’) (Cyra et al. ), hu-SS18-SSX2 (5'-CAACAGCAAGATGCATACCA-3’, 5'-TTTTGGGTCCAGATCTCTCGTG-3’) (Minami et al. ), hu-ACTIN (5’-CTTCCAGCCTTCCTTCCTGG, 3’-CTGTGTTGGCGTACAGGTCT), hu-BCL2 (5'-CGTGCCTCATGAAATAAAGATCCG-3’, 5'-CCCCAGAGAAAGAAGAGGAGTT-3’) synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and hu-GAPDH (5’-ATCATCCCTGCCTCTACTGG, 3’-TGGGTGTCGCTGTTGAAGTC) synthesized by Suzhou Genepharma Co.,Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Use the CFX Maestro Software to determin the relative gene expression and the graphpad prism 8 was used to analysis the data. We used hu-ACTIN gene or hu-GAPDH gene as reference genes for mRNA expression analysis.

## Detect the cell proliferation by cell cloney
Plated cells in 6-well plate at 1000 cells/well first, then cultured in L-15 or DMEM medium with 10% fetal serum at 37℃ for over 2 weeks. Once finished culture, washed cells with PBS, then fixed the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10–15 min at room tempreture. When fixed finished, washed the cells with water and add crystal violet ammonium oxalate solution (0.1%) dye for 15 min, then discarded the dye and wash the cell with water and dry for couple days and pictured by UPV.

## Detect the cell proliferation by CCK-8
Plated cells in 96-well plate at 5000 cells/well first, then cultured in L-15 or DMEM medium with 10% fetal serum at 37℃ for a certain period of time. Finished culture, added CCK-8 dye (1:10) to the cell supernatant, and keep culturing at 37℃ for 2 to 4 h, then collected the cell supernatant and detected the OD450 by Spark multimode microplate (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland).

## Measurement of apoptosis by annexin V staining
First plated cells at 1.5 × 105 cells per well into 6-well plates and incubate at 37 °C in L-15 or DMEM medium until cells grown to 80% confluence. Then, collected the cell supernatant, the detached cells in medium and the adherent cells and washed by cold PBS. Once washing finished, harvested these cells at 1 × 103 rpm centrifugal for 5 min, then used 1 mL binding buffer from Annexin V-Alexa Flour 647/PI Kit to resuspended the cells, and harvested the cells again at 1 × 103 rpm centrifugal for 5 min, then resuspended by 100 µl binding buffer which contain 5 µl APC-annexin V and incubated for 5 min at room temperature without light. Once incubation finished, 400 µl PBS which contained 10 µl propidium iodide were added to resuspended the cells, followed applied to two-color flow cytometry detection using a FACScan 5 (Becton–Dickinson, San Jose, CA). Then we used Flow Jo-V10 to analyze the data to discriminate between apoptotic versus necrotic cells.

## Immunohistochemistry
We used the rabbit or mouse monoclonal antibody to TYK2 (dilution 1:500), P-STAT3 antibody (dilution 1:150) and Bcl2 (dilution 1:150) to perform the immunohistochemical staining. The immunohistochemistry Images were acquired using Zeiss Axiolab 5 Microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, German). All tissue sections are from Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, has been supported by Hospital Ethics Committee (2022–03–019).

## Tumor cell injections
The animal experiments performed in this paper were approved by the Animal Center of The First Affiliated Hospital, Jiangxi Medical College, Nanchang University with IACUC policies (CDYFY-IACUC-202302QR071). Four-week-old male nude mice were got from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). 1 × 107 shNC, shTYK2−1 or shTYK2−2 HS-SY-II cells were carefully subcutaneously injected into the left axilla of each mouse (n = 7 mice per group). We measured the tumor volum every other day and calculated with the formula: tumor volume (mm3) = Volume = longth × Width2 × π/6. Harvested the xenograft tumors 5 weeks later, and the fluorescence imaging was used to show the tumor size by MILabs-U-OI7 (MILabs, The Netherlands).

## PCR and sanger sequence
We applied the direct sequencing of PCR products that spanned the breakpoint of the fusion genes to identify the SS18-SSX fusion transcripts. The SS18-SSX transcripts in SW982 cells’ as well as HS-SY-II cells’ cDNA were screened by PCR and then Sanger sequencing by Guangzhou Ruibio Biotechnology Co., Ltd using ABI3730xl. The primers we used for PCR here are listed as below: hu-SS18-SSX1 (5'-CCAGCAGAGGCCTTATGGAT-3’, 5'-GGTGCAGTTGTTTCCCATCG-3’), hu-SS18-SSX2 (5'-CCAGCAGAGGCCTTATGGAT-3’, 5'-TTTTGGGTCCAGATCTCTCGTG-3’).

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation and analysis (CHIP Assay)
Cells underwent CHIP beginning with cross-linking using 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, then quenched the reaction by 125 mM glycine. Subsequently, harvested the cells and washed by cold PBS, then resuspended and isolated nuclei by hypotonic lysis buffer. The nuclear pellet we got befor was then washed by cold PBS and used the SDS lysis buffer to resuspended the pellet. Resultant nuclear extracts were sonicated using a Q800R sonicator to yield chromatin fragments averaging 0.3 kb. Clarified chromatin lysate was obtained by centrifugation and then diluted tenfold with CHIP dilution buffer. The solution was pre-cleared with Invitrogen Dynabead Protein G or A, subsequently immunoprecipitation using an anti-SS18-SSX antibody or control IgG. DNA–protein complexes were precipitated using Dynabead Protein A or G, and used the extraction buffer to elute the DNA–protein complexes, then, used the Proteinase K and RNase A to reverse cross-links. Once the above steps have been completed, we used the QIAquick or MiniElute PCR purification kits to purify the DNA and followed by analyzing by CHIP-seq. The SS18-SSX DNA-binding motif’s significant enrichment was assessed using the MEME program, with methodological validity dependent on enrichment degree for DNA fragments bound by the SS18-SSX antibody. ## Results

## Elevated TYK2 promote SS progression
Our previous study demonstrated high TYK2 expression in 89% of SS tissues (Qin et al. ). To investigate its potential as a diagnostic biomarker or therapeutic target, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) on full tissue sections from two PDSS, four MSS, and six BSS. The results showed significantly stronger TYK2 staining in MSS and BSS, both associated with malignant SS development (Fig. A-B). This finding led us to focus on elucidating the role of TYK2 in SS progression. 
To further substantiate the role of TYK2 in SS cells, we employed shRNA-mediated knockdown of TYK2 expression or cDNA-mediated overexpression of TYK2 in two SS cell lines, SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells (Fig. A-D). Our results demonstrated that TYK2 knockdown significantly impeded cell proliferation as well as the colony formation capacity in both cell lines (Figs. C and E). Conversely, ectopic overexpression of TYK2 in SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells led to a marked increase in cell proliferation as well as colony formation abilities (Fig. D and F). These findings indicate that TYK2 presence significantly promotes SS cells growth. Moreover, in vivo xenograft tumor experiments in mice, TYK2 knockdown significantly suppressed HS-SY-II tumor growth (Fig. ), resulting in a substantial reduction in tumor volume (Fig.  G). Therefore, we conclude that TYK2 functions as an oncogene, actively contributing to the progression of SS cells.

## TYK2-driven SS progression hinges on the induction of apoptosis evasion
To elucidate the precise mechanism by which TYK2 promotes SS progression, we conducted a series of investigations. Notably, our anti-apoptotic assays revealed an obviously increased in apoptotic cell ratios in SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells following TYK2 knockdown (Figs. A and C). Conversely, TYK2 overexpression could led to a marked decrease in apoptotic cell ratios in these cell lines (Figs. B and D), suggesting that TYK2 is essential for the anti-apoptotic processes of SS cells. To further delineate the impact of TYK2 on the apoptotic pathway, we detected the expression levels of several apoptosis-related proteins. Our results demonstrated that TYK2 knockdown in SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells significantly upregulated the levels of cleaved-caspase-3 as well as cleavedPARP1 (Figs. E and G,), while TYK2 overexpression led to a significant downregulation of cleaved-Caspase-3 as well as cleavedPARP1 (Figs. F and H). To further verify above results, we used flow cytometry to detect the proportion of cells marked by cleaved caspase-3, indicative of apoptosis. The data exhibited a clear trend of increased cleaved caspase-3 proportion in TYK2 knockdown SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells (Fig. S3).Taken together, we conclude that the apoptosis evasion effect orchestrated by TYK2 may be a significant mechanism underlying its promotion of SS progression. 

## TYK2-mediated apoptosis evasion is contingent upon the regulation of Bcl2 expression via STAT3
While diverse mechanisms of TYK2-mediated apoptosis evasion have been identified in various cancer types, the precise downstream targets of TYK2 remain elusive (Hervas-Stubbs et al. ; Leitner et al. ). Therefore, elucidating these mechanisms in SS cells holds significant importance. Subsequently, we investigated the activation status of various pathways in TYK2 knockdown cells and observed a significant downregulation of STAT3 phosphorylation in both SS cell lines (Figs. A and C). This suggests that TYK2 contributes to STAT3 activation. To further explore their correlation, we overexpressed TYK2 and indeed observed a substantial upregulation of STAT3 activation (Figs. B and D). These findings align with previous reports indicating that STAT3 is a substrate of TYK2 and is regulated by its activation (Gamero et al. ; Krueger et al. ). The subsequent question we addressed was how TYK2-activated STAT3 modulates the apoptotic pathway. 
STAT3 activation has been reported to promote the transcription of anti-apoptotic genes, including BCL2(Li et al. ; You et al. ). Therefore, we investigated and found that TYK2 knockdown-mediated STAT3 inactivation caused a significant decrease in the expression of the Bcl2 (an anti-apoptotic protein), while the expression of the Bax (an pro-apoptotic protein) remained unchanged (Figs. A and C). Conversely, in the overexpression system, we observed that increased TYK2 expression and subsequent STAT3 activation led to a marked upregulation of Bcl2 (Figs. B and D). Furthermore, when we treated TYK2-overexpressing SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells with the STAT3 inhibitor V to blockade the activation of STAT, the protein expression levels of TYK2 and STAT3 were not impacted (Figs. E-1 and F-1). However, the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 were reduced and showed a positive correlation with the expression of TYK2. Additionally, the mRNA expression of BCL2 correlated with the levels of phosphorylated STAT3 (Figs. E-2 and F-2). Remarkably, during the evaluation of HS-SY-II tumor-bearing mice, a positive correlation was identified between the protein and mRNA expression levels of Bcl2/BCL2 and TYK2/TYK2 (both protein and mRNA, Fig.  G). Simultaneously, in the assessment of patient tumors, it was observed that the levels of phosphorylated STAT3 and Bcl2 aligned with TYK2 expression, exhibiting the highest levels in BSS, followed by MSS, and the lowest in PDSS (Fig.  H). This substantiates the premise that the co-expression of TYK2 and Bcl2 affects the malignant progression of SS, with the degree of STAT3 activation serving as the connecting factor between TYK2 and Bcl2 co-expression.
The aforementioned results indicate that the expression levels of Bcl2 are influenced by STAT3 activation, while TYK2 directly regulates the activation of STAT3. This establishes a regulatory axis involving TYK2/STAT3/Bcl2, which represents the mechanism by which TYK2 mediates apoptosis evasion (Fig.  I).

## SS18-SSX-mediated apoptosis evasion is dependent on TYK2
The fusion oncogene SS18-SSX is pivotal in advancing SS progression and maintaining its malignant phenotype, particularly in enabling apoptosis evasion. In our prior exploration of this mechanism, we have established the crucial function of TYK2 in mediating apoptosis evasion, with its expression pattern intensifying in parallel with the increasing malignancy of the SS phenotype. Consequently, we postulate a plausible correlation between the apoptosis evasion process driven by SS18-SSX and TYK2. Prior to examining their interrelation, it is imperative to ascertain the subtype and expression profile of SS18-SSX within the utilized cell lines. Following reverse transcription of the cells' mRNA into cDNA, we amplified DNA fragments encompassing the SS18-SSX1 and SS18-SSX2 fusion sites through PCR, subsequently followed by sequencing for identification (Fig.  A-1). The findings disclosed that while both SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells were susceptible to amplification with primers targeting SS18-SSX1 or SS18-SSX2, only the PCR product targeting SS18-SSX1 in HS-SY-II cells exhibited perfect alignment with the target sequence, whereas the SS18-SSX2 PCR products encompassed a mixed array of DNA sequences. Conversely, in SW982 cells, only the SS18-SSX2 PCR product exhibited perfect alignment with the target sequence, not SS18-SSX1 (Fig.  A-2/3), indicating that the fusion gene in SW982 cells is type II, whereas HS-SY-II cells correspond to type I. Building on these results, we progressively unveiled the association: SS18-SSX knockdown resulted in the inactivation of STAT3 and a reduction in Bcl2 expression (Figs. B-C), resembling the pro-apoptotic effect (Figs. B-C). Moreover, we noted a substantial decrease in sensitivity to the Bcl2 inhibitor following SS18-SSX knockdown, implying that the presence of SS18-SSX is likewise essential for targeting Bcl2 (Figs. A-B). This led us to propose a mechanistic connection between SS18-SSX and TYK2, meriting further investigation. 
To further delineate the upstream and downstream relationship between SS18-SSX and TYK2, we enhanced TYK2 expression in SS cells with either silenced or intact SS18-SSX (Figs. A-B), subsequently observing the resultant impact on apoptosis. Regardless of the SS18-SSX levels, TYK2 overexpression led to augmented activation of STAT3 and elevated Bcl2 expression in both SW982 cells and HS-SY-II cells (Figs. D and F). Even when SS18-SSX knockdown triggered TYK2 downregulation, resulting in decreased STAT3 activation and Bcl2 expression, replenishing TYK2 through overexpression restored STAT3 activation and Bcl2 expression levels akin to those in wild-type cells with TYK2 overexpression (Figs. D and F). These findings suggest that TYK2 levels are pivotal for STAT3 activation and Bcl2 expression, while SS18-SSX functions as an upstream regulator of TYK2 expression. Moreover, functional assays related to apoptosis demonstrated that supplementing TYK2 in SS18-SSX knockdown cells resulted in analogous ratios of apoptosis and impaired cell proliferation as observed in wild-type cells overexpressing TYK2 (Figs. E, G and A-B). This further corroborates the concept that TYK2 is a central mediator in the evasion of apoptosis, with the anti-apoptotic effect induced by SS18-SSX being executed through TYK2 modulation.

## SS18-SSX facilitates the transcription activity of TYK2
The aforementioned mechanism has revealed that SS18-SSX functions as an upstream regulator of TYK2, and its anti-apoptotic effect relies on the expression level of TYK2. It is known that SS18-SSX often binds to DNA within the nucleus, acting as a transcriptional regulator. Consequently, an investigation was conducted to ascertain if there is a direct transcriptional regulation of TYK2 by SS18-SSX. In an SS18-SSX knockdown system, a decrease in TYK2 mRNA expression was observed corresponding to the reduction in SS18-SSX levels, aligning with the anticipated outcome (Figs. A and S7A). Following the observed mRNA expression patterns, we proceeded to characterize protein levels and found that the alterations corresponded closely with those at the mRNA level. With the downregulation of SS18-SSX, there was a concurrent decline in TYK2 protein levels, accompanied by a reduction in the downstream Bcl2 levels (Fig.  B). To further substantiate that this regulation is attributed to the fusion protein SS18-SSX rather than to SS18 alone, we overexpressed SS18 in control cells and noted no impact on the expression levels of TYK2, thereby leaving Bcl2 levels unchanged. More importantly, in SS18-SSX knockdown cells, the overexpression of SS18 failed to reveal any SS18-driven upregulation of TYK2, indicating the absence of a compensatory mechanism and affirming that the transcriptional regulation of TYK2 is exclusively governed by SS18-SSX (Fig.  B). Hence, we developed an expression evaluation system and observed that with the increased expression levels of SS18-SSX, there was a corresponding elevation in the mRNA levels of TYK2 (Fig.  C). Consequently, the protein levels were similarly augmented, along with an increase in the expression of downstream Bcl2 (Fig.  D). This robustly substantiates the stimulatory effect of SS18-SSX on the amplification of TYK2 expression levels. Furthermore, we sought to validate whether SS18-SSX directly engages in the regulation of TYK2 transcriptional activity. 
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments were employed to assess whether SS18-SSX directly influences the transcriptional activity of TYK2. The specific procedures, as depicted in Fig.  E, involved capturing and identifying DNA fragments within chromatin bound by SS18-SSX1 in HS-SY-II cells. The findings suggest that SS18-SSX predominantly binds to promoter regions of genes (Fig. B), with a notable enrichment of captured promoter sequences on chromosome 19 (Fig. S7C). Coincidentally, the promoter sequence of the target gene TYK2 ranks highly in terms of enrichment on chromosome 19 (All DNA Peaks listed in Tab. ), suggesting that SS18-SSX has the potential to bind to the TYK2 promoter sequence. To further verify any direct influence, the most enriched TYK2 promoter sequence bound by SS18-SSX on chromosome 19 (CCAATGGTGAC, −79-89 bp, rank of 1/1114) was inserted into a dual luciferase reporter system (Table  and Fig.  F). Concurrently, reporter systems were constructed with mutated versions of this sequence to assess the effect of the promoter sequence on the transcriptional activity of luciferase (Fig.  G-1). The results demonstrated that upon knockdown of SS18-SSX, only the transcriptional activity of the wild-type TYK2 promoter sequence bound by SS18-SSX was significantly downregulated, with no effect on the mutated sequences (Fig.  G-2). This indicates that SS18-SSX is a crucial factor in the regulation of TYK2's transcriptional activity.

## Discussion
SS are malignant mesenchymal tumors that commonly affect adolescents and young adults. The histogenesis of these tumors remains uncertain. Studies have indicated that different forms of the fusion protein, such as SS18-SSX1 and SS18-SSX2, are associated with distinct characteristics. SS18-SSX1 tumors exhibit a higher proliferative Ki67 index and tend to be BSS(El Beaino et al. ; Ren et al. ). Conversely, for SS18-SSX2 tumors showes a lower Ki-67 index, are more likely to be MSS. Researchers have also speculated that SS18-SSX4 may play a role in the formation of PDSS(Ladanyi et al. ). Despite the potential therapeutic significance of SS18-SSX as a unique molecular target, current attempts to neutralize its expression have been unsuccessful(Nielsen et al. ). Extensive research has demonstrated that the fusion protein SS18-SSX contributes to the evasion of apoptosis in cells, which has been thought closely associated with the malignant progression of SS. Therefore, the identification of the key factor responsible for the apoptosis evasion driven by SS18-SSX could lead to the development of novel therapeutic strategies for SS.
In transcriptional regulation, SS18-SSX interacts with the non-canonical PRC1.1, which affects the function of the SWI/SNF-KDM2B complex on unmethylated CpG islands. This interaction ultimately regulates the transcription of genes that promote SS progression(Banito et al. ; Benabdallah et al. ). Additionally, SS18-SSX plays a role in chromatin remodeling through SNF1 and promotes phase separation, contributing to SS progression(Cheng et al. ). There are reports suggesting that SS18-SSX drives the malignant phenotype of SS by transcriptionally activating the YAP/TAZ signal(Isfort et al. ) or the Wnt/β-catenin pathway(Trautmann et al. ). Remarkably, SS18-SSX not only facilitates transcriptional activation but also imposes an inhibitory influence on the transcriptional regulation of certain genes. For example, SS18-SSX redirects BAF complexes from enhancers to extensive polycomb domains, opposing PRC2-mediated repression and activating bivalent genes(Kadoch and Crabtree ; Li et al. ; McBride et al. ). Moreover, SS18-SSX has been found can serves as a conduit between ATF2 and TLE1, culminating in the repression of ATF2 target genes(Su et al. ). This information suggests that the regulatory role of SS18-SSX in gene transcription is not exclusively promotive but is multifaceted, meriting further investigation and elucidation. Overall, the primary mechanisms of SS18-SSX involve its impact on transcriptional signals in SS cells. This article delves into the direct positive regulation facilitated by SS18-SSX and explores its regulatory substrate, TYK2. We uncover that TYK2 undergoes direct transcriptional regulation by SS18-SSX. Subsequent functional validation reveals that the anti-apoptotic effect mediated by SS18-SSX hinges on its role in augmenting the transcriptional activity of TYK2 and is contingent upon the level of TYK2 expression. These findings offer an updated perspective on SS18-SSX-mediated apoptosis regulation and, for the first time, propose a comprehensive mechanism for the anti-apoptotic action of SS18-SSX via TYK2.
The SS18-SSX fusion gene expression is a common phenomenon in SS; however, as evidenced by clinical data, there is considerable variability in expression levels among patients. Analyzing this variability, especially the differences between the most common fusion forms, SS18-SSX1 or SS18-SSX2, can help predict different prognoses. High SS18-SSX expression is often associated with poor SS prognosis, indicating that the distribution of SS18-SSX levels is a critical indicator affecting SS treatment outcomes(de Bruijn et al. ; McBride et al. ; Nilsson et al. ; Ren et al. ). Furthermore, an article published involving clinical analysis of 243 SS patients definitively points out that differential clinical subtyping based on SS18-SSX1 or SS18-SSX2 is primarily completed in MSS and BSS subtypes(Ladanyi et al. ). In PDSS, SS18-SSX expression is negative or very low, suggesting a non-expression of this fusion gene in PDSS. This conclusion aligns with our findings that SS18-SSX mediates the promotion of TYK2 transcription and expression, allowing us to speculate that the lower expression level of TYK2 in PDSS might be due to the weak expression of SS18-SSX, indicating a trend of co-expression between them (Fig.  ). 
Previous studies have reported the involvement of TYK2 in cell apoptosis in various tumors (Ide et al. ; Leitner et al. ). However, our results first provide the evidence of TYK2 playing a crucial role in the apoptosis evasion mechanism specific to SS. In our mechanistic study, we have made a groundbreaking discovery that TYK2 plays a crucial role in mediating the effect of apoptosis evasion through the STAT3/Bcl2 axis (Fig.  ). This novel finding, combined with the observed changes in TYK2 expression in SS with varying degrees of differentiation, offers valuable insights for developing anti-tumor strategies focused on targeting TYK2 or inducing apoptosis via STAT3 and Bcl2. Currently, the primary treatment for SS remains induction or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, primarily utilizing doxorubicin and ifosfamide, followed by surgical resection (Eilber et al. ; Vlenterie et al. ). The contribution of chemotherapy to therapeutic efficacy is limited, as evidenced by the inadequate apoptosis of tumor cells mediated by chemotherapeutic drugs observed in clinical practice (Stacchiotti and Van Tine ). This indicates that apoptosis evasion is a significant challenge for chemotherapy regimens. Therefore, developing targeted strategies based on the key mechanisms revealed in this study, such as targeting TYK2, STAT3, or Bcl2, may produce a synergistic effect that enhances chemotherapy efficacy and improves the current low-efficiency clinical treatment outcomes. It is noteworthy that no drug targeting STAT3 has been approved to date, though promising candidates remain in clinical trials spanning Phases I, II, and III. However, SS has not been included as an indication in these trials (Hu et al. ; Zou et al. ). The Bc2 inhibitors’ development represents a significant focus in anti-tumor therapy research. The FDA approved the first selective Bcl2 inhibitor, Venclexta (ABT-199), in 2016, primarily for treating hematological malignancies (Ashkenazi et al. ; Forconi et al. ). Similarly, ongoing trials have excluded SS. This omission is largely due to the limited understanding of SS progression and mechanisms of action, coupled with its classification as a relatively rare cancer type. This study offers crucial evidence for the potential use of STAT3 or Bcl2 inhibitors in treating SS, paving a new direction for drug development. Overall, our innovative research work provides robust scientific evidence that expands the treatment options available for SS. It is important to note that currently, there are no clinically approved specific inhibitors for TYK2. Therefore, the development of new drugs specifically targeting TYK2 will be a crucial aspect of our future research goals.
