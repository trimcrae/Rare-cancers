## Abstract
The persistence and drug resistance of leukemic stem cells (LSCs) are major challenges in the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Ferroptosis, a novel form of programmed cell death, has emerged as a promising strategy for eradicating LSCs. This review provides a systematic analysis of LSC ferroptosis resistance and explores the interplay between iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense mechanisms. We propose a novel predictive model based on single-cell multiomics data that integrates iron homeostasis regulators (TfR1, GPX4, and FTH1) to assess the susceptibility of LSCs to ferroptosis. A key innovation of this study was the in-depth exploration of LSC ferroptotic heterogeneity and its interaction with the tumor microenvironment, shedding light on new approaches for precision AML therapy. Based on these findings, we introduced an innovative treatment paradigm combining ferroptosis inducers (e.g., erastin, RSL3) with immunotherapies (such as PD-L1 inhibitors and CAR-T cell therapy) to enhance LSC clearance and minimize measurable residual disease (MRD). This review fills a critical knowledge gap in the study of ferroptosis in LSCs, providing a theoretical foundation and translational insights for future AML treatment strategies. 
Keywords: Leukemia, Leukemic stem cells, Ferroptosis, Iron metabolism, Hematopoietic stem cells, Measurable residual disease, GPX4, TFR1, FTH1

## Introduction
Leukemia encompasses a heterogeneous group of aggressive hematologic malignancies characterized by clonal proliferation of immature myeloid progenitor cells within the bone marrow (BM) and peripheral blood. Despite significant therapeutic advances, chemotherapy and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remain the cornerstones of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) treatment [ ]. Apoptosis induction is the basis for conventional therapy [ ]. Recently, immunotherapy has reshaped the landscape of cancer treatment, demonstrating remarkable efficacy in cases of relapse or resistance to standard therapies [ , ]. However, owing to biological heterogeneity and differential immune responsiveness among leukemia subtypes, the clinical utility of immunotherapy remains limited.
An increasing body of evidence indicates that leukemia stem cells (LSCs) represent the ‘root’ cellular population responsible for sustaining disease progression, mediating therapeutic resistance, and driving relapse. These cells were first identified in AML [ ], constitute a rare subpopulation capable of self-renewal and differentiation into leukemic lineages [ ]. Similar to normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), LSCs maintain disease persistence, exhibit a heightened survival capacity, and demonstrate marked resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, thereby contributing to the persistence of MRD and disease relapse. Notably, increasing evidence indicates that LSCs generally exhibit resistance to ferroptosis, in contrast to bulk leukemic cells. This resistance, driven by robust antioxidant defences and metabolic adaptability, constitutes a major therapeutic challenge [ – ]. Notably, recent studies show that such resistance may be potentially reversible, providing a rational basis for novel therapeutic strategies.
Ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic form of regulated cell death, is mechanistically distinct from apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis and plays a critical role in cancer biology [ – ]. First described by Dixon et al. in 2012 [ ], ferroptosis is triggered by small molecules such as erastin and (1 S,3R)-RSL3, which either inhibit system Xc⁻ (the cystine/glutamate antiporter) or suppress glutathione peroxidase 4(GPX4) activity [ ]. Mechanistically, the inhibition of system Xc⁻ depletes intracellular glutathione (GSH), leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and lipid peroxidation-driven cell death [ , ]. Ferroptosis has been implicated in various human diseases including neurodegenerative disorders, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and cancer [ , , ]. Its role in leukemia remains unclear; nonetheless, accumulating evidence suggests that targeting iron homeostasis can induce differentiation and apoptosis in leukemia cells, indicating a promising therapeutic avenue [ – ]. Studies on solid tumors have revealed the crucial role of ferroptosis in regulating tumor cell survival, invasion, and resistance to therapy. However, the resistance of LSCs to ferroptosis and its underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Existing reports show that LSCs effectively evade lipid peroxidation-induced cell death by upregulating antioxidant defence systems, including GPX4 and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) [ ]. However, the precise molecular determinants and the reversibility of this resistance remain insufficiently elucidated. The aim of this review was to (i) systematically delineate the metabolic vulnerabilities and regulatory networks underlying ferroptosis resistance in LSCs; (ii) clarify the current knowledge gaps that constrain therapeutic translation; and (iii) propose a theoretical model for targeting ferroptosis as a novel strategy to eradicate LSCs.

## Biological and Functional Heterogeneity of LSCs
The concept of LSCs was first introduced in 1994 when Lapidot et al. identified a self-renewing leukemic cell subpopulation in AML [ ]. In subsequent studies by Bonnet and Dick, a hierarchical organization of AML was established, revealing that CD34⁺CD38⁻ primitive cells recapitulate leukemia in immunodeficient models [ ]. These findings indicate that LSCs serve as leukemia-initiating cells (LICs) that drive disease progression, resistance, and relapse [ – ]. The development of molecular biology techniques and the refinement of mouse transplantation models have revealed that LSCs are found in blood and in solid tumors, which are now commonly referred to as cancer stem cells [ , ].
Within the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment, LSCs coexist with normal HSCs in the CD34⁺CD38⁻ compartment, complicating their identification and therapeutic targeting [ , ]. The CD34 marker, widely expressed in HSCs, is frequently used alongside CD38 to delineate LSCs, as CD34⁺CD38⁻ cells exhibit primitive stem-like properties and resistance to therapy [ ]. The presence of CD34⁺CD38⁻ cells in patients with AML has been validated as a hallmark of LSCs, and higher frequencies of this population are closely associated with poor prognosis, increased drug resistance, and relapse [ , ]. In addition to AML, LSCs in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) reside within similar immunophenotypic fractions but exhibit subtype-specific variations in their molecular and metabolic adaptations [ – ]. Recently, surface markers such as TIM-3 and CD123 have emerged as specific to LSCs. TIM-3, an immune checkpoint receptor, is highly expressed in AML LSCs and facilitates immune evasion, thereby promoting leukemic cell survival [ – ]. TIM-3 mediates immune escape by interacting with its ligand, galectin-9, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates the persistence of leukemia. Studies on TIM-3 expression in CML and ALL are limited, preliminary evidence suggests that similar immune escape mechanisms may exist in these malignancies [ ].
Similarly, CD123 (IL-3Rα) is aberrantly overexpressed in LSCs, making it a promising therapeutic target [ – ]. Despite these advances, the ability of LSCs to evade conventional therapies remains a challenge. Their unique metabolic properties, enhanced DNA repair capacity, and interaction with the BM niche contribute to their persistence and therapeutic resistance [ , ]. For instance, LSCs exhibit metabolic plasticity, favoring oxidative phosphorylation over glycolysis, enabling their survival in hypoxic BM environments and resisting apoptosis [ ]. Furthermore, LSCs exploit interactions with niche components, such as stromal cells and cytokines, which provide pro-survival signals and protect them from chemotherapy-induced cytotoxicity [ , ].

## LSC Self-renewal and Maintenance of Stemness
Self-renewal is a hallmark feature of LSCs, allowing them to sustain malignant clones and drive leukemia progression. This process involves asymmetric division, generating progeny cells that retain stem cell-like properties with unlimited proliferative and multilineage differentiation potential while also producing more differentiated progeny. Aberrant enhancement or dysregulation of LSC self-renewal contributes to the continuous expansion of leukemic cells and underlies their capacity for therapeutic resistance and relapse [ ]. Similar to normal HSCs, most LSCs exist in a quiescent or hypoproliferative state, enabling them to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents that predominantly target rapidly dividing cells [ , ]. However, upon exposure to intrinsic and extrinsic cues, such as epigenetic modifications and transcription factor activation, LSCs can swiftly become dormant and re-enter the cell cycle, leading to disease progression [ , ]. This ability to remain latent within the BM microenvironment for extended periods and to persist after standard therapies significantly contributes to MRD persistence and subsequent disease relapse [ , ]. LSCs are highly responsive to environmental stimuli, including cytokines, adhesion molecules, and signals derived from the niche. When exposed to these stimuli, they initiate proliferative programs, generating new leukemic clones that mainly contribute to disease recurrence and treatment failure [ ]. A growing body of evidence suggests that several key surface markers are critical in maintaining LSC stemness (Table ), including CD123, CD33, CD96, TIM-3, CD44, CLL-1, c-MPL, MHDA2, CD25, and CD32 [ , , , , , ].
 The Wnt glycoprotein is abundantly expressed in the BM and substantially contributes to regulating self-renewal and long-term maintenance of normal HSCs [ ]. Within the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, β-catenin serves as a central mediator, facilitating transcriptional regulation of genes essential for cell proliferation and survival [ , ]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is aberrantly activated in LSCs, contributing to their enhanced self-renewal and leukemogenic potential [ ]. Moreover, synergistically activating the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/Akt pathways drives the transformation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells into LSCs, thus promoting leukemogenesis [ , ]. In AML, β-catenin plays a crucial role in initiating leukemia from HSCs or granulocyte–macrophage progenitors, whereas in CML, Wnt/β-catenin-related genes are also highly expressed, contributing to disease progression and therapy resistance [ , ]. At the transcriptional level, the β-catenin/TCF4 complex can directly occupy the GPX4 promoter and promote its transcription, thereby suppressing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [ ]. Nonetheless, the canonical Wnt downstream effector MYC can transcriptionally activate solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) (or upregulate it via the MYC–ZNF706 circuit), enhancing cystine import and the GSH–GPX4 axis, which further elevates the ferroptosis threshold [ , ].
In LSCs, the role of B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) family proteins (such as BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1) extends beyond inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis to encompass metabolic reprogramming. Substantial evidence indicates that LSCs maintain a metabolic profile characterized by low ROS levels and a strong dependence on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), which supports their long-term quiescence and self-renewal capacity [ – ]. Meanwhile, these proteins regulate mitochondrial ROS production and clearance, thereby reducing the baseline level of lipid peroxidation and raising the threshold for ferroptosis induction [ – ]. For instance, given the high reliance of LSCs on OXPHOS, inhibiting BCL-2 directly downregulates OXPHOS and eliminates quiescent LSCs. Conversely, elevated expression of BCL-2/BCL-XL/MCL-1 is associated with maintaining mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and respiratory chain activity, which shifts the intracellular ROS/lipid peroxidation baseline and consequently modulates ferroptosis sensitivity [ , ]. Therefore, LSCs are rendered more vulnerable to ferroptosis inducers [ ]. Notably, this process is often accompanied by decreased ROS generation, attenuating the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and reducing sensitivity to ferroptosis. Collectively, the BCL-2 family proteins play a pivotal role in sustaining the anti-ferroptotic phenotype of LSCs and other tumor cells. Their pharmacological inhibition (e.g., with venetoclax) can exploit this vulnerability to achieve the selective eradication of LSCs in clinical settings [ , , ].
The role of the Notch signaling pathway in HSC maintenance and differentiation is well established; however, its function in LSC biology remains controversial [ ]. In T cell ALL and chronic lymphocytic leukemia, Notch signaling is crucial for sustaining LSC survival and preventing differentiation [ – ]. Notch signaling has been proposed to act as an antagonist of granulocyte–monocyte progenitor (GMP) differentiation, and its dysregulation may shift hematopoietic differentiation toward leukemic transformation [ ]. LSC maintenance is regulated by a complex interplay between cellular surface markers, functional criteria, and molecular signaling pathways (Table ). However, the precise regulatory mechanisms that drive LSC self-renewal and stemness remain an area of active investigation. In the future, elucidating the specific context in which these pathways promote or inhibit leukemic progression is required to facilitate the development of targeted therapies aimed at disrupting the persistence of LSCs.

## Role of Epigenetic Inheritance in the Maintenance of LSC Stemness
The genome of LSCs usually has an aberrant DNA methylation pattern that silences certain genes that inhibit cell proliferation or promote differentiation, thereby maintaining the stem cell-like properties of LSCs [ ]. Common mutations in epigenetic regulators, including DNMT3A and TET2, are frequently observed in leukemia, and sustain LSC self-renewal and survival [ – ]. Notably, Six1 plays a crucial role in the maintenance and/or progression of leukemia. It provides a carbon source for leukemic cells via aerobic glycolysis [ ]. The promoter methylation of GPX4 and ACSL4 regulates ferroptosis sensitivity through diametrically opposite mechanisms, exerting negative and positive modulation, respectively. GPX4 functions as a critical inhibitor of ferroptosis [ – ], whereas ACSL4 serves as a key promoter [ , ]. In AML, demethylating agents act synergistically with ferroptosis inducers via the MAGEA6–AMPK–SLC7A11–GPX4 axis, suggesting that DNA methylation can influence the GPX4 pathway through upstream regulatory circuits [ ]. These genetic alterations underscore the rationale for using hypomethylating agents such as 5-azacitidine and decitabine in leukemia treatment.
In addition to DNA methylation, post-translational modifications of histones such as acetylation and methylation exert profound effects on the transcriptional regulation of key stemness-associated genes, including HOXA, MEIS1, and RUNX1 [ , – ]. Histone-modifying enzymes such as enhancer of zeste homolog 2, mixed-lineage leukemia 1, and disruptor of telomere silencing 1-like are integral to the epigenetic control of LSC function and leukemic progression [ , ]. Furthermore, MYST acetyltransferase HBO1 (also known as KAT7 or MYST2) and its associated protein complexes have been identified as key regulators of LSC maintenance [ ]. Interactions between transcription factors, such as HOXA9, Meis1, and RUNX1, and epigenetic modifiers establish a robust “transcriptional–epigenetic” regulatory circuit that sustains LSC stemness by modulating gene expression programs [ , ]. Within this regulatory circuit (Fig. ), epigenetic modifications can selectively activate or silence specific motifs, thereby altering the efficiency of transcription factor–gene binding.
 Among the HOX genes, HOXA9 plays a critical role in regulating LSC self-renewal and differentiation. In normal hematopoiesis, HOXA9 regulates lineage commitment and cellular differentiation; however, in leukemia, it is frequently overexpressed or activated owing to epigenetic dysregulation, thereby sustaining an undifferentiated state and promoting leukemogenesis [ , ]. Experimental models have shown that forced overexpression of HOXA9 enhances leukemic transformation and correlates with poor prognosis in patients with AML [ , , ]. Reports show that coexpression of HOXA9 and Meis1 in murine models rapidly induces AML-like diseases and enrich LSC populations [ ]. Targeting the HOXA9–Meis1 axis represents a potential therapeutic approach for eradicating LSCs and preventing disease relapse [ ].
RUNX1 (also known as AML1), a key regulator of hematopoiesis, is frequently mutated or dysregulated in leukemia, resulting in impaired differentiation and enhanced self-renewal of LSCs [ ]. RUNX1 interacts with core-binding factor β (CBFβ) and other transcriptional cofactors to regulate gene expression programs critical for LSC maintenance [ ]. Loss-of-function mutations in RUNX1 have been implicated in the persistence of undifferentiated LSCs and their resistance to conventional therapies [ ]. Given the interplay between transcription factors and epigenetic modifiers, targeting these regulatory networks may provide novel therapeutic opportunities to selectively disrupt LSC maintenance, while preserving normal hematopoiesis.

## Metabolic Characterization of LSCs and Drug Resistance

## Metabolic Adaptations in LSCs
Metabolic plasticity is a defining feature of LSCs that enables their survival, self-renewal, and resistance to therapeutic interventions. Compared with normal HSCs, LSCs exhibit distinct metabolic adaptations, including altered glucose metabolism, OXPHOS, and iron homeostasis (Table  ).
 LSCs exhibit remarkable metabolic plasticity, which enables them to dynamically switch between different metabolic pathways in response to fluctuations in nutrient availability and hypoxic conditions in the BM microenvironment [ , ]. These stem cells are highly dependent on glycolysis, commonly referred to as the Warburg effect, which facilitates the rapid generation of ATP and the biosynthesis of metabolic intermediates necessary for cell proliferation and survival. Metabolic reprogramming is driven by the upregulation of key glucose transporter proteins and glycolytic enzymes, such as hexokinase 2 and pyruvate kinase M2, which collectively enhance glucose uptake and use [ , , ]. In addition to their reliance on glycolysis, LSCs retain significant mitochondrial function and metabolic adaptability. Under conditions of hypoxia or nutrient deprivation, certain LSC populations shift their energy production toward mitochondrial OXPHOS, allowing for efficient ATP synthesis and redox homeostasis [ , , ]. Notably, mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, including mitophagy and ROS regulation, play pivotal roles in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and reprogramming the LSC energy metabolism to support cellular survival in stressful microenvironments [ ]. Notably, LSCs exhibit a remarkable capacity for transition between glycolysis and OXPHOS, enabling them to adapt to metabolic challenges and optimize energy production based on environmental demands. This metabolic flexibility is a key factor contributing to LSC resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies, as it allows them to evade the metabolic stress induced by treatment interventions [ ].
In HSCs, low ROS levels are essential for maintaining stemness and quiescence, whereas excessive ROS accumulation can lead to DNA damage, cellular senescence, and functional exhaustion [ ]. In contrast, LSCs have developed sophisticated antioxidant defense mechanisms to modulate intracellular ROS levels, primarily through key antioxidant pathways upregulation, such as the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and the glutathione/thioredoxin system [ – ]. In addition, SIRT1 regulates autophagy and intervenes in the epigenetic reprogramming of stem cells in response to oxidative stress and nutritional demands [ ]. By activating these pathways, LSCs can effectively neutralize oxidative stress, thereby preventing ROS-induced cellular damage and apoptosis. Interestingly, unlike HSCs, LSCs can tolerate moderately elevated levels of ROS, which serve as signaling molecules to activate proliferative and pro-survival pathways, including the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways (Fig. ). Activation of these pathways enhances LSC adaptation, metabolic reprogramming, and expansion within the BM microenvironment. To maintain this delicate balance, LSCs finely regulate ROS homeostasis by minimizing excessive oxidative damage while strategically driving signaling cascades that promote survival and leukemic progression using ROS. This dual regulation allows these stem cells to thrive under oxidative stress, conferring a significant survival advantage and contributing to developing resistance to therapy.
 LSCs possess unique characteristics such as quiescence, self-renewal capacity, and inherent resistance to chemotherapy, which frequently contribute to relapse and treatment failure in patients with AML. They are predominantly enriched in the G0 phase or exist in a state of low proliferative activity, allowing them to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents, which significantly complicates AML treatment strategies. Following chemotherapy, rapidly proliferating leukemic cells are effectively eliminated, whereas residual quiescent LSCs retain the ability to repopulate the leukemic burden at a later stage, serving as a major driver of disease relapse [ ]. In LSC quiescence regulation, the Wnt signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in maintaining the dormant state, thereby contributing to long-term persistence and therapeutic resistance [ ]. Drug resistance and disease relapse remain the most significant challenges in pediatric and adult AML cases, with LSC persistence recognized as the primary cause of recurrence [ , ]. LSCs also possess multiple anti-apoptotic mechanisms that contribute to their resistance. During AML treatment, apoptosis-related signaling pathways are often dysregulated, enabling LSCs to evade cell death (Fig. ). Key apoptosis-regulating proteins such as B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2), myeloid leukemia sequence 1 (MCL1), and p53 play crucial roles in conferring apoptosis resistance to LSCs, particularly when they are aberrantly expressed or mutated [ , – ]. In AML, LSCs are highly enriched in the Lin⁻CD34⁺CD38⁻ population, whereas normal BM comprises HSCs and non-HSC multipotent progenitor cells. Notably, LSCs exhibit elevated expression of CD47, a “don’t eat me” signal that inhibits phagocytosis by immune cells. This differential expression pattern provides a means to distinguish LSCs from normal HSCs and multipotent progenitor cells, and has been proposed as a potential therapeutic target [ ]. Additionally, NRF2-mediated upregulation of iron storage proteins, such as ferritin and heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), enhances the proliferative capacity of LSCs while increasing their resistance to therapy [ ]. Consequently, LSCs are inherently resistant to proliferation-inhibiting therapies, further underscoring their critical role in AML relapse [ ]. Emerging evidence also reveals that the reduced expression of basement membrane glycoproteins, such as lumican, is associated with decreased apoptosis and enhanced chemoresistance in human B-cell precursor leukemic cells [ ]. Furthermore, targeting immune checkpoint molecules, such as TIM-3, can potentially inhibit LSC function and enhance their sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, making TIM-3 a promising target for LSC-directed therapies [ , ]. LSCs commonly overexpress anti-apoptotic proteins, including BCL-2 and MCL-1, which confer resistance to chemotherapy-induced apoptosis by delaying or preventing apoptotic pathway activation even under conditions of DNA damage [ ]. Moreover, LSCs exhibit heightened DNA repair capabilities with elevated activity of repair enzymes such as DNA-dependent protein kinase and RAD51, further enhancing their survival following chemotherapy-induced genotoxic stress [ ].
 Recent studies have highlighted the ability of LSCs to evade immune- and chemotherapy-induced cell death by modulating their susceptibility to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death. This phenomenon plays a prominent role in chemoresistance development in AML [ ]. Findings from clinical trials have shown that targeting ferroptotic pathways in LSCs can significantly improve chemotherapy sensitivity. For instance, the ferroptosis inducer erastin in combination with the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax was evaluated in a phase I clinical trial (NCT05029999) on relapsed AML. The results showed a significant reduction in the residual LSC population and prolonged progression-free survival [ ]. Furthermore, the telomerase inhibitor imetelstat reportedly weakens LSC resistance to ferroptosis by activating lipophagy, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for overcoming drug resistance [ ]. Microenvironmental factors also contribute to LSC escape from ferroptosis. High iron ion concentrations within the BM niche induce upregulation of the iron storage protein FTH1, which enhances the antioxidant capacity of LSCs and further inhibits ferroptotic cell death. Preclinical models have shown that inhibiting FTH1 expression can increase the sensitivity of AML cells to ferroptosis inducers, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [ ]. Therefore, an in-depth study of ferroptosis susceptibility in LSCs is crucial for overcoming chemoresistance and preventing relapse.

## Mechanisms of Ferroptosis Resistance in LSCs
As discussed previously, ferroptosis is a unique form of regulated cell death that is mechanistically distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy (Fig.  ). It is characterized by three hallmark features: oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs), accumulation of redox-active iron, and failure of lipid peroxide repair mechanisms. These features are commonly used as key indicators to assess the extent of ferroptosis, during which cells undergo iron accumulation, ROS generation, and lipid peroxidation.
 

## Specific Iron Homeostasis Regulation and Ferroptosis Sensitivity in LSCs
LSCs, recognized as the root cause of leukemia initiation, progression, and relapse, exhibit unique biological properties, particularly iron metabolism, which differ significantly from those of normal leukemic cells and HSCs [ ]. Among these differences, the expression patterns of key iron transporter proteins, such as transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) and ferroportin (Fpn), play crucial roles in regulating intracellular iron homeostasis (Table ). Notably, the transferrin receptor 1 has been identified as a specific marker of ferroptosis, further emphasizing its functional relevance [ ]. In normal HSCs, TfR1 is stably expressed to ensure sufficient iron uptake for daily hematopoietic processes, whereas Fpn facilitates the exocytosis of excess intracellular iron to maintain iron homeostasis [ , ]. n contrast, normal leukemia cells, which are characterized by rapid proliferation, often exhibit abnormal TfR1 upregulation, leading to increased iron uptake to support biosynthesis. Concurrently, Fpn expression is disrupted and typically reduced, which limits iron efflux and results in intracellular iron retention. This trend is even more pronounced in LSCs, where TfR1 expression is significantly elevated, providing them with a competitive advantage in iron acquisition. Simultaneously, Fpn expression is markedly suppressed, severely restricting iron efflux [ ]. This dysregulated iron metabolism enhances the ability of LSCs to sequester iron efficiently, supporting their survival and proliferation, and further distinguishes them from normal HSCs and leukemia cells.
 Normal HSCs maintain stable expression levels of the iron storage protein FTH1 to regulate the storage and timely release of iron ions, thereby ensuring iron homeostasis. In normal leukemia cells, FTH1 expression is upregulated to buffer fluctuations in iron demand and to counteract oxidative stress [ , ]. Under oxidative stress caused by external stimuli such as chemotherapy-induced ROS production, iron ions stored in highly expressed FTH1 contribute to the antioxidant response. These stored iron ions help mitigate harmful free radical generation by participating in the regulation of the reverse Fenton reaction. Furthermore, stored iron can be rapidly mobilized by LSCs to meet elevated iron demands for continuous self-renewal and proliferation. Interestingly, FTH1 expression is higher in HSCs than in LSCs. This elevated expression allows HSCs to maintain robust iron homeostasis and effectively resist ferroptosis. In contrast, LSCs exhibit lower FTH1 expression, resulting in a reduced buffering capacity against fluctuations in iron content and a diminished ability to counteract ferroptosis [ ].
Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) is a specific receptor for ferritinophagy that mediates the transport of ferritin to lysosomes for degradation, which releases free iron. Excessive intracellular iron can disrupt redox homeostasis and ultimately trigger ferroptosis. Reportedly, NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy induce ferroptosis through ferritin degradation and iron overload [ ]. Targeting NCOA4, by modulating FTH1 phase separation–driven autophagy has been recently shown to effectively influence ferroptosis sensitivity [ ]. In addition, NRF2 plays a central role in the cellular antioxidant response. By binding to its antioxidant response element, NRF2 acts as a key transcription factor that upregulates SLC7A11, GPX4, and multiple other genes involved in the antioxidant and thioredoxin systems, thereby enhancing lipid peroxidation clearance and elevating the ferroptosis threshold [ ]. Evidence from intestinal epithelial models indicates that the HIF-2α–NCOA4 axis couples NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy with systemic iron demand. Moreover, hypoxia can downregulate NCOA4 through transcriptional and miRNA-dependent mechanisms, leading to the suppression of ferritinophagy [ ].Given the crucial role of iron metabolism in leukemia progression, factors such as NCOA4, FTH1, and TFRC (the gene encoding transferrin receptor 1, TfR1) are regulated by stress- and metabolism-responsive transcriptional networks (including NRF2 and the HIF/iron homeostasis axis), which in turn modulate upstream regulation of the GPX4 axis and determine the lipid peroxidation threshold.
Regarding iron ion endocytosis, exocytosis, and ferroptosis sensitivity, HSCs exhibit a degree of ferroptosis tolerance owing to their balanced iron transport and storage systems [ , ]. n normal leukemia cells, altered intracellular iron concentrations result from increased iron uptake and decreased exocytosis, leading to reduced sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. LSCs exhibit remarkable resistance to ferroptosis primarily because of their enhanced iron homeostasis and antioxidant defences. Ferritinophagy, a selective form of autophagy that degrades ferritin to release free iron, has been recently implicated as a key regulator of the sensitivity of LSCs to ferroptosis. Larrue et al. revealed that LSCs actively engage in ferritinophagy to prevent excessive labile iron accumulation, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced ferroptotic cell death [ ]. This process is largely mediated by NCOA4, which facilitates ferritin degradation and intracellular iron release. Notably, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of NCOA4 leads to iron overload and increased susceptibility to ferroptosis in LSCs, suggesting its potential therapeutic vulnerability in AML [ ]. These findings suggest that targeting ferritinophagy either through NCOA4 inhibition or ferritin degradation blockade may enhance the efficacy of ferroptosis-based therapies in AML.
In summary, the unique iron metabolic pattern of LSCs enables them to survive and proliferate in a challenging tumor microenvironment. An in-depth investigation of the mechanisms underlying this metabolic adaptability could significantly advance the diagnosis and treatment of leukemia (Table ). Targeting the “enhancement strategies” of LSCs in iron transport and storage—such as inhibiting TfR1, reducing FTH1 expression, or restoring Fpn function—may substantially increase their sensitivity to ferroptosis. In drug-resistant or relapsed leukemia, combining ferroptosis inducers, such as erastin and RSL3, with drugs targeting iron homeostasis could enhance LSC clearance and reduce relapse rates [ ]. The precise modulation of LSC iron metabolism and the induction of ferroptosis should be prioritized as strategies for achieving effective leukemia eradication in future studies.
 

## Antioxidant Systems
In terms of antioxidant mechanisms, LSCs typically exhibit elevated levels of antioxidant enzymes and molecules, with GSH playing a central role as the major intracellular antioxidant [ ]. The pivotal role of GSH in cellular redox homeostasis was initially reported in the 1970 s by Bannai et al., marking an important milestone in our understanding of oxidative stress regulation [ ]. A decade later, Ursini et al. successfully isolated GPX4, an enzyme capable of inhibiting iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation, which laid the foundation for ferroptosis research and the discovery of ferroptosis regulatory genes [ – ]. The core mechanism of ferroptosis was subsequently reported to be associated with the irreversible accumulation of lipid peroxidation and functional impairment of GPX4 [ , ]. For example, in ferroptosis induction targeting GPX4, bridging the target protein with heat shock protein 90, can induce intracellular GPX4 degradation to trigger ferroptosis [ ]. Additionally, compound 5i (ZX703) induces lipid ROS accumulation in HT1080 cells, which significantly degrades GPX4 via the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy–lysosome pathways in a dose- and time-dependent manner, thereby inducing ferroptosis [ ].

## SLC7A11
SLC7A11 is a transmembrane protein that plays a crucial role in cysteine uptake and is a key precursor in GSH synthesis [ – ]. LSCs may upregulate SLC7A11 expression to enhance cysteine uptake, which promotes GSH production and reinforces antioxidant defenses [ , ]. This mechanism supports GPX4-mediated neutralization of lipid peroxides, effectively blocking ferroptosis. In addition to its role in inhibiting ferroptosis, upregulated SLC7A11 contributes to intracellular redox homeostasis by regulating cysteine and glutamate level [ ]. LSCs evade ferroptosis in various ways, with the upregulation of SLC7A11 emerging as a prominent adaptation [ , ]. By enhancing SLC7A11 function, LSCs ensure a stable cysteine supply that maintains high levels of GSH and GPX4 activity. However, alternative antioxidant pathways independent of the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis may also be activated by these LSCs. For instance, they may upregulate other antioxidant enzymes or molecules, such as peroxiredoxins or thioredoxins, which directly scavenge ROS and protect cells from oxidative damage [ , – ]. These pathways provide an additional defence mechanism, further enhancing the resistance of LSCs to ferroptosis.

## GSH
GSH serves as the central “fuel/brake” of ferroptosis: it is an essential cofactor of GPX4, which reduces lipid hydroperoxides (PLOOH) to lipid alcohols (PLOH) to block chain peroxidation. Additionally, it is a key molecule maintaining overall cellular redox capacity, coupled with NADPH and cysteine metabolism. As a critical ferroptosis inhibitor, GPX4 utilizes GSH as a cofactor to reduce lipid peroxides, thereby preventing ferroptosis [ , ]. NADPH consumption is pronounced during ferroptosis, and carbon metabolism provides reducing power to the GSH/GPX4 axis as well as the apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 2 (FSP1)/CoQ antioxidant network [ ]. Supplementation or regeneration of NADPH can reinforce antioxidant defences and delay ferroptosis. GPX4 acts as the key suppressor of ferroptosis by reducing PLOOH to PLOH using GSH. In contrast, RSL3 directly inhibits GPX4, while erastin/BSO inactivate GPX4 by disrupting GSH supply, both ultimately leading to the irreversible accumulation of PLOOH [ ]. LSCs strongly rely on the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis to sustain antioxidant defence, forming a fundamental basis of their ferroptosis resistance [ ]. It is hypothesized that LSCs provide an abundant supply of GSH to GPX4, enabling the sustained scavenging of ROS and lipid peroxides [ , ]. Concurrently, the antioxidant defence system is compromised, significantly reducing GSH levels and decreasing expression of GPX4, a critical enzyme responsible for detoxifying lipid peroxides [ , ]. LSCs may have an enhanced ability to synthesize or recycle GSH, a function directly linked to the activity of GPX4 [ ]. This adaptive mechanism may represent a key strategy through which LSCs evade ferroptosis and survive under oxidative stress conditions.

## GPX4
At the molecular level, GPX4 is considered a critical “gatekeeper” for ferroptosis inhibition. A decrease in GPX4 protein levels or impaired enzymatic activity is a key marker of the onset of ferroptosis and can be assessed using immunoblotting or enzymatic activity detection kits [ ]. Meanwhile, excess iron reduces the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11, thereby accelerating the onset of ferroptosis [ ]. Studies have shown that the downregulation or inhibition of SLC7A11, a cystine transporter, reduces cellular cystine uptake, leading to diminished GSH synthesis. Similarly, changes in the expression of FSP1 are closely associated with cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis [ , , , ]. These molecular and morphological changes provide robust evidence for ferroptosis and offer critical insights into its regulatory mechanisms and diagnostic markers.
At the transcriptional level, NRF2 serves as a direct or indirect transcriptional regulator of multiple anti-ferroptotic genes, including GSH, SLC7A11, and GPX4. Reportedly, NRF2 can directly upregulate SLC7A11, GCLC, GCLM, GSS, and GSR, thereby enhancing cystine uptake, promoting GSH synthesis and regeneration, and sustaining the functional activity of GPX4 [ ]. In contrast, reports from other studies showed that BACH1 (a transcriptional repressor responsive to oxidative stress) suppresses the expression of GSH-related genes, thereby increasing ferroptosis sensitivity [ , ]. Evidence further indicates that in AML, cell survival is significantly dependent on GPX4 and FSP1 (AIFM2); high expression of SLC7A11/GPX4 correlates with poor prognosis and an anti-ferroptotic phenotype [ ]. Mechanistically, NRF2 activation in AML promotes GPX4 upregulation, enabling ferroptosis evasion and contributing to therapy resistance, whereas dual inhibition of NRF2 and GPX4 can synergistically trigger ferroptosis [ ]. These findings suggest that the ferroptosis threshold in LSCs is not only determined by the metabolic state but is also finely tuned by transcriptional regulatory networks. In summary, the homeostasis of the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis is tightly controlled by transcriptional networks: NRF2-driven gene activation strengthens ferroptosis resistance, whereas repressors such as BACH1 promote vulnerability. This transcription–metabolic coupling provides LSCs with a survival advantage and represents a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

## Metabolism and Lipid Remodeling

## Lipid Peroxidation and PUFAs
Lipid peroxidation is initiated by the attack of oxidizing agents, including free and non-free radical species, on lipids containing carbon-carbon double bonds. PUFAs are vulnerable to oxidative damage because of their unique structures, particularly the presence of highly reactive hydrogen atoms at bis-allylic positions [ , ]. During peroxidation, the insertion of oxygen leads to the formation of lipid peroxyl radicals and lipid hydroperoxides, which in turn exacerbate oxidative damage. At the cellular level, lipids are the fundamental structural components of cell membranes, playing crucial roles in maintaining cellular integrity and regulating various biological functions. However, their widespread presence and functional importance make them primary targets for ROS-induced damage [ ]. Lipid peroxidation can occur via enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways, with the key distinction being that free radicals mediate non-enzymatic peroxidation, whereas enzymatic peroxidation is catalyzed by specific enzymes, such as lipoxygenases (LOXs) and cyclooxygenases. LOXs, a family of non-haem iron-containing enzymes, play a significant role in ferroptosis by catalyzing the oxidation of PUFAs. The activation of these enzymes requires the oxidation of Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ at their catalytic center, a process that is intricately linked to the cellular redox state, with intracellular GSH levels serving as a critical regulator [ ]. Notably, LOX activation is not an isolated event, but rather a process closely coupled to the overall balance of oxidative stress within the cell.
LOX and ACSL4 work synergistically to promote the esterification and oxidation of PUFAs, forming a “ferroptosis switch.” Low ACSL4 expression may weaken the catalytic efficiency of LOX, resulting in resistance to ferroptosis [ ]. Meanwhile, the downregulation of certain LOX isoenzymes (e.g., ALOX15) has been found to reduce lipid peroxidation levels, thereby conferring resistance to ferroptosis [ ]. LOX may influence the iron homeostasis of LSCs by regulating ferritin or TfR1, indirectly modulating ferroptosis [ , ]. LOX inhibition can protect cells from erastin-induced ferroptosis, suggesting that LOX plays an essential role in ferroptosis regulation [ ]. In addition, the pro-oxidative effect of LOX antagonizes the antioxidant function of GPX4 [ ]. The inhibition of GPX4 (for instance, using ML210) suppresses LOX, thereby enhancing ferroptosis [ ]. In Breakpoint Cluster Region–Abelson Murine Leukemia Viral Oncogene (BCR–ABL)–driven CML models, Alox5 (5-lipoxygenase, 5-LOX) has been identified as an essential gene for the maintenance of LSCs. Genetic ablation of Alox5 blocks disease initiation and eradicates LSCs [ , ]. These findings provide solid mechanistic evidence supporting the inhibition of the LOX axis as a strategy for LSC clearance, and this concept has been further extended to studies of stem-like cells in AML [ ]. By catalyzing the oxidation of PUFAs, LOX regulates the ferroptotic sensitivity of LSCs; low LOX expression or inhibition of its activity is a key mechanism by which LSCs resist ferroptosis. In LSCs, targeting the LOX pathway, such as by activating ALOX15 or inhibiting ALOX12, may be a promising new strategy for eradicating LSCs, particularly when combined with ferroptosis inducers.
PUFAs are indispensable substrates for lipid peroxidation and are essential for initiating ferroptosis. Studies have shown that phospholipids containing two PUFA chains efficiently drive ferroptotic cell death [ ]. It has been hypothesized that lipid crosslinking during lipid peroxidation represents a potential mechanism of membrane damage during ferroptosis. The abundance of PUFAs within the membrane lipid composition determines the number of available peroxidation sites, which in turn play a decisive role in cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis [ ]. In AML, high expression of fatty acid desaturase 1 is reportedly associated with poor survival, indicating a dependency on PUFA biosynthesis [ ]. Moreover, reports show that dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA, an ω−6 PUFA) trigger ferroptosis in AML cells through ACSL4-mediated phospholipid remodeling, further validating the synergistic role of PUFA enrichment and ACSL4 activity [ ]. Collectively, accumulating evidence links the axis of PUFA metabolism–lipid peroxidation–ferroptosis to the survival and therapeutic resistance of LSCs. These processes highlight the critical role of lipid peroxidation in ferroptosis, and underscore the potential of targeting lipid metabolism as a therapeutic strategy against ferroptosis-related diseases.

## ACSL4
ACSL4 belongs to the long-chain fatty acid CoA ligase family and primarily catalyzes the conjugation of PUFAs, such as arachidonic acid and AdA, with coenzyme A to generate PUFA-CoA. As a key regulator of ferroptosis, ACSL4 influences the cellular sensitivity to oxidative stress by synthesizing PUFA-PLs. Its activation promotes the biosynthesis of PUFA-PLs, leading to lipid peroxide accumulation, which ultimately induces ferroptosis [ ]. In multiple cancers, ACSL4 exerts dual pro- and antitumor effects by regulating lipid metabolism, ferroptosis, and membrane phospholipid remodeling, with its expression levels closely associated with therapeutic response and prognosis [ , ]. In LSCs of patients with AML, ACSL4 downregulation may confer resistance to ferroptosis, and low ACSL4 expression correlates with poor erastin efficacy in primary patient samples [ ]. Studies have shown that selective inhibition of diacylglycerol acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) reduces lipid droplet formation and significantly enhances ferroptosis sensitivity in AML cells. Combined with dietary PUFA (e.g., DHA) supplementation, DGAT1 further synergistically promotes ferroptosis, providing an innovative strategy for overcoming AML drug resistance by targeting lipid metabolism [ ]. Additionally, quiescent LSCs rely on NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy to maintain iron homeostasis; inhibiting NCOA4 blocks iron release and induces ferroptosis in LSCs, synergizing with ACSL4 to enhance ferroptotic effects [ ]. In a murine model, AS-252,424 effectively inhibited lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in human and murine cells, suggesting that targeted intervention against ACSL4 may a novel strategy to eradicate LSCs [ ].

## Non-Canonical Pathways: FSP1–CoQ10 Axis
The FSP1–CoQ10–NAD(P)H and GSH–GPX4 pathways serve as parallel lipid antioxidant pathways [ ]. Among these, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10, also known as ubiquinone) is a lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidant that can block the propagation of lipid peroxides and inhibit ferroptosis [ ]. ts reduced form, coenzyme Q10H2 (ubiquinol), effectively diminishes oxidative stress and prevents lipid ROS accumulation [ ]. In this process, FSP1 uses NADPH to reduce CoQ10 to Q10H2 and regenerates this coenzyme via NAD(P)H [ ]. Therefore, the FSP1–CoQ10–NAD(P)H pathway functions independently of the system Xc⁻–GSH–GPX4 axis to exert an antioxidant effect, thereby suppressing ferroptosis. Once FSP1 is inhibited, the antioxidant effects of this pathway are blocked, leading to ferroptosis.
The FSP1–CoQ10 axis reportedly inhibits ferroptosis by regulating cholesterol metabolism. By reducing CoQ10, FSP1 blocks the oxidation of phospholipid membranes, thereby enhancing resistance to ferroptosis in LSCs [ ]. Single-cell sequencing results also indicate a marked upregulation of the FSP1–CoQ10 axis in LSCs, and this metabolic reprogramming confers stronger tolerance to ferroptosis in LSCs [ ]. Moreover, in CML LSCs, overactivation of the FSP1–CoQ10 axis leads to resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors. By co-inhibiting GPX4 and FSP1, the sensitivity of BCR–ABL⁺ LSCs to ferroptosis can be significantly increased, providing a novel potential strategy for CML treatment [ ]. Notably, the transcription factor Nrf2 regulates ferroptosis via the FSP1–CoQ10 axis. In a study, FSP1-mediated suppression of ferroptosis was markedly weakened after Nrf2 siRNA treatment [ ]. The NRF2 pathway transcriptionally regulates FSP1, which serves as a key downstream anti-ferroptotic factor of NRF2 [ ]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the FSP1–CoQ10 axis in leukemia, underscoring the need for further investigation.

## Survival Advantage of Iron Storage or Transport Capacity of LSCs in High Oxidative Stress Environments
LSCs exhibit enhanced iron storage and transport capacities, providing them with a distinct survival advantage under conditions of high oxidative stress. Studies on leukemia resistance mechanisms have revealed that LSCs can regulate the expression and function of key iron transport proteins, such as TfR1, Fpn, and FTH1, to counteract oxidative stress. Under such stressful conditions, cells are typically overwhelmed by excessive ROS [ ]. In normal cells, ROS oxidize critical components such as unsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane, ultimately leading to cell death [ ]. By contrast, LSCs have evolved sophisticated strategies to leverage robust iron-handling systems. First, they upregulate TfR1 expression, thereby enabling increased iron uptake [ ]. These acquired iron ions can be incorporated into the synthesis or regulation of intracellular antioxidant enzymes such as iron-containing superoxide dismutase, thereby enhancing ROS scavenging. Second, the elevated expression of FTH1 in LSCs serves a dual purpose [ , ]. It acts as a reservoir by sequestering excess iron ions to prevent the intracellular Fenton reaction, which otherwise exacerbates ROS production. Simultaneously, FTH1 facilitates the rapid release of stored iron when cells require it to support antioxidant defense or other metabolic processes under stress, thereby providing the necessary adaptability to survive.
Recently, significant strides in developing targeted therapies for leukemia have been made in translational research. Studies have indicated that disrupting the iron metabolism pathway in LSCs by inhibiting TfR1 function or interfering with FTH1-mediated iron storage can significantly enhance their sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents [ ]. TThese findings highlighted the critical role of iron metabolism in LSC resistance and viability. For instance, novel iron chelators and small-molecule compounds designed to disrupt proteins associated with iron metabolism have been actively explored for their therapeutic potential. Such interventions have been shown to increase LSC susceptibility to oxidative stress and induce ferroptosis, thereby potentially improving leukemia treatment outcomes [ ]. By perturbing iron homeostasis, these strategies expose LSCs to heightened oxidative stress, rendering them more vulnerable to ferroptosis and providing a promising avenue for advancing leukemia therapy. Ferroptosis inducers, such as erastin and RSL3, have shown significant potential when combined with other therapeutic agents, such as BCL-2 inhibitors (e.g., venetoclax) and traditional chemotherapies, to selectively target LSC populations. These strategies exploit LSC-specific vulnerabilities such as dysregulated iron metabolism and weakened antioxidant defenses to induce ferroptosis and overcome resistance mechanisms [ ]. Recent advances in single-cell sequencing have provided critical insights into this heterogeneity, revealing distinct LSC subpopulations with unique ferroptosis sensitivities [ , ]. For instance, some subpopulations demonstrate increased TfR1 expression and diminished Fpn activity, leading to increased intracellular iron accumulation and susceptibility to ferroptosis [ , ]. Incorporating these ferroptosis biomarkers into patient stratification frameworks could enable personalized AML therapy development, enabling the precise targeting of resistant LSC subsets. This approach could potentially aid in optimizing therapeutic efficacy, minimizing residual disease, and improving the long-term outcomes of AML.

## Single-Cell Omics Unravel Ferroptosis Heterogeneity in LSCs
LSCs play a critical role in leukemogenesis, drug resistance, and relapse. Ferroptosis is emerging as a promising strategy for overcoming LSC resistance. However, LSCs exhibit striking heterogeneity in ferroptosis sensitivity, with distinct subsets displaying either high susceptibility or resistance, profoundly impacting treatment outcomes [ ]. Single-cell omics technologies, including scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, scProteomics, and scMetabolomics, have emerged as powerful tools for dissecting intrapopulation heterogeneity during LSC ferroptosis, offering new directions for personalized therapy. Notably, scRNA-seq can resolve ferroptosis sensitivity across LSC subsets and uncover key molecular pathways [ ]. For example, LSC subsets with a high expression of GPX4, FSP1, and Nrf2 exhibit robust resistance to ferroptosis, whereas those with elevated TfR1 and low GPX4 expression are more vulnerable to ferroptosis induction [ ]. In hematologic malignancy samples, targeted single-cell sequencing enables the detection of metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis-resistance signatures, providing a direct technical approach to quantifying ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs)—such as ACSL4, LPCAT3, ALOX15, SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH1/FTL—at the single-cell level and stratifying cells accordingly [ ]. Across multiple hematologic malignancy datasets (including entries from the AML/TISCH database), FRG-based scoring has been applied at the single-cell level, revealing marked differences in ferroptosis signatures among distinct malignant clones and cellular states [ ]. Additionally, scRNA-seq has revealed that the hypoxic niche suppresses ferroptosis via the HIF-1α signaling axis, enabling LSCs to evade cell death [ ], highlighting how microenvironmental cues modulate ferroptosis in LSCs. Notably, in chemotherapy-refractory AML, miR-126-high LSCs, enriched at diagnosis and relapse, exhibit enforced stemness and quiescence traits [ ].
Regarding personalized therapy, single-cell transcriptomic technologies provide new avenues for dissecting the heterogeneity of ferroptosis sensitivity in LSCs. Reportedly, LSCs in patients with AML are typically enriched within the CD34⁺CD38⁻ cluster, and FRG scores at the single-cell level reveal pronounced heterogeneity in lipid peroxidation and iron homeostasis across these subpopulations [ ]. For example, transcriptomic and single-cell analyses have been used to identify distinct gene expression programs in cycling versus quiescent subpopulations. Quiescent leukemic cells, associated with the LSC phenotype, exhibit unique transcriptional profiles in which low FTH1 expression may serve as a defining feature of ferroptosis-susceptible LSC subsets [ ]. Moreover, comparative analyses of samples from seven patients and three healthy donors revealed that, relative to normal counterparts, CD34⁺CD38⁻ stem and progenitor cells from CMML displayed upregulation of genes associated with cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways. This finding suggests that transcriptional alterations in iron metabolism–related genes may be associated with the functional properties of CD34⁺CD38⁻ cells [ ], thereby providing a framework for further delineation of low-FTH1 CD34⁺CD38⁻ subpopulations in single-cell omics. In studies comparing AML and CML, while CML CD34⁺/CD38⁻ LSCs display relatively stable aberrant expression profiles, AML CD34⁺/CD38⁻ cells exhibit variable expression of abnormal membrane antigens. Notably, surface markers such as CD25, CD33, and CD123 are significantly upregulated compared with normal bone marrow stem cells. These findings facilitate the single-cell-level discrimination of LSC subsets and provide surface antigen–based evidence for defining ferroptosis-prone CD34⁺/CD38⁻ populations [ ]. T Collectively, insights from single-cell omics and translational studies suggest that, in research settings, identifying CD34⁺CD38⁻ LSC subsets characterized by high ACSL4 and low FTH1 expression may justify the design of clinical trials to evaluate ferroptosis-targeted interventions. Such strategies could be implemented in combination with standard LSC-directed regimens (e.g., venetoclax ± hypomethylating agents), by incorporating agents that inhibit the System Xc⁻–GSH–GPX4 axis or the lipoxygenase pathway, thereby exploiting ferroptosis vulnerabilities in AML.
Collectively, single-cell omics have provided unprecedented insights into the heterogeneity of ferroptosis in LSCs(Fig.  ). When integrated with deep learning and AI-driven single-cell data analysis, these approaches could potentially improve the precision of ferroptosis-targeted therapies.
 

## Therapeutic Strategies: from Ferroptosis Induction To Immune Synergy

## Targeting LSCs Based on Ferroptosis
Recently, ferroptosis inducers have revealed significant potential to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, and epigenetic modulators by disrupting iron homeostasis and lipid metabolism. This approach has been particularly effective in overcoming drug resistance in LSCs and reducing MRD. With respect to strategies targeting iron homeostasis, in AML, inhibiting NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy selectively eliminates CD34⁺/CD38⁻ LSCs by elevating labile iron levels [ ]. Similarly, nanoparticles encapsulating salinomycin, a polyether antibiotic with iron-chelating properties, successfully induced ferroptosis in AML cells and synergized with the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax, thereby overcoming venetoclax resistance [ ]. In CML, where BCR–ABL signaling supports LSC survival, ferroptosis inducers markedly enhanced the efficacy of imatinib by promoting lipid peroxidation via iron-dependent oxidative stress, reducing MRD [ – ]. With respect to strategies targeting the inhibition of antioxidant defence systems, studies highlight that the dual approach of regulating iron homeostasis together with inhibiting GPX4 significantly enhances the sensitivity of LSCs to ferroptosis and helps to overcome classical drug resistance barriers [ ]. This demonstrates that disrupting the GPX4-dependent antioxidant shield is critical for reversing ferroptosis tolerance. In BCR–ABL⁺ leukemias, combining GPX4 inhibition with tyrosine kinase inhibitors selectively impairs LSCs, providing direct evidence for a combined strategy of “antioxidant axis blockade plus pathway inhibition” [ ]. Within the NRF2 regulatory network, NRF2 activation enhances GSH synthesis, SLC7A11 expression, and lipid peroxidation clearance; conversely, NRF2 inhibition markedly lowers the ferroptosis threshold and increases LSC sensitivity to combination therapies. In AML, suppressing BRD4/c-Myc/NRF2 axis reportedly promotes synergy between BET inhibitors (BETi) and ferroptosis inducers [ ]. With respect to strategies targeting lipid metabolism, beyond iron and antioxidant pathways, ferroptosis inducers further impair LSC viability by amplifying lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. For instance, FLT3 inhibitors suppress LSC proliferation and self-renewal by blocking FLT3 signaling. Nevertheless, when combined with ferroptosis inducers, they downregulate downstream anti-apoptotic genes such as BCL-2, thereby enhancing drug sensitivity and reducing resistance [ , ]. In AML cells and in vivo models, knocking out ACSL4 significantly suppresses DGLA-induced ferroptosis; conversely, DGLA enrichment in vivo restricts leukemia progression and promotes ferroptosis [ ]. In AML, NSUN2 enhances resistance to ferroptosis by upregulating FSP1 through RNA 5-methylcytosine modification. Targeting either NSUN2 or FSP1 renders AML cells more susceptible to lipid peroxidation [ ].
Collectively, ferroptosis inducers targeting iron homeostasis, antioxidant defences, and lipid metabolism show strong potential to synergize with chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, and epigenetic modulators. This combined approach offers a promising strategy for eradicating resistant LSC populations and reducing MRD, although further studies are needed to clarify their safety, optimal dosing, and efficacy in specific LSC subpopulations.

## Ferroptosis Inducers in Clinical Trials
Recently, ferroptosis has become a transformative research frontier in cancer therapy, neurodegenerative diseases, immunomodulation, and metabolic disorders. By targeting key regulators—including GPX4, the Xc⁻ system, iron metabolism, and lipid peroxidation—ferroptosis inducers (FIs) selectively eliminate cancer cells resistant to conventional therapies. Several FIs have now entered Phase I and II clinical trials, assessing their efficacy in both tumors and hematological malignancies. In this section, their clinical development was reviewed, focusing on mechanisms of action, therapeutic indications, and associated challenges (Table  ). While the field is transitioning from “mechanism discovery” to “clinical translation,” three critical bottlenecks remain: limited selectivity, resistance heterogeneity, and multi-organ toxicity.
 

## Crosstalk between Ferroptosis and Anti-Leukemia Immunity
LSCs, which are characterized by unique metabolic reprogramming, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and multiple immune evasion mechanisms, are refractory to conventional therapies. Emerging evidence reveals a bidirectional crosstalk between ferroptosis and the immune system in LSC elimination. Ferroptosis not only potentiates anti-leukemia immunity but also modulates it through immune cells by regulating iron homeostasis.
During ferroptosis, LSCs release damage-associated molecular patterns, including HMGB1, ATP, and immunogenic lipid peroxidation products (4-HNE and MDA), which enhance phagocytosis by dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, promote antigen presentation, and augment CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against LSCs [ ]. Concomitantly, ferroptosis-induced lipid peroxidation directly amplifies CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxicity, bolstering anti-leukemia immune responses [ ]. However, the immune system regulates the susceptibility of LSCs to ferroptosis by reprogramming iron metabolism, antioxidant systems, and lipid pathways. For instance, interferon (IFN)-γ secreted by T cells upregulates TfR1 on LSCs, increasing iron uptake and ferroptosis vulnerability [ ]. IFN-γ also downregulates GPX4 and SLC7A11, further sensitizing LSCs to ferroptosis [ ]. On the flip side, LSCs secrete IL-10 and TGF-β to induce regulatory T cell (Treg) expansion, dampening anti-leukemia immunity. Notably, ferroptosis reduces the abundance of Tregs and attenuates the suppression of CD8+ T cells, thereby restoring immune-mediated cytotoxicity [ ]. Additionally, LSCs exploit the TIM-3/galectin-9 axis to evade immunosurveillance. The ferroptosis-promoted degradation of galectin-9 disrupts TIM-3 signaling, rescues CD8+ T cell effector functions, and enhances anti-leukemia activity [ ]. This reciprocal regulation of ferroptosis and immunity underscores the translational potential of ferroptosis-centric immunotherapy for leukemia, offering a dual strategy for eliminating treatment-resistant LSCs while overcoming immune-evasive phenotypes.

## Immunotherapy Combinations and Biomarker-Driven Approaches
Owing to their high drug resistance, low proliferation rates, and immune evasion capabilities, LSCs play critical roles in leukemia relapse and drug resistance. With ongoing technological advances, the combined use of FIs and immunotherapy represents a new therapeutic approach for targeting LSCs. When used alongside immune checkpoint inhibitors or chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, leveraging biomarker-driven precision strategies can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing unnecessary side effects. For example, high programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression can undermine T cell-mediated ferroptosis; thus, the co-administration of the PD-L1 inhibitor (atezolizumab) and the ferroptosis inducer (ML210) can effectively improve the clearance rate of LSCs [ ]. Moreover, integrating scRNA-seq with AI can help predict the sensitivity of individual LSCs to ferroptosis and immunotherapy, offering the potential for personalized treatment [ ]. Ferroptosis-induced immunogenic cell death can release danger signals such as HMGB1 and ATP, thereby activating DC-mediated CD8+ T cell responses. However, through NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy that maintains low intracellular iron levels and high PD-L1 expression, LSCs can form a “ferroptosis tolerance–immune evasion,” indicating a dual protective mechanism [ , – ].
With biomarker-driven precision immunotherapy strategies, further optimization of ferroptosis-based immunotherapy for LSCs requires individualized patient selection based on multidimensional biomarkers. Muhammad et al. [ ] combined Raman spectroscopy and microfluidics to systematically investigate the mechanisms of ferroptosis induction. They demonstrated that GPX4, VDAC2, and NRF2 play pivotal roles in cellular responses to oxidative stress and in maintaining redox balance. In the context of an autoimmune imbalance, high levels of MDA can lead to the inactivation of GPX4, thereby inducing ferroptosis. Additionally, treatment of THP-1 cells with erastin and IMP-366 depletes GSH, leading to increased production of lipid degradation by-products (e.g., MDA and 4-hydroxynonenal, 4-HNE), which in turn heightens THP-1 cell susceptibility to erastin-induced ferroptosis [ ]. TIM-3, another key regulator of LSC immune evasion, collaborates with the PD-1/PD-L1 axis to suppress T cell-mediated antitumor activity. Under conditions of M1/M2 macrophage imbalance, PD-L1⁺TIM-3⁺ LSCs readily establish an immune-evasive microenvironment [ , ].
Therefore, modulation of the macrophage subtype ratio is a promising strategy for combination immunotherapy. For instance, blocking PD-L1 and TIM-3, combined with an M1 macrophage activator (e.g., GM-CSF), could potentially improve clinical outcomes. Indicators such as the TfR1/FTH1 ratio, serum 4-HNE levels, and FLT3-ITD mutation status can predict tumor sensitivity to FIs, whereas the proportion of PD-L1⁺TIM-3⁺ LSCs and M1 macrophage infiltration levels can guide the selection of individualized immunotherapy combinations. Achieving higher LSC clearance rates while minimizing relapse and drug resistance may be possible by integrating multiple biomarkers and AI analysis.

## Clinical Translation and Challenges
In the context of clinical translation, studies have proposed a scalable method for preparing zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) that exhibit antileukemic effects by inducing ferroptosis in LSCs [ ]. At the molecular level, circFAM193B, which is downregulated in LSCs, promotes chemotherapy-surviving LSCs by regulating energy metabolism and redox homeostasis. In vitro and in vivo experiments further showed that circFAM193B significantly inhibits LSCs, reduces chemoresistance, and blocks the progression of AML [ ]. Comprehensive studies revealed that protein arginine methyltransferase PRMT6 and the N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) reader IGF2BP2 are critical for maintaining human and murine LSCs. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of PRMT6 severely impairs AML development and LSC function, indicating that PRMT6 is a key factor in the maintenance of AML stem cells [ , ]. Unlike normal hematopoiesis, the m⁶A demethylase ALKBH5 is also essential for AML progression and self-renewal of LSCs/LICs [ ]. Additionally, the histone deacetylase inhibitor CS055 combined with the PPAR agonist chiglitazar synergistically induces ferroptosis in LSCs by reprogramming lipid metabolism [ ]. Conversely, deleting the stress sensor GADD45A enhances LSC self-renewal and resistance to ferroptosis [ ]. Inhibition of HOXA9 and LGR4 restores LSC sensitivity to ferroptosis, offering potential novel targets for personalized AML therapy.
Despite the theoretical advantages of the ferroptosis-targeted elimination of LSCs in reducing relapse and improving patient outcomes, critical challenges, including safety, specificity, drug combination strategies, biomarker development, resistance mechanisms, and personalized treatment optimization are encountered in its clinical translation. FIs may cause toxicity in normal HSCs and impose systemic risks on iron-rich tissues (e.g., the liver, kidney, and heart). Moreover, ferroptosis-resistant genes (e.g., FSP1, GPX4, SLC7A11) are expressed in normal cells and LSCs, complicating the elimination of specific LSCs without causing collateral damage. Thus, urgent advancements in precision targeting via mechanistic insights and technological innovations are required to enhance the safety and selectivity of ferroptosis-based therapies, offering more effective strategies for treating hematological malignancies such as AML.

## Conclusions and Perspectives
As the root cause of leukemia relapse and residual microscopic lesions, LSCs play a critical role in regulating ferroptosis because of their unique drug resistance and survival advantages. Their highly regulated internal iron homeostasis, enhanced antioxidant mechanisms, and specifically remodeled lipid metabolism pathways make them a significant obstacle in leukemia treatment, as they exhibit poor responsiveness to conventional chemotherapeutic agents and targeted therapies. However, targeting ferroptosis in LSCs could potentially aid in reducing relapse rates and improving patient prognosis. This can be achieved by modulating iron homeostasis, disrupting antioxidant defences, or employing a combination of therapeutic strategies. Despite these promising prospects, numerous challenges remain unanswered.
From a basic research perspective, it is crucial to elucidate the genetic and epigenetic basis of LSC sensitivity to ferroptosis at the molecular level. Single-cell sequencing technology can be used to analyze LSC heterogeneity at the cellular level, enabling a subpopulation-specific target population. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling, in particular, can be used to identify ferroptosis-sensitive LSC subpopulations, enabling precision medicine approaches. Leveraging this technology enables the detailed mapping of LSC subpopulations with heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis.
At the clinical translation level, challenges related to safety, specificity, drug combination strategies, and biomarker development should be addressed. For example, it is critical to optimize the dose and timing of FIs to minimize their adverse effects on normal stem cells. Designing highly specific targeted therapies, exploring synergistic therapeutic combinations, and identifying predictive molecular markers to guide individualized treatments are key areas requiring further investigation.
