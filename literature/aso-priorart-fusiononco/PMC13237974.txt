## Abstract
SOS1 is a crucial guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) that plays a key role in the activation of eukaryotic RAS GTPases, serving as a critical node in the highly conserved RAS/MAPK signaling pathway. Dysregulation of the RAS-MAPK pathway, which is frequently driven by oncogenic RAS mutations, is implicated in approximately 30% of all human cancers, making it one of the most prevalent oncogenic drivers. As a direct activator positioned upstream of RAS, and in view of the quick appearance of resistance to a wide variety of recently developed allele-specific KRAS inhibitors, SOS1 constitutes a highly promising therapeutic target for a wide spectrum of RAS-driven malignancies, offering a strategy to inhibit oncogenic signaling independently of specific RAS mutations and to potentially help overcome resistance mechanisms associated with direct RAS inhibitors. Over the past three decades, a substantial body of preclinical evidence has accumulated regarding the potential of SOS1 as a therapeutic target. Focusing preferentially on data generated using patient-derived experimental contexts, we will provide in this review a comprehensive evaluation of a variety of recently developed small-molecule SOS1 inhibitory drugs and SOS1 degraders. Specifically, we describe here their specific therapeutic responses elicited in a variety of preclinical, in vitro and in vivo models involving different specific tumor types and cell lines harboring different oncogenic driver mutations. Furthermore, we highlight promising synergistic outcomes of the SOS1 inhibitors in combination with other RAS/MAPK pathway antagonists and discuss cases where SOS1 inhibition was reported to prevent or overcome resistance to current, FDA-approved RAS inhibitors. We also outline the clinical trials currently underway evaluating SOS1 inhibitors. Additionally, we explore the relatively underestimated potential of SOS2 as a therapeutic target, alongside the current state of development for SOS2 inhibitors. Finally, we address several underexplored clinical avenues regarding SOS1 inhibition for the treatment of specific cancer subtypes.

## Background
The RAS proteins are critical signal transduction regulators that govern signaling cascades crucial for diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival. These small GTPases dynamically cycle between an inactive, GDP-bound state (RAS⋅GDP) and an active, GTP-bound conformation (RAS⋅GTP). This process is strictly regulated by both negative modulators, GTPase activating proteins (RASGAPs), and the guanine nucleotide exchange factors (RASGEFs), which are positive regulators [ – ]. Among the main RASGEF families (SOS, GRF, and GRP), extensive studies have recognized Son of sevenless (SOS) family as the most widely expressed and functionally relevant GEFs involved in activating both RAS and RAC in response to upstream cellular stimuli [ – ].
The revolutionary discoveries of the first human oncogenes during the early 1980s defined the RAS family of oncogenes [ – ]. Decades of intensive —and for many years frustrating— research followed, particularly given the failure of farnesyl transferase inhibitors [ ] and the ensuing perception of RAS as “undruggable”. This finally resulted in the development and approval of the first direct allele-specific inhibitors for the KRASG12C oncogenic mutation [ , ], establishing a paradigm shift in the treatment of tumors characterized by this prevalent KRAS alteration, with the first clinical trial of this type of drugs being approved in May 2021 [ ]. However, this revolutionary therapeutic advance has been rapidly challenged by the quick appearance of, intrinsic, adaptive or acquired resistance mechanisms [ – ]. This disappointing situation certainly warrants a continued search for new therapeutic approaches designed to target both the intrinsic impaired function of RAS and the regulatory members acting upstream or downstream of this key signaling pathway.
Importantly, oncogenic transformation driven by constitutively active mutant RAS proteins, which show impaired intrinsic GTPase activity, does not completely eliminate the requirement for upstream signaling regulation [ , ]. In fact, RAS oncogene-driven tumor cells frequently retain an important dependence on GEF activity mediated by SOS proteins [ , ]. Consequently, increased attention has been placed on the SOS proteins, especially SOS1, as promising and realistic, therapeutic targets for RAS-dependent malignancies. The rationale for this potential therapeutic option is mainly twofold: to circumvent acquired drug resistance and to find new treatments for specific RAS mutant alleles that currently lack dedicated drugs.
We acknowledge the extensive body of preclinical evidence accumulated over the last three decades demonstrating the antitumor effect of SOS1. However, this review focuses exclusively on data derived from patient-derived models. Consequently, despite their scientific significance, several critical aspects of SOS1 biology—such as mechanistic insights of SOS1 activators—fall outside the scope of this work and have not been included in this review.
In the following sections, we aim to (i) give an historical perspective summarizing the main events that allowed SOS1 to become a feasible therapeutic target in RAS-driven cancers (ii) provide a detailed assessment of current and newly developed strategies available to target SOS1 through different mechanisms (specific inhibitors and degraders) and their beneficial properties according to the oncogenic context of RAS-related human malignancies, focusing on the clinical landscape. (iii) Additionally, we will offer an updated analysis of clinical trials currently underway to evaluate the therapeutic potential of SOS1 inhibitors (SOS1i) across specific oncologic subtypes.

## The SOS familiy of GEFS: a historical perspective
The discovery of the first Sos-encoding gene occurred over three decades ago through investigations into eye development in Drosophila melanogaster. These studies demonstrated that the Sos gene product functioned downstream of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as a putative RAS activator [ – ]. Two years later, a highly expressed human gene, named SOS1, was identified that exhibited significant sequence and structural homology to the Drosophila Sos gene [ , ]. Soon after (1994), the precise location of the highly related SOS2 gene in the human genome was also identified [ ]. During the following decade, most research concerning SOS proteins relied primarily on in vitro experimental approaches focused on understanding the precise involvement of these proteins in regulating various signaling pathways —predominantly those downstream of different receptors tyrosine kinase (RTK)— as well as identifying the distinct structural domains of SOS proteins and their potential interactions with various scaffold proteins [ , , ].
The development of the first in vivo loss-of-function model was a crucial inflection point in understanding the functional relevance of SOS proteins. These models revealed the critical role of SOS1 in the successful completion of embryonic development [ , ] as the constitutive knock-out (KO) was embryonic lethal. Conversely, adult, constitutive Sos2KO mice exhibited normal viability with no noticeable phenotypic anomalies [ ]. These findings likely exerted a considerable influence, leading to SOS1 being regarded as the most relevant GEF and the primary RAS activator warranting intensive investigation.
The main challenge in the comprehensive functional characterization of SOS1 using murine in vivo models was the consistent observation of embryonic lethality in Sos1KO models. This impediment was overcome when the group led by Dr. Lawrence Samelson developed a floxed Sos1 allele model, which permitted the specific ablation of Sos1 expression [ ]. Taking advantage of this model, our group subsequently generated the first, tamoxifen-inducible, Sos1-null mouse strain that allowed for the precise temporal control of Sos1 gene deletion throughout the whole-body during adulthood [ ]. This strain was then bred to constitutive Sos2KO mice to further evaluate the in vivo effect of concomitant Sos1/Sos2 removal at the cellular, tissue, organ, and organismal levels [ ]. Importantly, our data demonstrated functional redundancy between Sos1 and Sos2 for homeostasis and survival of the full organism, although Sos1 plays a more predominant role than Sos2 [ ]. These findings established that SOS2 may play a more substantial, pivotal role than previously recognized, fulfilling a critical function when SOS1 is absent. Indeed, as detailed throughout this review, relevant findings from various research groups demonstrate that the combined inhibition or degradation of SOS1 and SOS2 elicits a potent synergistic effect within certain tumor contexts. Thus, this dual co-targeting approach emerges as a therapeutic strategy worthy of serious consideration for the management of RAS-dependent malignancies.
Up until that point, most of studies evaluating the potential therapeutic role of SOS1 in various tumor contexts employed genetic tools (e.g., shRNA, siRNA, knock-out or knock-in models, or CRISPR, among others) to successfully ablate SOS1 gene expression [ ]. A substantial milestone was achieved in 2014 with the report of the first molecule capable of inhibiting the SOS1::RAS interaction and consequently suppressing the SOS1-mediated RAS signal transmission [ ]. Although this compound exhibited a limited efficacy, it served as an essential proof-of-concept molecule. This established the foundational principles for the subsequent development of a newer, more potent, and orally bioavailable SOS1i. The number of small molecules designed to disrupt SOS1::RAS (mainly KRAS) interaction and related publications concerning the potential antitumor effect of SOS1 inhibition has grown significantly over the last decade (Supplementary Fig. 1). Some of them are being assessed in clinical trials (see the corresponding section below).
The acquisition of resistance is the primary issue identified for the specific RASi drugs. Certain SOS1i have shown the ability to overcome or prevent these mechanisms in certain contexts. To date, no resistance mechanisms associated with the currently available SOS1i have been reported, although further studies will be required to comprehensively identify resistance to these agents. In this regard, proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology presents a new experimental tool that serves to potentially abrogate SOS1 expression and, hypothetically, avoid the development of resistance mechanisms. PROTACs facilitate the proteasome-mediated degradation of a protein rather than merely inhibiting its activity. This typically causes more profound and durable effects, often showing superior antiproliferative activity and potentially overcoming the resistance seen with the small-molecule inhibitors [ ]. The first-in-class specific SOS1 PROTAC was developed in 2022 [ ] and the number of newly generated PROTACs for SOS1 has continuously increased over the last two years. Most of these compounds have been assessed in KRAS-driven human cancer models, often demonstrating superior antitumoral properties compared to SOS1i. Although these compounds show great potential, certain bioavailability targets must be achieved before they enter clinical trials.
Establishing a crucial milestone in the investigation of SOS1 as a potential therapeutic target for RAS-driven malignancies, the first phase I clinical trial evaluating the efficacy of an SOS1i (in combination with an allele-specific KRASi) for the treatment of certain solid tumors started in 2022. This timeline is summarized in Fig.  .
 Overall, just 3 decades after the discovery of the mammalian gene encoding SOS1 and thanks to the significant accumulation of knowledge from numerous preclinical studies, the first clinical trials using SOS1i are now ongoing in patients harboring KRAS-mutated solid tumors. Further studies are needed to address important unanswered questions, such as the possibility of acquiring resistance to current SOS1i, the clinical translation of SOS1 degraders, and the development of new molecules with reduced in vivo toxicity.

## Small-molecule SOS1 inhibitors
Given its critical role in regulating RAS/MAPK signaling cascades, various experimental strategies have been employed to achieve SOS1 silencing, ablation, and inhibition to subsequently impair or attenuate oncogenic signaling in tumors characterized by an altered RTK-RAS-ERK signaling axis [ ]. Prior to the development of specific SOS1i or degraders, target validation was primarily accomplished using genetic tools to suppress SOS1 gene or protein expression, including specific (i) shRNAs, (ii) siRNAs, (iii) CRISPR/Cas9 system, (iv) miRNAs, (v) dominant-negative constructs and (vi) KO or knock-in models [ ]. While extensive literature documents the antiproliferative and antitumor effects of genetically or pharmacologically mediated SOS1 ablation (using in vitro, ex vivo, or in vivo models), this review concentrates on the clinical translational aspect of SOS1 inhibition as a viable therapeutic target. Accordingly, we will focus exclusively on the agents and key findings tested in human cancer cell lines, as well as those currently undergoing clinical assessment.
Nevertheless, the clinical viability of SOS1 inhibition warrants some previous critical considerations. As outlined through this section, these small-molecule inhibitors demonstrate binding affinity not only for oncogenic RAS alleles but also for their wild-type (WT) counterparts. Since RAS proteins are crucial mediators in the systemic regulation of myriad physiological processes, it could be argued that the systemic inhibition of SOS1-mediated nucleotide exchange could precipitate significant off-target toxicities or deleterious homeostatic imbalances. However, the empirical evidence, as detailed below, have demonstrated that KRAS-mutant cells show a heightened dependency on SOS1-mediated nucleotide exchange to maintain threshold levels of GTP-loading and downstream signaling (Fig.  ). In contrast, most RASWT cells remain relatively resilient to SOS1 inhibition, thereby facilitating a favorable therapeutic window of the SOS1i, and supporting the notion of these inhibitor molecules as feasible therapeutic approaches in RAS-dependent malignancies.
 The following paragraphs describe a series of developed drugs (ordered by discovery timeline) that were characterized by their ability to disrupt functional protein-protein interaction (PPI) between SOS1 and RAS proteins. The descriptive framework adopted for all SOS1i is structured as follows: (i) a concise characterization of the chemical properties of each SOS1i; (ii) a summary of main in vitro findings, both as monotherapy and in combination therapies, where applicable; (iii) a brief overview of the primary in vivo outcomes, evaluating both single-agent efficacy and synergistic potential; and (iv) additional considerations regarding the reported impact of some of these inhibitors on acquired resistance mechanisms.

## NSC-658974
This was the first small molecule reported (2014) as capable of directly targeting SOS1::RAS PPI [ ]. Mechanistically, this thiazole ring-enriched molecule (Supplementary Table 1) directly and specifically binds to the SOS1 catalytic site, disrupting the SOS1::RAS PPI. In vitro data demonstrated that the antiproliferative effect of this compound was notably restricted to cell lines expressing the KRASWT allele, although it was achieved at high concentrations (> 40 µM), with no efficacy observed against KRAS-mutant cells [ ]. NSC-658497 did not progress into advanced preclinical or clinical development despite its thorough in vitro and biochemical characterization. Subsequently, the same research group developed two related small molecules, UC-773587 and UC-857993, which also inhibited the SOS1::RAS PPI but only exhibited antiproliferative effects at prohibitively high concentrations (> 60 µM) [ ].

## BAY-293
BAY-293 is an early-generation SOS1i that was identified by Bayer via fragment-based and high-throughput screenings assays [ ]. This molecule (Supplementary Table 1), functions as an allosteric inhibitor, binding to the catalytic pocket of SOS1 to disrupt the PPI between SOS1 and KRAS (IC50 of 21 nM) [ ]. Although initially conceptualized as a pan-RAS activator inhibitor, its therapeutic effects have been primarily investigated in the context of oncogenic KRAS [ ]. BAY-293 monotherapy has demonstrated dose-dependent antiproliferative and cytotoxic activity across a broad spectrum of cancer cell lines, albeit with high IC50 values (ranging from 1 µM to 18 µM) compared to subsequently developed SOS1i. BAY-293 was tested in cell lines from different cancer types with distinct KRAS mutations (G12C/D/S/V, G13D, or Q61H) as well as some KRASWT cell lines (Table ) [ – ]. Notably, BAY-293 did not show significant selectivity for oncogenic KRAS tumor cells compared with KRASWT cells [ ]. BAY-293 also inhibited cell proliferation in EGFR- and SOS1-mutated human NSCLC cells (Table ) [ , ]. Other RAS/MAPK-related tumor cells also exhibit dose-dependent sensitivity to BAY-293 (Table ) [ , ]. In addition, BAY-293-mediated SOS1 inhibition significantly suppresses tumor cell growth in vitro and markedly increases the sensitivity of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells to Imatinib [ ] (Table ).
 Interestingly, the antiproliferative properties of BAY-293 are significantly enhanced when combined with other RAS pathway-related inhibitors, including EGFRi, KRASG12C inhibitors, and MEKi, as well as glucose metabolism modulators, and certain chemotherapeutics [ , , , ]. All this information is shown in Table .
 Although BAY-293 has served as a crucial tool for in vitro research, extensive in vivo xenograft studies have not been reported, probably because of its poor bioavailability [ ]. In this regard, Bayer has developed a supposedly optimized version, termed BAY-3498264, which is currently undergoing a phase I clinical trial. The preclinical characterization of BAY-3498264 demonstrated a potent and selective inhibition of the SOS1::RAS interaction [ ]. Importantly, BAY-3498264 inhibited the rebound of RAS/MAPK signal transmission induced by the KRASG12C inhibitor Sotorasib, resulting in a sustained decrease in ERK phosphorylation when dually administered [ ]. Additional in vitro proliferation assays demonstrated the synergistic properties of BAY-3498264 with certain MAPK pathway inhibitors [ ]. These findings, including synergistic effects observed in KRASG12C mutant CDX and PDX models in vivo, supported its clinical assessment in combination with KRASG12C inhibitors and other RAS/MAPK-directed therapies [ ].
In summary, although not a clinical candidate, BAY-293 has been preclinically assessed in vitro, laying the groundwork for small-molecule-based SOS1 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy in RAS/MAPK-related malignancies. Although often assayed at elevated concentrations, BAY-293 exhibits antiproliferative properties in synergistic combinations with KRASG12C inhibitors, rather than as a monotherapy therapeutic approach.

## BI-3406
Among all available small-molecule SOS1i, BI-3406 is arguably the most extensively characterized to date. A landmark study by Hofmann et al. (2021) identified BI-3406 as a potent, selective, and highly effective drug against SOS1 but not SOS2 [ ]. Developed by Boehringer Ingelheim, this small molecule was discovered through a high-throughput screening process, similar to that leading to the identification of the SOS1i BAY-293 [ ].
Sharing a quinazoline scaffold with BAY-293 (Supplementary Table 1), BI-3406 specifically inhibits the interaction between SOS1 and inactive, GDP-bound, KRAS (IC50 of 5 nM) [ ]. By occupying the catalytic domain of SOS1, this molecule effectively prevents the formation of active KRAS, thereby downregulating RAS-mediated signal transmission [ ]. Therefore, this mechanism of action highlighted its potential as a therapeutic agent for KRAS-driven cancers [ ].
Since its discovery, BI-3406-mediated SOS1::KRAS inhibition has been assessed in a broad range of RAS/MAPK-driven cancers. Of note, in a comparative analysis of the antiproliferative effects SOS1i across a panel of 24 human tumor cell lines, BI-3406 exhibited superior potency compared to BAY-293 [ ]. In vitro studies demonstrated that BI-3406 monotherapy decreased the formation of GTP-loaded RAS and showed antiproliferative activity (or sensitivity), with IC50 values ranging from 16 to 52 nM in 3D proliferation assays [ ]. These results underscored the importance of using 3D culture models to accurately assess the anti-proliferative efficacy of these SOS1i compounds, indicating that this is an important point to be considered when planning to analyze the effect of these kinds of inhibitors [ , – ] (Table ). In addition to the antiproliferative activity of BI-3406 against tumor cells, this compound has demonstrated an important antitumor effect mediated through its modulation of the tumor microenvironment, the specifics of which will be described in a subsequent section. BI-3406 monotherapy also showed antiproliferative effects in cell lines with EGFR mutations or in a human NSCLC cell line harboring an atypical SOS1 activating mutation (Table ) [ ]. A similar impairment of cell growth has also been found in patient-derived tumor spheroid models holding KRASG12C/D/S/V and KRASG13D mutations [ ].
However, BI-3406 monotherapy showed limited efficacy in 2D cultures for certain KRAS mutant human tumor cell lines (G12D/V and G13D) (Table ) [ , ]. It also lacked direct inhibitory effects (both in 2D and 3D cell cultures) against less frequent, oncogenic KRAS mutations (KRASG12R or KRASQ61H/L/K), the other canonical forms of RAS: NRAS (NRASG12D, NRASQ61L/R/K) and HRAS (HRASG12D/G13D) [ , , , ] (Table ). Similarly, cancer cell lines harboring BRAF or NF1LOF mutations remained resistant to BI-3406 [ , , ] (Table ). Finally, differing from BAY-293, in vitro BI-3406 monotherapy also demonstrated negligible, or restricted antiproliferative effects in most of KRASWT tumor cells analyzed (Table ) [ , , , , ].
 The antiproliferative activity of BI-3406 is markedly enhanced when administered in combination with other inhibitors targeting the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway. These synergistic combinations include tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKi), allele-specific KRASi, MEKi or the PI3K/Akt inhibitor Buparlisib. These effects were evaluated across an extensive panel of human cancer cell lines—predominantly derived from CRC, NSCLC, and PDAC—harboring, most of them, EGFR and KRAS mutations (Table ) [ , , , , , – ]. This synergy has been even observed in dually BI-3406 and Avutometinib (MEKi) treated NF1del melanoma (MEL) cells [ ]. Comprehensive data regarding these synergistic interactions are detailed in Table .
However, it must be emphasized that such combinations do not consistently cause a synergistic antiproliferative effect. For instance, certain KRASG12C oncogenic human cells of CRC (B8182), NSCLC (SW1573, HOP-62, HCC44, NCI-H1792, and NCI-H2030), and esophageal cancer (KYSE-410) did not (or barely) exhibit additive effects of Adagrasib and BI-3406 [ , ]. It has been proposed that the lack of effect of dual G12Ci: SOS1i could be due to SOS2-mediated compensatory mechanisms. In fact, synergy was recovered when SOS2 was genetically depleted, thereby reinforcing the idea of SOS2 as an ancillary therapeutic target under certain conditions [ ]. The specific mutational landscape of the cell lines analyzed conditioned potential synergistic effects of BI-3406 with other RAS/MAPK inhibitors. In this regard, human cancer cell lines harboring the KRASQ61 mutations (Calu-6, NCI-H460, NCI-H650, and NCI-H1155) were resistant to BI-3406 plus Trametinib combination [ ], likely due to differences in the RAS GDP-GTP cycling. In fact, BI-3406 and Trametinib synergy was only effective in cell lines that lacked PIK3CA co-mutations, as demonstrated by using the LU99A cells [ ].
Of crucial importance from the clinical perspective, it has been reported that combined G12Ci: SOS1i treatment overcame intrinsic/adaptive resistance to G12Ci and further limited and delayed the acquisition of resistance [ ]. In this regard, BI-3406 prevented adaptive resistance to Trametinib-driven MEK inhibition [ , ]. Importantly, this effect was further enhanced following, genetically mediated, SOS2 depletion [ ]. This is probably so because BI-3406 attenuates MEKi-induced feedback reactivation and enhances sensitivity to them, making it a valuable combination partner across various KRAS-mutant contexts. BI-3406 also prevented resistance, limited the development of acquired resistance and re-sensitized established resistant cells to Osimertinib in EGFRmut NSCLC cells [ ].
 In vivo assays demonstrated that in monotherapy, BI-3406-mediated SOS1 inhibition (50 mg/kg; bid; 21 days) resulted in potent tumor volume reduction in certain KRAS oncogenic xenograft models including G12C (PDAC: Mia PaCa-2), G12S (NSCLC: A549), G12V (PDAC: PATX53; CRC: SW620), and G13D (CRC: LoVo) [ ]. Nevertheless, and strikingly different from the data obtained in vitro, single-agent administration of BI-3406 did not demonstrate an impact on tumor progression in other KRASG12C-mutated xenograft models (NSCLC: NCI-H2122; CRC: B8032, C1047, F3008, SW837) [ , ]. However, preclinical evidence in NRAS- or HRAS-driven tumors and in difficult KRAS alleles (e.g., G12R; Q61H/L/K) remains sparse or refractory, and most in vivo evaluations were conducted in xenograft/PDX models without intact immunity, limiting broad generalization to all RAS-driven malignancies. In fact, according to the data obtained in vitro, a PDX model of PDAC with the KRASQ61K mutation remained unresponsive to BI-3406 monotherapy [ ]. Similarly, this small molecule did not exhibit any significant effect when applied as a single agent in MEL, BRAF-mutated or NF1del xenograft models [ , ].
To validate the in vitro data, combo treatments involving BI-3406 together with different selective inhibitors of the RAS/MAPK pathway has also been extensively studied using in vivo models. In concordance with most in vitro observations, BI-3406 was more potent when combined with other inhibitors of the EGFR-RAS-ERK axis than when administered as a single agent. In this regard, KRASG13D-mutated CRC xenograft models showed synergistic antitumoral effects when BI-3406 and the EGFRi Afatinib were dually applied [ ]. Thatikonda and co-workers recently demonstrated that BI-3406 synergized with Adagrasib, leading to a significant tumor growth inhibition (TGI) in KRASG12C-mutated PDX models of NSCLC (NCI-H2122) or CRC (B8032, F3008, SW837) [ ]. Moreover, BI-3406-mediated SOS1 inhibition overcomes in vivo acquired Adagrasib resistance [ ]. Hofmann and colleagues demonstrated that BI-3406 single administration resulted in TGI in 2 out 4 different KRAS mutant PDX models. Importantly, they demonstrated significant synergistic effects of BI-3406 and Trametinib in all KRAS-mutated (G12C, G13D) PDX models used in this study [ ]. This synergy was also observed in the oncogenic KRASG12V (PATX53) and KRASQ61K (PATX216) PDX models of PDAC [ ]. Similarly, although BI-3406 monotherapy did not show any impact on tumor progression in oncogenic NF1del MEL xenograft models, BI-3406 demonstrated a potent TGI when combined with the MEKi, Avutometinib, in concordance with in vitro assays previously detailed [ ]. In summary, combining BI-3406 with KRASi or MEKi significantly improved the depth and durability of response, particularly in NSCLC and CRC cancer models, thus overcoming, at least in part, KRASi resistance. These results highlighted the potential of SOS1i to broaden the clinical response to KRASi.
As described throughout this review, virtually all studies that have employed SOS1i as a therapeutic tool were aimed at treating RAS-driven tumors. Recently, the group of Dr. Santos has also evaluated the therapeutic potential of this molecule for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), driven by the BCR/ABL1 fusion [ ]. Remarkably, they demonstrated, in two BCR/ABL-mutated human CML cell lines (K562 and KCL22) and in primary bone marrow cells obtained from patients, that combined administration of BI-3406 and Imatinib synergically decreased proliferation and enhanced apoptosis compared with single-agent-treated tumorigenic cells [ ]. Further analyses revealed that the concomitant administration of BI-3406 and the TKi Imatinib or Ponatinib, significantly restored Imatinib sensitivity in CML patient cells holding Imatinib-resistant mutations, and that BI-3406 also enhanced the sensitivity of K562 and KCL22 cells to later generation TKi such as Dasatinib or Nilotinib [ ].
Although the potential synergistic effects of BI-3406 have been evaluated mostly in combination with inhibitors of the RAS/MAPK pathway, its effect has also been assessed in combination with other, not-related to the RAS/MAPK pathway, inhibitors or with compounds possessing chemotherapeutic activity. Thus, the combination of BI-3406 and the chemotherapeutic drug, Irinotecan was described as a promising therapeutic option for KRAS-driven CRC [ ]. This promising therapeutic approach motivated a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04627142) for patients with advanced KRAS-mutated CRC, which was, unfortunately, discontinued in 2022. More recently, preclinical studies have shown additive and synergistic cytotoxicity when BI-3406 is combined with the BCL-2i Venetoclax in KRAS mutant human cancer cells [ ].
Interestingly, BI-3406 has sparked discoveries not only in oncology, but also in RAS-related diabetes research. In particular, BI-3406 has recently been defined as a potential therapeutic approach to regenerate pancreatic insulin-producing β-cells [ ].
A summary of relevant conclusions regarding the specific therapeutic effects of BI-3406 reported in the literature may include the following: (i) Unlike BAY-293, single-agent BI-3406 administration does not affect the majority of tumor cells harboring RAS alleles, even those with BRAF mutations; (ii) Its primary antitumor activity is strongest in KRAS-mutant cancers and those with EGFR mutations, with minimal effects in tumors dependent on NRAS and HRAS; (iii) BI-3406 is most effective against G12 and G13 allele mutations (albeit with certain exceptions, such as the G12R mutation), showing poor therapeutic effects against Q61 mutations; (iv) The compound´s efficacy is significantly enhanced when combined with other inhibitors of the EGFR-RAS-ERK axis; (v) BI-3406 has proven to be useful to prevent or overcome acquired resistance to certain clinically relevant RAS/MAPK pathway inhibitors.

## MRTX0902
MRTX0902 (now identified as BMS-986509) is a potent, selective, brain-penetrant, and orally bioavailable SOS1i that was developed in 2022 by Mirati Therapeutics [ ]. Its ability to cross the blood-brain-barrier is likely attributed to its smaller molecular size and moderate lipophilicity compared to other SOS1i. The core pyridopyridazine structure of MRTX0902 (Supplementary Table 1), acts as an allosteric inhibitor by binding to the SOS1 catalytic site. This binding then prevents the PPI between SOS1 and KRAS (IC50 values ranging from 13.8 to 30.7 nM depending on the specific KRAS mutational status), thereby blocking RAS signaling pathway activation [ ].
When administered as a single-agent, MRTX0902 (at doses ranging from 15 to 220 nM) significantly reduces RAS/MAPK-mediated signaling and cell viability under 3D cell culture conditions. This effect has been observed in a wide range of cancer cell lines harboring a variety of mutations related to the RTK-RAS-MAPK axis including: EGFRmut, KRASG12C/S, KRASG13D, class III BRAF G466V/G596R, NF1LOF mutations, SOS1 activating mutations and even a KRASWT cell line (Table ) [ , , ]. However, this therapeutic effect was reported to be milder in other KRASWT or KRAS oncogenic human cancer cell lines [ , ] (Table ). As far as we know, in vitro synergistic effects of MRTX0902 in human cancer cell lines have been only proven with Adagrasib in the Mia PaCa-2 cells [ ] (Table ).
 In vivo studies of MRTX0902 showed TGI in a dose-dependent manner when administered as a single agent (50 mg/kg; bid; 25 days) in the Mia PaCa-2 KRASG12C-mutated or NCI-H1435 NF1K615N-mutated xenograft models [ , ]. On the other hand, MRTX0902 monotherapy (50 mg/kg; bid; 25 days) did not exhibit antitumoral properties in oncogenic KRASG12C CDX (NCI-H2122) or PDX (LU1692 or CR6256) models [ ]. Notably, MRTX0902 (at the same dosage than as single agent) exerted synergistic effects with Adagrasib [ ]. This synergy was observed in a majority of oncogenic KRASG12C patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models across several cancer types, including NSCLC (SW1573, NCI-H2122, LU11692, LU99), PDAC (Mia PaCa-2), and CRC (CR2528, CR6256, SW837) [ , ].
The combination of MRTX0902 with the MEKi Avutometinib (models harboring oncogenic NF1K615N or PTPN11A72S mutations) or the TKi Osimertinib (models harboring oncogenic EGFRex19del or EGFRL858R/T790M mutations) further enhanced antitumor efficacy and was well tolerated in CDX models. Furthermore, the dual treatment regimen of MRTX0902 and Adagrasib was shown to overcome acquired resistance that is often associated with KRAS amplification [ ]. Taken together, these results support the use of MRTX0902 along with other RAS/MAPK-related inhibitors as a viable strategy for tumors characterized by upregulated RAS/MAPK pathway [ ].
MRTX0902 (BMS-986506) is currently being evaluated in a Phase 1/2, open-label, multicenter, multiple expansion cohort clinical trial (NCT05578092, Table  ). This study is assessing its efficacy as monotherapy and in combination with Adagrasib and includes patients with advanced solid tumors characterized by mutations within the KRAS-MAPK pathway, such as SOS1, PTPN11, KRASG12C, class III BRAF, EGFR, or inactivating NF1 mutations. A key feature is the inclusion of patients with brain metastases. Recent data presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting (2025) demonstrated that the triplet combination of BMS-986,506, Adagrasib and an EGFRi achieved superior antitumor efficacy in KRASG12C mutant cells and PDOs compared with doublet or monotherapy regimens (https://news.bms.com/news/corporate-financial/2025/Bristol-Myers-Squibb-to-Present-Data-at-ESMO-2025-Showcasing-Progress-of-Oncology-Portfolio-Across-Diverse-Tumor-Types/default.aspx).
 

## Other small-molecule SOS1 inhibitors
Following the detailed descriptions of the above mentioned SOS1i drugs, the discovery and optimization of novel inhibitors remains a sustained research priority. A clear indicator of the growing relevance of SOS1 as a potential therapeutic target for treating RAS-dependent tumors is the high number of novel, recently developed molecules, which exhibit the capacity to inhibit the SOS1::RAS PPI and may serve as additional therapeutic options in the future.
RMC-0331 (also known as RMC-023) is a small molecule inhibitor developed by Revolution Medicines that was presented at the meeting of the American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) (2021). This compound features a pyrrolo[3,4-d]pyrimidin scaffold [ ] (Supplementary Table 1) and is characterized as a potent, selective, and orally bioavailable SOS1i, preventing KRAS::SOS1 PPI at IC50 of 71 nM [ ]. However, it did not exhibit any effect on ERK activation or cell proliferation (at least at 1 µM) in BRAFV600-mutated human MEL cells (WM858 and WM983B) [ ]. Oral administration resulted in a dose-dependent (100–250 mg/kg; q.d; immunocompetent mice) TGI in KRAS-dependent cancers [ ]. Furthermore, enhanced antitumor activity was observed when RMC-0331 was co-administered with other RAS pathway inhibitors [ ].
Compound 8u is the selected member of a newly designed SOS1i series, disrupting SOS1-mediated KRASG12D activation at an IC50 of 95.4 nM [ ]. From a chemical point of view, it is based on the pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidin-7-one scaffold, which differs from the aminoquinazoline core used in the design of BI-3406 or BAY-293 (Supplementary Table 1) [ ]. This small molecule demonstrated (at 1 µM) in vitro downregulation of RAS/MAPK and PI3K/Akt signal transmission in the KRASG12D-mutated AsPC-1 cells [ ]. Consistently, compound 8u impaired cell proliferation in a 3D cell growth assay in a panel of distinct KRAS-mutated (G12C/D/S/V) human cancer cells (Supplementary Table 2), with IC50 values ranging from 3.5 to 115 nM. In contrast, it did not affect the proliferative capability of the A375 KRASWT MEL cells [ ]. This compound additionally showed synergistic antiproliferative effects when combined with the KRASG12C inhibitor MRTX849 and the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 [ ].
RGT-018. Regor Therapeutics Group developed this compound in 2024, as a novel, potent, and highly selective SOS1i [ ]. RGT-018 is a pyridopyrimidine-based compound (Supplementary Table 1) specifically designed to bind to the catalytic domain of SOS1 with high affinity, demonstrating exceptional specificity over its paralog, SOS2. This compound induces significant antiproliferative effects across a wide panel of cancer cell lines (3D growth) harboring various KRAS mutations (Supplementary Table 2) at concentrations ranging from 100 nM to 1 µM [ ]. This inhibitor also demonstrated efficacy in human cell lines expressing the EGFR mutations or the activating SOS1N233Y mutation (Supplementary Table 2), a known mechanism of acquired resistance to the current KRASG12C or MEK inhibitors [ ]. In contrast, RGT-018 exhibited marginal antiproliferative activity in the KRASWT A375 cells. The in vivo effect of this compound was evaluated using oncogenic KRASG12C xenograft models (NCI-H358 and Mia PaCa-2) or CT26 syngeneic allografts (KRASG12D). In monotherapy, RGT-018 (100 mg/kg; q.d) successfully suppressed KRAS signaling and substantially induced TGI [ ]. In vitro and in vivo studies have established that RGT-018 synergizes with various RAS/MAPK-related targeted therapies [ ]. Combinations of RGT-018 with MEKi (Trametinib or Cobimetinib) or inhibitors of KRASG12C (Sotorasib or Adagrasib), KRASG12D (MRTX1133), EGFR (Afatinib and Cetuximab), or CDK4/6 (Abemaciclib) have been shown to enhance antitumor efficacy, leading to tumor regression in animal models in some cases [ ].
CK-1001 (Cyrus Therapeutics) is a novel, potent, selective, orally bioavailable SOS1i that effectively disrupts PPI between SOS1 and H/N/KRAS (WT and mutant) with IC50 values ranging from 10 to 300 nM [ ]. In vitro drug combination studies demonstrated potent synergistic effects when CK-1001 (5 µM) was combined with EGFRi (Lazertinib or Osimertinib), KRASG12C inhibitors (Sotorasib, Adagrasib, JDQ443), or the KRASG12D inhibitor, MRTX1133 [ ]. Furthermore, CK-1001 has demonstrated value in managing drug resistance by effectively delaying the acquisition of therapeutic resistance in KRASG12C and EGFR-mutated cell lines [ ]. Its antitumoral properties were further evaluated in vivo in KRAS-driven xenograft models. Monotherapy with this compound (25 mg/kg; q.d; 21 days) resulted in a significant TGI, comparable to that obtained upon single administration of Sotorasib (10 mg/kg; q.d; 21 days), in a KRASG12C-mutated model (NCI-H358). In this very same model, CK-1001 and Sotorasib showed a potent antitumoral synergy, similar to that obtained by combining the SOS1i MRTX0902 and Sotorasib but required a lower dose of CK-1001 (25 mg/kg) versus MRTX0902 (50 mg/kg). Analogous results were obtained across other KRASG12C models of NSCLC (NCI-H1373, NCI-H2122) or PDAC (Mia PaCa-2) [ ]. Furthermore, the combination of CK-1001 (50 mg/kg; q.d; 21 days) with Osimertinib in an EGFR-mutated (NCI-H1975) mouse xenograft model synergized and increased overall survival [ ].
Compound 4. Differing from what has been published for the rest of SOS1i, Zeng and colleagues recently reported the synthesis of a novel molecule showing the capability of dually inhibiting both EGFR (IC50 = 14.6 nM) and SOS1 (IC50 = 8.3 nM) [ ]. This compound is based on a quinazoline ring system, a linker, a piperidine ring, and an acrylamide-based warhead that supposedly allows a covalent modification and subsequent inhibition of both RAS/MAPK-related proteins (Supplementary Table 1) [ ]. This molecule demonstrated a higher antiproliferative activity than BI-3406 in oncogenic KRASG13D tumor cells, and further significantly reduced 3D cell proliferation in KRASG12C/S/V mutant cancer cells (Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. In contrast, compound 4 showed no inhibitory effects in the KRASWT A375 cells, suggesting that this molecule preferentially serves for KRAS-driven malignancies.
B5a is a recently developed SOS1i based on the 5,8-dihydropyrido[4,3-d]pyrimidin-7(6 H)-one scaffold (Supplementary Table 1) [ ]. This compound binds to the catalytic domain of SOS1 and disrupts SOS1::KRASG12C PPI (IC50 of 11.11 nM) [ ]. B5a demonstrated a similar reduction in ERK phosphorylation and the consequent impairment in K562 cell proliferation to that observed with BI-3406 [ ].
Compound 8d belongs to a series of SOS1i that were developed by attaching certain rings to a tricyclic quinazoline scaffold (Supplementary Table 1) and was recently developed and characterized by Qing and co-workers (2025). Through its binding to the allosteric pocket of the catalytic domain of SOS1, this small molecule efficiently disrupts SOS1::KRASG12C interaction (IC50: 5.1 nM) [ ]. In vitro assays demonstrated that this compound (IC50 values ranging from 0.02 to 0.12 µM) exhibit a more potent antiproliferative activity (3D cultures) than BI-3406, in the KRASG12C/V and KRASG13D human cancer cells (Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. In contrast, the antiproliferative properties were milder in the KRASWT NCI-H520 cells [ ]. The good pharmacokinetic (PK) properties showed by 8d in vivo encouraged the authors to evaluate the antitumoral properties (50 mg/kg; bid; 21 days) of this small molecule in a KRASG12C-mutated PDAC xenograft model (Mia PaCa-2). These data suggested that compound 8d exhibits superior performance in suppressing tumor growth compared with BI-3406 [ ].
Compound 12f is a newly characterized SOS1i that belongs to a novel chemical class of nicotinamide derivatives (Supplementary Table 1), which disrupt SOS1:KRASG12C PPI (IC50 of 8.29 nM [ ]. Compound 12f showed superior antiproliferative activity compared with BI-3406 (IC50: 174 vs. 322 nM, respectively) in the KRASG12C-mutated NCI-H358 cells in a 3D proliferation assay (Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. In addition, when evaluated in vitro, this SOS1i reduced ERK activation in PC-9 (EGFRmut) cells, similar to the effect achieved with BI-3406 [ ]. Although orally bioavailable in mice, its overall PK profile is considered suboptimal, likely due to its high systemic clearance [ ].
Compound 21 is a newly developed SOS1i featuring a quinazoline core similar to that of BI-3406 or BAY-293 [ ]. The novelty of this small molecule, compared to BI-3406, lies in a slight structural modification -the addition of a cyclopropyl group- to improve properties such as permeability and solubility (Supplementary Table 1) [ ]. 3D proliferation in vitro assays demonstrated a potent antiproliferative activity in KRASG12C cancer cells (Supplementary Table 2) as well as synergistic effect with Sotorasib in Mia PaCa-2 cells [ ]. Compound 21 was also evaluated in vivo, revealing that it is orally bioavailable with a PK suitable for systemic distribution. Compound 21 administration (12 mg/kg; bid; 9 days and 50 mg/kg, bid for two additional weeks) significantly induced TGI, showing similar, although slightly higher, values to those obtained with BI-3406 in the KRASG12C oncogenic (Mia PaCa-2) xenograft model [ ].
HH0043. Developed in 2024 by Haihe Biopharma (Supplementary Table 1) [ ]. This compound interrupted the interaction between SOS1::KRAS with an IC50 value of 5.4 nM [ ]. In vitro and vivo studies of HH0043 revealed a significant antiproliferative effect and TGI in a KRASG12C (NCI-H358) xenograft model, that was even superior to that of BI-3406 when administered at the same doses [ ].
HH100937 is a recently, specific, SOS1i developed by Haihe Biopharma [ ]. HH100937 disrupted SOS1::KRASmulti (WT, G12C/D/V) PPI with IC50 values lower than those showed by BI-3406 or MRTX0902 (from 7.3 nM for KRASWT to 11.7 nM for KRASG12V) [ ]. In vitro studies demonstrated that this compound synergized with Sotorasib in the NCI-H2122 KRASG12C-mutated cells and with the ERKi HH2710 in a wide panel of KRAS-mutated human cells [ ]. Oral administration of HH100937 revealed its efficacy (higher than that showed by MRTX0902) when used as single-agent (at both 25 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg; bid; 28 days) in both KRASG12C (Mia PaCa-2) and PTPN11A72S (LN229) PDX models [ ]. Moreover, HH100937 showed a potent TGI when combined with Sotorasib in KRASG12C oncogenic CDX and PDX models [ ].
HM101207, developed by Hanmi pharmaceutical, it has been recently presented at the AACR meeting (2025) [ ]. HM101207 inhibited SOS1::KRASmulti (WT, G12C/D/R/S/V, and Q61H) PPI with IC50 values ranging from 18 nM for KRASWT to 110 nM for KRASG12R [ ]. In vitro studies demonstrated that HM101207 reduced (at ∼1 µM) 3D spheroid growth in a similar way as MRTX0902 in cancer cell lines mutated in the KRAS/MAPK pathway, such as KRASG12C, gain-of-function PTPN11 mutations, and NF1LOF mutations (Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. It showed synergy with RTKi, Adagrasib or the MEKi, Avutometinib, Trametinib and Binimatinib in the KRASG12C-mutated NCI-H2122 (NSCLC) and SNU-1411 (CRC) [ , ]. In vivo studies showed that single-agent administration (100 mg/kg; q.d; 21 days) inhibited tumor growth in a PDAC KRASG12C mutated model (Mia PaCa-2) but not in the KRASG12C CRC (SW837) or the KRASG12D PDAC (AsPC-1) models. Importantly, HM101207 significantly enhanced TGI when combined with Adagrasib, Sotorasib or the MEKi, Binimatinib in KRASG12C/D PDAC models [ , ]. This molecule is currently undergoing new drug-enabling GLP-toxicity studies to support its further clinical development [ ] (https://hanmipharm.com/science/pipeline/focused/hm101207.hm). According to their results, when combined with inhibitors of the RTK-RAS-ERK pathway, this compound exhibits more potent synergism than MRTX0902.
HW071021 is a novel compound developed by the Wuhan Humanwell Innovative Drug R&D Center, and its preliminary data were presented at the 2025 AACR meeting [ ]. This compound has an IC50 of 8.5 nM in the SOS1 binding assay. Preclinical in vitro assessments showed that HW071021 treatment attenuated the RAS/MAPK signaling cascade and a notable reduction in ERK phosphorylation in a KRASG13D-mutated cell line (Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. Oral administration of HW071021 as a single-agent significantly caused TGI (78%) in a murine xenograft model of KRASG12C-driven oncogenicity (Mia PaCa-2 cells) [ ]. When combined with the EGFRi Osimertinib, HW071021 demonstrated enhanced antitumor activity compared with single-agent administration in an EGFRmut (NCI-H1975) xenograft model [ ]. This compound has been approved by the Chinese regulatory authority to start clinical trials in human subjects (NCT06882135) [ ].
SGR-4174 appears as a novel, potent, and highly selective inhibitor of the SOS1::KRASG12C complex. This compound was rationally designed using the Schrödinger, Inc computational platform (Schrödinger, Inc., https://www.schrodinger.com), and its features were presented at the 2025 AACR meeting [ ]. Mechanistically, SGR-4174 binds to the catalytic domain of SOS1, disrupting SOS1::KRASG12C PPI (IC50 of 13 nM) [ ]. Preclinical in vitro studies demonstrated that SGR-4174 administration (IC50 values ranging from 42 to107 nM) resulted in a robust impairment of RAS-mediated signal transduction and reduced cell viability (3D cell cultures) across a wide spectrum of human cancer cell lines harboring KRASG12C/D/V or EGFR mutations (since we had no access to the panel of the specific cells employed in this study, this information is not included in Supplementary Table 2) [ ]. In KRASG12C-mutated tumor xenograft models, including NSCLC and PDAC, single-agent administration of SGR-4174 elicited dose-dependent TGI (please note that we were unable to find the precise dosing regimens employed in this study) [ ]. In addition, concomitant administration of SGR-4174 and Sotorasib resulted in a deeper and more sustained inhibition of KRAS signal transmission, pronounced inhibition of cell proliferation in 3D cell culture models, and tumor regression in xenograft models when compared to the respective monotherapies [ ]. Furthermore, along with Trametinib, SGR-4174 prevented the reactivation of the KRAS pathway, which is typically observed upon MEK inhibition, leading to increased tumor cell death and stasis [ ].
The use of advanced computational systems or machine learning systems are rapidly becoming indispensable tools for identifying novel SOS1i series or to unveil high-potential binding sites to inhibit PPI between SOS1 and oncogenic RAS variants, a strategy that will also be presumably essential for the future development of specific SOS2i. In this regard, advanced in silico analyses identified promising compounds that potentially exhibited the capability to inhibit SOS1::KRAS PPI [ ]. Recently, machine learning tools and subsequent biological activity validations have allowed the characterization of a novel structural series with a shared carboxylic acid backbone that can disrupt the SOS1::KRAS interaction [ ]. Leffler et al. (2025) used free-energy perturbation simulations to increase the potency of SOS1i by identifying and targeting new previously unexplored sites. Their findings demonstrated that forming salt bridges among the basic groups of the ligand and the solvent-exposed E906 and E909 acidic residues of the BI-3406 significantly improved the binding affinity and biochemical potency of BI-3406 to SOS1 [ ]. Taking advantage of these observations, the authors developed a new small molecule (tricyclic SOS1 derivative) that selectively targets SOS1 with potent ERK inhibition and cell viability impairment in KRASG12C NCI-H358 cancer cells [ ].
Further computational models recently led to propose two novel putative allosteric binding pockets (P1 and P2), located at the interdomain interface of the SOS1::KRASG13D complex [ ]. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations were employed to evaluate the stability and functional impact of candidate inhibitors bound to the complex. Among the evaluated molecules, STOCK1N-09823 significantly disrupted the crucial PPI required for SOS1-mediated KRAS activation [ ]. Another very recent manuscript based on structure-based virtual screening on the AstraZeneca compound collection identified several aminobenzothiazole dioxide binders as a promising starting point to develop specific molecules with the ability to disrupt SOS1::KRAS interaction through their action on the catalytic site of SOS1 [ ].
Although further experimental validation is required to fully demonstrate the theoretical efficacy of these compounds, these computational approaches provide a robust framework for identifying potential therapeutic targets and developing novel compounds capable of disrupting the interaction between SOS1 and oncogenic RAS variants.
In summary, most so far developed SOS1i exert their primary mechanism of action through highly specific binding to the catalytic domain of SOS1. Despite diverse chemical scaffolds (Supplementary Table 1), these molecules demonstrate a congruent therapeutic profile, although only MRTX0902 has demonstrated the capability to cross the blood-brain-barrier. Overall, these compounds exhibit efficacy (or they have been preferentially assessed) in solid tumors—mainly NSCLC, PDAC, or CRC—driven by EGFR and, notably, in KRAS mutants harboring G12C mutation. In this regard, while demonstrating discernible antiproliferative activity as monotherapy (Fig. a), the current body of experimental evidence confirms that SOS1i primarily function to potentiate the efficacy of KRASG12C inhibitors, such as Sotorasib and Adagrasib [ ]. The synergistic potential of SOS1i and GDP-bound state-selective RAS inhibitors (e.g., G12C inhibitors) and is fundamentally based on the dynamics of the RAS nucleotide cycle (Fig. b). Despite their oncogenic nature, many RAS mutants continue to cycle between active and inactive states, with mutations at codon 12 (particularly G12C) maintaining relatively high cycling rates [ – ]. By inhibiting SOS1, the transition to the active GTP-bound state is ameliorated, effectively sequestering RAS in its inactive GDP-bound form and expanding the substrate pool available for covalent modification, thereby positioning SOS1 inhibition as a strategic approach to enhance the therapeutic window of GDP-selective RAS targeting. SOS1i also demonstrate synergy when combined with EGFRi or MEKi, a mechanism primarily attributed to the suppression of compensatory feedback loops (Fig. c, d).
On the other hand, most of these compounds demonstrate negligible antitumoral activity in the majority models harboring HRAS or NRAS mutations. Interestingly, these mutations frequently impact at codons 13 or 61, and often exhibit severely impaired intrinsic hydrolysis, hence remaining predominantly in the GTP-bound state [ – ] and thus reducing the potential therapeutic effect upon SOS1 inhibition. Nevertheless, an important fact to be considered in future studies using SOS1i under these conditions, is the recent demonstration that SOS1 inhibition resulted in enhancement of antitumor immunity through cGAS–STING signaling reactivation in NRAS-mutant tumors [ ]. These findings suggest that, while SOS1 inhibition lacks a direct inhibitory effect on HRAS or NRAS oncogenic variants, it may nonetheless elicit therapeutic responses by modulating auxiliary signaling pathways associated with the TME.
Of note, a significant pharmacological trend is observed when comparing emerging, next-generation SOS1i when compared to established benchmarks such as BI-3406 and MRTX0902. The data suggests that recent novel inhibitors achieve therapeutic parity or superiority relative to BI-3406 and MRTX0902 while operating at significantly lower effective concentrations across both in vitro and in vivo models. This enhanced potency underscores a substantial translational value, as it may facilitate a wider therapeutic window, and potentially mitigate, dose-dependent toxicities.

## SOS1 PROTACs
PROTACs represent a novel, wise strategy for targeted protein degradation, offering an alternative to small-molecule inhibition by permanently depleting a target protein´s expression. These heterobifunctional molecules are meticulously designed to simultaneously bind a protein of interest (POI) and specific E3 ubiquitin ligase (E3 ligase), with a linker binding both structures. This ternary complex facilitates the formation of polyubiquitin chains on the POI, leading to proteasome-mediated protein degradation.
The design of a SOS1 PROTAC involves three critical components: the SOS1 ligand (warhead), the E3 ligase ligand, and the linker connecting them, The SOS1 ligand can be either inhibitor-based (the majority), which blocks function and removes the protein permanently, or agonist-based, which mimics an active function before removing SOS1 (and potentially its scaffolding role) via a unique conformational change [ ]. The most employed E3 ligases in PROTAC design are Cereblon (CRBN) and Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL). CRBN-based PROTACs are often preferred because of their potentially superior oral availability compared to those recruiting VHL [ ]. The linker, which typically consists of polyethylene glycol units, alkyl chains, or other flexible or rigid scaffolds, connects the SOS1 ligand and the E3 ligase ligand, and its properties significantly influence the PROTAC’s ability to form a productive ternary complex and its overall pharmacological profile. The current literature on SOS1 PROTACs overwhelmingly focuses on KRAS-driven cancers. These degraders predominantly use SOS1i as warheads and recruit CRBN. Han and colleagues (2025) provided a detailed review of the chemical and structural composition of the currently designed PROTACs for SOS1 [ ].
Compound 9d. This compound was characterized as the first-in-class agonist, (VUBI-1)-based, selective, SOS1 PROTAC [ ]. It links a benzimidazole-derived SOS1 agonist to a VHL E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand [ ]. 9d induces potent, time- and dose-dependent SOS1 degradation (92% at 1 µM, after 24 h) in NCI-H358 cells and inhibits 3D cell proliferation across various human oncogenic KRASG12C/D/S/V tumor cells (IC50 values ranging from 0.115 µM to 0.525 µM) (Table ) [ ]. In contrast, it failed to impair the proliferation in KRASG12R-mutated human thyroid gland anaplastic carcinoma cells (CAL-62) despite exhibiting a potent SOS1 degradation activity [ ]. Compound 9d did not induce SOS2 degradation or compensatory changes in SOS2 transcription and demonstrated in vivo antitumor efficacy (at 10 or 20 mg/kg daily for 3 weeks by i.p dosing) in a KRASG12C-mutated (NCI-H358) xenograft model [ ].
 P7. This PROTAC uses a BAY-293-based quinazoline core linked to a CRBN-based E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand (a lenalidomide derivative) [ ]. The specific attachment points on the SOS1 ligand (e.g., the 6- and 7-OH groups of the quinazoline core) are critical for optimal formation and orientation of ternary complex. P7 achieved near-complete, time- and dose-dependent SOS1 degradation in oncogenic and WT KRAS human CRC cells (Table ) and KRAS-mutant patient-derived organoids (PDOs) [ ]. Furthermore, it displayed variable efficacy against CRC PDOs growth, yielding nevertheless in some of them an IC50 value 5-fold lower and a higher reduction of RAS/MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling compared to BI-3406 [ ]. This enhanced potency is attributed to the complete removal of SOS1, including its crucial scaffolding function, offering a significant therapeutic advantage over the SOS1i strategy.
ZZ151. This SOS1 PROTAC was designed and characterized by Zhou et al. (2023). Structurally, ZZ151 is inhibitor-based, using a BI-3406 analogue as the ligand and the VHL E3 ubiquitin ligase for degradation [ ]. ZZ151 exhibited potent SOS1 degradation (92% at 1 µM for 24 h in NCI-H358 cells) and enhanced antiproliferative (3D culture) activity compared with BI-3406 in a panel of KRAS-mutated human cancer cells (G12C/D/S/V and G13D) (Table ) with IC50 values ranging from 6.97 to 105 nM [ ]. However, ZZ151 demonstrated limited cell growth inhibition in cells harboring KRASG12R and KRASQ61H mutations, suggesting SOS1-indepedence or a specific resistance mechanism [ , ]. With well-tolerated toxicity profiles, ZZ151 (20 mg/kg; q.d; 21 days) also demonstrated in vivo TGI in KRASG12D (AsPC-1) and KRASG12V (SW620) xenografts [ ], thus documenting a superior therapeutic effect when compared to BI-3406 in the KRASG12D model [ ].
Compound 23 is an inhibitor-based SOS1 PROTAC that was recently developed as a promising approach to overcome acquired resistance to Sotorasib in RASG12C-driven cancers [ , ]. To build this PROTAC up, the authors developed 28 different compounds using BAY-293 and BI-3406 as inhibitors, VHL and CRBN as E3 ligase, and modifying the length of the linkers, with compound 23 resulting the most efficient one [ ]. This degrader was designed and optimized by linking BI-3406 to a VHL E3 ligase ligand and exhibited a potent SOS1 protein degradation (90% at 1 µM) in both KRASWT and KRASG12C human cancer cells (Mia PaCa-2, PANC-1, and NCI-H358). 3D proliferation assays revealed that it exhibited a significant antiproliferative activity on both KRAS mutant and WT human cancer cells (Table ) with IC50 values of 98 nM and 0.535 µM, respectively [ ]. Moreover, compound 23 was also able to sensitize KRASG12C-mutated cells to Sotorasib [ ]. This PROTAC (5 mg/kg or 25 mg/kg; i.p; 21 days) is well tolerated in vivo and TGI was observed in a K562 xenograft model [ ]. The combined in vivo administration of compound 23 and Sotorasib showed potent synergistic antitumor effects in KRASG12C NSCLC (NCI-H358) xenografts without noticeable systemic harmful effects [ ].
LHF418. This selective, SOS1 PROTAC, carries a BI-3406-based warhead linked through a 7-methylene unit to the CRBN-based E3 ligase [ ]. Of a series of SOS1 degraders, LHF418 was found to be the most potent degrader in this study, showing a high SOS1 protein degradation (∼80%) in a dose- and time-dependent manner in KRASG12S mutant cells (A549). Nevertheless, it should be noted that this level of degradation was observed at a dose of 3 µM. It also degraded SOS1, although less effectively, in HPAF-II and Mia PaCa-2 cancer cells [ ].
BTX-6654/BTX-7312. Begovich et al. (2024) reported the development of two additional PROTACs for SOS1. As with most currently developed SOS1 degraders, both compounds are inhibitor-based and CRBN-recruiting. Differing from the previously described SOS1 degraders, they are composed of two different SOS1i, (#37, a phthalazine derivative or #38 a pyrrolopyrimidine derivative) as the warhead [ , ]. Both compounds demonstrated potent, selective, rapid, and sustained SOS1 protein degradation (∼95%) in the KRASG12C/D (NCI-H358 and Mia PaCa-2) and KRASG13D (LoVo) cells [ ].
In vitro assays demonstrated that BTX-6654 and BTX-7312 caused a significant reduction in RAS/MAPK and PI3K/Akt activation and the subsequent reduction and proliferation rate in Mia PaCa-2 cells, even higher than that observed with their corresponding SOS1 ligands [ , ]. Moreover, a detailed analysis carried out in a broad panel of human cancer cell lines demonstrated that BTX-6654 or BTX-7312 impaired cell viability in those cells lines harboring RAS/MAPK-related mutations including: EGFRmut, KRASA59T, KRASG12A/C/S/V, KRASG13C/D, KRASamp, HRASQ61H, NF1LOF mutations and even in RAS-independent cancer cells (Table ) [ ]. Nevertheless, the viability of other cancer cell lines harboring the same mutations was unaffected following the treatment with either BTX-6654 or BTX-7312 [ ]. The potential in vitro synergistic effect of these degraders with certain EGFR-RAS-MAPK inhibitors, such as Afatinib, Sotorasib, MRTX1133, and Trametinib, was demonstrated in the Mia PaCa-2 (G12C) and the GP2D (G12D) cell lines [ ].
BTX-6654, likely due to its PK properties, was found to be the more suitable than BTX-7312 for in vivo studies [ ]. BTX-6654 administration studies resulted in a significant TGI, similar to that of BI-3406 in both Mia PaCa-2 and NCI-H358 xenograft models [ ]. Moreover, BTX-6654 exhibited a potent synergistic effect with Sotorasib, and Trametinib in the Mia PaCa-2 xenograft model [ ].
The same authors recently presented BTX-10908, a new, first-in-class, potent, orally bioavailable, CRBN-based, SOS1 degrader (not yet published in a peer review journal) [ ]. This PROTAC demonstrated rapid, sustained and potent SOS1 degradation in oncogenic KRASG12C/V, KRASG13D and EGFRmut cells [ ]. Consistent with BTX-6654 and BTX-7312, BTX-10908 showed a potent antiproliferative activity, higher than that observed with MRTX0902, across various KRAS, RTK, and NF1 mutant cells (Table ). Strikingly, this compound was particularly efficient in human cell lines from hematological malignancies (Table ) [ ]. In contrast, the A375 and HT-29, BRAFV600E-mutated cells remained resistant to BTX-10908 [ ].
Further in vitro assessment revealed that BTX-10908 synergized with various RTK-RAS-ERK pathway inhibitors, such as Osimertinib, Sotorasib, Trametinib, or Quizartinib as well as with the TKi, Imatinib and Dasatinib [ ]. Moreover, this degrader resensitized Sotorasib-resistant Mia PaCa-2 cells [ ]. In vivo studies indicated that oral administration of this degrader resulted in dose-dependent SOS1 degradation and TGI in KRASG12C (NCI-H358) and KRASG12V (NCI-H441) xenografts models [ ].
Compound 4. Pang et al. (2024) identified this BI-3406-based CRBN-recruiting, with a six-methylene unit as a linker, as a SOS1 degrader. This compound (at 13 nM) revealed potent, selective, rapid, SOS1 protein degradation (∼88%) in the NCI-H358 cells. Interestingly, the SOS1 degradation ability of compound 4 was impaired at very high concentrations [ ]. This PROTAC showed more potent inhibition than BI-3406 in cell proliferation (3D) assays across various KRAS-mutated cells (G12C/V and G13D; Table ), with IC50 values ranging from 5 to 63 nM. In contrast, it showed milder effects when examined on the KRASG12D-mutated GP2D cells [ ]. Because of its excellent PK profiles and the absence of systemic harmful effects, this compound was tested in oncogenic KRASG12C xenograft models, demonstrating a significant in vivo antitumor activity (30 mg/kg; bid; 21 days), being even higher than that obtained with BI-3406 [ ].
Compound 11o. This compound was designed, synthesized, and selected from a series of novel CRBN-recruiting SOS1 degraders using pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidin-7-one-based SOS1i as the warhead [ , ]. This PROTAC showed significantly higher degradation potency than any other compound tested in those reports, with a maximum SOS1 protein degradation (∼90%) obtained, in a dose- and time-dependent manner, in the SW620 cells [ ]. 11o also effectively induced SOS1 degradation (although reduced in comparison with the SW620 cells) in the A549 and DLD-1 cells [ ]. 11o-mediated impaired 3D cell growth in KRASG12S/V and KRASG13D oncogenic cells (Table ) with IC50 values ranging from 36.7 nM to 169 nM, and it was comparable to that obtained with BI-3406 [ ].
UBX-144 / UBX-164 / UBX-204. This new series of SOS1 degraders, developed by Ubix Therapeutics Company, was presented at the recent AACR 2025 meeting [ ]. These compounds are CRBN-based PROTACs, although the published abstract did not explicitly disclose the precise chemical structure of the SOS1-binding warhead used in these molecules. The lead compound, UBX-144, demonstrated potent degradation activity (at sub-nanomolar DC50 levels), with the half-maximal degradation of SOS1 within 2 h of treatment in the KRASG12C-mutated NCI-H358 cells [ ]. According to the presented abstract, both UBX-144 and the analog UBX-164 significantly reduced cell proliferation in vitro across a broad panel of human cancer cell lines, including several derived from gastric, colon, breast, lung, and brain cancers, as well as CML [ ]. A further analog, UBX-204, exhibited synergistic antiproliferative effects when combined with selected inhibitors of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway in KRASG12C oncogenic cells [ ]. In vivo PK analyses confirmed that UBX-144 (10 mg/kg) effectively degraded the SOS1 protein (> 95%) within tumors in a U87-MG (NF1F1423M) xenograft model, resulting in a robust TGI [ ].
SIAIS562055. This recently developed PROTAC employs a BI-3406 analog as its warhead [ ] to recruit a CRBN ligand (likely a thalidomide or lenalidomide derivative). This compound efficiently blocks PPI of KRASG12C and KRASG12D to SOS1 (IC50 values of 95.7 nM and 134.5 nM, respectively) and potently degrades SOS1 (at 1 µM for 16 h) in NCI-H358 cells [ ]. In vitro studies (3D proliferation assays) showed superior antiproliferative effects of SIAIS562055 in KRASG12C/D/V mutant cells (Table ), with IC50 values ranging from 2.4 to 16.9 nM, compared to BI-3406 [ ]. In contrast, SIAIS562055 had no significant effects on KRASWT BxPC3 cells [ ]. In vivo, this compound demonstrated promising antitumor potency as monotherapy (40 mg/kg; q.d; 24 days) in KRASG12C-mutated xenograft models of PDAC and CRC [ ]. It also showed synergy with KRASi (Sotorasib, Adagrasib, and MRTX1133) and the TKi Imatinib and was successful in overcoming acquired resistance [ ].
These data indicate that SOS1 PROTACs predominantly employ SOS1i as the warhead, rather than SOS1 agonists, and most of them recruit CRBN as the E3 ubiquitin ligase. Functionally, these SOS1 degraders have uniformly demonstrated potent antiproliferative effects, both in vitro and in vivo, across a wide spectrum of KRAS mutant cancer cell lines, and in cancer cell lines harboring other oncogenic alterations within the EGFR − RAS−ERK signaling axis. Of note, the effects of SOS1 PROTACs in NRAS- or HRAS-mutated organoid or PDX models have not yet been evaluated in detail probably due to the significantly higher prevalence of KRAS, over HRAS/NRAS mutations in most aggressive solid cancers.
Notably, their effect on TGI was often superior to that achieved with the conventional SOS1i. Nevertheless, these degraders have not yet progressed into clinical trials despite considerable advances in PROTAC-mediated degradation of SOS1 [ ]. Several essential functional challenges persist. Primarily, the compensatory role of SOS2 following SOS1 removal [ , ] may attenuate the therapeutic efficacy of SOS1 degraders via an induced crosstalk with the PI3K signaling pathway [ ]. Consequently, the development of dual SOS1/2-degrading PROTACs based on small molecules that bind the conserved allosteric site represents a compelling strategy for KRAS dependent malignancies, contingent upon the existence of a sufficient therapeutic window [ ]. However, if achieved, it should be carefully evaluated in vivo to determine whether concomitant SOS1/2 protein degradation recapitulates the systemic toxicity observed in murine models upon combined SOS1/2 genetic deletion. The physicochemical profiles of PROTACs, where their intrinsic high molecular weight and increased polar surface area may restrain their viability for oral administration, may represent an additional critical constraint [ ]. Another important technical limitation is the potential drug resistance associated with recruiting CRBN or VHL, which may be linked, at least in part, to the downregulation or mutational inactivation of their respective E3 ubiquitin ligases [ ]. Thus, novel E3 ligases and corresponding ligands applicable to the targeted degradation of SOS1 may still represent an unmet need.
In summary, following the characterization of current SOS1i and SOS1 degraders, a critical interrogation of their respective clinical potentials is required. While both modalities have demonstrated comparable antiproliferative and antitumoral efficacy across identical oncogenic contexts and mutational profiles, their translational trajectories still diverge significantly. To date, SOS1i have established a favorable safety profile characterized by a reversible mechanism of action; conversely, while PROTACs exhibit superior potency at lower concentrations and effectively ablate the non-catalytic scaffolding functions of SOS1, they face substantial pharmacological caveats. Specifically, current SOS1 degraders lack oral bioavailability and remain susceptible to specific resistance mechanisms involving E3 ligase dysregulation. Consequently, from our point of view —at least in the medium term— small-molecule inhibitors represent the more viable clinical strategy with superior translational potential.

## SOS1i clinical trials
The primary goal of the extensive preclinical evaluations of the SOS1i or degraders is to facilitate their translation to the clinical level to assess their safety, tolerability, and ultimately, their antitumor potential in patients. Based on the most recent information available in clinical trial and drug development databases, only four active clinical trials are evaluating the effect of SOS1i: BAY-3498264, BI-1701963, MRTX0902, and HW071021 (Table  ).
BAY-3498264 is currently being assessed in a Phase I clinical trial (NCT06659341). The main objective of this trial is to determine the maximum tolerated dose and recommended phase II dose of BAY-3498264 when administered in combination with the KRASG12C inhibitor, Sotorasib (Table  ). An update on the ClinicalTrials.gov website (September 2025) confirms that the study is actively seeking to enroll new patients, and no published or presented results are yet posted.
BI-1701963 is a modified analog of BI-3406. Although its molecular structure has not been yet publicly disclosed, the modifications from BI-3406 were presumably made to improve the PK and safety/tolerability profiles for human clinical trials. A phase I open-label dose escalation trial of BI-1701963 (NCT04111458) was initiated to evaluate BI-1701963 in monotherapy and in combination with the MEKi, Trametinib to determine its maximum tolerated dose and recommended Phase II dose as monotherapy and in combination with Trametinib (Table ). This study included patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring KRAS mutations. Preliminary data reported that disease stabilization was observed in about 23% of participants over an 18-month period [ ]. However, no further updated information has been released.
Two additional clinical trials (NCT04975256 and NCT04627142) were initiated in 2020/21 to evaluate the potential antitumoral synergy of BI-1701963 with the KRASG12C inhibitor Adagrasib, in patients with KRASG12C-mutated advanced solid tumors or with Irinotecan in patients with advanced KRAS-mutated CRC [ ]. However, both trials were terminated in 2022 due to a change in the sponsor´s development strategy.
In December 2022, a phase 1/2 multiple expansion cohort trial (NCT05578092) with MRTX0902 was initiated (Table  ). The current status of the study is “active, not recruiting”, with completion estimated in the second half of 2026. This study seeks patients with advanced solid tumors (preferably, but not limited to, NSCLC and CRC) harboring mutations in the KRAS/MAPK signaling pathway (e.g., EGFR, KRAS, SOS1, PTPN11, class III BRAF, and NF1). The study aims to evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, pharmacodynamics, and antitumor activity of MRTX0902, either as monotherapy or in combination with Adagrasib. Specifically, the inclusion criteria establish that participants must have a histologically confirmed diagnosis of unresectable or metastatic solid tumor, with no other available curative treatment (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05578092?tab=Table).
HW071021 has been approved for evaluation (Phase I open-label study) as monotherapy in patients with advanced solid tumors, including NSCLC, CRC, PDAC, or cholangiocarcinoma (NCT06882135). However, whether the specific mutational status will be considered as an inclusion criterion for this trial is not indicated.
To date, a notable disparity exists between the expansive repertoire of small-molecule SOS1i identified to date and the limited number of active clinical investigations. However, this observation must be contextualized since several novel SOS1i have been unveiled only within the recent years. Thus, a substantial expansion of the clinical landscape is likely anticipated as these candidate compounds transition into early-phase human trials in a near future.

## The search for SOS2 inhibitors
The SOS family of RASGEFs comprises two highly homologous and ubiquitously expressed members: SOS1 and SOS2. Despite their marked structural and expression similarities [ ], initial studies using KO murine models yielded somehow unexpected, contrasting results: SOS1KO mice were embryonically lethal [ ], whereas SOS2KO mice were perfectly viable [ ]. This functional disparity positioned SOS1 as the main RASGEF linking external stimuli to downstream RAS-regulated signaling, obscuring the potential functional significance of SOS2. However, many subsequent investigations, employing various SOS1/2 genetic and pharmacological ablation approaches, have more precisely delineated the functional specificity and redundancy of both RASGEFs [ , ]. Critically, the systemic defective phenotypes observed in concomitantly deleted SOS1/2 double-knockout (DKO) contexts are frequently much more pronounced than those in single SOS1KO physiological scenarios and are often undetectable in a single SOS2KO context [ , ]. These observations not only confirm the functional redundancy between the two paralogs, with a functional prevalence of SOS1 over SOS2, but also strongly suggests that SOS2 may play a significant ancillary role in the absence of SOS1.
Due to their pivotal role in regulating the RAS/MAPK pathway, the SOS GEF proteins (with SOS1 being the predominantly studied isoform) have been consistently postulated as potential therapeutic targets for treating tumors driven by mutations in the EGFR − RAS−ERK axis. Indeed, over the past decade numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated the antitumoral effect of targeting SOS1. All these studies, employing a wide battery of techniques, including either genetic ablation (CRISPR, shRNA, siRNA, and various KO models) or the use of SOS1i or degraders, converge to validate SOS1 as a viable therapeutic target, especially in RAS-dependent malignancies [ ]. In this regard, findings from various groups have demonstrated that the concomitant ablation of SOS1 (genetically or pharmacologically) and SOS2 (genetically) significantly enhances anti-proliferative and antitumoral effects in RAS/MAPK-mutated contexts compared to when SOS1 is individually depleted [ , , , ]. Given the rapid lethality observed in SOS1/2DKO mice during adulthood [ ] it could be hypothesized that a combined, pharmacologically mediated, SOS1/2 inhibition might induce significant systemic toxicity in patients. Strikingly, our recent work demonstrated that, in contrast to the systemic toxicity associated with the genetic ablation of both SOS1 and SOS2 [ ], the administration of BI-3406 resulted in negligible systemic effects in healthy, naïve Sos2KO mice [ ]. This finding supports the notion of using dual SOS1/2 inhibition/degradation as a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of oncogenic RAS tumors. Despite this supporting rationale, to date, no SOS2i/degraders are clinically (or even preclinically) currently tested with all efforts focused only on drugs or molecules designed to inhibit or degrade SOS1.
Despite the notable structural homology of both SOS paralogs, SOS1i are highly selective for SOS1 without any effect on SOS2. A potential explanation for the absence of SOS2i compared to the numerous SOS1i already developed may be linked to a single, non-conserved, amino acid residue difference in the ligand-binding site [ ]. Briefly, in SOS1, the residue at position 905 is a histidine, whereas in SOS2, the analogous residue at position 903 codes for valine. The presence of histidine allows for a crucial π-stacking interaction with the quinazoline scaffold used in many SOS1i [ ]. In this regard, the SOS2 Val903 residue not only lacks this capability but also causes a steric clash, preventing the binding of SOS1-targeted compounds to SOS2 [ ]. This minimal structural difference renders the existing SOS1i scaffolds unsuitable for a SOS2i drug development program, highlighting the need for the development of specific SOS2i, which the scientific community has already initiated.
A recent study has reported the first-ever discovery of five fragment hits that can bind to the SOS2 protein [ ]. For this purpose, the authors used three combined technical approaches (surface plasmon resonance, TINS, and X-ray crystallography), which resulted in the identification of liganded SOS2 structures, which could establish the basis for the development and discovery of new small-molecules able to specifically inhibit the SOS2::RAS PPI [ ]. The discovery of the binding modes of these fragments allows for a structure-based drug design approach, which is essential for developing potent and selective SOS2i.
A new step in this regard was recently achieved. Zak et al. (2025) reported the discovery and initial optimization of small molecules that selectively bind to SOS2 [ ]. Interestingly, this work identified two different classes of compounds depending on the sites where they can bind to the SOS2 protein, the catalytic site or a novel interdomain site [ ]. The catalytic site binders are quinazoline-based compounds and were designed to bind to the catalytic site of SOS2, in analogous form to the binding pocket targeted by most SOS1i. Among them, compound 6 was the most potent. A 19FNMR-based fragment screening revealed a new site at the binding interface with the GDP-loaded RAS protein. Compound 11, which binds to both SOS2 and SOS1 with similar affinity, was also identified in this study. Importantly, the discovery of this compound demonstrated a previously unreported binding pocket on SOS2 and represents a new potential allosteric “hot spot” for developing new SOS2i [ ].
Computational studies may also serve as a new tool to identify novel, specific, SOS2i. A recent report described the use of a multidisciplinary computational approach (e.g., pharmacophore modeling, molecular docking, molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area calculations, and molecular dynamics simulations) to identify three specific enamine fragments (Z284596, Z501753, and Z481369) as potential SOS2i [ ]. These fragments exhibited superior docking scores and binding free energies compared with a crystallized reference compound that was already bound to SOS2, indicating a high predicted affinity for the SOS2 target. Additionally, these fragments exhibited a reasonable molecular dynamics profile, suggesting that their interaction with SOS2 is stable over time. Nevertheless, further experimental validation is needed to empirically demonstrate this prediction.
In summary, based on the extensive data described here, we surmise that, should SOS2-selective inhibitors be developed with a pharmacological profile analogous to that of SOS1 (impacting preferably oncogenic rather than WT-mediated RAS signaling), their clinical utility would likely need combined administration with SOS1i. Within this framework, SOS2i monotherapy appears fundamentally suboptimal. Regarding the development of SOS2-targeted PROTACs, we anticipate a potent, synergistic antiproliferative response when evaluated in combination with SOS1 degraders. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the systemic co-administration of dual PROTACs in vivo may result in significant adverse systemic effects, as previously evidenced in genetic KO murine models [ ].

## Future clinical perspectives
Despite significant progress over the past decades regarding the functional relevance of SOS1 not only in numerous physiological processes but also as a therapeutic target in RAS-dependent tumors, several translational or clinical aspects remain undefined and warrant deeper analysis herein.
The most promising clinical results associated with SOS1i have been observed when combined with allele-specific KRASi or MEKi, rather than when administered as single agents. Consequently, searching combinatorial therapeutic strategies designed to attenuate potential compensatory feedback loops within the RAS/MAPK axis represents the prevailing clinical paradigm in the field. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the simultaneous blockade of these pivotal signaling nodes may induce dose-limiting systemic toxicities, resulting in a narrow therapeutic window that need to be more defined in future studies. Preclinical data have further suggested that such an approach is successful not only in addressing the primary malignancy but also in delaying the emergence of acquired resistance or circumventing established mechanisms of therapeutic recalcitrance to specific pathway inhibitors. Although preclinical or clinical data for co-administering SOS1 and the recently developed RAS(ON)i are currently lacking, the biochemical rationale is compelling. It is known that GTP-bounded active RAS elicits positive feedback signals by binding to the allosteric site of SOS1 [ ]. Thus, SOS1i could potentially disrupt this “feed-forward” loop. Moreover, recent evidence demonstrated that RAS(ON)i stimulate RAS-GTP hydrolysis accelerating the transition to the GDP-bound state [ ] and, theoretically, favor SOS1i to trap RAS in the inactive state (Fig. b).
In this context, preliminary synergy studies demonstrating an exceptionally high antiproliferative effect were first observed when both SOS1/2 were simultaneously ablated [ ]. These observations revealed that SOS1 and SOS2 exhibit functional redundancy in the regulation of proliferation, and their combined removal markedly enhanced antitumor effects [ ]. In fact, the impact on cell proliferation was so significant that the resulting cellular or organismal (in mice) phenotype closely resembled that observed in murine models where the expression of all three canonical RAS isoforms was concomitantly depleted [ , ]. However, it is important to note that the combined genetic deletion of SOS1/2 in in vivo models resulted in overall systemic toxicity [ ], thereby precluding this potential strategy from clinical translation. Nevertheless, in contrast to the genetic model findings, administering BI-3406 in a SOS2KO murine model did not cause any relevant systemic toxicity in the animals [ ]. Consequently, if a specific inhibitor/PROTAC for SOS2 is successfully developed, its evaluation together with SOS1i/degraders is justified. The development of a dual SOS1/2i/degrader would facilitate this task.
The tumor microenvironment (TME) may exert deleterious roles, not only in the control of tumor progression but also over the modulation of the clinical responses or resistances elicited by different cancer therapies [ – ]. Targeted therapies, including the FDA-approved KRASG12C inhibitors (Sotorasib and Adagrasib), the selective KRASG12D inhibitor (MRTX1133), and the newly developed RAS(ON)i, exert their antitumor effects not only through their direct action on the tumor cell per se but also significantly through modulation of the TME [ – ]. However, the potential therapeutic effect of SOS1 inhibition/degradation through its action on the TME has been barely considered, despite the recognized key role of SOS1 in maintaining the homeostasis of the cell types that often constitute the TME [ , , , – ]. In this regard, recent studies in an allograft model expressing the human KRASG12D mutation have demonstrated that BI-3406 may also impact the surrounding stroma and delay tumor progression [ ]. Therefore, further investigations should be encouraged to extend the study of SOS1i/PROTACs beyond the direct effects on the tumor cell to also consider the potential therapeutic efficacy these agents might exert through TME modulation.
In the context of combinatorial therapeutic paradigms, given that proper RAS-mediated signaling is required for a robust cellular immunity [ , ] the integration of immunotherapy with SOS1i could seem, at first principle, to be biologically counterintuitive. However, the viability of this approach could be mechanistically supported by several considerations: (a) SOS1i demonstrate a preferential impact on RAS-addicted malignant cells; (b) effector preservation would be likely bolstered by SOS2 compensation (Fig. a). Furthermore, in specific immune subsets, RAS activation is predominantly regulated by the GRP family of RASGEFs rather than the SOS family [ ], providing a biological “bypass” that spares immune cells from SOS1 inhibition; and (c) the implementation of intermittent dosing regimens protocol may establish temporal “immune windows,” allowing for the GDP-GTP cycle activation and expansion of immune populations while maintaining sustained selective pressure on the primary oncogenic driver.
Another important consideration that is often overlooked in investigations of the antitumor relevance of inhibiting SOS1 is its bifunctional GEF activity toward both RAS and RAC small GTPases, thereby participating in the intricate regulation of cytoskeletal reorganization [ , , ]. Previous studies using murine models demonstrated the importance of SOS1-mediated RAC activation in the initiation and progression of BCR-ABL-driven CML [ , ]. In this regard, BI-3406 has been recently shown to synergistically enhance the efficacy of BCR/ABL TKi and overcome resistance in CML [ ]. In this study, the authors demonstrated that BI-3406 not only downregulated RAS-mediated signal transmission but also reduced GTP-loaded RAC levels in CML tumor cell lines, although it remains to be elucidated whether this outcome is due to a direct inhibition of the SOS1::RAC PPI or a result of any kind of RAS/RAC intracellular crosstalk. Despite these findings, it seems that the potential —likely indirect— impact of SOS1 inhibition on RAC-mediated signaling does not result in significant functional consequences, at least within the context of RAS-dependent solid tumors.
RASopathies comprise a precisely defined spectrum of inherited congenital disorders (e.g., Noonan syndrome, Neurofibromatosis type 1 or Costello syndrome, among others) resulting from germline pathogenic variants in genes encoding components or regulatory elements of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway (e.g., SOS1, PTPN11 or NF1), which results in sustained hyperactivation of the RAS-dependent signaling cascade [ ]. Most therapeutic investigations involving SOS1i have been conducted within the context of RAS-dependent oncogenesis. Consistent with the compelling efficacy profiles of the available SOS1i/degraders described in RAS-dependent tumors, the same experimental therapeutic approaches could potentially be applied and evaluated for specific clinical manifestations of certain types of RASopathies. Advancing RASopathy therapies has emerged as a new National Cancer Initiative that focuses on developing effective therapies and prevention strategies for clinical manifestations, specifically for non-NF1 RASopathies characterized by somatic RAS mutations [ ]. Although information regarding studies involving the use of a SOS1i/degrader for the treatment of non-cancer RAsopathies is very limited or non-existent, we speculate that SOS1i/degraders (probably in combination with other inhibitors such as MEKi) could be effective, particularly in SOS1-dependent RASopathies. Thus, while the primary focus of SOS1i development is currently directed toward oncology, their intrinsic capacity to attenuate the hyperactivation of the RAS signaling cascade establishes them as a highly promising and mechanistically relevant therapeutic strategy for RASopathies, particularly those driven by activating mutations in SOS1, but likely requiring safer compounds.
Finally, the identification of predictive and pharmacodynamic biomarkers for SOS1 inhibition remains a largely nascent field of investigation. Optimizing therapeutic outcomes needs both a good stratification of patient cohorts likely to exhibit preferential responses and a protocol to validate target engagement. Regarding patient stratification, molecular profiling may be conducted via tissue biopsy or minimally invasive liquid biopsy. Based on the evidence presented in this review, the presence of EGFR or KRAS G12X mutations appears to correlate with favorable therapeutic sensitivity. Conversely, oncogenic variants at codon 61 or within the HRAS/NRAS isoforms seems to be associated with poor prognosis. In relation with the pharmacodynamic assessment, the evaluation of the activation of RAS-MAPK axis effectors (e.g., p-ERK and p-RSK) appears as an intuitive strategy. Notably, recent studies in CRC PDOs harboring KRAS mutations suggest that a SOS1/SOS2 expression ratio > 1 may serve as a potent predictor of enhanced sensitivity to SOS1i [ ], likely based on the potential of SOS2 to bypass the effect of these compounds.
