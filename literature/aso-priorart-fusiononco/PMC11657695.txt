## Abstract

## Background
Ewing sarcoma (ES), the second main pediatric bone sarcoma, is characterised by a chromosomal translocation leading to the formation of fusion proteins like EWS::FLI1. While several studies have shown that potassium channels drive the development of many tumours, limited data exist on ES. This work therefore aimed to study the transcriptional regulation of KCNA2 and define the involvement of the Kv1.2 channel encoded by KCNA2 in a key function of ES development, cell proliferation.

## Methods
KCNA2 expression in patients and cell lines was measured via bioinformatic analysis (RNA-Seq). The presence of a functional Kv1.2 channel was shown using patch-clamp experiments. Molecular biology approaches were used after EWS::FLI1 silencing to study the transcriptional regulation of KCNA2. Proliferation and cell count assessment were performed using cell biology approaches. KCNA2 silencing (siRNA) and RNA-Seq were performed to identify the signalling pathways involved in the ability of KCNA2 to drive cell proliferation. The regulation of the Hippo signalling pathway by KCNA2 was studied by measuring Hippo/YAP target genes expression, while YAP protein expression was studied with Western-Blot and immunofluorescence approaches.

## Results
This research identified KCNA2 (encoding for a functional Kv1.2 channel) as highly expressed in ES biopsies and cell lines. The results indicated a correlation between KCNA2 expression and patient survival. The data also demonstrated that KCNA2/Kv1.2 is a direct target of EWS::FLI1, and identified the molecular mechanisms by which this chimeric protein regulates KCNA2 gene expression at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, the results indicated that KCNA2 expression and Kv1.2 activity regulate ES cell proliferation and that KCNA2 expression drives the Hippo/YAP signalling pathway. Using the specific Kv1.2 channel inhibitor (κ-Conotoxin), the results suggested that two complementary mechanisms are involved in this process, both dependently and independently of Kv1.2 functional channels at the plasma membrane.

## Conclusion
This study is the first to describe the involvement of KCNA2 expression and Kv1.2 channel in cancer development. The study also unveiled the involvement of KCNA2 in the regulation of the Hippo/YAP signalling cascade. Thus, this work suggests that KCNA2/Kv1.2 could be a potential therapeutic target in ES.

## Background
Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a primary malignant bone tumour that mostly affects children, adolescents, and young adults [ , ]. Although patient management has improved in recent decades, the survival rate of patients drops drastically to 30% for high-risk patients (resistance to chemotherapy or presence of metastases at diagnosis) [ , ]. In 85% of cases, ES is characterised by the formation of the fusion protein EWS::FLI1 [ ], and in 10% of cases, by the chimeric protein EWS::ERG [ ], both acting as an oncogene.
Over the last decade, research has demonstrated the involvement of ion channels, such as potassium channels, in tumour development, by highlighting their participation in key processes of tumorigenesis, such as cell proliferation, migration, or death [ – ]. The potassium channels family is the largest family of ion channels, with more than 70 genes encoding these structures, classified into four classes, based on the number of transmembrane segments and pore domains [ , ]. Among all these channels, the voltage-dependent potassium channels (Kv) compose one of the largest subfamilies, with over 20 channels [ ]. They form tetramers of α subunits to be active, and these subunits consist of six transmembrane segments and a pore domain. To date, the two main channels of this Kv subfamily studied in cancer research are Kv1.3 and Kv1.5. Multiple studies have shown an overexpression of the genes encoding these channels and their role in the development of cancer such as lung cancer and osteosarcoma [ , ]. To authors’ knowledge, no study has yet described the expression or the role of Kv1.2 potassium channel in a biopsy of cancer patients or in a tumour cell. The present study describes for the first time the involvement of KCNA2 expression and Kv1.2 channel in the cancer development of Ewing sarcoma. This study demonstrates that (i) KCNA2 is exclusively highly expressed in two tumours entities, ES and gliomas; (ii) KCNA2 expression is not significant in any healthy tissue except the brain and the tibial nerve; (iii) KCNA2 expression correlates with patient survival; (iv) EWS::FLI1 regulates KCNA2 transcription and Kv1.2 translation, by binding to an open-chromatin region enriched in the GGAA sequence; (v) KCNA2 encodes a functional Kv1.2 potassium channel; (vi) KCNA2/Kv1.2 is involved in the regulation of ES cell proliferation; (vii) KCNA2 expression regulates the Hippo/YAP signalling pathway.

## Methods

## Cell culture and reagents
Ewing sarcoma cell lines (Table S1) and A-673/TR/shEF/ cell line, stably transduced with a doxycycline-inducible shRNA targeting EWS::FLI1 [ ], were kindly provided by Olivier Delattre (Curie Institute, France). Osteosarcoma (OS) cell lines were purchased from ATCC (LGC Standards, Molsheim, France) as previously described [ ]. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovin Serum (FBS, Lonza). The selective Kv1.2 channel inhibitor, κ-Conotoxin RIIIK (Alomone Labs, Israel), was used at a concentration of 200 nM. The selective YAP inhibitor CA3 was purchased from Tocris (Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN). All cell lines were subjected to a complete validation process and were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling analysis (Eurofins, Germany). Experiments were performed using mycoplasma-free cell lines.

## Luciferase reporter assay
Transient cell transfections were performed with jetPEI™ (Polyplus transfection, Illkirch, France). The pRLTK-Renilla luciferase expression vector was co-transfected in all experiments to monitor transfection efficiencies. Luciferase activity was determined with the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, Charbonnieres, France). The 8xGTIIC-Luc (gift from Stefano Piccolo, Addgene plasmid #34,615; http://n2t.net/addgene:34615; RRID:Addgene-34615, (TEAD)8-lx in the text) construct was used as a reporter construct specific for YAP/TEAD-driven signalling.

## Bioinformatics analyses
The R2: Genomics Analysis and Visualisation Platform website provided open access datasets from patients’ biopsies. Multiple datasets were used to compare KCNA2 expression in various cancers (Table S2). The platform and normalization method for each data set are listed Table S2. Kaplan Meier analysis was done to compare the overall survival rate and the event-free survival rate of ES patients according to the expression of genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels. Differences between groups were calculated using the logrank test.
The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx, https://gtexportal.org/home/) and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopaedia (CCLE, https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle/) databases were used to compare KCNA genes expression between healthy tissues (GTEx version 8) and tumorous cell lines (CCLE—DepMap 22Q4), respectively.

## RNA-Seq analyses
RNA-seq for the different ES and osteosarcoma (OS) cell lines (GSE229906) as well as RNA-Seq for A-673 cell line transfected with a siRNA CT or a pool of siRNAs targeting KCNA2 (GSE246854) were carried out as part of this study, and performed by ActivMotif. Gene-level quantification was realized as previously described [ ]. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation kit as triplicates. Next, sequencing was performed on Illumina NextSeq 500 in a 2 × 42 bp (PE42) format and Illumina NovaSeq 6000 in a 2 × 151 bp (PE) respectively. For the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), the GSEA tool was used on KEGG ontologies, except those linked to non-cancer-related diseases. A-673/TR/shEF RNA-Seq data without induction (Day 0) and with induction of shRNA targeting EWS::FLI1 (Day 7) came from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO) GSE164373 [ ]. These data were filtered and aligned to the same reference genome as well as quantified with the annotation file from RefSeq associated with that genome. The packages used for the analyses are detailed in Table S3.

## ChIP-Seq analyses
The ChIP-Seq data came from GSE176400 [ ], GSE129155 [ ], and GSE94278 [ ] in the GEO database. GSE176400 contains ChIP-Seq from 18 Ewing sarcoma cell lines targeting FLI1 or ERG based on cell line specific EWS fusion partner, histone marks H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, and an input serving as a control. GSE129155 contains ChIP-Seq data targeting FLI1 in the A673/TR/shEF cell line. Cells were treated for 7 days with doxycycline (Day 7) to silence EWS::FLI1 expression. The effect of EWS::FLI1 re-expression was then studied 3 (Day 7 + 3), 4 (Day 7 + 4) and 10 (Day 7 + 10) days after cell treatment was stopped, respectively. GSE94278 contains Bigwig files of normalized ChIP-Seq values of Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs) transfected with a control plasmid, and FLI1 or EWS::FLI1 expression plasmids. These data were filtered and aligned to the GRCh38.p14 reference genome analysis set from NCBI. The packages used for the analyses are detailed in Table S4.

## Electrophysiology
A-673 cells were seeded 1–2 days before experiments into 35-mm Petri dishes at 2000 cells per cm2. Voltage clamp experiments were performed using the conventional whole-cell recording configuration of the patch-clamp technique as previously described [ ]. The physiological saline solution (PSS) had the following composition (in mM): NaCl 140, MgCl2 1, KCl 4, CaCl2 2, D-glucose 11.1 and HEPES 10, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. The pipette solution contained (in mM): K-glutamate 125, KCl 20, MgCl2 1, Mg-ATP 1, HEPES 10. The solution’s pH was adjusted to 7.2 with KOH and pCa = 6.4 (0.7 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM EGTA). Experiments were conducted using the Axopatch 200B patch-clamp amplifier (Axon Instrument) and data, digitised with the 1322-A Digidata converter (Axon Instrument), and stored on a computer using Clampex of pClamp 9.2 software (Axon Instrument). Outward currents were generated by stepwise 10 mV depolarising pulses (500 ms duration; 4 ms intervals) from a constant holding potential of −90 mV up to + 80 mV. To build the I/V relation, the steady state current elicited at the chosen membrane potential was calculated as the average of the current recorded during the latest 200 ms of the pulse. The κ-Conotoxin-sensitive current was defined as the difference between outward currents recorded in drug-free bath solution and after superfusion with 200 nM κ-Conotoxin.

## RT-qPCR (Reverse transcription – quantitative polymerase chain reaction)
RNA from cell lines were extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus Kit (Macherey–Nagel, Duren, Germany). Reverse transcription was performed from RNA with the “Maxima H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit” (ThermoFischer, Illkrich, France). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction was realised using SYBR Select Master Mix (ThermoFischer) and primers (see Table  ). Target gene expression was normalised to HPRT, B2M or ActinB levels in respective samples, and the Cycle threshold (Ct) method was used to measure the relative quantification of target mRNA.

 

## Western blot
Western blots were realized as previously described [ ]. The antibodies used were Kv1.2 (Proteintech, Planegg-Martinsried, Germany), FLI1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), YAP (Proteintech, France) and β-tubulin (Cell Signalling Technology, Denver, MA, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with a secondary fluorescent antibody (Donkey anti-rabbit IRDye 680RD; Goat anti-mouse IRDye 800CW, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA), and the antibodies binding was visualised with the Odyssey Fc fluorescence system (LI-COR).

## siRNA transitory transfection
ES cells were transfected with a pool of five siRNAs targeting EWS::FLI1 (siRNA EWSR1, ON-TARGET, SmartPool, Dharmacon, Horizon, Perkin-Elmer, Cambridge, UK), targeting KCNA2 (Kv1.2 siRNA (h), SantaCruz, Dallas, TX, USA) or a pool of five control siRNAs using Opti-MEM medium and the Lipofectamine RNAiMax (ThermoFischer).

## Real-time proliferation assays
Twenty-four hours after siRNA transfection, real-time proliferation assays were performed on re-seeded cells. With the XCelligence device (ACEA Biosciences, Agilent Technologies France, Les Ulis, France), the cell proliferation was monitored during 55 h, with scans every four hours. On the other hand, 24 h after seeding, cells were treated with κ-Conotoxin (200 nM) and the treatment was renewed every 24 h. Cell proliferation was measured with the Omni device from CytoSMART (Axion Biosystems, Atlanta, GA, USA).

## Crystal violet assay
After siRNA transfection, the number of cells was estimated by labelling them with crystal violet solution. Cell wells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline to remove as many dead cells as possible, and were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde, and then stained with crystal violet. After the staining, the crystal violet was solubilised in Sorensen solution, and absorbance at 570 nm was measured with Victor2 (Perkin-Elmer).

## Immunofluorescence
Twenty-four hours after siRNA transfection, cells were seeded onto Ibidi overnight, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0,3% Triton for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with anti-YAP antibody (Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C and next washed in phosphate-buffered saline. After this, cells were incubated with a secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit Alexa-Fluor 488, Thermofischer) for one hour at room temperature. Nuclei were stained using DAPI. Images were acquired with a Nikon A1 confocal microscope (Champigny/Marne, France) using X60 Plan Apo objective N.A. 1.4. Acquisition and quantification were performed respectively with NIS V 5.42 and NIS General Analysis 3 (GA3) V 5.42, respectively.

## Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Software. Comparison of KCNA (KCNA1, KCNA2, KCNA3, KCNA4, KCNA5, KCNA6, KCNA7 and KCNA10) genes expression in patients suffering from ES was performed via a Kruskal–Wallis test followed with a Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. The comparison of KCNA2 gene expression between multiple tumours was also performed via a Kruskal–Wallis test and a Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. The comparison of KCNA2 gene expression between ES patients and MSCs coming from healthy donors was performed by an Mann–Whitney test. For in vitro experiments, Mann–Whitney tests were used to compare two groups. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for patch-clamp assay. α = 0.05 and p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant: * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001.

## Results

## KCNA2 expression in ES correlates with patient survival
The expression of genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels was studied in ES and various tumour biopsies using the R2 public database. Among the eight genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels (KCNA1, KCNA2, KCNA3, KCNA4, KCNA5, KCNA6, KCNA7 and KCNA10), KCNA2 is the only one highly expressed in ES patients, with the others barely expressed (Fig.  A). The evaluation of KCNA2 expression in various tumour entities reveals strong expression of KCNA2 only in two tumour entities, namely ES and glioma (Fig.  B). Genes encoding other Kv1-type channels are variably expressed in all tumours (Figures S1A-S1G, upper panels), with the majority of them being weakly expressed, except for KCNA1 and KCNA5, which are highly expressed in medulloblastoma (Figures S1A and S1D). On the other hand, KCNA2 overexpression is correlated with the event-free (Fig.  C) and the overall (Figure S1H) survival rate of ES patients. With the exception of KCNA5 (Figure S1D), the expression of the other Kv1-encoding genes is not significantly correlated with survival in ES patients (Figures S1A-S1G, lower panels).
 KCNA2 expression was then studied in Mesenchymal Stems Cells (MSCs, the putative cell of origin of ES) (Fig.  D) and in various healthy tissues (Fig.  E), according to the R2 and the GTEx databases, respectively. KCNA2 is only highly expressed in brain tissues and in the tibial nerve, with all other healthy tissues and MSCs barely expressing KCNA2. The expression of the other genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels is more diverse (Figures S2A-S2F). KCNA1 and KCNA4 are mainly expressed in brain tissues, KCNA7 is expressed only in skeletal muscle, KCNA3 appears to be ubiquitously expressed whereas KCNA10 is not expressed in any healthy tissue, and KCNA5 is mainly found in artery and brain tissues (Figures S2A-S2F).
RNA-Seq was then performed on seven ES cell lines and six osteosarcoma (OS) cell lines (GSE229906, Fig.  A). Of all the genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels, KCNA2 is the only one that is expressed in all ES cell lines (with the exception of the TC-71 cell line) but is not expressed in any OS cell lines. The majority of the other genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels are expressed in some ES and OS cells, or only in OS cells. The results for KCNA2 were confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig.  B). Using the CCLE database, the expression of all genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels was assessed in 66 tumour-derived cell lines (Fig.  C and Figures S3A-S3G). Interestingly, KCNA2 is the only gene to show high-restricted expression in ES cells (Fig.  C). The seven other genes are poorly expressed in most tumour derived cell lines, including ES cell lines (Figures S3A-S3G).
 Altogether, these results indicate that KCNA2/Kv1.2 is highly expressed in ES biopsies and ES cell lines and that its overexpression is correlated with patient survival.

## KCNA2 encodes a potassium Kv1.2 channel
To determine if KCNA2 encodes a functional Kv1.2 potassium channel at the plasma membrane in ES cells, potassium currents of A-673 cells were recorded in absence and presence of the specific inhibitor of Kv1.2, κ-Conotoxin (κ-Cono) [ ]. Figure  D presents typical examples of whole-cell outward currents recorded in one cell before (Control) and after (κ-Conotoxin RIIIK) application of 200 nM κ-Cono. Compared with control cells, κ-Cono causes a reduction of the net whole-cell potassium current. Of the 15 A-673 cells tested, 10 cells exhibited a reduction in outward currents after drug application (Fig.  D). The current density of these 10 cells was also significantly decreased compared to control cells with a 42% reduction in current density at + 60 mV (Fig.  E, upper panel). Further analysis revealed that the κ-Cono-sensitive current has a typical electrophysiological profile of Kv1.2 current activating around −20 mV and with outward rectification (Fig.  E, lower panel).
Overall, these results clearly demonstrate the presence of Kv1.2 currents in A-673 ES cells.

## KCNA2/Kv1.2 expression is regulated by EWS::FLI1 and EWS::ERG, which binds to GGAA microsatellites
The regulation of KCNA2 expression by EWS::FLI1 or EWS::ERG was first studied, using various ES cells, stably transduced with a doxycycline-induced shRNA targeting EWS::FLI1 or EWS::ERG. Transcriptomic data indicated that reduction of EWS::FLI1 expression significantly reduces KCNA2 expression in 12 of the 15 cell lines studied (Fig.  A and Figure S4A). Interestingly, reduction of EWS::ERG expression significantly reduces KCNA2 expression in 2 of the 3 cell lines tested (Figure S4B).
 The regulation of KCNA2 expression was then studied using RT-qPCR and Western blot assays. As shown in Fig.  B, doxycycline treatment of the A-673 cell line stably transduced with a doxycycline-inducible shRNA targeting EWS::FLI1 (A-673/TR/shEF cells [ ]), decreases EWS::FLI1 expression by more than 50% after 8 h, until reaching 95% EWS::FLI1 silencing after 24 h (Fig.  B, left panel). Similarly, KCNA2 expression decreases after 8 h of treatment, until reaching 75% silencing after 24 h (Fig.  B, middle panel). As previously published, a decrease in EWS::FLI1 protein expression was observed as early as 8 h of cell treatment with doxycycline [ ], while a decrease in Kv1.2 expression was observed as early as 24 h of cell treatment (Fig.  B, right panel). These results were confirmed in three different ES cell lines, after silencing EWS::FLI1 by a pool of five siRNAs (Figure S4C). All these results for KCNA2 at mRNA level were confirmed at protein level using Western blot analysis (Figure S4D).
The expression of the multiple KCNA2 transcripts was then studied in various cell lines. According to NCBI RefSeq, six KCNA2 transcripts are described, two of which are protein-coding transcripts (NM_001204269.2 and NM_004974.4, named B and F, respectively), and the other four being predicted model protein-coding transcripts (XM_011541398.3, XM_011541400.3, XM_017001213.2 and XM_011541396.3, named A, C, D and E, respectively) (Figure S5A). Comparing their expression in A-673 cell lines by RNA-Seq analysis based on alignment with the transcriptome sequence, it appears that three KCNA2 transcripts are expressed (D, E and F transcripts, Fig.  C), with the F transcript being predominantly expressed in all ES cell lines (Fig.  C and Figure S5B). In addition, EWS::FLI1 silencing (GSE164373) decreases the expression of all three KCNA2 transcripts expressed in A-673 cells (Fig.  D).
ChIP-Seq data (GSE176400) show a significant peak of H3K4me3 (an epigenetic modification marking active promoters) at the promoter of the F transcript of KCNA2 in 17 of the18 ES cell lines (Fig.  A, left and middle panels, and Figures S6), some of them also displaying significant H3K27ac peaks (an epigenetic modification marking active promoters and enhancers). Near the H3K4me3 peak, an FLI1 peak (with a significant q.value) is observed in close proximity to the F transcript promoter for 13 of the 15 ES cell lines characterised by EWS::FLI1 fusion protein (Fig.  A, left and middle panels, and Figures S6). Off note, the cells for which no EWS::FLI1 peak or a weak EWS::FLI1 peak was observed in the vicinity of the KCNA2 promoter did not show a decrease in KCNA2 expression after EWS::FLI1 silencing.
 Interestingly, the same results were obtained for the CHLA-25 and EW-3 cell lines, characterized by the EWS::ERG fusion protein (Fig.  A, right panel and Figure S6). These fusion proteins both bind in the vicinity of the KCNA2 promoter to sites containing between 12 and 16 GGAA microsatellites repeats, indicated by the red vertical lines depending on the cell line. (Fig.  and Figures S6, Table S1).
FLI1 ChIP-Seq data performed on the A673/TR/shEF cell line (GSE129155) were then used to determine EWS::FLI1 binding in the vicinity of the KCNA2 promoter as a function of EWS::FLI1 expression level (Fig.  B). Interestingly, after seven days of doxycyline treatment, no FLI1 peak was observed near the promoter of the F transcript (Day 7, Fig.  B). The FLI1 peak progressively reappears around its binding site after re-expression of EWS::FLI1 following the interruption of doxycycline treatment (Day 7 + 3, Day 7 + 4, Day 7 + 7 and Day 7 + 10, Fig.  B).
As expected, seven days after treatment of the cells with doxycycline, correlated with a low expression of EWS::FLI1 (Day 7, Figure S6P, left panel), a low expression of KCNA2 expression was observed (Day 7, Figure S6P, right panel). Four, seven and ten days after interruption of the doxycycline treatment, a restoration of KCNA2 expression parallel to that of EWS::FLI1 was gradually observed (Day 7 + 4, Day 7 + 7 and Day 7 + 10, Figure S6P).
Finally, overexpression of EWS::FLI1 in MSCs was performed and ChIP-Seq data (GSE94278) were used. An absence of H3K27ac and FLI1 peaks was observed around the KCNA2 promoter in MSCs transfected with the control plasmid or the FLI1 overexpression plasmid (Fig.  C). In contrast, when MSCs were transfected with the EWS::FLI1 overexpression plasmid, a weak H3K27ac peak appeared, along with a higher FLI1 peak around the promoter of the KCNA2 F transcript (Fig.  C).
Taken together, these results indicate the predominant expression of the KCNA2 F transcript in ES cell lines, and its transcriptional regulation by both EWS::FLI1 and EWS::ERG fusion proteins, which are able to bind GGAA microsatellites repeats in the close vicinity of the KCNA2 promoter.

## KCNA2/Kv1.2 expression regulates ES cell proliferation and Hippo/YAP signalling cascade
To investigate whether KCNA2 overexpression in ES is linked to a key process in tumour development, we compared the RNA sequencing transcriptional profiles of the A-673 cell line transfected with a pool of five siRNA against KCNA2 (siRNA KCNA2) or with a control siRNA (siRNA CT, GSE246854). As shown in Fig.  A, siRNA CT- and siRNA KCNA2- transfected cells display distinct transcriptional profiles, with multiple genes significantly and differentially expressed (Fig.  A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and the the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) reveals various ontology signatures in siRNA CT samples as compared to siRNA KCNA2 samples (Fig.  B). Interestingly, siRNA CT-transfected cells present an enrichment in the cell cycle ontology compared to the siRNA KCNA2-transfected cells (Fig.  C), with multiple genes differentially expressed. The expression of two thirds of these genes are significantly decreased in the siRNA KCNA2-transfected cells (Fig.  D). Differentially expressed genes include CCNE2, CCNDBP1 and GLI1, whose decreased expression was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig.  E). In this context, the involvement of KCNA2 in ES cell proliferation was then studied. The proliferation of A-673 cells was investigated using real time proliferation assays (Fig.  F). A significant decrease in cell proliferation was progressively observed after KCNA2 silencing using siRNA (Fig.  F). To confirm these results, the regulation of cell proliferation by KCNA2 was also assessed by evaluating the number of two ES cell lines by labeling them with crystal violet (A-673 and SK-ES-1, Fig.  G). Fourty-eight hours after transfection of the cells with a pool of siRNAs against KCNA2, KCNA2 expression was depleted at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure S7), and the number of cells was significantly reduced in both ES cell lines, compared with siRNA CT-transfected cells (Fig.  G). Interestingly, treatment of cells κ-Cono, seemed also to inhibit the ability of A-673 or SK-ES-1 cells to proliferate (Fig.  H and I).
 KEGG pathway analysis having revealed a Hippo signature (Fig.  B), a signalling pathway known to be involved in the control of proliferation in many cancer cells such as bone sarcoma cells [ , ], the effects of KCNA2 expression silencing on the activation of this cascade were investigated next. As shown in Fig.  A, an enrichment of the Hippo signalling pathway ontology is observed in the siRNA CT-transfected cells, compared to the siRNA KCNA2-transfected cells (Fig.  A). These cells display distinct transcriptional profiles related to the Hippo signalling cascade, with multiple genes being significantly and differentially expressed (Fig.  B). YAP target genes, such as CYR61 and CTGF (known as CCN1 and CCN2 respectively) were down-regulated (Fig.  B and C). Similar data were obtained for major players in the Hippo signalling pathway, such as ITGB2 and ITGB3 (Figs.  B and D). Additionally, KCNA2 silencing led to a decrease in YAP expression at the protein level in two ES cell lines (Fig.  E). Through YAP immunostaining in A-673 cell line, a decrease of nuclear YAP was also observed in the siRNA KCNA2-transfected cells compared to the siRNA CT-transfected cells (Fig.  F). Interestingly, two partner signalling pathways to the Hippo pathway, the TGF-β and Wnt pathways, were also affected by KCNA2 expression (Figures S8). Surprisingly, treatment of cells with κ-Cono, did not significantly affect YAP protein levels in the two ES cell lines tested (Fig.  G). Off note, the selective inhibitor of the Hippo/YAP pathway CA3, which inhibits the transactivation of the specific TEAD reporter construct (TEAD)8-lux, reduced the ability of A-673 cells to proliferate, thus mimicking the effects of KCNA2 silencing on ES cell proliferation (Figure S9).
 In sum, these results highlight the involvement of KCNA2 expression and Kv1.2 channel in the regulation of ES cell proliferation. Furthermore, these results show that KCNA2 expression, independently of a functional channel, regulates the Hippo/YAP signalling pathway.

## Discussion
Although clinical outcomes for patients with Ewing sarcoma (ES) have improved over the last decades, survival rates remain poor for high-risk patients, requiring the discovery of new therapeutic targets [ ]. Over the last decade, the involvement of potassium channels has been demonstrated in several cancers progression and development [ – ].
Within the potassium channel family, there are multiple subfamilies, including the voltage-dependent potassium channels (Kv), which are themselves classified into various categories, including the Shaker-related potassium channels (Kv1). In the last few years, several studies have thus reported overexpression of genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels, such as KCNA1 in cervcal cancer [ ] or KCNA3 in glioblastoma [ ]. Recently, a meta-analysis study identified KCNA2 as predominantly expressed in skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), without observing a difference in expression with healthy tissue or a correlation with patients survival rate [ ]. A second and more recent study revealed a high expression of KCNA2 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) without demonstrating the existence of a functional Kv1.2 channel and an association with a key function in tumour development [ ]. To the authors’ knowledge, the present study highlights for the first time the overexpression of Kv1.2, the potassium channel encoded by KCNA2 in one cancer, namely ES. Indeed, our results show an expression of KCNA2 in the biopsies of ES patients, as well as in ES cell lines. In addition, Western blot analysis confirmed the expression of Kv1.2 in ES cells. Regarding the expression of other genes encoding Kv1-type potassium channels in cancers, they are weakly expressed in ES patients and cell lines, and only KCNA1 and KCNA5 are highly expressed in medulloblastoma biopsies. Moreover, KCNA2 is found weakly expressed in healthy tissues, excepting the brain. While previously KCNA3 and KCNA5 expression have been shown to correlate with survival in lung squamous cell carcinoma and stomach adenocarcinoma patients, respectively [ ], this study demonstrates that KCNA2 expression correlates with the overall and event-free survival of ES patients. Thanks to the specific Kv1.2 channel blocker, κ-Conotoxin, and with the use of patch clamp assays, this study demonstrated that KCNA2 encodes for a functional Kv1.2 potassium channel.
This work also focused on the regulation of KCNA2 by both chimeric proteins, EWS::FLI1 and EWS::ERG. These fusion proteins have been shown to regulate many genes [ ], including transcription factors, such as NR0B1 [ , ], or other proteins involved in tumorigenesis, like LOXDH1 [ ], by binding at GGAA microsatellites repetitions [ , ]. This study demonstrates the regulation of KCNA2 (and Kv1.2) by EWS::FLI1 and the binding of both EWS::FLI1 and EWS::ERG at GGAA microsatellites repetitions, in close vicinity to the promoter of the KCNA2 canonical transcript (NM_004974.4), with the other KCNA2 transcripts barely being expressed in ES cell lines. Furthermore, in the ES cell lines used, the number of GGAA microsatellites repetitions is between 12 and 16, strengthening a previous study that proved that four or more GGAA repetitions activates EWS::FLI1-regulated genes [ ]. When binding at these microsatellites, EWS::FLI1 is able to recruit the BAF chromatin-remodelling complex [ ], which plays a crucial role in the chromatin accessibility around promoters and enhancers areas [ ].
Concerning the involvement of KCNA2 in ES tumorigenesis, KCNA2 silencing induces a decrease in ES cell proliferation. Various studies have described the involvement of potassium channels in the regulation of different cancer hallmarks, like cell proliferation, according to different mechanisms. These mechanisms are dependent (canonical) or independent (non-canonical) of a functional membrane channel [ ]. Among canonical potassium channel functions, it has been demonstrated that potassium channels are able to regulate membrane potential [ , ] and therefore can regulate calcium signalling via the hyperpolarisation of the plasma membrane [ ]. Among non-canonical functions, it has been determined that potassium channel gene expression regulates various signalling pathways [ ].
The data here using κ -Conotoxin suggest that part of the regulation of ES proliferation by KCNA2 expression is mediated via Kv1.2 functional channels. In this context, different functional Kv1 channels have been shown to be able to regulate cell proliferation [ ], cell invasion/migration [ ], and the cell cycle [ ]. Moreover, the results show that KCNA2 expression regulates Hippo/YAP signalling pathway as well as associated signalling pathways, such as Wnt and TGF-β. Indeed, KCNA2 knock-down in ES cells leads to (i) a decrease in some target genes of the Hippo pathway involved in the regulation of cell proliferation and (ii) a decrease in YAP expression, and in particularly of its translocation to the nucleus. It may be hypothesised that the modulation of the Hippo signalling pathway by KCNA2 expression could drive ES cell proliferation. Indeed, one study has demonstrated that YAP modulates the ability of Ewing sarcoma cells to proliferate [ ]. Interestingly, it was also demonstrated that the Hippo pathway was involved in the modulation of proliferative capacities according to expression of EWS::FLI1 [ ]. Other Kv1-type potassium channels, such as Kv1.3 are able to modulate different signalling pathways, like AKT/mTOR [ ], TGF-β/SMAD3 [ ] or MEK/ERK signaling pathways [ , ]. Here, the data using κ-Conotoxin, suggest that KCNA2 expression modulates Hippo/YAP signaling pathway independently of a functional Kv1.2 channel, i.e. according to a non-canonical process. Consistent with this hypothesis, it has been shown that the expression of a non-conducting Kv1.3 mutant is able to promote cell proliferation [ ].

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this novel study demonstrates for the first time the overexpression of KCNA2 in biopsies from ES patients. Moreover, this study supports the regulation of KCNA2 transcripts by both EWS::FLI1 and EWS::ERG, and the involvement of KCNA2 in the regulation of one of the key processes of tumour development, namely cell proliferation. Although the mechanisms by which KCNA2 drives ES cell proliferation require further elucidation, the results suggest that two complementary mechanisms may be involved in this process, both dependently and independently of Kv1.2 functional channels at plasma membrane. Identification of the Kv1.2 channels as molecular targets accounting for the effects in the control of cell proliferation, a pivotal process in tumor expansion, opens up the way to consider the use of inhibitors of these channels in cancer. Thus, this work highlights the KCNA2/Kv1.2 channel as a potential therapeutic target in ES. This hypothesis is all the more relevant given that KCNA2 is not expressed in any healthy tissue, except the brain. It can therefore be hypothesised that the use of a therapeutic target that would not cross the blood brain barrier, limiting the development of side effects.
