## Abstract
Lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1), encoded by the gene KDM1A, is overexpressed and correlates with poor patient prognosis in Ewing sarcoma. LSD1 and the pathognomonic fusion oncoprotein, EWSR1::FLI1, colocalize throughout the genome, suggesting that LSD1 is a critical co-regulator driving the progression of Ewing sarcoma. However, therapeutic targeting of LSD1 by competitive and noncompetitive inhibitors has yielded mixed results. Irreversible, enzymatic inhibition seems ineffective, but reversible noncompetitive inhibition has predominant off-target mechanisms, leaving open the question of LSD1 function in Ewing sarcoma. In this study, we take a robust approach through multiple methods of depletion in multiple Ewing sarcoma cell lines to define enzymatic and nonenzymatic contributions of LSD1 to transcriptional regulation. We define a core set of 22 genes that are commonly repressed by LSD1 in all cell lines and that repression of these genes downregulates synapse functioning and e-cadherin target genes. Derepression of these genes with LSD1 loss is an early and sustained genotype in all cell lines tested. We further define distinct gene sets in each cell line that are regulated by enzymatic and nonenzymatic LSD1 activity and find repression of e-cadherin target genes to be nonenzymatically regulated. This finding supports the growing body of evidence that in addition to their canonical catalytic activity, chromatin regulatory enzymes serve essential noncanonical roles as well. Furthermore, we uncovered evidence through use of the irreversible inhibitor OG-L002 that 2D cytotoxicity and proliferation assays may be insufficient to determine Ewing sarcoma response to LSD1 inhibition.

## Significance:
In this study, we address a long-standing question in the field surrounding LSD1 and define the distinct enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma. In doing so, we have created a robust data set using genetic and pharmacologic techniques in multiple models to thoroughly characterize LSD1 function in Ewing sarcoma cell lines.

## Introduction
Histone lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) was the first chromatin demethylase identified, revealing histone methylation to be dynamically regulated ( ). LSD1 is comprised of three main structured domains: an N-terminal SWIRM domain important for protein–protein interactions and substrate recognition, an elongated tower domain essential for key protein–protein interactions, and a C-terminal bilobed amine oxidase domain housing the catalytic pocket ( , ). Through an FAD-dependent oxidative reaction, LSD1 removes monomethyl or dimethyl marks from lysine residues on the histone H3 tail, resulting in either an open or closed chromatin state depending on which lysine residue is targeted ( ). Demethylation of histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4) is repressive, and demethylation of lysine 9 (H3K9) is activating ( ). Despite the nomenclature, LSD1 can also demethylate nonhistone proteins, including p53 ( ), mef2d ( ), and HIF-1α ( ). LSD1 lacks a DNA-binding domain and therefore must be incorporated into a larger protein complex to access its substrate in chromatin ( ). Most commonly, LSD1 binds to a SANT domain–containing protein, such as CoREST 1/2/3 or metastasis-associated (MTA) 1/2/3, within a larger chromatin regulatory complex (i.e., REST, BHC, NuRD, etc; refs. , ) in which it also serves a key structural role by recruiting histone deacetylases (HDAC; ref. ). In concert with other complex members, LSD1 binds and demethylates the target lysine on the H3 tail shaping the local chromatin state.
The full scope of physiologic LSD1 functions has been reviewed elsewhere ( – ). As chromatin state plays an important role in development, LSD1 is critical for regulating the balance between stem cell pluripotency and lineage-specific gene expression programs in early development. In embryonic stem cells, LSD1 promotes stem cell proliferation and expression of pluripotency genes ( – ), but in other contexts, LSD1 induces skeletal muscle or osteogenic differentiation ( , – ). In nonneuronal tissues, LSD1 represses neuronal specific genes to allow for nonneuronal differentiation ( ). Disruptions in differentiation and developmental state are hallmarks of cancer, and so aberrant LSD1 expression and activity have been implicated in numerous disease states as well. LSD1 is overexpressed in many high-grade sarcomas, including Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and osteosarcoma ( , ). Ewing sarcoma cell lines have the second highest levels of LSD1 RNA overexpression behind neural cancers, and higher levels of LSD1 correlate with poor patient prognosis ( ), making LSD1 an attractive therapeutic target.
Ewing sarcoma is a devastating adolescent and young adult cancer, with no targeted or precision treatments available ( , ). In 85% of cases, Ewing sarcoma is driven by the t(11;22)(q24;q12) chromosomal translocation, resulting in the fusion oncogene EWSR1::FLI1 ( ). The remainder of cases are driven by alternative FET::ETS fusions, such as EWSR1::ERG ( ). Unfortunately, these fusion transcription factors remain undruggable due to the lack of a concave binding pocket and the disordered FET domain. In response, extensive work has been done to determine whether targeting coregulators like LSD1 could disrupt the downstream oncogenic activity of the fusion. LSD1 is overexpressed, correlates with prognosis, and colocalizes with the fusion in multiple cell lines, supporting LSD1 as a promising therapeutic vulnerability ( , ). As such, LSD1 inhibition has been pursued, but the results have been difficult to interpret. Irreversible enzymatic inhibition of LSD1 via the tranylcypromine (TCP) derivative GSK-LSD1 seemingly has no cytotoxic effect on Ewing sarcoma cell lines ( ). In contrast, small molecule noncompetitive inhibition of LSD1, targeting enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions, is potently cytotoxic in Ewing sarcoma cell lines, but we have recently shown this inhibitor is predominantly acting through off-target mechanisms ( ). Despite considerable interest in LSD1 as a therapeutic target in Ewing sarcoma, disparate results with different inhibitors pose challenges for interpreting its function and relevance yet highlights the possibility of significant nonenzymatic functions of LSD1.
The specific nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma have yet to be defined. LSD1 has mostly been studied for its enzymatic, demethylase function, but there is a growing body of evidence that it has nonenzymatic functions, such as scaffolding, in other contexts. Within the CoREST complex, LSD1 is required for recruitment and proper functioning of HDACs ( , ). Other DNA-binding proteins such as GFI1B, ZNF217, and SNAI1 recruit larger repressive complexes through binding LSD1 ( , , , ). LSD1 scaffolding also stabilizes DNMT1 to preserve the DNA methylation signature essential for proper ESC differentiation ( ). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that LSD1 has distinct nonenzymatic roles in Ewing sarcoma. We have created a molecular suite of tools to deplete LSD1 via multiple methods to define the distinct set of genes regulated by LSD1 and delineate the enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma.

## Materials and Methods

## Reagents and constructs
OG-L002 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (cat. #S7237). UM171 was purchased from MedChemExpress (cat. #HY-12878). shRNA constructs against luciferase (iLuc), LSD1 (iLSD1), and EWSR1::FLI1 (iEF), as well as pMSCV_empty vector (puro), have been previously described (bioRxiv 2024.01.31.578127; ref. ). HA- and Twin-Strep–tagged LSD1 with a HA-tagged full-length wild-type (WT) construct (LSD1wt) and a HA-tagged enzymatically dead construct (LSD1ed) were generated in house. pMCSV_puro was first digested with XhoI as described by NEBcloner. WT LSD1 sequence was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified out of a preexisting plasmid (pET28a_hLSD1), and a gene block with the Twin-Strep and 3xHA tag was ordered from GenScript. These two sequences were cloned into the pMSCV_puro backbone following Gibson cloning ratios. LSD1ed was generated from the LSD1wt plasmid by digesting with BamH-HF followed by BsaBI with heat inactivation. A GenScript gene block with the K661Q and A539E mutations was then cloned into the digested LSD1wt backbone following Gibson cloning ratios. 3xFLAG-tagged EWSR1::FLI1 cDNA was generated as previously described ( ). All plasmid sequences were verified by sequencing (Plasmidsaurus). All guide RNA (gRNA) sequences, gene block sequences, and PCR primers are included in Supplementary Table S1.

## Cell lines and tissue culture
All Ewing sarcoma cell lines A673 (RRID: CVCL_0080), CHP-100 (RRID: CVCL_7166), and TTC-466 (RRID: CVCL_A444) were originally provided by Dr. Stephen Lessnick. A673 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Corning Cellgro 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS: Gibco 16000-044), 1% penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine (P/S/Q; Gibco 10378-016), and 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco 11360-070). CHP-100 cell lines were maintained in McCoys 5A Medium (Gibco, 16600082) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (heated for 30 minutes at 56°C) and 1% PSQ. TTC-466 cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium (Corning Cellgro 15-040-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PSQ. All cells were maintained in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Every experiment was performed within 2 months of thawing a cell line, and all cell lines were tested for Mycoplasma annually using the Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit (ATCC 30-1012K) and short tandem repeat–profiled (LabCorp) upon thawing.

## LSD1 knockout line generation
LSD knockout (KO) cell lines were created from A673, CHP-100, and TTC-466 parental cell lines, in collaboration with the Nationwide Children’s Hospital Abigail Wexner Research Institute’s CRISPR core. The core designed CRISPR gRNAs against KDM1A and used an electroporation delivery protocol optimized for Ewing sarcoma cell lines to deliver the guides preloaded onto the Cas9 enzyme. Guides targeted exons 17 and 18 of the LSD1 gene to prevent transcription of a fully functional mature mRNA strand. Guide sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. As a control, parental WT cells were electroporated alongside KO cells with PBS. Surviving cells were allowed to grow until confluent, and then the pellet was tested for LSD1 KO via sequencing and immunoblotting. All KO cell lines are stable, and KO in the polyclonal population was confirmed by immunoblot with every experiment.

## Antibodies
The antibodies used for immunodetection are as follows: anti-LDS1 (Cell Signaling C69G12, RRID: AB_2799915), anti-lamin (Abcam ab133741, RRID: AB_2616597), anti-HA (Abcam ab18181, RRID: AB_444303), IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG (LI-COR Biosciences, 926-32210, RRID: AB_621842), and IRDye 800CW goat anti-rabbit IgG (LI-COR Biosciences, 926-32211, RRID: AB_621843).

## Retrovirus production and transduction of cells
All short hairpin RNA (shRNA) knockdown (KD) and rescue constructs were delivered to cells via retroviral transduction as previously described (bioRxiv 2024.01.31.578127; ref. ). For shRNA-mediated KD of LD1 and EWSR1::FLI1, cells were treated with guides targeting luciferase, LSD1, or EWSR1::FLI1 in a pMSCV-puromycin backbone. After 3 days of selection, KD cells were rescued with cDNA expressing either empty pMSCV-hygro vector of 3xFLAG-EWSR1::FLI1. Double puromycin/hygromycin selection proceeded for 7 days before immunoblot confirmation of KD and rescue of the fusion.
Retrovirus was produced using HEK-239T-EBNA cells stably expressing Epstein–Barr nuclear antigen (EBNA) under neomycin selection. Ten μg of cDNA vector, vsv-g, and gag-pol were delivered in separate plasmids using Opti-MEM and Mirus to transfect the HEK-293T-EBNA cells. After 2 days, virus was collected in viral collection media (DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% P/S/Q, and 2% 1 mol/L HEPES, pH 7.5) in aliquots over 2 days. Virus was passed through a 2-μmol/L filter and immediately added to cells to be infected. Target cells were transduced with retrovirus supplemented with 2.5 μL of 8 mg/mL polybrene. Cells incubated in the viral mix for 2 hours and then were supplemented with 8 mL of fresh media and 8 μL of 8 mg/mL polybrene. The next day, cells were passed into selection media containing 2 μg/μL puromycin (Sigma P8833) for selection for 3 days. Following selection, cells were seeded for Incucyte cell proliferation assays and soft agar assays, and pellets were collected for protein and RNA isolation for subsequent Western blot analysis and RNA extraction for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq).

## Incucyte cell proliferation assay
Ewing sarcoma cells were seeded at a density of 2,000 cells per well for A673 and CHP-100 and 5,000 cells per well for TTC-466 in triplicate in a 96-well clear tissue culture plate (Corning). Phase-contrast images were taken every 4 hours until confluent or growth arrest (approximately 9 days) in the Incucyte ZOOM Kinetic Imaging System (Essen BioScience). Cell confluence was measured using Incucyte ZOOM 2016A software (Essen BioScience) and plotted to display a growth curve. Each data point represents the mean ± standard error of the mean from a minimum of three replicates.

## Soft agar colony formation assay
Soft agar assays were performed as previously described ( , ). For A673 and CHP-100 cell lines, 7,500 cells were seeded per agar, and for TTC-466, 18,000 cells were seeded per agar. Every plate was fed twice a week with 500 μL of media supplemented with selection antibiotic or drug and then imaged at 2 weeks after seeding. Colonies were counted at 2 weeks using ImageJ software.

## Dose–response curves
A total of 10,000 cells per well in 200 μL of media were plated in triplicate in a 96-well clear tissue culture plate (Corning). After 24 hours, a 9-part serial dilution of UM171 in media was prepared in PCR tubes. Equivalent volumes of the drug dilution were added to each of the wells. Following 96 hours of incubation, 110 μL of media was removed from each well and replaced with 80 μL of CellTiter-Glo (Promega) reagent. The plate was covered with aluminum foil to reduce light and agitated at 300 rpm for 15 minutes at room temperature. Afterward, 160 μL of the cell solution was transferred to an opaque-walled 96-well plate (Corning), and luminescence was measured on a Synergy H1 plate reader (Agilent). Cell viability was calculated relative to an untreated DMSO control well.

## Protein isolation and Western blot analysis
1 to 5 million cells per sample were pelleted and then flash-frozen at the desired time point after drug treatment of viral infection. Whole cell protein was extracted using Pierce RIPA buffer, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube for quantification using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific 23225). Ten μg of each protein sample was run on a precast 4% to 15% gradient Tris-Glycine gel (Bio-Rad 4561084) at 90 V for 20 minutes and then 110 V for 1 hour. Proteins were then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific IB23002) using a Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad, 1703940) at 15 V for 1 hour. The membranes were then blocked for 60 minutes at room temperature with Odyssey RBA Blocking Buffer (Li-Cor 927-40003). Primary antibody was applied at 1:1,000 concentration overnight at 4°C. The next day, the membrane was washed three times for 5 minutes with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20, and then goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody was applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were washed as previously described and imaged on the Odyssey LI-COR CLx Imager (LI-COR Bio, discontinued).

## Quantitative reverse-transcriptase PCR
Cell pellets of 200,000 to 500,000 cells were flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen prior to preparation. Samples were thawed on ice, and total RNA was extracted using an RNAeasy kit (Qiagen). RNA concentration was then measured on a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA was amplified using one-step quantitative reverse-transcriptase PCR (qRT-PCR) and SYBR Green fluorescence for quantification. After amplification, a melting curve analysis was performed. Normalized fold enrichment was calculated relative to RPL30. Primer sequences are available in Supplementary Table S1.

## RNA isolation for RNA-seq
A total of 250,000 cells were pelleted and flash-frozen after treatment for RNA isolation and sequencing. Total mRNA was extracted from the cell pellets following the Qiagen RNeasy kit. A gDNA removal column and DNase treatment were used on all samples to purify for RNA. RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop, and 1 μg of RNA per sample (20 μL of 50 ng/μL) was submitted to the Abigail Wexner Research Institute Genomic Services Laboratory for library preparation (TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit; Illumina 20020594), library quality control, and next-generation sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000). The target sequence depth was 50 million 150-bp paired end reads. The raw BCL files were converted to FASTQ files and returned for in-house analysis.

## RNA-seq analysis
FASTQ files from the Abigail Wexner Research Institute Genomic Services Laboratory were analyzed using an in-house–developed RNA-seq analysis pipeline ( ). The resulting DESeq2 output was used for data visualization. Overlap analysis of commonly differentially expressed genes (DEG) was performed using base R packages [DESeq2, ggplot2 (RRID: SCR_014601), VennDiagram, grid, and pbapply]. Pathway analysis was performed using the R package enrichR ( ) and clusterProfiler ( ). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA_4.4.0 (RRID: SCR_003199, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). The direct A673 and TTC-466 fusion target list used has been previously defined (bioRxiv 2024.01.31.578127; ref. ).

## Results

## LSD1 represses neural synaptic functioning and e-cadherin target genes in Ewing sarcoma
Ewing sarcoma is a heterogenous disease; therefore, we chose to study LSD1 functions using orthogonal methods in multiple cell lines. Our three-cell line panel, consisting of A673, CHP-100, and TTC-466, is derived from both primary tumor and metastases, includes three different fusions, and harbors both WT and mutated versions of the most frequent patient mutations in Ewing sarcoma, p53, CDKN2A, and STAG2 ( ; ref. ).
 To define LSD1 function, we sought to deplete LSD1 at the DNA, mRNA, and protein levels. We used CRISPR-Cas9–mediated gene editing to create unselected, stable, polyclonal LSD1 KO cell lines from all three parental Ewing sarcoma lines. We also dosed parental Ewing sarcoma cells with 800 nmol/L of UM171 for 96 hours to degrade the LSD1 protein (UM; ). UM171 functions as a molecular glue, stabilizing the interaction between its substrate and the KBTBD4 subunit of an E3 ligase allowing for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation ( , ). In all three cell lines, we achieved KO with transient CRISPR-Cas9 editing and significant depletion of LSD1 protein with UM171 ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B).
 LSD1 KD via shRNA in A673 cells has been previously reported ( , ). However, we were unable to get comparable KD across all cell lines using a consistent shRNA design (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Furthermore, despite no KD in CHP-100 or TTC-466, we saw a modest reduction in 2D cellular proliferation, and a pronounced reduction in 3D growth in soft agar (Supplementary Fig. S1D–S1F). Confounding factors thus hampered our ability to determine the effect of LSD1 loss with shRNAs. We proceeded using our consistently successful depletion methods, KO and UM, for further analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1G).
To identify transcriptional changes resulting from LSD1 loss, we performed RNA-seq on our KO and UM samples. When analyzed together, cell line identity was the primary source of variation between samples, supporting the heterogenous nature of Ewing sarcoma (Supplementary Figs. S1H and S2A–S2C). The number of DEGs varied greatly with mechanism of LSD1 depletion and cell line, with substantially more upregulated DEGs than downregulated DEGs, consistent with LSD1’s defined repressive role ( ). Notably, LSD1 KO in TTC-466 only resulted in 176 total DEGs.
Despite the variability, we identified a core set of 22 genes that are commonly upregulated following KO or UM171 treatment across all cell lines ( ). There are no genes commonly downregulated across cell lines and conditions. We define these 22 upregulated genes as high-confidence LSD1-regulated genes in Ewing sarcoma. Pathway analysis of these genes indicates that LSD1 represses synaptic and neurotransmitter receptor activity, as well as e-cadherin (CDH1) target genes ( and ). The pathway titled “Onder CDH1 targets Dn” refers to genes that are downregulated with loss of CDH1 and are therefore activated by endogenous CDH1. Hence, LSD1 represses genes that are activated by CDH1. Loss of CDH1 is a hallmark of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis in tumor cells ( , ). Repression of CDH1 targets therefore suggests LSD1 contributes to a prometastatic phenotype. Cell line–specific pathway analysis reveals that these common pathways are not always the most enriched following LSD1 depletion, but repression of CHD1 target genes consistently ranks among the top 2 enriched pathways by gene ratio in all cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3F).
We checked whether these core LSD1 genes are co-regulated by the oncogenic driver fusion. EWSR1::FLI1 and EWSR1::ERG are known to regulate distinct sets of genes in different cell lines ( ). We thus reanalyzed published RNA-seq data from KD/rescue experiments in A673 and TTC-466 cells and generated new KD/rescue datasets for CHP-100 cells to define fusion-regulated target genes in each cell line (Supplementary Fig. S4J and S4K). We then defined fusion target genes as those commonly regulated in all three cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). Only three genes were present in the overlap between common LSD1 and common fusion genes, but we noted significant overlaps in cell line–specific fusion gene targets, both activated and repressed, and LSD1 repressed targets (Supplementary Fig. S4D–S4F). Despite significant overlap, gene set enrichment between LSD1 target genes and fusion targets was not significant in any cell line, indicating little to no functional relationship between LSD1 and fusion function in these cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S4G–S4I). Pathway analysis of the overlap between LSD1-regulated genes and fusion-regulated genes revealed repression of e-cadherin target genes to be the only pathway common between all three cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S5A and S5B). Commonly repressed genes in A673 related to migration and taxis, whereas CHP-100 common genes are involved in smooth muscle cell proliferation and platelet activation, and TTC-466 genes are enriched in long-term synaptic potentiation (Supplementary Fig. S5B). There are no pathways shared between all cell lines of LSD1-repressed genes and fusion-activated genes (Supplementary Fig. S5C and S5D). Interestingly, genes repressed by LSD1 and activated by the fusion in A673 are enriched in e-cadherin target genes, as well as Ewing sarcoma progenitor genes and known LSD1 (KDM1A) targets (Supplementary Fig. S5C and S5D). These results suggest that in A673, EWSR1::FLI1 and LSD1 functions are not as strongly linked and sometimes function in opposition.

## Derepression of e-cadherin target genes is early and sustained
The KO cell lines here are stable polyclonal populations, as cells have adapted following electroporation CRISPR editing and gene loss. In contrast, UM171-induced protein degradation allowed for temporal analysis of the changes in gene expression following loss of LSD1 protein. Six hours of UM171 treatment is the earliest time point we see sufficient LSD1 degradation (Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B). We therefore dosed our three cell lines with 800 nmol/L of UM171 for 6, 24, and 96 hours to deplete LSD1 and analyzed each time point by RNA-seq to identify early (direct) and late (indirect) gene targets of LSD1 ( ).
 We defined DEGs at each time point as those common to KO and UM171 treatment to minimize inclusion of non-LSD1 targets of UM171. As time progresses, there are more overlapping upregulated genes between the KO and UM samples in all cell lines and common between the three cell lines, likely corresponding to progressive downstream effects of LSD1 loss ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6F–S6H). Interestingly, the number of genes regulated by LSD1 is strongly dependent on cell line, supporting a context-dependent role of LSD1 within Ewing sarcoma ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S6C–S6E).
Having identified LSD1’s common role in repressing CDH1 target genes across all cell lines, we performed pathway analysis on each time point in each cell line to determine the kinetics of derepression following LSD1 loss. Derepression of CDH1 target genes is apparent at 6 hours of LSD1 depletion and sustained throughout 96 hours of depletion in all cell lines ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7A–S7D). This was surprising considering only four genes were common between all cell lines as early as 6 hours of UM171 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6G). LSD1 likely performs this common function through slightly differing gene targets in each cell line.

## LSD1 degradation results in a cell line–specific deficit in 3D proliferation in agar
Prior work has demonstrated significant impairment of 2D cellular proliferation and 3D growth in soft agar with shRNA-mediated LSD1 loss ( ). However, as discussed above, shRNA-mediated depletion is inconsistent across cell lines. Therefore, we sought to determine whether loss of LSD1 via KO or UM171 degradation affects 2D and 3D cellular proliferation as with shRNA KD and treatment with noncompetitive LSD1 inhibitors.
We were surprised that KO of LSD1 in two of three cell lines did not reduce cellular proliferation, with only a slight lag in proliferation in TTC-466 [ (top)]. Furthermore, when KO cells are plated in soft agar, there is no reduction in 3D cellular proliferation in any cell line [ (bottom)]. In TTC-466, the colonies seem smaller, but the number is no different than DMSO treatment.
 We then measured cellular proliferation and 3D growth in soft agar with cells pretreated for either 6 or 96 hours with UM171 and again saw a cell line–specific response. A673 and CHP-100 cells showed a slight delay in 2D cellular proliferation, whereas TTC-466 has a more pronounced response [ (top); Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8C (top)]. We were again surprised to find different responses to treatment in anchorage-independent growth in soft agar. Like KO, UM171 treatment did not reduce A673 cell growth in soft agar, but surprisingly in CHP-100 and TTC-466, we see a significant decrease in colonies when seeded after either 6 or 96 hours of UM171 treatment [ (bottom); Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8C (bottom)]. This is not due to differing IC50 values of UM171 between the cell lines or differing levels of baseline LSD1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S8D–S8F).
Stable KO of LSD1 does not create a phenotype, but early loss of LSD1 by degradation decreased 3D proliferation in a subset of cell lines. The differences in colony formation could be due to selective pressure exerted on cells during electroporation at genome editing, off-target effects of the CRISPR KO machinery, off-target effects of UM171, or all of the above. We therefore sought an alternative approach to test whether the effects seen in soft agar were related to impaired LSD1 function. To do this, we used an irreversible LSD1 inhibitor to directly and specifically block LSD1 demethylase function in Ewing sarcoma cells ( , ).

## Enzymatic inhibition of LSD1 is cell line–specific
OG-L002 is a TCP derivative that blocks LSD1 demethylase activity by forming an adduct with the FAD cofactor ( ; ref. ). Both OG-L002 and another TCP derivative, GSK-LSD1, showed minimal cytotoxicity in 2D assays of Ewing sarcoma cell growth, but 3D growth in agar following OG-L002 or GSK-LSD1 treatment has only been studied in A673 cells ( , ). As expected, in A673, OG-L002 does not delay 2D proliferation nor impair 3D colony growth in soft agar ( ). However, despite minimal defects in 2D proliferation in CHP-100 and TTC-466, we were surprised to find both cell lines showed a statistically significant reduction in colonies following OG-L002 treatment ( and ). This finding suggests that inhibiting LSD1 enzymatic activity could be more important for anchorage-independent growth than proliferation, with cell line–specific sensitivity to this treatment.
 We performed RNA-seq on our OG-L002–treated samples and found that the degree of transcriptomic changes correlates with the cell line–dependent reduction in 3D growth in soft agar ( ; Supplementary Fig. S9A and S9B). There is only one upregulated DEG and one downregulated DEG in A673 with OG-L002 treatment, compared with 756 upregulated and 166 downregulated DEGs in CHP-100 and 1,287 upregulated and 1,496 downregulated DEGs in TTC-466 ( ). There are no genes commonly regulated in all three cell lines; however, 101 upregulated and 15 downregulated DEGs are common between CHP-100 and TTC-466 ( ). The substantial difference between the number of DEGs and the few overlaps between CHP-100 and TTC-466 suggest that LSD1 may have different enzymatic functions in each cell line. Therefore, we performed individual pathway analysis of each cell line to define the top 5 enriched pathways in CHP-100 and TTC-466 ( ). None of the top 5 pathways overlapped between cell lines, and none of the top 5 pathways in CHP-100 or TTC-466 are shared with the core LSD1 pathways previously defined ( ). This raises an interesting question regarding the distinct enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma cell lines. Emerging evidence indicates that LSD1 performs critical scaffolding and recruitment functions independently of its enzymatic activity in other contexts ( , ). Additionally, the reversible noncompetitive LSD1 inhibitor, SP-2509, was originally theorized to be more cytotoxic than other LSD1 enzymatic inhibitors due to its ability to disrupt the nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 ( , ). However, the specific nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma remain unclear.

## LSD1 functions predominantly nonenzymatically in A673
Having defined common and cell line–specific LSD1 target genes, we next sought to use both genetic and pharmacologic approaches, as above, to differentiate between LSD1 enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions in Ewing sarcoma cell lines. To our knowledge, LSD1 had not yet been knocked down and rescued in an Ewing sarcoma cell line. With our CRISPR KO cell lines, we set out to rescue LSD1wt and LSD1ed delivered by retroviral transduction ( ). To render LSD1 enzymatically dead, we mutated the lysine at residue 661 to glutamine (K661Q) and the alanine at residue 539 to glutamate (A539E), which interferes with the interaction between the FAD cofactor and the H3 tail and has been shown to disrupt LSD1 enzymatic activity both on naked DNA and nucleosomes ( ; ref. ). Endogenously, LSD1 has 2 splice isoforms expressed in Ewing sarcoma with and without an additional 60 base pair insert between exons 2 and 3 (LSD1 and LSD1 + 2A), but we were unable to doubly rescue both isoforms at the same time. We achieved rescue of LSD1wt and LSD1ed without the 2a exon in both A673 and CHP-100, but not TTC-466, validated by protein and qRT-PCR ( ; Supplementary Fig. S11A and S11B).
 We performed RNA-seq on our KO cells expressing the rescue constructs to define the genes specifically regulated by LSD1 enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions. However, we found virus treatment itself is the strongest driver of variability ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10A and S10B). In both cell lines, the number of DEGs between the WT and LSD1 KO cells (CRWT vs. CRKO) is significantly greater than the number of DEGs between these same cells after treatment with an empty virus control (LSD1endo vs. LSD1KO; Supplementary Fig. S10C and S10D). This suggests treatment of cells with virus confounds our ability to analyze the rescue experiments (Supplementary Fig. S10A and S10B). In CHP-100, all the virally treated samples clustered together and separately from the samples not treated with virus (CRKO and CRWT) samples in principal component analysis (Supplementary Fig. S10B). As a result, the transcriptomic signature of LSD1 expression was almost completely negated by empty virus treatment. Compared with LSD1KO, rescue with LSD1wt resulted in 8 activated and 3 repressed DEGS, and rescue with LSD1ed resulted in 14 activated and 0 repressed DEGs (Supplementary Fig. S10D). As such, we were unable to determine differences between enzymatic and nonenzymatic activity of LSD1 in CHP-100 using this cell line model. Although our A673 samples were subject to the same confounding factor of viral treatment, we still saw significant transcriptomic changes with rescue of both LSD1wt and LSD1ed compared with LSD1KO, allowing us to compare enzymatic versus nonenzymatic activity in this particular model. Rescue with LSD1wt resulted in 1,381 activated and 2,191 repressed DEGs, and rescue with LSD1ed resulted in 1,579 activated and 2,322 repressed DEGs ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10B). Using these DEGs, we differentiated between enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 in A673.
We define the nonenzymatic targets to be the 1,852 genes repressed by both LSD1wt and LSD1ed, and the enzymatic targets to be the 313 genes that are solely repressed by LSD1wt and not LSD1ed ( ). There was a similar ratio of genes that are activated by LSD1 nonenzymatically (1,039) and enzymatically (310), although there were no enriched pathways activated enzymatically (Supplementary Fig. S11C–S11E). Pathway analysis on LSD1-repressed genes revealed CDH1 target genes to be repressed both enzymatically and nonenzymatically; however, there were more nonenzymatic genes in that pathway ( ). In addition, Ewing sarcoma progenitor gene targets and the neural reactome are repressed nonenzymatically. Only two pathways from Gene Ontology Biological Processes were enriched by LSD1 enzymatic targets ( ). As there are no transcriptomic changes with OG-L002–mediated enzymatic inhibition, our data support LSD1 to function predominantly nonenzymatically in A673 cells.

## Repression of CDH1 target genes is nonenzymatically regulated
Due to the limitations of the genetic rescues, we took an orthogonal pharmacologic approach combining KO, UM171, and OG-L002–treated cells to define genes that LSD1 regulates enzymatically and nonenzymatically. Using this method, we define our nonenzymatically regulated genes to be those common between KO and UM171 treatment but not OG-L002 treatment, and our enzymatically regulated genes as those common between all three. In CHP-100, there are 104 nonenzymatically and 127 enzymatically upregulated genes ( ). In TTC-466, there are 75 nonenzymatically and 34 nonenzymatically upregulated genes ( ). Using this approach, there are 555 nonenzymatically and 0 enzymatically upregulated DEGs in A673 ( and ). There are 50 downregulated nonenzymatic DEGs in A673, 14 downregulated nonenzymatic DEGs in CHP-100, and none in TTC-466 (Supplementary Fig. S12A–S12C). We performed pathway analysis on these gene sets in each cell line. In both CHP-100 and TTC-466, repression of CDH1 target genes is regulated nonenzymatically in addition to other EMT pathways ( and ). Of note in CHP-100, we found that LSD1 represses genes that are upregulated with expression of exogenous EWSR1::FLI1 in human mesenchymal stem cells ( ). This suggests that LSD1 counteracts EWSR1::FLI1 activity specifically in the mesenchymal stem cell context. In addition, in TTC-466, we find that LSD1 nonenzymatically represses previously published LSD1 targets ( and ). Thus, in light of our data showing OG-L002 has no transcriptional effect in A673 cells, repression of CDH1 is regulated nonenzymatically by LSD1 in all three cell lines, suggesting that LSD1 represses these gene targets in a different manner than previously shown in epithelial cells ( ). Nevertheless, our results address a longstanding question in the field and define the specific enzymatic and nonenzymatic gene targets of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma cell lines.
 

## Discussion
There has been considerable interest in LSD1 inhibition in Ewing sarcoma since the early 2010s when LSD1 was discovered to be overexpressed in Ewing sarcoma ( ). Since then, LSD1 has been shown to correlate with poor patient prognosis, co-localize with EWSR1::FLI1, and regulate a similar gene set as EWSR1::FLI1 ( , ). However, we recently demonstrated that the off-target pharmacologic effects of noncompetitive LSD1 inhibition are responsible for the disruption of the fusion transcriptional program ( ). These off-target effects include disrupted iron–sulfur cluster biogenesis, this activity correlates with disruption of EWSR1::FLI1–mediated gene regulation, and the relationship between Fe-S cluster biogenesis and EWSR1::FLI1 function are an area of active study ( ). These recent findings are further supported with our data presented here demonstrating a general lack of common gene targets between LSD1 and the fusion when using multiple LSD1 depletion methods to define LSD1-mediated gene regulation. Prior studies defined LSD1-regulated genes using shRNA-mediated KD, and this was the least reliable method in our study. Whereas LSD1 and EWSR1::FLI1 certainly colocalized throughout the genome ( ), previously used gene expression signatures for LSD1 may reflect RNAi-related technical artifacts. The specific function of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma has thus remained undefined, both enzymatically and nonenzymatically. Through a combination of orthogonal genetic and pharmacologic techniques, we identified gene sets commonly regulated by LSD1 across three Ewing sarcoma cell lines, including repression of neurotransmitter synaptic functioning and e-cadherin target genes. Of note, these pathways were predominantly regulated by noncanonical nonenzymatic functions of LSD1 ( ). Through use of multiple orthogonal depletion methods and analysis of proliferation, colony formation, and gene expression in a small panel of Ewing sarcoma cell lines, we uncovered the potential of 3D growth assays to identify cell line–specific sensitivity to LSD1 that has not been explored.
 The power of our study comes from the use of orthogonal genetic and pharmacologic methods to define our LSD1-regulated gene sets. However, there are some limitations to our approach. shRNA-mediated KD is a longstanding method of choice to study protein function, yet our results suggest that viral treatment of cells was the main driver of phenotypic changes seen with LSD1i, and thus we were unable to use this technique. Cells undergoing CRIPSR-Cas9 gene editing are placed under a significant selective pressure during electroporation that selects for cells with the strongest growth potential, changing the phenotype of the cells and possibly masking a signal due to LSD1 loss ( ). UM171 directly binds HDACs and CoREST, in addition to LSD1, so this degrader is not LSD1-specific ( , ). Furthermore, use of viral transduction to express LSD1 constructs significantly masked the transcriptomic changes resulting from LSD1 expression. Despite these limitations, we leveraged a range of available techniques in parallel to curate a robust data set defining LSD1 gene regulation in Ewing sarcoma.
Our transcriptomic studies revealed the role of LSD1 in repressing neurotransmitter synaptic functioning. These are pathways that are activated in neurologic development, and as LSD1 is highly expressed in early development, our data suggest that LSD1 represses neurologic development in Ewing sarcoma. This finding aligns with the established role that LSD1 represses neurologic differentiation in nonneuronal tissues ( ). However, there is also debate surrounding the cell of origin for Ewing sarcoma whether it derives from a neural crest stem cell or a mesenchymal stem cell. The results from Vasileva and colleagues ( ) demonstrate that neural crest stem cells can express EWSR1::FLI1 leading to tumors in zebrafish. Furthermore, data from Miller and colleagues show Ewing sarcoma cells exist somewhere along an axis of neuroendocrine to mesenchymal and pluripotent to differentiated ( , ). In this case, LSD1 could be contributing to holding cells in a more neurologic progenitor, undifferentiated state and therefore in a more aggressive state. This role of LSD1 is consistent with patient data demonstrating higher levels of LSD1 correlate to poor patient prognosis ( ).
Further supporting LSD1’s contribution to a more aggressive phenotype is the role we uncovered for LSD1 repressing e-cadherin (CDH1) target genes. This creates a pseudo “loss of e-cadherin” environment which is a hallmark of EMT and metastasis. There is prior evidence that LSD1 is recruited through the SNAG domain of Snai1 to repress e-cadherin by demethylating H3K4me2 at the e-cadherin promoter ( ). Our data suggests LSD1 represses CDH1 target genes in a demethylase-independent mechanism, possibly by HDAC recruitment or another scaffolding role. An alternative explanation could be LSD1 interacting with a MTA protein as a member of the NuRD complex. However, previous reports have shown this interaction is antimetastatic in breast cancer. Furthermore, in each cell line, repression of CDH1 target genes is early and sustained through 96 hours of UM171 treatment even with so few overlapping genes as early as 6 hours. Therefore, LSD1 regulates this pathway through different mechanisms. This is likely due to the different cell line environments whether from differing tumor stages or different fusions creating a different oncogenic environment. Nonetheless, LSD1 is a highly context-dependent protein and works through different nonenzymatic mechanisms to achieve the same prometastatic phenotype.
Considering these results, we were surprised to see that whereas loss of LSD1 does not consistently affect 2D cellular proliferation, there are cell line–specific defects in 3D growth when treated with UM171. We first rationalized this finding to be the result of off-target effects of UM171. However, we see the same cell line–dependent response when we treat with enzymatic inhibitor, OG-L002, which binds tightly and specifically to LSD1. Instead, 2D toxicity or proliferation assays may be insufficient as a reporter assay for LSD1 function. The only 3D growth assay of TCP derivatives published to date solely included A673 cells, missing potential effects in other cell lines ( ). It is reasonable that LSD1’s role in repressing e-cadherin target genes contributes to the observed defect in 3D growth by reducing cell-to-cell junctions. Although we do not consistently see a 3D growth delay in all cell lines, A673 is a particularly odd cell line as it harbors a BRAF mutation and is one of the only Ewing sarcoma cell lines able to tolerate fusion KD. One possible explanation is the STAG2 status of each cell line. A673 and CHP-100 both express WT STAG2, and TTC-466 expresses a loss of function translocation of the protein. Loss of STAG2 has been demonstrated to result in similar dysregulation of neurodevelopmental programs and increased metastatic potential, resulting in decreased patient survival ( – ). It is possible that WT STAG2 dampens the activity of LSD1. Therefore, in the setting of STAG2 loss, LSD1 becomes a stronger contributor to the deregulated neuronal programs and metastatic potential, and so cell lines with STAG2 loss are more responsive to LSD1 inhibition.
Another possible explanation is that OG-L002 has been shown to disrupt LSD1 binding to SNAG domain proteins. Differing expression of SNAG domain–containing proteins in different cell lines could account for the varied response to OG-L002. Future experiments using new LSD1 enzymatic inhibitors with Grob fragmentation that do not disrupt SNAG binding are needed to ascertain whether SNAG domain–containing proteins account for the cell line–specific response to LSD1 inhibition or whether this 3D growth defect is strictly due to enzymatic disruption. If expression of SNAG domain–containing proteins correlates with response to OG-L002 treatment, perhaps expression of this family of proteins could act as a biomarker to indicate response to LSD1i. Should SNAG expression not correlate with response to OG-L002, our data still indicate that some cell lines respond in 3D assays. Our current 3D soft agar protocol is not optimized for high-throughput screening, but perhaps we could develop a pipeline to take patient tumor tissue and seed it into 3D soft agar assays and determine whether that patient is susceptible to LSD1 inhibitor therapy.
Taken together, our data begin to answer longstanding questions surrounding the function of LSD1 in Ewing sarcoma and supports a small and distinct role for LSD1-mediated gene regulation in Ewing sarcoma. We set out to define the enzymatic versus nonenzymatic LSD1 functions in Ewing sarcoma and have uncovered a previously undefined role of LSD1 in repressing neurotransmitter functioning at synapses and CDH1 target genes. Both roles could promote a more developmental and metastatic phenotype, contributing to the worse outcomes seen for patients with higher LSD1 expression. Furthermore, LSD1 predominantly functions nonenzymatically in these pathways. With a new emphasis on noncanonical functions of LSD1, it is even more crucial to characterize the full scope of LSD1 complexes to define the mechanism by which LSD1 regulates these genes. Our data suggest that certain cell lines, and by extension certain tumors, could be susceptible to LSD1 inhibition targeting these predominant nonenzymatic functions. A larger cell line panel is needed to validate correlation, but tracking SNAG domain expression or developing a technique to screen 3D response to LSD1i from patient tumor tissue could identify an avenue in which LSD1 inhibitor therapy is indicated. LSD1 inhibitors are currently in clinical trials for other cancers and could be repurposed and used as a combinational therapeutic for certain LSD1-sensitive Ewing sarcoma tumors.
