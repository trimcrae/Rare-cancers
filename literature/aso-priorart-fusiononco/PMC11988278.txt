## ABSTRACT
Gene fusions represent important oncogenic driver mutations resulting in aberrant cellular signaling. In up to 17% of all solid tumors at least one gene fusion can be identified. Precision therapy targeting fusion gene signaling has demonstrated effective clinical benefit. Advancements in clinically relevant next-generation sequencing and bioinformatic techniques have enabled expansion of therapeutic opportunity to subpopulations of patients with fusion gene expression. Clinically, tyrosine inhibitors have shown efficacy in treating fusion gene expressing cancers. Fusion genes are also clonal mutations, meaning it is a personal cancer target involving all cancer cells of that patient, not just a subpopulation of cancer cells within the cancer mass. Thus, both fusion signal disruption and immune signal targeting are effective therapeutic directions. This review discusses fusion gene targeting, therapeutic resistance, and molecular biomarkers.
KEYWORDS: Fusion gene, liquid biopsy, clonal neoantigen, clonal mutation, solid tumor

## Plain Language Summary
Genes are a segment of DNA that encode the instructions for proteins within every cell. Mutations occur when there is a change in the gene sequence. One type of mutations are gene fusions, where two genes combine and become fused together. When this happens between genes that effect normal cell functions, the cells can become cancerous. In up to 17% of all solid tumors, at least one gene fusion can be identified. Precision therapy, which can target a patient's specific cancer and gene fusion, has demonstrated effective clinical benefit. Opportunities for precision therapy have increased due to scientific advancements that allow us to sequence DNA and analyze it quickly. Current treatment for cancer with known gene fusions includes tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Fusion genes are also clonal mutations, meaning it is a personal cancer target involving all cancer cells of that patient, not just a subpopulation of cancer cells within the cancer mass. This review discusses fusion genes and ways to target these cancer signals.

## Introduction
Fusion genes reflect the rare but highly complex instability of genome infrastructure, resulting in random events of DNA breakage and fusion. While some alterations occur in non-coding areas (passenger mutations) with no functional consequences, other fusions involve disruptions in tumorigenesis-related to the processing of apoptotic response, DNA damage, cell proliferation, survival, and spread. Advances in molecular techniques such as gene expression profiling, next-generation sequencing (including RNA sequencing), and bioinformatics “omics” analyses have allowed for the detection of complex fusions amenable to therapeutic interventions and for the purpose of diagnostic delineation of histologic assessment.
Constitutive kinase activation is the most common end-product of two-gene fusions, whereby one of the fusion partners is a kinase, and the new fusion protein acts as a strong oncogenic driver and provides a ligand-independent, constitutive activation of downstream molecular signaling [ ]. Constitutive kinase activation can also result from alterations in upstream regulatory elements or loss of regulatory mRNA without direct involvement of the kinase protein in the fusion product, as well as conformational changes that favor an active state of a protein kinase with a loss of its intrinsic autoinhibitory N-terminal domain [ ]. Constitutive kinase activation has been described in both tyrosine-kinase and serine-threonine kinase fusions such as ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK1/3, and FGFR1/2/3 (tyrosine-kinase), as well as BRAF, CRAF, and MAST1/2 [ ]. Frequently, these fusions increase downstream signaling in growth and survival-promoting pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways. They are also ideal targets for targeted therapies with several inhibitors currently being investigated or routinely used in clinical practice to treat solid tumors harboring such fusions.
Another fusion protein-mediated mechanism is aberrant transcription, whereby a chimeric protein includes a transcription factor, and leads to cell transformation [ ]. One example is the EWSR1-FLI1 translocation, resulting in a protein fusion that binds and alters DNA transcription in Ewing sarcoma [ ]. Similarly, in prostate cancer, the TMPRSS2:ERG fusion protein downregulates androgen receptor (AR) expression in parallel to binding and inhibiting ARs, thus disrupting cellular differentiation (via increased histone-lysine N-methyltransferase EZH2-mediated cellular de-differentiation).
Fusion of a promoter to a proto-oncogene is another mechanism that either augments the expression of the proto-oncogene or disrupts a transcriptionally repressive upstream DNA sequence, eventually leading to higher expression of the proto-oncogene. An example seen in dermato-fibrosarcoma protuberans is the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion, resulting in loss of the upstream PDGFB regulation that typically involves collagen expression.
Finally, other albeit more rare fusions can result in i) the inactivation of tumor-suppressor genes (TSH), via deletion of the TSG promoter [ ], ii) metabolic deregulation, iii) abnormal Wnt/β-catenin and TGFβ pathway activation, and iv) chromatin remodeling. In summary, key fusion gene signals can override inactivating signals and support driving force in uncontrolled cellular tumorigenesis, growth, replication, and spread. Fusion genes also provide a tantalizing target to immunotherapy approaches. Hypothetically, a potential target that could limit unintended off-target toxicity. The incidence of fusion genes is inconsistent across solid tumors as a whole, with some exhibiting high rates of fusion genes including Ewing's sarcoma (90%) and prostate cancer (50–70%).

2.

## Therapeutic targeting of fusion proteins in solid tumors
Successful therapies for oncogenic driver tyrosine kinase fusion proteins have been made possible by the rapid development of small-molecule protein-kinase inhibitors (TKI) (Supplementary Table S1). TKIs targeting fusion genes act via several mechanisms of action, generally classified into five types: type I and II are competitive inhibitors that occupy the ATP-binding pocket of the target fusion protein in its active and inactive conformations, respectively. Type III and IV TKIs are allosteric inhibitors that bind next to the ATP-binding site, and outside the cleft, respectively [ ]. Given the highly conserved ATP-binding site of different kinases, small TKIs have been designed to target multiple kinases given the difficulty of designing highly selective inhibitors with “off-target” toxicities as a subsequent side effect [ ]. As a result, to bypass these off-target toxicities, type V inhibitors have been designed to be bivalent, targeting two distinct regions of the protein tyrosine kinase.
2.1.

## ALK fusion
There are currently several FDA-approved fusion protein-targeted therapies (Suplementary Table S1). The EML4:ALK fusion is the most prevalent combination although other ALK fusion partners have been identified (TGF, TFG, KIF5B, KLC1, STRN, WDCP, TPM1/3/4, PPP4R3B, GTF2IRD1, VCL, and DCTN) [ , ]. ALK fusions have mostly been described in lung adenocarcinoma (2–7%), particularly in younger, never/light smokers [ ]. However, these fusions have also been found in Spitz tumors, breast, colorectal, esophageal, thyroid, renal cell, and bladder cancers, sarcoma, melanoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and renal medullary carcinoma [ , , ].
The upstream partner of ALK provides a dimerization domain for the chimeric oncoprotein, resulting in ligand-independent dimerization and subsequent constitutive activation. The 5’ fusion partner/variant also dictates sensitivity to TKI. Currently, there are five FDA-approved ALK fusion inhibitors: crizotinib, ceritinib, alectinib, brigatinib, and lorlatinib.
Crizotinib is a first-generation, and the first ALK-targeted inhibitor to be approved. It is a type I TKI, initially approved in for second-line ALK-fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) following progression on a platinum-containing regimen, showing higher objective response rates (ORR) compared to chemotherapy in both treatment-naïve and pre-treated patients (74% and 65% vs. 45% and 20%, respectively) [ ]. One major limitation of this drug is the early emergence of acquired resistance, prompting the development of second-generation ALK inhibitors: ceritinib, alectinib, and brigatinib. Compared to crizotinib, these inhibitors are more potent, have superior efficacy in treating CNS metastases, and can overcome the acquired resistance mediated by secondary mutations [ , ].
Ceritinib was initially evaluated in the ASCEND-4 trial, which compared the efficacy of ceritinib to first-line chemotherapy in treatment-naïve ALK fusion-positive advanced NSCLC [ ]: patients treated with ceritinib have significantly longer (and clinically meaningful) median progression-free survival (PFS) compared to standard-of-care chemotherapy (16.6 versus 8.1 months), leading to FDA approval as first-line therapy for ALK fusion-positive NSCLC.
Alectinib was also granted FDA approval as a first-line treatment based on results from J-ALEX trial, with a PFS advantage for alectinib compared to crizotinib (median PFS not reached vs. 10.2 months respectively) [ ]. Similar results were later shown in the ALEX trial [ ].
Brigatinib is also a second-generation ALK TKI that demonstrated efficacy against nine different crizotinib-resistant mutants of the EML4-ALK fusion [ ]. Clinically, it is currently a treatment option for ALK-positive NSCLC intolerant to crizotinib.
Lorlatinib is a third-generation ALK inhibitor. Compared to second-generation inhibitors, it is a small and compact inhibitor with improved metabolic stability, lower frequency of P-glycoprotein-mediated efflux, and more active CNS penetrance. It was the last ALK inhibitor to be FDA-approved for the first-line setting therapy of ALK-positive NSCLC, in 2021, following previous second- and third-line approval in 2019. The first-line approval was based on data from the CROWN study, a randomized multicenter, controlled trial of 296 patients with treatment-naïve ALK-positive metastatic NSCLC [ ]. Patients were randomized 1:1 to crizotinib vs. lorlatinib which demonstrated an improved PFS (HR: 0.28; median PFS: not reached vs. 9.3 months), a more durable intracranial ORR in patients with CNS disease at baseline (82% vs. 23). More recent data presented at the 2024 ASCO annual meeting validated benefit at 5-year follow-up [ ]. Of note, while lorlatinib has demonstrated superior efficacy namely in patients with the G1202R mutation that confers resistance to other ALK inhibitors, concerns over its toxicities have been raised [ ].
Other agents targeting ALK-positive tumors are being investigated at various stages of development. These include ensartinib (phase II/NCT03215693), repotrectinib (phase I/NCT03093166), WX-0593 (phase I/NCT03389815), CT-707 (phase I/CTR20200770) and CT-3505 (phase I/CTR1900025619).
Common toxicities seen with the use of ALK inhibitors include gastrointestinal adverse events (increased transaminase levels, diarrhea, vomiting, hypophosphatemia), neutropenia, hypertension (specific to brigatinib), as well as edema, peripheral neuropathy, hyperlipidemia, and transient adverse cognitive effects (specific to lorlatinib).

2.2.

## ROS1 fusion
ROS1 is an insulin receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family member that undergoes genomic rearrangements and fusions across multiple solid malignancies (NSCLC, glioblastoma, cholangiocarcinoma, Spitz tumors, ovarian cancer, gastric adenocarcinoma, as well as colorectal cancer, angiosarcoma, and epithelioid hemangioendothelioma) [ , ]. The identified fusion partners include CD74, CCDC6, EZR, FIG, KDELR2, and others. ROS1 fusions induce the activation of several signaling pathways involved in cancer cell survival and growth, via constitutive activation of its kinase activity [ ]. However, unlike ALK fusion, ROS1 fusion can trigger downstream signaling as monomers and without dimerization, through an unknown mechanism [ ].
ROS1 fusions are most commonly described in NSCLC patients with a 1–2% prevalence. Patients with ROS1 fusions are typically younger and never or light smokers [ ]. Therapies targeting ROS1 fusion include type I (crizotinib and entrectinib) and type II (cabozantinib and foretinib) TKIs. Currently, only crizotinib and entrectinib are FDA-approved for targeting ROS1 fusion-positive cancers.
Crizotinib is FDA-approved for treating ROS1-fusion-positive metastatic NSCLC based on results of a single-arm, multicenter trial showing ORR of 72% and median PFS of 19.2 months [ ]. Compared to other inhibitors (ceritinib, alectinib), crizotinib displayed significantly improved activity against ROS1 kinase. Acquired resistance to crizotinib typically occurs with the acquisition of G1202R or G2032R mutations.
Entrectinib is a multi-kinase inhibitor that functions as an ATP competitor with effect against ROS1 and ALK fusions and pan-NTRK [ ]. It was FDA-approved for treating ROS1 fusion-positive metastatic NSCLC, with an additional tumor-agnostic indication for NTRK-fusion-positive solid tumors. Pooled analysis of 3 phase 1/2 trials revealed promising results in the treatment of ROS1-fusion-containing cancers with 40 times higher potency than crizotinib. In terms of side effects, entrectinib commonly leads to congestive heart failure (2%), CNS effects (25%), bone fracture (2%), fatigue (28%), and dysgeusia (47%). Unlike the challenge of resistance faced with crizotinib, entrectinib is an indazole derivative with higher blood-brain-barrier penetrance (apical efflux ratio: 1.1–1.15 vs. crizotinib and larotrectinib: ≥2.8) [ ] and so far, lesser rates of acquired resistance (28%) based on the phase II basket trial (no head-to-head comparison).
Other TKIs are currently being investigated for targeting ROS1-fusion positive tumors. These include ceritinib (phase II/NCT01964157), reprotrectinib, DS-6051b (phase I/NCT03093166), and WX-0593 (phase I/NCT03389815).

2.3.

## RET fusion
Rearranged during transfection (RET) is a member of the RTK family, with RET fusions initially reported in papillary thyroid cancer (occurs in one-third of thyroid cancer patients), with higher frequency in patients exposed to ionizing radiation [ ]. RET fusions also occur in 1–2% of patients with NSCLC (predominantly adenocarcinoma, younger age, and never smokers), as well as breast cancer, colon cancers, and Spitz tumors [ ]. Various fusion partners have been described, including CDCC6, NCOA4, KIF5B, LSM14A, TRIM33, and ZNF477P [ ]. The RET fusion encodes a protein with the kinase domain that induces overactivation of downstream signaling via partners’ dimerization or oligomerization [ ].
Several RET-targeted multi-kinase inhibitors have been investigated, including some approved for treating RET fusions, such as cabozantinib, vandetanib, lenvatinib, ponatinib, sunitinib, and sorafenib.
Cabozantinib binds both the ATP-binding site and the adjacent hydrophobic site. In a phase II trial of cabozantinib in patients with RET-fusion-positive NSCLC, ORR was 28% with a median PFS of 7 months [ ]. The drug, however, is associated with off-target toxicity including diarrhea, nausea, hypertension, weight loss anemia, and hypothyroidism. In addition, the use of cabozantinib is limited by acquired resistance, via specific mutations including L730L, E732K, and V871I.
Vandetanib is a multi-kinase inhibitor of several TRK including VEGFR2–3, EGFR, and RET. It acts by inhibiting the autophosphorylation of these RTK and thus blocking downstream signaling. In a phase II trial in RET-fusion-positive NSCLC conducted in Japan, the ORR was 53%, with significantly lesser rates in a separate study conducted in Korea (ORR: 18%) [ ]. Despite potentially wide usage and targets, vandetanib has been associated with serious adverse events including QT prolongation, tachycardia, and sudden death, hence limiting its clinical utility. Moreover, several drug-resistant mutations have been frequently identified.
Lenvatinib is an oral multi-kinase inhibitor with an effect on various target receptors including VEGFR 1/2/3, FGFR 1/2/3, and RET. Its mechanism of action includes hindering the autophosphorylation of KIF5B:RET, CCDC6:RET, and NCOA4:RET fusions, hence preventing the development of tumors harboring these RET fusion oncogenes. The drug has shown efficacy in RET-positive thyroid cancer and lung cancer. A study of Lenvatinib in RET-fusion-positive NSCLC revealed a modest ORR of 16% while data from a multicenter registry analysis of patients with RET fusions receiving off-label Lenvatinib, revealed an ORR of 23% across different tumor types [ ].
Selpercatinib is a small-molecule, ATP-competitive agent with very high selectivity [ ]. While it was originally approved for RET-positive thyroid cancer in 2020 (accelerated approval), it gained tumor-agnostic approval for use in patients with advanced RET fusion-positive solid tumors, following results from the LIBRETTO-001 study in 2022 [ ]. The highest sensitivity to selpercatinib has been reported in patients with CCDC6:RET and KIF5B:RET fusions. With its small lipophilic formulation, selpercatinib is capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier, potentially resulting in higher and more prolonged antitumor activity in the CNS. The drug was evaluated in LIBRETTO-001 (NCT03157128), a multicenter, open-label multicohort trial that evaluated patients with RET fusion-positive tumor: in patients with treatment-naïve and previously treated NSCLC, selpercatinib resulted in a significant reduction of tumor growth and minimal toxicity. Patients with medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) had robust and durable anti-tumor activity. In 41 pretreated patients with RET-fusion-positive tumors other than NSCLC and thyroid cancer, ORR was 44% and DoR was 4.5 months [ ].
Pralsetinib is another TKI targeting approved to treat adult patients with metastatic NSCLC harboring RET gene fusion (previously treated) based on the ARROW trial, a phase I/II study showing rapid, potent, and durable clinical activity with acceptable toxicity (NCT03037385) [ ]. The drug is also being evaluated in the first-line setting for patients with advanced RET-fusion-positive NSCLC (phase III/NCT0422292), as well as in patients across other solid tumors (phase I/II; NCT03037385).

2.4.

## NTRK fusion
Fusions in the neurotrophic receptor-tyrosine-kinase family (NTRK) were initially described in patients with papillary thyroid cancer and colon cancer [ , ]. The family of NTRK fusions includes NTRK1, 2, and 3. Fusions generally occur at a low frequency (<1%) across various tumor types, and the most common fusions include EML4:NTRK3, TPM3:NTRK1, and QKI:NTRK2 [ ]. NTRK fusions occur in select rare tumor types such as secretory carcinoma of the breast, but NTRK1 fusions have been reported in papillary thyroid cancer, colon cancer, NSCLC, sarcoma, and cholangiocarcinoma [ ].
Entrectinib is approved by the FDA for treating NTRK gene fusion-positive solid tumors in both adult and pediatric patients. In early trials, it results in objective responses in three of four patients with NTRK fusions with the fourth achieving a 60% reduction in tumor burden. The systemic effect including tumors of the CNS has been observed [ ], and clinical studies have shown that entrectinib is safe and well tolerated.
Larotrectinib is a highly selective TRK inhibitor approved for the treatment of NTRK fusion-positive solid tumors in both adults and children. A pooled analysis of data from three phase I/II studies demonstrated confirmed objective responses based on RECIST 1.1 in 38/50 patients (75%) across 17 different tumor types [ ].
Two major second-generation TRK inhibitors are being evaluated as for their potential to overcome these acquired point mutations: selitrectinib (phase I/NCT03215511) and reprotectinib (phase I/II, NCT 03093116 and NCT04094610).

## FGFR1/2/3 fusion
FGFR1 fusions have been identified in glioblastoma, breast cancer, and squamous lung carcinoma (fusion partner genes include TACC1, TACR1, BAG4, ERLIN2, RHOT1, NSD3, ZNF577, ZNF791) [ ]. FGFR2 fusions on the other hand have been most frequently reported in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (partners: CASP7, CCAR2, CCDC6, TACC2, CCAR2, CIT, AFF3, OFD1, SLC45A3, and BICC1. Last, FGFR3 fusions are most widely recognized fusion partner [ , ]. Unlike ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions, patients with FGFR fusion-positive NSCLC patients are more likely to be smokers. Currently, three FGFR inhibitors are FDA-approved: erdafitinib, futibatinib, and pemigatinib.
Erdafinib is a pan-FGFR inhibitor approved in 2024 for treating adult patients with advanced urothelial carcinoma who are not eligible for or have not received prior immune checkpoint inhibitors. In the first-in-human study of the drug, ORR was 45.5% in patients with fusion-positive disease [ ].
Futibatinib was approved based on results from FOENIX-CCA2, a single-arm multicenter, phase II trial. The median ORR was 41.7% with a median disease control rate of 82/5%, median PFS of 8.9 months, and 12-month OS of 73.1% [ ].
Pemigatinib was approved in 2020 based on data from the phase 2 FIGHT 202 trial. Among patients with FGFR2 fusions, the trial showed ORR of 35.5%, median DoR of 7.5 months median PFS of 6.9 months, and median OS of 21.2 months [ ].
Infigratinib is an FGFR1–3 TKI that is currently being investigated for treating previously treated advanced FGFR3-rearranged urothelial cancer (phase II/NCT04197986), and in previously treated advanced FGFR2-rearranged cholangiocarcinoma (phase II/NCT02150967).
All FGFR inhibitors share common class toxicities (grade ≥3%), including hyperphosphatemia (19%), rash (9%), ocular toxicities (8%), gastrointestinal toxicities (3–11%), and hand-foot syndrome (3%).

2.6.

## EWS:FLI1 fusion
Ewing Sarcoma (EWS) is a highly aggressive and metastatic bone and soft tissue malignancy in the round cell sarcoma family and is the second most common bone malignancy affecting children. It is most commonly present in the first and second decades of life. EWS primarily affects the metaphysis of the long bones and is more likely to present in the lower extremities (45%), followed by the pelvis (20%), and upper extremity (13%). EWS is characterized by the chromosomal translocation of the Ewing Sarcoma Gene (EWS), encoding an RNA binding protein, with a member of the Erythroblast Transformation Specific (ETS) family of transcription factors. The fusion of EWS:FLI1 at the t(11;22)(q24;q12) location is common in Ewing Sarcoma and is present in 80–90% of tumors, and can involve a multitude of chimeric foci. Less common additional fusions include EWS:ERG (5–15%), EWS:FEV (<5%), EWS:ETV (approximately 1%), and EWS:E1AF [ ]. Excluding the presence of the EWS fusion, Ewing sarcoma tumors have an unusually low tumor mutation burden [ ].
Management of EWS is multifaceted, relying on combination chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy. Targeted immunotherapies are not currently first line due to high sensitivity of the tumor to cytotoxic agents, with a 70–75% 5-year survival rate in non-metastatic disease patients [ ]. Although metastatic disease patients have only 25% survival within 1 year with standard of care therapy. In metastatic patients, however, high recurrence rates and tumor progression belie the requirement for further investigation into alternative treatments, including therapy specifically targeting the EWS fusion [ ].
NTRK fusions, driving tumorigenesis through elevated Trk expression, have an uncommon, but documented, prevalence in round cell sarcomas. In Ewing Sarcoma, an investigation assessing NTRK expression in 96 cases identified two patients positive for ETV(6):NTRK(3) fusions by next-generation sequencing (NGS), and six additional patients with Trk expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC). Additionally, fusions involving the BCL6 gene have been implicated in neoplastic upregulation of NTRK3 [ ]. While pan-Trk expression can be related to NTRK upregulation, investigations into the validity of using it as a confirmation of NTRK fusion events have estimated an overall sensitivity of 88%, and its presence does not confirm NTRK fusions independent of genetic confirmation. The relevance of pan-Trk expression in the absence of BCOR or NTRK fusions is unknown, as is the benefit of targeted therapies, including kinase inhibitors, specifically designed against NTRK in these patients [ ].
While rare, aberrant fusions of ALK, ROS1, and RET have been implicated in Ewing’s sarcoma and related sarcomas, providing potential actionable targets for treatment. The Pediatric Molecular Analysis for Therapy Choice (MATCH) trial (NCT03155620) is an active, expansive, 13-arm trial investigating precision medicine in patients aged 1–21 years, with inclusion criteria including many common oncogenes and their respective targeted therapies. These include NTRK1–3 fusions, (larotrectinib – Trk inhibitor) and genetic alterations in ALK and ROS1 (ensartinib – TKI), and RET (selpercatinib – tyrosine kinase RET inhibitor).
Following promising preclinical results, regorafenib a multikinase inhibitor affecting several angiogenic receptors including VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2, VEGFR-3, PDGFR-β was tested in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter phase 2 study [ ]. Investigators reported results involving 36 patients, 23 receiving regorafenib and 13 receiving placebo. Thirteen patients (56%) were progression free at 8 weeks on regorafenib versus 1 placebo patient (7.7%). Progression-free survival was 11.4 weeks on regorafenib versus 3.9 weeks on placebo. Over ten patients crossed over to receive regorafenib after progression. On the most recent National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines (NCCN Version 1.2025 — 20 August 2024) both cabozantinib and regorafenib are second-line therapy (relapsed/refractory or metastatic disease) recommended regimens. Given the poor advances in the treatment of Ewing sarcoma over the past several decades, the addition of tyrosine kinase inhibitors like regorafenib, to the upfront chemotherapy backbone or as post-treatment maintenance is an appealing approach to consider by several oncology consortiums.

## EWSR1 fusion
The term clear cell sarcoma (CCS) originates from the histologic characteristic of “clear cytoplasm,” and prior to pathognomonic fusion signal discovery was referred to as malignant melanoma of soft tissue [ ]. CCS is a rare malignancy arising from EWSR1 fusion in neural crest cells of tendons and aponeuroses. It makes up 1% of all sarcomas and most commonly presents in the third to fourth decade of life and affects the lower limb or hip. Each year about 1600 new CCS cases occur in the United States. Unfortunately, CCS presents with latent symptoms and consequently typically manifests at a more advanced stage with early metastasis to the lungs and lymphatic system. A review of the National Cancer Database indicated the majority of patients present with either stage I (34%) or stage IV (38%) disease. Five-year survival of Stage 1 patients with localized disease is 75%, but decreases to 15% for patients with advanced stage with distant metastasis [ ].
CCS and other rare orphan sarcomas with unmet medical need have a vast majority of abnormal karyotypes ( ) involving the EWSR1 RNA-binding protein encoding gene as the primary oncogenic driver. The most common form of EWSR1 translocation in CCS involves fusion with ATF1, with a prevalence of 70–85%. This chimeric protein likely contains the EWS transactivating domain linked to an ATF1 bZIP domain. This results in loss of ATF1 regulation by cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and altered transcriptional regulation of genes normally controlled by ATF1. Within this fusion, four chimeric variants have been identified [ ]. Approximately 50–56% involves an in-frame fusion of EWSR1 exon 8 with ATF exon 4, with the other three variants being found in conjunction with the first variant, or more rarely as the single mutation driving mutagenesis [ ].

 Recently, the use of MET inhibitors (crizotinib, tivantinib) and other multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, cabozantinib) to target ATF1 fusions have been explored [ ]. A phase II trial involving the use of crizotinib in metastatic CCS resulted in a median OS of 277 days (232–442, 95% CI) and PFS of 53.8% (34.6–73.0, 95% CI), comparable to single-agent doxorubicin [ ]. Sunitinib has been used successfully in isolated instances, including a case where sunitinib-malate with radiotherapy resulted with reduction in tumor size and disease regression for the 3 months preceding publication. Furthermore, a case report investigating the use of cabozantinib in conjunction with a Programmed Cell Death Protein-1 PD-1 inhibitor and hapten di-nitrophenyl modified autologous tumor cells in metastatic CCS attained partial response with promising tumor regression and progression-free survival for 2 years [ ]. These results suggest a potential role for targeted therapies independently or in combination with additional modalities, and trials involving atezolizumab (NCT04458922) and cabozantinib-S-malate (NCT02867592) are in process.
Trabectedin is the first marine-derived anti-neoplastic drug approved for the treatment of advanced soft tissue sarcoma. Results were shown that trabectedin has potent antitumor activity against clear cell sarcoma cells by inducing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and promoted melanocytic differentiation through inactivation of ERK signaling [ ]. Trabectedin-induced DNA damage appears to related to binding of the minor groove of DNA, specifically to the exocyclic N2 amino group of guanines. This leads to interference of DNA repair transcription factors. It also inhibits over-expression of MDR-1 protein and regulates activity of tumor-associated macrophages thereby modulating tumor vascularity. Trabectedin was also used in sequence prior to irinotecan in 12 advanced refractory metastatic sarcoma patients with a variety of fusion driver genes. One patient demonstrated a partial response by RECIST criteria, five had stable disease, while six patients had progressive disease. The majority of patients on study demonstrated grade 3 or 4 hematological toxicity including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia [ ]. Reversible liver toxicity and diarrhea were also observed in some patients. Overall, the results support further clinical assessment of trabectedin in advanced sarcoma.

## Molecular biology of fusion genes
3.1.

## Rare fusion driver gene vs common driver gene rat sarcoma (RAS)
Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the third leading cause of cancer death from age of 50–79 in the USA. Unfortunately, due to nonspecific symptoms and advanced stage at presentation, 90% of tumors present as locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic. Prognosis is typically grim, with a median survival of 17–23 months for local or resectable disease, and 4–6 months for metastatic disease [ ]. Pancreatic cancer has been historically unresponsive to targeted immunotherapies, with treatment primarily involving chemotherapy with Leucovorin, Fluorouracil, Irinotecan, and Oxaliplatin (FOLFIRINOX) FOLIRNOX, or surgery and radiation [ ]. Surgery when early detection is observed remains the only form of curative therapy. Rapid diffuse metastasis or local spread into the liver and regional vasculature limits applicability [ ]. Factors associated with improved prognosis are thus important.
The majority of PDAC tumors, up to 90%, harbor a Kirsten Rat Sarcoma Proto-Oncogene (KRAS) mutation as driver gene control [ ]. A small portion of PDAC tumors, however (under 2%), have fusion genes including BRAF (33.7% of tumors with fusion genes), FGFR2 (17.4%), RET (12.7%), and ALK (10.1%). Fusions with NTRK, Neuregulin 1 (NRG1), Rapidly Accelerated Fibrosarcoma 1 (RAF1), RET, and ROS1 has also been identified [ , ]. Interestingly, 98% of tumors with fusion genes are KRAS wild type, which is a reverse of other PDAC tumors [ , ]. Fusion gene driven PDAC patients have prognostically improved survival [ ]. PDAC patients with fusion genes also appear to have a better response benefit to standard of care and better overall prognosis compared to PDAC tumors with KRAS mutations [ , ].
One review assessing 59 treated PDAC patients with fusion mutations found that the achievement of complete response and partial response occurred more frequently with standard of care, FOLFIRNOX [ ]. Fusions involving FGFR2, NTRK, and RET particularly were also associated with a more positive prognosis, although more studies are needed to verify clinical benefit [ ]. Interestingly, one case report documents a patient with a MET fusion mutation who demonstrated a complete response at greater than 12 months following experimental administration of crizotinib (RTK inhibitor). Both larotrectinib (NTRK inhibitor) and entrectinib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) have demonstrated clinical benefit in solid tumors with NTRK fusions, including PDAC, and have been FDA approved for PDAC treatment with the appropriate mutational burden [ ]. Selpercatinib (a RET inhibitor), is being investigated in a multitude of solid tumors with RET mutations, including PDAC with RET fusion mutations [ ].

3.2.

## Fusion gene formation
Fusion genes are a product of genomic or transcript instability, ultimately resulting in constitutively active proteins that promote oncogenesis. Genomic fusions are more common and occur primarily via (a) chromosomal translocation where regions from two chromosomes break off and swap places [ ], (b) chromosomal inversions where a region of a chromosome breaks off and inserts itself in the opposite orientation [ ], (c) chromosomal deletions where a region of the genome is deleted and two previously separated genes are brought in closer proximity to each other [ ], or (d) chromosomal duplications where a gene sequence is duplicated in tandem resulting in additional protein products [ ].
In addition to chromosomal rearrangements, there are rare instances where fusion RNA products are formed via read-through errors or trans-splicing despite an unadulterated genome. For example, transcription read-through occurs when the transcription machinery fails to terminate at the end of a gene and continues to transcribe into the adjacent gene. This has been documented in prostate cancer where a chimeric SLC45A3:ELK4 chimeric transcript from chromosome 12 serves as a long, non-coding RNA (lncRNA) to modulate transcription. While traditional genomic fusions are transcribed and eventually translated into proteins, chimeric transcripts, including SLC45A3-ELK4, are enriched in the nucleus and serve as transcription factors to up-regulate genes associated with cell proliferation. Trans-splicing occurs when the spliceosome complex recognizes two pre-mRNA molecules from separate genes and their exons are ligated together to form a mature chimeric mRNA molecule [ ]. These events are rare and difficult to identify, but studies have suggested that trans-splicing may be responsible for fusion proteins in an unadulterated genome.

3.3.

## NTRK fusion genes
The family of NTRK genes (NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3) encodes tropomyosin receptor kinases that, when fused with the promoter of a highly expressed gene, result in activation of downstream signaling pathways such as MAPK, PI3K-AKT, and PLC-gamma. This promotes cell proliferation, survival, and migration [ ]. A variety of cancers use NTRK fusion genes for their oncogenicity including secretory breast carcinoma, infantile fibrosarcoma, and thyroid cancer to name a few. The ETV6-NTRK3 gene fusion is a specific chromosomal translocation involving the ETS translocation variant 6 (ETV6) gene on chromosome 12. It is seen in >90% of secretory breast carcinomas, up to 85% of infantile fibrosarcoma cases, and an estimated 5–25% of pediatric papillary thyroid carcinomas. Despite the relatively low effect size of NTRK fusion genes in cancer, their identification is critical because they represent a druggable target. Several small-molecule inhibitors, including larotrectinib and entrectinib, target the kinase activity of the TRK fusion protein resulting in inhibition of downstream signaling pathways [ ].

3.4.

## ALK fusion genes
ALK fusion proteins also augment a variety of downstream signaling pathways including RAS-ERK, PI3K-AKT, and JAK-STAT. Similar to NTRK fusion genes, small-molecule inhibitors targeting ALK have shown significant efficacy. Crizotinib was the first ALK inhibitor approved for the treatment of ALK-positive NSCLC demonstrating a median progression-free survival of 7–10 months compared to 3–4 months with chemotherapy alone [ ]. Subsequent generation inhibitors, including ceritinib, alectinib, and brigatinib, were developed to overcome resistance mechanisms to crizotinib, providing a broader and more durable response [ ]. Alectinib, for example, has shown a significant increase in progression-free survival of 34.8 months compared to crizotinib alone. Taken together with TRK inhibitor therapy, targeting fusion genes often results in higher response rates compared to conventional chemotherapy due to their ability to selectively bind and inhibit unique fusion proteins [ ].

3.5.

## Co-mutations in fusion gene-positive cancers
Co-mutations in EML4-ALK-positive NSCLC tumors include KRAS and TP53. TP53 mutations are relatively common co-mutations in NSCLC and are associated with poor prognosis ultimately through the loss of function of the p53 protein and potentiation of oncogenesis through the natural selection of advantageous mutations. KRAS mutations are rare in ALK-rearranged NSCLC, but when they do occur, they are associated with aggressive disease due to the potent activation of downstream signaling pathways including MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT independent of ALK signaling. This results in a synergistic activation of these pathways, enhancing tumorigenicity and resistance to ALK inhibitors.

3.6.

## Methylation and fusion genes
Hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes can suppress their function leading to unopposed activity from a constitutively active fusion gene. This is seen frequently with the methylation of the P16INK4a promoter region exacerbating the effects of NTRK and ALK fusion genes [ ]. In addition, hypermethylation in the BCR-ABL promoter region can reduce its transcription and result in relatively modest oncogenic activity. Gene methylation may also play a secondary role by providing the necessary environment for gene fusions to occur.
For example, DNA methylation plays a significant role in fusion-positive pediatric rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) and demonstrates how epigenetics interact with mutation profiles to create distinct cancer subtypes. Pediatric RMS can be generally divided into two histological subtypes: alveolar and embryonic. The majority of alveolar RMS cases are fusion-positive cancers driven by PAX3/7:FOXO1 fusion proteins that ultimately promote tumorigenesis by serving as unregulated transcription factors that both stimulate oncogenic genes while suppressing apoptotic genes [ ]. These fusion-positive cancers have very few recurrent genomic mutations; whereas, the fusion-negative embryonic subtype has multiple recurrent genomic mutations including those in the RAS gene [ ]. There are multiple theories for why distinct methylation profiles are associated with fusion-positive cancers including RMS. One such theory suggests that the PAX3/7:FOXO1 fusion protein directly interacts with methyltransferases to modulate targeted gene methylation [ ]. Another theory suggests that fusion-positive RMS creates an environment that is more conducive to epigenetic modification as a means to propagate tumorigenesis as opposed to co-mutations that may be more likely to be detected by the body’s adaptive immunity [ ].

3.7.

## Other fusions and targeted therapies
Other oncogenic fusion proteins with ongoing evaluations of targeted therapeutics. BRAF fusions secondary to rearrangements have been described across multiple solid tumors with >30 fusion partners identified [ ]. Such fusions were enriched in specific and rare variants of malignancies: pilocytic gliomas (30%), spitzoid melanomas (75%), and acinar pancreatic cancers (67%), compared to <5% prevalence in gliomas (non-pilocytic), melanomas (non-spitzoid), and non-acinar pancreatic cancers. In terms of therapies, preliminary evidence suggests that BRAF fusions may not be sensitive to first-generation inhibitors such as vemurafenib, with a reported paradoxical activation of the MAPK pathway. MEK inhibitors, on the other hand, have demonstrated efficacy in patients with BRAF fusions. CRAF fusions have also been described in melanoma, thyroid cancer, and prostate cancer [ ]. CRAF is also known as RAF1 and is another member of the RAS family, with multiple fusion partners already reported [ ]. Tumors harboring CRAF fusions do not respond to first-generation RAF inhibitors, but ongoing evaluations of novel RAF-dimer inhibitors, such as L×H254 and LY3009120, and MEK inhibitors, are underway. A variety of novel fusions involving many kinases have been described [ , , ]. For example, translation in CSF1 is seen in most patients with tenosynovial giant cell tumors, a rare but aggressive sarcoma. Pexidartinib is a CSF1R inhibitor currently under investigation, with reported prolonged tumor responses in the majority of patients with this disease [ ]. EGFR fusions have also been reported in patients with NSCLC with responses to erlotinib.

3.8.

## Novel fusion targeted mechanisms of therapy
While the majority of fusion protein targeted therapies have involved inhibitors that target the constitutively active kinase (or its downstream signaling), other fusions can exert their oncogenic effects via non-kinase mediated mechanisms. For example, fusions that result in transcriptional dysregulation have been described in both solid and hematologic malignancies thus raising the need for novel therapeutic strategies [ ].
In patients with Ewing sarcoma, the EWSR1:FLI1 fusion results in a chimeric transcription factor that binds to DNA and acts as a constitutively active transcription factor that enables alternative splicing [ ]. TK216 is a novel small-molecule inhibitor that blocks that is currently evaluated in a phase I trial (NCT02657005) and acts by blocking the binding of RNA helicase A to the spliceosome protein complex that is needed for EWSR1:FLI1 oncogenic function. In patients with prostate cancer, the TMPRSS2:ERG fusion leads to overexpression of the ERG transcription factor [ ]. Strategies and targeted therapies are under investigation to selectively target TMRSS2:ERG-fusion-positive cancers, including the development of inhibitors that disrupt the interactions of ERG with DNA, as well as peptidomimetics that lead to ERG degradation, and modulators of ERG signaling such as PARP and HDAC inhibitors. Dutasteride in combination with bicalutamide has been shown to effectively inhibit and slow the growth of TMPRSS2:ERG fusion-positive cells. ABBV-075, a BET protein inhibitor, is also a potent inhibitor of TMPRSS2-ETS fusion. Patients with malignant liposarcoma (MLS) harbor FUS-DDIT3 fusion protein in >90% of cases. DDIT-3 is a DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 that acts as a cellular stress sensor and the fusion is suggested to activate the JAK-STAT signaling and the PI3K/AKT signaling. No specific therapy targets this fusion, although most JAK, GSK-3, and PI3K/AKT signaling inhibitors have shown efficacy in MLS [ ].

3.9.

## Immune intervention targeting fusion proteins
The role of fusion proteins toward anti-tumor immune response therapeutic development is of limited assessment. Current theory posits that mutated proteins exposed on the surface of tumors are identified as “non-self” by the immune system, which subsequently mounts a response against cells expressing those proteins, termed “neoantigens.” In this way, immune cells target cancer cells as non-self and have an integral role in eliminating early seeds of neoplasms before they erupt into malignancy [ ]. Tumor cells have developed methods to evade the immune response, which permits their expansion and metastasis. A burgeoning area of cancer treatment includes identifying immune evasive mechanisms and blunting them, thereby liberating the immune system to provide intrinsic defense against cancer.
Moreover, fusion proteins represent a potential biomarker to predict response to targeted immunotherapies. Nemunaitis et al. summarize clonality as a mediator of tumor mutation burden in their 2023 paper [ ]. In summary, the research community has identified an association between high tumor mutation burden and response to immune therapy including checkpoint inhibitors. This association is refined further when we determine the clonality of the tumor mutations [ ]. Clonal mutations are mutations that occur early in the genesis of a tumor; therefore, as the tumor continues to divide, these clonal mutations remain present in all progeny cells of the tumor ( ). Subclonal mutations occur later in a cell’s division and represent a branch point, so they are present only in a subpopulation of cells [ ]. Evidence demonstrates that the presence of clonal neoantigens strengthens the potential for immunotherapy compared to the presence of subclonal neoantigens [ , ]. If an immune cell primed against a neoantigen can recognize that neoantigen among all tumor cells (as is the case for clonal neoantigens), then the priming of that one response against one antigen can be effective against all tumor cells. By contrast, priming against a subclonal neoantigen only mounts a response against a subpopulation of cancer cells, leaving the cells emerging from other branch points untargeted by that primed cell [ , ].

 The idea that clonal neoantigens invite a more potent immune response than subclonal neoantigens begs the question – are there tumor biomarkers that suggest clonality? In several cancers, fusion proteins represent a clonal neoantigen [ ]. In the case of Ewing sarcoma, fusion proteins are the cancer-defining mutation and are present in all iterations of the tumor [ ]. If a patient’s immune system is able to mount a response against a fusion protein’s signature, it would theoretically have a target that is present on all tumor cells in the body. Consequently, upon activation against the clonal neoantigen (fusion protein), the immune system would seek and destroy all tumor cells baring that fusion protein’s neoantigen, effectively eliminating the patient’s cancer [ ].
Presently, this idea remains theoretical pending further research. Some findings suggest plausibility, including a mouse study by Peng et al. in which mice were treated with dendritic cells pulse with the EWS:FLI1 peptide; these mice subsequently rejected tumor inoculation of EWS cells. In this same study, an assay confirmed the ability of 3 EWS:FLI1 peptides to upregulate MHC-1, in addition to having a high affinity for MHC-1. However, a subsequent in vitro analysis demonstrated only modest cytotoxic activity against each of the selected EWS:FLI1 epitopes, suggesting that although EWS:FLI1 peptides are capable of binding to MHC-1, this is not sufficient to induce immunity. Nemunaitis et al. have demonstrated clinical efficacy of Vigil, an autologous tumor cell immunotherapy in relapsed Ewing’s sarcoma patients. Vigil is constructed from harvested tumor tissue and transfected with a plasmid which expresses GM-CSF and bi-shRNA to knockdown furin. A phase I clinical trial demonstrated both safety and efficacy of Vigil with 1-year OS of 73% compared to 23% in matched controls. All patients enrolled on study had an EWS:FLI1 fusion gene. Vigil would provide the relevant clonal neoantigens through the use of autologous tumor tissue, thus enhancing clinical outcomes [ ].

3.10.

## Biomarkers of response and resistance
A classic example of TKI resistance is the targeting of EGFR. Treatment with these first and second generation (erlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib icotinib). EGFR TKIs resulted in on-target mutations within the drug target site. In about half of all patients resistance develops due to a specific mutation, T790M. Third-generation EGFR TKIs were developed to specifically overcome resistance and target this mutation. Off target alterations also result in EGFR resistance. The two most common examples of this are ERBB2 and MET amplification.
Cluster of differentiation (CD151) is also a potent therapeutic target for regulating tumor metastasis. It involves maintaining normal cellular integrity, cell-to-cell communication, wound healing, platelet aggregation, trafficking, cell motility, and angiogenesis. The relationship of CD151 with integrins appears to offset cancer invasion and metastatic spread. Based on the role of CD151 in metastatic spread and primary tumor growth, it has been identified as an important target and biomarker. A trial involving the clear cell sarcoma lines, HS-MM transplanted to the aponeuroses of the thighs in SCID-beige mice supported the effectiveness of CD151. HS-MMhigh is a clear cell line that shows high CD151 expression. Infusion of anti-CD151 antibody significantly suppressed lymphatic invasion, peritoneal dissemination, and distant metastasis of the present clear cell sarcoma model without affecting local tumor growth at the transplantation site. Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated downregulation of CD151 did not alter cell proliferation, but did significantly inhibit Matrigel invasion activity of HS-MMhigh cells. Studies displayed that with the down regulation of CD151 using shRNA or gene knockout (KO) proved the decrease of cellular communication between cancer cells [ ]. Preliminary evidence supports further scientific assessment of CD151 as a relevant biomarker in sarcoma management.
When fusion oncogenes are the driver mutation of tumorigenesis, the fusion itself serves as a biomarker of response to ongoing therapy. The continued optimization of next-generation sequencing enabled the detection of clonal and sub-clonal fusion events, despite the relatively high false-positive results. Various other strategies have been evaluated to overcome this, including using a quantitative PCR-based assay. On the other hand, RNA sequencing enables the identification of chimeric RNAs that result from trans/cis splicing and are not detectable at the DNA level [ ]. False-negative results can nonetheless occur with RNA sequencing, especially when performed on archival formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue. The use of liquid biopsies may overcome the challenge surrounding the invasiveness of repeating a tissue biopsy, while also allowing the detection of rearrangement and fusions via circulating tumor (ct)DNA-based or cell-free (cf) RNA gene-based fusion assays; however, limitations in sensitivity are directly correlated to disease stage, as the amount of circulating tumor DNA directly correlates with tumor burden [ ]. The FDA-led Sequencing Quality Control 2 Consortium (SEQC-2) has endeavored to address these limitations in sensitivity and specificity by creating standardized practices and quality-control metrics in the collection and preparation of cancer specimens, as well as the bioinformatic analysis of this “big data.”
ctDNA has been shown to be a useful clinical tool not only for diagnosis but to monitor for disease status and treatment response [ ]. The use of ctDNA has been shown to be a useful clinical to several studies including Ewing sarcoma [ ]. Anderson et al. showed a prognostic correlation between detectable ctDNA in Ewing sarcoma patients. Detectable ctDNA in newly diagnosed localized Ewing sarcoma was associated with an inferior 3-year event-free survival (48.6% vs. 82.1%; p = 0.006) and overall survival (79.8% vs. 92.6%; p = 0.01) when compared to patients with undetectable levels [ ]. The use of EWS ctDNA levels is being incorporated in future pediatric oncology consortium groups not only as a prognostic biomarker but to further classify group risk and intensive treatment.

4.

## Conclusion
Despite the small incidence of fusion gene detection involving cancer overall, numerous therapeutic approaches have demonstrated clinical benefit in relationship to fusion gene targeting and immune response modulation via precision therapy. We also demonstrate the specific relationship of fusion gene presence to impact on cancer initiation, detection, and progression. However, evidence of fusion gene signal detection provides support to the argument that they are “clonal” mutation signals present on all cancer cells within the tumor population. Clonal signals represent an innovative targeted therapeutic strategy enabling personalized approaches to therapy. In simple terms, target the target that is unique to cancer, necessary for cancer survival and spread and associated with clinically objective response and survival benefit.
