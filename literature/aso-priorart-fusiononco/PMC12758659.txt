## Abstract

## Background/Aim
Ewing’s sarcoma is an aggressive pediatric malignancy driven by specific chromosomal translocations, predominantly leading to the oncogenic fusion protein EWS-FLI1. Runt-related transcription factors 1 and 2 (RUNX1 and RUNX2), which are key transcription factors involved in osteoblastic differentiation and skeletal morphogenesis, are not expressed in Ewing’s sarcoma. Our study aimed to elucidate the relationship between the RUNX genes and EWS-FLI1.

## Materials and Methods
We used Ewing’s sarcoma cell lines (A673 and NCR-EW2). EWS-FLI1 knockdown was achieved using shRNA, while inducible expression of RUNX1, RUNX2, EWS-FLI1, or EWS-ERG was performed using a Tet-on system. Gene and protein expression levels were quantitatively assessed by qPCR and Western blot analysis, respectively. Cell growth was evaluated using spheroid formation and cell viability assays. Truncated RUNX1 or RUNX2 constructs were employed to identify functional domains, with their intracellular localization confirmed by immunostaining.

## Results
We observed low expression of RUNX1 and RUNX2 in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. Notably, EWS-FLI1 knockdown increased RUNX2 gene expression. Conversely, inducible expressions of either RUNX1 or RUNX2 suppressed the expression of EWS-FLI1 target genes and inhibited cell proliferation and spheroid formation. Additionally, the RUNT domain of RUNX1 and RUNX2 was not essential for their inhibitory effect on EWS-FLI1 target gene expression. Importantly, both EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG down-regulated RUNX2 gene expression in hTert-RPE1 cells.

## Conclusion
EWS-FLI1 suppresses RUNX2 gene expression in Ewing’s sarcoma cells, and re-expressing RUNX2 inhibits cell growth. These findings suggest that RUNX2 has a negative impact on the oncogenic process in Ewing’s sarcoma.

Keywords: Ewing’s sarcoma, EWS-FLI1, EWS-ERG, RUNX1, RUNX2

## Introduction
Ewing’s sarcoma is an aggressive pediatric and adolescent malignancy characterized by specific chromosomal translocations that lead to the formation of oncogenic chimeric transcription factors. These aberrant fusions, predominantly involving FET family genes (EWSR1 or FUS/TLS) and ETS transcription factors (FLI1, ERG, ETV1, or ETV4), drive tumor development in bone and soft tissues ( - ). Notably, the EWS-FLI1 fusion, resulting from the t(11;22)(q24;q12) translocation between EWSR1 and FLI1, is found in 85- 90% of cases ( , ). Despite advances in treatment, the prognosis for patients with metastatic Ewing’s sarcoma remains poor, with 5-year survival rates below 30%. Therefore, a critical unmet need exists to elucidate the transcriptional regulatory pathways modulated by these aberrant chimeric transcription factors, particularly by identifying their interacting molecular partners and complexes associated with the oncogenic process. Such an understanding is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets that could improve clinical outcomes for this disease.
The ETS family of transcription factors is characterized by its conserved ETS domain, which enables binding to a core GGAA/T DNA motif. EWS-FLI1, a prominent oncogenic fusion protein in Ewing’s sarcoma, leverages these GGAA microsatellite sequences to drive oncogenesis, creating de novo enhancers that interact with target promoters ( - ). Conversely, EWS-FLI1 also represses genes by displacing wild-type ETS transcription factors from conserved enhancers, particularly at single GGAA sequences ( ). For instance, numerous genes, including CALD1, CYR61, DKK1, IGFBP5, IGFBP7, ARHGAP29, RND3, ADM, TFP1, and TNFRSF12A, were consistently repressed after EWS-FLI1 was down-regulated in A673, SKNMC, and EW24 cells, or was overexpressed in mesenchymal stem cells ( ). Furthermore, the down-regulation of EWS-FLI1 has been associated with the up-regulation of RUNX2, which is subsequently followed by up-regulation of COL10A1 and SOX9, recognized as markers of the chondrocyte lineage ( ). This suggests a complex interplay between EWS-FLI1 and the mesenchymal differentiation pathway.
The RUNX family of transcription factors plays a fundamental role in cellular differentiation, proliferation, and lineage specification. These genes encode proteins characterized by the RUNT domain, a highly conserved DNA-binding motif first identified in Drosophila melanogaster, where the runt gene was found to be essential for proper segmentation during embryogenesis ( , ). In mammals, the RUNX family consists of three distinct genes: RUNX1, RUNX2, and RUNX3, each with specialized functions in development and disease regulation.
RUNX1 is critical for hematopoiesis, serving as a key transcriptional regulator of hematopoietic stem cells. RUNX1 is also essential for mesenchymal stem cell proliferation ( ). Its mutations or dysregulation are strongly associated with leukemogenesis, contributing to various hematological malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) ( ). Meanwhile, RUNX2 is indispensable for osteoblast differentiation and chondrocyte development, governing the transition from mesenchymal stem cells to mature osteoblasts ( , ). Dysfunction of RUNX2 has been implicated in disorders such as cleidocranial dysplasia ( ). In contrast, RUNX3 functions as a tumor suppressor gene across multiple organs ( - ). Its function can be compromised by hypermethylation of the RUNX3 promoter region or a loss of function in the RUNX3 protein. The tumor-suppressive activities of RUNX3 are primarily attributed to its ability to regulate cellular apoptosis, differentiation, and proliferation through interactions with key signaling pathways, including TGF-β and Wnt ( ).
Investigations into the expression of the RUNX protein family members in Ewing’s sarcoma cells have shown interactions between EWS-FLI1 and RUNX proteins. Previous studies demonstrated that using GST pulldown analysis, EWS-FLI1 directly binds to the RUNT domain of RUNX1 and RUNX, and antagonizes the RUNX2 transcriptional activity, as determined by a reporter assay in mouse myoblast C2C12 cells ( , ). However, RUNX3 has been shown to be highly expressed in many Ewing’s sarcoma cell lines and primary tumors compared to RUNX1 and RUNX2 ( ). It is also noteworthy that a previous study indicated EWS-FLI1 did not alter the expression level of RUNX2 protein in mouse myoblast C2C12 cells, despite including changes in cell morphology and cell proliferation ( ).
Based on these observations, we hypothesized that EWS-FLI1 may down-regulate RUNX1 and RUNX2 in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. The present study therefore aims to elucidate the roles of RUNX genes in Ewing’s sarcoma cells, particularly focusing on their expression and functional interactions with EWS-FLI1.

## Results
RUNX1 and RUNX2 were not detected in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. Consistent with previous findings ( ), we confirmed that the protein and mRNA expression levels of RUNX1 and RUNX2 were low in Ewing’s sarcoma cells compared to mesenchymal stem cells and bone sarcoma cells ( A and B). NKX2-2 and RUNX3 were expressed in Ewing’s sarcoma cells, as previously reported ( , ) (Figure 1A). We investigated whether EWS-FLI1 represses the expression of RUNX1 or RUNX2.
 A previous study suggested that EWS-FLI1 knockdown in A673 cells exhibits mesenchymal features, as evidenced by the expression of RUNX2 mRNA under both normal and osteogenic differentiation conditions ( ). We hypothesized that RUNX1 or RUNX2 is repressed in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. To explore whether RUNX1 or RUNX2 is expressed by EWS-FLI1 knockdown, lentivirus expressing shFLI1 was transduced into A673 and NCR-EW2 cells. EWS-FLI1 knockdown reduced NKX2-2 and NR0B1 protein expression ( A and B). In addition, RUNX2 protein expression increased in both cell lines, but RUNX1 only increased in A673 cells (Figure 2A and B). On the other hand, RUNX3 protein expression was not altered in both cells. PPP1R1A, which is one of the target genes of EWS-FLI1, was down-regulated at mRNA levels after EWS-FLI1 knockdown in A673 and NCR-EW2 cells (Figure 2C and D) ( ). RUNX2 mRNA increased in both cell lines, whereas RUNX1 mRNA only increased in A673 cells (Figure 2C and D). These results suggest that EWS-FLI1 might repress RUNX2 gene expression in Ewing’s sarcoma cells.
 RUNX1 and RUNX2 inhibit EWS-FLI1-target gene expression. To understand why Ewing’s sarcoma represses RUNX genes, we expressed RUNX1 or RUNX2 genes using the Tet-on lentivirus system. The induction of RUNX1 or RUNX2 by doxycycline treatment down-regulated NKX2-2 and NR0B1 proteins in A673 and NCR-EW2 cells ( A and B). Conversely, the induction of RUNX1 and RUNX2 resulted in the up-regulation of EWS-FLI1, whereas EWSR1 remained unchanged (Figure 3A and B). The induction of RUNX1 or RUNX2 also down-regulated PPP1R1A and GLI1 mRNA, whereas EWS-FLI1 mRNA was up-regulated in both cell lines (Figure 3C and D).
 We also tested whether RUNX1 or RUNX2 can reduce cell growth in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. The induction of RUNX1 or RUNX2 by doxycycline treatment reduced spheroid formation in A673 and NCR-EW2 cells ( A and B). In addition, the induction of RUNX1 and RUNX2 also reduced cell growth under adhesion conditions in both cell lines (Figure 4C and D). These results imply that RUNX1 and RUNX2 might suppress the progression of the oncogenic phenotype in Ewing’s sarcoma cells.
 The RUNT domain is not essential for RUNX-mediated inhibition of the EWS-FLI1 gene expression. To identify which region in RUNX2 is responsible for the inhibition of the target genes of EWS-FLI1, we constructed truncated RUNX2 genes ( A). RUNX2-N, M, and C did not localize in the nucleus (Figure 5B). In contrast, RUNX2-MC was localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 5B). In addition, induction of the truncated forms of RUNX2 did not inhibit either NKX2-2 or NR0B1 expression (Figure 5C). To enhance a truncated form of RUNX2 in the nucleus, we fused two nuclear localization signals (NLS: PKKKRKV) from Simian Virus 40 large T antigen (SV40) to the C-terminus of RUNX2-MC. RUNX2-MC-2NLS was clearly localized in the nucleus (Figure 5E). The induction of RUNX2-MC-2NLS down-regulated NKX2-2 and NR0B1 and up-regulated EWS-FLI1, similar to the effect of full-length RUNX2 in A673 cells (Figure 5F). Furthermore, when the experiment was conducted with RUNX1-MC that had the RUNT domain deleted ( A), it localized in the nucleus without the addition of the NLS (Figure 6B). RUNX1-MC down-regulated NKX2-2 and NR0B1 protein expression, similar to the effect of full-length RUNX1 in A673 cells (Figure 6C). These results suggest that the RUNT domain of RUNX1 and RUNX2 genes is not essential for inhibition of EWS-FLI1 target gene expression.
 EWS-FLI1 co-immunoprecipitates with RUNX2-MC. Previous studies indicated that RUNX2 interacts with EWS-FLI1 via a GST pulldown assay ( , ). To verify whether RUNX1 and RUNX2 co-immunoprecipitate with EWS-FLI1, we conducted an immunoprecipitation experiment. FLAG-tagged proteins were isolated using a DDDDK-tag antibody, and the precipitates were analyzed by Western blot. Results showed that EWS-FLI1 co-immunoprecipitated with both RUNX1 and RUNX2 ( A). Subsequently, to evaluate if RUNX2-MC, which lacks the RUNT domain, can bind EWS-FLI1, these proteins were also pulled down with a DDDDK-tag antibody and examined via Western blot. EWS-FLI1 was not present in the EGFP immunoprecipitates but was detected in those with RUNX2 or RUNX2-MC-2NLS (Figure 7B). These results indicate that RUNX2 can associate with EWS-FLI1 even in the absence of the RUNT domain.
 EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG down-regulate RUNX2. A previous study indicated that mouse RUNX2 remained unchanged when EWS-FLI1 was expressed in mouse myoblast C2C12 cells ( ). We hypothesized that EWS-FLI1 or EWS-ERG might alter the gene expression of RUNX1 or RUNX2 genes in human cells. We transduced lentivirus expressing the EWS-FLI1 or EWS-ERG genes controlled by the Tet-on system into hTert-RPE1 cells. EWS-FLI1 or EWS-ERG were localized in the nucleus ( A and D). The induction of EWS-FLI1 or EWS-ERG by doxycycline treatment up-regulated NR0B1, but did not change SOX6, which is the target of EWS-FLI1 ( ) (Figure 8B and E). The induction of EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG down-regulated RUNX2 but did not change RUNX1 (Figure 8B and E). Additionally, EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG down-regulated RUNX2 mRNA expression (Figure 8C and F). Mesenchymal stem cells possess the capacity for transformation upon expression of EWS-FLI1 ( , ). We transduced lentivirus expressing EWS-FLI1 controlled by the Tet-on system into hMSC-BM. EWS-FLI1 was localized in the nucleus (Figure 8G). The induction of EWS-FLI1 by doxycycline treatment up-regulated NR0B1 in hMSC-BM (Figure 8H). Additionally, the induction of EWS-FLI1 down-regulated RUNX2 in hMSC-BM, consistent with the results in hTert-RPE1 cells (Figure 8H). These results suggest that EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG can inhibit RUNX2 gene expression.
 High RUNX2 mRNA expression in Ewing’s sarcoma is associated with improved patient survival. We were unable to determine how certain factors affect the regulation of the RUNX2 gene expression in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. In sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma (sRCC), RUNX2 shows elevated expression levels in tumor tissue samples relative to adjacent tissues ( ). On the other hand, patients with high RUNX2 mRNA expression showed significantly better overall survival than those with low RUNX2 expression in Ewing’s sarcoma (raw p-value=4.13×10-4, Bonferroni p-value=0.03) ( A). Additionally, patients with high RUNX2 mRNA expression showed marginally significant event-free survival compared to those with low RUNX2 expression (raw p-value=7.56×10-4, Bonferroni p-value=0.055) (Figure 9B). miR-378, a microRNA, antagonizes RUNX2 mRNA and protein expression in SCC cell lines ( ). EWS-FLI1 may up-regulate miR-378 to decrease RUNX2 mRNA levels in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. It would thus be informative to determine if miR-378 controls the RUNX2 gene in Ewing’s sarcoma cells.
 

## Discussion
Our study shows that RUNX2 expression negatively affects the oncogenic process in Ewing’s sarcoma. The summary of this study is shown in . Several studies have reported that mesenchymal stem cells are considered a source of Ewing’s sarcoma ( , , , ). Upon down-regulation of EWS-FLI1, there is an up-regulation of RUNX2 under both normal and differentiation conditions ( ) (Figure 2). RUNX2 is a key transcription factor necessary for osteoblast differentiation, playing roles in both cell maturation and growth (Figure 10A). When EWS-FLI1, caused by a chromosomal translocation, is expressed, it inhibits RUNX2, which impairs osteoblast differentiation and induces growth caused by EWS-FLI1-mediated transcription (Figure 10B). Two possible mechanisms are proposed: one where EWS-FLI1 directly blocks RUNX2 gene expression, and another where it indirectly suppresses RUNX2 by inducing an unknown factor. The exact process is still unclear. EWS-FLI1 can activate target genes by binding to GGAA microsatellite repeats independently of RUNX2 (Figure 10C). However, when RUNX2 is present, it may influence EWS-FLI1’s transcriptional activity through interaction, even if RUNX2 lacks the RUNT domain (Figure 10D). Thus, EWS-FLI1 mainly regulates its target genes by suppressing RUNX2.
 Our findings indicate that RUNX1 and RUNX2 suppress the target genes of EWS-FLI1 (Figure 5). The RUNX2-MC, which lacks the RUNT domain of RUNX2, was exclusively unable to translocate into the nucleus (Figure 5B). However, the addition of two NLS to RUNX2-MC, as well as RUNX1-MC, enabled nuclear translocation, allowing RUNX2 to inhibit the target gene expression of EWS-FLI1 without the RUNT domain (Figure 5F and Figure 6C). In addition, the RUNX2-MC could immunoprecipitated with EWS-FLI1 (Figure 7B). The transactivation domain (TAD) and inhibitory domain (ID) regions are contained within RUNX1-MC and RUNX2-MC ( , ). These regions are predicted to contain disordered regions according to the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database ( ). The N-terminal region of EWS-FLI1, corresponding to EWS, contains a prion-like domain responsible for recruiting BRG1/BRM-associated factors (BAF) complexes to the GGAA microsatellite region ( ). Therefore, RUNX1-MC and RUNX2-MC could potentially induce transcriptional repression of EWS-FLI1 target genes by forming complexes with the prion-like domain of EWS-FLI1 (Figure 7).
RUNX3 is recognized as a tumor suppressor that is frequently inactivated through gene silencing or protein mislocalization in various tumors, including those of the stomach, bladder, colon, kidney, glioma, and lung ( - ). Conversely, RUNX3 can act as an oncogene in ovarian cancer ( , ). RUNX3 is expressed in Ewing’s sarcoma, but is not present in mesenchymal stem cells (Figure 1) ( ). Our results showed that the knockdown of EWS-FLI1 did not alter RUNX3 protein and gene expression, suggesting that RUNX3 gene expression is independent of EWS-FLI1 (Figure 2). RUNX3 supported the EWS-FLI1-dependent gene expression of targets such as NKX2-2, PPP1R1A, and NR0B1 ( ). This suggests that inhibiting EWS-FLI1-dependent gene expression through RUNX1 or RUNX2 expression may prevent the interaction between EWS-FLI1 and RUNX3.
Meanwhile, the expression of RUNX1 and RUNX2 also led to an increase in the quantity of EWS-FLI1 protein (Figure 3). EWS-FLI1 is subject to proteasomal degradation through the polyubiquitination at a single lysine residue ( ). Additionally, TRIM8 modulates the amount of EWS-FLI1 protein to adjust to a suitable level by ubiquitination ( ). The deubiquitinase USP19 stabilizes EWS-FLI1 protein levels ( ). Treatment with a proteasomal inhibitor led to elevated levels of EWS-FLI1 protein and its target NKX2-2, which is also a proteasome target ( , , ). However, the induction of RUNX1 and RUNX2 down-regulated NKX2-2 but not EWS-FLI1 (Figure 3). Thus, the induction of RUNX1 and RUNX2 may stimulate the expression of the USP19 gene or other kinds of deubiquitinases.
A previous study reported that EWS-FLI1 did not regulate the expression of RUNX2 protein in mouse myoblast C2C12 cells, which can undergo morphological changes similar to those seen in Ewing’s sarcoma ( ). Our findings indicate that EWS-FLI1 and EWS-ERG down-regulate RUNX2 mRNA, leading to decreased RUNX2 protein levels, but do not affect RUNX1, in hTert-RPE1 cells (Figure 8A to F). RUNX2 down-regulation by EWS-FLI1 expression also occurred in the mesenchymal stem cells (Figure 8G and H). These discrepancies could arise from differences between human and mouse genomes or the epigenetic states of both species that might affect the RUNX2 gene expression. We need to assess these differences in the future.

## Conclusion
Our research demonstrated that EWS-FLI1 can suppress the expression of the RUNX2 gene, resulting in a reduction of EWS-FLI1 target gene levels. The RUNT domain of RUNX1 and RUNX2 is not responsible for the inhibition of EWS-FLI1 gene expression. Moreover, the induction of RUNX1 and RUNX2 can inhibit cellular proliferation in Ewing’s sarcoma cells. Nevertheless, excessive induction of RUNX genes is not a sustainable anti-tumor approach in these cells. If a small peptide derived from RUNX genes can be identified to regulate EWS-FLI1 target gene expression specifically, gene therapy may emerge as a viable treatment modality.
