## Abstract
The spatiotemporal control of Wnt signaling orchestrates embryonic development and tissue homeostasis, while dysregulation of this pathway has been linked to a variety of diseases, including cancer, fibrosis and neurodegeneration. Although nuclear accumulation of β-catenin serves as a hallmark of Wnt pathway hyperactivation, the mechanisms controlling nuclear β-catenin remain obscure and are subjected to some conflicting hypotheses. In this narrative review, we summarize current understanding of the complex interplays fine-tunning the nucleocytoplasmic trafficking and subcellular distribution of β-catenin. We also present computational analysis to explore candidate molecules regulating nuclear β-catenin and suggest new perspectives to future study. Finally, we discuss how these insights could pave the way for mechanism-based approaches to target “undruggable” Wnt signaling for Wnt-driven diseases.

## Overview of the canonical Wnt signaling
Ever since the discovery of the first Wnt family member in 1982 [ ], Wnt signaling has received increasing attention due to its necessity in development and tissue maintenance. Integrase-1 (Int-1) was uncovered as a proto-oncogene that can be activated by the insertion of the mouse mammary tumor virus and ultimately caused breast cancer [ ], while Wingless (Wg) was characterized by its essential role in developing Drosophila wings [ ]. Later, the term “Wnt” was coined to reflect the shared sequences and function between Wg and Int-1 [ ]. To date, 19 Wnt proteins and over 15 receptors and co-receptors have been identified in mammals, underscoring the intricate nature of Wnt signaling [ – ]. Wnt cascades can be divided into canonical and noncanonical pathways based on β-catenin’s involvement in transcriptional activation. The canonical Wnt pathway is characterized by the translocation of β-catenin into the nucleus, where it activates Wnt targets and regulates cellular processes including cell fate specification, proliferation, differentiation, survival and migration [ , ]. In contrast, the noncanonical branch comprises two pathways: the Planar Cell Polarity and the Wnt/Ca2+ pathway, both of which function in cellular polarization and migration [ ].
The Wnt/β-catenin pathway progresses through four stages based on signaling locations: extracellular, membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus (Fig. ) [ ]. Extracellular signals are primarily mediated by Wnt proteins, among which Wnt1, Wnt3, and Wnt3a are major activators of the canonical Wnt cascade [ ]. Reception of Wnt signaling at the cell membrane depends on the seven-pass transmembrane receptor Frizzled (Fzd) and the single-pass lipoprotein receptor-related protein 5/6 (LRP5/LRP6) [ ]. Other extracellular components, such as the transmembrane E3 ubiquitin ligases ZNRF3 and RNF43, suppress Wnt activity by selectively ubiquitinating Fzds and Lrps, and promoting their degradation (Fig. ) [ ]. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling operates in two states: Wnt OFF and Wnt ON. In the absence of Wnt stimulation, cytoplasmic β-catenin level is low due to its continuous phosphorylation by the destruction complex (DC), which includes the scaffolding protein AXIN, adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), casein kinase 1 (CK1), and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β). As a ubiquitin E3 Ligase, β-transducin repeat-containing protein (β-TrCP) recognizes phosphorylated β-catenin and ubiquitinates it for proteasomal degradation [ ]. Opposite to ubiquitination, the deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) remove ubiquitin and stabilize target protein [ ]. Upon binding of Wnt ligands to their receptors, activated Dishevelled (Dvl) protein disrupts the degradation machinery, the accruing β-catenin shuttles into the nucleus, where it binds to T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) transcription factors to initiate the transcription of target genes (Fig. ) [ , , , ].
 β-catenin is a “dual-role” protein with a structural function as cell adhesion stabilizer and a key transcription factor controlling Wnt targets [ ]. Accumulation of nuclear β-catenin, the rate-limiting step for pathway activation, requires stringent spatiotemporal control [ – ]. Here, we provide an up-to-date overview of the regulatory machinery modulating β-catenin subcellular distribution, with a specific emphasis on proteins affecting nuclear transport (import and export), degradation and retention processes. We also highlight the growing blueprint of targeting nuclear β-catenin and discuss its implications in disease therapies.

## Nuclear β-catenin, the defining component controlling pathway activity
Appropriate subcellular localization is essential for the function of biomolecules. To activate Wnt targets, β-catenin must enter the nucleus. The longer β-catenin stays in the nucleus, the higher Wnt activity it triggers. However, when interacting with different compartmental partners, β-catenin can be trapped in the cytoplasm or cell membrane, thereby influencing its nuclear level and Wnt activity [ ].

## The β-catenin nuclear transport pathway

## Nuclear pore complex and nucleocytoplasmic trafficking
The nuclear compartment is separated from the cytoplasm by a double-layered nuclear envelope. Small molecules less than 40 kDa passively diffuse across the nuclear pore complex (NPC), whereas molecules larger than 5–9 nm move into or out of the nucleus in a signal- and energy-dependent manner [ ]. Each NPC is an octameric proteinaceous channel comprising over 30 types of nucleoporins (Nups). Its conserved architecture features five structural modules: cytoplasmic filaments, cytoplasmic rings, inner ring, nuclear rings, and the nuclear basket [ , ]. Nups fall into four classes: core scaffold, transmembrane, phenylalanine-glycine (FG), and linker Nups. The scaffold Nups assemble into ring-like structures to create the NPC’s core architecture, while the transmembrane Nups anchor the complex to the nuclear envelope [ , ]. FG Nups form a selective diffusion barrier in the central transport channel and interact with transport receptors (importin and exportin) to help cargos shuttle through the complex [ ]. By bridging scaffold Nups, linker Nups confer plasticity to the inner ring, enabling it to undergo reversible dilation and constriction. Such adjustments alter FG-repeat density in the central channel, thereby modulating the flux and selectivity of cargo [ ].
The nuclear transport systems contain three components: Nups that form the nuclear membrane-embedded NPC; transport factors (karyopherin) include importin or exportin that translocate cargo proteins; the GTPase Ran that defines transport directionality and efficiency [ ]. In general, proteins with classic nuclear localization signal (NLS) enter the nucleus in three steps: form cargo-receptor complex in the cytoplasm by binding to importin-β via importin-α adaptor; transport the complex through NPC, facilitated by FG repeats on Nups; in the nuclear compartment, importin-β interacts with RanGTP, causing a conformational change that unloads the cargo proteins from the importin-α/importin-β complex [ , ]. Importins and RanGTP are then recycled back to the cytoplasm where RanGAP (Ran GTPase-activating protein) stimulates the hydrolysis of RanGTP to RanGDP, and releases importins for the next round of import [ ]. CRM1 (Chromosomal Region Maintenance 1), also known as exportin 1, mediates nuclear exit of various cargo proteins with leucine-rich nuclear export signal (NES). At the nuclear side, the CRM1-RanGTP-cargo complex interacts with Nups to traverse the NPC into the cytoplasm, where the complex is disassembled via RanGTP hydrolysis. CRM1 and RanGDP are returned to the nucleus and RanGDP is recharged to RanGTP by RanGEF (Ran guanine nucleotide exchange factor) to prepare for the next export cycle [ ].

## Nuclear import of β-catenin
β-catenin, with a molecular weight of 92 kDa, is too large to passively diffuse through nuclear pores [ ]. Structurally, β-catenin is organized into three domains: an N-terminal region, a central 12 Armadillo (Arm) repeat, and a C-terminal transactivation domain [ , ]. The lack of recognizable NLS sequences on β-catenin suggest its nuclear import requires the assistance of chaperone proteins [ ]. On the other hand, importin-β competitively inhibits the movement of β-catenin to the nucleus, implicating that β-catenin may potentially interact with nuclear envelop via shared importin-β-binding sites on nucleoporins [ ].

## Chaperone-assisted nuclear import of β-catenin
A wide range of cytonuclear shuttle proteins have been proposed to assist the nuclear entry of β-catenin (Fig. ). In glioma cells, the forkhead box M1 (FOXM1), a NLS-containing nuclear shuttling protein, binds directly to the Arm repeats of β-catenin, enabling its transport through the NPC [ ]. Several groups show that the androgen receptor (AR) contains a NLS motif within its DNA-binding domain and translocate β-catenin into nucleus dependent on Ran GTPase and importin α/β complex [ – ]. Interestingly, except its function in mediating Wnt signaling transduction within the nucleus, the LEF-1 gene also serves as a molecular chaperone to assist nuclear transit of β-catenin [ – ]. The insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) is a docking protein for insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1) and insulin receptors. In response to IGF-1, IRS-1 undergoes phosphorylation and binds to the C-terminal region of β-catenin to facilitate its nuclear entry [ ].
 LIM domain kinase LIMK1 and LIMK2 are serine/threonine protein kinase with two LIM motifs [ ]. In colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, suppressing LIMK2 while overexpressing LIMK1 synergistically maximize nuclear level of β-catenin, leading to a hypothesis that LIMK1 binds β-catenin to translocate it into nucleus, while LIMK2 interacts and stabilizes β-catenin in the cytoplasm [ ]. Similar observations have been made with Kap-β2/Transportin (TNPO1), which mediate β-catenin nuclear import through a conserved proline-tyrosine nuclear localization signal (PY-NLS) [ ]. Consistently, incorporating the PY-NLS into β-catenin markedly enhanced its binding affinity to TNPO1, and β-catenin nuclear translocation was abrogated in Tnpo1-deficient Xenopus embryos [ ]. Another study shows that in Xenopus embryos, IQ motif containing GTPase activating protein 1 (IQGAP1) complexed with Dvl and β-catenin to promote their nuclear translocation via importin-β5 protein [ ]. Interestingly, several groups reported that SMAD family member 3 (SMAD3) interacts with the N-terminal and mid-region of β-catenin to mediate its nuclear entry by direct engaging nucleoporins Nup214 and Nup153 [ – ].
More recently, β-catenin has been found to undergo Ran-independent nuclear import. Studies by Griffin and colleagues indicate that Rap guanine nucleotide exchange factor 5 (RAPGEF5) regulates β-catenin nuclear localization independently of the importin β1 and RanGTP [ ]. Mechanistically, Rapgef5 activated nuclear GTPases Rap1a/Rap1b by promoting their GTP-bound states, which then bind to β-catenin and induce its nuclear localization [ ].

## Chaperone-independent nuclear entry of β-catenin
The structural similarity between β-catenin Arm domains and HEAT motifs in importin-β suggest β-catenin may enter the nucleus through direct interaction with specific nucleoporins (Fig. ) [ , ]. Accordingly, nucleoporin93 (Nup93), a core scaffold Nup of inner ring complex, interacts with and stimulates β-catenin nuclear translocation, independent of importin [ ]. Collectively, these findings provide evidence supporting context-specific nuclear import of β-catenin.

## Nuclear export of β-catenin

## CRM1-dependent nuclear export of β-catenin
Like its nuclear import, β-catenin nuclear export is also facilitated through NES-containing chaperones and CRM1 receptor (Fig. ) [ , ]. Interestingly, some of the best characterized β-catenin export chaperones are negative regulators of Wnt signaling. APC, which contains two NESs on the amino terminus, is also present in the nucleus [ ]. Nuclear APC forms a ternary complex with β-catenin and CRM1, leading to cytoplasmic re-localization of β-catenin [ ]. Quantitative binding assays indicate the third 20 aa repeat in the central region of APC has the strongest binding affinity for β-catenin, which is frequently disrupted in CRC cells, underscoring the importance of APC-β-catenin interaction in intestinal carcinogenesis [ ]. Likewise, AXIN also shuttles between the cytoplasm and nucleus in which AXIN, β-catenin, CRM1 and RanGTP co-transport through the nuclear pore [ ]. Chibby, a β-catenin antagonist, stimulates nuclear exclusion of β-catenin by forming chibby-β-catenin-14-3-3 tripartite complex [ ]. Furthermore, Menin encoded by the multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) gene, leucine zipper putative tumor suppressor 2 (LZTS2), p21-activated kinase 4 (PAK4) and Kank (a regulator of actin polymerization), all possess NES and cooperate with the CRM1 receptor to stimulate the flux of β-catenin out of the nucleus [ – ]. Lastly, Emerin, the inner nuclear membrane protein, facilitates CRM1-dependent β-catenin nuclear exit through a direct interaction mechanism (Fig. ) [ ].

## CRM1-independent nuclear export of β-catenin
Apart from canonical nuclear export pathway, β-catenin may exit the nucleus independent of CRM1 and Ran (Fig. ) [ ]. For instance, RAN binding protein 3 (RanBP3) binds directly to β-catenin when stimulated by RanGTP, and relocates nuclear β-catenin to the cytoplasm independently of CRM1 [ ].

## Nuclear retention and degradation of β-catenin
β-catenin can be retained in the nucleus by recruiting it to immobile structures or by spatially constraining nuclear envelope trafficking (Fig. ). Following Wnt stimulation, Twa1/Gid8 and β-catenin translocate into the nucleus, where Twa1 promotes the formation β-catenin-TCF4 complex to enhance its nuclear retention [ ]. Similarly, phosphorylated myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2), mediated by p38 MAPK, interacts with β-catenin to promote its nuclear accumulation [ ]. A study reported BCL9, in collaboration with Pygopus (Pygo), stabilized β-catenin within the nucleus by sterically restricting its passage through the nuclear pores [ ]. Transcriptional factor LEF-1 represents another molecule for β-catenin nuclear retention as it sequesters β-catenin away from APC, thereby blocking APC-mediated nuclear export of β-catenin [ ]. Likewise, peptidylprolyl cis/trans isomerase Pin1 isomerizes nuclear β-catenin and induces a conformational change. The isomerized β-catenin cannot bind to nuclear APC, and is retained in the nucleus (Fig. ) [ ].
 Nuclear β-catenin also undergoes compartment-specific proteolysis, adding another layer of control (Fig. ). In the Wnt-off phase, β-catenin is continuously targeted for ubiquitination and degradation in the cytoplasm. Upon Wnt stimulation, c-Cbl, a ubiquitin E3 Ligase, was translocated into the nucleus, where it binds to and ubiquitinates nuclear β-catenin [ ]. Another study reported by Xue and colleagues shows tripartite motif-containing protein 33 (TRIM33), as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, interacts with and degrades nuclear β-catenin (Fig. ) [ ].

## Membrane-localized β-catenin
Without Wnt stimulation, most β-catenin is bound to cell membrane and remains outside the nucleus (Fig. ). N-cadherin, a classical calcium-dependent cell adhesion molecule, anchors β-catenin at the cell membrane [ ]. Similarly, the cytoplasmic domain of E-cadherin forms a complex with β-catenin, which increases membrane-bound β-catenin and suppresses Wnt activity [ ]. In colon cancer cells, tumor suppressor MCC (mutated in colorectal cancer) interacts with E-cadherin and β-catenin at the cell membrane, thus sequestering it away from the nucleus [ ]. By tethering β-catenin to E-cadherin at the cell surface, α-catenin and small GTPase RHOA limit β-catenin’s availability for nuclear Wnt signaling [ , ]. Likewise, cell-cell junction protein p120-catenin [ ] and vitamin D receptor (VDR) [ ] increase the level of E-cadherin in the plasma membrane, thus indirectly blocking the redistribution of β-catenin to the nucleus. Furthermore, naked cuticle homolog 1 (Nkd1) also sequester β-catenin on the cell membrane to prevent its nuclear translocation (Fig. ) [ ] .
 On the other hand, the β-catenin/cadherin complex can be destabilized, which increases β-catenin availability for nuclear or cytoplasmic signaling (Fig. ). The membrane mucin Muc4 promotes lysosomal degradation of E-cadherin, thereby disrupting E-cadherin/β-catenin complex and facilitating β-catenin nuclear translocation [ ]. Similarly, CUB-domain containing protein 1 (CDCP1), a type 1 transmembrane protein, enhances Wnt signaling by reducing membrane-bound E-cadherin/β-catenin [ ]. Chronic infection with H. pylori strains carrying CagA gene (H.pylori CagA) increases the risk for gastric adenocarcinoma as CagA oncoprotein physically interacts with and destabilizes E-cadherin/β-catenin complex [ ]. Following study from Zheng’s group demonstrates LRP8, a member of the LDL receptor family, further amplifies H. pylori-induced β-catenin nuclear localization by forming CagA/LRP8/β-catenin complex, which disrupts E-cadherin/β-catenin complex and releases β-catenin for nuclear signaling [ ]. Finally, it has been shown that overexpression of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2T (UBE2T) leads to E-cadherin loss and subsequent β-catenin nuclear translocation [ ].
Of notes, post-translational modification also regulates the dissociation of β-catenin-adhesion complex. Abelson kinase (Abl) phosphorylates β-catenin on Tyrosine 489, resulting in a decrease in N-cadherin-bound β-catenin, and promoting its nuclear translocation (Fig. ) [ ].

## Cytosolic retention and degradation of β-catenin

## Cytosolic retention of β-catenin
β-catenin can be found in three compartments, the nucleus, the cytoplasm and the plasma membrane. Increasing evidence suggested cytoplasmic stabilization and retention is also a nuclear β-catenin controlling mechanism [ ]. SH3 domain binding protein 4 (SH3BP4) localizes at the perinuclear region to retain β-catenin in the cytoplasm although the underlying mechanism remains elusive (Fig. ) [ ]. Interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), a key player in antiviral defense, is inactive in the cytoplasm while the activated form moves to the nucleus. In resting state, cytoplasmic IRF3 binds to the Arm domain of β-catenin and prevents its nuclear shuttling [ ]. Similarly, leucine zipper transcription factor-like 1 (LZTFL1) interacts with β-catenin to stabilize it in the cytoplasm whereas β-catenin is predominantly detected in the nucleus when LZTFL1 is downregulated (Fig. ༉ [ ]. In human colon tumors, nuclear Sirtuin1 (SIRT1) inversely correlates with the nuclear localization of β-catenin [ ]. Overexpression of SIRT1 in CRC cells significantly reduces the nuclear β-catenin pool by deacetylating β-catenin and keeping it in the cytoplasm [ ]. The Kinesin-2/IFT-A complex is a molecular machinery critical for intraflagellar transport (IFT). Disruption of the Kinesin-2/IFT-A complex in Wnt-activated cells results in elevated cytoplasmic β-catenin [ ]. Molecularly, the Kinesin-2/IFT-A complex interacts with β-catenin via IFT140 (a core subunit of the IFT-A complex) to protect β-catenin from cytoplasmic retention following pathway activation (Fig. ༉ [ ].
 

## Cytosolic degradation of β-catenin

## Cytosolic β-catenin degradation under physiological conditions
The activity of β-catenin is predominantly regulated via protein degradation, a process primarily mediated by phosphorylation [ – ]. Under basal (Wnt-off) condition, β-catenin is phosphorylated on Thr41/Ser33/Ser37/Ser45 via a multiprotein destruction complex comprising AXIN, APC, GSK3β, and CK1. This phosphorylation marks β-catenin for ubiquitination by β-transduction repeat-containing E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase 1 (β-TrCP1), targeting it for proteasomal degradation (Fig. ). These processes have been extensively reviewed in several excellent papers [ – ].
 Recently, neddylation, a ubiquitylation-like protein modification where a small molecule Nedd8 is covalently attached to target proteins, has been implicated in cytosolic β-catenin degradation [ – ]. Wang et al. reported that the E3 ligase β-TrCP2, but not β-TrCP1, is associated with neddylation and degradation of endogenous β-catenin [ ]. Although GSK3β and APC are not required for β-catenin neddylation, they are essential for subsequent degradation of neddylated β-catenin [ ]. Similarly, another study also provided evidence for Nae1-mediated β-catenin neddylation, and demonstrated that inhibiting β-catenin neddylation increased its nuclear accumulation and Wnt signaling [ ].
Interestingly, cytoplasmic β-catenin also can be degraded independent of destruction complex (Fig. ). In vascular smooth muscle cells, X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), a key component of the endoplasmic reticulum stress, exists as unspliced (XBP1u) and spliced XBP1 (XBP1s) forms [ ]. XBP1u directly interacts with and target β-catenin for ubiquitination-degradation in a DC-independent manner (Fig. ) [ ]. Conversely, by inducing XBP1 mRNA splicing and subsequent XBP1s protein in fibroblast, heat shock protein 47 (HSP47) represses β-catenin degradation to enhance its nuclear translocation (Fig. ) [ ].

## Cytosolic β-catenin degradation in cancer cells
Aberrant accumulation of nuclear β-catenin is a hallmark of many cancers. Owing to mutations in the key pathway components, epigenetic modification, and crosstalk with other signaling cascades, β-catenin can be stabilized and translocate into the nucleus to drive tumor initiation and progression [ , ]. Emerging evidence suggests the regulatory functions of the Rac1/PAK1 signaling in this context. Rac1, a small GTPases, activates its downstream kinase p21 activated kinase 1 (PAK1) to phosphorylate β-catenin at Ser675 [ , ]. The phos-S675 β-catenin is more stable than non-phosphorylated form and is almost free from ubiquitination, which then moves to the nucleus and activates the pathway in colon cancer cells [ , ]. AXIN, the key scaffolding protein of the β-catenin destruction complex, is a critical negative regulator of nuclear β-catenin. In hepatocellular carcinomas, aberrant tyrosine phosphorylation of β-catenin by oncogenic mutants of RON and MET receptor tyrosine kinase disrupts its association with AXIN, leading to β-catenin stabilization and nuclear accumulation [ ]. Likewise, in Bcr-Abl-positive chronic myeloid leukemia cells, Bcr-Abl physically binds to and phosphorylates β-catenin at Y86 and Y654 residue [ ]. Tyrosine-phosphorylated β-catenin failed to be recruited to the DC complex, thereby enhancing its nuclear abundance and oncogenic activity [ ]. SUMOylation of RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF146 in hepatocellular carcinoma stimulates its interaction with AXIN, thus promoting AXIN degradation and subsequent Wnt activation [ ]. Like β-catenin, γ-catenin (a functional homologue of β-catenin) is also involved in cell adhesion and is regulated by the same destruction complex [ ]. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, γ-catenin is frequently overexpressed, which promotes the stabilization of β-catenin and its nuclear localization (Fig. ) [ ].
In many cancers, genetic mutations or aberrant activation of upstream signals lead to stabilized β-catenin in the cytoplasm, deubiquitination becomes a key molecular event modulating cytosolic β-catenin level (Fig. ). Upon genotoxic treatment, OTULIN, a linear linkage-specific deubiquitinase, interacts with and inhibits β-catenin linear ubiquitination in breast cancer, thus promoting its stabilization [ ]. Similarly, by cleaving Lys 48-linked polyubiquitin chains, OTU domain-containing ubiquitin aldehyde-binding protein 1 (OTUB1) [ ], ubiquitin-specific protease 9X (USP9X) [ ], and USP4 [ ] stabilize cytoplasmic β-catenin and promote its nuclear translocation in bladder carcinoma, glioma cell, and colorectal cancer, respectively. Several ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), including USP2a [ ], USP39 [ ], USP47 [ ], and USP20 [ ] also directly bind to and deubiquitinate β-catenin in the cytoplasm, leading to elevated expression of Wnt target genes. Beyond direct interaction with β-catenin, DUBs also engage with the DC complex to indirectly modulate cytoplasmic β-catenin stability. For instance, the deubiquitylase USP10 was reported to antagonize ubiquitylation of AXIN through K48-specific deubiquitination, thereby enhancing its stability and function in the Wnt pathway (Fig. ) [ ]. Interestingly, ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase like 5 (UCHL5), a deubiquitinating enzyme, stabilizes Axin by blocking ubiquitination of Axin1 DIX domain, which is independent of its deubiquitinating activity [ ].
DUBs can form complexes with other proteins, which regulates their activity and substrates specificity [ – ]. For example, RNF220, a RING domain E3 ubiquitin ligase, forms a complex with USP7 to deubiquitylate and stabilize β-catenin in CRC cells [ ]. Another group shows that USP9X interacts with E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP1 to remove ubiquitin from multiple lysine residues on DVL2, and positively regulates Wnt signaling [ ]. In response to Wnt activation, the USP46 ternary complex (USP46-UAF1-WDR20) is assembled and deubiquitinates LRP6 at the plasma membrane, counteracting ZNRF3/RNF43-mediated ubiquitination and supporting canonical Wnt activation [ ].
Subsequently, β-TrCP1 recognizes the phosphorylated β-catenin and promotes its ubiquitination. In parallel, β-TrCP2 acts as a neddylation-dependent E3 ligase and participates in the Nedd8-mediated β-catenin degradation. XBP1u facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin in a DC-independent manner, while HSP47 inhibits the expression of XBP1u. (2) cytosolic β-catenin degradation in cancer cells: γ-catenin, RNF146, Bcr-Abl, oncogenic RON and MET mutants suppress β-catenin degradation via inhibiting the destruction complex. Rac1/PAK1 blocks β-catenin ubiquitination by phosphorylating β-catenin at Ser675. Deubiquitinase (USP2a, USP4, USP9X, USP20, USP39, USP47, OTUB1, OTULIN) and deubiquitinase complex USP7/RNF220 deubiquitinate β-catenin, and inhibit its degradation in the cytoplasm.
The notion that nuclear β-catenin level is a direct readout of its cytosolic pool oversimplifies the complex control exerted by compartment-specific machinery. In fact, the nuclear abundance of β-catenin is dynamically regulated through diversified mechanisms, including: (1) nucleocytoplasmic shuttling mediated by canonical or non-canonical nuclear transport machinery; (2) spatiotemporal localization and distribution of β-catenin, which encompasses compartment-specific retention and degradation. Considering the oncogenic potential of nuclear β-catenin, targeting context-dependent regulatory circuits may offer therapeutic benefits for Wnt-driven diseases.

## Nuclear β-catenin: emerging modulators and approaches

## Identify novel regulators through interaction structure analysis
As discussed above, nuclear β-catenin is dictated by a wide range of chaperones that modulate its protein stability and subcellular localization. Thus, a comprehensive examination of the structural basis underlying these interactions may reveal novel modulators of Wnt pathway. By integrating sequence alignment, and docking-based prediction (Fig. A), we prioritize candidate proteins collected from several interaction databases (HINT, Genemania, STRING) based on structural and functional similarity to known β-catenin interactors. Protein interactions typically occur through hydrophobic forces, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic interactions [ ]. Guided by this framework (Fig. A), we analyzed crystal structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Key β-catenin binding partners, including TCF4 [ ], TCF3 [ ], LEF1 [ ], AXIN2 [ ], APC [ ], BCL9 [ ], ICAT (inhibitor of β-catenin and TCF-4) [ ], and E-cadherin [ ], all interact with the Armadillo repeat region of β-catenin (Fig. B).
 

## Sequence and structural analysis of β-catenin interactors
β-catenin interacts with a broad range of proteins primarily through its Armadillo repeat region, which serves as the major docking site mediating hydrophobic, hydrogen-bonding, and electrostatic contacts with partner proteins [ ]. To further define how this docking site recognizes different interactors, we first examine the sequence conservation of binding motifs among known partners.
The Armadillo region of β-catenin forms a right-handed superhelix with grooves along the helix in which lysine residues at positions 312 and 435 in β-catenin and Asp16 in Tcf3 protein are crucial for their interactions [ ]. Subsequently, a consensus motif “Dxθθxφx2–7E” (where θ = aliphatic nonpolar amino acids and φ = aromatic amino acids) was predicted to fit the groove formed by the Arm domain of β-catenin, and many validated β-catenin binding partners contain sequences matching or closely resembling this conserved motif [ ]. To further investigate whether recognition of this domain underlies interaction specificity, we performed multiple sequence alignment of binding regions from structurally resolved β-catenin interactors (TCF, LEF, APC, E-cadherin, etc.) and found that these regions either overlapped with or contained the consensus motif (Fig. C). The analysis revealed moderate-to-high sequence conservation, supporting the hypothesis that β-catenin selectively recognizes conserved linear motifs exemplified by the consensus sequence.
Among the validated interactors, APC is a representative example in which the β-catenin binding region harbors the consensus sequence, and its interaction is further regulated by phosphorylation of the 20-aa repeats. This post-translational modification greatly boosts the binding affinity to the Armadillo region of β-catenin, and competitively blocks TCF binding [ ], exemplifying how sequence motifs and their 3D structure determine binding specificity. We then calculate the alignment score of candidate proteins by computing pairwise comparisons against the reference interactor set. To mitigate protein length-based biases, binding regions were restricted to alignment-matched segments, followed by local alignment scores recalculation [ ]. For each candidate protein, we identified all regions that could be locally aligned to the reference binding motifs from established interactors. The alignment score reported for each candidate corresponds to the highest local alignment score among all such regions, indicating the best potential match to β-catenin-binding motif. Next, we selected validated regulators of nuclear β-catenin (Table ) as positive controls. Proteins with ≥ 4 β-catenin-associated citations in the HINT database [ ] were designated as the test group (Table ). To establish a baseline alignment score distribution, we created in silico 1000 amino acid sequences (100–1000 aa) by uniformly sampling 20 standard amino acids. Alignment scores were then computed between these sequences and known β-catenin binding regions using the described approach.
 Following alignment scores computation, the positive controls showed significantly higher scores compared with the random sequences (One-sided Wilcoxon test, p = 0.0018) (Fig.  D), consistent with the hypothesis that conserved motifs provide a rational basis for candidate selection. Although alignment scores for positive controls had significantly higher alignment scores than random sequences, the test candidate group as a whole did not show a statistically significant difference from the random group (Fig.  D), suggesting that sequence alignment alone may have limited power to discriminate novel candidates from background noise. Nevertheless, three candidates (TCF7, vinculin, and CADH3) had alignment scores exceeding the 95% confidence interval of the random distribution (Fig.  D), indicating that for a subset of candidates, strong sequence conservation could be a useful predictive feature.
To complement the sequence-based analysis and overcome its limitations in identifying novel interactors, we further incorporated HADDOCK2.4 docking simulations with predefined restraints [ , ], using conserved Armadillo domain residues as the β-catenin docking site, to quantitate β-catenin binding affinity. For proteins with available PDB structures, we applied these models directly. For remaining candidates, we predicted 3D structure via AlphaFold3 and selected the top-ranked alignment-derived region as the docking interface. We defined β-catenin docking site using conserved Armadillo domain residues and guide the docking procedure with ambiguous interaction restraints (AIRs). The negative group was generated by selecting 29 sequences from the random cohort with alignment scores below zero (Table , Supplemental Table ). AlphaFold3-predicted conformations enabled docking onto β-catenin following same HADDOCK protocol. Docking scores were then retrieved from top-ranked clusters during the first refinement iteration (IT1) of the HADDOCK workflow.
 As presented in Fig.  E, positive and test candidates had significantly higher docking scores compared with the negative group, suggesting that structural complementarity at the conserved docking site serves as a potential determinant of β-catenin interactor selection. Together, the integrated sequence and structural analyses highlight that conserved motif recognition combined with favorable docking geometry can effectively prioritize β-catenin–binding candidates for further experimental validation.

## Composite score calculation and interactor prediction
Finally, we integrate the normalized alignment and docking simulation to formulate a composite score:
 in which alignment and docking scores were min-max normalized, with scaling parameters derived from the training data within each cross-validation fold [ , ]. We systematically changed the weight ( ) assigned to each component and performed 10-fold cross-validation to assess classification performance across different weighting schemes [ ]. This analysis revealed that a weight of 62% ( =0.62) assigned to the normalized alignment score yielded the highest mean Area Under the Curve (AUC) (0.908 ± 0.091, Fig. F), indicating that, in our dataset, sequence conservation provides modestly greater discriminative power than docking scores alone. However, it should be noted that alignment-based interactor discovery is restrained by the small number of high-scoring candidates (only three from the test set), whereas docking identified a larger set of plausible binders. Thus, the composite score leverages the strengths of both approaches in which sequence alignment can prioritize candidates with strong motif conservation, while molecular docking expands discovery to structurally compatible candidates without strict motif similarity.
To assess the robustness of the composite score, we conducted bootstrap resampling (1000 iteration with replacement) on the combined dataset (Positive + Negative) using the statistical methodology established by Bland [ ]. Within each bootstrap iteration, the composite score was used to compute the AUC, and the 95% confidence interval was defined using the empirical quantiles from the bootstrap-derived AUC distribution. As illustrated in Fig. F, the AUC distribution had a mean of 0.908 (95% CI: 0.814–0.979), demonstrating the model’s resistance to sampling variances and data perturbation.
Following this strategy, 5 top candidates with the highest composite score were selected for further analysis (Fig. G). Among them, KMT2A (lysine (K) methyltransferase 2 A) and CREBBP (cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) binding protein) were identified through strong docking performance, even though their sequence alignments were moderate. In contrast, the other three candidates (TCF7, vinculin, and CADH3) exhibit high alignment scores, demonstrating the power of combined sequence conservation and structural compatibility. Through cNLS Mapper [ , ] and LocNES [ ], we uncovered strong NLS or NES motifs in those candidates, indicative of their potential involvements in nuclear trafficking. One notable example is KMT2A, which encodes a H3K4 methyltransferase with strong nuclear localization signal [ ]. As KMT2A is predominantly located in the nucleus, we hypothesized that KMT2A may interact with β-catenin to promote its nuclear retention. Consistently, Deng et al.. reported that upregulation of KMT2A enhanced the nuclear concentration of β-catenin in gastric cancer cells [ ]. Similarly, CREBBP has been shown to interact with β-catenin to promote Wnt gene transcription [ ]. One possible mechanism involves CREBBP-mediated acetylation of β-catenin, which stabilizes the protein and enhances its nuclear level. Furthermore, TCF7, one candidate with a high sequence alignment score, was reported to interact with β-catenin [ ]. Collectively, these findings highlight the complementary roles of sequence-based and structure-based screening for reliable interactor prediction.
It is worth mentioning that some confirmed or candidate interactors lacked experimentally resolved structures in the PDB. In such cases, we can only utilize structures predicted by AlphaFold3. However, regions with low predicted local distance difference test score may reduce docking accuracy, as their structural conformations could deviate substantially from those present in the native environments [ ]. For example, despite strong structural alignment, HIF1A, ANDR, and the cadherin family members (CADH2, CADH5, CADH8) exhibit poor docking performance, possibly due to the absence of experimentally defined PDB structures. In line with this view, nuclear VE-cadherin was found to be associated with β-catenin and modulated its degradation [ ]. Therefore, candidates with AlphaFold-predicted structures warrant special consideration as structural inaccuracies may exclude legitimate binding partners. In summary, our integrated alignment- and docking-aided screening has identified some promising candidates, which necessitates further validation to evaluate their modulation of nuclear β-catenin.

## Genomic approaches to identify new modulators of nuclear β-Catenin
While our computational approaches have uncovered candidate interactors, integrative multiomic analysis also shows potential in delineating functional molecules associated with nuclear β-catenin. Using affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP-MS), Ewing and co-workers has successfully identified compartment-specific β-catenin binding partners, matching its distinct subcellular location [ ]. For example, DNA methyltransferase Dnmt1, along with its interaction partner UHRF1 and USP7, were enriched in the nucleus of cells expressing stabilized mutant β-catenin [ ]. This finding reinforces earlier reports in which Dnmt1 and β-catenin forms a mutual stabilization complex in cancer cell nuclei [ ]. However, those context-dependent interactors, especially structural or general transcriptional components, may not regulate nuclear β-catenin abundance [ ]. Hence, functional genomics is required to validate bona fide regulators that directly impact nuclear β-catenin level. Perturb-seq, which couples pooled CRISPR screening with single-cell RNA-seq, allows direct functional testing of dozens to hundreds of candidate regulators in parallel. Given the recent success of Perturb-seq in analyzing gene function in immune signaling [ ], its adaptation to the Wnt pathway should help resolve functional modulation at single-cell resolution.

## Nuclear β-catenin, a target for drugging the Wnt pathway

## A brief overview of Wnt-related therapies
The complexity of the Wnt cascade offers multiple levels of intervention. Over the past decades, numerous studies have identified biological agents modulating different components along the pathway, ranging from inhibiting Wnt secretion, impairing ligand-receptor binding, degrading cytosolic β-catenin, to destroying the β-catenin-TCF/LEF complex [ ]. Although a detailed discussion of those approaches is beyond the scope of this review, several key studies are listed below.
Wnt itself could be a target regulating the pathway activity. IWPs (inhibitors of WNT production), including IWP-1 and IWP-2, have been shown to block Wnt secretion by deactivating Porcupine, an enzyme facilitating the lipid modification necessary for Wnt release [ ]. By interacting with the Frizzled receptors, monoclonal antibody OMP-18R5 disrupts the ligand-receptor binding and the canonical Wnt signaling [ ]. Besides, the PDZ domain of DVL protein binds to Frizzled receptors, transducing the Wnt signal to downstream effectors. Through structure-based virtual ligand screening, small molecules like FJ9 [ ], 3289–8625 [ ], and NSC668036 [ ] were identified to disrupt the Frizzled–PDZ interaction in the micromolar range, thus down-regulating canonical Wnt activity. Targeting the β-catenin-TCF/LEF complex with small molecules enables direct modulation of Wnt targets, providing a means to circumvent upstream mutations. Lepourcelet et al. utilized high-throughput ELISA screening to uncover two fungal derivatives PKF115-584 and CGP049090, which antagonize β-catenin function by preventing its association with TCF factor [ ]. Similarly, AXIN-derived stapled peptides (StAx) competitively bind the TCF-docking site on β-catenin, thereby blocking pathway activity [ ].

## Targeting nuclear β-catenin for Wnt Inhibition
The ubiquitous nature and central effects of Wnt signaling pose substantial risks and concerns when perturbing such a fundamental pathway. Regardless of where disease-causing mutations occur, β-catenin must translocate into the nucleus to exert its transcriptional control. Moreover, nuclear β-catenin is frequently observed in disease states but not in normal, non-mutant cells. Therefore, nuclear β-catenin-based therapy may offer better selectivity, potentially reducing toxicity and unwanted pathway crosstalk. Here, we highlight reagents capable of suppressing β-catenin nuclear import/retention or enhancing its nuclear export/extranuclear localization and discuss their clinical potential.

## Preventing β-catenin nuclear import
To activate Wnt targets, β-catenin must first enter the nucleus. Compounds interfering with the interactions between β-catenin and its import chaperones should regulate nuclear β-catenin levels (Table ). For example, Robert Costa Memorial drug-1 (RCM-1), a potent FOXM1 inhibitor, inhibits nuclear β-catenin accumulation and tumor cell proliferation by suppressing the FOXM1–β-catenin interaction [ ]. As mentioned earlier, the yeast Kap104/mammalian TNPO1 facilitates nuclear import of β-catenin via a conserved PY-NLS domain in β-catenin [ ]. M9M, a TNPO1 peptide inhibitor, exhibits high-affinity binding to the TNPO1 NLS binding site, thereby competitively blocking its association with β-catenin PY-NLS motif [ ]. Small molecules selectively modulating the Nups and β-catenin interaction represents another appealing strategy [ ]. Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) bind directly to the NPC and inhibit the nuclear import of β-catenin [ ]. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells with constitutively active FMS-like tyrosine kinase-3 (FLT3) (e.g., FLT3- internal tandem duplication mutations), activated FLT3 phosphorylates β-catenin at tyrosine residues, and induces its nuclear localization [ ]. FLT3 inhibitor sorafenib effectively reduce β-catenin nuclear localization by preventing its phosphorylation [ ].
 

## Increasing extranuclear β-catenin to attenuate its nuclear activity
Redirecting β-catenin to extranuclear compartments represents another appealing approach inhibiting pathway activity (Table ). Several reagents, including plant flavonoid apigenin [ ], tetraspanin proteins CD82 [ ], vitamin D [ ], green tea component (-)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) [ ] and soy isoflavone genistein [ ], all upregulate E-cadherin expression and enhance β-catenin anchoring to the cell membrane. In cancer cells, glucose-induced acetylation of β-catenin drives its nuclear localization [ ], while SRT1720, a specific SIRT1 activator, counteracts this effect by promoting β-catenin cytoplasmic retention [ ]. Following Wnt3a stimulation, Pin1 retains β-catenin in the nucleus by blocking APC-mediated nuclear exclusion [ ]. Conversely, inhibiting Pin1 with Juglone and dipentamethylene thiuram monosulfide (DTM) triggers β-catenin nuclear export and suppresses Wnt3a-induced β-catenin signaling [ ]. Together, these findings point out the translational potential of targeting β-catenin’s extranuclear localization to mitigate its oncogenic activity.
 

## Promoting β-catenin degradation
Pharmacological interception of cytosolic β-catenin has been extensively explored for blocking Wnt hyperactivity (Table ). DIF-3, a morphogen essential for Dictyostelium stalk cell differentiation [ ], and 6-CEPN, a semisynthetic naringenin derivative [ ], both potentiate GSK-3β activity, thereby accelerating β-catenin degradation. Fingolimod (FTY720), an S1P receptor modulator, antagonizes S1P receptors to reverse Akt-mediated inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK3β. By restoring GSK3β activity, FTY720 enables β-catenin phosphorylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation [ ]. The Rac1-PAK1 axis, as described early, phosphorylates β-catenin at Ser675 and protects it from ubiquitination-mediated degradation [ ]. Pharmacological blockade of Rac1 using NSC23766 disrupts this phosphorylation cascade, thus reducing β-catenin nuclear levels [ ]. AXIN function in both β-catenin export pathway and the destruction complex. Tankyrase inhibitors (IWR and XAV939) stabilize AXIN protein and the destruction complex, therefore inhibiting β-catenin nuclear activity [ , ]. While these compounds primarily reduce the overall β-catenin levels by stabilizing the destruction complex, they also hold potential to reduce nuclear β-catenin by promoting its nuclear exit, which must be validated further.
 

## Emerging techniques for β-catenin-based therapy
Despite decades of research, no Wnt-targeted therapies are clinically available. A critical hurdle is the risk of side effects, arising from the pleiotropic roles of Wnt signaling and non-selective reagent dissemination. Fortunately, new technologies have emerged to overcome these limitations. Stimuli-responsive nanovector system enables targeted release triggered by internal (pH, oxygen, redox potential) or external (light) stimuli, which offers more precise and less toxic delivery mechanism [ ]. Ganesh and his colleagues developed EnCore lipid nanoparticles that showed enhanced targeting in cancer cells compared with normal adjacent tissues [ ]. Using this delivery platform, they achieved tumor-specific distribution of β-catenin Dicer-substrate siRNA (DsiRNA), effectively suppressing tumor growth in Wnt-dependent colon cancers (Fig. ) [ ].
 Recently, targeted protein degradation (TPD) has transformed drug discovery by selectively eliminating disease-causing proteins. Proteolysis-Targeting Chimera (PROTAC), bifunctional molecules comprising one ligand that binds to the protein of interest and another that recruits E3 ubiquitin ligase, has gained substantial attention for β-catenin-directed therapies [ , ]. Liao et al.. presented the first attempt to utilize PROTAC-mediated β-catenin degradation to treat Wnt-dependent cancers [ ]. The compound xStAx-VHLL couples xStAx, a peptide mimicking the β-catenin binding motif of AXIN, with a ligand for the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) E3 ubiquitin ligase. By directing β-catenin to proteasomal degradation, xStAx-VHLL potently inhibits the growth of CRC xenografts and APCmin/+-driven intestinal tumors (Fig. ) [ ].
Molecular glues are small molecules (< 500 Da) that facilitate or stabilize the natural interaction between two different proteins [ ]. Compared to traditional PROTACS, molecular glues offer advantages in size (smaller molecules), design simplicity (monofunctional structures), and mechanisms (mimicking endogenous protein-protein interactions), thereby reducing the risk of poor solubility while increasing the likelihood for oral bioavailability [ ]. Mutant β-catenin often evades β-TrCP binding, preventing its ubiquitylation and degradation [ ]. Simonetta and co-workers design NRX-252,114, a molecular glue that potentiates the mutant β-catenin:β-TrCP interaction, thus restoring ubiquitylation-dependent degradation of the mutated protein (Fig. ) [ ].

## Future perspective: can we safely target the “undruggable” Wnt pathway?
The extent of Wnt hyperactivation largely depends on the level of nuclear β-catenin. Despite the absence of canonical NLS and NES signals, β-catenin continuously shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm under different signaling states. Here, we reviewed the regulatory networks governing nuclear β-catenin at three hierarchical levels: (1) protein stability via degradation; (2) subcellular localization through nuclear transport; (3) compartment-specific retention. Key processes include nuclear-cytoplasmic trafficking, cytoplasmic and nuclear degradation of β-catenin, and its sequestration at the cell membrane or in the cytoplasm (limiting nuclear availability). However, our current knowledge of these regulatory circuits remains limited, future studies uncovering signaling hubs that govern nuclear β-catenin should offer potential targets for Wnt therapy.
At the core of nuclear β-catenin regulation is the dynamic interplays between β-catenin and its binding partners across various cell types and pathological conditions. For example, β-catenin plays roles in both cell adhesion and transcriptional activation. How is specificity achieved for each function? One proposed explanation is that fluctuations in the binding affinity and/or relative concentrations of β-catenin and its interactors vary in different states, which reshapes the competitive binding landscapes around β-catenin. Consistent with this view, low concentrations drive Drosophila β-catenin to adherens junctions, implying a stronger binding affinity for cadherin [ ]. Later, Gottardi et al.. demonstrated that Wnt signaling triggers a conformational change in cytoplasmic β-catenin for TCF binding, whereas the β-catenin-α-catenin dimer favors the assembly of adhesive complexes [ ]. Moreover, β-catenin phosphorylation at Tyr142 enhances its binding to BCL9-2, a paralog of human proto-oncogene BCL9, thereby precluding its association with α-catenin [ ]. Together, these findings underscore a context-dependent interaction selectivity of β-catenin.
Like most therapies, the primary obstacle of Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors is selective targeting: suppressing hyperactive Wnt in mutant cells while sparing normal Wnt in healthy tissues. Pathological accumulation of nuclear β-catenin in Wnt-driven diseases offers a unique opportunity to exploit disease-specific vulnerability. Furthermore, blocking nuclear β-catenin is ideal for most Wnt-mutated diseases as they often carry mutations in APC or other regulatory components upstream of nuclear β-catenin. As discussed earlier, molecules modulating nuclear β-catenin effectively suppress pathway activity, while technological improvements like EnCore nanodelivery and antibody-drug conjugates (ADC) provide more precise drug targeting. Thus, combining nuclear β-catenin inhibitors with advanced drug delivery platforms becomes a promising therapeutic approach. For instance, to overcome the low nuclease stability and poor membrane permeability of therapeutic peptides, Yang and co-workers loaded E-cadherin derived peptide (cECRV) into gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), which efficiently disrupt β-catenin-BCL9 interaction in cancer cells with acceptable toxicity and favorable biocompatibility [ ]. It is now widely accepted combination therapy outperforms monotherapeutic intervention. In agreement, administration of β-catenin/BCL9 peptide inhibitor with anti-PD-1 antibodies achieved robust tumor regression with improved immune response [ ].
Despite over four decades of extensive studies since the ground-breaking discovery of Wnt signaling, the regulatory machinery controlling nuclear β-catenin remains fragmented with existing data being confused if not contradictory. Given the pivotal roles of aberrant nuclear β-catenin in disease pathogenesis, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the complex interactome underlying nuclear β-catenin regulation. Although preclinical studies have shown promising efficacy both in vitro and in vivo, none of Wnt-based therapy has been approved. With growing knowledge of Wnt/β-catenin pathway and advances in drug delivery, we hold cautious optimism that combination therapies such as integrating nuclear β-catenin inhibitors with immune checkpoint modulators may achieve clinically meaningful responses in the near future.
