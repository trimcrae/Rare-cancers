It leverages the body’s immune system to specifically target and eliminate cancer cells, sparing normal cells and tissues. , This strategy has transformed the field of cancer treatment, , offering a beacon of hope for individuals battling various types of cancers.
One of the most remarkable breakthroughs in cancer immunotherapy has been the advent of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Immune checkpoints like Programmed cell death protein 1(PD-1), Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4)，play a crucial role in regulating immune responses and are often exploited by cancer cells to evade detection or attack by immune effector cells, such as T cells, NK cells and macrophages. Checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab have shown tremendous success across a variety of cancers, including melanoma, lung cancer, kidney cancer, and bladder cancer. These inhibitors work by blocking the checkpoint molecules, essentially reinvigorating the immune system’s ability to recognize and combat cancer cells. 
Another promising avenue is Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, which involves genetically modifying a patient’s own T cells to express CARs targeting specific cancer-associated antigens. CAR-T therapy has demonstrated efficacy, particularly in hematological malignancies, such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. , 
Concurrently, cancer vaccines have gained renewed interest combating solid tumors. These vaccines are designed to stimulate the cellular immune system, particularly T cells, to identify and attack tumor cells. The development of mRNA-based vaccines, exemplified by the success of COVID-19 vaccines, has driven innovation in cancer vaccine research. Personalized cancer vaccines, tailored to a patient’s unique tumor antigens known as neoantigens, are currently under investigation and have shown promising clinical outcome in early-stage trials. They hold the potential for highly targeted and effective cancer therapies. , 
Many factors affect the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. For tumor cells, tumor mutational burden (TMB), the number of genetic mutations, affect patients’ responses to ICBs. Tumours with higher TMB tend to produce more neoantigens and are more sensitive to ICBs. Epigenetic changes in the tumor microenvironment and microbiome of a patient all positively or negatively affect the efficacy of immunotherapy. 
The tumor microenvironment (TME) encompasses a complex network of cells, including immune cells (such as T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells), stromal cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, as well as extracellular matrix. , Tumor cells interact with these components, creating an environment that can either promote or inhibit immune responses. As cancer progresses, the TME tends to become more immunosuppressive, thereby impeding antitumour responses. Notably, exhausted T cells, regulatory T cells, and follicular helper T cells have been demonstrated to negatively impact the efficacy of PD-1 or CTlA-4 inhibition. Additionally, macrophages, hypoxia, and TGF-β signaling can hinder the effectiveness of ICBs. Factors such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and inhibitory cytokines in the TME can contribute to the exhaustion of CAR-T cells and limit their infiltration into tumors.

## Current endeavours to increase the efficacy of immunotherapy
Until now, only a fraction of patients have responded to ICBs. CAR-T therapy has yet to demonstrate effectiveness against solid tumors, while therapeutic vaccines have not been widely adopted in clinical practice. There is still significant room for progress in cancer immunotherapy.
Ideally, immunotherapy should furnish a sufficient quantity of high-quality effector cells, either through active immune responses or passive transfer methods (such as T cells, CAR-T, TILs, or T cell receptor engineered T cells (TCR-T)). These effector cells should have the capability to migrate to the tumor site. Additionally, the TME should not be hostile, enabling the effector cells to effectively target and eliminate the tumor cells. Therefore, when devising combination therapies, it is essential to make strategic choices that fulfill the aforementioned requirement. This includes the use of therapeutic vaccines or CAR-T therapies to provide effector cells, treatments that facilitate effector cells trafficking to the tumor, and most critically, interventions that improve the TME.

## Generating more numbers or providing more and better effector cells

## Therapeutic vaccines
One of the major problems facing therapeutic vaccines is their limited ability to elicit enough numbers of vaccine induced T cells. Genetic vaccine, including both DNA and mRNA vaccines may present a resolution. Compared with conventional vaccine utilizing protein or peptide of weak antigenicity, genetic vaccines themselves, have the advantage of strong antigenicity, stimulate innate immune system directly. The mRNA can be recognized by TLR7 and/or TLR8, while DNA from the backbone of the vaccine can be sensed by TLR9. 
Oncept is an innovative xenogeneic human tyrosinase plasmid DNA vaccine approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) for the treatment of canine malignant melanoma. As of now, the FDA has officially approved several mRNA vaccine technology products, which have achieved significant results in combating the COVID-19. These products include Pfizer-BioNTech’s Comirnaty vaccine and Moderna’s Spikevax vaccine, both of which have received full FDA approval for the prevention of COVID-19 in individuals aged 16 and older, the mRNA vaccines are superior than the conventional vaccines regarding the protection rate.
In the field of cancer vaccines, mRNA technology also shows a broad application prospect. For instance, Moderna’s mRNA-4157 is undergoing clinical trials for a variety of solid tumors and is currently in Phase I/II studies, exploring its safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy. BioNTech’s BNT111 vaccine, specifically targeting melanoma, is also in the early stages of clinical research, in Phase I/II as well. These mRNA vaccines leverage their unique advantages, such as rapid development, production flexibility, and the ability to elicit a strong immune response, providing innovative alternatives to traditional vaccines and treatment methods.
Neoantigens are precisely targeted, and provide a large number of personalized and tailored treatments, enabling cancer patients to achieve better remission. Therapeutic vaccines based on neoantigens mainly include peptide, nucleic acid, and dendritic cell (DC) vaccines. In a Phase I immunotherapy clinical trial for patients with disseminated synovial sarcoma, a vaccine based on the SYT-SSX neoantigen peptide successfully induced specific CTL responses in four patients, and no serious adverse reactions were observed throughout the treatment process. In addition, immunostimulatory adjuvants and multimeric formulation techniques are being developed to enhance the immunogenicity of personalized peptide vaccines. For example, a vaccine containing the poly-ICLC adjuvant and synthetic long neoantigen peptides effectively activated CD8 T cells and CD4 lymphocytes in patients with advanced melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), or bladder cancer. Currently, in vitro transcription (IVT) is the main method used to create mRNA containing neoantigen sequences. After IVT, a cap structure is added to each mRNA to increase its stability and reduce its immunogenicity. Multiple clinical trials are investigating the efficacy and safety of neoantigen DC vaccines in solid tumors such as melanoma, colorectal cancer, esophageal cancer, and gastric cancer. New antigen cancer vaccines (NCVs) identify and target specific tumor-specific mutations in cervical cancer and melanoma, stimulating the patient’s own immune system, especially CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, while reducing damage to normal tissues. These vaccines have shown safety in clinical trials and have induced tumor-specific immune responses in some patients, providing new possibilities for cancer treatment. 
Besides，blockade of IL-10 signaling at the time of therapeutic vaccination is able to increase vaccine-induced CTL responses, and better prevent tumor growth (our review in this journal). ISA 101 is a long-peptide HPV16 vaccine that can prolong the survival of HPV16-positive cancer patients in combination with anti-PD-1 antibodies. 

## CAR-T
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, as a genetically engineered immunotherapy, have made breakthroughs in the treatment of hematologic malignancies, among which two CAR-T cell products targeting CD19, Kymriah and Yescarta, have been approved by the US FDA for the treatment of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), respectively. , However, CAR-T cell therapy faces some significant challenges in solid tumors, including limited durability, poor transportation and tumor infiltration, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which limit its efficacy in the treatment of solid tumors, so CAR-T cells need to be engineered to improve their efficacy. 
In recent modification strategies, the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy has been enhanced through various approaches, such as overexpressing Bcl-2 family proteins to prolong CAR-T cell lifespan and improve treatment efficacy for lymphomas and leukemias. By overexpressing CXCR6, CAR-T cells have been enabled to overcome the challenge of infiltrating tumors, leading to improved tumor invasion and control, as well as enhanced antigen-specific recognition of pancreatic cancer cells, which in turn has increased the effectiveness of pancreatic cancer treatment. Transgenic IL-10-expressing CAR-T cells have shown remarkable efficacy in multiple tumor models, effectively resolving T cell dysfunction within the tumor microenvironment and achieving long-term clearance of solid tumors and metastases. Furthermore, CAR-NK cells armed with interleukin-15(IL-15) have significantly increased their in vivo expansion and persistence, markedly extending survival in the Raji lymphoma mouse model and demonstrating potent antitumor capabilities against B-cell malignancies. Additionally, the combination of dendritic cell (DC) vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has proven to be an effective therapeutic strategy, with preliminary studies indicating that this combined approach can reduce tumor volume and improve overall survival rates in hepatocellular carcinoma. Recently, CAR macrophages, targeting HER2-expressing solid tumors, have emerged as a novel alternative therapy, with ongoing clinical trials showing promising antitumor activity. These advancements underscore the ongoing innovation within the field of CAR-T cell therapy, aimed at overcoming current limitations and enhancing patient outcomes in cancer treatment. 
In summary, although CAR-T cell therapy has achieved remarkable results in hematological malignancies, further innovation and improvement are still needed in the treatment of solid tumors to overcome existing challenges and improve therapeutic effects.

## Improving the tumour suppressive microenvironment

## More ICBs and combination therapy
The response rates to monotherapy, such as ICBs, therapeutic vaccine, or CAR-T, are notably low. CAR-T therapy, when applied to solid tumors, encounters issues related to limited trafficking and penetration into the tumor, as well as facing the hostile tumor microenvironment. Similarly, cancer therapeutic vaccines face similar challenges, including low T cell responses, limited trafficking to tumor site, and the presence of a hostile TME.
Numerous efforts have been undertaken to enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy, aiming to overcome resistance to ICBs and achieve more complete responses. , Combinations of CD3 or PD-1 with other immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been widely explored and have shown promising results across various cancer types, as they target distinct immune regulatory pathways. Furthermore, combining ICBs with targeted therapies, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, cancer vaccines, and adoptive cell therapies has been investigated and has yielded improved outcomes in experimental animal models and clinical settings. 

## Biomarker selection
While these therapies have significant potential, effective combination strategies often require patient stratification. Biomarkers that can predict responses to specific combinations are crucial for developing personalized treatment approaches.
In breast cancer, one of the most intuitive and fundamental biomarker is PD-L1 expression. Initially identified as a predictor of treatment response to ICBs in non-small cell lung cancer, PD-L1 expression has been shown to have a significant impact on response rates in breast cancer, as demonstrated by the PANACEA (IBCSG 45–13/BIG 4–13/KEYNOTE-014) trial. Another predictive marker is the presence of TILs, which indicates the level of immune system interaction within the TME and can influence responses to ICI therapy. Cold tumors, characterized by a relatively low immune system interaction and immune cell infiltration within the TME, tend to be less responsive to immunotherapy. Overcoming the immunosuppressive nature of cold tumors remains a significant challenge in cancer treatment.

## Oncolytic viruses and bacteria
Oncolytic viruses (OVs) and bacteria are gaining attention as a novel immunotherapeutic due to their ability to induce antitumor immunity through multiple mechanisms. They can exploit the host immune system, cause direct lysis of tumor cells, and inflame cold tumors to make them more immunologically favorable for other treatments. Clinical trials have reported that OVs generally have a low-grade safety profile, with adverse events being mostly constitutional and local injection site reactions. Objective responses were reported in 9% of patients, and disease control was achieved in 21.1% of patients in a clinical trial.
The adenovirus is the most common OV used in clinical trials, with studies targeting melanoma and gastrointestinal cancers. Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) showed better objective response rates (ORRs) compared to systemic treatments alone. T-VEC is a modified herpes simplex virus (HSV) that infects tumor cells and promotes their destruction that was approved by FDA for subsets of patients with melanoma. Salmonella, Clostridium, Listeria are commonly used as oncolytic bacterial strains; SGN1, a first-in-class salmonella oncolytic bacteria with broad-spectrum antitumor activity, currently in clinical trials (NCT05038150, NCT05103345). However, safety concerns, side Effects: which led to the abandonment of some therapies in the past have been observed. delivery and compliance is another concerns of these types of therapy. The field of oncolytic viruses and bacteria therapy is still developing, and while there are challenges, the potential benefits in cancer treatment are significant.

## Caerin 1.1/1.9 increase the efficacy of immunotherapy

## Caerin 1.1/1.9 drastically improve the efficacy of ICBs and therapeutic vaccines
Caerin 1 peptides are a member of the antimicrobial peptide family. Caerin 1 is derived from the skin secretions of Australian tree frogs, notably Litoria species. It is characterized by a conserved amphipathic α-helical structure, which is pivotal for its antimicrobial function. The amino acid sequences of Cearin1.1 and Cearin1.9 are as follows: Cearin1.1 is ‘GLLSVLGSVAKHVLPHVLPHVVPVIAEHL-NH2’ and Cearin1.9 is ‘GLFGVLGSIAKHVLPHVVPVIAEKL-NH2.’ The chemical structures are depicted in . The hydrophobic regions of the peptide insert into the lipid bilayer, disrupting membrane integrity and leading to cell lysis. Additionally, caerin 1 has been suggested to permeabilize membranes by forming pores, which contributes to its bactericidal or anti-tumor activity. 
 
Caerin 1 peptides, specially caerin 1.1 and 1.9, have demonstrated anticancer potential by inducing apoptosis in cancer cell types. They inhibit the growth of several human and murine cancer cell lines, including cervical cancer (HeLa cells), thyroid cancer (B-CPAP and CAL-62 cells), breast cancer (MCF-7 and Skbr-3 cells), esophageal cancer (TE-1 cells), human lung cancer (A549), and glioma (U87 and U118 cells) (unpublished data), as well as murine TC-1, B16 and 4T1 cells, with IC50 range from 5 to 13 μg/ml. Caerin 1.1 and Caerin 1.9 inhibit the HIV infected T cells while having no toxic side effects on normal T cells. Caerin 1.1 and 1.9 has synergetic effects against tumor cell growth. 
In vivo studies using xenograft tumor models have shown that caerin 1.1/1.9 inhibit the growth of human thyroid, lung, and esophageal tumor cells and murine TC-1, 4T1 and B16 (submitted and unpublished data) cells.
In a TC-1 tumor model, these peptides have demonstrated synergistic effects with PD-1 antibodies and enhanced the efficacy of therapeutic vaccine, leading to prolonged survival of TC-1 tumor-bearing mice. Notably, the combination of caerin1.1 and 1.9 (caerin 1.1/1.9) drastically improve the efficacy of PD-1 antibodies and therapeutic vaccine containing interleukin 10 inhibitor, resulting in reduced tumor weight and extended survival in tumor-bearing mice. In this model, approximately 30% of TC-1 tumor-bearing mice completely eradicated TC-1 tumors, and all mice were able to clear a second TC-1 tumor challenge. In contrast, the death rate of the TC-1 tumor-bearing mice receiving PD-1 antibody and therapeutic vaccine treatment was 100%, although survived longer than untreated mice. In a melanoma B16 tumor model, similar effects were observed (submitted), suggesting that the enhancement of the antitumor effects of PD-1 and therapeutic vaccine by caerin1.1/1.9 is not limited to one tumor model.

## The possible mechanisms of caerin 1.1/1.9
The unexpected findings that caerin1.1/1.9 drastically enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 and therapeutic vaccines in the TC-1 and B16 tumor model are exciting. F1/F3 activates the IFN-α, Stat1 signaling pathway of tumor macrophages in TC-1 tumor-bearing mice treated with PD-1 blockade and therapeutic vaccination. It also modulates the heterogeneity of tumor-associated macrophages, significantly reducing M2-type macrophages, increasing M1-type macrophages, and promoting the secretion of IL-12 by tumor-infiltrating macrophages. This suggests that these peptides have the potential to improve the outcomes of cancer immunotherapy. Understanding the mechanisms behind the actions of caerin 1.1/1.9 is of paramount importance. However, the target of this peptide has not yet been determined. The direct tumor cytotoxic abilities of caerin1.1/1.9 lay the foundation for the subsequent remodeling of the TME. These peptides appear to have a multifaceted impact of anti-tumor responses, making them intriguing candidates for further research and development in the field of cancer immunotherapy.

## Immune cell tumour infiltration
Following intratumoral injection or topical application of caerin 1.1/1.9 in a temperature sensitive gel form, the number of CD45+ cells increased significantly within the tumor, in contrast to control peptide or untreated mice. , Furthermore, these peptides attracted more T, NK cells into the TME. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that caerin 1.1/1.9 are associated with the expansion of populations exhibiting high immune activation levels and are involved in stimulating pro-inflammatory activity of NK, macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs) that are closely associated with INFα response. , 
Cebpb seems to play a key role in altering the function of all Arg1hi macrophages in the mice treated with caerin peptides. Notably, caerin 1.1/1.9 were found to recruit almost two-fold more activated CD8+ T cells to the tumor microenvironment compared to untreated tumors. These findings underscore the potential of caerin peptides in modulating the tumor immune landscape and enhancing the presence of immune cells with anti-tumor activity.

## Activate and repolarise tumour associated macrophages
Caerin 1.1/1.9-treated mice receiving anti-PD-1 and therapeutic vaccine, a noteworthy shift in the tumor-infiltrating macrophage landscapes was observed. Specifically, there were more inflammatory tumor-infiltrating macrophages and fewer tumor-resident macrophages in the TME. Both subpopulations exhibited an altered cytokine profile, secreting more IL-12 and less IL-10 compared to mice receiving anti-PD-1 and therapeutic vaccine along. These observations strongly suggest that caerin 1.1/1.9 induce the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages from an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype to an M1-like pro-inflammatory state. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA) and proteomic analysis revealing a significant reduction in M2 macrophages. Moreover, there was substantial activation of the IFNα and Stat1 signaling pathways in macrophages. This was accompanied by a downregulation of transcription factors associated with M2 macrophages, such as Arg1, Mmp13, Pf4 and Hmox1, which are linked to tumor angiogenesis and invasiveness. In addition, the expression of IFNα receptor in macrophages increased by Western Blot, and macrophages secreted more IFNα after caerin 1.1/1.9 treatment (submitted). Notably, the success of caerin1.1/1.9-mediated anti-tumor responses with anti-PD-1 and therapeutic vaccine depends on the presence of macrophage, depletion of macrophages resulted in the loss of the anti-tumor response triggered by caerin 1.1/1.9, even in the presence of anti-PD-1 treatment and therapeutic vaccine. The role of macrophages by caerin1.1/1.9 mediated antitumor response was observed in B16 tumor-bearing mice (submitted).

## Tumour microenvironment remodeling
Caerin 1.1/1.9’s capacity to influence macrophage polarization has far-reaching implications for the tumor-associated inflammation and immune evasion. These peptides led to a substantial expansion of macrophage and NK cell populations with heightened immune activation. Antigen-presenting cells, including conventional DCs (cDCs) and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), experienced activation. Furthermore, the treatment induced an increase in the population of activated CD8+ T cells, particularly within the memory and effector-memory CD8+ T cell subsets. The integration of proteomic and single-cell transcriptomic data through computational analysis further supported these observations. This integration revealed the activation of pathways regulated by Stat1, leading to apoptosis and a significant reduction in the immune-suppressive functions of B cells following caerin 1.1/1.9 treatment. As a result, the TME underwent a significant transformation toward a pro-inflammatory state after the administration of these peptides.

## A proposed mode of action of caerin 1.1/1.9
The exact mechanisms how caerin 1.1/1.9 remodel the TME are not yet fully understood, but several factors may be involved. One possible mechanism is that caerin 1 peptides induce apoptosis in tumor cells, leading to the release of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, which could promote the differentiation and activation of tumor-associated macrophages. These activated macrophages, in turn, contribute to the pro-inflammatory environment within the TME.
Another possible mechanism may be resulted from caerin 1.1 and 1.9 induce the secretion of IL-18, a typical characteristic cytokine of cell pyroptosis. Caerin 1 peptides have been shown to form pores on the membrane of bacteria or cancer cells, Gasdermin (GSDM) is well documented to form pores on cell membrane during pyroptosis. Reduced Gasdermin E (GSDME) function has been observed in most tested cancer-associated mutations. GSDM ectopic expression inhibits tumor growth. GSDME expression enhances tumor-associated macrophage phagocytosis and the number and functions of tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T lymphocytes. 
It can be speculated that caerin 1.1/1.9 enhances the efficacy of PD-1 antibodies and therapeutic vaccine by initially inducing pyroptosis and apoptosis in tumor cells. This, in turn, leads to the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and IL-18. These cytokines activate and polarize tumor macrophages from M2 subtype to M1 subtype. These M1 macrophages secrete more IL-12 and TNFα while producing less IL-10, amplifying the pro-inflammatory cascade within the TME. This cascades subsequently activate NK cells, T cells, and DCs, finally resulted in tumor clearance by T cells. ICBs, such as PD-1 blockers, further enhance tumor clearance by blocking immune checkpoints like PD-1 ( ).
 

## Future direction
Unlike ICBs that only have one or two targets, caerin 1.1/1.9 modifies multiple suppressive factors in the TME. Furthermore, caerin1.1/1.9 can be repeatedly administered and have the advantage compared with oncolytic viruses and bacteria, as live viruses and bacteria will ultimately elicit immune responses against themselves after repeated usage, resulting in the reduction or disappearance of their antitumour ability. The remarkable enhancement of the efficacy of ICBs and therapeutic vaccine-based immunotherapy by caerin 1.1/1.9 underscores the importance of gaining a deeper understanding of its mechanisms. It is critical to identify the specific target molecule(s) responsible for initiating pyroptosis or apoptosis in tumor cells , and to determine whether IL-18 is the principal cytokine driving the activation of tumor macrophages and their transition into the M1 subtype. Additionally, while caerin 1.1/1.9 demonstrates stability, its relatively short half-life, which approximately 1.5 h in SD rats, and susceptibility to proteases, particularly in serum, pose challenges due to suboptimal pharmacokinetics. It is vital to explore strategies for optimizing its pharmacokinetic properties through chemical engineering, which could potentially improve its therapeutic efficacy.
In summary, the key to enhancing immunotherapy efficacy lies in disrupting the immunosuppressive TME to attract a sufficient number of high-quality effector cells capable of effectively eradicating tumor cells. As a promising alternative approach, caerin 1 peptides stand out as a compelling avenue worthy of more extensive exploration and development.
