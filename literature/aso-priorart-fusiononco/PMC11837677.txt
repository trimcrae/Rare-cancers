In this review, we focus on a class of conserved proteins known as the Muscleblind-like (MBNL) family. As RNA-binding proteins, MBNL family members interact with pre-mRNAs through evolutionarily conserved tandem zinc finger domains and play critical roles in various RNA metabolic processes, including alternative splicing, mRNA stability, trafficking, regulation of subcellular localization, and alternative polyadenylation. Dysregulation of MBNL proteins can lead to severe consequences. Initially, research primarily associated MBNL proteins with myotonic dystrophy. However, recent studies have revealed their involvement in a broad spectrum of physiological and pathological processes, such as embryonic tissue differentiation and circulatory disorders. Furthermore, the emerging role of MBNL proteins in cancer sheds light on a novel aspect of these evolutionarily ancient proteins. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the MBNL family, emphasizing its structure, the mechanisms underlying its biological functions, and its roles in various diseases.

## Introduction
The Muscleblind-like (MBNL) protein family consists of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) that play essential roles in intracellular RNA processing and regulation. In humans, the MBNL family includes three members: MBNL1, MBNL2, and MBNL3. These proteins recognize and bind specific RNA sequence elements via two pairs of tandem zinc finger domains, each comprising three cysteine residues and one histidine residue (CCCH) [ , ]. MBNL proteins are essential regulators of RNA metabolism, participating in processes such as alternative splicing (AS), mRNA trafficking, modification, and translation [ , ]. Additionally, the MBNL family regulates key biological processes, including subcellular localization, mRNA stability, and alternative polyadenylation, highlighting their versatility in the regulation of intracellular gene expression [ , – ]. While structurally conserved, the three MBNL family members exhibit significant differences in tissue and organ distribution. MBNL1 and MBNL2 are generally expressed in various tissues, including the brain, heart, and muscle, whereas MBNL3 is predominantly expressed in the placenta [ , ]. Among the three, MBNL1 is the most abundant in human tissues, with the exception of the brain. Notably, MBNL1 and MBNL2 can partially compensate for each other’s functions [ ]. For instance, when MBNL1 function is impaired, MBNL2 expression is upregulated to partially mitigate the loss of function [ ]. This functional redundancy highlights the regulatory complexity of the MBNL family, suggesting that changes in the expression of individual MBNL proteins may not lead to drastic phenotypic effects.
As a class of evolutionarily conserved proteins, MBNL proteins are present in both protostomes, such as nematodes, and deuterostomes, but are absent in bacteria, fungi, and plants [ , ]. This evolutionary conservation underscores their essential roles, which have been preserved through natural selection. Additionally, the high sequence and structural similarity within the family supports functional interchangeability across species [ ]. For instance, the genomic structure of the MBNL gene is identical between fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) and humans, and the MBNL proteins of both species can functionally substitute for one another in vivo [ ]. Even in distantly related species, such as Ciona intestinalis and Trichoplax adhaerens, MBNL homologs regulate alternative splicing in mouse cells [ ]. These conserved features make MBNL proteins attractive targets for drug development, as therapies targeting these proteins may have broad applicability while minimizing side effects.
The pathological roles of MBNL proteins are particularly evident in diseases such as myotonic dystrophy (DM), where the loss of MBNL function results in misregulated RNA processing [ ]. Recent advances in therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring MBNL function have shown significant promise. These include RNA-targeting antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small molecules that disrupt pathological RNA-protein interactions, and gene therapy approaches designed to enhance MBNL protein expression [ , ]. These strategies not only emphasize the critical role of MBNL proteins in maintaining cellular homeostasis but also highlight their therapeutic potential, making them a compelling focus of ongoing research.
In addition to their involvement in DM and other RNA misregulation disorders, recent studies have uncovered an emerging role for MBNL family members in cancer. Aberrant MBNL expression has been implicated in tumorigenesis and cancer progression through the regulation of alternative splicing and mRNA stability [ , ]. Conversely, overexpression of MBNL proteins has demonstrated tumor-suppressive effects in experimental models, underscoring their potential as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets [ ]. Moreover, the conserved nature of MBNL family members makes them attractive candidates for drug development, as therapies targeting MBNL function could have broad applicability across various cancers with minimal off-target effects. As research into the roles of MBNL proteins in cancer expands, these findings pave the way for innovative cancer therapies, marking an exciting frontier in MBNL-related research.
In this review, we will first provide an overview of the structure and biological roles of MBNL family members, with a particular focus on the structural basis of MBNL-RNA interactions. Next, we will discuss the functional roles of MBNL proteins and their associated mechanisms. We will then examine the impact of MBNL dysfunction in various diseases, with an emphasis on myotonic dystrophy. Finally, we will explore the potential translation of these findings into innovative therapeutic approaches, highlighting the importance of restoring MBNL protein function and their emerging role in disease treatment.

## Structure of human MBNL family proteins

## The same zinc finger domains determine the main function of the MBNL protein
MBNL family members all contain four zinc finger (ZnF1-4) domains, which enable them to bind specifically to RNA molecules. Each zinc finger domain typically contains a zinc ion that forms a stable structure by binding to amino acid residues in the protein, thus providing a platform for interaction with RNA. The MBNL family proteins all present the CCCH phenotype, meaning they include three cysteines and one histidine [ ], and can specifically recognize RNA motifs composed of the GpC dinucleotide and the pyrimidine YGCY (where Y is either U or C) [ , ]. Additionally, the structure of the four zinc fingers displays two distinct sequence patterns in a two-by-two tandem arrangement. Starting from the N-terminus, the four ZnF domains are named 1–4, with ZnF1 and ZnF3 containing the sequence pattern CX7CX6CX3H, and ZnF2 and ZnF4 containing the pattern CX7CX4CX3H (where X is any amino acid) [ ]. The spacing between these amino acid residues is highly conserved, ensuring structural stability and consistent RNA-binding properties across different family members. In terms of relative positions, the tandem ZnF1 and ZnF2 (TZF1/2) are located at the N-terminus of the protein, whereas ZnF3 and ZnF4 (TZF3/4) are positioned in the central region of the protein sequence. This spatial organization forms a tandem zinc finger (TZF) structure, which plays a critical role in the specific recognition and binding of RNA motifs. While the tandem zinc finger arrangement is a known structural feature in RNA-binding proteins, the specific TZF structure in MBNL proteins displays unique sequence and spatial properties. These distinct features suggest that the MBNL TZF structure may serve as a specialized RNA-recognition motif, which warrants further investigation [ ]. However, the binding affinities of TZFs to target RNAs differ. For instance, in MBNL1, mutants with alanine substitutions of key amino acids in the RNA-binding region of TZF1/2 exhibit lower RNA-binding affinities than those with corresponding substitutions in TZF3/4 [ , ]. This change underscores the impact of key amino acid substitutions on the motif, but larger studies are required to determine if this is a generalizable phenomenon.
The linker sequence between TZFs is also critical for the MBNL family’s ability to recognize and bind to RNA. It has been shown that deletion of this sequence severely reduces the binding affinity of MBNL1 [ ]. It is logical to speculate that this may be because the linker supports protein flexibility. This phenomenon, where secondary structures severely affect the phenotype, further reflects the conserved nature of the MBNL family.

 

## Structural features highlighting the conserved nature of MBNL family proteins
With the exception of the zinc finger domain, all members of the MBNL protein family exhibit partial structural similarities that underscore their evolutionary conservation. For example, the linkage lengths between ZnF1-ZnF2 and ZnF3-ZnF4 are identical, with 14 residues for ZnF1-ZnF2 and 16 residues for ZnF3-ZnF4. Additionally, the conservation among family members is evident in the extensions of the zinc finger structure. All N-terminal extensions of ZnF1 include the highly conserved sequence WLXLEV before the first Cys ligand residue of ZnF1. Another conserved motif consists of an NGR core sequence followed by either valine or asparagine, flanked by two highly conserved regions. This motif can be summarized as the consensus sequence V/IX0-2NGRV/NXA/L, where X represents any residue.
Aromatic amino acid residues with aromatic ring structures often play key roles in protein stability and function. Within the LEV motif of the MBNL family, one tryptophan and four phenylalanine or tyrosine aromatic residues are consistently retained in the same positions. The benzene ring side chains of these aromatic residues stabilize the internal structure of the protein through π-π stacking interactions [ ]. These non-covalent interactions are crucial in molecular recognition processes, such as enzyme-substrate binding and drug-receptor interactions [ , ], and we hypothesize that they may similarly enhance RNA recognition by MBNL proteins.
Certain regions in MBNL proteins are enriched in specific types of amino acids, contributing to their functional specialization. These low-complexity regions are conserved to varying degrees among MBNL family members, particularly between the two pairs of zinc finger domains. In human MBNL1, the region between the zinc finger pairs contains an alanine-rich sequence. Alanine, a nonpolar amino acid with a very small side chain, enhances the alpha-helical structure of proteins, thereby improving protein stability [ ]. In contrast, MBNL2 and MBNL3 exhibit proline-rich regions between the tandem zinc fingers. These proline-rich domains are much longer than their alanine-rich counterparts, often exceeding the length of the zinc finger domains. Proline, with its unique heterocyclic structure, facilitates the formation of stable protein conformations, particularly when structural rigidity is required [ ].

## Molecular mechanisms of MBNL family involvement in pathological/physiological activities
MBNL family proteins play important roles in a variety of pathological and physiological activities through a variety of molecular mechanisms. Post-transcriptional, dynamic interactions of MBNL proteins with pre-mRNAs or mRNAs in the nucleus and cytoplasm regulate their alternative splicing, polyadenylation, trafficking, subcellular localization, and stability/degradation. ## MBNLs regulate alternative splicing and are also regulated by alternative splicing
AS is a crucial gene expression regulatory mechanism that allows a single gene to produce various mature mRNA transcripts, encoding diverse protein isoforms by selectively including or excluding specific pre-mRNA exons during the splicing process. AS is a prevalent mechanism in mammalian cells, with over 90% of human genes estimated to undergo AS [ – ]. AS regulation is influenced by complex intra- and extracellular signaling pathways, including the synergistic effects of transcription factors, splicing factors, RNA-binding proteins, and other elements [ ]. Additionally, AS tends to alter the phosphorylation capacity of proteins, thereby modulating kinase signaling pathways [ ]. Therefore, AS plays a pivotal role in disease development. For instance, in tumorigenesis studies, AS can have diverse or even opposite roles in tumor behaviors such as proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, drug resistance, and metastasis [ , – ].
As an important class of tissue-specific AS regulators, the MBNL family of proteins play a key role in regulating AS processes. They modulate hundreds of alternative splicing events leading to the expression of adult-specific mRNA isoforms. The location and number of MBNL recognition sites play a critical role in determining the direction of AS splicing activity, but the underlying mechanisms involve multiple factors. Experimental studies and RNA structural analyses suggest that the position of MBNL binding sites relative to the regulated exon significantly influences exon inclusion or exclusion. For example, binding of MBNL proteins to intronic regions upstream of an exon often leads to exon skipping, whereas binding downstream typically promotes exon inclusion [ , ]. This positional effect is thought to arise from steric hindrance or facilitation of spliceosome assembly. Additionally, the number of binding sites also correlates with splicing efficiency: a higher density of MBNL motifs in the downstream intron enhances exon inclusion, likely due to cooperative interactions between MBNL molecules [ , ].
The three members of the MBNL family—MBNL1, MBNL2, and MBNL3—exhibit both overlapping and unique functions in regulating RNA splicing. Genome-wide analyses have shown that MBNL1 predominantly regulates AS in skeletal muscle and heart tissues [ , ], whereas MBNL2 plays a more significant role in the central nervous system, particularly during neural development [ , ]. MBNL3, in contrast, is highly expressed in the placenta and embryonic tissues, where it modulates AS events associated with early development [ ]. During development, MBNL1 transitions from fetal to adult splicing patterns, particularly in muscle tissues, whereas MBNL2 expression peaks during neural development and declines in adulthood. MBNL3, in contrast, is predominantly expressed in embryonic and placental tissues, with minimal expression in adult stages [ ].
Although all three MBNL members recognize common RNA motifs, such as the 5′-YGCY-3′ sequence, they exhibit differences in binding affinity and target specificity. For instance, MBNL1 preferentially binds transcripts involved in muscle-specific functions, such as the stress fiber-associated protein LIMCH1 [ ], whereas MBNL2 targets RNAs essential for synaptic function and neurogenesis [ ]. MBNL3, on the other hand, has been implicated in the regulation of RNAs that control embryonic development and placental growth [ ]. Studies using CLIP-seq and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) have further revealed distinct sets of RNA targets for each paralog [ , ], although significant overlap exists among their binding sites. The differential roles of MBNL paralogs in tissue-specific regulation contribute to distinct disease phenotypes. For example, MBNL1 dysfunction is closely associated with skeletal muscle and cardiac symptoms in DM, while MBNL2 is implicated in CNS-related manifestations such as cognitive impairment [ , ]. MBNL3’s role in early development may explain the severity of congenital DM [ ]. These differences suggest that MBNL1 is a key therapeutic target in adult DM, whereas MBNL2 and MBNL3 may provide additional insights into CNS and developmental abnormalities. Furthermore, in DM 1, when MBNL1, MBNL2 and MBNL3 are co-expressed in the same cells, they bind the same RNA motifs with different affinities. MBNL1 has the highest splicing activity, while MBNL3 has the lowest splicing activity. In forming RNA foci, MBNL1 is the most mobile paralogous relative, whereas MBNL3 is static and has the highest density on CUGexpRNA. MBNL paralogs and gene-specific isoforms have inherent functional differences, and this result could be used to improve therapeutic strategies for DM 1 [ ].
The MBNL family itself is also affected by AS and has the potential to lead to serious consequences. MBNL1, for example, contains 12 exons, six of which (exons 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9) undergo AS, leading to the expression of at least seven MBNL1 mRNA isoforms [ , , ]. These isoforms encode proteins with distinct intracellular localization and functions [ , ]. For example, the inclusion of exon 5 introduces a nuclear localization signal (NLS), enabling MBNL1 to translocate into the nucleus, while the exclusion of exon 5 results in cytoplasmic localization. Targeted exon 5 repair using blockmiRs (microRNAs blocking splicing sites) significantly alters MBNL1 localization, linking AS regulation with subcellular localization [ ].
In Caenorhabditis elegans, the mbl-1 gene generates two major protein isoforms by an alternative splicing mechanism: a long isoform that contains a specific exon (e.g., exon 7) and a short isoform that does not contain this exon [ ]. The long isoform contains a double KR motif (double lysine-arginine motif) that forms a NLS, allowing the protein to localize in the nucleus. The short isoform, lacking this nuclear localization signal, is primarily localized in the cytoplasm. MBNL proteins can bind their own mRNAs and inhibit the inclusion of certain exons. For example, when MBNL protein levels are elevated, they inhibit the inclusion of exon 7 in their own mRNA, decreasing the production of the long isoform and increasing the production of the short isoform. This negative feedback regulatory mechanism helps to maintain appropriate levels of MBNL protein in cells, preventing its overexpression or underexpression [ ].
Similarly, MBNL2 and MBNL3 are also regulated by alternative splicing, resulting in the production of distinct isoforms that perform specific biological functions depending on the tissue and developmental stage. MBNL2 undergoes alternative splicing in critical exons, such as exon 6 and exon 9, which significantly influence its intracellular localization and stability. For example: Inclusion of exon 6 introduces a NLS, allowing the MBNL2 protein to localize in the nucleus where it regulates pre-mRNA splicing events Inclusion of exon 9 removes a PEST degradation domain, leading to increased protein stability and prolonged half-life. These alternative splicing events are particularly prominent in skeletal muscle, heart, and brain tissues and are believed to serve as a compensatory mechanism during MBNL1 deficiency [ ]. This highlights the adaptive role of MBNL2 isoforms in maintaining RNA metabolism under stress or disease conditions. MBNL3, on the other hand, is known for its involvement in tissue-specific alternative splicing regulation, particularly during embryonic development and placental growth. For instance, in vertebrate species, a truncated MBNL3 protein isoform can also be generated from alternatively spliced transcripts that initiate from the ancestral transcription start sites but skip exon 2, which encodes the canonical start codon [ ]. These self-regulatory mechanisms, governed by alternative splicing, are essential for maintaining normal RNA metabolism and preventing aberrant expression, which could lead to pathological consequences.
AS and isoform expression of the MBNL family are also regulated specifically by tissue and developmental stage. At different tissues and developmental stages, the ratio of long and short isoforms of MBNL proteins changes to accommodate different physiological needs. For example, during the development of the nervous system, the transition from the long isoform to the short isoform is critical for normal neural development and function [ ]. Through this complex self-regulatory mechanism, the MBNL gene can accurately regulate the expression and function of its own proteins to ensure normal intracellular RNA metabolism. In humans, MBNL isoforms containing partial exons enhance the sequestration of MBNLs in the nuclei of cells from patients with DM, worsening the phenotypic severity of the disease [ ]. MBNLs regulate AS in two ways, acting as both splicing activators and splicing repressors. The position and number of MBNL recognition sites play a key role in determining the direction of AS splicing activity, while being modulated by RNA secondary structures and interactions with other splicing factors. For instance, MBNLs binding to the alternative exon or the upstream intron promotes skipping of alternative cassette exons (CE), whereas binding to the CE 3′-end or the downstream intron facilitates exon inclusion [ , ]. Evidence suggests that the efficiency of alternative exon exclusion or inclusion is influenced by the binding affinity, functional activity, and relative abundance of MBNL paralogs [ , ]. The outcome of AS is determined by a complex network of interactions involving the core components of the splicing machinery, such as small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs), along with cis-acting splicing elements—including the 5′ and 3′ splice sites (ss), branch point, polypyrimidine tract—and various RNA regulatory sequences and RBPs [ ]. MBNL proteins tend to bind specific RNA secondary structures, such as specific sequences in hairpin structures. This binding property allows MBNL to influence the recognition and processing of pre-mRNAs by the spliceosome and thus play a role in AS [ ]. However, the direction of splicing activity does not absolutely correlate with disease severity. In general, one of the potential therapeutic strategies for DM is the precise overexpression of MBNLs [ , ].
The MBNL family regulates AS with high precision [ – ]. Beyond the binding sites themselves, secondary RNA structures and interactions with co-regulators further modulate MBNL’s splicing activity. RNA folding near MBNL recognition sites can either facilitate or hinder protein binding, influencing splicing outcomes. For instance, specific stem-loop structures in target transcripts enhance MBNL affinity, effectively amplifying its regulatory effects [ , ]. Furthermore, MBNL does not act in isolation; its activity is modulated by antagonistic or synergistic interactions with other splicing factors, such as CELF proteins, RBFOX1, and PTBP1. These factors compete or cooperate with MBNL to recruit or block spliceosome components, adding an additional layer of complexity to AS regulation [ , ].
The splicing activity among the four zinc finger structures within the MBNL family is similarly variable. Both global and local residue fluctuations on each domain were found to significantly affect alternative splicing through conventional molecular dynamics and steered molecular dynamics simulations, suggesting that local motions of the RNA-binding domains can modulate both their affinity and specificity [ ]. Regarding the TZF domain, HALE et al. noted a 5-fold increase in splicing activity of their designed MBNL synthetic proteins with similar TZF1/2 domains and a 4-fold decrease in splicing activity of MBNL synthetic proteins with TZF3/4 domains, compared to MBNL proteins with typical zinc-finger domain arrangements. This difference is due to RNA binding specificity between the two TZF domains rather than binding affinity [ ]. Despite the structural similarity of the two TZFs, they may not be responsible for the same functions, with TZF1/2 likely recognizing the RNA motif and driving splicing regulation, whereas TZF3/4 act as RNA-binding domains. This prompted further associations: could MBNL regulation of AS also be related to the structure of its binding RNA? Indeed, although AS is regulated by MBNL homologs and isoforms, structured RNAs can alter the efficiency of AS [ ]. Quantitative studies show that MBNL regulation of AS also exhibits concentration-dependent features. Different splicing events require different amounts of MBNL1 [ ].
Some studies targeting the upstream and downstream mechanisms of MBNL-regulated AS provide a more detailed perspective. Penna et al. point out that the MBNL family is a major regulator of alternative splicing of the stress fiber-associated protein LIMCH1 in skeletal muscle, and incorrect LIMCH1 splicing is one of the main manifestations of DM type 1 (DM1) [ ]. Additionally, MBNL can affect the function of transcription factors that maintain the pluripotency of stem cells by regulating AS [ ]. On myoblast membranes and neurites, MBNL forms motile and immobilized granules and selectively binds to the kinesins Kif1bα and Kif1c through its ZnF domains [ ]. The MBNL family also affects DM complications through AS. Mis-splicing of adult exon 6B to fetal exon 6 A occurs in the cardiac sodium channel SCN5A in DM-associated cardiac diseases, and MBNL1 modulates the AS of SCN5A mRNA, alleviating associated symptoms [ ].

## MBNLs affect mRNA stability, trafficking and subcellular localization
The duration of mRNA degradation indicates its stability. mRNA degradation time varies widely, with some lasting only a few minutes and others exceeding 24 h. mRNA stability is usually proportional to gene function, with more important genes tending to produce more stable transcripts [ ]. MBNL proteins regulate the stability of target transcripts by binding to specific mRNA elements such as the 3’UTR [ , ]. This may be due to the fact that the AU-rich element (ARE) is located in the 3’UTR of the mRNA, and binding of RBP to it affects the activity of the mRNA degrading enzyme and ultimately the target protein stability [ ]. If MBNL binds to the repeat-expanded CUG (CCUG) sequences, it can affect the stability of its normally bound RNA and lead to the appearance of corresponding pathological features [ ].
RNA-binding proteins bind to intracellular mRNA, exit the nucleus, and are transported to regions of different specificity, ultimately exhibiting different subcellular localizations. During mammalian tissue development, mRNA localization covers most of the transcriptome [ ]. mRNA localization contributes to the post-transcriptional fine-tuning of gene expression, controlling fundamental processes such as cell motility, polarity, and differentiation [ ]. MBNL proteins regulate mRNA stability and degradation by binding to cis-regulatory elements within the 3’ untranslated regions (3’ UTRs) of target mRNAs. For example, in mouse myoblast cell lines, MBNL1 binding to 3’ UTRs can significantly reduce the half-life of certain mRNAs, promoting their degradation [ ]. Conversely, in some cases, MBNL proteins stabilize mRNAs by forming RNA-protein complexes that shield transcripts from exonucleolytic or endonucleolytic decay. A key example is the localization of integrin alpha3 mRNA to the plasma membrane by MBNL2, where its translation is tightly regulated [ ]. These dual roles in stabilizing or degrading mRNAs highlight the dynamic regulatory functions of MBNL proteins, which are often tissue- and context-specific. Moreover, MBNL-mediated mRNA stability is critical for maintaining proper gene expression in neurons and muscle cells. In Drosophila, for example, depletion of MBNL proteins disrupts the localization and stability of cytoskeletally transported mRNAs, leading to functional abnormalities.3 These findings suggest that MBNL proteins not only regulate mRNA degradation but also influence subcellular localization to ensure precise post-transcriptional regulation [ ].
Furthermore, post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression mediated by differences in the subcellular localization of RNA-binding proteins themselves is equally critical for embryonic development and the maintenance of organismal homeostasis [ , ], and the MBNL family is no exception. In humans, MBNL1 and MBNL2 show translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus during adult myoblast culture and postnatal development [ , ]. This change in subcellular localization implies a shift in the AS pattern from fetal to adult, and thus unexpected events in the subcellular localization of MBNLs may lead to abnormal phenomena.
A particularly well-studied example involves exon 5 of MBNL1, which plays a crucial role in determining the nuclear localization of the MBNL1 protein. Inclusion of exon 5 introduces a NLS, enabling MBNL1 to translocate into the nucleus [ , ]. Targeted repair of exon 5 using blockmiRs (microRNAs blocking splicing sites) significantly modifies MBNL1’s subcellular localization and intensity [ ]. Importantly, this approach has proven highly effective in the treatment of DM1, as it restores normal localization and function of the MBNL1 protein, alleviating disease symptoms [ ]. This approach not only restores MBNL1’s normal localization and function but also represents a promising therapeutic strategy to alleviate DM1 symptoms.

## MBNLs and alternative polyadenylation
Alternative polyadenylation (APA) is one of the dynamic processing modes of primary RNA transcripts [ ]. APA generates mRNA isoforms with different 3’ ends by adding a polyadenylation (pA) tail to different polyadenylation sites. APA can regulate the stability, translational efficiency, and subcellular localization of mRNAs by selecting different pA sites, thereby playing an important role in cell development, differentiation, and response to environmental changes [ ]. MBNL proteins regulate thousands of APA events by binding near polyadenylation sites, directly influencing site selection. In mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), MBNL depletion results in widespread dysregulation of APA, leading to aberrant expression of fetal isoforms in adult tissues [ ]. Specifically, MBNL protein binding sites are frequently found near polyadenylation sites, the selective use of which is regulated by MBNL proteins. Studies targeting the gene level have similarly confirmed this. Single or combined knockout of the Mbnl gene in mice disrupts the AS and APA patterns, leading to aberrant expression of fetal or nonmuscle protein isoforms in adult tissues, a key feature of DM [ ]. This is particularly evident in congenital DM (CDM), where immature APA patterns caused by the loss of MBNL1 and MBNL2 disrupt tissue differentiation and lead to severe phenotypes [ ]. Coordination of APA and AS by MBNL proteins is critical in maintaining proper transcriptomic regulation. For example, in DM1, MBNL-regulated AS and APA events often occur within the same transcripts, leading to the production of dysfunctional protein isoforms that exacerbate disease pathology. This interplay between APA and AS highlights the multifaceted regulatory roles of MBNL proteins.

## MBNLs are involved in microRNA processing
MicroRNAs (miRs) are a class of small non-coding RNAs involved in the regulation of gene expression by binding to target mRNAs, leading to their degradation or translational repression [ ]. The MBNL proteins play an important role in the processing of miRs, particularly in the regulation of miR-1 processing [ ]. MBNL1 binds to sites specific to pre-miR-1, including the UGC motif, which is highly conserved across species and miR-1 family members [ ]. This binding is achieved through the zinc finger domain of MBNL1, whose binding capacity can be reduced by mutations. Specifically, the ability of MBNL1 to bind to pre-miR-1 is impaired by the competitive binding of CUG repeat sequences, resulting in decreased expression of mature miR-1 in patients with DM. The deletion or impaired function of MBNL1 also directly affects miR-1 processing. Additionally, MBNL1 silencing mediated by short hairpin RNA mimics the action of CUG repeat sequences and significantly reduces the levels of mature miR-1 without affecting the level of pre-miR-1 [ ]. This suggests that MBNL1 plays a critical role in the processing of pre-miR-1. In the heart, the downregulation of miR-1 is associated with a variety of cardiac pathologies, such as cardiac conduction defects and arrhythmias [ ], which further demonstrates the importance of MBNL1 for microRNA processing. The regulation of miR-1 by MBNL1 is not limited to its own binding but also involves interactions with other proteins. For example, the LIN28 protein inhibits miR-1 maturation by blocking the cleavage of pre-miR-1 by the Dicer enzyme, while deletion of MBNL1 enhances this inhibitory effect of LIN28 [ ]. In this way, MBNL1 not only directly regulates microRNA processing but also indirectly influences microRNA maturation through interactions with other regulatory proteins. MBNL also contributes to normal survival by affecting microRNAs. In Caenorhabditis elegans, the cytoplasmic shorter isoform of MBL-1 maintains a normal lifespan by regulating a set of miRs [ ].

## MBNL family proteins in diseases
The MBNL family of proteins plays a pivotal role in the regulation of RNA metabolism. Given their fundamental functions, dysregulation of MBNL proteins has been implicated in the pathogenesis of a variety of human diseases. From degenerative conditions like Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) to complex disorders such as cardiac diseases and neurodegenerative conditions including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease, MBNL proteins exhibit diverse mechanisms of action that underline their critical role in cellular homeostasis and tissue development. In this section, we will explore the involvement of MBNL family proteins in these diseases, highlighting their regulatory mechanisms and pathological consequences (Table  ).
 

## Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy
Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is an age-related degenerative disease of the postmitotic corneal endothelium that affects 4% of the U.S. population over the age of 40 [ ]. Patients are usually diagnosed between the ages of 50 and 60 years, and initial symptoms of blurring and glare can progress to severe vision loss. FECD is a genetic disorder characterized by the amplification of the CTG trinucleotide repeat within intron 2 of the TCF4 gene (the CTG18.1 triple-repeat polymorphism), which accounts for 70% of FECD cases [ , ]. The MBNL1 protein co-localizes with amplified CUG repeat sequence RNA in FECD patient-derived corneal endothelial cells with TCF4 or DMPK amplification [ , ]. Indeed, FECD cells or tissues with TCF4 amplification exhibit changes in AS of key MBNL-sensitive genes relative to normal cells [ ]. This may be because extended repeat sequences within the TCF4 gene bind MBNL proteins and reduce available MBNL proteins, thereby inducing global splicing changes that ultimately lead to cellular dysfunction and degeneration. By quantifying MBNL proteins and their interactions with foci in corneal endothelial cell lines derived from patients with FECD and in human corneal endothelial tissues, investigators demonstrated that depletion of both MBNL1 and MBNL2 reduces nuclear RNA foci formed by repetitive sequences, suggesting that both are required for FECD foci [ ].

## Cardiac diseases
The MBNL family plays a crucial role in cardiac development, affecting the expression of cardiac-specific genes mainly by binding to RNA and regulating its splicing and pA sites [ , ]. MBNL1 is not expressed prior to cardiac ring formation but begins to be significantly expressed afterward, suggesting a negative regulatory role in the endocardial development of the cardiac cushion and outflow tract [ ]. MBNL1 promotes cardiac hypertrophy by increasing the stability of Myocardin mRNA through binding to its 3’-UTR. Additionally, MBNL1 may be involved in cardiomyocyte apoptosis by affecting the TNF-α signaling pathway [ ]. In contrast, MBNL2 is expressed in the embryonic heart [ , ]. Similarly, knockdown of MBNL2 in zebrafish (Danio rerio) leads to cardiac abnormalities, including dilation and disorganization of myofibrils [ ].
In cardiac disease, the fibrotic response is a key pathological process involving the conversion of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, which play a central role in the formation of scar tissue after myocardial infarction. However, overactivation of this fibrotic response leads to cardiac dysfunction and heart failure [ , ]. In patients with dilated cardiomyopathy, MBNL2 expression is significantly upregulated and is crucial for aging-associated cardiac fibrosis [ ]. MBNL2 promotes aging-associated cardiac fibrosis by regulating the small ubiquitin-related modification (SUMO) of KLF4 and upregulating the TGF-β1/SMAD3 signaling pathway [ ]. Recent studies have revealed the critical role of MBNL1 in myocardial fibrosis and injury repair. Overexpression of MBNL1 promotes the transformation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, while deletion of MBNL1 blocks this transformation and affects the fibrotic phase of wound healing in a mouse model of myocardial infarction and skin injury [ ]. MBNL1 promotes myofibroblast differentiation by directly binding to and regulating transcripts related to differentiation specificity and cytoskeleton/matrix assembly. Notably, MBNL1 modulates mRNAs for serum response factor (SRF) and calmodulin phosphatase Ab, enhancing their activities and promoting myofibroblast differentiation. Using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, researchers found that mutations in the MBNL1 binding site in the 3’-UTR of SRF impaired myofibroblast differentiation, while deletion of the SRF gene in fibroblasts in vivo impaired wound healing and fibrotic processes [ ]. These findings reveal a new RNA-dependent paradigm for MBNL1 in myofibroblast formation. Not coincidentally, Bugg et al. found that after cardiac injury, MBNL1 expression was upregulated, promoting the conversion of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts and contributing to scar tissue maturation. Overexpression of MBNL1 accelerates cardiac fibroblast maturation, thereby enhancing cardiomyocyte remodeling and scar stabilization. Conversely, MBNL1 deletion limits scar formation and stabilization, possibly due to reduced myofibroblast activity. Furthermore, MBNL1 activity is associated with the proliferative capacity of cardiac fibroblasts. After myocardial infarction, overexpression of MBNL1 inhibits the proliferation of cardiac fibroblasts, whereas MBNL1 deletion results in the expansion of quiescent fibroblasts, which may adopt characteristics of cardiac MSCs [ ]. These findings suggest that MBNL1 is not only a regulator of cardiac fibroblast state transitions but also a key regulator of repair and remodeling after cardiac injury. Recent findings on the identification of MBNL1 targets have shown that MBNL1 affects αSMA expression by regulating p38 expression in mouse cardiac fibroblasts [ ]. These studies lay the foundation for understanding the network mechanism underlying the central role of fibrosis. In another large-scale RNA sequencing study of hearts from heart failure patients, results showed that MBNL1 expression was highly variable and correlated with splicing differences in its downstream targets found in mice. This suggests that aberrant splicing of MBNL1 may contribute to the pathogenesis of heart failure [ ].

## Diseases of the nervous system
Neurological disorders are often characterized by progressive loss of neuronal structure and function, accompanied by cognitive decline. The MBNL family proteins, particularly MBNL2, play essential roles in neuronal development and are critical regulators of RNA processing during the transition from fetal to adult stages [ , , ]. Dysregulation of MBNL proteins is implicated in various neurological diseases, where they contribute to aberrant RNA splicing and cellular dysfunction.

## Alzheimer’s disease
In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the reduction of MBNL2 expression disrupts RNA processing, reverting splicing patterns to an embryonic state. This change is closely linked to cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. The key mechanism involves calpain-2, a calcium-dependent cysteine protease, which degrades MBNL2 following calcium homeostasis dysregulation and NMDA receptor-mediated excitotoxicity [ ]. Mouse models of AD (e.g., APP/PS1 and THY-Tau22) confirm that reduced MBNL2 expression correlates with the exclusion of specific exons in target RNAs, exacerbating the pathology of AD [ ]. Targeting the calpain-2-mediated degradation of MBNL2 offers a promising therapeutic strategy to restore normal RNA processing and mitigate disease progression.

## Parkinson’s disease
MBNL2 has also been linked to Parkinson’s disease (PD) through genetic studies. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified enhancers associated with MBNL2 expression in oligodendrocytes [ ]. Disruption of these enhancers may increase the risk of neurodegenerative changes and PD susceptibility [ ]. Furthermore, SNPs in the MBNL2 gene (e.g., rs4771268) have been associated with an increased risk of PD, particularly in specific populations, such as in southern China [ ]. These findings suggest that genetic variants affecting MBNL2 expression and function contribute to the pathogenesis of PD and highlight MBNL2 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

## Huntington’s disease
Huntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant disorder [ ]. Huntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant disorder [ ]. MBNL proteins are sequestered by the mutant CAG RNA, leading to their functional depletion. This sequestration exacerbates RNA splicing defects and contributes to the disease’s neurodegenerative progression. Therapeutic compounds targeting mutant CAG RNA have been developed to prevent MBNL protein aggregation, restore RNA processing, and alleviate disease symptoms. For example, pyridine-containing coumarin derivatives have shown efficacy in reducing MBNL sequestration and improving cell viability, offering a potential avenue for HD treatment [ ].

## Myotonic dystrophy
Myotonic dystrophy is a multisystem disorder characterized by a genetic basis and diverse clinical manifestations [ , ]. The incidence and prevalence of DM vary according to available epidemiologic data, but overall, the global average prevalence of DM is approximately 1 in 8000 people [ ]. This rate may vary in certain regions and populations. DM is mainly categorized into DM1 and type 2 (DM2), both showing an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. DM1 is caused by an amplification of a CTG trinucleotide repeat sequence in the 3’-UTR of the DMPK gene, while DM2 is caused by an amplification of a CCTG tetranucleotide repeat sequence in intron 1 of the CNBP gene [ – ]. In both forms, the amplified DNA is transcribed into an RNA transcript that interferes with the cellular mechanisms controlling RNA biogenesis and gene expression. These transcripts with extended repeat sequences are retained in discrete foci [ ]. We show the main features of the two DMs and the way they affect the MBNL proteins (Fig.  ).

 
In DM1 and DM2, CUG and CCUG produce pre-mRNA transcripts containing extended CUG and CCUG repeat sequences in the DMPK and CNBP genes, respectively. Subsequently, in DM1, the RNA containing the repetitive amplified sequence will sequestrate MBNL protein and activate CELF; whereas in DM2, the RNA will sequestrate both MBNL and RNA Binding Fox-1 Homolog (RBFOX). These changes result in an imbalance of selective splicing in adult tissues and enhanced production of fetal mRNA isoforms.
DM1 and DM2 share core clinical features, such as decreased muscle tone, muscle weakness, and multisystem involvement with cardiac, endocrine, and neurologic complications. However, certain symptoms, such as cognitive dysfunction and congenital onset, are predominantly associated with DM1. Phenotypic variability in DM1 is influenced by CTG repeat length, with longer repeat lengths typically associated with more severe symptoms and earlier onset [ ]. Despite many similarities, the clinical course of DM2 is usually milder than that of DM1 [ ]. DM2 symptoms are usually milder and include slower disease progression, less severe disease, less cardiac involvement, later onset of weakness in respiratory, facial, and bulbar muscles that is less pronounced, less likely to be induced by muscle tonus, and maintenance of better social and cognitive skills [ , ]. Indeed, muscle cell culture experiments on muscle cells from different DM patients corroborated this. Muscle cell cultures from DM1 patients showed markedly reduced fusion capacity and AS abnormalities, whereas myoblast cultures from DM2 showed only minor or no apparent fusion defects or splicing abnormalities [ ]. Interestingly, despite the milder expression of DM2 at the clinical and cellular levels, CNBP mRNA was expressed at levels 3 to 5 times higher than DMPK mRNA. Additionally, the average length of extended repeat sequences in DM2 (up to approximately 11,000 CCTG repeats in blood) was generally longer than in DM1 (up to approximately 1,000 CTG repeats in blood) [ ].

## The role of Muscleblind-like family in myotonic dystrophy
The pathogenesis of DM is primarily driven by toxic RNA gain-of-function mechanisms. Amplified repetitive sequences in mutant transcripts sequester RNA-binding proteins, particularly members of the MBNL family, leading to widespread AS defects [ , ]. This disruption affects numerous genes and contributes to the multisystemic symptoms of the disease [ ]. MBNL proteins are also involved in alternative polyadenylation and mRNA localization, further exacerbating disease pathology [ ]. Loss of MBNL1 and MBNL2 replicates DM1 features in human induced pluripotent stem-cell-derived skeletal muscle cells, highlighting their role in late myogenic maturation [ ]. In the brains of DM1 patients, amplified CUG RNA causes neurodegeneration, with reduced MBNL1 cytoplasmic expression as an early pathological response [ ].
The CUG RBP elav-like family member 1 (CELF1) is activated in DM1 and antagonistically interacts with MBNLs, disrupting the splicing of key transcripts such as CLCN1, INSR, TNNT3, and SCN5A. These mis-splicing events are linked to clinical symptoms, including insulin resistance, myasthenia gravis, cardiac insufficiency, and myofibril maintenance defects [ – ]. Nuclear RNA foci formed by repetitive RNA sequences and resulting splicing dysregulation remain central to DM pathogenesis, emphasizing the role of RNA toxicity in this disease. Studies using neuromuscular models derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) revealed developmental defects in neuromuscular junction formation caused by MBNL protein loss [ ]. Transcriptomic analyses showed that specific neuron-related processes regulated by MBNL proteins are misregulated in DM1. For example, defective MBNL1 function alters mTOR signaling, leading to reduced neuronal proliferation and increased autophagy. Functional restoration of MBNL1 rescued these phenotypes, highlighting its central role [ ].
MBNL defects resulting in DM correlate with the degree of RNA binding. In DM brains, MBNL2 preferentially binds extended RNA repeats, reducing its ability to bind normal RNAs. This misbinding causes AS errors, including the incorrect splicing of MAPT exons, leading to fetal Tau isoform expression in DM1 brains [ ]. These findings introduce new tools to study RNA-mediated toxicity in DM and related diseases.
MBNL1 function in DM is influenced by post-translational modifications. In DM1 mouse brains, extended CUG RNA induces deubiquitination of MBNL1, leading to its nuclear translocation and dysfunction [ ]. Extended CUG RNA induced deubiquitination of MBNL1 in the cytoplasm, which led to the nuclear translocation and morphological damage of MBNL1. This damage is ameliorated by inhibiting K63-linked polyubiquitin chain degradation. Ubiquitination of MBNL1 is necessary for its localization in the cytoplasm, while the nuclear translocation of deubiquitinated MBNL1 in the DM1 brain is pathogenic. The deletion of MBNL1 increases the inclusion of exon 6 and exon 9 of MBNL2, leading to increased MBNL2 translocation to the nucleus and protein stability. Deletion of MBNL1 increases nuclear localization and stability of MBNL2 through the inclusion of exons 6 and 9, providing a compensatory mechanism [ ]. This compensatory mechanism was validated in a DM1 mouse model, emphasizing its potential role in DM1. Furthermore, in mice lacking MBNL1 and MBNL3, aberrant accumulation of Clc-1 RNA on monosomes and primary polysomes resulted in Clc-1 translation defects that exacerbated dystonia [ ]. This suggests that Clc-1 RNA translation defects, in conjunction with splicing errors, are key contributors to the pathogenesis of dystonia. Similar Clc-1 defects were observed in the DM1 mouse model, further confirming that DM1 pathology requires both splicing errors and translation defects. Phosphorylation modifications also play a significant role in DM. As a tissue-specific AS (alternative splicing) regulatory factor, MBNL proteins can modulate the phosphorylation potential of proteins, thereby influencing their stability, enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, and other characteristics [ ]. In DM1, the antagonistic protein of MBNL, CUGBP1, undergoes phosphorylation mediated by Protein Kinase C (PKC), which alters its stability and function [ ]. Moreover, MBNL1 enhances the activity of Src family kinases (SFK) by interacting with the Src homology 3 domain of Src family kinases through its proline-rich regions [ ]. This process is accompanied by the overexpression and nuclear localization of the protein product of MBNL1 transcripts containing exon 5 [ ]. Downregulation of MBNL1 reduces tyrosine-phosphorylated protein levels and restores the splicing pattern of MBNL1 exon 5 [ ]. These findings provide a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of DM1 and may offer new targets for therapeutic strategies.
In DM2, bidirectional transcription of CCTG-CAGG sequences produces toxic ras-related nuclear (RAN) proteins, such as LPAC and QAGR, which accumulate in neurons and glial cells independently of RNA [ ]. Overexpression of MBNL1 reduces LPAC RAN protein levels, suggesting a protective role for MBNL proteins in DM2 [ ]. These findings highlight distinct molecular mechanisms in DM2 and the therapeutic potential of targeting RAN protein production. The deletion of MBNL3 in mice contributes to age-related DM1 pathology through mechanisms distinct from embryonic splice isoform retention. Mbnl3ΔE2 mice exhibit accelerated diabetes, cardiac dysfunction, and cataracts but lack embryonic splicing patterns of RNAs such as INSR and Tnnt2, providing further insights into age-related differences in DM pathogenesis [ ].
Molecules that act synergistically or antagonistically with MBNL similarly influence the DM phenotype. A number of other RNA-binding proteins, such as the RBFOX family and the CELF family, also participate in AS as splicing factors and influence the DM phenotype [ ]. Rbfox, an RBP crucial for muscle development, forms a co-regulatory splicing network with MBNL1 in human embryonic muscle cell lines [ ]. Recent studies have started to characterize the intersections within their regulatory networks. For example, analysis of mRNA-seq datasets indicates that MBNL and RBFOX motifs surrounding the exome are often evolutionarily conserved and preferentially enriched [ ]. The regulatory effects of CELF, RBFOX, and MBNL on muscle are often antagonistic. For instance, in skeletal muscle, 65% of exons responsive to CELF1 and MBNL1 are antagonistically regulated, and exons affected by CELF1 or CELF2 induction are usually responsive to MBNL1 depletion and tend to be developmentally regulated [ ]. RBFOX1 RNA-binding proteins can specifically bind to extended CCUG repeat sequences but not extended CUG repeats [ ]. RBFOX1 competes with MBNL1 for binding to CCUG repeats. RBFOX1 overexpression partially releases MBNL1, trapped by CCUG RNA aggregates, from DM2 muscle cells and corrects CCUG repeat-induced AS abnormalities and muscle atrophy. In a Drosophila model, RBFOX1 expression alleviated muscle atrophy as well as climbing and flight deficits caused by extended CCUG repeat sequences. These findings suggest that RBFOX proteins, by competing for and reducing MBNL1 capture in CCUG RNA aggregates, may in part explain the milder pathogenicity of DM2. These studies partially explain the differences in the age of onset of different DMs, and modeling these relevant molecules may lead to earlier predictions related to DM onset.

## Potential therapeutic strategies targeting MBNL functional restoration
In recent years, a variety of therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring the functional activity of MBNL proteins have garnered significant attention [ ]. Therapeutic strategies for DM can be broadly categorized into three main groups: (1) inducing degradation of mutant RNAs containing CUG/CCUG extensions, (2) releasing MBNLs from pathogenic RNA aggregates, and (3) increasing MBNL expression [ , ]. The first class of strategies focuses on inducing degradation of mutant CUG/CCUG extended RNAs via specific chemical agents or genetic interventions. The goal of this approach is to reduce the accumulation of pathogenic RNAs in the cells, thereby mitigating the resulting pathological effects. The second class of strategies involves the use of small molecules, multivalent compounds, small peptides, or ASOs to block the binding of pathogenic RNAs to MBNL proteins. By inhibiting the formation of pathogenic RNA-MBNL complexes, these approaches are effective in ameliorating AS defects and restoring normal cellular function. A third class of strategies involves modulating and enhancing the endogenous pool of MBNL proteins. Overexpression of MBNL1 protein has been shown to alleviate the pathogenesis of DM1 by reversing aberrant AS and rescuing related symptoms [ ]. In addition, gene therapy tools for MBNL overexpression have been recognized as a promising strategy to compensate for the lack of function by directly increasing the expression of MBNL proteins.
The ASO strategy has shown significant potential. ASO enables specific gene knockdown in diseases involving gain-of-function effects of RNAs or proteins. In DM1, ASO significantly reduced the levels of toxic RNA by either blocking the binding of CUG-expanded RNA to MBNL or through RNase H-mediated degradation [ ]. For example, researchers found that specifically designed ASOs were effective in reducing CUG RNA foci and restoring MBNL1-dependent splicing events in cellular models. However, the systemic use of ASO remains limited because many tissues, including skeletal and cardiac muscle, exhibit poor ASO uptake. Wheeler et al. demonstrated that nuclear-retained transcripts containing expanded CUG (CUG(exp)) repeats are unusually sensitive to antisense silencing. In a transgenic mouse model of DM1, systemic administration of ASOs rapidly knocked down CUG(exp) RNA in skeletal muscle, leading to the release of chelated MBNL1 protein, increasing its splicing regulatory activity, and ultimately reversing phenotypic and transcriptomic features [ ]. Indeed, ASO drugs have already entered the experimental stage. For example, IONIS 486,178, an unbound interstitial ASO, effectively reduced DMPK expression in vivo by enhancing RNase H-mediated degradation and showed significant therapeutic activity in skeletal muscle and heart in DMSXL and DM200 mouse model [ , ]. Recent studies have shown that DMPK mRNA targeting significantly eliminated the formation of aberrant foci, achieved nuclear redistribution of MBNL1/2 proteins, and corrected aberrant splicing events in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived DM1 neuronal cells [ ].
Small molecules and others exhibit significant therapeutic potential by acting on the molecular pathology of DM1 through multiple mechanisms. We will present some of the small molecule compounds based on MBNL function restoration. Some of the small molecule compounds can deregulate MBNL by blocking the binding of CUG(exp) RNA to MBNL protein. Wojciechowska et al. cultured human DM1 myoblasts and fibroblasts using two small-molecule ATP-binding site-specific kinase inhibitors, C16 and C51, and found that both inhibitors attenuated the dominant negative effect of CUG repetitive sequence amplification and reversed the DM1 molecular phenotype [ ]. Pentamidine, a drug approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), consists of two phenylamidine groups as a diamide, joined by a five-carbon methylene connector. In cells and model organisms from DM1 patients, Pentamidine disrupted CUG foci, released the MBNL1 protein, and reversed the aberrant AS of a number of pre-mRNAs that are normally incorrectly spliced in DM1. These effects were caused by binding of the compound to CUG repetitive sequences [ ]. However, its effects on the DM1 molecular phenotype were subsequently found to be attributed to either reduced transcription of CUG transcripts or increased degradation of toxic RNA [ ]. Phenylbutazone (PBZ) and ketoprofen, two nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), enhanced MBNL1 expression in proliferating and differentiating C2C12 cells [ ]. In addition, PBZ inhibited the interaction between CUG RNA foci and MBNL1 and reduced the proportion of MBNL1 in mutant transcripts [ ]. In an earlier study, PBZ also increased survival in the DM1 Drosophila model [ ]. Osteotriol also upregulates MBNL1 expression and corrects aberrant splicing in skeletal muscle by activating the promoter of MBNL1 in C2C12 myogenic cells [ ]. Furamidine, an antiviral drug, was found to cause upregulation of MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein levels and reduction of ribonucleic foci in HSALR DM1 mice [ ]. Furamidine upregulates MBNL1 and 2 proteins by disrupting the MBNL-CUG RNA complex and may affect transcriptional and/or stability of CUG RNA [ ]. Another commonly used antimicrobial drug, erythromycin efficiently binds to CUG repeat sequences, preventing MBNL1 capture, thereby reducing RNA aggregate formation and correcting splicing abnormalities [ ]. In DM1 model cells and mice, erythromycin demonstrated significant effects, and was able to reverse splicing abnormalities and ameliorate myasthenia gravis symptoms by oral or systemic administration. As a drug that has been widely used in humans, erythromycin is well tolerated and safe, and is therefore considered a potential therapeutic agent for DM1. The study suggests that erythromycin is expected to be an effective treatment for DM1 through drug repositioning and further clinical trials are feasible [ ]. Combination treatment of furamidine with erythromycin enhances the therapeutic effect. In the DM1 mouse model, the combination of oral erythromycin and prodrug of furamidine (pafuramidine) showed additive and synergistic mis-AS rescue effects. More strikingly, gene expression was only slightly affected, with more than 40% of the genes showing significant changes in expression but returning to WT expression levels after rescue. In addition, the combined treatment partially rescued the dystonic phenotype in DM1 mice [ ]. Exogenous supplementation of MBNL protein may also be therapeutic. Direct injection of (CUG)91 repeat-containing mRNA into zebrafish single-cell embryos induces neurological and muscle toxicity during early development. Co-injection of mbnl2 RNA inhibits (CUG)91 RNA toxicity and reverses associated behavioral and transcriptional abnormalities [ ]. Some microtubule inhibitors that selectively reduce the level of r(CUG)480 and partially rescue MBNL-dependent mis-AS. the mechanism of action involves a selective transcriptional reduction of CTG expansion, which may be related to the LINC (linker of the nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton) complex [ ]. In addition, Lomofungin and its dimer Dilomofungin showed significant therapeutic potential through potent inhibition of MBNL1-CUG RNA interaction [ ]. However, Dilomofungin was less effective in vivo than in vitro, possibly because of its additional molecular targets. Some ligands, such as Ligand 1, efficiently deregister MBNL1 by recognizing U-U mismatches in CUG RNA hairpins, significantly reducing CUG RNA focus in a HeLa cell model [ ]. Another improved compound, New Ligand 1, increased affinity for CUG RNA through a multivalent binding strategy and showed excellent desegregation in the DM1 cell model [ ]. Luu et al. on the other hand report an RNA-targeted agent that is relatively non-toxic to HeLa cells. As a potent inhibitor of the MBNL1-rCUG complex, this agent lysed intranuclear foci and corrected insulin receptor splicing defects in DM1 model cells. In addition, the compound was able to ameliorate two different disease phenotypes in the Drosophila DM1 model: adult external eye degeneration and larval crawling defects. However, the maximum tolerated dose of the compound in mice is relatively low and may be limited by cellular uptake [ ]. This provides insight into future directions for development.
Based on the favorable therapeutic effects of these compounds, some researchers have focused on small-molecule screening. Zhang et al. developed a flow cytometry-based screening system for small-molecule compounds that enhance MBNL1 expression by integrating a ZsGreen fluorescent tag into the endogenous MBNL1 gene locus of HeLa cells [ ]. A preliminary screen identified more than thirty compounds that more than doubled MBNL1 expression, with two HDAC inhibitors, ISOX and Vorinostat, significantly increasing MBNL1 expression in DM1 patient-derived fibroblasts and partially repairing splicing defects caused by CUG repeats [ ]. This finding demonstrates the feasibility of utilizing a flow cytometry screening system to identify MBNL1 upregulators and provides new ideas for the treatment of DM1. Different throughput screening is also an important method for discovering potential therapeutic compounds. In the study by O’Leary et al. they generated immortalized myoblast cells by constitutive overexpression of human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) and inducible overexpression of myoblast determination factor (MYOD) from healthy myoblasts (DMPK CTG5) and DM1 patient myoblast cells (DMPK CTG1000) to generate immortalized myoblast cell lines for direct observation of MBNL1-containing nuclear foci therein, and validated the accuracy of the system by high-throughput screening (HTS) of approximately 13,000 small molecules [ ]. The medium throughput phenotypic assay screen identified compounds Ro 31–8220 and Chromomycin A3 that significantly reduced intranuclear foci in the DM1 cell model and corrected INSR and ATP2A1 mis-splicing by deregulating MBNL1 [ ]. There are far fewer cellular models of DM2 compared to DM1. Using DM2 patient-derived fibroblasts, Jenquin et al. found that the small molecule compound diamidines partially rescued the molecular phenotype of DM2 fibroblasts [ ]. Some studies have focused on drug accessibility. Small molecule inhibition of the interaction between CUG(exp) and MBNL1 was detected by in vitro chelation and fluorescence titration assays, and Nakamori et al. selected the small molecule drug with the highest affinity for CUG(exp) among 20 FDA-approved small molecule compounds and confirmed that it inhibited the binding of CUG to MBNL1 [ ].
Gene therapy, including gene editing and RNA interference, is also thought to play an important role in the treatment of DM [ – ]. Specific miRNAs have also been identified as regulators of MBNL proteins. In a recent study, miR-23b and miR-218 were identified as inhibitors of MBNL1 and MBNL2 translation [ ]. Antagonists of miR-23b and miR-218 miRNAs increased MBNL protein levels in DM1 myoblasts and rescued pathogenic misexchange events. In HSALR DM1 model mice, systemic administration of these “antagomiRs” similarly elevated MBNL expression and improved DM1-like phenotypes, including altered splicing, histopathology, and muscle tone [ ]. In addition, researchers used BlockmiR technology to increase translation of the MBNL protein by blocking sites where specific microRNAs (miR-23b and miR-218) bind to the MBNL transcript [ , ]. In muscle cells from DM1 patients and skeletal muscle tissues from 3D mice, BlockmiR not only increased MBNL1/2 protein expression, but also corrected aberrant splicing and did not trigger an immune response in mice. BlockmiR also demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in a mouse model of HSALR, increasing MBNL1/2 protein expression, improving grip strength, and correcting the abnormal histologic phenotype. This suggests a potential application of BlockmiR technology in DM1 therapy. However, gene therapy tools may trigger MBNL overactivity, which can alter the metabolism of many RNAs. To overcome these limitations, the investigators designed a self-regulated MBNL1 overexpression system. This system enables self-regulation of MBNL1 protein expression by inserting an ATP2A1 gene fragment into the MBNL1 coding sequence and including an MBNL-sensitive variable exon [ ]. This self-regulatory system was able to significantly correct AS abnormalities in the DM1 model, demonstrating good therapeutic potential. In a study it was also found that endogenous expression of a single MBNL muscle-blind homolog in Drosophila, Dmel\mbl, is directly regulated by miR-277 and miR-304. By intercepting each miRNA using a sponge structure, the expression of myoblind proteins was increased in wild-type Drosophila and a DM1 model Drosophila expressing a CUG trinucleotide repeat sequence. Deregulation of myoblastin was sufficient to reverse significant splicing alterations in the DM1 Drosophila model and improve pathological phenotypes including muscle function and longevity [ ]. Other miRNA inhibitors of MBNL1, MBNL2 and MBNL3 such as miR-30a-5p and miR-30e-5p have also been identified as potential future candidates for increasing endogenous MBNL expression through miRNA inhibition therapy [ , ]. More interestingly, the researchers combined phosphodiester morpholino oligonucleotides (PMOs) with cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) to design antimiRs targeting miR-23b and miR-218, with a therapeutic window as much as 5.38-fold higher than that of previous antagomir [ ]. In DM1 cells, CPP-PMOs significantly increased MBNL1 levels. In addition, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated MBNL1 overexpression was shown to be effective in restoring normal splicing of multiple MBNL-sensitive genes in a DM1 mouse model [ ].
In summary, multiple potential therapeutic strategies targeting MBNL function restoration have shown significant therapeutic potential in cellular and animal models (Table  ). These diverse therapeutic approaches share the common goal of restoring MBNL function and alleviating the pathological features of DM through different mechanisms.
 

## MBNL proteins in cancer
There is growing evidence that MBNL family proteins are aberrantly expressed in tumors. They play a crucial role in regulating RNA splicing and gene expression, affecting various cancer types through different mechanisms. During tumorigenesis, decreased expression of MBNL proteins promotes a shift in the AS pattern to a more undifferentiated phenotype and enhances the proliferation and migration of tumor cells. ## The role of MBNL1 in cancer
MBNL1 plays a key role in regulating the behavior of cancer cells by a mechanism that is mainly mediated through the regulation of AS and transcript stability. The role of MBNL1 as a tumor suppressor (Fig.  ) or oncogene (Fig.  ) varies depending on the type of cancer, and its study as a potential therapeutic target is of great importance.

 
Drug sensitivity (left panel): MBNL1 transcription is activated by Nrf2 in the nucleus via the ARE region. However, MBNL1 promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of Nrf2 through the Cul3 complex, which increases drug sensitivity by downregulating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense. Metastasis regulation (middle and right panels): Middle panel: MBNL1 suppresses metastasis by promoting the degradation of Snail mRNA in P-bodies, thereby enhancing E-cadherin transcription and maintaining epithelial integrity. Right panel: MBNL1 stabilizes TACC1 and DBN2 mRNAs, which are involved in regulating cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization, indirectly inhibiting metastatic potential. Prevention of DNA fragmentation (bottom panel): MBNL1 influences splicing regulation through SRSF3 to promote the production of the Acin1-L isoform over Acin1-S. This shift reduces DNA fragmentation, mitigating tumor progression in colorectal cancer (CRC). These mechanisms collectively highlight MBNL1’s critical tumor suppressor functions in drug sensitivity, metastasis inhibition, and genomic stability maintenance.

 
Differential functional expression of different splice isoforms in cancer cells. Exon 7 (ex7) of the MBNL1 gene is highly contained in cancer cells and tissues [ ]. While overall MBNL1 expression is decreased in cancer tissues, consistent with its role as a tumor suppressor, its ex7 inclusion is significantly increased. This differential exon inclusion is thought to be a key molecular event in cancer progression and may play a role in promoting tumor growth in multiple cancer types. Further studies revealed that multiple components of the U2 spliceosome, such as SF3B1, SF3A1, and PHF5A, are critical for efficient MBNL1 ex7 inclusion. By using splice-converting antisense oligonucleotide (AON) technology, it is possible to selectively interfere with the ex7 splicing of MBNL1, thereby altering the ratio of its isoforms [ ]. Isoforms of MBNL1 differ significantly in their roles in cancer cells. MBNL1 isoforms lacking ex7 (MBNL1 Δex7) exhibited significant anti-survival effects in cancer cells, resulting in DNA damage, decreased cell viability and reduced migration. In contrast, the ex7-containing isoform contributes to cancer cell survival and migration. Understanding the specific roles and regulatory mechanisms of MBNL1 isoforms is important for the development of new cancer therapeutic strategies. Through further studies, the molecular mechanisms of MBNL proteins in cancer occurrence and development can be more comprehensively revealed, which in turn will provide new ideas and methods for cancer treatment.
MBNL1 significantly affects the behavior of breast cancer cells by regulating RNA splicing and transcript stability, and inhibition of its function is closely associated with breast cancer progression and deterioration. It was shown that MBNL1 inhibited breast cancer cell invasion and metastasis by binding to the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of transcripts, such as Drebrin-like (DBNL) protein and transforming acid spiral protein 1 (TACC1) [ ]. High expression of MBNL1 is significantly associated with longer metastasis-free survival in breast cancer patients. In addition, MBNL1 is one of nine splicing factors involved in aberrant splicing in breast cancer [ ]. By calculating the Pearson correlation coefficients between the mRNA expression levels of MBNL1 and the mean ψ values of differentially spliced exons (CERs) in breast cancer, Wen et al. found that MBNL1 expression levels were significantly correlated with the ψ values of at least one of the three groups of differentially spliced exons in ψ values of one group, further confirming the important role of MBNL1 in splicing regulation in breast cancer [ ]. It was shown that MBNL1 can affect the JNK signaling pathway by regulating the AS of MAP2K7, thereby modulating cancer stem cell properties. It was found that splicing deletion of MAP2K7 exon2 was closely associated with activation of JNK signaling and increased expression of cancer stem cell markers. deletion of MBNL1 leads to skipping of MAP2K7 exon2, which in turn activates the JNK signaling pathway and promotes stem cell-like properties of cancer cells. A small tRNA precursor-derived RNA, tRF-1-Ser, promotes the proliferation and stem cell properties of breast cancer cells by inhibiting the function of MBNL1 [ ]. Specifically, tRF-1-Ser was able to activate the JNK signaling pathway by binding to MBNL1 and preventing its entry into the nucleus, which in turn promoted stem cell-like properties and proliferative capacity of cancer cells. In addition, it was found that 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) was able to down-regulate the expression of tRF-1-Ser, thus indirectly restoring the function of MBNL1 and inhibiting the proliferation and stem cell properties of breast cancer cells [ ]. In conclusion, MBNL1 has a complex and diverse mechanism of action in breast cancer, affecting the behaviors of cancer cells, such as proliferation, migration and invasion, by regulating multiple signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms. Therefore, MBNL1 is considered as a potential cancer therapeutic target, and by targeting MBNL1 or its related regulatory pathways, it can provide new strategies and directions for breast cancer treatment.
MBNL1 likewise plays a key role in colorectal cancer (CRC). It was found that MBNL1 inhibited metastasis of CRC cells by blocking Dicer treatment of the c-allele pre-MiR-1307 and decreasing the expression of miR-1307, which upregulated the expression of Bcl2 [ ]. In addition, MBNL1 limited colorectal cancer cell metastasis by suppressing the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) gene. In CRC, by whole transcriptome analysis, the investigators identified reprogrammed expression and splicing profiles of MBNL1 transcripts in cancerous and adjacent normal tissues [ ]. The study showed that the total amount of MBNL1 transcripts was reduced in cancerous tissues compared to normal tissues, and the inhibitory effect of MBNL1 proteins on DNA fragmentation in CRC cells was further diminished by an increase in MBNL1 8 isoforms mediated by SRSF3. Specifically, interactions between SRSF3 and the CA-enriched elements of MBNL1 exons 5 and 7, which encode a two-part NLS and a conformational NLS, promote the inclusion of these exons. this interaction results in the enrichment of the MBNL1 8 isoform in cancerous tissue. In addition, differences between MBNL1 isoforms also have a significant impact on DNA fragmentation in CRC cells. For example, increased MBNL1 8 isoforms drove the shift from Acin1-S to Acin1-L in the nuclear chromatin-concentrating inducer of apoptosis 1 (Acin1) isoform, whereas Acin1-L had a lower effect on DNA fragmentation and apoptosis than the Acin1-S isoform. Overall, SRSF3-MBNL1-Acin1 constitutes an emerging axis that is associated with pro-apoptotic features and post-transcriptional events in CRC cells. In addition, MBNL1 inhibits EMT by regulating Snail mRNA stability, thereby reducing the migration and invasive capacity of cancer cells [ ]. Up-regulation of MBNL1 significantly enhances the recruitment of Snail transcripts to the processing vesicles (P-vesicles), leading to an increase in the degradation of Snail mRNA and subsequent translational silencing. And Snail, as a key transcription factor, can bind to the promoter of epithelial cadherin (E-cadherin) and inhibit its transcriptional activity, leading to loss or reduction of E-cadherin expression [ ].
Translocation of the MLL to several different fusion partners leads to the development of MLL-rearranged (MLL-r) leukemia [ ]. This leukemia is known for its aggressiveness and poor prognosis. By analyzing the gene expression profiles of patients with MLL-r leukemia, MBNL1 was found to be one of the genes consistently overexpressed [ ]. MLL fusion proteins directly promote the overexpression of the MBNL1, which leads to changes in the AS of the target RNA transcripts. MBNL1 is predominantly regulated by the exclusion of introns in the cells of MLL-r leukemia. Thereby affecting the expression of several genes. These genes include DOT1L and SETD1A, which play key roles in the development of MLL rearrangement leukemias. The specific mechanisms of MBNL1 in MLL-r leukemias still need to be further investigated. For example, how MBNL1 selectively affects the splicing of specific genes, the interactions of MBNL1 with other RNA-binding proteins and splicing factors, and the specific functions of MBNL1-regulated mRNA isoforms in leukemia cells need to be elucidated by further studies.
MBNL1 is ubiquitously expressed in glioblastoma (GBM), but its expression level and function are significantly affected by tumor subtype and microenvironment. In the mesenchymal subtype of GBM, the expression level of MBNL1 is significantly higher than that in healthy brain tissue, and this expression positively correlates with the overall survival of patients [ ]. In a hypoxic environment, MBNL1 is rapidly translocated out of the nucleus, leading to inhibition of MBNL1 splicing activity and promoting self-renewal of glioblastoma stem cells and tumor formation [ ]. Forced expression of MBNL1 significantly inhibited glioblastoma progression and prolonged survival time in tumor transplantation models. In addition, MBNL1 was significantly down-regulated in glioblastoma cells, while its overexpression significantly inhibited lactic acid production and cell proliferation [ ]. The specific mechanism includes that MBNL1 promotes the expression of circNTRK2 by binding to NTRK2 pre-mRNA, which encodes a novel protein, NTRK2-243aa, that phosphorylates PAX5, reduces its half-life, and thus inhibits PAX5-mediated expression of glycolysis-related genes HK2 and PKM2. It was verified by mouse experiments that the combined overexpression of MBNL1 and circNTRK2 and the knockdown of PAX5 significantly inhibited tumor formation in GBM and significantly prolonged the survival time of mice [ ]. The MBNL1/circNTRK2/PAX5 pathway plays an important role in the regulation of glycolysis in GBM, and it may become a new target for the treatment of GBM.
MBNL1 also regulates the development of GBM through interactions with other key molecules and signaling pathways. MBNL1 inhibits the proliferation, migration, invasion, and lumen-forming ability of GBM cells by regulating the expression of vasculogenic mimicry (VM)-associated proteins MMP2 and LAMC2 [ ]. BUD13 and CDK12 expression was upregulated in GBM tissues and positively correlated with pathological grading. BUD13 enhances its mRNA stability through m6A methylation, which up-regulates the expression of CDK12. CDK12 regulates the stability of MBNL1 through phosphorylation, which affects the biological behaviors of GBM cells. The BUD13/CDK12/MBNL1 axis plays a crucial role in the malignant progression of GBM, providing a new potential treatment for GBM provides new potential therapeutic targets.
MBNL1 also plays an important role in other cancer types. In gastric cancer, MBNL1 is positively regulated by CREB1, resulting in upregulation of its expression [ ]. Interestingly, MBNL1 is not regulated by CREB1 in normal gastric mucosa. This differential regulatory mechanism suggests that MBNL1 may play an important role in gastric cancer progression by affecting AS of genes, for example. Therefore, MBNL1 has the potential to be a target for gastric cancer therapy. In cervical cancer, MBNL1 enhances the sensitivity of HeLa cells to cisplatin by stabilizing Cul3 mRNA, thereby increasing the ubiquitination of Nrf2 protein [ ]. Interestingly, Nrf2 can activate MBNL1 expression through ARE site reversal. This may be a self-protective mechanism of the cell. Highly expressed MBNL1 can reduce the drug tolerance of tumor cells through the Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway, providing a new direction for the treatment of cervical cancer. In skin squamous cell carcinoma (SSCC), down-regulation of MBNL1 expression promoted cell metastasis in SSCC, while up-regulation of MBNL1 expression decreased cell metastasis in SSCC. Down-regulation of MBNL1 inhibited the protein expression of T-cell intracellular antigen 1 like protein (TIAL1), myogenic determinant cluster 1 (MyoD1), and caspase-3 [ ]. MBNL1 inhibited the metastatic ability of SSCC through the TIAL1/MYOD1/Caspase-3 signaling pathway. In addition, MBNL1 is an important regulator of esophageal cancer metastasis and a correlate of poor prognosis. MBNL1 promotes the migration of esophageal cancer cells KYSE150 by stabilizing MRTF-A mRNA [ ]. MRTF-A, in turn, activates the transcription of the MBNL1 promoter, leading to the up-regulation of MBNL1 expression. The positive feedback regulation may be a potential therapeutic target for interrupting esophageal cancer migration. However, Wang et al. found that MBNL1 had an oncogenic role in esophageal squamous carcinoma [ ], and knockdown of MBNL1 significantly enhanced cancer cell migration, invasion, proliferation and tumor growth. In addition, MBNL1 deletion regulated the expression profiles of several circRNA, leading to extensive alterations in the expression of many downstream long chain non-coding RNAs (lncRNA) and mRNA. The specific role of MBNL1 in esophageal cancer needs to be further studied and explored. Li et al. found that mTORC1 activated the expression of miR-130b-3p through the transcription of the downstream effector molecule, STAT3, which in turn inhibited the expression of its expression of the direct target MBNL1, promoting angiogenesis and tumor growth [ ]. MiR-130b-3p is significantly up-regulated in a variety of cancers, and its level is positively correlated with the mTORC1 signaling pathway. Inhibition of miR-130b-3p attenuated tumor angiogenesis and growth, particularly in a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). This study reveals the importance of the STAT3/miR-130b-3p/MBNL1 feedback loop in mTORC1-mediated tumor progression.
Overall, the regulatory mechanisms of MBNL proteins in cancer emphasize their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Their ability to regulate key pathways of cell migration, invasion and survival is crucial for the development of new cancer therapeutic approaches. The multifaceted roles of MBNL proteins in different cancer types provide valuable insights into their therapeutic potential and pave the way for future exploration of their clinical applications.

## The role of MBNL2 in cancer
The regulatory role of MBNL2 protein in cancer is multifaceted and intricate, involving several key cellular processes and signaling pathways (Fig.  ). In breast, lung, and liver cancers, MBNL2 expression is significantly down-regulated, and this reduction is typically associated with increased methylation of its promoter region [ ]. This methylation alteration is likely a key factor contributing to the reduced expression of MBNL2, thereby affecting its tumor suppressor function. One of the key mechanisms by which MBNL2 regulates cancer cell migration and invasion in these cancers is through the PI3K/AKT-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway. Studies have shown that MBNL2 expression is directly suppressed by miR-182, which is up-regulated in these cancer types. Overexpression of MBNL2 reversed the miR-182-induced increase in cancer cell migration and invasion, suggesting that MBNL2 exerts a tumor-suppressive role in these cancers [ ]. In prostate cancer, MBNL2 expression is lower than in normal samples [ ]. Low MBNL2 expression was associated with poorer recurrence-free survival in this clinical cohort study, and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified MBNL2 as an important prognostic factor. Additionally, MBNL2 mRNA expression was negatively correlated with DNA methylation status, suggesting that MBNL2 may be a methylation-driven gene. MBNL2 may influence the biological behavior of prostate cancer through its regulation of DNA damage repair pathway gene expression, and in the high-risk group, high expression of DNA damage repair genes was associated with low expression of MBNL2. Based on the expression status of MBNL2, the authors concluded that prostate cancer patients could be classified into different risk groups to develop personalized treatment strategies. Patients in the high-risk group responded poorly to cisplatin and bicalutamide, suggesting that MBNL2 expression status may influence drug sensitivity [ ]. Thus, MBNL2 is not only an important prognostic factor in prostate cancer, but also has value as a potential therapeutic target.

 
MBNL2 has been found to enhance the anticancer ability of certain natural compounds. For example, Zhang et al. found that the natural compound Neobractatin (NBT) reduces cancer cell motility and invasiveness through the pAKT/EMT signaling pathway by upregulating MBNL2 expression [ ]. This finding suggests a potential application of MBNL2 in regulating the anticancer efficacy of natural compounds, which may enhance the therapeutic effect by modulating the expression of related genes.
MBNL2 also plays a critical role in the DNA damage response. Cai et al. showed that MBNL2 regulates the DNA damage response by stabilizing p21 mRNA, thereby reducing cancer cell proliferation [ ]. This mechanism is critical for maintaining genomic integrity and preventing uncontrolled cell growth. The role of MBNL2 in the DNA damage response pathway makes it a potential target for improving the efficacy of DNA damage therapy.
The role of MBNL2 in cancer is paradoxical. Perron et al. demonstrated that MBNL2 regulates a variety of cancer-related transcripts in cancer cells, suggesting an important role in maintaining cancer cell homeostasis and promoting tumorigenic processes [ ]. MBNL2 expression was upregulated in metastatic clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC) tissues compared to primary tumors. Further functional validation in ccRCC cell lines supported the abundance of cancer-associated transcripts. Specifically, MBNL2 inhibition impaired renal cancer cell proliferation and activated the apoptotic pathway, suggesting an oncogenic function for MBNL2. In a recent study, MBNL2 was found to promote tumor growth and metastasis in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) by regulating Bcl-2/Beclin 1-mediated autophagy process [ ]. Specifically, M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) secrete CCL2, which enhances the expression of MBNL2, which inhibits Beclin 1-dependent autophagy and confers malignant properties to RCC cells by directly binding to Bcl-2 mRNA and increasing its stability.
MBNL2 also plays an important role in the hypoxic response of tumors Fischer et al. found that MBNL2 was induced and regulated the expression and AS of hypoxia-inducible genes (e.g., VEGFA) under hypoxic conditions. Knockdown of MBNL2 in A549 and MCF-7 cells completely inhibited hypoxia-dependent induction of VEGF-A protein. MBNL2 does not affect VEGFA mRNA stability, but can directly recognize the 3’UTR of VEGFA which in turn affects VEGF-A protein secretion. In response to hypoxia in tumors, its paralogous homologs MBNL1 and MBNL3 are unaffected or even reduced at the mRNA and protein levels, suggesting a specific role for MBNL2 in hypoxic adaptation. This specific regulation promotes proliferation and migration of lung and breast cancer cells under hypoxic conditions, suggesting that MBNL2 plays an important role in tumor acclimation and survival, especially tumor growth in harsh microenvironments [ ].

## The role of MBNL3 in cancer
Studies of MBNL3 in a variety of cancers have revealed its oncogenic role and regulatory mechanisms. For example, high MBNL3 expression was positively correlated with paclitaxel resistance and poor prognosis in ovarian cancer [ ]. MBNL3 enhances the viability and reduces apoptosis of cancer stem cells by up-regulating the Nanog gene. MBNL3-Nanog axis is a potential therapeutic target for overcoming PTX resistance in ovarian cancer. MBNL3 has also been identified as a key regulator of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) invasion and key regulator of migration (Fig.  ) [ ]. Through CRISPR screening, the researchers found that knockdown of MBNL3 significantly impaired the invasive ability of PDAC cells, suggesting its potential therapeutic target in pancreatic cancer. Expression of lncRNAs SBF2-AS1 and MBNL3 was up-regulated, while miR-302a expression was down-regulated in radiation therapy-resistant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissues [ ]. Inhibition of SBF2-AS1 or upregulation of miR-302a could inhibit tumor growth by increasing radiosensitivity and promoting apoptosis in NSCLC cells through downregulation of MBNL3.

 
LncRNAs have been identified as key factors in cancer progression through interaction with MBNL proteins. For example, it was shown that the lncRNA FIRRE regulates PXN expression by interacting with MBNL3 to promote hepatocellular carcinogenesis (HCC) proliferation and metastasis [ ]. The interaction of FIRRE with MBNL3 enhances its splicing activity, which in turn affects the expression of genes involved in the pathogenesis of HCC. This network of interactions between lncRNAs and MBNL proteins provides a complex regulatory mechanism for therapeutic intervention. Yuan et al. found that knockdown of MBNL3 almost completely eliminated HCC. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that MBNL3 induced lncRNA-PXN-AS1 exon 4 inclusion [ ]. Transcripts lacking exon 4 bind to the coding sequence of PXN mRNA, causing dissociation of translation elongation factors from PXN mRNA, thereby inhibiting translation of PXN mRNA. In contrast, the transcript containing exon 4 preferentially binds to the 3’ untranslated region of PXN mRNA and protects PXN mRNA from microRNA-24-AGO2 complex-induced degradation, which increases PXN expression and promotes HCC development. In contrast, MBNL3 expression is regulated by the stem cell transcription factors NANOG, OCT4 and SOX2. These factors promote the expression of MBNL3 by directly binding to the corresponding binding sites on the MBNL3 promoter, a mechanism that is particularly evident in HCC cells. Cao et al. found that TATA box-binding protein (TBP) promoted HCC metastasis by enhancing MBNL3 expression, and that MBNL3, in turn, stimulated the exon 4 inclusion of lncRNA-PXN-AS1, which resulted in the upregulation of PXN and the subsequent EMT induction [ ].
MBNL3 expression was significantly reduced in acute phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) stem cells [ ]. This change coincided with increased expression of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) and fibroblast growth factor 5 (FGF5), which promote embryonic development, stem cell differentiation and proliferation, and tumor growth. Knockdown of MBNL3 led to an increase in CD44v3 transcripts, which contain variable exons 8–10, and enhanced OCT4 expression. Knockdown of CD44v3 reduces stem cell expansion, whereas expression of CD44v3 may increase cell expansion and enhance malignant reprogramming in a context containing the breakpoint cluster region-ABL proto-oncogene 1 (BCR-ABL1). In addition, CD44v3 overexpression enhanced hematopoietic colony formation, showing a trend toward increased self-renewal capacity. In conclusion, downregulation of MBNL3 activates the embryonic AS program, leading to malignant reprogramming and CD44v3 overexpression. This finding provides an important opportunity for selective detection and therapeutic elimination of leukemic stem cells.

## Conclusion and future perspectives
In our review, we structurally understand and explain the mode of action of the MBNL protein family in different diseases. We summarize the DM pathogenesis and also explain the segmented studies of the MBNL family in different branches of this big tree. In addition, we enumerate the emerging studies of MBNL in other diseases where the molecular functions of the ancient myoblast family of proteins are beginning to emerge. The more microscopic aspects of some long-established functions, such as AS, are beginning to manifest. As a class of RBPs, many questions have been answered about how MBNLs regulate RNAs and how the two interact. And in the MBNL family, the interactions between members also influence the final outcome. For example, in DM, differences in the dominant member within the MBNL family lead to phenotypic differences, and members can either substitute for or exclude each other. Countless questions remain unanswered regarding these intricate regulatory mechanisms and the delicate relationships among them.
Dysregulation of MBNL protein function has important implications in a number of seemingly unrelated diseases. The mechanisms between a subset of diseases have been explained, particularly in diseases with partial RNA repeats, and also highlight the important role of MBNL proteins in regulating RNA function. However, there are still some diseases in which alterations in MBNL proteins are difficult to explain. In addition, different symptoms in the same disease cannot all be explained by the binding of MBNL to RNA foci. For example, dysfunction of MBNL protein can explain the dysregulation of DM muscle activity, but degradation or dysfunction of MBNL protein in neurological disorders has also been associated with cognitive decline and neurodegenerative changes, which still requires further studies.
We discussed some multiple potential therapeutic strategies targeting functional recovery of MBNL proteins, including various types of compounds, antisense oligonucleotides, gene therapies, and protein replacement therapy, etc., but most of them are still in the preclinical stage or theoretical research and have not yet been widely utilized in the clinic. More specifically, MBNL protein function restoration has only been studied in DM, but not in other neurological diseases, circulatory diseases and malignant tumors.
In cancer, MBNL proteins influence the proliferation, migration and invasive ability of tumor cells by regulating splicing patterns. However, MBNL proteins can also lead to different outcomes for the same cancer in different studies. For example, MBNL1 exhibits a dual role as both a tumor suppressor and an oncogene in different types of cancer, a phenomenon largely attributed to its isoform-specific functions, molecular regulatory networks, and influence of the tumor microenvironment. First, the functional diversity of MBNL1 isoforms is central to this duality. For example, the inclusion of exon 7 (ex7) produces isoforms that promote tumor cell survival and migration, whereas isoforms lacking ex7 (MBNL1 Δex7) exhibit anti-tumor activities, including DNA damage induction, reduced cell viability, and decreased migration. This isoform-specific behavior is particularly evident in cancers such as breast and colorectal cancer, where Δex7 isoforms act as potent tumor suppressors. Second, the expression of these isoforms is tightly controlled by splicing regulators such as SRSF3 and components of the U2 spliceosome. For instance, SRSF3 facilitates the inclusion of ex7, driving a shift toward oncogenic isoforms and attenuating the tumor-suppressive role of MBNL1 in colorectal cancer. Third, the tumor microenvironment plays a crucial role in shaping MBNL1’s functions. In glioblastoma, hypoxia triggers the translocation of MBNL1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, impairing its splicing activity and promoting stem-like traits and tumorigenesis. Additionally, MBNL1 interacts with distinct molecular networks depending on the cancer type. In breast cancer, MBNL1 inhibits invasion and metastasis by stabilizing key transcripts such as DBNL and TACC1, while in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, it stabilizes MRTF-A mRNA, promoting migration. These examples demonstrate that the dual role of MBNL1 is a result of multilayered regulatory mechanisms, including dynamic shifts in splicing events, interactions with tumor-specific regulatory factors, and environmental influences. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for unraveling the context-dependent roles of MBNL1 and developing therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting specific isoforms or regulatory pathways in a cancer-type-specific manner.
Although significant progress has been made, much remains to be explored about the MBNL protein family, especially its precise roles in various diseases and the mechanisms underlying these roles. To fully elucidate the biological functions of MBNL proteins, future research must prioritize interdisciplinary collaboration. Integrating bioinformatics, structural biology, pharmacology, and clinical medicine will be essential for addressing the complexity of MBNL-related regulatory networks. Tools like AlphaFold and advanced bioinformatics platforms for identifying RNA-binding targets of RBPs will be instrumental in uncovering previously uncharacterized functions and guiding rational drug design [ ].
While therapeutic strategies targeting MBNLs have been extensively studied in the context of DM, their potential application in other diseases is gaining attention. In cancer, MBNL1 has been shown to regulate the alternative splicing of oncogenes and tumor suppressors, such as NUMB and VEGFA, in multiple malignancies, including gastric, colorectal, and breast cancers. Although no direct therapies currently target MBNL proteins in cancer, modulating MBNL-regulated splicing pathways represents a promising avenue for treatment. In heart disease, MBNL proteins play critical roles in regulating fibrosis and tissue remodeling. MBNL2 promotes cardiac fibrosis through KLF4 SUMOylation and TGF-β1/SMAD3 signaling, while MBNL1 regulates myofibroblast differentiation by targeting serum response factor (SRF) and calmodulin phosphatase Ab mRNAs. Targeting these downstream pathways could provide an indirect means to mitigate MBNL-related pathological changes in cardiac conditions. In neurological disorders, MBNL dysfunction has been implicated in diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Huntington’s disease (HD). In AD, calpain-2-mediated degradation of MBNL2 disrupts RNA splicing, reverting neuronal splicing patterns to an embryonic state. Inhibiting calpain-2 activity has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy to restore normal MBNL2 function and mitigate cognitive decline. In HD, small molecules targeting mutant CAG RNA have shown preclinical efficacy in preventing MBNL sequestration, restoring RNA processing, and alleviating disease symptoms. Despite these promising directions, significant challenges remain, including the development of tissue-specific delivery methods, minimizing off-target effects, and understanding the disease-specific roles of MBNLs. These limitations highlight the need for further investigation into MBNL-targeted therapies across a broader spectrum of diseases.
Ultimately, future research should also focus on the context-specific roles of MBNL proteins in the broader cellular and disease environments. As MBNL proteins are highly sensitive to regulatory networks and microenvironmental factors, addressing these context-dependent effects will be key to uncovering their full potential as therapeutic targets. By combining the strengths of multiple disciplines and leveraging emerging technologies, the intricate roles of MBNL proteins can be unraveled, leading to transformative advances in both understanding and treatment of MBNL-related diseases.
