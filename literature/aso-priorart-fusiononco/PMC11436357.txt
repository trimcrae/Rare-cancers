However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these changes are largely unknown. In this study, we identified RNA Exonuclease 5 (REXO5/LOC81691) as a novel gene with elevated mRNA expression levels in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) patients. Additionally, using the REXO5 knockout (KO) K562 cell lines, we revealed a novel role for REXO5 in the DNA damage response (DDR). Compared to wild-type (WT) cells, REXO5 KO cells showed an accumulation of R-loops and increased DNA damage. We demonstrated that REXO5 translocates to sites of DNA damage through its RNA recognition motif (RRM) and selectively binds to R loops. Interestingly, we identified that REXO5 regulates R-loop levels by degrading mRNA within R-loop using its exonuclease domain. REXO5 KO showed ATR-CHK1 activation. Collectively, we demonstrated that REXO5 plays a key role in the physiological control of R-loops using its exonuclease domain. These findings may provide novel insights into how REXO5 expression changes contribute to CML pathogenesis.

## Introduction
Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a malignant myeloproliferative disorder that arises from the clonal expansion of a transformed multipotent hematopoietic stem cell [ ]. The hallmark of CML lies in the generation of the BCR::ABL1 chimeric oncoprotein, endowed with constitutive ABL1 tyrosine kinase activity [ , ]. The aberrant ABL1 tyrosine kinase activity orchestrates a cascade promoting cell survival and proliferation while concurrently suppressing apoptosis [ ]. The advent of ABL1-specific tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has revolutionized CML therapy, exhibiting selective efficacy against BCR::ABL1-positive CML cells both in vitro [ ] and in vivo [ ]. TKIs targeting BCR::ABL1, such as imatinib, nilotinib, and dasatinib, constitute the standard therapy for CML. Resistance to these TKIs is associated with a grim prognosis due to primary or acquired resistance in some patients. In the chronic phase (CP), TKI resistance heightens the risk of disease progression to advanced stages [ – ]. Consequently, TKIs have become integral components in the clinical management of CML [ ].
Genetic materials are always exposed to a variety of risks, resulting in a build-up of mutations that lead to significant defects in genomic integrity [ ]. Unless those genetic lesions were not properly recovered, the function of genes is impaired and resulting genetic instability leads to diseases including cancer [ ]. Against the genotoxic stress, cells have evolved a variety of controlled signaling pathways, and a series of processes for sensing and repairing DNA damages called DNA damage response (DDR) is activated for maintaining genetic integrity [ , ]. DNA double-strand break (DSB) is the most deleterious type of damage which would cause genetic defects including chromosome translocation, deletion, or mutant [ ]. Homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) are two major mechanisms for DSB repair and these pathways are sophisticatedly elaborately controlled according to the cell cycle. HR is preferred in S/G2 phases since sister chromatid is required as templates, whereas NHEJ tends to utilize in G1 phase [ , ].
Besides the HR and NHEJ, multiple mechanisms contribute to maintaining genomic stability including R-loops. R-loops are nucleic acid structures of RNA-DNA hybrids which formed when an RNA strand invades double-stranded DNA during transcription displacing non-template DNA strand as single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) [ ]. R-loops involve in lots of physiological processes, but recent studies revealed that R-loops could be a source of DNA damage while also could both inhibit or promote DNA repair after DNA damage [ – ]. Several factors are regulating R-loops carefully, such as RNA binding and processing factors, helicases, and nucleases [ , ]. The p210 BCR::ABL1 fusion protein compromises the DNA damage response by interfering with DNA repair mechanisms, disrupting cell cycle checkpoints, and directly causing DNA damage. This multifaceted disruption contributes to the genomic instability observed in CML, driving disease progression and resistance to therapy [ – ].
REXO5 (RNA Exonuclease 5), also known as NEF-Sp/LOC81691, is identified as a highly conserved exonuclease of Rexo family in eukaryotes from a genome-wide study of human RNA-binding proteins. REXO5 is highly conserved 3′-5′ RNA exonuclease with single-stranded RNA activity, known to perform crucial roles in RNA processing and germline development [ , ]. REXO5 belongs to the Rexo family in eukaryotes, known as Rex proteins in S. cerevisiae. While not functionally characterized in higher eukaryotes, the role in maturation of rRNA and small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) in S. cerevisiae and Drosophila was reported [ – ]. Moreover, the function of REXO5 in DNA repair and R-loop formation is yet unrevealed. Since REXO5 has nuclease activity and contains RNA recognition motif (RRM), REXO5 may involve in regulating resolving R-loops in response to DNA damages.
In this study, REXO5 is first identified as a novel gene of which mRNA expression level was elevated in CML patients. In addition, REXO5 accumulates at the DNA damage site after laser microirradiation via its RNA recognition motif (RRM). Furthermore, REXO5 regulates R-loop levels by degrading mRNA in R-loop using its exonuclease domain. REXO5 KO showed ATR-CHK1 activation and had enhanced sensitivity to ATR inhibitor. Collectively, we demonstrate that REXO5 plays a key role in the physiological control of R-loops using its exonuclease domain. These findings may provide novel insights into how REXO5 expression changes contribute to CML development or transformation from chronic phase (CP) to blast phase (BP).

## Materials and methods

## Patient’s samples
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from 6 chronic phase CML (CML-CP) patients after diagnosis and 6 healthy donors for microarray analysis. For Western blotting analysis and qPCR experiment, mononuclear cells (MCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) density gradient centrifugation. Samples were viably frozen in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich), stored in liquid nitrogen, and later thawed for analysis. All human samples were provided from the Korea Leukemia Bank and approved by institutional review board (IRB NO: UEMC 2022-01-019). Patient consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Statistical analyses
All results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of the determinations. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using two-way ANOVA. For not normally distributed, Mann–Whitney test was used. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 software (GraphPad).
The details of other experimental methods are described in the supplementary information.

## Results
There has been considerable research on CML, a hematopoietic cancer characterized by abnormal proliferation of myeloid precursor cells, a lack of lymphoid differentiation, and reduced apoptosis [ , ]. To identify candidate genes that may play a crucial role in the onset of CML, we performed microarray-based transcriptome screening using samples from six CML-CP patients at diagnosis and six healthy donors (Fig.  ). Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis of microarray data revealed 735 up-regulated genes and 694 down-regulated genes in CML patients (Fig.  , Supplementary Table  , and Supplementary Fig.  ). The principal component analysis (PCA) conducted on up-regulated genes revealed an elevation in the expression level of REXO5, a newly identified gene, in CP at diagnosis in comparison to healthy donors (Fig.  ). Subsequent analysis of mRNA and protein expression levels in serial patient samples, collected at various time points since diagnosis, demonstrated higher expression of REXO5 in BP compared to CP or accelerated phase (AP) (Fig.  ). This result shows that REXO5 might increase with leukemia progression. Furthermore, cohort analysis using TCGA data (https://xena.ucsc.edu/) revealed that REXO5 exhibits the highest expression in acute myeloid leukemia cell line (LAML) compared to various cancer types (Fig.  ). 

## REXO5 depletion induces global DNA damage
To further analyze REXO5 expression in cells of myeloid malignancies, we conducted Western blotting analysis on various cell lines, including HEK293T, HeLa, CML cell line (K562), AML cell lines (THP-1, U937, MOLM-14, KG-1, Kasumi-1, HL-60), and MDS cell line (SKM-1). REXO5 is expressed in myeloid cell lines including K562 CML cell (Fig.  ), suggesting its potential involvement in the initiation or progression of myeloid malignancies. To determine the effects of REXO5 inhibition on human CML cell proliferation and survival, we employed knockout (KO) (Supplementary Fig.  – ) or knockdown (KD) strategies using REXO5-specific guide RNA or siRNAs in K562 CML cells. Western blot analysis confirmed successful inhibition of REXO5 expression in REXO5 KO or KD K562 CML cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). These results demonstrated a substantial suppression of cell growth in REXO5 KO or KD K562 CML cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating a promotional role of REXO5 in human CML cell growth. We further performed the cell cycle analysis using REXO5 WT and KO K562 cells. Analysis of the ability of REXO5 KO cells to incorporate EdU by flow cytometry revealed a decrease in the percentage of EdU-positive cells that is accompanied by more cells are in G1 and less in S-phase (Fig.  ). REXO5 is closely related to the REXO1/Rex 1 family and contains RNase T domain which enables REXO5 to function as an RNA exonuclease, and paired RRM motifs suggesting RNA binding feature of REXO5 [ , ]. Given the known functions of RNA binding proteins and nucleases in DNA damage by regulating R-loop formation [ – ], we explored whether REXO5 is involved in the DNA damage response pathway. Increased DNA damage, evidenced by elevated levels of γH2AX, was observed in REXO5 KO or KD K562 CML cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , ). Next, we assessed whether REXO5 affects R-loop formation. REXO5 KO or KD increased R-loop signals which are visualized by S9.6 immunofluorescence (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , ), indicating accumulation of R-loops. Overexpression of GFP-RNase H1 inhibits S9.6 signal induced by REXO5 depletion, confirming the role of REXO5 in R-loop regulation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , ). We further confirmed that overexpression of GFP-RNase H1 restored γH2AX signal induced by REXO5 KO (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , ). Significant increased levels of R-loop induced by REXO5 KO were repeatedly observed in assays using dotting assay and R-loop signals is shown to be reduced when REXO5 KO cells were treated with recombinant RNase H (Fig.  ). We also observed that overexpression of GFP-RNase H1 restored cell growth inhibition induced by REXO5 depletion (Fig.  ). To determine whether the functions of REXO5 associated with DNA damage are generally functional in leukemia, we generated the REXO5 KO AML-derived cell line Kasumi-1 (Supplementary Fig.  – ) and the CML-derived cell line MEG01 (Supplementary Fig.  – ). And upon REXO5 KO, we observed decreased cell proliferation and accumulation of γH2AX and S9.6 levels in both Kasumi-1 (Supplementary Fig.  – ) and MEG01 (Supplementary Fig.  – ). 

## REXO5 is recruited to the DNA damage sites through its RRM domain
To function as a player in R-loop processing, REXO5 should be spatially located at the loci of R-loop. We performed the spatiotemporal interaction between REXO5 and R-loop using the proximity ligation assay (PLA) with antibodies targeting S9.6 and GFP-REXO5. The PLA signals, depicted in red, demonstrated that REXO5 physically accumulated at the loci of R-loops in K562 cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, we confirmed REXO5′s presence at R-loop through immunoprecipitation with S9.6 antibody in HEK 293 T and K562 cells (Fig.  ). The binding between REXO5 and R-loops was further affirmed using purified GST-REXO5 (Fig.  ). These findings collectively support the recruitment of REXO5 to R-loops and indicate its potential role in both the formation and resolution of R-loops. We next examined the recruitment of REXO5 to DNA damage sites using a laser microirradiation assay. GFP-REXO5 is recruited on laser stripes within 1 min after irradiation and persisted at later time points (Fig.  ) and colocalized with γH2AX, a hallmark of DNA damage sites (Fig.  ). These findings suggest that GFP-REXO5 translocates to DNA damage sites. Furthermore, to identify the specific region of REXO5 responsible for the translocation to DNA damage site, a series of internal deletion mutants of GFP-REXO5 (GFP-REXO5 D1-NLS to D5, N and C) (Fig.  ) were constructed. The analysis for the candidate nuclear localization signal (NLS) sequence of REXO5 was predicted using the NLS Mapper program (http://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp) and revealed that REXO5 contains a NLS at residues 43–55. To compensate this, a REXO5 internal deletion mutant which contains NLS was constructed (GFP-REXO5 D1-NLS). As shown in Fig.  , REXO5 WT as well as D1-NLS, D2, D3 and D5 deletion mutants localized to the laser stripe, whereas only REXO5 D4 lacking two RNA recognizing motifs (RRM) failed to accumulate on the laser stripe. Moreover, the requirement of RRM motif has been demonstrated in further experiment using N-terminal and C-terminal constructs of REXO5, showing that REXO5 C including RRM motif accumulated on the laser-induced DNA damage lesion, while REXO5 N did not. To further confirm that the RRM domains were responsible for REXO5 translocation to DNA damage sites, we generated a deletion mutant comprising the RRM domains only (REXO5-RRM-RRM; Fig.  ) and observed accumulation of REXO5-RRM-RRM at DNA damage sites (Fig.  ). Together, REXO5 RRM motif is required for the accumulation to sites of DNA damage. 

## REXO5 depletion causes R-loop-dependent replication fork stalling
The dysregulation of transcription machinery regulation acts as a barrier to the progression of replication forks, resulting in collisions between transcription and replication processes known as Transcription-Replication Conflicts (TRC). These conflicts lead to fork collapse and trigger the ATR-dependent DNA damage response associated with replication stress. Our observation of cells depleted of REXO5, which displayed an accumulation of R-loops, led us to hypothesize that this accumulation induces replication stress and slows down replication forks. To test this hypothesis, we conducted experiments using REXO5 KO K562 cells. Employing EdU pulse-labeling to identify cells in the S-phase, we subsequently immunostained the cells for γH2AX and examined them through fluorescence microscopy to compare EdU and γH2AX signals. As shown in Fig.  , REXO5-depleted cells exhibited the reduction of EdU signal and accumulation of γH2AX foci only in EdU-positive cells. This suggests that unregulated R-loop accumulation, resulting from REXO5 deletion, causes DNA damage during the S-phase due to collisions between transcription and replication machinery. To further investigate this hypothesis, we measured the frequency of transcription-replication conflicts in REXO5-depleted cells using a proximity ligation assay (PLA) with antibodies targeting PCNA and RNA polymerase II [ ]. Notably, REXO5 depletion significantly increased transcription-replication conflicts, as indicated by PCNA-POLII PLA foci (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we examined fork slowing mediated by these conflicts by performing a DNA fiber assay in REXO5-depleted cells. In this assay, we labeled thymidine analogs (CIdU: 5-chloro-2′-deoxyuridine, IdU: 5-Iodo-2′-deoxyuridine) to assess replication fork speed. The results showed that the CIdU and IdU track lengths, as well as the overall fork speed, were decreased in REXO5-depleted cells (Fig.  ). Moreover, treatment with the transcription inhibitor (triptolide) reduced DSBs in REXO5-depleted cells (Fig.  ). This implies that the depletion of REXO5, leading to R-loop formation, disrupts the replication fork process through transcription-replication conflicts. DNA damage occurring in the S-phase typically triggers the ATR-dependent DNA damage response for replication stress, activating cell cycle checkpoints that facilitate DNA repair and restore replication fork integrity, thereby preventing genomic instability [ , ]. This replication stress DNA damage checkpoint involves the recruitment of RPA, ATRIP, and ATR to stretches of exposed single-stranded DNA at stalled forks. ATR then initiates a series of phosphorylation events, ultimately resulting in the recruitment, phosphorylation, and activation of Chk1 and the induction of the intra-S and/or G2/M checkpoints [ ]. To assess replication stress in our system, we analyzed the ATR-mediated DNA damage signaling. We observed increased phosphorylation of RPA and CHK1 (RPA2 pS4/S8, RPA2 pS33, CHK1 pS317, CHK1 pS345) in REXO5-depleted cells (Fig.  ). This suggests that unregulated R-loop formation leads to transcription-replication conflicts, promoting replication stress DNA damage response and fork stalling. 

## REXO5 control the R-loop level via its exonuclease activity
REXO5 has been characterized as a highly conserved nuclear 3′-5′ RNA exonuclease with the ability to degrade single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) while sparing single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) [ ]. Thus, we investigated the involvement of REXO5 in R-loop resolution through its nucleolytic activity. For this, we first purified the recombinant REXO5 protein using a baculovirus-insect cell expression system (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we synthesized a fluorophore (Cy3)-conjugated RNA/DNA hybrid along with R-loop structures (Supplementary Fig.  , ). The purified recombinant proteins GST-REXO5 underwent a nuclease assay with a Cy3-conjugated RNA/DNA hybrid (Supplementary Fig.  ). Our findings revealed that REXO5 exhibited the degradation of RNA within the hybrid. We also performed in vitro nuclease assay with R-loop substrates. We found that purified GST-REXO5 protein degraded the R-loop (Fig.  ). We further conducted the in vitro nuclease assay using three purified proteins: REXO5 WT, an exonuclease dead mutant (ExoIII M; E234A/D315A), and a deletion mutant D4 (Fig.  ). As shown in Fig.  , the REXO5 wild-type variant exhibited efficient RNA degradation within the R-loop structure. In contrast, both the REXO5 ExoIII M and REXO5 D4 mutants failed to accomplish this RNA degradation. These results underscore the critical role of the exonuclease activity of REXO5 wild-type in the breakdown of RNA within the R-loop structure. 

## REXO5 overexpression rescues R-loop and R-loop-mediated genome instability in an exonuclease-dependent manner
We investigated the effects of overexpressing wild-type (WT) and exonuclease-deficient mutants of REXO5 on suppressing R-loop accumulation and R-loop-mediated genome instability. For this purpose, we generated the REXO5-WT, -ExoIII M, and -D4 expressing stable REXO5 KO#1 cells (REXO5KO#1-REX05-WT, -ExoIII M, and -D4) (Fig.  ). Initially, we assessed the restoration of R-loop levels in these cell lines using a dot blot assay. The results shown in Fig.  indicate a reduction in R-loop signals specifically in REXO5-WT-expressing REXO5KO#1-REXO5-WT cells. Subsequently, we examined DNA damage through comet assay, R-loop accumulation, and γH2AX foci formation. Figure  demonstrated that the expression of REXO5-WT reduced the accumulation of R-loops and γH2AX foci as well as R-loop-dependent genomic instability. In contrast, the two mutant forms failed to suppress the elevated levels of DNA damage. Additionally, our analysis revealed that only REXO5-WT expression restored cell proliferation (Fig.  ). Lastly, our observations revealed that REXO5 KO#1, -ExoIII M, and -D4 cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to an ATR inhibitor (VE-821, Fig.  ). Furthermore, we generated the REXO5-WT, -ExoIII M, and -D4 expressing stable REXO5 KO#1 Kasumi-1 cells (REXO5KO#1-REX05-WT, -ExoIII M, and -D4) (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also observed that the expressing only REXO5-WT in REXO5 KO Kasumi-1 cells reduced the accumulation of R-loops and γH2AX foci as well as R-loop-dependent genomic instability, and restored cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig.  ). In summary, our findings suggest that REXO5 possesses the capability to utilize its RNA exonuclease activity to eliminate R-loops. 

## Discussion
The research findings presented in this study highlight the significant role of REXO5 in the pathogenesis and progression of CML. By identifying the REXO5 exonuclease network as a potential biomarker for early myeloid leukemia, this study may contribute significant to our understanding of hematologic malignancies. In this discussion, we will provide a broader implication of these findings within the context of existing research on hematological malignancies, with a specific focus on DNA damage response (DDR) and RNA processing mechanisms. This is consistent with existing studies highlighting the importance of DDR in leukemia [ – ]. Like many other leukemias, CML is characterized by genetic instability and increased DNA damage [ , ]. This study further strengthens this understanding by indicating the involvement of REXO5-mediated DNA damage response and R-loop formation in CML pathology.
Distinct patterns of REXO5 expression across disease stages, from absence in normal cells to strong expression in blast phase (BP) samples, suggest its potential contribution to disease progression through induction of DNA damage. However, although this study hints a correlation between REXO5 expression and BCR::ABL1 gene fusion, further investigation is needed to fully elucidate this aspect.
The involvement of REXO5 in RNA processing, particularly its role in regulating R-loop formation, provides a novel perspective on the molecular mechanisms driving CML. The study’s finding that REXO5 depletion leads to increased R-loop accumulation and subsequent DNA damage underscores its role in maintaining genomic stability. This is particularly significant given that similar mechanisms have been observed in other myeloid malignancies. For instance, in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), mutations in genes such as SRSF2, U2AF1, SF3B1, and PIWIL4 have been linked to aberrant RNA processing and R-loop accumulation, contributing to DNA damage and disease progression [ – ]. By identifying REXO5 as a regulator of R-loop dynamics in CML, the current study bridges a gap in the understanding of RNA processing-related DNA damage across different types of leukemia.
Functional studies demonstrating the critical role of REXO5 in promoting human CML cell growth further underscore its significance in disease pathogenesis. The association of REXO5 with the REXO1/Rex 1 family, its RNase T domain, and paired RRM motifs suggested its involvement in RNA processing. Subsequent experiments revealed that REXO5 depletion induced global DNA damage, with increased levels of γH2AX and R-loop accumulation. The mechanistic link between REXO5 and DNA damage was further supported by experiments showing the abrogation of DNA damage signals upon overexpression of GFP-RNase H1, emphasizing the regulatory role of REXO5 in R-loop formation. Additionally, the study’s exploration of REXO5′s impact on replication fork dynamics suggests a model wherein REXO5 depletion leads to replication fork stalling, exacerbating transcription-replication conflicts and ATR-mediated DNA damage signaling.
Crucially, the study provided insights into the molecular mechanism underlying REXO5′s function. The exonuclease activity of REXO5 was demonstrated through in vitro assays, showing its ability to degrade RNA within the R-loop structure. The rescue experiments with overexpression of wild-type and exonuclease-dead mutants of REXO5 further emphasized the exonuclease-dependent role of REXO5 in mitigating R-loop accumulation and associated genome instability.
In summary, this study positions REXO5 as an important regulator of DNA damage response pathways in CML, highlighting its role in R-loop formation and genome stability. By extending the understanding of RNA processing-related DNA damage mechanisms to CML, this research paves the way for novel therapeutic approaches targeting REXO5 and similar RNA processing enzymes. Further research into REXO5′s functions and interactions will be crucial for developing targeted therapies that can improve outcomes for patients with myeloid malignancies characterized by dysregulated DNA damage responses.
