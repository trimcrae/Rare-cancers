## Background
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is recognized as the most prevalent type of lung cancer worldwide, with delayed diagnosis significantly contributing to high mortality rates and poor overall survival [ , ]. Despite intensive research aimed at identifying biomarkers for early detection, prognosis, and treatment stratification, a highly sensitive and specific biomarker has yet to be discovered [ , ]. This gap underscores the urgent need for reliable biomarkers that can facilitate early diagnosis, limit disease progression, and ultimately improve patient survival. Achieving these goals requires a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underpin NSCLC development and progression.
In recent years, epigenetic modifications have emerged as critical contributors to cancer pathogenesis, with extensive research focusing on their role in NSCLC [ – ]. Epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and regulation by noncoding RNAs, profoundly influence gene expression and tumor behavior [ , ]. DNA methylation, in particular, has been scrutinized extensively since its discovery. Aberrant methylation patterns, characterized by the hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters coupled with global hypomethylation, are known to facilitate tumor initiation, maintenance, and progression [ – ]. Among these alterations, hypomethylation of long interspersed nuclear element 1 (LINE1), the most abundant repetitive element in the human genome, has been closely associated with genomic instability and dysregulated gene expression in a range of diseases, including cancer [ – ]. Clinical studies have linked LINE1 hypomethylation to adverse outcomes, such as poor prognosis, reduced recurrence-free survival, and increased tumor invasiveness [ , , ]. Moreover, LINE1 hypomethylation has been detected in liquid biopsies, including cell-free DNA (cfDNA), extending its relevance beyond tumor tissues and highlighting its potential as a minimally invasive biomarker for NSCLC diagnosis and prognosis [ , ]. These findings have prompted speculation that LINE1 methylation could also serve as a therapeutic target for NSCLC, reinforcing the need for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies aimed at this epigenetic modification.
Given the promising implications of LINE1 methylation in NSCLC, further exploration into its role in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment is both timely and crucial. This review aimed to provide an updated and comprehensive summary of the current knowledge surrounding the potential of LINE1 methylation as a biomarker and therapeutic target in NSCLC. It incorporated findings from experimental and clinical studies to offer a holistic perspective while identifying key areas for future research in this evolving field.

## Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, responsible for approximately 1.8 million deaths and 18% of all cancer cases annually [ ]. It is identified as the most prevalent cancer type, with an estimated 2.5 million new cases reported globally [ ]. The five-year survival rate following a lung cancer diagnosis typically ranges from 10 to 20% [ , ]. A significant proportion of lung cancer-related deaths worldwide is attributed to modifiable risk factors, including tobacco consumption, elevated levels of outdoor air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and inhalation of smoke from burning solid fuels during cooking or agricultural activities [ , ]. Additionally, occupational exposures, such as asbestos and radon, along with comorbidities like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and respiratory infections, further contribute to lung cancer mortality, particularly among underground workers [ , , ]. Non-modifiable factors, including age, family history, gender, and ethnicity, have also been implicated in lung cancer risk, influencing cellular differentiation, cancer metastasis, and overall survival outcomes in affected patients [ ].
According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2021), lung cancer is classified based on morphological characteristics and immunohistochemical staining into two major subtypes: small-cell lung cancer (SCLC), which accounts for 15% of cases, and NSCLC, which constitutes 85% of cases. NSCLC is further categorized into three primary histological subtypes: lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and lung large-cell carcinoma (LULC) [ – ]. Among these, LUAD is the most prevalent, followed by LUSC, while LULC is the least common form of NSCLC [ ]. Accurate and timely identification of lung cancer subtypes and stages is critical for prognosis and treatment decision-making [ ]. Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), often combined with chest X-ray, is the primary imaging modality for early detection of NSCLC in high-risk populations [ ]. This imaging approach provides critical information regarding tumor size, shape, and location and is instrumental in monitoring treatment response [ , ]. Additional imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) scans, are utilized to assess disease progression. Staging is determined using the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification system, which evaluates tumor size and extent (T), lymph node involvement (N), and metastasis (M). This system provides valuable prognostic insights and guides treatment strategies [ – ].Despite advances in imaging technology, approximately 70% of NSCLC cases are diagnosed at advanced stages [ ]. Diagnosis of NSCLC subtypes typically requires invasive procedures such as fine-needle aspiration and exfoliative techniques to obtain biopsy and cytology specimens [ ]. 
Beyond diagnosis, effective treatment strategies are pivotal in influencing disease progression and patient outcomes. For early-stage NSCLC (stage I–II), surgical resection remains the most effective therapeutic option. Across both early and advanced stages of the disease, platinum-based chemotherapy, with or without radiotherapy, continues to be the standard of care [ , ]. However, recent advances in molecular technologies have expanded therapeutic options, incorporating targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Targeted therapies are specifically designed to address distinct genetic mutations or rearrangements characteristic of NSCLC subtypes. For example, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) inhibitors are employed in advanced-stage LUAD patients harboring mutations in EGFR, ALK, or proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase ROS1 (ROS1) [ , , ]. Additionally, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression has emerged as a key biomarker guiding the use of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies in patients with recurrent tumors following platinum-based chemotherapy [ ].
Despite significant advancements in NSCLC treatment, resistance to current therapeutic options has become increasingly prevalent, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies [ , ]. Therefore, it is essential to conduct comprehensive research into the molecular mechanisms underpinning NSCLC tumorigenesis to identify new therapeutic targets and develop more effective treatment modalities.

## Epigenetic dysregulation in NSCLC
The regulation of gene expression is a highly intricate process governed by both genetic and epigenetic modifications. Epigenetic mechanisms play a pivotal role in controlling gene expression through chemical modifications or interactions between structural and regulatory proteins with DNA, all while preserving the integrity of the genetic sequence [ , ]. The primary epigenetic mechanisms include histone modification, non-coding RNAs, and DNA methylation [ ]. These processes are significantly influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors, such as diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, physiological stress, and exposure to environmental pollutants [ , ]. Aberrant epigenetic modifications have been strongly implicated in various diseases, particularly cancer, including NSCLC [ , , ].
Histone modifications are orchestrated by the enzymatic activities of histone acetyltransferases (HATs), histone deacetylases (HDACs), histone methyltransferases (HMTs), and histone demethylases (HDMs) [ ]. Numerous studies identified abnormal histone modifications in NSCLC [ , – ]. Notably, early-stage LUAD is characterized by increased acetylation of histone (H) 4 at lysines (K) 5 and 8, along with demethylation of H4 at K20 [ , ]. Additionally, altered modifications in H2 and H3, including increased acetylation of H2AK5 (H2AK5Ac) and H3K9 (H3K9Ac) as well as demethylation of H3K4 (H3K4diMe), have been observed in early-stage NSCLC [ ]. Interestingly, HDAC1 expression was significantly elevated in patients with early-stage NSCLC compared to those with advanced-stage disease, further highlighting the role of histone modifications in disease progression [ ].
Besides histone modifications, the regulation of non-coding RNAs,including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs),is closely linked to DNA methylation. This intricate regulatory interplay promotes cellular proliferation, tissue invasion, and other malignant processes that contribute to the pathogenesis of NSCLC [ , , ].

## DNA methylation in NSCLC
DNA methylation, the first identified epigenetic modification, remains a cornerstone of cancer research due to its critical role in gene regulation and tumorigenesis [ , ]. This process governs several fundamental biological mechanisms, including gene silencing, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and the regulation of tissue-specific gene expression [ , , ]. In mammalian somatic cells, over 98% of DNA methylation occurs at CG dinucleotides, where a methyl group is covalently attached to the 5th carbon of cytosine bases. This reaction is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), primarily DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B [ ]. De novo DNA methylation is governed by DNMT3A and DNMT3B and DNMT1 maintains the methylation [ ].
DNA methylation suppresses gene expression through two primary mechanisms: first, by inhibiting transcription factors from binding to their DNA regulatory regions, and second, by recruiting methyl-binding proteins (MBPs), which subsequently activate histone deacetylases to establish a heterochromatin structure [ ]. Conversely, gene reactivation occurs through demethylation, a process mediated by the Ten-eleven translocation (TET) methylcytosine dioxygenases. This dynamic balance between methylation and demethylation is essential for maintaining proper gene expression [ , ]. Dysregulation of DNMT and TET enzyme activities has been widely implicated in cancer. Elevated DNMT1 expression was reportedly correlated with poor prognostic outcomes in NSCLC [ ], while DNMT3A knockdown in LUAD mouse models demonstrated its role in tumor growth and progression [ ]. Furthermore, TET enzyme dysregulation was observed in NSCLC, with TET1 upregulation in 40–70% of tumors, whereas TET2 overexpression was linked to reduced proliferation and metastasis in LUAD [ , , ]. These enzymes regulate methylation levels within CG dinucleotides, which are non-uniformly distributed across the genome and predominantly clustered within CpG islands [ ]. Approximately 60% of human genes contain CpG islands within their promoter regions, which remain largely unmethylated across various tissue types to allow transcription [ , ]. However, hypermethylation of promoter CpG islands is a hallmark of tumorigenesis, leading to the silencing of key tumor suppressor genes. In NSCLC, aberrant promoter hypermethylation was frequently observed in genes such as cadherin 13 (CDH13), cadherin 1 (CDH1), death-associated protein kinase (DAPK), O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A/p16), and Ras association domain family member 1 A (RASSF1A) [ , , , ]. In contrast, global DNA hypomethylation, often associated with genomic instability and cancer progression, predominantly affects repetitive sequences, which constitute approximately two-thirds of the human genome [ – ]. Given these findings, the methylation status of repetitive elements presents a promising avenue for the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in NSCLC.

## The role of LINE1 retrotransposons in cancer development and NSCLC
The human genome consists of merely 1.2% coding sequences, with the majority comprising non-coding DNA, often referred to as “junk” DNA due to its repetitive nature [ , ]. However, emerging research revealed that non-coding DNA elements play a crucial role in gene evolution and regulation [ , ]. Nearly half of the human genome is composed of transposable elements (TEs), also known as mobile DNA [ , ]. Generally, TEs are classified into two categories based on their mechanisms of action: Class I (retrotransposons), which propagate via an RNA intermediate using a “copy-and-paste” mechanism, and Class II (DNA transposons), which mobilize through a “cut-and-paste” mechanism without RNA involvement [ ]. Among these, retrotransposons are the most abundant in humans, with LINE1 being the predominant type, constituting approximately 17% of the genome [ , ].

## LINE1 retrotransposition mechanism and its regulation by DNA methylation
The distinctive bicistronic structure of LINE1 facilitates its autonomous replication by generating RNA intermediates and the requisite proteins for retrotransposition. Furthermore, these RNA-derived proteins assist in the mobilization of non-autonomous retroelements, including short interspersed nuclear elements (SINE) such as Alu and SINE-VNTR-Alus (SVA) [ , ]. A full-length LINE1 spans ~ 6 kb and consists of an AT-rich 5’ untranslated region (UTR), two open reading frames (ORF1 and ORF2) separated by a 63-bp linker, and a 3’ UTR that includes a polyadenylation signal [ – ]. Retrotransposition is initiated by RNA polymerase II, which transcribes LINE1 from an internal promoter located in the CpG-rich 5’ UTR, producing a 5’ capped and 3’ polyadenylated transcript (LINE1RNA) [ ]. LINE1RNA is subsequently translated into two proteins: ORF2 protein (ORF1p, 40-kDa) – RNA-binding protein with nucleic acid chaperone activity, and ORF2 protein (ORF2p, 150-kDa) – a multifunctional protein with endonuclease (EN) and reverse transcriptase (RT) activity [ , ]. Both ORF1p and ORF2p bind to LINE1RNA in a cis-preferential manner, forming LINE1 ribonucleoproteins (LINE1RNPs) that are transported into the nucleus [ , ]. Once inside, ORF2p introduces nicks in the host DNA, generating 3’ hydroxyl (3’OH) ends, which serve as primers for target-site primed reverse transcription (TPRT). Using LINE1RNA as a template, ORF2p synthesizes complementary DNA (cDNA), completing the integration of LINE1 into the genome [ , ]. The human genome contains approximately 100,000 LINE1 copies, yet only 80–100 remain retrotranspositionally active due to mutations, internal rearrangements, and 5’ truncations [ , ]. ## LINE1 and cancer: retrotransposition-dependent and independent mechanisms
Numerous studies showed that LINE1 elements contribute to carcinogenesis through both retrotransposition-dependent and independent mechanisms [ , – ]. LINE1-derived regulatory RNA, including miRNAs, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and lncRNAs, as well as LINE1 chimeric transcripts and LINE1-mediated transcriptional interference, represents retrotransposition-independent functions of LINE1 that contribute to unfavorable gene regulation and expression, thereby promoting cancer [ , , , ]. The inactivation of target genes, chromosomal rearrangements, and epigenetic modifications represent the retrotransposition-dependent functions of LINE1 that facilitate carcinogenesis. LINE1 insertions have the potential to inactivate tumor suppressor genes or activate oncogenes [ , , ]. Additionally, LINE1 insertions can introduce pseudogenes, premature stop codons, polyadenylation sites, 3’ transduction, genomic deletions, translocations, duplications, and inversions, thereby instigating chromosomal rearrangements that lead to genomic instability, a critical factor in cancer development [ , , , – ]. ORF1p exerts its oncogenic effect by facilitating cellular proliferation, invasion, and the activation of oncogenes [ – ]. Furthermore, ORF1p and ORF2p are notably expressed across various cancers, including breast cancer, pediatric germ cell tumors, gastric cancer, and lung cancer, particularly NSCLC [ , , ].

## LINE1 as a biomarker and therapeutic target of NSCLC
Given its oncogenic role, ORF2p is being investigated as a therapeutic target aimed at reversing carcinogenesis and reinstating cellular differentiation through the use of reverse transcriptase inhibitors [ , ]. Given that LINE1 is a crucial regulator of retrotransposition, its methylation status has been extensively studied in cancer development and progression [ , – ].

In normal somatic cells, the CpG islands within the internal promoters of LINE1 are typically methylated, leading to retrotransposon silencing [ , ]. However, LINE1 elements in tumor cells exhibit hypomethylation and an elevated copy number, suggesting the activation of retrotransposition [ , – ]. Given its status as the most abundant repetitive element in the human genome, LINE1 methylation might serve as a valuable metric for evaluating global DNA methylation levels. A variety of malignancies, including NSCLC, were associated with cancer progression in relation to LINE1 methylation [ , , , , , , ]. Daskalos et al. demonstrated that LINE1 hypomethylation was correlated with genomic instability in NSCLC, suggesting that LINE1 activation plays a pivotal role in lung tumorigenesis [ ]. Consequently, it is plausible to hypothesize that LINE1 methylation could serve as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target in NSCLC.

 

## LINE1 methylation as a potential biomarker of NSCLC
A limited number of studies explored the relationship between LINE1 methylation and NSCLC. In these investigations, LINE1 promoter methylation levels were observed to be significantly lower in malignant tissues compared to non-malignant tissues of NSCLC patients [ , , – ]. A majority of studies focused on the diagnostic and prognostic utility of LINE1 methylation in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most prevalent NSCLC subtype [ , , ]. Notably, LINE1 hypomethylation was significantly associated with malignant features,including pleural, lymphatic, and vascular invasion, as well as elevated histological grade,contributing to disease progression in LUAD [ , ]. Furthermore, decreased LINE1 methylation was correlated with poorer recurrence-free survival (RFS) outcomes in stage IA LUAD patients [ ], and LINE1 hypomethylation has been identified as an independent prognostic factor in stage I NSCLC [ , ]. In contrast to these results, Ikeda et al. reported that LINE1 hypomethylation was associated with advanced-stage (stage II and III) LUAD with vascular invasion [ ]. The study also revealed that lower LINE1 methylation levels were significantly correlated with reduced disease-free survival (DFS). This association was exclusively noted in patients with stages II and III, with no such trend observed in stage I patients [ ]. Conversely, another study showed an inverse correlation between LINE1 methylation levels and prognosis in stage I LUAD, suggesting that LINE1 hypomethylation might be associated with improved survival outcomes in this cohort, although this finding remains singular [ ]. However, this remains the only study indicating an inverse relationship between LINE1 hypomethylation and improved survival outcomes in NSCLC patients.
Aside from LUAD, the relationship between LINE1 methylation and LUSC, the second most common subtype of NSCLC, has also been examined [ – , , , ]. Interestingly, most studies demonstrated significantly lower LINE1 methylation in LUSC compared to LUAD [ , , , ]. In addition to these, Kitkumthorn et al. evaluated LINE1 methylation levels in peripheral blood mononuclear cells across six cancer types, including LUSC, and observed a pronounced reduction in methylation in LUSC patients. They reported a significantly higher proportion of LINE1 loci exhibiting two unmethylated CpGs (% uCuC) compared to healthy controls. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further indicated that % uCuC could serve as a promising biomarker for distinguishing LUSC from healthy individuals, with a cutoff value of 37.15%, an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.769, a sensitivity of 86.11%, and a specificity of 54.17% [ ].
Tumor biopsies remain the standard for molecular analyses in cancer, aiding in the identification of molecular characteristics in advanced NSCLC to optimize patient management [ , ]. However, spatial and temporal heterogeneity in tissue biopsies can result in misclassification of NSCLC subtypes, potentially leading to erroneous clinical decisions regarding diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment [ ]. Moreover, the invasive nature of tissue collection can pose clinical challenges and may not always yield sufficient material for comprehensive biomolecular analysis [ ]. As an alternative, cfDNA obtained from liquid biopsies offers a non-invasive means to assess the molecular status of patients comprehensively [ , ]. Despite its potential, research into LINE1 methylation in cfDNA within the context of NSCLC remains sparse [ , , – ]. For instance, Gainetdinov et al. reported a significant difference in LINE1 methylation levels in cell-surface-bound circulating DNA (csbDNA) extracted from blood leukocytes and erythrocytes between NSCLC patients and healthy controls [ ]. While ROC analysis suggested the potential for csbDNA LINE1 methylation as a non-invasive biomarker, no significant differences were observed between early (I - II) and late (III - IV) stages of the disease [ ]. A comparative study further noted that although circulating plasma DNA did not exhibit significant differences, csbDNA from NSCLC patients showed a 1.4-fold reduction in LINE1 methylation compared to healthy individuals [ ]. Park et al. compared the discriminative accuracy of the Alu index versus LINE1 methylation in plasma cfDNA from lung cancer patients and healthy controls. The LINE1 methylation assay achieved a superior AUC of 0.848 (cut-off ≤ 89.65), with 75% sensitivity and 87.5% specificity, outperforming the Alu index in distinguishing NSCLC cases [ ]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), which originates directly from tumors, captures much greater molecular heterogeneity than cfDNA and is especially relevant in tracking minimal residual disease (MRD) and cancer recurrence [ , ]. Several studies investigated promoter methylation of oncogenes and tumor suppressors in ctDNA across various cancers including NSCLC [ , ]. These studies typically showed that hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes was correlated with poorer prognosis and maintains high specificity but low sensitivity, especially in early-stage disease, leading to false negatives [ ]. Given these limitations, investigating LINE1 methylation in ctDNA, particularly in MRD monitoring, recurrence, postoperative surveillance, and advanced-stage NSCLC, using high-precision techniques could enhance assay precision, sensitivity, and clinical relevance. Apart from cfDNA and ctDNA, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) offer another avenue for epigenomic biomarker development. Kitkumthorn et al. showed that LINE‑1 hypomethylation in PBMCs distinguishes NSCLC patients from healthy controls [ ]. However, validation in larger, independent cohorts is needed. Extracellular vesicle-associated DNA (EV-DNA) represents an emerging, stable, and disease-specific liquid biopsy substrate [ ]. Studies using droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), PNA-mediated PCR, and targeted NGS on EV-DNA from pleural effusions, plasma, and BALF have shown superior mutation detection and robust promoter methylation signatures of tumor suppressors, outperforming cfDNA in both specificity and sensitivity for NSCLC identification [ – ]. Although LINE1 methylation in EV-DNA remains underexplored, mechanistic studies in lung cancer cell lines demonstrated that LINE1 RNA can be horizontally transferred via EVs, triggering retrotransposition and elevating L1-RNA, ORF1p, ORF2p, and RT activity in recipient cells [ , ]. Bowers et al. further observed elevated EV-contained LINE‑1 mRNA and ORF1p in plasma from advanced NSCLC patients, mirroring tissue-level profiles [ ]. Additionally, decreased LINE1 methylation has been reported in exosomal DNA from gastric cancer patients [ ]. Since LINE1 activity is epigenetically regulated, investigating LINE1 methylation in EV-DNA as a noninvasive biomarker for NSCLC detection and progression is a highly promising research direction. Soukiasan et al. measured global DNA methylation (genomic 5-methylcytosine, 5mC) in exfoliated epithelial cells from patients with high-risk benign chronic lung disorders (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COPD and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, IPF) and NSCLC patients. They reported significantly lower 5mC levels in early-stage NSCLC, suggesting potential for early detection among high-risk populations [ ]. These findings encourage further investigation of LINE1 methylation in sputum as a field-based, noninvasive biomarker.
Despite the implications of LINE1 methylation in NSCLC, studies assessed the relationship between LINE1 methylation and other pathological conditions related to pulmonary diseases. Decreased LINE1 methylation was observed in early-stage NSCLC compared to COPD, bronchitis, and IPF patients [ , ]. Moreover, lower LINE1 methylation was observed in NSCLC patients with COPD compared to non-COPD NSCLC patients, but not statistically significant. Another study performed by Lange et al. reported a correlation between LINE1 hypomethylation and accelerated lung function decline in older individuals, though no direct link with COPD was established. However, no significant association was found between LINE1 methylation and COPD [ ]. These inconsistencies highlight the need for larger, more definitive studies to clarify the diagnostic and mechanistic implications of LINE1 methylation in preneoplastic pulmonary disease contexts.
Collectively, these findings imply that LINE1 hypomethylation could serve as a biomarker to differentiate NSCLC patients from healthy individuals and may possess both prognostic value and therapeutic potential. ## Therapeutic potential of LINE1 methylation in NSCLC
LINE1 methylation is widely recognized as a surrogate marker for global DNA methylation, and its hypomethylation is implicated in genomic instability,a hallmark of many diseases, particularly cancer [ , , ]. Given this biological significance, strategies aimed at enhancing LINE1 methylation have been proposed as a means to restore genomic stability [ , , , ]. Apart from its diagnostic and prognostic implications, LINE1 methylation has also been explored as a biomarker in the treatment monitoring of NSCLC, although studies in this area remain limited. One investigation assessing LINE1 methylation in csbDNA from NSCLC patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy with paclitaxel and carboplatin prior to surgical resection observed a significant increase in methylation following chemotherapy, with a further 1.4-fold increase post-surgery [ ]. Despite these findings, no significant association was observed between LINE1 methylation and overall survival or histological subtypes, possibly due to the limited sample size [ ]. In advanced NSCLC patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, an ultra-deep sequencing method (iMethyl) was employed to monitor LINE1 methylation dynamics. Progressive hypermethylation from baseline to early during treatment was observed in ICB responders [ ].
Since epigenetic modifications are inherently reversible, targeting LINE1 methylation holds promise as a therapeutic strategy in NSCLC. To date, however, direct evidence remains limited. In vitro, treatment of lung cancer cell lines with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine (DAC) significantly reduced LINE1 methylation and increased its transcriptional activity, whereas trichostatin A (TSA), a histone deacetylase inhibitor, alone did not alter LINE1 methylation. However, the combination of DAC and TSA synergistically enhanced LINE1 expression, underscoring the interplay between DNA methylation and histone acetylation in regulating LINE1 activity [ ]. Similarly, treatment with azacitidine (AZA) or DAC in NSCLC cell lines triggered rapid depletion of DNMT1 within 20 h and progressive LINE1 hypomethylation at low-to-moderate doses. Furthermore, AZA and DAC exhibited differential downstream effects: AZA robustly induced DNA damage, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest, whereas DAC achieved comparable DNMT1 depletion and hypomethylation but did not elicit equivalent cytotoxic responses [ ]. Gene expression analysis revealed that AZA and DAC regulated overlapping but distinct gene sets, underscoring complex molecular consequences of LINE1 hypomethylation within NSCLC cells [ ]. Animal models further support the causative role of DNA methylation in LINE1 regulation. In an Apc^Min/+ mouse model, Dnmt1 knockout led to acute LINE1 demethylation, increased DNA damage, and accelerated adenoma formation, confirming the tumor-suppressive significance of maintaining LINE1 methylation [ ]. Advancements in clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based epigenome editing enable targeted modulation of LINE1 methylation. Fusions of catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) with TET1 or DNMT3A effectors have been demonstrated to precisely demethylate or remethylate specified genomic loci, providing powerful tools to elucidate LINE1 function and therapeutic potential in vitro and in vivo [ , ]. It has been shown that DNMT1 knock-out in an intestinal cancer mouse model (ApcMin/+-Adenomatous polyposis coli heterozygous mutant mouse model) accelerated acute LINE1 methylation, DNA damage, and formation of adenomas [ ]. From this, epigenome editing is a novel therapeutic approach that could precisely methylate and demethylate specific genomic loci. Liu et al. proposed a modified CRISPR system that can fuse catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) with the Tet1 enzymatic domain or Dnmt3A, allowing targeted CpG demethylation or re-methylation [ ]. Collectively, these studies highlight that pharmacological demethylation, epigenome editing, and loss of DNMT activity can significantly modulate LINE1 expression and function. This converging evidence suggests that precision targeting of LINE1 methylation may represent an innovative avenue for NSCLC therapy, meriting further exploration with advanced, locus-specific technologies.
Despite the effects of pharmacological agents, numerous studies evaluated the modulation of LINE1 methylation with dietary supplementation and dietary compounds. It has been acknowledged that folate plays a crucial role in DNA methylation, and dietary folic acid supplementation has been shown to modulate DNA methylation patterns [ ]. Several studies suggest that folate deficiency might contribute to global DNA hypomethylation, thereby promoting tumorigenesis [ , ]. Jin et al. reported a significant difference in folate concentrations between NSCLC tissues and adjacent normal tissues, with a positive correlation between folate levels and LINE1 methylation in NSCLC [ ]. Notably, LUSC samples exhibited significantly lower folate concentrations and reduced LINE1 methylation compared to LUAD tissues. These findings were correlated with subtype-specific differences in folate metabolism: FOLR1, the folate transporter, was markedly overexpressed in LUAD, while GGH, which degraded intracellular folate, was expressed at lower levels in LUAD than in LUSC. This molecular profile, compatible with elevated folate uptake and retention in LUAD, mirrored the higher LINE1 methylation observed in this subtype, underscoring a mechanistic link between folate metabolism and global DNA methylation differences across NSCLC histological types [ ]. These evidences support the potential use of LINE1 methylation for evaluating the effects of dietary folic acid supplementation, as well as the impacts of fluoropyrimidine and antifolate therapies in NSCLC. Notably, this correlation was not observed in normal lung tissues, and although folate supplementation significantly influenced LINE1 methylation, no association was found between zinc,a key cofactor for DNA methylation, and LINE1 methylation or folate concentrations [ – ]. Besides these results, additional studies underscore the potential chemopreventive and disease-modifying effects of various therapeutic and dietary compounds on LINE1 methylation [ – ]. In a murine study, green tea extract and Chinese bayberry extract increased LINE1 methylation in the liver and spleen of mice treated with 7,12-dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA), while coffee extract exhibited renal protective effects [ ]. Moreover, a combination of flavonoids and resveratrol significantly enhanced LINE1 methylation in multiple organs of DMBA-treated mice compared to controls, including the liver, spleen, and kidneys [ ]. Corroborating these findings, resveratrol was shown to induce LINE1 methylation and upregulate DNMT expression in human retinal pigment epithelial cells under oxidative and inflammatory conditions [ ]. Conversely, in a diabetic retinopathy model, curcumin normalized glucose-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production via upregulation of DNMT expression without altering LINE1 methylation, emphasizing the complex interplay between DNMT expression and LINE1 methylation [ ]. A clinical study by Rozek et al. demonstrated that soy isoflavone supplementation increased LINE1 methylation in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) in a duration-dependent manner, although no significant association with overall survival or recurrence was observed during a relatively short follow-up period [ ]. Collectively, these studies indicate that several compounds can enhance LINE1 methylation, potentially mitigating disease progression and recurrence by promoting genomic stability. Table  summarizes the predictive performance of LINE1 methylation in NSCLC treatment monitoring and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target. Multiple clinical trials have explored combinations of demethylating agents, such as decitabine, with novel compounds, chemotherapy, and/or immunotherapy for advanced NSCLC [ – ]. Some of the trials suggest that DNMT inhibitors might potentially overcome drug resistance and improve the efficacy of anticancer therapies [ , ]. However, both in vitro and clinical studies revealed that demethylating agents can induce LINE1 hypomethylation, potentially leading to genomic instability [ , , , ]. Consequently, it is imperative to comprehensively investigate the impact of LINE1 hypomethylation on NSCLC epigenetic reprogramming and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying DNMT inhibitor actions prior to their broader clinical application.

 

## Future directions
In light of the aforementioned considerations, the cumulative evidence positions LINE1 methylation as a critical factor in NSCLC, with dual utility as a diagnostic biomarker and a tool for molecular subtyping. Hypomethylation of LINE1 has been repeatedly identified as an independent prognostic indicator in NSCLC. Prior studies established that LINE1 hypomethylation is an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes in NSCLC [ , , ]. These findings align with observations in colorectal cancer [ ], gastric cancer [ ], and hepatocellular carcinoma [ ], irrespective of the methylation analysis methods used. However, discrepancies remain regarding its association with clinical staging [ , ]. While some studies report no correlation between LINE1 hypomethylation and specific gene mutations,such as EGFR mutations or EML4-ALK gene fusions [ , , ],others identified a significant association with tumor protein P53 (TP53) mutations [ , ], a relationship also observed in other malignancies [ , ], though the underlying mechanisms driving this relationship remain incompletely understood. Notably, despite strong evidence linking smoking and COPD to changes in DNA methylation, studies assessing these factors in NSCLC yield mixed results with respect to LINE1 methylation [ , , , ]. This inconsistency highlights a critical need for prospective clinical studies investigating the interplay between modifiable risk factors, early-stage preneoplastic lung conditions, and LINE1 methylation dynamics.
Although accumulating evidence suggests that LINE1 hypomethylation has been implicated in its reactivation during cancer, the precise regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood [ , ]. Emerging data suggest that oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6 (IL6), exacerbate LINE1 hypomethylation and expression, thereby engaging oncogenic Wnt/β‑catenin signaling [ – ]. Despite these insights, the epigenetic regulation of LINE1 in NSCLC pathogenesis remains underexplored. Given the functional significance of LINE1-mediated reverse transcriptase (RT) activity, several RT inhibitors, such as nevirapine and efavirenz, demonstrated anticancer effects by suppressing proliferation, inducing differentiation, and reprogramming gene expression. Importantly, the reversible nature of RT inhibition has been underscored, as cancer recurrence has been observed following the cessation of treatment [ , ]. Hence, targeting LINE1 methylation represents a promising therapeutic strategy. To support the above notion, multiple studies suggest that LINE1 hypomethylation might serve as a prognostic and predictive marker in NSCLC and could be modulated through the use of therapeutic agents and dietary compounds [ , , , , , , , ]. However, additional research is needed to clarify the relationship between LINE1 methylation and clinical outcomes.
There remains substantial potential for developing innovative therapeutic compounds targeting LINE1 methylation in cancer. Nonetheless, investigations with in vitro cancer models and in vivo models using techniques such as epigenome editing systems are mandatory to fully elucidate these relationships and therapeutic effects. Moreover, although LINE1 methylation in liquid biopsies, including cfDNA and EV-DNA, has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity using methods such as dPCR, ddPCR, and pyrosequencing, these findings require validation in large, prospective cohorts. Future clinical trials must confirm the diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive utility of LINE1 methylation measurements in NSCLC.

## Conclusion
Limitations of existing NSCLC diagnostics and therapeutics underscore the urgent need for highly sensitive and specific biomarkers. Correspondingly, this review highlights the role of LINE1 methylation in NSCLC progression, its association with malignant characteristics and survival outcomes, and its potential utility in diagnostic monitoring and therapeutic targeting. Although emerging evidence suggests that LINE1 hypomethylation holds promise as a diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarker as well as a therapeutic target, further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and ultimately improve patient outcomes. In view of the foregoing considerations, LINE1 methylation represents both a valuable biological surrogate and a promising therapeutic target, offering novel insights to bridge existing gaps in NSCLC biomarker discovery and treatment strategies.
