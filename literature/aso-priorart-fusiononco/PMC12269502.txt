## Abstract
This research utilized multiomics data to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance in pediatric B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia driven by the MLL-AF9 fusion gene. Transcriptome data from B-ALL patients in the Gene Expression Omnibus and Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments databases were analyzed using weighted gene coexpression network analysis, identifying IGFBP7 as a critical gene associated with MLL-AF9 rearrangement. The MLL-AF9 fusion upregulated IGFBP7, activating ABCB1 transporters and the DNA-PKcs-mediated non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair pathway, thereby promoting chemoresistance. In vitro experiments demonstrated that MLL-AF9-overexpressing B-ALL cells exhibited reduced sensitivity to doxorubicin (DOX), cyclophosphamide (CTX), and cisplatin (DDP). Proteomic and functional assays confirmed elevated ABCB1 and DNA-PKcs expression in MLL-AF9 positive cells, enhancing DNA repair and suppressing apoptosis. Chemoresistance was effectively reversed by the ABC transporter inhibitor Verapamil and the NHEJ inhibitor NU7441 in in vitro and in vivo models. These findings highlight MLL-AF9’s role in mediating chemoresistance via ABCB1 and the NHEJ pathways, offering potential therapeutic targets for MLL-AF9-positive B-ALL.
Keywords: ABC transporters, IGFBP7, MLL-AF9 fusion gene, non-homologous end joining repair pathway, pediatric B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, proteomics, transcriptomics

Pediatric B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is the most common childhood cancer, characterized by aggressive progression and high relapse rates ( ). Although treatment strategies have advanced significantly, chemotherapy resistance remains a major cause of therapeutic failure ( , ), adversely affecting patient survival and quality of life ( ). This resistance arises from multiple mechanisms, including increased drug efflux, altered drug metabolism, and enhanced DNA repair ( ). Thus, elucidating these resistance pathways is crucial for improving therapeutic efficacy. Recent studies highlight the role of genetic alterations, particularly the MLL-AF9 fusion gene, in driving chemoresistance, providing new insights into B-ALL treatment strategies ( ).
The MLL-AF9 fusion gene results from a chromosomal rearrangement between MLL (chromosome 11) and AF9 (chromosome 9) ( ). This genetic alteration frequently occurs in pediatric B-ALL and correlates with aggressive disease and unfavorable outcomes ( ). The chimeric protein functions as an oncogenic transcription factor that dysregulates multiple signaling pathways, driving leukemic cell proliferation and survival ( , ). In addition, MLL-AF9 mediates chemoresistance through molecular mechanisms including anti-apoptotic protein activation and enhanced DNA repair capacity, conferring resistance to multiple chemotherapeutic agents ( , ).
In pediatric B-ALL, the ABC transporter family and the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair pathway are essential for chemotherapy resistance. ABC transporters are transmembrane proteins that pump chemotherapy drugs out of cancer cells, leading to drug efflux and resistance in various cancers ( , ). The NHEJ repair pathway is a primary mechanism for repairing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) ( ), allowing leukemia cells to effectively repair DNA damage caused by chemotherapy and evade the lethal effects of the drugs ( ). The MLL-AF9 fusion gene activates key proteins in these pathways, such as IGFBP7 ( ), enhancing leukemia cells' survival and resistance by modulating the activity of other related factors.
This study utilized multiomics approaches to explore the impact of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene on chemotherapy resistance in pediatric B-ALL. Through transcriptomics, proteomics, and bioinformatics analyses, we pinpointed crucial molecules and pathways linked to resistance conferred by the MLL-AF9 fusion gene. Integrating multidimensional data bolstered our comprehension of the disease complexity, enabling enhanced prediction of disease progression and treatment response. Furthermore, these technologies may offer precise clinical biomarkers for targeted therapy.
This study aimed to validate MLL-AF9-mediated chemoresistance in pediatric B-ALL through ABC transporter and the NHEJ repair pathway activation. Using in vitro and in vivo models, we demonstrated that MLL-AF9 upregulates IGFBP7 to promote these resistance mechanisms. Our findings not only elucidate MLL-AF9-associated chemoresistance but also identify potential therapeutic targets for MLL-AF9-positive B-ALL patients. These results may contribute to more precise treatment strategies and improved clinical outcomes.

## Results

## Significant upregulation of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene in B-ALL patients
This study integrates bioinformatics analysis with in vivo and in vitro experiments to investigate chemotherapy resistance mechanisms in pediatric B-ALL. The experimental procedures are detailed in .
B-ALL often displays chromosomal translocations resulting in fusion gene expression, which is closely linked to chemotherapy response and prognosis ( ). According to Closa et al. ( ), B-ALL can be classified into six major fusion groups: KMT2A-r, ETV6-r, TCF3-r, P2RY8-r, BCR-r, and PAX5-r, with KMT2A-r being the most prevalent ( A). KMT2A, also referred to as MLL, is known for its frequent chromosomal translocations, and B-ALL with MLL translocations typically has a poor prognosis ( ). BloodSpot database analysis indicates higher MLL expression in ALL compared to other leukemia subtypes ( B).
 Research has demonstrated that the MLL-AF9 fusion gene (also known as KMT2A-MLLT3), resulting from translocations between chromosomes 9 and 11, promotes the advancement of ALL ( ). The fusion transcript of MLL-AF9 is depicted in . Analysis from the LeukemiaDB reveals higher fusion scores for MLL-AF9 in B-ALL compared to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) ( C). Furthermore, Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database data show elevated expression levels of MLL and AF9 in The Cancer Genome Atlas-diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (TCGA-DLBC) and TCGA-AML ( D), indicating the potential broad impact of MLL and AF9 in leukemia and other hematologic malignancies. In conclusion, the significant upregulation of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene in B-ALL patients implies a close association with B-ALL progression.

## Identification of genes associated with MLL-AF9 rearrangement through differential and WGCNA analysis
To explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying MLL-AF9 B-ALL, we obtained the B-ALL dataset GSE19475 related to the MLL-AF9 fusion from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Utilizing the R package “limma,” we identified 975 genes significantly differential expression between B-ALL patients with and without MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement ( , A and B). Similarly, analysis of another MLL-AF9-related ALL dataset, GSE68720, revealed 1158 differentially expressed genes ( , C and D). By overlapping the differentially expressed genes from both datasets, we pinpointed 136 commonly upregulated and 53 commonly downregulated genes ( , E and F).
 Further analysis of the GSE19475 dataset using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed gene modules significantly associated with the MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement. Initially, the samples were hierarchically clustered ( A), and a soft threshold of β = 4 (R2 = 0.9) was chosen to construct a scale-free network ( B), resulting in the identification of 13 modules ( C). The gene TOM is depicted in D. Among these modules, the green, green-yellow, and yellow modules exhibited the strongest correlation with the MLL-AF9 fusion gene ( , E and F). Further analysis that intersected these module genes with the differentially expressed genes revealed 132 overlapping genes ( G), indicating their significance as key players in B-ALL with MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement.
 

## Prognostic risk model based on 13 genes accurately predicts B-ALL patient outcomes
In the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) B-ALL dataset, a univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the prognostic significance of the 132 identified essential genes. This analysis pinpointed 42 genes significantly correlated with B-ALL patients prognosis ( A). Following this, the TARGET B-ALL samples were randomly split into training and testing sets at a 1:1 ratio. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) analysis on the training set identified 13 genes for constructing a prognostic risk model for B-ALL ( , B and C). Based on the median risk score, patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis performed on the training set, testing set, and the complete dataset demonstrated that individuals with low-risk scores had notably longer survival rates compared to those with high-risk scores ( , D–F).
 Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis revealed that in the training set, the area under the curves for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival predictions were 0.840, 0.979, and 0.969, respectively ( G). In the testing set, the corresponding area under the curves were 0.707, 0.753, and 0.759 ( H); and for the entire dataset, they were 0.748, 0.863, and 0.843 ( I). These results underscore the robust predictive performance of the model.
Using the median risk score as a threshold and plotting survival status diagrams revealed a higher probability of death among patients with elevated risk scores ( , A–F). Consistent expression trends of the 13 genes were depicted in , G–I across the training set, testing set, and entire dataset. Furthermore, principal component analysis results for the three datasets demonstrated a clear separation between the low-risk and high-risk groups ( , J–L). These findings affirm the accurate predictive ability of the prognostic risk model based on these 13 genes in assessing outcomes for B-ALL patients.
 

## MLL-AF9 fusion gene may promote B-ALL chemotherapy resistance by upregulating IGFBP7 and activating ABC transporters and the NHEJ repair pathway
Research has demonstrated the role of oncogenic MLL fusions in inducing drug resistance, leading to unfavorable outcomes in B-ALL patients ( ). To explore how MLL-AF9 contributes to chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL, we conducted a differential expression analysis on chemotherapy-sensitive and chemotherapy-resistant B-ALL patients sourced from the GEO database. This analysis revealed 375 differentially expressed genes ( , A and B). Intersection of these genes with the 13 prognostic genes pinpointed the gene IGFBP7 as a common link, exhibiting high expression in chemotherapy-resistant B-ALL patients ( , C and D). Furthermore, data from GSE19475 highlighted elevated IGFBP7 expression in B-ALL patients with MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement ( E). In addition, the GEPIA database validated the high expression of IGFBP7 in TCGA-DLBC and TCGA-AML datasets ( F). These results suggest that the MLL-AF9 fusion gene may play a role in promoting B-ALL chemotherapy resistance by upregulating IGFBP7.
 Proteomics analysis was conducted on MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells, revealing 647 differentially expressed proteins between the control (CTR) group and MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells. Among these proteins, IGFBP7 showed significant upregulation in MLL-AF9 positive cells ( , A and B). Subsequently, based on IGFBP7 expression levels, the MLL-AF9 positive cells were categorized into high-expression and low-expression groups, displaying a notable difference in IGFBP7 protein levels ( C). Further analysis unveiled 67 proteins with significant discrepancies between these groups ( , D and E). Functional enrichment assessments using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes indicated enrichment in the NHEJ and ABC transporter pathways in both groups ( , F–I). Many chemotherapy drugs can cause DNA damage, leading to activation of DNA damage response factors, cell cycle checkpoints, and DNA repair pathways. NHEJ, a key DNA repair pathways, is commonly elevated in cancers ( ). ABC transporters, a group of transmembrane proteins, play a role in multidrug resistance by expelling anticancer agents from cells. This action reduces the intracellular drug levels ( ). These results imply that the MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement might enhance chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells through IGFBP7, ABC transporters, and the NHEJ repair pathway.
 

## MLL-AF9 fusion gene promotes chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells
MLL-AF9 significantly contributes to drug resistance in leukemia, especially in AML ( ), while bioinformatics analysis suggests its involvement in chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL as well. To confirm these findings, we established B-ALL cell lines with MLL-AF9 overexpressing for investigate. Western blot analysis demonstrated heightened expression of crucial drug resistance genes in B-ALL, such as Survivin ( ), Bcl-2 ( ), and FLT3 ( ), in the MLL-AF9 group compared to the control group ( A). We evaluated cell viability using three common chemotherapy drugs for B-ALL treatment: doxorubicin (DOX) ( ), cyclophosphamide (CTX) ( ), and cisplatin (DDP) ( ). In the CTR group, cell viability notably decreased post treatment with any of the mentioned chemotherapy drugs compared to PBS treatment. Conversely, in the MLL-AF9 group, cell viability remained unaffected following treatment with the same drugs compared to PBS ( B). These findings suggest effective reduction in cell viability by chemotherapy drugs in the CTR group, while they fail to impact cell viability in the MLL-AF9 group, indicating MLL-AF9's role in promoting chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells.
 In order to further explore the impact of MLL-AF9 on the phenotype of B-ALL cells, we administered the conventional chemotherapy drug DOX to the cells. Apoptosis analysis via flow cytometry indicated an elevation in the apoptosis rate within the CTR+DOX group compared to the CTR+PBS group, while no notable difference was observed in the apoptosis rate between the MLL-AF9+PBS and MLL-AF9+DOX groups ( C). In addition, flow cytometry examination of the cell cycle illustrated that the CTR+DOX group exhibited an increase in the G0/G1 phase and a decrease in the S and G2/M phases in comparison to the CTR+PBS group; conversely, no significant alterations were detected in the MLL-AF9+PBS and MLL-AF9+DOX groups ( D). The colony formation assay revealed decreased colony formation in the CTR+DOX group compared to the CTR+PBS group, while no significant difference noted between the MLL-AF9+PBS and MLL-AF9+DOX groups ( E). In Transwell invasion assays, invasive capacity decreased in the CTR+DOX group relative to the CTR+PBS group, while no substantial variance was observed between the MLL-AF9+PBS and MLL-AF9+DOX groups ( F). In addition, wound healing assays indicated reduced migration ability in the CTR+DOX group compared to the CTR+PBS group, with similar results between the MLL-AF9+PBS and MLL-AF9+DOX groups ( G). These findings highlight the role of MLL-AF9 fusion gene overexpression in promoting chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells.

## MLL-AF9 upregulates IGFBP7 to activate ABC transporter ABCB1 and NHEJ repair factor DNA-PKcs, promoting DNA damage repair and chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells
To explore MLL-AF9-induced chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL, bioinformatic analysis identified IGFBP7 as a key contributor. Subsequent analysis of differentially expressed genes in IGFBP7-high B-ALL cells revealed enrichment in the ABC transporter and NHEJ repair pathways. Within the ABC transporter pathway, ABCB1 plays a crucial role in drug resistance in B-ALL ( ), facilitating the efflux of chemotherapeutic agents and contributing to resistance. Inhibiting ABCB1 can result in the accumulation of DOX, leading to increased DNA damage and cell death ( ). Moreover, DNA-PKcs, a key kinase in the NHEJ pathway, is involved in drug resistance in B-ALL ( ). Western blot analysis conducted on MLL-AF9-positive B-ALL cells revealed elevated expression levels of ABCB1, DNA-PKcs, and IGFBP7 in the MLL-AF9 group compared to the control group ( A). These findings suggest that MLL-AF9 may enhance chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells by upregulating IGFBP7, which in turn activates the ABC transporter pathway and the NHEJ repair pathway. This mechanism enhances the cells' capability to repair DNA damage and withstand chemotherapy treatment.
 To further explore the impact of IGFBP7, ABCB1, and DNA-PKcs on drug resistance in B-ALL cells, we established IGFBP7 knockdown cell lines, as well as cell lines with overexpressed ABCB1 or DNA-PKcs ( A). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays demonstrated a decrease in cell viability in the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group. Notably, cell viability increased in both the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups when compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( B). Analysis of apoptosis through flow cytometry revealed elevated apoptosis rates in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group in contrast to the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group, while decreased apoptosis rates were observed in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( C). In addition, flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle indicated that the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group exhibited an increase in the G0/G1 phase and a decrease in the S and G2/M phases compared to the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group. Conversely, the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups displayed a decrease in the G0/G1 phase and an increase in the S and G2/M phases relative to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( D). Colony formation assays demonstrated a decrease in the proliferation of B-ALL cells in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group compared to the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group. Conversely, proliferation rates increased in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups when compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( E). According to the Transwell assays, the invasive capacity was reduced in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group in contrast to the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group, while invasion rates escalated in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( F). Moreover, the wound healing assays indicated a decrease in the migration rate of B-ALL cells in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX group relative to the MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX group, whereas migration rates were elevated in the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX and MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX group ( G). These observations suggest that MLL-AF9 stimulates the upregulation of IGFBP7, activating ABC transporter ABCB1 and the NHEJ repair factor DNA-PKcs, thereby enhancing chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells.
The findings indicate that the activation of IGFBP7, ABCB1, and DNA-PKcs contributes to chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL. ABCB1 shields cells from genotoxic agents and aids in DNA repair ( ), while DNA-PKcs initiate NHEJ and facilitate DNA repair ( ). The interplay between these pathways and DNA damage repair underscores the significance of DNA repair capacity as a pivotal factor in B-ALL chemotherapy resistance.
Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry were utilized to further validate the hypothesis by detecting the expression of the DNA repair marker γH2A.X ( ). The results demonstrated a significantly higher efficiency in DSB repair in the MLL-AF9 group compared to the CTR group ( , B and C). CCK-8 assays further showed increased cell viability in the MLL-AF9 group relative to the CTR group, suggesting that the elevated viability was a result of activated DNA repair ( D). Moreover, treatment of MLL-AF9-positive cells with the ABC transporter inhibitor Verapamil ( ) or the NHEJ repair inhibitor NU7441 ( ) led to a notable reduction in γH2A.X expression, indicating a decrease in DSB repair efficiency in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil and MLL-AF9+NU7441 groups compared to the MLL-AF9+PBS group ( , E and F). Consistently, CCK-8 assays demonstrated decreased cell viability in these inhibitor-treated groups compared to the MLL-AF9+PBS group, further confirming suppression of DNA repair activity ( G). These findings suggest that MLL-AF9 upregulates IGFBP7, thereby activating ABCB1 and DNA-PKcs, enhancing DNA repair capacity, and facilitating chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells.

## Inhibitors of ABC transporters and the NHEJ repair pathway reverse chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells
To investigate the potential reversal of chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells by inhibitors targeting ABC transporters and the NHEJ repair pathway, a CCK-8 assay was conducted to evaluate cell viability post treatment with inhibitors. Results demonstrated a significant decrease in cell viability in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( A). Analysis of apoptosis via flow cytometry indicated an increase in apoptosis rates in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups relative to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( B). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle revealed an increase in the G0/G1 phase and a decrease in the S and G2/M phases in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups in comparison to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( C). Colony formation assays revealed decreased cell proliferation in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( D). Transwell assays demonstrated reduced cell invasion in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups relative to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( E). Similarly, wound healing assays indicated decreased cell migration in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( F). Western blot analysis further confirmed decreased expression of drug resistance genes Survivin, Bcl-2, and FLT3 decreased in the MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX groups in comparison to the MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX group ( G). These findings indicate the effective reversal of chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells by inhibitors targeting ABC transporters and the NHEJ repair pathway.
 

## Inhibitors of ABC transporters and the NHEJ repair pathway reverse chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL mouse models
To further confirm the link between chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL mouse models and the ABC transporter and NHEJ repair pathways, we conducted in vivo validation of the effects of inhibitors. The Model group showed weight loss compared to the Sham group. There were no significant differences in body weight between the Model and Model+DOX groups. However, an increase in body weight was observed in the Model+Verapamil+DOX and Model+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the Model+PBS+DOX group ( A). White blood cell counts revealed decreased numbers in the Model group compared to the Sham group. No significant changes were observed in the Model+DOX group compared to the Model group. On the other hand, white blood cell counts increased in the Model+Verapamil+DOX and Model+NU7441+DOX groups relative to the Model+PBS+DOX group ( B). Histological examination using H&E staining revealed increased immune infiltration in the Model group compared to the Sham group in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. There was no significant change in immune infiltration between the Model and Model+DOX groups. However, immune infiltration decreased in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow of the Model+Verapamil+DOX and Model+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the Model+PBS+DOX group ( C). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of spleen tissues showed increased expression of ABCB1, DNA-PKcs, γ-H2AX, and IGFBP7 in the Model group compared to the Sham group. No differences were observed in the expression of these proteins between the Model and Model+DOX groups. However, compared to the Model+PBS+DOX group, ABCB1 and γ-H2AX expression decreased in the Model+Verapamil+DOX group, while DNA-PKcs and γ-H2AX expression decreased in the Model+NU7441+DOX group ( D). In Western blot analysis, ABCB1, DNA-PKcs, γ-H2AX, and IGFBP7 expression increased in the Model group compared to the Sham group. There were no significant changes in the Model+DOX group. ABCB1 and γ-H2AX expression decreased in the Model+Verapamil+DOX group, while DNA-PKcs and γ-H2AX expression decreased in the Model+NU7441+DOX group compared to the Model+PBS+DOX group ( E). TUNEL assays showed no significant changes in apoptosis between the Model and Model+DOX groups. However, apoptosis increased in the Model+Verapamil+DOX and Model+NU7441+DOX groups compared to the Model+PBS+DOX group ( F). These findings suggest that the ABC transporter inhibitor Verapamil and the NHEJ repair pathway inhibitor NU7441 can reverse chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL mouse models.
 

## Discussion
Pediatric B-ALL, a highly aggressive form of leukemia primarily affecting children, presents significant challenges in clinical treatment, most notably chemotherapy resistance ( , ). This resistance poses a major obstacle to successful treatment outcomes and contributes to poor prognosis ( ). Recent research efforts have been dedicated to unraveling the molecular mechanisms responsible for chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL, with a specific focus on identifying potential therapeutic targets. Among these studies, the MLL-AF9 fusion gene has emerged as a key area of interest due to its prevalence in B-ALL and critical role in disease progression ( ). Not only is the MLL-AF9 fusion gene associated with rapid disease advancement ( ), but is also intricately linked to the development of chemotherapy resistance ( ).
This study systematically investigates the molecular mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance in pediatric B-ALL, driven by the MLL-AF9 fusion gene. The research utilizes integrative multiomics data analysis and experimental validation ( ). Transcriptomic data from the GEO and TARGET databases undergo analysis through differential expression analysis and WGCNA to pinpoint genes significantly linked to MLL-AF9 fusion gene rearrangement. The results suggest that the MLL-AF9 fusion gene boosts chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL by elevating IGFBP7 levels. This elevation activates the ABCB1 transporter and the DNA-PKcs-mediated NHEJ repair pathway, enhancing B-ALL cell resistance to various chemotherapy drugs such as DOX, CTX, and DDP.
 In vitro experiments showed a significantly increase in chemotherapy resistance in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells, with a noticeable decrease in the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy drugs. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence assays indicated reduced expression of the DNA repair marker γH2A.X in MLL-AF9 positive cells, suggesting higher repair efficiency during the DNA damage repair process. This improved repair capacity was associated with elevated expression of ABCB1 and DNA-PKcs, affirming the role of MLL-AF9 in modulating these molecules to enhance DNA damage repair in B-ALL cells and suppress apoptosis.
To validate the clinical significance of our discoveries, we performed in vivo tests on a mouse model of MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL. Our findings demonstrated that the administration of Verapamil, an ABC transporter inhibitor, and NU7441, an NHEJ repair inhibitor, effectively reversed the resistance to chemotherapy in MLL-AF9 positive mice. This reversal was characterized by enhanced apoptosis of leukemia cells and a substantial enhancement in the effectiveness of chemotherapy drugs in vivo. These results highlight the pivotal roles played by the ABCB1 transporter and the NHEJ repair pathway in mediating chemotherapy resistance induced by the MLL-AF9 fusion gene.
This study uncovers the influence of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene on chemotherapy resistance in pediatric B-ALL, presenting a novel treatment target for clinical use. Resistance to chemotherapy in MLL-AF9 positive individuals is fueled by the increased expression of IGFBP7, which triggers the ABC transporter and NHEJ repair pathways. This revelation deepens our comprehension of the intricacies of B-ALL resistance and has immediate clinical significance. Targeting inhibitors to these pathways could serve as a viable approach to combat resistance, potentially enhancing chemotherapy effectiveness. Moreover, these biomarkers could potentially forecast treatment response and relapse, allowing for tailored treatment strategies that improve patient outcomes and quality of life.
Although this study offers valuable insights into the involvement of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene in B-ALL, it has some limitations. Firstly, the reliance on cell and animal models may not entirely reflect human conditions. Validation of MLL-AF9 protein expression levels in human patient samples is essential. The intricate and diverse nature of human diseases may introduce variations between experimental outcomes and clinical settings. Furthermore, though the responsiveness of MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL cells to NHEJ repair and ABC transporter inhibitors was observed, the long-term effects and safety profile of these inhibitors necessitate verification in larger clinical trials. Lastly, the intricate regulatory mechanisms governing the expression and function of MLL-AF9 fusion gene warrant further exploration.
Future research should be directed toward two key areas. First, validating the study findings using clinical samples from B-ALL patients is crucial. This validation should encompass evaluating the expression and role of the MLL-AF9 fusion gene, IGFBP7, ABC transporters, and the NHEJ repair pathway in real-world clinical settings. Understanding the correlation of these biomarkers with clinical characteristics and prognosis, as well as their potential as therapeutic targets, is imperative. Secondly, exploring the synergistic effects of ABC transporter and NHEJ repair inhibitors with conventional chemotherapy agents in MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL patients is essential. A multitargeted treatment approach may enhance treatment outcomes, surpass the limitations of single therapies, improve efficacy, and lower relapse rates. Investigating the pharmacological properties of these inhibitors and refining combination treatments will play a pivotal role in achieving these objectives.
In conclusion, this study is the first to reveal, from a multiomics perspective, that the MLL-AF9 fusion gene induces chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells by activating ABCB1 and the NHEJ repair pathway. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrated that inhibiting ABCB1 and the NHEJ repair pathway significantly reverses this resistance, offering new potential therapeutic strategies for MLL-AF9 positive B-ALL patients. Future research should explore combining ABC transporter and NHEJ repair inhibitors with existing chemotherapy drugs to enhance treatment outcomes for these patients.

## Experimental procedures

## Part 1

## Public data sources
The transcriptome data for pediatric B-ALL were obtained from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including (1) the following: 14 non-MLL-rearranged and 8 MLL-AF9-rearranged B-ALL samples from GSE19475 (2); 17 non-MLL-rearranged and 6 MLL-AF9 rearranged ALL samples from the GSE68720 for validation (3); 66 L-asparaginase-sensitive and 40 L-Asparaginase-resistant B-ALL samples from GSE653 and GSE654, respectively. In addition, transcriptome data from 160 B-ALL cases with survival information were extracted from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/).
MLL expression across leukemia subtypes was analyzed using the t test method via the BloodSpot database (https://www.bloodspot.eu/). MLL-AF9 fusion transcripts and B-ALL expression profiles were obtained from the LeukemiaDB database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/LeukemiaDB/#!/document). Differential gene expression between AML, DLBC, and normal blood samples were assessed using TCGA data through the GEPIA2 platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index).

## Differential expression analysis
Differential expression analysis was conducted using the “limma” R package (version 3.58.1) with a significance threshold of (|logFC|) > 0.5 and p < 0.05. Results were visualized as volcano plots and heatmaps.

## Weighted gene co-expression network analysis
For gene co-expression network analysis, the “WGCNA” R package (version 1.72–5) was used. Low-variance genes were first filtered out, followed by sample clustering using hierarchical clustering. The optimal soft threshold was selected to generate a gene expression similarity matrix, which was converted to a topological overlap matrix. Gene modules were detected through dynamic tree-cutting, with significant trait-associated modules identified by correlation analysis.

## Part 2

## Construction of the prognostic risk model
Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the intersecting genes between module genes and differentially expressed genes using the survival package, identifying prognosis-related genes (p < 0.05). TARGET B-ALL samples were randomly split into training and testing set in a 1:1 ratio. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis was conducted on the training set using the “glmnet” package (version 4.1–8) to construct the prognostic model. Risk scores were calculated as the sum of each gene's regression coefficient multiplied by its expression level. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Survival differences between groups were evaluated by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (p < 0.05). Model accuracy was assessed using ROC curves generated by the “timeROC” package. The model was subsequently validated in the testing set.

## Cell transfection and grouping
To establish MLL-AF9 fusion gene-positive and gene-negative B-ALL cell line models, we used NALM-6 cells (CRL-3273, American Type Culture Collection). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (11875093, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5670701, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Following a previously published study ( ), the cells were maintained under a low-oxygen environment (21% O2) to enhance adhesion and promote adherent growth.
The retroviral plasmid pMIG-FLAG-MLL-AF9 (Addgene, #71443) was used to establish stable MLL-AF9 expression. Phoenix 293T packaging cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL-3216) were transiently transfected with the plasmid using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus-transfection, #101000001) to generate retroviral supernatant. NALM-6 cells infected with this supernatant comprised the MLL-AF9 group, while empty vector-infected cells served as the CTR. MLL protein expression was confirmed by Western blot.
To assess the effect of MLL-AF9 on chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL cells, we established eight experimental groups: CTR+PBS (PBS-treated control cells); CTR+DOX (doxorubicin (DOX)-treated control cells); CTR+CTX (cyclophosphamide (CTX)-treated control cells); CTR+DDP (cisplatin (DDP)-treated control cells); MLL-AF9+PBS (PBS-treated MLL-AF9 cells); MLL-AF9+DOX (DOX-treated MLL-AF9 cells); MLL-AF9+CTX (CTX-treated MLL-AF9 cells); and MLL-AF9+DDP (DDP-treated MLL-AF9 cells). Cells were plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. Treatments included the following: 200 μl of 500 nM DOX (D1515, Merck) for 24 h; 200 μl of 8 mM CTX (6055–19–2, Merck) for 24 h; 200 μl of 200 μM DDP (15,663–27–1, Merck) for 24 h.
To investigate how MLL-AF9-regulated IGFBP7 activation affects ABCB1 and DNA-PKcs in drug-resistant B-ALL cells, we established five experimental groups: MLL-AF9+shNC+DOX (MLL-AF9 B-ALL cells with nontargeting shRNA control + DOX); MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+DOX (MLL-AF9 B-ALL cells infected with nontargeting shRNA control + DOX); MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-NC+DOX (MLL-AF9 B-ALL cells with IGFBP7 knockdown and empty overexpression vector + DOX); MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-ABCB1+DOX (MLL-AF9 B-ALL cells with IGFBP7 knockdown and ABCB1 overexpression + DOX); MLL-AF9+shIGFBP7+oe-DNA-PKcs+DOX (MLL-AF9 B-ALL cells infected with with IGFBP7 knockdown and DNA-PKcs overexpression + DOX). All recombinant lentiviruses were constructed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). For infection, logarithmic phase cells were adjusted to 5 × 104 cells/ml and seeded at 2 ml per well in 6-well plates. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, cells were infected with lentivirus (final concentration 1 × 108 TU/ml). Experiments were performed 48 h post infection ( ).
The shRNA sequences used were the following: shNC sequence: 5′-CCGGGAGGGTAATCAATACCTGTTTCTCGAGAAACAGGTATTGATTACCCTCTTTTTG-3′; shIGFBP3 sequence: 5′-CCGG CATCCAATTCCCAAGGACAGCTCGAG CTGTCCTTGGGAATTGGATGTTTTTG 3′.
To examine the effects of the ABC transporter inhibitor Verapamil and the NHEJ repair pathway inhibitor NU7441 on MLL-AF9 positive cells, we established three treatment groups: MLL-AF9 (PBS-treated control); MLL-AF9+Verapamil (10 μM Verapamil-treated); MLL-AF9+NU7441 (6 μM NU7441-treated). Cells (5 × 105/well in 6-well plates) were treated with 200 μl of either 10 μM Verapamil (Merck 152–11–4) or 6 μM NU7441 (Merck SML3923) for 24 h.
We examined how Verapamil (ABC transporter inhibitor) and NU7441 (NHEJ repair inhibitor) affect drug resistance in MLL-AF9-positive B-ALL cells through three experimental groups: MLL-AF9+PBS+DOX (PBS pretreatment then 500 nM DOX), MLL-AF9+Verapamil+DOX (10 μM Verapamil pretreatment then DOX), and MLL-AF9+NU7441+DOX (6 μM NU7441 pretreatment then DOX). Cells (5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates) received 200 μl pretreatment solutions (PBS, 10 μM Verapamil [Merck 152–11–4], or 6 μM NU7441 [Merck SML3923]) for 48 h before 200 μl 500 nM DOX treatment for 24 h.

## Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry proteomics analysis
The trypsin-digested sample was resuspended in 120 μl of mobile phase A (10% acetonitrile/0.26 M formic acid) and fractionated using an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with a PolySULFOETHYL A column (200 Å pore size, 5 μm diameter, The Nest Group Inc). The separation employed a 60-min linear gradient at 200 μl/min with mobile phase B (10% acetonitrile/1 M ammonium formate/0.26 M formic acid), collecting fractions at 5-min intervals. Fractions were desalted using ZipTipC18 pipette tips (Millipore) and eluted with 4 μl buffer B (90% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid, 10% water, and 0.02% TFA), then diluted with 80 μl buffer A (95% water, 0.1% formic acid, 5% acetonitrile, and 0.02% TFA). From this, 40 μl was injected via autosampler onto a 2-cm C18 trap column (internal diameter 200 μm) followed by separation on a 5-cm C18 analytical column (internal diameter 75 μm, 8-μm tip). The HPLC system was coupled to an LTQ 2D linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with nanoESI source in data-dependent acquisition mode. MS/MS data were processed using Mascot Daemon and extract_msn (Matrix Science) to generate DTA files.
The mass spectrometry data were processed using the both Mascot (Matrix Science) and X!Tandem (Global Proteome Machine Manager, Beavis Informatics Ltd) search engines with the following parameters: carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation as a variable modification. Peptide and protein identifications were validated in Scaffold (Proteome Software Inc) using stringent probability thresholds (95% for peptides and 80% for proteins) based on the PeptideProphet algorithm. For differential expression analysis, we applied significance thresholds set at |logFC| > 0.5 and p < 0.05 to identify differentially expressed proteins.

## Functional enrichment analysis
The analysis of differentially expressed proteins utilized the “ClusterProfiler” R package (version 4.10.1) for GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses. GO analysis encompassed biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing PMSF (P0013B) and quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). In addition, 50 micrograms of protein were combined with 2 × SDS sample buffer, heated at 100 °C for 5 min, and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane using a wet transfer technique. Following a 1-h blocking step with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: DNA-PKcs (ab32566, Abcam), ABCB1 (MA5-13854, Thermo Fisher Scientific), IGFBP7 (ab171085, Abcam), MLLN (05–764, Merck), Survivin (LS-B2787–250, LSBIO), Bcl-2 (A19693, ABclonal), FLT3 (A12437, ABclonal), and γH2AX (ab81299, Abcam). After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membrane was exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP, ab97051, 1:2000, Abcam) for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was treated with a mixture of Pierce enhanced chemiluminescence Western Blotting Substrate kit components A and B (32209, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a dark room and imaged using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+system.

## CCK-8 assay
Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CCK-8 kit (C0037, Beyotime). MLL-AF9 positive NALM-6 cells and control NALM-6 cells were seeded at 4 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates with 100 μl of medium. After a 24 h incubation, cells in each group were treated accordingly. Following a 20 h incubation at 37 °C, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured after an additional 120 min of incubation.

## Cell apoptosis detection
MLL-AF9 positive NALM-6 cells and control NALM-6 cells were centrifuged at 1000g for 5 min in precooled PBS, the supernatant was discarded, and the process was repeated twice. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in 500 μl of precooled PBS and assessed for apoptosis using the Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) Double Staining Kit (C1062S, Beyotime) via flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

## Cell cycle analysis
Cell cycle status was assessed by PI (ST1569, Beyotime) staining of DNA content followed by flow cytometry analysis. MLL-AF9 positive NALM-6 cells and control NALM-6 cells were prepared into single-cell suspensions and fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol (1.00983, Merck) at 4 °C for a minimum of 1 hour. Subsequently, samples were then treated with 100 μg/ml RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #EN0531) at 37 °C for 30 min, and then stained with 50 μg/ml PI at room temperature for 5 min. DNA content was evaluated using the BD Fortessa LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Each sample collected at least 300,000 events, and data analysis was carried out using FlowJo V10 software.

## Colony formation assay
MLL-AF9 positive NALM-6 cells and control NALM-6 cells were cultured in 6-cm dishes until visible colonies formed after 1 to 2 weeks. The colonies were then washed twice with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) for 45 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (C0121, Beyotime) for 20 min. Finally, the colonies were photographed and counted under a microscope.

## Wound healing assay
MLL-AF9 positive and control NALM-6 cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. A 10 μl pipette tip was then used to create a scratch on the cell monolayer, which was washed three times with PBS to eliminate debris and floating cells. After another 24 h incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, images of the scratch were taken before and after the incubation. The wound healing ability was analyzed using ImageJ software to determine the percentage of wound closure after 24 h.

## Transwell
The invasiveness of MLL-AF9 positive and control NALM-6 cells was evaluated using Transwell chambers (353097; BD Biosciences) coated with Matrigel basement membrane matrix (356234; BD Biosciences). These chambers were equipped with a polycarbonate membrane, and both upper and lower chambers were filled with culture medium. Following coculture treatment, cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were harvested, placed in the upper chamber, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. After 48 h, cells on the upper side of the chamber were wiped off with a cotton swab. The cells that migrated to the lower side of the membrane were stained with 0.05% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime) and observed under an inverted microscope.

## Flow cytometry analysis
MLL-AF9 positive and control NALM-6 cells were harvested with cold PBS and suspended in flow cytometry buffer (1x PBS buffer containing 1% bovine serum albumin). The cells were then labeled with FITC anti-H2A.X Phospho (Ser139) antibody (613403, BioLegend) on ice for 30 min and analyzed using a flow cytometer (C500). FlowJo software was utilized for data analysis.

## Immunofluorescence staining
MLL-AF9 positive and control NALM-6 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, ABCLONAL) for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Cell permeabilization occurred at room temperature using PBS with 0.5% Triton X-100 (P0096, ABclonal) for 15 min. Blocking of nonspecific binding sites was achieved with 5% bovine serum albumin (ST2254, ABCLONAL) in PBS for 1 hour, followed by three 5-min washes. The MLL-AF9 positive NALM-6 cells were then labeled with the primary antibody gamma H2A.X (phospho S139) (ab11174, Abcam), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150077, Abcam) or Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse IgG (ab150115, Abcam) at room temperature. Counterstaining with DAPI (C1002, ABclonal) in the dark was performed before final washing in PBS. The cells were imaged using a Nikon microscope (Az100; Nikon) equipped with a fluorescence illuminator (L200/D; Prior Scientific) and a digital camera (DS-Vi1; Nikon) connected to a computer and were used for further analysis.

## Animal experiments
NOD/Jslac mice for the experiments were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co Ltd. To create a B-ALL mouse model with MLL-AF9, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were obtained from wanwusw (Delf-12133, wanwusw). Lineage-negative cord blood cells (Lin-CB) were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells using the EasySep Human Progenitor Cell Enrichment Kit II (18000, Scientists Helping Scientists). Following the same method, the MSCV-MLL-AF9 retrovirus was prepared, and lineage-negative cord blood cells were prestimulated for 12 h in a medium supplemented with 10 ng/ml interleukin-6 (IL-6, HY-P7044, MCE), 100 ng/ml FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L, HY-P73067, MCE), 100 ng/ml stem cell factor (SCF, HY-P7064, MCE), 10 ng/ml granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF, HY-P70554, MCE), and 15 ng/ml thrombopoietin (TPO, HY-P7088, MCE). Subsequently, the cells underwent four rounds of viral infection at 12-h intervals using the same medium and cytokines. Twelve hours after the last infection, the cells were collected and injected into immunodeficient mice intramedullary at a dosage of 2.0 to 2.5 × 105 cells per mouse. The model was successfully established after 19 weeks of cultivation ( ).
The study groups were categorized as follows: Sham (mice undergoing sham surgery treated with PBS), Model (mice with the MLL-AL9 B-ALL model), Model+DOX (MLL-AL9 B-ALL model mice treated with DOX), Model+DOX+PBS (MLL-AL9 B-ALL model mice treated initially with PBS followed by DOX), Model+Verapamil+DOX (MLL-AL9 B-ALL model mice treated with Verapamil followed by DOX), and Model+NU7441+DOX (MLL-AL9 B-ALL model mice treated with NU7441 followed by DOX). Treatments were administered via intraperitoneal injection. Mice were given 200 μl of 3 mg/kg DOX daily for three consecutive days, along with PBS, 200 μl of 5 mg/kg Verapamil, or 200 μl of 10 mg/kg NU7441, respectively. Following treatment, mice were weighed, peripheral blood was collected, and upon euthanasia, the bone marrow, spleen, and liver tissues were collected for further analysis.

## White blood cell count
Red blood cells were isolated from mouse blood by adding an equal volume of Dextran solution (D5376, Merck) into a 15 ml heparinized tube (367874, BD) for sedimentation. The Dextran solution was prepared by dissolving 1.9 g of dextrose in 50 ml of 0.9% NaCl solution (106404, Merck). The resulting white blood cells in the supernatant were then centrifuged over Histopaque-1083 (10831, Merck) at 500×g for 30 min at 20 °C, yielding a pellet of polymorphonuclear leukocytes and a “buffy coat” layer containing mononuclear and lymphocytes. The white cell fractions were purified by centrifugation at 500g for 10 min and resuspended in PBS. Residual red cells in the polymorphonuclear leukocyte pellet were removed through hypotonic lysis and subsequent centrifugation (500×g, 2–3 times). Total blood cell counts and the purity of each cell fraction were analyzed using a KX-21N hematology analyzer (Sysmex Europe GmbH).

## H&E staining
Following euthanasia, tissue samples from the bone marrow, spleen, and liver were collected and fixed in a 4% formaldehyde solution. The skin was paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 4 μm slices. The sections were then stained with H&E staining reagent (C0105M, Beyotime) for 1 minute, washed with tap water, and counterstained with eosin solution for 15 s. Subsequently, the tissue sections were then dehydrated in a series of ethanol solutions (95%, 100%) and xylene. The slides were sealed with a neutral resin mounting medium (C1795, Sigma-Aldrich) and air-dried before microscopic imaging. Quantitative analysis of the H&E-stained liver, spleen, and bone marrow images for B-ALL cells was conducted using ImageJ software. These cells were identified by distinct characteristics such as enlarged and irregular morphology, irregular nuclei, and decreased cytoplasm. Statistical analyses were carried out based on observations from five different fields of view for each sample. The B-ALL cell counts were reported as cells per square millimeter (/mm2).

## Immunohistochemistry
The bone marrow, spleen, and liver samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution (P0147A, Beyotime), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Antigen retrieval was conducted by heating the sections in EDTA solution (P0085, Beyotime) using a microwave. Subsequently, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide and blocked with goat serum (C0265, Beyotime). Primary antibodies, including ABCB1 (sc-13131, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), IGFBP7 (500-P234-1MG, Thermo Fisher Scientific), γH2AX (MA1-2022, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and DNA-PKcs (sc-5282, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), were applied to the sections and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the sections underwent incubation with biotinylated HRP reagent for 20 min, followed by signal detection using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine, reagent (P0202, Beyotime). The slides were scanned and analyzed using a Pannoramic Midi scanner (3DHISTECH).
The IHC staining intensity was categorized as negative (0), weak (1), moderate (2), or strong (3). The H-score was computed using the formula: H-score = (1 × % weak staining)+(2 × % moderate staining)+(3 × % strong staining). Both the staining intensity and H-scores were assessed separately by two researchers.

## TUNEL assay
Apoptosis was assessed with a TUNEL Assay Kit (C1088, Beyotime). Paraffin-embedded sections underwent deparaffinization and Proteinase K treatment (ST533, Beyotime) for nuclear protein removal. Subsequently, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by incubation with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (EP0161, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 1 hour. Streptavidin-FITC (S3762, Merck) was then applied and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to visualize apoptosis using a confocal microscope (LMS710, ZEISS).

## Statistical analysis
Data from a minimum of three independent experiments were reported as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Group comparisons were carried out using independent two-sample t tests for pairs of groups. When comparing three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test if significant differences were detected. The Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis H test was utilized for nonnormally distributed or unequally varianced data. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc) and R language, with a significance level of 0.05 set for all tests and two-sided p values less than 0.05 deemed statistically significant.
