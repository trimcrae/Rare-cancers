## ABSTRACT

## Objective
Malignant gliomas pose significant therapeutic challenges. This study aimed to identify and characterize a novel chimeric RNA in glioma and assess its clinical and functional significance for precision treatment.

## Methods
The C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs were identified through RNA sequencing and validated by polymerase chain reaction. Their expression in tumor core and peritumoral tissues was quantified and compared via quantitative polymerase chain reaction, whereas their prognostic significance in glioblastoma was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis. The formation mechanism was investigated through genomic analysis, and western blotting was performed to assess fusion protein translation. The CCK‐8 assay was performed to assess the chimeras' effect on glioma proliferation.

## Results
C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs were detected in 88.9% (144/162) of glioma core tissues, significantly higher than in peritumoral tissues (65.2%, 30/46, p < 0.001). Quantitative analysis showed no significant expression difference between variants in peritumoral tissues, but the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 variant was significantly more abundant in tumor core tissues. High expression of this variant correlated with poor prognosis in glioblastoma patients. Mechanistically, C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeras were generated via cis‐splicing of adjacent genes, without DNA rearrangement. Although Western blot confirmed the translation of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 into a fusion protein in 293 T cells, no endogenous fusion protein was detected in glioblastoma tissues or cells. Functional assays demonstrated that downregulation of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeras significantly suppressed the proliferation of patient‐derived glioblastoma cells.

## Interpretation
This study identifies novel C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeras formed through cis‐splicing, which might function as noncoding RNAs to promote glioblastoma proliferation. These chimeras may serve as potential prognostic markers and therapeutic targets in gliomas.

Keywords: C19orf47‐AKT2, chimeric RNA, cis‐splicing of adjacent genes (cis‐SAGes), fusion gene, glioma

## Introduction
Glioma is the most common malignant primary brain tumor in adults [ ]. Despite standard treatments such as maximal surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis remains poor, with a median survival of less than 21 months [ ]. Among these, glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive subtype and is highly prone to recurrence, with a median survival of only 12 months [ ]. These limitations underscore the urgent need to discover novel therapeutic approaches.
In recent years, chimeric RNA comprising nucleotide sequences from distinct parental genes has garnered increasing interest in the field of molecular biology [ ]. Following the groundbreaking identification of the BCR‐ABL fusion gene in chronic myeloid leukemia, there has been a surge in the discovery of chimeric RNA transcribed from fusion genes across various tumor types [ , , ]. Some of these have been recognized as potent therapeutic targets [ , , , ]. Initially, chimeric RNAs were perceived as the exclusive transcriptional products of fusion genes. However, the advent of advanced DNA and RNA sequencing technologies has facilitated a more comprehensive understanding of these fusion events. It is now recognized that chimeric RNAs can also arise at the RNA level through intergenic splicing mechanisms, including cis‐splicing of adjacent genes (cis‐SAGes) [ , , ] and trans‐splicing [ ]. These findings have expanded our understanding of chimeric RNA biogenesis and function. Importantly, certain tumor‐associated chimeric RNAs hold promise as novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer treatment.
In glioma research, attention has largely focused on chimeric RNAs derived from DNA‐level rearrangements, such as PTPRZ1‐MET fusion identified in approximately 14% of secondary GBM [ ]. Clinical translation of such discoveries is exemplified by the MET‐specific inhibitor PLB‐1001, which has shown safety and efficacy in clinical trials [ ] and was approved for clinical use in 2024. However, the clinical applicability of fusion gene‐targeted inhibitors is limited by the low incidence of these fusions. In contrast, RNA‐level chimeric transcripts, especially those generated through cis‐splicing of adjacent genes, may be more prevalent [ , ] and thus offer a broader therapeutic potential. Despite this, RNA‐derived fusions remain largely uncharacterized in glioma, and their biological functions are poorly understood.
In this study, we report the identification of a novel chimeric RNA, C19orf47‐AKT2, derived from cis‐splicing of adjacent genes, on the basis of paired‐end RNA sequencing data. The fusion transcript was validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing, and its expression profile, splicing mechanism, and biological relevance were further explored.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Patient Recruitment and Sample Collection
Tumorous and peritumoral tissues were retrospectively collected from the glioma specimen bank at Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University. Inclusion criteria were: [ ] patients who underwent craniotomy with a postoperative pathological diagnosis of glioma and [ ] availability of frozen tumor tissue and complete clinical data. Samples with insufficient quantity or poor quality that precluded RNA extraction were excluded.
On the basis of these criteria, 162 tumor samples and 46 peritumoral samples were included. Among them, 42 paired samples of glioma core and peritumoral tissue, and 4 paired with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), were used to assess fusion RNA expression across different tissues. 90 samples with complete clinical follow‐up (all were GBM) were analyzed for prognostic value. Additionally, brain tissue from 5 patients who underwent epilepsy surgery (the total available in the database) was included to examine fusion RNA expression in normal brain tissue. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The characteristics of patients enrolled in this study were summarized in Table  .

2.2.

## Nucleic Acid Extraction
Total RNA was extracted from fresh‐frozen tumor samples using TRIzol reagent, following the protocol established by Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory [ ]. Concurrently, total DNA was isolated from the same samples using the SteadyPure Universal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, AG21009). The concentration and integrity of the extracted RNA and DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

2.3.

## Identification of Fusion Transcripts
Total RNA was extracted and sent for sequencing and analysis (BGI Co. LTD., Shenzhen, China). Briefly, total RNA was fragmented, and mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads, followed by cDNA synthesis. Double‐stranded cDNA was purified and enriched by PCR amplification, after which the library products were sequenced using the BGISEQ‐500 sequencer.
Raw paired‐end reads were processed using the SOAPfuse pipeline with default parameters [ ]. Initially, reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using SOAP2 (v2.21) and categorized into three types: paired‐end mapped reads (PE‐S01), with both ends properly aligned and appropriate insert sizes; single‐end mapped reads (SE‐S01), where only one end mapped or the insert size was abnormal; and unmapped reads (UM‐S01). PE‐S01 reads were used to assess insert size distribution. UM‐S01 reads were then aligned to annotated transcripts, generating additional single‐end mapped reads (SE‐S02) and remaining unmapped reads (UM‐S02). UM‐S02 reads were realigned to annotated transcripts using BWA (v0.5.9) to eliminate those unmapped owing to small indels, resulting in filtered unmapped reads (FUM). If more than 20% of FUM reads had overlapping 3′ ends, SOAPfuse performed iterative trimming: five bases were removed from the 3′ end, then the 5′ end, until reads could be mapped or their length dropped below 30 nucleotides. Successfully mapped reads from this process were retained as SE‐S03.
Candidate gene pairs were identified from discordantly aligned reads by detecting span‐reads, including both uniquely mapped and trimmed reads with multiple hits. Filters were applied to exclude gene pairs from the same gene family or those with overlapping or homologous exon regions. Default parameters were used for the maximum number of hits per span‐read and other filtering steps. Fusion events with substantial supporting evidence from spanning and junction reads, or involving critical oncogenes, were selected for further manual review.

2.4.

## Validation of Fusion Transcripts by RT‐PCR and Sanger Sequencing
Reverse transcription (RT) reactions were performed on 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa), adhering to the manufacturer's instructions. For PCR amplification, 500 ng of the resulting cDNA was combined with custom‐designed primers Table  ) in a 50 μL reaction mixture containing 2X Accurate Taq Master Mix (Accurate Biology), following the manufacturer's protocol. A nontemplate control, in which no cDNA was added, was included as negative control to validate the RT‐PCR procedures. The tumor sample obtained from patient 1, which exhibited strong expression of both chimeric RNA variants confirmed by PCR and Sanger sequencing, served as the positive control. Additionally, amplification of GAPDH transcript was performed to assess the efficiency of the RT reaction and to quantify the expression of each fusion variant. The amplification products were then collected, purified, and subjected to Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech).

2.5.

## Expression Plasmid Construction
The C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 fusion expression plasmid was generated by PCR using the index case as a template and confirmed by sequencing. The expression plasmid was FLAG‐tagged at the C‐terminus and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (GenePharma, Shanghai).

2.6.

## Cell Lysis and Immunoblotting
Tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors at 4°C. Protein lysates were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Solarbio). The membranes were incubated in blocking buffer for 1 h and then probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein detection was carried out using an ECL Western blotting substrate kit (Solarbio, PE0010).
HeLa cell lysates, which express endogenous AKT2, were used as a positive control for the AKT2 antibody. Owing to the novel nature of the potential fusion protein, definitive positive or negative controls were unavailable. However, the AKT2 antibody's epitope is preserved in the chimeric sequence. Thus, if the fusion protein is expressed, two bands are expected: one at ~60 kDa for endogenous AKT2 and another at ~93 kDa corresponding to the predicted fusion protein.

2.7.

## 
RNA Interference
A universal siRNA with no homology to the target gene sequence was used as a negative control (5’‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT‐3′ and 5’‐ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT‐3′). Two specific siRNAs targeting C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 (siRNA1, 5’‐GCACCAAGAACUGUGCCCUTT‐3′, 3’‐AGGGCACAGUUCUUGGUGCTT‐5′) and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 (siRNA2, 5’‐GCACCAAGAGUGAAUACAUTT‐3′, 5’‐AUGUAUUCACUCUUGGUGCTT‐3′) were designed and synthesized. The siRNA transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

2.8.

## 
CCK8 Assay
Patient‐derived GBM cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 10 [ ] cells per well and incubated for 4 days. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK8 (Solarbio, CA1210) and 90 μL of serum‐free medium were added and incubated at 37°C for 2 h every 24 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Bio‐Tek microplate reader.

2.9.

## Statistical Analysis
Categorical variables, including WHO grade, mutation status of key molecular markers, and the presence or absence of chimeric RNA, were expressed as counts and percentages. Detection rates of chimeric RNAs between different groups were compared using the Chi‐square test. The expression levels of two chimeric RNAs in tumor core and peritumoral tissues were compared using paired t‐tests. Survival outcomes were analyzed with Kaplan–Meier curves and compared via log‐rank tests. Differences in the proliferation rate of GBM cells were assessed using multivariate two‐way repeated measures ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1).

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Identification and Validation of the C19orf47‐AKT2 Chimeric RNA

Paired‐end RNA sequencing analysis was conducted on six GBM samples, leading to the identification of a novel C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNA in three cases (Figure  ). Utilizing the SOAPfuse algorithm, two distinct chimeric transcripts were detected, both sharing an identical breakpoint in the C19orf47 coding sequence but differing in their breakpoints within AKT2. The first variant, which we designated as C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2, comprises exons 1–9 of C19orf47 (with a breakpoint in exon 9 at chr19:40827935) fused to exons 2–14 of AKT2 (with a breakpoint at the start of exon 2 at chr19:40771258). This out‐of‐frame fusion introduces a premature stop codon within the AKT2 segment. The second variant, referred to as C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3, also includes exons 1–9 of C19orf47 (same breakpoint), but is fused to exons 3–14 of AKT2 (with a breakpoint at the start of exon 3 at chr19:40762961), resulting in an in‐frame fusion. This in‐frame variant is theorized to encode a fusion protein that loses the initial 15 amino acids and substitutes the sixteenth amino acid from glycine to serine in the AKT2 PH domain (Figure  ). The presence of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 was supported by 6 junction reads (Figure  ) and 13 spanning reads (Figure  ), whereas C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 was supported by 1 junction read (Figure  ) and 2 spanning reads (Figure  ).
 To confirm the presence of the detected chimeric RNA in gliomas, a primer pair flanking exon 9 of C19orf47 and exon 4 of AKT2 was designed (Table  ) to amplify both C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 chimeric RNAs. Both chimeric variants were successfully validated using the RT‐PCR approach followed by Sanger sequencing. Visualization of PCR products on a 1.5% agarose gel revealed two bands of approximately 610 bp and 480 bp for the primer pair used (Figure  ). Sequence analysis and further homology searches (BLASTN) within the genomic dataset confirmed that the PCR product comprised the C19orf47 fused to the AKT2 gene (Figure  ). Screening of 162 glioma core tissues, including 107 WHO IV gliomas, 13 WHO III gliomas, 35 WHO II gliomas, and 7 WHO I gliomas, confirmed the presence of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs in 90, 13, 34, and 7 cases, respectively. Additionally, among the 46 cases of peritumoral tissue screened, 30 tested positive for the C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs (Table  , Figure  ). The detection rate was significantly higher in glioma core tissues than in peritumoral tissues (88.9% vs. 65.2%, p < 0.001). These chimeric RNAs were also detectable in commonly used glioma cell lines, namely, A172, Ln229, T98, U251, and U87 (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## Prognostic Role of C19orf47‐AKT2 Chimeric RNAs

To further investigate the prognostic value of the C19orf47‐AKT2 chimera, we compared their expression level in tumor core tissues with their peritumoral counterpart. As illustrated in Figure  , these C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs were undetectable in the peritumoral tissues of the three tumor samples where the fusion was initially identified, suggesting a potential association with tumor progression. We further analyzed 42 pairs of tumor and peritumoral tissues (including the initial three pairs mentioned above), revealing detection rates for C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 in the tumor core tissues of 38/42 (90.48%) and 35/42 (83.33%), respectively (Figure  ). These rates were higher than those observed in the peritumoral tissues, which were 30/42 (71.43%) and 27/42 (64.29%), with p < 0.05 and p = 0.08, respectively (Figure  ). However, semi‐quantitative analysis of gray values derived from PCR results indicated no significant differences in the expression levels of these two fusion forms between the tumor core and peritumoral tissues (Figure  ). A comparison of the expression levels of the two fusion variants revealed that the expression of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 in tumor core tissues was significantly higher than that of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 (Figure  , left panel, p = 0.0001). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the two forms in the peritumoral tissues (Figure  , right panel, p = 0.4102). This finding suggests that the imbalance in the ratio of these fusion variants may be linked to tumor progression.
 Considering that the peritumor region may not accurately represent normal controls owing to potential tumor cell infiltration, we conducted a comparison of the expression levels of both chimeric RNAs between GBM tissues and their corresponding PBMC samples. The results indicated that the positivity rate for chimeric RNAs was higher in tumor tissue (4 out of 4) than in normal controls (2 out of 4, p = 0.428). Additionally, the expression levels of both chimeric variants, C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3, were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to PBMC (p = 0.04 and p = 0.01, respectively). Furthermore, the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 variant was found to be more abundant than C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 in tumor tissues (Figure  , p < 0.001), consistent with the previous findings. We also obtained five brain tissue samples from patients who underwent resection for epileptic foci to assess chimeric RNA expression in normal brain. As shown in Figure  , we observed faint bands in only one sample (N4). We excised the agarose containing these bands for Sanger sequencing, but the sequencing was unsuccessful owing to the weak signal. These results reinforce the oncogenic role of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs.
Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to assess the prognostic value of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs. As illustrated in Figure  , high expression of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 was associated with poor overall survival in GBM patients (Figure  = 0.048). In contrast, the presence of the C19orf47‐AKT2 chimera, the level of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3, and the total level of both chimeric variants were not identified as prognostic predictors for glioblastoma (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## C19orf47‐AKT2 Chimeric RNA Generated by Cis‐Splicing of Adjacent Genes (SAGes)
To elucidate the mechanism underlying the formation of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric transcripts, we initiated an investigation to screen for genomic DNA rearrangements, a well‐established mechanism for generating chimeric RNAs. It was hypothesized that, at the DNA level, exon 9 of the C19orf47 gene could fuse with various regions within the AKT2 gene, specifically the beginning of intron 1, exon 2, intron 2, or exon 3, leading to the generation of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric transcripts either directly or through an alternative splicing process. The genomic breakpoint could also potentially reside within AKT2 intron 1 or intron 2, resulting in the formation of chimeric transcripts via the use of alternative 3′ splice sites. To test this hypothesis, four primer pairs were designed to flank the breakpoint at exon 9 of the C19orf47 gene and the corresponding regions of the AKT2 gene (Figure  , Table  ). However, despite utilizing DNA extracted from glioma tissues confirmed to contain C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs, RT‐PCR with these primer pairs did not yield the expected amplification products (Figure  ).
 Given the extensive intron sequences within AKT2, further screening for breakpoints was not pursued. Instead, considering the proximity of C19orf47 and AKT2 on chromosome 19, it was suspected that the chimeric transcripts might arise from cis‐splicing of adjacent genes, where a premature RNA transcript spans the gene boundary, leading to the splicing together of exons from both parental genes. To test this hypothesis, two primer pairs were designed to target regions between the C19orf47 and AKT2 genes to amplify potential intergenic transcripts (Figure  , Table  ). The intergenic transcripts were successfully detected by gel electrophoresis (Figure  ) and validated through Sanger sequencing (Figure  ). These findings supported the hypothesis that the C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric transcripts are derived from cis‐SAGes in the absence of genomic rearrangements.
 
3.4.

## Coding Potential of the C19orf47‐AKT2 Chimeric RNA

On the basis of the junction sequence of the chimeric RNAs, it was predicted that translation of the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 transcript would result in a fusion protein containing in‐frame coding regions from both C19orf47 and AKT2. This fusion protein was anticipated to encode most of AKT2, including its kinase domain. However, it would lack the first 15 amino acids from the N‐terminus. These amino acids encode the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, which is essential for membrane targeting and activation of AKT2.
To investigate the potential translation of the predicted C19orf47‐AKT2 fusion protein in glioma patients, Western blot analysis was performed on tissues and cells derived from patients with GBM who were positive for C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs. However, the AKT2 antibody failed to detect an additional band corresponding to the predicted fusion protein (~93 kDa), which is distinct from the wild‐type AKT2 (~60 kDa) (Figure  ). Considering the possibility that certain proteins might be rapidly degraded after functioning, rendering them undetectable, patient‐derived GBM cells were pretreated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, prior to protein harvest. Despite these efforts, the predicted 110 kDa band remained undetected (Figure  ).
 To facilitate further investigation, we constructed an expression plasmid encoding the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 with a carboxyl‐terminal FLAG tag, enabling detection of its translation. Introduction of this expression plasmid into 293 T cells resulted in the appearance of an additional band at the predicted molecular weight in a dose‐dependent manner, as detected by the AKT2 antibody. Forced expression of the fusion protein was further confirmed through anti‐Flag blotting (Figure  ).

3.5.

## In Silico Prediction of the Oncogenic Potential of C19orf47‐AKT2

An in silico prediction analysis was conducted using the Oncofuse classifier and Bayesian probability to assess the oncogenic potential of specific fusion transcripts. The analysis revealed a high probability that both C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 (p < 0.001) and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 (p < 0.001) chimeric RNAs function as drivers in the oncogenic process. The complete results of the Oncofuse classification are presented in Table  .

3.6.

## Biological Function of C19orf47‐AKT2 Chimeric RNAs

To investigate the biological function of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs in glioma, we designed siRNAs targeting the fusion sites of the chimeric transcripts (siRNA1 for C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and siRNA2 for C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3). Using two primary cell cultures derived from GBM tissue confirmed to express C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs, we found that siRNA1 and siRNA2 significantly reduced the levels of C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric transcripts (Figure  ). CCK‐8 assay results indicated that the primary cells in the chimeric RNA knockdown groups exhibited decreased proliferation capacity compared to the control group (both p < 0.001; Figure  ). These findings suggest that C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs contribute to the proliferative capacity of GBM cells.

## Discussion
Fusion RNA profiling has emerged as a critical tool in the diagnosis and molecular characterization of gliomas, enabling more precise classification and targeted therapeutic strategies. The 2021 WHO Classification of CNS tumors incorporated several pediatric glioma subtypes defined by recurrent gene fusions, such as the MYB‐ or MYBL1‐altered diffuse astrocytomas [ ] and the infant‐type hemispheric gliomas characterized by fusions involving ALK [ ], ROS1 [ ], NTRK1/2/3 [ ], and MET [ ]. However, since the report by Zhang et al. demonstrated that the SLC45A3‐ELK4 chimera in prostate cancer is generated through cis‐splicing, which overcomes the read‐through block imposed by intergenic CCCTC insulators bound by the CTCF repressor protein [ ], there has been limited progress in this area. Recently, an additional study has identified UBA1‐CDK16, a female‐specific chimeric transcript formed by cis‐splicing between two adjacent X‐linked genes originating from the inactive X chromosome. This transcript is evolutionarily conserved, enriched in the myeloid lineage, and plays a regulatory role in myeloid differentiation, with potential implications for sexual dimorphism, immune responses, and COVID‐19 pathogenesis [ ]. In this context, our study expands the scope of chimeric RNAs derived from cis‐splicing by reporting two novel variants: C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3. These chimeric RNAs promote GBM proliferation and correlate with patient prognosis, highlighting their biological relevance in glioma.
The upstream fusion partner, C19orf47, is a protein‐coding gene whose function remains to be determined. In contrast, the downstream fusion partner, AKT2, is a well‐characterized oncogene that encodes one of the three isoforms of serine/threonine protein kinases, which play crucial roles in human physiology and disease [ ]. AKT2 has been reported to regulate the motility, invasion, survival, and growth of glioma cells. Moreover, the expression level of AKT2 has been shown to increase with glioma grade and is inversely correlated with patient outcomes [ ].
In this study, C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs were undetectable in all five nontumorous brain tissue samples from epilepsy patients but were present in a significant proportion of glioma samples, indicating tumor specificity. Intriguingly, C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs were also detected in some peritumoral tissues. This likely reflects the inherent limitation of radiological boundaries, which often fail to capture the full extent of glioma cell infiltration. Indeed, previous studies have shown that glioma cells commonly invade beyond margins visible on imaging [ ]. Additionally, low‐level expression was also observed in PBMCs from some patients. This may result from glioma cell apoptosis or necrosis, releasing chimeric RNAs into circulation [ ], or from tumor‐derived extracellular vesicles transferring RNA to PBMCs [ ]. Importantly, we found that the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 was associated with poor prognosis of GBM patients, suggesting its potential as a circulating biomarker. However, further studies are needed to experimentally validate this hypothesis.
Further quantitative analysis of the expression levels of these chimeric variants revealed no significant difference between tumor core and peritumoral tissues (Figure  ). When normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH, no difference in expression was observed between the two variants in peritumoral tissue. Interestingly, the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 variant was found to be more abundant than C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 in tumor core tissue. These findings collectively suggest that the dysregulation of the two fusion variants, with a skew toward C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2, may contribute to glioma progression. The significant association of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 expression levels with patient prognosis further supports this claim.
To explore the formation mechanism of the novel C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs, several primer pairs were meticulously designed to characterize the DNA breakpoint(s) using genomic DNA PCR. Despite considerable effort, no amplification product of the predicted size could be generated with these primer pairs. The investigation then shifted to whether the C19orf47‐AKT2 chimera results from cis‐splicing, as it fulfills several criteria associated with cis‐SAGes: 1) C19orf47 and AKT2 are neighboring genes on the same strand; 2) the distance between C19orf47 and AKT2 is within 30 kb; and 3) the chimera follows the 2–2 rule, where the penultimate exon in the 5′ gene is joined to the second exon in the 3′ gene [ ]. Surprisingly, PCR amplification with two primer pairs targeting the intergenic region between C19orf47 and the AKT2 gene yielded positive results. Thus, this study reports that the newly discovered C19orf47‐AKT2 chimeric RNAs are generated by the cis‐splicing mechanism of adjacent genes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of intergenic splicing‐derived chimeric RNA in glioma.
In this study, we identified two chimeric variants of the C19orf47‐AKT2 fusion: the in‐frame C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 and the out‐of‐frame C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2. The in‐frame variant is predicted to encode a fusion protein containing domains from both parent genes, and this coding potential was validated by transfecting an expression plasmid into 293 T cells. However, no endogenous fusion protein was detected in GBM tissues or patient‐derived GBM cells, even after proteasome inhibition. These results suggest that the C19orf47‐AKT2 fusion may not function through protein translation. Moreover, we found that the out‐of‐frame C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 fusion variant is significantly associated with the prognosis of GBM patients, suggesting a potential oncological role for this fusion form. Additionally, when we used siRNA to downregulate the total fusion RNA, the proliferative capacity of GBM primary cells was significantly inhibited. In the absence of endogenous fusion protein, this result further supports the idea that the fusion RNA may act through noncoding mechanisms. Quantitative analysis revealed no significant difference in the expression of either variant in peritumoral tissues. However, C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 was markedly upregulated in tumor core tissues. On the basis of these findings, we propose that C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 and C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 are alternative splicing products of a read‐through transcript. Under normal conditions, their expression is balanced. However, for reasons yet to be elucidated, this balance is disrupted, shifting toward the C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 form. This variant may contribute to tumor growth as a long noncoding RNA, similar to the SLC45A3‐ELK4 chimeric RNA reported in prostate cancer [ ].
Our study has several limitations. First, although high expression of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 was significantly associated with poor prognosis in GBM patients, this chimeric RNA was also detected in other glioma subtypes. However, owing to the limited follow‐up data available exclusively for GBM patients, we were unable to assess its prognostic value in other pathological types. Future prospective studies with broader glioma cohorts are needed to clarify this issue. Second, sequence analysis revealed that C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 is an in‐frame fusion RNA with coding potential. Indeed, fusion protein expression could be detected upon overexpression in 293 T cells. However, the endogenous fusion protein was undetectable in either GBM tissues or patient‐derived GBM cells, even after proteasome inhibition. Drawing on prior reports of coding‐potential chimeras functioning as lncRNAs [ ], we propose that C19orf47‐AKT2 chimera may exert oncogenic effects in a noncoding manner. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation. Future work will focus on exploring RNA stability and lncRNA‐specific mechanisms to better understand their biological function.
In conclusion, this study identifies a novel C19orf47‐AKT2 fusion event, comprising an out‐of‐frame C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2 variant and an in‐frame C19orf47e9‐AKT2e3 variant in glioma. These chimeric RNAs are produced through the cis‐splicing of the adjacent C19orf47 and AKT2 genes. The dysregulation of these two fusion variants, particularly the predominance of C19orf47e9‐AKT2e2, contributes to glioma malignancy. Specifically, C19orf47‐AKT2 might act as lncRNAs that enhance glioma proliferation. This novel fusion event may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for glioma (Figure  ).
