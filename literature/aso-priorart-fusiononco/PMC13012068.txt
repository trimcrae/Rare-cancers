## Abstract
INAFM2, the human homolog of the Drosophila inaF, is a predicted membrane protein with no known function in vertebrates. Through an in vivo genome-wide transcriptional activation screen, we uncovered INAFM2 as a potent driver of metastasis, leading us to propose naming the vertebrate gene and its protein product ROME (regulator of metastasis). We discovered ROME’s subcellular localization, posttranslational modifications, and transcriptional profiles related to its expression. ROME negatively regulates the canonical Wnt pathway by directly binding to β-catenin. Blocking rome expression in zebrafish embryos results in severe developmental defects and early mortality, which can be reversed by inhibiting the canonical Wnt pathway. Notably, we demonstrate that ROME expression regulates human cancer cell motility and invasion in vitro and metastasis in vivo in both zebrafish and immunodeficient mice via tail vein and orthotopic injection models. ROME-mediated increase in cancer cell intravasation is dependent on its direct interaction with vimentin. Furthermore, we show that elevated ROME expression correlates with poorer patient survival in multiple human cancers. Taken together, this is the first report of the vertebrate ROME gene producing a biologically active plasma membrane glycoprotein that is critical for normal development and metastasis.

## Significance:
This is the first report of a detailed characterization of the molecular features of ROME (INAFM2) protein in mammalian cells and its biochemical and biological functions related to vertebrate development and cancer metastasis.

## Introduction
Despite many advances in cancer treatment, metastasis remains the major cause of mortality; more than 90% of cancer-related deaths are due to metastasis ( ). We chose Ewing sarcoma as a model to study cancer metastasis as the 5-year survival rate for patients with Ewing sarcoma with metastatic disease is less than 25%, and there are no effective therapies available to specifically target metastatic disease ( , ). Ewing sarcoma is the second most common bone and soft-tissue tumor in children and adolescents and is characterized by a chromosomal translocation that creates a chimeric protein, most commonly the EWS::FLI1 fusion protein ( ). The EWS::FLI1 protein is essential for both tumor initiation and maintenance in Ewing sarcoma ( – ). A few potential proteins involved in Ewing sarcoma metastasis have been identified, including ZEB2, TWIST1, LOXHD1, HDGF, ICAM1, CREB1, and TCF7L1 ( – ). Interestingly, Ewing sarcoma cells expressing low levels of the EWS::FLI1 oncoprotein are more invasive and metastatic, whereas cells with high levels of EWS::FLI1 oncoprotein are more proliferative ( ). Furthermore, low EWS::FLI1 levels have been associated with poorer patient outcomes (bioRxiv 2025.05.16.654502).
In Drosophila, the inaF locus encodes the proteins INAF-A, INAF-B, INAF-C, and INAF-D ( ). Three INAF proteins (A–C) associate with the transient receptor potential (TRP) protein, which is an essential calcium channel in the photoreceptors of Drosophila ( – ). INAF-B and INAF-C are considered auxiliary subunits for TRP, and their loss significantly affects TRP function ( – ). The four INAF proteins share homology in their transmembrane domain, and this shared sequence is defined as the inaF motif ( ). The human homolog INAFM2 is a single-exon gene comprising 3,024 nucleotides, is located on chromosome 15, and is predicted to encode a 153–amino acid protein product ( , ). INAFM2 has been mentioned in several genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and a tumor microenvironment prognostic signature correlation study and was also identified to be part of the TRP complex in mice ( – ). However, beyond these correlative investigations, INAFM2 has not been studied in depth, and neither the protein expression profile nor the function of INAFM2 is known.
We identified INAFM2 as the top hit in an in vivo CRISPR/Cas9 transcriptional activation screen and demonstrated that the human INAFM2 gene is a key driver of metastasis. To our knowledge, we are the first to report that the human INAFM2 gene produces a functional protein product. On the basis of its biological function, we propose to name the vertebrate protein product ROME (regulator of metastasis); for consistency, we also propose renaming the vertebrate gene INAFM2 to ROME.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell culture
STA-ET-7.2 (RRID: CVCL_9693), TC-71 (RRID: CVCL_2213), and 769-P (RRID: CVCL_1050) cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 media (Gibco, #11875119) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma, #F0926). A4573 (RRID: CVCL_6245) and TC-32 (RRID: CVCL_7151) cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% HEPES (Gibco, #15630080). The CHLA-10 (RRID: CVCL_6583) cell line was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #11965118) supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% insulin, transferrin, and selenium (Sigma, #I13146). Hs746.T (RRID: CVCL_0333), HEK293T (Lenti-X; purchased directly from Takara Bio, #632180), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), and LS180 (RRID: CVCL_0397) cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. All cell lines were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Cell lines were kept in culture for a maximum of 15 passages between freeze downs. Cell lines were tested for Mycoplasma contamination monthly by using the MycoAlert system (Lonza, #LT07-318) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
STA-ET-7.2 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Heinrich Kovar, Children’s Cancer Research Institute. A4573, TC-32, and TC-71 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Tim Triche, Children’s Hospital Los Angeles. CHLA-10 cells were kindly provided by the Children’s Oncology Group. Cell identity for STA-ET-7.2, A4573, TC-32, TC-71, and CHLA-10 cells was confirmed by fingerprinting between 2019 and 2022. 769-P, Hs746.T, HCT116, and LS180 were obtained directly from the Georgetown University Medical Center Tissue Culture and Biobanking Shared Resource and HEK293T were obtained directly from Takara Bio. Cells were kept in culture for less than 6 months and these cell lines were not fingerprinted in the lab.

## Plasmid and siRNA transfections
To create a TC-71 cell line stably expressing dCas9 protein, cells were transfected in six-well plates with the lenti dCAS-VP64_Blast plasmid (a gift from Feng Zhang, RRID: Addgene_61425) using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Sigma, #6365787001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blasticidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #R210-01; final concentration 5 μg/mL) was added 24 hours after transfection and antibiotic selection was maintained for 2 weeks. To create TC-71 cell lines stably overexpressing different cDNAs of interest, cells were transfected in T25 flasks with the individual GenEZ cDNA ORF (GenScript) and cloned in the pcDNA3.1(+)-C-DYK backbone in-frame with a FLAG tag by using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent. The accession numbers for the open reading frame (ORF) clones were CD68 (#NM_001251.3), CXCL8 (#NM_000584.4), ELMO2 (#NM_133171.5), ROME (INAFM2; #NM_001301268.1), KCTD20 (#NM_173562.5), KDR (#NM_002253.3), RAB9A (#NM_004251.4), and REL (#NM_002908.3). The expression of each ORF construct was confirmed by qPCR. Geneticin (G418, Sigma, #345812), at a final concentration of 400 μg/mL, was added 24 hours after transfection and maintained for 2 weeks. pcDNA3.1_V5-TWIST1 was obtained from Addgene (gift from Joanna Wysocka, RRID: Addgene_215030; ref. ).
Plasmids expressing ROME-GFP, ROME-HA, and ROME-MYC were generated from the original ROME (INAFM2)-pcDNA3.1(+)-C-DYK plasmid by cloning the ROME ORF into pcDNA3.1(+)-C-HA, pcDNA3.1(+)-C-Myc, and pcDNA3.1(+)-C-eGFP plasmids. The MYC-BioID2-MCS (gift from Kyle Roux, RRID: Addgene_74223) plasmid was used to generate the MYC-BioID2-ROME expressing construct ( ). All constructs have G418 resistance gene as the selection marker.
Tet-on-ROME-HA construct was created by subcloning the ROME-HA gene synthesis fragment into pLVX-TetOne-Puro-GFP (gift from Jason Sheltzer, Addgene, #171123; RRID: Addgene_171123).
Custom ROME siRNA with a sense sequence of 5′-GGACGAGGACGAAGAGGAAUU-3′ and custom vimentin siRNA with a sense sequence of 5′-GAAUGGUACAAAUCCAAGU-3′ were purchased from Horizon Discovery. The nontargeting siRNA used as a control was the Silencer Select Negative Control No.2 siRNA (Invitrogen, #4390846). DharmaFECT 1 (Horizon Discovery, #T-2001-03) was used for transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocol with the highest recommended ratio of the transfection reagent to siRNA.

## Creating ROME knockout cell lines
For knocking out ROME expression, the Gene Knockout Kit v2 was used (Synthego, #10217858-1). A pool of guide RNAs (gRNA) targeting the ROME gene locus was transfected along with recombinant Cas9 protein according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences of the gRNAs were 5′-UUGUCCCCGGUGUAGGUGGC-3′, 5′-GAGCACGUAGGCGAACACGG-3′, and 5′-AUCUGGCAGCCGGUGGGCGC-3′. Individual colonies were expanded by separating the cells in 96-well plates by limiting dilution. Colonies were screened for knockout of ROME expression by qPCR at the mRNA level and by Western blotting at the protein level. ROME knockout (KO) was further verified by genomic DNA (gDNA) sequencing.

## Immunoblotting
Total cellular protein extract was prepared by using phospho-lysis buffer (50 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.9, 100 mmol/L NaCl, 4 mmol/L sodium pyrophosphate, 10 mmol/L EDTA, 10 mmol/L sodium fluoride, and 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with vanadate (2 mmol/L), phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (1 mmol/L), aprotinin (2 μg/mL), and leupeptin (2 μg/mL). Protein concentration was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A65453). Samples were resolved by an SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to an Immobilon-P polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Sigma, #IPVH00010). The membrane was blocked for 1 hour at room temperature in 5% nonfat dry milk diluted in 1x TTBS (20 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mmol/L NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20) and then incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Antibodies used were anti-Cas9 (10C11-A12) monoclonal antibody (1:1,000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #MA1-202, RRID: AB_2633310), anti-FLAG antibodies (1:1,000 dilution; GenScript, #A01428-100, RRID: AB_1720817; Cell Signaling Technology, #14793S, RRID: AB_2572291; and Sigma, #F7425, RRID: AB_439687), anti–α-tubulin (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #2144, RRID: AB_2210548), anti–lamin A/C (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #2032, RRID: AB_2136278), anti-CD99 (1:1,000 dilution; Abcam, #ab75858, RRID: AB_1310063), anti–HA-tag (1:1,000 dilution; GenScript, #A01963 and 1:5,000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, #3724, RRID: AB_1549585), anti–MYC-tag (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #2276, RRID: AB_331783), anti-TRPC1 (1:1,000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #MA5-35432, RRID: AB_2849333), anti-FLI1 (1:1,000 dilution; Abcam, #ab15289, RRID: AB_301825), anti–β-catenin (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #8480, RRID: AB_11127855), anti-TCF4/-TCF7L2 (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #2569, RRID: AB_2199816), anti–V5-tag (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #13202, RRID: AB_2687461), anti-vimentin (1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #5741, RRID: AB_10695459), and anti–phospho-vimentin (Ser56; 1:1,000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, #7391, RRID: AB_11142601).
After primary antibody incubation, the membrane was rinsed three times in 1x TTBS and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in anti–rabbit or anti–mouse IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–linked whole antibodies (Cytiva, #NA934, RRID: AB_772206 and #NA931V, RRID: AB_772210, respectively). The membrane was then washed three times in 1x TTBS and developed by using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Sigma, #WBKLS0500). The LI-COR Odyssey imaging system was used to visualize the membrane.
Cell fractionation experiments were done with the Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit for Cultured Cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #78840).
For immunoprecipitation, lysates were first precleared with mouse (G3A1) mAb IgG1 isotype control (Cell Signaling Technology, #5415, RRID: AB_10829607) or normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729, RRID: AB_1031062) and protein G magnetic beads (Active Motif, #53033) for 1 hour, tumbling at 4°C. Precleared lysates were then incubated with mouse (G3A1) mAb IgG1 isotype control, normal rabbit IgG, β-catenin antibody, or ROME antibody (ICD-6) tumbling at 4°C overnight. Immunoprecipitation was then completed using protein G magnetic beads following the manufacturer’s protocol.
Deglycosylation experiments were done with the Glycoprotein Deglycosylation Kit (Sigma, #362280) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. As input, 40 μg of the total cell lysate was used for each deglycosylation reaction.

## Zebrafish xenograft experiments
Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were raised, maintained, and crossed as described ( ). Zebrafish lines used and reported elsewhere were wild-type (WT) EK, Tg(kdrl:grcfp)zn1, or mutant Cas {“Casper” [mitfaw2/w2(“nacre”, nac);mpv17a9/a9(roy)]}. The development of embryos was at 28°C, and staging was determined by both hours post fertilization (hpf) and morphologic characteristics ( ). All procedures were in accordance with NIH guidelines on the care and use of animals and were approved by the Georgetown University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Protocol 2017-0078.
To prepare for zebrafish xenograft injections, fish were put in breeding chambers as pairs or groups in the evening and for natural breeding the following morning, when the embryos were collected and cleaned of debris and unfertilized eggs. Embryos were raised at 28°C in fish water (0.3 g/L sea salts) containing 0.003% 1-phenyl 2-thiourea. On day 1 after fertilization, embryos were dechorionated in 200 μg/mL pronase. Ewing sarcoma cells were grown to 70% to 90% confluency. The cells were harvested by trypsinization and counted by a hemacytometer, and 1 million cells were resuspended in 100 μL of media. Cells were labeled with Vybrant CM-Dil Cell-Labeling solution (1:100 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #V22888) for monitoring under a fluorescent microscope after injection. On day 2 after fertilization, embryos were anesthetized with tricaine (160 mg/L) and aligned on agarose injection plates for injection into the yolk sac. The injected embryos were checked under the microscope to confirm the presence of a primary tumor in the yolk sac and to confirm that no cells were injected into the circulation of the fish. After injection, fish were kept at 33°C. Cells were tracked over time by using a fluorescent microscope at 5× magnification. Metastasis to the tail was evaluated on days 1 to 5 after injection. Fish with at least two cells in the tail were considered to have metastases. χ2 tests were used to determine statistical significance. Zebrafish embryo injections were performed by Georgetown University Medical Center’s Zebrafish Shared Resource.

## Zebrafish morpholino oligonucleotide and mRNA injections
Morpholino (Gene Tools) and mRNA injections were performed at the indicated doses into one-cell- (mRNA) or two- to four-cell-stage embryos [morpholino oligonucleotide (MO)]. Coinjection was performed sequentially. The rome translation blocking MO used in this study was 5′- CTCTCCATATTGGGCATGTAGTCTC-3′ (translation start site underlined). Full-length zebrafish rome mRNA was generated after rome amplicon was first cloned into the pME-mcs plasmid (a gift from the Chien Lab, University of Utah) and then gateway-cloned into the pCSDest plasmid (a gift from the Lawson Lab, University of Massachusetts-Amherst). rome mRNA was in vitro transcribed using the mMessage mMachine Kit (Invitrogen, #AM13144). All injections were carried out as described previously and were performed by Georgetown University Medical Center’s Zebrafish Shared Resource ( ). ICG-001 was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-14428).

## CRISPR RNA selection, Cas9 ribonucleoprotein preparation, and embryo microinjection
A reverse-strand protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence was identified within the first 100 nucleotides of the single exon of rome (inafm2) and used to generate a CRISPR Cas9 RNA (crRNA). The following target sequence was synthesized as a crRNA at Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT): 5′-TAAGACGTATGCTTTCACCGTGG-3′ (PAM not included in oligo). The crRNA and tracrRNA (1072532; IDT) were resuspended in duplex buffer (IDT) at 100 mmol/L and annealed by mixing 1:1 to make a 50 mmol/L stock, followed by stepping from 98°C to 20°C over 15 minutes in a thermocycler. Alt-R S.p. Cas9 nuclease V3 was obtained from IDT (1081058). The ribonucleoprotein (RNP) was prepared by complexing 1 μL of Cas9 protein with 1 μL of 25 μmol/L rome (inafm2) cr:tracrRNA in 3 μL sterile water, incubating at 37°C for 5 minutes and placed on ice. The RNP complex was injected at 1 to 2 nL into zebrafish embryos at the one-cell stage. Control injections utilized RNP without the crRNA.

## RNAScope and IHC
Embryos (E10.5-E18.5) were harvested from timed pregnant C57BL/6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664), fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hours at room temperature, and then placed in 70% isopropanol for dehydration. Samples were embedded in paraffin, cut in 5-μm sections, and mounted on slides. RNAscope 2.0 Assay, using a custom target probe for mouse Rome and the RNAscope 2.5 HD Detection Kit – BROWN (cat. #322310), was performed for sagittal sections from embryos at E10.5, 11.5, 12.5, 13.5, 14.5, 15.5, 16.5, 17.5, and 18.5, following the manufacturer’s protocol by the Harvard Neurobiology Imaging Facility ( ). IHC was performed for CD61 (Integrin β3). Five-micron sections from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized with xylenes and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed by immersing the tissue sections at 98°C for 20 minutes in Low-pH solution (Dako, #K8005). IHC staining was performed using an HRP-labeled polymer (Dako, #K4003) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, slides were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide and 10% normal goat serum for 10 minutes each and then exposed to primary antibody for Integrin β3 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, #13166, RRID: AB_2798136) for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were then exposed to the appropriate HRP-labeled polymer for 30 minutes and DAB chromagen (Dako) for 5 minutes. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin (Dako), blued in 1% ammonium hydroxide, dehydrated, and mounted with Acrymount. Consecutive sections with the primary antibody omitted were used as negative controls. Wash buffer used was 1X TBS with 0.05% Tween 20 (Fisher). IHC staining was performed by Georgetown University’s Histopathology and Tissue Shared Resource.

## CRISPR/Cas9 genome-wide screening
gDNA was isolated from the fish tails by grinding the tails in SNET lysis buffer [20 mmol/L Tris-Cl (pH 8), 5 mmol/L EDTA (pH 8), 400 mmol/L NaCl, 1% (w/v) SDS, and 20 mg/mL proteinase K], followed by shaking at 55°C overnight. gDNA was extracted by mixing the lysate with an equal volume of phenol–chloroform (Sigma, #77617) and rocking at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by spinning down for 5 minutes at 16,100 × g. The aqueous phase was transferred into a new tube with an equal volume of isopropanol. gDNA was precipitated by spinning down at 16,100 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. DNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The gRNA sequences were PCR amplified by using a mix of eight P5 and one P7 primers (Supplementary Table S1) for the Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform as previously described ( ). Nested PCR with pXPR_502 primers (Supplementary Table S1) was first performed at the thermocycler conditions of 95°C for 3 minutes, (95°C for 30 seconds, 57°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 1 minute) × 35, and 72°C for 10 minutes. Then 10% of the nested PCR product was used in a second PCR with the library sequencing primers at the thermocycler conditions of 95°C for 3 minutes, (95°C for 30 seconds, 52°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 1 minute) × 40, and 72°C for 10 minutes. PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP purification system (Beckman Coulter, #A63880) and the purification was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The purified PCR product was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform by using the MiSeq 8 Reagent Kit v2 (300 cycle), 2 × 150 bp reads (Georgetown University Medical Center Genomics and Epigenomics Shared Resource). Reads were analyzed utilizing the PinAPL-Py (Platform-independent Analysis of PooLed screens using Python) web application ( ).

## RNA isolation and qPCR
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74134). Then 1 μg total RNA was converted to cDNA by using the SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11754050). Reactions for qRT-PCR were prepared by using SYBR Green ReadyMix (Sigma, #KCQS00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and run on a Roche LightCycler 480 instrument with the following conditions: 95°C for 10 minutes and then (95°C for 15 seconds, 59°C for 30 seconds, and 72°C for 30 seconds) × 40, followed by melting curve determination. 18S was used as an internal control for normalization. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

## Scratch, cell proliferation, and Boyden chamber assays
For the scratch assay, cells were plated in a 12-well plate at ∼50% confluency. Once cells reached 90% confluency, horizontal scratches were made in the cell monolayer by using a 200-μL pipette tip. Scratches were imaged immediately after the scratch and again at 16 to 24 hours after scratching. Images were quantified using the ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070) Wound_healing_size_tool plug-in ( ). Student t test (unpaired, two-tailed) was performed to determine statistical significance.
Cell proliferation rates were determined by using the xCELLigence RTCA DP instrument. The wells of an E-Plate 16 PET (Agilent, #300600890) were coated with 1:50 dilution of 0.1% collagen (Sigma-Aldrich, #C8919) for 1 hour at 37°C and washed twice with PBS, and then 100 μL of cell culture media were added to each well. The plate was allowed to equilibrate for 30 minutes at room temperature and background measurements were acquired. Cells were then added at a density of 10,000 cells (in 100 μL) per well. Cell growth was monitored by electrical impedance readings (calculated as cell index) every 10 minutes over the course of 96 hours.
For the Boyden chamber assay, cells were first serum starved in their corresponding media containing 1% to 1.5% FBS for overnight before transferring them into Boyden chambers the next day. The cells were seeded in 12-well plates containing a TC Plate Insert, PC Membrane (VWR, #10769-212) at 100,000 to 250,000 cells per insert in serum-free media. Serum-containing media were then added to the bottom of the well. Cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 20 to 24 hours. The inserts were then washed with PBS, and the nonmigrated cells from the top surface were removed by using cotton swabs. Migrated cells on the bottom surface were fixed in 100% methanol at room temperature for 20 minutes and then stained with 0.2% crystal violet stain for 10 minutes. The inserts were washed with PBS and then imaged by using a light microscope at 10× magnification. Ten images were taken per well and the number of cells per image was quantified using ImageJ. Statistical significance was determined by the Student t test (unpaired, two-tailed).

## RNA sequencing
Cells were allowed to grow to 70% to 90% confluency and total RNA was isolated as described above. Poly-A tail–enriched libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq PE 150 platform (Novogene). RNA sequencing experiments were done in either biological duplicates (for transiently ROME-overexpressing A4573 and ROME-KO TC-71 cells) or biological triplicates (all other RNA sequencing experiments). Data were processed either on the CLC Genomics Workbench (Qiagen, RRID: SCR_011853) or by using hisat2 (RRID: SCR_015530) for alignment and featcount for quantifying the read counts for each gene. Data were further analyzed by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; RRID: SCR_003199) using the classic enrichment statistic and a Browser-based Tool for the Exploration And Visualization of RNAseq data (BEAVR; refs. , ).

## Fluorescence imaging
Sterilized coverslips were coated with 1:50 dilution of 0.1% collagen (Sigma-Aldrich, #C8919) for 1 hour at 37°C in 12-well plates. The coverslips were then rinsed with PBS two times before seeding cells. Cells were allowed to grow at least overnight and then fixed with 10% formalin for 6 minutes at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with PBS, incubated with blocking solution (5% BSA in PBS) for 1 hour on the rocker, and then washed again three times for 10 minutes each with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS. Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, #P36941) and imaged on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

## Mass spectrometry
For phosphorylation and N-glycosylation site identification, gel bands from Coomassie-stained gel were treated with 20 mmol/L dithiothreitol (Sigma) for 30 minutes and then 40 mmol/L iodoacetamide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in dark for 30 minutes. Proteins were then digested with trypsin and PNGase F at 37°C overnight. Resulting peptides were eluted and dried down with a SpeedVac (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Peptides were analyzed with a nanoAcquity UPLC system coupled with the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specifically, peptides in each sample were loaded onto a C18 Trap column (Waters, AcquityUPLC M-Class Trap Symmetry C18, 100 Å, 5 μm, 180 μm × 20 mm) at 10 μL/minute for 4 minutes. Peptides were then separated with an analytic column (Waters, Acquity UPLC M-Class, peptide BEH C18 column, 300 Å, 1.7 μm, 75 μm × 150 mm), which was temperature controlled at 40°C. The flow rate was set as 400 nL/minute. A 90-minute gradient of buffer A [2% acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.1% formic acid] and buffer B (0.1% formic acid in ACN) was used for separation: 1% buffer B at 0 minute, 5% buffer B at 1 minute, 22% buffer B at 60 minutes, 36% buffer B at 75 minutes, 50% buffer B at 80 minutes, 90% buffer B at 85 minutes, and 90% buffer B at 90 minutes. Data were acquired with the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer using an ion spray voltage of 2.4 kV and an ion transfer temperature of 275°C. Mass spectra were recorded with Xcalibur 4.0 (RRID: SCR_014593). Mass spectrometry (MS) parameters were OT MS: detector type: Orbitrap; Orbitrap resolution: 60,000; scan range (m/z): 375 to 1,500; RF lens (%): 30; AGC target: standard; maximum injection time mode: auto; and microscans: 1; charge state(s): 3 to 8; exclusion duration (s): 40. Data dependent mode: cycle time; time between master scans (sec): MS/MS parameter: ddMS2 OT HCD: isolation mode: quadrupole; isolation window (m/z): 1.6; activation type: HCD; HCD collision energy (%): 35; detector type: Orbitrap; Orbitrap; resolution: 30,000; and normalized AGC target (%): 200.200.
The MS raw data were processed using the Proteome Discoverer platform (version 2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_014477) and Sequest HT algorithm. MS/MS data were searched against the human proteome database. Two missed tryptic cleavages were allowed, and the minimum peptide length was set to seven amino acids. Variable modifications included oxidation (M) and biotin (K). For identification of phosphorylation and glycosylation sites of ROME, variable modifications included phosphorylation (STY) and conversion of asparagine (N) to aspartic acid (D). Fixed modification included carbamidomethylation (C). MS and MS/MS ion tolerances were set at 10 parts per million and 0.02 Da, respectively. The FDR was estimated using the fixed value PSM validation. Mass spectrometry studies were performed by Georgetown University Medical Center’s Proteomics Shared Resource.

## Mouse xenograft studies
All mouse studies were conducted under a protocol approved by Georgetown University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: 2022-0027) in accordance with NIH guidelines for the ethical treatment of animals. For the orthotopic Ewing sarcoma model experiments, 1 million TC-71 cells [WT and ROME KO or stably expressing ROME cDNA versus an empty vector (EV) control cell line] were injected into the tibial crest of 6-week-old female SCID beige mice (Charles River Laboratories, RRID: IMSR_CRL:250). Mice were monitored for tumor formation, and tumor volume was determined by the formula [(D × d × d)/6] × 3.14, in which D is the longer diameter and d is the shorter diameter. Once tumors reached a volume of 100 to 200 mm3, the tumor-bearing leg was amputated. Mice were then monitored by physical exam for tumor recurrence and pulmonary metastasis. Animals were euthanized if tumor volume reached 1.5 cm3 at the local recurrence site or respiratory distress was evident because of the presence of lung metastases. The study was ended at 8 weeks after amputation, and upon euthanasia, lungs were collected from each animal and analyzed by histology [hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)] and qPCR.
For the tail vein experiments, 1 million TC-71 cells (WT and ROME KO or stably expressing ROME cDNA versus an EV control cell line) were injected into the tail vein of 6-week-old female SCID beige mice (Charles River Laboratories, RRID: IMSR_CRL:250). The mice were monitored by physical examination for evidence of metastasis development. Mice injected with TC-71 cells expressing ROME or EV control were euthanized at days 25 to 26 after injection and all gross metastases were collected for analysis. Mice injected with TC-71 WT cells or cells with ROME KO were euthanized at days 28 to 29 after injection and all gross metastases were collected for analysis. Gross metastases collected from each animal were weighed, visually documented, and were analyzed by histology (H&E). H&E slides were prepared by Georgetown University Medical Center’s Histopathology and Tissue Shared Resource.
A board-certified pathologist (M. Ozdemirli) blinded to the experimental conditions confirmed the presence of tumor cells in all histologic samples.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
Zebrafish embryos injected with rome MO (20 ng) or control nontargeting MO (10 ng) were collected at 48 hpf and dissociated to single-cell suspensions following the procedure described in Sur and colleagues (n = 12 embryos per group; ref. ). Cell number was quantified by the Luna FL cell counter and viability was more than 95% for all samples. Four libraries (two per condition, targeting 10,000 cells per library) were prepared with the 10X Genomics Chromium NextGEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 (Georgetown University Medical Center Genomics and Epigenomics Shared Resource). Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus (PE150) instrument, targeting 200 million paired-end reads per sample (Novogene). Data were processed on the 10X Genomics Cloud using CellRanger Count v7.2.0. Filtered feature matrix outputs were then read into Seurat v5 (RRID: SCR_016341; ref. ). Data were filtered for 200 to 5,000 features per cell, <20,000 counts per cell, and <10% of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes to eliminate potential doublets and dying cells. The samples were merged and NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures (nfeatures = 2,000), and ScaleData were run. RunPCA was done with the Variable Features and the number of principal components to keep was determined by Elbow plot and JackStraw analysis. The layers were then integrated (CCA Integration) and joined before running the FindNeighbors and FindClusters analyses using 30 dimensions. A total of 49 clusters were obtained. Clusters were annotated by finding the conserved markers for each cell population and comparing the top differentially expressed markers with previously published zebrafish development atlases ( , ). Once each cluster was annotated, FindMarkers was used to determine the differentially expressed genes between the control and rome MO samples for each cluster (Wilcoxon test). Pseudobulk differential expression analysis was performed using FindMarkers across control and rome MO without regards to cluster identity (Wilcoxon test). Cutoffs used for significant genes were P adjusted < 0.05 and log fold change of 2 × SD + mean log fold change. Module scores were then calculated for each cluster with the pseudobulk upregulated and downregulated genes as inputs. Statistical significance for module scores was calculated by nonparametric ANOVA (Kruskal–Wallis test), followed by the post hoc pairwise Dunn test. FishEnrichr was used to conduct pathway enrichment analysis ( ). Adaptively thresholded low-rank approximation (ALRA) was performed to impute rome expression for visualizations ( ). Dirichlet multinomial regression was used to calculate statistical significance for the cell type proportion comparisons ( ).

## Recombinant protein purification
Bacterial codon-optimized extracellular domain (ECD; amino acids 57–153) and full-length human ROME cDNA were cloned into pET-21b and pET-28b backbone, respectively, along with in-frame C-terminal 6xHis tag. Recombinant protein expression was induced by isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside in the BL-21 Codon-Plus E. coli strain (VWR, #76193-370). Cell lysates were prepared by dissolving the bacterial pellet in A1 lysis buffer (20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer, 500 mmol/L NaCl, 30 mmol/L imidazole, and EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablet, pH 7.4). Lysozyme was then added and the dissolved pellet was incubated on ice for 15 minutes. The pellet was then sonicated five times for 15 seconds each with 45-second rests in between and then centrifuged for 10 minutes at 13,500 × g at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-µm syringe filter. Purification was done on an AKTA Pure 25 Explorer instrument (Cytiva) with a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva, #17524701) that was preequilibrated with A1 buffer. Following the sample application and A1 buffer washing steps, the recombinant protein was eluted with a linear gradient of B1 buffer (20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer, 500 mmol/L NaCl, and 1 mol/L imidazole, pH 7.4). Fractions that contained ROME-ECD protein were further purified on a size exclusion column (Superdex 200, 10/300 GL SEC). The eluted fractions were resolved on SDS-PAGE gel and proteins were stained with Coomassie blue or analyzed by Western blotting with His tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9991; RRID: AB_2797714) and ROME monoclonal antibodies ECD-5 and ICD-6.

## Development of monoclonal ROME antibody
Monoclonal antibodies were generated at Abmart. We used six peptides [three in the predicted intracellular domain (ICD) and three in the predicted ECD] to immunize Balb/c mice. Ascites from a total of 29 hybridoma clones were tested by Western blotting with ROME-HA–overexpressing TC-71 lysates and by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) using different ROME domains. Representative Western blot results from two antibodies are shown in . Some of the antibodies (representative example ICD-6) performed similarly to a commercially available HA antibody in Western blotting in detecting ROME-HA and detected three distinct bands. Some of the other antibodies (representative example ECD-5) detected only one band. The endogenous ROME protein expression level was too low to show a strong band in Western blots using total cell lysates. In order to validate the ICD-6 antibody specificity, we knocked out ROME expression in TC-71 cells by CRISPR/Cas9 and the efficiency of CRISPR deletion was confirmed by both qPCR and genomic sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B). We then performed an immunoprecipitation (using ICD-6 antibody), followed by Western blotting using the ICD-6 ROME antibody, which was able to detect the endogenous ROME expression that was absent in the CRISPR clone ( ).
 Anti-ROME antibody specificity and the affinity were further validated by SPR using a custom-synthesized 35 amino acid long ICD of ROME (ROME-ICD) as a single peptide and bacterially expressed and purified recombinant proteins of 97 amino acid long ECD of ROME (ROME-ECD) and 153 amino acid long full-length ROME (ROME-FL; ). ICD-6 (an antibody coming from a peptide against a portion of the ICD) bound the ROME-ICD and ROME-FL with a KD of 0.8 and 0.4 nmol/L, respectively ( ). ECD-5 bound the ROME-ECD and ROME-FL with a KD of 1.8 and 3 nmol/L, respectively.

## SPR
All SPR experiments, except antibody development experiments, were conducted using a Biacore T200 instrument at 25°C. In experiments involving binding of ROME monoclonal antibodies to ICD, ECD, and ROME-FL, both ROME domains (ICD and ECD), as well as ROME-FL, were used as ligands to immobilize onto CM5 sensor chips, using standard amine coupling chemistry. During ligand immobilizations, 10 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer at pH 4 was used as the immobilization buffer to dilute ligands. HBS-P (10 mmol/L HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, and 0.05% v/v surfactant P20) was used as the immobilization running buffer. ICD was immobilized onto one of the three active flow cells (FC2) to a level of 20 RU. ECD was immobilized onto FC3 to a level of 60 RU. ROME-FL was immobilized onto FC4 to a level of 90 RU. FC1 was used as the reference flow cell. FC1 followed the same surface chemistry as in FCs2 to 4 but no proteins were immobilized onto this reference flow cell. Various concentrations (200 nmol/L down to 6.25 nmol/L, two-fold dilution) of ICD-6 monoclonal antibody and ECD-5 monoclonal antibody in HBS-P were then injected as analytes to flow over the ligand-immobilized surfaces. The same concentration of each analyte was injected at least twice to monitor technical reproducibility. Two 20-second pulses of 1:500 H3PO4 (H3PO4:ddH2O, v/v) were injected for surface regeneration. Sensorgrams obtained for analysis were both reference (response corresponding to FC1) and blank (buffer only response) subtracted. SPR sensorgrams from analyte–ligand bindings were fitted to the 1:1 kinetics binding model using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software version 3.2.1.
During the development of monoclonal ROME antibodies, 24 different antibodies were captured as ligands onto a protein A/G–coated (amine coupled; 10 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.5 was used as the immobilization buffer to dilute protein A/G) CM5 chip and different ROME domains were injected as analytes. The Biacore 8K+ instrument was used during these developmental studies. Two 20-second pulses of a custom regeneration solution [1:1 (v/v) mixture of glycine pH 2 and 1:250 (H3PO4:ddH2O, v/v) H3PO4 solution] were injected for surface regeneration. This regeneration took away all captured ligands. Therefore, fresh antibodies were captured at the beginning of each cycle. Biacore Insight Evaluation Software version 5.0 was used to fit the reference- and blank-subtracted sensorgrams to the 1:1 kinetics binding model. HBS-P was used as the capture buffer to dilute antibodies, as running buffer during protein A/G immobilization and antibody capture, and during analyte–ligand bindings.
For experiments involving binding of ROME-FL to vimentin (Novus Biologicals, #NBP2-35139-100 μg) and β-catenin (Novus Biologicals, #NBP3-18198-100 μg), vimentin and β-catenin were immobilized as ligands onto CM5 chip surfaces. As the immobilization buffer, 10 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer at pH 4 was selected to dilute β-catenin. For vimentin, 4 mmol/L HCl was used as the immobilization buffer. The ligand immobilization level was ∼10,800 RU for β-catenin and ∼3,000 RU for vimentin. HBS-P was used as the running buffer during ligand immobilizations. One flow cell was used as the reference flow cell as mentioned above. Various concentrations (2.5 to 0.01 μmol/L, three-fold dilutions) of ROME-FL protein in HBS-P were injected (in two technical replicates) as the analyte to flow over the ligand-immobilized surfaces. Two 20-second pulses of 2 mol/L NaCl were injected for surface regeneration. The reference- and blank-subtracted sensorgrams for analyte–ligand bindings were fitted to the 1:1 kinetics model fitting using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software version 3.2.1. SPR experiments were conducted by Georgetown University Medical Center’s Biacore Molecular Interaction Shared Resource.

## Measurement of intracellular calcium levels
Intracellular Ca2+ levels were measured using the Fluo-4 Direct Calcium Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #F10471). For this, cells were seeded at 10,000 cells/well in 96-well, black-wall, clear-bottom plates. On the next day, cells were serum starved with 1% FBS overnight. After serum starvation, cells were incubated with the Fluo-4 AM dye for 30 minutes at 37°C, followed by an additional 30 minutes at room temperature. Cells were then stimulated with either 10% FBS or 200 μmol/L ATP and fluorescence readings (excitation at 494 nm and emission at 516 nm) were collected every 20 milliseconds for 180 seconds on a BioTek Synergy H4 Hybrid plate reader.

## Luciferase reporter assay
Cells were plated in six-well plates and co-transfected with the TOPFlash luciferase reporter plus Renilla luciferase control vector (pRL-SV40) in a 10:1 ratio. On the next day, cells were stimulated with recombinant Wnt3a (R&D Systems, #645-WN-010/CF) for 4.5 hours, and then luciferase assay was conducted with the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System Kit (Promega, #E1960) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## ROME expression across different cancer types
The expression of ROME was measured across the transcriptional classes corresponding to different cancer types, described by Comitani and colleagues ( ), with the Ewing sarcoma sample cohort expanded to include 143 samples. Expected counts of all samples were generated using the STAR + RSEM Toil RNA-seq pipeline and covered more than 58,000 genes ( ). Log2 (counts per million + 1) normalization and the gene expression analysis were performed using edgeR version 4.2.1 (RRID: SCR_012802) in R 4.4.1 (RRID: SCR_001905; ref. ).

## Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism software (RRID: SCR_002798) with the appropriate statistical test for the data type as specified for each experiment in the figure legends. In summary, χ2 analysis was used for zebrafish metastasis experiments (for which the outcome was scored as yes or no metastasis and the presence of more than one tumor cell in the fish tail was defined as metastasis), MO phenotype rescue experiments, and analyzing differences in gross metastases to the lungs in the mouse Ewing sarcoma amputation model experiments. Data from other experiments, including scratch wound assays, Boyden chamber assays, tail vein mouse experiments, and calcium signaling assays, were analyzed by the Student t test. Statistical significance was defined as P value < 0.05. The schematics shown in , , and were created with Biorender.com.
 

## Results

## A genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 transcriptional activation screen identifies ROME as a metastatic driver of Ewing sarcoma
We performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 transcriptional activation screen in zebrafish, an established model for studying the invasive potential of human cancer cells in vivo ( ), to identify novel prometastatic genes in Ewing sarcoma ( ). In this assay, the metastasis-like phenotype was defined as the active intravasation of cells from the injection site (yolk) to the tail of the fish (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We injected four human Ewing sarcoma cell lines, A4573, TC-32, TC-71, and STA-ET-7.2, at different tumor volumes to establish the optimal cell line, cell number, and time point to use for the CRISPR/Cas9 screen ( ). We chose to continue with the TC-71 cell line on day 2 after injection for screening on the basis of the low background invasion rate at this timepoint and the potential for increased invasion with a longer time or greater cell number. To further validate the model system, we stably expressed V5-TWIST1 (a known metastasis regulator) in TC-71 cells and injected the cells into the yolk of zebrafish. On day 2 after injection, we observed that 43% (12 of 28) of the fish injected with TWIST1-overexpressing cells had cells in the tail compared with 21% (11 of 52) of fish injected with EV cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B). This confirmed the validity of the model in measuring the metastasis-like phenotype.
We then established a TC-71 cell line with stable expression of dCas9-VP64, a catalytically dead endonuclease conjugated to a transcriptional activator that enhances gene expression when directed to the target gene promoter by a gRNA (Supplementary Fig. S1C). We transduced dCas9-expressing TC-71 cells with a lentiviral gRNA library (Calabrese human CRISPR activation pooled library) that targets 18,885 genes, with three unique gRNAs per gene and an additional 500 nontargeting control gRNAs ( ). After lentiviral transduction and selection, the cells were injected into the yolk of 1,898 zebrafish. On day 2 after injection, 12.6% of the fish in the library group had human cancer cells in the tail, whereas 4.3% of the negative control TC-71–injected fish had human cancer cells in the tail (P < 0.05; χ2 test).
We collected the tails of 239 fish with metastases in the library group for DNA extraction, amplified the gRNA sequences from the human cancer cells, and performed next-generation sequencing. We detected 848 different gRNAs corresponding to 812 different genes ( ; Supplementary Table S3). The presence of known metastasis driver genes, including CDK4/6, CREB1, CTNNB1, FGF3, LOXHD1, MYC, SPOCK1, and TWIST2, among the 812 hits confirmed the validity of the CRISPR/Cas9 transcriptional activation screen in this model. We chose eight genes from the screen for further validation: CD68, CXCL8, ELMO2, ROME, KCTD20, KDR, RAB9A, and REL. The following criteria directed our selection: a potential role in cancer metastasis based on current literature, the abundance of read counts per gRNA sequence, the number of unique gRNAs detected that correspond to the gene, the negative correlation of gene expression with survival curves of patients with Ewing sarcoma (patient tumor microarray data), and upregulation in Ewing sarcoma cells with low EWS::FLI1 protein expression as this has been correlated with increased metastatic capacity. We expressed each gene in TC-71 cells and evaluated its effect on migration via scratch assays and invasion via zebrafish xenograft assays ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S1D). The overexpression of ROME in TC-71 cells led to the strongest and most consistent phenotype among multiple independent scratch wound assays and zebrafish xenograft experiments. Additionally, ROME was the top hit in the CRISPR screen in terms of abundance in read counts and was represented by two unique gRNAs in the sequencing results of the screen ( ). Thus, we chose to continue an in-depth investigation of ROME.

## The human ROME gene produces a single-exon transcript encoding a 153–amino acid transmembrane protein with posttranslational modifications
To characterize ROME expression and function, we first developed two monoclonal mouse antibodies against the ICD and ECD of the human ROME protein as detailed in the “Materials and Methods” section and confirmed their specificity and binding affinities ( ). We used the CRISPR/Cas9 system to generate TC-71 and CHLA-10 clonal cell lines with ROME KO for our experiments (Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B).
The human ROME gene was predicted to encode a 153–amino acid transmembrane protein ( and ; ref. ). Topological analysis suggests that the first 35 amino acids at the amino terminus are intracellular, followed by a 21–amino acid transmembrane domain and an ECD comprising the remaining 97 amino acids ( ). The three-dimensional structure of ROME predicted by AlphaFold suggested that the structure outside the transmembrane domain was mainly intrinsically disordered ( ; ref. ). The ROME transmembrane domain has significant homology to the Drosophila inaF motif ( ) and based on homology analysis of the entire human ROME protein sequence, the ROME protein is highly conserved throughout vertebrates ( ).
To determine whether ROME localizes to the cell membrane, we expressed an eGFP-tagged ROME construct in TC-71 cells. We observed ROME-eGFP primarily on the cell membrane ( ). Cell fractionation of TC-71 cells transiently expressing ROME-HA or an EV control demonstrated that ROME was present predominantly in the membrane fraction ( ). Unexpectedly, we detected ROME protein in the soluble nuclear fraction too. We then fractionated TC-71 WT cells along with TC-71 cells with ROME KO and detected the endogenous ROME protein via Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. S2C). We detected endogenous ROME protein in both the membrane and soluble nuclear fractions but not the cytoplasmic fraction, and no protein was detected in any fraction from ROME-KO cells. We therefore concluded that the human ROME protein preferentially localizes to the cell membrane and it may also be present in the nucleus.

In silico analyses (NetNGlyc, NetOGlyc, and NetPhos) of the ROME amino acid sequence predicted 10 phosphorylation sites and 12 glycosylation sites ( ; refs. – ). To determine whether ROME was glycosylated in mammalian cells, we treated lysates from TC-71 cells stably expressing FLAG-tagged ROME protein (ROME-FLAG) or an EV with a mixture of deglycosylase enzymes. Western blot analysis with anti-FLAG showed a substantially lower molecular weight of the ROME-FLAG protein in the deglycosylase-treated TC-71 lysate compared with that in the untreated control lysate ( ). To further study the posttranslational modifications (PTM) of ROME, we expressed a biotin-tagged ROME in TC-71 cells, pulled down the protein with streptavidin beads, and analyzed it by MS. We confirmed that ROME was N-glycosylated at N81 and phosphorylated at S127, both as predicted in silico, and was also phosphorylated at S152, which was not predicted by the in silico analyses (Supplementary Figs. S2D, S2E, and S3A). We observed that purified recombinant ROME-FL and ROME-ECD (expressed in bacteria) also had higher molecular weights than expected ( ). Earlier studies have established that proteins can be glycosylated in bacteria ( , ). To determine whether the recombinant ROME was also glycosylated in bacteria, we performed MS analysis of the ECD and detected N-glycosylation at the previously confirmed N81 residue and also at N97, which was not previously detected (Supplementary Fig. S3B and S3C). It is most likely that the glycosylation patterns were different in human cells and bacteria, which resulted in observed differences in apparent molecular weights of bands corresponding to ROME in gel electrophoresis. In summary, we discovered that the ROME protein localizes primarily to the cell membrane but can also be detected in the nucleus, and it undergoes PTMs, including phosphorylation and N-glycosylation at multiple residues.

## ROME is expressed in mouse and zebrafish embryos and is critical for normal development
As ROME is conserved throughout vertebrates ( ), we first evaluated its expression in zebrafish embryos via ISH. We observed the highest rome expression in Rohan beard neurons, lateral line ganglia, and olfactory placodes in zebrafish embryos at 24 to 36 hpf ( ). We also looked at rome expression in a single-cell RNA sequencing database for zebrafish embryo development (Daniocell) and found that it is expressed in olfactory and neural clusters, which coincides with our ISH findings ( ). Given its expression in multiple embryonic tissues, we next investigated whether rome is essential for normal zebrafish development. We used a translation-blocking rome morpholino (MO) to silence the expression of rome in zebrafish embryos. Rome knockdown resulted in substantial developmental defects, including cardiac edema, bent tail, severe head and tail necrosis, development of cell masses with no clear organ localization, absence of the head or tail, and axial trunk extension defects ( ). We observed MO dose-dependent increases in mortality and in the severity of developmental defects ( ). By 48 hpf, 80% of the embryos injected with the lowest dose of rome MO had mild-to-severe developmental defects or had already died (Supplementary Fig. S4A). To confirm the on-target effect of the rome MO, we performed a rescue experiment in which we injected zebrafish embryos with rome MO alone or coinjected rome MO and rome mRNA ( ). Rome mRNA coinjection significantly rescued the survival of embryos injected with rome MO, supporting the specificity of the MO targeting. Additionally, we used the CRISPR/Cas9 system to delete rome in zebrafish embryos. The RNP complex [Cas9 + rome cr:tracrRNA or Cas9 + tracrRNA (Ctl)] was injected into zebrafish embryos at the one-cell stage. Similar to the translation-blocking morpholino, rome deletion led to significant developmental defects ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4B and S4C). We thus concluded that rome expression is important for normal zebrafish embryonic development.
 To study ROME in the context of mammalian development, we analyzed Rome expression in mouse embryos from E10.5 to E18.5 via RNAscope ( ). In the early part of this period (E10.5–E13.5), Rome mRNA was ubiquitously expressed in all tissues (Supplementary Fig. S5A). In later stages, the main sites of expression were the hematopoietic cells in the liver and the intestinal submucosa ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5A). To confirm the hematopoietic identity of the Rome-expressing cells in the liver, we performed IHC with an anti-CD61 antibody (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The cells that stained positive for CD61 had a very similar expression pattern and morphology to the Rome-expressing cells, confirming a megakaryocyte/myeloid identity. Additionally, we evaluated ROME protein expression in adult mouse organs via immunoprecipitation and Western blotting. We detected ROME expression in all organs tested, namely, the brain, heart, kidney, liver, lung, and skeletal muscle ( ). However, the expression patterns showed significant variation between different mouse tissues, which suggested that the level and type of PTM of the protein may be tissue specific and may result in altered functional states. In conclusion, we observed the expression of ROME in zebrafish embryos, mouse embryos, and adult mice, and the high rate of defects following rome translation-blocking MO injection or gene deletion suggests that the ROME protein may have a key function in normal development.

## Developmental pathways regulated by rome in zebrafish embryos
To understand the role of rome in zebrafish development at the molecular level, we conducted single-cell dissociation and RNA sequencing of control MO– and rome MO–injected zebrafish embryos at 48 hpf. After quality control filtering, we analyzed 20,392 control MO–injected cells and 26,893 rome MO–injected cells. We identified 49 different cell clusters, which were annotated based on two previously published zebrafish development atlases ( ; Supplementary Table S4; refs. , ). We did not focus on expression changes of genes in the p53 apoptotic pathway because this is a known off-target effect of morpholinos ( ). In the raw data, we detected rome expression in the majority of the clusters although in only a few cells per cluster. This was anticipated as rome is a low-abundance mRNA (low read counts were detected in databases of RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing studies), and single-cell RNA sequencing offers limited resolution for targets with low expression levels. To better understand rome expression in different embryonic tissues, we used ALRA to impute rome expression in our dataset ( ). This revealed that the highest expression of rome was in many of the neuronal clusters such as the Gill neuroepithelium/neurons, cranial ganglia, spinal cord interneurons, retinal differentiating neurons_2, and neuronal_1/2 clusters, as well as ionocytes (Supplementary Fig. S6A).
 Next, to understand the general landscape of genes affected by rome knockdown, we performed a pseudo-bulk differential expression analysis on the dataset. We found many upregulated genes, including calcium signaling–related genes such as s100t and icn2 and Wnt pathway–related genes such as rspo1, and downregulated genes such as gcgb, vsx1, and akap6 ( ). To find the clusters that are driving these differential expression changes, we calculated a module score for each cluster based on the set of upregulated genes and set of downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6C). For the upregulated gene set, the lens cluster had the highest module score whereas for the downregulated gene set, the fast muscle, photoreceptors, photoreceptor precursors, and nucleate erythrocyte clusters had the highest module scores. We then used FishEnrichr to perform pathway analysis on the gene sets ( ). The upregulated genes were enriched for terms such as ion and amino acid transmembrane transport (adjusted P value = 1.55e−18 and 2.02e−19, respectively), eye development (adjusted P value = 1.32e−11), and stress-related pathways such as cellular response to reactive oxygen species (adjusted P value = 7.56e−14) and response to hydrogen peroxide (adjusted P value = 1.55e−18; ). On the other hand, the downregulated genes were enriched for terms such as retinal metabolic process (adjusted P value = 0.0001), synapse assembly (adjusted P value = 0.0002), neuron cell–cell adhesion (adjusted P value = 0.0002), photoreceptor cell development (adjusted P value = 0.0002), and actin filament network formation (adjusted P value = 0.008; ).
We further looked to see whether loss of rome expression led to changes in the proportions of cell populations (Supplementary Fig. S6D and S6E). Using Dirichlet multinomial regression analysis, we found that upon rome MO injection, there was a significant increase in the proportion of cells for the epidermis, iridophores, periderm, and retinal-pigmented epithelium clusters, whereas the proportion of cells in the retinal interneurons cluster was significantly decreased ( ).
Because rome contains the inaF motif, which is known to play an important role in calcium signaling in Drosophila, we then focused on changes in the expression of calcium signaling–related genes. We examined the neuronal lineage clusters, in which rome was most highly expressed, and detected significantly greater expression of the icn, s100t, and rspo1 genes in rome MO–injected embryos than in control MO–injected embryos ( ). Icn and s100t are both involved in Ca2+ binding and signaling, whereas rspo1 is involved in the canonical Wnt signaling pathway in zebrafish ( – ). Thus, rome knockdown in zebrafish embryos may have led to significantly increased, unregulated Ca2+-induced signaling and canonical Wnt activity and, consequently, developmental defects. We also observed significantly greater expression of ctslb and sesn3 in rome MO–injected embryos ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6F). Ctslb is a lysosomal protease and sesn3 is a stress-induced protein that may be responsible for the necrosis and lethality ultimately observed with rome knockdown ( , ).
As the single-cell RNA sequencing experiments suggested that a lack of rome may cause increased Wnt activity, which in turn may explain the observed defects in zebrafish development, we tested the outcome of Wnt inhibition in low-rome conditions. We tested to see whether treatment with a Wnt pathway inhibitor (ICG-001) could improve the defects observed with rome MO knockdown. We found that ICG-001 treatment in rome MO–injected embryos significantly rescued the developmental defects at 24 hpf and 48 hpf compared with a DMSO control, which suggested that increased Wnt activity following loss of rome was partially responsible for the observed phenotypes ( ).
Interestingly, we also detected significantly decreased cav1 expression in the notochord of rome MO–injected zebrafish embryos (Supplementary Fig. S6G). Caveolae are required for zebrafish notochord integrity, and disruption of caveolae via cav1 or cavin1b knockdown in zebrafish leads to impaired neuromast formation and significant lesions to the notochord, affecting movement ( , ). We conclude that rome knockdown may have led to the downregulation of cav1 in the notochord, contributing to the morbidity and mortality that we observed in the zebrafish embryos. Overall, we observed significant changes in the expression of genes involved in calcium signaling, the Wnt pathway, and stress-related pathways when rome was knocked down in zebrafish embryos, further supporting an important role for rome in development and cellular homeostasis.

## ROME participates in Wnt and calcium signaling in human cancer cells
We next investigated the effect of modulating ROME expression levels on the global gene expression profile of human cancer cells. We stably overexpressed ROME-FLAG in four human Ewing sarcoma cell lines (A4573, STA-ET-7.2, TC-32, and TC-71) and one human stomach adenocarcinoma cell line (Hs746.T; Supplementary Fig. S7A). We knocked out ROME via CRISPR/Cas9 in CHLA-10 and TC-71 (human Ewing sarcoma cell lines) and knocked down ROME expression via siRNA in Hs746.T, 769-P [a human clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) cell line], and TC-71 cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7B). We performed RNA sequencing to determine the genes and pathways that are upregulated when ROME is overexpressed (Supplementary Fig. S7C–S7F) in A4573 cells (transiently for 24 hours) and in TC-71 cells (stably). GSEA revealed numerous significantly positively enriched pathways, including branching microtubule nucleation, microtubule RHOA signaling, TLR2 to 4 MAPK signaling, Wnt signaling, tight junction actin signaling, and Wnt5a ROR signaling. We also performed RNA sequencing of TC-71, CHLA-10, and Hs746.T cells with reduced ROME expression. Specifically, we sought to identify genes and pathways that are significantly downregulated in ROME-knockdown cells compared with cells transfected with control nontargeting siRNA control or WT cells, via RNA sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S7G–S7N). We observed significant downregulation of motility-related pathways, including branching microtubule nucleation, retrograde axonal transport, and tight junction actin signaling. We also observed a downregulation of GF–RTK–RAS–PI3K signaling, calcium signaling, Wnt5a ROR signaling, and Wnt signaling, further implying a role for ROME in those pathways.
On the basis of these RNA sequencing analyses, we carried out a closer investigation of the effect of ROME expression on canonical Wnt pathway activity in Ewing sarcoma cells (TC-32 and TC-71) and human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T). In all three of these cell lines, cells overexpressing ROME had lower canonical Wnt pathway activity upon Wnt3a stimulation as measured by the TOPFlash β-catenin–responsive luciferase reporter ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S8A). As a control, we tested FOPFLASH reporter activity in response to Wnt3a stimulation and observed no difference (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Additionally, there was no change in cell proliferation rate upon Wnt3a treatment compared with the PBS-treated control (Supplementary Fig. S8C). We also observed that Wnt3a-treated TC-71 cells with siRNA knockdown of ROME exhibited significantly increased Wnt pathway activity compared with control cells transfected with nontargeting siRNA ( ). To better understand which part of the canonical Wnt pathway might be affected by ROME, we investigated the effect of ROME expression on colorectal cancer cell lines with endogenous Wnt pathway–activating mutations in β-catenin. In both HCT116 and LS180 cells, canonical Wnt pathway activity was reduced in cells with ROME overexpression compared with control cells transfected with EV ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8D). To confirm that ROME does not simply inhibit luciferase enzyme activity nonspecifically, we tested the effects of ROME overexpression and knockdown on the EWS::FLI1-responsive NROB1 luciferase reporter. ROME expression did not inhibit and ROME knockdown did not activate the NROB1 luciferase activity (Supplementary Fig. S8E and S8F). We also used lithium chloride (LiCl) as an inhibitor of GSK3β activity and found that ROME expression still reduced TOPFlash activity in TC-71 cells compared with EV control (Supplementary Fig. S8G). ROME knockdown also significantly potentiated the effect of LiCl on Wnt pathway activity as assessed via TOPFlash (Supplementary Fig. S8H). These findings lead us to postulate that ROME likely does not directly engage with canonical Wnt pathway proteins at the membrane but rather works further downstream to antagonize β-catenin activity.
 To further investigate this, we tested whether ROME and β-catenin exist in the same protein complex by performing a co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP). ROME was transiently overexpressed in HCT116 cells and co-IP was performed by pulling down ROME with an anti-ROME antibody and Western blotting for the endogenous β-catenin or pulling down endogenous β-catenin with an anti–β-catenin antibody and doing a Western blot for ROME. In both cases, ROME and β-catenin came down together, indicating that they are in the same protein complex ( and ). We next confirmed a direct interaction between ROME and β-catenin by SPR. β-catenin recombinant protein was immobilized on the chip surface and ROME-FL recombinant protein was flowed over the surface as the analyte. ROME directly bound β-catenin with a KD of 330 ± 95 nmol/L ( ). We then tested whether ROME expression in HCT116 cells affects the interaction between β-catenin and TCF4 in co-IP experiments. We observed that the interaction between β-catenin and TCF4 is substantially decreased in ROME-expressing cells compared with an EV control ( ). We conclude that ROME is a negative regulator of canonical Wnt signaling and this effect is carried out by directly binding to β-catenin.
Furthermore, considering the homology of the human ROME protein with the Drosophila inaF motif, we investigated the role of the human ROME protein in Ca2+ signaling. We measured the cytosolic Ca2+ elevation in response to ATP and FBS stimulation in A4573, STA-ET-7.2, and TC-71 cells by using a Fluo-4 Direct Calcium Assay Kit. ROME expression reduced the magnitude of the Ca2+ response to external stimulation compared with that of control cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8I). We also observed that, compared with WT cells, TC-71 cells with ROME KO had a greater Ca2+ response to ATP stimulation ( ). These findings led us to conclude that ROME negatively regulates Ca2+ signaling.
As the INAF and TRP proteins interact in Drosophila and mice, we investigated the interaction between ROME and TRPC1 (the human homolog of trp) and confirmed its occurrence in multiple Ewing sarcoma cells by co-IP (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Unlike in Drosophila, we did not observe a dependence of TRPC1 protein levels on ROME expression (Supplementary Fig. S9B and S9C).

## ROME regulates cancer cell migration and chemotaxis in vitro
We next investigated the role of ROME in cancer cell migration via scratch assay and chemotaxis via Boyden chamber assays. Compared with EV control cells, TC-71 cells overexpressing ROME exhibited significantly increased wound closure rates in scratch assays, whereas TC-71 cells with ROME KO exhibited significantly decreased wound closure rates in the same assay ( ). Consistent with these observations, ROME overexpression significantly increased the chemotaxis of A4573, TC-71, and Hs746.T cells in a Boyden chamber assay ( ). We also observed significantly reduced chemotaxis when ROME expression was decreased in CHLA-10, TC-71, 769-P, and Hs746.T cells ( ). Furthermore, rescue of ROME expression in CHLA-10 and TC-71 ROME-KO cells by introducing an exogenous ROME cDNA construct (Supplementary Fig. S9D–S9F) restored cell chemotaxis in both cell lines ( and ).
 To rule out the possibility that the observed changes in cell migration and chemotaxis may be attributable to dynamic changes in cell numbers during the experiment, we evaluated the effect of altering ROME expression on cell proliferation. TC-71, A4573, and Hs746.T cells overexpressing ROME in cell culture were monitored in real time by electrical impedance. We did not observe a meaningful difference in cell proliferation between cells overexpressing ROME and EV-transfected controls (Supplementary Fig. S9G–S9I). ROME KO and rescue of ROME expression in TC-71 and CHLA-10 cells did not affect the proliferation rate either (Supplementary Fig. S9J and S9K). Additionally, in the Broad Institute’s DepMap database, ROME KO was not associated with growth dependence in 24 different cancer cell lines (cell lines for which ROME dependency data were available from the current DepMap Public 24Q2+Score, Chronos release; ref. ). Therefore, we concluded that ROME expression levels positively correlate with migration and chemotaxis, but these effects are not due to changes in proliferation rates.
Because EWS::FLI1 expression is inversely associated with invasion and metastasis in Ewing sarcoma cells ( ), we also evaluated EWS::FLI1 protein levels in Ewing sarcoma cells after modulation of ROME expression. We did not observe a difference in EWS::FLI1 protein expression in Ewing sarcoma cells overexpressing ROME or with knockout or rescue of ROME, indicating that ROME does not affect EWS::FLI1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S9B and S9C).

## ROME drives intravasation through vimentin
Both Wnt and Ca2+ signaling pathways are generally associated with increased cell motility and invasion. Therefore, inhibition of Wnt or Ca2+ pathways by ROME is unlikely the molecular mechanism for ROME-mediated invasion and motility. Wnt pathway regulation by rome was a contributing factor for developmental defects in zebrafish ( ). As expected, we did not see a rescue in cell migration when TC-71 cells with ROME knockdown by siRNA were treated with the Wnt pathway inhibitor ICG-001 (Supplementary Fig. S9L). Instead, there was an even further reduction in cell migration compared with the DMSO control. This indicates that ROME’s role in the Wnt pathway may be relevant to normal development of zebrafish embryos but not for its role in migration and invasion of human cancer cells. Therefore, we investigated additional pathways that may be regulated by ROME.
To investigate the outcome of altering ROME expression in vivo, we first utilized a zebrafish xenograft model ( ). We tested three Ewing sarcoma cell lines (STA-ET-7.2, TC-32, and TC-71) and observed increased intravasation out of the yolk and into the tail of zebrafish embryos when ROME was overexpressed ( ). TC-32 cells displayed the same trend as STA-ET-7.2 and TC-71 cells but had a P value of only 0.13. When TC-71 cells with ROME KO were tested in the same model, we observed significantly less intravasation ( ). In the MS data from ROME pulldown experiments for PTM identification, we observed that vimentin may be immunoprecipitating with ROME. We then confirmed that vimentin and ROME interact by co-IP in three different Ewing sarcoma cell lines: TC-71, A4573, and TC-32 ( ). We tested whether ROME and vimentin directly interact by SPR and found that ROME binds vimentin with a KD of 300 nmol/L ( ). We then investigated the effect of ROME expression on total vimentin and phosphorylated vimentin levels. We did not observe a difference in total vimentin protein level with ROME expression. However, we noted a decrease in vimentin phosphorylation at S56 when ROME expression was induced by doxycycline treatment in a tet-on system, which was consistent across four independent experiments ( ). S56 phosphorylation is a negative regulator of vimentin function and correlates with reduced metastasis phenotype ( , ). Therefore, ROME-mediated reduction in S56 phosphorylation is likely driving the increased invasion of Ewing sarcoma cell lines tested here. We next tested whether vimentin knockdown by siRNA would block ROME-mediated intravasation. We first validated an effective vimentin knockdown in STA-ET-7.2 cells by Western blotting ( ). We then injected STA-ET-7.2 cells with EV or ROME overexpression and nontargeting siRNA or vimentin siRNA transfection (four groups total) into the yolk of zebrafish embryos and observed that ROME-expressing STA-ET-7.2 cells with vimentin knockdown no longer had a significant increase in intravasation compared with the EV ( ). This indicates that ROME requires vimentin for intravasation. To further support this, in analyzing patient tumor RNA sequencing data, we found a significant positive correlation between ROME and vimentin mRNA expression ( ).

## ROME expression drives metastasis in vivo
The zebrafish model provided invaluable data toward the ROME-induced increase in intravasation. To test the role of ROME in metastasis in in vivo mammalian models, we injected ROME-overexpressing TC-71 cells into the tail vein of SCID beige mice and monitored for the development of metastases ( ). Compared with mice that received TC-71 cells transfected with EV, mice injected with TC-71 cells overexpressing ROME had a significantly greater total mass of metastases ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10A and S10C). Conversely, mice that were injected with ROME-KO TC-71 cells had decreased total mass of metastases compared with mice that received WT TC-71 cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10B and S10D).
Tail vein injection in mice recapitulates only the later stages of the metastatic process: extravasation and colonization at the new site. In order to evaluate the early stages of metastasis, we performed an orthotopic xenograft study in SCID mice, in which TC-71 cells overexpressing ROME or transfected with EV were injected into the tibial crest. The primary tumors were allowed to reach a volume of 100 to 200 mm3, at which point the tumor-bearing leg was amputated to allow monitoring of the long-term development of metastases in the mice ( ). There was no difference in the growth rate of the primary tumors from implantation to amputation between the groups (Supplementary Fig. S10E). Individual mice were euthanized when recurrence developed at the amputation site or clinical signs of metastasis emerged, and all surviving mice were euthanized at 60 days after amputation as the study endpoint. We collected the lungs from each animal at the time of euthanasia. A significantly greater number of mice developed gross metastases in the lungs among the group injected with TC-71 ROME-overexpressing cells (four of 10) than among the EV control–injected group ( ). We further confirmed the presence of metastatic nodules in the lungs of these four animals via H&E staining.
We also investigated the effect of ROME KO on the development of lung metastases in an orthotopic leg amputation model. Initially, there were no differences in primary tumor growth or time to amputation between the groups (Supplementary Fig. S10F). However, unlike the experiment with ROME-overexpressing cells, we observed a significantly high rate of rapid primary tumor recurrence at the amputation site with TC-71 WT cells, which precluded the measurement of long-term metastasis. When the control groups in the ROME overexpression and knockout experiments were compared, the TC-71 WT group in the ROME-KO study had substantial postsurgery tumor recurrence compared with the TC-71 EV-transfected control group in the ROME overexpression study. Because the animals with local recurrence had to be euthanized according to the study design, the timeline of the experiment was too short to permit lung metastasis development. Nevertheless, there was significantly decreased primary tumor recurrence in the legs of the mice injected with the TC-71 ROME-KO cells than in those injected with TC-71 WT cells, which was reflected by an increase in the event-free survival rates ( ), suggesting that ROME may contribute to the malignant phenotype in this model in the form of rapid local recurrence. In summary, ROME overexpression led to significantly increased occurrence of gross metastases in both the orthotopic Ewing sarcoma amputation model and the tail vein metastasis model. Additionally, knockout of ROME significantly decreased primary tumor recurrence in the orthotopic Ewing sarcoma model and decreased the total metastasis burden in the tail vein model. Therefore, these findings demonstrate that ROME is a key driver of metastasis in multiple in vivo models.

## ROME expression is correlated with poor patient survival in many cancer types
To investigate the role of ROME in human patients with cancer, we first performed a search of publicly available patient tumor RNA sequencing data. We examined ROME mRNA expression in many different tumor types ( ) and found significantly higher expression of ROME in different tumors than in normal tissues, including Wilms tumor, osteosarcoma, neuroblastoma, ccRCC, acute myeloid leukemia, skin cutaneous melanoma, prostate adenocarcinoma, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (Supplementary Fig. S11; ref. ). ROME expression also correlated with significantly worse patient survival in ccRCC, stomach adenocarcinoma, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma ( ; ref. ). Analyses of patient outcomes for Ewing sarcoma were not productive because in these databases, the small Ewing sarcoma cohorts were combined into the overall category of “Sarcomas,” and ROME was not included in the microarrays that were used in early tumor expression studies. Overall, the data available to us thus far indicate that ROME may also play an important role in the clinical context of patients with cancer.

## Discussion
We discovered and characterized novel functions of the previously unstudied vertebrate gene ROME and demonstrated that it is critical for normal development in vertebrates. Furthermore, we show that ROME supports metastatic tumor spread in mouse models of Ewing sarcoma. Previously, the human ROME gene was predicted to code for a protein product, but neither the gene nor its potential protein product had been studied in-depth. Two GWAS studies mentioned this locus, and it has also been included in a tumor microenvironment prognostic signature for ccRCC ( – ). Additionally, a study investigating novel transcripts of unknown function in human mesenchymal stem cells revealed that OGU1 (a ROME alias) was upregulated during osteogenesis ( ). However, the authors of this article considered it to be a noncoding RNA transcript ( ). Thus, our work illuminates and fills a significant gap in knowledge about this highly conserved but unstudied human gene, which could have implications for understanding the pathogenesis of Ewing sarcoma or other cancers.
The transmembrane domain of ROME shares homology with the inaF motif, which has been studied in Drosophila. The predicted mouse ROME protein has been found in the TRP interactome of mice through affinity MS studies but has not been investigated further ( ). The human genome contains two genes encoding single-pass transmembrane domain–containing proteins that have sequence homology (approximately 21 amino acids) to Drosophila INAF proteins, namely, INAFM1 (not studied in this article) and ROME (INAFM2). Interestingly, ROME arose earlier than INAFM1 evolutionarily and is highly conserved throughout vertebrates, implying that it may have an important function ( ). Homologs of the ROME protein are also found in many additional invertebrate species; for example, the Haemonchus contortus (a nematode) protein has more than 30% homology to the human ROME protein.
Publicly available existing genome-wide sequencing data show no known deletions or mutations of the ROME gene associated with any human disease. This may suggest that any functionally significant mutation or deletion of ROME is incompatible with life, which is supported by the high mortality we observed in zebrafish embryos with rome knockdown and knockout ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4). The ultimate answer to this question can be obtained by creating a Rome-KO mouse model, which will most likely be embryonically lethal. Therefore, a conditional inducible knockout approach will be more appropriate for obtaining invaluable information about the function of ROME during embryogenesis and adult homeostasis.
We demonstrated that the human ROME gene produces the predicted ROME protein in cancer cells. We further showed that the ROME protein localizes primarily to the cell membrane and the nucleus and that it is highly posttranslationally modified via glycosylation and phosphorylation ( ; Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). We hypothesize that the ROME protein is a type II transmembrane protein because it contains a span of hydrophobic residues that likely serve as the signal peptide and membrane anchor. We were surprised to find the ROME protein in the nucleus as its amino acid sequence does not contain a nuclear localization signal. Thus, we speculate that ROME may be transported to the nucleus through its interaction with other proteins, including β-catenin.
In addition to our findings in human cells, we demonstrated that ROME is expressed throughout zebrafish embryos, mouse embryos, and adult mouse organs ( ). We observed rome expression in Rohan beard neurons, lateral line ganglia, and the olfactory placodes of zebrafish embryos at 24 to 36 hpf ( ). As the development of these organs is known to be controlled by the Wnt and BMP pathways in zebrafish, we concluded that ROME may be involved in those pathways ( – ). To further understand the function of ROME in vertebrates, we carried out single-cell RNA sequencing of zebrafish embryos injected with rome MO and multiple RNA sequencing experiments with overexpression or knockdown of ROME in different cancer cell lines. In both zebrafish embryos and human cancer cells, the Wnt and calcium signaling pathways were affected by modulation of ROME expression. Notably, the effect of ROME on Wnt and calcium signaling may be important under normal physiologic conditions, and this may be a completely separate function from the pathologic role of ROME in metastasis. Wnt pathway modulation did not rescue the effect of ROME on migration in Boyden chamber assays (Supplementary Fig. S9L). ROME was expressed in all mouse embryos and adult mouse organs tested although there was substantial variation in the overall level and the predominant posttranslationally modified version of the protein ( and ). Taken together, these findings lead us to hypothesize that ROME may have different functions in various embryonic and adult cell types, which we are just beginning to explore.
Functionally, we demonstrated that ROME protein expression affects Ewing sarcoma cell migration and metastasis through a variety of in vitro (scratch wound assays and Boyden chamber assays; ) and in vivo (zebrafish xenografts, orthotopic Ewing sarcoma amputation model, and tail vein metastasis model; and ) assays. We further showed that ROME may function through vimentin to increase intravasation and metastasis ( ). We used Ewing sarcoma cell lines as the main experimental model, but we also demonstrated that ROME may play a role in the metastasis of other cancers. We showed that the overexpression of ROME increased chemotaxis in the stomach adenocarcinoma cell line Hs746.T and that ROME knockdown decreased the chemotaxis of Hs746.T cells, as well as 769-P ccRCC cells, in Boyden chamber assays ( , , and ). We discovered that ROME is expressed in many different tumor types and that ROME expression is significantly higher in tumors than in normal tissues ( ; Supplementary Fig. S11). Importantly, we also demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between ROME expression and worse patient survival in multiple cancer types ( ). Given the limited availability of samples from patients with Ewing sarcoma, we could not show a robust correlation between ROME expression and outcomes of patients with Ewing sarcoma. As cohorts with more robust sample sizes are gathered and more data become publicly available, a stronger correlation for ROME in Ewing sarcoma may emerge. Overall, we showed the existence of the ROME protein in a variety of different cancer cell types and demonstrated that it is a strong modulator of metastasis, making ROME a potentially attractive target for the development of drug inhibitors to prevent metastases in the clinic. The measurement of ROME expression may also be useful for stratification of patients most at risk of developing metastases.
To our knowledge, our group is the first to report a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen using human cells as xenografts in zebrafish. Although our screening conditions were not optimal, as we had fewer cells per gRNA than recommended, we provide proof of principle that an in vivo screen in zebrafish can produce informative data. As automated injection and imaging methods for zebrafish continue to advance, this model may become a more popular screening tool for identifying metastatic phenotypes.
Although we provide a broad characterization of ROME utilizing human cancer cells, zebrafish, and mouse models and applying a variety of techniques, including single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk RNA sequencing, many questions remain. Future studies will continue to investigate the function of ROME, including its potential activity in the nucleus, and reveal more details about the mechanism through which ROME affects metastasis. Additionally, we presented preliminary correlations between ROME expression and poor outcomes in patients, but additional studies in human patients are needed to strengthen these conclusions. This will also help to define the patient population that may receive the most clinical benefit from diagnostics or therapies targeting ROME.
