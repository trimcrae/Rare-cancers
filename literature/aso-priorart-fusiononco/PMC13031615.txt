## Abstract
Cancer continues to pose a major global health burden, with conventional therapies often hindered by systemic toxicity, drug resistance, and poor tumor specificity. Exosomes, nanoscale extracellular vesicles (EVs) naturally secreted by cells, have emerged as superior nanocarriers for targeted cancer therapy compared with synthetic carriers such as liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles, due to their intrinsic biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to cross biological barriers. This review explores the therapeutic potential of exosome-based nanocarriers, highlighting their applications in the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, RNA therapeutics, and CRISPR/Cas9 systems. EVs have been successfully engineered to encapsulate drugs such as paclitaxel, doxorubicin, and cisplatin, achieving improved targeting efficiency, reduced off-target toxicity, and enhanced therapeutic outcomes across multiple cancer models. Notably, studies indicate that paclitaxel-loaded exosomes have exhibited 50-fold higher cytotoxicity against resistant cells in vitro, and achieved in vivo efficacy comparable to 1000 times the dose of free drug. Moreover, EVs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in delivering functional RNAs (miRNAs, lncRNAs, siRNAs) and genome-editing tools, allowing for gene silencing or correction in tumor environments. Despite these advances, challenges remain regarding their isolation, heterogeneity, scalability, and stability. Addressing these limitations is crucial for clinical translation. Future directions include the development of multifunctional and hybrid EVs with enhanced drug loading and targeting capacities, and integrating exosome-based platforms into personalized and combinatorial cancer treatment regimens.

## Introduction
Cancer continues to be one of the leading causes of death worldwide, responsible for millions of deaths annually [ ]. Despite advances in cancer diagnostics and treatment, conventional therapies such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy face significant limitations [ ]. These non-specific therapies frequently result in systemic toxicity, harming healthy tissues and causing serious side effects that compromise patient quality of life [ ]. Additionally, challenges such as drug resistance and insufficient tumor targeting continue to hinder the success of these approaches [ ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop therapeutic approaches that are more accurate, highly effective, and associated with reduced toxicity [ ].
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as novel candidates in the development of targeted cancer therapies [ ]. Among the different types of EVs, exosomes, with a size range of 30–150 nm, have garnered particular interest due to their unique biological properties [ ]. Unlike synthetic nanocarriers such as liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles, EVs are naturally non-immunogenic, enabling them to circulate for extended periods provoking adverse immune responses [ , ]. These vesicles are enriched with biomolecules reflective of their parent cells and are naturally equipped with mechanisms to cross biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [ ], making them promising vehicles for delivering therapeutic agents directly to target sites [ , ].
Exosome-based delivery systems in cancer therapy present significant advantages over traditional methods [ ]. Their natural composition ensures high biocompatibility and minimal immunogenicity, reducing the likelihood of adverse immune reactions [ , ]. Moreover, EVs can be customized to enhance their therapeutic effectiveness [ ]. They can be loaded with chemotherapeutics, nucleic acids, or immunomodulators and modified with targeting ligands or antibodies to direct their cargo specifically to tumor cells while protecting healthy tissues [ , ]. This targeted approach boosts therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects [ ].
This review aims to explore the multifaceted potential of exosome-based nanocarriers in cancer therapy, specifically examining their utility in targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, alongside novel therapeutic modalities such as functional RNAs and CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing systems, as well as their role in facilitating photothermal and photodynamic therapies. It highlights the biological advantages of exosomes, their engineering possibilities, and their ability to overcome the limitations of existing cancer therapies. Additionally, this review discusses challenges and future perspectives for integrating exosome-based platforms into clinical practice (Fig. ).
 

## Mechanisms of exosome formation and cargo loading
Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles formed through a tightly regulated process within the endosomal system. Unlike microvesicles, which bud directly from the plasma membrane, exosomes are formed through the inward budding of endosomal membranes, generating intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) [ , ]. Their biogenesis begins with endocytosis of extracellular material and contributions from intracellular organelles like mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and the trans-Golgi network, leading to early-sorting endosomes (ESEs) that mature into late-sorting endosomes (LSEs) and subsequently MVBs [ , ]. The Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) machinery is crucial in this process, mediating the sorting of ubiquitinated proteins into ILVs through coordinated actions of its subunits ESCRT-0, I, II, and III, with assistance from proteins like ALIX and TSG101 [ – ]. Parallel ESCRT-independent pathways involve ceramide-induced membrane budding and tetraspanins such as CD9, CD63, and CD81, which regulate vesicle formation and cargo selection [ ]. Beyond the ESCRT pathway, Rab GTPases are key regulators of intracellular vesicle trafficking and are known to play critical roles in exosome formation and secretion [ ]. Among them, Rab27a, Rab27b, and Rab35 are particularly involved in mediating the docking of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) to the plasma membrane, thereby facilitating exosome release [ ]. Cargo loading into exosomes is a selective process, regulated by sorting proteins like ALIX and RNA-binding proteins such as hnRNPA2B1, enabling the packaging of specific proteins, lipids, and RNAs (mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA) [ ]. Lipid composition, especially ceramide and sphingolipid-rich microdomains, further influences cargo recruitment [ ]. Additionally, advances in exosome engineering have made it possible to load therapeutic molecules, including chemotherapeutic drugs, through methods like electroporation to improve loading efficiency [ ]. Once MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane, ILVs are secreted as exosomes, capable of delivering their bioactive cargo to recipient cells [ ]. After their formation, MVBs can follow one of three possible routes: they may fuse with the plasma membrane to release their ILVs as exosomes, or be targeted for degradation either by lysosomes or autophagosomes [ , ]. ## Engineering strategies for exosome-based therapies
Engineering strategies for exosome-based therapies have evolved in response to challenges related to targeting accuracy, controlled cargo release, and functional stability, giving rise to a more integrated design framework. Targeted modification engineering enhances the homing capacity of exosomes by tailoring their surface with disease-specific ligands, peptides, or antibodies, either via genetic or chemical modification, thereby enabling receptor-mediated uptake and precise delivery to pathological tissues [ – ]. Building on this spatial precision, intelligent response engineering introduces stimulus-responsive features that allow exosomes respond to disease-specific stimuli, such as acidic pH, redox imbalance, or enzyme overexpression [ ]. This ensures that therapeutic cargo is released in a controlled and site-specific manner after cellular internalization [ ]. Combined use of bioactive materials with exosomes has also emerged as a powerful strategy, integrating polymers, lipids, hydrogels, or inorganic nanomaterials to improve structural stability, extend circulation time, and enable sustained delivery [ , ]. These integrated engineering strategies reshape native exosomes into versatile, multifunctional platforms that combine precise targeting, responsive drug release, and improved therapeutic efficacy, highlighting their promise for next-generation precision medicine.

## Mechanisms of tumor targeting by exosome nanocarriers
Exosome nanocarriers exploit a multilayered targeting strategy that integrates passive accumulation, active molecular recognition, and inherent biological tropism to achieve precise delivery to tumor sites. At the passive level, exosomes take advantage of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [ ]. The inherent leakiness and defective tumor vasculature allow these nanosized vesicles to extravasate from the bloodstream and penetrate the tumor microenvironment [ , ]. This is further compounded by impaired lymphatic drainage, which prevents efficient clearance of exosomes, enhancing their retention within the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Complementing this passive process, exosomes engage in active targeting via an array of surface molecules that bind selectively to receptors and ligands on cancer cells [ ]. These interactions enhance membrane fusion and internalization of exosomes via several mechanisms, including membrane fusion, lipid-raft mediated endocytosis, phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, caveolin- mediated and clathrin-mediated endocytosis [ ]. Beyond these interactions, exosomes possess an intrinsic tropism determined by their parent cell origin [ ]. Their membrane integrins and lipid profiles guide them toward specific tissues, including particular tumor subtypes or metastatic sites [ , ]. This organ-specific homing has been demonstrated to influence biodistribution patterns, enabling exosomes to selectively migrate to environments that mirror the biological state of their parent cells [ ]. Collectively, these targeting mechanisms establishes exosomes as a uniquely superior platform compared to synthetic nanocarriers, making them a promising platform for precision oncology applications.

## Role of exosome-based nanocarriers in cancer therapy

## Drug delivery and targeting efficiency
Exosome-based nanocarriers have emerged as a revolutionary platform for delivering chemotherapeutic agents directly to tumor sites [ , ]. Specific chemotherapeutic drugs, including paclitaxel (PTX) (also known as Taxol), doxorubicin (DOX), and cisplatin, have been loaded into exosomes to target different malignancies [ – ]. For instance, an in vitro study by Saary et al., loading PTX into autologous extracellular vesicles derived from prostate cancer cells, enhanced its cytotoxic activity [ ]. Similarly, an in vitro study by Kim et al., has shown that PTX-loaded exosomes to treat multidrug resistance (MDR) cancer exhibited 50-fold higher cytotoxicity against drug-resistant cancer cells in vitro compared to free PTX [ ]. It has also been reported that macrophage-derived exosomes loaded with paclitaxel (exoPTX) can serve as an effective drug delivery system for lung cancer treatment. By incorporating an aminoethylanisamide-polyethylene glycol (AA-PEG) targeting ligand, researchers developed AA-PEG-exoPTX nanoparticles capable of specifically targeting sigma receptors overexpressed in lung cancer cells. This targeted exosome formulation demonstrated high drug loading efficiency, enhanced tumor accumulation after systemic administration, and improved therapeutic outcomes both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Exosomes derived from natural killer (NK) cells and loaded with DOX (DOX-NK-Exos) demonstrated potent anticancer activity, inducing apoptosis, and strongly inhibiting the proliferation of human breast carcinoma masses [ ]. In another study by Bagheri et al., exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were functionalized with the mucin-1 (MUC-1) aptamer and loaded with DOX, creating a targeted nanocarrier with about 35% drug-loading efficiency. These MUC1-modified exosomes showed enhanced uptake and stronger cytotoxicity in MUC1-positive cancer cells, and in a C26 mouse model a single intravenous dose significantly slowed tumor growth with superior tumor accumulation compared to free and non-targeted DOX [ ]. Exosomes from MSCs have shown promise in enhancing both the delivery and therapeutic efficacy of DOX in patients with osteosarcoma, Wei et al. showed that MSC-derived exosomes can effectively enhance doxorubicin delivery and antitumor activity in osteosarcoma. After loading DOX (Exo-Dox), particle size increased modestly, and the engineered vesicles displayed stronger cytotoxicity across MG63, HOS, and 143B cells, with IC50 values consistently lower than free DOX. In vivo imaging confirmed efficient tumor targeting, while histology revealed reduced Ki-67 levels and markedly lower cardiotoxicity compared with free DOX [ ]. It has also been shown that dendritic cell (DC)-derived exosomes engineered with the iRGD targeting peptide can effectively deliver DOX to tumors with low immunogenicity and toxicity. These iRGD-modified exosomes, loaded with DOX via electroporation, demonstrated efficient targeting of αv integrin-positive breast cancer cells and markedly suppressed tumor growth in vivo without causing systemic toxicity [ ]. In addition, cisplatin delivered by exosomes into cisplatin-resistant cancer cells via clathrin-independent endocytosis enhances its anticancer efficacy by bypassing endosomal trapping in both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. However, Xu et al. demonstrated that exosomes released from cisplatin-induced dormant A549 cells promote cancer cell growth and metastasis in the bone marrow by reprogramming bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) [ ]. Vincristine (VCR) sulfate is another FDA-approved drug, classified as vinca alkaloids, which derives its anticancer effects by disrupting microtubule fibers, leading to cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase [ ]. A study by Farouk et al. demonstrated VCR sulfate-Exos effectiveness by reducing the proportion of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in the T47D breast cancer cell line from an average of 10.5% to 2.8% across the entire cell population [ ]. However, despite its significant impact on reducing CSCs, the cytotoxicity of VCR sulfate-Exo remained nearly identical to that of VCR sulfate [ ]. Lapatinib is a small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [ ]. In a study conducted by Değirmenci et al., it has been reported that lapatinib-loaded exosomes reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis in HER2-positive breast cancer cells (SKBR-3) more effectively than free lapatinib, even at lower doses [ ]. In another study, HEK293T-derived exosomes displaying IL3 on their surface were loaded with either imatinib or BCR-ABL siRNA. These IL3-targeted exosomes specifically recognized IL3 receptor-overexpressing Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells, delivering their cargo efficiently and significantly inhibiting cancer cell growth both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Nanoparticles that mimic natural exosomes offer a promising strategy to improve targeted drug delivery by combining the advantages of nanotechnology with the biological functions of exosomes [ ]. For instance, Yong et al. developed tumor cell-derived, exosome-mimicking porous silicon nanoparticles loaded with DOX (DOX@E-PSiNPs) that enhance targeted drug delivery by improving tumor penetration and accumulation. These nanoparticles effectively eliminate both bulk cancer cells and CSCs, leading to reduced tumor growth and stem cell populations across multiple cancer models [ ]. Temozolomide (TMZ) is the standard first-line chemotherapeutic agent for glioblastoma (GBM); however, its clinical efficacy is limited by the BBB and the development of chemoresistance. To overcome these barriers, Xu et al. developed a biomimetic tumor cell exosome membrane–based nanoplatform (EMNPs@TMZ) for the co-delivery of TMZ and luteolin using ROS-responsive fucoidan nanoparticles. In vitro, EMNPs@TMZ showed efficient cellular uptake, reduced GBM cell viability and migration, and markedly enhanced apoptosis and ferroptosis through ROS accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and suppression of the Nrf2/GPX4/SLC7A11 axis. In vivo, EMNPs@TMZ effectively crossed the BBB, selectively accumulated in GBM tissue, significantly inhibited tumor growth, and prolonged survival in mouse models, while exhibiting minimal systemic toxicity [ ].
Mechanistically, exosomes facilitate drug delivery by leveraging their lipid bilayer structure, which provides stability and protects drugs from premature degradation [ ]. Cellular uptake occurs primarily through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with drugs being released intracellularly via endosomal escape mechanisms [ , ]. This ensures targeted cytotoxicity while minimizing systemic side effects [ ]. Melzer et al. demonstrated that MSC-derived exosomes offer strong biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and highly efficient drug-delivery potential, showing 80–90% cytotoxicity in A549, SK-OV-3, and MDA-hyb1 cancer cells when loaded with taxol, despite carrying markedly lower drug amounts than free taxol. In vivo, taxol-loaded MSC exosomes reduced primary breast tumor growth by over 60% and decreased metastasis by about 50%, achieving effects comparable to taxol but with nearly 1,000-fold less drug [ ]. In another study on targeted DOX delivery to A33-positive colon cancer cells, exosomes loaded with DOX and coated with A33 antibodies (A33Ab-US-Exo/DOX) significantly decreased the viability of LIM1215 cells in vitro and exhibited strong tumor-targeting ability in vivo. Moreover, treatment with A33Ab-US-Exo/DOX suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival in LIM1215 tumor-bearing mice, showing enhanced antitumor efficacy and reduced cardiotoxicity compared to free DOX [ ].
Exosomal carriers have also been used to deliver combination therapies [ ]. In vitro, A15-EXO co-loaded with DOX and Cho-miR159 exhibited synergistic therapeutic effects in MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells. In vivo, delivery of miR159 and DOX through this vesicular system efficiently silenced the TCF-7 gene and enhanced anticancer efficacy, without causing any observable adverse effects [ ]. Liang et al. also developed an engineered exosome-based delivery system capable of co-delivering 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and a miR-21 inhibitor (miR-21i) to HER2-positive, 5-FU-resistant colorectal cancer cells. These exosomes, loaded via electroporation, significantly enhanced drug uptake, downregulated miR-21 expression, and restored the expression of its targets PTEN and hMSH2. This led to cell cycle arrest, reduced tumor proliferation, and increased apoptosis in resistant cancer cells. In vivo studies confirmed that combined delivery of 5-FU and miR-21 using exosomes effectively reversed drug resistance and reduced tumor growth [ ]. More recently, a multifunctional tumor-derived exosome co-delivery system was developed to enhance antitumor efficacy while reducing chemotherapy-related toxicity. Exosomes isolated from OCI-Ly3 DLBCL cells were loaded with doxorubicin and the herbal compounds gingerol and atractylenolide (TEX@DOX-Herb). In vitro, TEX@DOX-Herb significantly increased DOX uptake by tumor cells, triggered more apoptosis, and showed stronger cytotoxicity than free DOX, without notable toxicity from the exosomes themselves. In vivo, these exosomes effectively homed to the tumor and remained there for an extended period, leading to significant tumor growth inhibition (~ 90% vs. ~70% with DOX alone) and longer survival time. Simultaneously, these exosomes exhibited intestinal tropism, leading to a nearly 40% reduction in severe chemotherapy-induced diarrhea by suppressing NF-κB–driven inflammation and restoring intestinal barrier integrity [ ]. Beyond chemotherapeutics, exosomes have shown great potential in optimizing natural and semi-synthetic compounds for melanoma therapy. For instance, to address the poor solubility of Hesperidin (HES), researchers formulated HES-loaded exosomes, which improved oral bioavailability by ~ 2.5-fold and achieved superior in vivo anti-tumor efficacy compared to the free drug [ ]. Similarly, bovine milk-derived exosomes were utilized to encapsulate Dihydroartemisinin (DHA), an FDA-approved anti-malarial agent. This formulation overcame solubility issues and provided a sustained release profile. Although limited to in vitro studies, Exo-DHA demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity and anti-migration effects against melanoma cells via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and mitochondrial pathways compared to free DHA [ ].
Recent advancements in synthetic approaches have further optimized drug loading and delivery [ ]. Endogenous loading is a drug-loading approach that harnesses exosome donor cells, achieving a loading efficiency of 50%, which is higher than other methods like electroporation, sonication, or heat shock [ ]. Injected hybrid exosomes have demonstrated superior stability and prolonged circulation times [ ]. For example, in a study, multifunctional hybrid exosomes were designed and synthesized using exosomes derived from M1 macrophages and genetically engineered CD47-overexpressing tumor cells to activate both innate and adaptive immunity. The results demonstrated that the resulting hybrid exosomes exhibited prolonged blood circulation and could effectively target tumor cells [ ]. Additionally, advanced surface modifications using ligands such as transferrin have improved specificity for cancer cells, minimizing off-target effects [ ]. Phung et al. showed that T-cell-targeting exosomes modified with costimulatory molecules enhanced T-cell binding, triggered a tumor-specific T-cell response, and ultimately inhibited tumor growth [ ]. ## Delivery of RNA therapeutics
Exosome-based nanocarriers have proven highly effective in delivering RNA therapeutics, including siRNA, miRNA, and mRNA, for cancer treatment [ , ]. For instance, a study on mouse model of pancreatic cancer revealed that exosomes loaded with siRNA or shRNA targeting KrasG12D significantly reduced KrasG12D mRNA and phosphorylated-ERK protein levels in Panc-1 cells. These Exosomes also selectively inhibited Ras activity in Panc-1 cells compared to BxPC-3 cells (KRASWT). Furthermore, Exosomes impaired proliferation and enhanced apoptosis in Panc-1 cells, while leaving other KRAS mutant and wild-type cancer cells unaffected [ ]. Similarly, Deng et al. demonstrated that Exosomes loaded with microRNA-34a were released from HEK293T cells and taken up by HN6 oral squamous carcinoma cells (OSCCs), resulting in a marked reduction in HN6 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by downregulating SATB2 expression [ ]. Bai et al. also showed that engineered exosomes expressing the tLyp-1 targeting peptide can effectively deliver siRNA to lung cancer and CSCs. In this approach, HEK293T cells were transfected with tLyp-1-lamp2b plasmids to produce targeting exosomes, which were then loaded with siRNA via electroporation. These tLyp-1-modified exosomes showed high transfection efficiency, successfully silenced target genes, and reduced the stemness of cancer stem cells [ ]. It has also been shown that engineered exosomes displaying DARPin G3 ligands can specifically target HER2-positive breast cancer cells. In this study, HEK293T cells were genetically modified to express a LAMP2b-DARPin G3 fusion protein on the surface of exosomes. These targeted exosomes, after being loaded with siRNA, successfully delivered their cargo to SKBR3 cells and downregulated TPD52 gene expression by up to 70% [ ]. In another study, 293T cells were genetically modified to express a CD63-Apo-A1 fusion protein on the surface of their exosomes, allowing these vesicles to specifically recognize and bind to scavenger receptor class B type 1 on HepG2 cells. After loading with miR-26a by electroporation, the exosomes successfully delivered the miRNA into target cells, leading to reduced cell proliferation and migration through suppression of cell cycle-related proteins [ ]. In addition, Ohno et al. reported that exosomes engineered with GE11 peptides can effectively deliver miRNA to EGFR-expressing breast cancer cells. In this approach, donor cells were modified to express a fusion of the platelet-derived growth factor receptor transmembrane domain with the GE11 peptide, enabling the exosomes to specifically target EGFR-positive tumor cells. After intravenous injection, these exosomes successfully delivered let-7a miRNA to breast cancer xenografts in mice [ ].

## Exosome-based delivery of the CRISPR-cas system
In addition to therapeutic RNA delivery, exosomes have been engineered to carry CRISPR-Cas9 components for precise genome editing [ ]. Wan et al. showed that Exosome-mediated delivery of Cas9 ribonucleoproteins showed strong therapeutic potential in mouse models of acute liver injury, chronic liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma by targeting p53 up-regulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA), cyclin E1 (CcnE1), and K (lysine) acetyltransferase 5 (KAT5), respectively [ ]. In another study, exosomes were engineered with CD63-GFP and Cas9 fused to GFP nanobody to enhance Cas9 loading via GFP-GFP nanobody interaction. The sgRNA: Cas9 complexes were efficiently packaged into these exosomes and delivered into recipient cells, where successful gene editing was achieved, as confirmed by fluorescence activation in A549 reporter cells [ ]. Similarly, McAndrews et al. showed that engineered exosomes can serve as a nonviral delivery system for CRISPR/Cas9, enabling the encapsulation and delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid DNA into pancreatic cancer cells. Their results demonstrated successful targeting of the mutant KrasG12D allele, leading to reduced cell proliferation and suppressed tumor growth in both subcutaneous and orthotopic pancreatic cancer models [ ]. ## Photodynamic and photothermal therapy
Exosomes can serve as effective delivery platforms for carrying chemotherapeutics and other therapeutic agents, including those used in phototherapy and photothermal therapy [ ]. In a study by Wang et al., exosomes loaded with DOX and coated with magnetic nanoparticles conjugated with molecular beacons targeting miR-21 for imaging and gene silencing were designed and synthetized. Magnetic nanoparticles guide the exosomes to the tumor site under an external magnetic field, where near-infrared (NIR) radiation induces localized hyperthermia and triggers drug release. The released molecular beacon targets miR-21 for imaging and silencing, while DOX kills cancer cells, achieving a 91.04% cancer cell death rate with Exo-DOX-Fe₃O₄@PDA-MB under NIR [ ]. Similarly, in another study on Exosome-liposome hybrid-based vehicles (ELV), Liu et al. showed that NIR-C12-EL demonstrated excellent colloidal stability, biocompatibility, strong light-harvesting ability, high NIR-II photoconversion efficiency (62.28%), and tumor-targeting capability. These properties enabled effective tumor diagnosis and ablation, significantly extending the lifespan of mice treated with NIR-C12-EL and continuous 1064 nm laser irradiation [ ]. In the context of OSCC, Li et al. engineered tumor-derived exosomes co-loaded with Indocyanine green and Gefitinib to achieve synergistic phototherapy and molecular targeted therapy. These nanoparticles leveraged homologous targeting for deep tumor penetration, where the combination of photothermal and photodynamic effects facilitated Gefitinib release, leading to effective tumor ablation and suppression of lymphatic metastasis [ ]. In parallel, addressing TNBC, Hu et al. designed a hybrid nanoplatform (IG Exo-Lipo) by fusing liposomes with SIRPα-engineered exosomes to co-deliver IR780 and a ROS-responsive gambogic acid prodrug. Upon NIR irradiation, the generated ROS triggered the on-demand release of gambogic acid, which downregulated heat shock protein 90 (HSP90). This unique mechanism sensitized tumor cells to mild hyperthermia, resulting in a potent synergistic chemo-photothermal effect [ ]. In a mouse model of prostate cancer, Liu et al. utilized magnetic particle imaging (MPI) and fluorescence molecular imaging (FMI) to evaluate the in vivo retention of exosomes, providing valuable insights into a potential combined photothermal and magnetic thermal therapy for prostate cancer [ ].

## Clinical use of exosome-based therapies in cancer
Currently, 150 clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov are investigating exosome-based interventions, with the majority of these diagnostic and therapeutic efforts concentrated on cancer and neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. Clinical efforts to translate exosome-based therapeutics into oncology have expanded steadily, with several early-phase trials demonstrating both feasibility and biological activity. Among the most advanced approaches are DC–derived exosomal vaccines, which have been evaluated in melanoma, NSCLC, and colon cancer. In melanoma, a Phase I study using autologous immature DC–derived exosomes pulsed with MAGE-3 peptides showed favorable tolerability with no Grade II toxicities, although antigen-specific CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T-cell responses were not detected [ ]. Similar strategies were tested in NSCLC; early Phase I work demonstrated safety and modest immunogenicity, including MAGE-specific T-cell activation and increased NK cell cytotoxicity in a subset of patients [ ]. These findings supported a Phase II trial using interferon-γ–matured DC-exosomes loaded with multiple tumor antigens (MAGE-A1, MAGE-A3, NY-ESO-1, Melan-A/MART-1), which again confirmed good tolerability and enhanced NK-cell activity, highlighting the immunostimulatory potential of DC-exosomes [ ]. Another notable Phase I study in colon cancer employed exosomes isolated from autologous ascites fluid, enriched with tumor-associated antigens and heat shock proteins; when combined with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), these vesicles elicited tumor-specific antitumoral cytotoxic T-lymphocyte responses while maintaining a safety profile [ ]. Beyond DC-derived preparations, several trials on ClinicalTrials.gov are currently exploring exosome-based approaches in solid tumors [ ]. For instance, a Phase I trial in metastatic pancreatic cancer (NCT03608631) investigates MSC-derived exosomes carrying KRASG12D siRNA, whereas another trial in bladder cancer (NCT05559177) evaluates chimeric exosome-based tumor vaccines. Additional studies include a colon cancer trial testing plant-derived exosomal curcumin formulations (NCT01294072) and a hepatocellular carcinoma trial assessing the exosomal drug candidate CDK-004 (NCT05375604). Collectively, these clinical trials underscore the therapeutic versatility of exosomes, confirming their strong safety profile and their growing potential as effective cancer therapeutics.

## Immune modulation via exosome-based nanocarriers

## Activation of anti-tumor immune responses
Exosomes hold tremendous potential for cancer immunotherapy, as they have the ability to evolve into powerful cancer vaccines and carriers of targeted antigens and drugs, playing a significant role in regulating cancer progression [ , ]. For example, exosome-based vaccines derived from melanoma cells are rich in tumor-specific antigens and, after appropriate processing, they can enhance the activity of DCs and CD8⁺ T cells, promoting a robust antitumor immune response in vivo [ ]. Delivery of a neoantigen-exosome nano vaccine significantly inhibited tumor growth, prolonged survival, and delayed tumor onset with long-term memory [ ].
In a study on HLA-A2/HER2 mice, Xie et al. developed a heterologous human/rat HER2-specific T cell vaccine, HuRt-TEXO, has demonstrated the ability to stimulate both humoral and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, resulting in robust antitumor immunity. This vaccine shows promise as a novel therapeutic approach for patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, particularly those with trastuzumab-resistant HER2-positive tumors [ ]. Similarly, Rossowska et al. demonstrated that tumor-derived exosomes (TEX) engineered to carry IL-12 and/or depleted of TGF-β1 hold significant potential as adjuvants, effectively enhancing the induction of specific antitumor immune responses [ ].

## Suppression of immune evasion mechanisms
Evidence suggests that TEX significantly contribute to reprogramming host immune cells, which often lead to their dysfunction and impaired immune response against tumors [ ]. In a study by Zhang et al., biomimetic exosome vesicles (apoA1-bExo) were engineered by integrating exosome membranes, apoA1, and phospholipids. These vesicles were subsequently incubated with cholesterol-modified siRNA to produce apoA1-bExo loaded with siRNA (apoA1-bExo/siRNA). This innovative approach effectively inhibited ExoPD-L1 secretion, significantly enhancing the clinical efficacy of PD-L1 antibodies and activating a systemic antitumor immune response by targeting ExoPD-L1 secretion within tumors [ ]. It has been also demonstrated that exosomal delivery of STAT3 inhibitors has effectively disrupted immune evasion pathways in colorectal cancer cells, helping to restore sensitivity to 5-FU in RKO/P cells by triggering the caspase cascade. These findings highlight the importance of identifying biomarkers for chemotherapy resistance [ ].

## Modulation of tumor-associated macrophages
Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are the most prevalent immune cells within the tumor microenvironment and play a crucial role in promoting tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion [ ]. Recent studies indicate that cancer cell–derived exosomes can induce a tumor-promoting phenotype in TAMs. By carrying proteins, DNA, and RNA, these exosomes may contribute to macrophage reprogramming, enhancing their ability to support tumor development. For example, exosomes derived from melanoma cells deliver miR-125b-5p to macrophages, contributing to the development of a tumor-promoting phenotype in TAM [ ]. Conversely, TAM-derived exosomes themselves actively contribute to cancer progression through both protein and non-coding RNA contents [ ]. M2-type TAM-derived exosomes are enriched in tumor-promoting proteins like cathepsin B and S, facilitating tumor growth and angiogenesis, while simultaneously showing reduced levels of inflammatory regulators such as FCER1G, TBK1, and PRKDC, favoring an immunosuppressive environment [ ]. In gastric cancer, TAM-derived exosomal ApoE activates PI3K/Akt signaling and enhances migration, while IL-6 enriched exosomes from macrophages promote breast cancer proliferation via STAT3 activation [ , ]. However, certain TAM-derived proteins, such as ADAM15, delivered via exosomes, have been shown to inhibit tumor progression by reducing tumor growth and prolonging survival [ ]. Additionally, TAM-derived exosomal non-coding RNAs significantly influence tumor biology. MicroRNAs like miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-223, delivered through TAM exosomes, promote invasion, metastasis, immune escape, and proliferation in various cancers by targeting key pathways such as BRG1, Mef2c-β-catenin, and STAT3 [ – ]. Contrarily, exosomal miR-628-5p from M1 macrophages exerts tumor-suppressive effects in hepatocellular carcinoma by downregulating METTL14 [ ]. Similarly, lncRNAs carried by TAM exosomes, such as lncMMPA in hepatocellular carcinoma and CRNDE in gastric cancer, promote glycolysis, proliferation, and drug resistance, while lncRNA AFAP1-AS1 supports esophageal cancer progression through modulation of the miR-26a/ATF2 axis [ , ]. CircRNAs within TAM-derived exosomes also play dual roles: circ_0020256 enhances invasion and metastasis of cholangiocarcinoma via miR-432-5p/E2F3 signaling, while circRNA-BTG2 acts as a tumor suppressor in glioma by targeting the miR-25-3p/PTEN pathway [ , ].

## The dual role of exosomes
Exosomes possess dual functionality that can both support and hinder therapeutic outcomes, depending on their cellular origin and molecular composition. On the beneficial side, exosomes derived from immune cells, such as DCs or macrophages, often contain immune-activating molecules including MHC class I/II, co-stimulatory ligands (e.g., CD80/CD86), and cytokines that can facilitate antigen presentation and enhance T-cell-mediated anti-tumor responses [ ]. Conversely, tumor-derived exosomes frequently carry immunosuppressive factors such as PD-L1, TGF-β, and IL-10, which can downregulate immune cell activity, suppress cytotoxic T lymphocyte function, and contribute to tumor immune escape [ ]. This duality presents a major consideration in exosome-based therapeutics, as the intrinsic bioactivity of the cargo may either align with or undermine the intended therapeutic effect. To overcome this duality, bioengineering techniques have been developed to improve exosome stability and enhance their targeting capabilities. These improvements make engineered exosomes promising and efficient drug delivery vehicles in therapeutic applications [ ]. Moreover, selecting the appropriate exosome source, especially those derived from less differentiated cells or from autologous or allogeneic origins, can significantly minimize immunogenicity and enhance overall safety [ ]. Ultimately, the clinical success of EV-based nanocarriers will depend on how effectively this biological duality is controlled. With rigorously standardized isolation methods and advanced engineering approaches that preserve their therapeutic advantages while minimizing immunosuppressive risks, exosomes have the potential to shift from a double-edged system to a safe and highly powerful modality for cancer treatment.

## Challenges and limitations in exosome-based therapies

## Heterogeneity and isolation techniques
Exosome heterogeneity remains a critical challenge in their therapeutic application, as variations in size, content, and surface markers can affect their functionality and targeting efficiency [ ]. Traditional isolation methods, such as ultracentrifugation (UC) and density gradient (DG) centrifugation, hinder the scalability of EV production due to intra- and inter-batch variations in EV preparations, leading to inconsistent quality and yield [ , ]. Advanced isolation methods, such as microfluidic-based platforms, have shown promise in enhancing the purity and specificity of isolated exosomes [ ]. For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that Chet et al. reported a microfluidic device that isolates exosomes from 100 µl of undiluted whole blood in 25 min, achieving 98% purity and 82% yield [ ]. Nevertheless, standardizing these techniques for clinical use remains an unresolved issue [ ].

## Stability and storage concerns
Exosome stability during storage is another major limitation, as they are prone to degradation under suboptimal conditions [ ]. A study by Sivanantham and Jin have shown that freeze-thaw cycles and prolonged storage did not affect EV yield in human serum [ ]. Optimal storage conditions, such as lyophilization or storage at − 80 °C with cryoprotectants, have been explored to maintain their functionality [ , ]. However, these methods are not universally applicable, as they depend on the exosome’s origin and intended use [ ].

## Regulatory and ethical challenges
Exosome-based therapies show great therapeutic promise; however, several challenges must be overcome for successful clinical translation [ ]. Lack of efficient and standardized methods for exosome isolation and purification remains a major barrier to the clinical translation of exosome-based therapies [ ]. Despite projections of significant market growth, the field continues to face substantial scientific and regulatory challenges, including the absence of universally accepted protocols and the complexities of navigating regulatory frameworks such as those established by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [ ]. Scalability of exosome production, purification, and modification techniques further limits clinical application, as current approaches are labor-intensive and time-consuming [ , ]. Ethical considerations add an additional layer of complexity. EVs derived from human sources, particularly stem cells and tumor cells, raise concerns regarding donor consent, long-term safety, and potential tumor promoting properties [ , ]. Moreover, inconsistencies in isolation and preparation protocols can influence both efficacy and safety, making the establishment of universal regulatory guideline difficult [ , ]. Eventually, high production costs and functional variability limit accessibility and pose significant barriers to widespread implementation [ , ]. Establishing standardized protocols and clear guidelines is therefore essential to ensure the safe and reproducible use of EV-based therapies in clinical settings.

## Conclusion and future perspective
Exosome-based nanocarriers are rapidly emerging as one of the most promising frontiers in cancer therapy, due to their natural biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to move across biological barriers. Their flexible structure allows them to carry a wide range of therapeutic cargos, while maintaining a physiological profile that synthetic nanocarriers often fail to achieve. However, despite their remarkable potential, significant obstacles continue to slow their progress toward clinical application. Heterogeneity of exosome populations, limited yields during isolation, and long-term stability concerns remain major challenges that must be overcome before these vesicles can be used reliably in clinical translation.
Future research should focus on the development of next-generation, multifunctional EVs. Emphasis should be placed on surface engineering to enhance tissue-specific targeting, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity. EVs also hold considerable promise in combination therapy, where they could simultaneously deliver chemotherapeutic agents or immune modulators to help overcome multidrug resistance. On the technological side, the incorporation of AI-assisted design and microfluidic technologies could revolutionize the scalable production and purification of exosomes. Ultimately, to ensure these innovations reach patients, the field requires the establishment of rigorous regulatory guidelines and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards to guarantee safety, efficacy, and reproducibility in large-scale clinical trials.
