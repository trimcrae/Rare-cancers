## Abstract

## Background
Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are highly conserved multifunctional signaling proteins with pleotropic effects throughout embryonic development. BMPs are aberrantly expressed in many diseases including cancer and Alzheimer’s disease. Recent studies suggested that BMP signaling negatively regulates mitochondrial bioenergetics. The mechanisms by which BMP signaling regulates bioenergetics and cell survival are not known.

## Methods
We utilized BMP type 2 receptor (BMPR2) inhibitor (JL189), BMPR2 kinase domain KO, BMPR2 siRNA, and BMP loss of function mutants in C. elegans to inhibit BMP signaling (BMPR2i). The effects of BMPR2i on mitochondrial bioenergetics were examined by measuring differences in TCA cycle intermediates (mass spectrometer), mitochondrial respiration (Agilent Seahorse), and mitochondrial mass (MitoTracker Green/TFAM). Fluorescent mitochondrial Ca2+ sensors Rhod-2AM and LAR-GECO were used to detect changes in mitochondrial Ca2+ levels in cell culture and C elegans respectively. The KO and siRNA of the mitochondria uniporter (MCU) were used to determine the mechanisms BMPR2i regulates the uptake of Ca2+ into the mitochondria. We compared the responses of BMPR2i in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines, leukemia cells, breast cancer cells, and HT-22 mouse hippocampal cells to assess whether the biological response varied depending on the cell type.

## Results
BMPR2i increased mitochondrial Ca2+ (mtCa2+) levels in all cells lines and in C. elegans, suggesting its regulation of Ca2+ transport is conserved. BMPR2i induced increase in mtCa2+ levels were dependent on the MCU, which effected mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell survival. In addition, our data suggests that BMPR2 regulation of mtCa2+ transport is mediated by TAK1-d splice variant. In leukemia cells, BMPR2i induced significant cell death that was attenuated by MCU KO. In NSCLC and HT-22 cells, BMPR2i increased mitochondrial bioenergetics and induced minimal cell death.

## Conclusion
These studies reveal that BMPR2 signaling regulates TAK1-d splice variant to mediate mitochondrial Ca2+ transport, which is dependent on the MCU. Our studies suggest that BMPR2 signaling utilizes mtCa2+ transport to regulate both mitochondrial bioenergetics and/or cell survival. Our studies provide novel insight into how aberrant BMPR2 signaling is pathogenic and suggests that the response could vary depending on the cell type.

## Background
There are more than 20 bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) ligands that signal through type 1 (Alk2, Alk3, Alk6) receptors (BMPR1) and type 2 (BMPR2A, ACVR2A, and ACVR2B) serine-threonine kinase receptors. In the canonical BMP pathway, ligand binding to BMPR1 promotes phosphorylation by constitutively active BMPR2, leading to the activation of the Smad-1/5 transcription factor [ ]. The noncanonical BMPR2 pathway signals independently of Smad-1/5 [ ]. Smad-1/5-dependent signaling includes the transcriptional activation of inhibitor of DNA binding proteins (ID1-3) [ , ], which control stem cell self-renewal and differentiation. ID1 has also been shown to regulate cancer cell metastasis and stemness of cancer stem cells [ ]. The noncanonical BMPR2 pathway induces the expression of the potent anti-apoptotic proteins X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis (XIAP) and TGF-ß activated kinase (TAK1), as well as PI3K independent of BMPR1 [ – ].
Our recent studies show that BMP signaling negatively regulates energy homeostasis. Inhibition of BMP signaling activates the master regulator of catabolism, AMP-activated kinase (AMPK), in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines. Importantly, genetic deletion of BMP ligands in C. elegans [ ] also activated AMPK, suggesting that BMPR2 regulation of energy homeostasis is conserved. BMPR2 signaling promotes PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling in several cell types including in NSCLC cell lines and in C. elegans [ – ]. Interestingly, the BMP2 ligand causes a rapid decrease in tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediates and nonessential amino acids in NSCLC cells [ ]. BMPR2 signaling has also been shown to promote survival of cancer cells through its upregulation of XIAP and TAK1 [ ]. These findings suggest that BMPR2 signaling may regulate both mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell survival.
During development, the BMP signaling cascade is multifunctional and effects a wide range of different cell types. Post-development, aberrant BMP signaling is associated with several diseases including cancer [ , ], obesity [ ], and cardiovascular disease [ ]. Activating Alk2 mutations causes fibrodysplasia ossificans progressive (FOP) and Alk2 mutations occur in 25% of pediatric diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas [ ]. BMP ligand overexpression is 30-fold higher in some cancers and in the brains of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients compared to normal tissue [ ]. Studies suggest that BMP signaling is growth promoting in several cancers including NSCLC and leukemias, while its effect in AD is poorly understood [ ]. Revealing the mechanisms by which BMP signaling regulates mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell survival may provide insight into the mechanism by which BMP signaling has pleotropic effects in disease.
Using a small inhibitor of the BMP type 2 receptor (BMPR2) and genetic deletion of BMPR2, we show that the inhibition of BMPR2 (BMPR2i) signaling increases mitochondria Ca2+ (mtCa2+) levels that are dependent on the mitochondrial uniporter (MCU). Our studies suggest that BMPR2i regulation of mitochondrial Ca2+ levels is mediated by the TAK1-d splice variant. Furthermore, our studies show that the biological response to BMPR2i induced increase in mtCa2+ levels vary between different cell types. BMPR2i in leukemia cell lines induces high mtCa2+ levels and significant cell death. In NSCLC and HT-22 hippocampal cell lines, there is a significant improvement in mitochondrial bioenergetics and little cell death. These studies reveal a novel mechanism by which BMPR2 signaling regulates mtCa2+ levels to control mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell survival.

## Cell viability analysis
Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with selected agents. After the predetermined time point, live and dead cells were counted by a Vi-CELL BLU cell viability analyzer (Beckman Coulter Brea, CA, USA), which stains dead cells with trypan blue. The IC50 was calculated using GraphPad Prism.

## Metabolomics analysis by LC-MS
Cells were harvested with a 40:40:20 (v: v:v) solvent of methanol: acetonitrile: water and 0.1 M formic acid. LC − MS analysis of the cellular metabolites was performed on the Q Exactive PLUS hybrid quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) as previously reported [ ]. The LC separation was performed on Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system with an XBridge BEH Amide column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.5 µM particle size, Waters, Milford, MA) with the corresponding XP VanGuard Cartridge. The liquid chromatography used a gradient of solvent A (95%:5% H2O: acetonitrile with 20 mM ammonium acetate, 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.4), and solvent B (20%:80% H2O: acetonitrile with 20 mM ammonium acetate, 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.4). The gradient was 0 min, 100% B; 3 min, 100% B; 3.2 min, 90% B; 6.2 min, 90% B; 6.5 min, 80% B; 10.5 min, 80% B; 10.7 min, 70% B; 13.5 min, 70% B; 13.7 min, 45% B; 16 min, 45% B; 16.5 min, 100% B. The flow rate was 300 µl/min. The column temperature was set to 25 °C. The autosampler temperature was set to 4 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL. The MS scans were in negative ion mode with a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was 3 × 106 and the scan range was 75 − 1000. Metabolite features were extracted in MAVEN [ ] with a mass accuracy window of 5 ppm.

## Seahorse mitochondrial respiration assay
Mitochondrial respiration was analyzed by Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress assay as per manufacturer protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded in XF plate one day before experiment and next day cells were treated with vehicle or JL189. Cells were maintained at 80–100% confluency at the time of the assay. The culture medium was replaced with Seahorse assay medium. The drugs oligomycin 1 µM, FCCP 1 µM, and rotenone/antimycin A 1 µM were added sequentially, and changes in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to cell number. For each arm of the study 4 independent biological replicates were used.

## Cell titer Glo ATP assay
ATP levels were analyzed using the CellTiter-Glo ATP assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 reagent was added directly to the culture medium. Plates were gently mixed for 2 min to facilitate cell lysis and then incubated at room temperature for 10 min to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was recorded using a plate luminometer. ATP levels were expressed as relative luminescence units (RLU) and normalized to control values to determine % ATP levels.

## Cytoplasmic and mitochondrial calcium analysis by flow cytometry
Mitochondrial and cytoplasmic calcium levels were analyzed using Rhod-2AM (2 µM) and Fluo-4AM (3 µM) staining, respectively. In brief, equal amounts of Rhod-2AM or Fluo-4AM stock solution and the nonionic detergent Pluronic F-127 (0.02% (w/v)) were diluted in media. The cells were stained for 45 min at 37 °C. Cells were also stained with DAPI to assess dead cells. After staining, the cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and the data was acquired on a Cytek Aurora and attune I flow cytometer. Dead cells (DAPI positive) were excluded from analysis. Single cells were selected by using forward scatter area (FSC-A) vs. forward scatter height (FSC-H) gating. DAPI was detected using the UV7 filter, and Rhod-2AM was detected using the B4 filter. Unstained cells were used as negative controls for setting the gates. Approximately 20,000 live cells were analyzed per treatment arm. To normalize mtCa2+ levels to mitochondrial mass, treated cells were stained with Rhod2AM (2 µM) for 45 min then MitoTracker Green (50 nM) for 30 min, washed and approximately 20,000 cells analyzed by flow cytometry.

## MitoTracker green analysis by flow cytometry
For the determination of mitochondrial mass, the cells were treated with DMSO (control) or JL189 2.5 µM for 24 h then stained with MitoTracker Green (50 nM) for 30 min at 37 °C. After staining, the cells were washed with PBS, and the data were acquired on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Single cells selected by FSC-A vs. FSC-H gating and approximately 2000 cells were analyzed.

## Rhod-2AM Immunofluorescence imaging
In brief, cells were plated onto sterile coverslips and treated for the designated time points. The cells were stained with Rhod-2AM (2 µM) for 45 min and then washed with PBS. The cells were examined using oil-immersion objective (60X) with the fluorescence Nikon Eclipse TE300 inverted microscope using the green excitation/red emission filter set. Five images were taken from each treatment arm and the fluorescence from the greyscale images of 25 cells was quantified from each arm using Image J. Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated using the following formula CTCF = Integrated Density – (Area of selected cell X Mean fluorescence of background readings).

## Rhod-2AM Immunofluorescence imaging analyzed by confocal microscopy
Cells were seeded onto sterile imaging dishes with glass bottom coverslips and the next day treated overnight. The cells were stained with Rhod-2AM (2 µM) for 45 min, then washed with PBS, then stained with MitoTracker Green (50 nM) for 30 min. After washing with PBS, cells were placed in FluoroBrite DMEM media. Images were obtained of live cells using a spinning disk confocal imaging system: Zeiss Axiovert Z1 microscope equipped with X-Light V2 Spinning Disk Confocal Unit (CrestOptics), 7-line LDI Laser Launch (89 North), Prime 95B Scientific CMOS camera (Photometrics) with oil-immersion objective (63X) under GFP channel (excitation at 470 nm, emission at 510+/-50 nm) and under RFP channel (excitation at 555nm, emission at 610+/-75 nm) with 1000 ms exposure time at the same z-stack. Fluorescence images were captured using Metamorph 7.7 software. Five images were taken from each treatment arm and the fluorescence from the greyscale images of 25 cells from each arm was quantified using Image J. Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated using the following formula CTCF = Integrated Density – (Area of selected cell X Mean fluorescence of background readings). Mander’s and Pearson’s coefficients for colocalization were calculated using JACoP analytical software program in ImageJ.

## siRNA transfection
siRNA transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were plated in 6-well plates and transfected with siRNA (12.5 nM). siRNA and RNAiMAX reagent were diluted in Opti-MEM medium (Invitrogen). The diluted siRNA and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The siRNA-lipid complex was added dropwise to each well then incubated for 48 h in a CO₂ incubator.

## Transfection of cDNA expression vectors in adherent cells
Cells were seeded one day before transfection and were maintained at 80% confluence on the day of transfection. In tube 1, Lipofectamine 3000 reagent was diluted in Opti-MEM medium, and in tube 2 cDNA 2.5 µg and P3000 reagent were diluted in Opti-MEM medium. The diluted DNA and Lipofectamine reagent were then mixed at a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The DNA-lipid complex was added dropwise to each well and incubated in a CO₂ incubator for 24 h.

## Transfection of cDNA expression vectors in cells growing in suspension
Amaxa Nucleofector II was used to transfect plasmid cDNA (1 µg) of cells growing in suspension (THP1). One to two million cells were suspended in 100 µL of electroporation medium (10 mM HEPES [pH 7.2], 5 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 15 mM Na₂HPO₄, 15 mM sodium succinate, and 150 mM mannitol), and THP1 cells were transfected using program T-001. After electroporation, the cells were immediately transferred into complete medium and incubated for 24 h in a CO₂ incubator.

## Western blotting
Western blotting was performed as previously described [ ]. In brief, total protein was isolated by RIPA lysis buffer, subjected to SDS‒PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The band was visualized on X-ray films.

## Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)
Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was measured using tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) staining via flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were treated with either DMSO (control) or 2.5 µM JL189 for 18–24 h. After treatment, cells were stained with TMRM (50 nM) for 30 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. For positive control, cells were treated with 50 µM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) for 5 min before staining. After staining, cells were washed with PBS, and data were acquired on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Single cells were gated by FSC-A vs. FSC-H, and approximately 20,000 cells were analyzed.

## Immunoprecipitation (IP)
Calu-1 cells were transfected with TAB1-Flag or control expression vectors for 24 h and protein was extracted using nondenaturing lysis buffer. The DDKTrap™ resin (40 µL slurry, 20 µL bed volume) was washed with 0.5 ml nondenaturing lysis buffer by centrifugation. Clarified protein lysates was added in to the resin and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. To remove the unbound fraction, the resin was washed four times with 0.5 ml nondenaturing lysis buffer. To elute the bound protein, the resin was resuspended in 1× bed volume of 2× SDS sample buffer that was heated to 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 1 min, and the supernatant was collected for SDS-PAGE. Whole cell lysate was also obtained from transfected cells that were not immunoprecipitated.

## Lung cancer xenograft
The mouse protocol was approved by Rutger’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), ID number TR202400021. H1299 cells were mixed with 50% matrigel in PBS and 2 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of NCr nude mice (Taconic Biosciences). Tumors were isolated and single cell suspensions were prepared. 1 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously into the mice with 50% matrigel. When visible tumor had developed after approximately 10 days, the mice were randomized to vehicle or JL189. JL189 was dissolved in 5% NMP, 5% Solutol HS-15 and 90% Citric Acid. JL189 (30 mg/kg twice daily) was injected intraperitoneally for 5 days a week for 3 weeks. Mice were euthanized when tumors exceeded 15 mm x 15 mm, pain, or loss of 20% body weight. The mice were euthanized, and tumors were immediately excised and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Tissue was cut into small pieces with a surgical blade then homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Samples were incubated on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and used for SDS-PAGE.

## Statistical analysis
A paired Student’s t test, assuming unequal variances, was used to compare the means of the control with the mean of each treated group. Differences with p values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

## Results

## BMPR2 inhibition increases energy state of NSLC cells
Our prior studies showed that BMP 2 ligand within 1 h significantly decreased the levels of TCA metabolites [ ]. Since the TCA cycle is essential for oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, we asked if BMPR2i improved mitochondrial bioenergetics. Bioenergetics is defined by energy transducing pathways in the mitochondria that maintain cellular phosphorylation [ ]. Therefore, we examined whether BMPR2i effected energy transducing pathways in the mitochondria. A549 cells with knockout 104 bp deletion of exon 11in the BMPR2 kinase domain (BMPR2 KO) were used to examine the effects of BMPR2i on mitochondrial bioenergetics. A549 BMPR2 KO cells have lower expression of its downstream transcriptional target ID1 compared to wildtype (WT) cells confirming a functional BMPR2 KO (Fig. A). Metabolomic studies demonstrated that A549 BMPR2 KO cells have higher levels of the TCA intermediates citrate and alpha ketoglutarate compared to WT cells (Fig. B). A549 BMPR2 KO cells also demonstrated an increase in ATP levels, ATP/ADP ratio and NAD/NADH ratio compared to WT cells (Fig. B). The Seahorse analyzer demonstrated that A549 BMPR2 KO cells have significantly higher basal respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production compared to WT cells (Fig. C). These data suggests that BMPR2i significantly increases mitochondrial bioenergetics.
 To examine the acute response to BMPR2i, we utilized the small molecule BMPR2 inhibitor JL189 [ ]. JL189 at 2.5 µM effectively suppresses BMPR2 signaling in cancer cell lines. In H1299 lung cancer cell line, metabolomic studies demonstrated significantly higher levels of the TCA cycle intermediates alpha ketoglutarate, isocitrate, fumarate, malate, and succinate levels in JL189 treated cells compared to control cells within 24 h (Fig. D). The ATP levels and the ATP/ADP ratio were also higher in H1299 cells treated with JL189 (Fig. D). JL189 treated cells had significantly higher NAD + and NADH levels but the NAD/NADH ratio was decreased. An increase in NADH levels can occur with disruption of the electron transport chain (ETC), cellular stress, or high energy demand. However, JL189 increased spare respiratory capacity suggesting an increase in mitochondrial respiration and an intact ETC (Fig. E). The higher NAD + and NADH levels suggest that the reduction of NAD + exceeded the rate of NAD + utilization [ ]. Unlike the metabolomic studies, mitochondria ATP levels calculated by the Seahorse analyzer were lower in JL189 treated H1299 cells (Fig. E). Seahorse Analyzer calculates ATP production which can be affected by assay conditions. Metabolomic analysis of ATP levels provide an accurate measurement of ATP levels but does not determine whether the increased ATP levels was produced by glycolysis or mitochondrial respiration. However, the increase in TCA cycle intermediates, ATP levels, and NAD + levels do indicate a higher energy state in H1299 cells following treatment with JL189.
In A549 cells, JL189 increased TCA cycle intermediates fumarate and malate (Fig.  D). In A549 cells, JL189 did not increase significantly ATP levels but the ATP/ADP ratio trended towards significance (Fig.  D). Like H1299 cells, the NAD/NADH ratio decreased with JL189 treatment (Fig.  D). Basal respiration was significantly higher in JL189 treated cells (Fig.  F). The mitochondrial ATP production calculated by the Seahorse Analyzer was higher compared to control, which trended towards statistical significance. These studies suggest that JL189 does improve the energy state of A549 cells, although not as robust as in H1299 cells. Although the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) is higher in BMPR2 KO cells and cells treated with JL189, this does not distinguish whether it is from glycolysis or from increase from mitochondrial respiration rate (OCR). The increased TCA intermediates, ATP/ADP ratio, and OCR suggest that the increased energy state is from the mitochondria.

## JL189 increases mitochondria ATP production
Since the Seahorse Analyzer only calculates mitochondrial ATP production from the drop in oxygen consumption following ATP synthase inhibition with oligomycin, we measured ATP levels in H1299 cells pretreated with oligomycin A. H1299 cells were treated with vehicle or 1.5 µM of 99.9% pure oligomycin for 30 min followed by vehicle or JL189 for 2 h. JL189 increased ATP levels by 10% compared to control (Fig. G). Oligomycin A decreased ATP levels by 7.9% (Fig. G), which is consistent with what has been previously reported for H1299 cells [ ]. Oligomycin A had no inhibitory effect on ATP levels in H1299 cells treated with JL189. This is surprising since oligomycin decreased ATP levels by 7.8% without JL189. This suggested that 1.5 µM of oligomycin A was not preventing ATP production from ATP synthase in the presence of JL189. Since mtCa2+ and oligomycin directly regulate F1F0 ATP synthase, the rapid increase in mtCa2+ following treatment with JL189 may competitively inhibit oligomycin A [ , ]. Therefore, we increased the dose of oligomycin A to 10 µM to better inhibit ATP synthase. 10 µM oligomycin A significantly inhibited the increase in ATP levels induced by JL189 (Fig. H). These data show that JL189 increases mitochondrial ATP production in H1299 cells. By 4 h, JL189 increased ATP levels in H1299 cells by 31% compared to control and by 3.3% in Calu-1 cells (Fig. I).

## JL189 increases TCA cycle intermediates in mouse hippocampal neurons
In the mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line HT-22 cells, JL189 decreased the levels of the glycolysis intermediates lactate and pyruvate while maintaining or increasing the levels of TCA cycle intermediates (Fig S1). The malate/pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate/pyruvate ratios increased, suggesting that BMPR2i with JL189 increased in the energy state of HT-22 cells (Fig S1).

## BMPR2i increases TFAM levels and mitochondrial mass
Mitochondrial Transcription Factor A (TFAM) has an essential role in mitochondrial bioenergetics by regulating mitochondrial DNA replication, transcription, and DNA stability [ , ]. By immunofluorescent imaging, JL189 increased the intensity of TFAM fluorescence in A549 and H1299 cells (Fig S2A-B). Cytochrome b, a component complex III of the electron transport chain (ETC), is transcribed by mitochondrial DNA [ ]. Cytochrome b together with cytochrome c transfers electrons to complex IV [ ]. Cytochrome b levels were higher in A549 BMPR2 KO cells compared to WT cells (Fig S2C). Furthermore, JL189 increased the expression of cytochrome b in H1299 cells (Fig S2D). The mitochondrial probe MitoTracker Green is frequently used to evaluate mitochondrial mass. MitoTracker Green fluorescence was significantly greater in H1299, Calu-1, and A549 cells treated with JL189 for 24 h compared to vehicle control (Fig S2E). MitoTracker Green fluorescence was also greater in A549 BMPR2 KO cells compared to WT cells (Fig S2F). Together, these data suggest that BMPR2i increases expression of mitochondrial proteins essential for mitochondrial bioenergetics and increases mitochondrial mass in NSCLC cells.

## BMPR2 inhibition increases mitochondrial calcium (mtCa2+) levels
The influx of calcium ion into the mitochondria is a conserved mechanism that increases mitochondrial metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, ATP production, TCA cycle intermediates, and the activation of TFAM. However, when mtCa2+ levels become too high it triggers cell death [ , ]. We used the mitochondrial Ca2+ indicator Rhod-2AM quantified by flow cytometry to determine if BMPR2i regulated mtCa2+ levels. JL189 significantly increased mtCa2+ levels in H1299, A549, and Calu-1 NSCLC cell lines (Fig. A-C). The increase in mtCa2+ levels induced by JL189 occurred in the presence or absence of Ca2+ in the cell culture media, indicating that the source of the rise in mtCa2+ levels was not extracellular (Fig. D-E). A549 BMPR2 KO cells also had significantly greater mtCa2+ levels compared to A549 WT cells (Fig. F). The cytosol Ca2+ probe Fluo-4AM did not show a difference in fluorescence between the A549 WT and BMPR2 KO cells (Fig. G).
 

## JL189 increases mitochondrial Rhod-2AM signal intensity analyzed by immunofluorescence imaging
Immunofluorescence imaging using a Nikon Eclipse TE300 inverted microscope of H1299 and Calu-1 demonstrated a significant increase in Rhod-2AM fluorescence following treatment with JL189 (Fig.  H). Confocal microscopy was used to examine the co-localization of the Rhod-2AM signal with the mitochondrial probe MitoTracker Green. Confocal microscopy showed a clear increase in Rhod-2AM fluorescence in discrete organelles without change in cytoplasmic staining following treatment with JL189 (Fig.  I). Grayscale images better localized the Rhod-2AM signal and signal intensity compared to colored images (Fig.  I). Co-localizing the Rhod-2AM and Mitotracker Green signals did not show the anticipated color change to yellow due to the high intensity signal of MitoTracker Green, which masked the lower intensity signal of Rhod-2AM (Fig.  J). Co-localization analysis of the greyscale images using the JACoP analytical software program in Image J demonstrated clear co-localization of the Rhod-2AM and MitoTracker Green signals. In the analysis of approximately 96 cells, the Mendels’ and Pearson’s coefficients analysis demonstrated 90% and 99% respectively of the Rhod-2AM signal co-localizing with MitoTracker Green (Fig.  J). This study is consistent with the many other published reports demonstrating that Rhod-2AM is a useful tool to determine mitochondria Ca2+ fluxes. Low background staining of the cytosol and some nucleoli was detected but appeared to be the same between controls and JL189 treated cells. We cannot exclude that this low background had some effect on the results of the flow cytometry data.
To control for whether mitochondrial mass was affecting mtCa++ levels, we normalized the Rhod-2AM signal to the MitoTracker Green signal from the confocal microscopy greyscale images. The normalized Rhod-2AM signal was significantly higher following treatment with JL189 (Fig.  K). We also normalized the Rhod-2AM signal to the MitoTracker Green signal in H1299 cells analyzed by flow cytometry. After 4 h of treatment with JL189 there was a significant increase in Rhod-2AM fluorescence intensity with no change in MitoTracker Green (Fig.  L). The normalized Rhod-2AM intensity was significantly higher in H1299 cells treated with JL189 (Fig.  L). These studies demonstrate that the rise in mtCa2+ levels following BMPR2i is not due to an increase in mitochondrial mass.

## JL189 increases Rhod-2AM signal prior to a rise in cytosol Ca2+ levels
In Calu-1 cells in media without Ca2+, JL189 induced a rise in mtCa2+ levels within 20 s with peak expression in approximately 60 s (Fig.  M). There were no changes in the cytosol Ca2+ levels over the first 3 min following treatment with JL189 (Fig.  N, P). Thapsigargin, an endoplasmic Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) inhibitor, was used as a positive control (Fig.  O, P). This study suggests that BMPR2i induces the transfer of Ca2+ into the mitochondria by a mechanism that is mediated by a mitochondrial calcium transporter.

## BMP inhibition in C. elegans increases mtCa2+levels and mitochondrial mass
Since BMP signaling is conserved in all metazoans [ , ], we asked if BMP signaling regulates mtCa2+ levels and mitochondrial mass in C. elegans. We utilized C. elegans harboring dbl-1 (BMP ligand) loss-of-function (lof) mutants and the red fluorescent mitochondrial calcium sensor LAR-GECO [ ] under the control of the myo-3 promoter [ ]. DBL-1 is the main BMP ligand in C. elegans [ – ]. DBL-1 ligands function like vertebrate BMP2/4 ligands signaling through BMP type I receptors (SMA-6) and BMP type II receptors (DAF-4), which activate Smad transcription factors [ – ]. Worms were also crossed to generate animals that expressed the green fluorescent protein (GFP) transgene under the control of the myo-3 promoter, which localizes to the mitochondria (MitoGFP) and nucleus. Therefore, both the LAR-GECO and MitoGFP reporters localize to muscle mitochondria. MitoGFP was used to determine mitochondrial mass, and the LAR-GECO fluorescence intensity was normalized to MitoGFP to determine mtCa2+ levels. Compared to WT, the animals harboring the dbl-1 lof transgene had significantly greater mtCa2+ levels (Fig. A-B) and greater mitochondrial mass (Fig. A, C).
 

## BMPR2i regulation of mtCa2+in leukemia and other cell types
Since BMPR2 regulation of mtCa2+ levels appeared to be conserved, we analyzed other cell types. JL189 caused a significant increase in mtCa2+ levels in leukemia cell lines, Jurkat (T-ALL), THP1 (AML), Kasumi-1 (AML), and in the triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell line MDA-MB-231 (Fig.  A-D). JL189 also increased mtCa2+ levels in the mouse hippocampal neuronal immortalized cell line HT-22 cells within 2 h (Fig.  E). The Rhod-2AM signal normalized to MitoTracker Green following treatment of THP1 cells with JL189 was significantly higher compared to control (Fig.  F). This indicates that the increase in mtCa2+ levels was not due to an increase in mitochondrial mass. Like NSCLC cell lines, JL189 increased cytochrome b levels in Jurkat cells (Fig.  G). JL189 increased Mitotracker Green fluorescence in THP1 and Kasumi-1 cells but not in Jurkat cells (Fig.  H-J).
 Comparing cell death following treatment with JL189, leukemia cell lines demonstrated more cell death compared to the NSCLC cell lines (Fig.  K). The cellular IC50 was also lower in the leukemia cell lines (Table S1). To determine if the rise in mtCa2+ levels induced by JL189 varied, leukemia and NSCLC cell lines were directly compared. JL189 2.5 µM for 2 h induced a significantly higher increase in mtCa2+ levels in THP1 cells compared to Calu-1 cells (Fig.  L). JL189 2.5 µM for 2 h induced a greater increase in mtCa2+ levels in Jurkat cells compared to H1299 cells (Fig.  M).
Cell death was not seen in HT-22 cells treated with JL189 for 48 h (Fig.  N). Although JL189 significantly increased mtCa2+ levels in HT-22 cells, its baseline mtCa2+ levels are much lower than most leukemia and NSCLC cell lines examined (Fig.  O), which may influence whether cell death mechanisms are induced. Comparing mitochondrial membrane potential, the H1299 cells demonstrated a 27% increase in TMRM fluorescence following treatment with JL189 while in the Jurkat cells JL189 decreased fluorescence by 5% (Fig.  P-Q). These studies suggest that in NSCLC cell lines and HT-22 cells, BMPR2i favored an increase in bioenergetics over cell death when compared to leukemia cells. To examine for any other kinases inhibited by JL189 that may affect mtCa2+ flux, a 97 kinase kinome profiling was performed. Although JL189 at 10 µM did demonstrate some binding to other kinases, none of the inhibited off-target kinases were expected to increase mtCa2+ levels (Table S2).

## TAK1 37 kd splice variant regulates BMPR2i increase in mtCa2+ levels
TAK1 binding to TAB1 triggers TAK1 activation with autophosphorylation of Thr184 and Thr187 in the TAK1 activation loop, which is required for activation [ , ]. Serine 412 is an additional phosphorylation site enhancing TAK1 activation [ ]. BMPR2 is known to signal through full-length TAK1 by enhancing the binding of TAB1 to TAK1 [ ]. Since TAK1 is reported to be strongly upregulated in AML [ ], we compared activated TAK1 levels between leukemia and NSCLC cell lines. Activated TAK1 levels were determined using antibodies that detect the phosphorylation of Thr184/187 autophosphorylation site or the phosphorylation of serine 412. Full-length TAK1 is 75–82 kd, but there are 3 other slice variants reported [ ]. Phosphorylation of TAK at Thr184/187 was detected only at 37 kd and its expression was higher in Jurkat and THP1 cells compared to the NSCLC cell lines H1299 and Calu-1 (Fig. A). Phosphorylation of TAK1 at Ser412 was only detected in the full-length protein (75 kd) and there was no difference in expression levels between cell lines (Fig. A). Forced expression of TAB1 cDNA increased the phosphorylation of TAK1 at Thr184/187 at 37 kd and Ser412 at 82 kd, confirming that the TAK1 antibodies detected activation of full-length TAK1 and a 37 kd TAK1 splice variant (Fig. B). JL189 decreased the phosphorylation of the TAK1 Thr184/187 splice variant but not the full-length TAK1 in Calu-1 and THP1cells (Fig. C-D).
 Knockdown of BMPR2 with siRNA in Calu-1 cells decreased the phosphorylation of Smad1/5 and the 37 kd TAK1 splice variant at Thr184/187 but not full-length TAK1 (75 kd) at Ser412 or Thr184/187 (Fig.  E). A549 BMPR2 KO cells had lower expression of pTAK1 at 37 kd compared to WT cells, indicating BMPR2 KO inhibited both BMPR2 canonical and noncanonical signaling pathways (Figs.  A and F). The BMP type 1 receptor inhibitors, JL5 and DMH1, decreased the phosphorylation of Smad1/5 (canonical signaling) but did not decrease the phosphorylation of the 37 kd TAK1 splice variant in Calu-1 and THP1 cells (Fig.  G-H). These studies demonstrate that TAK1 37 kd splice variant is regulated through BMPR2 noncanonical signaling.
TAK1 is known to have 4 splice variants, TAK1-b, TAK1-a, TAK1-c and TAK1-d [ ]. The four splice variants are determined from alternative splicing of exons 12 and 16 at the carboxyl end [ ]. To determine the expression of the different splice variants, we used an anti-TAK1 antibody recognizing the N-terminal end. Four splice variants were detected in NSCLC and leukemia cell lines although the level of expression varied (Fig. I). All of cell lines examined expressed a 37 kd TAK1 splice variant, which was higher in the leukemia cell lines compared to lung cancer cell lines (Fig. I). In lung cancer cells, the phosphorylated 37 kd TAK1 band was faint but detectable (Fig. I). TAK1-d is known to induce the phosphorylation of p-38 [ ]. JL189 decreased the expression of phosphorylated p-38 in THP1 cells (Fig. J), while TAB1 cDNA overexpression increased the phosphorylation of p-38 (Fig. K). Supporting that the 37 kd band is a TAK1 splice variant, it was phosphorylated by TAB1 overexpression (Fig. B) and was pulled down with TAB1-Flag immunoprecipitation (Fig. M. Together, these data support that the observed phosphorylated 37 kd protein is a TAK1 splice variant that is most consistent with the TAK1-d splice variant (NM_154333.3).
 

## TAK1 regulates mitochondrial Ca2+ levels
We hypothesized that TAK1 inhibition mediated BMPR2 regulation of mtCa2+ levels. To test this hypothesis, cells were treated with the TAK1 inhibitor 5Z-7-Oxozeaenol (5Z-7-OXO). In the THP1 cells, 2 µM of 5Z-7-OXO was sufficient to increase mtCa2+ levels (Fig.  L). The Calu-1 cells required 4 µM to increase mtCa2+ levels, suggesting that THP1 cells were more sensitive to TAK1 inhibition (Fig.  M-N). Knockdown of TAB1 with siRNA in THP1 cells decreased the phosphorylation of TAK1 splice variant at Thr184/187 and caused a small but significant increase in mtCa2+ levels (Fig.  O-P). Conversely, forced expression of TAB1 cDNA decreased mtCa2+ levels (Fig.  Q). These data suggest that BMPR2 regulates mtCa2+ levels through the TAK1-d splice variant independent of Smad1/5 signaling.

## Examining whether BMPR2i regulates the voltage-dependent anion channels (VDAC)
VDAC1 and the MCU complex are the main regulators of the transport of Ca2+ into the mitochondria. VDAC1 transports Ca2+ into the mitochondria intermembrane space through Ca2+ channels formed between the ER and lysosomes [ , ]. We expected if VDAC1 mediated mtCa2+ transport, the ER or lysosome Ca2+ stores would be depleted after treating cells with JL189 for 24 h in Ca2+ free cell culture medium. Thapsigargin was used to deplete the remaining ER Ca2+ stores following treatment of H1299 cells with JL189 for 24 h. ML-SA1, which activates the lysosome Ca2+ efflux receptor TRPML-1 [ ], was used to deplete the remaining lysosomal Ca2+ stores after cells were treated with JL189 for 24 h. JL189 did not deplete either the lysosome or ER Ca2+ stores but a small decrease in lysosome Ca2+ levels was observed (Fig S3A-B). Chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) HAP-1 WT and HAP-1 cells with a 14 bp deletion of VDAC1 (HAP-1 VDAC1 KO) in exon 6 were studied. VDAC1 KO attenuated thapsigargan induced increase in mtCa2+ levels indicating a functional KO of VDAC1 (Fig S4A). JL189 increased mtCa2+ levels in both HAP-1 WT and VDAC1 KO (Fig S4B). Changes in mitochondrial mass determined by MitoTracker Green fluorescence and cell survival following treatment with JL189 was also not different between HAP-1 WT and HAP-1 VDAC1 KO cells (Fig S4C-D).

## BMPR2i induced increase in mtCa2+ levels, cell death, and mitochondrial mass is mediated through the MCU
The mitochondria uniporter (MCU) regulates the rapid entry of Ca2+ from the mitochondria intermembrane space into the mitochondrial matrix [ ]. To test whether BMP signaling mediates Ca2+ uptake through the MCU, we utilized Jurkat and MDA-MB-231 cells with MCU KO via CRISPR-Cas9 [ , ]. Western blot analysis confirmed the KO of MCU (Fig. A). JL189 induced significantly higher mtCa2+ levels in Jurkat WT cells compared to Jurkat MCU KO cells (Fig. B). Similarly, JL189 induced higher mtCa2+ levels in MDA-MB-231 WT cells compared MDA-MB-231 MCU KO cells (Fig. C). JL189 induced cell death was significantly greater in Jurkat WT cells than in MCU KO cells after 24 and 48 h (Fig. D). JL189 also induced more cell death in the MDA-MB-231 WT cells than in the MCU KO cells (Fig. E). JL189 increased the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker Green significantly more in the MDA-MB-231 WT cells compared to MCU KO cells (Fig. F). These studies suggest that BMPR2i induced increase mtCa2+ uptake is not controlled by VDAC1 but dependent on the MCU or MCU complex.

## TAK1 regulates TFAM1 expression that is dependent on the MCU
Since TAK1-d mediates BMPR2i induced mtCa2+ transport, we examined if TAK1 also regulated the levels of mitochondrial proteins that enhance mitochondrial bioenergetics. Mitochondrial Ca2+ flux increases the levels of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) [ , ], which increases the level of proteins transcribed by mitochondrial DNA [ , ]. Mitochondrial transcribed proteins mt-ATP8 and cytochrome b are essential regulators of the mitochondrial electron transport chain [ , ]. Activating TAK1 by overexpression of TAB1 cDNA decreased the level of TFAM1 and cytochrome b in THP1 and H1299 cells (Fig. G, I). Inhibiting TAK1 with 5Z-7-OXO increased levels of TFAM1 and cytochrome b in THP1 and H1299 cells (Fig. H, J). Next, we examined if TAK1 regulation of TFAM1 was mediated through the MCU. Partial knockdown of the MCU in H1299 cells attenuated 5Z-7-OXO induced increase of TFAM1 (Fig. K).
To assess whether BMPR2 inhibition increases the level of mitochondrial proteins in lung tumors, H1299 xenografts were treated with control or JL189 for 3 weeks. The mitochondrial transcribed proteins mt-ATP8 and cytochrome b levels were higher in tumors of mice treated with JL189 compared to controls (Fig. L). Mt-ATP8 is a subunit of ATP synthase that has an essential role in oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. Cytochrome c, which regulates electron transport with cytochrome b in complex III, also had significantly higher levels in tumors treated with JL189.

## TAB1-Flag immunoprecipitated with 37 kd TAK1 variant and phosphorylated MCU
We next examined whether TAK1 directly associates with the MCU. Calu-1 cells were transfected with an insertless expression vector or TAB1-Flag plasmid. TSB1-Flag was immunoprecipitated and separated by gel electrophoresis. Whole cell lysate was also obtained from cells transfected but not immunoprecipitated. Immunoblots demonstrated that TAB1-Flag co-immunoprecipitated with phosphorylated 37 kd TAK1 (Fig. M). TAB1-Flag did not co-precipitate with either MCU (Fig. M) or its paralog MCUb. The MCU is phosphorylated at Ser92 in the N-terminal redox region, which promotes conformation change to the MCU [ ]. Interestingly, TAB1-Flag did co-precipitate with the MCU phosphorylated at Ser92 (Fig. M). This study shows that the activated 37 kd TAK1 splice variant binds to the MCU when it is phosphorylated at Ser92. These preliminary studies suggest that TAK1-d, which is a serine kinase, may directly regulate the MCU complex.

## BMPR2i regulation of mitochondrial ATP production in leukemia cells
We next examined the effects of JL189 on ATP production in leukemia cells. We first tested whether MCU KO effected ATP production induced by JL189. In Jurkat WT cells, JL189 increased ATP production by 3% in comparison to control after 4 h (Fig.  N). In Jurkat MCU KO cells, JL189 did not significantly increase in ATP production (Fig.  N). In Jurkat MCU KO cells, oligomycin A did not decrease ATP production, while in Jurkat WT cells it decreased ATP levels by 8.7% (Fig.  N). This indicates that MCU KO cells produce little ATP from the mitochondria due to low mtCa2+ levels. These data suggest that BMPR2i induced mitochondrial ATP production is mediated through Ca2+ influx through the MCU. In THP1 cells, JL189 increased ATP levels by 3% compared to control after 2 h (Fig.  O). Unlike H1299 cells, after 4 h of treatment with JL189, the ATP levels in THP1 cells decreased by 2% (Fig.  O), further demonstrating the differences between leukemia and lung cancer cells in their response to BMPR2i.

## Discussion
Although BMP signaling is one of the oldest conserved signaling pathways in metazoans, its role in regulating energy homeostasis has only recently been recognized [ ]. Our prior reports suggested that BMP signaling negatively affects energy homeostasis, which may have implications in cancer and other age-related diseases [ ]. In this study, we show that BMPR2i significantly increases mitochondrial energy state, as demonstrated by an increase in TCA cycle intermediates, oxidative phosphorylation, increased ATP/ADP ratio, increased NAD + levels, ATP production, mitochondrial protein expression, and mitochondrial mass. An improvement in mitochondrial energy state following BMPR2i was shown not only in cancer cells but also in normal mouse hippocampal neurons and in C. elegans, suggesting that the regulation of energy homeostasis by BMPR2 is conserved. BMP ligands are highly expressed in several age-related diseases including cancer [ , ], Alzheimer’s disease [ – ], obesity and metabolic syndrome [ ], and cardiovascular disease [ ]. Further investigation is needed to evaluate if aberrant BMP signaling negatively effects mitochondrial bioenergetics and mitochondrial health in other diseases.
Our studies reveal for the first time that BMPR2 signaling regulates the influx of Ca2+ into the mitochondria. Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of BMPR2 increased mtCa2+ levels in NSCLC, leukemia, breast cancer, and hippocampal cells, and in C. elegans, suggesting that BMPR2 regulation of mtCa2+ levels is conserved. The regulation of mtCa2+ levels is a highly conserved mechanism controlling mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell death. Increasing mtCa2+ levels activate three TCA cycle dehydrogenases, thereby indirectly increasing ATP production by increasing NADH [ ]. Elevated mtCa2+ levels directly increase ATP production by stimulating F1Fo-ATP synthase [ ]. mtCa2+ also increases ATP production by increasing TFAM1, which enhances mtDNA replication and transcription of mitochondrial transcribed proteins required for the ETC. Furthermore, mtCa2+ increases the entry of pyruvate and glutamate into the TCA cycle increasing TCA cycle intermediates leading to an increase in ATP production [ ]. This suggests that the increase in energy producing pathways induced by BMPR2i may be mediated by an increase in mtCa2+ levels. However, when mtCa2+ levels become too high, it triggers cell death by inducing the opening of the mitochondrial transition pore (mPTP), causing mitochondrial failure and the release of mitochondrial cell death factors [ – ]. Importantly, our studies provide evidence indicating that BMPR2 regulation of mtCa2+ can affect both cell survival and improve the cellular energy state, which may be dependent on the cell type. In the NSCLC cells, BMPR2i increased in ATP production without significant cell death, while in leukemia cells there was a higher level of cell death and a decline in ATP production over time. These studies raise the question whether aberrant BMPR2 signaling dysregulates mitochondrial Ca2+ influx, which negatively effects mitochondrial health in other diseases.
Full length TAK1-TAB1was originally described to regulate BMPR2 mediated ventralization of xenopus embryos [ , , ]. We report here that in cancer cells and mouse hippocampal cells, BMPR2 activates a 37 kd TAK1 splice variant, which regulates Ca2+ transport across the MCU. Our studies are most consistent with the phosphorylated 37 kd band regulated by BMPR2 being the TAK1-d splice variant. TAB1 is required for TAK1 activation. TAB1 overexpression increased the phosphorylation of the 37 kd band recognized by our anti-pTAK1 antibody and TAB1 siRNA decreased it. A second antibody recognizing the N-terminal end of TAK1 also detected a 37 kd band. In addition, the phosphorylated 37 kd band was immunoprecipitated with TAB1-Flag. Further supporting that the phosphorylated 37 kd TAK1 variant is TAK1-d, is the demonstration that JL189 decreases the phosphorylation of p-38. It is not known why BMPR2 would preferentially regulate one TAK1 splice variant over another. BMPR2 has been shown to activate TAK1 by stabilizing the binding of XIAP to TAB1 [ ]. TAB1 binds the N-terminal end of TAK1 while TAB2 and TAB3 bind the C-terminal end. TAK1-d lacks the C-terminal binding sites for TAB2/TAB3 [ ]. TAB2/TAB3 do not directly activate TAK1 but serves as adaptor for TRAF6 that is required for TAK1 activation by IL-1 [ ]. Whether TAB2/TAB3 binding to TAK1 inhibits its interaction with BMPR2 is not known.
The MCU complex is composed of the MCU, MCUb, mitochondrial calcium uptake 1–3 (MICU1-3), and EMRE [ ]. The MCU regulates the rapid entry of Ca2+ into the mitochondria matrix, which is inhibited by MCUb and MICU [ ]. There is limited understanding of the mechanisms controlling the MCU complex’s precise control of Ca2+ transport. Interestingly, we show that activated TAK1-d binds the MCU phosphorylated at Ser92 but not the non-phosphorylated MCU or MCUb. The N-terminal cysteine 97 in MCU regulates redox sensing [ ]. Phosphorylation of Ser92 in the MCU is reported to increase the transport of Ca2+ across the MCU [ ]. MCUb, a paralog of MCU, acts as a dominant-negative regulator of the MCU significantly decreasing Ca2+ influx into the mitochondria matrix [ ]. The N-terminal redox sensing region of the MCU is conserved in MCUb, containing both Ser92 and cysteine 97 [ ]. Therefore, the phosphorylated Ser92 could be either MCU or MCUb or both. Since TAK1 is a serine kinase, it is of significant interest to understand whether TAK1-d phosphorylates MCU and/or MCUb. Future studies will also examine how the phosphorylation of Ser92 at MCU and/or MCUb regulates mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, whether this is cell type specific, and if this signaling pathway is altered in diseases because of aberrant BMP expression.

## Conclusion
Our studies show for the first time that inhibiting BMPR2 signaling downregulates the TAK1-d splice variant leading to an increase in Ca2+ transfer through the MCU complex regulating signaling events that regulate mitochondrial bioenergetics and/or cell survival. These studies suggest that BMPR2 overactivity causes mitochondrial dysfunction in some cell types by inhibiting the transport of Ca2+ into the mitochondria thereby decreasing mitochondrial function. However, in diseases such as leukemia, BMPR2 signaling may enhance cell survival by preventing Ca2+ overload. These studies reveal a novel mechanism by which BMPR2 signaling regulates mtCa2+ levels to control mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell survival, which may have implications in cancer and other diseases.
