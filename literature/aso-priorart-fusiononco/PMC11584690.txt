## Highlights
•Artemis protein appears to play an important role in cancer.
•It is involved in the non-homologous end joining repair and V(D)J recombination.
•Loss of its function causes cellular radiosensitivity.
•Its deregulation correlates with poor outcomes and treatment resistance in cancer.
•It can be targetable for cancer therapies by non-oncology and novel drugs.

Artemis is a key nuclease involved in the non-homologous end joining repair pathway upon DNA double-stranded breaks and during V(D)J recombination. It participates in various cellular processes and cooperates with various proteins involved in tumorigenesis. Its hereditary mutations lead to several pathological conditions, such as severe combined immunodeficiency with radiation sensitivity. Recent studies suggest that Artemis deregulation plays an important role in cancer and is associated with poorer oncologic outcomes and resistance to treatment including radiotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapeutics. Artemis emerges as an attractive candidate for cancer prognosis and treatment. Its role in modulating sensitivity to ionizing radiation and DNA-damaging agents makes it an appealing target for drug development. Various existing drugs and novel compounds have been described to inhibit Artemis activity. This review synthesizes the up-to-date information regarding Artemis function, its role in different malignancies and its clinical utility as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in Oncology.

## Introduction
Cancer, despite significant advances in its prevention and treatment, remains one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide. Each year, nearly 20 million new cancer cases are diagnosed, and approximately 10 million deaths are attributed to it [ ]. These statistics underscore the urgent need for improvements in cancer prevention and treatment. The management of cancer patients is increasingly shifting towards precision medicine approaches, such as gene-directed, individualized, biomarker-based diagnostics and treatment [ ]. Such approaches are transforming cancer management by tailoring interventions based on specific genetic and molecular profiles of cancer. Hence, identification of novel oncology molecular targets is of paramount importance in modern basic and translational cancer research. One such emerging molecular target is the Artemis protein, a critical endonuclease involved in the repair of DNA double-stranded breaks (DSBs) through the non-homologous DNA end joining (NHEJ) pathway [ , ] or the somatic V(D)J recombination mechanism, essential for the development of immune diversity [ , ]. DSBs can be triggered by various causes, such as DNA replication errors, ionizing radiation, reactive oxygen species, or be a part of physiologic somatic V(D)J recombination. Since DSBs can cause the loss of large chromosomal regions, they can lead to a variety of pathologic conditions, including cancer.
Repair of DSBs in mammalian cells can occur through different mechanisms, with one of the predominant ones being the NHEJ pathway. While various NHEJ subpathways exist depending on the type of DSB damage, the main mechanism involves recognition of the DSB by the Ku70–Ku80 heterodimer, which serves as a loading platform for the recruitment of other NHEJ proteins, namely the DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) to form the DNA-PK complex (reviewed in [ ]) ( ). When DNA resection is required, DNA-PKcs is recruited in complex with Artemis nuclease that trims the damaged DNA ends for their subsequent repair. DNA-PKcs relieves autoinhibition of Artemis’ endonuclease activity [ , ], enabling it to remove 5′ and 3′ DNA overhangs and to subsequently allow the X-ray repair cross-complementing protein 4 (XRCC4)-DNA Ligase IV complex to ligate DNA. 
DSBs can also be induced during physiologic V(D)J recombination, a process critical for immune cell maturation. During this process, recombination activating genes (RAG1 and RAG2) cleave the DNA at recombination signal sequences found between the V and J segments generating hairpin ends. The Ku70/80 heterodimer recognizes these DNA ends and facilitates the recruitment of DNA-PKcs and Artemis to the hairpins. Through its endonucleolytic activity, Artemis processes the DNA overhangs, after which the ligase complex joins the V and J segments together to form a VJ coding joint [ , , ] ( ). Through this mechanism, Artemis plays a vital role in the development of immune cells such as B- and T- lymphocytes, evidenced in that functional mutations in the Artemis gene are known to cause severe combined immunodeficiency with sensitivity to radiation (RS-SCID) and other hereditary immunodeficiency disorders [ , ].
While Artemis is relatively well-characterized for its roles in the NHEJ pathway, V(D)J recombination and RS-SCID, growing evidence suggests that it also plays an important role in other cellular processes and cancer. This review discusses the potential roles of Artemis in the development, progression and treatment responses in cancers, highlighting its emerging significance as a precision oncology target beyond its well-known functions in NHEJ repair and immune cell development.

## Artemis protein and its functions

## Artemis structure and functions in DNA repair
Artemis is encoded by the DCLRE1C (DNA cross-link repair 1C) gene, located on chromosome 10p13. It is also referred to as SNM1C (sensitivity to nitrogen mustard) or PSO2 (sensitivity to psoralen) due to its original identification as part of the small SNM gene family using genetic screens in yeast [ , , ]. Subsequent studies for mammalian homologs identified DCLRE1C and genetically characterized it as defective in human RS-SCID [ ].
Human Artemis is a 692 amino-acid-long protein that is a member of the metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) family of nucleases [ ], containing conserved MBL, β-CASP and C-terminal domains ( ). The core MBL fold with an inserted β-CASP domain is contained within the N-terminus that confers nucleic acid binding and the nuclease catalytic activity required for the DNA break repair and V(D)J recombination pathways [ , , , , , ]. The structural analysis shows that, unlike other SNM protein family members, a unique zinc (Zn) binding motif is present in the β-CASP domain, leading to structural reorientation of the putative DNA-binding surface and revealing distinct catalytic features of Artemis in NHEJ [ , ]. Catalytic and regulatory domains of Artemis interact with the N-HEAT and FAT domains of DNA-PKcs [ ]. This interaction results in the phosphorylation of Artemis and enhances its single-stranded and double-stranded DNA endonuclease activity [ , ]. In addition to its endonuclease activity, it also exhibits exonuclease activity, thereby expanding its role to a wider range of nucleic substrates [ ].
The C-terminal region of Artemis (aa 362–692) mediates protein-protein interactions, contains post-translational modification sites and directs subcellular localization [ , , , ] ( ). At aa 454–458 it interacts with its own N-terminal catalytic domain (aa 1–7) to inhibit the endonuclease activity [ , ]. Regions within the C-terminal domain interact with DNA Ligase IV and the FAT domain of DNA-PKs [ , , ]. The C-terminal domain also contains sites that act as a substrate for other phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks), such as ATM (Ataxia telangiectasia mutated) [ , , ] and ATR (Ataxia telangiectasia and RAD3-related) [ ], which phosphorylate Artemis in response to ionizing or UV radiation and regulate Artemis-dependent processing of radiation-induced DSBs [ , , ].
Besides its function in NHEJ repair and V(D)J recombination, Artemis also plays a role in antagonizing homologous recombination (HR) repair ( ), acting as a PTIP-binding partner downstream of the p53 binding protein 1 (53BP1) pathway that participates in the prevention of DNA end resection [ ]. In response to DSBs, PTIP is recruited by 53BP1 to protect the damaged DNA ends from resection to antagonize HR repair. Artemis, which interacts with PTIP ( ), promotes repair of damaged DNA ends by NHEJ. 

## Other functions in cellular physiology
Aside from its roles in DNA damage repair, Artemis also plays a role in various cellular processes. It, through several mechanisms ( ), participates in the regulation of cell cycle at various checkpoints. In response to radiation-induced DNA damage and via regulation by ATM and ATR, Artemis takes part in the maintenance of normal DNA-damage-induced G2/M cell cycle arrest [ , ]. It acts as a negative regulator of recovery from the G2/M checkpoint. Upon DNA damage induced by ionizing radiation, ATM phosphorylation of Artemis (at Ser516 and Ser645) inhibits its negative regulation of Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (Cdk1)-cyclin B activation creating a molecular switch to allow normal checkpoint recovery [ ]. The same phospho-sites are phosphorylated by ATR in response to UV radiation, causing prolonged S phase checkpoint recovery [ ]. This effect is mediated through Artemis's enhanced interaction with the F-box protein Fbw7, which in turn promotes ubiquitylation and the ultimate degradation of cyclin E [ ]. Artemis might also have a role in the regulation of G1 phase through p53 and p27 tumor suppressor pathways [ , ]. Its depletion causes phosphorylation and stabilization of p53 with resulting G1 arrest and apoptosis [ ]. It has been proposed that oxidative stress activates DNA-PKcs to phosphorylate and stabilize p53 and that this pathway is negatively regulated by Artemis [ ]. Artemis also interacts with the Cul4A-DDB1DDB2 ubiquitin E3 ligase, thus regulating the degradation of a Cyclin D kinase (CDK) inhibitor and a tumor suppressor, p27, subsequently affecting G1 to S or G0 to S phase cell cycle progression in normally proliferating and serum-deprived mammalian cells [ ]. These findings also define the role of Artemis in protein ubiquitylation [ , ]. Altogether, Artemis plays a key role in guarding chromosomal stability in physiology and following exposure to radiation and oxidative stress at different stages of the cell cycle [ , , , , ] ( ).
Artemis participates in control of cellular energy homeostasis through its strong interaction with the energy sensor AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [ , ]. It promotes activation of AMPK [ ] and stabilizes the complex of AMPK with its upstream kinase (AMPKK) and tumor suppressor liver kinase B1 (LKB1). Artemis co-immunoprecipitates and co-localizes with the AMPK α-subunit and its overexpression enhances the phosphorylation of AMPK, markedly increasing the physical association between AMPKα2 and LKB1 [ ]. AMPK-Artemis interaction also appears to play a role in NHEJ repair, however, how this mechanism takes place is unclear [ ].
Aside from the aforementioned roles, Artemis has also been shown to participate in telomere maintenance [ , ] and has been suggested to facilitate site-specific chromosome breakage during apoptosis by enhancing MLL (mixed-lineage leukemia) gene cleavage at a breakage cluster breakpoint that is frequently translocated in leukemias [ ]. Based on mass spectrometry and coimmunoprecipitation experiments, Artemis might interact with other proteins such as RAD50, MRE11 and NBS1 (components of the MRN complex), p53, DDB1, DDX20, PPP6R1, ANKRD28, BRCA1, TopBP1, ERCC6 and XRCC5 [ , , ], suggesting yet unknown additional roles of this protein in cell physiology and disease.

## Artemis role in response to radiation and DNA-damaging agents
The role of Artemis in radiation response has been well established since its initial characterization in RS-SCID, where cells isolated from patients exhibited increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation. Increased sensitivity to radiation [ , , , , , ], radiomimetic [ , , , , ], oxidating [ ] and other chemotherapeutic agents [ , ] in the Artemis deficient or mutant background is observed in various human and mouse normal or cancer cell models. Artemis is essential for the repair of DSBs, however it is required for the repair of only a part of DSBs induced by ionizing radiation [ , , , ]. In Artemis-deficient mammalian cells, the relative repair of complex DSBs is reduced by nearly 2-fold compared with Artemis-complemented cells [ ]. In Artemis-deficient patient-derived fibroblasts, endogenous expression of mutant Artemis with defective endonucleolytic activity results in poor DNA DSB repair, continued sensitivity to γ-ray radiation, and DNA-damaging agents, which are restored in the presence of wild-type Artemis [ ]. This suggests that the endonucleolytic activity of Artemis is essential for the repair of radiation or chemically induced DNA DSBs.
Increased expression of Artemis has been observed to result in either radiation resistance [ ] or enhanced sensitivity [ ]. The mechanism of the latter is unclear but could be related to the fact that overexpression of Artemis can act in a dominant-negative manner. Others report that Artemis overexpression in human HEK293 embryonic kidney cells, confers marked radioresistance against high and low linear energy transfer radiation, which is mainly dependent on DNA-PKcs activity [ ]. Potentially, depending on the level of Artemis overexpression and/or genetic background of the cell lines, it might lead to either increased or decreased cellular sensitivity to radiation.

## Artemis in hereditary disease
A genome-wide search using linkage analysis in Athabascan-speaking Navajo and Apache Native American populations revealed a defective gene located on chromosome 10p, that was found to be associated with defective V(D)J recombination [ , ]. Subsequent studies led to the identification and characterization of Artemis gene defects, which were linked to RS-SCID [ ]. SCIDs include heterogeneous groups of inherited disorders characterized by severe defects in the immune system and lead to early death from overwhelming infections. Autosomal recessive loss-of-function mutations of DCLREC1/Artemis cause defective T- and B-cell development combined with increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation [ , ]. Hypomorphic, or partial loss-of-function, mutations in the Artemis gene can lead to a functional, but reduced, T-cell and B-cell repertoire with a more indolent clinical course called "leaky" SCID and immunodeficiency syndromes of varying severity [ , , ]. Several studies have identified a range of Artemis’ pathogenic homo- or heterozygous mutations in various populations [ , , , ], which have been associated with phenotypes ranging from atypical severe combined immunodeficiencies [ , , ], Omenn syndrome [ ] and inflammatory bowel disease [ ].

## Artemis in neoplastic disease
Changes in Artemis expression, including both downregulation and upregulation, have been reported in various cancer types ( ). In animal models with an altered p53 background, Artemis appears to act as a tumour suppressor. In certain cancer types, such as sarcomas, pro-B and thymic lymphomas, Artemis likely cooperates with p53 to suppress tumor development [ , , , , ] through maintaining genomic integrity and preventing genomic alterations, such as mutations, chromosomal rearrangements and genomic instability. Conversely, Artemis overexpression has been observed in various cancers [ , , , , , ] and has been correlated with poorer oncologic and/or treatment response outcomes in pre-B acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [ ], cervical and rectal cancers [ , ]. These observations suggest that, under certain conditions, Artemis might exhibit pro-oncogenic properties. While Artemis overexpression may directly drive oncogenesis in some cancers, it is also possible that its overexpression represents a compensatory mechanism to enhance DNA repair in cancer cells with high genomic instability. This hypothesis is supported by the data correlating Artemis overexpression with poorer responses to chemoradiotherapy [ , ], suggesting that cancer cells with increased Artemis levels may repair treatment-induced DNA damage more effectively, leading to treatment resistance. Thus, Artemis could be viewed as a protein with dual roles: as a tumour suppressor or as a pro-oncogenic driver, depending on the genetic background of the cancer cells and their ability to tolerate or repair DNA damage. These findings, alongside the roles of Artemis in a multitude of critical physiological processes, underscore the potential complexity of Artemis involvement in cancer biology and highlight the importance of its further studies in cancer. The various roles of Artemis in malignant conditions and clinical implications of its deregulation are discussed below and presented in . 

## Leukemias
Artemis dysregulation has been reported in non-solid hematological human malignancies [ , , , ]. Its expression has been shown to be reduced in BCR-ABL–positive chronic myeloid leukemia cell lines [ ] and upregulated in various myeloma cell lines [ ] and pre-B ALL [ ]. Gene expression analyses from children with high-risk pre-B ALL showed that high Artemis expression correlates with poorer overall and relapse-free survival compared to those with low Artemis expression [ ], suggesting that Artemis can be utilized as a biomarker and a target for cancer treatment in this disease.

## Lymphomas
Artemis deregulation in p53 deficient mice accelerates the timing of tumor onset compared with p53 mutation alone [ ]. In a p53 null background, Artemis double knockout mice predominantly develop pro-B lymphomas [ , ], while mice with Artemis’ heterozygous deletions [ ] or partial loss-of-function mutations lead to thymic lymphomas [ ]. Conditional knockout of p53 in the thymus in Artemis-null mice is also associated with the development of thymic lymphomas [ ]. In tumorigenesis, Artemis cooperates with p53 to suppress chromosomal translocations and tumor development and as such acts as a tumor suppressor [ , , ]. A model has been proposed by which, B-lineage cells that are deficient for both Artemis and p53 undergo complex chromosomal rearrangements with Igh and c-myc or n-myc co-amplification, while Artemis null and p53-heterozygous B-linage cells acquire a premalignant state before p53 loss-of-heterozygosity with subsequent oncogenic changes distinct from Igh/myc chromosomal rearrangements [ , ]. In humans, somatic Artemis mutations have been identified in diffuse large B cell lymphomas [ ] and hereditary hypomorphic Artemis mutations have been reported to contribute to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated B-cell lymphomas [ , , ]. These studies suggest a role of Artemis in pathogenesis of lymphomas.

## Sarcomas
Sarcomas are characterized by a high level of genomic instability and can be induced by ionizing radiation. In radiation-induced sarcomas devoid of both active p53 and Rb1, loss of heterogeneity was observed in the Artemis gene without associated changes in the mRNA expression [ ]. These findings led authors to conclude that Artemis and other NHEJ pathway proteins are not involved in sarcomas with high genomic instability induced by radiation [ ].
On the other hand, studies from mouse models suggest that Artemis, in cooperation with p53, acts as a tumor suppressor, including in relation to sarcomagenesis [ , ]. Artemis hetero or homozygous knockout mice with an altered p53 genetic background develop various sarcomas [ , ]. In a large cohort of Artemis knockout mice with a p53 heterozygous background 6.1% developed tumors of any kind with 30.4% of these developing various sarcoma subtypes, with a similar incidence but altered spectrum and shorter latency than observed in p53 heterozygous- only animals [ , ]. Artemis deficiency leads to a preneoplastic state in the primary multipotent mesenchymal stem/progenitor cells (MSCs) that are considered a putative source of sarcomagenic cells [ ]. Its deficiency leads to chromosomal damage and enhanced resistance and proliferative potential in primary MSC under various stress conditions [ ]. Overall, in heterozygous p53 background, Artemis deficiency appears to accelerate tumorigenesis, suggesting that in specific genetic backgrounds it may play the role of a tumour suppressor [ ].

## Carcinomas
There is growing evidence regarding the potential role of Artemis in various malignancies of the epithelial origin ( ). Data from experimental carcinoma models with genetic manipulations of Artemis suggests that it has broad roles in cancer pathogenesis and treatment responses. Artemis downregulation correlates with antitumorigenic effects, including a decrease in cell viability and proliferation [ , ]. Some colorectal cancer cells lines with Artemis genetic downregulation exhibit slower growth and proliferation while others do not [ , ]. These discrepancies might be, at least in part, related to the differences in the levels of depletion of Artemis and the genetic background of the studied cell lines. Changes in Artemis expression have also been linked to changes in cellular apoptosis. In cervical cancer cell lines, siRNA downregulation of Artemis increases apoptosis following exposure to an oxidizing agent [ ]. Artemis might play a role in bladder cancer cell invasiveness via E2F1 transcription factor, which has putative binding motifs on the DCLRE1C promoter [ ]. Similar to non-cancerous cell lines, Artemis inhibition, downregulation or mutations have been reported to increase sensitivity to radiation and radiomimetic drugs in cancer cell models [ , , , , ]. Artemis downregulation in cervical and ovarian cancer models with deregulation of BRCA 1 or 2 respectively, renders cells resistant to PARP inhibitors [ , , ]. These studies provide further experimental evidence that Artemis plays important roles in cancer and responses to treatment.
DCLRE1C/Artemis mutations and deregulation of its expression are reported in patients with various cancer types ( ). Germline genetic mutations in DCLRE1C/Artemis have been reported in cancer patients. In a large cohort of the Latin American population with increased risk for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndromes (HBOC), germline genetic pathogenic variants were found in HBOC-unrelated genes, including Artemis [ ]. An allelic variant of Artemis has been identified in breast cancer patients from BRCA1 and BRCA2-negative breast cancer families with elevated cancer risk [ ]. Hereditary deletion in the Artemis allele has been reported in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 familial and early-onset breast cancer cases [ , ], suggesting an alternative source of genetic variation influencing hereditary risk for breast cancer.
Changes in Artemis mRNA or protein expression have been observed in several cancer types. Some of these changes appear to correlate with clinical outcomes, opening avenues for investigation of Artemis as an oncology biomarker. Overexpression of DCLRE1C/Artemis has been reported in various cancers, such as cervical, hepatobiliary, colorectal and breast cancers [ , , , , , ] ( ). Downregulation of DCLRE1C/Artemis has been observed in patients with prostate [ ], non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ] and in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) as compared to normal tissue samples.
Upregulation of Artemis might correlate with improved oncological outcomes in some cancers [ ] and worse responses to treatment in others [ , ]. These differences can be interpreted in the light of different genetic backgrounds of different cancer types. High levels of Artemis protein and DCLRE1C mRNA in high E2F1-expressing invasive bladder cancer cell lines have been observed [ ]. Moreover, high DCLRE1C mRNA levels in bladder cancer patients with an elevated expression of E2F1 were associated with better overall survival compared to those with low Artemis DCLRE1C mRNA [ ]. On the contrary, overexpression of Artemis has been reported to be correlated with worse oncologic or treatment outcomes in cervical and rectal cancers [ , ]. Patients with cervical cancer with elevated Artemis expression, receiving radical chemoradiotherapy had a significantly worse overall survival compared to those with low Artemis expression, implying that, in agreement with experimental studies, elevated Artemis expression is associated with resistance to radiotherapy [ ]. High Artemis expression has been found to be associated with poor treatment response without significant effect on the overall survival in rectal cancer patients [ ]. Histochemical analysis observed higher expression of Artemis relative to adjacent normal tissue in human rectal cancer samples [ ]. Variable levels (low to high) of Artemis expression have been seen in pretreatment biopsy specimens from the treatment-naive locally advanced rectal cancer patients who underwent preoperative chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery [ ]. The tumor response to chemoradiotherapy was found to be poorer in patients with high versus low Artemis expression pre-treatment. The 5-year overall survival showed no significant differences between the low and high Artemis expression groups [ ].
Artemis has been studied as a potential biomarker for prognosis and treatment responses in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [ ]. DCLRE1C deletions and downregulation in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) as compared to matched normal tissue samples have been reported [ ]. Multiple-genes-based signatures, that included DCLRE1C, have been utilized to develop models for stratification of HNSCC patients into high and low risk groups. The low-risk group showed a significantly higher overall survival and sensitivity to various chemotherapeutic drugs [ ]. These results emphasize the potential for using Artemis as a prognostic and treatment response biomarker in various cancers and as a drug target to modulate responses to chemo and radiotherapy [ , , ].

## Therapeutic strategies for targeting artemis
Artemis represents an attractive therapeutic target. Therapeutic agents might target the Artemis N-terminal catalytic domain due to its unique structural differences with other members of the SNM1 family [ , ]. Since the metal ions in the active site of Artemis are critical for its nuclease activity, compounds designed to coordinate with a divalent metal ion may provide an effective approach to drug development [ ]. Crystal structures of Artemis reveal that it contains an additional metal binding site in its β-CASP domain unique to this protein that leads to structural reorientation of the putative DNA-binding surface and extends the substrate-binding pocket to a new pocket [ , ]. The substrate-binding surface exhibits a dominant and extensive positive charge distribution compared with that in the structures of other SNM family members. These structural features specific to Artemis provide opportunities for designing selective Artemis inhibitors [ ].
The possibility of inhibiting Artemis through binding to the Zn finger motif in the β-CASP domain has been explored using fluorescence-based nuclease assays [ ] with compounds known to result in Zn ion displacement through reacting with Zn fingers’ thiol groups [ ]. Interestingly, the addition of excess Zn2+ has been shown to inhibit Artemis endonuclease, as well as its 5′-exonuclease activity [ ], possibly through the excess Zn-monohydroxide binding to the active form of the Zn-dependent enzyme, thereby preventing the catalytic site from binding active Zn [ ]. Compounds that are known to result in Zn ion displacement through reacting with Zn fingers’ thiol groups, such as ebselen, disulfiram and auranofin ( ) have been tested vis-à-vis inhibition of the catalytic activity of Artemis. Ebselen and disulfiram inhibited Artemis nuclease activity with IC50 values around 8.5 μM and 10.8 μM respectively, while auranofin showed less potent inhibition (IC50 46 μM) [ ]. Importantly, these drugs, that are historically used for treatment of non-cancerous pathologies, have been studied as anticancer agents and shown effectiveness in various cancer types ( ), creating an opportunity for their repurposing for Oncology. 
Artemis's MBL-fold domain is similar to that of bacterial MBLs that can be inhibited by β-lactam antibiotics that are used for treatment of microbial infections. It has been shown that the third generation cephalosporine, ceftriaxone, a widely used β-lactam antibiotic, competitively inhibits other SNM family member proteins [ ]. In a nuclease assay, ceftriaxone has been shown to modestly inhibit Artemis catalytic activity with an IC50 of 65 μM, while cefotaxime and 7-aminocephalosporanic acid did not [ ]. Another β-lactam antibiotic, ampicillin, has been found through a small drug screen to be able to inhibit both endonuclease and 5′-exonuclease activities of Artemis in parallel [ ]. Availability of structural data regarding ceftriaxone bound to Artemis could aid in the development of selective Artemis inhibitors [ ], either binding to the active site or, for example, the unique Zn finger domain of the protein. Therefore, the aforementioned drugs are attractive candidates for drug repurposing for cancer treatment [ , , , , ] and, given their purported properties to inhibit Artemis, they might also be used for radiosensitization in cancer.
Efforts to develop novel compounds that can lead to Artemis inhibition have been made in recent years [ , ]. A high throughput compound screen analysis has identified various structurally distinct small molecules, of which, SBI-713 dramatically inhibited both the endonuclease and the 5′-exonuclease activities of Artemis [ ]. Four compounds (827171, 827032, 826941, and 825226), generated from a large screen evaluating inhibition of the Artemis catalytic domain and nuclease activity, have been tested as an Artemis inhibition strategy in ALL [ ]. A biochemical assay using a purified Artemis in complex with DNA-PKcs showed that 827171, 827032, 826941 and 825226 have nanomolar IC50 values for its inhibition. Among the four, the 827171 compound has the smallest effects on B-ALL or mature B cell viability, but substantially reduced B-ALL proliferation without markedly affecting normal B cell lines. This data provides a basis for further evaluation of pharmacological Artemis inhibition of proliferation of B- and T-ALL [ ] ( ).
Targeting the regulatory C-terminal domain of Artemis might also represent a viable strategy for its inhibition via disrupting its interaction with other binding partners such as DNA Ligase IV and DNA-PKcs. A peptide BAL, consisting of the interaction residues between Artemis and DNA Ligase IV, has been synthesized to block their interaction and to test its utility as a radiosensitizer [ ]. BAL significantly enhanced the radiosensitivity of gallbladder carcinoma and cervical cancer cell lines, prolonging DNA repair after irradiation and demonstrating synergistic anticancer effects with radiation on cell proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis [ ]. In addition, BAL increased radiosensitivity of gallbladder cancer xenografts, compared to controls [ ]. Thus, disrupting Artemis and DNA Ligase IV interactions represents a promising strategy for anticancer drug development. Cryo-electron microscopy structures of Artemis and DNA-PKcs bound to the DNA showed mutually exclusive binding sites for Artemis and XRCC4 on DNA-PKcs and that the XRCC4 peptide ( ) competes with Artemis and disrupts the Artemis complex formation with DNA-PKcs [ ]. These studies provide evidence that the disruption of the Artemis interaction with its key partners, thus preventing its normal function in DNA repair, can be a useful strategy for drug development [ , ].
Finally, strategies to enhance Artemis catalytic function can be developed based on targeting the autoinhibitory regions in the C-terminal domain. DNA-PKcs are required for the relief of Artemis autoinhibition of its endonuclease activity [ ] and truncation of the C-terminus of Artemis permits it to be active in the absence of DNA-PKcs. A systematic series of C-terminus deletions was assessed to identify the minimal extent of these deletions needed for Artemis function in a DNA-PKcs-independent manner [ ]. When aa 446 to 692 are truncated, the autoinhibition of endonuclease activity is reduced or eliminated, making the enzyme constitutively active in the absence of DNA-PKcs. This vulnerability could be further explored to modulate Artemis activity for therapeutic purposes.

## Conclusions and perspectives
Despite advances in cancer therapies, the development of treatment resistance still poses a major challenge to attaining desired clinical outcomes [ ]. Deregulations in the NHEJ pathway can lead to tumorigenesis and resistance to therapy [ , ]. In this review we highlight the importance of Artemis, a key component of the NHEJ pathway, in regulating various cellular processes and in cancer biology and treatment. It cooperates with various proteins which are key players in cancer pathogenesis. Its downregulation leads to development of sensitivity to radiation and radiomimetic drugs, suggesting that Artemis can be used for increasing cancer sensitivity to treatments. While in some cancers Artemis elicits properties of a tumour suppressor, in others it is found to be upregulated, raising a question of its role as a pro-oncogenic driver. Upregulated levels of expression of Artemis observed in a variety of cancers might also represent an adaptive response to upregulate DNA repair in reaction to increased DNA damage observed in cancer cells and can be explored as a therapeutic vulnerability. Artemis deregulation correlates with poor oncologic outcomes and resistance to therapy in patients with various cancer types, providing an opportunity for investigating it as a prognostic and treatment-response biomarker. Overexpression of Artemis and its correlation with poor oncologic outcomes observed in some cancers points to the possibility that inhibition of Artemis can be used as a stand-alone treatment modality or be added to the existing radio or chemotherapy regimens to enhance anticancer effects. Importantly, downregulation of Artemis function appears to decrease cell proliferation in some cancer models, however its deficiency in normal cells does not appear to significantly impact proliferation, suggesting a good therapeutic window for its inhibition. To that end, several Artemis inhibitors have been described, ranging from existing non-oncology drugs to novel compounds. Their translational utility in various cancers has yet to be further investigated. Artemis structural information allows for novel drug designs targeting its catalytic and DNA binding capacities, as well as the ability to interact with its key binding partners. Overall, this manuscript supports further investigations of Artemis in cancer biology research and Oncology and confirms its strong translational potential as a biomarker and a therapeutic target in cancer.
