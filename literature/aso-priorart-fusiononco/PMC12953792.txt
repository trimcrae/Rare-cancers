## Abstract
CAR T cells targeting CLL-1 have shown antileukemia efficacy in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). However, CLL-1low/− AML cells are present in some patients, suggesting that the development of CAR T cells with high sensitivity is necessary to eradicate all AML clones in these patients. Here, we identified CAR T cells derived from a novel, high-affinity anti-CLL-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) that exhibited a significant anti-AML effect in vivo. We generated 13 new mAbs against CLL-1 and established CAR T cells from them. Of these, CAR T cells derived from mAb 2–23, which demonstrated greater affinity for CLL-1 than M26, the anti-CLL-1 mAb used in many previous studies, exhibited significant potential for cytokine production and cytotoxicity when co-cultured with AML cells. Treatment with 2–23 CAR T cells eradicated AML cells and significantly prolonged survival in AML xenograft models. Additionally, treatment with revumenib, a menin inhibitor, increased CLL-1 expression in AML cells harboring mixed-lineage leukemia (MLL) fusion genes or the NPM1 mutation, making them more susceptible to 2–23 CAR T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro. These findings suggest that the clinical efficacy of CAR T cells derived from the novel, high-affinity anti-CLL-1 mAb 2–23 should be tested in patients with CLL-1-positive AML and also that combining 2–23 CAR T cells with revumenib may benefit some patients with CLL-1low/− AML.

## Introduction
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in treating B-cell lymphomas and multiple myeloma. However, no CAR T cells have yet been approved for acute myeloid leukemia (AML). There is a critical need to develop CAR T cell therapy for patients with refractory AML. Several antigens including CD33, CD123, C-type lectin-like molecule 1 (CLL-1), and ADGRE2 have been investigated as potential CAR T cell targets in AML [ – ].
CLL-1 is highly expressed on AML cells in many patients [ ]. CLL-1 is absent from normal hematopoietic stem cells, T cells, B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, red blood cells, and nonhematopoietic tissues, but is expressed in all granulocytes and monocytes [ ]. CLL-1-targeted CAR T cell therapy has demonstrated antileukemic activity against CLL-1high AML cell lines and AML cells from patients [ – ]. Several clinical studies have shown the efficacy of CLL-1 CAR T cell therapy [ – ], but prolonged and severe neutropenia occurred unless patients underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT). Based on these observations, CLL-1 CAR T cell therapy is being evaluated as a bridge to allo-HCT rather than as a stand-alone treatment.
Importantly, CLL-1 is expressed on AML stem cells but not on normal hematopoietic stem cells, making it an attractive therapeutic target in CLL-1-positive AML. However, the proportion of CLL-1-positive cells varies among patients, and CLL-1 antigen density differs substantially within CD34+CD38− leukemia stem cell-enriched AML populations [ , ]. Previous reports indicate that CLL-1 CAR T cell activity against CLL-1low AML cells is limited in vitro [ ]. These findings suggest the need for either a more sensitive CAR capable of recognizing CLL-1low AML cells or an approach to increase CLL-1 expression, enabling effective eradication of all AML clones in patients with CLL-1low AML cells.
In this study, we developed several new anti-CLL-1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and generated CAR T cells from each. We found that CAR T cells derived from a high-affinity mAb outperformed those derived from other antibodies in cytokine production when co-cultured with AML cells. These CAR T cells exhibited significant antileukemia effect in in vivo xenograft models. In addition, we screened for drugs capable of inducing CLL-1 expression in CLL-1low/− AML cells, thereby sensitizing them to CLL-1 CAR T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

## Methods

## Cell lines
MOLM13, THP-1, and U937 cells were purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank. OCI-AML3 cells were obtained from the DSMZ cell line bank. Several frozen stocks were generated shortly after receipt of the cells and used for experiments. Ba/F3 cells expressing human CLL-1 and U937 cells expressing luciferase and green fluorescent protein (GFP) were generated via retroviral transduction of CLL-1 or luciferase-IRES-GFP, followed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of CLL-1+ or GFP+ cells.
CLL-1-knockout (KO) U937 cells were established using CRISPR–Cas9, as described previously [ ]. Guide RNA (gRNA) for CLL-1 was synthesized using the GeneArt Precision gRNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The target forward and reverse primers were TAATACGACTCACTATAGATGTTTGGACATCATCACTT and TTCTAGCTCTAAAACAAGTGATGATGTCCAAACAT, respectively. Cas9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the gRNA were transfected into U937 cells using the 4D-Nucleofector X Unit System (Lonza). CLL-1-knockdown (KD) U937 cells were established by lentiviral infection of the plasmid encoding for short-hairpin RNAs (Sigma-Aldrich), which target the CLL-1 mRNA for degradation (shRNA: CCGGCGCACCTGACTTAAATAACATCTCGAGATGTTATTTAAGTCAGGTGCGTTTTTG). After transduction, cells were stained with anti-CLL-1-PE and CLL-1-negative or low cells were purified by FACS for use in experiments.

## Generation of mAbs targeting human CLL-1
Six-week-old BALB/c mice (CLEA Japan) were immunized by footpad injection with Ba/F3 cells transfected with human CLL-1. Lymphocytes from popliteal lymph nodes were fused with SP2/0 murine myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol (Roche Applied Science) to generate hybridomas. Ba/F3, U937, and U937 CLL-1 KO cells were incubated with hybridoma supernatants, treated with PE-conjugated antimouse IgG antibody (BioLegend, 405,307), and analyzed by flow cytometry to identify mAbs specific for CLL-1.

## Generation of CAR T cells
The complementary DNA (cDNA) of the variable region of each mAb was obtained by 5′-RACE PCR using a SMARTer RACE PCR Kit (Clontech) and then sequenced. The cDNAs of the variable regions of the κ light chain and heavy chain were fused with the cDNAs of CD28 and CD3ζ by overlapping PCR, as previously described [ ]. A T2A-truncated (t) EGFR sequence was fused with each CAR construct to measure transduction efficiency by staining with anti-EGFR antibody. The resulting CLL-1 CAR construct was inserted into a retroviral vector. CAR T cells were generated as previously reported [ ].
Briefly, to generate transient viral supernatants, 293 T cells were co-transfected with the retroviral vector, gag-pol, and VSV-G envelope plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Supernatants containing the retrovirus were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy donors were activated with anti-CD3 (OKT3, eBioscience) and anti-CD28 (CD28.2, eBioscience). Activated T cells were infected with the CAR-carrying retrovirus. The next day, recombinant human IL-2 (Shionogi Pharma) was added to the culture at a final concentration of 100 IU/ml. The cells were harvested 48 h after activation and then subjected to retroviral transduction using RetroNectin (Takara Bio). Following transduction, the cells were cultured in the presence of 100 IU/ml IL-2 for 8 days. Biotin-conjugated anti-EGFR antibody/streptavidin-APC (BioLegend) was used for flow cytometry analysis to measure the transduction efficiency of CAR.

## Flow cytometry
Single-cell suspensions were stained using fluorochrome-conjugated mAbs. The following antibodies were used for staining: anti-CLL-1-PE (50C1, BioLegend), anti-CD4-APC (OKT4, BioLegend), anti-CD8-FITC (SK1, BioLegend), anti-CD3-BV421 (UCTH1, BioLegend), anti-PD-1-BV605 (EH12.1, BD Biosciences), anti-TIM3-APC Cy7 (F38-2E2, BioLegend), anti-LAG3-PE Cy7 (11C3C65, BioLegend), and goat antimouse IgG-PE (Poly4053, BioLegend). CAR transduction efficiency was measured by staining the cells first with biotinylated antibodies against EGFR and then with streptavidin-APC (BioLegend). Analysis and cell sorting were performed on a FACS Canto II, FACS Aria II, or FACS Celesta (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

## Cytokine release assays and cytotoxicity assays
For comparisons between CAR T cells with different transduction efficiencies, experiments were performed by matching the number of cells based on transduction efficiency. CAR T cells or control (mock-transduced) T cells (1 × 104 cells) and target cells (1 × 105 cells or 1 × 104 cells) were co-cultured for 16 h. Cytokines secreted into the culture supernatant were measured using ELISA kits for IFN-γ and IL-2 (R&D Systems). To measure the cytotoxic potential of CAR T cells, killing assays were performed using both 51Cr release and luciferase-based assays. 51Cr release assays were performed as previously described [ ]. Briefly, target cells were labeled with 51Cr (PerkinElmer) for 1.5 h. Labeled target cells (1 × 104 cells) were then incubated with effector cells for four hours. The release of 51Cr in the harvested supernatant was then measured using a gamma counter. Cytotoxicity was also assessed using a luciferase-based killing assay. Luciferase-expressing target cells (1 × 104 cells) were co-cultured with effector cells for 24 h, followed by the addition of D-luciferin, and bioluminescence was measured using a plate luminometer.

## Repeated antigen stimulation assay
For repeated antigen stimulation, CAR T cells were co-cultured with U937 cells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:4 in 96-well plates (effector, 1.5 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL; target, 6 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL) without IL-2 supplementation. Cells were restimulated and replated every 3 days.

## In vitro synthesis of mAbs
The heavy and light chain variable region sequences were inserted into the expression vectors pFUSE-CHIg-mG1 and pFUSE2-CLIg-mk (InvivoGen), respectively. The plasmids were co-transfected into CHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the FreeStyle MAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 7 days, supernatants were collected, and antibodies were purified using a Protein G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (GE Healthcare) and dialyzed in PBS.

## Xenograft model
U937 cells were injected intravenously into NOG mice to establish a disseminated AML model. On Day 0, 1 × 106 luciferase-expressing U937 cells were injected intravenously via the tail vein into female NOG mice aged 6–8 weeks (irradiated with 2.4 Gy). On Day 3, the mice were administered 150 mg/kg body weight VivoGlo Luciferin (Promega) intraperitoneally and imaged using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) with Living Image software (PerkinElmer). The following day, 1 × 106 CAR-positive T cells or control (mock-transduced) T cells were administered intravenously. Mice were analyzed using the IVIS at the indicated time points.

## Anti-AML drugs
Revumenib (Selleck) and decitabine (Selleck) were prepared as 10 mM stock solutions in 100% DMSO and stored at − 80 °C. Azacitidine (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 50% acetic acid and prepared as a 10 μM stock solution immediately before experiments. Revumenib was tested at 0, 10, 100, and 1,000 nM; decitabine at 0, 0.1, and 1 μM; and azacitidine at 0, 0.5, and 1 μM, consistent with prior studies [ , ].

## Animal experiments
Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation. This study complies with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (United States National Institutes of Health) and is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical comparisons were made using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test. Analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software) or EZR 1.61 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R 2.7–2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [ ]. In the animal experiments, sample sizes were determined based on prior experience with variability in AML xenograft models. No mice were excluded from analysis. Mice were allocated into groups with equivalent average baseline tumor burdens prior to treatment.

## Results

## CAR T cells derived from a new anti-CLL-1 mAb 2–23 were identified as having high potential to produce cytokines when co-cultured with AML cells
We immunized Balb/c mice with Ba/F3 cells that express CLL-1. Then, we generated a number of hybridomas by fusing lymphocytes from the popliteal lymph nodes with SP2/0 myeloma cells. We identified hybridomas producing anti-CLL-1 mAb as those that react with Ba/F3 cells expressing CLL-1 and U937 cells, but not CLL-1-deficient U937 cells (Fig.  a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). CAR constructs derived from the variable regions of these mAbs were created by fusing them with the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of CD28 and the cytoplasmic domain of CD3ζ (Fig.  c). Truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (tEGFR) was co-expressed with the CAR construct to identify CAR-transduced T cells. The 13 CAR constructs were transduced into T cells separately (Fig.  a, d, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Five candidate CAR T cells (CAR T cells derived from the 2–23, 2–177, 2–361, 2–821, and 2–877 mAbs) were selected based on their ability to produce IL-2 when co-cultured with AML cells (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Of these five candidate CAR T cells, we selected the CAR T cells derived from the mAb 2–23 (hereafter referred to as 2–23 CAR T cells) because they demonstrated the greatest potential for IL-2 production when tested with different donors (Fig.  a, e). The 2–23 CAR T cells produced cytokines and exhibited cytotoxicity when co-cultured with U937 cells or HL-60 cells, both of which are CLL-1-positive, but not CLL-1-deficient U937 cells (Fig.  f, g, Supplementary Fig. 1f, g).
 

## CAR T cells derived from the 2–23 mAb are more sensitive than those derived from the preexisting anti-CLL-1 mAb, M26
The binding kinetics of the 2–23 mAb to U937 AML cells were compared with those of the M26 mAb, which was used as the source of the antigen recognition domain of the anti-CLL-1 CAR in previous preclinical and clinical studies [ ]. The affinity of 2–23 for CLL-1 was higher than that of M26 (Fig.  a).
 U937 cell clones expressing low levels of CLL-1 were established by transduction of shRNA for CLL-1 and single-cell sorting using FACS. CAR T cells were developed derived from 2–23 or M26 and co-cultured with the U937 cell clones expressing various levels of CLL-1 expression (Fig.  b). The 2–23 CAR T cells produced higher amounts of IFN-γ and IL-2 when co-cultured with any of the U937 leukemia cell clones. Notably, IL-2 production was detectable when the 2–23 CAR T cells were co-cultured with the U937 leukemia cell clones expressing low levels of CLL-1 (clones 2–99 and 1–5 in Fig.  b), although it was not detectable when M26 CAR T cells were co-cultured with these clones. These results suggest that CAR T cells derived from 2–23 mAb, which has a high affinity for CLL-1, are more sensitive than those derived from M26, whereas both 2–23 CAR T cells and M26 CAR T cells exhibited cytotoxicity against the U937 cell clones with low CLL-1 expression.
The 2–23 CAR T cells and M26 CAR T cells were repeatedly co-cultured with the wild-type U937 cells to examine whether the expression of exhaustion markers was induced in them (Supplementary Fig. 2a). After the fifth challenge with U937 cells, the frequencies of PD1+Tim3+LAG3+ exhausted CAR T cells and PD1−Tim3−LAG3− nonexhausted CAR T cells were comparable between the 2–23 CAR T cells and the M26 CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

## Treatment with 2–23 CAR T cells eradicated AML cells in an in vivo xenograft model
We developed a mouse xenograft model by transplanting luciferase-expressing U937 AML cells into immunodeficient mice. Then, we examined the anti-AML efficacy of 2–23 CAR T cells in vivo (Fig.  a). The leukemia cells were eradicated, and the survival of the mice treated with 2–23 CAR T cells was significantly prolonged. By contrast, all of the mice that were injected with the control T cells developed leukemia rapidly and died within 25 days (Fig.  b - d). Distinct human CD45+CD3+CAR+ T cells were identified in the BM and spleen of a mouse treated with 2–23 CAR T cells, 42 days after treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3).
 

## Treatment with the menin inhibitor revumenib increases CLL-1 expression in AML cells with the MLL fusion genes or the NPM1 mutation and sensitizes them to cytotoxicity by 2–23 CAR T cells in vitro
We examined whether revumenib, azacitidine, or decitabine—all of which are used as antileukemia drugs in clinical practice—enhance CLL-1 expression in AML cell lines. MOLM13 (MLL-AF9 +) and THP-1 (MLL-AF9 +) cell lines weakly express CLL-1. When these cell lines were treated with the menin inhibitor revumenib (1,000 nM) for four days, the AML cells became distinctly CLL-1-positive. Another CLL-1-negative cell line, OCI-AML3 cells with the NPM1 mutation, also became CLL-1-positive after seven days of revumenib treatment (Fig.  a). AML cells were treated with revumenib for seven days. Then, the cells were cultured without revumenib for an additional three days. CLL-1 expression was maintained (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Enhancement of CLL-1 expression was undetectable or trivial in leukemia cells treated with azacitidine or decitabine (Supplementary Fig. 4b, 4c). Consistently, 2–23 CAR T cells co-cultured with MOLM13 cells treated with revumenib produced significantly higher amounts of IFN-γ and IL-2 than those co-cultured with untreated MOLM13 cells (Fig.  b). Furthermore, 2–23 CAR T cells killed MOLM13 cells treated with revumenib, but not untreated MOLM13 cells (Fig.  c).
 

## Discussion
In this study, we developed CAR T cells derived from 13 new anti-CLL-1 mAbs. We then identified CAR T cells derived from the 2–23 mAb as having the high potential to produce cytokines and to exert cytotoxicity when co-cultured with AML cells expressing CLL-1. CAR T cells derived from 2–23 exhibited a significant antileukemia effect. The 2–23 mAb has a higher affinity for CLL-1 than the M26 mAb, which was used to construct the CAR used in clinical trials. High-affinity mAbs are favored in CAR construction because they can robustly activate T cells and detect low levels of antigen [ ]. Indeed, we demonstrated that 2–23 CAR T cells reacted with some CLL-1low leukemia cells, which M26 CAR T cells could not recognize, although the antileukemia efficacy of 2–23 CAR T cells and M26 CAR T cells should be compared in vivo xenograft models in the future. Therefore, 2–23 CAR T cells may be appropriate for eradicating AML cells prior to allo-HCT. On the other hand, some reports suggest that CAR T cells generated using low-affinity mAbs exhibit increased expansion and prolonged persistence [ ]. However, long persistence may not be important for CLL-1 CAR T cells, because they are eliminated when allo-HCT is performed after CLL-1 CAR T cell therapy.
In some patients, AML cells do not express CLL-1 [ ]. In these cases, AML clones cannot be eradicated by CLL-1 CAR T cells. To extend the application of CLL-1 CAR T cells to more AML patients, therefore, we need to develop methods to induce CLL-1 expression in CLL-1-negative AML cells. In this study, we found that treatment with revumenib, a menin inhibitor, induced CLL-1 expression in AML cell lines harboring the MLL fusion gene or the NPM1 mutation, sensitizing them to 2–23 CAR T cell cytotoxicity against MOLM13 cells, while we should confirm this effect in other AML cell lines and patient samples. These results suggest that revumenib treatment may synergize with CLL-1 CAR T cell therapy, although we must confirm the synergistic effect of revumenib and CLL-1 CAR T cells in in vivo xenograft models. Revumenib treatment may enhance CLL-1 expression in not only AML cells but also in normal immature myeloid cells. This suggests that we need to be careful about the off-tumor on-target effects of CLL-1 CAR T cells. CLL-1 expression by revumenib is likely to be caused by differentiation induction, as CLL-1 is a differentiation antigen in the myeloid lineage. Indeed, upregulation of genes associated with differentiation, such as CD11b and CD14, was observed and differentiation syndrome was reported in some AML patients treated with menin inhibitors[ ]. Other drugs that have the potential to induce differentiation in AML cells may also induce CLL-1 expression [ ].
In summary, CAR T cells derived from the new, high-affinity anti-CLL-1 mAb 2–23 demonstrated significant anti-AML efficacy against CLL-1-positive AML cells and should be tested in clinical trials. Furthermore, we propose combining revumenib with CLL-1 CAR T cell therapy to potentially treat some patients with AML who lack CLL-1 expression.
