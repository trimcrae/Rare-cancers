## Abstract
Osteoarthritis (OA), as a complex degenerative disease, involves the interaction among macrophages, chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in its pathogenesis. In recent years, extracellular vesicles (EVs), as important mediators of intercellular communication, have attracted much attention due to their potential in tissue repair and immune regulation. This review explores the role of EVs in the treatment of OA, particularly their functions in the crosstalk among macrophages, chondrocytes and MSCs. EVs carry bioactive molecules such as miRNAs and proteins, and influence cell behavior by regulating cell signaling pathways and gene expression. In OA, EVs can promote chondrocyte proliferation, inhibit inflammation, promote cartilage repair, and regulate macrophage polarization. Additionally, EVs can also be used as drug delivery systems to enhance drug targeting and bioavailability. Despite the great potential of exosomes in the treatment of OA, there are still challenges in their extraction and purification, dose determination, and long-term safety assessment.
Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Extracellular vesicles, Macrophage polarization, Chondrocytes, Cell-to-cell communication, Mesenchymal stem cells

## Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA), the predominant form of degenerative joint disorder, manifests through progressive cartilage breakdown, subchondral bone alterations, and synovial inflammation, culminating in persistent pain and functional impairment [ ]. The disease mechanism involves intricate cross-talk among articular tissues, where dysregulated communication among chondrocytes, macrophages, and MSCs drives pathological advancement. It is worth noting that meniscus injury, as an important initiating factor in the pathological process of osteoarthritis, disrupts the biomechanical properties of the joint and triggers an inflammatory cascade reaction, further exacerbating tissue damage [ ]. Contemporary clinical management primarily targets symptomatic relief rather than disease modification, with conventional approaches like NSAIDs and intra-articular corticosteroids offering transient pain control without impacting underlying disease mechanisms [ ]. In advanced cases, joint arthroplasty remains the last resort despite its associated risks and finite lifespan. These limitations have spurred ongoing investigation into innovative regenerative approaches capable of reestablishing joint equilibrium and facilitating structural restoration [ ].
In recent years, EVs have emerged as a novel approach for the treatment of OA [ ]. These biologically derived nanoscale vesicles, produced by diverse cell types such as mesenchymal stem cells and macrophages, transport regulatory molecules including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids that influence recipient cell activity. Unlike cellular therapies, EVs offer distinct benefits including minimal immune reactivity and enhanced tissue permeability, circumventing risks associated with viable cell implantation. Growing research demonstrates their capacity to regulate macrophage phenotypic switching, enhance chondrocyte viability, and facilitate MSC-driven cartilage regeneration, positioning them as promising therapeutic candidates for addressing OA’s complex multicellular pathology [ , ].
This review systematically elaborates for the first time the interactions among macrophages, chondrocytes and MSCs in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis, with a particular focus on the regulatory functions of EVs. We innovatively integrate the current research findings on how EV-mediated cell communication affects key pathological processes of osteoarthritis, including inflammatory responses, tissue regeneration and meniscus repair. The review particularly emphasizes the analysis of the enhancing effects of EV engineering strategies such as biomaterial encapsulation and miRNA carriers on therapeutic efficacy. Meanwhile, we also discuss the challenges and future development directions of clinical translation of EV therapy. By clarifying these mechanisms, this review aims to deepen the understanding of the pathophysiology of osteoarthritis and promote the development of novel targeted therapeutic strategies for joint degeneration.

## Pathological mechanism of osteoarthritis
A common degenerative joint disease, OA is characterized by a complex pathogenesis involving the interplay of multiple pathological processes. These include meniscal injury, synovitis, articular cartilage degeneration, and subchondral bone sclerosis. These factors mutually reinforce one another, forming a vicious cycle that ultimately leads to joint structural destruction and functional impairment [ , ]. The meniscus, a fibrocartilaginous structure situated between the femur and tibia, plays a crucial role in cushioning impact and stabilizing the joint during movement [ ]. When the meniscus is damaged due to trauma or degeneration, it not only loses its mechanical protective function, leading to abnormal stress distribution within the joint, but also releases inflammatory mediators that subsequently affect the synovium, articular cartilage, and subchondral bone [ ]. The synovial membrane, articular cartilage and subchondral bone are functionally closely related and jointly maintain the homeostasis of the joint.
Research shows that when the meniscus is injured, on the one hand, it leads to abnormal mechanical loading, directly causing damage to chondrocytes; on the other hand, it releases inflammatory factors, triggering inflammatory changes in the synovium [ ]. When synovitis occurs, stimulated macrophages secrete various inflammatory factors, disrupting the balance among macrophages, promoting the polarization of macrophages towards pro-inflammatory phenotypes (such as M1 type), and inhibiting anti-inflammatory phenotypes (M2 type), thereby exacerbating inflammation, suppressing the synthesis of cartilage matrix, and accelerating cartilage degeneration [ ]. At this time, MSCs from the joint or bone marrow are attracted to the injury site by inflammatory signals, attempting to differentiate into chondrocytes and secrete matrix components to repair cartilage. However, in a persistent inflammatory and mechanical injury environment, the chondrogenic differentiation ability and paracrine function of MSCs are impaired, resulting in low repair efficiency. In some cases, they may even lose their immunomodulatory function due to microenvironmental imbalance [ ]. After cartilage degeneration, its protective effect on subchondral bone weakens, abnormal mechanical loads are directly transmitted to the subchondral bone, stimulating the activation of bone cells; meanwhile, inflammatory factors released by cartilage and synovium regulate the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, leading to subchondral bone sclerosis and osteophyte formation [ ]. Subchondral bone sclerosis further increases the stress on the articular surface and accelerates cartilage degeneration.
The development of OA involves dynamic interactions among articular cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium and meniscus. Meniscus injury accelerates cartilage degeneration and synovial inflammation by altering the mechanical environment of the joint and releasing inflammatory mediators; cartilage degeneration gradually weakens its protective function and further changes the mechanical properties of subchondral bone tissue. Meanwhile, changes in synovial inflammation further aggravate joint destruction and meniscus degeneration, thus forming a vicious cycle that promotes disease progression. Key cellular components—especially macrophages, MSCs and chondrocytes—play important regulatory roles in the pathogenesis of OA [ ]. Therefore, effective management of OA must simultaneously target these interrelated pathological mechanisms to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

## The roles of functional cells in osteoarthritis

## 1The role of macrophages in osteoarthritis
Macrophages represent a heterogeneous population of monocyte-macrophage lineage cells with powerful phagocytic, antigen-presenting and cytokine-secreting capabilities [ ]. In the joints, macrophages are mainly distributed within the synovial tissue and act as key participants in the synovial inflammatory response, playing a significant role in the OA pathogenesis [ ].
Studies have shown that macrophages can polarize into pro-inflammatory M1 type and anti-inflammatory M2 type under inflammatory stimulation. In OA, this polarization balance is disrupted, manifested as excessive activation of M1 type macrophages and suppression of M2 type function. This imbalance will aggravate joint inflammation, promote degradation of cartilage matrix, and disrupt the homeostasis of the joint microenvironment, thereby accelerating the pathological process of OA [ ]. Research has demonstrated that M1-type macrophages exhibit significant proliferation within the synovium and cartilage of patients diagnosed with OA. These M1-type macrophages are usually activated by pattern recognition receptors—Toll-like receptors when recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby initiating signaling pathways such as NF-κB. This process leads to the massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α). The production of these cytokines not only induces the senescence and apoptosis of chondrocytes but also reduces the synthesis of key Extracellular Matrix (ECM) components, such as proteoglycans, aggrecan, and type II collagen. Ultimately, these changes result in alterations in the structure of articular cartilage and synovium [ , ]. Conversely, M2-type macrophages can be induced by interleukin-4 (IL-4) or interleukin-10 (IL-10), generating anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10, which can alleviate joint inflammation and promote cartilage repair [ ]. Numerous studies have shown that the enhancement of M1 polarization and the weakening of M2 polarization are the key factors leading to OA. Lizette et al. [ ] demonstrated the impact of M1-type macrophages on OA. By applying an OA cartilage model in vitro and co-culturing it with M1 macrophages induced by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and TNF-α, they found that it affected OA cartilage by up-regulating the expression of pro-inflammatory factors. Zhang et al. [ ] demonstrated that M1 polarization of macrophages exacerbated experimental collagenase-induced OA, while M2 polarization mitigated the development of OA. These findings clearly indicate the crucial role of M1 and M2 type macrophages in the development of OA.
Macrophages, as a key factor in the treatment strategies for OA, play a complex and diverse role in bone and joint tissues. They not only participate in the regulation of inflammatory responses but also play a significant role in cartilage damage and protection. Recent studies have proposed some new approaches to alleviate OA by modulating the polarization state of macrophages. Feng et al. [ ] found that myeloid cell 2 (TREM2) can be expressed on the surface of immune cells such as macrophages. When applied to an OA mouse model, TREM2 can promote the transformation of macrophages from M1 type to M2 type and down-regulate the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the inflammatory response and alleviating OA symptoms. Ou et al. [ ] discovered a natural anti-aging substance—spermidine (SPD), which can inhibit ERK/MAPK and p65/NF-κB signaling in macrophages, converting them from M1 type to M2 type, thereby alleviating inflammation and improving cartilage degradation. Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) is a cation channel associated with pain perception and inflammation. Lv et al. [ ] injected TRPV1 agonists into rat models of OA and found that it could inhibit M1 macrophage polarization and alleviate OA progression by reducing the positive expression of M1 macrophage-related factors such as CD80 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (INOS). In addition, the natural compound wogonin can inhibit M1 polarization by targeting the LSD1-p65 epigenetics axis, while the endogenous factor three-prime repair exonuclease (TREX1) can drive M2 polarization by activating the AP-1/c-Fos pathway, thereby alleviating the progression of OA [ , ]. These new findings highlight the significant role of modulating macrophage polarization in alleviating OA.
In conclusion, given the crucial role of macrophages in OA, they are not only significant participants in the inflammatory response but also key factors in alleviating the progression of OA (Fig.  ). Therefore, by modulating the polarization state of macrophages, new approaches for the treatment of OA are expected to be provided.
 

## The role of chondrocytes in osteoarthritis
Chondrocytes, as the predominant cellular constituents of cartilage tissue, perform essential functions including ECM synthesis and maintenance of cartilage structure. Under various pathological stimuli, chondrocytes undergo morphological alterations that subsequently induce cartilage degeneration. Given that articular cartilage destruction and functional impairment represent hallmark features of OA, chondrocytes play a pivotal role in OA pathogenesis[ ].
When chondrocytes are stimulated by inflammation or injury, they mainly aggravate OA by promoting the degradation of Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs), inducing chondrocyte apoptosis and oxidative stress. Specifically, mechanical stress or injury triggers chondrocytes to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which subsequently enhance MMPs expression, particularly MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-13. These proteinases potently degrade cartilage collagen, ultimately causing structural disintegration of the cartilage extracellular matrix [ ]. Ansari et al. [ ] found that inflammatory factors promote chondrocyte apoptosis by enhancing the activity of the pro-apoptotic factor caspase-3, thereby accelerating the progression of OA. Additionally, under oxidative stress conditions, mitochondrial function in chondrocytes is impaired, leading to a reduction in ATP synthesis. To meet energy demands, glycolysis levels increase, generating a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further accelerates the degradation of the ECM and the production of MMPs, ultimately resulting in the disintegration and degeneration of cartilage tissue [ ].
Emerging evidence suggests that therapeutic approaches targeting reactive oxygen species (ROS) suppression, prevention of chondrocyte programmed cell death, and downregulation of MMPs may effectively mitigate OA progression. Gao et al. [ ] demonstrated that charged multivesicular body protein 5, a crucial regulator of vesicular trafficking and membrane dynamics, alleviates OA pathogenesis through dual mechanisms: by interacting with USP15 to stabilize IκBα and block NF-κB pathway activation, and by protecting chondrocytes from inflammatory mediator-induced extracellular matrix breakdown and cell death. In complementary research, Qiu et al. [ ] leveraged the antioxidative and anti-inflammatory capacities of Glycyrrhiza glabra extract in an OA murine model. Their findings revealed the extract’s ability to markedly suppress chondrocyte apoptosis through modulation of apoptotic regulators—diminishing caspase-3 while enhancing B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) expression. Furthermore, the treatment attenuated cartilage matrix deterioration by inhibiting MMP production. Another mechanistic study by Gao et al. [ ] identified the target gene of hsa_piR_019914 as lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) through small RNA sequencing and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). They found that hsa_piR_019914 alleviates cartilage destruction by regulating the expression of LDHA, reducing LDHA-dependent ROS production, maintaining the expression of cartilage synthesis genes such as aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen (Col II), and decreasing the expression of MMPs. These investigations collectively underscore that multifaceted interventions targeting chondrocyte protection through distinct molecular pathways represent promising therapeutic avenues for OA management.
In summary, chondrocytes serve as pivotal regulators of cartilage homeostasis, critically maintaining tissue architecture and facilitating cartilage development. Their functional and morphological alterations are intimately linked to OA pathogenesis (Fig.  ). Consequently, therapeutic modulation of chondrocyte activity and biological functions represents a fundamental strategy for OA intervention.

## The role of mesenchymal stem cells in osteoarthritis
Characterized by self-renewal ability and multi-lineage differentiation potential, MSCs are found in numerous tissues throughout the body, such as bone marrow, peripheral blood, umbilical cord blood, adipose tissue, muscle, lung, liver, and pancreas [ ]. Under specific conditions, MSCs can differentiate into various cell types, including osteoblasts and chondrocytes, thereby participating in the process of tissue repair and regeneration. Currently, the most extensively studied MSCs mainly include the following three types: bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs), and umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs). Thanks to their unique biological characteristics, MSCs have received extensive attention in the biomedical field, especially in the application potential in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [ ].
However, in OA, the alteration of the joint microenvironment leads to a significant decline in the proliferation and chondrogenic differentiation ability of MSCs [ , ]. Therefore, supplementing with MSCs from healthy donors is crucial for the treatment of OA. Its therapeutic effect on OA is remarkable, mainly reflected in its ability to repair cartilage, promote cartilage regeneration, and alleviate joint inflammatory responses [ ]. Studies have shown that MSCs treatment for OA can significantly reduce the levels of inflammation-related cytokines, including IFN-γ, interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and interleukin-12 (IL-12), thereby effectively suppressing joint inflammation [ ]. In addition, MSCs can also enhance the expression of PLCE1, CaMKII-β and its downstream factor NFATc1, activate the Wnt signaling pathway, increase the expression level of chondrogenic marker SOX9, and enhance the ability of chondrogenic regeneration by regulating the TGF-β/Smad2 signaling pathway [ , ]. Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (HUC-MSCs) have demonstrated significant chondroprotective and repair effects in the treatment of OA. Studies have shown that HUC-MSCs can significantly down-regulate the expression of key factors in the OA pathological process, including MMP-13, collagen X (Col X), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (ADAMTS-5), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby effectively reducing joint inflammation and inhibiting cartilage degradation. Additionally, HUC-MSCs have the potential to differentiate into chondrocytes and can synthesize cartilage-specific matrix components such as Col II, SOX9, and aggrecan, further promoting cartilage tissue regeneration and repair [ ]. In addition, given the close relationship between meniscus injury and OA, research has shown that synovial mesenchymal stem cells (SMSC) can effectively promote meniscus repair in a novel porcine meniscus injury model [ ]. In summary, HUC-MSCs exhibit remarkable anti-inflammatory, chondroprotective, and regenerative potential in OA treatment through multiple pathways and targets.
Although MSCs have shown promising applications in the treatment of OA, their application still faces many challenges. For example, challenges that MSCs may encounter during joint injections include physical damage during the injection process, cell migration problems, and the lack of ideal conditions to fully realise their therapeutic potential. Therefore, there is an urgent need for better methods to fulfil the role of MSCs [ ]. In recent years, hydrogel technology has been widely used as an advanced delivery system for MSCs to enhance their efficacy in OA therapy. Hydrogels possess a unique internal porous structure, providing an ideal microenvironment for the encapsulation of MSCs. This not only enhances the survival rate and activity of cells but also alleviates the problem of restricted cell proliferation or cell apoptosis caused by insufficient nutrient supply in the joint cavity, thereby creating favorable conditions for MSCs to exert their repair potential [ , ]. Moreover, the latest research indicates that applying hydrogels loaded with MSCs to the injured meniscus area can offer excellent microenvironmental support and bioactive signals for meniscus regeneration, effectively promoting the repair of meniscus tissue. This provides a highly promising new strategy for blocking and alleviating the progression of OA at its root cause [ , ].
In conclusion, MSCs exhibit multifaceted therapeutic effects against OA, encompassing anti-inflammatory activity, chondroprotection, and tissue regeneration capabilities, positioning them as a pivotal therapeutic approach (Fig.  ). Subsequent investigations should prioritize the development of innovative delivery systems to enhance MSC viability, homing efficiency, and functional persistence. Such advancements would optimize their therapeutic efficacy and offer improved clinical outcomes for OA management.

## The biological characteristics of extracellular vesicles
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are vesicle-like bodies with a double-membrane structure. They can be classified into Exosomes (Exos), microvesicles (MVs), and apoptotic bodies (ABs) based on their origin and size [ ]. Among them, Exos have a diameter of approximately 30–150 nm, formed by inward budding of the inner membrane within multivesicular bodies and released to the extracellular space after the fusion of multivesicular bodies with the cell membrane. MVs generally have a diameter of 100 nm to 1000 nm, formed directly by budding from the plasma membrane. ABs, with a diameter of approximately 1000 nm to 5000 nm, are larger membrane structures formed during the process of apoptosis [ , ]. At present, in the field of biomedicine, EVs, which mainly participate in intercellular information transmission and biological function regulation, have become a research hotspot. They play an important role and have broad application prospects.
Due to the different classifications of EVs, they also exhibit diverse characteristics in biological properties, mainly including serving as messengers between cells, participating in intercellular information transmission, material transportation, and functional transfer, etc. The surface of EVs often contains tetraspanins (such as CD9, CD63, CD81), integrins, and glycoproteins, which play a key role in the binding and targeting of EVs to receptor cells [ ]. EVs also carry nucleic acid molecules such as miRNA, RNA, and DNA inside. These substances can regulate the gene expression of recipient cells through the transfer and release of EVs, playing a role in intercellular communication and providing new ideas and methods for disease treatment. For instance, Zhao et al. [ ] found that miR-182 carried by Exos derived from MCS can alleviate myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury by targeting the TLR4/NF-κB/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to regulate macrophage polarization. Ye et al. [ ] found that in hypoxia-induced inflammation, miRNAs in EVs are involved in transcriptional regulation through different pathways to promote or reduce the inflammatory response. Among them, miR-223 can alleviate the inflammatory response by targeting Pknox1 to inhibit the activation of M1 macrophages and promote the activation of M2 macrophages. These studies demonstrate the unique role of nucleic acid molecules carried by EVs in cell communication.
Moreover, as research on EVs deepens, it has been found that the unique lipid bilayer structure of EVs makes them highly biocompatible and capable of effectively protecting the biomolecules they carry from degradation. This also determines that they can be ideal carriers for drug delivery, used in disease diagnosis and treatment. By carrying specific targeting genes, they can achieve precise drug delivery and improve the therapeutic effect of diseases. Previous studies have shown that most drug delivery functions are applied in cancer treatment. For instance, attenuated Klebsiella pneumoniae-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) as a biological drug delivery carrier can transport the chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin (DOX) into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) A549 cells to inhibit tumor growth, and the loading of paclitaxel onto EVs derived from autologous prostate cancer cells enhances its cytotoxicity [ , ]. Recent studies have shown that the characteristics of EVs in drug delivery also have a good therapeutic effect on OA. It is well known that curcumin has a good anti-inflammatory effect, but its bioavailability is limited. Li et al. [ ] encapsulated curcumin in hBMSC-derived EVs through experiments, making hBMSC-derived EVs have better targeting. The EVs encapsulating curcumin can alleviate OA by reducing the apoptosis and phosphorylation of Erk1/2, PI3K/Akt and p38 MAPK, and regulating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. Digoxin is a glycoside compound with strong cardiac effects and is widely used in the treatment of heart diseases. Recent studies have shown that digoxin also has significant anti-inflammatory effects [ ]. Jia et al. [ ] found that the application of digoxin-treated M1 macrophage-derived EVs to a collagenase-induced OA mouse model could significantly improve joint inflammation and alleviate cartilage degeneration. The experimental results indicated that digoxin-treated EVs demonstrated superior effects to untreated EVs in inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors, reducing cartilage matrix degradation, and promoting cartilage protection. These studies provide new strategies for the treatment of OA and highlight the broad application prospects of EVs in disease treatment.
Due to the unique biological characteristics of EVs, they have shown great application potential in disease treatment. In future research, it is necessary to further explore more possibilities of their use in treating diseases, especially OA.

## The crosstalk between macrophages and chondrocytes in osteoarthritis
With the continuous deepening of research on OA, the role of EVs has become increasingly prominent, especially in the crosstalk among macrophages, chondrocytes and OA. The crosstalk between macrophages and chondrocytes mediated by EVs has different effects on OA, including both positive effects that alleviate OA symptoms and negative effects that accelerate OA progression (Fig.  ). This dual impact provides new perspectives and potential targets for the treatment of OA.
 Multiple studies have shown that the crosstalk between EVs and macrophages and chondrocytes mainly aggravates OA by promoting inflammatory responses and cartilage degradation. Among them, EVs derived from macrophages can exert negative effects on OA by acting on chondrocytes. Lin et al. [ ] stimulated macrophages with bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to simulate the state of inflammatory macrophages and used the derived EVs for in vitro chondrocyte stimulation and intra-articular injection in mouse models. The study found that EVs from inflammatory macrophages induced overexpression of pyroptosis-related factors such as NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 (Nlrp3) and gasdermin D (Gsdmd) by promoting the activation of the caspase 4/caspase 11-GSDMD signaling pathway related to non-canonical pyroptosis, leading to pyroptosis and cartilage degradation of chondrocytes and thereby exacerbating the symptoms of OA. In addition, when joints are exposed to adverse stimuli from the external environment such as mechanical overload, it prompts macrophages to polarize into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, leading to joint inflammation. Lin et al. [ ] found that excessive mechanical load-induced M1 macrophage-secreted Exos are rich in miR-350-3p. This miR-350-3p targets chondrocytes, reduces the expression of H3K36 methyltransferase NSD1 and the levels of H3K36 mono- and di-methylation, thereby inhibiting the progression of OA.In addition, Si et al. [ ] also found through experiments that Exos derived from M1-type macrophages can enhance the inflammatory response of chondrocytes and promote chondrocyte apoptosis by releasing miR-363 and targeting G3BP2, further accelerating cartilage degeneration.
It is well known that chondrocytes play a crucial role in promoting cartilage formation. However, chondrocyte-derived EVs may accelerate the pathological changes of OA by targeting macrophages. Lv et al. [ ] indicates that chondrocyte-derived Exos can partially down-regulate ATG4B expression through miR-449a-5p, inhibit autophagy in macrophages, and promote inflammatory responses, thereby increasing damage to OA cartilage. In addition, Exos derived from dysfunctional chondrocytes can inhibit autophagy in macrophages by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, promote M1 polarization of macrophages and enhance the inflammatory response, thereby exacerbating OA [ ]. Although the above-mentioned studies all focused on the negative impact of EVs on OA in the crosstalk between macrophages and chondrocytes, they also provided potential therapeutic targets for OA alleviation.
However, not all EVs exert negative effects on OA in the crosstalk between macrophages and chondrocytes. Some EVs also demonstrate positive roles. It is well known that M2-type macrophages play a significant role in alleviating the symptoms of OA. The EVs derived from them can regulate the chondral microenvironment by targeting chondrocytes, thereby effectively delaying the progression of OA. Studies have shown that miR-26b-5p in Exos from M2-type macrophages promotes the expression of COL2 and SOX9 proteins by targeting COL10A1, thereby inhibiting chondrocyte hypertrophy. Meanwhile, miR-26b-5p can also alleviate cartilage damage by regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, further alleviating the pathological process of OA. [ , ]. Guo et al. [ ] found through research that EVs derived from M2 macrophages can also protect cartilage by delivering miR-370-3p to chondrocytes to inhibit the transcription of SOX11, thereby playing a protective role in the proliferation and inflammatory response of chondrocytes stimulated by IL-1β. Tang et al. [ ] discovered the role of extracellular nucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase 3 (Entpd3) secreted by macrophage-derived EVs in chondrocytes through bioinformatics analysis. Entpd3 alleviates the damage and mitochondrial dysfunction of OA chondrocytes by inhibiting the AKT/Notch2 signaling pathway, thereby reducing cartilage damage. In addition, Qin et al. [ ] applied macrophage-derived ABs to the OA mouse model established by anterior cruciate ligament transection. The research results showed that ABs could significantly promote chondrocyte proliferation and effectively alleviate cartilage damage. In conclusion, EVs derived from macrophages can exert excellent therapeutic effects on OA by targeting chondrocytes. Similarly, EVs derived from chondrocytes can also alleviate the symptoms of OA by targeting macrophages. Zheng et al. [ ] extracted primary chondrocyte-derived Exos and found that they could alleviate joint inflammation and delay the progression of OA by promoting M2 macrophage polarization and reducing M1 macrophage polarization. Furthermore, although the role of EVs derived from inflammatory chondrocytes in the treatment of OA has received little attention, Xu et al. [ ] found that they can effectively promote M2 polarization of macrophages, inhibit inflammation, and alleviate the degeneration of articular cartilage.
In summary, EVs play a significant regulatory role in the crosstalk among macrophages, chondrocytes and OA. Currently, through continuous in-depth research, many potential therapeutic mechanisms for OA have been discovered, providing more possible targets for treatment. However, studies have shown that multiple factors seem to weaken the effectiveness of EVs in clinical practice. For instance, the commonly used animal models and cell models at present may not fully reflect the complexity and diversity of OA patients. Moreover, further in-depth research is still needed on the specific mechanisms of action, safety and side effects of EVs to enhance their therapeutic effect on OA.

## The crosstalk between mesenchymal stem cells and macrophages in osteoarthritis
It is well known that EVs have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic effects in the treatment of various diseases, and they also hold great therapeutic potential in the field of OA treatment. These bioactive vesicles exhibit significant therapeutic potential by facilitating intercellular crosstalk between MSCs and macrophages, which contributes to OA symptom amelioration. Notably, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) demonstrate potent anti-inflammatory effects through macrophage polarization modulation, offering a novel therapeutic avenue for OA management (Fig.  ).
 Studies have shown that MSCs-EVs of different tissue sources can alleviate the progression of OA by regulating the polarization state of macrophages. Li et al. [ ] isolated Exosomes from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC-Exos) and evaluated their therapeutic effects in a rat OA model established through anterior cruciate ligament transection and medial meniscus destabilization. Their investigation revealed that BMSC-Exos administration suppressed the PINK1/Parkin pathway, downregulated pro-inflammatory markers (CD86 and INOS) characteristic of M1 macrophages, while upregulating anti-inflammatory markers (Arg-1 and CD206) associated with M2 phenotype. These findings demonstrate that BMSC-Exos exert both anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects in OA through macrophage polarization modulation. Zhang et al. [ ] demonstrated that intra-articular administration of BMSC-Exos in a rodent OA model markedly attenuated cartilage destruction and osteophyte development. Complementary in vitro studies confirmed that BMSC-Exos modulate macrophage polarization, suppressing pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype while enhancing anti-inflammatory M2 activation. Parallel research by Cosenza et al. [ ] also compared the therapeutic effects of BMSCs-derived MVs and Exos on OA. The study found that both MVs and Exos have excellent therapeutic effects on OA, and they can both inhibit the inflammation of OA by regulating the polarization state of macrophages. In addition, BMSCs-Exos can also regulate the polarization state of macrophages and alleviate OA through RNA molecules carried on the surface. Recent studies have shown that long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) TUC339 is closely related to BMSC and has an important impact on inhibiting inflammation in organisms [ ]. However, the mechanism of TUC339 derived from BMSCs-Exos in OA remains unclear. Chen et al. [ ] found through experiments that the expression level of TUC339 was lower in OA patients. Additionally, they applied BMSCs-Exos to a OA mouse model, and the results showed that this treatment could enhance the expression of TUC339 and further promote the polarization of M1-type macrophages to M2-type, thereby suppressing inflammation and enhancing chondrocyte activity to improve the condition of OA. Wang et al. [ ] found that BMSCs-Exos stimulated by TGF-β1 could carry highly expressed miR-135b. miR-135b promotes the expression of Arg-1, a factor related to M2 macrophage polarization, by inhibiting the overexpression of mitogen-activated protein kinase 6 (MAPK6), which is highly associated with OA, while reducing the expression of INOS, a factor related to M1 polarization, thereby slowing down the process of cartilage degradation.
In addition to extracellular vesicles derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs-EVs) being capable of regulating the polarization of macrophages, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (hUC-MSCs-sEVs) also have the same regulatory effect. Li et al. [ ] demonstrated that co-culturing macrophages with hUCMSCs-EVs could significantly upregulate the expression of M2 macrophage markers Arg1 and CD206, while downregulating the expression of M1 macrophage markers INOS and CD86. Further research revealed that treatment with a concentration of 80 μg/mL of hUCMSCs-EVs was most effective in promoting M2 macrophage polarization. Similarly, Klyucherev et al. [ ] also found that hUCMSCs-EVs could suppress joint inflammation and alleviate OA symptoms by reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines characteristic of the M1 macrophage phenotype, such as IL-6 and TNF-α.Chen et al. [ ] extracted Wharton’s jelly (WJ) tissue from the umbilical cord and obtained Exosomes (WJMSC-sEVs) from Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells. In the study, RAW264.7 cells were cultured for 24 h under LPS and WJMSC-sEVs treatment. RT-qPCR analysis results showed that LPS treatment significantly promoted the inflammatory response. However, WJMSC-sEVs treatment effectively inhibited M1 macrophage polarization, promoted M2 macrophage polarization, and significantly enhanced the anti-inflammatory effect during cartilage repair. In addition, studies have shown that extracellular vesicles (SMSCs-exo) derived from synovial mesenchymal stem cells can also alleviate OA by regulating the polarization of macrophages. BMP-7, a member of the bone morphogenetic protein subfamily, has been proven to play a highly significant role in protecting cartilage [ ]. Sun et al. [ ] transfected BMP-7 overexpression plasmids into SMSCs to obtain BMP-7 overexpressing SMSCs-Exos and explored their role in macrophage polarization. Further research revealed that BMP-7-modified SMSCs-Exos could alleviate OA by promoting M2 polarization of macrophages.
In summary, EVs play a very important role in the crosstalk between MSCs and macrophages in the treatment of OA and provide many potential targets for the treatment of OA. However, current research still has some limitations. For example, many studies are limited to in vitro experiments, and their therapeutic effects in vivo are not clear. In addition, the number of related factors detected in the experiments is limited, which may affect the accuracy of the results. At the same time, the extraction process of EVs may also have errors, affecting their quality and function. In future research, more precise methods for extracting EVs should be developed, more targets related to the occurrence and development of OA should be explored, and the therapeutic effect of EVs on OA should be improved.

## The crosstalk between chondrocytes and mesenchymal stem cells in osteoarthritis
MSC-EVs not only alleviate OA by regulating macrophage polarization, but also directly act on chondrocytes to inhibit their inflammatory response and catabolism, and promote anabolism, thereby improving the pathological process of OA through multiple pathways (Fig.  ). Research shows that BMSCs-EVs, fat or umbilical cord can significantly inhibit the expression of catabolic factors related to chondrocytes, such as MMP-13 and ADAMTs5, and promote the production of anabolic factors related to cartilage, such as COL2 and ACAN [ , , , ]. In addition, MSCs-EVs can further improve the function of chondrocytes by targeting them with the RNA molecules they carry. For instance, Li et al. [ ] established an OA rat model and extracted MSCs-EVs from the subcutaneous adipose tissue of the thigh of patients who underwent liposuction. In vitro experiments revealed that miR-376c-3p carried by MEVs inhibited the WNT-β-catenin pathway by targeting WNT3/WNT9a, thereby further reducing chondrocyte degradation. Wei et al. [107]discovered through in vitro cell experiments that Exosomes (ASC-Exos) derived from adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs) overexpressing miR-338-3p have a good effect on inhibiting the inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α of chondrocytes and can target RUNX2 to promote chondrocyte proliferation. Zheng et al. [ ] found that Exos derived from SMSCs with overexpressed miR-212-5p also have a significant effect on inhibiting inflammatory factors such as IL-6, COX-2, and INOS, and can further alleviate the symptoms of OA by targeting ELF3 to inhibit chondrocyte degeneration. Although the above experiments have demonstrated the alleviating effect of EVs carrying RNA molecules targeting chondrocytes on OA, these studies are mainly based on in vitro experiments and still lack in vivo experiments to further verify their therapeutic effect in OA models.
 Studies have shown that MSCs-EVs can not only alleviate OA by regulating the inflammatory response and metabolic process of chondrocytes, but also improve the pathological condition of articular cartilage by regulating the apoptosis and autophagy of chondrocytes. To verify the effect of hBMSC-EVs on chondrocytes, Li et al. [ ] used live and dead cell staining assay, BrdU incorporation assay, and caspase 3/7 activity assay to evaluate the effect of EVs on the proliferation and apoptosis of OA-related chondrocytes. The results showed that after treatment with hBMSC-EVs, the number of chondrocytes significantly increased, and their apoptosis was effectively inhibited. Jiang et al. [ ] also obtained similar experimental results by extracting Exos from the OA mouse model. Compared with the control group, the apoptosis of chondrocytes in the OA group treated with Exos was significantly inhibited. Guillen et al. [ ] evaluated the effect of MVs derived from ADMSCs on the expression of autophagy marker LC3B in OA chondrocytes stimulated by IL-1β through techniques such as immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy. The results indicated that MVs could significantly enhance the expression of LC3B and the number of related autophagosomes in chondrocytes. In addition, the RNA molecules carried on the surface of MSCs-EVs play a very important role in regulating the apoptosis and autophagy of chondrocytes. For instance, Li et al. [ ] co-cultured ADSC-Exos miR-93-5p with IL-1β-treated chondrocytes and observed that the expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-αin chondrocytes was significantly inhibited, and at the same time, the apoptosis of chondrocytes was also notably alleviated. Wen et al. [ ] verified through in vitro cell experiments that the IncRNA KLF3-AS1 of MSC-Exo can activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway in chondrocytes, and further inhibit the expression of autophagy-related proteins LC3-II/LC3-I in chondrocytes, while significantly increasing the level of p62. Additionally, the study found that KLF3-AS1 can effectively reduce the apoptosis of chondrocytes. Recent studies have shown that circular RNA homologous interaction protein kinase 3 (circHIPK3) plays a significant role in various diseases and is abundant in EVs [ – ]. However, its mechanism of action in OA remains unclear. Li et al. [ ] established an OA mouse model and isolated chondrocytes from the knee joints of the mice. These chondrocytes were co-cultured with MSCs-EVs overexpressing circHIPK3, and the results were detected by flow cytometry. The findings indicated that MSCs-EVs circHIPK3 could alleviate OA symptoms by inhibiting chondrocyte apoptosis.
In addition to the mechanism by which MSC-EVs alleviate OA by regulating chondrocytes as mentioned above, a few studies have shown that they can further alleviate OA symptoms by inhibiting the senescence and ferroptosis of chondrocytes. Boulestreau et al. [ ] established an etoposide-induced DNA damage senescence model and an IL-1β-induced inflammatory senescence model, and applied ASC-EVs in both models. The results showed that the addition of ASC-EVs could regulate the balance between anabolic and catabolic processes in OA chondrocytes and effectively prevent chondrocyte senescence caused by DNA damage. In addition, ASC-EVs significantly reduced the number of SA-β-Gal positive cells and the secretion of SASP factors such as IL-6, MMP-3, and MMP-13, effectively alleviating IL-1β-induced inflammatory senescence. Similarly, studies have shown that BMSC-Exos can also significantly inhibit the senescence of chondrocytes induced by IL-1β [ ]. In addition, Shi et al. [ ] found that BMSC-Exos can also inhibit the indicators related to ferroptosis of chondrocytes, such as Fe2+, MDA and ROS, thereby effectively preventing the occurrence of OA.
In addition, as meniscus injury serves as a significant initiating factor for OA, promoting meniscal repair appears to be an effective strategy for delaying OA progression. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential therapeutic value of EVs derived from synovial and endometrial mesenchymal stem cells in meniscus repair. Kawata et al. [ ] demonstrated that sMSC-EVs significantly promoted chondrocyte proliferation in vitro and enhanced type II collagen expression while reducing fibrosis, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration in a mouse meniscus defect model. Furthermore, Schwartz et al. [ ] found that eMSC-EVs could effectively penetrate meniscal tissue, supporting their potential as delivery vehicles for therapeutic molecules. Although these experiments did not directly prove that these extracellular vesicles alleviate osteoarthritis by repairing the meniscus, given the close association between meniscus injury and OA, their meniscus-repairing effects may represent another potential mechanism for OA mitigation.
In conclusion, MSCs-EVs exhibit considerable therapeutic potential for OA management. These vesicles effectively mitigate OA progression through multimodal regulation of chondrocyte pathophysiology, encompassing inflammatory responses, metabolic reprogramming, apoptosis modulation, autophagy regulation, and senescence control. While current evidence primarily derives from in vitro studies, emerging preclinical data demonstrate their translational efficacy in vivo. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms and optimizing clinical translation strategies to develop more effective EV-based therapies for OA patients.

## Targeted extracellular vesicle therapy for osteoarthritis
EVs are nano-sized membrane structures secreted by cells, featuring low immunogenicity, high stability, and the ability to cross biological barriers. They play a crucial role in intercellular communication and tissue repair, thus demonstrating great potential in the treatment of OA. However, due to the density of cartilage tissue and the limited penetration ability of EVs in cartilage, their therapeutic effect is significantly affected within the cartilage [ , ].
To enhance the therapeutic effect of EVs in OA treatment, an increasing number of studies are dedicated to developing new methods to improve their therapeutic potential (Table  ). These methods mainly aim to increase the yield of EVs and enhance their biological effects, thereby further strengthening their therapeutic effect on OA. It is well known that hydrogels possess excellent biocompatibility, degradability, tunable mechanical properties, and superior cell adhesion, demonstrating broad application prospects in tissue engineering, drug delivery, and regenerative medicine. Numerous studies have shown that combining hydrogels with EVs can significantly improve their quality and targeting ability, and enhance therapeutic effects, achieving precise treatment [ , ]. Song et al. [ ] successfully prepared injectable HP hydrogel by dynamically crosslinking hyaluronic acid (HA) with Pluronic F-127. This hydrogel can effectively load M2 Exos and continuously release them, significantly increasing the yield of Exos. This process promotes the generation of synovial lymphatic vessels, enhances the lymphatic drainage function of the synovium, and slows down the development of OA. Yang et al. [ ] combined hyaluronic acid methacryloyl (HAMA) with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) to construct hydrogel microcarriers and encapsulated stem cell recruitment peptide (Pep) and Exos within these microcarriers. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that this hydrogel microcarrier could significantly enhance the therapeutic effect of Exos, improve the pathological condition of cartilage, and its therapeutic effect was more significant compared with the control group. Wan et al. [146] developed a GelMA-encapsulated small molecule inhibitor LRRK2-IN-1 modified engineered Exos WYRGRL (W) peptide loading system (W-Exo-L@GelMA). The research results showed that this Exos packaged in hydrogel significantly enhanced the targeting effect on chondrocytes in vivo and promoted the anabolic metabolism of chondrocytes. Cy5 fluorescence detection found that GelMA hydrogel prolonged the retention time of Exos in the joint cavity, thereby enhancing its effect on cartilage repair. In addition, hydrogel-delivered EVs have demonstrated remarkable potential in meniscus repair. Huang et al. [ ] developed a novel injectable hydrogel adhesive composed of oxidized dextran/carboxymethyl chitosan/poly-L-lysine/synovial MSC-derived EVs (OD/CS-PL@EVs). Owing to its superior meniscus-adhesive properties, this system significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of EVs in meniscus repair. When applied in a rabbit meniscus injury model, it effectively modulated inflammatory balance, promoted meniscus regeneration, and consequently delayed the progression to OA. Similarly, Kang et al. [ ] constructed a composite system using CD56⁺ umbilical cord MSC-derived EVs (CD56⁺Exos) loaded into decellularized Wharton’s jelly hydrogel (DWJH). This formulation not only promoted meniscal chondrogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling but also remarkably protected articular cartilage and inhibited secondary degeneration, thereby accelerating meniscus tear healing. Although these studies did not directly evaluate OA improvement, their success in repairing meniscus injuries at the source and preventing subsequent cartilage degeneration provides novel strategies for early OA intervention. Overall, hydrogels, as a new type of biomaterial, have opened up new avenues for the delivery and controlled release of EVs, improving the limitations of EVs such as their circulating half-life and retention.
 In addition to loading EVs through hydrogel carriers to enhance their application effect in the treatment of OA, the combined use of EVs and drugs can further improve the therapeutic effect. Some drugs with good anti-inflammatory effects, such as curcumin and cinnamaldehyde, when used in combination with EVs, can significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy of EVs [ , ]. Studies have shown that whether it is BMSC or ADMSC, the EVs released after curcumin treatment exhibit stronger activity in inhibiting chondrocyte apoptosis. Moreover, in vivo experimental results indicate that these treated EVs can significantly repair articular cartilage damage [ , ]. 1Similarly, Li et al. [ ] found that BMSC-EVs treated with cinnamaldehyde could inhibit the expression of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways and significantly reduce the levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, thereby further enhancing their inhibitory effect on OA. In addition to these two drugs, the combination of parathyroid hormone, digoxin and other drugs with EVs can also enhance the therapeutic effect of EVs on OA [ , ]. This method of combined use with drugs not only enhances the therapeutic effect but also may improve the stability and bioavailability of EVs through the synergistic action of different mechanisms, thereby achieving better therapeutic efficacy in the treatment process.
Beyond conventional approaches, isolating EVs under hypoxic conditions and optimizing culture protocols with novel chemically-defined, contaminant-free media can substantially boost EV yield while improving their therapeutic efficacy against OA [ ]. Research findings indicate that EVs induced under hypoxic conditions perform better than those induced under normoxic conditions in suppressing inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 and promoting the generation of cartilage markers. Additionally, EVs extracted under a low oxygen partial pressure of 1%, whether derived from BMSC-EVs or ADSC-Exo, exhibit superior effects in suppressing joint inflammation and promoting cartilage regeneration compared to those processed under normoxic conditions [ , ]. In their study, Hanai et al. [ ] employed two distinct culture media to extract EVs: one medium contained obvious particles, while the other was free of visible contamination. The experimental results demonstrated that EVs extracted from the medium without visible contamination exhibited higher purity and greater yield. These EVs showed the best performance in promoting chondrocyte proliferation, migration, differentiation into chondrocytes, and inhibiting cell apoptosis. The optimized culture conditions significantly increased the yield of EVs, providing more raw materials for the clinical application of EVs and significantly enhancing the therapeutic effect on OA.
Multiple studies have shown that pretreating EVs with cytokines can significantly enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Commonly used cytokines include LPS, IL-1β, and TNF-α, among others. For instance, Ao et al. [ ] found that compared with untreated SMSC-EVs, LPS-pretreated SMSC-EVs demonstrated significant effects in promoting chondrocyte proliferation, migration, and inhibiting apoptosis, and could effectively promote the expression of aggrecan and COL2A1, thereby protecting cartilage and effectively inhibiting the progression of OA. Similarly, Wu et al. [ ] demonstrated that TNF-α pretreatment significantly enhanced the secretion of Exos by infrapatellar fat pad mesenchymal stem cells (IPFP-MSCs). In the experimental model of OA, the injection of IPFP-MSC-Exos pretreated with TNF-α could reduce osteophyte formation and make the cartilage surface smoother and more intact. Furthermore, Chen et al. [ ] also demonstrated that HucMSCs pre-treated with IL-1β can secrete more Exos with anti-inflammatory properties. These Exos not only promote the polarization of M2 macrophages but also suppress inflammation and promote the formation of cartilage matrix, thereby facilitating cartilage repair.
In addition to the above content, researchers have proposed various methods to increase the yield of EVs and enhance their therapeutic effects. For instance, physical factors or 3D printing technology can be used to boost the production of EVs, and Exos can be modified with targeting peptides to enhance their therapeutic efficacy [ – ]. We have summarized the new research methods in recent years. Due to the large number of methods, it is not convenient to present them one by one here. For detailed information, please refer to Table  . In conclusion, the development and application of these methods not only provide new ideas and tools for the basic research and clinical transformation of EVs, but also bring new hope for the treatment of various diseases, including OA (Fig.  ). With the deepening of research and the advancement of technology, the application of EVs in disease treatment will become more extensive and precise in the future, and is expected to provide more effective treatment options for patients.
 

## Conclusions and prospects
As a common degenerative joint disease, OA substantially diminishes the quality of life for hundreds of millions worldwide. Characterized by progressive destruction of articular cartilage, synovial inflammation, and osteophyte formation, OA leads to persistent pain, joint dysfunction, and even disability [ ]. In this pathological process, abnormal polarization of macrophages, metabolic disorders of chondrocytes, and dysregulation of MSCs repair functions constitute the core links of disease progression. In recent years, EVs with their unique biological properties, have become a research hotspot. These nanoscale vesicles can carry a wealth of bioactive molecules and have shown significant advantages in regulating macrophage phenotypic conversion, maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis, and promoting MSCs chondrogenesis, providing new ideas for treating OA [ ]. This review comprehensively expounds the role of EVs in OA, particularly their key position in mediating the interaction among macrophages, chondrocytes, and MSCs. We have for the first time systematically summarized the mechanisms by which EVs influence the progression of OA by modulating inflammatory responses and promoting tissue repair, and explored the relationship between EVs and meniscus injury—an important contributing factor of OA. Research indicates that EVs can significantly enhance meniscus repair, offering a potential novel strategy for treating OA through meniscal regeneration. Furthermore, this review highlights recent advances in engineered EVs, including technologies such as biomaterial-based encapsulation, miRNA delivery, and pretreatment strategies. These approaches markedly improve the targeting ability, stability, and bioactivity of EVs, opening new avenues for the precise treatment of OA.
Despite the promising prospects, the clinical application of EVs in OA treatment still faces many challenges. The heterogeneity of EVs isolation and purification methods leads to differences in therapeutic effects, while issues such as short half-life and rapid clearance in vivo limit their sustained action [ ]. Additionally, the mechanism of EV-mediated macrophage-chondrocyte-MSC interaction is not yet fully understood, and the long-term safety of EVs remains to be verified. Current research mainly relies on in vitro and animal models, which cannot fully simulate the complexity of human OA, highlighting the necessity of strengthening preclinical and clinical studies [ ].
In future research, it seems that cutting-edge technologies such as single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics can be utilized to deeply reveal new targets and key signaling pathways of EVs’ actions, providing a theoretical basis for precise treatment. At the same time, focusing on optimizing EVs preparation processes, improving delivery systems, and verifying their efficacy through clinical trials can enhance the therapeutic effect of EVs on OA [ ]. Moreover, combining EVs with cutting-edge technologies is expected to further enhance the treatment effect. By breaking through existing limitations and integrating innovative technologies, EVs therapy is expected to lead a revolution in OA treatment, ultimately improving patient prognosis and quality of life [ ].
