To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/.Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a resilient subpopulation within tumors, capable of driving progression, metastasis, and recurrence. One mechanism that enables this plasticity is asymmetric cell division (ACD), a process by which CSCs generate both a self-renewing stem cell and a differentiated daughter cell. While traditionally associated with tissue development, ACD is now recognized as a dynamic and regulated feature of cancer biology, particularly in response to stress conditions such as hypoxia and radiation. In this review, we provide a comprehensive and mechanistic synthesis of how intrinsic factors such as polarity complexes, cell fate determinants (CFDs), spindle orientation cooperate with extrinsic cues from the tumor microenvironment to orchestrate ACD in CSCs. We explore how this process contributes to tumor heterogeneity, therapy resistance, and the emergence of quiescent, drug-tolerant CSCs across multiple malignancies, including brain, breast, colorectal, and hematologic cancers. Importantly, we highlight recent efforts to pharmacologically disrupt or redirect ACD using inhibitors of NOTCH, WNT, AURORA kinases, and MSI1, presenting ACD as a therapeutic vulnerability rather than a static trait. By shifting the focus from CSC markers to division mode, this review introduces a novel conceptual framework for targeting tumor hierarchy and plasticity. Understanding and manipulating ACD offers a promising frontier in precision oncology - one where altering the balance of cell fate decisions could limit relapse, reduce intratumoral complexity, and enhance long-term treatment outcomes.

## Introduction
Cancer, often referred to as “the emperor of all maladies,” remains one of the most lethal diseases in human history. Despite significant advancements in cancer treatment, the disease remains largely uncurable due to its intra-tumoral heterogeneity [ ]. Solid tumors exhibit substantial cellular diversity, where some cells respond well to therapy, while others show resistance. Notably, a distinct subpopulation of tumor cells is inherently resistant to treatment, making cancer therapy particularly challenging. These rare and resilient cells, known as cancer stem cells (CSCs), are the primary cancerous cells present at the onset of tumor formation [ ].
CSCs exhibit essential properties such as multipotency, self-renewal, clonogenicity, and the ability to proliferate, all of which significantly contribute to tumor initiation and progression in various cancer types [ ]. The maintenance of CSC populations within tumors relies on both symmetric and Asymmetric cell division (ACD). Through ACD, CSCs produce one self-renewing cell and one differentiated progeny, fostering tumor heterogeneity. In contrast, symmetric cell division results in two daughter cells with identical characteristics. ACD is a key driver of tumor diversity, which is strongly linked to cancer persistence, patient prognosis, and response to therapy. Additionally, ACD is crucial for regulating the equilibrium between cellular proliferation and apoptosis. Any disruption in asymmetric division can shift the balance between self-renewal and differentiation in colon cancer-initiating cells (CCIC), ultimately influencing tumor expansion [ ]. Similar findings have been observed in CSCs from other cancer types [ ].

## Asymmetric cell division
ACD is a precisely regulated process influenced by both internal and external factors. This mechanism results in the formation of two daughter cells of unequal size [ ]. The larger daughter cell retains the characteristics of the original cell, while the smaller one, often referred to as the progenitor cell, undergoes multi-lineage differentiation in the future [ ]. ACD progresses through four key stages. First, a polarity axis is established. Second, cell fate determinants (CFD) proteins localize at specific cellular poles. Third, the spindle apparatus aligns according to the predetermined axis, ensuring the appropriate distribution of fate determinants proteins during mitosis. Finally, the process concludes with the successful separation of the daughter cells through cytokinesis [ ].

## Intrinsic factors
Polarity in ACD is established through specific proteins known as fate determinants. The positioning of these proteins defines two distinct poles: apical and basal. Two essential intrinsic components governing asymmetric division are fate-determining protein complexes and spindle assembly. Certain adaptor proteins play a crucial role in ensuring the asymmetric distribution of these determinants during cell division [ ]. In Drosophila neuroblasts, this process is regulated by both apical and basal determinants. Apical determinants include atypical protein kinase C (aPKC), partition defective 6 (PAR6), and lethal giant larvae [L(2)GL], while basal determinants consist of Miranda, Brat, and Prospero [ ].
During the interphase of normal cell cycles, aPKC localizes at the apical region, forming a complex with PAR6 and L(2)GL (Fig. ). Aurora A, a serine-threonine kinase, initiates apical signaling by phosphorylating PAR3 at Ser962, enabling its interaction with the PAR-6–aPKC complex to establish the PAR complex. Additionally, Aurora A phosphorylates PAR6, reducing its inhibitory effect on aPKC. This phosphorylation event activates aPKC, which in turn phosphorylates L(2)GL, releasing it and allowing PAR3 to associate with PAR6 and aPKC, forming the PAR3-PAR6-aPKC complex [ ] (Fig. ). Within this complex, aPKC phosphorylates NUMB at the apical cortex, causing NUMB to be displaced and confined to the basal side of the daughter cell [ ]. NUMB is a well-known inhibitor of Notch signaling, and its displacement leads to increased Notch activity, granting self-renewing potential to the apical daughter cell [ ](Fig. ). In mammalian stem cells, the small GTP-binding protein CDC42 facilitates the accumulation of the aPKC/PAR6/PAR3 complex at the apical region, ensuring the stability of the apical adherens junction [ , ].

 

Once the mitotic spindle and centrosomes are asymmetrically positioned, adaptor proteins like Miranda and Pon localize to the basal region of the cell [ ]. These adaptor proteins play a crucial role in recruiting fate determinants that drive differentiation. Specifically, Miranda facilitates the asymmetric distribution of key differentiation regulators, including Brat, a translational repressor, and Prospero, a homeodomain transcriptional repressor [ ](Fig. ). Within the basal determinant complex, Miranda undergoes degradation, which subsequently releases Prospero (known as PROX1 in vertebrates). This release activates a transcriptional program that promotes cellular differentiation [ ](Fig. ).
During metaphase and telophase, the mitotic spindle must be precisely aligned with the cell’s polarity axis during asymmetric stem cell division to ensure that fate determinants are distributed to only one of the daughter cells. This orientation results in both daughter cells remaining within the epithelial layer, maintaining tissue integrity [ ].
The cytoskeletal adaptor protein Inscuteable migrates to the apical region, where it is recruited by PAR-3, which subsequently facilitates the recruitment of Pins [ ]. The presence of Pins helps establish the asymmetric alignment of the mitotic spindle along the apical-basal polarity axis. Inscuteable interacts with the Dynein-Dynactin complex at the microtubule ends, effectively stabilizing spindle orientation. At the same time, microtubule-associated kinesin Khc73 forms a complex with Disc large (Dlg) and Pins on the same side. This larger assembly connects to the apical cytoskeleton via Inscuteable, ensuring accurate spindle positioning [ , ].
During asymmetric cytokinesis, the apical daughter cell, which is larger in size, inherits self-renew-promoting factors and remains as the stem cell lineage. In contrast, the smaller, basal cell that inherits differentiation factors, such as Numb, Prospero, and Brat, undergoes differentiation [ ]. Following unequal segregation into basal daughter cells, Numb induces differentiation by inhibiting Notch which is enriched in the apical side in mother cells and segregated into daughter stem cells. The transcription factors Pros and Brat, which inhibit ribosome biogenesis and cell growth, also function as differentiation-inducing fate determinants [ ].

## Extrinsic factors
The extracellular environment also plays a crucial role in regulating ACD. CSCs exist within a specialized microenvironment composed of neighboring non-CSC tumor cells, surrounding stroma, and the extracellular matrix, collectively referred to as the CSC niche [ ]. Certain microenvironmental factors, such as inflammatory cytokines and hypoxic conditions, can promote CSC survival and proliferation [ ].
Broad evidence indicates that cytokine networks, stromal inputs, and oxygen stress re-shape the niche to maintain stem programs and promote treatment resistance across many cancers [ ]. In parallel, extracellular vesicles (EVs) released by stromal, endothelial, and tumor cells shuttle proteins, miRNAs, and metabolites that reprogram recipient cells and can tilt divisions toward asymmetric or symmetric outcomes [ ]. Together, these cues help ensure that at least one daughter cell retains stem-like traits after mitosis.
During division, stem cells maintain their mitotic spindle perpendicular to the niche surface, ensuring that only one daughter cell remains in contact with the niche (Fig. ). While both daughter cells may initially appear identical, their differing proximities to the niche lead to distinct fates. The cell in direct contact with the niche retains its self-renewal capacity, whereas the other undergoes differentiation (Fig. ). Spatially restricted signaling and cell-cell junctions from the niche help sustain the undifferentiated state of the proximal daughter cell. Unlike intrinsic regulatory mechanisms that follow a predetermined program, niche-dependent extrinsic regulation offers greater adaptability [ ].

 

The tumor microenvironment appears to lack the polarity signals that typically guide ACD in CSCs, and the unequal distribution of CFDs may not always be essential for determining cell fate. Several potential explanations exist for this paradox. First, some residual tissue structures or specific cell types within the tumor microenvironment may still provide polarized cues for ACD. For instance, endothelial cells in tumors can act as niche components [ , ]. Second, the concept of “niche competition” may apply when niche availability is limited, and CSC interactions with the niche are not stably maintained. In such cases, only the progeny that remain near the niche retain CSC properties, while those displaced by more differentiated cells lose their stemness. This concept also known as neutral drift, has been described in normal tissue stem cell homeostasis, such as intestinal crypt stem cells [ , ]. Third, CSCs might have the ability to regenerate their own niche by producing various cell lineages that support their stem-like properties. Hematopoietic stem cell progeny, for example, are known to provide regulatory feedback to maintain stem cell populations [ ].
The ECM–integrin axis actively sustains stemness. Collagen I can maintain stem-like programs in both malignant and non-malignant settings. Engagement of α2β1 integrin by collagen promotes nuclear BMI1, a stemness-inducing transcription factor downstream of Hedgehog signaling; BMI1 is a Gli1 target, with FAK/Ras enhancing Gli1 expression. Laminin-511 supports breast CSC traits through α6β1 integrin in a TAZ-dependent manner, and nuclear TAZ elevates OCT4, SOX2, NANOG. Fibronectin’s extra domain A (EDA) likewise promotes self-renewal via α9β1 integrin/FAK/ERK/AKT/β-catenin signaling [ ].
Adhesion systems not only anchor cells but also orient divisions. Cadherins and integrins provide spindle-orientation cues in Drosophila and mammalian tissues. Niche-derived ligands can also impose intrinsic asymmetry (Fig. ): a nearby Wnt source—or immobilized Wnt3a—can align the division axis, bias LRP6/β-catenin distribution, and even skew centrosome inheritance in embryonic stem cells (Fig. ). Whether this is mediated by transcriptional changes downstream of Wnt or by more direct coupling to the cytoskeleton is still unresolved [ ]. More broadly, E-cadherin–based junctions and integrins (binding collagen/laminin or vascular ligands CD54/CD106) influence quiescence, signaling (e.g., FGF–N-cadherin), and spindle orientation; loss of these molecules randomizes division angles in tissues where geometry dictates symmetric vs. asymmetric outcomes (Fig. ) [ ]. Chemokines are common components in niche interactions. Notably, SDF-1α/CXCR4 ensures HSC maintenance and homing and is co-opted in cancers to localize CSCs and preserve stem-like potential (Fig. ) [ ].
In vivo, cancers show both symmetric and asymmetric divisions [ ], but—unlike normal precursors—the balance is shifted by oncogenic drivers, niche cues, tumor type, and available space [ ]. For example, ACD is common in papillomas, whereas symmetric division dominates in colon and breast cancers [ ]. Overall, the niche—through cytokines/hypoxia [ , ], EVs [ ], ECM–integrin–mechano-transduction pathways, and adhesion-guided spindle control—acts as a context-dependent selector that biases each CSC division toward self-renewal or differentiation [ , ].

## Organelle distribution in asymmetrical division
Besides molecules such as RNAs and proteins, organelles like lysosomes, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) are also distributed unevenly during cell division.

## Selective segregation of sister chromatids
Cells that divide asymmetrically often retain the older, template DNA strands in the less-differentiated daughter (Fig. ), consistent with the immortal-strand model: by keeping original templates, adult stem cells limit replication-borne mutations [ , ]. Stem cells adopt this approach, reducing mutation load by preferentially preserving these original DNA strands. Additionally, stem cells favor apoptosis over error-prone DNA repair, thereby improving their resilience to transient DNA damage. Thus, stem cells maintain genetic integrity, with mutations predominantly appearing and subsequently being eliminated in differentiated progeny [ ]. At the epigenetic level, 5-methylcytosine (5mC) is a stable, heritable mark used for gene regulation and transposon control. After replication, copying 5mC to the nascent strand requires an active maintenance step; if this process is incomplete (passive dilution) or if active demethylation occurs, sister chromatids can diverge in methylation density (Fig. ). Bisulfite sequencing has revealed strand-biased DNA demethylation, providing a route to epigenetic asymmetry between sisters (Fig. ). Beyond cytosine methylation, N6-methyladenine (6 mA) can also participate in inheritance logic: in C. elegans, 6 mA interacts with H3K4me2 during intergenerational transmission, and elevated 6 mA correlates with stemness, whereas removal of 6 mA favors differentiation [ ] (Fig. ). Finally, the histone variant H2A.Z preferentially marks chromosomes carrying “immortal” DNA strands, supporting controlled, non-random chromatid segregation during asymmetric divisions [ ].
Furthermore, the histone variant H2A.Z distinctly marks chromosomes containing “immortal” DNA strands, aiding the controlled, nonrandom segregation of DNA during asymmetric division [ , ].

## Histone
Numerous studies indicate that histone inheritance during stem cell division is asymmetrical. Research in Drosophila male germline stem cells (GSCs) has shown that pre-existing histones H3 and H4 predominantly remain within the stem-cell daughter, while newly synthesized histone H3 tends to accumulate in the differentiating cell (Fig. ). This selective histone allocation arises due to differing dissociation rates of PCNA from leading and lagging DNA strands during slowed or stalled replication, resulting in differential distribution of old versus new histone complexes [ ]. Furthermore, phosphorylation of histone H3 at threonine 3 acts as a marker, differentiating older histones from new variants, further mediating selective inheritance [ ]. Similar asymmetric distributions of H3 and H4 histones have also been observed in Drosophila intestinal stem cells [ , ]. However, contrasting reports indicate that histones H2A and H2B are evenly distributed during GSC division [ , ]. The distinct retention of older histones within stem cells suggests their role in preserving essential epigenetic information, while newly synthesized histones might undergo modifications necessary to trigger differentiation [ , ].

## Non-Coding RNAs in regulation of ACD
microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are central regulators of the balance between symmetric and ACD [ ]. Asymmetric lncRNA programs tune chromatin and translation: LincGET binds CARM1, promotes its nuclear entry, elevates H3R26me, and activates differentiation genes (e.g., SOX21) (Fig. ) [ ].

 

the lncRNA Cherub accumulates in the differentiating daughter via Staufen binding, yet cherub-high cells can later de-differentiate, linking its asymmetry to tumorigenesis; Staufen itself localizes RNAs asymmetrically and operates with ACD adaptors such as Miranda (Fig. ) [ , ].
Epigenetic/translational control also converges on fate: BRD4 and ITGB1 segregate with H3K56Ac [ ], while the antisense lncRNA LIBR represses BRD4 translation via RCK, lowering the fraction of ACD events, raising a therapeutic caution that systemic BRD4 inhibition could favor symmetric self-renewal (Fig. ) [ ].
miRNA–lncRNA circuits shape NOTCH and WNT/β-catenin axes that specify daughter fates. miR-34 directly represses NOTCH; when miR-34 partitions asymmetrically, one daughter favors stemness while the other differentiates, and lnc34a reinforces this by transcriptionally repressing miR-34a to create spatial imbalance [ , ]. H19 modulates WNT by sequestering hnRNP-U, acting as a decoy to protect c-MYC from let-7, and sponging miR-141/miR-22 [ ]. CCAT1 similarly relieves MYC repression via let-7, LINC01123 sponges miR-449b-5p to de-repress NOTCH1, and TUSC7 sponges miR-146 to preserve NUMB and restrain NOTCH signaling (Fig. ) [ , ].
In NSCLC, lncRNA PKMYT1AR sequesters miR-485-5p, boosting PKMYT1, blocking E3-ligase SCF-TrCP binding to β-catenin, and activating WNT [ ]. This, in turn, constitutively activates the Wnt pathway because it blocks the binding of the E3-ligase SCF-TrCP to-catenin and reduces its degradation [ ]. Among miRNAs controlling CSCs in lung traits: miR-128 suppresses B-cell-specific Moloney murine leukemia virus insertion site 1(BMI-1) and Mucin 1-C(MUC1), downshifting MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and WNT pathways; miR-19 targets glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), sustaining β-catenin signaling [ ].
In breast cancer miR-221 enhances CSC properties by repressing DNA methyltransferase 3b (DNMT3B) and de-repressing OCT4/NANOG [ ] miR-217 acts as oncogenic miRNA to enhance stem cell markers’ expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and promote tumorsphere formation by suppressing the expression of DKK1, a Wnt antagonist, leading to hyperactivation of the Wnt/-catenin pathway [ ].

## Mitochondria
As primary cellular energy producers, mitochondria must be accurately allocated during cell division, directly influencing cell fate. The older, less metabolically active mitochondria, characterized by reduced oxidative phosphorylation capacity, are preferentially segregated to the differentiated daughter cell, while younger, more metabolically active mitochondria are inherited by the stem-like daughter cell (Fig. ) [ , ]. This asymmetric distribution is regulated by mitochondrial fission and quality control mechanisms involving proteins such as Drp1 and Fis1. For instance, in human hematopoietic stem cells, studies have shown that mitochondria with higher membrane potential, indicative of greater metabolic activity, are selectively inherited by the daughter cell retaining stemness, correlating with increased expression of stemness markers and proliferative capacity [ ]. This differential mitochondrial inheritance leads to distinct metabolic profiles, with the stem-like daughter cell exhibiting enhanced ATP production and oxidative metabolism, supporting its self-renewal capacity, while the differentiated daughter cell adopts a more quiescent metabolic state conducive to terminal differentiation [ ].

## Lysosome
Lysosomes contain Notch receptors whose activation is facilitated by the organelle’s acidic environment. Consequently, lysosomes serve as central signaling hubs, creating differences in Notch pathway activity between daughter cells during asymmetric division [ ]. In human hematopoietic stem cells, lysosomes and metabolically active mitochondria are asymmetrically inherited through carefully regulated mechanisms [ ]. The uneven distribution of lysosomes impacts subsequent differences in cell-cycle progression, differentiation capacity, and the expression of stemness-related markers in daughter cells [ ].

## Endoplasmic reticulum
The ER, the largest membrane-bound cellular organelle involved in lipid and protein synthesis, requires precise partitioning during asymmetric division. Accurate ER distribution depends on asymmetrically formed astral microtubules, a conserved mechanism across multiple animal species. Consequently, daughter stem cells derived from asymmetric division receive a greater ER content compared to their differentiating counterparts [ ](Fig. ).
Collectively, these observations underscore the critical role of organelle inheritance patterns in shaping or reflecting distinct cellular identities resulting from asymmetric stem cell divisions.

## Mechanisms governing asymmetric cell division
For cells to effectively adjust to environmental stimuli, they must be capable of interpreting external cues. This responsiveness is shaped by their interactions with neighboring cells and the extracellular matrix, which play crucial roles in modulating cellular functions and responses [ ].
Whether in normal physiology or pathological conditions like cancer, the choice between symmetric and asymmetric division is influenced by a network of molecular regulators. Central to this regulatory framework are several well-established signaling cascades such as Wnt, Hedgehog, and Notch as well as essential components of the cell cycle including p53 and CDKs. These regulatory signals orchestrate key aspects of stem cell biology, from self-renewal to lineage specification, and govern the behavior of various stem cell populations like CSCs and NSCs across multiple tissues [ ].

## Role of Notch in stem cell regulation
The Notch pathway is a pivotal player in determining cell identity and managing the balance between proliferation and differentiation during tissue development [ ]. Disruptions in this pathway have been implicated in cancer initiation and progression. Notch functions in a context-dependent manner, acting as either a tumor promoter or suppressor. In the context of CSC division, Notch primarily supports oncogenic activity by sustaining stemness and influencing lineage commitment. Canonical Notch signaling is generally associated with the inhibition of differentiation, whereas its noncanonical counterpart often promotes it [ ].
Mammalian cells express four Notch receptors (Notch1 through Notch4), which interact with ligands from the Delta-like (DLL1, DLL3, DLL4) and Jagged (JAG1, JAG2) families [ ]. Upon ligand engagement, the receptor undergoes proteolytic processing via two cleavage events, first by ADAM-family metalloproteases, then by γ-secretase resulting in the release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). This domain moves into the nucleus, where it binds the transcription factor RBPJ (also called CSL) and forms a complex with co-activators such as MAML proteins. This complex initiates transcription of downstream targets like HES and HEY family genes, which govern cell cycle control and inhibit premature differentiation, thus preserving progenitor cell characteristics [ ].
Experimental evidence indicates that Notch1 and Notch2 accumulate in the nuclei of mammary progenitor cells. Constitutive activation of Notch4, however, causes aberrant mammary gland development and leads to poorly differentiated tumor phenotypes. In vitro, overactive Notch4 interferes with the normal cell division of breast epithelial cells [ ].
Aberrant Notch activity has also been connected to prolonged self-renewal in mammary stem cells and is frequently detected in epithelial lesions and various carcinomas, suggesting its involvement as a proto-oncogenic driver. Additionally, Notch signaling is involved in facilitating epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process often observed during CSC formation in pancreatic tumors [ ].
Animal models have shown that genetic ablation of Notch signaling components such as DLL1 or RBPJ accelerates differentiation of pancreatic progenitor cells into endocrine lineages. High Notch activity observed in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma highlights the role of this pathway in maintaining a self-renewing pool of CSCs [ ].
Overall, the Notch signaling network acts as a key modulator that determines when and how stem cells undergo asymmetric division, particularly under conditions of cellular stress or tissue regeneration demands.

## Numb: A regulator of cell fate decisions
Numb was the earliest cell fate determinant identified to be unevenly distributed between daughter cells during mitosis, thereby directing their distinct developmental trajectories [ ]. Its pivotal role lies in its ability to negatively regulate the Notch signaling pathway by binding to and suppressing Notch receptor activity, thereby modulating differentiation processes. Structurally, Numb associates with the cell membrane through an N-terminal myristoylation site and a domain enriched in positively charged residues, which likely aid its interaction with phospholipids [ ].
In mammals, alternative splicing of the Numb transcript gives rise to at least four protein isoforms (65–72 kDa) that execute diverse cellular functions. These include roles in vesicle trafficking, adhesion, cellular motility, substrate ubiquitination, and regulation of multiple signaling networks such as Notch, Hedgehog, and p53 pathways. Among these, their involvement in ACD and lineage commitment is particularly prominent. The four isoforms differ due to the selective inclusion of exons 3 and 9. Exon 9-containing variants are prevalent in undifferentiated cells and are downregulated in mature cell types. Tissue-specific regulation of exon 9 has been documented in the human brain, retina, pituitary gland, and in mouse cortical tissue [ , ].
Overexpression of exon 9-inclusive isoforms has been observed in multiple malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer, cervical squamous carcinoma, urothelial tumors, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Importantly, ERK/MAPK pathway activity promotes inclusion of exon 9 in certain cancers, suggesting a possible link to oncogenic transformation [ ].
Loss of Numb in mammary stem cells, especially alongside functional p53, skews cell division toward symmetric outcomes, fostering excessive stem cell renewal and expansion. At the progenitor level, Numb depletion triggers EMT, enhancing stem-like traits and contributing to the emergence of CSCs [ ].
Among the isoforms, Numb 2 and Numb 4 have been found to be markedly elevated in non-small cell lung cancers, where they activate Notch target gene expression and promote cell proliferation [ ].
Functionally, Numb limits CSC self-renewal by suppressing Notch signaling, which is commonly co-expressed with stemness markers like CD133 in undifferentiated daughter cells. In blood cancers, diminished Numb disrupts polarity, encouraging symmetric divisions. A notable drop in Numb expression is associated with leukemic progression from chronic to acute phases. Experimental models suggest that enhancing Numb levels can reduce symmetric renewal and restrain the growth of malignant hematopoietic progenitors [ ].

## Numb–Notch interactions
In dividing progenitor cells, establishing polarity is essential for generating diversity in daughter cell outcomes. A central player in this process is the adaptor protein Numb, which acts by blocking responsiveness to the Notch pathway. As cells divide, the side of the cell that receives Numb effectively “shuts off” Notch activity, favoring commitment to a specialized lineage. In contrast, the side without Numb maintains sensitivity to Notch, preserving characteristics of a stem-like state [ ].
Rather than functioning in a uniform way, Numb’s influence varies based on which isoform is expressed. The mammalian gene produces multiple variants through alternative splicing, which shape the behavior of cells by adjusting interactions with signaling networks such as Notch, p53, and Hedgehog. Some Numb isoforms tend to support stemness by modulating signal intensity, while others push cells toward differentiation by strongly repressing renewal-associated pathways. This partitioning process during mitosis is not passive; it is governed by cytoskeletal dynamics and protein transport mechanisms that ensure one daughter inherits more Numb than the other. As a result, only one cell in the pair is permitted to respond robustly to Notch cues. Disruptions in this delicate polarity either through absence or uniform distribution of Numb can have significant consequences, including unchecked proliferation or premature differentiation [ , ].
Crucially, the Numb–Notch axis functions as a gatekeeper, determining how a cell interprets its signaling environment. In the context of tissue maintenance or cancer, misregulation of this balance may tip stem cells toward abnormal expansion or loss of regenerative capacity. This makes Numb not just a marker of polarity, but a functional determinant of fate.

## Musashi proteins: regulators of asymmetric division and cancer stemness
Musashi-1 (MSI1), a highly conserved RNA-binding protein, has emerged as a critical factor in the regulation of asymmetric division, particularly in sensory organ precursor cells. Its elevated expression in several human malignancies highlights its role in stem cell maintenance, differentiation control, and tumor progression [ ]. MSI1 operates predominantly at the translational level, suppressing the synthesis of proteins that inhibit cell cycle progression. By binding directly to the mRNAs of cell cycle regulators such as p21, p27, and p53, MSI1 prevents their translation, allowing stem cells to bypass inhibitory checkpoints and proceed into the S phase of the cell cycle [ ].
In cancers of the breast and gastrointestinal tract, MSI1 has been associated with enhanced proliferation of CSCs. In particular, CD133-positive spheroid cultures derived from breast tumors exhibit increased MSI1 expression. Silencing MSI1 in these systems reduces proliferation and downregulates markers critical for stemness, including Notch1, Oct4, Sox2, and c-Myc [ ]. In hormone receptor-positive breast cancers (ER/PR+), which rely on asymmetric division to maintain their epithelial progenitor pool, MSI1 is implicated in sustaining the self-renewing potential of these cells [ , ].
MSI1 also modulates the Notch signaling pathway by targeting the mRNA of Numb, a known inhibitor of Notch. This interaction takes place at the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of the Numb transcript, repressing its translation without altering transcription levels. As a result, Notch signaling remains active, supporting the undifferentiated state of stem and progenitor cells [ ].
Musashi-2 (MSI2), a close homolog of MSI1, functions in a similar manner and plays a pivotal role in hematopoietic stem cells. Depletion of MSI2 leads to reduced hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell populations [ ], while its overexpression results in their expansion and has been implicated in leukemogenesis by sustaining Notch pathway activity. MSI2 functions as a repressor of NUMB, thereby enhancing Notch signaling. Its activation in hematopoietic stem cells has been linked to leukemia, driving neoplastic expansion in myeloid cells through the stimulation of this pathway [ ].
One of the most well-characterized downstream targets of Musashi proteins is the CDKN1A gene, which encodes the p21^WAF1 cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor. MSI1 binds conserved motifs within the 3′-UTR of CDKN1A mRNA, suppressing its translation and thereby removing a critical barrier to cell cycle progression. Experimental overexpression of MSI1 leads to reduced p21^WAF1 levels and significantly enhances entry into the S phase, promoting proliferation. This mechanism is particularly relevant in cancers such as colon and endometrial carcinoma, where silencing MSI1 leads to increased p21 expression and decreased tumor cell growth [ ].
In the mammary gland, MSI1 has been proposed as a marker of adult mammary stem cells. It supports the formation of luminal progenitor populations in mammary epithelial cells through the activation of both Notch and Wnt signaling cascades. Recent studies have identified MSI1, along with Notch1, as a central regulator of asymmetric division in human mammary epithelial stem cells [ ].

## Wnt signaling and its role in asymmetric division
Stem cells rely on their environment to make decisions about whether to stay in a regenerative state or begin the process of specialization. One of the most influential signaling systems guiding this decision is the Wnt pathway. Rather than simply turning genes on or off, Wnt signals serve as positional instructions telling cells where they are within a tissue, and what role they should adopt.
These signals work through a network of surface receptors and intracellular proteins. When Wnt molecules bind to receptors on a cell’s surface, they block a degradation process that normally keeps a protein called β-catenin at low levels. Stabilized β-catenin then moves into the nucleus, where it activates genes that help maintain stem-like qualities. In the absence of Wnt, this pathway is silent, and the cell is more likely to proceed toward differentiation [ ]. One striking feature of Wnt signaling is its role in asymmetric outcomes during stem cell division. Cells dividing within a tissue often experience different levels of Wnt depending on their position. The daughter cell closer to a high Wnt source retains stem properties, while the other, further away, begins a path toward becoming a mature cell. This uneven distribution of Wnt cues ensures that tissues stay balanced renewing themselves without losing structure or function [ ].
Beyond normal development, this signaling pathway also appears in disease. In certain cancers, especially those involving stem-like tumor cells, Wnt activity becomes abnormally high. This shift pushes cells to divide symmetrically, creating more stem-like cells instead of differentiated ones. For instance, in prostate cancer models, Wnt signaling is elevated in cells with high telomerase activity, which are known to initiate and sustain tumors. These cells also show increased expression of chromatin-remodeling proteins, further promoting long-term self-renewal [ ].
There’s also evidence that Wnt influences how cells handle their genetic material during division. In gastrointestinal cancers, blocking Wnt reduces the selective inheritance of original DNA strands a hallmark of asymmetric division suggesting that excess Wnt may override this protective mechanism [ ].
Altogether, Wnt is far more than a growth signal; it is a contextual coordinator, telling cells how to behave depending on where they are and who they might become. This makes it a compelling focus in regenerative medicine and cancer therapy, especially for targeting the balance between renewal and specialization in stem-like cell populations.

## p53 and its role in controlling division patterns in stem cells
While widely recognized for its role in protecting cells from becoming cancerous by stopping their growth or triggering cell death after DNA damage, p53 also shapes how stem cells divide [ ]. Instead of merely blocking abnormal cells, it helps decide whether a stem cell will create another identical stem cell or one that begins to specialize. In normal circumstances, p53 helps limit unnecessary stem cell expansion especially after DNA stress. Interestingly, early stem cells are often less responsive to this protein, making them more likely to bypass protective checkpoints that would otherwise slow or stop the cell cycle [ ]. When p53 is missing or dysfunctional, stem cells often undergo balanced division, producing two self-renewing cells instead of one. This can lead to an overgrowth of undifferentiated cells and accelerate tumor development [ ].
In cancer, when p53 is lost, another molecule, p21, can sometimes take over part of its role. But this compensatory pathway often leads to an increase in stem-like cancer cells, as p21 doesn’t enforce asymmetric splitting as effectively. When researchers restore p53 activity in cancer models, it shifts the pattern of division, reducing the number of tumor-initiating cells [ ]. This highlights the importance of the p53–p21 interaction in deciding whether a stem cell should maintain itself or create a more specialized cell [ ].
Conditions in the surrounding tissue also influence this balance. Environments that lack proper p53 signaling tend to promote equal chromosome distribution, resulting in unchecked stem cell growth. But reactivating p53 encourages the use of older, more stable DNA during division an indicator of regulated, directional cell fate [ ].
In breast tissue, elevated p53 levels have been associated with slower cell population growth, largely because more stem cells divide in a way that limits self-renewal. A protein called Numb further supports this function by stabilizing p53 and shielding it from degradation, which is usually triggered by a protein called MDM2 [ , ].

## Integrated regulatory network of pathways governing asymmetric cell division
While individual signaling pathways such as NOTCH, WNT, and p53 play pivotal roles in regulating ACD, their functions are not isolated; instead, they form an interconnected network that dynamically balances self-renewal, differentiation, and adaptation to microenvironmental cues in CSCs. This network involves crosstalk at multiple levels, including shared downstream effectors, feedback loops, and modulation by RNA-binding proteins like Musashi (MSI1/MSI2) and cell fate determinants like NUMB.
Central to this integration is the NUMB-NOTCH axis, which acts as a hub for pathway convergence. NUMB, asymmetrically distributed during ACD, inhibits NOTCH signaling in the differentiating daughter cell by promoting ubiquitination and degradation of the NOTCH intracellular domain (NICD) [ ]. This inhibition is reinforced by p53, which stabilizes NUMB and prevents its degradation by MDM2, thereby favoring ACD and limiting symmetric self-renewal [ ]. In parallel, MSI1/MSI2 represses NUMB translation, sustaining NOTCH activity in the stem-like daughter cell. This creates a feedforward loop where active NOTCH upregulates MSI1 expression, further amplifying stemness [ ].
WNT signaling intersects this network by stabilizing β-catenin, which translocates to the nucleus and promotes transcription of stemness genes (e.g., MYC, SOX2). WNT/β-catenin activity is enhanced in the apical (stem-like) daughter during ACD and can indirectly antagonize NUMB via miR-146a-mediated repression in colorectal CSCs, favoring symmetric division under inflammatory conditions [ ]. Conversely, NOTCH can synergize with WNT in maintaining CSC quiescence under hypoxia, where HIF-1α stabilizes β-catenin and upregulates NOTCH ligands like DLL4 [ ]. p53 adds another layer by suppressing WNT-driven symmetric division through p21, which enforces ACD in response to DNA damage or radiation [ ].
Extrinsic factors further modulate this network: hypoxia stabilizes HIFs, which activate NOTCH and WNT while repressing p53, shifting toward symmetric division for CSC expansion [ , ]. Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6) activate NF-κB, which intersects with WNT/PI3K/AKT to promote EMT and ACD plasticity [ ]. Disruptions in this network, such as NUMB loss or MSI1 overexpression, skew toward symmetric division, driving tumor heterogeneity and resistance [ , ].
This integrated framework underscores how pathway crosstalk enables CSCs to adapt division modes contextually, offering potential multi-target therapeutic strategies (e.g., combined NOTCH/WNT inhibitors) to disrupt ACD-driven relapse.

## Radiation-Induced division bias in Stem-Like tumor cells
The way cells divide isn’t determined solely by internal programming environmental forces, including medical treatments like radiation, can shift how cells choose to reproduce. In the case of CSCs, radiation does more than cause damage; it may influence how these cells divide, especially in their choice between making identical copies or producing cells with different identities.
Several observations suggest that after repeated radiation doses, some tumor cells particularly those with stem-like traits, become more resilient and adjust their division pattern to support survival. This shift can involve asymmetric division, which allows one daughter to retain the ability to regenerate while the other differentiates, helping the tumor rebuild after therapy. Some studies also point to radiation contributing indirectly to genetic changes, which may tilt the balance between symmetric and asymmetric divisions over time. Radio-oncologists hypothesize that radiation-induced depletion of healthy tissue is restored by ACD-driven repopulation of daughter cells [ ]. A similar process is observed in tumors, where ACD replenishes progeny cells following radiation. Consequently, researchers have demonstrated that cancer recurrence after radiation is linked to the ACD of CSCs [ ](Fig. ).

 

At the molecular level, exposure to radiation activates signaling systems that are closely tied to cell identity decisions. One such pathway, Notch, becomes active in stem-like cancer cells and may direct one daughter cell to enter a dormant state, effectively preserving it from further damage [ , ]. Another pathway, involving TGF-β, also responds to radiation, possibly contributing to changes in how tumors maintain structural balance during regrowth [ ].
This growing body of evidence has prompted scientists to reconsider the effects of radiation. While its role in destroying tumor cells is well-established, its influence on how surviving cells divide and rebuild the tissue environment is still unfolding and could explain why relapse sometimes occurs even after initially effective therapy.

## Hypoxia and its influence on asymmetric cell division
When cells are deprived of oxygen, their behavior changes dramatically. This low-oxygen condition, known as hypoxia, is common in solid tumors due to a mismatch between how quickly cancer cells multiply and how slowly new blood vessels form to nourish them. As the tumor grows, its center often becomes oxygen-starved, while the outer regions continue to receive blood and nutrients [ ]. This layered structure gives rise to three functional zones: an active outer ring where cells divide, a middle region where oxygen becomes scarce and cell activity slows, and a core where the environment becomes so harsh that many cells die. Cells in the oxygen-poor areas adapt by switching on survival mechanisms controlled by a family of proteins known as hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which help them cope with stress [ ](Fig. ).

 

Each HIF molecule includes an oxygen-sensitive α-subunit (like HIF-1α or HIF-2α) and a constitutively expressed β-subunit. Under normoxic conditions, the α-subunit is rapidly degraded (Fig. ) However, in hypoxia, it becomes stabilized, translocates to the nucleus, and forms a functional complex with its β counterpart. This complex then binds to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in the genome, regulating genes involved in metabolism, angiogenesis, cell survival, pH regulation, and metastasis [ ](Fig. ). HIF-1α, in particular, is commonly elevated in stem cells under low-oxygen conditions and is believed to play a role in maintaining their regenerative capacity [ ]. The role of hypoxia in shaping CSC behavior is becoming increasingly clear. Hypoxic conditions help preserve the self-renewal capacity of CSCs and contribute to therapy resistance. In one study, cancer-associated fibroblasts in low-oxygen tumor regions activated the transcription factor GLI2 through Hedgehog signaling, reinforcing stem-like properties and protecting CSCs from chemotherapy [ ]. Interestingly, the effects of hypoxia on ACD appear to vary depending on the context. In normal myoblasts, low oxygen levels may promote ACD, but in lung cancer cells, hypoxia especially when paired with serum deprivation can impair this process [ , ].
Reoxygenation, or the return of oxygen to previously hypoxic tumor areas, has been shown to reverse this suppression and reinitiate ACD in CSCs. In normal stem cells, HIF-2α also plays a role in driving differentiation by influencing key signaling networks like PI3K/Akt/FoxO [ ]. This dynamic shift from hypoxia-induced dormancy to oxygen-driven division may help explain how CSCs survive harsh environments and reemerge following treatment [ ].
In line with these findings, Hurt et al. reported that when prostate cancer cells are exposed to low oxygen and serum-free conditions, they lose differentiated markers and gain stem-like features, further highlighting how metabolic stress influences cell fate [ ]. The oxygenation state whether hypoxic or reoxygenated can therefore have opposing effects on ACD, either inhibiting or promoting division asymmetry depending on timing and conditions [ ].
Inflammation within the tumor microenvironment further amplifies this process. Cytokines like IL-6 and IL-8, which are frequently elevated in cancer, activate NF-κB signaling and promote glycolysis via the PI3K/AKT pathway. These metabolic shifts enhance the self-renewal capacity of CSCs and contribute to tumor progression and spread. Targeted therapies that interfere with IL-6 and IL-8 signaling are currently under investigation as potential strategies to selectively eliminate CSCs [ ].
Moving forward, there is a growing need to better understand how shifts in cellular metabolism, particularly under hypoxic stress, influence CSC division behavior and therapeutic resistance. Future therapies may benefit from combining metabolic intervention with agents that block hypoxia-responsive pathways and cytokine signaling to more effectively target cancer at its regenerative root.

## Asymmetric cell division and drug resistance
CSCs are highly adaptive and capable of shifting between multiple cellular states. These transitions may involve changes in activity levels ranging from dormancy to active proliferation or shifts in identity between stem-like and more differentiated forms. This remarkable flexibility helps CSCs evade therapeutic attacks and contributes significantly to tumor regrowth and long-term resistance [ ].
At the heart of this adaptability is the stem cell’s ability to undergo both symmetric and asymmetric divisions. In the latter case, one daughter cell retains self-renewal capacity while the other moves toward differentiation. However, when environmental stressors like chemotherapy pressure the tumor, a switch to symmetric division may occur, expanding the CSC population and making the cancer more aggressive and difficult to treat [ ].
This behavior fuels intratumoral diversity, leading to inconsistent responses to treatment across patient populations [ ]. One proposed mechanism involves the division of tumor cells into two distinct populations: one that divides rapidly and supports tumor expansion, and another that divides slowly and avoids detection by drugs designed to target fast-cycling cells. These slower cells often serve as a reservoir for recurrence after therapy has been completed [ ].
In breast cancer, asymmetric division of fast-dividing cells can give rise to daughter cells with a dormant, G0-like profile marked by lower levels of AKT and reactive oxygen species (ROS), and higher expression of Hes1. This pattern appears to rely on uneven inhibition of AKT signaling during the final phase of cell division [ ]. Additional evidence from triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) suggests that specific subpopulations with mixed characteristics—such as K14+K18+ progenitor-like cells arise through asymmetric division. Granit et al. demonstrated that manipulating the balance between luminal and progenitor-like cells in TNBC can alter treatment resistance and tumor behavior [ ].
In colorectal cancer, two primary stem-like cell populations have been described. One is fast-cycling and dependent on MYC signaling, expressing markers like LGR5 and CD44, while the other grows more slowly and expresses genes such as BMI1, hTERT, and HOPX. Interestingly, these two groups are not locked into fixed states. Through asymmetric division, they can transition back and forth, providing the tumor with both rapid growth and resistance potential [ , ]. The ability of CSCs to divide asymmetrically also supports the unequal inheritance of protective traits. For example, some daughter cells preferentially inherit membrane-bound drug efflux pumps such as ABCB1 and ABCG2, which actively remove chemotherapeutic agents. In neuroblastoma models, this has been shown to create one daughter cell that is highly drug-resistant and another that is more drug-sensitive, contributing to the persistence of therapy-resistant subpopulations [ ].
Another contributor to treatment failure is the presence of polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs). These cells contain multiple sets of chromosomes and show altered transcriptional activity. As they undergo meiosis-like depolyploidization, PGCCs often divide asymmetrically, giving rise to daughter cells with stem-like features. Elevated expression of proteins like cyclin D1 and cyclin E key regulators of the cell cycle appears to support this process, helping PGCCs maintain both growth potential and resistance to chemotherapy [ ].
Ironically, standard treatments that aim to kill cancer by targeting rapidly dividing cells may inadvertently strengthen the very population most capable of surviving the slow-cycling, stem-like cells. Over time, this selection pressure fosters relapse and persistent resistance, particularly when asymmetric division is involved in preserving or generating treatment-resistant clones [ ].

## Asymmetric division in different cancers

## Colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality both in the United States and globally. Colorectal cancer stem cells (CCSCs) exhibit strong resistance to conventional chemotherapy, and exposure to cytotoxic treatments has been shown to increase the proportion of CCSCs within CRC tumors [ ]. The regulation of CCSC fate, balancing between self-renewal and differentiation, is primarily influenced by the Notch and Wnt signaling pathways, both of which are downstream targets of CFDs like Numb. Notably, studies have revealed that asymmetric distribution of Numb occurs in approximately 24% of CCSC divisions, while the majority still follow a symmetric division pattern [ , ]. Additionally, microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play a crucial role in modulating the transition between symmetric and ACD. MicroRNAs, a class of small non-coding RNAs, regulate gene expression by binding to the 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of target mRNAs, either suppressing their translation or triggering their degradation [ ]. Elevated expression of miR-34a drives ACD, leading to the formation of differentiated tumor cells. Conversely, silencing miR-34a disrupts asymmetric division and favors symmetric self-renewal, ultimately enhancing the proliferation of colorectal cancer-initiating cells (CCICs) in both sphere cultures in vitro and xenograft models in vivo. Notably, miR-34a is frequently silenced in tumors from advanced-stage (III and IV) CRC patients, which promotes the predominance of symmetric divisions, resulting in more uniform and undifferentiated tumor populations [ ]. Beyond its role in cell division, miR-34a regulates multiple genes involved in various cellular functions. Acting downstream of the p53 signaling pathway, miR-34a directly targets key molecules such as Wnt1 and MAP2K1 (which influence proliferation), Foxp1 (linked to pluripotency), MycN (an oncogene), SIRT1 and HDAC1 (epigenetic regulators), CD44 (associated with adhesion and migration), and BCL2 (involved in apoptosis). This broad regulatory network allows miR-34a to serve as a critical determinant in CSC fate decisions, positioning it as a master regulator of asymmetric division [ ].
Similarly, the overexpression of miR-Let-7 A has been shown to promote ACD in CSCs. miR-Let-7 A reduces the core pluripotency network of stem cells by targeting transcription factors such as Lin28A, Lin28B which are essential for maintaining self-renewal in normal stem cells. In fact, Wnt pathway-driven self-renewal can be suppressed by increased Let-7 A expression, further highlighting its role in modulating CSC behavior [ ].
CCICs expressing Snail, miR-146 directly targets Numb, leading to the stabilization of β-catenin, a key effector of Wnt signaling, thereby promoting symmetric cell division. In turn, Wnt signaling enhances the expression of both Snail and miR-146, creating a self-perpetuating feedback loop [ ]. Notably, suppressing miR-146a, a crucial downstream mediator of Snail signaling, has been shown to induce ACD and diminish the self-renewal capacity of CSCs [ ]. Snail plays a pivotal role in maintaining the mesenchymal characteristics of CSCs and driving EMT. Overexpression of miR-146a enhances Wnt signaling by preventing the proteasomal degradation of β-catenin, which is normally regulated by Numb. This shift favors symmetric division, leading to the expansion of the CSC population. Current research suggests that CCIC fate determination is predominantly governed by Notch and Wnt signaling pathways, both of which are essential for CCIC self-renewal. Emerging evidence indicates that stem cells may activate ACD in response to stress or during tissue repair following damage [ ]. Furthermore, under inflammatory conditions, fast-cycling Lgr5 + crypt base columnar (CBC) cells and slow-cycling + 4 Bmi1 + cells can undergo direct interconversion through ACD, a phenomenon that has also been observed in CCICs [ , ]. This suggests that the ability of CCICs to undergo ACD may have originated as an adaptive stress response mechanism in normal colonic stem cells [ ].

## Brain tumor
Glioblastoma (GBM), one of the most aggressive forms of brain cancer, is sustained by a subpopulation of CSCs that share molecular characteristics with neural stem cells (NSCs). Both human NSCs and glioma-derived CSCs commonly express markers like Nestin and CD133 (Prominin-1), a transmembrane protein concentrated at cellular projections such as microvilli. In glioblastoma, CD133 is more than a surface marker—it correlates with resistance to radiation therapy and a higher likelihood of tumor recurrence [ ]. Recent research has uncovered that the glycoprotein progranulin plays a pivotal role in reinforcing stemness in CD133 + cells. It does so by promoting CD133 expression, activating DNA repair mechanisms, and upregulating genes associated with stem cell behavior [ ]. In addition to their ability to self-renew, CD133 + CSCs have been shown to divide asymmetrically. High-resolution studies of single GBM cells reveal that CD133 localizes to one side of the cell cortex during mitosis, creating an unequal distribution between the daughter cells. This behavior depends on mitotic regulators such as Polo-like kinase 1 (Plk1), hinting at the presence of a specialized checkpoint system that coordinates polarity and division symmetry in resistant CSCs. CD133 also interfaces with pro-survival signaling. It binds to the regulatory component p85 of the PI3K/Akt pathway. one of the most frequently activated cascades in gliomas—amplifying signals that drive tumor growth and stem cell persistence [ ].
Meanwhile, Numb, a known cell fate determinant, has been observed to segregate asymmetrically in CD133-high glioblastoma cells. further reinforcing the idea that asymmetric division underpins heterogeneity in GBM [ ].
Interestingly, CD133 + cells tend to proliferate more slowly than their CD133 counterparts and are less affected by MAPK pathway inhibition, including drugs targeting BRAF mutations [ ]. This may be due to their reliance on asymmetric division, which produces quiescent or therapy-resistant progeny. Targeting molecules that regulate this division mode could potentially reprogram these cells to divide symmetrically, expanding their numbers and paradoxically making them more vulnerable to chemotherapy [ ].
Not all brain tumor propagating cells use asymmetric division. Cells marked by NG2, derived from untreated glioma patients, primarily undergo symmetric divisions [ ]. These NG2 + cells appear to lack long-term self-renewal capacity and may instead differentiate over time, which could partially explain the slower disease progression seen in some gliomas, such as oligodendrogliomas. Their continuous symmetric cycling may also increase their sensitivity to chemotherapy, as they avoid entering a quiescent state that often shelters CSCs from drug effects.
The broader implications of these findings touch on why some brain tumor subtypes are more treatable than others. The division strategy used by cancer cells whether symmetric or asymmetric can significantly influence therapeutic response. For example, both embryonic and adult NSCs, along with oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, have been shown to divide asymmetrically under certain conditions, whereas astrocytes tend to divide symmetrically. This suggests that the capacity for asymmetric division in CSCs may be influenced by microenvironmental factors, developmental origin, or intrinsic programming [ ].
Understanding how division polarity emerges and why it is retained in some tumor cells but not others may open new pathways for treating highly resistant brain cancers.

## Hematologic malignancies
The process of blood formation depends on hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) making a decision: either produce another stem cell to preserve the pool or give rise to a cell that matures into one of many blood lineages. These decisions are not random. They’re shaped by the context in which the cell divides, and often by the orientation of that division. Asymmetric division, in particular, is one mechanism that allows these stem cells to balance renewal with differentiation over time [ ]. However, in certain blood cancers like AML and CML, the control over these division choices is lost. One example of this disruption involves the fusion oncogene NUP98-HOXA9, which has been found to tip the balance toward continuous self-renewal when expressed in early hematopoietic cells. When these cells are placed in supportive environments (like with stromal cells), they tend to divide symmetrically, expanding the malignant clone instead of contributing to functional blood cell development [ ].
Proteins associated with cell polarity such as Llgl1 seem to act as internal stabilizers of healthy division modes. In leukemia, a loss of Llgl1 expression has been linked to poor outcomes, likely due to its role in preserving the ability of cells to differentiate. In fact, therapies that promote differentiation tend to improve survival in both AML and CML, supporting the idea that restoring division balance could be beneficial [ ].
Notch signaling and its regulators are also implicated in leukemia’s manipulation of division symmetry. As disease advances, factors that normally suppress excessive Notch activity like Numb are diminished, while inhibitors of Numb, including the RNA-binding protein Musashi, become more prominent. The result is a microenvironment where leukemic stem cells are encouraged to renew rather than differentiate. This pattern has been observed not only in gene expression studies but also in experimental models, where knocking down Musashi or reintroducing Numb can slow disease progression [ , ]. Division polarity isn’t just about what genes are active—it also depends on how cells physically align their internal machinery. Proteins like Lis1 are essential for correct spindle orientation. Without them, divisions become randomly aligned, which ironically seems to increase the frequency of asymmetric divisions, but in a way that may be biologically disorganized or unstable [ ].
Some of the same polarity networks that govern early embryonic development are at play here. The distribution of fate regulators like Numb depends on protein complexes such as Scribble, Dlg and Lgl. When these are impaired, the ability of stem cells to maintain normal renewal patterns diminishes, potentially driving leukemogenesis through unchecked expansion of poorly differentiated cells. Understanding these polarity-driven shifts in division mode is key to decoding why leukemia persists and often resists therapy. By restoring the machinery that governs how these cells split—either genetically or pharmacologically, future therapies may be able to exhaust leukemic stem cell pools more effectively [ ].

## Breast cancer
In certain aggressive breast tumors, a small number of cells slow down their proliferation dramatically behaving more like dormant or quiescent cells than active tumor drivers. These cells often emerge through an uneven division process, where only one of the resulting daughter cells maintains a low-activity profile. Such cells tend to show reduced metabolic activity, low levels of proliferation markers like MCM2 and MKI67, and diminished AKT signaling. Interestingly, this behavior seems to result from a selective downregulation of AKT during cell division, a feature that does not appear exclusive to any single subpopulation but may instead be an adaptable trait that cancer cells acquire under stress [ ].
Further exploration into the mechanics behind this behavior pointed to a network of molecular players—β1-integrin, FAK, mTORC2, and AKT1—that collectively guide how rapidly dividing cancer cells split into one highly active daughter and one that adopts a slower, more resistant profile [ ]. These slow-dividing cells, though less involved in immediate tumor growth, are notoriously hard to eliminate through chemotherapy and may be a reservoir for recurrence when treatment ends. Some breast cancer cell populations also demonstrate the ability to divide in a way that preserves the original DNA strands unequally between daughters—a marker of asymmetric division. This mode of division appears to enhance their ability to migrate and invade, traits commonly linked with metastatic potential. On the flip side, when p53 is mutated or Notch signaling remains chronically active, the balance tilts toward symmetric division, leading to an expansion of the more aggressive, stem-like cells [ , ]. Evidence also suggests that in mammary CSCs, asymmetric division generates daughter cells that are less metabolically active and more resistant to chemotherapy. These cells, while not driving rapid tumor expansion, act as a hidden force behind treatment failure and relapse [ , ].

## Other cancers
ACD has been observed in various types of cancer. In lung cancer, CD133-positive CSCs undergo ACD, leading to the asymmetric segregation of template DNA into the CSC daughter cell [ ]. Research indicates that reducing ACD in lung adenocarcinoma can shrink tumor size by more than 90% [ ].
Similarly, metastatic gastric CSCs with high CXCR4 expression exhibit ACD. Treatment with TGF-β not only induces ACD but also promotes differentiation, thereby increasing the effectiveness of docetaxel therapy. This combined approach has even shown efficacy against dormant gastric CSCs [ ].
In human thyroid cancer, a subset of CSCs retains labeled DNA and divides either symmetrically or asymmetrically. ACD in these CSCs is associated with increased cellular diversity and heightened chemotherapy resistance [ ]. ACD has also been documented in prostate cancer. During the development of the prostatic epithelium, luminal cells predominantly undergo symmetrical division, whereas basal cells divide through both symmetrical and asymmetrical mechanisms [ ]. These differing division patterns contribute to the hierarchical structure of prostate epithelial cells. Within both normal and tumor prostate tissues, ACD helps maintain monoallelic regulation of TMPRSS2 transcription, which becomes more relaxed as cells differentiate [ ]. Additionally, research by Qin et al. identified distinct functional characteristics between Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) negative and PSA-positive prostate cancer cells. They found that PSA-negative cells undergo ACD, displaying greater proliferative capacity and resistance, whereas PSA-positive cells primarily undergo symmetric division [ ] ACD has also been reported in CSC-like cells within ovarian cancer cell lines [ ]. Furthermore, a shift from asymmetric to symmetric division has been proposed as a contributing mechanism in oral cancer development [ ].

## Therapeutic targeting of asymmetric cell division in cancer
Efforts to disrupt cancer at its roots increasingly focus on eliminating CSCs, which often serve as the engine behind recurrence and metastasis. One of the key challenges in targeting CSCs lies in their ability to divide asymmetrically—a process that not only sustains their own population but also produces more differentiated tumor cells that contribute to overall tumor growth.
This asymmetric division gives CSCs an edge in surviving conventional therapies. While one daughter cell remains stem-like and treatment-resistant, the other fuels the tumor bulk, making complete eradication difficult. Because of this, the molecular machinery that governs ACD has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target [ ]. In the search for more effective treatment strategies, researchers have begun to explore chemical compounds that can interfere with this process. The goal is twofold: to prevent the self-renewal of CSCs and to push them toward a state where they are more vulnerable to standard therapies. The following section reviews several promising agents that disrupt ACD and discusses how they could be integrated into future cancer treatment approaches.

## NOTCH inhibitors
Some of the most therapy-resistant cancer cells rely on the NOTCH signaling pathway to preserve their identity and divide unevenly—a strategy that helps them escape treatment and repopulate tumors. To interfere with this process, researchers have turned their attention to blocking NOTCH activation at its core: the γ-secretase complex.
Among the small-molecule γ-secretase inhibitors (GSIs), DBZ (C₂₆H₂₃F₂N₃O₃) (dibenzazepine) stands out for its ability to block the proteolytic cleavage of NOTCH, thereby preventing the release of the active intracellular domain (NICD). This suppression reduces the expression of downstream effectors such as HES1 and HEY2, ultimately limiting CSC-driven tumor growth in several cancers, including breast, lung, and colorectal [ , ].
Another widely studied GSI, DAPT, has demonstrated inhibitory effects on asymmetric division. In glioblastoma cells, DAPT treatment leads to marked reductions in Hes1 levels and disrupts NOTCH signaling by indirectly modulating NUMB, a protein essential for regulating division polarity. Its effects extend beyond ACD—DAPT has been shown to reduce the overall CSC population while increasing the proportion of more differentiated, non-stem-like cancer cells [ , ].
MK-0752, another potent NOTCH inhibitor, impacts both CSC phenotype and inflammation-related signaling. By lowering IL-6 expression and suppressing key genes like NICD, MYC, HEY1, HES5, and HES1, MK-0752 significantly impairs the stemness profile of CD44+/CD24 − and ALDH + CSC populations in breast cancer [ ].
Beyond these compounds, additional NOTCH-targeting agents such as Brontictuzumab, Tarextumab, RO4929097, LY900009, and MRK-003 have shown potential in preclinical models. These inhibitors work by either blocking NOTCH ligand-receptor interactions or interfering with downstream gene activation, and they appear to disrupt CSC expansion by altering division symmetry [ , – ].
As research evolves, targeting asymmetric division through selective NOTCH inhibition could offer a valuable approach for weakening CSC-driven resistance and improving long-term treatment outcomes.

## WNT inhibitors
The WNT/β-catenin pathway is a well-recognized regulator of CSC behavior, particularly in how these cells divide and maintain their stemness. In the context of ACD, WNT activation often occurs selectively in one daughter cell, helping sustain tumor-initiating capacity and contributing to therapeutic resistance [ ].
One compound of interest is Axitinib, an FDA-approved drug typically known for its anti-angiogenic properties. Interestingly, it has been reported to interfere with WNT signaling during CSC division by selectively promoting β-catenin degradation in the nucleus of one daughter cell. This disruption reinforces asymmetric division, supporting CSC survival and possibly complicating cancer treatment outcomes. While Axitinib’s effects may offer regenerative benefits in stem cell biology, its influence on CSC maintenance raises concerns in oncology, particularly where residual stem-like cells are involved in recurrence [ ].
Other small molecules with WNT-suppressing capabilities have also shown potential in targeting CSCs across a range of tumor types. Agents such as Disulfiram, Sulforaphane, FH-535, Sorafenib, and PNU-74,654 have demonstrated inhibitory effects in models of prostate, ovarian, and hepatic cancers [ , ]. Sorafenib, a multi-kinase inhibitor, is known for its role in targeting RAF/MEK/ERK signaling but also exerts significant effects on the WNT/β-catenin pathway. In HCC, Sorafenib has been shown to suppress WNT signaling by reducing β-catenin stability and inhibiting its nuclear translocation, thereby decreasing the expression of stemness-associated genes such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1 [ ]. This suppression shifts CSC division from symmetric, which expands the stem-like pool, to asymmetric, producing differentiated progeny with reduced tumor-initiating capacity. For instance, it has been demonstrated that Sorafenib, when combined with the Wnt inhibitor PRI-724, synergistically induces apoptosis and reduces clonogenicity in HCC cell lines (Hep3B, HepG2, MHCC-97 H) by inhibiting β-catenin/CBP/c-Myc and ERK/AKT signaling, thereby decreasing the CSC population [ ].
PNU-74,654 specifically inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by disrupting the interaction between β-catenin and TCF/LEF transcription factors, thereby blocking the transcription of genes critical for CSC maintenance. In CRC, PNU-74,654 reduces tumor growth by inhibiting cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and decreasing migration through modulation of Cyclin D1, survivin and.
E-cadherin [ ]. In pancreatic cancer, PNU-74,654 induces G1 cell cycle arrest and inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ].

## AURORA kinases inhibitors
Mitotic regulation plays a crucial role in determining how cancer cells respond to therapeutic stress. Among key regulators, the AURORA kinase family particularly AURORA is essential for proper centrosome function and spindle organization during cell division. Interestingly, recent evidence suggests that inhibition of this kinase does not always trigger cell death. Instead, it may push tumor cells into an altered state of dormancy, forming PGCCs with the potential to survive treatment and re-enter the cell cycle [ ]. These PGCCs, though initially non-proliferative, have demonstrated the capacity to regenerate daughter cells through ACD once drug pressure is removed. This ability suggests a survival mechanism that favors the preservation of stem-like traits and contributes to tumor relapse [ , ].
The selective AURORA A kinase inhibitor Alisertib (MLN8237) has been extensively studied for its mitotic disruption effects. It blocks critical autophosphorylation at the Thr228 site, which is necessary for kinase activation and function during spindle assembly [ ]. Treatment with Alisertib has been shown to impair bipolar spindle formation and increase chromosomal misalignment, particularly in cancer stem-like cells undergoing ACD [ ].
Although these findings highlight the potential of AURORA inhibition to reduce proliferation, they also reveal an adaptive route where cancer cells temporarily evade mitotic stress and later reemerge with increased resilience. As a result, understanding the relationship between mitotic blockade, ACD, and stem cell–like survival is essential to optimizing the therapeutic window and avoiding unintended enrichment of drug-resistant subclones.

## Other molecules
Beyond the well-characterized signaling pathways, several polarity-related proteins have emerged as key regulators of ACD. Among these, PAR6, PAR3, and atypical PKC (aPKC) have been shown to coordinate cellular polarity and fate decisions in both normal and cancerous tissues.
A critical interaction occurs between PAR6 and PKC through their PB1 domains—a connection that appears essential for the proper execution of ACD. Disruption of this interaction has been investigated as a therapeutic strategy. In particular, Aurothioglucose (ATG) and Aurothiomalate (ATM), two gold-based compounds traditionally used in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, have been found to inhibit this PB1-mediated interaction. Preclinical studies suggest that these agents can impair ACD in cancer cells, offering a potential avenue to suppress tumor progression and recurrence driven by CSCs [ ]. Meanwhile, PAR3—another component of the PAR6/aPKC complex has attracted interest for its broader roles beyond polarity maintenance. It interacts with multiple protein networks involved in both junction integrity and spindle orientation. Notably, PAR3 has also been implicated in modulating chemotherapy sensitivity. Research by Chen et al. found that PAR3 can enhance the cytotoxic effect of Paclitaxel, a microtubule-stabilizing agent commonly used in breast cancer treatment [ ]. Mechanistically, PAR3 appears to associate with the motor protein dynein, supporting microtubule stability during cell division. Overexpression of PAR3 has been shown to lower the IC50 of Paclitaxel, making cancer cells more responsive to therapy [ ]. Taken together, these findings highlight the dual importance of polarity proteins not only in directing asymmetric division but also in enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Targeting or modulating the expression of PAR proteins like PAR3 could therefore offer a two-pronged benefit: limiting CSC-driven ACD while boosting the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents.

## Selective targeting of asymmetric cell division in cancer stem cells
While ACD is a fundamental mechanism in normal stem cells for maintaining tissue homeostasis — for example, in Drosophila neuroblasts, mammalian NSCs, and intestinal crypts [ , ], it is co-opted in cancer stem cells (CSCs) to drive tumor heterogeneity, quiescence, and therapy resistance [ ]. Key differences enable selective targeting: CSCs often exhibit dysregulated pathway amplification (e.g., hyperactive NOTCH/WNT under hypoxia) and reliance on oncogenic factors such as MSI1/MSI2, which are overexpressed in malignancies but minimally active in normal stem cells [ ]. For instance, normal stem cells balance ACD via niche-dependent cues to maintain equilibrium, whereas CSCs exploit “niche competition” and neutral drift for uncontrolled expansion [ ]. However, challenges such as off-target effects on normal stem cells remain.
Notch pathway blockade with γ-secretase inhibitors (GSIs) such as DAPT, DBZ (dibenzazepine), and the clinical agent MK-0752 reduces NICD signaling and depletes cancer stem cell (CSC) populations in multiple preclinical breast and colorectal models. For example, GSIs decreased breast CSC markers and mammosphere/tumor-graft CSC frequency and potentiated chemotherapy in mouse tumor-graft studies as well as in an early MK-0752 + docetaxel clinical program. Because Notch is also required in normal intestinal stem cells, GSIs can produce gut/ISC effects in some settings; intermittent dosing schedules or selective delivery (e.g., intermittent PF-03084014 regimens) have been explored preclinically and clinically to limit normal-stem-cell toxicity while preserving anti-CSC activity [ , , ].
WNT/β-catenin inhibitors such as FH-535 and PNU-74,654 block β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity and have shown anti-proliferative and anti-CSC effects in prostate, ovarian, and other tumor models. However, preclinical and review literature cautions that systemic Wnt blockade can produce toxicities in Wnt-dependent normal tissues, so defining therapeutic windows and developing targeted delivery remain active areas of investigation [ , , ].
AURORA kinase inhibitors like alisertib selectively induce polyploidy and dormancy escape in therapy-resistant CSCs, but on-target mitotic effects on proliferating normal tissues remain a concern and require dose/schedule optimization or tumor-targeted strategies [ , ]. Polarity disruptors (e.g., ATG/ATM targeting PAR6/aPKC) impair CSC ACD by blocking PB1 interactions; preclinical studies report reduced tumor growth, disrupted polarity/EMT, and selective effects on cancer stem–like compartments in multiple models, consistent with a degree of tumor selectivity [ , ].
Emerging clinical strategies aim to exploit CSC-specific vulnerabilities using biomarker-guided delivery and rational combinations. Ligand- or antibody-directed platforms (for example, CD133- or CD44-targeted nanoparticles, antibody-drug conjugates, and peptide-drug conjugates) have been developed to concentrate small-molecule inhibitors, cytotoxics, or siRNA/LNP payloads in CSC-enriched compartments, improving on-target potency [ ]. Cellular therapies (CD133/CD44-directed CAR-T or CAR-NK constructs) [ ] and targeted siRNA approaches against RNA-binding oncogenes (e.g., MSI1/MSI2) arJiao e being evaluated preclinically and in early clinical studies to selectively deplete CSC pools [ ]. Combination therapies (e.g., NOTCH/WNT dual blockade with radiation to exploit hypoxia-induced ACD shifts, and microenvironmental targeting such as HIF inhibitors in hypoxic niches) also offer promise for precision approaches [ , ] Future studies should validate selectivity in patient-derived models to minimize toxicity while eroding CSC-driven relapse (Table ).

 

## Conclusion
Despite major therapeutic advances, tumor relapse remains common, with abundant evidence that cancer stem cells (CSCs) fuel recurrence, metastasis, and treatment resistance [ ]. CSCs toggle between symmetric self-renewal (expanding the stem-like pool) and asymmetric cell division (ACD) (producing one self-renewing and one differentiating daughter), modes that are jointly governed by intrinsic fate programs and dynamic microenvironmental cues. Over the past decade, ACD has emerged as a context-dependent regulator of tumor architecture: while it often supports differentiation and may restrain unchecked growth, stressors such as therapy or hypoxia can bias outcomes; in some settings, ACD generates slow-cycling, quiescent survivors that persist through treatment [ ].
CSCs tend to favor symmetric division during rapid expansion or stress, then revert to ACD to rebuild heterogeneity during metastasis, relapse, or early tumor formation [ ]. Early clinical success with ACD-oriented differentiation therapy in APL spurs interest in exporting similar strategies to solid tumors [ ], but broader mechanistic gaps—including incomplete maps of ACD regulation across tissues, the role of progenitors, and links to EMT—still limit translation [ , ]. In many cancers, symmetric divisions dominate in vivo, raising the possibility that ACD is not uniformly suppressive and may, under specific pressures, enable drug tolerance; moreover, ACD may not be exclusive to CSCs, but reliable markers are lacking. Whether ACD pathways are conserved or tumor-specific remains unresolved; either way, shifting tumors from symmetric toward asymmetric division—or leveraging ACD to exhaust the CSC pool—could open new therapeutic windows.
To accelerate progress, next-generation readouts now allow division-resolved, longitudinal analysis in vivo. Continuous lineage tracing and single-cell transcriptomics quantify clonal histories and, with appropriate reporters, division modes [ ]. Label-free metabolic optical biomarkers track fate transitions live and can be paired with polarity/NUMB or spindle-orientation reporters to score asymmetric metabolic partitioning and link it to daughter outcomes [ ]. Intravital (multiphoton/light-sheet) imaging resolves spindle orientation, polarity-complex dynamics, and niche contacts in orthotopic tumors to measure in situ ACD frequency across treatment windows [ ]. Image-guided “tracking-then-omics” pipelines (e.g., trackSeq) isolate sister cells after time-lapse imaging for downstream multi-omics, while related sister-cell inference approaches (e.g., ReSisTrace) mine transcriptomic similarity to identify primed resistance states and link them to division mode [ , ] Finally, spatial/multi-omic lineage tracing maps where ACD-biased clones expand within hypoxic, fibrotic, or immune-suppressed niches, correlating local ecology with division symmetry [ ].
With the combined power of these modalities and continued advances in our understanding of asymmetric cell division, several decisive questions can realistically be answered in the next five years: What is the absolute in vivo frequency of ACD across tumor ecotypes, and how does therapy reshape it over time? How do microenvironmental factors regulate ACD in CSCs—and can they be therapeutically tuned to favor ACD? Can we develop predictive biomarkers and composite signatures of ACD that generalize across cancers? Does division-mode switching display hysteresis/epigenetic memory, or is it purely input-driven? Can metabolic rewiring (e.g., lysosomal AMPK sensing) force mode changes? And how does ACD shape immune evasion by CSCs? Addressing these questions with the tools outlined above should close key knowledge gaps and reveal combination windows to prevent ACD-driven relapse.
