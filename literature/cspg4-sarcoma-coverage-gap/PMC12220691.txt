## Abstract
The major aim of stroke therapy is to stimulate brain repair and improve behavioral recovery after cerebral ischemia. One option is to stimulate endogenous neurogenesis in the subventricular zone and direct the newly formed neurons to the damaged area. However, only a small percentage of these neurons survive, and many do not reach the damaged area, possibly because the corpus callosum impedes the migration of subventricular zone-derived stem cells into the lesioned cortex. A second major obstacle to stem cell therapy is the strong inflammatory reaction induced by cerebral ischemia, whereby the associated phagocytic activity of brain macrophages removes both therapeutic cells and/or cell-based drug carriers. To address these issues, neurogenesis was electrically stimulated in the subventricular zone, followed by isolation of proliferating cells, including newly formed neurons, which were subsequently mixed with a nutritional hydrogel. This mixture was then transferred to the stroke cavity of day 14 post-stroke mice. We found that the performance of the treated animals improved in behavioral tests, including novel object, open field, hole board, grooming, and “time-to-feel” adhesive tape tests. Furthermore, immunostaining revealed that the stem cell marker nestin, the neuroepithelial marker Mash1, and the immature neuronal marker doublecortin-positive cells survived in the transplanted area for 2 weeks, possibly due to reduced phagocytic activity and supportive angiogenesis. These results clearly indicate that the transplantation of committed subventricular zone stem cells combined with a protective nutritional gel directly into the infarct cavity after the peak of stroke-induced neuroinflammation represents a feasible approach to improve neurorestoration after cerebral ischemia.

## Introduction
Despite massive investment in research, the only available therapeutic options for ischemic stroke remain mechanical thrombectomy and thrombolysis with a tissue plasminogen activator. Therefore, the major aim of stroke therapies remains to stimulate brain repair and improve behavioral recovery after cerebral ischemia. However, brain tissue replacement does not occur after focal ischemia, stimulating the search for alternative strategies for brain repair and behavioral recovery after stroke. One option is to stimulate endogenous neurogenesis and direct newborn neurons to the damaged area. Indeed, in both young and old rodent models of brain ischemia, stroke itself induces neurogenesis in the hippocampus and subventricular zone (SVZ). However, only a small percentage of the newly generated neurons survive, and many do not reach the damaged area, possibly because the corpus callosum impedes the migration of SVZ-derived stem cells into the lesioned cortex (Ahlenius et al., 2009; Balseanu et al., 2014; Kathner-Schaffert et al., 2019; Ermine et al., 2020; Dias et al., 2021; Rahman et al., 2021). External interventions, such as motor activity or an enriched environment, play a nerve repair role in restoring functional activities after central nervous system (CNS) injury and add a further layer of complexity by independently modulating the process of adult neurogenesis (Ceanga et al., 2021; Han et al., 2022).
According to earlier investigations of postmortem human brains, older individuals with stroke may still experience cell growth and neuroblast development in the SVZ (Jin et al., 2006; Macas et al., 2006; Xie et al., 2020). This idea is supported by the observation that mature striatal neurons continue to be generated in the adult human brain (Ernst al., 2014).
Researchers have not yet determined whether endogenous neurogenesis aids in recovery after stroke (Tang et al., 2023). Additionally, the healing process may be significantly influenced by age, comorbidities, physical condition, and disease severity (Williamson et al., 2023).
A second barrier to restorative cell therapy is neuroinflammation. Neuroinflammation after ischemic stroke can exacerbate tissue injury and delay the healing and recovery process. Thus, after stroke, microglia can acquire an M1 phenotype and release pro-inflammatory factors, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), leading to the suppression of neurogenesis and neuronal apoptosis, thereby increasing neurological deficits, especially in aged individuals (Liu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2023).
The production of new cells in neurogenic regions can be modulated by physical activity, an enriched environment, or epileptic drugs (Buga et al., 2012; Korosi et al., 2012). It is also known that abnormal brain electrical activity results in a robust increase in the number of neuronal precursors in the adult hippocampus and subventricular zone (Parent et al., 1997).
In previous work, we showed that electrical stimulation of endogenous neurogenesis in aged rats increased the potential for brain self-repair and improved behavioral recovery, including recovery of spatial long-term memory (T-maze) after focal ischemia, in aged male rats subjected to two sessions of electrical nonconvulsive stimulation at 7 and 24 days after middle cerebral artery occlusion. Histologically, there is a robust increase in neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus and SVZ of the infarcted hemisphere and a considerable number of neurons expressing tubulin beta III in the infarcted area (Balseanu et al., 2020). However, in remote areas such as the cortex, few newborn cells survive; therefore, their contribution to neurological recovery is questionable.
In this study, we used a mouse model of stroke and hypothesized that transplanting newly formed, pre-committed neuronal precursor cells could enhance stroke recovery. These cells, derived through electrical stimulation of neurogenesis in the SVZ, were combined with a nutritional hydrogel and transplanted into the stroke cavity. The phenotypes of the transplanted cells and functional recovery of the animals were analyzed 4 weeks after stroke using immunohistochemistry and a battery of tests.

## Methods

## Animals
C57BL6 male mice (3–4 months of age; 32–36 g) were used in this study. The mice were bred and housed in our pathogen-free vivarium and kept at a temperature of 22 ± 1°C and a humidity of 55% ± 5%. They were exposed to a 12-hour light‒dark cycle, with the lights turning on at 7:00 a.m. The animals were housed four or five times to avoid fighting and had free access to food and water.
On the basis of our experience, 19 animals per group were used for the behavioral analysis of neurological recovery after stroke. This calculation is founded on an expected effect size of 25% of the mean values, which will yield statistically significant effects (αn error < 0.05) with a power of 0.80. This assumption considers that common standard deviations are 25% of the mean values. Before surgery, the animals were randomized into the control group (n = 10) and treatment/transplantation group (n = 19) and survived the 28-day study period on the basis of weight and performance distribution. Additionally, the surgeries were performed randomly to avoid operating only on control or treated animals. Five young mice were used to isolate the subventricular zone after electrostimulation (see below). The experiments are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments; Percie du Sert et al., 2020). The animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Board of the State of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern as meeting the ethical requirements of the German National Act on the Use of Experimental Animals and by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Medical University of Craiova (approval No. 192/10.10.2020, approval date October 10, 2020).

## Surgery
Before surgery, the mice were fasted overnight to reduce blood glucose levels. After craniotomy, the middle cerebral artery (MCA) was exposed and slowly lifted with a tungsten hook attached to a micromanipulator (Maerzhaeuser Precision Micro-manipulator Systems, Fine Science Tools) and electrocoagulated. Both common carotid arteries were then occluded by tightening the prepositioned thread loops for 90 minutes. Throughout surgery, anesthesia was maintained by spontaneous inhalation of 1%–1.5% isoflurane in a mixture of 75% nitrous oxide and 25% oxygen. Body temperature was controlled at 37°C by a Homeothermic Blanket System (Harvard Apparatus, Hugo Sachs Elektronik, Breisgau, Germany). The local changes in blood flow were monitored via a laser Doppler device (Perimed, Stockholm, Sweden). A decrease in the laser Doppler signal to < 20% of the control value was considered successful MCA occlusion. After 90 minutes, the common carotid arteries were re-opened. The muscle and soft tissue were replaced, and the skin was closed with 5–0 nylon sutures. Buprenorphine was given subcutaneously twice, every 6 hours after surgery, at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg for peri-operative pain relief (Balseanu et al., 2020).

## Nutritional hydrogel preparation
The hydrogel was prepared in Neural Stem Cell Basal Medium, which is a defined, serum-free, growth factor-free medium optimized for the in vitro growth of neural stem cells derived from rodents (SCM003, Sigma‒Aldrich, Munich, Germany). The hydrogel was created by mixing 4.8 mL of medium with 60 mg of hyaluronic acid (Sigma‒Aldrich) and 1.4 g of glycerol. The mixture was allowed to swell overnight and was then ready for use.

## Behavioral testing
The testing procedures involved two persons, one person who performed the surgery and was in charge of handling the animals according to group assignment and another person who tested the animals and was not aware of the groups’ identities. To evaluate changes in neurological function associated with ischemia, the mice were subjected to a variety of somatosensory, motor, and memory tests before and after surgery. All testing was performed from 9 to 11 a.m. The animals were moved to the testing room one hour before the behavioral testing was started to acclimatize. All animals were evaluated prior to stroke to establish a baseline; 3, 7, and 14 days after stroke; and 17, 24, and 28 days after graft or hydrogel implantation. Additionally, all the testing equipment was washed with 75% ethanol after each animal to eliminate odor. For the open field and novel object recognition tests, a camera (Basler acA1300-60gb GigE) was used for recording, and the EthoVision XT 17 (Noldus Technology Wageningen, Netherlands) was used for analyzing the test results.

## Grooming behavior test
Grooming is a maintenance behavior test that includes licking the fur, grooming with the forepaws, or scratching with any limb to maintain the physiological comfort and appearance of the mouse (Seibenhener and Wooten, 2015). This behavior was observed during a 5-minute trial.

## Open field
The open field test reflects a mouse’s capacity and willingness to move freely and engage in exploratory behavior (Seibenhener and Wooten, 2015). Each animal was positioned in the center of a 50 × 33 × 15 cm3 open test space (arena) equipped with walls to prevent escape and was allowed to explore freely for 10 minutes. The duration of the animal’s exploration of the center of the arena was video recorded.

## Novel object recognition
The novel object recognition test is used to evaluate cognition, particularly recognition memory (Lueptow, 2017). This test was based on the spontaneous tendency of rodents to spend more time exploring a novel object than a familiar object. First, each animal was positioned in the center of a 50 × 33 × 15 cm3 open rectangular test area with walls to prevent the animal from escaping. In this confined space, two identical objects were positioned in opposite corners, 15 cm from the walls, and the animal was given 6 minutes to explore the objects freely. At the end of the 6-minute period, the animal was placed back in the home cage. After 60 minutes of video recording, one of these objects was replaced by a new object (of a different size, color, and shape). The animal was then positioned in the center of the test arena and videotaped for an additional 6 minutes. The preference index was defined as the proportion of time spent investigating the same object relative to the total amount of time spent exploring both objects. The recognition index was defined as the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object of the total time spent exploring both objects.

## Hole board test
The hole board test (Labots et al., 2015) is primarily utilized to evaluate exploratory behaviors in rodents. This test apparatus consists of a gray plate with dimensions of 40 × 40 cm2 and a thickness of 2.2 cm. It has 16 holes, each with a 3 cm diameter and detectors positioned 1 cm below the plate’s surface. The plate was placed 15 cm above the ground and connected to a counter that records the number of heads dipping into the hole. The tested animals were positioned in the center of the plate, and the number of head dips was video recorded over the course of 5 minutes.

## Adhesive removal tape
This adhesive removal test (Balseanu et al., 2014) is used to evaluate sensorimotor asymmetries resulting from stroke-related unilateral brain damage. It involved the use of adhesive tapes, measuring 0.3 × 0.4 cm2, placed on the palmar side of the animal’s front limbs. Tape was applied to the palmar side of the forelimbs with equal pressure on each limb. The animal was then placed in the test cage, and two parameters were counted: (1) the time it took for the animal to feel the tape and (2) the time required to remove it. The animals were subjected to 2 minutes to complete the test. Animals with unilateral brain damage were biased toward faster “time-to-feel” and faster “time-to-remove” the tape from the unimpaired ipsilateral forelimb. The tactile response was defined as the time it took for the animal to feel the adhesive tape, which is represented by a limb shake or the mouth touching the adhesive tape. The complete removal of the adhesive tape signified either the completion of the test or exceeding the allotted time. Animals typically removed adhesive tape with their mouths or by “grooming.” Training was performed for 2 days before surgery. To evaluate the functional symmetry between the forelimbs, asymmetry scores for “time-to-feel” and “time-to-remove” are calculated as a quotient, [R–L/R+L], where R represents the time needed to feel or remove the adhesive tape from the right forelimb (ipsilateral forelimb), and L represents the time needed to feel or remove the adhesive tape from the left forelimb (contralateral forelimb).

## Stimulation of endogenous neurogenesis by non-convulsive electrostimulation
The mice (n = 5) were stimulated by the application of ear-clip electrodes on both sides using a device from World Precision Instruments (Friedberg, Germany). Ear-clip electrodes were moistened with saline and attached to the pinnae. Endogenous neurogenesis was stimulated by a subconvulsive train (3 mA, 60 pulses/s, 0.5 ms pulse width, 1 second duration, and a total of 5 seconds) for 5 consecutive days (Balseanu et al., 2020). The treatment was performed under mild anesthesia (subcutaneous administration of 85 mg/kg ketamine and 0.85 mg/kg PromAce, provided by a local veterinary supplier).

## Bromodeoxyuridine labeling
To label newly generated cells in the SVZ, the mice were given daily injections of bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU; 50 mg/kg body weight, i.p.; Sigma) immediately after electric stimulation, followed by four additional injections over the next 4 days.

## Isolation of the subventricular zone
After the final session of electrical stimulation, the animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal (ip.) injection of ketamine (75 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg, provided by a local veterinary supplier). The whole brain was extracted and rinsed in ice-cold Leibovitz’s L-15 medium to remove excess blood. The brain was then transferred to a 100 mm culture dish pre-filled with 10 mL of ice-cold Leibovitz’s L-15 medium for microdissection via the procedure described by Liu et al. (2022). The samples were pooled and mixed with a hydrogel supplemented with nutritional medium to support the transplanted cells in the first 24 hours. The mixed SVZ-hydrogel was then used to fill the stroke cavity on day 14 after stroke. Just before being filled with the SVZ-hydrogel mixture, the necrotic area was surgically removed and cleaned with physiological saline, leaving a clean cavity. The control animals received a hydrogel supplemented with nutritional medium alone. Random assignment was carried out for (i) group assignment, (ii) surgery assignment, and (iii) treatment assignment. After 28 days, the young mice were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine and xylazine and perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% freshly depolymerized paraformaldehyde in 50 mM PBS. The brains were removed, post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, cryoprotected first in 15% glycerol/50 mM PBS, flash-frozen in isopentane, and stored at –70°C until cryostat sectioning.

## Immunohistochemistry
Sections (25 μm thick) were cut on a freezing microtome and processed for immunohistochemistry as previously described (Dumbrava et al., 2022). The sections were blocked in 3% donkey/goat serum/10 mM PBS/0.3% Tween 20 and incubated overnight at 4°C with secondary biotinylated antibodies raised in a donkey (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) or a goat (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Hamburg, Germany). Donkey serum was obtained from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories (West Grove, PA, USA).
For BrdU detection, free-floating sections were incubated in 2 M HCl at 40°C for 1 hour and rinsed in 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 8.5) at room temperature for 10 minutes. The sections were incubated with a rat anti-BrdU antibody (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4°C for 24 hours. BrdU-positive nuclei were visualized using goat anti-rat-Cy2 or goat anti-rat-Cy5 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). For phenotyping, the tissue was incubated with (i) rabbit anti-NeuN (1:1000, Novus Biologicals, Wiesbaden Nordenstadt, Germany), (ii) guinea pig anti-doublecortin (DCX; 1:2000; MilliporeSigma, Darmstadt, Germany), (iii) mouse anti-nestin (1:12,000, MilliporeSigma), (iv) mouse anti-rabbit collagen IV (1:1000, Abcam), (v) rabbit anti-Mash1 (1:5000, Abcam), (vi) rabbit anti-annexin A3 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany), (vii) mouse anti-ED1 (1:500, Abcam), (viii) rabbit anti-laminin-1 (L9393, Sigma‒Aldrich), (ix) rat anti-mouse CD31 (BD Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany), (x) rabbit anti-ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1; Wako Chemie, Neuss, Germany) or (xi) goat anti-vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1; R&D Systems, Wiesbaden, Germany) at 4°C overnight. The next day, the sections were rinsed with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor® 488/568 goat anti-rabbit IgG, Alexa Fluor® 488 goat anti-guinea pig IgG or Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-mouse or Alexa Fluor® 488 donkey anti-goat IgG for 24 hours at 4°C. Finally, the sections were washed with PBS and covered with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant fluorescence medium (Antisel, ThessalonikiThermi, Greece).
Nestin-positive cells used for scanning were labeled with goat anti-mouse Alexa 488, and slides were scanned as large TIFF files utilizing a Nikon 90i motorized microscope (Nikon Europe BV, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) equipped with plan apochromat objectives, a high-resolution low-noise 16.25 Mp DS-Ri Nikon CMOS camera (7.3 × 7.3 µm pixel size yielding images of 4.908 × 3.264 pixels), a Prior high-resolution motorized stage, LED fluorescence modules, and the Nikon NIS-Elements Advanced Research image analysis package on a dedicated Dell Precision Tower 7910 graphic station [2× Intel Xeon 8-Cores E5-2630 v3 2.40 GHz, 64 GB DDR4, NVIDIA (NVIDIA Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA) Quadro M4000 8 GB GDDR5].

## Counting of doublecortin- and ANXA3-positive cells
The number of cells expressing DCX and ANXA3 within the infarcted area of the animals that were administered SVZ cells was determined. This mixture was compared to controls that received only hydrogel, as previously described (Dumbrava et al., 2022). Notably, a significant number of cells positive for ANXA3 were found to coexist with ED1-positive cells, as detailed previously in our work (Junker et al., 2007). To quantitatively determine the number of ANXA3+ cells, we used a cell counting approach in designated 250 × 250 μm2 regions of interest. This was done via a “randomized systematic” method, which involved initiating a systematic sequence from a random starting point every 10th section throughout the infarcted volume via ImageJ software (version 2.14.0/1.54f, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012). The cells of interest covered approximately 30% of the infarct area, with the labeled cells having a cross-sectional area ranging 90–160 μm2.

## Counting of colocalized doublecortin/bromodeoxyuridine, Mash1/bromodeoxyuridine, and Nestin/bromodeoxyuridine
DCX+/BrdU+ and Nestin+/BrdU+ double-labeled cells were analyzed in the region adjacent to the SVZ as previously described (Popa-Wagner et al., 2010). To this end, a sequence of confocal images measuring 161 × 242 μm2 was scanned at 0.1 μm steps across the 25 μm-thick sections. Double-labeled cells were concentrated in these images in an area adjacent to the SVZ that covered ~30% of the total image area. Hence, the number of double-labeled cells was calculated by multiplying the number of counted cells per image by 3.3. The data are expressed as the number of cells per mm2.
Nestin+/BrdU+ double-labeled cells and Mash1+ cells were analyzed in the lesioned areas of treated and control animals in a similar way. Cell counting was performed by two independent observers, and the mean values were calculated.

## Microvascular density
Microvascular density was assessed using the “hotspot” method, which covered 30% of the infarcted area, as previously described (Buga et al., 2014). In short, areas with microvessels positive for VCAM1, RECA, and Lm were designated hotspots using a 40× objective lens. These areas were subsequently examined, and microvessel counts were conducted using a 20× objective lens, corresponding to a microscopic field measuring 0.7386 mm2. The counting process was performed by two separate observers, and the results are presented as the mean values and standard deviations (SDs).

## Statistical analysis
The main effects of time and treatment for behavioral testing were evaluated by two-way repeated measures analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test using GraphPad software 9.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). For the analysis of the histological data, an unpaired t-test was conducted to compare the numbers of blood vessels and nestin/BrdU- and DCX/BrdU-colocalized cells after ES and after transplantation into the stroke cavity using GraphPad software 9.0. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM (longitudinal analyses evaluating behavioral data) or mean ± SD (cross-sectional analyses evaluating histochemical data). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05.

## Results
The experimental setup is illustrated in A (top panel), while B presents a schematic representation of the transplantation process. For the first 3 days after stroke, the animals in all the groups were fed soft pellets to facilitate nutrition. Thereafter, they were allowed to eat ad libitum. The mortality rate was nearly identical, at 8% for each group. All the animals that died did so within the first 7 days after stroke. In the figures showing post-stroke behavioral recovery, we have provided the number of animals that survived the 28-day testing period.
 

## Electrical stimulation leads to increased neurogenesis in the subventricular zone
To assess whether ES led to an increased number of newborn cells in the SVZ, we performed double immunostaining with the neuroepithelial cell marker nestin and the cell proliferation marker BrdU (Balseanu et al., 2020) in both control ( C) and transplanted animals ( D, and inset). Analysis of the number of colocalized Nestin/BrdU in the ipsilateral SVZ revealed a significant 4.3-fold increase (P = 0.001) in transplanted animals compared with controls ( G). By performing triple immunostaining to detect the immature neuronal marker DCX, the mature neuronal nuclear marker NeuN, and the proliferation marker BrdU in control ( E) and transplanted animals ( F, and inset), we found that the number of BrdU+/DCX+ cells in the ipsilateral hemisphere of ES animals outnumbered that in control animals by a factor of 3.3 (P = 0.001; H).

## Transplantation of subventricular zone cells improves behavioral recovery
Compared with control animals, treatment with SVZ cell grafts led to increased exploration time in the open field test. Two-way repeated measures analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of treatment (P = 0.0007) and of [treatment × time] (P = 0.0027; A). Starting from the second week post-grafting, the movement velocity of the animals that received SVZ cell grafts increased from 4.9 m/s to 9.5 ± 1.6 m/s (P = 0.0022; Additional Figure 1).
 Exploratory behavior was assessed via the Hole Board test. We detected significant differences between the treated and control groups ( B). Thus, while the number of head dips continuously decreased in control animals, SVZ-graft treatment led to a constant increase in the number of head dips. Indeed, two-way repeated measures analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of treatment (P = 0.004). However, [treatment × time] was not significant (P = 0.20). Notably, the animals that received SVZ graft cells were less anxious than the controls.
Before surgery, the number of grooming bouts recorded during an observation session was 0.724 ± 0.649. After stroke, the animals did not show any significant changes in grooming behavior. However, after the implantation of SVZ cells, the number of grooming bouts was significantly greater in treated animals compared with control animals (P = 0.004; C). However, [treatment × time] was not significant (P = 0.484).
In the novel object recognition test, we noted that with increasing testing time, the memory index decreased because of repeated stimulation (familiarization). However, treatment led to an improvement in short-term memory starting on day 3 post-transplantation. Indeed, animals receiving SVZ cell grafts demonstrated a better discrimination index compared with controls (P = 0.008; D). [Treatment × time] was not significant (P = 0.579).
In the adhesive tape removal test, stroke surgery was performed on the right half of the brain. Therefore, animals that received SVZ cells recovered better in terms of motor and sensory ability on the left half of the body, i.e., they felt faster and were able to remove the adhesive tape more quickly. Thus, the asymmetry score for “time-to-remove” increased significantly (P = 0.012) in treated animals ( E). However, the asymmetry score for “time-to-remove” was not significantly different between the two groups ( F).

## Nestin is colocalized with bromodeoxyuridine in the infarcted area of treated animals
Examination of brain morphology revealed that the SVZ cell grafts completely filled the infarct cavity 28 days after hydrogel administration at 14 days post-stroke ( A). Histologically, we were unable to observe the infarct border in the cortex of animals treated with SVZ. However, in the control animals, the stroke cavity was clearly evident ( B). Scanning coronal sections of the brains of treated and control animals immunostained with anti-nestin antibodies clearly revealed that the transplanted SVZ graft formed a “bridge” between the infarct lesion and the SVZ zone in the treated animals ( C). In control animals, there was a thin stream of nestin-positive cells connecting the infarct core (IC) periphery with the SVZ zone, possibly via the corpus callosum highway ( D). Finally, examination of the lesioned hemisphere via double immunohistochemistry revealed numerous Nestin/BrdU double-labeled cells in the lesion filled with the SVZ grafts ( E and G). In control animals, BrdU-labeled cells were not observed ( F).
 The neuroepithelial marker Mash1 is expressed during brain development but is downregulated in the adult brain (Popa-Wagner et al., 2007). However, ES led to vigorous re-expression of proneural Mash1 in the SVZ ( A and H), hippocampus ( B), and choroid plexus ( C, green). Mash1 expression in the choroid plexus was closely associated with blood vessel staining with anti-collagen IV antibodies ( C). Mash1 immunostaining in the lesioned hemisphere of treated mice revealed that in the transplanted region, Mash1 immunoreactivity was observed in neuronal-like cells ( D and I). In the hydrogel containing Mash1+ cells, many NeuN+ neuronal nuclei that survived ischemic stroke were clearly visible ( D). Like those in the choroid plexus, some Mash1-positive cells ( E, green) in the treated animals were tightly associated with blood vessels, as visualized with an anti-Col IV antibody ( E, inset, red). Indeed, in the postnatal mouse brain PN7, Mash1 was robustly expressed in tubular, thread-like structures ( F). Similar expression was observed in the transplanted region of ischemic mice ( F1, inset). Finally, in control animals, there was diffuse green staining, most likely resulting from nonspecific binding of anti-Mash1 antibodies to cellular debris caused by tissue necrosis after ischemia, and some apoptotic nuclei were stained with the anti-NeuN antibody ( G).
 

## Microvascular density is high in areas transplanted with subventricular zone cells
VCAM-1, also known as CD106, is abundantly present in the brain endothelium following stroke. Its presence has been associated with an augmented process of angiogenesis (Kaur et al., 2023). As a result, the microvasculature within the SVZ-transplanted region showed robust upregulation of VCAM-1. This was evident by a 2.2-fold increase at 28 days, in contrast to the animals in the control group that were administered hydrogel only ( A, B, and G). However, the expression of the other two angiogenesis markers, laminin and CD31, was not significantly different between the control group and the transplanted animals ( A, B, and G).
 Microglial/macrophages play a pivotal role in angiogenesis following ischemic stroke. An increase in microvessel density post-stroke has been observed exclusively in regions containing macrophages. This finding implies a potential association between angiogenesis and the proliferation of macrophages (Mcnay et al., 2006).
In previous research conducted by our team, ANXA3 was identified as a marker for both resting and activated microglia (Junker et al., 2007). In the present study, we observed lower levels of ANXA3 in the cortical region of animals that were transplanted with SVZ cells than in those that were administered only hydrogel ( C, D, and H).

## Doublecortin expression is increased in the area transplanted with subventricular zone cells
Next, we investigated the presence of the immature neuronal marker doublecortin by immunofluorescence in the peri-infarct area of animals after stroke. We observed scattered DCX-positive cells against a background of inflammatory cells expressing Iba1 in the perilesional area of the control animals ( E). The infarct cavity filled with the hydrogel containing SVZ cells contained many more (2-fold) neurons expressing DCX ( F and I). The microglia marker Iba1 did not significantly differ between the control and treated animals ( E and F).

## Discussion
Although rehabilitation is crucial for enhancing functional recovery if it is initiated early after stroke, it does not replace lost tissue. Transplanting multipotent neural stem cells is one method for replacing lost cells. In this work, we enhanced neurogenesis in the SVZ of mice via electric pulses and transplanted the stimulated cells into the infarct area of the mice after mixing them with a nutritional hyaluronic acid-based gel at 14 days post-stroke. Behaviorally, at 2 weeks after treatment, the animals that received the transplanted cells performed significantly better in the open field test, novel object recognition test, hole board test, and grooming test and had a better score for the “time-to-feel” index in the adhesive tape test. At 28 days post-stroke, the infarct area was fully covered with healthy soft tissue. This tissue was filled with cells expressing the neuroepithelial marker nestin, as well as numerous cells expressing the transcription factor Mash1 (Ascl1), a marker of neural progenitor cells. Additionally, numerous cells expressing DCX, a marker of immature neurons, presented increased angiogenesis and decreased inflammation.
In previous work, we reported that the enhancement of neurogenesis in the SVZ zone by ES in post-stroke aged rats led to improved functional recovery of spatial long-term memory (T-maze) but not in tasks requiring complex sensorimotor skills at the rotating pole (Balseanu et al., 2020). However, the corpus callosum may act as a barrier, thereby preventing the migration of neuronal precursor cells from the SVZ to the lesioned cortical area (Dias et al., 2021). Therefore, we reasoned that combining newly formed stem cells derived from neurogenic stimulation of the SVZ with a nutritional hydrogel that is then transplanted into the stroke cavity 2 weeks after the ischemic event may increase the likelihood of structural and behavioral recovery after stroke. Furthermore, by administering the SVZ graft at 2 weeks after stroke, we avoided the detrimental effects of phagocytosis on the transplanted cells (Gresita et al., 2019).
Improving behavior, including the ability to move, exploratory behavior, cognition, and memory, is essential for successful stroke therapy. We found that animals that received the SVZ graft performed better in a variety of behavioral tests, including the open field test, novel object test, hole board test, and grooming test. The open field test provides insight into general ambulatory ability and anxiety-related emotional behaviors (La-Vu et al., 2020). Similarly, in the open field test, the movement velocity of the animals that received SVZ cell grafts increased by a factor of 2 compared with that of the control animals, suggesting better recovery in muscle strength.
The object recognition test is commonly used to assess various aspects of learning and memory in mice and reflects the relative health of brain regions involved in short-term memory (Lueptow, 2017). We found that SVZ graft treatment led to an improvement in short-term memory, reflecting better preservation of this function in the brains of treated animals.
The hole board test can be used to assess exploratory, habituation, locomotor, and anxiety behavior. An increased number of head dips in the treated animals may reflect recovery of these functions, which progressively deteriorated in the control animals. Finally, a better score for the “time-to-feel” index in treated animals suggests better recovery of sensory function in the adhesive tape test.
In our experimental approach, we used ES to stimulate neurogenesis in the SVZ and increase the number of proliferating cells, including neuronal precursors, in the transplanted hydrogel. Indeed, the number of proliferating cells expressing BrdU, nestin, Mash1, and DCX greatly increased after repeated stimulation. Two weeks after transplantation, nestin/BrdU double-labeled cells, as well as numerous Mash1-positive cells, were detected. However, by immunohistochemistry, DCX-positive cells were not found in the transplanted gel, suggesting that DCX-positive cells neither survived nor differentiated into mature neurons after transplantation. Understanding neural stem cell regulation is essential for improving stem cell therapy for stroke (Genet and Hirschi, 2021). Nestin and DCX are well-known markers of cell proliferation in the SVZ (Ardaya et al., 2024). In a recent study, nestin was shown to expand in vitro, and its expression persisted in neurospheres grown in vitro after 7 days in culture derived from the SVZ zone of adult mice. Notably, DCX could not be detected in neurospheres after 7 days in culture, confirming our results (Liu et al., 2022). Indeed, the transplantation of glial-enriched progenitor cells derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells as well as human iPSC-derived long-term neuroepithelial-like stem cells shortly after stroke has demonstrated a remarkable therapeutic impact on axonal damage, demyelination, cognitive deficits, and impaired locomotor activity in rodent models (Piao et al., 2015; Duan et al., 2021; Llorente et al., 2021).
In previous work, we and others reported that DCX was robustly induced in the SVZ and hippocampus of young and aged rats, albeit at lower levels than those in young animals subjected to cerebral ischemia (Jin et al., 2004; Cutler and Kokovay, 2020; Kerever and Arikawa-Hirasawa, 2021; Wang et al., 2022). However, DCX does not reach the infarct area and does not contribute to neuronal repair after stroke (Balseanu et al., 2014).
Neural and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells in the adult brain express Mash1 (also known as Ascl1), which codes for a basic helix–loop–helix transcription factor. Proneural genes such as Ascl1 are known to promote cell cycle exit and neuronal differentiation when expressed in neural progenitor cells. Mash1 is another representative gene of the SVZ that can be expanded in vitro and is capable of differentiating into neurons when exposed to an appropriate environment (Pollard, 2013; Ardaya et al., 2024). In this work, we found that in the postnatal mouse brain, Mash1 expression is observed in tubular thread-like structures similar in appearance to the structures observed in the SVZ after ES. Most importantly, in the transplanted animals, Mash1 expression survived for at least 2 weeks in the lesional area. Thus, we found a striking parallel between nestin cells emanating from injured blood vessels in the ischemic area (Dias et al., 2021) and the tight association of Mash1 expression with Col IV expression by blood vessels in the infarcted region of transplanted animals. Notably, similar to the neuroepithelial marker Nestin, Mash1 is expressed during development in the hypothalamic neuroepithelium of mice and is downregulated in the adult brain (Popa-Wagner et al., 2007). Indeed, in the CNS, Mash1 mRNA is localized to the neuroepithelium, which corresponds to future functionally distinct areas of the brain (Ma et al., 1997).
During neurogenesis, MASH1 expression is confined to mitotically active precursor cells, which are involved in the early stages of lineage commitment. This process upregulates NeuroD in neural precursor cells to direct terminal differentiation. However, Mash1 expression during development is not restricted to the brain. It has also been described in the development of adrenal medullary chromaffin cells, ganglion cells, thyroid parafollicular C cells, pulmonary neuroendocrine cells, and even small cell carcinomas (Ito et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2007).
Quite surprisingly, Mash1 expression has not been described in detail in brain injuries. However, it has been used to convert NG2 glia into neurons in combination with two other transcription factors, Lmx1a and Nurr1, to form functionally mature neurons that integrate into existing brain circuitry after a lesion to the striatum in mice (Pereira et al., 2017). Similarly, Ascl1 in conjunction with Dlx2 has been used to genetically convert reactive glia into interneurons in a mouse model of epilepsy (Lentini et al., 2021).
Neural precursors sourced from different origins and utilized in various disease contexts have been shown to produce trophic factors. These factors could play a role in their therapeutic effects, potentially unrelated to the replacement of cells (Pluchino et al., 2005; Andres et al., 2011). Thus, the level of VCAM-1 expression was elevated in the region transplanted with SVZ cells, confirming that cells derived from the SVZ represent a significant source of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). These findings suggest that SVZ cytogenesis may contribute to neural repair, partially by generating trophic factors, especially VEGF. Indeed, the targeted removal of the Vegf gene in adult neural stem cells hampers the process of recovery and repair (Horie et al., 2011; Williamson et al., 2023). Recent research has also shown that mesenchymal stem cells derived from placental chorionic villi, which are abundant in VCAM-1, exhibit beneficial paracrine activity for angiogenesis. They also demonstrated effectiveness in treating hindlimb ischemia (Du et al., 2016). These findings corroborate our results and suggest that VCAM-1-enriched CV-MSCs could play a significant role in promoting therapeutic angiogenesis.
Transplanted embryonic mouse neural stem/progenitor cells express the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α transcription factor and VEGF, stimulating the formation of capillaries. Indeed, the protective influence of neural stem/progenitor cells on endothelial cells was inhibited by the use of pharmacological inhibitors targeting the VEGF signaling pathway (Roitbak et al., 2008).
Similarly, we observed an increase in the number of immature neurons expressing DCX in the area transplanted with SVZ cells compared with that in the control area. This phenomenon might have been facilitated by a reduced number of phagocytic microglia expressing ANXA3, a marker we first described in 2007 (Junker et al., 2007), in the region covered by the SVZ-hydrogel in treated animals. Indeed, subacute transplantation of human neurospheres that secrete VEGF into the ischemic brain correlated with improved functional recovery and suppression of the inflammatory response (Horie et al., 2011).
A similar concept to ours was recently highlighted in the study by Wang et al. (2023). This entails providing transplanted cells with metabolic support to ensure the survival of stem cells during the initial hours post-transplantation. This prolonged viability enables them to establish connections with blood vessels and differentiate into the specific cell types necessary for regenerating new tissue when introduced into a damaged region.
This study has several limitations that should be noted. First, the study was conducted using a rodent model of stroke. All therapeutic studies conducted in stroke models have, for various reasons, summarized very limited translatability to humans. One such translational issue is the transplantation procedure itself, which involves a craniotomy and the application of a therapeutic hydrogel containing stimulated cells—a procedure that is highly improbable to perform on patients. Second, our current knowledge of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying recovery from ischemic stroke is limited. Third, while stroke risk is notably elevated among older individuals with multiple comorbidities, the majority of studies are conducted on young, healthy animals in controlled laboratory settings. In these animal models, the observation period for post-stroke recovery usually ranges from 3 to 28 days, occasionally extending up to 60 days. In contrast, stroke recovery in humans is a more prolonged and complex process. Furthermore, young animal models tend to demonstrate significantly greater spontaneous recovery than those observed in human stroke patients. Finally, the number of animals, including controls, used per experimental condition is often quite small (Ruscu et al., 2024).

## Conclusion
The results described herein support the hypothesis that stroke recovery is enhanced by combining newly generated stem cells derived from the neurogenic stimulation of the SVZ with a nutritional hydrogel. This mixture is then transplanted into the stroke cavity 2 weeks after the ischemic event. Histologically, the stroke cavity was filled with nestin-positive, Mash1-positive, and DCX-positive cells. This occurred against a background of reduced phagocytic activity by microglia and increased angiogenesis. Behaviorally, animals that received the SVZ graft performed better in a variety of behavioral tests, including the open field, novel object, hole board, and grooming tests. Grafting primed cells from the neurogenic SVZ, which are embedded in a nutritive hydrogel, into the infarct cavity after local inflammation has diminished in intensity might be a viable option for improving brain recovery after cerebral ischemia.
