## Background
Vascular remodeling is a dynamic process characterized by changes in vascular identity that impact vessel structure and function. Molecular markers define cellular identity as arteries, veins, and lymphatic vessels: Ephrin-B2 and Notch determine arterial identity, EphB4 and COUP-TFII determine venous identity, and Prox1 determines lymphatic identity.

## Methods
This is a review of experimental literature.

## Results
These proteins determine identity during development before the first heartbeat. Hemodynamic changes in adulthood can also alter vascular identity. Changes in identity markers coincide with changes in vascular cell phenotype or disease and thus may play a role in regulating both normal and pathological vascular remodeling. Vascular diseases such as arteriovenous malformations and pulmonary hypertension are driven by changes in cell phenotype and vessel identity. Surgical interventions such as arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation and arterial bypass using vein grafts induce alterations in identity; vein grafts lose their venous identity, but do not acquire arterial identity, whereas venous limbs of AVF gain arterial identity while retaining their venous identity. After patch angioplasty, vascular patches remodel in their environment. Patches in the venous environment acquire venous identity and patches in the arterial environment develop arterial identity. Interestingly, patches in the venous outflow of AVF gain a mixed venous-arterial phenotype.

## Conclusions
Changes in vascular identity drive vascular remodeling in both physiological and pathological settings, with potential implications for therapeutic strategies targeting vascular diseases.

## Clinical Relevance
Vascular remodeling is essential for both physiological and pathological vascular adaptation. Changes in vascular identity occur in response to hemodynamic forces and mediate vascular remodeling during development, in disease states, and after surgical and endovascular interventions. Alterations in arterial and venous molecular markers of identity regulate cellular phenotype, the extracellular matrix, and vessel wall structure, ultimately determining long-term vessel function. Understanding the molecular regulatory pathways controlling vascular identity provides insight into understanding the mechanisms of vascular remodeling and may identify potential therapeutic targets to treat vascular disease and improve outcomes after vascular interventions.

## Article Highlights

•Type of Article: Review of experimental literature
•Key Findings: Vascular identity is dynamically regulated by genetic and environmental factors and influences both physiologic and pathologic vascular remodeling. Molecular markers such as Ephrin-B2 and Notch define arterial identity, whereas EphB4 and COUP-TFII define venous identity. Vascular identity exhibits remarkable plasticity in response to environmental change. Pathological conditions such as arteriovenous malformations and pulmonary hypertension involve aberrant vascular identity changes, which may highlight potential therapeutic targets. Surgical interventions, such as arteriovenous fistula creation, vein grafting, and path angioplasty induce alterations in vascular identity that impact vascular remodeling.
•Take Home Message: Understanding vascular identity changes in vascular remodeling provides insights into the molecular mechanisms driving vascular disease and postsurgical adaptions to hemodynamic changes. Future therapeutic strategies targeting vascular identity markers may improve surgical outcomes.

The circulatory system begins to form by the third gestational week in human embryonic development. The molecular identity of the arterial, venous, and lymphatic system are determined genetically before the first heartbeat, that is, before hemodynamic forces. A combination of specific transcription factor interactions and surface ligand-receptor interactions is central to establishment of vascular identity. Ephrin-B2 and Notch establish arterial identity, Ephrin type-B receptor 4 (EphB4) and chicken ovalbumin upstream promoter-transcription factor II (COUP-TFII) establish venous identity, and Prox1 determines lymphatic identity ( ). In the primitive capillary network, binding of Ephrin-B2 to its receptor EphB4 on adjacent cells induces bidirectional signaling that inhibits proliferation and promotes maturation of the vessels. Thus, interaction between Ephrin-B2 and EphB4 establishes the boundary between arteries and veins essential for normal arteriovenous specification. However, as the embryo develops, the identity of the vascular system can change in response to hemodynamic forces ; therefore, vascular identity is determined by an interplay of genetic and hemodynamic factors. 
Alterations in vascular identity are linked to pathological processes such as inflammation and cancer. In addition, after vascular surgery, such as after venous bypass or an arteriovenous fistula (AVF), altered hemodynamic forces drive change in identity. , These molecular identity changes are not passive markers, but actively drive vascular remodeling to adapt to a new hemodynamic environment. , , The venous outflow of AVF used for hemodialysis exhibit changes from a venous to a mixed venous and arterial identity. Veins used as grafts for bypass procedures lose their venous identity without gaining arterial identity. These changes in vascular identity seem to be essential for the vessels to remodel and function in altered hemodynamic environments; without these identity changes, vessels fail to remodel properly and/or lose patency. , , 
Vascular remodeling refers to the structural and molecular changes in blood vessels that occur in response to hemodynamic changes, endothelial cell (endothelial cell) injury, inflammation, and cardiovascular disease. , Types of remodeling include intimal or medial hyperplasia, changes to elastin and/or collagen composition, vascular smooth muscle cell (SMC) phenotypic changes, vessel diameter changes, or fibrotic changes ( ). , , Vascular remodeling occurs in the context of many vascular diseases. In essential hypertension, inward eutrophic remodeling is observed in which there is a reduced lumen diameter and increased media-to-lumen ratio without change to the total amount of wall tissue. These changes function to normalize circumferential wall stress in hypertensive arteries. In aortic aneurysmal disease, medial degeneration weakens the vessel wall, leading to dilation, thinning, and potential rupture. 
Vascular diseases and late failure of vascular procedures contribute to significant morbidity and mortality and highlight the importance of understanding vascular identity changes in vascular remodeling. This review focuses on recent progress in the understanding of vascular identity changes in the context of alterations in vascular structure and function in health and disease.

## Markers of vascular specification and identity
The vascular endothelium lines the lumen of all blood and lymphatic vessels and plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular function and identity. The tunica intima, composed of EC bonded by adherens and tight junctions, is supported by an elastic lamina. EC have numerous global cell-specific molecular markers ( ). , , However, arteries, veins, and lymphatics each express unique molecular markers, reflecting their specialized roles in the circulatory and lymphatic systems. , During early embryogenesis, molecular identity is determined to specify the arterial, venous, and lymphatic systems. , , 

## Arterial identity
Arteries typically have a thicker wall compared with veins and lymphatics, and contain a thicker tunica media with more SMC and extracellular matrix (ECM) components that supports their role as resistance vessels in a high-pressure hemodynamic environment ( ). , , Resistance arteries, compared with conduit arteries, have less elastin and rely more on SMC contractility to maintain tone. However, unique patterns of endothelial gene expression distinguish arterial identity. This includes cell surface proteins such as erythropoietin-producing hepatoma interactor B2 (Ephrin-B2), Neuropilin 1, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGF-R2), the transcription factor Sox17, Notch1, and Notch4, as well as their pathway components including the surface protein delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4) and transcription factors (NotchICD, Hey1/Hey2) ( , A, ). These proteins function in concert to promote EC arterial identity while suppressing venous and lymphatic identity. 
Notch1 and Notch4 are arterial EC-specific markers of identity and drive expression of their downstream transcription factors, Hey1 and Hey2. Notch signaling also promotes expression of its arterial specific cell-surface ligand, DLL4. Ephrin-B2 expression is highly enriched in embryonic arteries and persists in adult arteries, and is also downstream of Notch signaling. , , Ephrin-B2 is not only downstream of Notch but physically interacts with EphB4 to regulate arterial and venous specification. Ephrin-B2 signaling promotes arterial identity, and EphB4 signaling promotes venous identity. Sox17 is an EC-specific SoxF family transcription factor that is upstream of the Notch pathway, and its expression promotes arterial differentiation, indicating a key role of Sox17 in directing arterial identity. , Sox17 is a critical component of a mechanoresponsive signaling cascade that promotes arterial identity by integrating shear stress signals to upregulate arterial-specific genes such as ephrin-B2 and components of the Notch pathway ( , A). VEGF represses venous identity in adult venous EC without induction of arterial identity through inhibition of EphB4, a major venous EC identity determinant. VEGF also stimulates DLL4 expression without upregulating Ephrin-B2. , Notch signaling downregulates lymphatic marker Prox1 and venous marker COUP-TFII via Hey1 and Hey2. Thus, Notch suppresses the lymphatic and venous phenotype to induce arterial cell fate. 

## Venous identity
In contrast with arteries, veins and lymphatics have medial walls with fewer elastic laminae, reflecting their role as capacitance vessels, returning blood and lymph to the heart under a lower pressure. , The gradient in elastin and SMC layers is lesser in veins than in arteries and absent in lymphatics. Venous identity is governed by cell-surface receptors, ligand interaction, and transcription factor expression. EphB4 is the major venous cell surface receptor that guides venous differentiation during embryogenesis and maintains venous identity in adulthood ( ). EphB4 expression is relatively unique to veins and its expression pattern begins in the embryonic stage and persists within the adult vasculature. , , EphB4 expression, alongside Ephrin-B2, drives arterial and venous EC segregation, as when Ephrin-B2 comes into contact with its receptor EphB4 the cells are repulsed and migrate away from one another. In contrast with arteries, most veins and lymphatics have valves projecting into the lumen to prevent retrograde flow of blood or lymph, respectively , EphB4 is required for postnatal venous valve development, because EphB4 loss-of-function mutations cause venous valve aplasia. The persistence of EphB4 expression from embryonic development into adulthood suggests its significance in actively maintaining venous identity and function. EphB4 expression is promoted by COUP-TFII expression, a transcription factor. , , Expression of COUP-TFII is regulated by brahma-related gene 1, an ATPase integrated within an adenosine triphosphate-dependent chromatin remodeling complex, also known as an epigenetic reader protein ( , B). COUP-TFII expression, alongside Prox1, a transcription factor, inhibit expression of the transmembrane arterial identity markers VEGF-R2 and Neuropilin 1. 

## Lymphatic identity
Lymphatic vessels are distinct in their lack of smooth muscle layers and role in transporting lymph, which contains inflammatory mediators, fluid, antigens, and immune cells. , , , Lymphatic vessels arise from veins during embryogenesis and share some common identity markers with veins, but also have their own specific markers ( ). Prox1 expression drives lymphatic identity from venous EC, and Prox1 attenuates VEGF signaling and suppresses arterial markers including Notch; thus, Prox1 is a master regulator of lymphatic vessel development. Sox18 and COUP-TFII are expressed in venous EC and synergistically activate Prox1 expression ( , C). , , VEGFC and the receptor VEGF-R3 function as an essential signaling pathway for lymphatic vessel growth in physiological and pathological conditions. Podoplanin, a transmembrane protein in lymphatic vessels, is required for the separation of lymphatics from veins. Lymphatic vessels contain valves formed by lymphatic EC. Several proteins that are more widely expressed have been shown to have essential roles in lymphatic development and maintenance of lymphatic identity. Piezo1, a mechanically activated ion channel, regulates formation of lymphatic valves. Yes-associated protein and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif are indispensable for lymphatic valve generation and lymphatic plexus development. FOXP2 has been identified as a flow-sensitive regulator of transcriptional control in lymphatic morphogenesis. , 

## Biomechanical forces and vascular identity
Arterial, venous, and lymphatic systems are characterized by differing hemodynamic environments ( ). , EC lining these vascular systems form a monolayer in direct physical contact with blood flow that mediates remodeling of the vessel wall in response to hemodynamic forces. , Shear stress is a frictional force exerted on the EC by the blood flow. Laminar flow and high levels of shear stress promote the release of antithrombotic and pro-EC survival factors, such as tissue plasminogen activator and nitric oxide. EC regulate homeostatic levels of shear stress by directing remodeling of the luminal diameter of blood vessels—increasing diameter in response to high shear stress and decreasing diameter in response to low shear stress—to maintain optimal flow conditions. , EC possess a tripartite mechanosensory complex composed of PECAM-1 (CD31), VE-cadherin, and VEGF-R2; this complex transduces shear stress into molecular signals including src and phosphatidylinositol-3-OH kinase activation that regulate EC alignment, cytoskeletal reorganization, and nuclear factor-kappa B expression. 
Arteries and veins respond differently to biomechanical forces owing to each of their unique structure and function. Arteries transmit high-pressure blood and are exposed to high magnitudes of shear stress (>15 dyne/cm2 in the aorta). , This high-pressure environment requires a robust arterial wall to withstand outward mechanical forces. In contrast, veins and lymphatics are exposed to lower magnitudes of shear stress (0-4 dyne/cm2) and pressure, but are subject to compressive forces such as intra-abdominal pressure or by adjacent musculature. The magnitude of shear stress can shape arterial identity in adulthood. Increased magnitudes of shear stress drive the expression of arterial identity markers such as Ephrin-B2 and Notch in a dose-dependent manner. , Ephrin-B2 is upregulated during angiogenesis and arteriogenesis but typically decreases in expression during adult life. Changes in hemodynamics, and specifically increased shear stress, stimulates and upregulates Ephrin-B2 expression in adult life. Interestingly, shear stress promotes differentiation of endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) into arterial EC that express Ephrin-B2. Mechanosensitive molecules such as ENaC and Piezo channels detect hemodynamic forces and trigger vascular adaptation. Under homeostatic conditions, both arteries and veins experience predominantly laminar flow. , However, in lymphatics the lymph flow is variable, involving recirculatory and laminar flow that contributes to their specialized transport role. , Oscillatory flow contributes to lymphatic valve stability and maintenance via a FOXC2 mediated mechanism. 

## Vascular function, cell composition, and molecular markers
Blood vessels are composed of cell types defined by unique identity markers that contribute to their function. These markers not only distinguish the cells from one another, but also play roles in vascular homeostasis, remodeling, and disease. For example, EC express identity markers that control vascular permeability, whereas SMC markers play a role in mechanical strength and contraction ( ).

## Endothelial cells
EC form the inner lining of blood vessels and control vascular permeability. They express key identity markers that are well documented, including PECAM-1 (CD31), Tek/Tie2, VE-cadherin, and von Willebrand Factor (vWF) ( ). vWF is a glycoprotein that functions in primary hemostasis by adhesion to platelets and collagen and is regarded as a universal marker for EC. VE-cadherin is a classical cadherin specific to vascular EC that functions in cell-cell adhesion and control of vascular permeability and is universally expressed in both arteries and veins. However, phosphorylation patterns of VE-cadherin differ between arteries and veins owing to local hemodynamic forces, influencing how VE-cadherin functions in different vessel types. For example, VE-cadherin is phosphorylated under normal conditions in veins, but not in arteries, which may relate to the respective environmental differences in shear stress between the two vascular systems. 

## Smooth muscle cells
SMC are essential for providing the mechanical strength and contractile force of blood vessels. SMC have diverse phenotypes, including a contractile phenotype and synthetic phenotype ( ). In the physiological arterial environment, contractile SMC predominate. Contractile SMC have a low proliferative index and regulate vessel tone. Although vessel walls generally have low cellular turnover, when activated SMC can undergo phenotype switching and become synthetic SMC; synthetic SMC have increased proliferation and synthesis of proteins. SMC also have unique molecular markers, including genes that alter the SMC phenotype. Calponin, smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (MYH11), smooth muscle alpha actin (α-SMA), and SM22α are transcriptionally regulated in an SMC-specific fashion. Additionally, MYH11 and SM22α are smooth muscle-specific contractile proteins. , Transcriptomics has shown that α-SMA is highly expressed in differentiated, contractile SMC, and α-SMA expression diminishes in concert with the SMC phenotype switch to a proliferative state. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies of peripheral arteries and veins show that arteries contain a greater degree of contractile SMC, whereas veins have greater relative proportions of EC and fibroblasts. During vascular repair, veins have predominantly activated fibroblast derived cells whereas arteries show modulation of SMC phenotype. These differences in vascular wall cell composition are hypothesized to underlie the distinct responses of arteries and veins to vascular injury. 
There are notable differences in the composition and behavior of SMC between arteries and veins. SMC in arteries generally express higher levels of contractile markers, such as SM22α and MYH11, compared with veins. This increased expression of contractile proteins enables arteries to maintain a contractile phenotype, which is crucial for handling the higher blood pressure in the arterial circulation. In response to vascular injury, neointimal hyperplasia may occur, in which SMC undergo a phenotypic switch from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype ( , B). As they adopt this phenotype, SMC express markers associated with synthetic function, such as Krüppel-like factor 4 and non-muscle myosin heavy chain IIB.
Clinically, medial degeneration in abdominal aortic aneurysms is characterized by a loss of SMC and ECM density, which makes the arterial wall susceptible to dilation and aneurysm formation. Increased MMP expression favors medial degradation and alters the aortic wall phenotype from arterial to a more venous-like structure. However, markers of specific arterial and venous identity have not yet been studied in the context of abdominal aortic aneurysms.

## Adventitial cells
The adventitia, the outermost layer of the blood vessels, is primarily composed of fibroblasts and plays a critical role in control of lumen size and vascular wall remodeling responses. , Beside fibroblasts, the adventitia also contains pericytes, macrophages, T cells, and stem cells that carry out important immune and self-renewal functions. , 

## Pericytes
Pericytes are vascular support cells commonly found in the microcirculation. These cells regulate vessel diameter, permeability, and EC survival. Pericytes are poorly defined owing to their heterogeneity, and no single marker uniquely identifies them. However, markers such as platelet-derived growth factor receptor-beta and neuron-glial 2, a chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, are used to characterize pericytes. , , Other markers relatively specific to pericytes include CD146, a cell adhesion molecule, and desmin, an intracellular intermediate filament protein. , , Human cardiac pericytes express mesenchymal stem/stromal cell markers including CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105. Activated pericytes express aminopeptidase A, which plays a role in angiogenesis. 

## Fibroblasts
Fibroblasts are of embryonic mesenchymal origin, but are a difficult cell type to define, despite having a variety of markers. Fibroblasts reside in connective tissues and regulate ECM homeostasis. Fibroblasts also serve as progenitors for various mesenchymal cell types during injury and repair. When activated, fibroblasts express fibroblast activation protein and α-SMA. As generally quiescent cells in adulthood, fibroblasts undergo a phenotype switch to a myofibroblast when its resident tissue is damaged. Myofibroblasts have comparatively more contractile functions than resident fibroblasts. In neointimal hyperplasia, adventitial or medial fibroblasts are converted to myofibroblasts that proliferate and migrate to form the neointima. Interestingly, fibroblast functions are remarkably organ specific. , For example, fibroblasts in the skin exhibit specialized wound healing functions by differentiating into myofibroblasts that generate contractile forces to draw the wound edges together; in contrast, fibroblasts in the heart produce ECM that insulates portions of the heart to propagate electrical conduction. Several molecular markers are relatively specific to fibroblasts. Vimentin, fibroblast-specific protein 1, discoidin domain receptor 2, and Thy1 (CD90) are common markers of fibroblasts. , , Additionally, transcription factor 21 and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-alpha are expressed in cardiac fibroblasts. , 

## Perivascular adipose tissue: Adipocytes
Perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) is the specialized adipose tissue that surrounds the vasculature and is composed predominantly of adipocytes. Once thought to have the defined role of structural protection from nearby tissues, PVAT tissue is now known to have robust endocrine function and can modulate SMC function by promoting endothelial nitric oxide synthase phosphorylation and nitric oxide release. The adipocytes in the PVAT are close to the adventitia and maintain vascular homeostasis. PVAT adipocytes contain a mixture of white, brown, and beige adipocytes in varying proportions depending on the anatomic location. Uncoupling protein 1 is expressed in thermogenic PVAT and is found in large quantities in adipocytes near the thoracic aorta and expressed to a lesser extent in other vascular beds. CIDE-A and PRDM16 are markers of beige and brown adipocytes and function in thermogenesis, while CD137 and TMEM26 are specific markers of beige adipocytes and are found in aortic PVAT. , Functional PVAT adipocytes maintain vascular homeostasis, and dysfunctional or loss of PVAT adipocytes are currently being studied as potential mediators of vascular disease.

## Macrophages
Macrophages are the effector cells of the innate immune system and have specific markers. Macrophages are broadly categorized into M1 (proinflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) subtypes, although this classification is somewhat oversimplified. M1-type macrophages are classically activated, that is, they are activated by interferon-γ and produce a proinflammatory cytokine cascade including tumor necrosis factor-α. M1-type macrophages thus function in stimulation of the Th1 response, antigen presentation, and phagocytosis. M2-type macrophages are alternatively activated by interleukin (IL)-4, IL-10, and IL-13, and produce anti-inflammatory cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β. M2-type macrophages are regenerative and function in tissue repair, wound healing, and remodeling. However, some experts prefer not to use this polarized macrophage classification because it excludes the context-dependent nature of macrophage activation and function. CD14, CD68, and CD163 are defined as common markers for both M1 and M2. , M1-type-specific markers include CD80, CD86, and CXCL8, whereas CD206 and CCL22 are reported to be M2-type-specific markers. , 

## T cells
Regulatory T cells (Treg) play an anti-inflammatory role in vascular homeostasis and are crucial for angiogenesis and mitigating vascular disorders. , Treg, a subset of CD4+ T cells, regulate plaque regression in atherosclerosis and promote immune tolerance after heart transplant. Although Treg primarily exert immunosuppressive effects, conventional proinflammatory CD4+ T cells, including T helper type 1 (Th1) cells (marked by T-bet) and Th2 cells (marked by GATA3), can contribute to vascular inflammation. Th1 cells drive cell-mediated immunity and inflammation by producing interferon-γ and activating macrophages, whereas Th2 cells support humoral immunity by secreting IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 that enhance B-cell activation and eosinophilic responses. Additionally, CD8+ T cells (identified by CD3 and CD8) have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and vascular injury. Forkhead box P3 is expressed in Treg and works as a master regulator of development, lineage commitment, and Treg differentiation. , , Another common marker, cytotoxic T lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 is expressed on the cell surface of Treg and acts to maintain homeostasis. , However, T lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 is expressed on activated T cells so is not absolutely Treg specific. Additionally, tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 is used as a marker for defining highly functional Treg. Regulation of Treg is under control of programmed cell death 1-programmed cell death ligand 1 signaling, where programmed cell death 1 acts as a sensor at the Treg surface under constitutive expression in homeostatic conditions, but increases during infection to promote the inflammatory response. 

## Vascular stem cells
Vascular stem cells are present in all three layers of the vessel wall. , , These cells are generally quiescent in adult life, but renew and differentiate in response to vessel injury. Regeneration of vascular cells after vessel injury was previously thought to be limited to the proliferation of existing differentiated cells in the vessel wall. However, recent data show that vascular stem cells function to maintain normal homeostasis and also function in reparative processes such as SMC neointimal formation in response to EC injury. Vascular stem cells, as do all stem cells, have homing properties. For example, circulating EPC are recruited to sites of ischemia and contribute to tissue neovascularization. All stem cells have the potential for differentiation, and therefore their identity markers sometimes overlap with other cell types. Mesenchymal stem cells express CD105, CD73, and CD90 as defined surface markers. Stem cell antigen-1 is a surface receptor that is expressed on hematopoietic stem cells. CD34+/CD45–/Flk-1+/CD31+/Sca-1+/c-Kit+ cells are stem cells residing in the intima with potential to differentiate into EC, whereas Sca-1+/CD34+ double-positive cells reside in both the intima and media with the ability to differentiate into SMC. Protein C receptor is a marker for hematopoietic stem cells and mammary epithelial stem cells. , , 
Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) are reprogrammed somatic cells that exhibit properties similar to embryonic stem cells, including the ability to differentiate into virtually all somatic cell types. Notably, iPSC can be reprogrammed to cells of the vessel wall including EC, SMC, and pericytes, positioning them as a promising tool in regenerative medicine and vascular disease therapy. , , Because iPSC can be derived from any patient, they provide an accessible and patient-specific cell source. Current applications include the generation of functional blood vessels, modeling of vascular diseases, and investigating gene-drug interactions using iPSC-derived tissues. For example, iPSC-derived vascular SMC embedded in collagen scaffolds accelerate diabetic wound healing in mice. In addition, iPSC-derived vascular SMC increase angiogenesis in both diabetic wound healing and hindlimb ischemia in mice. 

## Pathological vascular remodeling and identity change

## Poststenotic dilation
Patients with bicuspid aortic valves are at increased risk of aortic dilatation. Fibrillin-1 is decreased in aortae distal to BAV compared with aortae distal to tricuspid aortic valves. Matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) expression is greater in aneurysms with BAV, , , whereas tissue inhibitor metalloproteinase (TIMP)-1, a repressor of MMP, decreases in thoracic aortic aneurysms with BAV. These alterations in the balance of proteinase expression favor degradation of the media layer and weakening of the aortic wall. BAV is also associated with dysregulation of the Notch signaling pathway, which is involved in arterial specification. , 
Additionally, poststenotic dilation is commonly observed distal to arteriosclerotic stenoses and is well-documented in the arteries of the lower extremities. The process of poststenotic dilation is believed to result, in part, from increased shear stress on the arterial wall distal to areas of stenosis; however, the specific molecular mechanisms driving this phenomenon, particularly in smaller arteries, is not well-described. 

## Hypertension
Hypertension is both a driver and consequence of vascular remodeling, characterized by endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular stiffness. , Arteries in hypertension undergo inward eutrophic remodeling with decreased luminal diameter and increased medial thickness; notably, there is no change in the total amount of arterial wall tissue, although there is an increased media-to-lumen ratio ( , A). These arterial wall adaptations function to normalize circumferential wall stress in hypertensive arteries. On a molecular level, the expression of MMP2, MMP9, and MMP14 increases in hypertensive aortae ( ). TIMP-1 also increases in hypertension. These changes in MMPs and their inhibitor, TIMP-1, contribute to the remodeling of the aortic wall by promoting ECM degradation and turnover, facilitating SMC migration and proliferation, and ultimately leading to the thickening and structural reorganization of the vessel. Both MMP and TIMP are associated with hypertensive disease and may be used as biomarkers. In addition, Notch3 is necessary for the adaptive response of the renal vasculature to vasoactive systems in angiotensin II-induced hypertension. Loss of Notch3 contributes to hypertensive vascular remodeling, facilitating endothelial dysfunction and vascular wall reorganization. 

## Atherosclerosis
Atherosclerosis is the deposition and accumulation of lipids and fibrous material in the intima of arteries, leading to plaques that have structural and physiological consequences. Atherosclerotic plaques can enlarge into hemodynamically significant stenoses or even occlusions driving coronary, carotid, and peripheral artery disease. Interestingly, atherosclerotic arteries possess the ability to maintain their luminal diameter until the enlarging plaque is approximately 40% of lumen diameter. This homeostatic regulation is termed the Glagov phenomenon: arteries adapt to stenoses by physiological enlargement in which the total cross-sectional area increases while maintaining luminal diameter without significant change to wall thickness (eutrophic outward remodeling) ( , A); the diameter of the lumen remains constant, whereas the size of the vessel wall increases in the presence of stenoses. Biomechanical forces mediate early atherosclerosis, because high shear stress is atheroprotective, whereas low shear stress, oscillatory shear stress, and disturbed flow promote atherosclerotic plaque formation. , Similar to other forms of vascular injury, atherosclerosis is characterized by an SMC phenotypic switch from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype. Interestingly, SMC originate from distinct embryonic lineages depending on their anatomical location, and these lineage differences may contribute to variations in SMC susceptibility to atherosclerosis across different vascular regions. For example, Homeobox (HOX) genes play a role in mediating this susceptibility or resistance to atherosclerosis; increased HOX gene expression is associated with atheroresistant regions, such as the thoracic aorta, whereas lower HOX gene expression is associated with atherosclerotic regions, such as in the aortic arch. This pattern has been observed consistently across mouse, rat, and porcine models. However, changes in vascular identity have not been studied widely in atherosclerosis.

## Pulmonary hypertension
The function of the pulmonary vasculature is somewhat reversed from systemic circulation, because the pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood away from the heart and the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood to the heart. Ephrin-B2, a systemic arterial identity marker, is expressed in developing pulmonary arteries and not veins, similar to systemic circulation. However, EphB4, a systemic venous marker, is expressed in both developing pulmonary arteries and veins but the functional consequence of its expression in pulmonary arteries is not fully understood. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a vascular disease characterized by progressive arterial remodeling, ultimately leading to increased right heart pressure and right heart failure. The pathophysiology of PAH involves vascular dysfunction and structural remodeling of small pulmonary arteries, leading to thickening of the vascular media, luminal occlusion, and the development of characteristic plexiform lesions. The specific signaling pathways and underlying molecular mechanisms are well studied but not fully characterized. Loss of function bone morphogenetic protein receptor 2 mutations causing aberrant transforming growth factor beta signaling is observed in patients with PAH. , Also, aberrant Notch3 signaling is necessary for the development of PAH. , With regard to EC markers, levels of vWF are elevated in PAH; therefore, vWF has been used as one of the biomarkers for PAH progression. 

## Arteriovenous malformations
Arteriovenous malformations (AVM), naturally occurring AVF, are a pathological connection between arteries and veins that bypass the capillaries and tissue. AVM often display abnormal vascular identity markers. Loss-of-function mutations in the venous marker EphB4 is associated with the development of AVM and vein of Galen mutations, a severe neonatal genetic cause of AVM. , Another venous marker, COUP-TFII is aberrantly expressed in humans with cerebral AVM. Tek/Tie2 mutations are associated with venous malformations, and loss of an exclusive vascular smooth muscle gene, Notch3, causes CADASIL, also known as cerebral autosomal-dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy, a hereditary small vessel vasculopathy mainly involving the blood vessels of the brain. Additionally, Notch signaling mutations are implicated in AVM formation, contributing to abnormal vessel identity and structure, underscoring the significance of arteriovenous identity markers in AVM pathogenesis. , , 

## Tumor-associated vascular remodeling
Tumor EC (TEC) differ significantly from their non-neoplastic counterparts, as TEC exhibit distinct phenotypes, becoming more proangiogenic and metabolically active compared with non-TEC. The vascular endothelium of tumor tissue expresses specific markers that alter their identity and phenotype ( ). In particular, aminopeptidase A, a constituent of activated pericytes, plays a role in tumor angiogenesis. Aminopeptidase N (CD13/APN) is also expressed in TEC and contributes to their metastatic ability. The extradomain B of fibronectin, although undetectable in homeostatic conditions, displays a vascular or diffuse stromal pattern of expression in the TEC of many aggressive solid tumors. , E-selectins may promote metastasis by facilitating heterotypic interactions between cancer cells and EC. TEC upregulate expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9. The expression of EphB4 and Ephrin-B2 is altered in some cancer cells. In Kaposi sarcoma, human herpesvirus type 8 drives expression of Ephrin-B2 while inhibiting EphB4; this expression change drives tumor cells to an arterial identity and phenotype. Conversely, squamous cell carcinoma displays aberrant expression of EphB4, and higher levels of EphB4 expression are observed in advanced and metastatic disease. 

## Vascular remodeling after vascular surgery and endovascular therapy
Vascular interventions, such as AVF creation, vein grafting, patch angioplasty, and endovascular procedures, provoke significant vascular remodeling, often accompanied by changes in vascular identity ( ). 

## Altered identity in AVF and vein grafts
Venous limbs exposed to the fistula environments distal to AVF display adaptive outward dilation and intimal thickening beyond the basal intimal thickening typical of the renal failure environment. These veins somewhat resemble the arterial wall; interestingly, venous limbs of the AVF have increased expression of EphB4 and Ephrin-B2, signifying dual arteriovenous identity. In vivo studies show that activation of Ephrin-B2 signaling is associated with adaptive venous remodeling in AVF. Notch and downstream effectors of the Notch pathway, such as Jagged1, DLL4, and Hey1/Hey2, are also significantly upregulated in AVF. Additionally, MMP2, an MMP that promotes SMC proliferation, is increased during venous adaptation in AVF. Taken together, AVF acquire a dual arteriovenous identity within the venous limb during adaptive venous remodeling.
Vein grafts are exposed to a high-pressure arterial environment and adapt mainly via increased wall thickening without significant outward dilation. EphB4 expression is reduced, whereas Ephrin-B2 expression is not gained in a human vein graft and a rat vein graft model. , Using human vein culture under arterial shear stress, EphB4 is reduced without the induction of Ephrin-B2. , As such, there is a difference between the venous identity changes in AVF and in vein grafts; vein grafts are characterized by a lack of vascular identity, whereas AVF are characterized by a dual identity. One possible mechanism may be the differing hemodynamic changes in AVF compared with vein grafts; AVF are exposed to supra-arterial magnitudes of flow owing to the low-pressure outflow of the venous system, with considerably higher shear stress and disturbed flow in AVF compared with vein grafts. 

## Vascular patch identity is based on the patch environment
Patch angioplasty is a common procedure used to repair an arteriotomy in vascular surgery. Commonly used material includes autologous vein or artery, bovine pericardium, or acellular synthetic material such as polyester. In a rat aortic patch angioplasty model, autologous vein patches lose their EphB4 expression and gain Ephrin-B2 expression, thus showing a loss of venous identity and gain of arterial identity in response to environmental change. When autologous vein is used as a patch in the inferior vena cava (IVC), the patch retains its venous identity. Because Ephrin-B2 expression is stimulated by increased magnitudes of shear stress, these data show that patched vein tissue identity is, at least in part, dependent on hemodynamic forces. However, in a polyester patch angioplasty model, EPCs that form the neointima acquire molecular arterial or venous identity within the patch, depending on whether the patch is sutured into the aorta or IVC, respectively. When sutured in the aorta, EPC form neointima on the patch and gain expression of molecular arterial identity markers such as Ephrin-B2 and Notch4. When the patch is sutured in the IVC, it gains expression of molecular venous identity markers such as COUP-TFII and EphB4. Interestingly, the addition of an aortocaval AVF to venous patches results in expression of arterial identity markers and retention of venous identity markers on the IVC polyester patch. These results suggest that, similar to vein patches, polyester patches acquire the identity of their surrounding vascular environment. However, because polyester patches are acellular, this finding suggests a role for infiltrating cells, such as EPC, in the determination of vascular identity that varies based on their environment. 

## Arterial balloon angioplasty and stenting
Endovascular therapy such as balloon angioplasty is favorable owing to its advantages of being less invasive, causing less physiological stress and fewer periprocedural complications. Increased efficacy of newer endovascular technologies has led to widespread adoption in practice. , On a molecular level, arteries have reduced Ephrin-B2 expression in a carotid artery balloon angioplasty injury model in aged rats. In a similar model but with younger rats, female and male rats demonstrate differing regulation of identity in response to balloon injury; female rats exhibit reduced phosphorylation of Ephrin-B2 and downstream signaling that correlates with less neointimal thickness compared with male rats. 
The advent of stents over the past 30 years has improved the patency rates of endovascular procedures significantly. However, neointimal hyperplasia causing in-stent restenosis is a major challenge that led to the development of rapamycin-coated stents and the important RAVEL trial (A Randomized Comparison of a Sirolimus-Eluting Stent With a Standard Stent for Coronary Revascularization). , The expansion of stents in the vascular bed causes EC injury and loss of the EC layer. EC denudation exposes the underlying vascular surface, promoting inflammation and shift of SMC phenotype from contractile to synthetic, the molecular markers of which are described elsewhere in this article. Synthetic SMC proliferate and expand into the lumen, causing restenosis owing to concentric neointimal hyperplasia. This process also occurs secondary to EC injury after balloon angioplasty ( , B, bottom). Focal EC injury, leading to endothelial dysfunction or denudation, can trigger a maladaptive phenotype switch in SMC from a contractile to a synthetic state. These synthetic SMC proliferate and migrate to the site of endothelial injury, where they form a focal eccentric neointimal layer. This layer, referred to as eccentric neointimal hyperplasia, can causes significant stenoses ( , B, top). In addition, SMC exposed to laminar flow—secondary to denuded endothelium undergo apoptosis leading to increased turnover and thus neointimal hyperplasia. The stented region, specifically the area distal to the stent struts, experiences dramatically altered hemodynamic forces, including regions of stagnant flow and areas of both low and high wall shear stress. These changes disrupt re-endothelialization and contribute to the development of restenosis by promoting the phenotypic switch of SMC from contractile to synthetic. However, changes in molecular identity after stent placement are not yet well-understood.

## Conclusions
Changes in vascular identity correlate with changes in vascular structure and function. Molecular markers of arterial, venous, and lymphatic identity display plasticity in development and throughout adulthood. Alterations to vascular identity markers, either genetically or in response to environmental change, drive vascular remodeling that contributes to health and disease. Genetic mutations of the venous identity marker EphB4 cause loss of arteriovenous cell fate differentiation and drive AVM. Venous limbs of AVF acquire arterial identity that drives adaptive dilation and thickening of the vessel wall in response to the changed hemodynamic environment. It is possible that modulation of vascular identity markers could treat vascular diseases and optimize outcomes after vascular surgical procedures, capitalizing on sex-specific differences in vascular identity may inform more tailored, personalized therapeutic approaches. Targeting key pathways that regulate homeostatic vascular identity may prevent maladaptive remodeling in the clinical setting.
