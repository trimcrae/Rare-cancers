## Abstract
Laminins (LMs) are a family of heterotrimeric glycoproteins that form the structural foundation of basement membranes (BM). By acting as molecular bridges between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) through integrins and other surface receptors, they regulate key cellular signals that influence cell behavior and tissue architecture. Despite their physiological importance, our understanding of the role of LMs in cancer pathobiology remains fragmented. In this article, we review the diverse functions of LMs in promoting cancer cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration—critical steps in cancer metastasis. Beyond their direct effects on tumor cells, LMs influence stromal interactions and modulate tumor microenvironment dynamics, affecting processes such as angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration, cancer-associated fibroblast activation, and immune evasion. Understanding the complex roles of LMs in cancer biology, as well as their differential expression patterns in malignancies, could provide new diagnostic tools for predicting disease outcomes and pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies, such as targeting LM-receptor interactions or modulating ECM dynamics to impede tumor growth and metastasis.
Keywords: Extracellular matrix, Basement membrane, Cancer Prognosis, Tumor biomarkers, Metastasis, Tumor microenvironment, Angiogenesis, Integrins

## Introduction
The extracellular matrix (ECM), composed of components such as collagen, fibronectin, integrins, laminins (LMs), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), plays a critical role in regulating tissue mechanics and serves as a key element in the mechanical signaling pathways of tissues. The ECM is a dynamic network of multidomain macromolecules organized in a tissue-specific manner, where its components interact to form a structurally stable framework that contributes to the mechanical properties of tissues. Within this framework, basement membranes (BM) represent specialized structures of the ECM, providing support beneath all epithelia, blood vessels, and capillaries, among other structures [ , ].
LMs are the major non-collagenous components of the basal lamina within BMs, performing various adhesive and stimulatory functions. The lamina densa, the electron-dense region of the basal lamina, is predominantly composed of polymeric networks of collagen and LM, integrated by crosslinkers such as nidogen and perlecan [ ].Both epithelial and mesenchymal cells contribute to the deposition of LMs into BMs, where they help form complex molecular networks essential for BM assembly and function. LMs also serve as reservoirs for cellular growth factors, influencing cell behavior and tissue physiology. Alongside LMs, other BM components such as collagen IV and VII, glycoproteins, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) play key roles.
Alterations in the biomechanical properties of BMs are central to malignant transformation, initiating a cascade of changes in cellular behavior that enhance tumor invasiveness and metastatic capabilities [ , ]. Evidence indicates that LMs within epithelial BMs are crucial regulators of interactions between tumor cells, the ECM, and other cell types in the tumor microenvironment (TME), thus influencing transformation, proliferation, stemness and migration. In addition to their direct effects on cancer cells, LMs in the endothelial BM are pivotal for forming capillary-like structures, guiding pericyte-endothelial cell interactions that promote vessel maturation and stability during tumor angiogenesis. Additionally, endothelial LMs maintain barrier function, regulating the movement and distribution of molecules, migrating cells, nutrients and oxygen into the tumor parenchyma [ , ].
In this review, we will first discuss the structure of LMs, their receptors, and their role in the vascular endothelium. We will then provide a comprehensive overview of the alterations in LM expression observed in various human malignancies and their consequences for tumor progression.

## The laminin family
LMs are a large family of high-molecular-weight extracellular glycoproteins (400–900 kDa), that form heterotrimers composed of disulfide-linked α, β, and γ chains [ ]. The five α, three β, and three γ chains combine to ensemble 16 known LM isoforms, which vary biochemically but maintain a conserved structural arrangement. Since 2005, LMs have been named based on their chain composition; for example, the trimer of α1, β1, and γ1 chains is designated LM-111 [ ]. Although all LMs isoforms fold into a conserved cross-shaped structure (Fig.  A), differences in chain amino acid sequences and the lost of specific structural domains result in variations in the final structure of each LM isoform.
 The long arm of LMs, approximately 80 nm in length, is formed by the C-termini of the α, β, and γ chains, arranged as a triple-stranded α-helical coiled-coil stabilized by disulfide bridges. The longer α chain C-terminus features a globular domain divided into five subdomains (LG1-LG5), which mediate receptor interactions; the β and γ chains lack these globular domains. The N-terminal ends of LMs make up the short arm, which contains the LN domains responsible for self-assembly and interactions with other ECM molecules in the BM [ ]. Isoform diversity originates largely from this short arm: some isoforms have a complete set of domains, while others lack one or more (Fig.  B) [ ]. Thus, LMs vary in domain composition, including isoforms with a full set of domains (LM-111, LM-121, LM-211, LM-213, LM-221, LM-511, LM-521, and LM-523), those missing the LN domain in the α-chain short arm (LM-3A11, LM-3A21, LM-411, LM-421, and LM-423), or with absent LN domains in β- and/or γ-chains (LM-3B32, LM-522). There is also one LM lacking N-terminal LN domains in all three chains (LM-3A32).
LMs with complete short-arms domains polymerize to form ternary nodes in a lattice-type supramolecular network with type IV collagen. LMs lacking the LN domain in the α-chain, however, cannot self-assemble in vitro but can still integrate into the BM through interactions with other LMs and ECM molecules [ ]. For instance, LM-3A32, which cannot self-polymerize due to short arm truncations, binds to LM-311, LM-321, and collagen VII. Its β2-chain associates with collagen VII, nidogen, and fibulin, connecting it to the collagen IV network and allowing for heteropolymer formation within the BM [ ]. Secreted HSPGs, such as agrin and perlecan, further stabilize LM-collagen networks: agrin binds the α1 chain of LM and connects with α-dystroglycan (α-DG) via its C-terminal LG domains, while perlecan interacts with LM through its heparan sulfate chains or indirectly via nidogen [ ].
LM subunits undergo specific posttranslational modifications. They are glycosylated in the endoplasmic reticulum, where N-terminal high-mannose oligosaccharide chains stabilize the subunits and protect them from degradation [ ]. Trimer assembly begins with the association of β and γ chains, followed by integration of the α chain. The assembled trimer then undergoes terminal glycosylation in the Golgi apparatus before secretion. Once secreted, LMs may be proteolytically processed, affecting receptor binding and function [ ]. For example, LM-332 undergoes multiple proteolytic modifications during secretion and after BM incorporation (Fig.  A). Synthesized as a precursor, LM-332 matures through limited proteolysis involving the N-terminus of the γ2-chain and both the N- and C-termini of the α3A-chain [ ]. This processing is mediated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP-2 or MMP-14, or MMPs from the tolloid family, such as BMP-1. Proteolytic maturation of the α3A-chain C-terminus does not affect LM-332 incorporation into epidermal BMs, but imparts specific properties [ ]. LM-332 isoforms lacking the LG4/5 domain support stable adhesion structures like hemidesmosomes, while isoforms retaining this domain are associated with wound healing and cell migration [ – ]. Given the central role of LM-332 in carcinogenesis, its proteolytic processing is often dysregulated in malignancy (Fig.  B).
 The distribution of LM chains varies by tissue- and cell-specific, adapting to developmental and pathological contexts. While α and γ chains are broadly expressed, β chains show more restricted expression, conferring tissue-specific functions to LM isoforms. For instance, LM-α1 is absent from endothelial BMs, yet LM-111 and LM-121 are prominent in epithelial BM during early embryogenesis but later restricted to reproductive organs, kidneys, and liver [ ]. In adult mammary glands, LM-111 and LM-332 are essential components of the BM encapsulating the gland [ ]. The α2-chain, in LM-211, LM-221, and LM-213, is mostly localized in neuromuscular systems, particularly skeletal and cardiac muscles. In contrast, the α5-chain, found in LM-511, LM-521, and LM-523, is broadly expressed across adult epithelial, neuromuscular, and vascular tissues, as well as during embryogenesis. LM-511 supports mouse embryonic stem cell renewal via integrin interactions (e.g., α6β1 and αvβ1) that sustain pluripotency markers like Oct-4, Sox2, Tert, UTF1, and Nanog [ ]. The α4-chain in LM-411, LM-421, and LM-423, is predominantly expressed in mesenchymal cells. LM-411 and LM-511 are the main isoforms in vascular BMs: LM-411 is found in all blood vessels, and LM-511 is restricted to capillaries and venules [ , ]. Additionally, LM-β2 appears in vascular BMs, suggesting the existence of LM-421 and LM-521 isoforms. LM-411 is also secreted by bone marrow-derived cells [ ]. The α3-chain, found in LM-332, LM-311, and LM-321, is primarily located in the skin and other epithelial tissues [ ], with LM-332 specifically localized to the BM beneath epithelial cells [ ].

## Integrin and non-integrin laminin receptors
The LG domains at the C-terminus of LM α-chains are key for interacting with cell surface receptors, transmitting both biochemical and mechanical signals from the ECM to the cell. LM receptors fall into two major groups: integrins and a diverse set of proteoglycans and glycoproteins referred to as non-integrin LM receptors. The five tandem LG domains show specificity, with LG1-3 primarily binding integrins and LG4-5 interacting with non-integrin receptors [ ], potentially enabling both receptor types to bind the same LM molecule and fine-tune intracellular signaling. Proteases can further regulate these interactions by cleaving the peptide link between LG1-3 and LG4-5 domains [ ].
Integrins are the most extensively studied LM receptors. They are heteromeric membrane proteins composed of α and β subunits; 24 integrins have been identified in mammals. Specific LM-binding integrins include α1β1, α2β1, α3β1, α6β1, α6β4, α7β1, α9β1, αvβ3, αvβ5, and α5β1 [ ]. The coiled-coil structure formed by the LM α, β, and γ chains, particularly the C-terminus of the α chains, contributes to integrin binding site. The α1 integrins serve as primary receptors for vascular laminins. Integrins α6β1 and α3β1 bind to LM-511, and α6β1 can also bind to LM-411. LM-511 features an RGD sequence at its N-terminus, recognized by integrins α5β1, αvβ1, or αvβ3.
LM-Integrin interactions play crucial roles in adhesion, migration, differentiation, and survival by activating signaling pathways, such as mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK), intracellular calcium, G-proteins, phospholipase D, cytoskeletal components, focal adhesion kinase (FAK), and small Rho GTPases [ , ]. Furthermore, LM-integrin support the assembly of other protein networks within the BM.
Non-integrin LM receptors include dystroglycans (DG), heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) like syndecans and agrin, Lutheran blood group glycoproteins (also known as basal cell adhesion molecule, BCAM; CD173), melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM) (also referred to as MelCAM or MUC18; CD146), melanoma-associated chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (MCSP), and galectins [ , ]. These receptors help anchor cells to the BM and convey signals related to proliferation, survival, and differentiation from the ECM. They can also modulate LM-integrin interactions and alter integrin-transduced signals [ ].
The extracellular alpha chain of DG is one of the most prominent non-integrin LM receptors. α-DG interacts with LG4-LG5 domains of LM-α1, LM-α2, and LM-α5, to influence nuclear architecture and transcription through tyrosine phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of β-DG [ , ]. Disruption of DG/LM-α2 interaction is associated with congenital muscular dystrophy, and defective LM/DG interactions have been linked to progression of epithelial tumors [ ]. Evidence suggests that LM/α-DG interaction may act as a tumor suppressor in many epithelial cancers by inhibiting Ras/MAPK activation, counteracting interactions between LM-111, LM-511, and LM-521, and α6β1 integrin in cancer cells [ ]. Interestingly, the LM/DG complex can also incorporate agrin, which interact with a domain formed by LM β- and γ-chains. The Z + agrin/α-DG complexes further localize integrins on the cell surface. Although the role of LM/DG/agrin interactions in cancer is still unclear, agrin expression has been linked with pro-tumor effects [ ].
MCAM, a receptor exclusive to LM-411, is linked to poor prognosis in melanoma and other cancers [ ]. MCAM/LM-411 also regulates inflammatory T-cell infiltration into the brain [ ]. In colorectal cancer, LM-411 is associated with CD8+ T cell infiltration and improved prognosis [ ]. BCAM-1, expressed on vascular cells, binds uniquely with LM-511 and outcompetes LM-511 binding to α3β1, α6β1, α6β4, and α7β1 integrins. BCAM-1, but not its shorter isoform BCAM-2, promotes cancer cell invasion by competing LM-511 interaction with β1-containing integrins [ ]. Heparan sulfates and sulfated glycolipids bind to basic surface regions in LG4-5, overlapping but disctinct from α-DG binding sites [ ].
This complexity highlights the intricate role of LM in cellular interactions and the challenges of dissecting LM-triggered signaling pathways in ECM dynamics.

## Role of laminins in the progression of human cancers
While mutations in genes encoding various cellular components are well recognized as drivers of malignant transformation, mutations in genes encoding ECM proteins are not typically regarded as cancer-causing factors. However, evidence points to a prominent role of LMs in various stages of oncogenesis, including the acquisition of the transformed phenotype. As summarized in Table  , alterations in the expression or localization of many LMs and specific LM chains have been implicated in the progression of most, if not all, human cancers. In the following sections, we will provide a comprehensive review of these changes in LM expression across the main cancer types, the epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation of LM chains, and the role of limited proteolysis of LM chains within the tumor interstitial space. Additionally, we will explore the significance of these processes in malignant transformation, metastasis, the acquisition of aggressive cancer hallmarks, and their impact on the prognosis of major human malignancies.
 

## Breast cancer
Breast cancer is among the most common cancers globally, with a higher prevalence in women. At the molecular level, breast cancer is classified into three subtypes based on hormone receptor presence (hormone receptor positive; HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 expression (HER2+), or the absence of these markers, known as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). While HR+ subtypes often exhibit higher laminin levels, the interaction between laminins and breast cancer is complex and influenced by factors within the TME.
Different LMs have been implicated in the breast carcinogenesis. One of the first studied was LM-111, since it was found that whereas mammary epithelial cells form polarized colonies resembling normal breast acini on 3D LM-111-rich gels, breast cancer cells form disorganized tumor-like structures [ ]. Later, it was found that myoepithelial cells from healthy breast tissue promoted luminal breast epithelium polarity and BM deposition more effectively than tumor-derived myoepithelial cells [ ]. These differences were associated with reduced LM-111 deposition by tumor-derived compared to normal myoepithelial cells. Importantly, restoration of colony architecture in LM-111 gels by downmodulation of integrins or oncogenic receptors in cancer cells, leads to reversion of the malignant phenotype and the formation of polarized growth-arrested colonies [ ]. These findings underscore the central role of LM-111 in myoepithelial-luminal cell interactions and suggest alterations in LM-111 deposition and basement membrane formation may contribute not only to breast cancer progression but even to malignant transformation. Supporting this, loss of cell-surface LM-111 anchoring is linked to increased tumor growth and poor clinical outcomes in breast and brain cancer patients, leading to cancer cell proliferation and chemotherapy resistance [ ]. The low cancer cell attachment to LM-111 may be partially related to the dysfunctional glycosylation of α-DG due to acetylglucosaminyl-transferase (LARGE1/2) enzyme downregulation [ ], a feature observed in several advanced cancers.
LM-332 has been also implicated in breast cancer progression, though evidence is partially contradictory. Initial immunohistochemical (IHC) studies suggested reduced LM-332 expression in breast adenocarcinomas compared to healthy breast tissue or benign epithelial lesions [ , ]. From these data, the authors inferred that reduced LM-332 expression might contribute to cancer progression by altering interactions between cancer cells and the surrounding environment. High LM-332 levels can be associated with a better prognosis, especially in luminal breast cancer subtypes. However, later studies indicated upregulation of LM-332 in distinct breast cancer subtypes, particularly in TNBC and HER2+ tumors, and its association with increased motility and invasion through enhanced β1 and β4 integrin signaling in cancer cells [ – ]. Supporting a pro-tumor role of LM-332, malignant transformation of breast epithelial cells has been associated with autocrine production of this LM-332, providing a critical survival signal to rescue cancer cells from anoikis through activation of the α6β4 integrin/Rac/NF-κB pathway [ ]. Notably, reversion of breast tumor cells to normal phenotype requires the upregulation of HOXD10 and the downregulation of the NF-κB pathway, which antagonistically regulate the expression of miR-34c, miR-30e and miR-144 [ ]. In this way, upregulation of these miRs inactivates both EIF5A2 and SCA1-induced transcription of matrix MMP-9, leading to BM stabilization. Regression of TNBC has been also associated with upregulation of miR-17/20a, which targets the LM-α3 chain [ ], reinforcing the idea that high LM-332 levels promotes breast cancer progression. Curiously, LM-332 stabilization has also been implicated in the breast cancer cell phenotype reversion [ ].
LM-332 upregulation mainly occurs in the stroma, particularly in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [ ]. This suggests that LM-332 promotion of cancer cell movement and tumor dissemination is primarily due to its upregulation in the TME [ ]. LM-332 induces metastasis through various mechanisms, including activation of signaling pathways downstream of the α3β1 integrin, leading to cytoskeletal remodeling involving PI3K and the Rho family of small GTPases [ ], increased expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 [ ], and activation of signaling pathways regulating EMT [ ].
Although most previous studies did not differentiate between LM-α3 chain isoforms, α3A is the predominant isoform in breast epithelium [ ]. Nevertheless, LMs consisting of the α3B chain combined with β1, β2, and γ1, have also been implicated in breast cancer by influencing stromal cell function. Specifically, LM-3B11 exerts a distinct effect on microvascular ECs [ ]. Interestingly, recombinant LM-3B11 exhibits relatively weak cell adhesion activity through both α3β1 and α6β1 integrins. Upon exposure to LM-3B11, microvascular and umbilical vein endothelial cells display notable formation of lamellipodial protrusions, membrane structures involved in cell movement and shape changes, in a Src- and PI3K-dependent manner. LM-3B11 induces stronger phosphorylation of Src and Akt compared to other LMs [ ]. IHC analyses of non-invasive and invasive breast carcinomas revealed a significant decrease in the number and intensity of α3B-positive vessels compared to normal breast tissue. The LM α3B-chain is often completely absent or minimally detected in blood vessels of invasive carcinomas. In contrast, the α4 chain shows increased expression in non-invasive and invasive carcinomas, particularly in diffusive invasive carcinomas. The α5 chain appears to decrease in expression in some invasive carcinomas, specifically in capillary vessels [ ]. This expression pattern might mirror the cytokine profile in the TME, as pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α downregulate LM-α3B but upregulate the α5 chain in cultured vascular endothelial cells [ , ]. Therefore, the downmodulation of LM-3B11 in breast cancer could trigger angiogenesis by providing a positive signal for the branching of capillaries infiltrating the tumor.
Several studies have explored how LM-511 influences breast cancer cell behavior, especially regarding invasion and the development of distant metastases. All these studies consistently show that LM-511 actively contributes to the dissemination and metastasis of breast cancer [ , ]. Although the connection between LM-511 expression and specific breast cancer subtypes is not fully understood, IHC of a limited number of high-grade human breast cancer specimens has demonstrated that the expression of the LM-α5 chain, a component of LM-511, is not exclusive to a particular subtype but is notably elevated in TNBC [ ]. These findings suggest the association between high LM-511 expression and the development of aggressive metastatic cancers.

## Prostate cancer
Prostate cancer (PCa) is a prevalent malignancy and one of the most common types of cancer in men. Typically, prostate cancer is an androgen-dependent disease, where androgens bind to and activate androgen receptors in prostate cells, leading to cell proliferation and survival. Evidence suggests that prostate cancers with higher Gleason scores, indicative of more aggressive tumors, are often associated with higher levels of androgen receptor expression [ ]. However, some prostate cancers may undergo a transition to a hormone-refractory state, also known as castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPCa), characterized by increased aggressiveness and resistance to hormonal therapies. As detailed below, various studies have demonstrated an association between LM expression and PCa progression and metastasis. Although differences in LMs between androgen-dependent and CRPCa are not well-established, it is plausible that the heightened aggressiveness of CRPCa might be linked to changes in LM and/or integrin expression patterns in cancer cells [ , ].
Disassembly of hemidesmosomes, involving proteins such as α6β4 integrins and plectin, is particularly crucial in the initiation of PCa [ ]. In normal prostatic tissue or in prostatic intraepithelial neoplastic (PIN) lesions, LM-332 binds to α6β4 integrin to maintain hemidesmosome integrity. However, in PCa, LM-332 forms complexes with α3β1 in actively migrating cells [ ]. This switch in integrin receptors is mediated particularly by EGFR signaling, which triggers hemidesmosome disassembly by inducing phosphorylation of the cytoplasmic tail of β4 integrin [ ]. Simultaneously, EGFR downregulates LM-332 expression in PCa cells [ ]. The downregulation of β4 integrin in aggressive CRPCa cell lines could also be a consequence of hypermethylation of the ITGB4 promoter region or the generation by alternative splicing of a shorter β4 variant that cannot heterodimerize with the α6 integrin chain [ ]. In highly metastatic PCa cell lines, there is a coordinated downregulation of β4 integrin and LM-332 chains during the induction of the EMT program by the transcription factor ZEB1 [ ]. It is notable that the lack of expression of LM-332 is quite specific for PCa, compared to colon or breast carcinoma samples assayed at the same time [ ]. Although protein levels are extremely low or undetectable in PCa tissue, the mRNA for LAMA3, LAMB3, and LAMC2 could be detected in most of the tumor samples by northern blot [ ]. This suggests that the loss of LM-332 at the protein level in PCa might be mediated by post-transcriptional mechanisms.
The above results strongly indicate the downregulation of LM-332 and β4 integrin as a central element in inducing an invasive and metastatic phenotype in PCa cells. However, other reports suggest that LM-332 or its subunits might play an active role in promoting PCa motility and invasion. Indeed, while Drake and coworkers showed that ZEB1 repressed LAMC2 and integrin β4 expression in PCa cells, they also found that in vitro migration of PCa cells not expressing LM-332 was enhanced when co-cultured with PCa cells overexpressing LM-332 [ ]. This cooperative migration was also observed in organoids formed by PCa cells and fibroblasts, and this enhanced motility of cancer cells in the cocultures correlated with an increased expression of LM-332 chain as well as other ECM proteins [ ]. It is interesting that co-culture of fibroblasts and PCa cells also triggered the expression of MMP14 and other proteases, such as cathepsin L, that can induce partial proteolysis of ECM proteins to generate bioactive fragments. In this sense, it has been shown that matriptase digestion of the LM-β3 chain enhances speed migration and directional persistence of PCa cells on LM-332 [ ]. Collectively, the results suggest PCa metastasis may be boosted by LM-332 downregulation in PCa cells, which reduce intercellular adhesion, but also by LM-332 upregulation and proteolytic processing in the stromal elements, to trigger PCa motility.
In addition to LM-332, other LMs may induce PCa metastasis in a paracrine manner. In an in vitro study, Graf and colleagues observed that the supernatant of cultured primary human mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) stimulated the migration of highly metastatic PCa cell lines, but not of those with low metastatic potential [ ]. The isolation of migratory factors secreted by MSC identified several ECM proteins, including LM-β2- and LM-γ1 chains, as the main proteins with the highest migration-inducing activity. Remarkably, recombinant LM421 induced migration in metastatic PCa cell lines, surpassing the migration response induced by MSC conditioned medium [ ]. Additionally, the LM-γ1-derived C16 peptide has been shown to enhance the formation of invadopodia in highly metastatic PCa cell lines [ ]. C16 regulates the function of two scaffold proteins important in the formation and function of invadopodia, namely Tks5 (also known as SH3PXD2A or Fish), a SH3-containing protein that interacts with actin-regulatory proteins and signaling molecules, and Tks4 (also known as SH3PXD2B or Tks4/Fish), which plays a central role in the stabilization of MMP14 on the cell surface of invadopodia [ ]. It is remarkable that the C16 peptide has also been implicated in the migration of other transformed cell lines.

## Ovarian cancer
Ovarian cancer ranks as the eighth most common cancer among women globally but stands out as one of the most lethal. This heightened lethality is attributed to challenges in diagnosis, often occurring at an advanced stage, and the absence of an appropriate therapeutic regimen [ ]. Ovarian cancer is, indeed, a heterogeneous group of diseases, with classification dependent on the cell type undergoing transformation. While the majority (90%) of ovarian cancers originate from ovary epithelial cells, tumors can also develop from germ cells or connective tissue cells (stromal tumors). Epithelial tumors are further subclassified based on histology and characteristics, with the most prevalent being serous ovarian carcinoma (~ 70%), followed by endometrioid (10–20%), clear cell (5–10%), and mucinous (< 5%) carcinomas. The undifferentiated carcinoma subtype is the most aggressive, associated with a very poor prognosis.
Various reports suggest a role for LMs in the pathogenesis of ovarian cancer. Interestingly, the expression and distribution of LMs may vary among ovarian cancer subtypes. LM-332 and LM-511 are frequently detected in serous ovarian cancers, while the BM of mucinous carcinoma also exhibits LM-α4 chain immunoreactivity. In contrast, endometrioid carcinomas show reduced expression of LM-332 but an enhancement of LM-111 [ ]. Proteomic characterization of ovarian clear cell carcinomas reveals abundant expression of LM-411 [ ], and histological studies indicate immunoreactivity against the LM-γ2 chain [ ]. Elevated LM levels have also been detected in the ascitic fluid of patients with peritoneal ovarian cancer dissemination [ ].
LMs exhibit both pro- and anti-tumor effects in ovarian cancer. Examination of 370 ovarian cancer samples revealed reduced expression of LAMA3 in carcinoma tissues compared to normal and para-carcinoma tissues [ ]. LAMA3 expression was also lower in para-carcinoma compared to normal tissues, indicating a role of oncogenic factors in LAMA3 repression. The mechanism behind this repression may be epigenetic, given that LAMA3 methylation was higher in carcinoma than in normal tissues. Crucially, the analysis of five-year survival rates demonstrated significantly higher recurrence-free survival and overall survival rates in patients with high LAMA3 expression compared to the LAMA3 low-expression group. These findings suggest a clinically relevant role for LM-α3 (and likely LM-332) as a suppressor of ovarian cancer progression.
However, other studies have reported pro-tumoral activities for LM-111 or its peptides in ovarian cancer cells. Intraovarian injection of cancer cells with LM-111 resulted in larger ovarian tumors with higher metastatic potential and ascites production compared to injection with gelatin [ ]. Certain LM-111-derived peptides, like AG73 and IKVAV, increased tumor volume, metastatic spread, and ascites production. Notably, the AG73T peptide, a scramble peptide of AG73, caused the disaggregation of ovarian tumor spheroids, rendering these cancer cells more sensitive to cisplatin [ ]. Stimulation of ovarian cancer cells with LM-111 or its peptides triggered cell adhesion through β1 integrins, proliferation through the ERK pathway, and survival through the PI3K pathway, along with the upregulation of pro-survival proteins like Bcl2. This provides a mechanistic framework for the pro-tumoral activities of this LM isoform [ ]. LM-111, AG73, and IKVAV also induced the concomitant expression of the tumor suppressor p53 and its inhibitor Mdm2, neutralizing the anti-tumoral effect of p53. Interestingly, the LM-111-derived peptides A12 and YIGSR, which reduced tumor growth and metastasis, failed to induce Mdm2 in ovarian cancer cells [ ]. However, the mechanism behind the anti-tumoral activity of A12 and YIGSR peptides remains undetermined.
LMs are implicated in the peritoneal metastasis of ovarian cancer through a paracrine mechanism. Overexpression of ETS1, a transcription factor associated with poor prognosis, induced the secretion of large-size exosomes containing high levels of LM-511 in ovarian cancer cells. LM-511 facilitated the uptake of ovarian cancer-derived exosomes by omental macrophages through interaction with the αvβ5 integrin. This uptake polarized peritoneal macrophages toward a pro-tumoral M2 phenotype expressing CXCL5 and CCL2, serving as chemoattractants that direct ovarian cancer cell migration to the peritoneum. Administration of ETS1-expressing exosomes enhanced omental metastasis of ovarian cancer cells, and this effect is inhibited by αvβ5 integrin inhibitors [ ].
In ovarian cancer, a noteworthy finding is the presence of a fusion gene known as LAMC2-NR6A1, suggested to act as a driver for oncogenic transformation in ovarian cells [ ]. This fusion gene results from the translocation between intron 12 of the LAMC2 gene on chromosome 1 and intron 1 of the NR6A1 gene on chromosome 9. It leads to the production of a short LM-γ2 chain, termed LM-γ2F, which cannot assemble with other LM chains but retains the EGF-like domains of the LM-γ2 DIII region. The LM-γ2F fragment stimulated the EGFR pathway in vitro, demonstrated by the phosphorylation of EGFR, ERK, and AKT, promoting anchorage-independent ovarian cancer cell growth and motility [ ]. Knockdown of LM-γ2F reduced, whereas LM γ2F overexpression promoted, the growth of ovarian xenografts implanted in immunodeficient mice. These analyses strongly suggest that the LM-γ2F fusion protein activates important signaling pathways in ovarian cancer progression, although more evidence is needed to confirm its role as a driver mutation for this malignancy.

## Oral cancer
Oral cancer constitutes a substantial portion of cancer cases and deaths worldwide. It encompasses various forms, with squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) being the most prevalent, affecting tissues like the lips, tongue, hard and soft palate, pharynx, and larynx. Understanding the factors contributing to oral cancer development and dissemination is crucial for effective prevention, early intervention, and improved patient outcomes.
Initial IHC analysis using a LM polyclonal antibody in a limited number of OSCC biopsies revealed a predominantly linear staining pattern at the interface in well-differentiated cases, while poorly-differentiated cases exhibited intense cytoplasmic expression within tumor cells [ ]. Recent research has unveiled the multifaceted involvement of LMs in oral carcinogenesis, encompassing interactions with tumor cells, stromal components, and the TME.
LM-332 is one of the most extensively studied LMs in oral cancer, with its expression detected in OSCC but not in dysplastic oral tissue [ ]. The LM-γ2 chain is present in tumor-budding areas surrounded by myofibroblasts, indicating its implication in the invasive phenotype of OSCC [ , ]. These findings propose LM-332, or its γ2 chain, as potential early diagnostic markers for malignant transformation, invasion and poor prognosis. However, in vitro studies present a more intricate scenario, indicating reciprocal regulation between E-cadherin and LM-332 levels. LM-332 binding to α3β1 integrin triggered E-cadherin-mediated adhesion, reducing motility in poorly invasive OSCC cell lines. Silencing of the LM-γ2 chain, which prevented heterotrimeric LM-332 formation, enhanced in vitro migration and in vivo tumorigenicity. LM-γ2 knockdown correlated with reduced α3β3 integrin levels and impaired E-cadherin-mediated cell-to-cell adhesion [ ], a hallmark of the EMT program necessary for cancer cell metastasis. Consistently, downregulation of LM-332 expression has been reported upon activation of the EMT program by overexpression of the Snail transcription factor in OSCC cell lines [ ].
While the role of heterotrimeric LM-332 remains controversial, there is substantial consensus regarding the association of elevated LM-γ2 chain levels with OSCC cell invasion. The regulation of LM-γ2 chain protein levels involves distinct mechanisms. Long non-coding RNAs CASC9 and BBOX1, typically upregulated in OSCC, act as sponges for miR-545-3p and miR-3940-3p, respectively. This interaction impeded the downregulation of LAMC2 by these miRs, consequently increasing LM-γ2 protein levels [ , ]. Although EMT induction by Snail overexpression downregulated heterotrimeric LM-332, LAMC2 levels remained unchanged, thereby promoting the deposition of processed LM-γ2 chain, known for its pro-migratory effects [ ]. As mentioned below, Snail may also regulate other LM chains, suggesting that a combination of LMs, rather than a single one, is necessary for EMT in OSCC cells. The monomeric LM-γ2 chain potentially affects OSCC cell motility and proliferation by activating the EGFR/ERK signaling pathway [ ]. This may occur through indirect mechanisms involving integrin receptors or via direct interaction of LM-γ2 proteolytic fragments derived from domain III with EGFR, as observed in other cancer types [ ]. Importantly, positive feedback transcriptional regulation has been proposed for EGFR and LM-γ2 chain in various OSCC types [ , ]. Monomeric LM-γ2 could also influence OSCC dissemination by affecting stromal cells. Extracellular vesicles enriched in LM-γ2 secreted by OSCC cells stimulates lymphangiogenesis [ ], potentially facilitating lymphatic dissemination.
Other LM-332 chains may also impact OSCC carcinogenesis. The amplification of LAMA3 and the increased expression of the LM-β3 chain has been associated with a more aggressive OSCC phenotype, including enhanced invasive potential and metastatic behavior [ ]. It has been suggested that the LM-β3 chain enhances the formation and activity of invadopodia [ ], specialized structures facilitating ECM degradation and tumor cell invasion. Although the interaction of the LM-β3 chain with integrin receptors is known, the specific LM-β3-induced signals are unknown.
Another LM involved in OSCC carcinogenesis is LM-111. Specifically, the LM-111-derived peptide AG73 positively modulates the migration and invasion of OSCC cells. AG73 triggered OSCC cell invasiveness by inducing syndecan-1- and β1 integrin-signaling pathways that upregulate the secretion of MMP-9 [ ], a key enzyme in ECM degradation, angiogenesis induction, and tumor cell migration. The C16 peptide, also derived from LM-γ1 cleavage, promoted OSCC invasiveness by activating the β1 integrin, Src kinase, and the ERK1/2 signaling pathway  [ ], all of them regulating invadopodia activity, ECM degradation and tumor cell invasion.
The LAMA4 and LAMA5 genes are also implicated in regulating the invasive phenotype of OSCC. EMT effectors, like Snail, upregulate LAMA4 while downregulating LAMA5 expression [ ]. Chromatin immunoprecipitation revealed direct binding of Snail to specific promoter regions of both genes. Notably, Snail-induced EMT also alters integrin and non-integrin LM receptor expression; it silences Lu/BCAM, a non-integrin receptor for LM-α5, switches integrin α6β4 to α6β1, and triggers neoexpression of integrin α1β1, a specific receptor for LM-411 [ ]. These changes contribute to OSCC invasion by weakening LM-411-mediated adhesion to fibronectin. In addition, EGF and TGFβ1-induced EMT silences both LAMA4 and LAMA5 expression while upregulating LAMC2 [ ]. Therefore, different signals may lead to different LM outcomes during EMT induction, influencing OSCC invasion and metastasis.

## Gastric cancer
Gastric cancer stands as one of the most aggressive and deadliest cancers, with poorly understood factors driving tumor progression and drug resistance. Identifying effective diagnostic and prognostic factors for this cancer is thus a crucial clinical need. Reports suggest that specific LMs may exhibit pro- and anti-tumor activities in gastric cancer. Iwamuro et al. characterized the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cell lines on LM-411, LM-421, LM-511, and LM-521 [ ]. All four LMs promoted tumor cell proliferation, with LM-521 exerting the strongest effect. However, none influenced cancer cell stemness, as indicated by CD44 and LGR5 expression. LM-511 and LM-521 affected cell morphology, with cells cultured on these LMs displaying larger cytoplasmic areas compared to controls. While LM-411 promoted cell migration, LM-511 reduced invadopodia formation and induced an adhesive phenotype in gastric cancer cells.
The pro-tumoral role of LM-411 in gastric cancer is also suggested by the analysis of its subunits. Wang and coworkers found that upregulated LAMA4 expression in gastric tumors may serve as an independent poor prognostic factor [ ]. Conversely, tumors with genetic alterations or epigenetic silencing of the LAMA4 gene showed better disease-free survival. High LAMA4 levels correlated with M2-type macrophage, dendritic cell, and CD4+ T cell infiltration but were negatively associated with CD8+ T cell infiltration [ ]. Elevated expression of LAMB1, the gene encoding the LM β1-chain, was associated with increased M2 macrophage infiltration and worse prognosis in two gastric cancer patient cohorts [ ]. Additionally, LAMC1, encoding the LM γ1-chain, positively correlated with advanced clinical stage, tumor depth, and lymph node and distant metastases in a cohort of 42 gastric cancer patients [ ]. LAMC1 knockdown significantly reduced cell invasion, and suppressed the Warburg effect through AKT signaling [ ].
High LM-332 expression is associated with gastric carcinoma progression and poor prognosis. Three studies reported upregulation of LM β3 and γ2-chains in gastric cancer, correlating with an aggressive phenotype and poor outcome [ – ]. It was suggested LAMB3 and LAMC2 expression regulation by histone modification, with reduced methylation observed in 20–40% of tumors and cancer cell lines [ , ]. Interestingly, the LAMA3 gene promoter was often silenced [ ]. Elevated LM-γ2 chain levels also appear to enhance the invasive capacity of gastric cancer cell lines by influencing MMP2 and MMP9 expression [ ].

## Colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death globally. CRC is a heterogeneous disease with distinct histological and molecular subtypes [ , ]. It primarily arises from epithelial cells lining the colon and rectum, with the BM playing a key role in tumor progression. LMs, particularly LM-332, significantly influence CRC proliferation, metastasis, and stromal-epithelial interactions, affecting prognosis. However, studies report conflicting results, indicating a complex interaction between CRC and LMs.
Sordat et al. analyzed LM-332 and its integrin receptors α3β1 and α6β4 in normal colon tissue, adenomas, and carcinomas [ ]. They found increased LM-332 levels in adenomas and CRC, though its distribution was discontinuous. Integrins were mainly at the basal surface of epithelial cells but showed altered localization in adenomas and carcinomas. Elevated stromal LM-332 correlated with CRC malignancy, and β3- and γ2-chain levels in tumor cells were linked to poor prognosis [ , ]. These chains were associated with tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance. However, other studies noted a decrease in the β3:γ2 mRNA ratio in metastatic vs. early-stage disease [ ], suggesting this ratio affects LM-332 function in cell anchoring and migration.
LMs with α5-chain also impact CRC. LAMA5 knockdown in HT29 cells reduced expression of genes related to epithelial and stem cell differentiation, causing partial dedifferentiation and increased apoptosis through WNT- and mTORC1-dependent pathways [ ]. LAMA5 silencing also reduced HT29 cell proliferation and migration on various LMs, including LM-332 and LM-411. Additionally, LM-521 promoted CRC cell self-renewal via STAT-3 phosphorylatio [ ], similar to its role in supporting cancer stem cell (CSC) self-renewal in vitro [ ].
Tumor cell stemness significantly contributes to metastasis. LM-511 and LM-521 enhance Matrigel invasion, correlating with higher risks of recurrence or metastasis [ , ]. LAMA5 is linked to decreased drug sensitivity (e.g., 5-fluorouracil), reducing survival in LAMA5 high CRC patients [ ]. High LM-511 levels associate with pro-angiogenic signatures, and microvessel density correlates with LAMA5 deposition in primary CRC and liver metastasis. LAMA5 silencing reduced liver metastasis by disrupting vessel structure, Notch signaling and perfusion [ ]. Cancer cell production of LAMA5 is driven by NF-κB activation from TNFα released by myeloid cells in the TME [ ], with NF-κB also regulating LAMA5 in endothelial cells [ ].
In contrast, LAMA4 upregulation in the tumor stroma is linked to better prognosis in low-grade CRC[ ]. Restoration of superoxide dismutase-3 (SOD3), often silenced in the TME, increased LAMA4 expression, stabilizing HIF-2α, activating WNT, and inducing FoxM1 [ , ]. SOD3 upregulation also reduced LAMA5 expression, promoting a favorable LAMA4/LAMA5 ratio and allowing better CD8+ T cell infiltration. High SOD3 and LAMA4 levels were associated with lower recurrence in stage II CRC patients, suggesting that the LAMA4/LAMA5 ratio may influence clinical outcomes [ , , ].

## Pancreatic cancer
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) poses significant clinical challenges due to the absence of reliable early biomarkers and its highly metastatic nature. The stroma plays a key role in PDAC progression [ , ]. The stiff ECM and desmoplasia not only provide structural support but also actively shape a TME that fosters tumor growth and metastasis, while limiting treatment efficacy by hindering drug and immune cell penetration.
LMs are implicated in the shaping of PDAC-associated ECM. LM-332, particularly, is linked to a malignant phenotype and poor prognosis [ ]. While trimeric LM-332 supports PDAC cell survival, proliferation, and invasion, individual chains can trigger more aggressive signals. LAMB3 overexpression correlates with poor prognosis and PI3K pathway activation, driving cell proliferation and EMT-related genes like SLUG and SNAIL [ ]. LAMB3 knockdown in PDAC cells reduces tumor growth and metastasis in mice. High LAMC2 expression also predicts reduced survival in PDAC patients [ , ]. The LM γ2 chain promotes EMT and cancer cell invasion, partly by enhancing Na+/H+ exchanger 1 (NHE1) activity, which regulates actin-binding proteins for cell movement [ ]. LAMC2 also upregulates mesenchymal markers and MMP-2/9 expression, aiding ECM degradation in metastasis [ ], and may contribute to gemcitabine chemoresistance by increasing ABC transporter levels [ ].
Beyond LM-332, studies also suggest LAMA4 plays a role in PDAC progression. LAMA4 methylation is inversely correlated with tumor grade and positively linked to overall survival in PDAC patients; this is compatible with LM-α4 upregulation in PDAC compared to healthy pancreas [ ]. LAMA4 is notably higher in highly metastatic PDAC and correlates with liver metastasis and increased stromal cell infltration, particularly CAFs [ ]. This suggests LAMA4 expression by PDAC cells may promote a microenvironment favorable for liver metastasis, reflecting aggressive tumor behavior and poor survival.

## Liver cancer
Liver cancer encompasses various diseases, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), infantile hepatoblastoma, angiosarcoma, and fibrolamellar carcinoma, a rare HCC variant affecting young adults. HCC and ICC account for over 90% of liver cancers, with most research focusing on these two types and less on angiosarcoma.
The interaction between cancer cells and ECM components, especially LMs, is crucial for HCC metastasis. IHC analysis of HCC, peritumoral, and normal liver tissues shows LM-332 mainly localized along the endothelial BM. In the liver parenchyma, LM-332 was specific to HCC tissues, with LM-γ2 chain expression found only in metastatic HCC nodules. This staining pattern correlated with poorer survival in patients [ ]. In ICC, LAMC2 upregulation was also linked to metastasis, osteopontin expression, and TGF-β pathway activation, correlating with patient outcomes [ ]. In HCC cell lines, the LM-γ2 chain promoted migration through ERK, RAC1, FAK, and AKT pathways, which are essential for cell adhesion and spreading [ ]. Elevated LM-γ2 levels were also seen in CSCs within HCC and ICC tumors, particularly in K19-positive hepatic progenitor cells. LM-332 induced CSC markers, inhibited HCC cell proliferation in vitro, and slowed xenograft tumor growth in vivo, suggesting LM-332 fosters a niche that supports stemness, chemoresistance, and quiescence [ ].
LM-332 plays varied roles in liver cancer progression, depending on the integrin receptors expressed by cancer cells. In certain HCC cell lines, LM-332 promotes proliferation via the ERK1/2 pathway, but not in others, despite ERK1/2 activation [ ]. LM-332 binds to α6β4 and α3β1 integrins, with α3 integrin being key. In responsive cells, knocking down the α3 chain halts proliferation, while its overexpression restores proliferative activity in resistant lines. It has been suggested that LM-332 interacts with α6β4 integrin, phosphorylating the β4 chain, which then engages α3β1 integrin to drive proliferation [ ]. Additionally, α3β1 integrin is linked to sorafenib resistance in HCC cell lines. LM-332 blocks ERK1/2 dephosphorylation and FAK ubiquitination, processes typically triggered by sorafenib, suggesting that LM-332 secreted by hepatic stellate cells induces chemoresistance via α3β1 activation. This activation enhances FAK membrane localization, preventing its degradation [ ]. LM-332 also contributes to sorafenib and doxorubicin resistance by promoting stemness, as previously discussed [ ].

## Lung cancer
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with LM-332, the main LM isoform expressed in lung cancers [ ], playing a significant role in disease progression. LM-332 localized at the tumor invasive front and its expression correlated with vascular invasion and CAFs activity [ ]. In stage I patients, LM-332 overexpression emerged as an independent prognostic factor, with worse outcomes when combined with vascular invasion [ ]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), LM-332 was more commonly expressed in males but was less frequent in adenocarcinomas. Although individual correlations between LM-332, PTEN, and phosphorylated EGFR (p-EGFR) were not significant, the collective expression of LM-332, PTEN, p-EGFR, and phospho-Akt showed significant differences between LM-332-positive and negative patients. Cox regression analysis identified stage, LM-332 combined with Akt, and PTEN as the three most independent prognostic factors in NSCLC [ ].
The poor prognosis linked with LM-332 may stem from its pro-metastatic influence. In lung CSCs, a feedback loop between LM-332 and FAK/AKT/GSK3β signaling was found, promoting stemness and EMT traits [ ]. Collagen XVII shedding was crucial in maintaining LM-332 expression and EMT characteristics. High collagen XVII and LM-332 levels predicted poor prognosis and promoted lung metastasis in both mice and patients [ ]. Similar to gastric cancer, LM-γ2 chain expression specifically regulated the invasive capacity of adenocarcinomas and squamous cell carcinomas [ , ]. LAMC2 was significantly upregulated in lung cancer cells from brain metastases, underscoring its role in metastasis [ ]. While non-invasive lung adenocarcinomas expressed all three LM-332 chains, invasive tumors silenced the LM-α3 and LM-β3 chains, leaving only the LM-γ2 chain [ ]. This selective LM-γ2 expression likely weakened BM structures, promoting invasive behavior and activating pro-metastatic pathways. Secretion of the LM-γ2 chain acted as a motility factor, interacting with β1 integrins and triggering ZEB1-dependent EMT signaling. Knockdown of LM γ2 or β1 integrin inhibited metastasis in mouse models, emphasizing their role in lung cancer progression [ ]. Overall, LM-332, particularly its γ2-chain, drives malignant traits in lung cancer, leading to metastasis and poor prognoses.

## Glioblastoma and Brain cancers
Brain cancer includes tumors originating within the brain or nearby tissues and can be divided into primary tumors, which originate in the brain, and secondary tumors, which result from metastasis. The latter, typically originating from lung, breast, colon, or melanoma cancers, are more common than primary brain tumors. This section focuses on primary brain tumors, particularly glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), the most aggressive type, arising from glial cells. Classified as a grade IV tumor due to its high malignancy, GBM is highly invasive and lack of effective treatments resulting in an average survival of 12–15 months post-diagnosis.
LMs are key components of brain BMs, including the blood–brain barrier, perivascular BMs surrounding blood vessels, and those supporting epithelial cells in specialized brain regions like the choroid plexus. Additionally, the glia limitans, a BM-like structure, forms a boundary between brain tissue and surrounding regions. Early IHC studies showed LMs predominantly in tumor vasculature, as punctate deposits within the tumor, and at the brain-tumor interface [ , ]. Alterations in LM composition may disrupt brain homeostasis, break physical barriers, promote tumor invasion and metastasis, and stimulate angiogenesis, making LMs potential biomarkers for brain tumor progression.
LM expression in brain tumors varies by tumor type and location within the TME. For example, LM-α2 chain expression is notably elevated in mesenchymal and classical GBM subtypes, serving as a biomarker for these subtypes [ ]. GBM-associated blood vessels show increased LM-411, LM-511, and LM-211 expression, while LM-β2 chain levels remain low [ ]. In contrast, GBM tumor cells have higher LM-β2 expression. A notable shift in LM isoforms occurs during tumor progression: LM-421 is predominant in low-grade glial tumors (grade I-II), while grade III and IV (GBM) primarily express LM-411 [ ]. This transition from β2-containing to β1-containing LMs mirrors observations in progressing breast cancers [ ].
LM-411 expression has emerged as a significant prognostic marker for predicting GBM recurrence and patient survival [ , ]. Elevated LM-411 levels correlate with higher glioma grades, shorter median survival, and quicker recurrence. Additionally, LM-α4 in the cerebrospinal fluid correlates with GBM tumor size [ ]. Genetic ablation or downregulation of LAMA4, or pharmacological inhibition of LM-411 in GBM cells, drastically reduces invasion in vitro and halts tumor progression in mice [ , ], reinforcing the role of LM-411 in GBM progression. Mechanistically, LM-411 influences GBM malignancy by upregulating the integrin β1/Notch pathway, boosting expression of β1 integrins, Notch-1, and ligands like Jagged1 and delta-like-4 (Dll4) [ ]. Notably, LM-411 is predominantly located in capillary-like structures within GBM tumors [ ], suggesting it may drive tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and Notch-induced angiogenesis [ , ].
Notch pathway activation in GBM cells appears to trigger CSC-related pathways. In vitro, LM-411 stimulation induced CSC markers like CD133, Nestin, and c-Myc[ ]. CSCs are typically located in defined niches, with the perivascular region being most prominent in brain tumors [ ]. Analysis of primary tumors showed LM-411-positive blood vessels near tumor cells expressing CSC markers, suggesting that perivascular LM-411 helps sustain the CSC niche. The LM-α2 chain, produced by non-CSC and ECs, is essential for maintaining this niche, enhancing CSC survival, self-renewal, and DNA repair after radiation therapy, a common GBM treatment [ ]. In 3D cultures, LM-521, LM-421, and LM-111 increased stemness markers and promoted clonogenicity in glioma cells grown on electrospun polystyrene (ESPS) scaffolds, compared to standard culture [ ]. ESPS/LM stimulation may boost expression of the stemness-linked α6β4 integrin, which could enhance migration by interacting with LM-332, known to promote GBM adhesion and invasion [ , ].
LMs with the α5-chain, like LM-521 and LM-511, may also impact GBM progression. LM-511 enhanced GBM migration via α3β1 integrin binding, and α3β1 inhibition reduced invasion into brain tissue [ ]. Conversely, LAMA5 knockdown in GBM cells increased microtumor formation and non-invasive cells in a zebrafish xenograft model, indicating LAMA5 might inhibit GBM invasion [ ]. These contrasting findings could reflect species- or tissue-specific differences in migration mechanisms. Despite some unclear mechanisms, the evidence emphasizes LMs, particularly LM-411, as key players in GBM malignancy, contributing to CSC niche maintenance and tumor cell invasion.

## Melanoma
Melanoma is an aggressive skin cancer originating from melanocytes. The most common type, superficial spreading melanoma, starts as a flat, discolored patch that spreads before growing deeper. Despite advances in immunotherapy, the 5-year survival rate for advanced melanoma (stage IV) is around 20%. The TME significantly influences melanoma progression and therapy response, with inflammatory and stromal cells regulating tumor growth, invasion, and immune response.
Basal keratinocytes and the specific BM, termed basal lamina, anchor melanocytes through cell adhesion [ ]. In melanoma, BM remodeling leads to the expression of new adhesion receptors, facilitating interactions with fibroblasts and endothelial cells, enhancing motility, migration, and cell fate. Chemokine receptors, integrins, and LM isoforms are central to these interactions. Aberrant BM remodeling in melanoma involves altered LM isoform expression, deposition, mislocalization, and degradation.
Key dermo-epithelial LM isoforms include LM-332, LM-511, and LM-211 [ ]. IHC studies showed strong LM-α4- and LM-α5 chain expression in melanoma cells, while LM-α3 staining is weaker, and LM α2/α1 are barely detected [ ]. Melanoma cells express LM-β1, LM-β2, LM-β3, and LM-γ1 chains, with tumor vasculature also showing LM staining. qPCR and immunoprecipitation of melanoma cell lines revealed that LM-111, LM-332, and LM-511 are secreted by all studied lines, indicating their presence in the melanoma ECM.
The relevance of altered LM expression is multifaceted. LM-111, LM-332, and LM-511 were shown to promote in vitro melanoma cell migration [ ]. Specifically, LM-332 activated the α3β1 integrin to facilitate this process [ ]. In mice, LM-511 played a dual role in melanocyte biology, stimulating their migration into hair follicle niches where differentiation takes place [ ]. Interaction of melanoma cells with keratinocyte-derived LM-511 could induce CXCR4 expression and CXCL12 secretion, crucial in the CXCR4/CXCL12 chemokine axis for melanoma metastasis [ ]. Additionally, LM-421 binds MCAM/CD146 and promotes lymphatic metastasis through interactions with α6β1 and α3β1 integrins and the tetraspanin CD151 [ , ].
Altered LM expression by melanoma cells profoundly impacts the TME. Beyond cell–matrix interactions, LMs significantly influence surrounding stromal cells, such as fibroblasts and ECs. When LM-332 bound to melanoma cells, it triggered MMP9 production, indicating a potential mechanism for ECM remodeling and tumor metastasis [ ]. Tumor-specific LMs also interact with fibroblasts, inducing transcriptional changes that affect tumor biology. For example, altered MMP secretion by melanoma-associated CAFs created an immune-suppressive TME by reducing NKG2D ligand expression on tumor cells, impairing NK-mediated cytotoxicity [ ].
Evidence suggests that aggressive melanoma cells interact with the ECM differently than less aggressive ones, with specific LMs potentially initiating genetic programs that yield a more pluripotent, embryonic-like phenotype typical of metastatic melanoma. One such phenotype is vascular mimicry, characterized by a tubular network formed by aggressive melanoma tumors, resembling embryonic vasculogenesis [ ]. These tubular structures were immunoreactive with LM antibodies [ ]. Aggressive melanoma cells secreted higher levels of LM-γ2 chain and modified the ECM by releasing various MMPs. Fragments of LM-γ2 chain, generated by MMP-2 and MT1-MMP-mediated proteolysis, played a central role in promoting vasculogenic mimicry [ ]. Furthermore, less aggressive cells formed patterned networks when placed on collagen gels preconditioned by aggressive cells, a process dependent on LM-γ2. These findings underscore the critical role of the ECM microenvironment in tumor progression and the acquisition of aggressive phenotypes in melanoma.

## Concluding remarks
Here, we comprehensively review how alterations in the expression or post-transcriptional modification of LM subunits impact the progression of 11 human cancers. These cancers were selected for their global clinical relevance and incidence, as well as the abundance of mechanistic evidence linking LMs to cancer biology and prognosis. In contrast, other cancer types, such as renal, endometrial, sarcomas, pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, mesothelioma, testicular, and hematological cancers, have limited or no studies on this topic. A few studies in bladder, uterine/cervical, and esophageal cancers have attempted to associate specific LM chains (mainly LM-332 and/or the LM-γ2 chain) or polymorphisms with prognosis, but their findings require independent validation. Thyroid cancer stands out as an exception, with several studies implicating the LM-β3 chain (LAMB3 gene) in malignancy and metastasis. For further details, readers may refer to a recent review on LAMB3 in cancer [ ], which complements the insights presented here.
Taking all the information together, it appears that while certain LMs can exert anti-tumorigenic effects at early stages, such as inhibiting the acquisition of a transformed phenotype, LM dysregulation is more frequently linked to tumor progression. Dysregulated LMs play diverse pro-tumorigenic roles, including promoting tumor cell proliferation, enhancing migratory and invasive capacities, and contributing to the maintenance of cancer cell stemness. Additionally, LMs influence the TME by regulating key stromal components, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), angiogenesis, and immune cell infiltration (Fig.  ). These changes in LM expression not only affect tumor cell behavior but also reshape the stromal landscape, collectively modulating the TME in ways that typically favor tumor progression.
 Despite advances, critical challenges remain in elucidating the complex roles of LMs within the TME, likely due to fragmented knowledge. LMs can act as tumor suppressors or promoters depending on the context. For instance, LM-332 is generally linked to enhanced invasion in epithelial cancers, whereas LM-111 may inhibit pro-tumorigenic signaling, particularly in breast tissue, by maintaining cell quiescence. The molecular basis of this dual behavior is not fully understood. One potential explanation is that the pro- or anti-tumor effects depend on the specific LM isoform expressed in the BM. Using breast cancer as an example, increased LM-332 expression is linked to greater tumor progression, while LM-111 inhibits the acquisition of a transformed phenotype [ ]. However, this relationship is not universal. For example, in endometrioid carcinomas, LM-332 expression is lower than that of LM-111, suggesting a pro-tumorigenic role for the latter [ ]. Similarly, LM-332 maintains cell–cell adhesion in prostate cancer, inhibiting disease initiation and progression [ ]. Conversely, LM-111 shows pro-tumorigenic effects in ovarian cancer [ ], oral cancer [ ], glioblastoma [ ], and melanoma [ ]. This evidence suggests that LMs’ dual roles in cancer are not simply due to the expression of specific isoforms but rather depend on their interactions within particular cellular or tissue contexts (Table  ).
These contexts may be shaped by the repertoire of LM receptors expressed by neoplastic cells or stromal cells. For example, the α6β4 integrin has been associated with anti-tumor signaling in prostate and breast cancers by maintaining hemidesmosome structure and preventing the acquisition of a transformed phenotype [ , ]. In contrast, integrins containing the β1 chain are often linked to increased tumor cell invasiveness. However, exceptions exist; LM-332-induced phosphorylation of the β4 chain mediates proliferation and invasion via α3β1 integrin in hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. Similarly, in other cancers, LM-332 activation of α6β4 integrin has been linked to anoikis resistance [ ], as well as enhanced stemness and invasion[ , ]. These seemingly contradictory findings reflect the complexity of LM receptor-mediated downstream signaling, further amplified by multiple redundant non-integrin receptors, which not only induce signaling but also modulate integrin-mediated signals.
Another underexplored topic is the impact of aging on BM properties and its implications for the pro- and anti-tumor roles of LMs. Sporadic cancer is closely linked to aging, during which the BM undergoes significant compositional and structural alterations. Aging increases collagen IV and agrin levels while reducing LM expression in the inner limiting membrane (ILM) of the retina and vascular BMs [ , ]. Additionally, aging thickens the ILM, as well as capillary, glomerular and epidermal BMs [ ]. Using the ILM as a paradigm, its thickness increases from approximately 70 nm in fetal stages to 300–350 nm by age 22, and to several micrometers by age 90. These compositional and structural changes alter the biomechanical properties of BMs, smoothing the collagen IV side that interfaces with the interstitial ECM but stiffening the LM side in contact with the cells [ ]. Notably, mounting evidence suggests that increased BM stiffness is linked to enhanced cancer metastasis and poor prognosis (reviewed in [ ]).
Further research is crucial to fully exploit the potential of LMs in cancer diagnostics and therapy. Certain LM chains, such as LM-γ2, are predominantly expressed at the invasive front of tumors and hold promise as biomarkers for early cancer detection and prognosis. However, developing specific and sensitive biomarkers based on LM expression remains a significant challenge. This difficulty arises partly from the lack of robust methodologies to detect specific LM isoforms in clinical samples. For instance, current antibodies often fail to distinguish between LM monomers and their trimeric forms. Additionally, there is a lack of specific reagents to detect proteolyzed LMs or their fragments, which are often critical mediators of LM-associated activities.
It is also needed to develop LM-based anti-cancer therapeutic strategies. One promising avenue involves leveraging LM-derived fragments for targeted drug or gene delivery to cancer cells with high levels of LM receptors. Early evidence supports this approach, utilizing peptides such as the LM-β1-derived YIGSR, targeting the 67-kDa laminin receptor frequently overexpressed in PCa; LM-α1-derived peptides IKVAV and AG-73, which target α3β1 and α6β1 integrin and syndecan-2, respectively; and the LM-γ1-derived C16Y peptide, which targets endothelial αvβ3 integrin involved in tumor angiogenesis [ – ]. These peptides have been incorporated into advanced delivery systems, including liposomes, nanoparticles, and ECM-based hydrogels, to enable localized delivery of therapeutic agents at tumor sites or intracellular targets. Despite these advancements, the clinical efficacy and safety of these delivery systems in anti-cancer therapy must be demonstrated. Rigorous preclinical and clinical investigations are essential to validate their potential and translate these promising findings into viable cancer treatments.
It is evident the broader complexity of the TME and the need for precise tools to manipulate and observe LM-related processes in cancer. It could be anticipated that understanding the intricate interplay between LMs, their receptors, and the TME will advance not only our knowledge but also the generation of new therapeutic strategies in cancer.
