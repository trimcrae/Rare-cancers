## Abstract
The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role at all stages of tumor progression. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), as the main components of this environment, display diversities in origin, phenotype, and function, which contribute to cancer progression by regulating the tumor cell biology via cell–cell contact, releasing numerous regulatory elements such as growth factors (GFs), cytokines, and chemokines, and even remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM). In this Review, we discuss the current understanding of CAFs’ pro- or anti-tumor functions in various hematological malignancies, including acute and chronic leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma (MM). The accumulating evidence highlighted that CAFs not only are implicated in these neoplasms’ initiation, development, and metastasis but also are involved in chemoresistance to various chemotherapy drugs such as daunorubicin and bortezomib. Intriguingly, cell culture-based methods’ results outlined that targeting signaling pathways that are used by CAFs to exert their pro-tumorigenic effects could be exploited in favor of tumor inhibition, which requires more investigation. Therefore, this therapeutic approach should be considered in future studies to develop a novel targeted therapy in hematological malignancies.
Keywords: Cancer-associated fibroblast, Hematological malignancies, Tumor microenvironment, Progression, Treatment

## Introduction
The term “hematological malignancy” refers to a variety of disorders, including leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma (MM). The first known case of these neoplasms is Hodgkin’s disease, which was established in 1832 by Thomas Hodgkin [ , ]. In hematological neoplasms, malignant cells require a specialized tumor microenvironment (TME) for tumor growth, where bone marrow (BM) and peripheral lymph nodes are provided to the tumor in an ideal combination [ ].
The TME significantly contributes to tumor initiation, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance; thus, it is considered an indispensable requirement of cancer development [ ]. This microenvironment represents a highly heterogeneous entity that comprises stromal cells, immune cells, and extracellular matrix (ECM) [ ]. Among plenty of stromal cells within TME, fibroblasts are one of the most critical ones, which can be modified into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) under the influence of tumor cells [ ].
CAFs are commonly characterized by α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), and fibroblast-specific protein 1 (FSP1) expression [ ]. It is noteworthy that besides the fibroblast, CAFs originate from multiple cell types residing in tumor niches or recruited from distant sites such as hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), adipocytes, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, pericytes (PCs), and stellate cells [ ]. In the context of blood neoplasms, CAFs, as a key cellular component of the hematological malignancy microenvironment, not only play multiple tumor-promoting roles but also are highly promising therapeutic tools [ ].
This review evaluates current knowledge regarding CAFs’ various impacts on hematological disorders. First, we will summarize the TME constituents and provide general information about CAFs, including definition, origin, heterogeneity, and markers. Then, we will focus on the pro- and anti-tumorigenic functions as well as the clinical implementation of CAFs in cancers. We will also  discuss CAF’s roles in leukemia, lymphoma, and MM development. Finally, we highlight the CAF’s therapeutic potentials and CAF-related biomarkers in these disorders.

## Tumor microenvironment (TME)
The TME, as a hypoxic, acidic, and nutrient-deprived niche surrounding tumor cells, impacts tumorigenesis [ ]. The ‘seed and soil’ theory, proposed by Paget et al. a century ago, provides better insight into TME’s undeniable role in cancer development. According to this theory, TME acts as ‘soil’ for tumor cells, which are ‘seeds’ in this scenario [ ]. Bi-directional communications between malignant cells and the diverse non-malignant cell populations within the TME regulate the biological behavior of tumors, so the TME with the special environmental entity creates a favorable niche for tumor development [ ].
As mentioned, TME is a multifactorial environment composed of cellular and non-cellular components that influence different aspects of tumors. The cellular parts, made up of malignant and non-malignant cells, include immune cells and stromal cells.
immune cells present in the TME have two distinct functions. The anti-tumor immune microenvironment compromise of Th1 cell, CD8+ T cell, neutrophil, natural killer (NK) cell, innate lymphoid cell 1 (ILC1), M1 macrophage, and dendritic cell 1 (DC1), while Th2 cell, regulatory T cell (Treg), ILC2, M2 macrophage, DC2, and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) are all involved in immune suppressive microenvironment [ , ]. Immune cells can be resident in TME or recruited from another site through chemokines and cytokine secretion [ ]. The stromal cells consist of MSCs, CAFs, endothelial cells, PCs, adipocytes, and stellate cells. These stromal cells are implicated in tumorigenesis through cell–cell interaction with malignant cells [ , ]. Among stromal cells resident in tumor niches, CAFs are one of the main cell types with both anti- and pro-tumorigenic effects [ ].
In addition, ECM, cytokines, growth factors (GFs), small RNAs, DNAs, and metabolites are considered as non-cellular components of TME [ , ]. Furthermore, since tumor cell growth requires nutrients, angiogenic vascular cells (AVCs) are embedded in TME too [ ].
The normal stroma is essential for maintaining the integrity of epithelial tissues and includes various cells and biological molecules that work together to sustain homeostasis within healthy tissues. The ongoing and reciprocal molecular communication between different components of the stroma occurs through either direct cell-to-cell contact or by secreted molecules [ ]. Therefore, although most of the host cells in the normal stroma possess particular tumor-suppressing functions, even small genetic alterations in one compartment during cancer development may significantly change the entire stromal system to provide a supportive and permissive environment for the malignant cell [ ].
In the primary phases of tumor development, the basement membrane undergoes degradation, leading to the activated stroma, containing fibroblasts, newly formed capillaries, and inflammatory infiltrates, coming into direct contact with the malignant cells. Additionally, the matrix of the basement membrane alters the cytokine interactions between fibroblasts and tumor cells, and all these tumor-induced stromal changes will facilitate tumor invasion [ , ].
Even though normal stroma in most tissues has a minimal number of fibroblasts that are associated with ECM, the tumor-activated stroma is associated with more ECM-producing fibroblasts, enhanced production of ECM, and increased vascularity [ ]. This particular malignant cell stroma formation at active malignant cell invasion sites has been termed tumor stromatogenesis, which is considered a critical part of cancer invasion [ ].

## Definition of fibroblasts and CAFs
Fibroblasts are a group of non-immune and non-epithelial cells with a possible origin of mesenchymal lineage that display thin, elongated, spindle-like shapes and planar polarity potential. They are broadly studied because they can be easily isolated and cultured due to their plastic adherence capability. These cells also have a fantastic potential to survive intense stress conditions that are usually fatal to other cells, and could be live-cultured from decaying tissues [ , ]. Furthermore, fibroblasts are considered crucial in ECM synthesis by producing fibronectin and collagens [ ]. Fibroblasts are commonly quiescent in normal tissues [ ]. However, they play a significant role in tissue repair and can become activated via proper stimuli following tissue injury [ ]. Activated fibroblasts are known to be the main components of scars [ ].
Fibroblasts, which are isolated from fibrotic tissue or the healing wound site, produce a higher amount of normal ECM components and proliferate more than their normal counterparts that are isolated from healthy organs. As expected, when the wound is fixed, the count of activated fibroblasts declines remarkably, and the resting phenotype is considered to be back [ ]. Unlike wound healing, the fibroblasts in the tumor region stay permanently activated [ ].
Fibroblasts are highly heterogeneous, and the lack of unique markers that are not expressed in any of the other cell types makes it quite challenging to define them properly. Therefore, they are often characterized by a combination of their tissue location, morphology, and absence of lineage markers for leukocytes, epithelial cells, and other cell types [ , ].
CAFs, also referred to as tumor-associated fibroblasts (TAFs), peritumoral fibroblasts, reactive stromal fibroblasts, or activated myofibroblasts, are a specialized group of activated fibroblasts with remarkable plasticity and heterogeneity in the TME. CAFs are the most prominent cell type in the tumor stroma that regulate the biological behavior of tumors [ , – , ].
They originate from various cells and play a significant role in cancer progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, drug resistance, and immune cell reprogramming via cell–cell communication, remodeling the ECM, and secreting different kinds of factors, including cytokines, GFs, chemokines, and other regulatory molecules [ , ]. Since many of the same issues relating to normal fibroblasts also apply to CAFs, there are no well-established criteria to distinguish CAFs from normal fibroblasts [ , ].
Large elongated spindle-shaped cells with a negative expression of epithelial, leukocyte, and endothelial markers, which express fibroblast markers and lack the mutations found within tumor cells, may be considered CAFs [ , , , ].
Compared to normal activated fibroblasts, CAFs can proliferate vigorously and exhibit enhanced migratory capacity, and previous studies have proved that the cellular metabolic processes of CAFs mimic those of robustly proliferating cells and depend on aerobic glycolysis [ ]
The transition signals, which mediate the transformation of normal fibroblasts into CAFs, are not entirely understood. However, in cell culture, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which mediates the activation of fibroblasts during fibrosis and wound healing, can induce the phenotypic features of CAFs. Thus, similar signaling pathways may also be involved in the appearance of CAFs in cancers [ ].

## Origin of CAFs

## Fibroblasts
Resident fibroblasts are known as the main source of CAFs in TME. Neighboring tumor cells can influence these fibroblasts in different ways, such as releasing exosomes. According to recent findings, cancer cell exosomes contain TGF-β, which, after being internalized by fibroblasts, can activate different signaling pathways (especially SMAD-dependent signaling), elevate the proliferation of these cells, and increase the expression of CAF markers like α-SMA, αFAP, periostin (POSTN), and FSP-1 (S100A4). Also, cancer cells can stimulate fibroblasts through secreting a variety of cytokines, GFs, and chemokines such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), sonic hedgehog (SHH), and interleukins (ILs). Other factors, like hypoxia and vitamins A and D, also increase the conversion of fibroblasts into CAFs [ – ].

## Epithelial cells
TME contains two groups of epithelial cells, including normal and cancerous ones. Both groups undergo a transition into mesenchymal cells under the influence of TGF-β. Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) refers to a process in which epithelial cells lose epithelial characteristics, such as adherence, cell-to-cell contacts, and expression of epithelial markers, and gain a mesenchymal phenotype with expression of fibroblastic markers. EMT is a source of active CAFs expressing S100A4 and αFAP in various cancers [ , , , ].

## Endothelial cells
In a similar process, endothelial cells may undergo a conversion to mesenchymal cells via TGF-β and SMAD signaling that occurs in different tumors and can foster tumor advancement in many ways. This process was primarily studied in the embryonic development of the heart, and is termed EndMT. Based on articles, during EndMT, which is responsible for the creation of up to 40% of CAFs, endothelial cell features such as CD31, VE-cadherin, and von Willebrand factor (vWF) decrease, mesenchymal markers like FSP1 and α-SMA elevate, and cells also achieve a spindle-shaped morphology with migratory and invasive effects. So, resident endothelial cells delaminate from an organized cell layer and invade the underlying tissue. Interestingly, it has been shown that even though cancer-derived exosomes can boost the transformation of endothelial cells into CAFs via EndMT, MSC-derived exosomes can inhibit this process [ , , – ].

## BM-MSCs
Tumors can simulate BM-MSC to transform into CAFs, and MSCs are responsible for up to 20% of the total CAFs population. MSCs can be activated and differentiated into CAFs via various factors secreted from malignant cells, like (C-X-C motif) ligand 12 (CXCL12) or stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) and TGF-β1, which trigger the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. MSC-derived CAFs express MSC markers such as CD73, CD105 (endoglin), and CD90 (THY1) along with αFAP, α-SMA, and vimentin (VIM) [ , , ].
Tumor-associated MSCs (TA-MSCs), on the other hand, are a group of non-CAF cells that derive from MSCs in the tumor site, but in comparison with MSC-derived CAFs, they have lower expression levels of CAF-related makers such as S100A4, VIM, and FAP and higher self-renewal. However, TA-MSCs may potentially transform into CAFs too [ ].

## Other sources
In addition to resident fibroblasts, several less common cell types can also transform CAFs through analogous mechanisms. These include adipocytes, which support tumor progression both by differentiating into CAFs and by remaining in an undifferentiated state, as well as pericytes (PCs), hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), cancer stem cells (CSCs), pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), smooth muscle cells, and fibrocytes [ , , , ].

## Heterogeneity of CAFs and their Phenotypic Markers
Extensive characterization of CAF subpopulations in solid tumors has significantly advanced our understanding of their phenotypic and functional diversity. Although similar classifications have yet to be fully established in hematologic malignancies, these solid-tumor findings offer valuable context. This section highlights key CAF subtypes based on the specific expression of different surface markers, the production and release of proteins, and transcriptomic profiles identified in several murine cancer models and human tumors, offering a comparative foundation that may guide future research into CAF heterogeneity in hematological malignancies [ ]. Table provides an overview of CAF subpopulations.

 The first identified subpopulation, known as CAF-1, is made up of CAFs expressing FSP-1 that increase the metastatic colonization of tumor cells [ – ]. This subtype is involved in VEGF-A-mediated angiogenesis and metastasis of an orthotopic breast cancer mouse model by producing tenascin [ ].
The expression of neural/glial antigen 2 (NG2), α-SMA, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ) characterizes the second sub-population of CAFs, known as CAF-2. This subtype of CAFs is a type I collagen source that contributes to the fibrotic connective tissue barrier formation, which further prevents the infiltration of cytotoxic T-lymphocytes into tumors [ , ].
Two distinct subpopulations of CAFs are represented as CAF-N (Normal) and CAF-D (Divergent); CAF-N releases hyaluronic acid (HA) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while CAF-D is a source of TGF-β. It has been demonstrated in human oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) that the former promotes tissue invasion by tumor cells and fibroblasts and creates an ECM that is immunosuppressive and rich in HA, whereas the latter causes EMT in tumor cells and stimulates cell migration [ , ].
Based on their transcriptomes, two more CAF subpopulations were described in human colorectal tumors: CAF-A, which has an abundant amount of αFAP, MMP2, and collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2) mRNA, and CAF-B, with ample amounts of PDGF-A, α-SMA, and transgelin (TAGLN) mRNA. However, the specific role of these subpopulations remained unknown [ ].
Various subpopulations of CAFs were recognized in human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tumors according to their meflin expression. Meflin is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein expressed in MSCs that preserves their undifferentiated form. It has been demonstrated that meflin-rich CAFs (rCAFs) repress the progression of PDAC by affecting cancer initiation, hypoxia, and ECM remodeling. On the other hand, meflin-poor CAFs (pCAFs) have been shown to play pro-tumorigenic roles in cancer [ , ].
In an in vivo study of PDAC tumors, PSCs were demonstrated to differentiate into myofibroblasts expressing α-SMA, referred to as myCAFs, which stimulate EMT and elevate the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion of tumor cells by producing TGF-β and ECM proteins such as collagen, or into inflammatory CAFs, referred to as iCAFs, that trigger angiogenesis and metastasis, and also play an immunomodulatory role by releasing leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), IL-6, and IL-11 [ – ]. Additionally, iCAFs can recruit Tregs by releasing CCL17 and CCL22, which results in an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. They also secrete CCL2 and SDF-1, which leads to recruiting monocytes and increasing their development into MDSCs that impede CD8 + T-lymphocytes [ – ]. It has also been reported in a recent study that patients with a specific subtype of myCAFs that express leucine-rich repeats containing 15 (LRRC15) are more likely to develop chemoresistance to targeted therapy against programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) [ ].
Another subpopulation of CAFs with critical immunomodulatory functions has lately been noted in PDAC, known as antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs). apCAFs express Podoplanin (PDPN), collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II family of proteins, including histocompatibility 2, class II antigen A alpha and beta 1 (H2-Aa, H2-Ab1), and CD74 that deliver epitopes of antigens to their receptors on CD4 + /CD8 + T-lymphocytes. This binding prevents lymphocyte activation and proliferation [ , ].
Based on the expression of six markers, including αFAP, α-SMA, integrin β1 (ITGB1, also known as CD29), FSP-1, PDGFRβ, and caveolin 1 (Cav1), Costa et al. uncovered four subpopulations of CAFs, including CAF-S1 to CAF-S4, in human breast adenocarcinoma tumor samples. They specified CAF-S1 as being substantially positive for all six markers, while CAF-S2 was found to be negative for all of the markers. On the other hand, CAF-S3 was determined to express abundant amounts of FSP-1, ITGB1, and PDGFRβ. Lastly, CAF-S4 was described as expressing α-SMA, along with the three markers being expressed in CAF-S3 [ ]. CAF-S1 and CAF-S4 activate the NOTCH signaling pathway by releasing TGF-β and SDF-1, promoting the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells. CAF-S1 also exerts immunosuppressive functions by secreting IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, and SDF-1 that cause the suppression of CD4 + T-lymphocytes, as well as the Treg activation and proliferation. However, the tumorigenic roles of CAF-S2 and S3 have not been fully clarified yet [ – ].
According to a study investigating breast adenocarcinoma’s patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumors, a distinct subpopulation of CAFs that express G protein-coupled C5a receptor 77 (GPR77 or C5AR2) and membrane metallo-endopeptidase (MME or CD10) provides a survival niche for CSCs that results in increased tumor formation and chemoresistance. Constant NF-B activation, which is sustained via complement signaling by GPR77, is responsible for driving CD10 + GPR77 + CAFs. Interestingly, targeting these CD10 + GPR77 + CAFs with neutralizing anti-GPR77 antibodies suppresses the formation of tumors and restores the chemosensitivity of tumors through the production of IL-6 and IL-8 [ ].
Using scRNA-seq combined with a negative selection process, Bartoschek et al. identify three different CAF subtypes in a genetically engineered breast cancer mouse model, referred to as matrix (mCAFs), vascular (vCAFs), and development (dCAFs). While mCAFs display matricellular proteins and are descended from resident fibroblasts, dCAFs express SC genes shared with the tumor epithelium (Sox9, Scgr1, and Sox10), and vCAFs, found in the perivascular niche, express endothelial cell markers [ ].
The above overview demonstrates the significant advancements made in our insights into the CAF population heterogeneity and in our ability to identify particular subpopulations of CAFs based on various criteria. Undoubtedly, these various CAF subpopulations play different roles in cancers that extend from direct effects on tumor cell formation, survival, proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance to effects mediated by releasing exosomes, changing the ECM, and manipulating immune cells in the TME.

## Functions of CAFs in cancer
As mentioned before, CAFs are identified as one of the most heterogeneous cell populations within TME. These cells interact with cancerous cells, affecting various aspects of the tumor, and contribute to either tumor progression or suppression. Owing to the heterogeneity of fibroblasts in different organs, the mechanisms and signaling pathways by which CAFs from various tissues may suppress or induce cancer initiation and progression are likely to differ as well [ ]. The following section summarizes the involvement of CAFs in tumor progression and suppression in detail.

## Pro-tumorigenic function

## Initiation & growth
CAFs can not only induce tumors but also promote their growth. A study by Orimo et al. has demonstrated that human breast carcinoma-derived CAFs may boost tumor growth remarkably more than the normal fibroblasts derived from the same patients due to the secretion of SDF-1. Tumor cells express C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4). Since CAFs have a high expression of SDF-1, they can directly improve tumor growth by paracrine stimulation of tumor cells through CXCR4 [ ].
CAFs release various GFs, like the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), FGF-1, FGF-7, and TGF, to elevate breast cancer cell proliferation by paracrine activation [ ]. Interestingly, in animal models, it was demonstrated that when Simian virus 40 (SV40)-transformed prostate epithelial cells combined with normal activated fibroblasts (NAFs) or CAFs were inoculated into mice, only the CAFs could lead to tumor formation [ ].

## Angiogenesis
Angiogenesis is a critical and essential part of tumor growth and metastasis. A tumor cannot expand more than 1‒2 mm in size without new blood vessels actively forming to provide oxygen and other nutrients. CAFs stimulate angiogenesis to fulfill the tumor growth requirements [ , ].
CAFs are a major source of angiogenic factors and produce ample amounts of TGF-β, vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), Platelet-Derived Growth Factor C (PDGFC), FGF2, osteopontin, SDF-1, and secreted frizzled-related protein 2 (SFRP2) that play critical roles in increasing the proliferation and differentiation of endothelial cells and induce or aggravate the angiogenesis in neoplastic tissues [ , , ]. Additionally, CAFs can indirectly modulate angiogenesis and blood flow in tumors by adjusting the stiffness, elasticity, and interstitial fluid pressure within the stroma of the tumor [ , ].

## Metastasis
Activated fibroblasts play a critical role in mediating the secondary tumor expansion at the metastatic sites. CAFs can promote metastasis at the primary site via secreting various cytokines and GFs into the circulation to directly or indirectly provoke the growth and invasiveness of tumor cells at a distant site [ ].
As mentioned before, CAFs have adjustory effects on ECM stiffness at primary tumors that can enhance cancer cell invasion. Also, CAFs generate ECM tracks that work as a guide for tumor cell invasion [ , ]. According to Peña et al., PDGF-stimulated CAFs may enhance tumor cell intravasation and distant formation by secreting stanniocalcin 1 (STC1) in colorectal cancer [ ].
In a recent study by Sato et al., adipocyte‐rich bone marrow (adipo‐BM) and hematopoietic cell‐rich bone marrow (hemato‐BM) were compared in bone metastasis specimens, and the findings demonstrated that adipo‐BM increased invasive‐front complexity, which relates to tumor aggressiveness, including cancer invasion and poor prognosis. They’ve also used immunohistochemistry to indicate that the invasive front of adipo‐BM was positive for α-SMA, one of the most common CAF markers. Taken together, these findings reveal that through CAF induction, BM-adipocytes provide a favorable TME for tumor progression of bone metastasized cancers [ ].
In an in vivo model of breast cancer, the secretion of IL-32 by CAFs leads to the β-integrin-mediated p38MAPK signaling activation in tumor cells, which further causes a loss of cell–cell adhesion and increases cell migration and metastasis [ , ].
Interestingly, tumor cells may undergo EMT to achieve a highly invasive phenotype. According to previous studies, via the paracrine TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway, CAFs can trigger EMT in breast cancer cells [ , ].

## Drug resistance
Several studies have revealed that CAFs have a critical role in drug resistance through various mechanisms, such as reducing drug bioavailability by producing a stromal barrier, increasing the cancer stem cells (CSCs), and secreting cytokines, exosomes, and miRNAs to reprogram the cancer cells and reshape the TME [ , ].
In non-small-cell lung carcinoma, abundant IL-6 secreted by CAFs promotes TGF-β-induced EMT and cisplatin resistance, and reciprocally, cisplatin increases the production of TGF-β by the cancer cells, which further leads to activation of CAFs [ ]. Additionally, in breast cancer, the CAF-secreted IL-6 enhances the survival of tumor cells by activating the ERK signaling pathway that stabilizes the anti-apoptotic protein MCL-1 [ ].
Studies indicated that gemcitabine therapy-resistant pancreatic cancer patients display higher levels of miR-21, which is a critical regulator of CAF activation, and an increased number of activated CAFs. Consequently, it could be assumed that upregulation of miR-21 may increase the CAF activation and promote gemcitabine therapy resistance [ ]. Also, Cetuximab therapy in colorectal cancer can affect CAFs to release an abundant amount of epidermal growth factor (EGF) that subsequently leads to EGF-mediated transduction of the MAPK signal to resist cetuximab [ ].
Taken together, all the given examples suggest that targeting CAFs in the TME may have potential therapeutic value.

## Anti-tumorigenic function
Accumulating evidence indicates the CAF’s involvement in suppressing the occurrence and development of tumors, which additionally supports the concept of CAF heterogeneity in the microenvironment of tumors.
CAFs can trigger NF-κB and YAP1/TAZ signaling in sarcoma and prostatic adenocarcinoma by releasing TGF-β, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and IL-6, leading to proliferation inhibition in tumor cells [ ].
In an in vivo model of intestinal tumors, the Ikkβ /NF-κB signaling activation in CAFs suppresses tumor growth, and the Ikkβ-deficient fibroblasts induce elevated HGF production, which is responsible for increased T reg cell polarization and recruitment [ ].
The α-SMA expressing population of CAFs demonstrated a promoted Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling activation in PDAC mouse models that hinders the VEGF, IL-8, and CXCL12 production, which impacts tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immunosuppressive processes [ ].
Interestingly, animal experiments in pancreatic cancer have indicated that Meflin+ /αSMA low CAFs (tumor-restraining CAFs) appear in the early stages of cancer. However, they can give rise to Meflin low/−/αSMA.+ CAFs (tumor-promoting CAFs) under the influence of TGF-β, hypoxia, stromal stiffness, mechanical force, and other TME factors [ , ]
In this case, targeting the entire population of CAFs to treat cancers may be inefficient or even harmful. Therefore, to get optimal results, tumor-promoting subsets of CAFs must be identified and distinguished from tumor-suppressing CAFs by more specific cell markers before targeting therapy.

## Clinical implementation of CAFs
As outlined above, CAFs represent a heterogeneous population with pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic characteristics. Considering the dual effect of CAFs in cancer, our attention is directed to using these cells as therapeutic targets [ ]. CAFs are involved in cancer treatment through a series of approaches, including 1) targeting CAF biomarkers, 2) intervention with CAF secretome, 3) interference with ECM proteins associated with CAFs, 4) reversing CAFs’ pro-tumorigenic phenotype to tumor-suppressing phenotype, and 5) CAFs as vehicles for drug delivery. These approaches are described below.
Santos et al. demonstrated that both genetically FAP null mouse model (genetic deletion of FAP in mouse) and wild-type mice treated with PT630, which is known as an FAP inhibitor (pharmacologic inhibition of FAP), correlated with reduced tumor growth [ ]. The same results were illustrated in α-SMA deleted transgenic mice [ ]. Regarding the CAFs inhibiting immunotherapeutic antibody function through secretion of CXCL12, targeting CXCL12 could provide unique anticancer therapies. It is suggested that AMD3100, a CXCL12 receptor inhibitor (CXCR4 inhibitor), enhances immunotherapy response of anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (α-CTLA-4) and α-programmed cell death ligand 1 (α-PD-L1) as a result of the accumulation of cytotoxic CD-8+ T cells in PDAC model [ ]. CAF also produces ECM-remolding proteins, which create a physical barrier that prevents oxygen and drug delivery. In the light of an investigation by Chauhan et al., it has been revealed that intervention with ECM protein production, such as hyaluronan and collagen, facilitates oxygen and drug delivery. This study showed angiotensin inhibitors, known as losartan, not only decreased CAF density, but also decreased hyaluronan and collagen production from CAFs due to affecting downstream signaling pathways [ ].
Regarding the fact that activated CAFs promote tumor development, quiescence of CAFs could be beneficial. For example, in the presence of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), PSCs acquired a quiescent state and showed alternation in morphology, proliferation, and motility-related gene expression that resulted in decreased proliferation, increased apoptosis, reduced invasion, and changed morphology in pancreatic cancer cells [ ]. In addition, CAFs are also considered to be an appealing vehicle for drug delivery [ ]. CAFs modified to sTRAIL-producing cells by using anticancer nanoparticles (NPs) exert an apoptotic effect in the bladder and pancreatic cancer cells in the xenogeneic model. Meanwhile, these modified CAFs facilitate second-wave nanotherapy by impeding residual fibroblast activation [ ].

## CAFs in hematological malignancies

## Origin of CAFs in hematological malignancies
HXWMF-1 is the first leukemia-derived CAF cell line designed in vitro [ ]. Li et al. injected ALL cell lines, HXEX, into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice. Then, tumor cells isolated from the mice’s tumor mass were continuously cultured and examined microscopically. The obtained cells exhibit CAF properties and are called HXWMF-1 [ ].
Regarding various studies in the context of hematological disorders, MSCs are the main source of CAFs. MSCs with special potential differentiate into various cell types present in the TME of hematologic malignancies. These cells act as a double-edged sword since they undergo tumor-proliferative and tumor-suppressive functions [ ]. MSCs co-cultured with cancer cells acquired a CAF phenotype, leading to unfavorable outcomes, including drug resistance, arrest in the cell cycle, and decreased cancer cell apoptosis. In addition to MSCs, fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs) and human lymphatic fibroblasts (HLFs) can be considered as CAFs origin in hematological disorders (Table  ).

 MSCs are multipotent stem cells with the ability to differentiate into various cell types, routinely identified in the BM. MSCs are also described as mesenchymal stromal cells owing to the morphological similarities between MSCs and fibroblasts [ ]. A recent study by Pan et al. demonstrated that MSCs co-cultured with B-ALL cell lines, Nalm-6, and RS4;11, increased TGF-β secretion and expression of FAP and α-SMA compared to normal controls. Also, since in the presence of LY2109761, a selective TGF-β receptor type I/II inhibitor, the expression of CAFs-related markers is inhibited, they concluded that TGF-β is a key stimulator factor in this transformation [ ]. Similar results were obtained in BM-MSCs isolated from AML patients [ ]. Another study indicated increased FAP and α-SMA expression on the surface of MM-derived MSCs, which were co-cultured with CAFs [ ]. Surprisingly, when BM-MSCs from healthy donors are co-cultured with leukemic cells, they can also be transformed into CAF phenotypes [ ]. It is noteworthy that exosomes cargos derived from leukemic cells internalized by MSCs also potentiate the transition of MSCs to CAFs [ , ].
Interestingly, CAFs originate from PDPN+ FRCs in DLBCL [ ]. FRCs co-cultured with malignant DLBCL cells display CAFs marker expressions such as α-SMA and FAP, as well as overexpressed immunomodulatory markers compared to FRCs co-cultured with non-malignant cells [ ]. Primary HLFs are another fibroblastic source of CAFs that negatively regulate cytotoxic-cell proliferation and migration [ ].

## The significance of CAFs in hematological malignancies
CAFs are identified as critical cells within the hematological malignancies’ microenvironment and are involved in various aspects of these malignancies’ development and treatment. This section provides an overview of functional roles and associated signaling pathways of CAFs in the hematological malignancies (Fig.  ).
 

## CAFs’ involvement in tumor progression
Leukemia. The elevated level of α-SMA and FAP expression on the surface of CAFs within the acute leukemia niche suggested that CAFs might be closely related to the evolution of theses malignancies [ , ]. A recent study demonstrated that α-SMA and FAP mRNA were significantly overexpressed in newly diagnosed patients with B-ALL compared to patients in remission and normal controls. CAFs expressing these markers are incorporated in ALL development by promoting the migration, invasion, and formation of leukemia cell colonies [ ]. In the field of AML, αFAP-positive CAFs display a poor prognostic factor in AML since CAFs protect leukemic cells from apoptosis through the β-catenin signaling pathway. In addition, overexpression of αFAP correlated with the increased proportion of myoblasts in BM and low survival rate in patients with AML [ ].
Another study by Pan et al. TGF-β conditioned MSCs promote ALL cell growth in the mouse model as well as trigger migration and invasive leukemic cells in in vitro cell experiments. Their further investigation highlighted that TGF-β-conditioned MSCs could interact with leukemic cells, Nalm-6/RS4;11, through two distinct molecules. First, TGF-β conditioned MSCs increased CXCR4 expression in tumor tissue, which interacts with SDF-1 secreted by conditioned MSCs, leading to SDF-1/CXCR4 signal axis activation, which is followed by activation of downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT. In addition, conditioned MSCs increase the level of MMP-9 in TME, which promotes leukemic cell metastasis and migration. Besides the SDF-1/CXCR4 signal axis, α5β1 integrin receptor on the surface of leukemia cells binds to fibronectin on TGF-β conditioned MSCs [ ].
In the context of chronic leukemia, it is reported that CAFs augmented the survival of leukemic cells. According to the Lutzny et al. study, stromal cells educated by CLL cells acquire a CAF phenotype accompanied by PKC-βII overexpression. The PKC-βII activates NF-kB signaling pathways, which improve CLL cells’ viability through upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α and IL-15 [ ].
Lymphoma. A cancer cell tends to proliferate and survive for long periods, so its biosynthetic requirement increases significantly [ ]. Previous studies have shown that CAFs support cancer metabolism through the secretions of several GFs, enzymes, and metabolites [ , ]. CAFs from patients with lymphoma have been shown to release large amounts of pyruvate. Following that, pyruvate absorption by cancer cells results in a decrease in intracellular ROS production and an increase in the Krebs cycle, which promotes cancer cell survival [ ]. According to a study by Aoki et al., CAFs isolated from human lymph nodes promote the glycolysis of lymphoma cells, demonstrated by increased expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) and hexokinase 2 (HK2). Of note, this process is mediated by MYC rearrangement [ ].
Dörsam et al.’s study proposed the role of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) cell-derived extracellular vehicles (EVs) in intercellular communication and creating a favorable microenvironment for lymphomagenesis [ ]. In this study, CD30+EVs derived from HL cells were internalized by fibroblasts and reprogrammed to represent a CAF phenotype that exhibits an inflammatory phenotype and upregulated expression of α-SMA. EV-treated fibroblasts release chemotactic cytokines, pro-inflammatory NF-κB-induced cytokines, GFs, and angiogenesis-stimulating factors into the TME, which is appropriate for the establishment of a tumor-favorable environment in HL. HL-derived CD30+ EVs also altered the migration abilities of fibroblasts and accelerated the progression of HL tumors [ ]. Recently, evidence has emerged to support the important role of CAFs as regulators of critical metabolic processes in cancer [ ]. Kunou et al. found that CAF-derived exosomes contribute to increased survival of the lymphoma cells via increased glycolysis and ATP production. Further investigation showed that results were reversed by inhibiting exosome secretion by suppressing the RAB27B, which plays a vital role in exosome secretion [ ].
In the context of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), previous studies have shown that CAFs play a significant role in the pathogenesis of DLBCL by regulating the immune system [ , ]. According to Apollonio et al. FRCs converted to CAFs obtain functional abilities to prolong DLBCL cells’ survival [ ]. FRCs stimulate T cell migration by producing chemokines such as CCL19 and CCL21. Hence, FRCs’ transformation to CAFs reduces T cell infiltration and cytotoxicity [ ]. Moreover, CAFs expressing the immune checkpoint ligand PD-L1 modulate anti-tumor immunity. The upregulating PD-L1 expression leads to reduced CD8+ T cell cytolysis-killing activity against DLBCL tumor cells [ ].
In Mycosis fungoides (MF), the cutaneous TME is primarily composed of mesenchymal stromal cells, and CAFs are predominant [ ]. These cells play a significant role in regulating tumor cell proliferation, motility, and the development of drug resistance [ ]. Aronovich et al. speculated that CAFs from punch biopsies of patients with MF enhance the skin infiltration and cell motility of MF cells and protect them from doxorubicin-induced cell death through the secretion of CXCL12 that interacts with CXCR4 expressed on the malignant T cells, as well as ECM-modulating genes, such as MMP-2 and collagen XI. The release of matrix proteins and the synthesis of MMPs by CAFs lead to the formation of a stiff tumor stroma, which not only supports cancer cell migration and invasion but also acts as a physical barrier, limiting the penetration of therapeutic drugs and obstructing immune cells from reaching the cancer cells [ ].
The findings of another study showed that normal fibroblasts and CAFs could have opposite effects on the MF cell line. Fibroblasts from normal skin co-cultured with MyLa cells result in suppressed expression of the MF biomarkers (TWIST1 and TOX genes), enhanced expression of the Th1- related genes (IFNγ and T-bet), and inhibited proliferation of MyLa cells. In contrast to normal fibroblasts, CAFs promoted a Th2-like transcriptional network but could not suppress the expression of MF biomarkers. The Authors suggest that normal fibroblasts can suppress the expression of disease-promoting genes from the malignant T cells in MF and create an unfavorable environment for their proliferation. The indolent disease progression in the early stage of MF may be, at least, partially explained by these tumor-suppressing effects of normal fibroblasts [ ].
In addition, the GFs expressed by CAF could play a role in hematological malignancies. In this regard, Joo et al. utilized single-cell sequencing to indicate two subtypes of CAF presented in the TME of ATLL, including CAF with higher expression of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGR) genes and CAF with lower expression of these genes. The CAF/EGRlow subgroup is associated with increasing expression of CAF-related markers and tumorigenesis, while the CAF/EGRhigh subgroup is accompanied by fibroblast proliferation, inflammatory responses, and cytokine induction [ ]. Further observation demonstrated that CAFs trigger CD4 T cell expansion via FGF7-FGFR1 and PDGFA- PDGFRA/B. On the other hand, CD8 T cells and NKT cells expansion is mediated by the AREG-EGFR signaling pathway. These interactions activate downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR in CD4 T cells and NF-kB and JAK-STAT signaling in CD8 T cells and NKT cells [ ].
MM. In MM, CAFs could support tumor growth. For example, during the evolution of MGUS to MM, exosomes derived from MM cells shuttled miR-27b-3p and miR-214-3p to resident fibroblasts. These miRNAs trigger α-SMA and FAP expression, and as a result, increased proliferation and apoptosis resistance were observed in MGUS fibroblasts [ ]. Another study also emphasizes that CAFs’ distribution is positively associated with MGUS progression to MM. The study results showed CD45− /α-SMA+ /FSP-1+ positive CAFs more largely infiltrated the BM of patients with active MM than MGUS, asymptomatic MM (aMM), and remission phase MM (rMM) [ ].
Besides the exosomes, CAFs, and malignant plasma cells communicate with each other by means of the SDF-1a/CXCR4 axis and integrins. This communication pathway endorses tumor proliferation and initiation, apoptosis resistance, angiogenesis, reprogramming ECM, adhesion, and chemotaxis as a result of the secretion of different types of cytokines and chemokines, including TGF-β, IL-6, SDF-1a, IGF-1, VEGF, and FGF-1 [ ]. Regarding Desantis et al.’s finding, reducing CAF activation by recombinant human erythropoietin (rHuEpo) led to increased apoptosis in MM and MGUS cells. The rHuEpo reduced α-SMA and FAP expression, as well as IL-6, IL-8, VEGF-A, FGF-2, and HGF expression in CAFs. Therefore, CAFs play an important role in tumor progression [ ].

## CAFs’ involvement in drug resistance
Leukemia. CAFs have been indicated to impact chemotherapy outcomes in leukemia. Regarding the study of Zhai et al., CAFs contributed to the accumulation of reticular fiber in the BM of the refractory AML patients compared to the group with complete remission. On the other hand, they showed that the neutralizing GDF15 or knockdown of GDF15 abrogated CAFs’ chemo-protective effect. Consequently, CAFs serve as a chemo-protective element in AML not only by producing abnormally proliferated reticular fiber but also by secreting GDF15 [ ]. Another study found that CAFs reduced B-ALL cells’ sensitivity to daunorubicin (DNR) by enhancing cell viability, increasing the number of leukemia cells arrested at the G0/G1 phase, and reducing DNR-induced apoptosis [ ]. Based on Burt et al.’s study, activated MSCs with CAF phenotype promoted ROS-inducing chemotherapy resistance through mitochondrial transfer mediated by tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) in ALL cells. Therefore, preventing this process leads to overcoming drug resistance [ ].
Lymphoma. Kunou et al. showed that exosomes derived from CAFs could mediate resistance to anti-pyrimidine drugs in lymphoma cells. Further investigations demonstrated that CAFs’ miR-4717-5p, transported to lymphoma cells by exosomes, reduced the expression of ENT2, a pyrimidine transporter protein, leading to the chemoresistance to gemcitabine and cytarabine [ ]. A study by Staiger et al. revealed that co-culture of follicular lymphoma (FL) cells with CAF population isolated from malignant follicular lymphoma patients protects FL cells from apoptosis in response to chemotherapeutic drugs such as Bortezomib and ABT-737 in vitro. This protective effect of CAFs is achieved through increased mRNA expression of the ABC-drug transporters(ABCC1 and ABCG2) and up-regulation of the anti-apoptotic BCL2L1 gene in FL cells [ ].
MM. Studies on the function of CAFs in MM have highlighted their important role in MM drug resistance [ , ]. Investigation of CAFs’ functions in MM revealed that activation of autophagy in CAFs could be associated with rescuing plasma cells from bortezomib-induced apoptosis. The bortezomib treatment triggers increased production and secretion of IGF-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TGF-β cytokines from CAFs. The TGF-β pathway activates autophagy as a pro-survival pathway [ ]. Fu-Ming et al. study provided the same results since the knockdown of FAP, CAF’s activating marker, sensitizes MM cells to apoptosis against bortezomib [ ].

## CAFs’ involvement in hematological malignancies' treatment
CAFs play an important regulatory role in different types of hematological malignancies. Therefore, considering the cross-talk between CAFs and tumor cells may serve to develop novel and potentially effective therapeutic strategies. These therapeutic approaches, together with their underlying signaling pathways, are summarized in Table  & Fig.  .

 Leukemia. Targeting the SDF-1/CXCR4 signal axis activated by MSCs with CAF phenotype with AMD3100, a specific CXCR4 inhibitor, reduced the progressive effect of MSCs with CAF phenotype on Nalm-6/RS4;11 cells. Further experiments revealed that AMD3100 reduced the expression of MMP-9, a mediator of migration and invasion, by inhibiting the expression of p-PI3K and p-AKT, the downstream signaling pathway of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis. Additionally, it decreased integrin α5β1 expression on leukemia cells, which impaired the interaction between MSCs with a CAF phenotype and cancerous cells [ ]. Besides the SDF-1/CXCR4 signal axis, targeting the β-catenin signaling pathway in AML [ ], inhibiting microtubule formation in ALL [ ], and harnessing interactions between CLL cell-derived exosomes and surrounding stromal cells in CLL [ ] may combat the progression of malignancy.
Lymphoma. Recent studies described how CAFs impact immunotherapy efficacy in lymphoma. CAFs originated from fibroblast-mediated immunosuppressive function in the TME of DLBCL. Therefore, a better understanding of this mechanism contributed to a better response to immunotherapy [ , ]. As mentioned before (Sect. “ ”), the downregulation of CXCL12 provides a new approach to pancreatic PDAC treatment [ ]. According to this, researchers proposed that inhibition of cross-talk between lymphoma cells and CAFs by targeting the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, alone or in combination with other cytotoxic drugs, may be a promising new therapeutic approach for MF [ ]. Additionally, modulating MMP-2 or reducing collagen XI turnover in combination with standard mono-chemotherapy may offer a potential strategy for enhancing the effectiveness of MF therapy, according to Aronovich et al.’s study [ ].
MM. CAFs from active MM patients support MM cell growth by cytokine secretion, the SDF-1a/CXCR4 axis, and integrin-mediated cell-to-cell contact. Therefore, targeting CAFs in MM patients may be considered as a novel therapeutic strategy [ ]. Furthermore, Ciavarella et al. demonstrated that u-PAR silencing plays an important role in suppressing MM progression since CAFs with higher levels of u-PAR expression cooperated with  pronounced proliferative and invasive attitudes in MM cells [ ]. In addition, MM cells treated with rHuEpo [ ], and TGF-β pathway inhibition [ ] could overcome the pro-tumorigenic activity of CAFs in MM as previously mentioned.
Additionally, CAFs harbor anti-myeloma activity of antigen-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy in patients with relapsed/refractory MM. Therefore, Sakemura et.al designed a dual CAR-T cell that simultaneously targets B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) on myeloma cells and signaling lymphocyte activation molecule family-7 (SLAMF7) expressed on CAFs, potentially overcoming CAF-mediated immunosuppression [ ].
Similarly, NPs that target both myeloma cells and CAFs exhibit superior anti-myeloma activity. Wang et al. created dual-targeting NPs by conjugating paclitaxel (PTX)-loaded polyethylene glycol-poly lactic acid NPs with a cyclic peptide (CNPs-PTX) targeting PDGF/PDGFR-β. PDGFR-β is highly expressed on both CAFs and myeloma cells, with greater uptake by CAFs and MM cells compared to conventional NPs loaded with PTX only (NPs-PTX). Further experiments in an in vivo mouse model showed that CNPs-PTX effectively accumulate in tumors, increase uptake by CAFs and myeloma cells, and demonstrate greater in vivo efficacy against myeloma [ ].

## CAF-related biomarkers in hematological malignancies
Regarding CAF’s involvement in different aspects of hematological malignancies, CAF-related biomarkers may serve as potential tools for disease prognosis, diagnosis or therapy response. In this line, a recent study by Martello et.al indicated high levels of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in MM are associated with tumor burden markers and inflammatory BM microenvironment. As well, patients with a high level of cfDNA at diagnosis were more frequently found to have metabolically active paraskeletal (PS) and extramedullary (EM) lesions. Their additional investigation revealed CAFs display upregulated cytokines, including IL-6, SerpinE1, and MIF, highlighting their role as major contributors to inflammation, high tumor burden, and high ctDNA levels. Consequently, cfDNA could be consumed as a CAF-associated marker in MM [ ]. In another study conducted by Miaomiao et al., investigated that CAF-derived exosomes carry microribonucleic acid-21 (miR-21) potentially enter to MM endothelial cells (MMECs) and trigger their proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis, thereby enhancing angiogenesis. As well, miR-21 transforms normal fibroblasts into CAFs, suggesting that exosomal miR-21 could function as a novel diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target in MM [ ].
In the context of classic Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), multiplexed single-cell analysis revealed that higher overall CAF proportions correlate with favorable outcomes. In contrast, PDGFRB-positive CAFs located adjacent to Hodgkin Reed-Sternberg (HRS) cells and a subset of FAP+ macrophages are linked to worse survival. This study highlights that PDGFRB-positive CAFs and FAP+ macrophages act as prognostic biomarkers for patient survival [ ]. Among NHLs, the CAF-related gene signature predicts clinical outcomes in patients with DLBCL. Cui et.al created a prognostic model using machine learning and Cox regression, categorizing patients into high- and low-risk groups. This model not only shows superior survival prediction compared with clinical factors alone, but also correlates with immune cell profiles and drug sensitivity, providing insights for personalized treatment strategies in DLBCL [ ].

## Concluding remarks
TME represents a highly complex entity with diversified constituents extended from immune cells and stromal cells to non-cellular components. Among stromal cells, CAFs are identified as critical cells within the tumor milieu, originating from various cells, especially fibroblasts and MSCs. Therefore, they constitute a heterogeneous cell population in terms of function, expression of markers, secretion of proteins, and transcriptome profiles. CAFs play an undeniable role in both tumor progression and tumor suppression through crosstalk with tumor and tumor bystander cells. This review summarized significant findings on the role of CAFs in different types of hematological malignancies, including acute and chronic leukemia, lymphoma, and MM.
Based on the previous investigation, CAFs generally originate from MSCs in hematological disorders, although other cellular sources, such as FRCs and HLFs, are also known. Recent co-culture-based evidence indicates that CAFs are involved in various aspects of these malignancies’ development and play prominent roles in progression, proliferation, migration, invasion, tumor cell metabolism, and leukemic cell colony formation. Furthermore, CAFs have been reported to be involved in chemoresistance, which is mediated by different pathways such as cytokine secretion, ROS production, and exosome secretion. Consequently, CAF interactions with cancerous cells inside hematological disorders’ microenvironments may be considered as unique therapeutic targets. For this purpose, silencing different signaling pathways such as the SDF-1a/CXCR4 axis, u-PAR, and TGF-β pathway was investigated. As well, CAF-related biomarkers could serve as potent prognostic, diagnostic or therapeutic tools in hematological malignancies. However, their specific roles in any of these processes are not yet fully clarified due to their phenotypic and origin heterogeneity and functional diversity, thereby constraining the  CAF-targeting strategies in  cancers treatment.
Finally, more studies should be designed to decipher the roles played by CAFs in hematological malignancies and the signaling pathways involved in these processes. Hopefully, modulating these pathways could benefit in managing patient outcomes.
