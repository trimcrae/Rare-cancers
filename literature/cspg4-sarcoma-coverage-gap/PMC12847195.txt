## Abstract
Huntington’s disease (HD) is a fatal, inherited neurodegenerative disorder marked by the progressive and selective loss of spiny projection neurons (SPNs), resulting in a characteristic triad of motor, cognitive, and psychiatric symptoms. Despite ongoing research, no disease-modifying treatments are available, and existing therapies are limited to symptomatic relief. Stem cell-based approaches represent a promising avenue to restore striatal circuitry by replacing lost neurons and/or delivering trophic support to preserve the remaining neural tissue.
In this review, we present a critical analysis of past and current clinical trials exploring cell-based therapies for HD. Early studies using human fetal tissue were hindered by sample heterogeneity and inconsistent outcomes, ultimately limiting their clinical applicability. More recent trials have shifted focus toward mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which are valued for their neuroprotective secretome but are not suitable for neuronal replacement. To address these limitations, human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) have emerged as a renewable and scalable source for the development of advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs). In vitro differentiation protocols mimic key developmental signaling pathways to generate striatal-like neural progenitor cells (NPCs). We review the cellular composition of these hPSC-derived ATMPs and summarize findings from preclinical transplantation studies, including data on graft survival, neuronal maturation, synaptic integration, and functional recovery. In addition, we discuss other emerging strategies such as direct neuronal reprogramming. Finally, we examine the major challenges that remain—such as ensuring graft safety, consistency, and regulatory compliance—and highlight the importance of international collaboration to overcome these barriers and accelerate clinical translation.

## Key Points

 

## Huntington’s Disease
Huntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant, progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting approximately 10.6–13.7 individuals per 100,000 in Western populations [ , ]. Its genetic basis lies in an expansion of the cytosine-adenine-guanine (CAG) trinucleotide repeat within the huntingtin (HTT) gene on chromosome 4. Alleles with more than 39 repeats are fully penetrant, while those with 36–39 repeats show reduced penetrance. This mutation leads to the production of a mutant huntingtin protein (mHTT), characterized by an expanded polyglutamine tract [ ].
The pathogenesis of HD is primarily attributed to a toxic gain of function of mHTT, which results in protein misfolding and aggregation, transcriptional dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, excitotoxicity, impaired protein degradation pathways, and neuroinflammation [ ]. The number of CAG repeats inversely correlates with the age of disease onset, which is typically followed by progressive functional decline and a reduction in life expectancy, with an average disease duration of 15–20 years after the onset of symptoms [ ]. Clinically, HD is characterized by a triad of motor, cognitive, and psychiatric disturbances [ ]. Motor symptoms include chorea, dystonia, and bradykinesia, often progressing to rigidity and akinesia in advanced stages. Cognitive deficits—particularly in executive function, attention, and memory—may appear early and even precede motor manifestations during the prodromal phase [ ]. Psychiatric symptoms are also prevalent and include depression, anxiety, irritability, apathy, and, in some cases, psychosis [ – ].
The underlying neuropathology of HD is defined by the progressive degeneration of striatal spiny projection neurons (SPNs), which disrupts basal ganglia circuitry and contributes to motor, cognitive, and emotional dysfunction [ ]. Additional neurodegeneration is observed in cortical and subcortical regions, such as the hypothalamus, further contributing to the heterogeneity of clinical symptoms [ , ]. Currently, available treatments are limited to symptomatic management and do not alter disease progression. Thus, there is a critical need for disease-modifying therapies. Among emerging strategies, stem cell-based implants are gaining traction as a potential approach to restore damaged circuitry and modify the disease course in HD [ ].

## Stem Cell Therapy
Stem cell-based therapies hold promise for treating neurodegenerative diseases, including HD, by offering the potential to replace lost or damaged neurons, provide neurotrophic support, modulate inflammation, or enhance neuroplasticity [ ]. Based on this regenerative capacity, transplantation therapies of several cell types are currently under investigation in basic research, preclinical studies, and clinical trials (Fig. ).
 

## Fetal Neural Stem Cells
Fetal neural stem cells (fNSCs), found within human fetal tissues, are multipotent stem cells capable of differentiating into neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [ , ]. Over the past two decades, early-phase clinical studies have evaluated fetal tissue transplantation in patients with HD, consistently demonstrating the safety of this approach but reporting only mild-to-modest effects on disease progression and symptomatology [ – ]. The therapeutic rationale behind these implants includes two potential mechanisms of action: (1) cell replacement therapy (CRT), wherein transplanted cells aim to replenish lost striatal neurons; or (2) neurotrophic support, achieved through the release of survival-promoting factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [ ]. Despite the initial promise, the use of fNSCs presents several limitations. Ethical concerns regarding fetal tissue sourcing, limited self-renewal capacity, restricted availability, and technical challenges related to isolation and expansion have constrained broader clinical application. To overcome these obstacles, stepwise differentiation protocols have been developed to obtain neural stem cells (NSCs)/neural progenitor cells (NPCs) from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) [ ].

## Human Embryonic Stem Cells
Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) are hPSCs derived from the inner cell mass of preimplantation blastocysts, typically at the 5- to 7-day stage of development [ ]. Owing to their unlimited proliferative capacity and ability to differentiate into all cell types of the body, hESCs represent a promising source for generating large quantities of disease-relevant cell types. As with fNSCs, the primary therapeutic goal of hESC-derived cell products is to replace the loss of neuronal populations. Over the past decade, multiple research groups have established in vitro differentiation protocols capable of directing hESCs toward a striatal-like NPC fate, with the aim of achieving functional cell replacement upon transplantation [ ]. HESC-based therapies have advanced into phase 1 clinical trials for Parkinson’s disease (PD) [ – ]. The outcomes of these trials may provide critical proof-of-concept and pave the way for similar applications in HD. Despite their potential, hESC-derived therapies raise important concerns. Ethical issues surrounding embryo-derived cell sources remain a matter of debate. In addition, the inherent risk of teratoma formation owing to residual undifferentiated cells, along with the possibility of immune rejection, necessitates stringent quality control measures and, in most cases, the use of immunosuppression [ – ].

## Human Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells
Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) are generated by reprogramming somatic cells—such as skin fibroblasts or peripheral blood cells—into a pluripotent state through the ectopic expression of defined specific transcription factors (TFs). Similar to hESCs, hiPSCs possess the capacity for unlimited self-renewal and can differentiate into any cell type, including region-specific NPCs. Importantly, hiPSCs can be derived from patients with HD themselves, offering the possibility of generating autologous cell products tailored for transplantation [ ]. This approach not only circumvents some of the ethical concerns associated with hESC use, but also holds the potential to significantly reduce the risk of immune rejection [ ]. However, an inherent limitation of patient-derived hiPSCs is that they retain the mHTT gene, which can compromise neuronal function and survival. This severely restricts their direct therapeutic application, as autologous grafts would carry the same pathogenic mutation present in the donor. Genome editing strategies (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9-mediated correction) or the use of isogenic control lines are being explored to overcome the limitation of HD-patient-derived hiPSCs [ ]. Despite these challenges, hiPSC-based therapies more broadly face additional hurdles, including the risk of teratoma formation from residual undifferentiated cells, as well as potential genetic abnormalities acquired during the reprogramming process [ ].

## Mesenchymal Stem Cells
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are multipotent cells commonly derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, or umbilical cord blood [ ]. Although their capacity to differentiate into mature neurons in vivo is limited, MSCs exert therapeutic effects primarily through paracrine signaling. They secrete a range of neurotrophic factors and immunomodulatory molecules that support neuronal survival, attenuate neuroinflammation, and modulate the immune response [ – ]. In addition, MSCs possess the intrinsic ability to migrate toward sites of injury—a phenomenon known as homing—[ ] and have been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier, enabling delivery via systemic (e.g., intravenous) administration [ ]. While MSCs are not considered candidates for direct CRT, their ease of isolation and expansion, low immunogenicity, and robust immunomodulatory profile make them an attractive option for HD treatment.

## Stem Cell-based Clinical Trials for HD

## Completed Clinical Trials
In humans, the striatum develops from the ganglionic eminence (GE), a forebrain structure that emerges around 7 weeks postconception. The GE comprises the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE), which generates mainly cortical and striatal interneurons, and the lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE), the principal source of SPNs and olfactory bulb interneurons [ , ]. Thus, transplantation of fetal GE tissue—containing fNSCs—into the striatum of patients with HD has been pursued as a strategy to replace lost neurons and repair the damaged neural circuitry. A summary of the clinical trials performed with fetal tissue transplants is presented in Table .

 Since 1990, a total of 116 patients with HD have received fetal tissue grafts, either whole ganglionic eminence (WGE) or isolated LGE, within open-label pilot and multicentric clinical trials [ ]. Pilot studies aimed to establish the safety and feasibility of transplantation of fetal neural tissue into the striatum of patients with HD. Efficacy results have been published for 30 patients, with only 4 individuals showing long-term clinical benefits. Surgical procedures were conducted in Los Angeles (USA) [ ], France [ ], Florida (USA) [ ], and the UK [ ]. While confirming the technical feasibility of the procedure, these early-phase clinical trials also documented significant adverse events, including subdural hematomas, infections, and cell overgrowth [ – ]. Post-mortem analyses from several of these studies provided evidence of graft survival, with grafts expressing markers of mature SPNs and receiving afferences from the host brain [ , , ]. However, the extent of integration and organization within the grafts was often limited or abnormal [ ]. Although some patients showed modest improvements in motor, functional, or cognitive scores [ , ], consistent clinical benefit across larger cohorts was not observed, and the duration of improvements was variable [ , , , , , , ]. The Florence study reported graft overgrowth in a significant proportion of patients, with some grafts invading the cortex [ , ]. Furthermore, positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) showed inconsistent correlations with clinical outcomes, with metabolic activity or dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) binding in the striatum not reliably predicting clinical improvement [ , , ].
The MIG-HD trial, a multicenter randomized controlled study, represented a landmark in evaluating fetal cell transplantation for HD. Although it did not demonstrate clear clinical benefit, the trial confirmed the technical feasibility of large-scale transplantation, addressed major safety concerns, and highlighted the critical role of immunosuppression in preventing graft rejection. Nevertheless, the use of immunosuppressants posed challenges, including side effects and poor patient adherence [ ], and debates persist regarding the necessity and intensity of such treatment owing to variability in host immune responses. Whereas some researchers argue that immunosuppression is essential [ ], others caution against aggressive regimens that may be counterproductive [ ]. The trial also revealed broader methodological challenges, such as variability in cell preparation, differences in surgical practice across centers, regulatory delays that hindered trial continuity, and the need for well-controlled designs to account for placebo effects and the natural heterogeneity of HD progression [ ]. Post-hoc analyses [ ] have identified additional factors that may have contributed to the disappointing outcomes, including donor tissue quality, advanced gestational age of transplanted fetuses, inadequate graft vascularization, and immune responses despite immunosuppressive therapy [ ]. Importantly, these findings indicate that the “failure” of MIG-HD was multifactorial rather than a reflection of the transplantation concept itself. The trial has informed the design of future studies, emphasizing the need for standardized protocols, sensitive and reliable outcome measures, exploration of alternative cell sources such as stem cell-derived products, and strategies to manage multicenter logistical challenges. Despite its limitations, MIG-HD represents a critical step toward rigorous evaluation of cell-based therapies in HD, providing essential lessons for the development of next generation of transplantation approaches.
In conclusion, while fetal tissue transplantation in HD has proven to be technically feasible and can result in graft survival within the host brain, evidence for robust clinical efficacy remains limited and inconclusive. This variability may be caused by a lack of standardization and sample heterogeneity, delivery methods, and immunosuppression regimens. Moreover, the absence of viable cryopreservation protocols for fetal tissue critically impedes rigorous quality control and precludes the pretransplant analysis of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) compatibility. These issues have likely driven the field towards exploring alternative cell sources and optimized transplantation strategies.

## Current Clinical Trials
Several ongoing clinical trials are now investigating the potential of MSCs and autologous stromal cells. An overview of these current clinical trials is presented in Table . One of the most developed stem cell therapies for HD is Cellavita HD, a dental-pulp-derived MSC-therapy administered via intravenous injection. Azidus Brazil initiated a nonrandomized, open-label phase 1 clinical trial (NCT02728115/SAVE-DH) in 2017 to assess its safety and effectiveness in six participants with manifest HD, receiving low or high monthly doses with a 5-year follow-up. Outcomes measured include adverse events, Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS) scores, and inflammatory markers [ ]. Furthermore, a randomized, double-blind phase 2 clinical trial (NCT03252535/ADORE-DH) began in 2018 to investigate the dose-response relationship of Cellavita HD in 35 participants with manifest HD receiving low or high doses or placebo, with UHDRS as the primary outcome measure [ ]. An open-label extension phase 2/3 trial (NCT04219241/ADORE-EXT), not yet recruiting, aims to assess longer-term safety and efficacy in participants who completed the ADORE-DH trial, receiving multiple doses over 24 months with various clinical and imaging outcomes being tracked [ ]. Building upon the Cellavita HD research, a new clinical trial, the STAR study, has been designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of NestaCell®. This product, an evolution of Cellavita, expresses high levels of BDNF, a neurotrophin relevant for HD [ , ]. The STAR study is a phase 3, multicenter, prospective, randomized double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial aiming to enroll 120 patients with HD [ ]. Although this phase 3 trial is not currently recruiting, it represents the investigation of another MSC-based approach that may offer enhanced regenerative capabilities and broader treatment applications.

 Autologous stromal cell therapies are also under investigation. Regeneris Medical is conducting a safety and efficacy clinical trial (NCT03297177) involving 300 participants with various neurological disorders, including HD [ ]. This approach, which requires either reprogramming mutated cells or isolating cells with the wild-type (WT) allele [ ], is supported by positive preclinical data in the R6/2 HD mouse model showing reduced striatal apoptosis, prolonged lifespan, reduced mHTT aggregates, and modulation of striatal neuronal loss following bilateral striatal transplantation of autologous adipose-derived stem cells [ ]. This ongoing nonrandomized, placebo-controlled study will assess the safety and change in neurological function of intravenously administered autologous stromal cells compared with saline over up to 5 years [ ]. These ongoing clinical trials represent a shift from fNSCS to more practical and ethically acceptable cell sources, with the potential to improve treatment outcomes for HD.

## Stem Cell Therapies Under Development

## New Developing MSC-Based Therapies
Beyond current clinical trials, MSCs are being tested in HD animal models owing to their self-renewal capacity and ability to release neuroprotective factors, with no observed teratomas or overgrowth [ ]. Preclinical studies using different types of MSCs have been performed using YAC128, R6/2, and quinolinic acid (QA)-lesioned HD mice models [ , ]. Striatal injections of umbilical cord and bone marrow-derived mouse MSCs in R6/2 mice showed improvements in spatial memory and reduced neuropathological deficits, although motor impairments were not affected [ , ]. These beneficial effects were likely owing to an increase in BDNF expression, which stimulated the proliferation of endogenous NSCs in the grafted striatum [ , ]. It has also been demonstrated that genetically engineered MSCs overexpressing BDNF can diminish apoptotic cell death and reduce brain atrophy in YAC218 mice [ ]. Positive outcomes on motor coordination, decreased gliosis, and striatal health were observed in 3-nitropropionic acid (3-NP) lesioned HD rats treated with umbilical cord-derived human MSCs [ ]. Systemic administration of bone marrow-derived rat MSCs in 3-NP HD rats also resulted in reduced neurological and behavioral deficits and the presence of striatal neurotrophic factors without significant inflammation or ventricular enlargement [ ].

## SPN Differentiation by Targeting Key Developmental Signaling Pathways
During striatal embryonic development, SPNs originate from the LGE, a dorsal region of the ventral telencephalon situated beneath the forming cortex and adjacent to the MGE [ ]. The specification of this neuronal subtype is orchestrated by a tightly controlled network of molecular signals that spatially and temporally regulate the expression of TFs. Morphogens involved in dorsoventral and rostrocaudal patterning of the forebrain include transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) family members, Sonic hedgehog (SHH), Wnt, and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) [ ]. Harnessing this developmental framework, researchers have successfully designed differentiation protocols to guide hPSCs into LGE-like NPCs by manipulating key signaling pathways (Fig. ). Although timing and molecular combinations vary between protocols, comparative analyses reveal a set of recurrently targeted pathways that define the trajectory toward SPN identity (Fig. and Supplementary Table 1).
 A consistent early step across most protocols is the induction of neural lineage through dual SMAD inhibition. Originally described by Chambers and colleagues [ ], this approach drives progenitors toward a default rosette identity and involves simultaneous inhibition of the TGF-β and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathways using small molecules such as SB431542 (ALK4/5/7 inhibitor), and LDN-193189 or dorsomorphin (inhibitors of BMP type I receptors ALK2, ALK3, and ALK6) [ – ]. This strategy is established as a foundational step for directing hPSCs toward a neuroectodermal fate. Beyond neural induction, SMAD2/3 activity is required for the LGE/striatal phenotype acquisition. The presence of TGF-β inhibitors in later stages has been reported to suppress the generation of telencephalic CTIP2+ neurons [ ]. FGFs have also been incorporated to enhance NSC/NPC proliferation and patterning. For instance, FGF2 is used in the early stages of neural induction and proliferation [ , ], suggesting a supportive role in expanding NSCs/NPCs before terminal differentiation.
Subsequent anterior-posterior patterning typically requires inhibition of Wnt signaling to avoid caudalization and promote ventral forebrain identity [ , ]. Several protocols incorporate Wnt antagonists such as Dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK1) [ , , , , ], XAV939 [ , ], or IWR1 [ , ] to bias differentiation toward telencephalic fates. SHH signaling also plays a critical role in the generation of ventral cell types along the whole rostrocaudal axis of the neural tube [ ]. Recombinant SHH is commonly added at various concentrations (e.g., 200 ng/ml [ , , ], 50–500 ng/ml [ ], 250 ng/ml [ ], 50 ng/ml [ ]), while small-molecule SHH agonists such as purmorphamine and SAG, serve as alternatives for Ma and colleagues [ , , ], offering a more chemically defined approach. Extended SHH exposure (e.g., days 2–26 [ ]) suggests a sustained requirement for proper ventral patterning. Despite many differentiation protocols using SHH, work by Arber and colleagues [ ] suggested that SHH is not a driver of LGE fate—marked by TFs such as NOLZ1, FOXP2, and CTIP2— but rather is involved in ventralizing NPCs towards MGE identity, characterized by TFs such as NKX2.1 and LHX8. Furthermore, blocking SHH signaling with cyclopamine had no effect on LGE marker expression, demonstrating that LGE fate was independent of SHH signaling with their protocol [ ]. In addition to these canonical pathways, several other signaling molecules have shown efficacy in SPN differentiation. Activin A, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, has been reported to support the induction of LGE-like NPCs [ , ]. Retinoic acid (RA) has also been used to promote caudalization and GABAergic identity [ , , ],
At later differentiation stages, neurotrophic factors and intracellular modulators are used to support maturation and functional integration. BDNF is the most consistently used factor across protocols [ – , , ], promoting survival and differentiation. Coadministration of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) further supports maturation and synaptogenesis [ , , , ].
Intracellular signaling modulators, such as cyclic AMP agonists (dibutyryl cyclic AMP (dcAMP), forskolin), and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors (valproic acid, valpromide) are also widely employed to promote late-stage neuronal differentiation and the expression of SPN markers, likely acting via epigenetic and transcriptional regulation [ , , , , ]. Additional molecules used in specific protocols include DAPT (a Notch inhibitor) for neural maturation [ ], and GABA itself to enhance functional properties [ , ]. Compounds such as PD 0332991 (a CDK4/6 inhibitor) and CHIR 99021 (a GSK3β inhibitor) reflect ongoing efforts to fine-tune Wnt and cell cycle signaling in terminal differentiation stages [ , ]. The ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 is also commonly included to improve cell survival and attachment during dissociation and replating [ – , ].
In summary, the differentiation of SPNs from hPSCs depends on sequential modulation of key developmental pathways. Dual SMAD and Wnt inhibition are fundamental for initiating neural induction and anterior patterning. SHH, Activin A, and FGF signaling further specify ventral forebrain identity and support the expansion of progenitor pools. Neurotrophic factors and intracellular modulators enhance neuronal maturation and functionality in later stages. Recent protocols have been increasingly refined through the substitution of recombinant proteins (DKK1, SHH) with defined small molecules, enhancing both the yield and purity of target cells. Unlike earlier protocols, which were time-consuming and prone to variability in efficiency, current strategies aim to produce clinically relevant NPCs with improved consistency and scalability.

## In Vitro Characterization and Yield of SPN Progenitors
The success of differentiation protocols aimed at developing a CRT is primarily evaluated by their ability to generate high yields of the desired cell type. A central objective across these studies is the derivation of homogeneous populations enriched in LGE-like NPCs —intended as the advanced therapy medicinal product (ATMP)—and mature SPNs, which serve as indicators of successful terminal differentiation. Although the derivation of striatal-committed progenitors has been achieved, yields remain variable, and protocols differ considerably in their balance between efficacy, maturity and purity. Comparative analysis reveal a set of recurrent trends and challenges that have shaped the evolution of SPN differentiation (Table ).

 The choice, concentration, and timing of morphogens and small molecules critically determine SPN yield. Early protocols combining SHH and WNT inhibition established the principle of ventral specification and successful generation of DARPP32+/GABA+ neurons. However, these processes frequently generated mixed populations that included nonstriatal and off-target, non-neural phenotypes [ ]. Optimization of SHH exposure later enabled much higher efficiencies, with up to ~75% of neurons acquiring a DARPP32+ GABAergic identity and functionality [ ]. Dual SMAD inhibition subsequently became a robust strategy for neural induction, providing the basis for highly efficient generation of LGE-like progenitors and mature SPNs [ – ]. More recently, Activin A has emerged as an alternative differentiation cue, capable of inducing LGE-like phenotypes in the absence of SHH and yielding SPNs with strong expression of CTIP2, DARPP32, and electrophysiological features consistent with native striatal identity [ , ]. These findings suggest that distinct developmental routes can converge on SPN specification but differ in their ability to minimize unwanted fates.
Beyond morphogen exposure, the structural context of differentiation also plays a decisive role. Most of the developed approaches rely on stromal cocultures, embryoid bodies or two-dimensional (2D) monolayers [ , – , , ]. However, the introduction of three-dimensional (3D) biomaterials has promoted maturation and increased the proportion of DARPP32+/CTIP2+ neurons with synaptic activity [ ]. At the same time, a progressive replacement of recombinant proteins with chemically defined small molecules has increased reproducibility and scalability, reducing differentiation timelines and enhancing compatibility with GMP standards [ , , ].
Despite these refinements, heterogeneity persists as a major limitation. Even the most recent protocols report contaminating cortical (TBR1+), interneuronal (somatostatin+), or dopaminergic (TH+) lineages [ , ]. Moreover, proliferative cells (Ki67+) are frequently detected at transplantation stages [ – ], raising safety concerns regarding incomplete lineage restriction. Transcriptomic analyses further indicate that, although canonical makers such as CTIP2, DARPP32, and calbindin (CB), are consistently expressed, late striatal transcriptional programs often remain only partially activated, reflecting incomplete terminal maturation [ ].
Functional validation has become a critical benchmark for protocol assessment. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [ ], patch-clamp recordings [ , , , ], and calcium imaging [ ] consistently demonstrate that hPSC-derived SPNs release GABA, fire action potentials, and exhibit postsynaptic responses, thereby confirming their functional competence in vitro.
In summary, SPN differentiation has progressed from early SHH-based approaches with modest yields to chemically defined, clinically compatible strategies combining dual SMAD inhibition, activin A signaling, and/or advanced culture systems. While functionally validated SPNs can now be produced with relatively high efficiency, residual heterogeneity and incomplete maturation remain key barriers for translation into the clinical application. Overcoming these challenges will be essential to ensure the safety, stability, and therapeutic potential of SPN progenitors as ATMP.

## Preclinical Assessment of Transplanted SPN Progenitors in Rodent Models
The ultimate validation of in vitro-differentiated SPN progenitors for CRT lies in their performance posttransplantation (PT) into relevant animal models. Preclinical rodent studies have been instrumental in assessing the survival, maturation, and functional integration of these hPSC-derived populations (Table ).

 Two main classes of HD models have been used for intrastriatal NPC transplantation: the excitotoxic QA lesion model, which induces acute striatal atrophy while lacking mHTT expression, and genetic models, which exhibit limited cell loss but better mimics the progressive molecular pathology of the disease [ ]. QA-lesioned animals remain the predominant paradigm [ , , , , , , , ], while transgenic models have been employed less frequently [ , , ]. Host immunological management varied among studies, with some groups relying on immunodeficient strains [ , , , , , ] and others applying systemic immunosuppression [ , , , , ]. The timing of transplantation also differed. Most QA lesion studies were performed in adult hosts, while transgenic models were targeted at disease-relevant ages, from early symptomatic R6/2 mice [ , ] to aged YAC128 mice exhibiting advanced pathology [ ]. Other authors opted for neonatal grafting [ , , ], which leverages developmental plasticity and can bypass the need for immunosuppression [ ]. Across these diverse approaches, grafts were generally well-tolerated [ – , , , ], although graft survival rates were not always documented.
Initial efforts investigated the optimal differentiation stage for grafting. Transplants derived from early neural precursors included teratoma-like regions, whereas late-stage postmitotic neurons survived poorly [ ]. The best outcomes for striatal in vivo differentiation were achieved with intermediate, striatal-committed NPCs aimed at a more efficient restriction to the SPN fate for the cell-based ATMP. Pluripotency markers were absent [ , ], and teratoma-like regions were not observed in the grafts [ – ], indicating that differentiation protocols eliminated the undifferentiated cell fractions before grafting. In addition, proliferative activity progressively declined and became rare or absent over time [ , , , , , ]. A robust demonstration of the absence of uncontrolled proliferation will be essential for clinical translation. In this context, a recent work has introduced CD200-based cell sorting to increase cell product homogeneity and reduce the potential for graft overgrowth [ ].
Preclinical studies demonstrate that hPSC-derived NPCs can differentiate into striatal neurons in vivo [ – , – , , , , ], though the efficiency and fidelity of SPN generation vary considerably. SPN-like identity within the grafts has been assessed primarily through histological analyses using combinations of neuronal and striatal markers, revealing the progressive acquisition of differentiation features. CTIP2+ cells typically appear earlier and more abundantly, whereas DARPP32+ cells emerge later [ , – , , , ], ranging from < 1% [ ] to nearly 60% [ ]. Nevertheless, DARPP32+ neurons did not reach the yields reported in vitro and exhibited lower and more variable signal intensity than host neurons [ ]. Additional striatal markers, including GABA, CB, FOXP1, and FOXG1, were frequently reported, whereas markers including GAD65/67, FOXP2, and MEIS2 appeared less consistently in the literature. Direct (substance P+/dopamine receptor D1+) and indirect (enkephalin+/DRD2+) pathway SPN-like cells [ , , ] were present with variable abundance, and grafts formed domains reminiscent of GE territories, including μ-opioid receptor+ patch-like zones [ ].
A recurrent observation was the presence of minor glial populations, including astrocytes [ , , , , ] and oligodendrocytes [ ], whereas microglial cells were absent in all grafts that examined this cell type [ , ]. Striatal interneurons also contributed to cellular diversity [ , , ], and additional assessments reported cholinergic, glutamatergic, dopaminergic, or serotonergic populations, which were found to be scarce [ , , , ]. Overall, these findings reflected the inherent heterogeneity of the transplanted cell products rather than a homogeneous SPN population.
The degree of striatal identity achieved by grafted cells raised the question of functional relevance, as the presence of SPN-like neurons may not be sufficient if they fail to integrate into host circuits. Preclinical studies have therefore investigated the extent to which transplanted hPSC-derived NPCs recapitulate the SPN circuitry within the basal ganglia. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed host TH+ fibers [ , , ] and VGLUT1+ puncta [ , ] within the graft core, suggesting dopaminergic and glutamatergic inputs, respectively. The synaptic markers synaptophysin and PSD95 were also detected, consistent with the formation of pre- and postsynaptic structures [ , , ]. Moreover, donor cells sent projections reaching canonical striatal targets [ , , , ].
Ultrastructural analyses corroborated functional integration. Electron microscopy at 3 months PT showed that grafted cells extended long axons toward the external globus pallidus, established inhibitory synapses, and received excitatory inputs from the host [ ]. In tracing studies, retrograde labeling with Fluorogold confirmed graft outputs to the substantia nigra [ ]. More recently, rabies virus-based monosynaptic tracing revealed local synapse formation and a degree of integration into pre-existing circuits by 2 months PT [ ], but only long-term engraftment enabled connectivity with long-range host afferents, with cortico-striatal projections being the predominant input source [ ]. In addition, environmental factors, such as enriched housing conditions, may further enhance connectivity. [ ]. Despite these promising results, the extent of functional integration remains limited, with neurochemical and electrophysiological evidence of synaptic function still scarce. To date, only one study has monitored graft function by in vivo imaging [ ]. PET and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested a graft-mediated dampening of the QA-triggered inflammation. However, analyses of DRD2/dopamine receptor D3 (DRD3) availability, assessed using [11C]raclopride, did not show changes in DRD2/DRD3-expressing cells [ ].
The ultimate test of hPSC-derived NPC grafts is whether anatomical and synaptic integration translates into functional recovery. In QA-lesioned animals, this has been primarily assessed using the apomorphine (APO)-induced rotation test, where contralateral turning reflects an imbalance in dopamine signaling between the lesioned and intact striatum, and recovery is indicated by a reduction in rotations [ ]. Results were variable, with some studies reporting no functional improvements [ ], whereas others observed signs of improvement that differed depending on the transplanted cell line [ ] or the PT time point assessed [ ].
Graft efficacy has also been evaluated using a range of motor tasks [ , , , , , , ]. Rotarod performance, reflecting balance and coordination, improved consistently in QA-lesioned [ , , , ] and in transgenic [ , ] models, with gains appearing from a few weeks to several months PT. Open field testing, which measures spontaneous locomotion and exploratory activity, showed comparable improvements in QA-lesioned animals [ , ], complemented by gait analyses [ ]. Skilled reaching improved in the staircase test [ ], and forelimb akinesia was ameliorated in stepping tests [ , ], whereas only a transient benefit was noted in vibrissae-evoked hand placing [ ]. In addition, transplanted R6/2 mice exhibited delayed onset and reduced clasping, alongside a lifespan extension of at least 19% compared to the untreated cohort [ ].
A common limitation of QA lesion studies assessing bilateral motor tasks is the absence of nonlesioned controls [ , , , , ]. Although sham or lesion-only groups were included, these do not provide a baseline for normal motor performance, making it difficult to gauge the extent of recovery. In fact, the environmental enrichment paradigm in QA-lesioned animals did not produce an additive effect on motor behavior when combined with the graft [ ]. By contrast, genetic model studies incorporated a WT group [ , ], enabling the assessment of graft efficacy relative to both pathological and physiological baselines. Beyond motor disturbance, cognitive and psychiatric impairments are also hallmarks of HD. While rodent fetal grafts have provided some evidence of improved cognitive performance [ – ], the impact of hPSC-derived grafts on both cognitive and psychiatric domains remains unexplored.
Another important dimension in the field is the use of NPCs derived from patient-derived hiPSCs, which offer personalized models but also pose disease-specific challenges. Transplanted NPCs derived from an HD-hiPSC line (HD-NPCs) displayed extensive neuronal differentiation at 12 weeks PT, including an SPN-like subset [ , ]. By 33 weeks PT, however, EM48 immunostaining revealed hallmark HD pathology in the HD-NPC group but not in control NPCs, indicating that HD-NPCs develop disease features over time in vivo [ ]. Consistently, other authors showed that neonatally engrafted HD-NPCs integrated into the mouse striatum and developed progressive, human-specific soluble and aggregated mHTT forms, seeding pathology and triggering murine cell death [ ]. Hence, correction of the HD mutation is likely required before HD-hiPSC grafting. A step in this direction was taken by An and colleagues [ ], who transplanted NSCs derived from a genetically corrected HD-hiPSC line into the striatum of R6/2 mice. At 2 weeks PT, grafted cells populated the host tissue and differentiated into MAP2+ neurons coexpressing DARPP32 and GABA, without evidence of pathology. Despite no functional assessments being conducted, this study provided the first demonstration for the use of genetically corrected patient-derived cells in an animal model of HD.
The degenerative host environment may also compromise graft viability. Indeed, healthy fetal striatal allografts in patients with HD displayed mHTT aggregates in postmortem samples [ , ], providing further evidence for the propagation of HD pathology by soluble mHTT species. Nuclear-localized mHTT aggregates were detected within the transplanted human neurons in R6/2 mice, correlating with small and sparse grafts [ ]. Altogether, these findings suggest that the progressively neurodegenerative environment of the HD striatum adversely impacts graft viability and integration, which must be considered to achieve robust and durable CRT.
In summary, preclinical studies have shown that hPSC-derived SPN progenitors can survive transplantation into the rodent striatum and differentiate into neurons with SPN-like features. Early concerns about graft overgrowth and poor integration have been mitigated by refining differentiation protocols and transplant timing. However, the diversity in starting cell lines, differentiation strategies, experimental models, and the specific readouts evaluated in each work limits cross-study comparability. Moving forward, multimodal evaluations—including molecular, neurochemical, electrophysiological, neuroanatomical, and behavioral assessments addressing motor, cognitive, and psychiatric domains—will help better understand the therapeutic potential and limitations of hPSC-derived grafts for HD.

## Other Cell Therapy Strategies: From Unpatterned NSCs to Direct Reprogramming
Beyond the use of SPN precursors generated through multistep hPSC differentiation protocols, alternative cell therapy strategies for HD are being investigated. These include the use of unpatterned NSCs/NPCs, glial progenitors, and directly reprogrammed cells. Selected studies illustrating these approaches are presented in Table .

 Song and colleagues [ ] differentiated hESCs into nestin+ unpatterned NPCs via coculture with PA6 stromal feeder cells and transplanted them into the striatum of QA-lesioned rats. Although grafted cells displayed an early neuronal phenotype and did not express striatal markers, transplanted animals exhibited functional recovery in the APO-induced rotation test during the 3 weeks PT. Later, Park and colleagues [ ] used hiPSC-derived NPCs lacking striatal specification in the YAC128 HD model. Motor and cognitive functions were significantly improved in transplanted animals over a 5-month evaluation period, during which donor cells differentiated into neurons including a DARPP32+ subset, as well as oligodendrocytes and astrocytes. In subsequent work, Park and colleagues [ ] knocked down SUPT4H1 in patient-derived HD-hiPSCs to explore whether graft efficacy could be enhanced by reducing mHTT expression in autologous HD unpatterned NPCs. At 3 months PT, a proportion of grafted cells expressed DARPP32 and MAP2, and motor function was improved compared with animals receiving unedited HD-NPCs and sham controls.
Reidling and colleagues [ ] transplanted a Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-grade hESC-derived NSC line into the striatum of R6/2 and Q140 model mice. MAP2 and βIII-tubulin but no specific SPN or striatal markers were reported. In R6/2 mice, grafts integrated into the host circuitry and improved motor deficits. NSCs also ameliorated motor and late-stage cognitive impairment in the Q140 model, despite the report of very low cell survival. In a follow-up study, Holley and colleagues [ ] extended this work using the heterozygous zQ175 HD mouse model to assess the long-term effects of NSC transplantation. NSCs survived for at least 8 months and improved behavioral deficits. Grafted cells differentiated into diverse neuronal subtypes including SPN- and interneuron-like cells and functionally integrated by receiving synaptic inputs and forming connections with host neurons.
Notably, a substantial proportion of grafted cells in these studies differentiated into astrocytes (84% in the study by Reidling and colleagues [ ] and 66% in that of Park and colleagues [ ]), underscoring the potential contribution of glial cells to functional recovery. Indeed, a previous study by Benraiss and colleagues [ ] demonstrated the potential of human glial progenitor cell (hGPC) transplants in HD. Fetal hGPCs were transplanted into the striata of newborn R6/2 × rag1−/− mice. hGPC transplants integrated as astrocytes and persistent GPCs, rescued aspects of electrophysiological and behavioral phenotype, and extended survival in R6/2 HD mice. This work suggests that colonization of the HD striatum with healthy glia may be a viable strategy for slowing disease progression. In a recent study by the same group, Villanueva and colleagues [ ] demonstrated that striatal chimerization with fetal hGPCs in young adult R6/2 × rag1−/− mice similarly rescued behavioral deficits and delayed disease progression. Following transplantation, animals exhibited improved motor performance (rotarod, open field), recognition memory (novel object recognition), and anxiety-related behavior (elevated plus maze), alongside a significant increase in lifespan. Using single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) to assess graft-induced transcriptional remodeling in HD, the authors showed that hGPC engraftment partially restored normal gene expression of host SPNs, highlighting the upregulation of synapse-associated pathways. Accordingly, rabies-based retrograde labeling of SPNs in neonatally transplanted mice revealed a substantial restoration of dendritic complexity in R6/2 SPNs. These results suggest that colonization of the HD striatum with healthy glia may be a viable strategy for slowing disease progression, rescuing both structure and function in the vulnerable HD SPNs.
Together, these studies support the idea that the mechanisms driving functional recovery in HD models are not limited to circuit reconstruction. The behavioral improvements observed in the absence of SPNs [ , , , , ] indicate that SPN-specific identity may not be strictly required for efficacy. In addition, trophic support and the reduction of pathological mHTT accumulation likely contribute to the observed therapeutic effects [ , ]. The predominance of astrocytic differentiation in several grafts and the functional improvements following hGPC engraftment underscore the relevance of glial-mediated mechanisms in modulating the host environment [ , ]. These findings provide a rationale for combinatorial transplantation strategies incorporating GPCs and NSCs/NPCs, which may offer synergistic effects and enhance functional recovery in HD.
Direct reprogramming provides an interesting alternative to hPSC-based methods for deriving striatal neurons. Maucksch and colleagues [ ] initially demonstrated the conversion of human fibroblasts into induced neural precursors through nonviral delivery of SOX2 and PAX6, using either plasmid DNA transfection or recombinant protein transduction. This approach was later refined through the use of chemically modified mRNA technology [ ]. By day 21 of reprogramming, the resulting induced neural precursors displayed high expression of striatal developmental markers, including GSX2 (91%), ASCL1 (59%), and MEIS2 (93%) [ ]. In parallel, Liu and colleagues [ ] employed the polypyrimidine-tract-binding knockdown method [ ] to reprogram human fibroblasts into neuron-like cells expressing NeuN, GABA, and DARPP32.
To our knowledge, only one study has reported intrastriatal transplantation of directly reprogrammed cells. Victor and colleagues [ ] reprogrammed human fibroblasts via ectopic expression of miR-9/9* and miR-124 combined with transcription factors enriched in the developing striatum. Cotransduction with BCL11B induced DARPP32+ neurons. The addition of DLX1 and DLX2 promoted a GABAergic phenotype, while MYT1L increased MAP2 expression. Following transplantation into the neonatal WT mouse striatum, reprogrammed cells persisted over 6 months, exhibiting electrophysiological properties consistent with native SPNs, dense dendritic spines, and projections to SPN target regions. Grafted cells were 93% MAP2+, 76% GABAergic, 91% DARPP32+, and 91% FOXP1. Despite these encouraging results, this approach has yet to be evaluated in HD rodent models.
A second approach to direct neuronal reprogramming is in vivo direct conversion. Wu and colleagues [ ] attempted to reprogram striatal astrocytes into GABAergic neurons by employing an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector to deliver the NEUROD1 and DLX2 transcription factors into the striatum of R6/2 and YAC128 mice. In R6/2 mice, this intervention extended lifespan and improved motor deficits. Subsequent lineage-tracing studies, however, highlighted potential limitations of AAV-based systems, particularly concerning reporter leakiness, and suggested that reporter-labeled neurons may have been preexisting endogenous neurons rather than newly converted ones [ ]. Thus, while these findings do not exclude the possibility of astrocyte-to-neuron conversion, the observed functional improvements in HD mice may instead reflect a neuroprotection effect, as NEUROD1 is required for neuronal survival [ ]. More recently, Zhang and colleagues [ ] used lentiviral delivery of DLX2 to reprogram astrocytes into ASCL1+ multipotent NPCs, which gave rise to neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes in the adult mouse striatum. Hence, reprogramming of resident glial cells may be harnessed to replace lost SPNs in the HD brain, although further work is needed to understand the cellular responses and molecular mechanisms governing the in vivo reprogramming process.

## Challenges
Despite promising advances in cell-based therapies for HD, clinical translation is hindered by challenges spanning biological, methodological, and regulatory domains [ ]. Overcoming these obstacles demands coordinated international efforts. The SC4HD consortium exemplifies such collaboration, bringing together clinicians and scientists to provide guidance and devise a clinical development plan for stem cell therapy for HD [ ].
Production and safety standards under GMP are required for clinical application, which needs adapting research protocols to a GMP-compliant quality system, implementing a risk-based approach to identify and mitigate high-risk points, and validating manufacturing procedures for robustness, homogeneity, and quality. The first step is defining the target product profile (TPP), which sets the minimal criteria for safety and efficacy, including cell number, dose, phenotype, and karyotype. Although the TPP will vary depending on the therapeutic aim (e.g., replacing degenerated SPNs versus delivering protective factors), establishing guiding principles for TPP determination is a goal. Achieving this, in turn, requires a deep understanding of the product’s biology (e.g., cell growth, morphology, proliferation rates, and specific cell type markers).
Within HD CRT, both the choice of starting cell type—hESCs versus hiPSCs—and differentiation reproducibility represent major hurdles, as variability in the yield and maturity of neurons and SPNs has been reported across cell lines and laboratories [ ]. Moreover, the extent to which graft-derived neurons recapitulate bona fide SPN profiles remains unclear. DARPP32 is the only marker consistently reported in vivo, but its expression is variable, often falling short of in vitro levels and displaying reduced signal intensity compared to host tissue [ ]. Such inconsistencies likely reflect the lack of standardized antibodies and technical protocols, which may lead to the recognition of different DARPP32 isoforms, including immature ones [ ]. Although SPNs are the principal neuronal subtype lost in HD, evidence from fetal grafts therapeutic strategies suggests that other striatal cell types, including interneurons and glia, may also be required for effective repair [ , ]. In this context, single-cell profiling of grafts—successfully applied in PD models [ ]—can provide a detailed view of graft cellular diversity and validate cell identity in vivo.
Current preclinical evidence for graft-mediated recovery in HD remains modest and is largely based on motor and drug-induced readouts assessed over relatively short periods. Long-term studies are crucial, as therapeutic benefits depend on sustained graft survival and maturation. Xenotransplanted human neurons undergo morphological and functional maturation over a months-long timeline, mirroring human neurodevelopment [ ]. Accordingly, preclinical models require prolonged monitoring to fully capture safety and efficacy. However, clear standards for toxicology testing are lacking, and thorough, long-duration safety evaluations have yet to be conducted. In parallel, the absence of standardized efficacy measures undermines reproducibility and cross-study comparisons. A robust translational framework is needed, integrating molecular, histological, electrophysiological, and behavioral assessments, aligned across research centers.
In summary, while the therapeutic potential of cell-based products for HD is increasingly recognized, clinical translation requires rigorous standardization, long-term evaluation, and the development of shared frameworks that ensure safety, efficacy, reproducibility, and comparability. Addressing these gaps will be essential to unlock the full potential of regenerative medicine for HD.
