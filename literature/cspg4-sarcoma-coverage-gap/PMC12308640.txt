## Graphical abstract

## Abstract
Over the past 30 years, the number of new cases of uterine leiomyomas (UL) in women of reproductive age has increased by 67.14% worldwide. The limitations of the current therapeutic options have led to the search for alternatives. Myrianthus arboreus P. Beauv., used for infertility and tumors, has never been tested for UL. In the present study, the decoction of its stem bark was evaluated in a model of UL induced in female Wistar rats. Animals were treated once daily by gavage for 30 days. Normal control and model groups received distilled water, positive groups received mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg) and the remaining three groups were treated with 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg M. arboreus extract, respectively. Compared to the model group, the extract at 50 and 100 mg/kg reduced the E2B- and progesterone-induced uterine horn asymmetry and thickening, and the relative uterine weight and diameter; myometrial thickness; and collagen density (at 100 and 200 mg/kg). Regarding cytokines, the extract decreased the uterine levels of TGF-β1 and VEGF (at 100 and 200 mg/kg) and TNF-α (at all doses tested). It also decreased the serum levels of estradiol (at 100 and 200 mg/kg). Despite positive trend in reducing oxidative damage (decreased MDA at 50 mg/kg, increased catalase activity at all tested doses, and increased GSH at 100 mg/kg), the level of oxidative stress is still elevated. By attenuating key cellular events involved in the growth and development of UL, such as inflammation, fibrosis and angiogenesis, Myrianthus arboreus may be a promising option for UL treatment and management.

## Lay summary
Uterine fibroids are non-cancerous tumors that originate from the smooth muscular wall of the uterus. They may cause severe symptoms, such as chronic pelvic pain, heavy menstrual bleeding, painful intercourse, frequent urination, back pain and obstetric complications (embryo implantation failure, fetal growth restriction, miscarriages, preterm delivery, and fertility impairment). The exact cause is not known, but hormonal changes may play an important role. Treatment options depend on the symptoms and desire to preserve the uterus, and may include medication, minimally invasive procedures and surgery. Their associated risks and limitations fuel searching for conservative alternatives. This study investigates the anti-uterine fibroid potential of the giant yellow mulberry (Myrianthus arboreus P. Beauv.) in rat. Consuming boiled M. arboreus stem bark in water reduced uterine wall thickness, inflammation, and collagen deposition, and increased the ability of the uterus to fight cell damage. Thus, this extract may prevent or shrink uterine fibroids by reducing uterine swelling, fibrosis and excessive cell growth.

## Introduction
Uterine leiomyomas (UL), also known as uterine fibroids or fibroma, are monoclonal benign pelvic tumors of the female reproductive system. They consist of abnormal smooth muscle cells arising from myometrium and abundant myofibroblasts secreting excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) ( , ). They represent the most common neoplasm in women of reproductive age and beyond. Using ultrasound, a cumulative incidence of 80% has been reported in black women in their 50s in USA ( ). In 2019, 9,643,336 new cases (crude incidence rate of 250.05/100,000) and 226,045,773 prevalent cases of UL were estimated in 2019 ( ). Data from sub-national studies indicate a prevalence of 3–79% across Africa ( ). As most cases are asymptomatic, the reported incidence/prevalence is undeniably below reality.
The global burden of UL has been increasing over past decades ( ) and will continue to increase. Although they do not or very rarely progress in cancer, UL is associated with debilitating symptoms in up to 50% of cases, significantly altering women’s daily lives and quality of life ( , , ). It generates severe medical and societal costs ( , ). These enormous costs are borne by both the patient and the healthcare system. The unpleasant side effects and consequences of UL include infertility, heavy or prolonged bleeding, anemia, pelvic pressure and pain, constipation, miscarriage and preterm birth ( ). Patients also experience a significant emotional distress, depression and anxiety ( ). In general, black women are disproportionately affected (higher incidence, larger tumors, more severe symptoms, and an earlier age of onset) ( , ).
The management of UL remains a real challenge, the patient’s preferences and quality of life (clinical situation) being the most important aspects to consider ( ). Patients are treated according to the severity of their symptoms, size and location of fibroids, fertility wishes and preferences for surgical treatment. Surgery (hysterectomy and myomectomy) remains an excellent option for severe symptoms. Although being a definitive solution, hysterectomy is invasive, not recommended for women with a desire to conceive, and associated with a high risk of surgical complications ( , ). Procedures that leave the uterus intact, including myomectomy and the minimally invasive approaches such as uterine artery embolization and endometrial ablation, are associated with a high rate of both UL recurrence and future surgery ( , ) and other complications ( , , ). In addition, women who have undergone myomectomy have higher rates of elective delivery and greater blood loss at delivery than women with myomas in situ ( ). Medical treatments are the first-line treatment to preserve fertility and avoid or delay surgery ( ). However, they are not able to make UL disappear permanently and are limited to a short-term of symptom control. The associated adverse effects limit their use to a few months, mainly as adjuvant treatment before surgery ( , , ). For instance, the off-label use of the selective progesterone receptor modulator (SPRM) mifepristone (5–50 mg/day) for 3 months significantly reduced UL volume and related symptoms ( , ), but its long-term use is associated with detrimental effects including endometrial hyperplasia ( ). The cessation of these treatments invariably leads to a rapid myoma regrowth and recurrence of symptoms. Overall, all treatment options, especially surgical procedures, are too expensive, especially for resource-limited populations such as those in Sub-Saharan Africa ( , ). In such area, where the traditional medicine is embedded in the community’s lifestyle, medicinal plants appear to be an inexpensive and safe alternative.
For centuries, hundreds of medicinal plants have been traditionally used in Cameroon to alleviate gynecological problems and tumors. When tested in vitro and/or in vivo, many of them, such as Myrianthus arboreus P. Beauv., have shown promising anti-tumoral properties but have not been tested against UL. Myrianthus arboreus P. Beauv., commonly known as ‘giant yellow mulberry’ or ‘monkey fruit’, a medicinal plant widely used in tropical Africa as a natural remedy for a wide range of diseases ( , , ). In Cameroon, leaf extracts are used against female infertility and to improve lactation ( , ). The aqueous leaf extract of M. arboreus was found to improve fertility ( ) and conditions associated with the polycystic ovary syndrome ( ) in female Wistar rats. Numerous other studies have reported anti-diabetic ( , ), antioxidant ( , , ), anti-inflammatory ( , ) and anti-tumoral ( , ) properties. The phytochemical investigation of an aqueous leaf extract of M. arboreus led to the identification of β-sitosterol, isocaryophyllene and methyl palmitate (CF Awounfack, unpublished work), which are known to have anti-fibrotic effects ( , , , , , , ). Flavanols (epicatechin and epigallocatechin) and triterpenoids (euscaphic acid and tormentic acid) have been identified in the stem bark of M. arboreus ( ). Studies have reported anti-tumoral activities of these compounds against various benign and malignant diseases ( , , , , , ), including UL ( , ).
The pathogenesis of UL remains poorly understood. However, reviews and original works have identified a series of key cellular events, such as proliferation, hypoxia, fibrosis, chronic inflammation and angiogenesis, that act in concert to drive UL development and growth ( , , , , ). Thus, natural products or substances able to attenuate all or most of these key cellular events involved in UL development may help to manage UL growth and symptoms. Given the aforementioned antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative and anti-fibrotic properties of M. arboreus extracts and compounds, this plant could be considered as a potential treatment for patients with UL. In line with this, the present study was designed to evaluate the anti-UL potential of the stem bark decoction of M. arboreus in female Wistar rats.

## Materials and methods

## Chemical and reagents
17β-estradiol benzoate (E2B) was from Cayman Chemicals (USA), while progesterone (product code CH21001388) was obtained from Caesar and Loretz GmbH (Germany). Serum estradiol and progesterone levels were assayed using ELISA AccuBind® kits (Monobind Inc., USA). ELISA kits Elabscience® (USA) were used to measure uterine IL-6 and TNF-α levels, while uterine VEGF and TGF-β1 levels were assayed using the Quantikine® (Abingdon Science Park, UK) and Cusabio® (USA) kits, respectively.

## Animals
The young adult (8–10-weeks-old), non-pregnant, nulliparous and healthy female Wistar rats (100–120 g) were obtained from the animal breeding house of the Animal Physiology Laboratory, Faculty of Science, University of Yaoundé 1 (Cameroon). They were maintained in clean plastic cages at room temperature (22 ± 3°C) and under natural light/dark cycle. The animals had free access to water and soy-free diet.

## Ethics approval
The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for Research on Human Health of the University of Douala, Cameroon (authorization no. 3614 CEI-UDo/05/2023/T), and animals were handled according to the guidelines from the European Communities Council directive 86/609/CEE.

## Plant material
The stem bark of M. arboreus was collected on March 4, 2023 (7–8 h) from Dschang, West Region of Cameroon. Leaves and fruits were also collected for authentication. The plant species was authenticated by NGANSOP T. Eric, a botanical expert from the Cameroon National Herbarium (HNC) in Yaoundé, by comparison with the voucher specimen deposited under the reference number 44061 SRF Cam.

## Plant extraction and the choice of doses
Three hundred grams of air-dried stem bark powder was boiled in distilled water for 30 min. After cooling at room temperature, the decoction was filtered using a Whatman No. 4 paper and evaporated using an oven setup at 50°C. The dry extract obtained (43.6 g) was then stored at 2–4°C until was used for the pharmacological tests.
According to the traditional prescription, the patients were given nearly 500 mL of the decoction per day for a total of 2,000 mg for 60 kg (33.33 mg/kg). Using allosteric extrapolation ( ), the equivalent dose in the rat was 206.67 mg/kg. This dose was rounded to 200 mg/kg and divided by 2 and 4 to obtain the three tested doses of 50, 100 and 200 mg/kg.

## Quantification of some phytochemical classes in the extract
The colorimetric methods were used for the quantification. The total polyphenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent ( ). The method of aluminum chloride was employed to quantify the total flavonoid content ( ). The total alkaloids in the extract was measured using the 1,10-phenanthroline method ( ), while the procedure proposed by and adjusted for dried plant material ( ) was used for total terpenoid content determination.
The results were respectively derived from the calibration curves of gallic acid, quercetin, quinine and oleanic acid, and expressed in mg equivalent per gram of dry extract.

## Establishment of the rat UL model and treatment
UL were induced in the model group (MG) by a multi-point intramuscular administration of E2B (2 mg/kg/day) and progesterone (1 mg/kg/day) dissolved in corn oil, each other day, for 60 days, while the normal group (NG) received the corn oil (50 μL/injection site). During this period, the animals were weighed once a week. The occurrence of UL was verified by laparotomy. At the end of the induction period, five animals from each group (NG1 and MG1) were sacrificed. The remaining normal and model animals were randomly divided into six subgroups of five animals each and treated as follow: the normal (NG2) and negative control (MG2) subgroups received distilled water, the second subgroup of model animals received mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg/day) ( ), and the remaining three subgroups of model animals received M. aureus stem bark decoction (MA) at the doses of 50, 100 and 200 mg/kg/day, respectively. Distilled water and ‘drugs’ (mifepristone and extract) dissolved in distilled water were once-daily administrated by gavage (1 mL/100 g body weight) for 30 consecutive days. Animals were also weighed weekly.
Twenty-four hours after the last administration, the animals were sacrificed at the metestrus phase under anesthesia (50 mg/kg ketamine + 10 mg/kg diazepam, i.p.) after a 12 h overnight non-hydric fasting. Blood samples were collected in dry tubes, kept for a few moments at room temperature and centrifuged at 1,500×g (15 min at 5°C). The serum was stored at −20°C until progesterone and estradiol levels were measured. The uterus, ovaries, liver, kidney, brain, lungs, adrenal glands and heart were removed and weighed. The diameter of the uterine horn was measured and the mean of the values was taken as the diameter of the uterus. After a visual examination, the left uterine horn and the ovaries were fixed in 10% neutral formalin for the histological analysis ( ). A part of the right uterine horn (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1 mL phosphate buffered saline on ice. After centrifugation (1,500×g for 15 min at 5°C), the supernatants were collected to measure uterine levels of cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6), growth factors (TGF-β1 and VEGF) and oxidative stress parameters (catalase, malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced glutathione (GSH)).

## Histological analysis
The 5 μm formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue sections were stained with either hematoxylin–eosin (uterus and ovaries) or Masson’s trichrome (uterus). The microphotographs were taken and analyzed using a Zeiss microscope equipped with an AmScope MU1803 18 MP CMOS C-Mount microscope camera with reduction lens.
The first and every tenth section of the ovary for a total of five sections were selected for counting the different ovarian follicles, and the results for all sections were summed. Graafian follicles were the largest and most developed follicle, with a large fluid-filled cavity at the center containing an egg cell located eccentrically and surrounded by layers of granulosa cells. Atretic follicles were characterized by a disrupted follicular architecture, cytoplasmic shrinkage and nuclear pyknosis of granulosa cells and detachment of these cells from the basement membrane. Sometimes, there was a complete absence of, or degeneration in, the oocyte. Collapsed follicles filled with blood and showing greatly enlarged theca interna and granulosa cells were identified as corpus lutea ( , ).
For the quantification of the myometrial collagen fibers, TIFF microphotographs (4.152 pixel/μm) of Masson’s trichrome-stained slides from three randomly selected areas of the myometrium were analyzed using ImageJ, processing as previously proposed by .

## Statistical analysis
The data expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (ESM) were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 5.03 software. After the induction period, Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the model subgroup with the healthy or normal subgroup. Comparisons between treated subgroups and controls were made using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-test, except for the body weight evolution where two-way ANOVA was used. Differences were considered significant at a P value < 0.05.

## Results

## Quantitative phytochemical screening of the decoction of the stem bark extract of M. arboreus
 summarizes the total polyphenolic, flavonoids, alkaloids and terpenoids contents of the M. arboreus stem bark decoction. Polyphenols, including flavonoids and terpenoids, are present in considerable quantities, while alkaloids are present in trace amounts. The content of flavonoids compared to the total polyphenols in M. arboreus was about 46%.
 

## General aspect and behavior of the rats during the UL induction phase
In the model group, the rats became increasingly agitated or active, while some showed irritability/aggressive behaviors over time. The induction of UL was also associated with decreased appetite and hair loss, the remaining hair being slightly yellow. From the 4th week of induction, some animals experienced writhing or abdominal pain (contraction of the abdominal muscles accompanied by extension of the forelimbs and elongation of the body). Many of these effects were diminished with the administration of the extract and mifepristone.

## Evolution of body weight, relative weight of some organs and ovarian follicles
Compared with the normal control group (NG1), the animals of the model group (MG1) displayed a slow increase in body weight ( ), and this effect became significant (P < 0.05) 7 weeks after the beginning of the induction. Treatment of animals with the extract and mifepristone for 30 days was not able to overcome this slow increase in body weight caused by the induction of UL.
 The relative weight of certain organs is shown in . The induction of UL did not affect the relative weight of liver and lungs, but resulted in a non-significant increment in the relative weight of kidneys (31.28%) and adrenal glands (30.26%). This increase in adrenal weight persisted for 30 days (38.63%) post-induction. A delayed increase (i.e. not observed at the end of the induction period, but rather during the treatment period) (P < 0.05) in brain weight was also observed. Ovarian weight was significantly lower (P < 0.01) in the model group 30 days after the end of the UL induction. Following the 30-day treatment period, the extract dose of 100 mg/kg reduced, albeit non-significant, the adrenal gland weight similar to that of the control NG2 subgroup, whereas mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg) increased it more (P < 0.05). In contrast to the extract, mifepristone reduced the increase in relative brain weight observed in the model animals. Regarding ovaries, despite an upward trend, the weight of the organ remained very low after treatment with M. arboreus extract at the doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg compared to the NG2 subgroup. At the dose of 200 mg/kg, a significant (P < 0.01) increase was observed, but the value remained lower than that of the NG2 subgroup.
 Compared with the normal subgroups (NG1 and NG2), the model subgroups (MG1 and MG2) showed a significant increase in atretic follicles, and a significant decrease in Graafian follicles and corpora lutea ( ). Compared to the MG2 subgroup, M. arboreus extract significantly reduced the number of atretic follicles at all tested doses, but only the 50 mg/kg dose increased the number of Graafian follicles and corpora lutea. Although not significant, the number of corpora lutea increased 5.5-fold and 3.0-fold in model animals treated with the extract at the doses of 50 and 200 mg/kg, respectively.

## Morphological and histological changes in the uterus
The uteri of rats in the normal subgroup were uniform in appearance (bilaterally symmetric or Y-shaped) without swelling or nodules ( ). In contrast, the horns of uteri in the model subgroup were asymmetrical in length (L-shaped), shorter, with a hard texture, and nodules/swelling. Treatment of the UL model rats with M. arboreus stem bark decoction, mainly at doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg, improved many of these features with a tendency toward normal.
 Compared to the NG1 group, a significant increase in uterine wet weight (P < 0.01) and diameter (P < 0.05) was observed in the model group MG1 ( ). Thirty days after the cessation of the UL induction, uterine weight was still significantly (P < 0.01) elevated in the model group (MG2) as compared to the normal group (NG2), while uterine diameter showed an increase, although non-significant, of 52.94%. The 30-day oral administration of the M. arboreus decoction at all tested doses decreased the uterine wet weight and diameter, the variation being significant at 50 and 100 mg/kg, respectively. Mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg) induced a significant decrease in the uterine wet weight (P < 0.05) and a non-significant decrease (33.65% reduction) of the uterine diameter as compared to the MG2 group.
A significant increase (P < 0.05) in myometrial thickness was observed in the model group after induction of UL compared to the normal group ( ). The value of this parameter remained elevated in the model group 30 days after the end of the induction. After 30 days of oral treatment, the aqueous extract of M. arboreus (at 50 and 100 mg/kg) and mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg) decreased the myometrial thickness, bringing the value back to that of the normal control.
Compared to the NG1 group, repeated injection of E2B and progesterone significantly (P < 0.0001) increased the myometrial collagen deposition in the model group (MG1) ( ). Collagen density was still elevated 30 days after the end of UL induction. Compared to the model group (MG2), the myometrium of animals treated with the M. arboreus stem bark decoction at 100 and 200 mg/kg showed significantly lower collagen densities (P < 0.05). The same effect was observed with the treatment with mifepristone (P < 0.001).
In contrast to the control rats (CG1 and CG2), the myometrium of the model animals (MG1 and MG2) was enlarged and elongated (hypertrophy and/or hyperplasia) ( ). The treatment with M. arboreus decoction, especially at the dose of 100 mg/kg, improved these histological features with an almost intact myometrium.

## Uterine levels of pro-inflammatory, angiogenic and oxidative stress biomarkers
Administration of E2B and progesterone for 60 days induced a significant increase in the uterine levels of TNF-α (P < 0.01) and TGF-β1 (P < 0.05) ( ). Thirty days after the end of the induction period, the uterine levels of these two pro-inflammatory cytokines were significantly higher than that of the normal group NG2. The 30-day treatment with the extract of M. arboreus significantly reduced the uterine levels of TNF-α and TGF-β1 in dose-dependent manner.
 A non-significant and delayed increase of 17.50% was observed in the uterine IL-6 levels ( ). In contrast to mifepristone, M. arboreus decoction was not able to decrease the uterine IL-6 levels compared to the MG2 group.
The induction of UL led to a delayed increase (P < 0.01) in the uterine levels of the pro-angiogenic cytokine VEGF ( ). The treatment with extract at 100 and 200 mg/kg and mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg/day) significantly (P < 0.01) decreased this parameter as compared to MG2 group.
As depicted in , the 60 days of E2B and progesterone treatment significantly (P < 0.01) increased the uterine levels of the pro-oxidant marker malondialdehyde (MDA). This increase remained significant (P < 0.001) 30 days after the cessation of UL induction. Following a 30-day oral treatment with M. arboreus extract, apart from the significant (P < 0.05; 33.33%) decrease in MDA observed at the dose of 50 mg/kg, the levels of this parameter remained globally elevated. Mifepristone (5.2 mg/kg) significantly (P < 0.001) thwarted the increase in uterine MDA content observed in the model animals.
Regarding the antioxidant biomarkers, uterine catalase activity was significantly lower in the model groups at the end of UL induction (P < 0.01) and 30 days post-induction (P < 0.001) compared to normal rats ( ). There was no change in the uterine GSH content at the end of UL induction, but a delayed decrease was observed 30 days’ post-induction. Compared to the MG2 group, administration of M. arboreus extract induced a significant (P < 0.05) increase in the catalase activity at all doses tested, and in GSH content at only 100 mg/kg (P < 0.05).

## Serum levels of estradiol and progesterone
Compared to the NG1 group, the model animals depicted higher serum levels of estradiol (P < 0.01) and progesterone (P < 0.05) at the end of the UL induction period ( ). However, 30 days post-induction, estradiol levels were still elevated while progesterone levels returned to NG2 normal levels. Treatment with the extract for 30 days induced a decrease in estradiol levels by 40.91% (P < 0.05) and 33.28% (P > 0.05) at the doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg, respectively.
 

## Discussion
In the present study, a multi-point intramuscular injection of E2B and progesterone was used to induce the development of UL in female Wistar rats. The decoction of the stem bark extract of M. arboreus P. Beauv. was then administered daily to the animals in order to evaluate its anti-UL potential.
The vast and diverse pharmacological effects of medicinal plants are fundamentally dependent on their secondary metabolite profiles. These phytoconstituents are very often classified into three major classes, including terpenoids, alkaloids and phenols ( ). Quantitative analysis of the decoction of stem bark extract of M. arboreus revealed significant amounts of flavonoids/polyphenols and terpenoids, while alkaloids are present in small amounts. Chronic inflammation, hypoxia-related oxidative stress, cell proliferation, angiogenesis and fibrosis are the fundamental characteristics of uterine fibroids. Today, a large body of preclinical and clinical evidence indicates the potential of flavonoids/polyphenols to prevent or treat various tumors ( , , , , , , ) through their potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic properties ( , , , ). The anti-fibrotic effects of plant polyphenols/flavonoids have been increasingly studied and demonstrated ( , ). Likewise, natural terpenoids ( , , , , , ) and alkaloids ( , ) are also well-known for their anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-tumor and anti-fibrotic effects. Accordingly, the presence of flavonoids, terpenoids and alkaloids in the decoction of the stem bark extract of M. arboreus suggests an anti-UL potential of the extract through the anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-angiogenic and anti-fibrotic properties of its secondary metabolites.
Body weight change is an important index for assessing the toxicity or impact of a xenobiotic on a living organism. In our study, repeated administration of E2B and progesterone led to a slow increase in body weight. Numerous studies in both humans and rodents have shown that estrogens reduce appetite (food intake), body weight gain and adiposity, while increasing the physical activity and energy expenditure ( , , , ). By activating ERα on pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)-producing neurons in the arcuate nuclei and on steroidogenic factor 1 (SF1)-expressing neurons in the ventromedial hypothalamus, estrogens induce leptin-like metabolic function and/or increase sensitivity to leptin by inhibiting food intake and increasing energy expenditure ( , , ). Data on progesterone are conflicting. However, showed that progesterone attenuated body weight gain in ovarian intact female mice. More recently, demonstrated that progesterone promotes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and increases cellular energy production and expenditure. All this information could explain, at least in part, the slow increase in body weight observed in the model group as compared to the normal animals. Although administration of the M. arboreus decoction for 30 consecutive days reduced the restlessness and irritability/aggressiveness of the animals, this was not enough to overcome the low body weight observed in the model group.
Like the body weight, relative organ weight changes are widely accepted as a sensitive indicator of chemically induced organ damage. For systemic toxicity assessments, organs such as the liver, kidneys, lungs, brain, adrenal glands and heart are commonly recommended ( ). Induction of UL resulted in a transient increment in the relative kidney weight, while that of the adrenal glands persisted for 30 days after the end of the induction. In addition, a delayed increase in the relative brain weight was observed. In rodents, estrogens activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis by increasing the gene expression in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, the expression of an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) precursor called POMC in the anterior pituitary or by directly acting on the adrenal cortex ( , ). These mechanisms lead to the overstimulation and growth of the adrenal cortex, and finally to an increase in the adrenal weight. The extract of M. arboreus at 100 mg/kg decreased the adrenal weight, suggesting either an inhibition of HPA axis activity (production of CRH and ACTH) or a direct effect on the adrenal cortex via specific receptors and/or by interfering with adrenocortical enzymes ( ). Treatment of model animals with mifepristone further increased the adrenal weight, probably through its anti-glucocorticoid effect ( ). By blocking glucocorticoid receptors, mifepristone increases ACTH concentrations, and thus leading to the overstimulation and hypertrophy/hyperplasia of the adrenal gland. The induction of UL was also associated with an increase in the relative brain weight. Indeed, estrogens and progesterone play a key role in brain growth/development, maturation and plasticity by increasing neurite outgrowth, spinogenesis and synaptogenesis, dendritic branching and myelination ( , , , , ). In contrast, the aqueous extract of M. arboreus was not able to decrease the brain weight.
UL is primarily characterized by myometrial cell proliferation and excessive accumulation of ECM ( ), resulting in a myometrial thickening and an increase in the uterine weight and diameter. In the present study, the model animals displayed increased myometrial thickness, uterine diameter and uterine weight, suggesting the establishment of UL in these animals. The increase in uterine weight in rats is the consequence of two ERα-dependent events including fluid imbibition and/or cell proliferation ( ). While estrogen is considered to be the major mitogenic factor of the uterus, data on the role of progesterone in the growth and development of the UL have often been conflicting. However, an increasing number of studies have reported an essential role of progesterone in the pathogenesis of UL ( , , ). The clinical use of selective progesterone receptor modulators (SPRMs) as an effective treatment for UL supports this hypothesis ( , , ). Estradiol promotes myometrial cell proliferation, and by increasing the expression of progesterone receptors, increases the responsiveness of the tissue to progesterone. Progesterone, in turn, increases both the number of fibroid cells and the size of fibroid tumors by increasing the expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) ( ). Progesterone may also promote the myometrial and UL growth by increasing the cellular energy required for proliferation ( ). All this information suggests that the hyper-responsiveness of the myometrium to progesterone is required for the complete development and proliferation of UL cells ( , ). By stimulating hyperplasia and hypertrophy of myometrial cells, estrogen and progesterone promote myometrial and UL growth ( ). Therefore, myometrial thickness can be considered a good indicator of the growth and development of the UL. In this study, the uteri of model animals displayed an elongated and enlarged smooth muscle, suggesting the hypertrophy and hyperplasia of myometrial cells. By reducing the myometrial thickness, the decoction of M. arboreus probably inhibited the myometrial growth and/or excessive deposition of ECM. Based on the Masson’s trichrome staining of the uterus, the model group showed an excessive deposition of collagen, the most abundant fibrous protein and a large component of UL ( , ). This result suggests the development of fibrosis in the model group. The 30-day treatment with the decoction of M. arboreus at the dose of 100 and 200 mg/kg reduced the myometrial collagen content, suggesting the anti-fibrotic effect of the extract.
An inflammatory environment is a prerequisite and fuel for virtually all tumors, including UL ( , ). By increasing the local production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, estrogen and progesterone stimulate uterine growth, which can ultimately lead to UL ( , , , ). In this study, increased levels of TNF-α, IL-6, TGF-β1 and VEGF were observed following the 60-day administration of high levels of E2B and progesterone. Elevated levels of TGF-β levels are known to stimulate the transformation of myometrial stem cells and fibroblasts into myofibroblasts ( ). These become resistant to apoptosis in dysregulated processes, such as chronic inflammation, and produce excessive and abnormal ECM under the stimulation of TNF-α and activin A, leading to fibrosis ( , ). In line with this, the decreased levels of TNF-α, TGF-β1 and IL-6 observed in the uterus of the model animals after the 30-day oral treatment with M. arboreus decoction at the doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg could be responsible for the aforementioned anti-fibrotic effect of the extract. As almost all solid tumors grow and proliferate rapidly, many of their cells end up far from the functional blood vessels, with limited oxygen and nutrient supply. In order to continue to develop and grow, hypoxic tumors upregulate their expression of VEGF to promote the formation of new capillaries from the existing vessels (angiogenesis). In this study, increased levels of VEGF were observed in the uteri of model rats, suggesting the stimulation of angiogenesis. The oral administration of the decoction of M. arboreus at the doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg reduced the uterine VEGF levels, indicating the anti-angiogenic effects of the extract.
UL are also characterized by hypoxia-related oxidative stress and impaired antioxidant cellular enzyme systems ( , ). In this study, the induction of UL increased the uterine levels of MDA, while decreasing the catalase activity and GSH content. These results indicate a severe oxidative stress and failure of antioxidant defense mechanisms. MDA is a marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage ( ), while catalase by dismutating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) protects cells ( ). Glutathione peroxidase catalyzes the reduction of H2O2 and lipid hydroperoxides at the expense of reduced glutathione ( ). Treatment of the model animals with M. arboreus extract increased the catalase activity at all tested doses, suggesting its capacity to reduce H2O2. However, only the dose of 50 mg/kg reduced lipid peroxidation through a moderate decrease in MDA. The consistent low levels of GSH observed at 50 and 200 mg/kg suggests that it is greatly used to reduce H2O2 and not enough recycled from oxidized glutathione. The consistent low levels of GSH observed at 50 and 200 mg/kg suggests that the antioxidant reserves are still being used or that there is a problem with the synthesis or regeneration of GSH. Accordingly, at 50 mg/kg, there is a positive trend in reducing the oxidative damage (as seen by the decrease in MDA), but the decrease in GSH highlights a potential vulnerability in antioxidant defenses. At 100 mg/kg, the oxidative stress is still present (elevated MDA), but the body may be responding by increasing the GSH and catalase activity.
In this study, adult female rats were exposed to supraphysiological levels of estradiol and progesterone to induce UL. Such levels of estradiol negatively feedback the secretion of FSH and LH, maintain FSH serum levels below the FSH-window, and thus promote multi-follicular atresia through granulosa cell apoptosis and autophagy ( , , ). In other words, follicles that do not receive sufficient FSH support at a critical stage of development are condemned to atretic degeneration ( , ). Enhanced follicular atresia is generally associated with a reduction in the number of Graafian follicles and corpora lutea counts after prolonged treatment with estradiol ( ), as observed in this study. Such variations are usually linked to ovarian atrophy, leading to a reduction in estradiol biosynthesis ( , ). However, high levels of estradiol were observed in model group 30 days after the end of UL induction, suggesting an extra-gonadal source of estradiol. Studies have reported local production of estrogens in fibroid tissue through increased aromatization of androgens by inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 ( , , ). However, these hormones act and are metabolized locally and cannot explain the elevated serum levels of estradiol. A hypothetical explanation could be that a small amount of the injected E2B is still present in the body. Numerous semisynthetic estrogens, such as E2B, estradiol valerate and estradiol cypionate, can confer a long duration of action ( ). The half-life of E2B in the oil-based preparation for intramuscular injection is usually between 2 and 5 days. indicated that plasma estradiol returned to physiological concentrations 5 days after a single i.m. injection of 2.5 mg of E2B to llamas of ∼100 kg. Accordingly, supraphysiological concentrations of estradiol-17β can be achieved and persist for long periods through the regular administration of E2B. In other words, the prolonged administration of E2B probably induced a kind of ‘bioconcentration’ of the hormone, greatly extending its half-life. In model animals treated with the decoction of M. arboreus at the doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg, serum estradiol levels diminished by 40.91 and 33.28%, respectively, to levels similar to those of normal animals. This effect suggests the ability of the extract to promote the elimination of estradiol, a factor contributing to the pathogenesis of UL.
The present study has some limitations. The small number of animals per group limits the statistical robustness and reliability of the results. The key proliferation markers, such as Ki-67 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), were not quantified in the myometrium in order to determine its degree of proliferation. The UL rat model used does not perfectly mimic the human disease features, rendering difficult the translation and applicability of findings. Thus, more preclinical and even clinical studies with more reliable UL models are required to further validate the reported anti-UL potential of M. arboreus.

## Conclusion
The knowledge and valorization of the African ethnobotanical heritage, particularly Cameroonian one, represents a promising opportunity in the field of anti-UL pharmacology. The present study provided in vivo evidence that M. arboreus stem bark decoction has beneficial effects on UL. It acts by reducing the serum estradiol levels, myometrial growth and fibrosis, uterine levels of pro-inflammatory (TNF-α and TGF-β1) and pro-angiogenic (VEGF) cytokines.
