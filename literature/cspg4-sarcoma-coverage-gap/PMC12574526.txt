First, the production process of CAR-T cells is intricate, time-intensive, and costly. The prohibitive expense restricts patient access to this treatment, thereby reducing its availability. In addition, the rapid progression of tumors often leads to a swift decline in the patient’s condition, which may preclude the possibility of receiving CAR-T therapy. Various nanomedicines, including micelles, nanoparticles (NPs), lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), polymeric hydrogels, and protein-based conjugates, have been extensively studied for the delivery of biological therapeutics and diagnostic agents in cancer treatment and yielded remarkable results in preclinical and clinical studies.[ ] Some researchers have explored the use of nano-delivery systems to facilitate the preparation of CAR-T cells and enhance their efficacy, yielding promising results to date. In the subsequent sections, we will provide an overview of the current state of CAR-T cell research, with particular emphasis on the existing challenges. We will then discuss recent advancements in nanomedicine that address these challenges. A summary of innovative nano-based platforms for CAR gene transduction will be presented, including delivery systems using lipids and polymers, which have demonstrated significant potential for clinical translation. Furthermore, strategies aimed at enhancing efficacy and reducing adverse reactions will be examined, with a focus on novel treatment modalities based on nanomaterials [Figure ]. In addition to highlighting the achievements of nanotechnology in advancing CAR-T therapy, we will also address the substantial challenges that remain and propose corresponding countermeasures.
 

## Limitations and Challenges of CAR-T Cell Therapy
Despite its notable success, CAR-T cell therapy continues to encounter substantial technical and biological challenges that constrain its clinical application. Among the technical obstacles are low transduction efficiency, which results in insufficient CAR expression on T cells, safety concerns related to viral vectors due to the risk of insertional mutagenesis, and the complexity of immunogenic responses to CAR vector components or the CAR construct itself, which can further undermine efficacy and safety. The biological challenges include limited infiltration of CAR-T cells into tumor sites, immunosuppressive TMEs that impair T-cell function, and therapy-associated toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity. Overcoming these challenges is crucial for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy and enhancing its safety and efficacy in the treatment of malignancies [Figure ].
 

## Low transduction efficiency
CAR-T cell therapy, an innovative cancer treatment, depends heavily on efficient gene delivery to modify T cells for targeting cancer. These delivery methods are divided into viral and non-viral techniques, each with distinct benefits and challenges affecting CAR-T cell production and use. Achieving high transduction efficiency in T cells represents a significant challenge in the context of CAR gene delivery. Non-viral methods, including electroporation and transposon systems such as Sleeping Beauty and PiggyBac, frequently yield suboptimal gene transfer rates. This inefficiency results in a reduced proportion of CAR-expressing T cells, thereby compromising the overall therapeutic efficacy. For instance, clinical studies using the Sleeping Beauty transposon system have reported variable transduction efficiencies, with one study reporting an efficiency of 28.8% ± 12.3%.[ ] This inefficiency leads to insufficient expression of CARs on the surface of T cells, which can diminish the therapeutic efficacy of the engineered cells. However, we noted that a recent study reported that PDA/DEX-PEI@HA nanoparticles incorporating hyaluronic acid (HA) and dextran (DEX) significantly enhance the transfection efficiency of the Sleeping Beauty transposon compared to nanoparticles lacking these components, suggesting their potential as an effective gene delivery strategy for CAR-T cell therapy.[ ]

## Safety challenges of viral vector-mediated gene delivery
The application of viral vectors, specifically lentiviruses and gamma-retroviruses, has played a crucial role in the advancement of CAR-T cell therapy, attributable to their high transduction efficiency and capacity for stable transgene integration into the host genome. However, this integration poses significant safety risks associated with insertional mutagenesis, where the insertion of viral DNA can disrupt or activate host genes, potentially leading to oncogenesis. Investigations into lentiviral integration sites indicate that lentiviral vectors may pose a reduced risk of insertional mutagenesis compared to gamma-retroviral vectors, owing to differences in their integration signatures. Notable examples include gene therapy trials for X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID-X1), where patients treated with gamma-retroviral vectors developed leukemia due to activation of the LMO2 proto-oncogene at the integration site.[ ] This case highlights the potential risk associated with the random integration of viral vectors, which may inadvertently disrupt tumor suppressor genes or activate oncogenes, thereby facilitating malignant transformations. Consequently, safety concerns with viral vectors are a major hurdle for clinical application, necessitating safer vector designs and stringent biosafety evaluations to mitigate insertional mutagenesis risks.

## Immunogenic responses
Immunogenic responses to vector components or the CAR construct pose major challenges in CAR-T cell therapy, as patients may develop immune reactions that neutralize viral vectors or destroy CAR-modified T cells, diminishing treatment efficacy. Pre-existing antibodies can also inactivate viral vectors, such as adenoviruses and lentiviruses, further limiting their effectiveness. In addition, murine-derived single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) used in CAR constructs can trigger human anti-mouse antibody (HAMA) responses, leading to rapid clearance of CAR-T cells from circulation. Wagner et al[ ] have reported that both pre-existing and treatment-induced immune responses to murine-derived CAR constructs, especially scFvs, can lead to rapid clearance of CAR-T cells, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Concurrently, patients receiving CAR-T cells generated by viral vectors may elicit humoral immune responses, leading to neutralization of the vector and diminished persistence of CAR-T cells. A recent study found that blocking interferon responses increased lentiviral vector transduction efficiency and improved CAR-T cell functionality by promoting naive-like differentiation, suggesting strategies to enhance CAR-T cell persistence and reduce immunogenic responses to viral vectors.[ ] Immunogenicity can cause adverse effects like hypersensitivity reactions and CRS.

## CAR-T cell trafficking and tumor infiltration
The immunosuppressive TME and physical barriers such as the tumor stroma, hinder the movement and accessibility of CAR-T cells. To surmount these obstacles, the implementation of non-systemic delivery pathways is required. In preclinical studies, the administration of CAR-T cells via intraventricular injection, specifically targeting GD2 and IL13Ra2, has improved glioblastoma treatment.[ , ] In a groundbreaking trial, three patients with recurrent glioblastoma were treated with intraventricular CARv3-TEAM-E T cells targeting EGFRvIII and wild-type EGFR. The treatment was well-tolerated, with no severe adverse events above grade 3 or dose-limiting toxicities.[ ] These findings have catalyzed the initiation of three clinical trials investigating intraventricular delivery of CAR-T cells for glioblastoma (NCT02208362, NCT03389230) and recurrent brain/leptomeningeal metastases (NCT03696030).[ ] In a parallel vein, preclinical studies on malignant pleural mesothelioma showed that intrapleural CAR-T cell injections greatly improved outcomes, prompting the launch of a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT02414269).[ ] Although the strategy of localized administration theoretically exhibits increased therapeutic efficacy, its application is predominantly limited to isolated tumor lesions or instances of oligometastatic disease.

## Immunosuppressive microenvironment
The TME is characterized by the presence of immunosuppressive cell populations, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). These cells infiltrate solid tumors and secrete a variety of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, thereby establishing a milieu that facilitates tumor progression and promotes tumorigenesis. Blaeschke et al[ ] created TIM-3-CD28 fusion proteins to transform inhibitory signals into T-cell activation. When combined with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells, these fusions enhanced proliferation, activation, and cytotoxicity. The modified CAR-T cells showed improved effectiveness and safety, as they only increased cytokine production in the presence of the CD19 antigen. Recent CAR-T cell therapy research aims to boost efficacy and persistence by strategically modulating cytokine and chemokine characteristics. For example, co-expression of IL-15 and IL-21 within the CAR structure was found to boost the proliferation, persistence, and survival of CAR-T cells.[ ] Our study further elucidated the role of IL-15 in augmenting the long-term targeted cytotoxic capabilities of CAR-T cells within the CAR construct.[ ] Chemokine receptors have been genetically engineered into CAR-T cells to enhance their targeting precision and effectiveness against tumors. This enhancement supports findings that altering CCL2 gradients in the TME can boost CAR-T cells’ targeting and antitumor effectiveness.[ ] Moreover, immune checkpoint pathways, such as those mediated by PD-1 and CTLA-4, further diminish the immune system’s ability to target and eliminate tumor cells. A major obstacle to the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy is the inadequate proliferation and limited persistence of T cells within the host, often exacerbated by T cell exhaustion due to the activation of these inhibitory pathways. Notably, in the treatment of hematologic malignancies, this combinatorial approach are associated with improved maintenance of CAR-T cells and enhanced patient prognoses, particularly among a cohort of pediatric patients with advanced B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), indicating significant advancements in the management of resistant tumor types.[ ] Another study using CRISPR-Cas9 to knockout CTLA-4 showed enhanced survival and tumor clearance of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells in refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL).[ ] Recently, Dacek et al[ ] investigated engineering CAR-T cells to secrete a high-affinity CD47-blocker, CV1, at tumor sites, boosting CAR-T cell effectiveness. This approach seeks to address the limitations of each monotherapy by reversing immunosuppression and reducing systemic toxicities.
The TME has a unique metabolic profile that significantly dampens immune responses, characterized by nutrient and oxygen scarcity and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts. Cancer cells consume large amounts of glutamine due to its crucial role in nucleotide and amino acid synthesis, whereas low glucose levels in the TME decrease immune cell activation and increase their death rates.[ ] Low glucose levels trigger the Warburg effect, causing an overproduction of lactic acid—a byproduct of glycolysis—that increases the acidity of the TME, reduces pH, impairs immune cell functionality, and contributes to immunosuppression.[ ] Sun’s investigation into the application of oxamate, an inhibitor of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), demonstrated that the reduction of lactate production leads to decreased concentrations of immunosuppressive agents, including adenosine. This modulation subsequently enhanced the efficacy of CAR-T cells by transforming the immunosuppressive TME.[ ] Hypoxia hinders the activation of effector cells like CD8+ T cells, reducing their survival and cytokine output, while promoting the growth of suppressive immune cells such as Tregs in the TME.[ ] A study developed a hypoxia-inducible chimeric antigen receptor (HiCAR) with a hypoxia response element to boost CAR expression in low-oxygen conditions, aiming to minimize off-target effects and enhance CAR-T cell activation in tumors, thus improving the selectivity and effectiveness of CAR-T therapy.[ ] Zhu et al[ ] also engineered hypoxia-responsive CAR-T cells using hypoxia-responsive elements, optimizing the 5H1P-CEA CAR structure to activate in low-oxygen conditions. This enhances cell function, metabolism, and proliferation, reducing hypoxia’s adverse effects. These cells showed less exhaustion, better phenotypes, and more sustained antitumor activity compared to traditional CAR-T cells.

## CAR-T cell-associated toxicities
Significant toxicities have hindered CAR-T cell therapy’s progress, with some patients experiencing severe effects like CRS due to high systemic cytokine levels and intense immune activation.[ ] In addition, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) can occur in CAR-T therapy, characterized by severe hyperinflammation, elevated serum ferritin, hemophagocytosis, and diminished NK cell activity.[ ] Another serious toxicity is immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), identified by high cytokine concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid and disruption of the blood–brain barrier.[ ] Mechanistically, CRS happens when CAR-T cells are overly activated, causing a surge of cytokines. Activated macrophages release IL-6, worsening inflammation and creating a feedback loop that attracts more immune cells, sustaining the cytokine storm. Among patients undergoing CAR-T cell therapy, 77–93% of leukemia patients and 37–93% of lymphoma patients experienced CRS. Notably, 46% of relapsed/refractory B-ALL patients treated with tisagenlecleucel and 13–18% of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma patients treated with axicabtagene or tisagenlecleucel developed grade 3 or higher CRS. CRS is mainly driven by IL-6, which is managed by blocking the IL-6 receptor with tocilizumab and administering corticosteroids.[ ] Although tocilizumab was approved by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for severe CRS and fatalities still transpire.[ ] Notably, HLH/MAS after CAR-T cell therapy often does not respond to IL-6 blockers and may require chemotherapy. Neurotoxicity is common in CAR-T cell therapy, with corticosteroids being the primary treatment, as IL-6 inhibitors are usually ineffective.[ ] At present, there are few therapies officially sanctioned for the prevention of these toxicities, highlighting the critical need for advancements in CAR engineering and the implementation of supplementary strategies to diminish the toxicities associated with CAR therapies. In the following sections, we review strategies that integrate nanomaterial design into CAR-T cell therapy to alleviate toxicity.

## Nanotechnology Boosting CAR-T Therapy
Nano-delivery systems are advanced platforms designed to transport therapeutic agents like drugs and genes at the nanoscale (10–200 nm). They use the unique properties of NPs to enhance drug solubility, stability, bioavailability, and targeted delivery. These systems include various carriers such as liposomes, polymeric NPs, dendrimers, and inorganic NPs like gold or silica. By encapsulating or attaching therapeutic agents to these nanocarriers, they can overcome biological barriers, resist degradation, and provide controlled release, improving treatment efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity. Nano-delivery systems have significant potential to transform drug delivery in fields like oncology, infectious diseases, regenerative medicine, and personalized therapeutics.[ ]

## Nano-based CAR transfection

## Polymeric NP-based nanosystems
Nano-based gene delivery systems represent a cutting-edge approach in the field of genetic engineering and medicine, using NPs as vehicles to transport genetic materials into cells. As part of non-viral gene delivery systems, they offer advantages over viral vectors, including lower toxicity, reduced immune response, and greater gene payload capacity. Nano-based gene delivery systems have several strengths.[ ] (1) Modularity and versatility: Nano-based systems are highly modular. Using fundamental components such as lipid or polymer backbones, a diverse array of delivery vehicles with varying parameters can be engineered through alterations in their chemical composition or assembly methods. By adjusting particle size, zeta potential, and surface ligands, NPs are capable of targeting and penetrating nearly all tissues and cells. Furthermore, nano-based systems impose no limitations on the size and structure of gene cargos. In addition to plasmid DNA, mini-circle DNA, and mRNA can be stably loaded onto nano-vectors and efficiently transfected.[ ] Moreover, the nano-based systems can carry multiple genes or combinations of genes and drugs simultaneously, facilitating intelligently designed combination therapies.[ ] (2) Safety: The main advantage of NPs over viral vectors is their superior safety, with significantly lower risks of immunogenicity and insertional mutagenesis.[ ] (3) Applicability to mRNA delivery: The past decades have witnessed the dramatic development of mRNA therapeutics. mRNA transfection introduces mRNA into cells for direct protein synthesis in the cytoplasm, bypassing DNA delivery and transcription. This method allows for temporary expression, reducing long-term risks.[ ] (4) Easier to scale up: NPs are easier to standardize due to simple chemical synthesis and assembly, and their stability across various temperatures simplifies logistics and storage. Unlike viral vectors, which need strict low-temperature storage, NPs reduce costs and ease distribution, especially in areas lacking cold chain infrastructure. This scalability enhances the accessibility and clinical application of CAR-T cell therapies. By far, a variety of nano-based gene delivery systems have been developed for CAR transfection. The most commonly used NPs are lipid- and polymer-based, with poly(beta-amino esters) (PBAEs) being the leading polymer for gene delivery. PBAEs aid endosomal escape by protonating in acidic conditions, creating osmotic pressure that disrupts the endosome. High-throughput screenings show that incorporating tertiary amines improves buffering at low pH and enhances endosomal escape.[ ] Additionally, the ester linkages within the structural backbone of PBAEs are susceptible to hydrolysis in aqueous environments, rendering PBAEs less toxic than other cationic polymers, such as polyethyleneimine (PEI).[ ]
Researchers have fabricated a series of NPs targeting T cells for the generation of CAR-T cells. They engineered synthetic DNA nanocarriers for the in situ programming of CD19 CAR-T cells for the treatment of leukemia.[ ] The NP featured a composite structure, incorporating plasmid DNA that encodes an all-murine 194-1BBz CAR alongside plasmid DNA encoding the hyperactive iPB7 transposase, all encapsulated within PBAE polymer 447. The polymer chains were conjugated with microtubule-associated sequences and nuclear localization signals (MTAS-NLS) peptides, facilitating the efficient transport of genetic material into the nucleus via the microtubule transport system. In addition, polyglutamic acid (PGA) conjugated with anti-CD3e f(ab’)2 fragments formed the outer membrane of the NP, which is responsible for targeting T lymphocytes and promoting receptor-mediated endocytosis. Following injection, NPs specifically targeted T cells, facilitating the efficient integration of 194-1BBz CAR genes into the genome with the assistance of iPB7. The NP-programmed CAR-T cells demonstrated significant proliferation and expansion, exhibiting a memory phenotype characterized by a high level of CAR expression (ranging from 5.8% to 7.1%). In immunocompetent mice with leukemia, the CAR-T cells programmed via DNA nano-complexes demonstrated remarkable antitumor efficacy, successfully eradicating leukemia in 7 out of 10 mice. This outcome was comparable to that achieved by conventionally generated CAR-T cells.[ ] This work’s implications are significant: in situ CAR-T cell programming eliminates the need for extracting patient cells, speeding up treatment and removing the need for chemotherapy preconditioning, thus lowering infection risks. It also preserves T cell viability and functionality by editing them in their natural environment.[ ]
This research group has further advanced the development of multiple mRNA nano-delivery platforms using PBAE polymer and PGA.[ , ] These nanomaterials are assembled into nano-complexes through a two-step, charge-driven self-assembly process. Positively charged PBAE polymer condensed the gene cargos to form the inner core and protected them from enzymatic degradation. In contrast to DNA, mRNAs exhibit a higher susceptibility to degradation and a propensity to activate Toll-like receptors, thereby initiating inflammatory responses. To mitigate the immunogenicity of mRNAs, researchers have used nucleoside modifications by replacing naturally occurring nucleosides with modified analogs. For instance, the substitution of uridine and cytidine with synthetic bases such as pseudouridine and 5-methylcytidine has been shown to synergistically obstruct recognition by innate pattern recognition receptors and enhance mRNA translation efficiency.[ , ] Ligand-conjugated PGA creates a negatively charged outer layer that extends circulation time, while the ligand—typically antibodies or their fragments—facilitates specific binding to target cells and enhances internalization. The mRNA delivery NPs were incorporated into the production process of lentivirus-transfected chimeric CAR-T cells. Initially, the extracted T cells were stimulated using CD3/CD28 beads and subsequently incubated with gene-editing mRNA-loaded NPs. This was followed by transduction with a lentiviral vector encoding the CAR. An example is NPs containing mRNA for the Foxo13A variant. Foxo1 is crucial for regulating CD8+ T cell phenotype and anti-tumor functions, promoting key transcription factors like TCF-1 for memory T cell development. It also enhances a memory-like metabolic profile with increased fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis. Culturing with Foxo13A nano-complex during ex vivo expansion yielded more CD62L+ central memory T cells (TCM), resulting in CAR-T cells with improved tumor-killing capacity.[ ]
Building on prior research, Stephan’s team advanced the development of nanoplatforms for the in vivo delivery of in vitro-transcribed (IVT) mRNA, facilitating transient expression of target genes in circulating T cells. The architecture of the mRNA nanocarrier mirrors that of the previously described nanocomplexes used for DNA delivery. The synthetic mRNA is condensed by PBAE polymer to form the inner core, while ligand-conjugated PGA constitutes the outer layer. Through formulation optimization, PBAE/PGA-anti-CD8 NPs with a moderate size of 106.9 ± 7.2 nm, a high encapsulation efficiency of 90.9% ± 6.2% and PBAE:mRNA ratio of 60:1 were achieved. The IVT mRNA-loaded NPs induced a high, yet transient, expression of CAR, peaking at 75% ± 11% on day 2 and gradually declining to 28% ± 6% by day 28. In vivo, leukemia-bearing NSG mice were treated weekly for six weeks with 1928z CAR mRNA-loaded nanoparticles (50 μg/dose). The positive control group received a single dose of 5 ×106 conventionally manufactured CAR-T cells, which eradicated tumors in 6 out of 10 mice and extended survival by an average of 32 days. The IVT mRNA NP-edited CAR-T cells eradicated tumors in 7 out of 10 mice, with an average survival extension of 37 days, showing comparable or better results than conventional methods.[ ] To assess the in situ production of CAR-T cells using mRNA nanodrugs in solid tumors, this method was also tested in mouse models of prostate cancer and hepatitis B-induced liver cancer. Results demonstrated that periodic IVT mRNA NP administration effectively generated ROR1-targeting CAR-T cells and HBcore18-27 TCR+ T cells, showing tumor-killing efficacy similar to those produced by viral vectors.

## Lipid-based nanosystems
LNPs are nano-delivery systems distinguished by dynamic ionization state, which is responsive to pH changes and plays a crucial role in their interaction with biological membranes and subsequent cellular uptake. Typically, the formulation of LNPs comprises four fundamental components: (1) Ionizable lipids, which form the core of LNPs, contain a tertiary amine structure that can undergo protonation in acidic environments. At physiological pH, these lipids remain neutral; however, within the acidic microenvironment of endosomes, they acquire a positive charge, thereby facilitating endosomal escape. (2) Phospholipids, which contribute to the stability and fluidity of the nanoparticle membrane. (3) Cholesterol, which is integrated into the lipid matrix, enhances the structural integrity of the LNP, modulating its rigidity and promoting fusion with cellular membranes. (4) Polyethylene glycolated (PEGlated) lipids, or polyethylene glycol-conjugated lipids, are incorporated into the outer shell of LNPs, forming a hydrophilic corona that prevents opsonization and extends circulation time in the bloodstream.[ , ] A primary advantage of LNPs is their exceptional safety profile, which significantly enhances their potential for clinical application. The U.S. FDA has approved several LNP products, including the widely used COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, Comirnaty (BNT162b2), and Spikevax (mRNA-1273). Another notable advantage of LNPs is their versatility. By adjusting the components and their respective proportions, along with implementing surface modifications, LNPs can be precisely engineered to target and efficiently deliver mRNA to a wide range of tissues and cell types, including lymphocytes. Furthermore, the enhancement of biomaterials endows LNPs with advanced properties, including the ability to enhance immune responses. Consequently, LNPs represent optimal instruments for CAR-T cell engineering, and significant advancements have been achieved in this domain.
Research has been focused on enhancing LNP composition and properties to boost specificity, efficacy, and safety, thus broadening their use in CAR-T therapies. Mitchell’s and June’s groups have made significant contributions in this area. Billingsley et al[ ] conducted an evaluation of an extensive library of LNP formulations for the ex vivo delivery of mRNA to T cells. This library included 24 distinct ionizable lipids, which were formulated in predetermined ratios with cholesterol, phospholipid, and PEG-lipid. These components were combined using a microfluidic system with mRNA to generate various LNP configurations. Initial screening identified seven LNP formulations that outperformed lipofectamine in enhancing mRNA delivery into Jurkat cells. Notably, the formulation designated as C14−4 demonstrated superior transfection efficiency and minimal cytotoxicity. Further optimization of C14−4 LNPs for primary T cell transfection indicated that LNPs composed of a purified, saturated ionizable lipid significantly enhanced mRNA delivery compared to those prepared with a crude C14−4 lipid mixture. The optimized C14−4 LNPs were used to encapsulate CAR mRNA, successfully producing functional CAR-T cells. Compared to electroporation-based mRNA delivery, C14−4 LNPs exhibited reduced toxicity with comparable levels of CAR expression. In co-culture assays with ALL cells, CAR-T cells engineered with C14−4 LNPs demonstrated substantial anti-cancer efficacy, comparable to those engineered through electroporation and viral vector methodologies. In addition to the individual components, the molar ratio at which these components are combined significantly influences transfection efficiency and cytotoxicity.[ , ] Consequently, researchers used the orthogonal design of experiments (DOEs) methodology to identify optimized C14–4 LNPs with varying excipient molar ratios. Through meticulous screening, the B10 formulation, characterized by molar ratios of C14–4, cholesterol, phospholipid, and lipid-anchored PEG at 40:30:25:25, was ultimately identified as the superior formulation.[ ] The CAR-T cells produced using B10 LNP exhibited comparable CAR expression and tumoricidal efficacy to those generated via electroporation. However, the cytotoxicity toward T cells was significantly reduced. Furthermore, this study indicated that higher ratios of 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine(DOPE), lower ratios of cholesterol, and moderate ratios of C14−4 and PEG were advantageous for the effective delivery of mRNAs to T cells.
An additional advantage of LNPs is their capacity to concurrently encapsulate two distinct types of nucleic acids.[ ] Hamilton et al[ ] developed an LNP platform designed for the co-delivery of mRNA and siRNA, which they used in the production of super CAR-T cells, with controllable PD-1 inhibition to counteract immunosuppression. The aforementioned C14–4 core was modified with 14-carbon alkyl epoxide tails to create the ionizable lipid C14–494. DOPE and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) were added as helper lipids. CAR mRNA and PD-1-targeting siRNA were combined with these lipids to form LNPs using microfluidics. These LNPs showed strong CAR expression (71%) and moderate PD-1 knockdown in activated human T cells. Notably, LNPs containing both mRNA and siRNA enhanced CAR expression more than mRNA-only LNPs, indicating a synergistic effect between the mRNA and siRNA.
As previously discussed, the in situ editing of CAR-T cells represents a significant avenue with substantial potential for clinical translation. Extensive exploratory research has been conducted on the in vivo delivery of CAR mRNA to T cells. Nevertheless, the issue of off-organ accumulation persists as a significant challenge.[ ] One promising strategy involves the development of LNPs with extrahepatic tropism, which facilitates the targeted delivery of mRNA specifically to T cells while reducing off-target effects in the liver. This approach leverages the concept of selective organ targeting (SORT), which has demonstrated the ability to enhance the specificity of mRNA delivery to various tissues.[ ] Billingsley et al[ ] developed LNP platforms for in situ CAR-T cell engineering by attaching antibodies targeting T cells to LNPs. They used maleimide-functionalized PEG in B10 LNPs to create maleimide-functionalized LNPs (mal-LNPs), which were linked to antibodies through thiol-maleimide interactions. To prevent the formation of specific CAR-T cell subsets, which may hinder anti-tumor functions, only antibodies against pan-T cell markers (CD3, CD5, and CD8) were tested. CD3-LNPs and CD7-LNPs successfully transfected CAR mRNA in circulating T cells, leading to significant B cell depletion, with CD3-LNPs showing the highest efficacy in CAR-T cell engineering.

## Nanomedicine in enhancing CAR-T efficacies
CAR-T therapy has been effective for leukemia and certain lymphoma subtypes, but its efficacy in solid and semi-liquid tumors remains limited. Nanomedicines enhance the efficacy of CAR-T therapy in several ways: (1) Effective infiltration and retention of CAR-T cells within the tumor tissues are critical prerequisites for successful tumor eradication. Bulky tumors frequently present physical barriers, characterized by a dense extracellular matrix and aberrant vasculature, which impede CAR-T cell penetration and restrict their access to all tumor regions, particularly the hypoxic core, resulting in incomplete tumor eradication. Consequently, it is imperative to reduce tumor burden and disrupt these barriers before the administration of CAR-T therapy. Nano-based photothermal therapy (PTT) and chemotherapy have demonstrated efficacy in addressing these challenges.[ , ] (2) Large tumors often have low oxygen and nutrient levels due to poor blood supply. PTT enhances local blood flow in tumors, improving oxygen and nutrient supply, which supports CAR-T cell proliferation and activation. (3) The hostile TME is characterized by the presence of numerous immunosuppressive cells, cytokines, and immune checkpoint molecules that diminish the functionality of CAR-T cells and promote exhaustion. Certain cytokines and immune stimulators possess the potential to counteract the detrimental effects of the TME and mitigate immune suppression. However, systemic administration of these agents may provoke unintended immune activation in other tissues and organs, leading to immune-related adverse events. Nano-based drug delivery systems offer a promising approach to selectively target immune-enhancing agents in tumor tissues, thereby optimizing the efficacy of CAR-T cells while minimizing associated side effects.[ , ]

## Nano-delivery systems for CAR-T therapy
As previously discussed, nano-based systems facilitate precise and targeted delivery of immune enhancers into the TME to enhance T cell function. Recently, researchers have innovatively used CAR-T cells as delivery vehicles, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to the simple combination of NPs loaded with immune boosters and CAR-T cells.
Activated T cells show a significant increase in free thiols and surface redox activity upon recognizing tumor antigens and interacting with antigen-presenting cells (APCs). The Irvine group used this response to create a surface-conjugated nanogel (NG) that reacts to the elevated reduction potential in T cells. The protein cargo, IL-15 super-agonist complex (IL-15Sa), was assembled using a reversible cross-linker (NHS-SS-NHS) that cleaved in response to reducing activity on T cell surfaces, releasing IL-15Sa at the tumor site. In addition, anti-CD45 antibodies were conjugated to NGs to securely anchor them to CAR-T cell surfaces, preventing unwanted internalization. This carefully designed NG system enhanced the cell surface half-life and accurately delivered IL-15Sa to tumors, resulting in a 16-fold expansion of T cells without toxicity.[ ] Luo et al[ ] engineered a nanochaperone based on human serum albumin (HSA) for the delivery of IL-12. This nanosystem integrated two essential functional moieties: Dibenzocyclooctyl (DBCO) groups enabled the attachment of the nanochaperone to azide-labeled CAR-T cells through a click-chemistry reaction, while intermolecular disulfide bonds conferred redox-responsiveness. CAR-T cells, equipped with these immunostimulant backpacks, demonstrated preferential trafficking to tumor sites. Upon encountering tumor antigens, the activated CAR-T biohybrids generated reductive thiols on their surface, which triggered the release of IL-12 from the nanochaperone. IL-12 not only enhanced the proliferation and activation of CAR-T cells but also recruited additional exogenous CAR-T cells, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies the immune response. High adenosine levels in the TME stimulate tumor growth by activating A2a receptors on T cells, which suppress their function. The A2a receptor antagonist SCH-58261 can restore T cell activity and enhance antitumor responses by blocking adenosine’s immunosuppressive effects, but its use is limited due to severe side effects.[ ] To address this issue, Siriwon et al[ ] developed cross-linked multilamellar liposomes (cMLVs) for the encapsulation of SCH-58261. These cMLVs were subsequently conjugated to CAR-T cells through the reaction of maleimide groups with thiols on the surface of T cells. The drug-loaded cMLVs, in conjunction with the CAR-T cells, effectively penetrated the deep tumor parenchyma, thereby significantly enhancing T cell functionality.

## Photothermal-remodeling CAR-T therapy
PTT is a minimally invasive treatment that uses photothermal agents to convert light into heat, creating localized hyperthermia to selectively destroy cancer cells while sparing healthy tissue. This approach is predicated on the principle that malignant cells exhibit increased susceptibility to elevated temperatures compared to normal cells.[ ] The photothermal agent is fundamental to this technique and can be broadly classified based on composition into organic, inorganic, and hybrid materials. Organic photothermal agents encompass compounds such as indocyanine green (ICG), IR780, some of which have received approval for clinical application. Inorganic agents, including gold NPs (e.g., nanorods, nanoshells, and nanocages), iron NPs (e.g., Prussian blue NPs, Fe3O4 NPs), and other metal-based nanostructures, are highly esteemed for their efficient photothermal conversion capabilities and adjustable optical properties. Upon irradiation with a near-infrared (NIR) laser, these photothermal agents experience electron excitation followed by non-radiative relaxation processes, which convert absorbed light energy into thermal energy. This heating effect induces denaturation and coagulation of cellular proteins, disrupts cell membranes, and ultimately leads to apoptosis or necrosis of the targeted cells.
PTT not only effectively eliminates tumors but also possesses the potential to synergize with CAR-T therapy. This approach significantly reduces tumor burden and disrupts the extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating the infiltration of CAR-T cells. The elevation of temperature leads to vasodilation, enhances blood circulation, and mitigates hypoxia and low pH levels, thereby reprogramming the TME from a hostile to a more favorable state. Furthermore, the necrosis and apoptosis of tumor cells result in the release of antigens and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which further promote the infiltration of CAR-T cells and other immune cells, effectively transforming cold tumors into hot tumors. In addition, NPs are capable of co-delivering photothermal agents and immune-potentiating drugs as a whole, with the agents released exclusively at the local tumor site upon NIR laser irradiation, minimizing systemic toxicities.[ ]
Chen et al[ ] developed a nanophotosensitizer-engineered CAR-T biohybrid system (CT-INPs), which comprises DBCO-modified ICG-loaded NPs and metabolically labeled N3-CAR-T cells. The bioorthogonal reaction facilitates the robust attachment of INPs to N3-CAR-T cells. In vitro experiments demonstrated that mild photothermal treatment at 45°C did not impair the viability or cytotoxic activity of CAR-T cells, whereas the viability of lymphoma cells was significantly reduced. This finding suggested a temperature window conducive to the synergistic application of PTT and CAR-T therapy. In the combination treatment group, mice received intravenous injections of CT-INPs, followed by irradiation with a NIR laser (808 nm, 0.5 W) for 15 minutes after a 6-hour interval. This treatment regimen exhibited significant anti-tumor efficacy, with four out of five mice remaining alive without recurrence during the observation period. The group also showed enhanced recruitment and infiltration of CAR-T cells and other innate immune cells, including monocytes and NK cells. This effect was attributed to the disruption of physical barriers and elevated levels of chemotactic chemokines and antitumor cytokines. Shi et al[ ] developed a multifunctional nanoplatform, designated as FA-Gd-GERTs@Ibrutinib, which is capable of executing multimodal imaging as well as chemotherapy, photothermal, and immunotherapy. This nanoplatform was composed of a gold-enriched rare-earth (GERT) core enveloped by an external mesoporous silica layer, with ibrutinib encapsulated and folic acid (FA) conjugated as a targeting ligand. By using an FA-mediated targeting strategy, the nanoplatform facilitates prolonged and precise multimodal imaging, specifically computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS). Notably, the high sensitivity and specificity of SERS imaging enable real-time monitoring of tumor, allowing clinicians to make timely and accurate assessments. The in vivo anti-tumor efficacy was notably promising; mice treated with FA-Gd-GERTs@Ibrutinib in conjunction with NIR laser irradiation and CD19 CAR-T cells remained macroscopically free of cancer at the conclusion of the study. Chen et al[ ] developed ICG-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs for photothermal ablation in melanoma, subsequently infusing CAR-T cells that target chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan-4 (CSPG4). The combination therapy demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to other treatment modalities. PTT reprogrammed the TME through several mechanisms, including the disruption of physical barriers, the improvement of local oxygen supply, and the elevation of inflammatory factors. This resulted in an increased accumulation of monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and CAR-T cells, ultimately leading to a more potent anti-tumor effect.
Despite its many benefits, PTT still faces numerous challenges that limit its clinical use. The main issue is the limited light penetration, especially in the NIR region, as tissues absorb and scatter light, restricting treatment to a few centimeters deep. This poses problems for deep tumors, causing uneven heat distribution and incomplete ablation, which may lead to recurrence.[ , ] Moreover, excessive local heating can harm nearby healthy tissues.[ ] Elevated levels of heat shock proteins (HSPs), such as HSP90 and HSP70, also raise concerns including promoting thermotolerance, preventing tumor cell apoptosis, aiding tumor recovery, and enhancing immunosuppression.[ ] Fortunately, pharmacological inhibitors of HSPs, like 17-allylamino-17-demethoxygeldanamycin (17-AAG), can counteract these proteins’ protective roles in tumor cells, making them more vulnerable to thermal damage. Penisuloxazin A (PNSA), a novel C-terminal HSP90 inhibitor, not only suppresses tumor progression processes such as metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition but also enhances the infiltration and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells.[ ] Researchers have developed NPs co-delivering photothermal agents and HSP inhibitors to reduce the influence of HSPs and enhance therapeutic outcomes. For instance, Tian et al[ ] designed a multifunctional nanoplatform by encapsulating PB within mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles (MONs), incorporating gambogic acid (GA), an HSP90 inhibitor, and coating it with thermo-sensitive lauric acid (LA). This innovative nanoplatform induced apoptosis in tumor cells, inhibited HSP90 production, enhanced the Fenton reaction, and facilitated dual-modal magnetic resonance/photoacoustic imaging. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that the combination of HSP inhibitors and PTT may effectively mitigate the adverse TME, thereby potentially improving the efficacy of CAR-T therapies.

## Implantable scaffolds for CAR-T therapy
Effective retention of CAR-T cells in TME is also crucial for their antitumor activity. However, the TME’s high interstitial pressure complicates the retention of systemically administered CAR-T cells. Localized cytokine concentrations are vital for CAR-T cell expansion and cancer cell elimination, but maintaining these levels is challenging due to cytokines’ low molecular weight and rapid clearance.[ ] Implantable scaffolds offer a promising solution for delivering CAR-T cells and cytokines locally.
Hydrogels, the most common implantable scaffolds, are 3D, water-absorbing polymer networks that mimic the extracellular matrix. Their porous structure supports nutrient and gas diffusion. With high water content, biocompatibility, and soft tissue-like properties, hydrogels are widely studied for medical uses like drug delivery, tissue engineering, and wound management.[ ] Thermo-responsive hydrogels are a class of stimuli-responsive polymers characterized by their ability to undergo reversible phase transitions in response to temperature fluctuations. These materials exhibit a distinctive behavior known as the lower critical solution temperature (LCST), at which the hydrogel transitions between hydrophilic (water-attracting) and hydrophobic (water-repelling) states. Below the LCST, the hydrogel remains in a swollen, hydrophilic state, enabling it to absorb substantial quantities of water and encapsulate therapeutic agents within its matrix. Conversely, above the LCST, the hydrogel experiences a phase transition, resulting in water expulsion and the formation of an in situ gel. For clinical applications, the LCST is typically engineered to be near physiological temperature, facilitating the injection of the hydrogel as liquid at lower temperatures, followed by its transformation into semisolid gel form within human body.
The Appel Group has developed a series of biomedical hydrogels. In their prior research, the dynamic polymer mesh within polymer-nanoparticle (PNP) hydrogels exhibited the ability to sustain prolonged protein retention and promote immune cell infiltration.[ ] Building on these findings, Grosskopf et al[ ] developed a PNP-based hydrogel that co-encapsulates CAR-T cells and IL-15, greatly boosting local T cell expansion. Dodecyl-modified hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC-C12) and NPs composed of arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD)-modified poly (ethylene glycol)-b-poly(lactic acid) (RGD-PEG-PLA NPs) were used to synthesize a series PNP hydrogels. Upon mixing the two solutions, dynamic multivalent and entropy-driven interactions among the components facilitated physical cross-linking, resulting in the formation of a robust hydrogel matrix. The PNP hydrogel exhibited a dynamic mesh structure that inhibited the passive diffusion of IL-15 while permitting the migration of CAR-T cells. In the hydrogel environment with high IL-15 levels, CAR-T cells proliferated significantly, increasing 100-fold compared to controls. Researchers observed beneficial phenotypic changes for tumor eradication, such as enhanced CAR expression, increased CD8+ and stem cell memory subsets. In a subcutaneous medulloblastoma model, the IL-15 and B7H3 CAR-T cell-loaded PNP hydrogel effectively eliminated both local and distant tumors.[ ] The promising results suggest this immune-boosting hydrogel could be a new treatment for metastatic and inoperable tumors. Postoperative inflammation is a significant factor in tumor recurrence and metastasis. In response to this challenge, Hu et al[ ] developed a HA-based multifunctional hydrogel. This hydrogel encapsulated CAR-T cells targeting CSPG4, IL-15 loaded PLGA NPs, and checkpoint inhibitors conjugated to platelets. In a melanoma-bearing mouse model, the implanted HA hydrogel served as an immunostimulant reservoir within the tumor resection cavity, facilitating CAR-T cell proliferation with the aid of IL-15. Moreover, postoperative inflammation activated platelets, leading to the formation of platelet-derived microparticles (PMPs) and the release of anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (aPD-L1) antibodies. By inhibiting PD-L1 on tumor cells with aPD-L1, the suppression of CAR-T cells was alleviated, thereby enhancing their tumor-killing efficacy. This hydrogel system achieved excellent therapeutic outcome: Mice treated with the CAR-T-P-aPD-L1@gel exhibited minimal tumor residues at week 3. Luo et al[ ] developed a sophisticated hydrogel system known as the injectable immune-microchip (i-G/MC). This alginate-based hydrogel encapsulated IL-15, HEMOXCell (a marine extracellular hemoglobin designed for oxygen transport), and CAR-T cells. Upon injection into the tumor, the hydrogel’s outer layer degrades rapidly, resulting in the release of HEMOXCell, which alleviates hypoxic conditions by providing oxygen. Concurrently, IL-15 promotes T cell proliferation and memory formation. This innovative gel system concurrently reprograms the TME and augments T cell proliferation and function, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy.
The lymph node-biomimetic scaffold constitutes a sophisticated and novel approach to augment the effectiveness of CAR-T cell therapy. Inspired by the physiological mechanisms of lymphocyte priming, activation, and tolerance occurring within lymph nodes, Gu’s research team has ingeniously engineered versatile lymph node-biomimetic scaffolds. Shi et al[ ] used patient-derived freeze-dried lymph nodes as a medium for loading CAR-T cells. The structural integrity of the lymph node meshwork, along with the cytokine and chemokine profiles, was well-maintained, thereby effectively enhancing the viability and functionality of the CAR-T cells. In comparison to hydrogels containing T-cell-supporting cytokines, the freeze-dried lymph nodes conferred a more robust tumor-killing capacity to the CAR-T cells. Liao et al[ ] engineered a PLGA scaffold using a microfluidic technique to simulate the lymph node microenvironment scaffold was modified with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies to emulate T-cell stimulatory and co-stimulatory signals, in conjunction with cytokines such as IL-7 and IL-15 to deliver essential activation signals from APCs. The biomimetic scaffold, designed to resemble an artificial lymph node, significantly enhanced CAR-T cell proliferation both ex vivo and in vivo. Notably, the CAR-T cells maintained robust cytotoxic activity following a 50-fold expansion. Furthermore, the scaffold facilitated sustained CAR-T cell activation and proliferation within tumor sites for up to 30 days, presenting a promising approach for effectively delaying tumor progression.
While implantable scaffolds offer many benefits for improving CAR-T-cell functionality, some issues remain unresolved. It is uncertain if hydrogel degradation products negatively impact CAR-T-cell viability and phenotype. In addition, different hydrogel administration methods, like direct injection vs. application to surgical incisions, might affect internal tissue tension and, consequently, T-cell activation and functionality. Therefore, thorough experimental research is still needed to address these concerns.

## Nanomedicine for alleviation of the adverse effects
As discussed above, current treatments fall short of addressing adverse effects associated with CAR-T therapy, creating significant unmet medical needs. Optimization of the cytotoxic potential of CAR-T cells, while simultaneously mitigating toxic side effects is the key challenge in this field. Recent advancements have seen researchers propose novel strategies using nanomaterials to address these issues. Despite the current paucity of research in this area, the existing studies exhibit highly innovative designs, advanced methodologies, and notable efficacy, suggesting considerable promise for clinical translation.
IL-6 antibody (siltuximab) and IL-6 receptor antibody (tocilizumab) are the main treatments for CRS, but they have drawbacks. They require frequent administration due to their short half-life, and tocilizumab may worsen neurotoxicity by increasing IL-6 levels in the central nervous system (CNS). In addition, CRS’s unpredictable onset often leads to these treatments being given only after symptoms appear. To tackle this intractable problem, Li et al[ ] used a temperature-sensitive poly (N-isopropylacrylamide-co-methacrylic acid) (P[NIPAAm-co-MAA]) hydrogel to develop an IL-6 absorbing nanosponge (IL6S). IL-6 antibodies were chemically conjugated to the P(NIPAAm-co-MAA) framework. The microstructure of the IL6S was designed with minimal spacing between adjacent antibodies, facilitating the dynamic capture of IL-6 molecules within a restricted area and thereby preventing their escape from the hydrogel matrix. The thermally responsive hydrogel can be administered subcutaneously before CAR-T cell infusion, without altering IL-6 levels under physiological conditions. As IL-6 concentrations increase in the bloodstream, the nanosponge effectively absorbs IL-6 molecules in real-time, thereby reducing IL-6 levels. IL6S effectively reduced CRS in both immunodeficient mice and those with human hematopoietic stem cell engraftment, closely mimicking clinical scenarios. It delayed CRS onset, decreased inflammatory markers, alleviated hypotension, and significantly improved survival compared to free IL-6 antibodies. In addition, IL6S can be easily removed via syringe after a cooling-induced gel-to-sol transition, minimizing tissue damage.
Following the onset of CRS, a multitude of cytokines are involved in mediating inflammatory responses. It is hypothesized that the simultaneous blockade or removal of these cytokines may mitigate the cytokine storm. In a study by Jiang et al,[ ] a biomimetic nanosystem was developed by encapsulating PLGA nano-core with macrophage membrane. This system retains receptors from the “parental” macrophages, including IL6R, TNFR1, and CCL2 to neutralize cytokines associated with CRS. The study evaluated membranes from three distinct macrophage phenotypes (M0, M1, and M2) and found that NPs camouflaged with M0 macrophage membranes demonstrated superior efficacy in neutralizing pro-inflammatory cytokines and significantly reduced organ damage in a murine model.
An increasing number of studies have shown that cytokines from macrophages and monocytes are key drivers of CRS and ICANS. However, depleting monocytes prevents CRS but also reduces CAR-T cell effectiveness, highlighting their dual role in CAR-T therapy. Research indicates that monocyte hyperactivation during CRS results from harmful interactions with CAR-T cells, as T cell surface proteins like CD40 ligand, CD69, and TNF-α can activate these immune cells. Thus, minimizing CAR-T cell interactions with macrophages and monocytes may help mitigate CRS.[ ] Motivated by this concept, Gong et al[ ] developed PEGylated CAR-T cells using a click chemistry technique. Azide groups were introduced to the CAR-T cell surface by incorporating azido glycans into the culture medium. The CAR-T cells not only targeted and lysed tumor cells but also activated monocytes, leading to CRS. Upon the onset of CRS symptoms, high doses (10 and 50 mg/kg) of DBCO-PEG600K were administered intravenously to mice. DBCO-PEG600K bound to the N3 groups on the CAR-T cell surface, forming a PEG polymeric barrier that inhibits direct cellular interactions. This modification mitigated the rapid proliferation and hyperactivation of both CAR-T cells and monocytes, thereby alleviating the manifestations of CRS. Subsequently, minute tumor antigens, primarily tumor-cell lysates, traversed this barrier, leading to progressive activation and proliferation of CAR-T cells. Over time, the PEG600K barrier on the surface of CAR-T cells diminished, gradually restoring intercellular communication. Interactions between CAR-T cells and lymphoma cells were reestablished more rapidly than those between CAR-T cells and monocytes, due to the relatively smaller size of B-cell lymphoma cells. This differential restoration created a therapeutic window during which tumor cell destruction occurred without inducing monocyte hyperactivation. Although this process delayed complete tumor eradication, all lymphoma cells were ultimately eliminated by day 35 following CAR-T cell infusion. Notably, mice treated with CAR-T-azide cells and DBCO-PEG600K exhibited no signs of neurological syndrome.

## Challenges and Future Perspective
Combination of nanomaterials with CAR-T cell therapies holds great promise but also faces numerous challenges. Beyond the limitations discussed in each section earlier, we identify additional hurdles in integrating nanomedicine with CAR-T therapy: (1) Research indicates that the immune system may identify NPs or their components as foreign, potentially triggering complex immune responses. It is uncertain if this could result in a cytokine storm or other immune-related issues. If nanomaterial-induced inflammation worsens the CRS caused by CAR-T therapy, the outcomes could be catastrophic. Long-term safety concerns about using nanomaterials with CAR-T cell therapies are largely unaddressed. Comprehensive data must be collected before starting clinical trials to mitigate potential risks. Safety investigations should use animal models that closely resemble actual diseases, like patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, but relevant research in this area is currently lacking. (2) Challenges in clinical translation: Few nanomaterials have FDA approval for clinical use, with many complex polymers and metal nanoparticles still unapproved, highlighting the gap in translating these materials into therapeutic strategies. (3) Holdbacks of industrialization: Unlike viral transfection systems for CAR-T cells, transfection efficiency, T cell toxicity, and immune response potential of nano-systems have only been validated in preclinical studies, with insufficient experience in large-scale production and quality control.
Despite the significant challenges, we remain optimistic about the strategy of enhancing CAR-T therapy through the use of nanomaterials for the following reasons: (1) mRNA-LNP systems have demonstrated excellent safety and efficacy in COVID-19 and influenza vaccines, as well as in treating rare genetic diseases. Clinical data support their potential for integration with CAR-T therapy. The validated safety and compelling therapeutic results highlight mRNA as a promising nanomaterial for enhancing CAR-T cell therapies. In addition, mRNA-LNP systems offer design flexibility and can be quickly adapted for personalized medicine by targeting various antigens. (2) Accumulation of clinical trial data: Numerous anticancer nanomedicines, including CPX351, have progressed to the clinical trial phase. A comprehensive analysis of clinical data from patients treated with both nanomedicines and immunotherapy could provide significant insights.[ ] (3) Advanced animal models: Improved animal models, like PDX and mice with reconstituted human hematopoietic systems, enhance research by better simulating the human body’s in vivo environment, aiding the understanding of nanomedicine interactions with tumors, CAR-T cells, and the immune system. (4) Artificial intelligence (AI): AI can speed up CAR-T cell therapy research by discovering new targets and materials, designing improved nano-systems for CAR transfection or enhancing CAR-T efficacy, predicting interactions with nanomaterials, and analyzing vast amounts of research literature to uncover emerging trends and insights. Clinically, AI can analyze data to identify patients who will benefit most from CAR-T therapy and predict treatment responses based on genetic and clinical profiles.[ ] In summary, although integrating nanomaterials with CAR-T cell therapy poses substantial obstacles—particularly regarding safety, clinical translation, industrialization, and economic feasibility, promising advancements in novel materials and supporting technologies offer a hopeful pathway to overcoming these challenges.

## Conclusion
CAR-T therapy has fundamentally transformed the field of tumor immunotherapy, offering a novel and promising therapeutic approach. Nonetheless, its implementation encounters several challenges, such as the limitations associated with viral transfection systems, insufficient infiltration and retention within tumors, reduced efficacy due to the complex and immunosuppressive TME, and severe adverse effects. Recent advancements in nanotechnology have shown considerable promise in both preclinical research and clinical trials, and its integration with CAR-T therapy has been actively investigated. Notably, nano-based systems are crucial at every stage of CAR-T therapy. Nano-based transfection systems can efficiently and safely modify CAR-T cells and facilitate in situ editing. LNP systems offer numerous advantages in the preparation of CAR-T cells, with relevant clinical trials currently in progress. Additionally, meticulously designed NPs, PTT, and implantable scaffolds enhance the overall efficacy of CAR-T therapies through various mechanisms, including reprogramming the hostile TME, promoting CAR-T cell infiltration and retention, and improving cell viability and tumor-killing capabilities through the targeted delivery of immunostimulatory agents. Despite progress, a substantial gap remains before the integration of CAR-T cell therapy and nanomedicine reaches the clinical stage, highlighting the necessity for extensive research. Nonetheless, we are confident that the swift advancements in materials science, bioinformatics, immunology, and AI will enhance our understanding of the interactions between CAR-T cells and nano-based systems. This enhanced understanding is expected to drive the development of more advanced combination therapies, ultimately yielding significant clinical benefits for cancer patients.
