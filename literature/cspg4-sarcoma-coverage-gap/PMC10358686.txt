## Abstract
Cynops orientalis (C. orientalis) has a pronounced ability to regenerate its spinal cord after injury. Thus, exploring the molecular mechanism of this process could provide new approaches for promoting mammalian spinal cord regeneration. In this study, we established a model of spinal cord thoracic transection injury in C. orientalis, which is an endemic species in China. We performed RNA sequencing of the contused axolotl spinal cord at two early time points after spinal cord injury – during the very acute stage (4 days) and the subacute stage (7 days) – and identified differentially expressed genes; additionally, we performed Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses, at each time point. Transcriptome sequencing showed that 13,059 genes were differentially expressed during C. orientalis spinal cord regeneration compared with uninjured animals, among which 4273 were continuously down-regulated and 1564 were continuously up-regulated. Down-regulated genes were most enriched in the Gene Ontology term “multicellular organismal process” and in the ribosome pathway at 10 days following spinal cord injury. We found that multiple genes associated with energy metabolism were down-regulated and multiple genes associated with the lysosome were up-regulated after spinal cord injury, indicating the importance of low metabolic activity during wound healing. Immune response-associated pathways were activated during the early acute phase (4 days), while the expression of extracellular matrix proteins such as glycosaminoglycan and collagen, as well as tight junction proteins, was lower at 10 days post-spinal cord injury than 4 days post-spinal cord injury. However, compared with 4 days post-injury, at 10 days post-injury neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions were no longer down-regulated, up-regulated differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathways associated with cancer and the cell cycle, and SHH, VIM, and Sox2 were prominently up-regulated. Immunofluorescence staining showed that glial fibrillary acidic protein was up-regulated in axolotl ependymoglial cells after injury, similar to what is observed in mammalian astrocytes after spinal cord injury, even though axolotls do not form a glial scar during regeneration. We suggest that low intracellular energy production could slow the rapid amplification of ependymoglial cells, thereby inhibiting reactive gliosis, at early stages after spinal cord injury. Extracellular matrix degradation slows cellular responses, represses the expression of neurogenic genes, and reactivates a transcriptional program similar to that of embryonic neuroepithelial cells. These ependymoglial cells act as neural stem cells: they migrate and proliferate to repair the lesion and then differentiate to replace lost glial cells and neurons. This provides the regenerative microenvironment that allows axon growth after injury.

## Introduction
Some urodele amphibians, like salamanders, can completely regenerate their spinal cord (SC) (Enos et al., 2019), providing a unique paradigm for studying regeneration in vertebrates. SC regeneration can repair the central nervous system (CNS), regenerate axons and neurons, and restore sensory and motor functions (Chen et al., 2022). In mammals with spinal cord injury (SCI), glial cells multiply within the injured area and create a glial scar, which inhibits SC regeneration (Okada et al., 2018). However, this phenomenon does not occur during SC regeneration in salamanders. SC repair in salamanders is mediated by ependymal cells, which differentiate from progenitor cell pools formed in the initial developmental stage. Different progenitor cells express specific combinations of transcription factors, such as oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2/NK6 homeobox 1, paired box 6/developing brain homeobox 2/NK6 homeobox 2, paired box 7/achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 1, and atonal bHLH transcription factor 1 (Wilson and Maden, 2005; Alaynick et al., 2011; Butler and Bronner, 2015). However, in mammals, this unique combination of dorsal and ventral transcription factors disappears during development. There is evidence that, following SCI in mice, a very small number of Nestin-positive cells in the dorsal horn being to express progenitor cell-related transcription factors such as paired box 6 and paired box 7 (Yamamoto et al., 2001; Nakamura et al., 2014), implying that SCI in mammals could potentially be treated by promoting the activity of this cell population at this stage of injury repair.
Studies of SCI in mammals have shown that ependymal cells are the only known stem cells that exhibit the ability to differentiate during SCI (Barnabé-Heider et al., 2010). In mice, ependymal cells can differentiate not only into astrocytes but also into neurons (Woźniak, 1999), and inhibiting astrocyte overactivation in mice can promote the repair of damaged spinal cords (Fan et al., 2018). In addition, glial cell proliferation leads to the formation of glial scars, which is the key to preventing SC regeneration after SCI; although it can prevent the spread of inflammation (Wanner et al., 2013; Stenudd et al., 2015), it ultimately leads to permanent loss of the SC’s ability to regenerate. However, in salamanders, ependymal cells maintain radial glial cell morphology, and astrocytes have not been shown to exist in salamanders (Messenger and Warner, 1989; Becker and Becker, 2015).
The cellular changes that occur during spinal cord regeneration in salamanders cannot be separated from the genes that are differentially expressed during this process. With the introduction of high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) technology, the mystery of salamander regeneration is gradually being unraveled. Stewart et al. (2013) reported that the SALL gene, as well as genes involved in angiogenesis, wound healing, defense/immunity, and bone development, are highly expressed during blastema formation and development, and they identified genes related to limb regeneration. Arenas Gómez et al. (2018) identified 602 DEGs that are associated with regeneration in Bolitoglossa ramosi. In addition to performing a salamander limb regeneration study, Walker et al. (2022) used RNA-seq to screen for DEGs involved in SC regeneration in newts (Ambystoma mexicanum). They took samples from uninjured animals and from animals 4 days after SCI, performed RNA-seq analysis, and identified 1163 genes that were differentially expressed in the injured animals compared with the uninjured animals. However, only studying DEGs at a single time point was insufficient to reveal the dynamic process of spinal cord regeneration in salamanders.
In addition, the salamander species most commonly used to study regeneration is Ambystoma mexicanum (Joven et al., 2019); however, this species has been bred specifically for laboratory use, such that many of its natural attributes may be obscured by artificially selected traits. In contrast, Cynops orientalis (C. orientalis; Chinese fire-bellied newt), which belongs to the Cynops family that is capable of central nervous system regeneration (Carducci et al., 2021), is widely distributed in the wild. Little is known about SCI in C. orientalis. Previous studies performed in salamanders have involved SCI in the tail (Walder et al., 2003; Monaghan et al., 2007; Walker et al., 2022). However, little SC tissue is present in the salamander tail compared with in the spine, and tail SCI does not accurately simulate most types of mammalian SCI, such as lower body paralysis.
In this study, we established a C. orientalis model of SCI at the level of the trunk vertebrae and investigated neuronal and transcriptomic changes related to SC regeneration at critical time points after injury.

## Methods

## Animals
A total of 102 C. orientalis salamanders (10 months old, male and female) were purchased from the Gold Coast Aquarium, Xiaojiuhua Commercial Street, Wuhu, Anhui Province, China. The salamanders were housed in an amphibious living environment in a glass aquarium maintained at a temperature of 22 ± 3°C with water at a pH of between 7.2 and 7.4 for 1 week. Housing conditions were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011). All animal care and handling procedures were conducted according to Chinese laws regulating the use of experimental animals, and the protocols were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Anhui Normal University on May 24, 2020 (approval No. 20200524002).

## Spinal cord injury models
SCI was induced by a modified marrow destruction method (Wang et al., 2022). In short, salamanders were anesthetized by immersion in 0.05% tricaine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) (pH 7.2–7.4). Skin and muscle were cut from the 7th to 9th (out of 14 segments in total) internodes of the trunk vertebrae to expose the spine. Then the dissection needle was inserted directly through the lamina to completely sever the SC. Afterward, the wound was rinsed with a sterile phosphate-buffered saline solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and closed with tissue glue. Finally, the animals were transferred to an amphibious environment containing dechlorinated water in a glass aquarium for recovery. The salamanders awakened about 15 minutes after surgery, and their movements were observed to confirm that the lower part of the body showed paralysis. Anesthesia was then administered again to take day 0 SC samples. In the Sham group, which served as the control group, the skin and muscle tissue between the 7th and 9th segments of the trunk vertebrae were cut, but the SC was not damaged.
The salamanders were randomly divided into three groups: Sham, 4-day, and 10-day (corresponding to 4 and 10 days after SCI). To confirm that the SC was completely transected, samples taken on day 0 days were subjected to modified Bielschowsky staining. To obtain enough RNA for RNA-seq analysis, 10 salamanders were included in each group, and RNA from all salamanders in each group was pooled for analysis. Tissue staining was performed on samples from one salamander from each group.

## Spinal cord preparation
The SC from segments 7 to 9 of the trunk vertebrae (approximately 0.5 cm in length) was isolated, and the SC fragments from all 10 animals in each group were placed in a single tube containing RNA storage solution. Then, the SC fragments were transferred to 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 24 hours, fully rinsed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China), decalcificated using ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) decalcifying solution (Servicebio) for 40 days, rinsed in running water for 2 hours followed by gradient alcohol dehydration (75%, 85%, 95%, 100%) (Wuxi Zhanwang Chemical Reagent CO., Ltd., Wuxi, Jiangsu, China), and cleared using xylene (Macklin, Shanghai, China).
Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sliced into coronal sections with a thickness of 5 μm for all experiments, except for nerve fiber staining, which was performed using 12-μm sagittal sections.

## Modified Bielschowsky staining
To evaluate and assess SC rupture and regeneration in the Sham, 4-day, and 10-day groups, modified Bielschowsky staining was performed. Sections were dewaxed using xylene, rehydrated in ethanol at varying concentrations, and then stained using a modified Bielschowsky staining solution (Leagene, Beijing, China). Finally, the sections were evaluated under an inverted microscope (Leica, Heidelberg, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). The same process was performed for samples taken at 0 days.

## Nissl staining
Nissl staining was performed to evaluate neuronal morphological alterations and survival. Sections were dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated in ethanol at varying concentrations, and then stained with Nissl staining solution (Boster, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The sections were observed under an inverted microscope (Leica). The cells that had discernible nuclei and normal neuronal shapes were counted. Image Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, Annapolis, MD, USA) was used to quantify the neurons (Jensen, 2013).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Reactive gliosis in the damaged SC was assessed using a glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) antibody. Paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated in ethanol at varying concentrations, and microwaved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. After being blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma) for 30 minutes and permeabilized with Triton X-100 for 12 minutes, the sections were incubated with the main GFAP antibody (mouse mAb, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA, USA, Cat# 3670, RRID: AB_561049) at 4°C overnight. Then, the sections were incubated for 1 hour with a secondary cyanine 3 (Cy3)-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200, Boster, Cat# BA1031) antibody at 25°C. Two drops of anti-fluorescence quenching agent (containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were added. Finally, an inverted fluorescent microscope (DMi8 and LAS X software, Leica) was used to view the sections mounted on the coverslips. Image Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics) was used to calculate the number of GFAP-positive cells. To assess the number and positional changes of mature neurons, an RNA binding fox-1 homolog 3 (NeuN) antibody was used. Immunofluorescence staining for NeuN (rabbit mAb, 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 36662) and with DAPI was carried out as described for GFAP, but the secondary antibody used was Cy3-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Boster, Cat# BA1032). The double-stained cells were observed and analyzed in the same way as cells stained for GFAP.

## RNA-seq
A Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to extract total RNA from damaged SC samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), the RNA quality was evaluated and verified using RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Total RNA was sequenced using a HiSeqTM 2500 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China). RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing, Unigene expression analysis, basic unigene annotation, trend analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis were performed. The unigene sequences were subjected to BLAST search against the Nr database, and the sequence that best aligned with each unigene was defined as the homologous sequence. The species to which the homologous sequence belonged was determined, and the number of homologous sequences in each species was calculated. If there was more than one homologous sequence, the first one was selected for further analysis. Next, we mapped all of the unigenes to the KOG database based on the annotation derived from the Nr protein database to predict potential gene functions and expression levels, as well as to clarify the macro-level characteristics of a species’ gene function distribution. Detailed methods are shown in Additional file 1.

## Validation of RNA-seq results
Using Trizol solution (Invitrogen), total RNA was isolated from the SC segments. Reverse transcription of mRNA was performed with MonScript™ RTIII Super Mix with dsDNase (Two-Step) (Monad, Lianyungang, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed with MonAmp™ ChemoHS qPCR Mix (Monad) on a LightCycler®96 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Five genes were randomly chosen for qRT-PCR analysis. The primer sequences are shown in (GENERAL BIOL, Chuzhou, Anhui, China). Using β-actin as the endogenous reference, the relative mRNA expression levels were evaluated using the 2–ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).
 

## Pathway analysis and differentially expressed gene screening
GO enrichment was classified according to cellular component, molecular function, and biological process, and then ranked by Q value from low to high to determine the top 20 enriched categories. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly enriched metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways, which were ranked by Q-value from lowest to highest to determine the top 20 enrichment positions. Then, the results from the trend analysis were further processed to identify genes that were consistently up-regulated or consistently down-regulated during recovery from SCI, and the top 20 DEGs were determined by calculating the mean value within groups and the standard deviation (STDEV) between groups. Finally, the pathway maps generated by GO and KEGG analysis were searched for in WikiPathways (Salomonis et al., 2022), and the resulting pathways were combined with the RNA-seq results to identify possible signaling pathways activated after SCI in salamanders, as well as genes whose expression changed dramatically after SCI.

## Statistical analysis
The GraphPad Prism 5 software program (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com) was used to conduct statistical analyses of the Nissl staining, immunofluorescence staining, and qPCR results, which are presented as mean ± the standard error of mean (SEM). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance followed by Student-Newman-Keuls test was used for comparisons among three groups. P-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. RNA-seq data analysis was performed using Omicsmart tools (https://www.omicsmart.com).

## Results

## Changes in nerve fibers during SC regeneration
Only skin nerves were damaged in the Sham group, while nerve fibers were damaged in the surgical group. The damaged nerve fibers were gradually repaired over time. At 4 days, although the hind limbs were still unable to be used for movement, there was a clear retraction of the broken nerve fiber ends. At 10 days, the damaged nerve fibers had regenerated, and there was a marked increase in the amount of connective tissue present at the injury site ( ).
 

## Changes in ependymal glial cells and mature neurons during SCI regeneration
SC was structurally intact in the Sham group, the number of neurons was low, and the neurons were mainly concentrated around the central canal. At 4 days, compared with the Sham group, the number of neurons in the meninges in the SCI group was markedly increased, and there was a marked increase in the number of neurons around the central canal. In addition, the number of neurons in the meninges in the SCI group was also increased markedly at 10 days compared with the Sham group ( and ).
 Based on the immunofluorescence staining results, there was clear increase in the number of GFAP-positive cells in the SC of salamanders at 4 days post-SCI compared with the Sham group. However, the number of GFAP-positive cells was markedly lower at 10 days than at 4 days ( and ). In addition, the NeuN signal was weak in the SC tissue of the Sham group, but a marked enhancement in NeuN staining in the meninges was seen at 4 days post-SCI, and by 10 days post-SCI, the number of NeuN-positive cells in the meninges was markedly increased compared with both the Sham group and the 4-day group ( and ).

## Basic functional annotation of unigenes expressed during SC regeneration
To determine the roles of all 152,247 unigene sequences, we exhaustively searched the Nr, Swiss-Prot, KOG, and KEGG databases. The Venn diagram in demonstrates how the total number of unique BLASTx hits from the Nr, Swiss-Prot, KEGG, and KOG databases equaled the total number of unique sequence-based annotations. In total, 56,118 (or 36.68%) of the unigenes were correctly tagged by at least one database. Out of the four datasets (from the Nr, Swiss-Prot, KOG, and KEGG databases), 20,657 unigenes (13.57%) were correctly annotated. The Nr database had specific matches for 55,426 (36.41%) unigenes, whereas the KEGG database had matches for 41,880 (27.51%) unigenes. Matches were found for 27,761 (18.23%) and 22,392 (14.71%) unigenes in the Swiss-Prot and KOG databases, respectively.
 Nr database homologous sequence results showed that the highest number of matches was for Rhinatrema bivittatum (5927 unigenes), followed by Microcaecilia unicolor (3691), Xenopus laevis (2745), and Homo sapiens (2314) ( ). In total, 29,192 unigenes were added to the KOG database and categorized into 25 different KOG categories ( ). The largest group of genes was categorized as being involved in general function (6751 unigenes (23.13%)), followed by signal transduction pathways (4169 unigenes (14.28%)) and posttranslational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones (2585 unigenes (8.86%)). Cell motility was the least represented group (84 unigenes (0.29%)).
 

## RNA-seq analysis of DEGs during SC regeneration in salamanders
RNA-seq was employed to compare DEGs between the SCI and Sham groups and investigate the probable mechanism of SC regeneration in salamanders. In all, 13,059 DEGs were found among the Sham, 4-day, and 10-day groups, including 4201 down-regulated genes and 8858 up-regulated genes, as illustrated in and . There were 6976, 11,458, and 1301 DEGs between the Sham and 4-day groups, between the Sham and 10-day groups, and between the 4-day and 10-day groups, respectively. Out of the 6976 DEGs identified between the Sham and 4-day groups, 3644 (52.2%) were up-regulated. The majority of the DEGs identified between the Sham and 10-day groups were down-regulated (59.7%)).
 

## Trend analysis of DEG enrichment during regeneration
The 13,059 DEGs were grouped into eight profiles, with 4886 (37.31%) genes in profiles 6 and 7, and 6537 (50.06%) genes in profiles 0 and 1 ( ). The genes in profiles 0, 1, 6, and 7 clearly differed from each other. The expression levels of all 3322 genes in profile 6 were notably elevated on day 4 following SCI and remained constant on day 10. In comparison with the Sham group, the gene expression levels at 4 and 10 days were greater. Furthermore, the expression levels of 1564 genes in profile 7 increased consistently from 4 to 10 days post-SCI, with the expression levels on day 4 being noticeably greater than those in the Sham group and the expression levels on day 10 days being noticeably higher than those on day 4 ( ). This implies that these genes are crucial for salamanders’ ability to recover from SCI. Thus, we concluded that SC regeneration in salamanders is mediated by genes in profiles 6 and 7.
 

## qRT-PCR validation of RNA-seq expression data
The validity of the RNA-seq data was demonstrated by the agreement between the RNA-seq expression data and the expression patterns of five representative DEGs, as determined by qRT-PCR ( ).
 

## GO and KEGG functional classification
Next, we performed GO enrichment analysis to determine which cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes the DEGs were associated with. The most enriched molecular functions were transducer activity (GO: transducer activity) and signal transducer activity (GO: 0004871). Regarding, biological processes, the most significantly enriched were cell communication (G0: 0007154), biological regulation (GO: 0065007), regulation of biological process (GO: 0050789), and system development (GO: 0048731). Meanwhile, the DEGs between the Sham and 10-day group were enriched in the biological process of cell communication (G0:0007154), system development (GO: 0048731), and developmental process (GO: 0032502). Finally, although the GO terms of DEGs between the 4-day and 10-day groups in terms of cell composition and molecular function differed significantly from those in the Sham vs. 4-day and Sham vs. 10-day groups, the main terms that were enriched in biological processes included phylogeny (GO: 0048734), cell differentiation (GO:0030154), cell migration (GO: 0016477), and developmental process (GO: 0032502). These results suggest that SC regeneration in C. orientalis is inseparable from cell differentiation and system development ( ).
 The differential genes identified by the KEGG enrichment analysis were classified into six categories, namely Environmental Information Processing, Organismal Systems, Genetic Information Processing, Human Diseases, Metabolism, and Cellular Processes. The differential genes between the Sham and 4-day groups were not enriched in the cellular process pathway, but were enriched in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. The DEGs between the Sham and 10-day groups were most significantly enriched for neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction. In comparison, the DEGs between the 4-day and 10-day groups were partially enriched in the cell cycle and P53 signaling pathways. Taken together, these results suggest that the regeneration after SCI in salamanders involves cell proliferation and differentiation ( ).

## Discussion

## Changes in GFAP-positive and neuronal cells
As in previous studies of tail regeneration, other studies have shown that all cells in the newly formed SC are GFAP-positive and continue to be attached to the central canal lumen (Arsanto et al., 1992; Dawley et al., 2012). In our study, however, we found that GFAP-positive cells could differentiate into other cell types. Nordlander and Singer (1978) discovered that ventricular meningeal cells can differentiate into neuronal cells during caudal regeneration, and Benraiss et al. (1999) showed that, during regeneration of the caudal SC in adult salamanders, regenerated neurons are derived from the proliferating ventricular meningeal layer of the ventricular canal. Our experimental results are in agreement with those of Okada et al. (2018), who showed that GFAP-positive glial cells were consistently present during SC recovery in C. orientalis. Even mature neurons in the meninges were positive for GFAP. While the number of GFAP-positive cells increased, the total number of neuronal cells also rose and gradually filled the gap. In addition, NeuN, a specific marker of mature neurons, increased in the meninges during SC regeneration, which suggests that GFAP-positive cells may have differentiated into mature neurons. Furthermore, Zukor et al. (2011) showed that meningeal cells and glial cells together provide a suitable microenvironment for SC regeneration in C. orientalis. Due to the specificity of the regenerative capacity of salamanders, we cannot ignore the role of meningeal cells. We only observed changes in the number of NeuN-positive cells in the meninges. These results indicate that there was a gradual increase in the number of mature neuronal cells, which could also be the result of meningeal cell dedifferentiation and redifferentiation into neurons, thus supporting SC regeneration. Finally, salamanders specifically express NeuN only in the meningeal structures surrounding the SC, in contrast to the overall high expression of NeuN in the mammalian brain, which may be similar to NeuN-associated immunity seen in the Xenopus laevis brain (Saito et al., 2017).

## Changes in glial scar
Glial scar formation has not been reported during SC regeneration in salamanders (Bolaños-Castro et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2022), and no glial scar formation was observed in the C. orientalis SC regeneration model established in the current study. After SCI, astrocytes become reactive glial cells and express more GFAP, NES, and vimentin, which causes the cells to become much larger and more numerous, migrate to the area surrounding the lesion, and then form a physical barrier (Zamanian et al., 2012). We hypothesize that this behavior may be specific to the glial cell type. Even though both mice and salamanders have ventricular meningeal cells, ventricular meningeal cells in mice can multiply into astrocytes, and certain genes are preferentially up-regulated in A1-type astrocytes (also known as A1 neuroinflammatory reactive astrocytes) (Zamanian et al., 2012). Based on our findings, GFAP-positive cells are clearly involved in SC healing in C. orientalis, although it is unclear at this time whether they are ependymal cell or astrocytes. Although the majority of glial scar cells are GFAP-positive, their proliferation is not necessary for the development of the glial scar. GFAP-positive cells are involved in SC repair (Yun et al., 2018).

## Changes in mRNA profiles
We identified 6996 DEGs between the Sham and 4-day groups, which is much more than the 1163 DEGs identified by Walker et al. (2022). This indicated that our model of trunk vertebrae SCI captured richer information than the newt model. MicroRNA 200a (miR-200A), which was identified by Walker et al. (2022) as affecting SC regeneration, was not identified in our analysis. MiR-200A promotes SC regeneration by repressing expression of the Jun proto-oncogene (C-JUN, also known as AP-1 transcription factor subunit (AP1)), but C-JUN expression was not markedly altered in our study (Sabin et al., 2019). In addition to the differences in experimental results that could arise from using different model species, in our model we performed SC transection at torso vertebrae 7–9, which better simulates SCI in humans. In contrast, the Walker et al. (2022) model was based on caudal amputation.
Based on bioinformatics analysis, far more genes were down-regulated than were up-regulated, and the predicted down-regulated genes were mainly associated with cell mitosis, suggesting that the rate of cell proliferation during salamander SC regeneration is lower than that seen in mice (Okada et al., 2018). Mice can produce 2 million new cells within a month after SCI (Stenudd et al., 2015). This could lead to axons regenerating more quickly in salamanders than glial scar can form, resulting in the gap closing completely before scar formation. Additionally, the pathway enrichment results indicated that signaling pathways involved in cellular metabolism were inhibited during the mid-stage of SC regeneration, especially those related to extracellular matrix secretion, which may result in a microenvironment that contains few inhibitors of axonal regeneration. This is similar to the environment postulated in a study by Fan et al. (2018) that allows spinal cord regeneration in mice. Ultimately, we hypothesize that rapid freezing after mammalian SCI could reduce the rate of cell proliferation, as well as the rate of extracellular matrix secretion, and perhaps allow for mammalian SC regeneration.

## Pathway comparison and differential gene prediction
KEGG pathway analysis revealed that some DEGs are enriched in immune-related signaling pathways, such as the TNF signaling pathway, the Jak-STAT signaling pathway, and the IL-17 signaling pathway. Previous studies have found that regenerative ability is impaired at later developmental stages, such as in adult or aged mice, which exhibit a stronger immune response compared with young or neonatal mice. However, the powerful regenerative capacity of salamanders seems to negatively regulate immune system-related pathways to facilitate the regeneration/healing process. The mammalian pathways associated with the genes whose expression levels change in SCI mice are relatively well established, and comparative studies have shown that expression of the corresponding human genes also changes following SCI.
The family of genes encoding SOX (SRY-related HMG-box) transcription factors associated with stem cell population maintenance, CNS differentiation, and neuronal development plays a key role in developmental regulation and cell fate determination. In particular, SOX9 is thought to be a marker for astrocytes involved in glial scar formation, and two other genes with similar functions, Acan, and Bcan, are consistently downregulated at the mRNA level in salamanders. The similar expression trends suggest that GAP43 may also play a role in neurite outgrowth, synapse creation, and regeneration, and mediate axon extension in salamander SC regeneration. GAP43 is frequently utilized as a marker of effective axonal regeneration in several species and neuron types.
In total, we identified 40 relevant candidate genes that may affect SC regeneration. Some of these genes have previously been shown to be associated with SCI. For example, PLP1 was mentioned in a study of astrocytes (Wang et al., 2017). All surviving neurons and reactive astrocytes had higher levels of AQP-1 expression after SCI, and continued overexpression of AQP-1 in ventricular canal cells may be a factor in the overproduction of cerebrospinal fluid and the development of cystic lesions filled with cerebrospinal fluid after SCI (Basaldella et al., 2007). In addition, CTSD (Moghieb et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019) has been identified as a candidate SCI biomarker. In animals with SCI, an increase in endoplasmic reticulum stress markers is associated with blockage of autophagic flux, as evidenced by a decrease in LAMP1 levels (Liu et al., 2015). SNAP25 is mainly localized in axons and nerve terminals (Aikawa et al., 2006), is vital for the formation of neural circuits, and can encourage nerve protrusion growth and elongation throughout the CNS (Bark et al., 2004; Maxfield and Tabas, 2005). NCP2 appears to play a role in SC demyelination, but its specific function is uncertain (Maxfield and Tabas, 2005). The fact that these genes are involved in SC regeneration aptly illustrates the ability of this screening approach to identify genes whose function is not yet clear in SCI that may be the key to promoting SC regeneration in mammals.
In conclusion, during SC regeneration in salamanders, the number of mature neurons in the meninges increased, and GFAP-positive glial cells eventually filled in the injury site, enabling the salamander to regain most of its movement by 21 days. In addition, no glial scar formation was observed despite the proliferation of GFAP-positive cells. In total, 13,059 DEGs were found via RNA-seq that together created a microenvironment suitable for SC regeneration. Our findings provide important information for understanding the molecular basis of salamander SC regeneration after SCI and a useful basis for future studies.
Editor’s evaluation:
This article investigated the modulation of salamander gene expression at 4 and 10 days after spinal cord injury. Different gene expression profiles and potential signaling pathways were identified and compared. In general, this manuscript was well written and was informative. It provides interesting and useful information related to spinal cord injury regeneration.
