## Abstract
With the advancement of high-throughput technologies, the pivotal role of non-coding RNA (ncRNA) as a master regulator of various biological functions has become increasingly apparent. Historically considered non-functional and labeled as "junk DNA," pseudogenes can be transcribed into RNA, indicating a potential role similar to ncRNAs. Recent research suggests that some pseudogenes can encode functional peptides or proteins. A growing body of evidence has revealed that pseudogenes and their derived functional molecules are involved in various biological processes and can serve as prognostic markers in cancers. This review comprehensively summarizes and discusses the current understanding of the functional roles of pseudogenes and their derived molecules in biological functions.
Keywords: Pseudogene, Junk DNA, Non-coding RNA, Peptide, Prognosis

## Highlights
•Pseudogenes encode functional molecules that are involved in cancer progression.
•Pseudogenes, such as methylation, are pivotal regulators in modulating RNA modification.
•Pseudogenes are clinically relevant in multiple cancers.

## Introduction
The traditional central dogma of molecular biology posits that a gene is transcribed into messenger RNA (mRNA), then translated into a protein that regulates most biological processes [ ]. Genes following this pathway are defined as protein-coding genes. It was previously understood that about 2% of the human genome consists of protein-coding genes, which produce proteins that modulate cellular functions. The remaining 98% of the genome was considered irrelevant and labeled as "junk DNA" [ ]. In addition to protein-coding genes, another type of gene, known as microRNA (miRNA), has been characterized in eukaryotes. MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) transcribed into RNA molecules but not translated into proteins [ ]. They are transcribed by RNA polymerase II, producing the primary form of miRNA (pri-miRNA) in the nucleus. This pri-miRNA is then enzymatically processed by Drosha and DGCR8 to form precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) [ ]. The pre-miRNA is subsequently transported to the cytoplasm, where Dicer furthers it into a mature miRNA (∼18–22 nucleotides). The mature miRNA associates with the Ago2 protein to form the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [ ]. This complex typically interacts with the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of the target gene's mRNA, promoting mRNA degradation or suppressing the target gene's translation [ ]. This well-established regulatory mechanism of ncRNAs is a significant area of research. After discovering miRNAs' roles in cancer progression and disease development, research on long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) has gained considerable attention, becoming a rising star in the field [ ]. The functional roles of lncRNAs in cancer development, their detailed regulatory mechanisms, and their clinical significance have been extensively studied and elucidated [ ]. In 1977, the first pseudogene, oocyte-type 5S RNA, was identified in Xenopus laevis [ ]. Researchers found several tandemly repeated sequences within the 5S DNA that encoded 5S RNA. Although pseudogenes are similar to their parental genes, they often contain disabling mutations, resulting in the loss of function of the target gene. Consequently, pseudogenes have been categorized as "a type of junk DNA" [ ]. Pseudogenes are designated in the literature in two ways: by adding the symbol “ψ” in front of the gene name or by adding the letter “P” after the gene name [ ]. For example, ψfepE and the pseudogene of PTEN, named PTENP1 [ , ].
There are ten proposed cancer hallmarks: sustaining proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, avoiding immune destruction, enabling replicative immortality, promoting tumor-associated inflammation, activating invasion and metastasis, inducing or accessing vasculature, promoting genome instability and mutation, resisting cell death, and deregulating cellular metabolism [ ]. Highly metastatic cancer cells have a high propensity to invade distant organs and successfully proliferate, eventually leading to the development of metastatic tumors [ ]. Cancer cell impairment of drug response is a long-term process regulated by intrinsic and extrinsic signaling, leading to the development of drug resistance. Increasing evidence supports that drug resistance is positively correlated with poor survival outcomes [ , ]. Additionally, cancer cells can effectively reprogram their cellular metabolism, including glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid metabolism, to produce energy and support proliferation [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cancer cells enhance glycolysis to counteract drug treatment [ ]. Therefore, understanding the underlying mechanisms of these phenotypes is essential for developing appropriate strategies to overcome these effects and improve the survival outcomes of cancer patients.
Notably, pseudogenes can produce transcripts, peptides, and proteins in various cellular processes. This review summarizes the background of pseudogenes, the effects of pseudogene-derived functional molecules on cancer hallmarks, and their clinical roles in cancer progression.
1.1.

## An overview of pseudogenes and their derived molecules
The number of studies on pseudogenes has gradually increased [ A]. Based on their origin and the mechanisms mediating their formation, pseudogenes are categorized into three groups: (1) processed pseudogenes, (2) unprocessed pseudogenes, and (3) unitary pseudogenes [ , ]. Compared to their parental genes, processed pseudogenes typically lack intronic sequences and harbor a poly-A tail. They arise from the retrotransposition of processed mRNA, which is converted into complementary DNA and then integrated into a different genomic location [ B]. This process is mediated by retrotransposons, including long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), which facilitate reverse transcription and integration. Additionally, processed pseudogenes lose the regulatory sequences present in their parental genes. In contrast, unprocessed pseudogenes retain their parental genes' intronic and regulatory sequences [ ]. Gene duplication is the major contributor to the generation of unprocessed pseudogenes [ C]. Unitary pseudogenes are defined by mutations occurring within the parental gene, leading to the creation of a premature stop codon, deletion, or frameshift mutation, which attenuates the protein-coding ability [ D]. Strikingly, pseudogenes can encode functional molecules such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), small peptides, and proteins. Multiple studies have revealed that pseudogene-derived lncRNAs mediate biological functions via their association with specific microRNAs (miRNAs) and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [ ]. Due to the high sequence similarity between pseudogenes and their parental genes, pseudogene-derived lncRNAs may act as competitors to sequester specific molecules, such as miRNAs and RBPs, which are associated initially with parental gene-encoded transcripts. This sequestration alters regulatory homeostasis and can lead to cancer development. Additionally, pseudogene-derived lncRNAs also interact with components of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), such as enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), contributing to gene expression regulation through epigenetic mechanisms [ , ]. Recently, RNA modifications such as methylation have been recognized as essential factors in cancer progression, and pseudogenes are involved in this pathway. The following section discusses how pseudogenes modulate cellular functions via interactions with miRNAs, RBPs, and other mechanisms.
 1.1.1.

## Pseudogenes and miRNAs
PTEN is a well-known tumor suppressor gene implicated in multiple cancers [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that a pseudogene of PTEN, named PTENP1, regulates PTEN gene expression [ , ]. Specifically, PTENP1 is transcribed opposite to PTEN, producing two transcripts: PTENP1-S (sense transcript) and PTENP1-AS (antisense transcript). The regulatory mechanisms of PTENP1-S and PTENP1-AS on PTEN expression are distinct. Poliseno et al. showed that the 3′UTRs of PTEN and PTENP1-S share common miRNA-targeting sites, including miR-17, miR-19, miR-21, miR-26, and miR-214 [ ]. Verification through qRT-PCR and luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that miR-19 and miR-20a repress the expression of PTEN and PTENP1-S via their interactions. Notably, overexpression of PTENP1-S with its 3′UTR led to interactions with these miRNAs, resulting in enhanced PTEN mRNA expression. These findings suggest that PTENP1-S acts as a tumor-suppressive pseudogene.
Recently, double homeobox A pseudogene 10 (DUXAP10) was identified via RNA sequencing and found to be highly expressed in aged adipose stem cells (ASCs) [ ]. Suppression of DUXAP10 enhanced cell growth and motility by interacting with miR-214-3p, leading to the upregulation of its target gene, ras association domain family member 5 (RASSF5), in ASCs. Another novel pseudogene, UBDP1, was identified and found to be upregulated in glioblastoma [ ]. The expression level of UBDP1 was positively associated with its parental gene, UBD. Clinically, elevated expressions of UBDP1 and UBD were correlated with poor prognosis. Functional assays in vitro and in vivo revealed that UBDP1 acts as an oncogene, inducing tumor formation and cell motility. Mechanistically, UBDP1 is a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to sequester miR-6072 from UBD mRNA, thereby promoting UBD expression.
LncRNA-apolipoprotein C–I pseudogene 1 (APOC1P1) was found to be expressed at lower levels in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues [ ]. Cellular fractionation assays demonstrated that APOC1P1 is enriched in the cytoplasm. Ectopic expression of APOC1P1 reduced cell growth and motility in a liver cancer cell line by competitively associating with miR-106a, which induced PTEN expression. In pancreatic adenocarcinoma, AK4P1 acts as an unfavorable prognostic marker [ ]. In vitro assays showed that AK4P1 may be associated with the tumor-suppressive miR-375, enhancing the expression of its target gene SP1. The expression levels of lncRNA SUMO1 pseudogene 3 (lncSUMO1P3) were increased in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) tissues [ ]. Overexpression of lncSUMO1P3 in ESCC cell lines promoted cell proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by competitively interacting with miR-486-5p, thereby regulating CD151 expression. Galbiati et al. showed that droplet digital PCR analysis revealed higher expression levels of SUMO1P3 in urine samples from patients with muscle-invasive urothelial bladder cancer compared to the control group [ ]. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicated that SUMO1P3 could be a predictive marker for monitoring bladder cancer progression through non-invasive testing. Other studies have also supported the oncogenic role of SUMO1P3 in regulating cell growth, migration, and invasion in cancer cells, including non-small cell lung cancer and glioma, through the modulation of miR-136, β-catenin, cyclin D1, E-cadherin, and N-cadherin [ , ]. MiRNA-inhibited target expression via mRNA degradation or inhibition of target gene translation usually occurs in the cytoplasm. Based on this concept, subcellular localization detection revealed that pseudogenes primarily expressed in the cytoplasm function as ceRNAs.
Regarding pseudogenes directly associating with miRNA, a previous study demonstrated that ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 C pseudogene 3 (UBE2CP3) interacts with insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7) to form an RNA-RNA duplex [ ]. This complex is also associated with interleukin enhancer binding factor 3 (ILF3) protein, which enhances the RNA stability of UBE2CP3, thereby inducing gastric cancer progression. These findings emphasize that RNA (pseudogene) to RNA (miRNA and mRNA) interactions play critical regulatory roles in cancer progression [ A].
 
1.1.2.

## Pseudogenes and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs)
Pseudogenes also influence cancer progression through their association with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), such as human antigen R (HuR) and cellular nucleic acid binding protein (CNBP) [ , ]. Previous studies identified ribosomal protein SA pseudogene 52 (RPSAP52) upregulated in pituitary adenoma [ ]. Elevated expression of RPSAP52 promotes cell proliferation via interaction with HuR, altering the colocalization of miR-15a, miR-15b, and miR-16. CNBP has been identified as interacting with small ubiquitin-like modifier 1 pseudogene 3 (SUMO1P3) [ ]. This complex enhanced cell growth and cell motility in gastric cancer. Another study showed that peptidylprolyl cis/trans isomerase, NIMA‐interacting 1 pseudogene 1 (PIN1P1), is upregulated in gastric cancer, and its higher expression is significantly correlated with poor survival outcomes [ ]. They found that cAMP-responsive element binding protein 1 (CREB1) directly associates with the promoter region of PIN1P1, activating its expression. Ectopic expression of PIN1P1 enhances cell growth and motility in gastric cancer cell lines, effects that are partially blocked upon depletion of CREB1. Additionally, PIN1P1 associates with Y box binding protein 1 (YBX1), which is involved in PIN1P1-mediated oncogenic functions.
Huang et al. demonstrated that disulfide isomerase family A member 3 pseudogene 1 (PDIA3P1) is highly expressed in human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [ ]. Ectopic expression of PDIA3P1 enhances cell growth, motility, and cancer stem cell features via interaction with octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (Oct4), stabilizing its protein. Furthermore, Oct4 directly binds to the promoter region of PDIA3P1, inducing its expression. These observations indicate that PDIA3P1 and Oct4 form a positive feedback loop to facilitate cancer progression. Previously, Jianguo et al. demonstrated that 5S rRNA pseudogene (RNA5SP) transcripts are upregulated in patients with severe alcohol-associated hepatitis (sAH) [ ]. The expression levels of RNA5SP are positively correlated with type I interferon (IFN). Using catRAPID software analysis, potential RNA5SP-interacting proteins were identified. Among them, zinc finger CCCH-type and G-patch domain containing (ZGPAT), epithelial splicing regulatory protein 2 (ESRP2), and APOBEC1 complementation factor (A1CF) were significantly downregulated in patients with sAH. RNA pull-down assays verified that ZGPAT and ESRP2, but not A1CF, were associated with RNA5SP transcripts. In THP-1 cells, RNA5SP modulates cell viability by regulating Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1).
These findings indicate that pseudogene-derived transcripts can directly associate with RBPs, regulate downstream molecule expression, and modulate disease development and cancer progression [ B].

1.1.3.

## Pseudogenes and zeste homolog 2 (EZH2)
Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) is a critical multiprotein complex involved in the regulation of gene expression through chromatin modification [ ]. PRC2 primarily functions by catalyzing the methylation of histone H3 on lysine 27 (H3K27me3), a key epigenetic mark associated with gene silencing. This modification leads to a compact chromatin structure that represses the transcription of target genes. PRC2 comprises several core subunits, including EZH2 (or its homolog EZH1), EED, SUZ12, and RBAP46/48. EZH2 is the catalytic component responsible for the methyltransferase activity, while EED and SUZ12 are essential for the complex's stability and function. RBAP46/48 acts as a histone-binding protein that helps recruit the complex to chromatin. The activity of PRC2 is critical for various biological processes, such as maintaining stem cell pluripotency, controlling cell differentiation, and regulating developmental pathways. Dysregulation of PRC2 function has been implicated in several diseases, including cancer, where it can contribute to tumorigenesis by silencing tumor suppressor genes [ ]. PRC2 is a critical epigenetic regulator that maintains proper gene expression patterns, thereby influencing cell fate and identity.
In breast cancer, the ATP binding cassette subfamily C member 6 pseudogene 1 (ABCC6P1) is implicated in multidrug resistance [ ]. Compared to the control group, upregulation of ABCC6P1 enhances cell viability upon treatment with doxorubicin, 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, and paclitaxel. Notably, the drug efflux assay indicates that overexpression of ABCC6P1 suppresses doxorubicin uptake. Another study showed that the expression levels of DUXAP10 are highly elevated in epidermal growth factor receptor kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI)-resistant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissues and cell lines [ ]. Depletion of DUXAP10 re-sensitizes cells to gefitinib both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, DUXAP10 regulates the gefitinib-resistant phenotype via EZH2/2′,5′-oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS2) cascade.
Overexpression of DUXAP8 enhances radioresistance through the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and interaction with EZH2, thereby inhibiting E-cadherin and ras homolog family member B (RHOB) expression [ ]. Another study confirmed that DUXAP8 interacts with EZH2, and this complex suppresses E-cadherin expression in colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines [ ]. The pseudogene misato family member 2 (MSTO2P) is highly expressed in CRC tissues [ ]. Functionally, inhibition of MSTO2P represses cell proliferation and invasion while enhancing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Furthermore, MSTO2P is associated with EZH2, leading to the epigenetic suppression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (CDKN1A) expression. Additionally, the PTEN pseudogene, PTENP1-AS, acts as a negative regulator of PTEN via chromatin remodeling [ ]. Depletion of DNMT3a and EZH2 in cell lines expressing endogenous PTENP1-AS enhances PTEN expression. Different PTEN pseudogenes affect PTEN expression through ceRNA action or epigenetic regulation.
These studies underscore the critical role of pseudogenes in cancer progression, as they can associate with the epigenetic regulator EZH2, thereby modulating the expression of downstream molecules [ C].

1.1.4.

## Pseudogene-derived siRNA
RNA interference (RNAi) is a crucial biological process that aids in eliminating viruses and transposons and regulating target gene expression [ ]. Research has revealed that pseudogenes in human cells can produce endogenous siRNAs (esiRNAs). For instance, Chan and colleagues demonstrated that the transcribed protein phosphatase, Mg2+/Mn2+-dependent 1K (PPM1K) pseudogene expressed inverted sequences, which led to the formation of a hairpin structure and the generation of esiRNAs [ ]. Specifically, the PPM1K pseudogene-derived siRNA targets the expression of NIMA-related kinase 8 (NEK8), thereby suppressing cell proliferation and colony formation in hepatoma cell lines. Notably, PPM1K pseudogene-derived esiRNA can be detected in HCC specimens using qRT-PCR. The results showed that the expression levels of PPM1K pseudogene-derived esiRNA were significantly downregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues. These observations indicate that pseudogene-derived esiRNA exists in cells and tissues, highlighting their potential role in regulating gene expression and influencing disease progression [ D].

1.1.5.

## Pseudogene: protein-coding or not?
Recent studies have challenged the traditional view of pseudogenes as non-coding entities by proving that some pseudogenes can encode functional peptides. A decade ago, Kim and colleagues analyzed the human proteome in normal tissues using high-resolution Fourier-transform mass spectrometry and identified several novel protein-coding regions, including pseudogenes and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) [ ]. Specifically, 107 pseudogenes could produce functional peptides [ ]. Bier and colleagues reported that the connexin 43 pseudogene (Cx43P) was aberrantly expressed in breast cancer tissues and could be translated into a protein that functioned as a regulator to reduce cell growth, similar to its parental gene, Cx43 [ ]. Mechanistically, depletion of Cx43P resulted in enhanced Cx43 expression at both RNA and protein levels and knockdown of Cx43P-sensitized breast cancer cells to paclitaxel and doxorubicin treatment.
Additionally, the POU class 5 homeobox 1 pseudogene 1 (POU5F1P1) encoded a putative protein primarily expressed in the nucleus, as determined by immunofluorescence assay, and also functioned as a transcription factor. The reporter assay demonstrated that POU5F1P1-regulated downstream gene expression was weaker than that mediated by the POU5F1 isoform 1 [ ]. The clinic's RT-PCR analysis revealed that POU5F1P1 was upregulated in prostatic carcinoma compared with adjacent tissues [ ]. Zhang's group discovered that the mitogen-activated protein kinase 6 pseudogene 4 (MAPK6P4) potentially encodes a 135-amino acid polypeptide (P4-135aa) that induces phosphorylation of KLF15 at S238. This phosphorylation enhances KLF15 protein stability and promotes vasculogenic mimicry (VM) formation in glioma cells, an alternative form of tumor angiogenesis that supplies blood and nutrients to cancer cells without the involvement of endothelial cells [ ]. They also found that the serine/threonine kinase 24 pseudogene 1 (STK24P1) encoded a functional peptide (P1-121aa) that regulated VM through enhancement of ELF2 phosphorylation in glioma cells [ ]. More recently, Chuang and co-workers used machine learning models for prediction and verification to reveal that ubiquitin-like with PHD and RING finger domains 1 pseudogene (UHRF1P) was upregulated in the peripheral blood T cells of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients [ ]. Interestingly, UHRF1P protein lacks the proteasome-binding ubiquitin-like (Ubl) domain of its parental gene, UHRF1. UHRF1P-transgenic mice exhibited enhanced IL-17A expression and autoimmune inflammation, and UHRF1P induced MAP4K3 expression by inhibiting UHRF1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. In addition to pseudogene-encoded functional peptides or proteins, it was found that lncRNAs and circular RNA (circRNA) could also encode functional peptides [ , ]. Through bioinformatics analysis, a functional peptide (84aa), named CRNDEP, encoded by lncRNA CRNDE, was identified [ ]. CRNDEP was predominantly expressed in the nucleus and involved in cell growth regulation. In addition, overexpression of CRNDEP promotes the formation of stress granules and may be involved in cellular metabolism, particularly oxygen metabolism. However, its detailed underlying mechanisms in cancer progression need to be explored in the future. A mitochondria-derived peptide mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA-c (MOTS-c) was encoded by mitochondrial 12S rRNA [ ]. Functional assays revealed that MOTS-c mediated insulin response and metabolic homeostasis. Recently, Lu et al. demonstrated that MOTS-c could reverse nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Mechanistically, MOTS-c modulates the stability of the B cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) protein through its interaction with Bcl-2. An in vivo study confirmed that MOTS-c has a protective role in improving the progression of NASH. Song and colleagues demonstrated that a novel secreted peptide, circZKSaa, was encoded by circZKSCAN1 (has_circ_0001727) in HCC [ ]. Both in vitro and in vivo assays revealed that circZKSaa exerts a tumor-suppressive effect by inhibiting HCC progression and sensitizing hepatoma cells to sorafenib by disrupting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Bioinformatics analysis using the circRNADb database identified two internal ribosomal entry sites (IRES) within circZKSCAN1. In vitro, reporter assays confirmed that both IRES sequences can induce ribosome entry. These findings suggest that the formation of circular RNA may facilitate IRES formation, thereby enabling the potential generation of a functional peptide. However, the mechanisms by which non-coding elements encode functional peptides or proteins in cancer cells remain unclear, making the identification, characterization, and prediction of these peptides or proteins challenging. To date, omics methods such as polysome profiling, ribosome profiling, ribosome affinity purification, and ribosome-nascent chain complex sequencing have been employed to explore the potential of lncRNAs encoding peptides or proteins [ ]. These high-throughput sequencing approaches are promising tools for investigating how non-coding elements can encode functional peptides or proteins expressed in various tissues and cells.
Additionally, several bioinformatics tools, including sORF finder [ ], PhyloCSF [ ], CNCI [ ], CPC [ ], CPC2 [ ], CNIT [ ], and ORF Finder [ ], are available to predict the potential of non-coding transcripts to encode peptides or proteins. These tools help researchers predict which non-coding elements may encode peptides or proteins, which can be verified using in vitro assays.
One possibility for pseudogene-encoded functional peptides is that, during genome evolution, mutations within pseudogene sequences cause a loss of their ability to generate complete proteins. Meanwhile, these mutations may have also introduced elements, such as IRES, that promote the translation of small functional peptides. These findings highlight the potential of pseudogenes, lncRNAs, and circRNAs to encode functional peptides, suggesting that the traditional view of pseudogenes as non-coding elements may need to be re-evaluated [ E]

1.1.6.

## Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) of pseudogenes in cancer
Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) serve as significant biomarkers and are involved in disease development and cancer progression [ , ]. This includes SNPs occurring in protein-coding genes, ncRNAs, and pseudogenes. Studies have shown that SNPs within pseudogenes can be correlated with various types of cancers. For instance, a case-control study found that the genotype rs8040855 of the chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 pseudogene 12 (CSPG4P12) was associated with a reduced risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCA) patients [ ]. In addition, the expression levels of CSPG4P12 were lower in ESCA tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues. Ectopic expression of CSPG4P12 suppressed cell growth, migration, and invasion in ESCA cells by inhibiting the expressions of PI3K and p-AKT. In another study, two SNPs (rs12490683 and rs12497343) of the myosin light chain kinase pseudogene 1 (MYLKP1) were identified as risk factors for colon cancer in African Americans [ ]. Functionally, elevated expression of MYLKP1 induced cell migration in the H441 and A549 cell lines, suggesting that it is an oncogenic pseudogene. Additionally, the genotype rs9909601 of the E2F transcription factor 3 pseudogene 1 (E2F3P1) was found to be a risk factor for HCC patients in China [ , ]. The E2F3P1 rs9909601 GG genotype was significantly associated with a poor prognosis compared to the E2F3P1 rs9909601 non-GG genotypes. These findings suggest that SNPs within pseudogenes contain valuable information that could be biomarkers for cancer and other diseases. Moreover, SNPs are used in studies of species evolution by comparing DNA sequences. Significant SNPs preserved during evolution might have essential functions that were previously unknown. The roles of genetic variations within pseudogenes warrant further investigation to understand their potential impact on disease and evolution fully.

## Pseudogene and RNA modification
In molecular biology, epigenetic regulation influences gene expression through chromatin remodeling, histone modification, and DNA methylation [ ]. Recent studies have highlighted the role of RNA modifications, including N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), pseudouridine, and adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing, in regulating cellular functions and their association with tumor development. Emerging evidence indicates that pseudogenes are involved in RNA modification, thereby modulating cancer progression. This section summarizes the role of pseudogenes in RNA modification to underscore their significance and provide insights for developing cancer treatments.
Wang and colleagues demonstrated that levels of alpha-2-glycoprotein 1, zinc-binding pseudogene 2 (AZGP1P2) were lower in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and prostate cancer stem cell (PCSC)-like cells [ ]. Clinically, elevated expression of AZGP1P2 correlated with better survival outcomes. Functional in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that AZGP1P2 was necessary for enhancing the sensitivity of prostate cancer (PC) to docetaxel treatment. RNA pull-down assays, mass spectrometry (MS), and Western blot analysis identified that AZGP1P2 interacted with ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (UBA1) and RNA binding motif protein 15 (RBM15), a component of the m6A writer complex. M6A-seq analysis indicated that RBM15 knockdown reduced the m6A signal on Tropomyosin 1 (TPM1). These findings suggest that AZGP1P2 regulates docetaxel response in PC via the UBA1/RBM15/TPM1 axis. Another study identified m6A-associated pseudogenes as prognostic markers in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) using The Cancer Genome Atlas data [ ]. Systematic analysis showed that the oncogenic pseudogene protein disulfide isomerase family A member 3 (PDIA3P1) and the tumor-suppressive pseudogene RRN3 pseudogene 3 (RRN3P3) were significantly associated with immune-related genes via ceRNA action. Specifically, PDIA3P1 competitively interacted with miR-34a-5p, leading to the upregulation of AKT1 expression, while RRN3P3 interacted with miR-26, enhancing EZH2 expression. These interactions were involved in the immune response in HNSCC.
Additionally, methyltransferase 3 (METTL3), a major m6A writer, was highly expressed in HCC tissues [ ]. RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analysis revealed that METTL3 upregulated glucosylceramidase beta 1-like pseudogene (GBAP1) in an m6A-dependent manner. GBAP1 was significantly correlated with tumor size and TNM stage in HCC. In vitro and in vivo assays revealed that ectopic expression of GBAP1 induced tumor growth and metastasis by interacting with miR-22-3p, which induced bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 1A expression. These findings suggest that pseudogenes regulate target gene expression via RNA modification [ F]. Due to the complexity of subtypes and stages in cancer patients, therapeutic approaches such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy vary. Immunotherapy has become an effective method for eliminating cancer cells by inhibiting immune checkpoint regulators, including programmed death 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA4). Multiple studies have shown that the expression levels of PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA4 serve as biomarkers for the immune response. A transcriptome-wide m6A methylation profiling analysis and clinical validation revealed that the pseudogene HSPA7, with m6A modification, acts as a prognostic marker in glioblastoma (GBM) and is associated with immunophenotype, stromal activation, and oncogenic pathways [ ]. Cell-based experiments (gain of function and loss of function) showed that HSPA7 enhances tumor macrophage migration to GBM via activation of the yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) pathway and upregulation of oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor 1 (LOX1) expression, suggesting that pseudogene HSPA7 may be involved in the immune response.
Previous studies reported that endogenous bornavirus-like nucleoprotein 3, pseudogene (EBLN3P) was highly expressed in CRC tissues [ ]. The m6A methyltransferase, KIAA1429, was a pivotal regulator inducing EBLN3P expression via m6A modification. On the other hand, EBLN3P enhanced the KIAA1429 expression through competitively interacting with miR-153-3p. Functional assays uncovered that the KIAA1429/EBLN3P axis promoted radioresistance of CRC and inhibited ferroptosis. Multiple studies supported that EBLN3P served as an oncogene in various cancers, including osteosarcoma [ ], colon cancer [ ], and lung cancer [ ].
Recently, a machine-learning-based study characterized the association between a tumor-infiltrating immune cells-related pseudogene signature (TIICPS) and immune response and survival outcomes in ovarian cancer patients [ ]. Comprehensive verification showed that ovarian cancer patients in the low TIICPS group responded favorably to immunotherapy. In contrast, patients in the high TIICPS group were positively correlated with higher tumor formation rates, EMT signatures, angiogenesis, and cancer-associated fibroblasts. The pseudogene signature may serve as a predictive marker for immune response in ovarian cancer patients.

## How to manipulate and detect pseudogene expression
Establishing gain-of-function and loss-of-function models is crucial for understanding the function of target genes. Typically, the coding sequences of the target gene are amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and then cloned into an expression plasmid such as pcDNA3 or a viral-based vector. Conversely, siRNA or short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) are synthesized and cloned into plasmids like the pLKO.1 vector. These plasmids are then delivered into cell lines to overexpress or suppress the target gene expression. Once overexpression or knockdown cell lines are successfully established, the biological phenotypes of the target gene can be studied. However, due to the sequence similarity between pseudogenes and their parental genes, traditional methods like knockdown present challenges for manipulating pseudogene expression.
CRISPR-Cas9 is a powerful tool for altering gene expression. This genome-editing approach allows for the specific elimination of gene expression (knockout), the introduction of specific mutations, and the insertion of specific sequences (knock-in) by manipulating DNA sequences [ ]. Additionally, CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) methods are effective for the positive or negative regulation of specific genes natively expressed in the genome, enabling the study of target gene function. SNPs within protein-coding or non-coding genes both serve as biomarkers in various cancers. Isogenic cell lines expressing alternative genotypes were successfully created by CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. Therefore, the phenotypes modulated by alternative genotypes could be studied. Ustiugova and colleagues demonstrated that a two-stage genome editing method established the alternative genotype rs12946510 in a T-helper cell line [ , ]. In brief, the first step involved transfecting target cells via electroporation with the pX458 plasmid, which expresses Cas9, GFP, and sgRNA-1, along with the donor-1 plasmid containing a dTomato cassette. Twenty-four hours after electroporation, the donor-1 plasmid containing the dTomato cassette was inserted into the rs12946510 locus through homologous recombination (HR). Cells with successful insertion, indicated by stronger red fluorescence, were then sorted and seeded into a 96-well plate. In the second step, after a few weeks, the cells were transfected again via electroporation with the pX458 plasmid and the donor-2 plasmid containing the heterozygous rs12946510 variant derived from MT-2 cells. Cells with the rs12946510 variant inserted via HR were sorted based on green fluorescence signals from the pX458 plasmid. The genotypes of rs12946510 (rs12946510-CC, rs12946510-TT, or rs12946510-TC) were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. By comparing different alleles, the rs12946510-CC genotype exhibited a more substantial effect on T cell activation than the rs12946510-TT and rs12946510-TC genotypes.
A previous study demonstrated that UBB pseudogenes (UBBP1-5) were expressed in different tissues via RNA-seq analysis [ ]. Notably, UBBP3 and UBBP4 encoded ubiquitin variants. Specifically, two open reading frames (ORFs) were identified in UBBP4 sequences. The first ORF could encode three ubiquitin variants, and the second ORF could encode a ubiquitin monomer. To explore its functional role, a UBBP4 knockout HeLa cell line was established using the CRISPR-Cas9 method [ ]. The UBBP4 knockout cells showed reduced cell growth. Interestingly, these cells also exhibited enhanced lamin A within nucleoli compared to the control group. Another study established a nanog homeobox retrogene p8 (NANOGP8) knockout cell line using the CRISPR-Cas9 approach. Knockout of NANOGP8 significantly impaired sphere formation, migration, and anchorage-independent growth of the prostate cancer cell line DU145. Additionally, the knockout re-sensitized the cells to docetaxel-mediated cell death.
In purine metabolism, uric acid is converted to 5-hydroxyisourate by uricase and subsequently to ammonia and urea, which are eliminated from the body. Dysregulation of this process leads to uric acid accumulation, known as hyperuricemia, associated with clinical disease [ ]. Balico et al. demonstrated using the CRISPR-Cas9 approach to generate an engineered allele expressing the uricase gene to address this. The in vitro assays showed that reactivating the uricase gene could decrease uric acid levels in human cell lines with severe hyperuricemia. Taken together, reactivating the uricase pseudogene using the CRISPR-Cas9 method effectively improved acute hyperuricemia [ ].
A limitation in investigating pseudogenes is their sequence similarity to parental genes, which might lead to misinterpretation of expression results upon pseudogene overexpression. Therefore, probes for detecting pseudogene expression via reverse transcription (RT)-PCR, quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), northern blot, and in situ hybridization assays are specifically designed. Next-generation sequencing (first- and second-generation) technologies focusing on short-read sequencing have become the gold standard for nucleic acid profiling in the last two decades. However, this method requires complicated algorithms to map small nucleic acid fragments to longer sequences, leading to inaccurate sequence assemblies [ ]. Recently, long-read sequencing methods have been developed to address this problem. Notably, long-read sequencing can construct spliced variants and duplicated or repeated genome sequences, including pseudogenes and Alu sequences [ ]. Furthermore, long-read sequencing has become a promising tool for clinical investigations. This method may help identify sequence differences in pseudogenes involved in cancer progression.

## Conclusion and perspectives
Our fundamental understanding is that pseudogenes can be transcribed into RNA rather than encoding proteins. This suggests that one aspect of pseudogene function in cells is similar to that of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), which are well-characterized in regulating downstream pathways. Accordingly, multiple studies have focused on investigating pseudogene-derived lncRNA-mediated cancer progression or disease formation. Notably, a few studies have shown that pseudogenes can encode functional peptides or proteins, each with unique functions in modulating cellular processes. The pseudogene-derived functional molecules are comprehensively summarized in [ ].
 With the rapid development of omics technologies, we expect that more lncRNA- and pseudogene-derived peptides and proteins will be identified. Additionally, SNPs within pseudogenes can serve as prognostic biomarkers in cancer patients, further highlighting their clinical relevance. While our current knowledge of the primary functional roles and clinical significance of pseudogenes is limited, existing studies support the idea that pseudogenes are clinically relevant and may suggest the potential for their inclusion in preclinical or clinical trials. Investigations have shown that extracellular vesicles (EVs) can package various functional cargoes such as DNA, lncRNA, miRNA, peptides, proteins, and metabolites. This implies that pseudogene-derived lncRNA, peptides, or proteins could be sorted into EVs and transported to target cells. This review aims to provide a perspective on the biological functions of pseudogenes and their derived molecules in cancer progression and disease development.
