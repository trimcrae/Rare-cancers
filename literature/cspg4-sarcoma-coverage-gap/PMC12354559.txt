## Background
CD109 is overexpressed in various tumors, but its role in hematologic malignancies, particularly acute B lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), remains unclear.

## Methods
CD109 expression was assessed at both mRNA and protein levels in B-ALL patients using real-time quantitative PCR (RQ-PCR) and multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC). The relationship between CD109 expression and clinical and laboratory parameters was examined. The potential of CD109 as a marker for minimal residual disease (MRD) detection was evaluated. Functional studies were conducted in leukemia cell lines.

## Results
CD109 mRNA was significantly upregulated in newly diagnosed and relapsed B-ALL patients compared to healthy controls and remission cases. MFC revealed CD109 positivity in 79.0% B-ALL patients, with higher rates in relapsed cases (85.7%) and CD34 + B-ALL patients. CD109 expression was minimal in mature B cells and precursors B cell. CD109 expression remained stable across disease phases including diagnosis, MRD positivity, and relapse. Functional assays demonstrated that CD109-positive Nalm6 cells exhibited significantly increased proliferative and invasive abilities.

## Conclusion
CD109 is upregulated in B-ALL and promotes leukemia cell proliferation and migration. Its consistent expression across disease stages suggests it may be a promising marker for MRD detection, highlighting its clinical significance in leukemia management.

## Introduction
B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is a hematologic malignancy originating from lymphoid progenitor cells, characterized by abnormal proliferation and accumulation of immature B lymphoid cells within the bone marrow and lymphoid tissues. Despite remarkable improvements in survival achieved through risk-adapted chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, relapse remains a major cause of treatment failure. Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring has become an essential prognostic tool and is widely used to guide risk-adapted treatment decisions [ ]. The identification of novel markers associated with the pathogenesis and progression of B-ALL is expected to improve risk stratification, enhance early detection of resistant clones, and facilitate the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
Contemporary classification of B-ALL integrates immunophenotypic and molecular genetic markers to define biologically and clinically distinct subtypes and establishes the paradigm for risk stratification and treatment personalization. Genomic profiling has revealed recurrent structural and sequence alterations with prognostic and therapeutic implications. Favorable cytogenetic features include high hyperdiploidy and t (12; 21) (p13; q22)/ETV6-RUNX1, whereas high-risk lesions encompass t (9;22)/BCR-ABL1, KMT2A rearrangements, and IKZF1 deletions. Emerging entities such as Ph-like (BCR-ABL1–like) ALL harbor kinase-activating lesions amenable to targeted inhibition [ ]. In parallel, immunophenotypically, multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) enables the delineation of B-lineage developmental stages, from pro-B to common and pre-B maturation phases, based on the expression of markers such as CD19, CD10, CD34, and cytoplasmic immunoglobulin. Advances in high-resolution flow cytometry have further identified novel antigens including CD123, CD200, NG2, and CD73 that improve diagnostic precision and facilitate MRD monitoring. Dikshat Gopal Gupta et al. analyzed hematological, clinical, immunophenotypic characterization, and treatment outcomes of prognostically significant genetic subtypes of 1021 B‐ALL patients from India and found that CD36 is a strong statistically significant predictive marker of BCR:: ABL1‐like ALL [ ].
CD109 is a cell surface protein expressed on various cell types, including activated T cells, platelets, CD34 + bone marrow cell subsets, and mesenchymal stem cells [ , ]. It is also present in tissues such as myoepithelial cells of the testes, salivary glands, lacrimal glands, and basal prostate cells [ ]. CD109 is highly expressed in various tumors, including squamous cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, and breast cancer, where it regulates key processes like proliferation, differentiation, and migration through TGF-β, JAK/STAT, and EGFR pathways [ – ]. These findings highlight its potential as a cancer therapeutic target.
CD109 has been studied predominantly in the context of solid tumors, but its biological significance and clinical relevance in hematologic malignancies remain largely unexplored and are gaining attention. Early studies identified CD109 expression in the CD34 + CD38lowCD90 + CD133 + hematopoietic stem cell subset, with expression decreasing as cells mature [ ]. Jason H et al. reported CD109 upregulation in elderly patients with NPM1-mutated AML harboring RUNX1 mutations [ ]. Roman Hornung et al. confirmed CD109 overexpression in RUNX1 + AML patients, suggesting its potential as a molecular target for AML [ ]. Sang et al. found high CD109 mRNA in CD34 + peripheral blood stem cells and fibroblasts [ ], while Maki et al. associated increased CD109 expression with poor prognosis in diffuse large B cell lymphoma [ ]. Recent studies have identified CD109 as a potential biomarker in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), implicating its involvement in immune modulation and inflammatory signaling [ ]. These observations suggest that CD109 may participate in the pathogenesis of hematologic malignancies and could serve as a prognostic marker.
In this study, we investigated the mRNA and protein expression levels of CD109 in patients with B-ALL patients using real-time quantitative PCR (RQ-PCR) and MFC. We analyzed the associations between CD109 expression and various clinical and laboratory parameters. We further investigated whether CD109 could be a promising marker for MRD detection. In addition, the cell function of CD109 in leukemia cell line was also explored.

## Patients and methods

## Oncomine database
The Oncomine database (http://www.oncomine.org) provides large-scale tumor gene microarray data from published literature, GEO, and TCGA, including RNA and DNA sequencing data.

## Patients
Bone marrow samples were collected from B-ALL patients treated in the department of Hematology, the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, from January 2019 to March 2023. A total of 75 B-ALL patients were enrolled for CD109 mRNA expression analysis, including 50 newly diagnosed cases, 8 cases in complete remission (CR), and 17 relapsed cases. Additionally, bone marrow samples from 17 healthy donors for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation were used as controls. For the detection of CD109 membrane protein by flow cytometry, an independent cohort of 62 B-ALL patients were analyzed, consisting of 41 newly diagnosed and 21 relapsed cases. Furthermore, bone marrow samples from 20 follow-up B-ALL patients with MRD-positive status, as determined by flow cytometry, were collected. To serve as controls for normal precursor B lymphocytes, bone marrow samples were obtained from 8 B-ALL patients in MRD-negative status and from 19 acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients in the recovery phase following complete remission (CR) after treatment. All samples were obtained with informed consent from the subjects. The diagnosis was based on the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [ ].

## RNA isolation and RQ-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 10⁶ cells using Trizol (Takara, Japan) and assessed for concentration and purity by spectrophotometry (OD260/OD280 ratio of 1.8–2.0) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg RNA with N6 primers using MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, USA). RQ-PCR was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan) on a 7500 Thermo Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). Cycling conditions included 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 61 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 31 s. Relative CD109 mRNA expression was quantified using the comparative Ct method, normalized to GAPDH, and calibrated to healthy donor samples. GAPDH was selected as the housekeeping gene (HKG) for normalization of gene expression, as studies have demonstrated its suitability and consistent expression stability in similar experimental settings [ ]. Primer sequences were: CD109 forward 5′-TAGCAGTCCACATGTCCGAAAGCA-3′, reverse 5′-AACCAGTAGCCACCCAAGAAGTGA-3′; GAPDH forward 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3′, reverse 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′.

## Flow cytometry analysis
At diagnosis, fresh EDTA anticoagulated BM cells (106 leukocytes per tube) were analyzed using a Navios flow cytometer (Beckman, USA). CD109 expression was analyzed within the gated leukemic blast population identified by CD45dim/SSC low characteristics combined with B-lineage markers and the presence of leukemia-associated immunophenotypic aberrancies (LAIP). Gating was performed sequentially as follows: 1) FSC/SSC to exclude debris and doublets, 2) CD45/SSC to identify the CD45dim/SSC low blast region, 3) identification of leukemic B-lineage cells using CD19, CD10, CD34, and LAIPs such as asynchronous marker expression (CD13, CD33, CD66c, etc.) or over/underexpression relative to normal maturation (CD38, CD81 et al.). Once the leukemic blast population was gated, CD109 expression was evaluated within this population. In the evaluation CD109 expression of MRD status, the combinations panel of mAbs for B-ALL MRD detection were CD81-FITC, CD123-PE, CD22-ECD, CD13/CD33-PC5.5, CD38-PC7, CD34-APC, CD10-APC-A700, CD19-APC-A750, CD20-PB, and CD45-KrO. After accurately identifying these aberrant B-ALL MRD populations, we assessed CD109 expression specifically within these gated MRD-positive cell clusters. All listed monoclonal antibodies are given in Supplementary Table 1.
The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD109 was calculated as the ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity of CD109-stained cells to that of the corresponding isotype control in the same gated leukemic population. A 20% threshold was used to define CD109 positivity, following standard guidelines for surface markers in hematologic malignancies. [ , ] Data were analyzed using Kaluza Analysis 2.1 software.

## Cell culture and flow cytometry sorting
The leukemia cell line Nalm6 was cultured with RPM-1640 supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum and incubated in 5% CO2. CD109-positive and CD109-negative cells were sorted separately by fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS) MoFlo Astrios (Beckman Coulter, USA). The purity of sorted CD109pos and CD109neg cells was assessed using a Navios flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using Kaluza Analysis 2.1 software.

## Cell proliferation assay
Cell proliferation was measured using cell counting and MTT assays. CD109pos and CD109neg Nalm6 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in 24-well plates, and cell numbers were counted daily using a hemocytometer for 5 days. For the MTT assay, cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were plated in 96-well plates with five replicates per group. On days 1–3, 100 μL of 1640 medium with 0.5 mg/mL MTT was added and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by medium replacement with 100 μL RPMI-1640. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

## Colony formation assay
Five hundred cells were plated in six-well plates with 1.5 mL methylcellulose medium and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 10–14 days. Colonies (diameter > 40 μm) were stained with 0.1% crystal violet, imaged, and counted.

## Transwell cell assay
Cells (2 × 105) in 200 μL serum-free medium were seeded into 8-μm Transwell inserts, with 900-μL medium containing 20% BSA in the lower chamber. After 48 h, non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells were fixed in methanol, stained with 0.4% crystal violet, and washed with PBS. Migrated cells on the underside of membranes were imaged (× 10 magnification) and manually counted in five random fields per membrane.

## Statistical analysis
Data analysis and graphical processing were performed using SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 9 software. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Differential expression analysis of the CD109 gene using the oncomine database
To investigate the clinical significance of CD109 in patients with B-ALL, we conducted data analysis using the publicly available Oncomine database [ ]. The median CD109 expression was 0.304 in normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), 1.298 in B-ALL, 1.694 in Pro-B-ALL, and 1.661 in pediatric B-ALL patients. Compared with normal PBMCs, CD109 expression was significantly upregulated in both Pro-B-ALL and pediatric B-ALL patients (P < 0.001). Additionally, CD109 expression was elevated in AML and T-ALL, while it was downregulated in CLL, CML, and MDS (Fig.  ). 

## CD109 gene mRNA expression in B-ALL patients and healthy donors
Relative quantification of CD109 gene mRNA expression levels was performed in bone marrow samples from 50 newly diagnosed, 8 complete remission, and 17 relapsed B-ALL patients using RQ-PCR. The results demonstrated a significant increase in CD109 expression in both newly diagnosed and relapsed patients compared to healthy donors (P < 0.001). The median expression levels were 12.725 (range 0–58.835), 8.535 (range 0.033–70.375), and 1.551 (range 0.352–8.957), respectively. No significant difference was observed between remission patients [median: 2.380 (range 1.111–10.246)] and healthy donors (P > 0.05) (Fig.  -A). These findings suggest that CD109 expression is upregulated in both newly diagnosed and relapsed ALL patients, consistent with data from the Oncomine database. We also monitored CD109 expression in 10 patients at both initial diagnosis and during remission. The results revealed that CD109 expression was significantly higher at initial diagnosis compared to the remission state (Fig.  -B). According to the immunological classification criteria for B-ALL, 38 newly diagnosed cases were further classified into three subtypes: Pro-B-ALL (n = 13), Pre-B-ALL (n = 12), and common B-ALL (n = 13). No significant differences in CD109 mRNA expression were observed among these subtypes (P > 0.05) (Fig.  -C). 

## Relevance of CD109 mRNA level to the clinical and laboratory features of newly diagnosed B-ALL patients
Pearson correlation analysis revealed no significant correlation between CD109 mRNA expression and gender, chromosomal karyotype, or gene mutations. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between CD109 expression and continuous laboratory variables in newly diagnosed B-ALL patients. The results demonstrated a negative correlation between CD109 mRNA expression and both peripheral blood hemoglobin (Hb) levels (Spearman correlation coefficient: r = − 0.277, P = 0.027) and serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels (Spearman correlation coefficient: r = − 0.414, P = 0.002). No significant correlations were observed between CD109 mRNA expression and other factors, including age, peripheral blood white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, uric acid (UA) levels, or bone marrow blast percentage.
To further investigate whether the correlations were independent of potential confounders, a multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted, including age, gender, cytogenetic abnormalities, CD34 positivity, WBC, PLT, UA, and BM blast percentage as covariates. The analysis revealed that Hb level remained significantly and negatively associated with CD109 mRNA expression (β = -0.40, P = 0.049). LDH did not retain statistical significance after adjustment (P = 0.602). A trend toward higher CD109 expression was observed in patients with cytogenetic subgroup 4 (P = 0.073). The detailed regression coefficients are shown in Table  . 

## Expression of CD109 membrane protein in B-ALL patients
A total of 62 fresh bone marrow samples were collected from B-ALL patients, including 41 newly diagnosed and 21 relapsed cases. The cohort included 25 males and 37 females, with a median age of 42.3 years (range 2–84). MFC analysis revealed that the positive expression rate of CD109 in blast cells from all B-ALL patients was 79.0% (49/62), with rates of 75.6% (31/41) in newly diagnosed cases and 85.7% (18/21) in relapsed cases.

## Relevance of CD109 membrane protein expression to the clinical and laboratory features of B-ALL patients
According to the immunological classification criteria, 56 newly diagnosed B-ALL cases were classified into three subtypes: Pro-B-ALL (n = 4), Pre-B-ALL (n = 38), and common B-ALL (n = 14). No significant differences were observed in either the CD109 expression rate or the MFI among the three subtypes (P > 0.05) (Table  , S Fig.  -A, B). 
In flow cytometric immunophenotyping, a cutoff value of 20% is commonly used to define positive expression of membrane proteins, whereas a cutoff of 10% is typically applied for cytoplasmic proteins. Given that CD109 is a membrane protein, a cutoff value of 20% was used to categorize the 62 B-ALL patients into CD109-positive (n = 49) and CD109-negative (n = 13) groups. Comparative analysis showed no significant differences between the two groups in gender, age, WBC count, Hb, PLT count, absolute neutrophil count, bone marrow blast cell percentage, abnormal chromosomal karyotype, or the presence of BCR-ABL gene (P > 0.05) (Table  ). 
Given the significant differences in immunophenotype, treatment, and prognosis between Ph + B-ALL and Ph- B-ALL (often accompanied by myeloid antigen expression), we compared CD109 expression in these two groups. The analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in the expression rate or MFI of CD109 membrane protein in blast cells between Ph + (n = 27) and Ph- B-ALL patients (n = 35) (P > 0.05) (S Fig.  -C, D). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the expression rate or MFI of CD109 membrane protein in blast cells between B-ALL patients with myeloid antigen (CD13 or CD33) expression(n = 26) and those without myeloid antigen expression(n = 36) (P > 0.05) (S Fig.  -E, F).
Among the 62 B-ALL patients, 43 were CD34 + and 19 were CD34-. Comparison of CD109 expression rates and MFI in blast cells between the two groups showed that the CD109 expression rate was significantly higher in CD34 + B-ALL patients than in CD34- patients (P < 0.0001). However, no statistically significant difference in MFI was observed between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Fig.  -A, B). Correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between both the expression rate and MFI of CD109 and those of CD34 in B-ALL blast cells (Fig.  -C, D). 
No statistically significant differences were found in the positive expression rates or MFI of CD109 between CD34 + CD38- and CD34 + CD38 + blast cell populations (P > 0.05) (Fig.  -A, B). In contrast, significant differences in both the expression rates and MFI of CD109 were observed between CD34 + CD38-CD123 + and CD34 + CD38-CD123- populations (P < 0.0001) (Fig.  -C, D). Similarly, significant differences were noted in the positive expression rates (P < 0.0001) and MFI (P < 0.001) between CD34 + CD10 + CD19 + and CD34-CD10 + CD19 + blast cell populations (Fig.  -E, F). 

## The expression of the membrane protein CD109 in normal B lineage precursor cells and mature B lymphocytes.
Based on the expression patterns of antigens such as CD19, CD10, CD34, and CD45, normal B cells can be classified into the following groups: (1) Mature B cells (Gate L): Characterized by an immunophenotype of CD19 + CD10-CD34- with the strongest CD45 expression; (2) Pre-B precursor cells (Gate J): characterized by an immunophenotype of CD19 + CD10 + CD34-, with relatively strong CD45 fluorescence intensity; (3) Pro-B precursor cells (Gate K): Identified as CD19 + CD10 + CD34 + with weaker CD45 fluorescence intensity. The three cell populations can also be distinguished in the CD45/SSC two-dimensional plot. The CD109 expression levels in pro-B cells, pre-B cells, and mature B cells were [0.65 (0.00–3.99)] %, [4.93 (0.00–18.92)] %, and [0.67 (0.00–4.23)] %, respectively (Fig.  -A). Thus, using a 20% threshold, CD109 is not expressed in normal B lineage precursor cells or mature B lymphocytes. Through analysis, we found that CD109 expression was negative (less than 20%) in normal mature B cells and B cell precursors at various stages. When comparing the CD109 expression rate and MFI of mature B cells, pre-B precursor cells, and pro-B precursor cells with B-ALL blasts, significant differences were observed between all three populations and B-ALL blasts (P < 0.0001) (Fig.  -B, C). 

## The potential application of membrane protein CD109 in the detection of MRD in B-ALL.
In a study of 20 MRD-positive B-ALL patients, CD109 expression in bone marrow leukemia cells was analyzed. The results revealed that 17 patients (85%) were CD109-positive, while 3 patients (15%) were CD109-negative. In a follow-up study of 8 CD109-positive B-ALL patients, CD109 expression was monitored at diagnosis, during MRD positivity, and at relapse. The findings demonstrated that CD109 expression remained consistently positive at all stages, indicating stable expression (Table  ). A comparison of CD109 expression rates and MFI between the initial diagnosis and MRD-positive states in these 8 patients showed no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) (S Fig.  ). 

## The impact of CD109 on the Nalm6 cell line.
CD109 expression in the Nalm6 leukemia cell line was assessed using MFC, which revealed a positive expression rate of 41.01%. To further investigate the role of CD109 in leukemia cells, CD109-positive and CD109-negative cell populations were sorted using a flow cytometer and verified. The results showed that the positive expression rates of CD109 in the CD109-positive and CD109-negative populations were 98.47 and 4.58%, respectively (Fig.  ). 
Cell growth curves and MTT assays demonstrated increased cell proliferation in Nalm6-CD109-positive cells compared to CD109-negative cells (P < 0.05) (Fig.  -A, B). Clonogenic assays revealed enhanced clonogenic ability in Nalm6-CD109-positive cells (P < 0.05) (Fig.  -C, D). Transwell migration assays showed that CD109-positive cells exhibited stronger invasive potential (P < 0.05) (Fig.  -E, F). 

## Discussion
The incidence of B-ALL has been increasing, with complete remission (CR) rates of 60%–90%, but only 30%–50% of patients achieve a disease-free survival (DFS) of three years or more. Most CR patients eventually relapse, leading to significant efforts to understand the biological mechanisms of B-ALL and develop targeted therapies [ ]. Therapies including CD19, CD20, CD22 antibodies, and CAR-T cells have improved prognosis but face challenges like drug resistance and relapse [ ]. Residual leukemia cells are the primary cause of relapse. MFC is commonly used to detect MRD, but immune phenotype drift in leukemia cells can compromise accuracy [ , ]. Identifying stable MRD markers before and after treatment is a key focus of research.
CD109 is highly expressed in various tumors, including lung, breast, and cervical cancers, and promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [7,8,910]. However, its role in B-ALL is not well studied. This study found that CD109 expression in bone marrow mononuclear cells of primary and relapsed B-ALL patients was higher than in healthy controls, while in remission, expression was similar to normal subjects. MFC showed a positive expression rate of 79% in newly diagnosed and relapsed B-ALL patients, suggesting that CD109 may represent a promising candidate marker for B-ALL leukemia cells.
An effective MRD marker for B-ALL should be specifically expressed on leukemia cells and stable on residual cells post-treatment. Distinguishing normal precursor B cells from B-ALL leukemia cells is challenging due to the differentiation of B lymphocytes in the bone marrow. CD34 and CD19 are commonly used in B-ALL immunophenotyping; however, both CD34 and CD19 are physiologically expressed on regenerating normal B cell precursors, making it difficult to distinguish between residual leukemic blasts and normal cells based solely on these markers. Myeloid antigens such as CD13, CD33, CD66, and CD123 are useful for MRD detection, but they have low positive rates. This study demonstrates that CD109 is not expressed in normal B precursor cells, but exhibits stable expression during initial diagnosis, MRD, and relapse phases of B-ALL. These findings suggest CD109 may serve as a promising biomarker candidate for MRD detection in B-ALL. Notably, CD109 should not be utilized as an independent MRD marker; instead, its application must be integrated into conventional MRD panels and gating strategies. As to whether CD109 enhances the sensitivity and specificity of conventional MRD cytometric methods, further verification through large-scale cohort studies will be required. We also acknowledge that the heterogeneity in CD109 expression exists among patients, the factors such as immunophenotypic diversity and disease stage may contribute to differential expression levels and combining CD109 with established MRD markers may help mitigate this heterogeneity and improve detection accuracy.
The study also identified CD109 expression in CD34 + CD38-CD123 + leukemic stem cells (LSCs), indicating that CD109-positive cells may serve as the source of B-ALL LSCs. Furthermore, cell function assays demonstrated that CD109 promotes the proliferation and invasion of leukemia cells, highlighting its potential role in the progression of B-ALL. Studies have shown that CD109 inhibits TGF-β signaling in hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs), and the lack of CD109 may increase the risk of phosphatidylinositol glycan class A (PIGA) reducing the sensitivity of HSPCs to TGF-β [ ]. Yokoyama M discovered that high expression of CD109 weakens the activity of Smad2 phosphorylation, which inhibits the TGF-β signaling pathway and that CD109 could be a biomarker for poor prognosis in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients [ ]. In-depth mechanistic studies employing CD109 knockdown or overexpression models will be essential to define its functional roles, including its regulation of key signaling pathways such as TGF-β and JAK-STAT in B-ALL.
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The relatively limited sample size, especially in subgroup analyses of newly diagnosed, relapsed, and remission cases, may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future research should focus on large-scale prospective validation of CD109 as an MRD marker across diverse patient populations, integration of CD109 profiling with established genomic risk factors such as KMT2A rearrangements or Ph-like profiles, and development of standardized detection protocols. Furthermore, while functional assays demonstrated that CD109 promotes leukemic cell proliferation and migration, the underlying mechanisms mediating these effects were not fully elucidated. Moreover, exploring therapeutic strategies targeting CD109 itself through monoclonal antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors—holds promise for advancing precision diagnostics and treatment in B-ALL.
In conclusion, CD109 is elevated in B-ALL and promotes the proliferation and migration of leukemia cells, making it a potential MRD marker. However, further research with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up is required to confirm its role in MRD monitoring and B-ALL pathogenesis.
