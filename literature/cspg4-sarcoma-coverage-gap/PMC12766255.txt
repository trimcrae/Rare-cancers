## Background
Anorexia is a complex eating disorder influenced by genetic, environmental, psychological, and socio-cultural factors. Research into its molecular mechanisms and neural circuits has deepened our understanding of its pathogenesis. Recent advances in neuroscience, molecular biology, and genetics have revealed key molecular and neural circuit mechanisms underlying anorexia.
Aim of review: Clarify the peripheral and central molecular mechanisms regulating various types of anorexia, identify key cytokines and neural circuits, and propose new strategies for its treatment.
Key scientific concepts of review: Anorexia animal models, including activity-induced, genetic mutation, and inflammation-induced types, are explored for their relevance to studying the disorder. Anorexic behavior is regulated by cytokines, hormones (like GDF15, GLP-1, and leptin), and neural circuits such as AgRP, serotonergic, dopaminergic, and glutamatergic pathways. Disruptions in these pathways, including GABAergic signaling in AgRP neurons and 5-HT2C and D2 receptors, contribute to anorexia. Potential therapies target neurotransmitter receptors, ghrelin receptors, and the GDF15-GFRAL pathway, offering insights for treating anorexia, immune responses, and obesity.

## Introduction
Anorexia is a broad term referring to severe eating disorders caused by various factors, characterized primarily by extreme restriction of food intake. Anorexia nervosa is a serious psychiatric disorder marked by self-imposed starvation and malnutrition, often accompanied by high rates of comorbid psychiatric conditions, significant resistance or lack of response to treatment, frequent medical complications, and an elevated risk of mortality . Anorexia nervosa predominantly affects females, with approximately 80 % to 95 % of diagnosed cases occurring in women , . The most common age of onset is between 12 and 25 years, with a particularly high incidence during early to mid-adolescence (ages 14 to 18) , . The lifetime prevalence of anorexia nervosa in females is estimated to be around 0.5–4 %, while the prevalence in males is significantly lower, approximately one-tenth that of females , . Anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric disorders, with studies indicating that its mortality rate may reach 1.9 % to 5.9 % , , , . The primary causes of death include suicide and complications related to severe malnutrition, such as heart failure and multi-organ failure.
Anorexia nervosa can be categorized into two subtypes based on specific clinical phenotypes: the restricting type (AN-R) and the binge-eating/purging type (AN-BP). Individuals with the AN-R achieve weight loss primarily through strict limitation of food intake, often characterized by extreme dieting behaviors, avoidance of high-calorie or high-fat foods, and reduction of total daily caloric intake . Patients with AN-BP often exhibit a combination of restrictive eating behaviors and episodes of binge-eating, followed by compensatory actions such as self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic use to prevent weight gain , . Both AN-R and AN-BP patients exhibit compulsive exercise for weight control, with AN-R patients sometimes showing a higher prevalence of excessive exercise .
In addition to anorexia nervosa, various other forms of anorexia are recognized in clinical practice, arising from diverse etiologies. These include cancer-related anorexia or cachexia-associated anorexia, geriatric anorexia, pregnancy-related anorexia, infantile anorexia, functional anorexia, drug-induced anorexia, anorexia associated with psychiatric disorders, and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder. Although these forms of anorexia differ in their etiologies and manifestations from anorexia nervosa, research indicates the overlapping mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of different types of anorexia. These mechanisms may represent crucial targets for addressing this complex disorder. Currently, various animal models of anorexia have been developed, including activity-based anorexia, cancer-related anorexia, inflammation-related anorexia, stress-induced anorexia, and genetically modified mouse models. Research utilizing these models has revealed the complex and interconnected underlying mechanisms of anorexia. Herein, we review high-quality animal studies on anorexia from the past decade, providing a comprehensive analysis of various forms of anorexia through the lens of animal models, molecular mechanisms, and therapeutic strategies. This review aims to elucidate both the distinct and shared aspects of different types of anorexia, offering theoretical guidance for the treatment of clinical anorexia.

## Animal models of anorexia

## Activity-based anorexia model
Researchers have developed various animal models to closely replicate the clinical characteristics of anorexia patients ( ). Among these models, the activity-based anorexia (ABA) model has emerged as one of the most representative and is currently the most widely used approach in anorexia nervosa research. The background of the ABA model originates from a study conducted by Routtenberg and Kuznesof in the 1960 s. They discovered that combining food restriction with voluntary access to a running wheel induced a state of self-imposed starvation in rats. This state is characterized by excessive running, resulting in significant weight loss and potentially leading to mortality . Subsequent studies confirmed that the voluntary, high levels of physical activity in food-restricted rats closely resemble the excessive exercise and self-imposed dietary restrictions observed in patients with anorexia nervosa , , , . Of interest, animal studies indicate that prolonged food restriction is associated with increased systemic ghrelin levels, correlating with shifts in activity preferences .Voluntary wheel running under food deprivation is known to modulate ghrelin consumption. This interplay may underpin the rationale for the development of the ABA model. Another neuropeptide, orexin, has been implicated in promoting physical activity in anorexia nervosa. In the ABA mouse model, significant activation of hypothalamic orexin neurons is observed, and orexin antagonists have been shown to alleviate hyperactivity in ABA model mice . 
Recent research has confirmed multiple phenotypes within the ABA model that align with the disease characteristics of anorexia nervosa patients. For example, proteomic analysis revealed an increase in mitochondrial fission in the hypothalamus of ABA model mice, accompanied by enhanced autophagy and protein synthesis, indicating adaptive response to stress or metabolic demands . The late-stage high frequency of gastric ulcers observed in rats within the ABA model, initially referred to as stress-induced ulcers, is analogous to the gastrointestinal complications seen in clinical patients with anorexia nervosa, which are significant contributors to mortality , , , . In fact, a critical benchmark for the success of the ABA animal model is achieving a weight loss of 25 % from baseline, while stress-induced ulcers generally occur when weight loss exceeds 30 % , , . Additionally, amenorrhea is a common clinical feature in female patients with anorexia nervosa , . In the ABA model, weight loss in female rats leads to the cessation of the estrous cycle and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, closely resembling the disruption of gonadal and adrenal hormones observed in young female patients with anorexia nervosa , , .
Systematic reviews of animal models of anorexia nervosa reveal that the rodent ABA model constitutes the majority of studies . Key parameters observed in these models include changes in body weight, physical activity, food intake, estrous cycle in female animals, behavioral alterations, as well as metabolic and hormonal changes. This model holds promise for advancing research into treatments and management strategies for anorexia nervosa.
Despite effectively simulating several core features of anorexia nervosa, the ABA model has limitations. Notably, the sex difference observed in the ABA model diverges from clinical patterns. Male C57BL/6 mice in the ABA model demonstrate greater reductions in food intake, more significant weight loss, and higher mortality rates compared to female C57BL/6 mice, contrary to the higher prevalence of anorexia nervosa observed in females clinically . However, female mice display increased physical activity during both feeding and post-feeding phases. Compared to acute starvation (40 % food intake of baseline over 2 days), female C57BL/6 mice in the chronic starvation ABA model (45 %-70 % food intake of baseline over 12 days) exhibit more pronounced symptoms of anorexia nervosa, including hyperactivity, amenorrhea, and brain atrophy . Additionally, mice with a 25 % reduction in body weight, compared to a 20 % reduction, demonstrate more severe anorexia-related symptoms such as hyperactivity and brain atrophy . Notably, approximately 50 % of C57BL/6 mice exhibit “resilience” in the ABA model (body weight loss < 25 % in 10-day ABA model), maintaining relatively stable body weight and survival through self-regulated food intake and physical activity, whereas 68 % show resilience under simple food restriction .
Overall, the ABA model effectively recapitulates two key features of anorexia nervosa: self-imposed food restriction and voluntary excessive exercise. However, variations in mouse age, sex, strain, and experimental protocols lead to inconsistencies in phenotype stability. Additionally, the model primarily captures exercise as the sole compulsive behavior, limiting its ability to fully reflect clinical manifestations. Therefore, continuous refinement of the ABA model is necessary to better represent the complex clinical and pathological characteristics of anorexia nervosa.

## Anorectic anx/anx mouse model
The Anorexia (anx) mouse model, first described by Maltais et al. in 1984, carries a lethal mutation associated with notable physiological and behavioral abnormalities, including reduced food intake, severe weight loss, premature death, head weaving, tremors, hyperactivity, and gait ataxia . Although anx/anx mice share certain features with human anorexia nervosa, such as low leptin levels and increased free fatty acids, their rapid health decline and short lifespan limit their application as a long-term model for anorexia research , . Additionally, these mice exhibit abnormal organ development, including increased brain and thymus weight, reduced spleen weight, pancreatic dysfunction, glucose intolerance, complicating their use for extended studies in anorexia nervosa . While these characteristics reflect the complex pathophysiology of the anx/anx model, they also present challenges in its application for sustained anorexia research.

## BDNF gene mutation model
Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is recognized as a central appetite suppressant, as selective deletion of BDNF in the adult mouse hypothalamus induces hyperphagia and obesity, while activation of BDNF/ tropomyosin receptor kinase B signaling enhances satiety , . However, meta-analysis reveals that serum BDNF levels in females with anorexia nervosa are significantly lower than those in healthy individuals . This reduction may result from chronic malnutrition and energy restriction, highlighting the distinct roles of central and peripheral BDNF in anorexia nervosa. Notably, during the recovery from anorexia nervosa, BDNF levels significantly rise, possibly reflecting neuroregeneration after damage induced by malnutrition , , .
Screening of the BDNF gene in patients with AN-R and healthy controls revealed a strong association between AN-R and the Met allele of the Val66Met polymorphism in the BDNF gene . Variants in multiple gene loci of BDNF, including the −270C/T, −374A/T, and −256G/A single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), correlate with pathological symptoms and personality trait in anorexia nervosa , , , , , , . Furthermore, SNPs alterations in the BDNF receptor, neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor type 2, are associated with susceptibility to eating disorders and related phenotypes . However, studies on anorexia nervosa population from different regions have not consistently shown a significant association between the BDNF Val66Met polymorphism and anorexia nervosa susceptibility or symptoms , , . This discrepancy adds complexity to evaluating BDNF as a reliable marker and therapeutic target for anorexia nervosa.
In animal studies, adolescent female SD rats exposed to the ABA model exhibit amygdala hyperactivity and downregulated BDNF signaling, which persist into the recovery phase . Further research in the ABA model revealed that in female BALB/CJ mice, transcription levels of BDNF in the mesocorticolimbic reward circuitry are modulated by both dietary restriction and exercise. Food restriction alone increased BDNF transcription in the hippocampus but decreased BDNF mRNA levels in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Wheel running elevated BDNF mRNA expression in the ventral tegmental area (VTA). Interestingly, in the ABA model, no significant changes in BDNF transcription were observed in these circuits . Furthermore, different mouse strains exhibit varying levels of BDNF expression when exposed to the ABA model, with A/J mice showing lower baseline and ABA-induced BDNF expression compared to C57BL/6J mice, suggesting a higher anorexia nervosa susceptibility in A/J mice . Studies with BDNF Val66Met allele variant SD rats revealed that neither heterozygous nor homozygous variants exhibited significantly increased sensitivity to the ABA model or notable differences in feeding behavior . In contrast, another study reported that adolescent BDNF-Val66Met variant mice exposed to repeated stress exhibited enhanced anorexic behaviors under food restriction .
Consistent with clinical observations, these findings underscore ongoing uncertainties regarding the role of BDNF in the anorexia nervosa animal model. While BDNF may represent a compensatory mechanism within the ABA model, its genetic variations alone do not yet fully recapitulate the clinical and pathological features of anorexia nervosa.

## Stress-Induced model
Chronic psychological and physical stress can trigger or exacerbate disordered eating behaviors by affecting neurobiological pathways involved in appetite, mood regulation, and decision-making. A key response mechanism to stress is activation of HPA axis, which, when dysregulated, can lead to increased cortisol levels . This hormonal imbalance can suppress appetite and lead to weight loss . In anorexia nervosa patients, stress can intensify restrictive eating patterns, sustaining the cycle of malnutrition and anxiety, thereby complicating recovery.
In animal models, stress is typically not applied in isolation but rather acts as a “catalyst”, enhancing the susceptibility of other models. For instance, the application of repeated stress to adolescent mice with BDNF gene mutations significantly enhances the anorexia-like phenotype . In the novelty-suppressed feeding assay, C57BL/6 mice exhibit increased feeding latency and reduced food intake when placed in a novel environment, coinciding with lower plasma ghrelin levels . Prolonged social isolation in adolescent female mice further exacerbates this phenotype, while male mice remain unaffected . Additional studies have demonstrated that under stress conditions, activation of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of female mice inhibits the signaling of ghrelin, thereby exacerbating anorexic behaviors . Following two weeks of isolation during the preadolescence, C57BL/6 mice demonstrate significantly increased food anticipatory activity (animals exhibit abnormally high activity levels before scheduled feeding, resembling the excessive exercise observed in individuals with anorexia nervosa) and greater weight loss in the ABA paradigm , indicating a heightened risk for anorexia nervosa in socially isolated adolescent females.
Patients with anorexia nervosa often perceive the experience of hunger as pleasurable, using it to alleviate anxiety associated with food restriction. Activation of hypothalamic agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons induces a state of hunger in mice, which is typically aversive under normal physiological conditions. Interestingly, repeated exposure to stress stimuli leads some female mice to develop a preference for AgRP activation, actively seeking out the hunger state . These females have been shown to be anxiety-prone, suggesting a potential link between stress-induced AgRP preference and anxiety susceptibility.
Maternal gestational stress also impacts susceptibility to anorexia and metabolic outcomes in offspring. Chronic unpredictable stress applied to pregnant ICR/CD1 mice results in sexually dimorphic eating disorders in their offspring, with female offspring demonstrating notable resistance to anorexia-like behaviors, while male offspring exhibit metabolic dysregulation . The observed resistance to anorexia in female offspring contrasts with the expected influence of stress on anorexia vulnerability, challenging conventional understandings of stress-related anorexia risk. However, subsequent research by Schroeder et al. provided further insight into this phenomenon, revealing that prenatal stress (PNS) leads to a marked reduction in microRNA-340 expression in the placenta of PNS-exposed fetuses. MicroRNA-340 is implicated in mediating anorexia susceptibility, and its increased methylation under PNS conditions enhances the expression of various nutrient transporters and neurotrophic factors, thereby reducing vulnerability to ABA .
Though stress alone does not constitute a comprehensive anorexia nervosa model, it significantly increases susceptibility when combined with other factors. Furthermore, the heightened vulnerability to anorexia observed in female mice under stress exposure suggests the importance of considering the risk of anorexia nervosa in adolescent females facing social, familial, and environmental stressors. This underscores the need for attention to stress-related factors in understanding and addressing the development of anorexia nervosa in this population.

## Inflammation model
Inflammation-induced anorexia refers to reduced food intake and weight loss triggered by the immune response to infection or chronic inflammation. During inflammation, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) are released, which can affect appetite regulation in the brain, particularly in the hypothalamus , . Clinical studies suggest that post-exercise appetite suppression may be linked to increased IL-6 and cortisol levels alongside reduced active ghrelin levels . In malignant pleural mesothelioma patients, anorexia symptoms correlate with elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, C-reactive protein, and vascular endothelial growth factor levels . Currently, clinical studies on peripheral and central inflammatory states in patients with anorexia nervosa remain limited.
Lipid polysaccharide (LPS) is the most commonly used agent for establishing animal models of inflammation-induced anorexia. In contrast to the high susceptibility observed in female mice within the ABA model, LPS induces a more severe inflammatory response and anorectic behavior in male mice . Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), acting synergistically with LPS, triggers microglial inflammatory responses upon ventricular injection of its ligand and leads to anorexia, reduced activity, and hyperthermia in mice . Activation of hypothalamic microglia induced by TLR2 enhances the activity of pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons, leading to increased α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) signaling to the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and exacerbating feelings of satiety. Additionally, LPS and pro-inflammatory factors have been shown to inhibit hypothalamic AgRP neurons, leading to a decreased sensation of hunger and reduced food intake , . These findings demonstrate that LPS disrupts the balance of central nervous system (CNS) activity involved in the regulation of hunger and satiety. Inflammation may increase gut permeability, allowing bacterial compounds such as LPS or peptidoglycan to cross the intestinal barrier into the blood stream , . This process can trigger a vicious cycle of chronic inflammation, further promoting anorexia.

## Anorexia model in cancer-cachexia syndrome
Cancer cachexia is a complex syndrome characterized by severe weight loss, muscle wasting, and metabolic dysregulation, often accompanied by anorexia . Subcutaneous or renal capsule implantation of HT-1080 tumor cells in female immunodeficient mice successfully established a tumor-induced cachexia-anorexia model . This model exhibited phenotypes such as reduced food intake, weight loss, systemic inflammatory response, and decreased activity levels. Numerous studies have shown that myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88) is a key mediator of cancer cachexia-induced anorexia. Blocking of MyD88 can alleviate cancer-associated anorexia and reduce both peripheral and central inflammatory responses , . Interestingly, the MyD88 responsible for mediating inflammation and cancer cachexia anorexia does not originate from brain endothelial or neuronal cells, but rather from hematopoietic/myeloid cells , .
Another cancer-related anorexia model uses cisplatin treatment to mimic neurotoxicity and anorexic behavior induced by clinical chemotherapy. In this model, cisplatin treatment induces anorexic behavior in mice by suppressing the activity of AgRP neurons and enhancing the activity of POMC neurons in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (Arc). Cisplatin-induced dysregulation of hypothalamic neuronal activity is triggered by the excessive production of endothelial peroxynitrite, leading to neuronal toxicity and oscillations by targeting caspase-1 . Consequently, neuroinflammation serves as a significant mechanism underlying anorectic behavior in both tumor-bearing and cisplatin treatment models.
Cancer cachexia-related anorexia is also influenced by altered hypothalamic neural activity. Proteomic analysis of the hypothalamus in mice implanted with methylcholanthrene-induced sarcoma, in comparison to food-restricted and ad libitum-fed mice, revealed increased expression of proteins such as dynamin 1, hexokinase, and pyruvate carboxylase in cancer cachexia-induced anorexia . These proteins mediate signaling activities related to melanocortin, inflammation, and glucose metabolism. Additionally, a significant upregulation of hypothalamic transcripts encoding cocaine-and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART) and nesfatin-1 was observed , both of which are neuropeptides that suppress food intake, highlighting the role of hypothalamic dysregulation in cancer-induced anorexia. However, different tumor cell transplantation models in mice may result in varying patterns of disordered eating. For instance, C26 tumor-bearing mice, generated by subcutaneous injection of C26 colon adenocarcinoma cells, exhibit significant weight loss without the development of anorexia , . These mice show an increase in food intake around 15 days post-injection, potentially linked to decreased central and peripheral 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT; serotonin) levels. Thus, 5-HT levels may be a key determinant of food intake across different types of cancer.
In addition to neurobiological alterations in the hypothalamus, activation of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) neurons in the parabrachial nucleus (PBN) has been observed in mice implanted with Lewis lung carcinoma cells. The activation of these neurons is associated with the transmission of anorexia-related signals. Notably, inhibiting CGRPPBN neurons not only reverses Lewis lung carcinoma −induced anorexia but also increases food intake in non-anorexic gastrointestinal tumor-bearing mice, leading to a reduction in weight loss .
Currently, widely used models, such as tumor cell implantation and cisplatin-induced chemotherapy models, replicate certain clinical features of cancer cachexia-related anorexia. However, the complexity and variability inherent in cancer-induced anorexia highlight that no single model fully captures all aspects of the condition, necessitating further refinement and development of more comprehensive models.

## Mycotoxin-induced anorexia models
Mycotoxin-induced anorexia models are used to study how mycotoxins—secondary metabolites produced by fungi—affect appetite suppression and feeding behavior in animals. Mycotoxins, such as deoxynivalenol (DON), aflatoxins, ochratoxins, and zearalenone, can contaminate food sources and then cause anorexia or reduced food intake. Following intraperitoneal administration of DON in C57BL/6 mice, male mice exhibited more pronounced anorexic behavior and heightened systemic inflammation . The prolonged hepatic retention of DON in male mice suggests a sex-specific metabolic difference, potentially contributing to the greater susceptibility to DON-induced anorexia in males.
In female B6C3F1 mice, oral administration of DON induces anorexic behavior and significant weight loss. This effect is accompanied by elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, specifically TNF-α and IL-1β, in the liver, spleen, and plasma . Preemptive blockade of these pro-inflammatory cytokines has been shown to alleviate the anorexic effects induced by DON. Additionally, oral or intraperitoneal administration of four distinct trichothecene mycotoxins (T-2 toxin, HT-2 toxin, diacetoxyscirpenol, and neosolaniol) in female B6C3F1 mice has been shown to induce severe anorexia . This effect is accompanied by elevated levels of peripheral 5-HT and substance P, suggesting a strong neuroimmune interaction that contributes to the anorexic response.
Mycotoxin-induced anorexia is closely associated with the reactive secretion of appetite-regulating hormones of gastrointestinal system. Type A trichothecenes have been shown to induce significant secretion of cholecystokinin (CCK) in the gut of mice , . Furthermore, mycotoxins also promote the secretion of various satiety-inducing gut peptides, including peptide YY (PYY), glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) , , . Specific receptor inhibitors for these hormones and peptides can mitigate anorexic behavior induced by mycotoxins. Additionally, it has been identified that the calcium-sensing receptor and transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 have been implicated in the regulation of CCK and PYY from gastrointestinal enteroendocrine cells . Elderly male mice exhibit more severe metabolic disturbances and inflammatory responses to mycotoxins, resulting in a heightened susceptibility to anorexia . In mice with anorexia induced by T-2 toxin, the increased abundance of gut microbiota, including Faecalibaculum and Allobaculum, positively correlates with elevated levels of satiety hormones, peptides, and inflammatory factors . This suggests that the alterations in gut microbiota may play a significant role in the mechanisms underlying anorexia, likely by affecting metabolic and inflammatory responses.
In addition to inflammatory responses and gastrointestinal endocrine mechanisms, mycotoxins stimulate the activity of several anorexigenic neurons in the NTS, Arc, PVN, and supraoptic nucleus. These neurons include those expressing POMC, CART, nesfatin-1 and melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) . This indicates that mycotoxins can directly affect the CNS and establish intrinsic connections with other anorexia models.

## Key signals regulating anorexic behavior

## Growth differentiation factor 15 signaling
Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), a stress-induced cytokine belonging to the TGF-β superfamily, has been implicated in inducing anorexia in animals by promoting nausea and vomiting . GDF15 transmits anorexigenic signals via the GDNF family receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), which is expressed only in the area postrema (AP) and NTS, projecting to the PBN and central amygdala (CEA) , . Further research has identified that GFRALAP/NTS neurons represent a subset of CCK neurons, and disrupting CCK signaling in cancer and chemotherapy models can block GDF15-GFRAL-mediated anorexic behavior . Additionally, recent research has identified a group of ADCYAP1+ neurons in the AP/NTS as necessary and sufficient mediators of LPS-induced anorexia . Chemogenetic activation of these neurons not only leads to anorexic behavior but also induces other sickness responses, highlighting the critical role of AP/NTS neurons in disease-induced anorexia.
GDF15 is regulated by its family member GDF11, with excessive GDF11 supplementation inducing SMAD family member 2/3 binding to the GDF15 promoter, thereby upregulating GDF15 expression and leading to cachexia and anorexia in mice . In chemotherapy models, cisplatin treatment elevates GDF15 expression in the liver and circulation, resulting in appetite suppression and weight loss through the inositol-requiring enzyme-1 (IRE1α)/X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) signaling pathway, which plays a role in cancer progression . Blocking IRE1α can rescue chemotherapy-induced anorexia mediated by GDF15. Thus, GDF15 serves as an intermediary signal mediating cancer cachexia-associated anorexia, regulated by upstream and downstream molecular pathways.
GDF15 serves as a biomarker for mitochondrial dysfunction. In transgenic mice with impaired mitochondrial function, GDF15 derived from muscle exhibits diurnal expression patterns, with elevated circulating levels during the day driving anorexia in the mice . Moreover, exercise significantly increases GDF15 expression in the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue of mice, which is associated with an increased ratio of glucagon to insulin during exercise . Since simple food restriction does not elevate circulating GDF15 levels in mice, exercise-induced GDF15 expression may contribute to the imbalance of feeding and exercise behavior in anorexia nervosa and serve as a potential molecular mechanism underlying ABA model development ( ). 

## Glucagon-like peptide-1 signaling
GLP-1 is a critical hormone produced in both the gut and brain that regulates appetite and glucose metabolism by promoting insulin secretion. GLP-1 exerts its anorexigenic effects by activating GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1R) both centrally and peripherally, contributing to the suppression of food intake. In the CNS, GLP-1R activation in the hypothalamus and brainstem regions regulates satiety signals, while peripheral activation slows gastric emptying, enhancing feelings of fullness .Meanwhile, IL-1 and IL-6 mediate the anorectic effects of GLP-1/GLP-1R in the hypothalamus and hindbrain . GLP-1R activation in the hypothalamus stimulates glycolysis in a glucose-dependent manner and inhibits AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation , . AMPK is a crucial nutrient and energy sensor that regulates cellular metabolism and plays a pivotal role in maintaining the balance between energy demand and supply. The reciprocal relationship between AMPK and inflammatory cytokines suggests that GLP-1R activation disrupts AMPK signaling and exacerbates inflammation, contributing to anorexia. Conversely, AMPK activation may be a promising therapeutic approach for managing anorexia.
Peripherally, in mouse models of T-2 toxin or DON-induced anorexia, plasma levels of GLP-1 and CCK are significantly elevated, and exogenous GLP-1 administration mimics this anorexic effect , . The inhibitory effect of GLP-1 signaling on food intake is particularly pronounced in obese individuals and mouse models, making it a potential strategy for obesity intervention , , . However, GLP-1 administration or enhanced GLP-1 secretion also significantly suppresses food intake in normally fed mice .Therefore, GLP-1 regulates appetite and glucose metabolism through central and peripheral GLP-1R activation, with its anorexigenic effects particularly pronounced in obesity but also effective in normal feeding conditions ( ).

## Adipokine-mediated signaling
Leptin is a hormone produced primarily by adipose tissue that plays a crucial role in regulating energy balance and appetite by signaling to the hypothalamus to suppress hunger. Leptin regulates hypothalamic appetite control by activating nucleobindin-2 (NUCB2)/nesfatin-1 in the PVN, while clusterin enhances leptin signaling via LDL receptor-related protein 2 (LRP2) and leptin receptor upregulation. Blocking NUCB2, clusterin, or LRP2 inhibits leptin-induced anorexia and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway activation , , . Recent studies have revealed that leptin activates a subset of neurons in the Arc expressing basonuclin 2 (BNC2), leading to a significant suppression of food intake. Deleting leptin receptors in BNC2 neurons results in pronounced hyperphagia and obesity . This finding resolves the paradox that leptin induces anorexia through the activation of POMC neurons, whereas acute activation of POMC neurons produces only mild anorexic behavior . These studies refine the distinct mechanisms of leptin signaling in hypothalamic nuclei regulating feeding, aiding targeted leptin-based interventions for anorexia.
Additionally, leptin regulates feeding behavior and energy metabolism based on cellular structural function. For instance, a selective shortening of neuronal cilia in the hypothalamus of leptin-deficient mice occurs, leading to increased food intake and resistance to the anorectic effects of leptin, insulin, and glucose . Leptin treatment promotes the reassembly of neuronal cilia, resulting in reduced food consumption and negative energy balance.
A novel adipokine called mimecan functions similarly to leptin but acts independently in the context of anorexia. Mimecan exerts its effects by upregulating the hypothalamic levels of IL-1 and IL-6, thereby generating satiety signals that inhibit food intake . Notably, this effect is observed in both leptin receptor-deficient mice and mice with ectopic expression of AgRP, highlighting its potential role in appetite regulation beyond the leptin signaling pathway.
Thus, adipokines play a crucial role in regulating food intake by modulating neuroendocrine signaling and inflammatory responses, highlighting significant potential for further exploration ( ).

## Tumor-associated signaling
Inflammatory cytokines and immune responses are key drivers of cancer cachexia-associated anorexia. In fact, tumor-derived factors and signaling pathways play a critical role in regulating food intake both peripherally and centrally. Tumor-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) exacerbates anorexia by promoting hypothalamic inflammation through the disruption of intestinal barrier function . Notably, PGE2-mediated anorexia varies by mouse strain, with the PGE2-synthesizing enzyme mPGES-1 knockout alleviating LPS-induced anorexia in DBA/1 but not in C57BL/6 mice . This suggests that genetic background influences susceptibility to PGE2-related anorexia, with genetic polymorphisms potentially affecting PGE2-mediated anorexic responses in humans.
In mice implanted with colon cancer cells, insulin-like peptide 3 (INSL3) levels were significantly elevated, which regulated the feeding hormones NUCB2 and neuropeptide Y (NPY) in the brain via the G protein-coupled receptor Lgr8. Similarly, Dilp8 (the drosophila homolog of INSL3) mediated the expression of appetite-regulating factors NUCB1 and neuropeptide F in the drosophila brain through Lgr3 . This conservation suggests INSL3 as a potential target for treating cancer-induced anorexia.
Bone-derived lipocalin 2 (LCN2) was initially discovered to cross the blood–brain barrier and act as a ligand to activate anorexigenic MC4R neurons in the PVN and ventromedial nuclei (VMN) . Subsequent studies revealed a significant upregulation of LCN2 in a pancreatic cancer mouse model, which mediates anorexia in an MC4R-dependent manner. The widespread expression of LCN2 is associated with the expansion and migration of neutrophils from the bone marrow in mice with cancer, and inhibiting LCN2 can reduce cancer-related anorexia, muscle wasting, and mortality . In a mouse model of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, there is significant myeloid cell infiltration into the velum interpositum, along with C–C chemokine receptor 2 expression . Activation of the C–C chemokine ligand 2/ C–C chemokine receptor 2 axis enhances neutrophil recruitment, creating a positive feedback loop that exacerbates anorexia. Additionally, inhibiting the purinergic receptor P2RX7 reduces anorexia mediated by immune cell infiltration . Thus, the migration of immune cells and cytokine secretion in the tumor microenvironment are significant contributors to the development of anorexia ( ).
Acyl-CoA binding protein (ACBP) is an appetite-stimulating factor, elevated in obese patients and decreased in individuals with anorexia nervosa. Injection or neutralization of ACBP in mice resulted in corresponding increases in food intake or anorexic responses . Furthermore, ACBP application in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Caenorhabditis elegans enhanced nutrient acquisition strategies, with ACBP promoting yeast sporulation to seek new food sources, highlighting its conserved role in nutrient-driven behaviors . Biotin-induced elevation of plasma ACBP, achieved through ACBP protein modification and chemogenetic approaches, reverses anorexia caused by chronic restraint stress or chemotherapy , . Mechanistically, ACBP reduces anorexigenic factor levels and inhibits the LCN2/MC4R signaling pathway in the hypothalamic VMN.

## Estrogen and estrogen receptor signaling
Gender differences in anorexia, particularly in anorexia nervosa, suggest that estrogen signaling plays a critical role. The whole genome sequencing has revealed rare missense mutations in the estrogen-related receptor alpha (ESRRA) and histone deacetylase 4 gene in the DNA of families with a history of eating disorders . Both mutations inhibited the transcriptional activity of ESRRA. Animal studies have demonstrated that ESRRA knockout in female mice leads to a diminished motivation for food acquisition, particularly regarding high-fat diets (HFD), along with social functional impairments. Additionally, adult female mice with mPFC-specific ESRRA knockout have exhibited similar phenotypes . Neuroelectrophysiology studies have shown that female mice with ESRRA deficiency exhibit abnormal miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents in the medium spiny neurons of ventral striatum, leading to a reduction in presynaptic glutamatergic vesicles and enhanced glutamate receptor 1 function . The ventral striatum is a critical neural structure involved in reward mechanisms and motivational regulation, which may explain the impaired food reward and motivation observed in ESRRA-deficient mice.
Recent research indicates that estrogen activates the STAT3 signaling pathway involved in anorexic behavior, but this occurs through the G protein-coupled receptor 30 (GPR30) rather than the classical ERα or ERβ . Intracerebroventricular administration of GPR30 agonists directly promoted the phosphorylation of STAT3 in the Arc. In female mice subjected to stress, the activation of ERα in the NTS exacerbates anorexic behavior, even in ovariectomized mice . However, administration of ERα antagonist reversed stress-induced anorexia by inhibiting the signaling of ghrelin, suggesting that ERα mediates anorexia independently of estrogen ( ). These findings suggest that ESRRA deficiency may lead to compensatory estrogen signaling through GPR30, thereby enhancing anorexic behavior via STAT3 activation, independent of classical ERα-mediated pathways.

## Specific nutritional factor signaling
When animals are subjected to a long-term diet lacking one or more essential amino acids, they exhibit rapid and sustained anorexia, a phenomenon referred to as “anorexia-like malnutrition” or “anorexia-like behavior,” serving as an adaptive response to nutritional deficiency .
In C57BL/6 mice fed a valine-deficient diet, a significant reduction in food intake and body weight was observed, alongside increased hypothalamic somatostatin mRNA expression . Similarly, male SD rats on a valine-deficient diet exhibited pronounced anorexic behavior and weight loss. In these rats, efferent projections from the anterior piriform cortex to the parasubthalamic nucleus (PSTh) activated corticotropin-releasing hormone neurons . Furthermore, a leucine-deficient diet activated general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2), a conserved protein kinase that senses intracellular amino acid deficiency, in the mouse hypothalamus. In response to leucine deprivation, GCN2 reduces protein synthesis by phosphorylating eukaryotic initiation factor 2α . Blocking the GCN2/ eukaryotic initiation factor 2α pathway rescues the anorexia induced by a leucine-deficient diet. Hypothalamic AMPK signaling-induced autophagy regulates food intake by modulating the expression of the appetite-stimulating neuropeptide NPY and the anorexigenic neuropeptide POMC . Thiamine (vitamin B1) deficient induces anorexia by suppressing hypothalamic AMPK phosphorylation . Thus, abnormal hypothalamic neural activity is a key mechanism underlying nutritional factor deficiency-induced anorexia. This pathway may also serve as a potential therapeutic target for other forms of anorexia.
Nutrient deficiencies may alter food responses, potentially triggering or exacerbating anorexia. Vulnerable populations, such as children or individuals with restrictive dietary patterns, may develop aversions to food in the face of nutritional inadequacy, creating a vicious cycle.

## Gut microbiota changes
Alterations in the gut microbiota and gut barrier dysfunction are key features in patients with eating disorders and animal model, and these changes in adolescent anorexia nervosa patients persist into the early stages of weight recovery , . Intestinal permeability in anorexia nervosa patients appears to be severity-dependent, with increased permeability observed in severe cases (BMI 14.1–15.2) and decreased permeability in patients with a BMI of 16.97 ± 1.22 , . In the mycotoxin-induced anorexia model, an increased abundance of Faecalibaculum and Allobaculum species was observed, which promoted elevated levels of gastrointestinal hormones (CCK, GIP, GLP-1, and PYY), neurotransmitters (5-HT), and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) . In the ABA mouse model, the primary cause of gut microbiota alterations is dietary restriction, with minimal influence from physical activity . In both food-restricted and ABA model mice, phosphoglycerate kinase levels are elevated in the feces, and proteomic modifications in Clostridiales bacteria in the gut promote ATP production as an adaptive response to starvation .
Gut microbiota changes not only serve as a biomarker for the onset of anorexia but also directly contribute to the progression of the disorder. Altering the gut microbiota of mice through antibiotic treatment or using germ-free mice in the ABA model leads to changes in the expression of both orexigenic and anorexigenic neuropeptides in the hypothalamus . Mice with antibiotic-induced gut microbiota depletion exhibited significant sex differences in ABA model induction, with distinct plasma adipokine expression patterns between males and females. Male mice exhibited reduced expected physical activity, accompanied by increased anxiety-like behavior .
These sex differences are also observed in fecal transplantation experiments involving anorexia nervosa patients. Significant differences in gut microbiota, virome and serum metabolomics were observed between female anorexia nervosa patients and matched healthy controls . These alterations correlated closely with the metabolic profile of anorexia nervosa patients. Compared to germ-free female mice transplanted with healthy human feces, those receiving feces from AN-R patients show reduced body weight, decreased food intake, and increased anxiety and compulsive behaviors , . Furthermore, fecal transplantation from anorexia nervosa patients increases hypothalamic BDNF expression in mice, consistent with BDNF’s role in central appetite suppression. AN-R-transplanted mice also exhibited lower brain 5-HT levels. Although Bacteroides supplementation rescued compulsive behavior, it fails to alleviate anorexic behavior or prevent weight loss. However, study found that fecal transplantation from female anorexia nervosa patients did not directly affect mouse body weight and was unrelated to the anorexia nervosa condition or disease progression . This discrepancy may be partly attributed to the small sample size (only four patients) and the use of mixed-gender mice in the experiment, which may have masked sex-specific effects.
Caseinolytic protease B (ClpB), produced by Escherichia coli and other gut bacteria, is a molecular mimic of α-MSH. Chronic gastric administration of E. coli in mice induces ClpB-specific IgG antibodies that cross-react with the anorexigenic peptide α-MSH, attenuating α-MSH-mediated satiety and anxiety-like behaviors . In eating disorders patients or ABA model mice, an increase in Enterobacteriaceae in the gut and elevated plasma ClpB levels have been observed , . Notably, transplantation of E. coli lacking ClpB does not affect feeding behavior in mice. Beyond the observed indirect link between gut microbiota and feeding behavior, recent studies show that bacterial peptidoglycans from the gut reach the brain, inhibiting GABAergic neurons via Nod-like receptor 2 (Nod2) in the hypothalamic Arc, thereby triggering anorexic behavior . This confirms that hypothalamic neurons can directly detect structural components of the gut microbiota and regulate feeding behavior.

## Endocannabinoid system
Endocannabinoids, such as anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol, enhance appetite and feeding behavior by binding to cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2). In the ABA model using female SD rats, a significant downregulation of the gene encoding CB1 was observed in the hypothalamus and nucleus accumbens (NAc), which correlated with enhanced DNA methylation of the gene’s promoter region . Administration of CB1/CB2 agonists alleviated the hyperactivity and weight loss observed in the ABA model . Positron emission tomography revealed increased availability of CB1 receptors in various brain regions of individuals with eating disorder, including the cortex and subcortical areas in patients with anorexia nervosa, which was closely correlated with the BMI , . Animal studies demonstrated a significant enhancement of CB1 availability in the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and entorhinal cortex of female ABA model rats . This increased receptor availability may result from a negative feedback mechanism induced by the inhibitory effects of the endocannabinoid system in anorexia ( ).

## Emerging signals in recent research
In addition to classical signaling pathways, recent studies have identified several novel regulatory mechanisms associated with anorexia. For example, it has been demonstrated that the activation of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) disrupts hypothalamic appetite-regulating neural activity by inhibiting the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor subunit NR2A, thereby exacerbating the phenotypes of ABA model . Hyperactivity, anxiety, and self-imposed food restriction in mice lead to the reactivation of SIRT1, creating a positive feedback loop. Blocking SIRT1 is crucial for breaking this cycle and providing a therapeutic approach for anorexia nervosa. Increased SIRT1 signaling in the basolateral amygdala mediates anorexia, anhedonia, and despair-like behaviors in mice under chronic unpredictable mild stress, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of anorexia nervosa .
Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that bile acids function not only as digestive agents but also as important signaling molecules that regulate food intake. Postprandial or peripheral administration, bile acids enter the brain and inhibits the release of the orexigenic neuropeptides AgRP and NPY in a G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (GPBAR1)-dependent manner, resulting in anorectic effects . Notably, both systemic and neuron-specific GPBAR1 knockouts in mice significantly increased food intake, further highlighting the critical role of peripheral cytokines or metabolic products in modulating CNS activity ( ).

## Key neurons and neural projections regulating feeding behavior

## AgRP neural circuits
AgRP is an orexigenic neuropeptide expressed in the Arc of the hypothalamus and regulates feeding behavior by modulating postsynaptic excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. AgRP neurons send GABAergic signals to the PBN, suppressing anorexia induced by excessive postsynaptic neuronal excitation . Notably, melanocortin-3 receptor (MC3R) is expressed in 97 % of AgRP neurons, and blocking AgRP neurons abolishes MC3R-induced feeding behavior. Activation of MC3R neurons increases food intake by promoting presynaptic GABA release from AgRP neurons onto MC4RPVN neurons . Ablation of AgRPArc neurons not only results in the loss of GABAergic signaling to the PBN but also induces excessive accumulation of the NR2B of the NMDA receptor in PBN , . Inhibition of NR2B maintains feeding behavior after AgRP neuron ablation. Together, these changes lead to hyperexcitability in the PBN, contributing to anorexia. Recent findings highlight a GABAergic regulatory circuit linked to feeding, involving lateral hypothalamic (LH) GABAergic projections to postsynaptic GABAergic neurons in the diagonal band of broca (DBB), driving food intake and reducing anxiety . Furthermore, hypothalamic AgRP neurons directly target anorexigenic CGRPPBN neurons. Anorexia induced by lithium chloride (LiCl) and LPS activates CGRP neurons, while optogenetic stimulation of AgRP neurons reverses CGRP neurons-mediated anorexic behavior , . The regulatory role of AgRP neurons in feeding has been well validated in the ABA mouse model, where chemogenetic activation of AgRP neurons during the progression of ABA can rescue anorexic behavior by increasing the frequency of food intake and regulating non-feeding behavior and energy metabolism , , highlighting the importance of the AgRPArc-to-PBN projections in regulating feeding.
Although AgRP neurons are widely recognized as essential for feeding and weight regulation, a study using different methods to ablate AgRPArc neurons of adult male and female mice observed no significant changes in free feeding or body weight . This appears to contradict existing findings; however, the study did not employ more restrictive feeding conditions, such as food restriction or the ABA model. Therefore, the activation and inhibition of appetite-regulating neurons do not necessarily produce strictly opposing effects ( ). 

## POMC neural circuits
Traditionally, POMC neurons are recognized as key regulators of feeding suppression, as noted earlier. However, recent studies suggest their role in feeding regulation is more nuanced. POMC neurons in the Arc and NTS exhibit distinct functions. Acute activation of POMCNTS neurons immediately suppresses feeding, whereas POMCArc neurons require chronic stimulation to reduce food intake . Therefore, leptin-induced anorexic behavior in the Arc is not primarily mediated by POMC neurons. While chronic inhibition of POMC neurons leads to hyperphagia and obesity, chronic activation of POMCArc neurons does not induce the expected anorexia or weight loss, further challenging previous findings . Instead, POMC neurons appear to primarily protect against weight loss.
AgRP and POMC neurons in the Arc represent prototypical feeding-promoting and −suppressing mechanisms. BDNF-expressing neurons in the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) receive monosynaptic input from AgRPArc and POMCArc neurons. Integrated signals activate BDNFVMH neurons, which project to the perimesencephalic trigeminal area (pMe5) to suppress mandibular movement, thereby reducing food intake. Inhibiting BDNFVMH neurons projections to pMe5 leads to hyperphagia ( ). Both orexigenic and anorexigenic neurons regulate feeding through more than a simple binary activation-inhibition model. Their feedback-driven homeostatic mechanisms may be crucial for maintaining normal food intake and energy balance.

## Glutamatergic neural circuits
Glutamatergic neurons inhibit feeding by transmitting excitatory signals via NMDA receptors. Direct glutamatergic projections from the NTS to the PBN suppress food intake. Genetic blockade of glutamatergic signaling in the NTS can reverse anorexic behavior caused by AgRP neuron ablation . The ventral hippocampus (vHPC), which is involved in regulating emotion and cognition, decreases food intake by projecting glutamatergic signals to the lateral septum (LS). Disrupting either the output or input of this circuit diminishes its inhibitory effect on feeding . Stress-related anorexia has been linked to glutamatergic pathways from the mPFC to the LH, where stimulation of these projections reduces food intake. Blocking this circuit can increase food consumption in mice under mild stress . Similarly, glutamatergic projections from the basal forebrain (BF) to the VTA also mediate stress-induced inhibition of feeding . These findings underscore the broad regulatory role of glutamatergic signaling in feeding behavior ( ).

## 5-HT signals
5-HT is a neurotransmitter that plays a crucial role in regulating mood, appetite, and energy balance. In mouse models of inflammation-induced anorexia, increased turnover of 5-HT in the hypothalamus has been observed . Dysregulation of the serotonergic system, particularly involving 5-HT receptors such as 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C, has been implicated in appetite regulation, where 5-HT imbalances contribute to appetite suppression and anxiety related to food intake , . However, variations in the location of 5-HT receptors and the dosage of agonists can lead to different effects on appetite behavior. Administration of 5-HT2C −specific agonist suppresses food intake in wild-type mice, but selective activation of 5-HT2C in Sim1PVN neurons significantly increases food intake. Correspondingly, selective ablation of 5-HT2C in Sim1PVN neurons leads to anorexic behavior . Mechanistically, activation of 5-HT2C in Sim1 neurons promotes G protein alpha coupling and activates postsynaptic KATP channels, resulting in hyperpolarization of PVN neurons and inhibition of anorexic behavior. Another study found that expressing 5-HT2C only in POMC neurons or activating MC4R in Sim1PVN neurons can also mediate 5-HT-induced anorectic behavior Activation of 5-HT1A in wild-type mice induces significant anorexic behavior; however, in 5-HT1A knockout mice, despite reduced expression of the anorexigenic neuropeptides POMC and CART, food intake and body weight are not increased . This discrepancy may be attributed to severe anxiety and stress responses resulting from 5-HT1A knockout.
The 5-HTR4 receptor subtype also plays a key role in food regulation. Knockout or overexpression of 5-HTR4 in the mPFC results in corresponding binge-eating and hypophagia under basal conditions . 5-HTR4 knockout suppressed stress-induced appetite loss, while re-expression of 5-HTR4 restored this phenotype. These feeding behaviors are associated with the modulation of 5-HT release from the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) and the expression of 5-HT1A and 5-HT transporters. 5-HTDRN neurons are also involved in a light-responsive neural circuit that regulates feeding. Light-dependent activation of lateral habenular nucleus (LHb) neurons promotes 5-HTDRN neuronal activity and reduces daytime food intake. Chemogenetic activation of the LHb-5-HTDRN neural circuit mimics daytime light conditioning and reduces nighttime feeding . Furthermore, overexpression of 5-HTR4 in the NAc upregulates NAc-CART, triggering anorexia and hyperactivity in mice . Blocking the NAc-5-HTR4/CART pathway can rescue ecstasy-induced anorexia and hyperactivity ( ).
These findings highlight the complex regulatory influence of the serotonergic system in feeding and anorexia. The inconsistent roles across various neurons make serotonin an essential target in understanding feeding balance.

## Dopaminergic neurons
DA plays a pivotal role in regulating emotion, motivation, and reward mechanisms, with DA neurons in the NAc being central to feeding behavior regulation. Knockdown of the DA transporter induces excessive DA accumulation, increasing anorexia susceptibility in C57BL/6J mice, characterized primarily by maladaptive running behavior and weight loss . DA receptors are critical hubs in the regulation of feeding balance. Overexpression of the D2 receptor in the NAc core increased physical activity and food intake in both male and female mice under normal conditions. However, in the ABA model, D2 receptor overexpression rescues hyperactivity and reduced food intake in male mice but has no effect in females . This suggests that D2 receptor overexpression in the NAc contributes to sex differences in ABA model development, further highlighting the heightened susceptibility of female mice to ABA. Recent studies suggest that the coordinated regulation of D1 and D2 receptor neurons in the NAc is central to maintaining feeding stability. Under ad libitum feeding conditions, the activation of D2 receptors increases food intake, while inhibition of D2 receptors decreases food intake. D1 receptors exhibit the opposite effect. By recording the activity of D1NAc and D2NAc receptor neurons, it was found that neuronal activity during feeding is biased towards D2 receptor neurons, and it is biased towards D1 receptor neurons during running . When the DA+ neuronal system is chronically imbalanced, neuronal activity tends to favor D1 receptor neurons, which may explain the anorexic behavior associated with dopamine accumulation. Chemogenetic activation of the VTA-NAc dopaminergic circuit induces hyperactivity in rats and enhances resistance and survival in ABA model mice , . However, DA release in the NAc is negatively regulated by glutamatergic neural activity from the BF to the VTA .
D1 and D2 receptor neurons directly influence 5-HTDRN neurons through VTA-DRN projections to regulate feeding behavior. High concentrations of DA in the VTA enhance D1 receptor neuron activity, activating 5-HTDRN neurons and inducing anorexia. Conversely, lower DA levels in the VTA induce D2 receptor neuron-dependent inhibition of 5-HTDRN neurons, increasing food intake in mice . Thus, D1 and D2 receptors on 5-HTDRN neurons act as a regulatory switch, influencing anorexic behavior in models such as ABA ( ).

## NTS neuronal projections
Neural connections between the NTS and the PBN play a critical role in conveying satiety signals. Following 24-hour fasting, refeeding activates CCK neurons and dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH) neurons in the NTS. Optogenetic activation of CCKNTS and DBHNTS directly leads to reduced food intake. These neurons directly activate CGRPPBN neurons through synaptic connections . This represents a form of aversive feeding suppression. Another neural projection from the NTS to the PBN involving the activation of calcitonin receptor (CALCR) neurons in the NTS activates non-CGRP neurons in the PBN to control energy balance through non-aversive feeding inhibition . Visualization of synaptophysin in the NTS of CCKcre and CALCRcre mice highlighted differences in the projection targets and neuronal activation levels. Similarly, optogenetic activation of terminal target neurons in these projections inhibits feeding. In addition to projections to the PBN, GLP-1NTS neurons project to the Arc, which is rich in GLP-1R, activating GLP-1/GLP-1R signaling and significantly suppressing food intake ( ).

## Central amygdala neurons
The lateral subdivision of the CEA contains a subset of neurons labeled with protein kinase C-delta (PKC-δ), known to mediate various anorectic signals, such as those induced by CCK, LiCl, and LPS. The pharmacogenetic inhibition of PKC-δ+ neurons can rescue anorexia caused by CCK and LiCl, although not for LPS-induced effects . The study further confirmed that PKC-δ- neurons exhibit opposing effects, emphasizing the distinct roles of these neuronal subsets in regulating feeding. Using optogenetics and c-Fos immunostaining, it was found that unilateral activation of PKC-δCEA neurons exerts disynaptic control over PSTh neurons, specifically activating PKC-δ+ input neurons while inhibiting PKC-δ- input neurons . Peripheral injection of CCK directly activates PSTh neurons, and inhibition of PSTh neurons increases food intake in mice injected with CCK, thus identifying the CCK-mediated CEA-PSTh anorexigenic neural circuit. However, the characteristics of these PSTh neurons have not yet been defined.
Although PKC-δCEA neurons are not the most sensitive neurons mediating inflammation-induced anorexia, a study has identified a group of PKC-δ+ neurons in the oval region of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (ovBNST) that can be activated by inflammation. Silencing PKC-δ+ neurons in ovBNST attenuate inflammation-induced anorexia . Like the inhibition of PKC-δ- neurons by PKC-δ+ neurons in CEA , PKC-δovBNST neurons inhibit neurons in the ventrolateral BNST (vlBNST). Tracing the upstream inputs to PKC-δovBNST neurons and the downstream targets of vlBNST neurons revealed that PKC-δovBNST neurons primarily receive anorexigenic signals from the Arc, PBN, and CEA, while vlBNST neurons project to the LH to promote feeding. Recent studies have found that PKC-δCEA and PKC-δovBNST neurons collectively mediate the development of anorexic phenotypes in the ABA model, and ablation of either neuronal group alone is insufficient to completely prevent ABA-induced anorexia . Activation of PKC-δCEA or PKC-δovBNST neurons alone was sufficient to suppress food intake, but only dual ablation suppressed ABA model formation ( ).

## Development of bioactive molecules for anorexia treatment

## Noncompetitive and competitive inhibition of neurotransmitter receptors involved in appetite regulation
Ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, blocks glutamate-induced NMDA receptor activation, thereby reducing excessive excitability in the CNS . A single or three consecutive injections of a non-anesthetic dose of ketamine (30 mg/kg) can effectively improve anorexia and anxiety behaviors in adolescent female mice in the ABA model, as well as enhance resilience to anorexia nervosa in adulthood , , . However, due to uncertainties regarding its long-term efficacy and potential side effects, ketamine remains an experimental treatment for anorexia nervosa. A pathological report documented a 29-year-old woman with a 15-year history of anorexia nervosa who achieved complete reversal of chronic symptoms, including obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviors, three months after treatment with a ketogenic diet and ketamine injections . However, other reports indicate that severe anorexia nervosa patients may experience respiratory depression following ketamine treatment, highlighting potential risks associated with sedative drugs .
In cisplatin-induced anorexia mice, injection of the 5-HT3 receptor competitive antagonist (granisetron) can alleviate early anorexic behavior but not the continuous phase . The combination of granisetron and dexamethasone has become the standard treatment for chemotherapy-induced nausea, vomiting, and anorexia, serving as both a foundation and a reference for developing new therapies . Additionally, the histamine receptor 4 competitive antagonist (JNJ7777120) can eliminate anorexia throughout the disease phase and reduce TNF-α expression in the hypothalamus and spleen . The histamine receptor 4 antagonist also promotes orexin expression in the hypothalamus, while the H3 antagonist (VUF5681) inhibits orexin receptor activity . The combined use of JNJ7777120 and the H3 inverse agonist (ciproxifan) can completely reverse cisplatin-induced anorexia.
DA receptor antagonists are also effective in treating anorexia. Both the D2/3 receptor competitive antagonists, sulpiride and amisulpride, as well as the selective D2 and D3 antagonists, including L-741626 and SB277011A, can effectively increase food intake and survival rates in ABA model mice . Studies show that amisulpride significantly promotes weight gain in patients with restrictive anorexia nervosa but has no notable effect on weight and body image anxiety or amenorrhea . The multi-receptor antagonist olanzapine also decreases food anticipatory activity and improves survival in the same model . Clinical randomized placebo-controlled trials confirm that olanzapine not only enhances appetite and weight gain in advanced cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy but also shows some efficacy in promoting weight recovery in adults with anorexia nervosa , .
A monoclonal antibody against the MC4R, along with its single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derivative 1E8a, has been developed to block MC4R signaling and inversely activate AgRP, effectively reversing LPS-induced anorexia . Pharmacokinetic analysis indicates that intravenously administered scFv 1E8a crosses the blood–brain barrier to reach target sites in the brain with minimal cardiovascular effects.

## Ghrelin and growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR) activation
Studies indicate that both female patients with anorexia nervosa and elderly patients with anorexia exhibit significantly elevated ghrelin levels, including total ghrelin, acylated ghrelin, and desacyl ghrelin, during both the acute and recovery phases compared to healthy individuals , , , . Notably, post-hospitalization, desacyl ghrelin shows a more pronounced decline, correlating negatively with BMI and positively with physical activity , . These findings suggest that ghrelin serves not only as a biomarker of disease progression but also as a continuous regulator of feeding and physical activity. The increased ghrelin levels observed in anorexia patients fail to enhance food intake, which may be a mechanism of “ghrelin resistance”.
Animal studies indicate that ghrelin and its analogs are effective in treating various types of anorexia , . Direct injection of ghrelin into elderly mice does not completely reverse age-related anorexia, which is associated with impairment of the NPY system downstream of ghrelin. However, food-derived soy-fortelin and the leaf protein rubisco-derived opioid agonists increase food intake in elderly mice by enhancing ghrelin sensitivity and activating the downstream δ-opioid receptor system, respectively , . Oral administration of the ghrelin receptor agonists Z-505 hydrochloride, HM01 and anamorelin can rescue anorexic behavior in chemotherapy and tumor-bearing mice , , . In contrast, inhibiting liver-expressed antimicrobial peptide 2 (LEAP2), an endogenous GHSR antagonist and inverse agonist that both blocks ghrelin activation and suppresses GHSR activity, enhances ghrelin sensitivity and increases food intake . In patients with cancer cachexia-associated anorexia, the plasma LEAP2/total ghrelin ratio negatively correlates with body weight changes, suggesting that LEAP2 inhibition represents a potential therapeutic strategy for anorexia .
A phase 3 randomized, double-blind clinical trial demonstrated that anamorelin significantly improves appetite and weight gain, particularly lean body mass, in patients with non-small cell lung cancer and cachexia , . Meanwhile, relamorelin accelerates gastric emptying in female anorexia nervosa patients, promoting food intake and weight gain .

## Inhibition of the GDF15-GFRAL pathway
The GDF15-GFRAL pathway plays a pivotal role in cancer cachexia-related anorexia. Neutralizing antibodies targeting GDF15, such as mAB1, are effective in reducing cisplatin-induced vomiting, anorexia, and weight loss in mice and cynomolgus monkeys . Furthermore, antibodies targeting GFRAL (3P10 and A11) have demonstrated efficacy in reducing anorexia in tumor-bearing and cisplatin-treated mice while improving survival rates by inhibiting ret proto-oncogene signaling within the GDF15-GFRAL axis , . Clinical studies show that the GDF15-neutralizing antibody ponsegromab reduces serum GDF15 levels in cancer cachexia patients, alleviates anorexia and weight loss, and demonstrates good safety and tolerability .

## AgRP signaling activation
Orexigenic AgRP neurons are transcriptionally regulated by tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 3 (TET3) gene. In response to leptin signaling, TET3 suppresses AgRP transcription via demethylation modifications, contributing to anorexic and anxiety-related behaviors . Genetic ablation of TET3 activates AgRP neurons and blocks leptin signaling. Based on this mechanism, the small molecule degrader Bobcat339, which depletes TET3 catalytic pockets, has been shown to activate multiple appetitive neurons and has emerged as a potential therapeutic agent for anorexia nervosa . Given the central role of AgRP signaling in regulating appetite, it remains a key focus in drug development targeting anorexigenic pathways.

## Chinese herbal extracts and formulations for anorexia treatment
Medicinal plant extracts represent a promising resource in drug development, showing significant efficacy in treating anorexia. Extracts from Atractylodes and onions have been shown to increase hypothalamic NPY expression, alleviating cancer- and chemotherapy-induced anorexia , . The extract of Embelia ribes Burm, specifically Embelin, along with the bioactive phenolic compounds found in citrus fruit peels, exhibits significant effects against LPS-induced anorexia , . Furthermore, aqueous extracts of saffron and safranal can effectively alleviate anorectic behaviors induced by stress . Additionally, some traditional Chinese herbal mixtures have been shown to effectively inhibit different types of anorexia, including cancer cachexia-related anorexia . Rikkunshito (traditional Chinese formula containing ginseng, atractylodes macrocephala, poria, licorice, tangerine peel, and pinellia) activates the mesolimbic reward circuitry VTA-NAc, alleviating stress-induced anorexic behavior , , . Sipjeondaebo-tang (traditional Chinese formula containing ginseng, atractylodes macrocephala, poria, licorice, prepared rehmannia glutinosa, white peony root, ligusticum chuanxiong, angelica sinensis, astragalus, and cinnamon bark) and Ninjin-yoeito (traditional Chinese formula containing ginseng, atractylodes macrocephala, poria, licorice, prepared rehmannia glutinosa, white peony root, ligusticum chuanxiong, angelica sinensis, astragalus, cinnamon bark, polygala tenuifolia, and schisandra chinensis) are effective in treating cancer- and chemotherapy-related anorexia , , , . However, the therapeutic effects of these herbal compounds on different types of anorexia remain unconfirmed by rigorous clinical studies.

## Protective role of anorexia during illness and its implications for obesity treatment

## Anorexia and immune resistance
During illness or infection, anorexia may not solely be a pathological symptom but could represent an adaptive response that enhances immune resistance. For example, in LPS-infected mice, a 50 % caloric restriction reduced fever, hyperactivity, and peripheral inflammatory factors while increasing serum corticosterone levels compared to infected mice with ad libitum feeding . This suggests an interaction between anorexic behavior and the immune system. During viral infection, type I interferons produced by plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) can activate somatostatin, which suppresses ghrelin and enhances the immune response of natural killer cells, pDCs, and CD8+ T cells at the cost of inducing anorexia . Additionally, in mice infected with Trypanosoma brucei, CD4+ T cells are found to dominate the first stage of anorexia and fat depletion, while CD8+ T cells dominate the second stage of muscle atrophy and cachexia . During the acute phase of viral infection, CD8+ T cell-induced anorexia results in profound alterations in the microbiota, including an enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila, which in turn suppresses the immune effects of CD8+ T cells . These studies highlight the complex relationship between immune function and anorexia during illness.
Although anorexia may limit the nutrients needed for pathogen replication, microbes might adapt to fasting, potentially increasing their virulence. In bacterial and viral infection models, anorexia leads to opposite outcomes. Anorexia is beneficial in bacterial sepsis, whereas glucose supplementation can prevent death caused by influenza and viral sepsis . Glucose supplementation during the anorexic phase induced by bacterial inflammation inhibited liver-derived fibroblast growth factor 21. This led to impaired thermogenesis, increased energy expenditure, and disrupted cardiac function in mice, making them more susceptible to death from endotoxemia and polymicrobial peritonitis . Research has shown that in Salmonella-infected mice, a 'tug-of-war' occurs between the bacteria and the host. Salmonella utilizes its effector SlrP to limit IL-1β-induced anorexia by inhibiting inflammasome activation. This suppression of anorexia helps maintain the host's health, facilitating the bacteria's transmission . These findings highlight the complexity and adaptive significance of anorexic behavior during disease states.

## Anorexia signaling and obesity control
Although anorexia is considered a pathological eating disorder, recent research has explored the potential of targeting anorexigenic signaling as a strategy for obesity treatment. The GDF15-GFRAL signaling pathway in the AP/NTS plays a role in both disease-induced anorexia and resistance to obesity under HFD conditions. Inhibition of GDF15-GFRAL leads to rapid obesity in HFD mice, accompanied by reduced glucose and insulin tolerance, increased inflammatory factors, and the development of fatty liver . Similarly, intact leptin signaling in the Arc is vital for obesity resistance. NG2 glial cells make direct contact with LepRArc neurons and maintain the structural and functional integrity. Ablation of NG2 glial cells directly leads to dendritic degeneration of LepRArc neurons and suppression of leptin signaling . The proper functioning of these anorexigenic signals is essential for maintaining weight and resisting obesity.
Targeting anorexigenic signaling offers a novel approach for obesity treatment. A long-acting polyethylene glycolated neurotensin peptide analogue has been developed that reduces food intake and body weight in obese mice through the melanocortin pathway without causing taste aversion . A novel H3 antagonist, tetrahydroquinazolinamines, can reduce food intake and body weight in obese mice but has no effect on normal-weight mice . The newly developed hexamethylenediamine inhibits hypothalamic NPY and increases POMC through the two binding proteins MYH9 and ACTG1, leading to reduced food intake and weight loss in diet-induced obese mice . Although hexamethylenediamine does not induce other pathological behaviors in obese mice, it does lead to weight loss in mice on a normal diet.
ClpB, a molecular mimic of α-MSH produced by intestinal bacteria, has led to the development of the probiotic strain Hafnia alvei HA4597, which effectively promotes weight loss and enhances satiety in obese mice and overweight individuals , . Given substantial evidence linking gut microbiota closely to central appetite regulation, targeted modulation of these microorganisms represents an important therapeutic strategy for anorexia and obesity.
Recently, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a promising therapeutic option for obesity management. Drugs like liraglutide and semaglutide, which mimic the effects of GLP-1, have been developed to improve glycemic control and promote weight loss. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists in promoting weight loss. For example, semaglutide, administered as a weekly injection, has shown an average weight reduction of 15–20 % in obese individuals over 68 weeks . However, challenges such as side effects, cost, long-term safety, and the need for ongoing treatment remain , . Future research may help address these issues, including the development of more convenient delivery methods, better side effect profiles, and long-term efficacy studies to fully establish GLP-1 agonists as a cornerstone in obesity management. Anti-obesity drugs developed using anorexigenic signals should focus on balancing the excessive excitation of neuronal activity without falling into the opposite extreme of food negative balance.

## Conclusion and perspective
Over the past two decades, extensive and in-depth research has been conducted on various types of anorexia, primarily focusing on anorexia nervosa, cancer cachexia-induced anorexia, as well as anorexic behaviors induced by inflammation and stress responses. While the clinical manifestations of these conditions can differ widely, extensive animal studies have constructed a comprehensive 'universe of anorexia mechanisms' that encompass both peripheral and central pathways. Overall, existing animal models of anorexia partially reflect clinical disease characteristics. For instance, the ABA and stress-induced models predominantly affect female mice, whereas anorexia associated with inflammation, mycotoxins, and gut microbiota alterations appears more pronounced in males. This distinction facilitates more targeted future research. However, current animal studies primarily focus on food intake suppression and neural mechanisms, lacking comprehensive long-term assessments of weight, psychological factors, and other clinically relevant parameters. This limitation makes it challenging to determine whether the models—particularly inflammation, stress, and food contamination models—fully align with clinical anorexia, despite their broad classification under the term. Advanced technologies such as optogenetics, chemogenetics, and gene editing have unraveled complex neural circuits involved in feeding behavior, primarily centered in the hypothalamus, and have identified novel neuron populations involved in appetite regulation. These insights have paved the way for the development of bioactive molecules targeting key signaling pathways, with promising therapeutic results across multiple forms of anorexia.
The potential protective and adaptive aspects of anorexia, especially in the context of obesity, underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of eating disorders. Going forward, we anticipate that emerging technological tools will enable a deeper, more precise exploration of anorexia. This will likely lead to the identification of the most crucial therapeutic targets, advancing the development of effective treatments for diverse anorexic conditions.
