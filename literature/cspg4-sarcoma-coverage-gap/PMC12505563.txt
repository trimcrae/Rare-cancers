Owing to multiorgan involvement, metastatic cancers are now clinically difficult to cure and often have a very poor prognosis. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have shown that the establishment of a premetastatic niche (PMN) is necessary for tumor metastasis. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which are closely related to tumor development, are deeply involved in regulating PMN establishment. By regulating EVs, metabolism and other pathways, CAFs actively shape microenvironmental characteristics, including inflammation, angiogenesis, increased vascular permeability, lymphangiogenesis, immunosuppression and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. With the development of single-cell sequencing technology, our understanding of the role and significance of heterogeneous CAFs in the PMN has improved. Multiple therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs in the PMN have also been developed. This article focuses on the crosstalk of different subtypes of CAFs with other stromal cells, the mechanisms by which CAFs of different origins mediate PMN formation, and the emerging cancer therapeutic strategies by which CAFs have been targeted in recent years.

## Introduction
Tumor metastasis, also known as the invasion-metastasis cascade, is a multistep process that involves the arrival of cancer cells at the target organ and their adaptation to the foreign tissue microenvironment [ ]. When tumor metastasis occurs, systemic dissemination of tumor cells is incurable. Therefore, metastasis remains the leading cause of cancer death [ ]. With the increasing research on tumor metastasis, a fundamental stage in the metastatic process is the establishment of the PMN prior to metastasis. The “seed and soil” hypothesis provides an explanation for organotropism in tumor metastasis: the PMN provides fertile “soil” for the colonization of metastatic tumor cell “seeds”, which promotes tumor metastasis [ ]. Specifically, primary tumors induce adaptive changes in the microenvironment of target organs through a series of chain reactions, providing a favorable environment for tumor metastasis and infiltration, and forming a premetastatic niche.
CAFs play a central role in the formation of this complex ecological niche [ ]. In fact, even before metastasis occurs, signals (e.g. exosomes, soluble factors) originating from the primary tumor can activate local fibroblasts in the distal organs or recruit circulating precursor cells to transform them into CAFs with pro-metastatic properties. These “tele-educated” CAFs become the main ‘architects’ and “founders” of the PMN. By remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment, remodeling the ECM, promoting neovascularization and increased permeability, recruiting and “domesticating” immune cells (e.g., forming an immunosuppressive environment), and secreting a variety of growth and chemokines, they collectively create a “fertile ground” conducive to the retention, survival, proliferation, and formation of metastatic foci by circulating tumor cells [ , ].
Therefore, to effectively block the fatal process of cancer metastasis, it becomes crucial to gain an in-depth understanding of the central role of CAFs in PMN formation and their regulatory mechanisms, and to develop targeted intervention strategies. In recent years, with the development of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomic approaches, many studies have identified an increasing number of CAF subpopulations and elucidated many aspects of tumor-mesenchymal interactions.
Here, we describe the current understanding of CAF biology, highlight CAF activation pathways and summarize recent advances in recent years in terms of the crosstalk of different subtypes of CAFs with other stromal cells. In addition, we extensively summarize recent advances in the characterization of PMNs formed by CAF infiltration. Finally, we also provide an overview of newly developed cancer therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs in recent years and discuss the implications and challenges of personalized therapy targeting CAF subtypes and specific molecular pathways, aiming to provide new ideas for therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs to inhibit PMN formation.

## Basic information on the premetastatic niche

## Cellular components involved in the premetastatic niche

## Immune cells
The premetastatic niche consists of an accumulation of abnormal immune cells and ECM proteins in target organs [ ]. Abnormal immune cells within the PMN remodel the local microenvironment by releasing inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and angiogenic molecules, which promote tumor cell development and metastasis. In particular, these abnormal immune cells play a key role in the development of immunosuppression, one of the hallmarks of the PMN. Here, we highlight the role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), neutrophils, macrophages and T cells in PMN formation and several recent research advances.
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells. MDSCs, which are differentiated from common myeloid progenitors, constitute a heterogeneous population that suppresses T-cell function and promotes tumor development [ ]. In pathological conditions such as tumors and inflammation, the differentiation of immature myeloid cells is influenced by inflammatory factors or tumor-derived factors, which induce them to become MDSCs [ ]. These cells universally express CD11b, which increases in number under pathological conditions [ ]. In tumors, MDSCs are produced, accumulate in large numbers and inhibit T-cell activation and proliferation, which is undoubtedly important in promoting an immunosuppressive environment in the PMN. MDSCs can contribute to immunosuppression in terms of both intrinsic and adaptive immunity. In terms of intrinsic immunity, cancer-expanded MDSCs can inhibit NK cell function via membrane-bound TGF-β1 [ ]. In adaptive immunity, MDSCs inhibit T-cell function mainly through high expression of ARG 1, iNOS and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ , ]. The premetastatic niche-promoting function of MDSCs is attributed mainly to the expression of integrins and various chemokines, including CCR5 and CCR6, inflammatory mediators, growth factors and angiogenic factors, in response to tumor-derived secreted factors (TDSFs) [ , ].
Neutrophils. Neutrophils, which are heterogeneous and multidimensional in cancer, are the predominant phagocytic cells in the peripheral blood. According to their different functions, neutrophils are divided into two distinct groups: N1 neutrophils, which have antitumor functions, and N2 neutrophils, which have protumor functions [ ]. With the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technology, an increasing number of studies have confirmed the role of neutrophils in the premetastatic niche [ ]. Neutrophils are recruited from the circulating blood in response to chemokines, EVs, and other bioactive factors to reach distant metastatic sites [ ]. Neutrophils differentiate into antitumor phenotypes (N1) and protumor phenotypes (N2) in response to the surrounding microenvironment [ ]. After undergoing chemotaxis and differentiation, neutrophils further promote PMN formation to facilitate the colonization of circulating tumor cells (CTCs). The neutrophil phenotype N2 is involved in one of the PMN features, inflammation, through the secretion of various molecular components, such as TNF-α. Neutrophils are also able to interact with CTCs, thereby promoting tumor development. For example, neutrophils retain melanoma cells in the lungs by promoting the anchoring of melanoma cells to endothelial cells [ ]. Therefore, adaptive changes in neutrophils and interactions with the surrounding environment undoubtedly promote PMN formation and the colonization of CTCs.
Macrophages. Macrophages are myeloid immune cells that are considered terminally differentiated blood mononuclear phagocytes [ ]. Like neutrophils, macrophages are categorized into two subtypes on the basis of their different functions: the classically activated proinflammatory M1 subset and the alternatively activated anti-inflammatory M2 subset, which play opposite roles in metabolic reactions. M1 macrophages can secrete large amounts of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α and are functionally proinflammatory and antitumorigenic, whereas M2 macrophages mainly produce anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10, TGF-β and Arg1 and have anti-inflammatory, proangiogenic and protumorigenic effects. The macrophages infiltrating the tumor tissue are called tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and account for more than half of the total number of tumor cells [ ].
In recent years, an increasing number of studies have confirmed the role of macrophages in the premetastatic niche. During the early stages of PMN formation, one of the features of PMN inflammation is the activation of a variety of cytokines and chemokines that promote the recruitment of immune cells, including macrophages [ ]. As macrophages accumulate in large numbers, they further release proinflammatory factors, including IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ, EGF, several proteases, and ROS [ ]. In this vicious cycle, macrophages are converted to the M2 phenotype under the stimulation of primary tumor-derived factors [ ]. An increasing number of studies have shown that M2 macrophages can release factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and EGF to promote tumor cell survival, proliferation, motility, angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis and affect the tumor microenvironment through paracrine cytokines such as IL-1, MMPs, and CSF-1, leading to tumor metastasis and invasion [ ]. With the development of the PMN, many CTCs infiltrate and aggregate from the vasculature. As tissue cancer progresses, the macrophage phenotype is converted to the TAM phenotype. TAMs stimulate tumor cell infiltration and metastasis through suppression of the immune response, promoting angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis regulating tumor cell metabolism, which further promotes PMN maturation [ ].
T cells. T cells, which originate from lymphoid progenitor cells in the bone marrow, are key cells involved in acquired immunity. T cells are categorized according to their function as helper T cells (Th cells), cytotoxic T cells or regulatory T cells (Treg cells) [ ]. Notably, during PMN formation, Tregs promote the immunosuppressive microenvironment through immunosuppressive functions. For example, in multiple cancer models, CXCR3 + Treg cells are increased in tumors and exhibit an activated phenotype, which limits the antitumor immunity of CD8 + T cells by interacting with CXCL9-producing BATF3 + dendritic cells (DCs) [ ].

## Stromal cells
One of the focuses of research on the PMN is ECM alterations and stromal reprogramming of the ECM. The host stromal environment consists of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, the ECM, and the vascular system, which play important roles in maintaining homeostasis of the body’s internal environment. Thus, the aberrant changes in stromal cells in response to tumor-derived factors promote the formation of a supportive stromal microenvironment for the tumor.
Fibroblasts. Fibroblasts, the main cellular component of loose connective tissue, are involved in a number of key biological processes, such as tissue repair and fibrosis [ ]. More importantly, fibroblasts are deeply involved in the PMN formation process [ ]. Fibroblasts organize the ECM by expressing fibronectin and MMP to accelerate PMN formation [ ]. NFs are activated as protumorigenic CAFs in response to stimulation by tumorigenic factors, including cancer-derived EVs [ ]. Activated fibroblasts induce PMN formation by secreting proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 [ ]. CAFs promote tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance by affecting metabolic reprogramming, ECM remodeling, immunosuppression and angiogenesis [ , ].
Endothelial cells (ECs). Endothelial cells are a thin layer of epithelial cells that cover the inner walls of all blood vessels. Endothelial cells form a barrier between blood vessels and tissues, controlling the entry and exit of substances. Therefore, ECs are the main channels for CTCs to enter distant organs. During the process of tumor metastasis, tumor cells spread mainly by disrupting the endothelial barrier, entering the circulatory system and colonizing distant organs [ , ]. For example, proangiogenic tumor-secreted factors downregulate endothelial TRAIL in the PMN, impairing the integrity of the vascular barrier, promoting the recruitment of inflammatory cells, and promoting metastasis [ ]. Endothelial cells are indispensable for the formation of the PMN because they play an important role in angiogenesis, which is one of the hallmarks of the PMN [ ]. In addition, neovascular endothelial cells can form an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports angiogenesis, which undoubtedly facilitates the development of the PMN [ , ].
Pericytes. Pericytes, also known as rouget cells, are a type of contractile wall cell located between the capillary endothelium and the basement membrane and play a role in regulating capillary blood flow. As gatekeepers in tumor vessels, pericytes promote PMN formation through multiple interactions with different components of the microenvironment [ ]. In cancer, pericytes can promote PMN formation by promoting angiogenesis [ ]. Furthermore, defective interactions between pericytes and ECs during carcinogenesis increase vascular permeability and lead to leakage of the tumor vasculature, which promotes PMN formation and tumor metastasis [ ]. In glioblastoma, activated pericytes develop an immunosuppressive phenotype, which reduces T-cell activation by inducing an anti-inflammatory response, which in turn achieves immunosuppression in the tumor [ ]. Immunosuppression is one of the key features of the PMN [ ]. It has been demonstrated that tumor-derived vascular pericytes negatively affect the activation and proliferation of CD4 + T cells, suggesting a new specific role for tumor-derived pericytes in the immune evasion paradigm in the TME [ ]. ECM remodeling is another important mechanism by which pericytes promote PMN formation in tumors [ ]. Prior to tumor metastasis, the PMN undergoes epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) to promote tumor cell infiltration and colonization [ ]. TCAF2 in TPCs obtained from CRC patients promotes tumor cell motility, EMT and CRCLM [ ]. There is a two-way conversation between pericytes and TAMs. Pericyte-derived CXCL14 promotes macrophage recruitment and polarization to an M2-like phenotype, and TAMs play an important role in PMN formation [ ]. In turn, TAMs can promote tumor angiogenesis [ ]. Abnormal action of pericytes often leads to the formation of immature and highly permeable tumor vessels, which promote the formation of a hypoxic microacidic microenvironment [ ]. The hypoxic microenvironment promotes the generation of various EMT-induced signals and promotes immune tolerance, resulting in the formation of the PMN, which facilitates the colonization and invasion of blood-borne metastatic cancer cells [ , ].

## Molecular components involved in the premetastatic niche

## Secreted tumor-derived factors
TDSFs are various signaling molecules secreted by tumors that function by communicating with other tissue cells. Under conditions of inflammation and hypoxia, tumor cells secrete large amounts of TDSFs to promote the establishment of PMNs in distant metastatic organs through a variety of mechanisms, which prepares the tumor for metastasis [ , ]. For example, in melanoma, tumor cells can promote tumor metastasis by secreting certain factors that weaken the integrity of the endothelial barrier and produce lymphangiogenic factors that promote lymphangiogenesis [ , ]. These TDSFs, such as VEGF, TGF-β, prostaglandins, and IL-6, are involved in alterations in tumor drainage lymph nodes (LNs), such as changes in lymphangiogenesis and ECM remodeling, which contribute to the formation of PMNs in tumor-draining LNs [ ]. In addition, numerous studies have shown that TDSFs promote PMN formation by activating fibroblasts and recruiting MDSCs to the PMN [ ]. TDSFs such as tumor cell galectin-1 can also influence MDSCs in the TME in a paracrine manner to stimulate their migration and function for future PMN formation [ , ].

## Extracellular vesicles
EVs are lipid bilayer-enclosed particles released by cells, broadly classified by size into small EVs (sEVs; <200 nm) and large EVs (lEVs; >200 nm) per MISEV guidelines. sEVs include endosomal-origin vesicles historically termed exosomes (50–150 nm), while lEVs encompass plasma membrane-derived vesicles historically called microvesicles or ectosomes (50–500 nm, up to 1 μm).Functional and contextual names such as prostasomes, tolerosomes, oncosomes, and migrasomes may denote specific EV subtypes but require validation through physical characterization (e.g., size, density) and biochemical markers to confirm biogenesis origin, as size ranges overlap significantly in the 50–200 nm transition zone [ ]. EVs are now thought to play a role in intercellular communication. During tumorigenesis, EVs can reach recipient cells and deliver their contents to trigger appropriate functional responses. Complex communication occurs between the PMN and tumor cells, and EVs are key molecules involved in communication [ , ]. During tumorigenesis, EVs can affect a variety of pathophysiological processes, such as vascular leakage, lymphangiogenesis, lymph node metastasis and immunosuppression, by secreting a variety of biomolecules, which promote PMN formation and tumor metastasis [ , ]. For example, melanoma-derived small EVs enhance lymphangiogenesis and tumor cell adhesion through the secretion of nerve growth factor receptor, which enhances the formation of PMNs in lymph nodes and metastasis [ ]. Osteosarcoma cell-derived EVs activate lung interstitial macrophages and recruit granulocyte myeloid-derived suppressor cells (gMDSCs) through the secretion of the chemokine CXCL2 to promote the formation of immunosuppressive PMNs and tumor cell colonization [ ]. In addition to tumor-derived EVs, stromal cell-derived EVs play an important role in PMN formation. In salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma (SACC) metastasis, CAF-derived EVs promote LF activation and ECM remodeling, which in turn induces lung PMN formation [ ]. Studying EVs not only helps to explore the PMN but also helps to identify new targets for cancer therapy. As research on EVs has progressed, their role as prognostic biomarkers for cancer metastasis treatment has become increasingly important [ ].

## Other molecular components
Other molecular components, such as cytokines, chemokines, and inflammatory factors, also play important roles in PMN formation [ , ]. A subpopulation of tumor-polarized alveolar type 2 epithelial cells inhibits the proliferation of CD4 + T cells and enhances the production of Treg cells by producing large amounts of interleukin (IL)−10, which promotes lung PMN formation [ ]. In osteosarcoma, the chemokine CXCL2 activates lung interstitial macrophages and strongly recruits gMDSCs, thereby promoting the formation of immunosuppressive PMNs and tumor cell colonization [ ]. Similarly, COX-2-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) stimulates EP3 receptors on DCs and promotes the elevation of stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) in DCs to promote the proliferation of Treg cells and lymphangiogenesis in lymph nodes, which induces PMN formation during lymph node metastasis [ ]. In summary, these molecular components help CTCs invade target organs for metastasis and colonization by establishing PMNs in distant metastatic organs.

## Formation of the premetastatic niche
Recent studies have shown that multiple niche-promoting molecular and cellular components interact with each other to initiate, transfer, and establish PMNs in prospective metastatic organs through a variety of mechanisms [ ]. Here, we focus on five major components of PMN formation: angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, ECM remodeling, and the generation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

## Inflammation
Inflammation is a well-defined feature of PMN and an important driver of tumor development and metastasis [ , ]. Many molecular components and signaling pathways regulate the link between inflammation and metastasis. Fibroblasts are a major component of the tumor stroma and are important for promoting inflammation in PMN [ ].

## Angiogenesis
Angiogenesis is a process of new blood vessel formation, and it is essential for tumors to establish a vascular supply of oxygen and nutrients as they grow. In the PMN, neovascularization and vascular permeability directly control CTC stagnation and extravasation and are key targets for tumor-derived adaptation in preparation for metastasis [ ]. Angiogenesis is influenced by multiple cellular interactions involving extensive crosstalk between ECs, pericytes, cancer cells, TAMs and CAFs [ ]. In the PMN, multiple proangiogenic signals result in disorganized, hyperpermeable, and abnormally pericyte- and EC-covered neovascularization. For example, in breast cancer, CAFs overexpress lnc-SNHG5 and promote hyperpermeable angiogenesis by regulating ZNF281, which plays an important role in PMN formation [ ].

## Lymphangiogenesis
In addition to hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic vessels can also facilitate tumor dissemination [ ]. In the PMN, lymphangiogenesis is actively involved in tumor metastasis, and lymphatic vessels can serve as the initial pathway for tumor lymphatic dissemination. Similarly, lymphangiogenesis in the PMN is influenced by a variety of cellular and molecular components. For example, in head and neck cancer, a variety of ECM macromolecules, cytokines, and growth factors are involved in the formation of the nodal premetastatic niche (NPMN), which forms a supportive microenvironment and promotes tumor lymph node metastasis [ ].

## ECM remodeling
The ECM, the noncellular component of the PMN, is a network containing fibrous proteins such as collagen, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans. ECM remodeling is key to PMN formation. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have shown that matrisome proteins are involved in the establishment of the PMN [ , ]. For example, genetically inactivated perivascular cells promote enhanced ECM generation through increased expression of the gene Klf4, which establishes a PMN rich in fibronectin [ ].

## Generation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment
The body’s immune system effectively monitors tumor development and progression. Therefore, evasion of immune surveillance through the establishment of an immunosuppressive PMN is essential for tumor development [ ]. The establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment is a multistage, complex process involving the secretion of multiple factors and EVs and the recruitment and polarization of aberrant immune cells [ ]. Representative examples include the recruitment of MDSCs by mett3-mediated m6A methylation and increased macrophage M2 polarization, both of which promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment in ovarian cancer (OC) progression [ ].

## CAF biology

## Biological characterization of CAFs in cancer
Fibroblasts, which are differentiated from embryonic mesenchymal cells, are quiescent mesenchymal cells embedded in the ECM of mesenchymal fibers and are involved in a number of key biological processes, such as wound healing and tissue repair [ ]. During the single-cell era, many studies on fibroblasts in cancer have emerged, and this heterogeneous cell population has been found to be closely associated with tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis [ ]. Therefore, all tumor-associated and activated fibroblasts found in or around the cancer are referred to as CAFs.
Quiescent fibroblasts are inert, exhibit a typical spindle-shaped morphology and are usually activated in response to wound healing. This activation involves key signaling pathways, such as TGF-β and Wnt signaling pathways, that drive the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts with reparative functions [ ]. Myofibroblasts secrete a variety of factors, including members of the TGF-β family, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), PDGF, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and interleukins, and they synthesize and remodel the ECM, primarily collagen types I and III, fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid, in order to promote granulation tissue formation, wound contraction and eventual scarring [ ].When wound healing is complete, the reversibility of this activation can be mediated by reprogramming and apoptosis of fibroblasts [ ]. Tumors are considered to be “wounds that do not heal”. Thus, the development of cancerous lesions provides a continuous injurious stimulus that leads to the sustained activation of CAFs, which is in part controlled by epigenetic mechanisms in CAFs. Compared with resting fibroblasts, CAFs are structurally more complex at the level of cell biology, including increased rough endoplasmic reticulum, free ribosomes, Golgi complexes, and abundant stress fibers, which endow CAFs with planar polarity. Functionally, activated CAFs acquired enhanced proliferative and migratory properties, further secretory phenotypes, immunomodulatory functions, specific ECM synthesis and remodeling capabilities, and potent autocrine activation, which further amplified CAF activation, recruitment, and proliferation [ ].
Notably, in terms of interactions with tumors, CAFs play dual roles and exhibit functional heterogeneity. Although many studies have demonstrated the potent protumorigenic effects of CAFs, some reports of tumor suppression by subpopulations of CAFs have been published. In PDAC, FAP + CAFs promote tumor growth, whereas αSMA + CAFs inhibit tumor growth [ ]. Notably, a recent study revealed that stimulator of interferon gene-induced interferon-responsive CAFs (ifnCAFs) inhibited tumor cell invasiveness and induced an antitumor phenotype in tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) [ ]. These studies suggest that targeting the entire CAF population may not be a good choice in cancer therapy. Therefore, in the future, precise treatments targeting protumor CAF subpopulations are needed. The therapeutic potential of antitumor CAF subpopulations also deserves further consideration.

## Cellular origin of CAFs
CAFs are a heterogeneous and plastic population. The major subsets of CAFs include myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), which exhibit distinct biological features and contribute to phenotypic diversity and functional heterogeneity in cancer progression [ ]. The heterogeneity of CAFs can be attributed to the multiple origins of precursor cells, which has been confirmed by a large body of evidence. CAFs are derived from various cell types activated by multiple signaling pathways [ , ], as shown in Fig. . 
Normal resident fibroblasts are among the main direct sources of CAFs. In different tumors, resident fibroblasts are activated into CAFs by stimulation with different regulators, including TGF-β, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ , ]. Different subpopulations of fibroblasts can be transformed into different kinds of CAFs. For example, in a mouse model of breast cancer, CD26 + NFs and CD26- NFs were transformed into iCAFs and myCAFs, respectively [ ]. In some tumors, stellate cells may be the cell of origin of CAFs. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), exosomal miRNA-21 secreted by cancer cells activates PDK1/AKT signaling in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which converts HSCs into CAFs [ ]. In addition to being derived from tissue-resident fibroblasts, CAFs are also derived primarily from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) that migrate toward the tumor site. The transformation of bone marrow-derived MSCs into CAFs is a complex multistep process involving EMT and stimulation by multiple cytokines [ ]. For example, in breast cancer, tumor-derived osteopontin (OPN) induces MSCs to express TGF-β1 in the microenvironment via the OPN-MZF1-TGF-β1 pathway, which in turn promotes the transformation of MSCs into CAFs [ ]. Adipocytes are also a cellular source of CAFs. Studies on gastric cancer (GC) have shown that adipocytes can transdifferentiate into CAFs when they are cocultured with GC cells in vitro, which promotes GC malignancy during the development of the cancer microenvironment [ ]. In addition, several other potential sources of CAFs have been identified. For example, in some cases, CAFs originate from epithelial cells via EMT and from ECs via endothelial‒mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [ , , ]. There are also less common potential cellular sources of CAFs, including pericytes and vascular smooth muscle cells, which undergo transdifferentiation [ , ]. Furthermore, monocytes can form a novel CAF subpopulation through macrophage-myofibroblast transformation (MMT) [ ]. In conclusion, the exact origin of CAFs has not been fully elucidated. ## Phenotypic heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs
The diversity of CAF origins results in a CAF population with a variety of complex fibroblast phenotypes and different functions in different cancer types. This high degree of heterogeneity is considered to be one of the main characteristics of CAFs [ ]. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has greatly assisted in the classification of CAF populations [ ]. The phenotypic heterogeneity of CAFs is evidenced by the fact that different subtypes of activated CAFs selectively express corresponding biomarkers. Numerous studies have demonstrated that fibroblast activation protein (FAP), α-SMA, fibroblast-specific protein 1, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), caveolin 1, etc., can be used as biomarkers of activated fibroblasts in the detection of CAF populations [ ]. Although none of these nonspecific markers are expressed by CAFs alone or by all CAF subgroups together, in combination, they may help to distinguish CAF subgroups. Plasticity is one of the most important features of CAFs. The plasticity of CAFs refers to their ability to functionally regulate their properties according to the requirements of the microenvironment by activating signaling cascade responses. During wound healing, quiescent fibroblasts activate into myofibroblasts, and once the wound is healed, they become quiescent again. The reversibility of this activation strongly supports the concept of fibroblast plasticity. Different subpopulations of CAFs are interconvertible through specific signaling pathways. For example, in breast cancer, cancer cells drive the transition of detoxification-associated iCAFs to immunosuppressive ECM-produced myCAFs through DPP4- and YAP-dependent mechanisms [ ]. These findings suggest that the CAF subpopulation is not a terminally differentiated cell and has a high degree of plasticity. In addition, transcription factors are critical for fibroblast homeostasis and can play key regulatory roles in the transformation of fibroblasts into different subsets through various signaling pathways [ ]. The master transcription factor PRRX1 orchestrates the functional drift of fibroblasts toward a myofibroblast phenotype through TGF-β signaling, which enhances cancer invasion and metastasis [ ].

 

## The role of CAFs in the formation of the premetastatic niche

## Activation of CAFs in the formation of the premetastatic niche
PMNs, as a tumor-supportive microenvironment, play crucial roles in tumorigenesis, progression, metastatic dissemination and ectopic colonization [ ]. However, the large number of NFs in the microenvironment play a tumor-suppressive role. Thus, the reprogramming of NFs into CAFs in response to various signaling pathways is an important part of the PMN formation process. In summary, numerous studies have shown that the establishment of PMNs in prospective metastatic organs is extremely important for tumor progression and that CAF activation is a critical stage in PMN formation [ , ]. As shown in Fig. , TDSFs, EVs and stressors in the local microenvironment often play important roles in CAF activation. 
During CAF production, TDSFs induce NF activation by stimulating certain different signaling pathways (Fig. A). In lung cancer, lactate produced by cancer cells promotes the activation of CAFs by facilitating nuclear translocation of NUSAP1 and transcriptional activation of DESMIN. DESMIN-positive CAFs recruit TAMs through secretion of IL-8, thereby creating an immunosuppressive environment conducive to tumor development [ ]. Furthermore, TDSF-induced activation of lung fibroblasts (LFs) p38α kinase leads to inactivation of type I interferon signaling and stimulation of FAP expression, and activation of p38α kinase plays a key role in CAF activation and PMN establishment [ ]. Indeed, the most common mechanism of CAF activation involves TGF-β-mediated signaling [ ]. Activin A (ActA), a member of the TGF-β superfamily secreted by breast cancer cells, has been found to systematically activate CAFs in the lung PMN through the blood circulation, up-regulate the expression of pro-fibrotic factors, enhance collagen deposition, and create a fibrotic microenvironment that is favorable for metastasis [ ]. EVs-packaged proteins and nucleic acids can also activate fibroblasts at secondary sites (Fig. B). For example, in a CRC model, primary tumor cells help resident fibroblasts in distant metastatic organs acquire a CAF phenotype by releasing integrin beta-like 1 (ITGBL1)-rich EVs into the bloodstream. Activated CAFs induce PMN formation and promote metastatic cancer growth by secreting proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 [ ]. Similarly, miR-181a-5p-rich EVs released by highly metastatic CRC cells can be taken up by HSCs. This promotes colorectal cancer liver metastasis by targeting SOCS3 and activating the IL6/STAT3 signaling pathway to sustainably activate HSCs, ultimately leading to hepatic PMN formation [ ].
Specific physical and chemical stressors in the local microenvironment also promote the activation of CAFs (Fig. C). It has been shown that matrix stiffness creates a microenvironment that promotes maintenance of the CAF phenotype by enhancing the activation of YAP transcription factors [ ]. Another study further showed that YAP1 forms a protein complex with TEA structural domain transcription factor 1 (TEAD1) that co-regulates SRC transcription in fibroblasts, which subsequently regulates downstream actin and cytoskeletal proteins, ultimately leading to the conversion of NFs to CAFs [ ]. In addition, hypoxia promotes fibroblasts to acquire an inflammatory gene expression signature and synergizes with cancer cell-derived cytokines to induce the formation of an iCAF phenotype in stromal cells in a HIF1α-dependent manner [ ].

## Crosstalk between CAFs of different subtypes and other stromal cells in the premetastatic niche
The PMN is a highly complex system composed mainly of immune cells (e.g., macrophages and neutrophils), stromal cells (e.g., CAFs, ECs, and pericytes), and various signaling molecules. PMN formation is dependent on a dynamic network among stromal cells and CAFs, which are key regulators whose crosstalk with other stromal cells directly affects PMN immunosuppression, vascular leakage, and ECM remodeling [ , , ]. In recent years, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics techniques have further revealed the heterogeneity of CAFs and their spatial distribution characteristics.
On the basis of literature-driven single-cell scRNA–seq data, a generalized CAF classification system classifies CAFs into different phenotypes, including iCAFs, matrix CAFs (mCAFs), apCAFs, vascular CAFs (vCAFs), tumor-like CAFs (tCAFs), reticular-like CAFs (rCAFs), developmental CAFs (dCAFs), and ifnCAFs. A phenotypic classification system for CAFs on the basis of single-cell transcriptomics revealed their high heterogeneity and functional plasticity in the TME. For example, iCAFs drive immunosuppression by secreting inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and CXCL12, whereas mCAFs promote tumor mechanical barrier formation through ECM remodeling, and apCAFs participate in immune escape by mediating nonclassical antigen delivery through MHC-II molecules [ ]. In this classification system, the authors defined several major CAF types on the basis of the unique gene expression profiles and spatial distribution patterns of CAFs, mainly iCAFs, mCAFs, apCAFs, vCAFs, and tCAFs [ ]. This functional differentiation provides a molecular framework for resolving the dynamic crosstalk between CAFs and other stromal cells (e.g., ECs, T cells, and macrophages). Different CAF subtypes can differentially regulate immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling through secreted cytokines, exosomes, or direct connection-mediated communication networks, ultimately influencing the process of PMN formation. In the following, we will present the progress of each of these major CAF phenotypes in terms of crosstalk with other stromal cells. As shown in Fig.  , there are different mechanisms for the dynamic crosstalk between iCAFs, mCAFs, apCAFs, tCAFs and vCAFs and other stromal cells. 
Recently, iCAFs, which are low in α-SMA expression but high in the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, were shown to shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment in patients with PDAC [ ] (Fig.  A). Specifically, PPY induces iCAFs by binding to EGFR and activating the NFκB2 signaling pathway. iCAFs polarize macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, recruit MDSCs, affect CD8 + T-cell infiltration and function to create an immunosuppressive environment [ ]. In CRC, fibroblast growth factor 19 induces iCAF formation via the FGFR4-JAK2-STAT3 pathway, and iCAFs promote neutrophil infiltration and mediate neutrophil extracellular trap formation in the hepatic PMN by producing complement C5a and IL-1β [ ]. In addition, iCAFs can inhibit CD8 + T cells through multiple pathways. iCAFs induce CD8 + T-cell apoptosis by interacting with CD8 + T-cell PTPRC receptors via galectin-1. iCAFs inhibit CD8 + T-cell activation and proliferation through the macrophage migration inhibitory factor-CXCR4 interaction or the secretion of TGFB1 [ ]. Notably, iCAFs can produce VEGF, which binds to VEGF receptors (FLT1, KDR, MET, and FLT4) on ECs to promote angiogenesis [ ].
mCAFs are linked to increased tumor cell migration and subsequent metastasis via ECM remodeling functions [ ](Fig.  B). Similar types of CAFs with associated gene tags are also referred to as myCAFs [ ]. Recently, it has been demonstrated that myCAFs synergize with SPP1 + TAMs to promote ECM deposition to form an immune‒physical barrier via SPP1‒CD44, SPP1‒ITGB5, and SPP1‒ITGB1 interactions [ ]. In addition to ECM remodeling, myCAFs can promote immunosuppression through metabolic reprogramming. In colorectal cancer liver metastases, one study characterized the myCA-derived exosomal lncRNA PWAR6. Mechanistically, PWAR6 inhibits the ubiquitination and degradation of NRF2 by competitively binding to Keap1. NRF2 promotes glutamine uptake by CRC cells through the increased expression of SLC38A2, which leads to glutamine depletion, followed by the apoptosis of NK cells, ultimately promoting immune evasion [ ]. Similarly, it has been shown that the ECM secreted by myCAFs specifically limits the cytotoxicity of NK cells [ ]. In addition to inhibiting NK cells, myCAFs reduce the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells through a Yes-Associated Protein 1 (YAP1)-dependent mechanism to promote immunosuppression [ ]. YAP1 expression also further promotes collagen deposition, enhances ECM cross-linking and stability, and increases the mechanical stiffness of the matrix to form a physical barrier for immune escape [ ].
apCAFs, which are defined on the basis of the expression of human leukocyte antigen complex/major histocompatibility complex class 2 (HLA-II/MHC-II)-related genes (e.g., HLA-DR and CD 74), often contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment by mediating immune modulation [ ] (Fig.  C). For example, in PDAC, mesothelial cells form apCAFs via IL-1 and TGF-β. Subsequently, apCAFs directly connect and induce naïve CD4 + T cells to transform into Tregs in an antigen-specific manner to promote immunosuppression [ ]. Similarly, in lung cancer, IFN-γ stimulates apCAF amplification through the JAK1/2-STAT1-IFI6/27 pathway. apCAFs express large amounts of PD-L2 and induce the accumulation of FOXP1 + Tregs through the PD-L2-RGMB axis, which in turn suppresses the function of CD8 + T cells [ ]. Notably, apCAFs can impair the antitumor function of immune cells through similar metabolic reprogramming. Recently, in ESCC, apCAFs were shown to promote tumor immune escape by inhibiting the metabolic pathways of immune cells and weakening their antigen-presenting ability. Specifically, apCAFs disrupt glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-acetylheparin sulfate/heparin metabolism, which is responsible for the generation of heparin sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), by inhibiting the expression of the HS3ST3A1 gene in immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and macrophages. The reduction in HSPGs directly inhibits the antigen-presenting ability of MHC molecules, rendering immune cells unable to effectively recognize tumor antigens, ultimately leading to tumor cells evading immune surveillance [ ].
tCAFs, whose gene expression profiles are highly similar to those of tumor cells, highly express markers such as CD10 (MME), CD73, and TMEM158 [ ] (Fig.  D). CD73 is a 5′-nucleotidase that dephosphorylates AMP to adenosine. Adenosine inhibits immunity by promoting the amplification of Tregs and binding to A2A receptors on the surface of immune cells (e.g., T cells and natural killer (NK) cells) [ – ]. Because of the concomitant upregulation of glycolytic pathways (e.g., ENO1 and GAPDH) and activation of stress response-associated proteins (e.g., heat shock proteins), tCAFs have also been referred to as energetic CAFs, metabolic CAFs, or glycolytic CAFs in some studies [ ]. Recent studies have shown that tCAFs are associated with poor patient prognosis, and angiogenic enrichment has also been shown via functional enrichment analyses [ ]. The cluster expresses high levels of VEGFA, which means that tCAFs can provide essential nutrients and oxygen that further support tumor growth and metastasis by recruiting and activating cells associated with angiogenesis, such as ECs and monocytes, to form new vascular networks [ ].
As a subpopulation of CAFs with proangiogenic properties, vCAFs are distinct from pericytes because of the lack of strong expression of RGS5, are mainly distributed in the perivascular region, and play a key regulatory role in tumor angiogenesis because of the strong expression of CD146, NOTCH 3 and collagen COL18A1 [ ] (Fig.  E). Recently, in endometrial cancer, IL-10 secreted by CD146 + CAFs was shown to induce epithelial‒endothelial transition in endometrial cancer cells through activation of the JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway. Direct binding of STAT3 to the promoter of cadherin5 (CDH5), a key molecule in vasculogenic mimicry (VM), promotes its transcription, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and VM formation [ ]. Similarly, CAFs with high expression of NOTCH3 can promote blood vessel formation and maturation by secreting proangiogenic factors or through the interaction of NOTCH3 with endothelial JAG1 [ ].
The intricate crosstalk between different CAF subtypes and stromal cells in the PMN highlights their critical role in promoting immunosuppression, angiogenesis and ECM remodeling (Fig.  ). Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have revealed the heterogeneity and dynamic communication networks of CAFs, providing potential therapeutic targets for disrupting stromal–tumor crosstalk. Future therapies may focus on inhibiting subtype-specific CAFs or modulating their signaling axis to reverse immunosuppression and ECM-driven resistance, ultimately impeding metastatic progression.

## Characteristics of the premetastatic niche formed by CAF involvement
Owing to increasing research on CAFs and tumor metastasis in recent years, many features of the PMN that require the involvement of CAFs in their formation have been elucidated very clearly. In the following, we describe the role of CAFs in each of these processes, including inflammation, angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, immunosuppression, and ECM remodeling (Fig.  ). 

## Inflammation
Tumor-associated inflammation (TAI) is characteristic of essentially all cancers, and the persistence of an inflammatory response is a risk factor for malignant progression [ ]. CAFs play an important role in promoting PMN formation by remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment (Fig.  A). As previously described, one of the key pathways by which CAFs form a peripheral inflammatory microenvironment in preparation for tumor metastasis involves the secretion of proinflammatory factors, which act directly on tumor cells and induce cancer cell proliferation. One of the representative proinflammatory cytokines is IL-6, which has been found to be involved in promoting immunity, cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [ ]. Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that in the lung PMN, highly metastatic HCC cells induce the transformation of LFs to CAFs through exosomal delivery of miR-1247-3p, and these activated CAFs are a key source of secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-8, which directly mediate the formation of PMN and tumor progression [ ]. Meanwhile, in the BEL7404 HCC cell model, environmental exposures such as benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P) induced cells to secrete exosomes enriched for the carcinogenic circ_0011496. As demonstrated by in vivo models, these exosomes are delivered to the lungs and taken up by LFs, where they competitively bind miR-486-5p, leading to the upregulation of TWF1. This process activates a cascade of responses that prompts LFs to transform into CAFs. These locally activated CAFs in lung tissues secret key pro-inflammatory (e.g., IL-11) and pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF and MMP9) that remodel the microenvironment in the lungs, resulting in the formation of PMN [ ]. Other important CAF-derived inflammatory mediators, including TGF-β, IL-10, IL-4, IFN-γ, COX-2, CXCL 1, and IL-2, enhance tumor immunosuppression and the inflammatory microenvironment [ ].
CAFs remodel the inflammatory microenvironment by secreting proinflammatory factors. This TAI promotes immunosuppression by recruiting and polarizing immune cells to release inflammatory mediators. CAFs are continuously activated in response to a number of active factors. Thus, this vicious cycle leads to the continuous development of inflammation in tumors.

## Angiogenesis
Rapidly proliferating tumors are inextricably linked to the development of a complex vascular system. Without active neovascularization to provide oxygen and nutrients, tumors are unable to sustain their own growth. PMNs, as the microenvironment prior to tumor metastasis, are inextricably linked to preformed blood vessels. Angiogenesis is important for tumor invasion and metastasis, especially in highly permeable blood vessels. Thus, the vasculature in the PMN influences the arrest and extravasation of CTCs and is critical for tumor metastasis [ ].
During PMN formation, CAFs are thought to play a prominent role in promoting hyperpermeable angiogenesis [ ]. This complex process involves extensive crosstalk between CAFs and other cells, leading to the formation of a complex and highly permeable vascular system that ultimately affects the oxygen and nutrient supply to the tumor [ ] (Fig.  B). The key to angiogenesis is the balance between pro- and antiangiogenic factors. A representative pathway of signal transduction in tumor angiogenesis involves the binding of VEGF and its cognate receptor (VEGFR). Activated CAFs in the PMN produce high levels of VEGF and other proangiogenic factors in a proangiogenic microenvironment that triggers the angiogenic switch and promotes cancer progression [ , ]. In addition, CAFs can promote angiogenesis in a number of VEGF-independent ways. The lncRNA SNHG5, which is highly expressed in CAFs of primary breast cancer origin, promotes hyperpermeable angiogenesis in the PMN of distal organs by regulating ZNF281 in CAFs. Mechanistically, the increased expression of ZNF281 within these CAFs located in the primary tumor transcriptionally upregulates the expression and promotes the secretion of CCL2 and CCL5. Subsequently, the secreted CCL2 and CCL5 from the CAFs enters the circulatory system and acts on the ECs in the distal PMN sites to activate their P38 MAPK signaling pathway. This tumorigenic hyperpermeable vasculature driven by primary tumor CAFs and occurring in the PMN of distal organs plays an important role in PMN formation [ ]. Notably, pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF) secreted by CAFs themselves are not only key signals driving angiogenesis, but are also thought to be among the upstream factors activating the p38α kinase in target cells such as LFs. p38α activation further induces FAP expression, which mediates ECM remodeling (e.g., fibronectin accumulation) and pro-inflammatory microenvironment formation in synergy with CAF-driven angiogenesis to build PMN that support metastasis [ ].

## Lymphangiogenesis
Lymph node metastasis is an important prognostic parameter in many different types of cancer. In addition to hematogenous metastasis, the nascent lymphatic vessels in the PMN are the gateway for the further spread of many different types of cancer to distant organs [ ]. NPMNs are a specific type of PMN located in the lymph nodes. NPMNs develop when a supportive microenvironment develops within the lymph node, which promotes cancer cell survival, growth, spread and invasion. This complex mechanism involves interactions between CAFs and other cells (Fig.  C). A variety of ECM macromolecules, cytokines and growth factors are involved in this process.
VEGF-C and VEGF-D, which are members of the VEGF family, have been identified as key factors that promote the process of tumor lymphangiogenesis and are associated with increased LN metastasis [ ]. CAFs can lead to LEC proliferation, migration and tube formation by releasing VEGF-C and VEGF-D, leading to neolymphatic vessel formation [ ]. Importantly, this CAF-driven lymphangiogenic activity is also active in the PMN. For example, in the lymph node PMN, POSTN secreted by FRCs did not directly induce lymphangiogenesis, but acted as a key synergistic factor to significantly enhance VEGF-C-driven the function (adhesion, migration, proliferation) of lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), thereby promoting lymphangiogenesis in lymph nodes and creating a favorable microenvironment for subsequent metastatic cell colonization [ ]. Particularly within the main anatomical site of the PMN, the tumor-draining lymph node (TDLN), fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs) that acquire CAF-like properties are likely to exacerbate localized lymphatic vascular remodeling and expansion through similar mechanisms (e.g., secretion of VEGF-C/VEGF-D) [ ]. This active lymphangiogenesis within the PMN (including the TDLN stroma) creates favorable channels and microenvironments for tumor cells to escape from the primary site, spread through lymphatic vessels, and ultimately establish metastases in lymph nodes.

## Immunosuppression
As targets for elimination by the immune system, tumors are vulnerable to immune surveillance at every stage of their progression and metastasis in vivo. Therefore, unique immunosuppressive mechanisms for tumors and their metastatic derivatives to protect themselves from the immune system are essential. For example, tumor growth and metastasis are promoted by establishing immunosuppressive PMNs that aid in the immune escape of tumor cells [ ]. Evidence for the critical role of CAFs in coordinating immunosuppression in immunosuppressive PMN formation has been identified as one of the key factors contributing to tumor progression [ ] (Fig.  D).
Significant infiltration of abnormal immune cells is an important feature of immunosuppressive PMN formation. On the basis of growing evidence, CAFs interact with immune cells by secreting multiple bioactive molecules to remodel immunosuppressive PMN and help tumor cells evade immune surveillance. For example, in the lung PMN of a breast cancer model, COX-2-expressing lung adventitial fibroblasts (AdvFs) identified by scRNA-seq are driven by IL-1β driven, remodeled the local immunosuppressive microenvironment by inducing DC dysfunction and monocyte suppression through the production of PGE2 [ ]. In addition, a study found that fibroblasts localized in the lung showed down-regulation of ACACA (acetyl-CoA carboxylase α) expression and senescence in the lung PMN induced by the MMTV-PyVT breast cancer model using scRNA-seq. These senescent fibroblasts specifically recruited immunosuppressive granulocyte-like myeloid-derived suppressor cells (G-MDSCs) through the secretion of CXCL1 chemokines, thereby effectively enhancing the immunosuppressive properties of the lung microenvironment and promoting metastasis formation [ ]. Not coincidentally, CAFs ingesting Lewis lung carcinoma exosome (LLC-exo) significantly upregulated CCL1 secretion in a lung PMN model pretreated with LLC-exo. This chemokine induces differentiation of Tregs through activation of its specific receptor CCR8, which together shapes immune-tolerant PMN [ ]. In conclusion, these data suggest that CAF-mediated formation of immunosuppressive PMNs at secondary tumor sites is necessary for the successful implantation, survival, and expansion of CTCs in the PMN.

## ECM remodeling
As an extracellular component essential for tumor cell survival, the ECM provides a structural framework, functional support and signaling mechanism for tumor cells. In fact, ECM remodeling in the PMN undoubtedly provides more fertile soil for tumor cell metastasis. This is because ECM remodeling in distant metastatic organs together with the immunosuppressive microenvironment support CTC infiltration, colonization, immune escape and growth [ ]. Therefore, ECM remodeling is a defining feature of PMN development and is important for promoting CTC settlement in the PMN [ ]. As the most prominent ECM-producing cells in the PMN, CAFs enhance the malignant behavior of activated tumor cells through ECM remodeling [ ] (Fig.  E).
The ECM is a complex network of macromolecules with various components, such as metalloproteinases, collagen, and glycoproteins, involved in its remodeling [ ]. CAFs are the main cellular source of collagen in the PMN. For example, in a breast cancer lung metastasis model, it was found that ActA secreted by primary tumors systematically acted on fibroblasts in the lungs through the blood circulation, inducing high expression of pro-fibrotic factors in these cells at the PMN stage, leading to significant deposition of ECM proteins, such as type I collagen, in the lung tissues, and creating a favorable fibrotic microenvironment for subsequent metastatic colonization [ ]. CAFs can alter matrix stiffness by creating a collagen-rich environment, which generates mechanical signals that induce CTC colonization [ ]. Interestingly, myCAF-expressing type I collagen in the hepatic PMN inhibits tumor growth by mechanically restricting tumor spread beyond its own stiffness-induced mechanical signaling [ ]. In addition, several studies have linked CAF-mediated fibrosis in the PMN with immunosuppression and cancer metastasis. On the one hand, CAF-derived periostin (POSTN), a stromal cell protein, is a key factor limiting PMNs in cancer cell lung metastasis by promoting self-renewal of breast CSCs. On the other hand, POSTN has been shown to be a key extracellular regulator of immunosuppressive PMN formation in the lung by recruiting MDSCs in the lung to promote immunosuppression [ ].

## Treatments targeting CAFs
The preceding discussion highlights the importance of CAFs as components of the PMN through a wide range of mechanisms. As the PMN has become a key destination for cancer metastasis, the intrinsic correlation between CAFs and the PMN makes them promising emerging therapeutic targets for cancer intervention. Therefore, targeted CAF therapy has become the focus of anticancer therapy. In recent years, many studies on CAF-targeted cancer therapy have been conducted. In Table 2, we summarize current clinical trials addressing different aspects of CAFs. As shown in Fig.  , we summarize the recently developed CAF-related therapies, which can be broadly classified into three categories on the basis of the physiology and pathology of CAFs: (1) direct CAF depletion; (2) targeting the CAF activation pathway; and (3) inhibiting the tumor-promoting function of CAFs.

 

 

## Direct CAF depletion
Owing to the expression of CAF surface markers, different types of CAFs have been identified in different tumor tissues [ ]. Indeed, this approach likewise provides a feasible strategy for targeting CAF surface markers to deplete CAFs. In particular, FAP, which is considered a specific protein for CAFs, has been extensively investigated as a therapeutic target for CAFs from various research perspectives with satisfactory results (Fig.  A) [ ].
FAPs are potential targets for adoptive T-cell therapy. In recent years, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell strategies that target FAP to deplete FAP + CAFs have emerged as an exciting new class of immunotherapy strategies [ ]. Off-target effects and immune-related adverse events were great challenges in previous studies. Studies have utilized TALEN-mediated gene editing to design allogeneic IF/THEN-gated dual CAR-T cells to target FAP + CAFs in solid tumors. This T cell upon activation induces expression of a mesothelin-targeting CAR. This strategy is safer than conventional methods because it enhances antitumor cytotoxicity while limiting “off-target” cytotoxicity [ ]. Vaccines targeting FAP also have good antitumor potential. Researchers have developed a nanoparticle-based vaccine that inhibits CAF-mediated ECM remodeling by depleting FAP + CAFs while producing few noticeable adverse effects. This nanoparticle-based vaccine exhibits synergistic effects when combined with chemotherapeutic drugs [ ]. One strategy involves the use of a gene vaccine to specifically eliminate CAFs, thereby eliminating their tumor-promoting effects. A synthetic consensus (SynCon) FAP DNA vaccine has been developed that supports the breaking of immune tolerance. This vaccine inhibits tumor growth by inducing CD8 + and CD4 + immune responses [ ]. However, since FAP is not expressed by CAFs alone, targeting FAP often results in severe side effects. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) utilizing photosensitizers and specific wavelengths may offer solutions. One study developed a PDT approach that eliminates site-specific CAFs while inducing broad-spectrum anticancer immunity [ ]. Similarly, novel near-infrared photoimmunotherapy (NIR-PIT) has attracted much attention for its ability to target FAP to eliminate CAFs. FAP-targeting NIR-PIT specifically eliminates CAFs to inhibit tumor growth and improve tumor immunosuppression [ ]. Furthermore, FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) inhibit CAF activity by specifically binding to FAP. Owing to the homing properties of tumor tissue, FAPI can be used in PET imaging for the diagnosis of FAP expression [ ].
Other surface markers of CAFs can also be used as therapeutic targets to inhibit tumor progression. For example, in breast cancer, the IL-6R inhibitor tocilizumab (TCZ) inhibits the proliferation and migration of CAFs by consistently suppressing the expression of various CAF biomarkers, including α-SMA and SDF-1 [ ]. Recently, in CRC, silibinin was shown to affect the growth and proliferation of CAFs by significantly reducing the protein and mRNA levels of α-SMA and periostin in CAFs [ ]. Although research on CAF surface marker targeting is ongoing, specific CAF surface markers are currently lacking. Therefore, further research is needed to find more precise, effective and safer CAF surface markers.

## Targeting the CAF activation pathway
Given that CAFs exert a protumorigenic function via activation, another feasible strategy is to target the CAF activation pathway to reduce the number of infiltrating CAFs in the PMN (Fig.  B). Sustained protumorigenic effects of CAFs require activation and maintenance of the protumorigenic phenotype mediated by specific signal transduction pathways. TDSFs play an important role in the CAF activation process. Recently, cancer cell-targeting nanoliposomes have been developed that inhibit exosome biogenesis and release from lung cancer cells to prevent LFs from differentiating into CAFs. In addition, this study revealed that CAF-targeted nanoliposomes can inversely activate CAFs to become quiescent fibroblasts by blocking two different nodes in the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR)-Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Combining nanoliposome therapy to simultaneously block fibroblast activation and maintain the CAF phenotype is certainly a promising therapeutic strategy [ ]. IL-6 and PDGF, as fibroblast activators, also activate CAFs [ ]. In PDGFRβ+-CAF-containing pancreatic cancer, NK-mediated synergistic treatment with nintedanib is expected to inhibit blockade of the platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) signaling pathway to suppress IL-6 secretion by CAFs. The combined application of nintedanib inhibits CAF activation and growth and enhances NK-mediated tumor killing [ ]. TGF-β signaling, a key factor driving fibroblast differentiation and reprogramming, has been intensively studied. An attractive strategy is to deplete TGF-β to make CAFs more “normal”. An example of this approach is TGF-β-TRAP, which regulates CAF heterogeneity by depleting TGF-β. Studies suggest that in PDAC, TGF-β-TRAP may modulate the CCL5/CCR5 axis to reprogram CAFs into antitumor immune agonists [ ]. EVs are key mediators of CAF intercellular communication and provide an entry point for targeted therapy [ ]. Engineered EVs provide new strategies for targeted reprogramming of CAF. For example, one study developed integrin α5-targeting peptide-modified EVs (IEVs-PFD/138) loaded with miR-138-5p and the antifibrotic drug pirfenidone (PFD). IEVs-PFD/138 synergistically inhibits CAF activation and fibrosis through a dual mechanism: miR-138-5p targets and inhibits the TGF-β signaling pathway and collagen synthesis while PFD inhibits TGF-β expression. This strategy effectively reprogrammed CAFs, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and enhancing the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy [ ].
CAFs with functional heterogeneity are classified into protumor and antitumor phenotypes. Reprogramming CAFs into antitumor phenotypes is certainly an attractive therapy. A recent study provided an exciting opportunity to specifically induce antitumor phenotypes of ifnCAFs with stimulator of interferon gene agonists that promote antitumor phenotypes of TANs in addition to inhibiting the prometastatic effects of CAFs on PDAC tumor cells [ ]. Notably, the activity and silencing of YAP1 significantly regulate the CAF phenotype; thus, YAP1 may be a key therapeutic target in the future [ ]. Furthermore, the TGFβ1-mediated signaling pathway is an important mechanism that regulates fibroblast activity. Studies have shown that TGFβ1-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) inhibitors block the TAK1 + CAF phenotype to promote the conversion of oncogenic CAFs to antitumor CAFs [ ].

## Inhibiting the tumor-promoting function of CAFs
Targeting the tumor-promoting function of CAFs provides another potential therapeutic option given their significance for tumor progression. Tumor-trained fibroblasts can create supportive ECM conditions and an immunosuppressive environment for CTCs to colonize and proliferate by promoting ECM remodeling and immunosuppression, which provides clues for targeting the tumor-promoting function of CAFs to inhibit PMN formation (Fig.  C) [ ].
Therapies to inhibit CAF-mediated ECM remodeling by targeting fibrosis-activated signaling pathways in CAFs are in development. CAF-mediated remodeling of the ECM results in a denser matrix, which creates a physical barrier that prevents drug penetration to reduce therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, the inhibition of CAF-mediated ECM remodeling helps increase tumor sensitivity to anticancer drugs. The previously discussed mechanisms of IEVs-PFD/138 for tumor treatment include the inhibition of CAF-mediated ECM remodeling. PFD in EVs is itself an antifibrotic drug, and miR-138-5p in EVs can inhibit proline-mediated collagen synthesis by directly targeting the FERMT2‒PYCR1 complex. The combination of the two can effectively remodel the TME, loosen the clotted matrix structure and enhance the perfusion of gemcitabine, thus improving the chemotherapeutic effect [ ]. Recently, Halofuginone (HF) was found to inhibit collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1) production via the mTOR-eIF2α-ATF4 axis in CAFs, which interferes with collagen deposition and promotes CD8 + T-cell infiltration [ ]. In addition, a recent study developed a novel dendritic polymer nanodrug that improves drug delivery and enhances antitumor immune responses by remodeling the ECM. Specifically, this nanomedicine was used for the sequential delivery of dasatinib (DAS) and epirubicin (Epi). DAS reduces ECM deposition by downregulating CAF collagen anabolism and energy metabolism, which promotes tumor penetration of Epi cells and thus improves therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Similarly, a CAF-associated ITGB1-inactivating peptide-enriched membrane nanodelivery system, which inhibits matrix production by CAFs through the binding of FNIII14 to integrin β1 (ITGB1), thereby reducing matrix pressure to facilitate drug perfusion, has been developed [ ]. FR17, an enzyme-activated assembly peptide, reportedly acts as a “flame retardant blanket” in the PMN to inhibit metastasis-supporting ECM remodeling and reverse vascular destabilization and angiogenesis, which inhibits the formation of a supportive tumor microenvironment and suppresses tumor progression [ ]. TGF-β is not only involved in activating CAFs but also plays an important role in ECM remodeling. Therapies targeting TGF-β signaling to inhibit CAF-mediated ECM remodeling are likewise promising strategies [ ].
Considering the critical role of crosstalk between CAFs and immune cells in inducing immunosuppression in the PMN, a therapeutic strategy in which CAFs are targeted to prevent the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment seems to be more feasible. The previously discussed in situ-assembled peptide nanoblinket prevents MDSC recruitment to the PMN in the lung by inhibiting metastasis-supporting ECM remodeling, which reduces PMN formation by inhibiting the generation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ]. As previously described, TAK1-activated TGFβ1 plays an important role in CAF activation and in the crosstalk between CAFs and immune cells. TAK1 inhibitors reduce the production of inflammatory factors, increase the number of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells to increase antitumor immunity, and reduce the number of immunosuppressive cells in vivo through multiple pathways associated with immunosuppression [ ]. Pharmacological interference with FN1-integrin α5β1 signaling via the use of cilengitide to remodel FAP + CAF-derived FN1-mediated M2-like macrophage polarization has also been shown to be effective [ ]. The massive infiltration of CAFs is an important factor in tumor resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. A study revealed that the administration of Am80, a synthetic retinoid, prior to ICB treatment enhanced the efficacy of ICB by increasing M1 TAM infiltration to increase Meflin + CAF infiltration [ ].
Metabolic reprogramming in CAFs, which are hijacked by tumor cells for energy, also provides a target for tumor suppression therapy. In pancreatic cancer, MME (membrane metalloendopeptidase)-overexpressing CAFs induce MYC overexpression via the glycolytic pathway to drive tumor cell survival and proliferation. The MME + CAF inhibitor IOX2 (a specific prolyl hydroxylase-2 inhibitor) specifically targets MME + CAFs to inhibit glycolysis. A previous study revealed that the combination of IOX2 with paclitaxel + gemcitabine (AG) and anti-PD1 could effectively target MME with good antitumor effects [ ]. CAF-expressed methyltransferase nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT)-mediated vitamin B3 (VB 3) metabolism is a key determinant of cancer cell fate and function. CAF-remodeling nanoparticles (NPs) containing siRNAs targeting NNMT and coated with CAF membranes can reprogram aberrant VB 3 metabolism in CAFs by downregulating the level of NNMT, providing an innovative perspective with broad applicability for overcoming cancer drug resistance [ ]. Furthermore, in response to the significant up-regulation of ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) in CAFs and driving ECM production, a study developed carrier-free nanoagents (CFNAs) formed by self-assembly of a specific ACLY inhibitor, NDI-091143, with doxorubicin (DOX) or paclitaxel (PTX) via non-covalent interaction. This strategy utilizes NDI-091143 to inhibit ACLY-mediated fatty acid synthesis and energy metabolism in CAFs, weakening their hyperactive state and decreasing ECM secretion, providing a new pathway for matrix-reduced potentiation chemotherapy [ ].

## Challenges and perspectives
In recent years, thanks to the development of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics approaches, many studies have revealed the heterogeneity of CAFs and its associated complex network of interactions. However, it is these studies that continue to highlight multiple key aspects of CAFs that have yet to be elucidated, and several challenges to CAF-targeted therapies need to be overcome in order to translate these findings from the laboratory to clinical practice.

Lack of subpopulation identification and origin tracing: lack of clear biomarkers to accurately identify CAF subpopulations and trace their cellular origins (e.g., NFs, MSCs, etc.) has led to clinical failure of therapies due to off-target toxicity caused by extensive depletion of stromal cells.
Difficulty in identifying tumor-promoting/tumor-suppressing subpopulations: existing biomarkers make it difficult to distinguish tumor-promoting subpopulations from tumor-suppressing subpopulations in a stable and specific manner, resulting in therapeutic inadvertent targeting of tumor-suppressing CAFs, which can undermine therapeutic efficacy and even promote tumor progression.
Dynamic plasticity and therapeutic resistance: CAF phenotype is dynamically regulated by complex microenvironmental signals (e.g., cytokines, mechanical stresses, metabolites), which can transform the tumor-promoting phenotype under therapeutic pressure, leading to failure of single-targeting strategies.
Insufficient knowledge of cross-cancer heterogeneity: the molecular features and functional classification of CAF subpopulations in different tumor types and their association with clinical prognosis/therapeutic response have not been systematically elucidated, hindering the design of precision targeting strategies.
Unexplained microenvironmental interactions: the dynamic regulatory networks (involving biochemical signaling, physical mechanics, metabolic reprogramming, and immune regulation) between CAFs and tumor cells, immune cells, and extracellular matrix need to be analyzed in depth and in multiple dimensions.
Limitations of preclinical models: most studies have relied on in vitro cultures or a single animal model. However, in vitro conditions are prone to alter CAF phenotypes, and animal models are difficult to mimic the complexity of the human tumor microenvironment, resulting in a disconnect between preclinical data and clinical trial results.

Currently, the lack of spatial context in the single-cell technologies applied in a large number of studies hinders the understanding of how CAF location and local signaling microenvironments determine its function [ ]. In the future, further development of spatial multi-omics technologies (spatial transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, spatial metabolomics), combined with spatial pathology and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven computational tools provide solutions [ ]. As noted in the Sahai et al. 2020 Consensus statement on the CAF field’s collective agreement towards research directions., to comprehensively analyze the heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs, researchers should develop and apply complex organoid models containing patient-derived CAFs, immune cells, tumor cells, and engineered ECMs, as well as genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), to dynamically and controllably study the mechanisms of CAF state transitions and validate the functions of specific CAF subpopulations in local microenvironments that are closer to the real ones [ ].

## Conclusions
Studies have shown that CAFs are deeply involved in the formation of PMN by mediating key processes such as inflammation, angiogenesis/lymphangiogenesis, immunosuppression and ECM remodeling, creating favorable conditions for tumor metastasis. Their metabolic reprogramming and secreted EVs play important roles in regulating PMN establishment, providing new ideas for intervening metastasis.
However, the complex origins, highly heterogeneous and dynamically moldable phenotypes and functions of CAFs have led to significant challenges in the existing clinical strategies for targeting CAFs: the lack of specific markers predisposes to off-target toxicity; it is difficult to differentiate between tumor-promoting/tumor-suppressing subgroups; and there is a risk of treatment-induced drug resistance.
On the one hand, the key to future breakthroughs lies in the use of spatial multi-omics, AI and advanced models (e.g., complex organoids, GEMMs) to deeply resolve the heterogeneity, spatial localization, and function of CAF subpopulations. On the other hand it is equally important to develop strategies that target specific tumor-promoting subpopulations or their key signaling pathways. In-depth understanding of the mechanisms by which CAF subpopulations regulate PMN in different tumors will drive the development of safer and more effective personalized therapies.
Limitations of the review.
This review highlights the crucial roles of CAFs in PMN formation. However, a key limitation in the underlying research must be acknowledged: many studies investigating fibroblast functions in the PMN rely heavily on in vitro models. These models inherently lack the full complexity of the dynamic, multi-cellular in vivo PMN microenvironment. Consequently, findings from simplified systems may not fully reflect physiological PMN biology.
This reliance underscores the relative scarcity of comprehensive in vivo studies specifically focused on PMN-resident fibroblasts. Although several in vivo studies demonstrating CAF involvement in distant organs (e.g., the roles of exosomal miR-1247-3p and circ_0011496 in lung PMN formation [ , ], the involvement of COX-2 + adventitial fibroblasts and senescent fibroblasts in lung immunosuppression [ , ], and ActA-induced ECM deposition [ ]) were discussed, direct investigation of fibroblast dynamics within the evolving in vivo PMN remains limited. Therefore, while in vitro data provide valuable mechanistic insights, extrapolation to the complex in vivo context requires caution. Future research prioritizing sophisticated in vivo models is essential.
