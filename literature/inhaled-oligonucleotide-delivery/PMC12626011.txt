## Significance
Intratumorally delivered STING-agonistic cyclic dinucleotides (CDN) exhibit therapeutic benefit in preclinical glioblastoma (GBM) models, but inadequate bioavailability limits CDN therapy to invasive intratumoral administration. Here, we have designed, synthesized, and developed a first-in-class cGAS- agonistic spherical nucleic acid (SNA) structure that can reprogram the immunosuppressive GBM immune microenvironment by triggering cGAS–STING pathway activation. When administered via noninvasive nose-to-brain delivery, this strategy elicits potent antiglioma activity and overcomes immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment resistance. The use of SNAs addresses the challenges of noninvasive nucleic acid delivery to intracranial tumor sites and establishes ISD45-SNAs as a immunotherapeutic modality for GBM treatment.
Keywords: DNA therapeutics, glioblastoma, cGAS–STING, tumor microenvironment, immunotherapy

## Abstract
The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-Stimulator of Interferon Genes (cGAS–STING) pathway is an important DNA-sensing mechanism that increases T cell trafficking and activation in tumors and reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of myeloid cells. Therefore, direct STING targeting using synthetic cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) is an attractive strategy for treating lymphocyte-depleted and myeloid cell–enriched tumors, such as glioblastoma (GBM). However, inadequate bioavailability and poor cellular accumulation limit the clinical development of CDNs, particularly for noninvasive administration strategies. Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) have emerged as promising modular constructs for creating therapeutic lead compounds for many diseases, including different forms of cancer. Here, we report the development of cGAS-activating SNAs that consist of gold nanoparticle cores functionalized with a shell of densely packed interferon-stimulatory DNA oligonucleotides (ISD45-SNAs). These nanostructures bind to cGAS, the sensor of cytosolic dsDNA upstream of STING, promoting the catalytic production of endogenous CDNs and downstream STING activation more potently than clinically tested CDNs. When administered intranasally or intratumorally to poorly immunogenic syngeneic GBM mouse models, ISD45-SNAs inhibit tumor growth more effectively than CDNs and promote long-term animal subject survival through specific cGAS–STING pathway activation. ISD45-SNAs induce a proinflammatory immune microenvironment enriched with effector T cells and proinflammatory macrophages. When coadministered with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), they abolish GBM tumor development and induce long-term antiglioma immunity. These studies establish ISD45-SNAs as an immune-stimulatory modality for triggering innate and adaptive immune responses and increasing ICI efficacy for GBM treatment.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-Stimulator of Interferon Genes (cGAS–STING) pathway is an important DNA-sensing mechanism that bridges innate and adaptive immunity. cGAS–STING activation triggers interferon and cytokine release and enhances T cell infiltration and activation within tumors while re-educating protumorigenic “M2-like” macrophages toward a proinflammatory, antitumorigenic “M1-like” phenotype, and reversing the immune suppressive phenotype of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) ( – ). On the molecular level, double-stranded (ds), but not single-stranded (ss) DNA, is recognized in the cytosol by the DNA sensor cGAS, ( ), resulting in the production of cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs), including 2′,3′–cyclic GMP-AMP (2′,3′-cGAMP), a potent agonist of the STING enzyme ( ). STING activation induces the expression of type I interferon (IFN) and proinflammatory cytokines by activating the TANK-binding Kinase 1-Interferon Regulatory Factor 3 (TBK1-IRF3) signaling axis, leading to the induction of both innate and adaptive immunity ( , ).
Tumor-associated myeloid cells (TAMCs) are a large proportion of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the glioblastoma (GBM) tumor microenvironment (TME) and represent the majority of immune cells in human GBM samples, compared to the small number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes ( , ). TAMCs strongly inhibit innate and adaptive immunity by creating an immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting TME and suppressing immune effector cell activity of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells ( , , ). The direct targeting of STING with synthetic cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) offers a promising therapeutic approach for treating tumors that are low in lymphocytes and rich in myeloid cells, as demonstrated in preclinical studies using immunocompetent murine GBM mice and spontaneous canine glioma ( – ). However, low cellular membrane permeability, inadequate bioavailability ( – ), and the potential for dose-limiting toxicities upon systemic administration are limiting factors in the clinical translation of synthetic CDNs and small molecule STING activators ( , ). Furthermore, while direct intratumoral administration of CDNs enables tumor-specific accumulation and reduces undesirable systemic distribution to normal tissues, such local administration approaches for GBM treatment are invasive and not amenable to repeat dosing schedules.
Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) are modular nanostructures that consist of a nanoparticle core densely functionalized with a shell of radially oriented oligonucleotides ( , ). SNAs exhibit properties vastly different from their linear counterparts, including their uptake in many cell types in high quantities without the need for auxiliary transfection agents. Comprehensive preclinical assessment of gene-regulatory, siRNA-based SNAs and immunostimulatory toll-like receptor (TLR)-9-agonistic SNAs carrying CpG-rich DNA oligonucleotides in GBM and other solid cancer models, together with their evaluation in early phase clinical trials (NCT03020017 and NCT03086278) ( – ), have shown that SNAs represent safe precision therapies for gene regulation and stimulating antitumor immune responses ( , ).
Here, we report the synthesis and characterization of cGAS-activating SNA architectures with high surface density of a 45 basepair (bp) double-stranded (ds) interferon-stimulatory (ISD) DNA on gold nanoparticle cores (ISD45-SNAs). Our data show that ISD45-SNAs activate the cGAS–STING pathway more potently than clinically tested CDNs. Moreover, when intratumorally or intranasally administered to immunocompetent GBM-bearing mice, ISD45-SNAs inhibit tumor growth more potently than CDNs, re-educate the TME from a tumor-promoting to a tumor-suppressive phenotype, and synergize with immune-mediated checkpoint inhibition to promote long-term animal subject survival through cGAS–STING pathway activation.

## Results

## ISD45-SNAs Bind to cGAS and Activate the cGAS–STING–IRF3 Pathway.
To investigate the efficacy of the SNAs as an immunostimulant for GBM treatment, we generated a cGAS-activating SNA nanoarchitecture by conjugating 15 nm gold nanoparticles (AuNP) with 45-bp double-stranded oligonucleotides (ISD45-SNAs; ), following established protocols ( , , ). A 45 bp interferon stimulating dsDNA sequence lacking contiguous CpG sequences represents the prototypic oligonucleotide for cGAS–STING-mediated induction of type 1 IFN responses ( ). Both sense and antisense oligonucleotides were hybridized, and the duplexes were then chemisorbed to the gold nanoparticle surface [see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C for the characterization of z-average hydrodynamic sizes, zeta potential, polydispersity (PDI), and oligonucleotide loading densities].
 The dependency of SNA-mediated cGAS activation on a functional cGAS–STING pathway was tested in isogenic cGAS, STING, and IRF3-deficient RAW-LuciaTM macrophages ( ), with results confirming abrogation of IRF induction following ISD45-SNA treatment or transfection of free ISD45-DNA in the absence of STING, cGAS, or IRF3. To assess the direct engagement of the cGAS enzyme by ISD45-SNAs, we developed an ex cellulo activation assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D) using a mixture of recombinant human cGAS enzyme, MgCl2, ATP, and GTP incubated with either cGAMP, ssDNAT45-SNAs, or ISD45-SNAs. After terminating the reaction by heat inactivation of the cGAS enzyme, the mixture was transfected into cGAS KO RAW-LuciaTM macrophages, carrying stable integration of an IRF-inducible Lucia luciferase reporter to quantify IRF induction, an indicator of STING activation. The reaction mixture composed of ISD45-SNAs activated IRF, whereas ssDNAT45-SNAs did not, since cGAS does not bind to ssDNA (negative control) ( ). Cultures transfected with an excess of cGAMP served as positive controls. Using an EMSA, we determined the dissociation constants (Kd) of ISD45-SNA bound to cGAS. The Kd for ISD45-SNA was 0.24 µM, while ssDNA45-SNA controls did not engage with cGAS ( ). In addition, we tested the impact of dsISD45 oligonucleotide surface density on gold nanoparticle cores on IRF activation. We found that higher DNA densities result in higher IRF induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E), demonstrating that, at the range of densities examined (2 to 10 nmoles of ISD45 oligonucleotides), denser arrays of surface ISD oligonucleotides do not impede access to the cGAS binding dsDNA binding pocket. Next, we explored the impact of longer dsDNA as a ligand for cGAS, since longer dsDNA molecules tend to enhance cGAS activation due to increased binding affinity and cooperative activation. Interestingly, the in vitro activity of SNAs carrying a 60 bp ISD sequence was reduced compared to our ISD45-SNAs, as evidenced by IRF reporter assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). PicoGreen-based quantification of dsDNA revealed comparable DNA loading with ISD60-SNA carrying about 90 dsDNA per NP core and ISD45-SNA carrying 112 dsDNA per NP core (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). We further analyzed the cell-free interaction of human recombinant cGAS with ISD45- or ISD60-SNAs using a well-established EMSA, which indicates that the Kd for ISD45-SNA (0.24 µM) is similar to that of the ISD60-SNA (0.25 µM) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). Cellular IRF activation in response to SNA treatment was preceded by robust uptake of SNAs into macrophages, as assessed by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis of gold detectable in cell pellets ( ).
GBM tumors are mainly composed of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) with an anti-inflammatory “M0/2-like” phenotype ( , ). Several studies have shown that activating the cGAS–STING pathway antagonizes tumor growth by promoting proinflammatory “M1-like” polarized macrophage activation ( ). Therefore, we examined whether ISD45-SNAs promoted “M0-like” to “M1-like” macrophage conversion. In RAW-LuciaTM macrophages, ISD45-SNAs (but not ssDNA45-SNAs) triggered robust TBK1 phosphorylation and reduced STING expression, likely due to STING proteasomal degradation as part of a negative feedback loop to prevent excessive inflammatory signaling ( ). Tumor cells treated with conditioned medium from ISD45-SNA-exposed macrophages ( ) or cocultured with ISD45-SNA-treated macrophages ( ) showed reduced proliferation over time, demonstrating an enhanced antitumor macrophage phenotype upon ISD45-SNA treatment. In addition, multiplexed cytokine profiling by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and antibody arrays revealed activation of NF-κB- and STAT6-induced proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNFα, and CCL2, in response to ISD45-SNA treatment ( ). RNA-Seq and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) of RAW macrophages treated with PBS, ssDNAT45-SNA controls, or ISD45-SNAs, revealed modulation of TNFα, cytosolic DNA signaling, viral infection, and NF-κB signaling pathways in response to ISD45-SNA treatment ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2) ( ), with Ifnb1, Cxcl10, and Tnfa representing the top differentially expressed genes in ISD45- versus ssDNA45-SNA-treated cells. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that ISD45-SNAs exhibited higher expression of genes encoding proinflammatory cytokines (Cxcl10, Ifnb1) compared to the control treatments in RAW macrophage cells ( ). Notably, the cGAS-activating properties of ISD45-SNAs stem from the densely functionalized and highly oriented nucleic acid shell and not from the nanoparticle core, as previously reported for other SNAs ( , ). Similar to gold-based architectures, ISD45-SNAs with 80-nm poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) polymer-based cores (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C) robustly accumulated in RAW macrophages via phagocytosis and scavenger receptor-mediated endocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D) and promoted IRF activation with lower EC50 values than Aduro-S100, a CDN evaluated in clinical trials for non-CNS solid cancers ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). For subsequent studies, we focused on gold-based SNAs, as these structures have been clinically vetted for treating GBM patients as assessed in a first-in-man phase 0 clinical trial ( ).

## Nose-To-Brain Delivery of SNAs Antagonizes GBM Progression and Overcomes Resistance to Immune-Check Point Inhibition.
To determine the antiglioma activity of ISD45-SNA in vivo, we sought to evaluate two delivery approaches, i.e., intranasal and intratumoral administration. Both strategies circumvent the limitations to tumor access imposed by the blood–brain barrier when systemically administering therapeutics, avoid first-pass metabolism, and minimize the occurrence of a systemic toxic response to treatment like cytokine storms ( ). The intranasal route of administration allows therapeutic access to tumors by trafficking material through the olfactory and trigeminal nerves that connect the nasal passage to the brain ( , ). The olfactory and trigeminal nerves form direct anatomical links between the central nervous system and the nasal passages. Olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) send their axons through the cribriform plate to form synapses on glomeruli within the olfactory bulb. The trigeminal pathway, particularly its V1 (ophthalmic) and V2 (maxillary) branches, innervates both the respiratory and olfactory regions of the nasal passages, connecting the brainstem and the nasal lamina propria. A broad spectrum of intranasally administered drugs and nanomaterials are associated with the epineurium, perineurium, and, to a lesser extent, the endoneurium of the trigeminal nerve or localized to the perivascular space ( – ), suggesting that CNS drug transport mainly occurs along the extracellular components of the trigeminal nerve, as it enters the pons from the nasal passages ( , , ). Our results from In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) and ICP-MS analyses (SI Appendix, Fig S4A) of Cy5 or Cy5.5-conjugated SNAs administered via a single droplet inoculation to the nasal cavity of GBM-bearing mice indicate robust SNA accumulation in isolated tumor-bearing brains ( ) and trigeminal nerves ( ). To identify which cell types internalize ISD45-SNAs, we conducted flow cytometry analysis on dissociated SB28 tumor 20 h post intranasal and intracranial SNA treatment. We enrolled SNAs cofunctionalized with the indocyanine green (ICG) dye, which fluoresces in the near-infrared. We used cell-type-specific markers (Live/dead marker, CD45, CD11b, and CD11c) to distinguish between TAMCs (CD11bhigh and CD45high), microglia (CD11bhigh and CD45high), and lymphocytes (CD45+, Cd11b−). Our data indicate that TAMCs exhibited the highest levels of ISD45-SNA uptake (eightfold higher via i.c. and twofold higher via i.n. administration, compared to untreated control), reflecting robust uptake of SNAs into myeloid cells in vitro, compared to tumor or endothelial cells ( ). Microglia and lymphocytes showed some uptake, but differences in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of SNAs did not reach significance in these cell types ( ).
 Similar to tumor-bearing mice, SNAs accumulated in non-tumor-bearing brains, (including trigeminal nerves) and, to a lesser extent, in lungs ( ), likely due to inhalation following intranasal administration. Sections of the trigeminal nerves were examined by immunofluorescence microscopy after intranasal administration of saline or Cy5.5-tagged SNAs, and neuronal cell bodies and axons were counterstained with antibodies specific to the neuronal marker protein neuronal nuclear protein (NeuN). Cy5.5-SNAs were localized to the perineurium (the space between axon bundles) and epineurium (the space in the outermost layer) of the trigeminal nerve and within perineural spaces of the endoneurium ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). These data are consistent with the conclusion that intranasally administered SNAs utilize perineural pathways associated with the trigeminal nerves to enter the CNS from the nasal lamina propria. To assess whether SNA lung accumulation upon intranasal delivery causes pulmonary inflammation, we measured transcript levels of the F3 gene (encoding the protein tissue factor; TF) in lung tissue samples isolated 24 h post intranasal delivery, using RT-qPCR. TF is a type I IFN-stimulated protein released from macrophages and represents a well-established lung injury maker ( ). Our findings demonstrate that ISD45-SNA treatment did not trigger acute type I IFN responses in lung tissue, as evidenced by low levels of F3 transcript level, as compared to control untreated samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). We further assessed lung toxicity through histopathological analysis of lung tissue isolated from ISD45-SNA-treated mice. Results from H&E and TUNEL staining did not reveal the presence of inflammatory infiltrates or tissue damage, suggesting that ISD45-SNAs did not cause pulmonary toxicity (SI Appendix, Fig S5B). Finally, observing mice after intratumoral or intranasal administration with ISD45-SNAs, revealed no behavioral changes, suggesting that ISD45-SNA treatment does not result in noticeable behavioral side effects.
Next, we studied the antiglioma activity of ISD45-SNAs following intranasal administration in the immunocompetent CT-2A GBM tumor model. ISD45-SNA intranasal administration extended animal subject survival to a greater extent than that of Aduro-S100 (ADU-S100), specifically in female ( ) but not in male animals ( ). The pathway specificity of SNAs was validated in female CT-2A glioma-bearing C57BL/6-STING goldenticket (gt/gt) syngeneic mice, harboring nonfunctional STING in the TME. Bioluminescence imaging revealed that a single intranasal administration of ISD45-SNAs reduced tumor growth in C57BL/6 wild-type mice ( ) but did not impact tumor growth or survival in STING-deficient goldenticket (gt/gt) mice ( ), demonstrating that ISD45-SNAs antitumor effect is dependent on STING expression in the host. When CT-2A-bearing animals were cotreated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (anti-PD-L1+anti-CTLA4; ), a one-time intranasal delivery of ISD45-SNAs abolished GBM tumor development in nearly all female animals ( ) and induced immunological memory as indicated by long-term survival of ISD45-SNA treated female mice after rechallenging with CT-2A tumor injected into the contralateral hemisphere ( ).
 

## Intranasally Administered ISD45-SNAs Re-Educate the Tumor Immune Microenvironment and Activate Immune Cells in the Cervical Draining Lymph Node.
A multispectral Cytek flow analysis of immune cells in both the TME and the deep draining cervical lymph nodes (dcLNs) (see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 for gating strategy) revealed enrichment of “M1-like” polarized TAMCs (CD45high CD11bhigh), characterized by the induction of proinflammatory marker expression (CD40, TNFα, INFγ, and 4-1BBL; ), increased abundance of effector T cells, and reduced level of regulatory T cells (Tregs) ( ). We then performed unbiased secondary flow cytometric analysis to assess lineage-specific phenotypic alterations in response to ISD45-SNA treatment. The immune cell composition within the dcLNs was analyzed using the FlowSOM algorithm, and the frequencies and counts of distinct immune populations in dcLNs from mice treated with control ssDNAT45-SNA or ISD45-SNAs were visualized using minimum spanning trees (MST). In ISD45-SNA-treated mice, we found enrichment of NK cell populations expressing significantly higher levels of granzyme B and the degranulation marker CD107a and activation of B cell populations compared to PBS or ssDNAT45-SNA controls ( ). We next used cluster identification, characterization, and regression (CITRUS), an unsupervised machine-learning algorithm, to define cell populations and identify statistically significant marker expressions that can differentiate between the ISD45-SNA and ssDNAT45-SNA control groups. CITRUS analysis identified the minimum cross-validated error rate (cv.min) model with a high predictive value (0% cross-validation error; SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). This model was characterized by 10 features that predicted immune composition differences between ISD45-SNA- and ssDNAT45-SNA-treated mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Radial hierarchy trees for live CD45+ leukocytes identified 3 clusters with significant differences between ssDNAT45- and ISD45-SNA-treated mice ( , see SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). Clusters 89998 and 89992 were found to be enriched in the TME of ISD45-SNA-treated mice, while cluster 89948 was prevalent in the TME of ssDNAT45-SNA-treated mice. Cluster 89998 was identified as a CD8+ T cell subset, and clusters 89992 and 89948 represented CD11b+ myeloid cells from marker expression of all 10 clusters showing the expression of the indicated markers on the significant clusters versus all other backgrounds ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Reduced abundance of CD11b+ cells, with concomitant increases in CD40, TNFα, MHCII expression on cluster 89992 indicated “M1-like” activation of myeloid cells in the TME with ISD45-SNA treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E–G). Cluster 89948 in ssDNAT45-SNA-treated mice was enriched for CD45+ CD11b+ cells that showed an immunosuppressive phenotype, as indicated by low CD40, TNFα, and MHCII marker expression ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). On the other hand, high expression of CD45, CD8, INFγ, TIM-3, TNFα, and LAG-3 on clusters 89998 and 89992 indicated an exhausted CD8+ T cell phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). These analyses demonstrated that intranasal administration of ISD45-SNA promoted a T cell inflamed TME, characterized by increased numbers of cytotoxic and exhausted CD8+ T cells and a decreased abundance of immunosuppressive CD11b+ cells.
 

## Direct Intratumoral Delivery of ISD45-SNAs Antagonize Tumor Progression in Multiple Immunocompetent GBM Models Via On-Target Activation of Type I IFNs.
To evaluate intratumoral delivery of cGAS-agonistic SNA as a potential therapeutic approach, we administered ssDNAT45- or ISD45-SNAs (corresponding to 0.15 mg/kg of dsDNA, the same amount as administered via nose-to-brain delivery) 7 d post tumor cell engraftment via a single intratumoral injection to CT-2A, SB28, and QPP7 glioma-bearing mice, the latter representing a syngeneic orthotopic model based on tumor neurospheres derived from a genetically engineered GBM mouse model. In this model, glioma formation is driven by tamoxifen-inducible CNS-specific deletion of Quaking, a STAR family RNA-binding protein that regulates multipotency and self-renewal in neural and glioma stem cells, and p53 and PTEN tumor suppressors (genotype: Nestin-CreERT2; QkiL/L; PtenL/L; Trp53L/L, referred to as QPP model) ( , ). An analysis of animal subject survival using the Kaplan–Meier method revealed that ISD45-SNAs increased survival in female mice, with long-term survival observed in 60 to 80% of female CT-2A and QPP7-bearing mice ( ), respectively, and a moderate increase in survival in ICI-resistant, female SB28-bearing mice ( ). To quantitively assess intratumoral IFN activation in response to ISD45-SNA treatment, we used a magnetic resonance (MR) and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging strategy to ascertain immune activation in the TME ( ). Recently, PET was used to visualize IFN signaling in the TME of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDACs) ( ). In PDACs, IFN activation in response to STING activation impacted nucleotide metabolism via the transcriptional induction of metabolism-associated genes, which reduced intratumoral levels of thymidine and, in turn, triggered an increase in [18F]FLT probe uptake ( ). Upon confirmation of intracranial CT-2A tumor growth and location by MRI ( ), we administered ssDNAT45- or ISD45-SNAs via intracranial administration, followed by intravascular injection of 60 μCi [18F]FLT. Treatment with ISD45-SNAs, but not control ssDNAT45-SNAs, increased median standardized uptake value (SUV) within intracranial tumor elements ( ), demonstrating that PET imaging can be used to noninvasively determine pharmacodynamic effects of ISD45-SNAs.
 Multiparameter high dimensional flow cytometry analysis of female mice bearing SB28 tumors treated with ISD45-SNAs revealed a significant increase in M1-like marker expression, including TNFα, IFNγ, and CD86 on immunosuppressive TAMCs, and a decrease in the M2-like marker CD206 as compared to the control groups ( ). Further dissecting specific TAMC populations, including TAMs (CD11b+Ly6GlowLy6Clow) and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) defined as CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow cells, we observed a similar trend of increased expression of TNFα and IFNγ in the ISD45-SNAs treatment group suggesting an overall shift to a proinflammatory TAM and PMN-MDSC state ( ). Additionally, ISD45-SNAs treatment also activated microglia and increased expression of IFNγ. Since higher CD86 provides the costimulatory signals necessary for T cell activation and survival, we analyzed CD4+ and CD8+ T cells for expression of activation and exhaustion markers. TNFα and IFNγ expression was twofold higher in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells isolated from mice treated with ISD45-SNAs as compared to controls ( ), and the abundance of Tregs defined by CD4, CD25, and FoxP3 coexpression was increased with ISD45-SNA ( ). Similarly, even though there was a twofold increase in TNFα and IFNγ expression on CD8+ T cells, the exhaustion marker programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) was expressed at higher levels in CD8+ T cells in the ISD45-SNAs group ( ). This upregulation of FoxP3 on CD4+ T cells and coinhibitory checkpoint receptor PD-1 on CD8+ T cells in the ISD45-SNA-treated group is likely induced via proinflammatory cytokines like IFNγ and TNFα. In addition to modulating T cell immunity, we found that ISD45-SNAs induced a significant increase in CD40, 4-1BBL, and CD86 expression on CD19+ B cells ( ). CD40 is known to improve B cell–mediated antigen presentation, whereas the costimulatory marker 4-1BBL (or CD137L) enhances CD8+ T cell antitumor cytotoxicity ( – ). Finally, with ISD45-SNA treatment, we also observe robust NK cell activation as evidenced by the upregulation of TNFα, IFNγ, 4-1BBL, and the degranulation marker CD107a on CD3– NK1.1+ populations ( ). Our analyses of the GBM immune environment show that ISD45-SNAs activate a wide array of immune cell types and activate innate and adaptive immune responses for robust antitumor effects (see for heatmaps summarizing immune changes in the TME of ISD45-SNA-treated SB28-bearing mice versus controls).
 

## Discussion
We have developed a first-in-class SNA immunotherapy, ISD45-SNAs, that engages the high-priority immune target cGAS by presenting cGAS-activating double-stranded oligonucleotides covalently conjugated to gold nanoparticle cores. We have found that ISD45-SNAs, when administered via intranasal or direct intratumoral administration, potently activate antitumor immunity in a sex-specific manner, antagonize GBM tumor progression, and increase animal survival as monotherapy and in combination with ICI, in multiple immune-suppressed GBM tumor models.
GBM is incurable in nearly all instances, as indicated by one of the poorest 5-y survival rates among all cancers ( ). ICIs can improve outcomes for patients with immunologically “hot” tumors, including melanoma ( ), nonsmall cell lung ( ), and renal cell cancers ( ), but have failed to provide survival benefits for patients with immunologically “cold” tumors (i.e., low or non-T cell-inflamed), including GBM ( ). A major limitation of using ICIs or other therapeutics for combating GBM immunosuppression is the low T cell effector response against GBM. cGAS–STING pathway activation bridges the innate and adaptive immune systems by triggering interferon and cytokine release that promotes T cell effector function and trafficking to the tumor and by reversing the suppressive phenotype of MDSCs and macrophages. ISD45-SNA conjugates activated the cGAS–STING pathway in macrophages more robustly than CDNs, with an EC50 for IRF3 induction >20-fold lower compared to the clinically tested CDN Aduro-S100.
Our previous studies with DNA oligonucleotide-functionalized gold-based SNAs provided insight into the intracellular fate of SNAs. These studies have shown that they enter cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis involving scavenger receptors ( ). SNAs progress into late endosomes, their final intracellular location. Some of these particles escape the endosome and are found in the cytosol, which engages nucleic acid sensors, such as the cGAS enzyme. Moreover, SNAs traffic to the late endosomes, independent of the surface oligonucleotide sequences and the core compositions. Finally, we showed that SNAs are partially broken down by nucleases within the late endosome, with degradation products differentially processed by the cellular transport machinery ( ). The core is retained in the late endosome, while the oligonucleotide fragments are cleared from the cell. Based on these studies, we propose that the ability of SNAs to regulate cGAS pathway activation may partly be due to the increased stability of the oligonucleotides on SNAs compared to their free forms. This may allow them a longer cytosolic residency time. The observation that most SNAs reside in the endosome suggests they are potent modulators of cytosolic signaling, as only a small fraction escapes the endosome. As any increased availability of SNAs to the cytosol will likely boost the therapeutic activity of SNAs, in future studies, we propose the design and synthesis of SNAs capable of more efficient endosomal escape.
When administered intranasally to immunocompetent GBM-bearing mice, ISD45-SNA treatment resulted in superior median survival relative to Aduro-S100-treated mice and induced complete tumor regression when combined with ICIs. The analysis of immune cells in both the TME and dcLNs revealed an increased abundance of effector T- cells, enrichment of “M1-like,” proinflammatory macrophages, and increases in activated NK and B cell populations in mice receiving ISD45-SNAs treatment, compared to PBS and ssDNAT45-SNA-treated animals. In the highly immune-suppressed SB28 model, increased IFNγ signaling observed in TAMCs, NK, and effector T cells likely augmented PD-1 exhaustion marker expression in T and B cells, suggesting that ISD45-SNAs can promote a proinflammatory microenvironment at the cost of a compensatory expansion of exhausted adaptive immune cells that may ultimately contribute to immune evasion. Previous studies reported that the dose of a STING agonist can affect local STING pathway activation and systemic expansion of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells ( ). High (“tumor-ablative”) doses of STING agonists, while effective in clearing tumor burden in a TNFα-dependent manner, can diminish tumor-specific T cell responses and negatively affect the formation of immunological memory. In contrast, lower (“immunogenic”) dosages facilitate T cell activation that can synergize with ICI treatment, resulting in tumor control that is type I IFN- and TNFα-independent ( ). These studies point to the importance of finding the optimal dosing of ISD45-SNAs for maximum therapeutic benefit.
We investigated both intratumoral and intranasal delivery of ISD45-SNAs. Both administration strategies circumvent the limitations to tumor access imposed by the blood–brain barrier when systemically administering therapeutics, limit the occurrence of a systemic toxic response to treatment, and pharmacokinetic clearance. In addition to the comprehensive analysis of CNS toxicity and the assessment for the potential of induced autoimmunity, additional lines of investigations will include the formulation of SNAs in a hydrogel for sustained delivery into the TME and the development of optimized intranasal dosing schedules. Of note, recent studies have implicated a chronically active STING pathway in microglia to be linked to the onset of neurodegeneration in preclinical disease models ( ). Further preclinical and clinical studies are required to determine the immunological profile of CNS immune cells, particularly microglia, in response to ISD45-SNAs and characterize the kinetics of cGAS–STING pathway activation in healthy brain tissue.
Recent studies suggest emerging roles of the immune system in driving sex differences in GBM prevalence, clinical features, and therapeutic responses to ICI and describe greater immune responses, including those driven by type I IFNs, specifically in female macrophages compared to males ( , ). Additionally, research by Skopelja-Gardner et al. showed that cGAS–STING signaling mediates a nearly 10-fold higher ultraviolet B-driven type I IFN response in the skin of females, implicating sex differences as a major driver of cGAS-mediated immune responses ( ). Multiple studies demonstrated that male T cells are more exhausted than female T cells, indicating the important role of androgens in driving T cell exhaustion ( , ). In line with these studies, we found sex-specific T cell responses to ISD45-SNA treatment with greater therapeutic benefit observed in female subjects.
Finally, this research highlights the potential of the SNA platform as a tool for investigating the intricate structure–function dynamics that influence nanoimmunotherapeutic effectiveness. Our group’s optimization of TLR9-agonistic SNAs ( ), together with other studies ( , ), indicated the need to consider a broad range of structure–activity relationships when designing SNAs and other nanomedicines. Optimization of TLR9-activating, CpG-oligonucleotides carrying SNAs has revealed that SNA properties are interrelated in nonobvious ways and emphasizes the risks of using limited datasets to make conclusions about optimal structural compositions. While the difficulty in preparing and characterizing structurally well-defined nanomaterials and the challenges in altering design elements in a modular fashion have limited the number of nanomaterial candidates that can be prepared, tested, and ultimately translated to the clinic, SNAs are modular structures in which oligonucleotide sequence, attachment chemistry, and density can be independently controlled and evaluated by high-throughput testing ( ). Therefore, our work will enable the rational and precise therapeutic design of advancing next-generation nanomedicines.

## Materials and Methods
SNAs were synthesized by conjugating thiol-modified oligonucleotides (SI Appendix, Table S1) to gold nanoparticles. Deidentified cell lines were obtained from Invivogen and other researchers, cultured following specific protocols, and maintained in appropriate media conditions. For the generation of immunocompetent GBM tumor models, female and male C57BL/6 mice were implanted with glioma cells and treated with SNAs via intranasal route or intratumor injection. Tumor growth and survival were monitored over time post treatment. Imaging techniques were employed to track tumor growth in vivo, and the distribution of SNAs in organs was studied ex vivo. The immune cell populations of glioma tumors were stained with 30-color multiplex panel of antibodies (SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4) and analyzed with five-laser Cytek Northern Lights™ flow cytometer. All animal-related experiments were performed in full compliance with animal protocols approved by the Northwestern University and Washington University in Saint Louis Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. A detailed description of materials and methods is provided in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
