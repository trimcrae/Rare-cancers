## Introduction
Cutaneous fibrosis caused by deep dermal injuries remains a global healthcare challenge – . Pathological scarring not only compromises skin barrier integrity but also leads to contractures, chronic pain, and a substantial socioeconomic burden , – . Although there are some approaches for prevention and treatment, such as surgery and intralesional corticosteroid injections, these are unsatisfactory due to high recurrence rates – . Therefore, it is necessary to understand underlying mechanism and develop new effective therapies , , , .
Chronic inflammation localized in the reticular dermis is a well-established driver of fibrotic progression , . Emerging evidence indicates that while inflammatory fibroblasts (iFB) serve as pivotal orchestrators of wound contraction through myofibroblast differentiation, their dysregulated activation drives fibrosis via excessive secretion of CSF-1 and CXCL12, which perpetuates neutrophil recruitment and monocyte infiltration , . Consequently, the targeted suppression of iFB within the reticular dermis during the acute inflammatory phase seems a promising strategy to achieve scarless healing , . However, two critical challenges remain to be addressed: exploring the upstream regulators of iFB inflammatory phenotypes while developing targeted delivery systems capable of spatiotemporally (reticular dermis and inflammatory phase) controlled intervention.
Macrophage-fibroblast interactions remain central to wound repair and scar formation, particularly macrophage-mediated regulation of fibroblast activity , , – . Emerging evidence highlights the immune-stromal crosstalk mediated by TAM (Tyro3/Axl/MerTK) receptors as a gatekeeper of tissue homeostasis – . In arthritic and other inflammatory models, MerTK⁺ macrophages suppress fibroblasts inflammation while coordinating efferocytosis to resolve inflammation, suggesting that TAM receptors participate in the inflammatory regulatory effect of macrophages on fibroblasts – . Preclinical studies have also revealed that TAM-deficient mice exhibit impaired tissue repair, yet the role of TAM ligands (GAS6/PROS1) in cutaneous wound healing remains unexplored , – . Crucially, while research on TAM inhibitors advances in oncology trials, that on the pharmacological activation of this pathway, a promising strategy to recalibrate inflammatory microenvironments, remains underdeveloped , , .
Lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated mRNA delivery has emerged as a transformative therapeutic platform, achieving remarkable success in vaccine development and oncology due to its high efficiency and transient expression profile – . However, the application of LNP-mRNA technology in dermatology, particularly for scar prevention, remains highly emerging. The work of Wang et al. demonstrated the feasibility of using LNP-based interleukin-4 (IL-4) mRNA delivery to accelerate wound healing, underscoring the promise of mRNA strategies for precision dermatology . Nevertheless, it remains a major challenge to achieve localized, stable, and efficient delivery of LNP-mRNA within the dynamic wound microenvironment. In this context, the combination of a hydrogel with LNP-mRNA enabled successful protein expression in vivo and conferred significant therapeutic benefits, positioning it as a promising platform for mRNA delivery in wound therapy , .
Here, we demonstrate persistent GAS6 deficiency as a critical determinant of pathological repair via an integrated analysis of keloid/wound patients, murine wound models, and in vitro inflammatory models. Functional validation using pharmacological inhibition (RU-301) confirms the mechanistic role of GAS6 . We subsequently engineered an injectable lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-GAS6 mRNA-loaded thermosensitive hydrogel, achieving spatiotemporally controlled GAS6 expression in murine, rabbit, and porcine models, surpassing the efficacy of conventional therapy . This approach promotes scarless healing by a dual mechanism: enhancing efferocytosis and attenuating inflammatory signals from fibroblasts. Notably, single-dose administration reduces the fibrotic area by 53.69% in porcine wounds, superior to the Recombinant Human-Epidermal Growth Factor Gel (h-EGF gel, 32.18%) , , demonstrating translational potential. By targeting the inflammatory upstream rather than downstream fibrotic effectors, our work proposes regenerative strategies and establishes the scientific basis of mRNA-based modulation of immune-stromal networks as a promising strategy in precision dermatology .

## Results

## GAS6 is a potential therapeutic target for scarless healing
Members of the TAM receptor family modulate inflammatory responses and orchestrate efferocytosis , , . To reveal their roles in wound healing and fibrogenesis, we integrated multi-platform analyses across clinical specimens, animal models, and in vitro systems. Transcriptomic profiling of GSE113619 revealed similar changes in TYRO3, AXL, and MERTK in both keloid patients and normal wound healing, while PROS1 and GAS6 exhibited different trajectories—upregulated in controls but downregulated or unchanged in keloid patients (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). GSE178411 analysis demonstrated elevated AXL/MERTK levels across patients with acute, late, and chronic wounds, contrasted by a decline in TYRO3, PROS1, and GAS6 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). In murine models, only GAS6 displayed consistent downregulation during the repair phases, reaching statistical differences in type 2 diabetic wounds (p = 0.0349, Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). The inflammatory challenge of macrophages with IFN-γ/LPS recapitulated this suppression, showing GAS6 reduction (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). An integrative analysis of human keloid tissues, acute/late/chronic wounds, murine acute/diabetic wounds, and cytokine-stimulated macrophages confirmed the presence of conserved GAS6 downregulation (Fig.  ), establishing its role as an inflammation-sensitive regulator of repair processes. Notably, GAS6 is the only TAM ligand capable of activating all three receptors (TYRO3/AXL/MERTK) , . To further identify the specific cell types in which GAS6 is expressed and dynamically regulated during wound repair, we performed single-cell analysis of wound tissues from both human (GSE241132) and murine (GSE186527) wound healing processes. Consistent with prior findings, GAS6 was downregulated during the early inflammatory phase and was predominantly changed in fibroblasts and macrophages (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Concurrently, its key receptors AXL and MERTK were also downregulated, thus highlighting a critical deficiency in the GAS6-TAM signaling axis during this pivotal window (Supplementary Fig.  ). 
To validate the functional necessity of GAS6 in wound repair, we administered RU-301, a pharmacological inhibitor blocking GAS6-TAM receptor interaction, to murine acute wounds . RU-301 treatment significantly delayed wound healing (p = 0.0039 on POD10, p = 0.0228 on POD14, Fig.  ) and exacerbated scar width on POD30 (p = 0.0221, Fig.  ). Given the established link between impaired efferocytosis and defective healing, coupled with the pro-fibrotic role of augmented dermal inflammatory priming, we hypothesized that GAS6 deficiency drives pathological outcomes through a dual mechanism , . Consistent with this premise, RU-301 increased apoptotic cell accumulation at the wound edges (p = 0.0156, Fig.  ), indicative of compromised efferocytosis. Concurrently, the dermal inflammatory signal intensified, as evidenced by the increased migration of macrophages induced by inflammatory dermis in Transwell assay (p = 0.0003, Fig.  ). These findings mechanistically implicate GAS6 downregulation in dysregulating efferocytosis and amplifying inflammatory cascades during repair. Therefore, it was contemplated that exogenous GAS6 supplementation may recalibrate this imbalance and become an effective strategy for scarless healing.

## Synthesis and characterization of LNP-mRNA
As the principal ligand for TAM receptors (binding to three types of TAM receptors), GAS6 exerts multiple effects through receptor binding. Given the functional heterogeneity and tissue-specific distribution of TAM receptors, targeting upstream ligands offers clear therapeutic advantages. Despite the clinical translation of TAM inhibitors in oncology, no agonist has yet been developed , . Capitalizing on mRNA technology validated by COVID-19 vaccines – , we engineered LNP-encapsulated GAS6 mRNA (LNP-GAS6 mRNA) to amplify its regenerative potential (Fig.  ). Physicochemical characterization revealed the following: diameter of 64.29 ± 5.85 nm (LNP) and 74.83 ± 11.64 nm (LNP-GAS6 mRNA) (Fig.  ), polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.09 ± 0.05 (LNP) and 0.16 ± 0.05 (LNP-GAS6 mRNA) (Fig.  ), Zeta potential of + 13.60 ± 1.65 mV (LNP) and + 13.21 ± 1.28 mV (LNP-GAS6 mRNA) (Fig.  ), encapsulation efficiency of 97.82 ± 0.55% (Fig.  ) and encapsulated mRNA concentration of 0.12 ± 0.01 μg/μL (Fig.  ). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed its uniform dispersion and spherical morphology (Fig.  ). 
Cytotoxicity screening in fibroblasts and macrophages established a safe therapeutic window (≤5 μg/mL mRNA), with enhanced fibroblast proliferation at subtoxic doses (Supplementary Fig.  ). This safety profile was further validated by live/dead staining, which demonstrated excellent cell viability in both cell types at 5 μg/mL mRNA (Supplementary Fig.  ). Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) revealed the progressive accumulation of secreted GAS6 protein in supernatants after LNP-GAS6 mRNA delivery, reaching maximum levels at the 72-h time point (macrophages: 592.14 ± 36.58 pg/mL, fibroblasts: 488.10 ± 11.75 pg/mL, Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed intracellular overexpression patterns of the GAS6 protein in both macrophages and fibroblasts (Fig.  ). These data establish LNP-GAS6 mRNA as a robust platform for GAS6 delivery, which can be further used as an agonist of TAM receptors.

## LNP-GAS6 mRNA enhances macrophage efferocytosis and suppresses the inflammatory fibroblasts in vitro
Macrophages are central orchestrators of wound repair, acting as both primary mediators of efferocytosis and upstream regulators of iFB , , , , . To delineate the mechanistic role of GAS6 in macrophage function, we employed siRNA-mediated GAS6 knockdown (Supplementary Fig.  ). Downregulation of GAS6 not only skewed macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype (marked by elevated Il1b and Tnf alongside suppressed Il10 expression) (Supplementary Fig.  ; Fig.  ), but also augmented fibroblast inflammatory priming via paracrine signaling (Fig.  ). Furthermore, GAS6 loss severely compromised efferocytic capacity (Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that GAS6 deficiency disrupts macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk by altering paracrine signaling and compromising efferocytosis. 
To investigate the immunomodulatory potential of LNP-GAS6 mRNA, we first assessed its direct effects on macrophages. Treatment with LNP-GAS6 mRNA significantly skewed macrophages toward an M2-like state, as evidenced by concomitant upregulation of the M2 marker Arg1 (Fig.  ). Given that GAS6 protein secretion peaked at 72 h in macrophages (Fig.  ), we harvested the conditioned medium (CM) following a 72-h LNP-GAS6 mRNA treatment for subsequent experiments. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that the CM from LNP-GAS6 mRNA-treated macrophages enhanced efferocytosis, as evidenced by both an increased percentage of macrophages that phagocytosed apoptotic cells (63.08 ± 0.38% vs. 50.43 ± 1.13% in the Blank group, Fig.  ) and a greater number of engulfed apoptotic cells (3372.25 ± 129.20 vs. 2463.50 ± 54.97 in the Blank group, Fig.  ). Notably, LNP-GAS6 mRNA demonstrated a stronger enhancement of efferocytosis than LNP-IL-4 mRNA (Fig.  ), indicating that GAS6 signaling exerts immunomodulatory effects superior to those of a classical M2-polarizing stimulus. These findings align with prior research and provide additional evidence for the ability of LNP-GAS6 mRNA to enhance efferocytosis.
To dissect the macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk, we established an inflammatory axis model, where fibroblasts were treated with either: CM derived from LNP-GAS6 mRNA-treated macrophages, or direct application of LNP-GAS6 mRNA (Fig.  ). First, we observed that LNP-GAS6 mRNA treatment reduced the expression of macrophage-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines and stimulated the anti-inflammatory factors (Il1b↓, Tnf↓, Il10↑, Fig.  ). The transfer of this conditioned medium to fibroblasts suppressed the expression of inflammatory initiators Csf1 and Cxcl12 (Fig.  ), while direct LNP-GAS6 mRNA application to fibroblasts showed no significant effect (Fig.  ). These data suggested a macrophage-dependent mechanism by which GAS6 indirectly regulates fibroblast inflammatory responses, rather than exerting direct inhibitory effects. In summary, our in vitro studies demonstrated that LNP-GAS6 mRNA drives macrophage M2 polarization and enhances efferocytosis, subsequently suppressing iFBs through macrophage-derived paracrine signaling.

## Characterization and validation of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel
During the inflammatory phase, the inflammatory priming of fibroblasts serves as a critical determinant steering wound repair toward either regenerative healing or fibrotic outcomes . Consequently, therapeutic intervention with GAS6 during the peak inflammatory window (typically 24–72 h post-injury) may optimize the clinical efficacy. Considering the limitations of transient GAS6 intervention (e.g., recombinant proteins) and the potential fibrotic risks associated with prolonged overexpression (e.g., viral vectors) – , we propose a co-delivery platform combining an injectable thermosensitive hydrogel with LNP-mRNA technology to ensure that GAS6 protein expression is synchronized with the inflammatory phase and positioned in the reticular dermis , . To enable the spatiotemporally controlled delivery of LNP-GAS6 mRNA in vivo, we engineered a thermosensitive PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel with phase transition at 25 °C (liquid at 4 °C, gel at 37 °C, Fig.  ). Topical application of the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel to murine acute/diabetic wounds induced rapid gelation within 15 s (Fig.  ). Confocal imaging confirmed the homogeneous distribution of PKH26-labeled LNPs within the hydrogel matrix (Fig.  ). Structural and functional characterization confirmed the suitability of the LNP-loaded hydrogel as a sustained-release delivery system. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that LNP incorporation did not alter the hydrogel’s porous architecture or mechanical properties, which maintained robust adhesive and tensile characteristics (Supplementary Fig.  ). The hydrogel exhibited microenvironment-dependent degradation, with slow kinetics in PBS but accelerated breakdown in collagenase I, mimicking the proteolytic wound environment (Supplementary Fig.  ). Release kinetics aligned with material degradation, as 84.21% of encapsulated mRNA was released within 96 h under collagenase I conditions (Supplementary Fig.  ). The rheological characteristics of this hydrogel platform remained stable under pathophysiological conditions (pH 6.5, 200 µM H₂O₂), confirming its robustness for applications in inflamed wound microenvironments (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistent with in vitro behavior, the hydrogel underwent near-complete degradation in vivo within 72 h (Supplementary Fig.  ). 
To validate the transfection efficiency, spatial distribution, and expression duration of our delivery platform in vivo, we first administered LNP-Fluc mRNA@Gel to murine wounds. Whereas free LNPs (without hydrogel) exhibited poor retention and uneven distribution within the wound bed, our hydrogel-based system ensured prolonged and localized delivery, yielding a sustained fluorescent signal that peaked between 6 and 24 h and remained detectable for 72 h, thus achieving precise temporal alignment with the inflammatory phase (Fig.  ). Building upon these successes, we subsequently developed LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel and applied it to murine acute wound models. To establish an optimal dosing regimen, we conducted a dose-response assessment of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (20, 50, and 80 μg/mL) in murine acute wounds. Immunofluorescence confirmed dose-dependent GAS6 upregulation, with pronounced and spatially restricted expression in the deep dermis at both the wound center and edge (Fig.  ). The 50 μg/mL dose achieved a near-maximal GAS6 expression, comparable to 80 μg/mL (Fig.  ). Although wound width was similar across groups, the 50 μg/mL treatment significantly enhanced granulation tissue thickness relative to controls (Fig.  ), indicating promoted proliferative repair. Based on this robust efficacy and a favorable saturation profile, 50 μg/mL was selected for all subsequent studies. ELISA further revealed that GAS6 protein expression reached its peak on POD3 (463.75 ± 80.17 pg/mg tissue) and subsequently declined, a kinetic profile that aligns with the critical inflammatory phase of wound healing (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Previous studies have established a strong correlation between persistent deep dermal inflammation and pathological scar formation – , and the spatially restricted expression of GAS6 in the reticular dermis was expected to enhance its therapeutic potential. These results established the hydrogel platform as a superior means of delivery compared to direct LNP injection, achieving spatiotemporally controlled GAS6 delivery.

## LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel promoted scarless healing by enhancing efferocytosis and reducing dermal inflammatory signaling in murine acute wounds
A comprehensive biosafety evaluation of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel was conducted prior to efficacy studies. Local assessment at injection sites showed no adverse effects on hair regeneration, follicular density, dermal thickness, or collagen volume fraction (CVF) after 30 days (Supplementary Fig.  ). Systemically, no gross or microscopic alterations were detected in major organs, and plasma inflammatory cytokine levels remained comparable to controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data collectively demonstrate that LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel is well-tolerated both locally and systemically.
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of GAS6 in wound repair and scar modulation, we administered LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel to murine full-thickness excisional wounds (6 mm diameter) with five experimental groups set: Control, Gel, LNP@Gel, LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel, and GAS6 recombinant protein (Fig.  ) – . Serial photographic tracking revealed accelerated early closure (POD3) in both the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel and recombinant protein groups (Ⅰ: 88.13 ± 9.87%, Ⅱ: 83.08 ± 12.36%, Ⅲ: 80.87 ± 11.88%, Ⅳ: 57.64 ± 4.48%, Ⅴ: 59.85 ± 7.03%, Fig.  ). However, by POD7-14, only the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel group sustained superior healing kinetics (Fig.  ), likely attributable to the transient pharmacokinetics of recombinant protein. Histology analysis at POD30 demonstrated a 71.61% reduction in scar width in the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel group versus the control group (Ⅰ: 1.15 ± 0.20 mm, Ⅱ: 1.20 ± 0.41 mm, Ⅲ: 1.17 ± 0.21 mm, Ⅳ: 0.33 ± 0.02 mm, Ⅴ: 0.69 ± 0.19 mm, H&E, Fig.  ) and enhanced extracellular matrix remodeling with basket-weave pattern resembling native skin (Ratio of CVF, Ⅰ: 0.34 ± 0.19, Ⅱ: 0.26 ± 0.06, Ⅲ: 0.33 ± 0.03, Ⅳ: 0.74 ± 0.18, Ⅴ: 0.49 ± 0.18, Masson’s trichrome, Fig.  ). Notably, while the recombinant protein group exhibited a modest scar width reduction (39.54%) and an elevated ratio of CVF (0.49 ± 0.18), while these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.42 and p = 0.52 vs. control group, Fig.  ), underscoring the therapeutic superiority of spatiotemporally controlled GAS6 expression via mRNA delivery. 
To delineate GAS6-mediated scarless healing mechanisms in vivo, we quantified the apoptotic cell burden and dermal inflammatory signal on POD3. Both the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel and recombinant protein groups exhibited lower apoptotic cell density (Fig.  ) and attenuated inflammatory signal (migrating macrophages numbers reduced, Fig.  ), confirming enhanced efferocytosis and early inflammation control. However, only the mRNA formulation sustained therapeutic efficacy in the long term (reduction of scar width on POD30 and better extracellular matrix remodeling, Fig.  ), likely due to the recombinant protein’s short half-life. The above results demonstrated that GAS6 promotes scarless healing through enhanced efferocytosis and the suppression of dermal inflammatory signal. Furthermore, LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel showed superior therapeutic efficacy compared to the recombinant protein, as evidenced by improved wound healing outcomes.

## The role of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel in promoting scarless healing appears to involve oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)
To delineate the specific contributions of TAM receptors, we inhibited AXL and MERTK signaling in murine acute wounds using Bemcentinib and UNC2025, respectively (Fig.  ). Both inhibitors significantly impaired repair, delaying wound closure and exacerbating scar formation (Fig.  ). Although attenuated, the therapeutic effect of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel remained significant, indicating that it promoted repair even when individual TAM receptors were blocked (Fig.  ). This functional compensation among TAM receptors not only helps coordinate the repair process but also highlights the therapeutic advantage of using a broad-spectrum TAM ligand agonist (LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel) over receptor-specific targeting. 
To elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel, we performed unbiased transcriptomic profiling of POD3 wound tissues. This revealed 1,079 upregulated and 719 downregulated genes (FDR < 0.05, |Log₂FC| > 1; Supplementary Fig.  ). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment (Fig.  ) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig.  ) highlighted oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as the high-ranked pathway, with 45 upregulated OXPHOS-related genes (e.g., Ndufa3, Cox7c, Uqcrb) versus only 3 downregulated ones (Atp7A, Mt-Nd5, Mt-Atp8) (Fig.  ). While OXPHOS activation is known to potentiate M2 polarization and efferocytosis, its specific link to GAS6 signaling warrants further exploration – .
To functionally link GAS6 with OXPHOS activation, we performed siRNA-mediated GAS6 knockdown in macrophages. Downregulation of GAS6 significantly suppressed key OXPHOS genes, including Ndufa3, Uqcrb, and Cox5a (Fig.  ). Given that OXPHOS drives ATP production (also critically required for efferocytosis), we next measured cellular ATP levels. While GAS6 knockdown substantially reduced ATP synthesis, treatment with LNP-GAS6 mRNA significantly enhanced cellular ATP production (Fig.  ), confirming its role in promoting mitochondrial energy generation. Collectively, these results establish that GAS6 potentiates efferocytosis and promotes scarless healing, at least in part, by augmenting OXPHOS-derived ATP availability.
Furthermore, GSEA also revealed profound suppression of pro-inflammatory and fibrotic signaling networks in LNP-GAS6 mRNA-treated mice compared to control group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Key pro-inflammatory pathways inhibited in the GAS6 group included NF-κB , MAPK , and TNFR1/IGF1R , , alongside downregulation of fibrosis-associated axes (GSK3β , HER2/EGF , ) (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data mechanistically align with reduced macrophages infiltration and histological evidence of scarless healing with improved collagen deposition in LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel-treated wounds.

## LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel attenuated hypertrophic scarring in rabbit ear wound models
To break through the limitations of murine scar models lacking hypertrophic features, we established a rabbit ear hypertrophic scarring model where full-thickness wounds (10 mm diameter, perichondrium excised) developed raised scars post-healing – . Four wounds per ear were treated with PBS, Gel, LNP@Gel, or LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (Fig.  ). LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel-treated scars exhibited flatter morphology (Fig.  ) with a 54.09% lower scar elevation index (SEI) (Ⅰ: 3.67 ± 0.72, Ⅱ: 3.38 ± 0.93, Ⅲ: 3.16 ± 0.72, Ⅳ: 1.69 ± 0.50, Fig.  ). Sirius red staining revealed regular collagen architecture resembling normal skin in the GAS6-treated group and disorganized, fine collagen fibers in the control groups (collagen I/III ratio: Ⅰ: 0.40 ± 0.15, Ⅱ: 0.43 ± 0.11, Ⅲ: 0.39 ± 0.04, Ⅳ: 1.07 ± 0.09, Ⅴ: 1.15 ± 0.31, Fig.  ). These data demonstrated that early LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel intervention prevents hypertrophic scar formation. 

## LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel promoted the scarless healing of acute wounds in Bama minipigs
Motivated by the therapeutic efficacy of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel in murine and rabbit models, we advanced to preclinical validation in Bama minipigs, a species with cutaneous anatomy and repair mechanisms closely mirroring human skin , . Four full-thickness dorsal wounds (15 mm diameter) per animal received Gel, LNP@Gel, LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel, or human EGF gel (Fig.  ). The LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel-treated wounds exhibited faster wound healing (Fig.  ) and a 53.69% smaller scar area at POD35 (Ⅰ: 23.63 ± 5.48%, Ⅱ: 23.73 ± 6.25%, Ⅲ: 10.94 ± 12.69%, Ⅳ: 16.03 ± 7.60%, Fig.  ). There was a 55.10% reduction in scar width at POD35 in the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel group (Masson’s trichrome, Ⅰ: 3993.67 ± 720.72 mm, Ⅱ: 3595.32 ± 572.62 mm, Ⅲ: 1793.33 ± 847.44 mm, Ⅳ: 2538.33 ± 784.65 mm, Fig.  ), surpassing the h-EGF gel group (36.44% reduction, Fig.  ). Collagen distribution resembling unwounded skin could be seen in the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel group, contrasting with collagen alignment in the control group (Ⅰ: 0.16 ± 0.12, Ⅱ: 0.18 ± 0.08, Ⅲ: 0.52 ± 0.29, Ⅳ: 0.33 ± 0.21, Fig.  ). There was a 1.56-fold higher CVF ratio vs. the h-EGF gel group in the LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel group (Fig.  ). The scar length (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ) and the ratio of CVF (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ) on POD14 demonstrated progression patterns parallel to POD35 outcomes, maintaining biological consistency despite the lack of statistical significance. These results validated the therapeutic effect of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel in promoting scarless regeneration over clinical benchmarks (h-EGF gel). 

## Discussion
Our integrated investigation spanning clinical specimens, cross-species models, and in vitro cell models identified persistent GAS6 deficiency as a hallmark of pathological wound repair. By leveraging LNP-mRNA technology to spatiotemporally controlled restore GAS6 signaling, we successfully reprogrammed the macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk, a critical axis governing inflammatory cascades, to promote scarless regeneration in murine, rabbit, and porcine models. By decoding the role of GAS6 as a gatekeeper of immune-stromal homeostasis, we established mRNA-based TAM receptor agonism as a promising strategy for fibrosis prevention (Fig.  ). 
Clinical and experimental evidence demonstrated that persistent GAS6 deficiency post-injury correlated with impaired healing and excessive scarring, with pharmacological GAS6 inhibition recapitulating these pathological phenotypes. This aligns with emerging insights into TAM receptor dysfunction in chronic inflammation while expanding their biological relevance to cutaneous repair . Previous studies have established TAM receptors as regulators of macrophage efferocytosis and inflammation resolution , , , . For instance, AXL signaling drives macrophage toward M2 polarization , . Our findings revealed that the GAS6-TAM axis dynamically orchestrates macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk through paracrine signaling, a change from the canonical macrophage-autonomous TAM effects. Notably, while TAM receptors promoted fibrotic remodeling in chronic diseases, our multi-species evidence defined GAS6 supplementation in acute inflammatory phase as a regenerative catalyst , , . In GAS6−/− non-alcoholic steatohepatitis model, GAS6 ablation reduced hepatic fibrosis , yet transient GAS6 delivery during cutaneous inflammation accelerated repair while suppressing scarring in our study. This contradiction suggested a time-dependent duality: physiological GAS6-TAM signaling coordinated macrophage-mediated inflammation resolution to enable regenerative healing, whereas its chronic activation drove pathological fibrosis . Current anti-scarring strategies targeting TGF-β or fibroblast activation protein (FAP) face inherent limitations due to the dual repair/fibrosis roles of fibroblasts . By reprogramming macrophage immunoregulation, our approach bypasses fibroblast targeting to achieve source control. Compared to downstream TGF-β inhibition, this upstream strategy targeting the source of inflammatory signal offers superior therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by better scar reduction versus clinical h-EGF benchmarks.
Mechanistically, GAS6 demonstrated dual functionality by enhancing efferocytosis and attenuating inflammatory signals from fibroblasts, indicating its capacity to intercept inflammation at multiple regulatory nodes. This establishes a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation resolution: GAS6-mediated efferocytosis reduced apoptotic cell accumulation and inflammatory infiltration, while M2 macrophage-derived anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., Il10) suppressed fibroblast inflammatory priming, thereby disrupting the pathological inflammation–fibrosis feedback loop. This regulatory hierarchy positions macrophage reprogramming as a key regulator for scar prevention. Further supporting this, transcriptomic profiling revealed that GAS6 significantly upregulated OXPHOS—a pathway critical for M2 polarization and efferocytosis. Functional studies confirmed that GAS6 knockdown suppressed OXPHOS gene expression and reduced ATP production, whereas LNP-GAS6 mRNA enhanced ATP synthesis, directly linking GAS6 to mitochondrial energy generation essential for efficient clearance of apoptotic cells. Moreover, studies using TAM receptor inhibitors (Bemcentinib and UNC2025) showed that although individual blockade of AXL or MERTK impaired repair, the therapeutic effect of LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel remained significant, underscoring functional compensation among TAM receptors and the advantage of using a broad-spectrum TAM agonist over single-receptor targeting. Together, these findings delineate a GAS6-driven mechanism that augments OXPHOS-derived ATP availability to potentiate efferocytosis and resolve inflammation, offering a multi-node strategy that contrasts with conventional anti-fibrotic approaches targeting single cytokines.
Therapeutically, our LNP-mRNA hydrogel platform pioneers spatiotemporally controlled gene therapy in dermal regeneration. Unlike viral vectors or systemic delivery, this system achieves inflammatory phase-matched GAS6 expression (peak at 72 h) confined to the deep dermis, avoiding systemic toxicity from chronic TAM activation and minimizing genomic integration risks , – . Conventional approaches, whether growth factors or anti-inflammatory agents, face certain inherent limitations: systemic administration risks off-target effects, while single-dose protein delivery fails to maintain therapeutic concentrations in dynamic wound beds , . Our platform overcomes these barriers through spatiotemporally controlled expression, resolving the critical translational difficulty of protein instability. Cross-species efficacy validation (murine/rabbit/porcine models) confirmed the evolutionary conservation of GAS6-TAM signaling in mammalian wound healing. Especially, with the porcine wound healing models recapitulating the human wound healing process, our LNP-GAS6 mRNA hydrogel platform demonstrated effective scar reduction, better than clinical benchmarks (h-EGF gel). Furthermore, our platform offers distinct advantages over existing delivery systems. While most targeted platforms rely on ligand-mediated approaches , , we achieve spatial precision through hydrogel retention in the deep dermis. The system provides passive responsiveness via temperature-triggered gelation and protease-accelerated degradation. Most importantly, using clinically validated components enables faster translation than systems requiring novel ligands or complex chemistries.
While cross-species efficacy (murine/rabbit/porcine) supported the clinical potential of our platform, species-specific divergence in TAM receptor distribution necessitated further investigation of their differential contributions to human skin repair , , . This work establishes GAS6-mediated TAM activation as a central regulatory hub of macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk during tissue repair. By integrating immunomodulation with mRNA therapy, our findings proposed precision strategies against pathological scarring. The LNP-mRNA hydrogel platform enabled the co-delivery of TAM ligands (e.g., PROS1) for potential synergistic anti-fibrotic effects. Crucially, the successful application of localized mRNA delivery therapy can provide a blueprint for other fibrotic diseases.
In summary, our study establishes persistent GAS6 deficiency as a pivotal driver of pathological scarring and demonstrates that its spatiotemporal restoration via an LNP-mRNA hydrogel promotes scarless regeneration across murine, rabbit, and porcine models. By resolving macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk through GAS6-TAM axis activation, this approach achieves inflammation resolution and fibrotic suppression, outperforming clinical benchmarks. This work not only advances our understanding of immune-stromal regulation in wound healing but also provides a translational framework for mRNA-based modulation of fibrotic pathways, with potential implications beyond cutaneous repair.

## Methods

## Ethical statement
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval Nos. 2024-147 and 2025-112). This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations.

## Dataset acquisition and data processing
The datasets GSE113619, GSE178411, GSE53986, GSE241132, and GSE186527 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Notably, GSE283228 and GSE309507 represent RNA sequencing data generated by our institution and have been deposited in GEO. Data processing, including identifier conversion, log₂ transformation for normalization, and extraction of TAM receptor/ligand expression matrices, was processed by R software (v4.3.0). Heatmaps and bar plots were generated to visualize expression patterns by GraphPad Prism (v9.0).
Single-cell RNA sequencing data from human (GSE241132) and murine (GSE186527) skin wound healing studies were analyzed using Seurat (v5.3.0). After initial quality control to retain cells with more than 300 genes and less than 20% mitochondrial reads, potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.6). The data were normalized, and the top 3000 highly variable genes were selected using the vst method. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v1.2.3) integration with orig.ident as the batch covariate. Cells were clustered at a resolution of 0.5 using the first 15 Harmony dimensions. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed with UMAP (v0.2.10.0) based on the same Harmony components. Cell types were annotated through a marker-based identification process. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, only positive markers, logFC threshold >0.25, and adjusted p value < 0.05). Cluster identities were assigned based on canonical cell-type markers.
No ethical approval or patient consent was required as all data were sourced from public repositories.

## Isolation of primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) from mice
Bone marrow was harvested from euthanized 4–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Femurs and tibias were disinfected with 75% ethanol, dissected, and flushed with cold medium using a syringe. Cells were filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min), and treated with Red Blood Cell (RBC) lysis buffer (Invitrogen, #00-4333-57). After washing, cells were cultured in macrophage induction medium (20 ng/mL M-CSF; Meilun Bio, # MA0608). Medium was partially replaced on day 3 and fully refreshed on day 5. Differentiated macrophages were utilized by day 7.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) cytotoxicity assay
Macrophages (BMDM) and fibroblasts (L929 from ATCC, #CCL-1) were seeded in 96-well plates and pre-cultured until reaching approximately 60% confluency. LNP-GAS6 mRNA (0.5–25 μg/mL) was added to the respective wells. After 24 h of incubation, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, #C0037) was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation (dark). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and bar graphs were generated based on the absorbance values.

## Calcein AM/PI staining for cell viability
The viability of fibroblasts or macrophages was assessed using Calcein AM/PI staining (Beyotime, #C1371M). After treatment, the culture medium was aspirated, and cells were gently washed once with PBS. A freshly prepared Calcein AM/PI working solution was added (100 µL per well for a 96-well plate), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope: live cells exhibited green fluorescence, while dead cells showed red fluorescence. All steps were carried out protected from light.

## Histopathological analysis of wounds/scars
Wound/scar tissues from animals were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4 μm). For H&E staining, sections were dewaxed, stained with hematoxylin (5 min) and eosin (1 min), then mounted. For Masson’s trichrome, sections were incubated in Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (10 min), Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin (5 min), and aniline blue (5 min) to visualize collagen. Sirius red staining involved picrosirius red solution (1 h), differentiation in acidified water, and polarization microscopy for collagen typing. All slides were imaged using brightfield/polarized light microscopy.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (1 h, room temperature). Primary antibodies (Cleaved Caspase 3 Polyclonal antibody, Proteintech, #25128-1-AP, 1:200; Rat Monoclonal GAS6 antibody, Abcam, #ab86059, 1:200; EMR1 Polyclonal antibody, Proteintech, #27044-1-AP, 1:200) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L, Abcam, #ab150077, 1:200; Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L, Abcam, #ab150116, 1:200; Fluoro 488 Conjugated AffiniPure Rabbit Anti-Rat, BOSTER, #BA1129, 1:200) were applied (1 h, dark, room temperature). Nuclei were stained with Fluoromount-G™ (Yeasen, #36307ES). Fluorescence signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

## Transwell migration assay
BMDMs (2 × 10⁵ cells/insert) in complete medium (10% FBS) were seeded in Transwell (8.0 μm, Corning, #3422) upper chambers. Wound-margin skin samples (1–2 mm³) were pre-equilibrated in complete medium (700 μL/well) for 30 min. Co-culture systems (24 h) were established by positioning inserts above tissue-containing wells . Migrated cells on lower membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, swab-cleared of non-migratory cells, and stained with crystal violet. Quantification was performed via microscopic imaging.

## LNP-mRNA formulation and characterization
GAS6 mRNA was synthesized via in vitro transcription from a linearized plasmid (Yaohai Bio.) using BspQ I digestion, followed by phenol-chloroform extraction or magnetic bead purification. The IVT reaction was performed with T7 RNA polymerase, modified nucleotides (including m1ψ-UTP), and either co-transcriptional capping (using cap analog) or enzymatic capping post-IVT (using VCE and 2’-O-methyltransferase). The resulting mRNA was purified using RNA magnetic beads (N412) and quantified by UV and capillary electrophoresis. The optimized mRNA sequence is provided in the file Supplementary Data  . IL-4 mRNA and Fluc mRNA were obtained commercially (Yaohai Bio. and Absin Bioscience Inc.). LNP-mRNA were prepared by microfluidic mixing of lipid components (SM102/DSPE-PEG2000/DSPC/Cholesterol, 50:1.5:10:38.5 mol%) with mRNA in citrate buffer (pH 4.0) at a 1:3 volumetric ratio. The N/P ratio for the LNP-mRNA formulation was 6:1. Removal of ethanol and unencapsulated mRNA was performed by dialysis (PBS, 4 °C, 18 h; Slide-A-Lyzer™ 20 K MWCO, Thermo Scientific) or ultrafiltration (4 °C, 3000 × g, 20 min; UFC805096, MerckMillipore). Sterilized LNP-mRNA (0.22 μm filtration) was stored at 4 °C. The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The encapsulation efficiency was quantified using the RNA-specific fluorescent dye Quant-iTTM RiboGreenTM (Thermofisher Scientific). Encapsulation efficiency (%) = [(Total mRNA – Free mRNA)/Total mRNA] × 100. The morphology and structure were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Mouse GAS6 (#E-EL-M0282), IL-6 (#E-EL-M0044), and TNF-α (#E-EL-M3063) ELISA Kit was purchased from Elabscience. For fibroblasts and macrophages, supernatants from LNP-GAS6 mRNA-transfected cells were collected at 24/48/72 h. For skin tissue, the supernatant extracted after lysis was subjected to subsequent testing (POD1-14). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, standards/samples (100 μL/well) were incubated with capture antibody (90 min, 37 °C), detection antibody (1 h, 37 °C, 3× PBS-T washes), horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate (30 min, 37 °C, 5× PBS-T washes), and substrate solution (15 min, 37 °C) before termination with stop solution. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured and concentrations calculated via standard curve.

## Flow cytometry analysis of efferocytosis
BMDMs were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well. Apoptotic Jurkat T cells were induced by 15-min UV irradiation, followed by 4-h incubation. Apoptotic cells were labeled with Carboxyfluorescein diacetate, succinimidyl ester (CFDA-SE, Beyotime, #C0051) for 15 min, resuspended in fresh medium for 30 min, and washed twice with PBS. Jurkat T cells were resuspended in medium and added to BMDMs (adjusted to a 5:1 ratio of apoptotic cells: BMDMs). Co-cultures were incubated for 2 h to allow efferocytosis. Non-engulfed apoptotic cells were removed by two PBS washes. The remaining cells were harvested and stained with PerCP/Cyanine5.5 Anti-Mouse F4/80 Antibody (Elabscience, #E-AB-F0995J) on ice for 15 min (dark). After two PBS washes, cells were transferred to flow cytometry tubes. Fluorescence-positive cell percentage and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of F4/80⁺/CFDA-SE⁺ populations were quantified using a flow cytometer. Detailed gating strategies for efferocytosis analysis are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Total RNA extraction and reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted and analyzed using commercial kits, reverse-transcribed into cDNA (Hifair® V one-step RT-gDNA digestion SuperMix for qPCR, Yeasen, #11142ES60), and diluted 5–10× before storage at −80 °C. SYBR Green-based qPCR (Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix, Yeasen, #11201ES08) was performed with gene-specific primers (Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table  ) targeting Gapdh (internal control), Gas6, Nos2, Arg1, Il1b, Il10, Tnf, Csf1, Cxcl12, Ndufa3, Uqcrb, and Cox5a, with relative expression quantified via ΔΔCt method.

## Hydrogel preparation and characterization
PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel (MeloPEG, #904330) was thawed (4–6 h, room temperature), dissolved in PBS to 20% (w/v) with periodic stirring (≥3× daily, 5 min/session, 4 °C) until forming transparent colloid. Gelation capacity was tested by mixing hydrogel with LNP/LNP-mRNA (1:1 vol ratio), followed by 37 °C incubation or in vivo application (murine wound). PKH26-labeled LNPs were blended with hydrogel (1:1 vol ratio) for confocal microscopy-based spatial distribution analysis. The hydrogel microstructure and pore size were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and its physical properties were validated on ex vivo mouse skin. LNP-Fluc mRNA was mixed with hydrogel at a 1:1 ratio. After gelation at 37 °C, the LNP-Fluc mRNA@Gel was incubated with PBS, complete medium, esterase (MedChemExpress, #HY-P2831), collagenase I (‌BioFroxx, #1904GR001), pH 6.5, or 200 µM H₂O₂ in PBS as degradation media. Supernatants were collected at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h for mRNA concentration measurement to calculate the cumulative mRNA release, and the remaining hydrogel mass was weighed to calculate the residual hydrogel ratio. Furthermore, amplitude sweep tests were performed to probe the mechanical integrity of the hydrogel under physiological (PBS, pH 7.4, 37 °C) and pathophysiological (pH 6.5, 200 µM H₂O₂, 37 °C) conditions, recording the storage (G’) and loss (G”) moduli across a strain range of 0.1% to 100% at 1 Hz. For in vivo degradation analysis, LNP-Fluc mRNA@Gel was subcutaneously injected into the dorsal skin of mice. The implants were retrieved at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, and the residual hydrogel mass was measured to determine the in vivo retention profile.

## Application of LNP-Fluc mRNA-loaded hydrogel
Under anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection, male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) received 6-mm full-thickness circular excisions on the dorsal skin. The wounds were stabilized using silicone rings and covered with 3 M™ Tegaderm™ Transparent Film Dressing to inhibit contraction. LNP-Fluc mRNA-loaded hydrogel (50 μg/mL mRNA) was administered into the wound bed through the film using a syringe and allowed to solidify completely for approximately 15 s. Bioluminescence signals were monitored at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-application using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) to quantify intensity and spatial distribution.

## Biosafety evaluation
Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized and depilated on the dorsal skin. A total of 50 μg/mL LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel was administered via subcutaneous multi-point injection. Dorsal hair regrowth was monitored for 30 days. On day 30, skin, visceral organs, and blood samples were collected for histopathological and biochemical analyses, including Masson’s trichrome staining, H&E staining, and ELISA-based detection of inflammatory cytokines.

## Transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis
Transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were conducted with the support of Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Full-thickness dorsal wound-edge tissues from mice were harvested at day 3 post-operation, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized in TRIzol for total RNA extraction. RNA integrity was verified via Agilent5300 (RQN > 6.5). Stranded mRNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on NovaSeq X Plus. Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1).

## ATP concentration assay
BMDMs were lysed directly in culture plates by aspirating the medium and adding 200 μL of ice-cold ATP assay lysis buffer per well (of a 6-well plate). Cells were lysed completely by repeated pipetting. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. ATP detection working solution (Beyotime, #S0026) was prepared by 1:9 dilution of the reagent. A total of 100 μL working solution was aliquoted into a luminometer plate, incubated at room temperature for 3–5 min, and then mixed with 20 μL of sample or standard. Luminescence (RLU) was measured after a 2-s delay. ATP concentrations were calculated and normalized to protein content (BCA assay).

## Animal experiments
For all in vivo wound healing studies, male C57BL/6J mice, female New Zealand White rabbits, and male Bama minipigs were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity. According to previous study, sex has no significant impact on wound repair.
A total of 134 male C57BL/6J mice (C57BL/6JRj substrain; Janvier Labs, France) were used in this study. For all wound healing experiments, male mice aged 6–8 weeks were employed. Mice underwent dorsal full-thickness 6-mm circular excisions under intraperitoneal injection anesthesia, stabilized with silicone rings and 3M Tegaderm film (inhibiting wound contraction). To identify the optimal concentration of LNP-GAS6 mRNA, the treatments were as follows: ⅰ. LNP@Gel, ⅱ. LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (20 μg/ml), ⅲ. LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (50 μg/ml), and ⅳ. LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (80 μg/ml). For the LNP-GAS6 mRNA therapeutic evaluation, the groups included: ⅰ. saline (Ⅰ), ⅱ. Gel (Ⅱ), ⅲ. LNP@Gel (Ⅲ), ⅳ. GAS6 mRNA-LNP@Gel (50 μg/ml) (Ⅳ), ⅴ. recombinant GAS6 protein (10 μg/ml, MedChemExpress) (Ⅴ). Healing parameters (wound area) were tracked at days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-operation. Peri-wound tissues were harvested at day 3 and underwent immunofluorescence detection of apoptotic cells and Transwell migration assays, while day 30 scar tissues were analyzed via H&E/Masson’s trichrome staining for scar length and collagen architecture. In separate inhibitor studies, RU-301, Bemcentinib, and UNC2025 (MedChemExpress) were applied topically at 50 μg/mL.
A total of 6 female New Zealand White rabbits aged 6–8 months with a body weight of 2.5–3.5 kg were used for the hypertrophic scar model. Four 1-cm-diameter circular full-thickness wounds (ventral surface) per ear, including perichondrium, were surgically excised in rabbits under intravenous anesthesia. These wounds were treated with ⅰ. saline (Ⅰ), ⅱ. Gel (Ⅱ), ⅲ. LNP@Gel (Ⅲ), or ⅳ. LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (50 μg/ml) (Ⅳ) under 3M Tegaderm film. Healing dynamics and hypertrophic scar formation were monitored on days 0, 3, 7, 14, 28, and 60, with collagen organization analyzed via Masson’s trichrome and picrosirius red staining in harvested scar tissues.
A total of 6 male Bama minipigs, aged 12–18 months with a body weight of 20–30 kg, were used for the preclinical wound healing study. Dorsal 15-mm-diameter circular full-thickness excisions were created in Bama minipigs under intramuscular and inhalation anesthesia. These wounds were treated with ⅰ. Gel (Ⅰ), ⅱ. LNP@Gel (Ⅱ), ⅲ. LNP-GAS6 mRNA@Gel (50 μg/ml) (Ⅲ), or ⅳ. h-EGF gel (Ⅳ) under 3M Tegaderm film. The wound healing and scar formation progression were tracked on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35, with scar tissues harvested at days 14 and 35, and analyzed via Masson’s trichrome staining for collagen deposition.

## Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (v9.0) or R software (v4.3.0), with ≥3 independent biological replicates per group and representative images illustrated. Normality was verified via Shapiro-Wilk tests. Between-group comparisons employed Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way/two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis (≥3 groups). Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
