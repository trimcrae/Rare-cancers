## Background
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive inflammatory disorder with rising global morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence suggests that systemic metabolic alterations, particularly dyslipidemia, contribute to COPD pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms linking lipid dysregulation to pulmonary inflammation and tissue injury remain poorly defined.

## Methods
Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on plasma samples from healthy individuals and patients with stage III-IV COPD to identify disease associated metabolic alterations. A high-cholesterol diet (HCD) mouse model, with or without chronic cigarette smoke exposure, was used to examine the impact of systemic cholesterol elevation on lung structure and inflammation. THP-1 derived and bone marrow derived macrophages were employed to assess cholesterol-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS production, and downstream inflammatory signaling. Transcriptomic profiling was conducted to identify key molecular mediators.

## Results
Plasma metabolomics revealed significant dysregulation of lipid pathways in COPD, with elevated cholesterol levels inversely correlated with lung function. In vivo, HCD feeding induced pulmonary inflammation and further exacerbated cigarette smoke induced alveolar destruction. In macrophages, combined cholesterol loading and cigarette smoke extraction treatment disrupted mitochondrial integrity, reduced respiratory capacity, and increased ROS production. Excess ROS upregulated PPIA, which activated NF-κB signaling and enhanced IL-1β secretion. Silencing PPIA or inhibiting ROS attenuated NF-κB activation and cytokine release. Consistent with these findings, lungs from HCD-fed, cigarette smoke exposed mice exhibited increased PPIA expression and NF-κB phosphorylation, and PPIA levels were elevated in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from COPD patients.

## Conclusions
This study identifies a cholesterol-driven metabolic–inflammatory pathway in which mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS-dependent activation of the PPIA–NF-κB axis in macrophages contribute to persistent pulmonary inflammation in COPD. These findings establish a mechanistic link between systemic cholesterol dysregulation and COPD progression and highlight cholesterol metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis as potential therapeutic targets.

## Background
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive inflammatory disorder characterized by irreversible airflow limitation, airway remodeling, and alveolar destruction. It remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, yet current treatments primarily alleviate symptoms without halting disease progression [ , ]. Increasing evidence indicates that impaired tissue homeostasis driven by systemic metabolic and inflammatory alterations plays a crucial role in COPD pathogenesis.
Among these systemic abnormalities, dyslipidemia has emerged as a prominent metabolic disturbance in COPD [ ]. Patients with COPD frequently exhibit abnormal lipid profiles, including altered levels of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein [ – ]. The lipid abnormalities are evident not only in circulation but also within lung tissues [ , ], suggesting a state of global lipid metabolic reprogramming. Mechanistically, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia of COPD can impair hepatic lipid processing and lipoprotein turnover, while cigarette smoke (CS) directly perturbs lipid transport and oxidation [ , ]. In turn, dyslipidemia amplifies systemic inflammation and oxidative injury, thereby exacerbating pulmonary damage and promoting disease progression [ , ]. Thus, dysregulated lipid metabolism represents a critical pathogenic link between systemic metabolic imbalance and chronic inflammation in COPD.
Recent metabolomic studies further reinforce this association by revealing profound metabolic reprogramming in COPD. Altered small-molecule signatures, particularly involving amino acids and lipids, have been shown to correlate with disease severity [ , , ]. A large-scale cohort study demonstrated specific amino acid and lipid metabolites associated with COPD independently of smoking status [ ]. Importantly, longitudinal metabolomic analyses have demonstrated that metabolic perturbations evolve with disease progression, with lipid metabolism displaying the most pronounced dysregulation pathogenesis [ , ]. These findings suggest that lipid metabolic disturbances may contribute not only to COPD progression but may also play a role in its early pathogenesis.
Cholesterol, a central lipid molecule in cell membranes and signaling, has been increasingly implicated in chronic diseases, including cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, and inflammatory disorders. Elevated plasma cholesterol levels have been reported to correlate positively with COPD progression and acute exacerbations while inversely affecting patient prognosis [ – ]. Moreover, cholesterol exposure has been shown to trigger inflammatory activation in pulmonary epithelial cells [ , ]. Macrophages, which is markedly increased in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from COPD patients, exhibit heterogeneous phenotypes shaped by their microenvironment. Disruption of cholesterol homeostasis in macrophages can lead to metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation, thereby contributing to sustained airway inflammation, tissue remodeling, and disease progression [ , ]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which cholesterol alters macrophage function in COPD remain poorly understood.
In this study, we combined metabolomics of human plasma with in vivo and in vitro experimental models to elucidate the role of cholesterol dysregulation in COPD pathogenesis. We demonstrated that plasma cholesterol levels were elevated in COPD patients and correlate positively with disease stage. In mice, a high-cholesterol diet (HCD) significantly aggravated CS-induced inflammation and lung injury. Mechanistically, cholesterol enrichment combined with CS exposure in macrophages impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and upregulated peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA), leading to NF-κB signaling activation and increased proinflammatory cytokine release. Collectively, these findings uncover a mechanistic axis linking systemic cholesterol imbalance to mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory response in COPD, suggesting that restoring cholesterol and mitochondrial homeostasis may offer a novel therapeutic strategy.

## Methods

## Participant recruitment
This study was registered in Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR-OO-14004258) at https://www.chictr.org.cn/, and the protocol was approved by Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. 2013–33). Participants were aged between 40 and 80 from the rural areas of Shaoguan city in Guangdong Province in China during 2017–2020. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) COPD patients with the ratio of post-bronchodilator-forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)%predicted to forced vital capacity (FVC)%predicted (FEV1/FVC%) < 70% were in a stable stage according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2022; (b) Participants must cooperate to questionnaire survey, respiratory function assessment, liver and kidney function test and blood collection; (c) All subjects must sign informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included: (a) Individuals suffered from metabolic disease, e.g. hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus and coronary heart disease; (b) Patients had pulmonary diseases other than COPD, including severe α1-antitrypsin deficiency, asthma, fibrosis, cancer, and tuberculosis; (c) Subjects suffered from systemic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and inflammatory bowel disease; (d) Patients with COPD underwent acute exacerbation within 4 weeks, additionally, healthy controls got acute respiratory symptoms, lung surgery history and medication or hormone therapy within 4 weeks. Based on the diagnosis criteria of COPD, the subjects were considered as GOLD Ⅰ with FEV1% predicted (> 80%), GOLD Ⅱ with FEV1% predicted (50–80%), GOLD Ⅲ with FEV1% predicted (30–50%) and GOLD Ⅳ with FEV1% predicted (< 30%) after bronchodilator inhalation. Pulmonary function was assessed using the Jaeger Spirometer (MasterScreen, Leibnizstrasse Hoechberg, Germany), including FEV1 and FVC.

## Sample collection
The discovery cohort comprised 30 participants: 10 healthy controls, 10 with stage Ⅲ COPD, and 10 with stage Ⅳ COPD. The validation cohort recruited 50 participants: 10 healthy controls and 10 subjects at each of the four COPD severity stages (Ⅰ-Ⅳ). BALF samples were obtained form 27 healthy controls and 62 COPD patients. Following a 12-h fasting period, blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein, and BALF was obtained via bronchoscopy. Both blood and BALF samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 800 g and 4◦C. After which the supernatant plasma and BALF was immediately aliquoted and stored at −80◦C.

## Sample preparation for metabolite extraction
A total of 100 µL of plasma samples was adequately mixed with 10 µL L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.3 mg/mL) and 300 µL methanol/acetonitrile (2:1, v/v), followed by ultrasound in an ice bath for 10 min and quiescence at −20◦C for 30 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 4◦C and 13,000 rpm for 10 min. 300 µL of the supernatants were taken, blown dry with nitrogen gas, redissolved with 300 µL methanol solution (1:4, v/v), and then centrifuged at 4℃ and 13,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 150 µL of the supernatants was filtered with 0.22 µm organic-phase filter into sample vials and analyzed via an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS, ACQUITY UPLC Ⅰ-Class, Waters, MA, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, MA, USA). Additionally, the quality control samples were made according to the above protocols by mixing 10 µL of each plasma sample.

## Untargeted metabolomics profiling and bioinformatics analysis
Ion information was acquired by electrospray ion sources in both positive and negative ion modes, respectively. The mass spectrum conditions were the same in both two ion modes and presented as follows: capillary voltages, 2.5 kV; collision voltage, 4 eV; source temperature, 115◦C and desolvation gas flow, 900 L/h. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: mobile phase A, 0.1% formic acid solution; mobile phase B, acetonitrile/methanol (2:3, v/v); flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; column temperature, 45◦C and injection volume, 1 µL. The raw data was pre-treated using Progenesis QI v2.3 software, including baseline filtering, retention time correction, peak alignment, missing value filling and normalization [ ]. Each compound was precisely identified by matching its mass spectrometry and chromatographic information in public metabolomic databases and local standard library. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discrimination Analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics, Sweden). The compounds which met P value < 0.05, fold change > 1.2 and variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1 obtained in the OPLS-DA model were considered as differential metabolites between COPD and healthy groups. Bioinformatics analysis of all differential metabolites, including pathway analysis and enrichment analysis, were carried out using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 software (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) [ ].

## Targeted metabolomic profiling of bile acid metabolism
Thirty milligrams of mouse lung tissues was added into methanol solution (1:1, v/v) containing isotope internal standard and fully ground by frozen tissue grinder (Wonbio-E, Shanghai, China). The mixture was stood at −20◦C for 30 min and centrifuged at 4◦C and 13,000 rpm for 10 min. 250 µL of the supernatant was blown dry with nitrogen gas, redissolved with 250 µL methanol solution (1:1, v/v), and filtered through 0.22 µm organic-phase filter. All samples were analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS (ACQUITY UPLC Ⅰ-Class, Waters, MA, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters). Mass spectrum conditions were as follows: collision voltage, 4.5 kV; source temperature, 450◦C. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: mobile phase A, 0.1% formic acid solution; mobile phase B, acetonitrile/methanol/isopropanol (1:1:1, v/v/v); flow rate, 0.45 mL/min; column temperature, 45◦C and injection volume, 5 µL. All standards used in this study were listed in Additional file : Table S1.

## Animal experiments
The animal study was approved by Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory (No. GZLAB-AUCP-2022–09-A02). Male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Guangdong GemPharmatech Co. Ltd. (Guangdong, China), and kept under specific-pathogen free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle at 24 ± 2◦C and 50–70% humidity in the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangzhou Medical University. COPD model was constructed by CS exposure. Briefly, mice were placed in a homemade box (60 cm × 57 cm × 100 cm) and exposed to CS 4 times daily (am 9:00–11:00, pm 3:00–5:00), 12 cigarettes (containing 1.3 mg nicotine and 13 mg tar per cigarette, Hongmei®, Yunnan, China) at one time for 1 h and 6 days a week. During the entire experiments, mice were given free access to normal water and diet.
CS exposure for the 4- and 6-month periods: twenty 6-week-old mice were randomly averagely divided into the CON4, CON6, CS4 and CS6 groups (5 mice/group). The animals in the CON4 and CS4 groups were exposed to normal air and CS for 4 months, respectively. Similarly, the rodents in the CON6 and CS6 groups suffered from normal air and CS for 6 months, respectively. At the end of each experiment, mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, and fresh liver tissue was immediately collected for subsequent analysis.
Mice with high-cholesterol diet: (a) Eight 6-week-old mice were randomly evenly divided into two groups. The animals in the Air-Normal and Air-Chol groups were fed with normal and high-cholesterol diets for 6 months, respectively. (b) Fourteen mice aged 6 weeks were randomly averagely divided into two groups. The animals in the CS-Normal and CS-Chol groups were administrated with normal and high-cholesterol diets for 6 months, respectively, meanwhile, suffered from the CS exposure. At the end of the experiments, all animals were anesthetized with 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (330 mg/kg), and their lung function, including FEV, FVC, functional residual capacity (FRC) and chord compliance (Cchord) was immediately assessed using DSI Buxco-PFT system (Buxco, USA). Following euthanasia via CO₂ asphyxiation and cervical dislocation, lung tissues of each mouse were filled with 1.6 mL sterile phosphate buffer saline solution and carefully aspirated 3 times; The BALF mixture was centrifuged at 4◦C for 3 min, then the supernatant was collected for biochemical analysis, and synchronously the macrophage percent in BALF was measured. Additionally, lung tissues of all mice were taken for the subsequent analysis.

## BALF macrophages counted by flow cytometry
BALF samples of each mouse were centrifuged at 600 × g and 4◦C for 5 min, and the precipitates were suspended with 1 mL PBS buffer. Subsequently, BALF macrophages were labeled with antibodies CD45 (BioLegend, CA, USA) and F4/80 (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA). Its number and proportion in the BALF were detected by flow cytometry (SONY ID7000, Japan).

## Biochemical analysis
Cholesterol concentrations of plasma from all participants and lung tissues from mice were determined using biochemical kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Levels of PPIA and inflammatory cytokines-including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-10 and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)-were detected by Enzyme-Linked Immunesorbent Assay Kit (MEIMIAN®, Jiangsu, China). All operations referred to the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density values were determined at 450 nm via a microplate reader (Thermofisher, MA, USA).

## Histopathological examination
Lung tissues suffered from fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde buffer, embedding, dehydration, section and then staining with hematoxylin and eosin. Subsequently, the lung histopathology of each mouse was analyzed by a digital image analysis system (Leica DM500B Microscope, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Oil red O staining
Lipid droplet of hepatic and lung tissues was analyzed by Oil Red O Stain Kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). All operations referred to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the frozen sections were stained by solution Ⅰ for 10 min and solution Ⅱ for 5 min, then washed for 30 s. Subsequently, the sections were sealed and analyzed using a digital image system (Leica DM500B Microscope, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Quantitative RT-PCR
The regents applied in this section were provided from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). Total RNA was extracted from hepatic and lung tissues and cells using an RNA-easyTM Isolation Reagent, and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript® Ⅲ SuperMix for qPCR. The qPCR was performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, and detected through CFX96™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression of each gene was calculated via 2−∆∆cycle threshold (Ct) method. Primers were designed using PrimerBank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/) and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnol. Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). All primer sequences were listed in Additional file : Table S2.

## Transcriptomics analysis
Total RNA of lung tissues was extracted and quantified by Nanodrop2000. The mRNA was isolated from the total RNA via Magnetic beads with Oligo dt and randomly broken into 300-bp fragments using fragmentation buffer. These small mRNA fragments were reversely transcribed into cDNA, followed by fragment purification and sorting, PCR amplification and library construction. Immediately, the DNA products were sequenced by NovaSeq X Plus platform. The raw data underwent the quality control. The clean reads were separately aligned to reference genome with orientation mode using HISAT2 software [ ]. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by StringTie in a reference-based approach [ ]. Expression levels of each transcript were calculated according to the transcripts per million reads method, and RSEM software was used to quantify gene abundances [ ]. Subsequently, bioinformatics analysis was performed, including PCA analysis, gene expression difference statistics, and protein interaction network analysis. Herein, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 [ ]. The genes with |log2Fold Change|≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered to be significantly different expressed. The protein–protein interaction was analyzed through the STRING database (http://string-db.org/), and visualized by the Cytoscape version 3.3.0 [ , ].

## Western blotting
Total protein of lung tissues and cells was extracted by RIPA Lysis buffer (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China) with phosphatase inhibitors (ThermoFisher, USA). After centrifugation at 4◦C and 300 rpm for 5 min, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 4◦C and 12,000 rpm for 30 min. The protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Rapid Protein Quantitative Kit (ThermoFisher, USA) and diluted to 20 µg/10 µL. All proteins were denatured at 98◦C for 10 min, and suffered from polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis under constant voltage of 120 V until bromophenol blue arrived at the PAGE gel bottom. Immediately, the proteins in the gels were transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride film under constant current of 300 mA for 120 min. The film was blocked by Protein-Free Rapid Blocking Solution (Servicebio®, Wuhan, China) and then incubated with primary antibodies including 3β-hydroxy-Δ5-C₂₇-steroid dehydrogenase (HSD3B7, Affinity Biosciences, OH, USA), peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), nuclear factor-κB p65 (P65, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), phosphorylated-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), overnight at 4◦C. Subsequently, the film was rapidly washed by Tris-Buffered Saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 3 times for 5 min once and then incubated with secondary antibody for 90 min. After the superfluous secondary antibody were washed away, the proteins in the film were developed by ECL chemiluminescence solution through the GelView 6000ProⅡ imaging system (iBIOGENE, Shanghai, China). Protein expression was quantified by Image J (NIH, USA).

## Single-cell RNA sequencing data
The single-cell RNA sequencing data of human lung tissue samples were obtained from healthy donors across a broad age range, available through the public LungMAP database (https://app.lungmap.net/) [ ].

## Cigarette smoke extract and cholesterol preparation
The smoke ingredients of two cigarettes were extracted with 10 mL RPMI 1640 (Gibco, NY, USA) through a modified tee device, which was considered as 100% cigarette smoke extract (CSE). Cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich, Lousi, USA) was dissolved in ethyl alcohol. The solution was subsequently added to methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) to form a 1 mM concentration of cholesterol.

## Cell experiments
The THP-1 monocytes used in this study were from our laboratory and grew in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell, Uruguay),100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA) at 37◦C in a CO2 incubator. The THP-1 monocyte cells were induced into macrophages by 100 nM phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (Sigma, Germany). Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was transfected by Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher, MA, USA). The sequencing was listed as follows: siRNA-PPIA-sense: GCUCGCAGUAUCCUAGAAUTT; siRNA-PPIA-nonsense: AUUCUAGGAUACUGCGAGCTT. The knockdown efficiency was assessed using RT-qPCR and Western blotting 48 h post-transfection. Human HepG2 cell line (HB-8065) was bought from ATCC and cultured in DMEM (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% FBS (ExCell, Uruguay), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA) at 37◦C in a CO2 incubator.

## Reactive oxygen species detection
The ROS was detected using flow cytometry (SONY ID7000, Japan). The operations were performed in accordance with the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The THP-1 derived-macrophages were cultured with the medium containing 10 µM DCFH-DA probe at 37◦C for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed 3 times for removing superfluous DCFH-DA by PBS and then incubated with the medium containing 0.75% CSE, 100 µM cholesterol or their combination for 2 h. The excitation and emission wavelengths were set 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively. The data was analyzed using the Flow Jo software (BD Life Sciences, USA) [ ].

## Mitochondrial morphology analysis
The mitochondrial morphology of THP-1 derived macrophages was observed using transmission electron microscopy (Talos L120C G2, ThermoFisher, MA, USA). Briefly, all cells that suffering from 0.75% CSE, 100 µM cholesterol or their combination for 2 h were harvested and fixed in ice-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde, then undergoing dehydration with ethanol containing 3% uranyl acetate, embedding with the epoxy resin/propylene oxide mix for 24 h, section at 70 nm thickness and staining with lead citrate. The mitochondrial morphology was evaluated at 120 kV.

## Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate test
The mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of cells was evaluated using the Seahorse Extracellular Flux (XFe-96) analyzer (Agilent, MA, USA). In brief, 5 × 104 THP-1 derived macrophages were stimulated with CSE 0.75%, cholesterol 100 µM or their combination for 2 h. The reagents in XF Cell Mio stress assay kit (Agilent, MA, USA), including 1 µM oligomycin, 2 µM 2-[[4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenyl]hydrazinylidene]propanedinitrile (FCCP) and 1 µM of the mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (1:1, v/v), were systematically added into each well in accordance with its instructions. The real-time mitochondrial OCR was measured on the analyzer. Bioenergetic parameters of OCR were analyzed using WAVE software (Agilent, MA, USA) [ ].

## Isolation and culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM)
Bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs and tibia of healthy 7-month-old C57BL/6 J wild-type mice. These cells were incubated and differentiated to macrophages for 7 days in M-CSF (25 ng/mL, Novoprotein Scientific Inc., Shanghai, China)-conditioned RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (ExCell, Uruguay), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Ginco, NY, USA). The ROS in the mature BMDMs was triggered and suppressed by the Rosup (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and Mito-TEMPO (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA), respectively. To knock down PPIA expression in BMDMs, a siRNA (its sense: GGACAGCUCUAAUUUCUUU; nonsense: AAAGAAAUUAGAGCUGUCC) was transfected by Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher, MA, USA). The knockdown efficiency was determined by RT-qPCR and western blotting after 48-h post-transfection.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons in all figures except Fig.  , for which one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD test was applied. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

## Results

## Plasma cholesterol levels are elevated in subjects with COPD
Patients with COPD are known to have an increased risk of cardiovascular comorbidities, suggesting possible systemic metabolic alterations [ , ]. To characterize these metabolic changes, we performed plasma metabolomic profiling in healthy individuals and patients with stage III–IV COPD. The demographic characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table  and Additional file : Table S3. Untargeted metabolomic profiling demonstrated a clear separation between the two groups in the discovery phase, suggesting a disease-associated alteration in metabolome (Fig.  A). A supervised OPLS-DA model further confirmed this separation (Fig.  B). Combined multivariate and univariate analyses identified 387 differential peaks between the two groups, including 199 significantly upregulated and 188 downregulated metabolites in COPD plasma (Fig.  C). 
Using obtained chromatographic and mass spectrometric information matching with public databases and internal standards, 59 metabolites were successfully annotated, most of which were lipids or lipid-like molecules (Additional file : Table S4). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant perturbations in 14 metabolic pathways, notably those related to glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Fig.  D). Further metabolite set enrichment highlighted nine significantly enriched pathways, with bile acid biosynthesis and betaine metabolism being most prominently dysregulated (Fig.  E). Given the central role of cholesterol in bile acid metabolism, we next examined its relationship with lung function. Correlation analysis demonstrated that plasma cholesterol levels were strongly and inversely associated with FEV₁/FVC ratio and FEV₁ % predicted (Cor > 0.5, P < 0.01; Fig.  F-G). Direct quantification confirmed a progressive elevation of plasma cholesterol levels with advancing disease severity, this finding further validated in both the primary study cohort and an independent COPD cohort (Fig.  H-I). Collectively, these results indicate that systemic cholesterol dysregulation is a prominent metabolic alteration in COPD and may contribute to disease progression through its association with impaired pulmonary function.

## High-cholesterol diet aggravates alveolar destruction and lung inflammation in CS-exposed mice
To further investigate the contribution of dietary cholesterol to lung pathology, we established a 6-month HCD mouse model with or without concurrent CS exposure (Fig.  A). Compared with mice fed a normal diet, HCD feeding did not significantly affect body weight in either air-exposed or CS-exposed groups; however, CS exposure itself led to a consistent reduction in body weight regardless of dietary condition (Fig.  B). As expected, tissue cholesterol levels were substantially increased in HCD-fed mice relative to normal-diet controls, and CS exposure further enhanced cholesterol accumulation in the lung (Fig.  C). Lipid droplet deposition was visualized by Oil Red O staining. CS exposure markedly increased lipid accumulation, with the greatest levels observed in mice receiving both CS and HCD (Fig.  D). These lipid droplets were predominantly localized within macrophages, whose abundance in lung tissue also increased under CS and HCD conditions (Fig.  D). Pulmonary function testing revealed significant elevations in Cchord and FVC in mice exposed to both CS and HCD (CS-Chol group) compared with CS-exposed mice on a normal diet (CS-Normal group) (P < 0.05, Fig.  E), indicative of impaired lung function. Histopathological analysis further demonstrated extensive alveolar destruction and airway epithelial thickening in CS-Chol mice, exceeding the damage observed in CS-Normal mice (P < 0.001, Fig.  F–G). Inflammatory profiling showed a higher percentage of macrophages in BALF and elevated IL-1β levels in lung tissues of the CS-Chol group (Fig.  J–K), whereas TNF-α levels remained unchanged (Fig.  L). Conversely, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was significantly reduced in CS-Chol mice (P < 0.05, Fig.  M). Together, these findings demonstrate that dietary cholesterol exacerbates CS-induced lung injury by promoting structural destruction, enhancing inflammatory infiltration, and altering cytokine homeostasis. These results support the conclusion that cholesterol accumulation accelerates COPD progression. 

## CS exposure disrupts hepatic lipid metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis
The liver serves as the central organ for cholesterol synthesis, catabolism, and overall lipid homeostasis. In mice exposed to CS, lipid droplets accumulated around hepatocyte nuclei, in stark contrast to mice maintained in normal air conditions (Fig.  A). Because the majority of cholesterol is converted into primary bile acids in hepatocytes, we performed targeted metabolomics on liver tissues collected after 4 or 6 months of exposure. This analysis revealed substantial hepatic cholesterol accumulation, with levels increasing progressively over the exposure period (P < 0.05, Fig.  B-C, and Additional file : Table S5). Concurrently, hepatic levels of multiple bile acid species, including 24(S)-hydroxycholesterol, 23-norcholic acid, ursodeoxycholic acid, and tauroursodeoxycholic acid, were markedly reduced following CS exposure (P < 0.05, Additional file : Table S5). To determine the mechanism underlying impaired bile acid synthesis, we examined expression of HSD3B7, a key rate-limiting enzyme responsible for converting cholesterol into primary bile acids. In hepatocytes treated with CSE, HSD3B7 expression was significantly downregulated in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  D-E). Consistent with these in vitro findings, liver tissues from CS-exposed mice also exhibited decreased HSD3B7 expression (Fig.  F-G). These results indicate that chronic CS exposure impairs hepatic cholesterol catabolism by suppressing HSD3B7, leading to cholesterol accumulation in the liver. This hepatic metabolic disturbance may contribute to the systemic and pulmonary cholesterol dysregulation observed in COPD pathogenesis. 

## Cholesterol exacerbates CS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming in macrophages
Oil Red O staining revealed marked lipid accumulation in alveolar macrophages from CS-exposed mice (Fig.  A). Both the number and size of macrophages were increased in the alveolar spaces of CS with HCD (CS-Chol) mice compared with those of CS-Normal mice (Figs.  D, J, and A), indicating cholesterol-driven macrophage hypertrophy and lipid loading. 
To further assess macrophage ultrastructure, we examined THP-1 derived macrophages under transmission electron microscopy after treatment with CSE, cholesterol, or their combination. Mitochondria in treated cells exhibited pronounced swelling, irregular distribution, and disrupted cristae, particularly in the CSE + cholesterol (CSE + Chol) group, suggesting severe mitochondrial injury (Fig.  B). Consistent with these morphological abnormalities, OCR analysis demonstrated that both CSE and cholesterol exposure markedly reduced basal and maximal respiration, with the most profound suppression observed under combined treatment (Fig.  C-E). Similarly, spare respiratory capacity and non-mitochondrial respiration were significantly reduced in cells exposed to cholesterol or the combined CSE + cholesterol treatment compared with controls (Fig.  F-G). Proton leak was also diminished in both the cholesterol and CSE + cholesterol groups (Fig.  H), whereas a significant reduction in ATP production was observed only in the CSE + cholesterol group (F g.  I). Collectively, these findings indicate that CS exposure disrupts lipid homeostasis in alveolar macrophages, and additional cholesterol loading exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction. The combined effects of CSE and cholesterol lead to impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics and metabolic reprogramming, contributing to macrophage dysfunction in COPD.

## High-cholesterol diet elevates PPIA expression in the lungs of CS-exposed mice
To explore how a HCD influences COPD progression, we performed RNA-sequencing on lung tissues from CS-Normal and CS-Chol mice. PCA plot revealed a clear separation between the two groups, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles associated with dietary cholesterol (Fig.  A). A total of 146 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between CS-Normal and CS-Chol mice, including 84 upregulated and 62 downregulated transcripts (Fig.  B). Protein–protein interaction network analysis of these DEGs highlighted peptidylprolyl isomerase A (Ppia) as a central node with extensive connections to multiple cholesterol-responsive genes, suggesting a potential regulatory role in cholesterol-augmented COPD pathology (Fig.  C). The transcript levels of Ppia were significantly elevated in the lungs of CS-Chol mice compared with CS-Normal controls (Fig.  D), a finding further validated by qPCR (Fig.  E). Western blot analysis also confirmed a marked increase in PPIA protein expression in the lungs of CS-Chol mice (Fig.  F–G). These results demonstrate that dietary cholesterol amplifies PPIA expression at both the transcriptional and protein levels in CS-exposed lungs. 

## Cholesterol-induced ROS production upregulates PPIA and activates NF-κB signaling in macrophages
Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from healthy human lungs revealed that PPIA is predominantly expressed in alveolar macrophages and myofibroblasts (Fig.  A). Given our earlier observations that lipid accumulation mainly occurred in alveolar macrophages of CS-Chol mice (Figs.  D and A), we next examined PPIA expression in THP-1 derived macrophages. PPIA expression was markedly elevated following exposure to CSE or cholesterol, with increasing trend under combined treatment (CSE + Chol) (Fig.  B-C). Since CSE + Chol is known to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and increase proton leak, we evaluated ROS generation in THP-1 derived macrophages. As expected, CSE + Chol treatment resulted in a pronounced increase in intracellular ROS levels compared with untreated controls (P < 0.001, Fig.  D-E). Furthermore, treatment with the ROS agonist Rosup enhanced PPIA expression, whereas inhibition of ROS effectively suppressed CSE + Chol induced PPIA upregulation in BMDMs (P < 0.05, Fig.  F-I). These findings suggest that cholesterol-induced ROS production promotes PPIA expression in macrophages. Given that PPIA has been implicated in activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [ ], we next assessed this axis under cholesterol and CSE stimulation. In vitro, CSE + Chol treatment robustly increased p65 phosphorylation in BMDMs, whereas Ppia knockdown significantly attenuated p65 activation (P < 0.001, Fig.  J–K). Correspondingly, silencing PPIA markedly reduced IL-1β secretion in both THP-1 derived macrophages and BMDMs exposed to CSE + Chol (P < 0.05, Fig.  L-M). Consistent with the in vitro findings, phosphorylated p65 levels were also elevated in lung tissues from CS-Chol mice compared with CS-Normal controls (Fig.  N-O). Finally, analysis of BALF from cohorts revealed elevated PPIA concentrations in COPD patients relative to healthy controls, further supporting the clinical relevance of these observations (P < 0.01, Fig.  P). 

## Discussion
In this study, we identify systemic cholesterol dysregulation as a major metabolic signature of COPD and uncover a mechanistic axis linking cholesterol accumulation to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and macrophage-driven inflammation. In vivo, HCD aggravated CS induced airway remodeling, alveolar destruction, and inflammatory cytokine production. Mechanistically, cholesterol enrichment impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, increased ROS generation, and upregulated PPIA, which activated NF-κB signaling and enhanced IL-1β release. Together, these findings define a cholesterol–ROS–PPIA–NF-κB axis that sustains pulmonary inflammation and tissue injury in COPD.
Our plasma metabolomic profiling demonstrated extensive lipid metabolic reprogramming in COPD, particularly involving glycerophospholipid, linoleic acid, and bile acid pathways. These alterations align with previous clinical metabolomic studies linking lipid dysregulation to airflow limitation and disease severity [ , , ]. Glycerophospholipids are essential structural components of cell membranes that regulate membrane integrity, signal transduction, and lipid homeostasis. In COPD plasma, several lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) species, including LysoPC(17:0), LysoPC(18:3), phosphatidic acid(20:1), and phosphatidylethanolamine(20:0/20:4), were markedly decreased, whereas phosphatidylinositols (PIs) such as PI(17:1/16:1) and PI(20:1) and phosphatidylserines (PSs) such as PS(17:2/22:4) and PS(22:6/17:0) were elevated. Reduced circulating LysoPCs have been reported in smokers and COPD patients and decline progressively with worsening lung function [ , ]. LysoPCs, generated from phosphatidylcholines by phospholipase A₂, help maintain alveolar surface tension and prevent alveolar collapse [ ]; their depletion may therefore compromise alveolar stability. Likewise, depletion of phosphatidic acid and phosphatidylethanolamine disrupts mitochondrial fusion and division via GTPase dysregulation, triggering mitophagy and lung injury [ – ]. Consistent with these mechanisms, combined CSE + Chol exposure in macrophages markedly suppressed OCR and enhanced ROS production, confirming cholesterol-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Elevated PIs and PSs may further amplify inflammatory signaling and impair phagocytic clearance, thereby exacerbating pulmonary inflammation [ – ].
Previous studies have shown that hypercholesterolemia predicts COPD progression and exacerbations [ – ]. However, direct mechanistic evidence has been limited. Our animal studies demonstrated that dietary cholesterol worsened pulmonary histological injury both in the presence and absence of CS exposure, leading to alveolar enlargement, airway epithelial thickening, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Moreover, CS exposure suppressed hepatic expression of HSD3B7, a key enzyme converting cholesterol to primary bile acids, resulting in hepatic cholesterol accumulation. Impaired hepatic catabolism may therefore promote systemic cholesterol elevation and redistribution to peripheral organs such as the lung. This liver–lung metabolic crosstalk provides a plausible explanation for how systemic dyslipidemia contributes to pulmonary injury in COPD.
A major mechanistic insight from this study is the identification of PPIA as a central mediator of cholesterol-induced inflammation. Transcriptomic and protein analyses showed that HCD feeding significantly increased PPIA expression in CS-exposed lungs, while PPIA levels were also elevated in the BALF of COPD patients. PPIA overexpression has been reported in other cholesterol-related inflammatory diseases such as atherosclerosis and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [ , ]. Cholesterol accumulation increases mitochondrial ROS in macrophages [ ], and ROS is a known inducer of PPIA expression [ , ]. In agreement with this, the ROS agonist Rosup enhanced PPIA expression in BMDMs, whereas ROS inhibition with Mito-TEMPO or PPIA silencing markedly attenuated CSE + Chol induced PPIA upregulation. These results indicate that cholesterol promotes PPIA expression through mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS-mediated signaling, which in turn activates NF-κB and IL-1β–driven inflammation.
Given the close relationship between dyslipidemia and COPD, targeting cholesterol metabolism represents a promising therapeutic avenue. Observational studies have reported that statin therapy is associated with lower exacerbation rates and mortality in COPD patients [ , – ]. Nevertheless, randomized trials have produced inconsistent results, suggesting that the benefits of statins may depend on individual metabolic profiles and the presence of dyslipidemia [ , ].
In parallel, dietary regulation of lipid intake offers a practical and complementary strategy. Our findings show that excessive cholesterol consumption intensifies pulmonary inflammation and mitochondrial injury, emphasizing the importance of limiting dietary cholesterol. For COPD patients, especially those with metabolic comorbidities, personalized nutritional interventions that lower cholesterol could help attenuate systemic metabolic stress and slow disease progression.
Finally, combination approaches that preserve mitochondrial function or inhibit PPIA activity may further enhance the efficacy of lipid-lowering therapy by directly interrupting the cholesterol-driven metabolic inflammatory cascade identified in this study. Together, these pharmacological and dietary strategies provide a rational framework for integrated metabolic management to mitigate chronic inflammation and progression of COPD.

## Conclusions
Our study demonstrates that systemic cholesterol dysregulation is a prominent metabolic hallmark of COPD and plays an active role in disease progression. By integrating untargeted metabolomics with a high‑cholesterol diet mouse model and mechanistic studies in macrophages, we show that cholesterol enrichment synergizes with cigarette smoke to exacerbate airway remodeling, alveolar destruction, and pro‑inflammatory cytokine production. At the cellular level, excess cholesterol impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics and elevates ROS, leading to upregulation of PPIA, activation of NF‑κB signaling, and enhanced IL‑1β release. Collectively, these findings identify a cholesterol–ROS–PPIA–NF‑κB axis that perpetuates pulmonary inflammation and tissue injury in COPD, offering mechanistic insight into how systemic dyslipidemia accelerates chronic lung disease and suggesting that interventions targeting cholesterol metabolism or mitochondrial homeostasis may slow COPD progression.
