## Background
Pulmonary injury in sepsis often develops into acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), in which the polarization state of alveolar macrophages (AMs) is a central pathogenic factor. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSCs-exo) have demonstrated therapeutic potential in septic ARDS through metabolic modulation, yet the specific mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated whether BMSCs-exo attenuate ARDS by regulating AM polarization via glycolysis inhibition and clarified the role of HOXA9.

## Methods
BMSCs were identified using flow cytometry and trilineage differentiation. BMSCs-exo were characterized by transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis and Western blot. An in vitro septic model was established by stimulating MH-S cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL). In vivo, ARDS was induced in C57BL/6 mice using either LPS (10 mg/kg) or cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), followed by caudal vein injection of BMSCs-exo. Glycolysis and macrophage polarization were evaluated with Western blot, RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence.

## Results
BMSCs-exo treatment improved survival in septic mice, alleviated pulmonary inflammation and edema, suppressed glycolysis in AMs, and restored M1/M2 balance. In vitro, BMSCs-exo downregulated glycolysis-associated markers in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells, suppressed M1 polarization, and facilitated M2 polarization. Notably, knockdown of the HOXA9 gene significantly diminished the ability of BMSCs-exo to suppress glycolysis and M1 polarization in MH-S cells.

## Conclusion
BMSCs-exo mitigate sepsis-induced ARDS by inhibiting HOXA9-mediated glycolysis and re-establishing AM polarization homeostasis, supporting their potential as a promising therapeutic approach for ARDS.

## Introduction
Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) represents a critical pulmonary condition marked by increased vascular permeability, pulmonary edema, alveolar damage, and impaired oxygen exchange [ ]. It can be triggered by diverse insults such as trauma, infection, and burns, with sepsis being the predominant cause [ , ]. The mortality of sepsis-associated ARDS remains as high as 40% [ ]. Current therapeutic strategies, including mechanical ventilation, antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and intensive supportive care, provide only limited benefit [ ]. Despite these interventions, survival outcomes have improved minimally in recent decades [ ]. The lack of effective targeted therapies highlights the urgent need to develop innovative treatment approaches for ARDS.
Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) possess the capacity to differentiate into multiple distinct cellular lineages. They have attracted increasing attention for their therapeutic potential in sepsis-induced ARDS [ , ]. Extensive evidence indicates that BMSCs exert strong anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory effects, leading to reduced lung tissue injury and improved survival rates [ ]. Nevertheless, the clinical application of BMSCs remains limited by concerns related to safety, ethical issues, potential tumorigenicity, and immune rejection. Accumulating evidence suggests that the beneficial actions of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are largely mediated by paracrine signaling, particularly through exosomes [ – ]. Exosomes are nanoscale vesicles (30–150 nm) that transport bioactive molecules and regulate intercellular communication [ ]. Due to their high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, favorable safety profile, and minimal ethical concerns, exosomes have emerged as promising candidates for clinical translation [ ]. In the acute lung injury (ALI) model, BMSCs-exo alleviate alveolar inflammatory responses by regulating miRNA-mediated inflammatory signaling pathways [ ]. In the spinal cord injury (SCI) model, engineered BMSCs-exo effectively restore neurological function [ ]. Furthermore, recent research indicates that melatonin-pretreated BMSCs-exo can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of skin flap repair by mitigating circadian rhythm-regulated ferroptosis [ ]. Collectively, these studies highlight the significant potential of exosome-based acellular strategies as novel therapeutic approaches. Our prior research has indicated that BMSCs-exo can effectively alleviate the pathological processes of ARDS [ ]. However, the precise therapeutic mechanism remains unclear and necessitates further research for clarification.
Alveolar macrophages (AMs), located at the air–tissue interface of alveoli, form the first line of defense against pathogens and environmental stimuli. Beyond immunity, they also contribute to surfactant homeostasis and pulmonary development [ ]. AMs can adopt different polarization states, mainly the pro-inflammatory M1 and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes [ ]. M1 macrophages secrete proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α to promote antimicrobial responses, whereas M2 macrophages highly express CD206 and facilitate inflammation resolution and tissue repair by producing anti-inflammatory factors such as Arg1 and Ym1 [ , ]. Increasing evidence shows that metabolic programming is crucial for macrophage polarization [ , ]. M1 polarization is commonly linked to enhanced glycolytic activity, which supports the increased energy demands of inflammatory responses and facilitates the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In contrast, suppression of glycolysis has been shown to attenuate macrophage-mediated inflammation, thereby reducing tissue injury and promoting resolution [ ]. In our previous research, we demonstrated that BMSCs-exo exert protective effects against ARDS. Mechanistically, these exosomes inhibited glycolytic metabolism in AMs, thereby suppressing their pro-inflammatory activation and ameliorating lung inflammation [ ].
Homeobox A9 (HOXA9), belonging to the HOX family of transcription factors, regulates various biological processes, including embryo implantation [ ], endothelial cell regulation [ ], vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation [ ], and leukemic transformation [ ]. Prior research has shown that administration of BMSCs enhances HOXA9 expression in the lungs during ALI. HOXA9 functions as a survival factor for pulmonary endothelial and epithelial cells [ ]. Emerging evidence also suggests a close relationship between cellular metabolism and HOXA9 [ , ]. Specifically, HOXA9 has been reported to suppress the transcription of pivotal glycolytic enzymes, such as hexokinase 2 (HK2), thereby impairing glycolytic flux. Nevertheless, whether HOXA9-mediated glycolysis inhibition contributes to the immunomodulatory effects of BMSCs-exo on AMs in sepsis remains unresolved.
In this study, we investigated the role of BMSCs-exo in attenuating pulmonary inflammation and ameliorating sepsis-induced ARDS. The study demonstrates that BMSCs-exo enhance intracellular HOXA9 expression, suppress glycolytic activity in AMs, and drive their polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby mitigating ARDS progression.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
Male C57BL/6 mice (7-8weeks, 18–22 g) were provided by Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The animals were kept in a controlled environment with ad libitum access to food and water, under a 12-hour light/dark photoperiod at 20–24 °C. Prior to experimentation, mice were acclimatized for at least one week. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji University School of Medicine and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH, USA).
Mice were anesthetized prior to the experiment to ensure that they were pain-free and to reduce stress during the experiment. Anesthesia was performed intraperitoneally using sodium pentobarbital (1%, 50 mg/kg). After anesthesia, the mice were observed for physiological responses, such as the absence of reflexes and apnea, to confirm the depth of anesthesia. Upon completion of the experiments, all mice were euthanized according to ethical requirements. Euthanasia methods were performed by cervical dislocation or overdose injection using sodium pentobarbital (1%, 150 mg/kg). The mortality status of the mice was immediately confirmed to avoid any resuscitation.

## Cell culture and intervention
The murine AM cell line MH-S was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, CA, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Based on prior studies and concentration gradient experiments (Supplementary Figure S1 A-C), MH-S cells were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL) to establish an in vitro sepsis model [ , ]. Depending on the experimental design, additional treatments were performed with vehicle control, 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 5 mM), or glucose (25 mM) for defined time periods.

## Isolation and characterization of BMSCs
BMSCs were harvested from the tibiae and femora of male C57BL/6 mice by bone marrow flushing and cultured in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and antibiotics under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After 72 h, non-adherent cells were removed by replacing the medium, which was subsequently changed every three days. Once the adherent cells reached approximately 90% confluence, they were subcultured at a 1:3 ratio. Cells at passage 3 were selected for subsequent experiments. Flow cytometry (FCM) confirmed BMSCs identity (CD29⁺, CD49e⁺, CD73⁺, CD90⁺, CD105⁺, CD45⁻). Multipotent differentiation was assessed using commercial induction media: adipogenesis (Oil Red O staining for lipid droplets), osteogenesis (Alizarin Red staining for calcium deposits), and chondrogenesis (Alcian Blue staining for glycosaminoglycans).

## Isolation and identification of exosomes
Exosomes were harvested from the conditioned medium of BMSCs (passages 3–5) by sequential ultracentrifugation. Briefly, cells were grown to ~ 80% confluence, washed with PBS, and incubated for 48 h in DMEM/F12 enriched with 10% exosome-depleted FBS. The culture supernatant was centrifuged at 300 × g for 15 min, 2,000 × g for 15 min, and 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris, then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Exosomes were pelleted at 100,000 × g for 90 min at 4 °C, resuspended in sterile PBS, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Exosome morphology and purity were confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blot for GM130, Calnexin, TSG101 and CD63.

## Coculture of MH-S cells with BMSCs
MH-S cells (5 × 10⁵/well) were seeded into the lower chamber of six-well Transwell plates. After 24 h, BMSCs were added to the upper chamber at ratios of 0.5-, 1-, 1.5-, and 2-fold relative to MH-S cell numbers. Coculture groups included: control, 5 × 10⁵ BMSCs, 5 × 10⁵ BMSCs pretreated with GW4869 (10 µM, an inhibitor of exosome release), and 5 × 10⁵ BMSCs pretreated with DMSO. In designated subgroups, cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h.

## Coculture of MH-S cells with BMSCs-exo
As demonstrated in previous experiments, MH-S cells (5 × 10⁵/well) were plated in six-well plates and cocultured for 24 h with either 100 µg of BMSCs-exo (diluted in 100 µL PBS) or PBS alone as a control [ ]. Furthermore, CCK-8 assays confirmed this dosage of BMSCs-exo exhibited no cytotoxicity toward MH-S cells (Supplementary Figure S5H). Experimental groups included: control, BMSCs-exo (100 µg), PBS, 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 5 mM, glycolysis inhibitor), and glucose (25 mM, glycolysis activator). Where indicated, LPS (1 µg/mL) stimulation was applied for 24 h.

## Transfection of MH-S cells with HOXA9 siRNA
MH-S cells (5 × 10⁵/well) were seeded in six-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cells were transfected with HOXA9-specific siRNA or negative control siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, USA). Total RNA and protein were collected at 36 h and 72 h post-transfection, respectively. Knockdown efficiency of HOXA9 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot.

## Luciferase assay
A dual-luciferase reporter system was employed to evaluate promoter activity. Overexpression plasmids were amplified in LB medium overnight and purified with a high-quality plasmid extraction kit. HEK-293 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured to ~ 70% confluence. Reporter plasmids were transfected with the HG transgene reagent, and cells were harvested 48 h later. Lysates were prepared with cell lysis buffer, and Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured with a microplate reader. Relative promoter activity was calculated by normalizing Firefly to Renilla luciferase signals.

## Establishment of sepsis-induced ARDS murine models and treatment with BMSCs-exo
For the LPS-induced ARDS model, C57BL/6 mice were randomly allocated into groups (n = 12/group): (1) Control group receiving intraperitoneal injection of 100 µL PBS; (2) LPS + PBS group injected intraperitoneally with lipopolysaccharide (10 mg/kg, diluted in 100 µL PBS); and (3) LPS+BMSCs-exo group receiving the same dose of LPS, followed 1 h later by tail vein injection of 100 µg BMSC-derived exosomes suspended in 100 µL PBS.
In the CLP-induced ARDS model, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (1%, 50 mg/kg). The cecum was exteriorized, ligated with 5 − 0 suture, punctured with a 20-gauge needle, and gently extruded to release a small amount of feces before repositioning and abdominal closure. Animals were rehydrated, kept warm, and closely monitored. Groups were as follows (n = 12/group): (1) CLP + PBS, CLP surgery with tail vein injection of 100 µL PBS; (2) CLP+BMSCs-exo, CLP surgery with tail vein injection of 100 µL PBS containing 100 µg BMSCs-exo (1 h post-CLP); (3) Sham, identical procedure without cecal ligation and puncture.

## Determination of mouse mortality rate
Mice were categorized into experimental and control groups, each comprising an adequate number of animals to ensure statistical reliability. The time of death for mice was monitored and logged within 7 days after injection of LPS and CLP for modeling. Survival analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism for comprehensive statistical evaluation.

## In vivo distribution of BMSCs-exo
BMSCs-exo were labeled with DIR fluorescent dye to evaluate their biodistribution in LPS-induced ARDS mice. Experimental groups included: (1) LPS group, intraperitoneal injection of LPS (10 mg/kg, 100 µL), followed by tail vein injection of 100 µL PBS containing DIR after 1 h; (2) LPS+BMSCs-exo group, intraperitoneal injection of LPS (10 mg/kg, 100 µL), followed 1 h later by tail vein injection of 100 µL PBS containing DIR and 100 µg of BMSCs-exo. Fluorescence signals were captured at 1, 6, 12, and 24 h post-LPS injection using the Visque Invivo Smart-LF imaging system.

## Protein and cell analysis of Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) with AMs isolation
Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (1%, 50 mg/kg), and the trachea was cannulated. BALF was obtained by flushing the lungs three times with 1 mL PBS. The lavage was centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min to separate cells from supernatant. Protein levels in the supernatant were quantified by BCA assay, while total inflammatory cells were counted under a microscope. For AM isolation, BALF cells were centrifuged (1200 rpm, 10 min), washed with PBS, plated, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow adherence.

## Lung histopathology and injury scoring
Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (5 μm), and stained with H&E. Pathological changes were examined microscopically, and lung injury was scored by four parameters: alveolar hemorrhage, vascular congestion, inflammatory infiltration, and alveolar wall thickening/hyaline membrane formation. Each was graded 0–4 (0 = normal, 4 = severe), with total scores ranging from 0 to 16.

## Lung wet-to-dry weight ratio
The right lung was excised and immediately weighed to obtain the wet weight. Samples were then dried at 80 °C for 24 h until a constant mass was achieved, and the wet-to-dry (W/D) ratio was calculated.

## Immunofluorescent staining
Paraffin-embedded lung sections were incubated with primary antibodies against iNOS, CD206, and F4/80 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat. Nos. 13120T, 24595T, 20395T), followed by fluorescent secondary antibody and DAPI counterstaining. Images were acquired using fluorescence microscopy, and positive signals were quantified with ImageJ.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA from MH-S cells or AMs was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed with the 5× HiScript II qRT SuperMix II kit (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR was carried out with a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Switzerland) using 2× Color SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). β-Actin served as the internal control. Primer sequences were designed with Primer 5.0 software (Table  ). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated utlizing the 2−ΔΔCt method.

 

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from lung tissues or MH-S cells using standard protocols. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes, which were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HOXA9, HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, LDHA, GM130, Calnexin, TSG101, CD63 and β-actin (diluted 1:1,000, Abclonal, China; Catalog Nos. A1908, A0994, A0268, A6982, A1146, A11408, A4846, A1692, A19023 and AC026, respectively). After washing, membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000; Abclonal, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using ECL substrate (Thermo Scientific) and imaged with a GE LAS4000 system. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

## Detection of lactate content and glucose consumption
Following treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected, and lactate levels as well as glucose consumption were measured using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
IL-1β and TNF-α levels in both BALF and serum were measured by ELISA. Standards and samples were processed according to the kit protocols, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm with a microplate reader. Cytokine levels were determined by interpolating absorbance values from the corresponding standard curves.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
MH-S cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates and subsequently incubated at 37 °C with CCK-8 reagent (Glpbio, USA). The corresponding procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At designated time points, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

## Bioinformatic analysis
Bulk RNA-seq data were obtained from the GEO dataset GSE263867, which included lung tissues from five LPS-induced ALI mice (10 mg/kg, intratracheal) and five controls. Genes or samples with > 50% missing values were excluded, and the remaining data were imputed using the “impute” R package, followed by median normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma package (v3.40.6), with thresholds set at |log2FC| > 1.5 and adjusted P < 0.05. Gene IDs were converted through the org.Mm.eg.db package, and functional enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler. Key pathways and processes associated with DEGs were visualized in bubble plots.

## Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), based on a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical evaluations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while differences among multiple groups were assessed via ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## LPS stimulation induced M1 polarization and Glycolysis in AMs
AMs represent the primary innate immune defense in the pulmonary system, and maintaining their polarization balance is critical for modulating ARDS-associated inflammation. To establish optimal conditions for the in vitro sepsis model, MH-S cells were stimulated with 1 µg/ml LPS for 3–48 h. RT-qPCR analysis revealed a time-dependent increase in M1-related cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) (Fig.  A-C), accompanied by a marked reduction in M2 markers (Arg1, Ym1, CD206), with the most pronounced changes observed at 24 h (Fig.  D-F).

 

Previous studies have shown that sepsis enhances glycolysis in macrophages, drives M1 polarization, and triggers excessive pulmonary inflammation leading to ARDS [ ]. Consistent with this, analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from sham and sepsis-induced ARDS mouse lungs (GSE263867) identified 2,097 DEGs (994 upregulated, 1,103 downregulated; Fig. G). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of these 2097 DEGs revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as “glucose homeostasis,” “glucose metabolic process,” “cellular response to glucose stimulation,” “glucose transmembrane transport,” and “glucose import” (p < 0.05), as depicted in the bubble plot (Fig. H). Among the DEGs, the key glycolytic enzymes HK2, PKM and GLUT1 were significantly upregulated, underscoring their critical roles in glucose homeostasis and metabolism within macrophages during sepsis. Consistently, Western blot analysis of LPS-treated MH-S cells demonstrated time-dependent upregulation of key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA), with maximal induction at 24 h (Fig. I, Supplementary Figure S1D-G). Furthermore, glucose consumption (Fig. J) and lactic acid content (Fig. K) in MH-S cells also showed a significant time-dependent increase after LPS stimulation. These findings indicate that LPS promotes M1 polarization, inhibits M2 polarization, and increases the level of glycolysis in AMs.

## Characterization of BMSCs
Primary BMSCs isolated from C57BL/6 mice adhered to the culture surface within 12–16 h and reached approximately 80% confluence after 5–6 days of incubation. Morphologically, the cells displayed a characteristic spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like appearance (Supplementary Figure S1H). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that the isolated cells fulfilled the International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT) latest standards for MSCs [ ], showing positive expression of MSCs-associated surface markers CD29, CD49e, CD73, CD90, and CD105 (Supplementary Figure S1I–M), while lacking expression of the hematopoietic marker CD45 (Supplementary Figure S1N). To verify their multipotency, in vitro differentiation assays were performed. Under lineage-specific induction conditions, BMSCs successfully differentiated into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes, as confirmed by Alizarin Red staining for mineralized matrix (Supplementary Figure S1O), Alcian Blue staining for cartilage matrix (Supplementary Figure S1P), and Oil Red O staining for lipid accumulation (Supplementary Figure S1Q), respectively. These results collectively demonstrate that the isolated primary BMSCs possess high purity and retain typical mesenchymal stem cell properties, including a strong capacity for trilineage differentiation.

## BMSCs ameliorated LPS-induced Glycolysis in AMs
To assess the influence of BMSCs on glycolysis in LPS-stimulated AMs, MH-S cells were co-cultured with BMSCs at different ratios (1:0.5, 1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2) for 24 h. Western blot analysis revealed that the 1:2 co-culture markedly reduced the expression of glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA) compared with LPS-treated controls (Supplementary Figure S2A-E). Moreover, BMSCs coculture substantially diminished glucose consumption (Supplementary Figure S2F) and lactate content (Supplementary Figure S2G) in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells. These results indicate the potent ability of BMSCs to effectively inhibit LPS-induced glycolysis in AMs.
Recent studies have highlighted BMSCs-exo as crucial mediators of intercellular communication, capable of suppressing proinflammatory M1 polarization and enhancing anti-inflammatory M2 polarization of AMs, thereby alleviating lung injury such as ARDS, ischemia/reperfusion injury, and pulmonary fibrosis [ – ]. To verify whether BMSCs exert their inhibitory effect on LPS-induced glycolysis in AMs via exosome secretion, we pretreated BMSCs with the exosome-specific inhibitor GW4869. Blocking exosome release markedly reversed the suppressive effect of BMSCs on glycolytic enzyme expression in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells (Fig. A and Supplementary Figure S2H-K). In addition, glucose consumption and lactate levels were significantly increased when MH-S cells were co-cultured with GW4869-pretreated BMSCs (Fig. B-C). These results suggest the potential of BMSC-exo in modulating glycolysis and glucose metabolism in LPS-stimulated AMs. RT-qPCR analysis further revealed that BMSCs pretreated with GW4869 displayed a weaker ability to suppress M1-associated markers (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and to enhance M2-associated markers (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells (Fig. D-I). These results indicate that BMSCs at least partially exert regulatory effects on macrophage polarization by secreting exosomes.

 

## BMSCs-exo can be taken up by LPS-stimulated AMs
Exosomes were isolated from BMSC-conditioned medium through sequential ultracentrifugation. TEM revealed that BMSCs-exo displayed a cup-shaped vesicle morphology, appearing mostly round or elliptical with well-defined boundaries and surrounded by a lipid bilayer, consistent with the typical characteristics of BMSCs-exo (Fig.  A). NTA indicated a size distribution mainly between 80 and 180 nm (Fig.  B). Western blot analysis revealed that GM130 and Calnexin were present in the lysate of BMSCs but undetectable in BMSCs-exo. In contrast, the canonical exosome markers Tsg101 and CD63 were detected in BMSCs-exo, verifying the successful isolation of high-purity BMSCs-exo (Fig.  C).

 

To examine uptake, BMSCs-exo were labeled with the fluorescent probe PKH26 and co-cultured with MH-S cells exposed to 1 µg/ml LPS for 24 h. Fluorescence microscopy revealed PKH26-associated fluorescent signals in MH-S cells under both control and LPS-stimulated conditions (Fig.  D), indicating effective cellular uptake and association of BMSCs-exo with AMs.

## BMSCs-exo suppressed Glycolysis and modulated polarization in LPS-stimulated AMs
To explore whether BMSCs-exo could suppress LPS-induced glycolysis and influence macrophage polarization, MH-S cells were exposed to LPS and subsequently treated with BMSCs-exo. Western blot analysis revealed that LPS stimulation significantly increased the expression of key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA) compared with untreated controls (Fig.  A and Supplementary Figure S3A-D), accompanied by elevated glucose consumption and lactate content (Fig.  B-C). Conversely, treatment with BMSCs-exo markedly attenuated the expression of these glycolytic enzymes, accompanied by reductions in glucose consumption and lactate content. Moreover, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of M1/M2 polarization-associated markers in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells after co-culture with BMSCs-exo. The results demonstrated that BMSCs-exo markedly downregulated M1-related genes (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) while upregulating M2-associated genes (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) (Fig.  D-I). These data indicate that BMSCs-exo attenuate LPS-induced glycolysis and drive macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, thereby contributing to the preservation of polarization homeostasis.

 

To further elucidate the regulatory effect of BMSCs-exo in regulating LPS-induced glycolysis and macrophage polarization, we applied two metabolic modulators: the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) and the activator glucose. Treatment with 2-DG, similar to BMSCs-exo, led to a notable decrease in the expression of glycolysis-related proteins HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA, along with reduced glucose uptake and lactate secretion in MH-S cells following LPS stimulation (Fig.  A-C and Supplementary Figure S3E-H). Importantly, no significant differences were detected between the LPS+BMSCs-exo and LPS + 2-DG groups. Moreover, relative to the LPS group, both treatments markedly downregulated M1-associated transcripts (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and upregulated M2-related genes (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) (Fig.  D-I).

 

Next, to determine whether glycolysis is essential for the anti-inflammatory activity of BMSCs-exo in LPS-stimulated AMs, we applied glucose as a glycolysis activator. We found that Glucose significantly reduced the inhibitory effects of BMSCs-exo on the protein expression of glycolysis-related key enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA) and glucose consumption and lactate content in LPS-treated MH-S cells (Fig.  A-C and Supplementary Figure S3I-L). Furthermore, relative to the LPS+BMSCs-exo group, the LPS + Glu+BMSCs-exo group exhibited significantly elevated expression of M1-associated markers (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and reduced expression of M2-associated markers (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) (Fig.  D-I).

 

Collectively, these results suggest that BMSCs-exo modulates the polarization balance of LPS-stimulated AMs primarily by suppressing glycolysis.

## HOXA9 is involved in BMSCs-exo-mediated Inhibition of Glycolysis and regulation of AM polarization
The HOXA9 gene, part of the evolutionarily conserved homeobox gene family, is situated on the short arm of human chromosome 7 (7p15-p14) and codes for the HOXA9 protein [ ]. Studies have demonstrated that the HOXA9 protein can bind to the promoter regions of key genes encoding glycolytic enzymes, leading to the inhibition of their transcription and consequently downregulating aerobic glycolysis in cells [ ]. Based on this evidence, we hypothesized that HOXA9 may participate in the BMSCs-exo–mediated suppression of glycolysis in LPS-stimulated AMs. Stimulation of MH-S cells with 1 µg/ml LPS markedly reduced HOXA9 protein expression, accompanied by increased expression of glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA) (Supplementary Figure S4A-F). Correlation analysis further revealed a negative relationship between HOXA9 mRNA expression and that of HK2 and GLUT1 (Supplementary Figure S4G-H). Using the JASPAR CORE database, we predicted potential HOXA9 binding sites within the promoter regions of HK2 and GLUT1. Promoter luciferase assays were performed to clarify whether HOXA9 binds to the HK2 and GLUT1 promoters. As illustrated in Fig. A, transfection of HOXA9 significantly inhibited the luciferase activity of the wild-type (WT) fragment containing the HOXA9 and HK2 mRNA binding sites, whereas the luciferase activity of this fragment was not affected by HOXA9 after mutation (MT) of the binding sites. Likewise, similar results were obtained by transfecting HOXA9 at the GLUT1 mRNA binding site (Fig. B). Subsequently, we employed the siRNA knockdown technique to evaluate the regulatory effects of HOXA9 on HK2 and GLUT1 expression. Firstly, we used the NC-CY3 negative control to determine the optimal siRNA interference concentration (100nM), as shown in Supplementary Figure S4I. Through RT-qPCR and Western blot screening, we identified the most efficient siRNA targeting the HOXA9 gene expression in MH-S cells (Supplementary Figure S4J-L). Transfection with si-HOXA9 attenuated the inhibitory effects of BMSCs-exo on HK2 and GLUT1 protein expression (Fig. C and Supplementary Figure S5A-C), glucose consumption (Fig. D), and lactate production (Fig. E). Moreover, compared with the si-NC + LPS+BMSCs-exo group, the si-HOXA9 + LPS+BMSCs-exo group exhibited elevated mRNA expression of M1-associated markers (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and decreased expression of M2-associated markers (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) (Fig. F-K). Collectively, these results indicate that BMSCs-exo regulate macrophage polarization in LPS-stimulated AMs by upregulating HOXA9, which suppresses key glycolytic enzymes.

 

## Recruitment of BMSCs-exo into the lungs of sepsis-induced ARDS mice and improved survival rate
To assess the in vivo functions of BMSCs-exo, a sepsis-induced ARDS mouse model was generated by intraperitoneal LPS injection. One hour after modeling, 100 µg of DIR-labeled BMSCs-exo in 100 µL PBS was administered via the tail vein, whereas the control group received DIR dye diluted in PBS. In vivo imaging revealed that BMSCs-exo accumulated in the lungs of septic mice from 1 to 24 h post-injection (Fig.  A). Consistently, lung tissues collected at 24 h exhibited strong DIR fluorescence signals in BMSCs-exo–treated mice, confirming their pulmonary recruitment (Fig.  B). Additionally, to investigate the biological function of BMSCs-exo in vivo, we established mouse models of sepsis-induced ARDS evoked by intraperitoneal LPS injection and CLP. In 7-day survival experiments, septic mice showed markedly reduced survival, whereas treatment with BMSCs-exo significantly improved survival outcomes (Fig.  C-D).

 

## BMSCs-exo alleviated sepsis-induced ARDS in mice
To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of BMSCs-exo in sepsis-induced ARDS, septic mice received an intravenous injection of 100 µg BMSCs-exo in 100 µL PBS one hour after modeling. Histopathological analysis at 24 h revealed pronounced lung injury in LPS-treated mice, characterized by alveolar wall disruption, interstitial edema, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, BMSCs-exo administration markedly ameliorated these pathological changes (Fig.  A-B). Consistently, the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio, which was significantly elevated in the LPS group, was notably reduced following BMSCs-exo treatment (Fig.  C). In addition, analysis of BALF demonstrated increased protein concentration and inflammatory cell counts in septic mice, both of which were significantly decreased after BMSCs-exo administration (Fig.  D-E). ELISA further confirmed that IL-1β and TNF-α levels in BALF and serum were markedly upregulated in the LPS group but significantly downregulated after BMSCs-exo treatment (Fig.  F-I). These findings demonstrate that BMSCs-exo exert anti-inflammatory effects and significantly alleviate lung injury in septic mice.

 

## BMSCs-exo inhibited lung tissue Glycolysis and AM M1 polarization in sepsis-induced ARDS mice
To determine whether the protective effects of BMSCs-exo in sepsis-induced ARDS were linked to glycolytic regulation and macrophage polarization, a murine model was established via intraperitoneal injection of LPS. One hour following tail vein administration of 100 µg BMSCs-exo, lung tissues and AMs were harvested for analysis. Western blot results demonstrated that LPS stimulation markedly upregulated glycolysis-associated enzymes (HK2, PKM2, and GLUT1) and suppressed HOXA9 protein expression relative to controls. In contrast, BMSCs-exo treatment significantly downregulated HK2, PKM2, and GLUT1 expression while restoring HOXA9 expression (Fig.  A and Supplementary Figure S5D-G). Lactate levels in mouse BALF and serum showed a substantial increase in the LPS group, which significantly decreased after BMSCs-exo administration (Fig.  B-C). RT-qPCR analysis of AMs further demonstrated upregulation of M1-associated markers (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and suppression of M2-associated markers (Arg1, Ym1, CD206) in the LPS group. These alterations were effectively reversed by BMSCs-exo treatment (Fig.  D-I). In line with these findings, immunofluorescence staining of lung sections revealed increased iNOS expression and reduced CD206 expression in septic mice, both of which were significantly corrected by BMSCs-exo administration (Fig.  J-K). Overall, the results suggest that intravenous injection of BMSCs-exo significantly inhibits sepsis-induced glycolysis and M1 polarization of AMs, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects.

 

## Discussion
In this study, we established an in vitro sepsis-induced ARDS model by stimulating MH-S cells with LPS and demonstrated that BMSCs-exos promote the polarization of MH-S cells towards the M2 phenotype while inhibiting their polarization towards the M1 phenotype, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Additionally, we established an in vivo mouse model of sepsis-induced ARDS via intraperitoneal injection of LPS and CLP. Intravenous administration of 100 µg BMSCs-exo significantly improved the survival rate of septic mice, markedly ameliorated lung inflammation, reduced levels of inflammatory cytokines, alleviated pulmonary edema, and regulated macrophage polarization balance, thereby mitigating lung injury in septic mice. Furthermore, following BMSCs-exo administration, expression of glycolysis-associated proteins in MH-S cells and septic lung tissues was downregulated, whereas HOXA9 expression was markedly upregulated. Notably, silencing HOXA9 expression substantially diminished the capacity of BMSCs-exo to suppress glycolysis and inflammatory responses. These findings indicate that HOXA9 is a pivotal mediator in the BMSCs-exo-induced inhibition of LPS-induced glycolysis in AMs and promotion of macrophage polarization balance, directly correlating with key glycolytic enzymes HK2 and GLUT1. Collectively, the study provides compelling evidence that BMSCs-derived exosomes mitigate sepsis-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome by suppressing LPS-driven glycolysis in AMs and restoring their polarization balance.
The prognosis for patients with sepsis-induced ARDS is significantly worse compared to those with sepsis or ARDS alone [ ]. Currently, no definitive pharmacological approach has been identified to elucidate the relevant molecular mechanisms underlying ARDS. Clinical interventions available for ARDS mainly consist of supportive measures. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate potential mechanisms for mitigating ARDS injury. Previous research has identified various mechanisms contributing to the progression of ARDS, including inflammation and oxidative stress [ – ]. The balance of AM polarization is considered a crucial factor in initiating and resolving pulmonary inflammatory responses during ARDS development. A significant body of research has demonstrated that modulating M1 macrophage activity or promoting macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype represents a promising and effective approach to mitigate sepsis-induced ARDS. For example, specific natural derivatives or synthetic compounds such as GTS-21 and 3,4-dihydroxyphenethyl alcohol glycoside have been found to ameliorate ALI in septic mice by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization [ , ]. Tu et al. [ ] provided evidence that methylprednisolone reduces LPS-induced ARDS by increasing M2 macrophage population and inducing M2 polarization. Our previous study yielded a series of findings, such as the finding that α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) can promote IL-4-induced M2 polarization of murine AMs by increasing the transcription of PPAR-γ and enhancing fatty acid metabolism, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses and pathological damage in the lungs of ARDS mice [ ]. Additionally, activation of the Nrf2 pathway can also promote M2 macrophage polarization, thus mitigating sepsis-induced lung injury [ ]. Consistent with these results, the present study revealed that BMSCs-exo similarly restore macrophage polarization balance in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells by suppressing M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization. In vivo experiments demonstrated that intravenous injection of BMSCs-exo can reduce lung inflammation and interstitial pulmonary edema in septic mice, inhibit M1 polarization of lung macrophages, and thus alleviate sepsis-associated ARDS lung injury.
Numerous studies have demonstrated that macrophage polarization is intricately linked to cellular metabolism [ , ]. Following septic infections and severe tissue injuries, metabolic shifts are decisive in directing macrophage functional states [ , ]. M1 macrophages predominantly rely on glycolysis for their metabolic processes, while M2 macrophages mainly depend on oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. Previous studies have validated that macrophages maintain an alternative activation state when the glycolysis inhibitor macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) is added; otherwise, they revert to the M1 polarization state [ ]. On the other hand, inhibition of glycolysis in macrophages induces their polarization towards the M2, resulting in anti-inflammatory effects [ ]. Zhuang et al. [ ] reported that Tiliroside ameliorates ulcerative colitis by modulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization via the HIF-1α/glycolysis pathway, highlighting the close link between glycolytic reprogramming and macrophage phenotype in inflammatory disorders. Key glycolytic enzymes are central to this process. For instance, chlorogenic acid alleviates colitis through suppression of PKM2-dependent glycolysis and NLRP3 activation [ ]. Knocking down the glycolytic key enzyme Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1) inhibits M1 polarization of macrophages and enhances their M2 activation [ ]. Through analysis of RNA sequencing datasets from mouse lung tissues with sepsis-induced ARDS, we identified significant enrichment in pathways such as “glucose homeostasis” and “glucose metabolic process.” This highlights the pivotal role of glycolysis in the development mechanism of sepsis-associated ARDS. In line with this, our results demonstrated that stimulation of MH-S cells with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h markedly upregulated the protein expression of key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA). Nevertheless, the addition of BMSCs-exosomes significantly suppressed glycolytic activity in MH-S cells. This effect was further validated by separate supplementation with 2-DG and glucose. The final results demonstrated that BMSCs-exosomes exerted a glycolysis-inhibiting effect akin to the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG. Simultaneously, BMSCs-exo inhibited M1 polarization while promoting M2 polarization of macrophages. In contrast, the glycolysis stimulator, glucose, attenuated this phenomenon. Our experiments demonstrated that BMSCs-exo can effectively regulate macrophage polarization by inhibiting glycolysis.
More importantly, our study identified HOXA9 as a pivotal transcription factor mediating the glycolysis-inhibitory effects of BMSCs-exosomes in MH-S cells. Zhou et al. previously reported that HOXA9 represses aerobic glycolysis by binding to the promoter regions of glycolysis-related enzyme genes, including HK2 [ ]. Consistent with this, bioinformatic prediction using the JASPAR CORE database revealed potential HOXA9-binding sites within the promoter sequences of HK2 and GLUT1. Dual luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that HOXA9 significantly suppressed the transcriptional activity of these promoters. Functional validation with siRNA-mediated HOXA9 knockdown further demonstrated that the ability of BMSCs-exosomes to inhibit HK2 and GLUT1 expression was markedly attenuated. Notably, silencing HOXA9 also weakened the anti-inflammatory action of BMSCs-exosomes, as evidenced by impaired suppression of M1 polarization and reduced promotion of M2 polarization. Collectively, these results highlight HOXA9 as a key downstream effector through which BMSCs-exosomes inhibit glycolysis and restore macrophage polarization balance under LPS stimulation.
Growing evidence indicates that exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) represent a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy with distinct advantages, including low immunogenicity, high biosafety, and superior stability compared to direct cell transplantation [ ]. These exosomes deliver bioactive molecules such as miRNAs, proteins, and lipids that modulate inflammation-related signaling pathways, thereby promoting the resolution and repair of acute lung injury. Under LPS-induced sepsis conditions, disruption of the pulmonary microvascular endothelial barrier and increased activation of alveolar macrophages enhance the retention and uptake of exosomes within lung tissue [ , ]. Additionally, as a critical immune filtration organ for circulating nanoparticles, the lung naturally enriches exogenous exosomes during systemic inflammatory processes [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that intravenously administered exosomes preferentially accumulate in the lungs under septic or inflammatory conditions, whereas traditional liposomes lack this inflammation-specific pulmonary targeting ability [ ], supporting the BMSCs-exo pulmonary enrichment phenomenon observed in this study. Recent studies have confirmed that MSC-derived exosomes can treat ALI by delivering miRNAs and immunomodulatory proteins that specifically inhibit alveolar macrophage pyroptosis, thus reducing pulmonary inflammatory responses [ ]. One study demonstrated that adipose-derived stem cell-derived exosomes (ADSCs-exo) can alleviate alveolar inflammation and mitigate cytokine storms in a sepsis-induced ARDS mouse model by suppressing macrophage M1 polarization [ ]. In another sepsis model, ADSCs-exo reduced oxidative stress and inhibited ferroptosis in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, ultimately mitigating lung tissue injury and lowering mortality [ ]. Similarly, our preliminary research revealed that BMSCs-exo mitigates sepsis-induced lung injury by inhibiting macrophage ferroptosis, a mechanism potentially linked to the lncRNA SNHG12 delivered within exosomes [ ]. This study demonstrates that BMSCs-exo alleviate sepsis-induced ARDS by inhibiting glycolysis and modulating macrophage polarization, thereby expanding the therapeutic potential of BMSCs-exo-based interventions targeting sepsis-induced ARDS across multiple mechanistic levels. Currently, exosome therapy has entered the clinical trial phase and has demonstrated beneficial effects in various diseases [ ]. However, the number of registered clinical trials involving BMSCs-exos related to ARDS remains limited. Due to the absence of standardized administration protocols and a lack of consensus guidelines, determining the appropriate dosage of BMSC-exos to achieve personalized therapeutic outcomes remains a critical challenge. Additionally, conducting rigorous safety assessments to minimize potential off-target effects of exosomes presents a significant obstacle. As research into extracellular vesicle biology advances, future studies should focus on establishing dose-response relationships and optimizing delivery protocols and routes. Meanwhile, developing standardized exosome isolation and characterization methods, alongside scalable and stable bioprocessing platforms, is crucial for advancing exosome-based acellular therapies from the laboratory to clinical application.
This study has several limitations. First, this study used intraperitoneal injections of LPS and CLP, respectively, to construct a sepsis mouse model. Future studies may need to evaluate the effect of BMSCs-exo in other ARDS models to validate its clinical significance. Second, how AMs take up BMSCs-exo and whether there are other modes of delivery remain unclear. Third, several studies have demonstrated that stem cells of various origins can play a lung-protective role in sepsis; however, we focused specifically on BMSC-derived exosomes in the present study. Fourth, BMSCs-exo contains many bioactive molecules, and further studies are needed to explore exactly what bioactive molecules may have beneficial effects on AMs inflammation in ARDS as part of our future research.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study is the first to reveal that BMSCs-exo alleviates sepsis-induced ARDS by regulating the expression of critical glycolytic enzymes HK2 and GLUT1 through HOXA9, inhibiting LPS-induced glycolysis in MH-S cells, and promoting macrophage polarization balance.
