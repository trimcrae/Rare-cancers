## Abstract

## Background
The intestine is one of the most sensitive organs to ionizing radiation (IR), and radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) impacts the quality of life of patients undergoing radiotherapy. There are limited early diagnostic biomarkers and specific medicines clinically approved for RIII. Therefore, we sought to identify new theranostic targets to prevent RIII and to facilitate the reestablishment of the intestinal epithelium. Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are widely appreciated as pervasive regulators of many diseases and multiple biological processes, while whether and how specific circRNAs are involved in radiation-induced intestinal injury remains largely unknown.

## Methods
Differentially expressed circRNAs were analyzed and verified via RNA sequencing. The function of an intestine-specific circRNA (termed circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)) on cell proliferation, apoptosis, and DNA damage level after radiation was explored in vitro, and the underlying mechanism was further investigated. Ultimately, intestinal organoids and mice model were used to verify the role of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) on radiation-induced intestinal injury.

## Results
Primarily expressed in intestinal stem cells, CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was downregulated in mice intestines after 14 Gy abdominal radiation and showed timely relationship with intestinal injury level. CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) promoted the proliferation and alleviated cell apoptosis and DNA damage level of intestinal epithelial cells and promoted organoids survival after radiation compared with control groups. In vivo experiments showed that compared with control groups, overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) could increase intestinal length; enhance epithelial integrity and the percentage of proliferative cells, stem cells, paneth cells, and goblet cells; and promote intestinal adaption after radiation. Mechanistically, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) protects intestines from IR via circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)/miR-125a-5p/STAT3.

## Conclusions
CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6), a novel promising RIII bio-marker, responses rapidly at the early stage after 14 Gy abdominal irradiation, and exogenous expression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) could promote intestinal fitness in RIII. We reveal that the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)/miR-125a-5p/STAT3 axis is important to the regeneration of the intestinal epithelium after radiation-induced damage, providing a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target for RIII.

## Background
The intestinal epithelium is one of the most rapidly self-renewing tissues in adult mammals; the entire epithelium is replaced every 3–5 days to support the absorptive, secretory, and barrier functions of the tissue [ ]. The integrity of the epithelium is continually challenged and potentially damaged by various microbiota and chemical and physical stresses [ ]. Intestinal stem cells (ISCs) and progenitor cells in the proliferation compartment of the intestinal crypts are the key cells responsible for intestinal homeostasis and regeneration after injury [ – ].
ISCs are traditionally categorized into two main populations on the basis of morphology and marker expression [ ]. Active intestinal stem cells (aISCs), also known as cycling intestinal stem cells or crypt base columnar cells, are characterized by the presence of leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 (LGR5) and are long-lived stem cells that continuously undergo the cell cycle [ , ]. Located near +4 positions from the bottoms of the crypts are quiescent stem cells, also called reserve intestinal stem cells (rISCs). These cells are resistant to stress and become activated upon perturbation of aISCs [ , ]. ISCs are essential to intestinal integrity and renewal in the process of homeostasis and in response to disturbances [ ]. aISCs continuously self-renew and rapidly produce proliferating progenitors into the transit-amplifying (TA) zone that divides upward and produces the mature functional intestinal epithelium composed of absorptive enterocytes, goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, and tuft cells [ , ]. Because they are rapidly replicating, the Lgr5+ stem cells are highly sensitive to ionizing radiation (IR) and are diminished upon injury [ , ] and reach their lowest level on the second day after IR [ ]. The damage and loss of aISCs contributes to a severe shortening and swelling of the villi between 2 and 4 days after IR as well as to threatening the integrity and renewal of the intestinal epithelium.
Worldwide, an estimated 19.3 million new cancer cases and almost 10.0 million cancer deaths occurred in 2020. It has been reported that approximately 80–90% of patients with pelvic or abdominal cancer that receive radiotherapy will develop gastrointestinal symptoms within a few weeks after therapy; these symptoms are partly caused by acute gastrointestinal inflammation [ , ]. Patients with acute radiotherapy enteritis manifest multiple symptoms, such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, nausea, constipation, hematochezia, and loss of weight [ ]. Histologically, high doses of IR lead to death of ISCs, apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells, and splitting of villi, leading to increased intestinal permeability and impaired renewal of the intestinal epithelium. These issues may resolve, but they can also develop into chronic alterations, including fibrosis of connective tissues and continuous cytokine activation in the submucosa [ ]. Unfortunately, there is a lack of standardized strategies for the prevention of these adverse responses, and radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) remains a matter of great concern.
Efforts have been made to relieve IR-induced intestinal disorders. The mainstream core strategy is to focus on intestinal stem cells [ ]. Lin Y. et al. raised that activation of vitamin D receptor (VDR) through agonism or upregulation of VDR expression would alleviate RIII and epithelial damage in intestinal stem cells in mice [ ]. The activation of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2 (NOD2) could protect Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells survival through clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by mitophagy [ ]. Besides, modulation of intestinal microbiota is another target for relieving RIII. Terrence E Riehl et al. found that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG protects the intestinal epithelium from radiation injury through the release of lipoteichoic acid, macrophage activation, and the migration of mesenchymal stem cells [ ].
Noncoding RNAs have also been investigated for their impact on RIII [ ]. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), in particular, have recently been identified as a new class of RNAs with gene regulatory roles; these molecules are characterized by covalently closed loop structures with neither 5′ to 3′ polarity nor a polyadenylated tail [ ]. The biological function of circRNAs has been explored in gastrointestinal tumors [ – ], and circRNAs have also been found to be involved in the self-renewal of ISCs [ ]. While circRNAs are important to the function of the intestines and ISCs, there has been little research into the roles and functional mechanisms of circRNAs in RIII.
In this study, a RIII mouse model was established, and transcriptome sequencing was performed. Among the differentially expressed circRNAs, a novel circRNA, which we named circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), drew our attention. Surprisingly, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was found to be specifically expressed in the gastrointestinal tract, especially in the crypts where ISCs dwell. Therefore, we hypothesized that this circRNA may be involved in the regeneration of the intestinal epithelium after IR. Upon testing this hypothesis, we found that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) promotes the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells through the sequestering of miR-125a-5p. Thus, our study provides a new potential therapeutic target for the treatment of RIII.

## Materials and methods

## Cell lines and cell culture
Murine intestinal epithelial cell line MODE-K (ZKCC62328) was purchased from Otwo Biotech Co. Ltd. Murine intestinal epithelial cell line CT26 (1101MOU-PUMC000275) and rat intestinal epithelial cell line IEC-6 (1101RAT-PUMC000110) were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College (PCRC). The 293 T (CRL-3216) cell line was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MODE-K and CT26 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (SH30809.01, Hyclone). The 293 T cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (SH30243.01, Hyclone), and IEC-6 was cultured in DMEM (SH30243.01, Hyclone) with 0.01 mg/mL insulin (I8830-20 mg, Solarbio). All cell media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HS-FBS-500, HAKATA), and all cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To construct cell lines stably overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), we transfected the control vector pLCDH-circNC or the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) overexpression vector pLCDH -circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) into MODE-K cells and CT26 cells, followed by selection with puromycin (A1113803, Sigma, USA) for 2–3 weeks until circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was stably overexpressed in the cells.

## Mouse model of RIII and development of Lgr5-IRES-creERT2 heterozygous mice
Male C57BL/6 J mice weighing 20–22 g were purchased from Beijing HFK Bio-Technology and then housed with a 12-h light–dark cycle and fed a standard laboratory diet with free access to food and water. A cesium-137 γ-irradiator (Atomic Energy of Canada, Chalk River, Ontario, Canada) was used to perform abdominal irradiation (ABI), with other body parts shielded from IR, with a 14 Gy total dose at a dose rate of 0.99 Gy/min. Mice were euthanized by inhalation of excess carbon dioxide at 6 h, 2 days, and 3.5 days after IR, and their tissues were harvested for further experiments. All procedures with animals were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the local animal care and use committee. Heterozygous mice that harbor an Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 knock-in allele that both abolishes Lgr5 gene function and expresses an EGFP–CreERT2 fusion protein were donated by Professor Haowen Zhang, Soochow University, China.

## RNA sequence analysis
Total RNA was isolated from the jejuna of mice with sham IR or 14 Gy ABI using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA purity and concentration were determined with a NanoDrop 8000 spectrometer. RNA high-throughput sequencing was performed using Cloud-Seq Biotech (Shanghai, China). Briefly, rRNA was removed from total RNA with GenSeq® rRNA Removal Kit (GenSeq, Inc.). Then, the rRNA -depleted samples were subjected to library construction with GenSeq® Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit (GenSeq, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were controlled for quality and quantified with a BioAnalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA). Library sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq instrument with 150 base pair (bp) paired end reads. Paired-end reads were harvested from the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer and quality controlled with Q30. After 3′ adaptor-trimming and removal of low-quality reads by cutadapt software (version 1.9.3), the high-quality trimmed reads were used to analyze circRNAs, lncRNAs, and mRNAs.

## Extraction of RNA and genomic DNA (gDNA)
Total RNA was isolated from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (15,596,018, Ambion) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA was used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA with a reverse transcription reagent kit (RR037A, TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions or to synthesize cDNA with miRNA-specific stem-loop primers. TIANamp Genomic DNA kit (no. DP304-02, TIANGEN) was used to extract gDNA.

## Separation of villi, crypts, and the remnants of the mice’s intestines
Crypts, villi, and the remnants of intestinal epithelia were isolated as previously described [ ]. Briefly, jejunum sections of approximately 10 cm were harvested and cut by scissors to approximately 2 mm length. Then the crypts and villi were released from jejunum by intestine via incubation with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) containing 2 mM EDTA at 4 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking followed by vigorous vortex in cold DPBS containing 54.9 mM d-sorbitol and 43.4 mM sucrose for 3 min. The suspension was collected and filtered using a 70 μm strainer. The crypt component was passed through the strainer and collected by centrifugation at 200g for 5 min and the villi component was trapped on the strainer and rinsed with cold DPBS. The intestine sediments were washed with cold DPBS three times and collected as the remnant component.

## Real time–polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR), RT–quantitative (q)PCR, RNase R treatment, and actinomycin D treatment
To analyze mRNA and circRNA, RNA was reverse-transcribed using a HiScript II1st Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (R211-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative RT–PCR was performed with a Bio-Rad CFX96TM Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primers for mRNAs and circRNAs were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and miRNA primers were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Gapdh and U6 were used as internal control standards for quantification of mRNA and miRNA, respectively. For RNase R treatment, 2.5 μg total RNA was digested at 37 °C for 20 min and 70 °C for 10 min with 2 U/μg RNase R (R0301, Guangzhou Geneseed Biotech, China) and then analyzed using RT–PCR. For actinomycin D treatment assay, intestinal epithelial cells MODE-K and CT26 with overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) were treated with actinomycin D (S8964, Selleckchem) at a final concentration of 4 μg/mL for 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 10 h. Total RNA of MODE-K and CT26 cells was extracted by TRIzol reagent (15,596,018, Ambion) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by reverse transcription. The cDNA was amplified by RT–qPCR with CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (BioRad, USA) and the content of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) and Dmbt1 was detected by RT–qPCR to calculate the RNA turnover rate and half-life (t1/2). 18 s were used as internal references, and the data were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

## Western blot
The proteins from cells and tissues were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio) containing 1% PMSF (P0100, Solarbio) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (P6730, Solarbio). The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (24,937–79-9, Merck Millipore, Germany), which were blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween® 20 (TBST) and then incubated with primary antibodies against GAPDH (60,004–1-Ig, Proteintech), β-tubulin (66,240–1-Ig, Proteintech), STAT3 (ab119352, Abcam), pSTAT3 (Y705) (ab76315, Abcam), and pSTAT3 (S767) (ab32143, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline with 1% Tween® 20 (T8220, Solarbio) (TBST) for 10 min per time, and then they were incubated with secondary antibody, Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate (SA000001-2, Proteintech), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), and HRP conjugate (SA000001-1, Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional 10-min washes with TBST, the membranes were developed with the enhanced chemiluminescence method. GAPDH or β-tubulin served as a loading control.

## RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
The Cy3-labeled circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) probes (5′-GGCCAAACCAGAATCTGTTCCTGCTGTGGTTTGTTCAGTTGG-3′) were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). RNA FISH was performed using an RNA FISH SA-Biotin System kit (F32202/50, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were acquired on a inverted fluorescence microscope (AMG EVOS, USA).

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay
The RIP assay was performed using the Magna RIP Quad RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation KitEZ-Magna RIP RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (17–700, Merck Millipore, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 293 T cells overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) were collected and lysed in RIP lysis buffer with protease and RNase inhibitors. The cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with AGO2 antibodies (2897S, CST) or IgG (12–371, Millipore) at 4 °C overnight. Then, the beads were washed and incubated with proteinase K (ST532, Beyotime) to remove proteins. Finally, RNA was extracted and subjected to RT–qPCR, agarose gel electrophoresis analysis, and digital PCR.

## Biotin-coupled probe pulldown assay
RNA pulldown assay was performed using the Pierce™ Magnetic RNA–Protein Pull-Down Kit (20,164, Thermo Sciectific). The biotin-labeled circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) probe (5′-GGCCAAACCAGAATCTGTTCCTGCTGTGGTTTGTTCAGTTGG-3′) and a negative control probe were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Crypts were extracted from a healthy mouse for protein extraction, and circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing 293 T cells were used for RNA extraction. The total RNA was incubated with the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) probe or a control probe at 80 °C for 5 min and then slowly cooled to room temperature. Streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads were washed with a solution containing 0.1 M NaOH and 0.05 M NaCl and then with 0.1 M NaCl twice and with 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5) twice. The beads were coated with capture buffer and then washed with 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5) twice. Then protein and probe-incubated RNA solution was fixed with pre-washed streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. The magnetic beads were washed three times with washing buffer, and then 60 mL of elution buffer was added, followed by shaking for 30 min at room temperature. Then SDS sample loading buffer was added, and the protein was denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, and western blotting analysis was performed. The input sample was 20 µL of the protein solution. The RNA pulldown products were also analyzed by mass spectrometry. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD041051 [ ].

## Luciferase reporter assay
The 293 T cells were seeded in 24-well plates (3 × 104 cells/well). Negative control mimics or miR-125a-5p mimics were co-transfected with a reporter plasmid into circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing 293 T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11,668,019, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, the cells were collected and lysed with passive lysis buffer. The luciferase activities were assessed using the Dual Luciferase Assay Kit (E1910, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

## Cell proliferation assay
Cell proliferation was detected with EdU and CCK-8 assays. The EdU assay was performed according to the protocol of the Cell-Light™ EdU Apollo®488 In Vitro Imaging Kit (C10310-3, RiboBio) and Cell-Light™ EdU Apollo®567 In Vitro Imaging Kit (C10310-1, RiboBio). Briefly, treated MODE-K cells were seeded in 24-well plates with cover glasses and incubated with 50 mM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C. After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, the cells were exposed to 100 mL of 1 × Apollo® reaction cocktail and then incubated with 5 μg/mL Hoechst 33,342 to stain cell nuclei. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (EVOS, AMG, USA). The percentage of EdU-positive cells was defined as the proliferation rate. For the CCK-8 assay, the treated MODE-K cells or IEC-6 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with 100 mL 10% CCK-8 solution for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a Synergy HT spectrophotometer (BioTek). All of the experiments were repeated three times in triplicate.

## Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining assay
Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (556,547, BD Biosciences) was used in this assay. MODE-K cells or IEC-6 cells were treated with trypsin and resuspended in cold PBS. Next, the cells were stained with FITC-Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark and then assessed by flow cytometry. Cell apoptosis profiles were analyzed using FlowJo_V10 software.

## TUNEL assay
The TUNEL assay was carried out using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, paraffin sections of mice jejuna were deparaffinized by xylene and alcohol and then incubated with proteinase K at 20 μg/mL for 20 min. The slides were rinsed three times with PBS and then incubated with a mix solution composed of the enzyme terminal deoxynucleotide transferase (TdT) and fluorescent labeling solution at 37 °C for 60 min. The slides were rinsed with PBS and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and sealed for observation under microscope.

## Mouse intestinal organoid culture
Crypts isolation and organoid culture were performed as previous described [ ]. Serial organoid passaging was performed every week. The size of organoids was evaluated by quantifying the percentage of budding crypts, average perimeters, and average surface area of horizontal cross section of organoids acquired from multiple random non-overlapping pictures using the Adobe Photoshop 2015 CC software. Organoid morphological measurements were performed using Adobe Photoshop CC 2015 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The procedure was conducted as follows. (1) Image calibration: A scale reference (e.g., a ruler or calibration slide of known dimensions) was included within the original image. Using the Ruler Tool, a line was drawn along the scale reference, and the Measurement Scale was set under Analysis > Set Measurement Scale > Custom to convert pixel values to real-world units (e.g., millimeters). (2) Shape delineation: The irregular shape of interest was isolated using the Polygonal Lasso Tool to ensure precise tracing of boundaries. (3) Perimeter measurement: The perimeter was calculated as follows: (a) activating the selection corresponding to the shape boundary, (b) generating a 1-pixel-wide outline of the selection using Select > Modify > Border (width = 1 pixel), (c) measuring the total pixel count of the outlined boundary under Image > Analysis > Record Measurements, and (d) converting the pixel count to real-world units using the pre-calibrated scale. The area was quantified as follows: (a) with the active selection representing the shape, select Navigate to Image > Analysis > Record Measurements, (b) in the Record Measurements dialog box, check the Area option under Measurement Types, and (c) click Record to generate a data log containing the selected region’s area in both pixel2 and converted real-world units (e.g., mm2).

## The immunofluorescence of intestinal organoids
The culture medium was removed, and Matrigel domes with organoids were rinsed with PBS. Organoids were slowly and gently resuspended with 4% cells and tissue fixative solution at 4 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 250g at 4 °C for 5 min. After removal of the supernatant, the pellets were washed three times with PBS for 10 min per time and then transferred into 2% PBST (2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for pre-rupture overnight at room temperature. Then, the organoids were fixed in 1 mL fixative solution (5% BSA within 1% Triton X-100 in PBS) at 4 °C overnight and centrifuged at 250g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed and the pellets were resuspended and incubated with diluted primary antibody at 4 °C for 2 days. Wash the pellets with washing buffer two times for over 1 h per time and resuspend the pellets with diluted second antibody at 4 °C for 1 day. Wash the pellets with washing buffer two times for over 1 h per time and resuspend the pellets with DAPI for 30 min. Wash the pellets with PBS three times for 30 min per time. Resuspend the pellets with RapiClear 1.47 (Sunjin Lab, CAS no. RC147001) and place a drop on the glass slide for observation under the fluorescence microscope.

## SiRNAs and miRNA mimics and inhibitor
Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen, USA) at an siRNA concentration of 20 Nm. Micro RNA (miRNA) mimics and inhibitor were synthesized by RibiBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA), 200 pmol per well, in 6-well plates. Cells were irradiated 24 h after transfection. Sequences of siRNAs and miRNA mimics are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

## AAV9-mediated intestine-specific circRNA overexpression
In vivo intestine-specific circRNA overexpression was achieved with AAV9 vectors. Recombinant AAV9 vectors carrying circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) or empty vector with a plasmid cytomegalovirus (pCMV) promotor (AAV9-pCMV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) or AAV9-pCMV-empty) were manufactured by GeneChem Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). AAV9-pCMV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)/empty vectors (5 × 1010 vector genomes/mouse) were delivered by tail intravenous injection. Subsequently, 2 and 3 weeks after AAV9 delivery, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) overexpression was verified by immunofluorescent microscopy and RT–qPCR.

## Tissue harvest
Mice were sacrificed immediately or 6 h, 2 days, and 3.5 days after IR. The jejuna were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for RNA and protein studies or fixed with paraformaldehyde for histological assessment and crypt isolation.

## Histology assessment
Intestinal lengths were measured. Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, gradually dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, cut into transverse sections (5-μm thickness) and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), KI67, OLFM4, lysozyme, and Alcian blue–periodic acid–Schiff (AB–PAS). Quantification of villus length, crypt depth, and crypt intensity was recorded from ten representative, well-oriented villus/crypt units, according to H&E staining. Crypt cell proliferation was quantified using KI67 (Abcam, USA) as a marker of active cell division. Histological score was assessed on the basis of macroscopic damage of intestines. The scoring criteria were defined as follows: 0, normal appearance; 1, localized hyperemia without ulceration; 2, ulceration absent, hyperemia or bowel wall thickening; 3, single-site ulceration with concomitant inflammation; 4, multifocal ulceration or inflammation (≥ 2 sites); 5, extensive lesions spanning 1 cm of colon length; 6–10, progressive scoring where each additional centimeter of affected tissue increased the score by 1 point.

## Statistical analysis
Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using unpaired t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were performed by means of one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s tests. A value of P < 0.05 denotes statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with significance determined as a P value < 0.05.

## Results

## The identification and characteristics of the novel circRNA named circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)
To identify new targets of RIII, we established a mouse model of acute radiation-induced injury. To establish this model, male C57BL/6 J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, weighing approximately 20 g, were treated with 14 Gy abdominal irradiation (ABI) or sham IR. The mice were sacrificed 6 h, 2 days and 3.5 days after IR. Jejunal crypt cells were isolated, and transcriptome sequence was performed at Shanghai Cloud-seq Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) (Fig.  A). Upon hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tissue slices, swelling, shortening, and other damage of the villi were found in mice 2 days and 3.5 days after 14 Gy ABI treatment as compared with the control group; in addition, a compensatory enlargement of the crypts was observed in tissues from mice 3.5 days after IR (Fig.  B). Quantitative analyses of crypt depth identified the most serious injury to the intestinal crypts at day 2 after IR (Supplementary Fig. S1A), and quantitative analyses of villi length identified the most serious injury to the intestinal villi at day 3.5 after IR (Supplementary Fig. S1B). This timing of damage is consistent with previous reports that have shown that the first 2 days following radiation could be classified into apoptotic phase with apoptosis occurring at a few hours after radiation [ ].
 Because of this timeframe of cellular injury, the profiles of differentially expressed circRNAs between sham-irradiated mice and ABI-treated mice were analyzed at day 2 following treatments. Compared with the sham IR group (NR), in the ABI treatment group (2 days), 32 circRNAs were found to be downregulated, and 7 circRNAs were found to be upregulated (cut-off > 2, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S1C). We aimed to find circRNAs that might be involved in RIII and primarily focused on those IR-sensitive circRNAs that downregulated after exposure to IR. Among the downregulated circRNAs, the most annotated circRNAs were from sequence data of brain or neural tissues, indicating their enrichment in the brain or nerves but not intestines. Therefore, the other unreported downregulated circRNAs were analyzed, and there were three circRNAs from mouse Dmbt1 pre-mRNA that are intestine-specific, suggesting that these derived circRNAs probably have intestinal specificity. We first focused on the circRNA with the lowest P value that was mapped to a sequence located on chromosome 7: 131,040,887–131046588+ (Fig.  C; Supplementary Table S1). The RNA sequence data were found to be consistent with the results of confirmatory RT–qPCR assays (Fig.  D). To verify whether this circRNA would respond to IR in intestinal tissue, we tested its expression level in mice jejunum at a different time after 14 Gy ABI. The RT–qPCR results showed a downward trend 6 h after IR compared with NR group and a recovery trend 3.5 days after IR compared with IR-2 day group (Supplementary Fig. S1D), suggesting this circRNA might be related to the early injury state of intestines after IR.
RNA sequence data showed that this circRNA contains 414 nucleotides and is derived from exons 3, 4, 5, and 6 of Dmbt1 (Fig.  E, top and lower left panel). Accordingly, we temporarily named the circRNA as circDmbt1(3,4,5,6). The Sanger sequencing data of the PCR amplification products of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) also permitted identification of a head-to-tail splicing site (Fig.  E, lower right panel). Since head-to-tail splicing occurs during genome rearrangement as well as in the generation of circRNAs, convergent primers and divergent primers were used to perform PCR from cDNA and gDNA isolated from mice jejunum tissues. PCR results, using β-actin as a negative control, showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) can only be amplified by the divergent primers from using cDNA as a template, but not from gDNA; this result rules out the possibility of this head-to-tail splicing arising from genome rearrangement (Fig.  F). We treated RNA from mouse intestinal tissues with RNase R and then performed a RT–qPCR analysis with Gapdh serving as a negative control. In this assay, RNase R readily degraded Dmbt1 mRNA, but it failed to decrease the concentration of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  G), supporting the existence of this sequence as a circRNA. To further confirm the stability of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), we treated circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing CT26 cells (Fig.  H) and circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing MODE-K cells (Supplementary Fig. S1E) with the transcriptional inhibitor actinomycin D (ActD). A subsequent RT–qPCR assay showed that the half-lives of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) are more than 10 h in CT26 cells and nearly 7.5 h in MODE-K cells at a concentration of 4 μg/mL ActD, respectively. While, the half-lives of Dmbt1(3,4,5,6) are 1.5 h in CT26 cells and less than 1 h in MODE-K cells at a concentration of 4 μg/mL ActD, indicating that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was more stable than its linear mRNA. Taken together, these results indicate that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is a bona fide circRNA.
To determine the distribution of expression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in vivo, we compared its expression level among various tissues of mice using RT–qPCR analyses. These assays indicated that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is primarily expressed in gastrointestinal tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1F). Furthermore, villi, crypts, and the remainder of the intestinal tissue from mice were isolated and subjected to extraction of total RNA. Quantification of RNA using RT–qPCR showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was mainly expressed in the crypts, where the intestinal stem cells reside (F g.  I). Similarly, RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization analyses of paraffin sections of intestinal tissues also suggested that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is mainly expressed in the intestinal crypts (Fig.  J). To pursue this result further, we used a mouse model in which expression of EGFP is driven by the stem cell-specific promoter of the Lgr5 gene (Lgr5-cre-EGFP). We isolated the intestinal epithelial cells of these mice and separated the Lgr5+ stem cells with EGFP from Lgr5− intestinal epithelial cells on the basis of the reported methods [ ] and performed RT–qPCR on total RNA extracted from these cells. Surprisingly, this quantitative analysis showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was mainly expressed in Lgr5+ intestinal epithelial stem cells (Fig.  K; Supplementary Fig. S1G) and downregulated in Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells 2 days after IR (Supplementary Fig. S1H), indicating that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) may participate in the injury and regeneration of intestinal epithelial cells after IR. Here we also analyzed the expression of linear mRNA Dmbt1 after radiation and found downregulation of Dmbt1 in mice jejuna 2 days after 14 Gy ABI compared with control group of mice (Supplementary Fig. S1I).

## CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) alleviates radiation-induced damage in intestinal epithelial cells
To determine whether circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) contributes to the regeneration of the intestinal epithelium after radiation-induced damage, we established circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) stably transfected mouse intestinal epithelial MODE-K cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S2A) and rat intestinal epithelial IEC-6 cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and knock down circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) via siRNAs in MODE-K cells (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Colony formation assays showed that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) enhanced the colony formation efficiency of MODE-K cells (Supplementary Fig. S2D) and IEC-6 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2E) after IR. EdU staining results showed that compared with control cells, the proliferation rates of MODE-K cells overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  A) were significantly increased after IR, while decreased in MODE-K cells knocking down of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  B), which is consistent with the results of CCK-8 assays in circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing IEC-6 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2F) and circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-knockdown MODE-K cells (Supplementary Fig. S2G). Similarly, overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) correlated with reduced irradiation-induced apoptosis both in MODE-K cells (Fig.  C; Supplementary Fig. S2H) and in IEC-6 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2I), while downregulated circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) increased irradiation-induced apoptosis (Fig.  D; Supplementary Fig. S2J). In comet assays, overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) reduced the Olive tail moment (Fig.  E), the tail length (Supplementary Fig. S2K), and percent tail DNA (Supplementary Fig. S2L) of MODE-K cells and IEC-6 cells (Fig. S2M–O) exposed to IR, while knockdown of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) increased these markers (Fig.  F; Fig. S2P, Q), suggesting the DNA protection of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) against irradiation-induced damage. Therefore, to further investigate the role of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in the DNA damage response or DNA repair, we performed immunofluorescence assays to detect the formation of the histone variant γ-H2AX, a marker of DNA damage and the DNA repair rate. Here, we found that compared with control cells, cells overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) displayed a lower number of γ-H2AX-positive cells at the same time point after IR both in MODE-K cells (Fig.  G) and IEC-6 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2R), indicating a lower degree of DNA damage. However, knockdown of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in MODE-K cells was found to increase the number of γ-H2AX foci present at multiple time points after IR (Fig.  H), indicating that the ablation of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) enhanced the production of irradiation-induced DNA breaks, especially DNA double strain breaks.
 

## Function of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) as a sponge of miR-125a-5p
Then we aimed to figure out the underlying mechanisms of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) on protecting intestinal epithelial cells from IR. It is known that most exonic circRNAs are localized in the cytoplasm, and the most frequently reported function of exonic circRNAs is their role as an miRNA sponge that binds and inhibits miRNAs, thus protecting target genes from miRNA-mediated degradation [ ]. Consistent with this role, our RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) results showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is primarily located in the cytoplasm (Fig.  A). Argonaute-2 (AGO2), an important component of the miRNA-induced silencing complex [ ], acts as a mediator between circRNAs and miRNAs [ ]. RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay showed that AGO2 binds to circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  B); absolute quantification by digital PCR also verified the results above (Supplementary Fig. S3A). RNA pulldown assays also identified AGO2 as the partner of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  C; Supplementary Fig. S3B). These immunoprecipitated proteins were further subjected to SDS–PAGE electrophoresis and mass spectrometric (MS) analysis, and the results showed that AGO2 is a circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-associated protein (Supplementary Fig. S3C), indicating circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) as a candidate miRNA sponge.
 We next predicted potential miRNA targets of the sponge activity of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) with the RegRNA 2.0 and circAtlas databases. The latter integrates multiple types of software, including miRanda, PITA, and TargetScan. A Venn plot identified two candidate miRNAs, mmu-miR-6955-3p and mmu-miR-6958-3p, within the intersections of the outputs of these three software programs (Supplementary Fig. S3D; Supplementary Table S4). No stable expression correlation was observed between circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) and mmu-miR-6955-3p or mmu-miR-6958-3p through RT–qPCR analysis of jejunum tissue 2 days after 14 Gy ABI (Supplementary Fig. S3E) or circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing CT26 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3F).
RegRNA 2.0 database predicted a single targeted miRNA of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6): mmu-miR-125a-5p (miR-125a-5p) (Fig.  D; Supplementary Fig. S3G). Analysis by RT–qPCR suggested a negative correlation between the expression of miR-125a-5p and circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig. S3H–J). An RNA pulldown assay using a circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) probe showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) can bind to miR-125-5p (Fig.  E). To determine whether circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) and miR-125a-5p interact directly, a negative control mimic RNA or an miR-125a-5p mimic was co-transfected with a wild-type circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) plasmid, a mutated circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) plasmid, or a negative-control plasmid, and dual luciferase assays were performed. The results indicated that there was a direct interaction between circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) and miR-125a-5p (Fig.  F).
To identify the role of miR-125a-5p in radiation-induced injury, MODE-K cells were transfected with NC mimics or miR-125a-5p mimics and were irradiated. Results of CCK-8 (Fig.  G) and EdU staining assays (Fig.  H) showed that miR-125a-5p inhibited the proliferation of MODE-K cells after IR. In addition, a flowcytometry analysis showed that overexpression of miR-125a-5p promotes apoptosis of MODE-K cells (Fig.  I). Comet assays also showed that overexpression of miR-125a-5p increased the Olive tail moment, tail length, and percentage of DNA in the tail in MODE-K cells after 8 Gy IR, suggesting that this miRNA leads to more severe DNA damage following IR (Fig.  J; Fig. S3K, L). Immunofluorescent determination of γ-H2AX expression showed that overexpression of miR-125a-5p aggravated radiation-induced double-stranded DNA breaks (Fig.  K).

## CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) alleviated intestinal epithelial cell injury after IR through the miR-125a-5p/STAT3 pathway
To investigate the functional target of miR-125a-5p, multiple miRNA target prediction databases were used, and 37 target genes were found to intersect in all four databases (Fig.  A) and are listed in Supplementary Table S5. Among these intersecting target genes was Stat3, the gene encoding signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), known to be involved in proliferation. As a member of the crucial STAT3 family, STAT3 fulfills its function in survival, cell proliferation, differentiation, immunoregulation, angiogenesis, and other aspects. STAT3 also plays a critical role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and protection through multiple mechanisms. Our team have reported that the activation of STAT3 signal pathway drives proliferation and repair of damaged epithelial cells during radiation-induced injury or infection [ ]. On the basis of its vital role in intestinal injury and repair, we focused on STAT3 in the following research. RT–qPCR and western blotting were performed to verify STAT3 as a target of miR-125a-5p (Fig. S4A, B, D), which is consistent with previous reports [ ]. Furthermore, western blot results also showed that transfection of miR-125a-5p mimics decreased phospho-STAT3 (S727) (Supplementary Fig. S4C), while transfection of miR-125a-5p inhibitor increased phospho-STAT3 (S727) (Supplementary Fig. S4E). To further determine whether miR-125a-5p directly targets Stat3, we constructed a Stat3 wild-type luciferase reporter vector (mStat3-WT) containing the binding sequences of miR-125a-5p and a mutant luciferase reporter vector (mStat3-MUT) in which the binding sequences were mutated, and a dual luciferase assay was performed. The results showed the direct binding between miR-125a-5p and Stat3 (Fig.  B, C). Then we knocked down Stat3 in MODE-K cells and found cell proliferation inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S4F), apoptosis promotion (Supplementary Fig. S4H), and increased level of DNA damage after IR (Fig. S4G, I–K).
 To confirm the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)/miR-125a-5p/STAT3 axis, we co-transfected an NC plasmid or a circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) overexpression plasmid with either NC mimics or miR-125a-5p mimics. EdU staining showed that compared with control cells, overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) increased cell proliferation after IR, while transfection of miR-125a-5p mimics reversed the proliferation-promoting tendency of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  D). Similarly, overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) reduced the rate of radiation-induced apoptosis, while transfection of miR-125a-5p mimics reversed the anti-apoptotic effect of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  E). Comet assays showed overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was found to decrease the Olive tail moment, tail length, and percent tail DNA in MODE-K cells after 8 Gy IR, while transfection of an miR-125a-5p mimic opposed the DNA damage protection effect of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  F; Fig. S4L, M). Immunofluorescent detection of γ-H2AX showed that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) reduced radiation-induced double-stranded DNA breaks, while transfection of miR-125a-5p mimics reversed the DNA damage protection effect of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) (Fig.  G).
In addition to acting as miRNA sponges, circRNAs can also bind to specific proteins and sequester them in appropriate subcellular locations, and they can modulate certain protein–protein and protein–RNA interactions [ ] The mass spectrometric (MS) analysis showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) may also bind with proteins that are involved in cell cycle, DNA damage response, and apoptosis. The relationship between them needs to be verified, and whether and how these proteins are involved in circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-mediated cell survival after IR are issues that require further study. Besides, some circRNAs have recently been shown to have protein-coding potential. The most explored translation mechanism is based on internal ribosome entry site (IRES), which requires both IRES and open reading frames (ORFs). IRES enables cap-independent translation initiation by allowing ribosomes to directly bind mRNA, maintaining critical protein synthesis during stress or viral infection. Besides, ORF contains a continuous coding sequence from start to stop codon, serving as the template for polypeptide synthesis and determining the amino acid sequence of the translated protein. IRES provides alternative initiation flexibility, while ORF ensures accurate coding information transfer, together supporting robust and adaptable protein translation. Bioinformatic analysis for predicting the presence of ORFs and IRES could be preliminarily used to assess the protein-binding ability of circRNA. Therefore, we explored whether circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) has an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) and open reading frame (ORF) with the IRES finder master tool (Supplementary Table S7) and ORF finder tool (Supplementary Table S8). The results showed that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) has four ORFs, but no IRES could be identified. Thus, the protein-coding potential of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was tentatively ruled out.

## Promotion of proliferation of intestinal organoids by circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) after IR
Our results demonstrated that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is mainly expressed in crypts, and in particular, Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells. Therefore, a mouse intestinal organoid model was established to identify the role of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in intestinal stem cells after IR. Microscope bright-field imaging revealed that control organoid cells amplified and budded beginning on the second day after seeding, while 4 Gy of IR reduced the budding rate and the perimeter and surface area of horizontal cross sections of organoids, and it induced the disintegration of organoids; these effects persisted for at least 3 days (Fig. S5A–D). RT–qPCR results suggested circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was downregulated after IR in intestinal organoids (Supplementary Fig. S5E), which was consistent with the results in mice (Fig.  D).
To further test whether circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is involved in the proliferation of intestinal stem cells after IR, we established a model in which circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was stably overexpressed in mouse intestinal organoid cells using a GFP-coupled lentiviral expression system. The overexpression efficiency was tested by fluorescence microscopy (Fig.  A) and RT–qPCR (Fig.  B). Bright-field microscopic images revealed that compared with control organoids, organoids overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) retained more budding potential and larger perimeters and areas after 4 Gy IR (Fig.  C). Similarly, cell titer-glo assay showed that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) increased the proliferation rate of mouse intestinal organoids after IR (Fig.  D). EdU staining (Figs.  E–F) and KI67 immunofluorescence assays (Figs.  G–H) also supported the promotion of organoid proliferation by overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) with or without IR results.
 

## Overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) alleviates irradiation-induced acute intestinal injury in vivo
Finally, to further confirm the intestinal protective function of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) after IR in vivo, a circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) overexpression mouse model was established using an adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) system. After tail vein injection of PBS, AAV-circNC, or AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), three groups of mice were weighted once daily for 2 weeks. After 2 weeks, the mice received 14 Gy ABI and were sacrificed at different time points for intestinal function and histological assessment. For early survival analysis, mice received 18 Gy ABI (Fig.  A). The level of overexpression was verified by RT–qPCR using total RNA from crypts isolated from mice of the three groups (Fig.  B). Consistent with in vitro experiments, miR-125a-5p was also downregulated (Fig.  C), and the phosphorylation of STAT3 (S727) was increased (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B) in intestines of AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-group mice compared with that of PBS and AAV-circNC-group mice. The average body weight of mice in the AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) group was higher than that of the other two groups (Supplementary Fig. S6C) 2 weeks after PBS or AAV injection. Early survival experiments indicated that the group overexpressing circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) had a significantly higher survival rate compared with the PBS and circNC groups after 18 Gy ABI (Fig.  D), with a slight body weight uptrend than the latter two groups (Supplementary Fig. S6D). Furthermore, 2 days after 14 Gy ABI, the average intestinal length AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) mice were longer than that of the AAV-circNC group (Fig.  E). As IR would be expected to destroy intestinal integrity, we further evaluated the effect of overexpression on intestinal permeability by intragastric administration of fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran average mol wt 4000 (FD4) followed by detection of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) fluorescence in the serum. The serum fluorescence of mice injected with AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was lower than that of the AAV-circNC group, indicating lower intestinal permeability in circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing mice (Fig.  F). The progression and severity of intestinal injury were evaluated macroscopically using a blinded histological scoring system adapted from established methodologies [ , ]. In the PBS + IR and AAV-circNC groups, histology revealed pathological changes in the mice, including mucosal injury, tissue structure loss, edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) delivery ameliorated these phenomena compared with the control group (Fig.  G, H). H&E staining of intestines showed that compared with mice injected with AAV-circNC, the intestines of mice injected with AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) were longer and presented with more integrated villi (Figs.  I, L). Immunohistochemical staining further indicated that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) increased the number of KI67+ proliferating cells (Fig.  J, M), OLFM4+ intestinal stem cells (Fig.  K, N), lysozyme+ Paneth cells (Supplementary Fig. S6E), and AB–PAS+ goblet cells (Supplementary Fig. S6F). Furthermore, TdT-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay showed overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) decreased intestinal epithelial apoptosis after IR (Figs.  O–P). γ-H2AX immunofluorescence showed that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in vivo reduces radiation-induced DNA double strand breaks (Fig. S6G, H). Taken together, these results indicated that overexpression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in vivo would alleviate RIII.
 

## Discussion
The GI system quickly forms proliferating and differentiating cells, making it one of the most sensitive organs to radiation. The GI system is disrupted by high levels of ionizing radiation, which damages the proliferating stem cells in the crypts and changes the intestine’s histology and physiology. Ionizing radiation exposure can acutely harm the gastrointestinal tract, potentially causing fatal damage. As of now, no medications exist to reduce radiation-induced intestinal harm [ ]. Amifostine, the only approved broad-spectrum small-molecule radioprotectant, mitigates radiation damage by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ]. However, its clinical utility is limited by severe side effects, including nausea, vomiting, fatigue, transient hypotension, and a narrow therapeutic window [ ]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel, precise therapeutic strategies, such as RNA therapy. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), a class of non-coding RNAs characterized by covalently closed structures lacking polyA tails, have emerged as promising candidates owing to their high tissue specificity, structural stability, and low immunogenicity. Nevertheless, the development of novel circularRNAs with high radiation-response efficiency and intestinal protective properties holds significant potential for prevention and mitigation of RIII.
In this study, we identified a previously uncharacterized radiation-sensitive circRNA, termed circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), through screening in a murine radiation-induced intestinal injury model. Its circular nature was confirmed via RNase R resistance, actinomycin D assays, and RT–PCR, demonstrating superior stability compared with its linear mRNA. CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) originates from Dmbt1 gene (deleted in malignant brain tumors-1), a putative tumor suppressor encoding a pattern recognition molecule implicated in immune defense, inflammation, and tissue regeneration [ – ]. Besides, the DMBT1 protein forms a protective mucosal layer, shields against pathogen invasion, modulates immune activity to reduce inflammation, and promotes epithelial regeneration [ ]. Manipulating circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) did not alter Dmbt1 expression according to the existing results (Supplementary Fig. S2A), and the linear Dmbt1 was more unstable than the former. Hence, we focused on circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) as a candidate for an intestinal radiation protector in the following explorations.
We validated the gastrointestine-specific expression of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), highlighting its safety and feasibility as a therapeutic target for intestinal injury [ , ]. In addition, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is highly expressed in crypts and Lgr5+ ISCs, which indicates its potential role in stem cell survival after IR. CircDmbt1(3,4,5,6) exhibited rapid transcriptional downregulation within 6 h post-irradiation after 14 Gy ABI, reaching minimal expression levels at 48 h—a timeframe coinciding with peak ISCs damage [ , ]. This dynamic expression profile, coupled with its intrinsic stability, suggests its potential as an early biomarker for radiation-induced intestinal injury. We first performed survival analysis using the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test. Mice treated with AAV-delivered circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) demonstrated significantly improved early survival rates compared with PBS and AAV-control groups. Although body weight trends favored the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) group, statistical significance was not achieved, likely due to the high lethality of 18 Gy abdominal irradiation (ABI), which caused rapid mortality in nonresistant groups, skewing intergroup comparisons. Notably, AAV-circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-treated mice exhibited marked weight recovery starting at day 5 post-irradiation, indicative of radioprotection. Further validation under 14 Gy ABI revealed preserved intestinal length, reduced FD4 intestinal permeability, and improved histopathological scores, collectively confirming circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)’s protective efficacy.
Furthermore, microscopic evaluation of intestinal damage, including proliferation, apoptosis, and DNA damage, was performed. Histological analysis (H&E staining) demonstrated elongated villi in the AAV- circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) group, while immunohistochemical profiling revealed increased populations of Lgr5+ stem cells, KI67+ proliferating cells, goblet cells, and Paneth cells compared with controls. Furthermore, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) delivery significantly mitigated radiation-induced epithelial apoptosis and DNA damage, particularly double-strand breaks. These findings, consistent with macroscopic observations, conclusively demonstrate that AAV- circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) enhances post-irradiation ISC survival and regeneration, thereby attenuating intestinal radiation injury.
In vitro cellular and intestinal organoid assays [ – ] corroborated its pro-survival effects. Subcellular localization indicated cytoplasmic enrichment, suggesting miRNA sponge activity. RNA pulldown and immunoprecipitation confirmed AGO2 binding, supporting miR-125a-5p sequestration, which was demonstrated by bioinformatic prediction and experiments (Fig.  ). Previous studies determined that miR-125a-5p induces apoptosis in mouse granulosa cells by targeting STAT3 [ , ]. Another report showed that overexpression of miR-125a-5p could inhibit hepatocyte proliferation by regulating STAT3 in vivo and in vitro [ ]. It was also reported that miR-125a-5p plays a role as a tumor suppressor in lung carcinoma cells by directly targeting STAT3 [ ]. The activation of STAT3 plays a pivotal role in cell proliferation after injury and it is activated through phosphorylation at either tyrosine 705 (Y705) or serine 727 (S727) residues [ ]. In this research we found that miR-125a-5p modulates the activation of STAT3 by inhibiting the phosphorylation at S727 residue, indicating that the circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) might alleviate the radiation-induced mitochondrial oxidative injury through STAT3 activation on the basis of phospho-S727 [ , ], which need further verification in our future studies.
This study verified the protective role of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) in RIII through circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)/miR-125a-5p/STAT3 axis, while other mechanism such as ROS scavenging and DNA damage and repair [ , ] need further exploration. On the basis of the fine intestinal protection function of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), optimized delivery systems such as lipid nanoparticles, engineered exosomes, or circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)-overexpressing organoids could enhance therapeutic efficacy in the future. In addition, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6)’s rapid radiation responsiveness positions it as a theranostic probe for early injury assessment, offering clinical potential to mitigate radiotherapy complications and nuclear emergencies.

## Conclusions
This study was the first to identify a previously unidentified circRNA, circDmbt1(3,4,5,6), as a proliferation-promoting and anti-apoptosis factor in the intestinal epithelium in the context of IR via the miR-125a-5p/STAT3 pathway. It seems that circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) is a marker of RIII and a passive repair factor, although how circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) was regulated needs further research. Exogenous upregulation of circDmbt1(3,4,5,6) could relieve intestinal damage levels. These findings provide a target for new therapeutic and preventative strategies for radiation-induced intestinal syndrome.
