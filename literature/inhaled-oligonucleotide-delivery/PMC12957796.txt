## Highlights
•Necroinflammation is involved in cadmium (Cd)-induced acute kidney injury.
•MLKL is an executioner in Cd-triggered necroinflammation.
•MLKL-mediated mitochondrial oxidative stress accounts for Cd-induced necroinflammation.
•Drp1 is a target of MLKL in Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress.
•MLKL-decreased antioxidant capacity aggravated Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress.

## Introduction
Acute kidney injury (AKI) induced by cadmium (Cd) manifests excessive inflammation with no effective therapy. Necroptosis, a novel manner of necrosis that regulates cell death, has been revealed important functions in the inflammation i.e., necro-inflammation.

## Objectives
Targeting necroptosis may be a potential therapeutic strategy for Cd-induced AKI, but the role of necroptosis in Cd-evoked nephrotoxicity is poorly understood. Thus, in vivo and in vitro studies were performed to clarify this issue.

## Methods and results
Data from RNA-sequencing analysis and its validation showed that necroptosis and NOD-Like Receptor Protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation were involved in Cd-induced AKI. Further investigations revealed that mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL)-dependent necroptosis drove Cd-induced inflammatory cascades in renal tubular epithelial cells, triggering necroinflammation. Significant mitochondrial dysfunction and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) overproduction due to MLKL activation were observed in this process. Notably, Cd-induced necroinflammation could be attenuated by mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, suggesting the key role of MLKL-dependent mtROS overproduction. Cd exposure triggered MLKL mitochondrial translocation-dependent dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) recruitment, driving excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and mtROS overproduction. And the effects were significantly ameliorated by the Drp1 inhibitor. In addition, mitochondrial calcium efflux due to activated MLKL-Drp1 axis impaired mtROS scavenging, further aggravating Cd-induced mtROS accumulation.

## Conclusion
These findings reveal that MLKL-Drp1 interaction is involved in Cd-induced necroinflammation via a novel mechanism to disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting MLKL-Drp1 axis in Cd-induced nephrotoxicity.

## Introduction
With the rapid industrialization and fast progression of society, the global environment is gradually deteriorating due to the discharge of a large number of environmental toxicants, which causes serious health problems [ ]. Cadmium (Cd), an environmental toxicant, has been widely spread in southwest China and causes accumulation and pollution in arable soils and grain crops [ ]. The flow of Cd in ecological systems is normally generated by fossil fuel combustion, industrial waste water discharge, application of phosphorus fertilizer, discarded batteries and cigarette smoking [ , ]. Dietary exposure to Cd and occupational inhalation can cause Cd deposition in diverse tissues including liver, kidney, brain and hematopoietic system [ , , , ]. Once absorbed, Cd exerts a long biological half-life (1 to 3 decades) due to its non-biodegradability [ , ]. The kidney is a primary target organ for Cd toxicity and renal tubular epithelial cells are the major target of Cd accumulation [ ]. Rates of various types of kidney injury characterized by inflammation are increasing globally[ , ]. Notably, previous evidence has shown that excessive inflammation due to Cd exposure is a key contributor to kidney diseases [ ]. Inflammation phenotype in Cd-induced kidney damage has been reported by previous study, but the detailed molecular mechanisms are unclear and need to be further clarified.
Necroptosis, a novel manner of necrosis that regulates cell death, can initiate inflammation creating an auto-amplification loop between cell death and inflammatory response (termed as necroinflammation)[ , ]. The classical signaling pathway is regulated by the activity of necrosome, which is consist of receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3). Upon caspase-8 inhibition, RIPK1 transitions from pro-survival signaling to forming the cytosolic necrosome with RIPK3. RIPK3 autophosphorylates and directly phosphorylates MLKL, inducing its conformational activation [ ]. Phosphorylated MLKL oligomerizes, translocates to membranes via its N-terminal domain, and disrupts membrane integrity, causing necrotic lysis. While RIPK1 often initiates RIPK3 activation, RIPK3 can independently phosphorylate MLKL in some cell types or stimuli [ ]. The programmed necrosis has the common morphological features with traditional necrosis, characterized by cell and organelle swelling, rupture of the membrane and leakage of intracellular contents [ ]. Recent studies have shown that Cd evoked necroptosis in various organs of different species, such as swine small intestine and chicken liver [ , ]. However, whether necroptosis is involved in Cd-triggered kidney damage is still not clear.
Baseline reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels are important for maintaining normal kidney structure and function, but overproduction or underproduction can disrupt the delicate balance required for healthy physiological processes. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that oxidative stress caused by excess ROS is a primary pathogenic cause of Cd-induced AKI [ ]. Notably, Cd-triggered ROS accumulation directly activates the NOD-Like Receptor Protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, amplifying inflammatory renal damage [ ]. In addition, intracellular ROS due to Cd exposure are thought to be unwanted by-products of oxidative metabolism, and mitochondrial dysfunction or damage can further increase ROS generation, creating a vicious cycle that cause cellular damage [ , ]. The mitochondria homeostasis not only regulates mitochondrial morphology and size, but also maintains their function by making content exchange between mitochondria [ , , ]. Excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, mediated by dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), results in abnormal mitochondrial fission, which is a common pathway that leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and resultant ROS overproduction [ , ]. Nevertheless, the potential effect of altered mitochondrial dynamics on Cd-evoked nephrotoxicity and its underlying molecular mechanisms remain unknown.
In this study, we firstly investigated whether necroptosis was involved in Cd-induced nephrotoxicity in vivo and in vitro. Subsequently, in vitro experiments were designed to clarify the role of MLKL-mediated necroptosis and its underlying mechanism in this process. Our findings shed light on the novel role of MLKL-Drp1 axis in regulating Cd-induced necroinflammation of rat proximal tubular cells via mtROS overproduction, which gives us an inspiration to prevent Cd-induced nephrotoxicity by targeting necroptosis pathway.

## Material and methods

## Reagents and antibodies
Reagents used were as follows: Cadmium chloride (CdCl2, >99 % purity; aladdin, C116342), CCK-8 Assay Kit (Beyotime, C0038), LDH Release Assay Kit (Beyotime, C007), Propidium Iodide (Beyotime, ST511), Hoechest 33,342 (Beyotime, C1022), MMP Assay Kit with JC-1 (Beyotime, C2006), ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0027), NADPH/NADP+ assay kit (Beyotime, S0179), GSH/GSSG assay kit (Beyotime, S0053), GSH-Px assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A005-1–2), SOD assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A001-3), CAT assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A007-1–1), urea nitrogen (Nanjing Jiancheng, C013-2–1), creatinine (Nanjing Jiancheng, C011-2–1), Mito-TEMPO (Glpbio, GC31682), Annexin V-FITC/PI Detection Kit (Vazyme, A211-01), Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778150), Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (Invitrogen, M7512), Fluo-4 AM (Invitrogen, F14201), Rhod-2 AM (Invitrogen, R1245MP), RNAiso Plus (Takara, 9109), Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, 04897030001), FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, 488252200).
Antibodies used were as follow: Caspase-8 (A0215), RIPK1 (A7414), RIPK3 (A5431), p-MLKL (AP0949), MLKL (A13451), HMGB1 (A2553), Caspase-1 (A0964), and IL-1β (A16288) were from ABclonal Technology Co.,Ltd. NLRP3 (19771–1-AP), β-actin (66009–1-Ig), LRP-2 (19700–1-AP), and GAPDH (10494–1-AP) were from Proteintech Group, Inc. Drp1 (ab184247) and Fis1 (ab156865) were from Abcam. COX Ⅳ (11967S), Caspase-3 (9664S), and Caspase-9 (9508) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. p-Drp1 (Ser616, AF8470) and p-Drp1 (Ser637, DF2980) were from Affinity Biosciences. Secondary antibodies used for immunoblotting analysis and immunofluorescence staining were from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

## Animal preparation and treatment
Toxicity tests were conducted on twelve Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 5-week-old, 100–110 g) provided by Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. After acclimation for 1 week at suitable conditions, rats were allocated to control (Cont) group and the Cd group. Based on our preliminary experimental results, the Cd group was performed using intraperitoneal injection of CdCl2 solution dissolved in saline (1 mg Cd2+/kg body weight) for 7 days, while the Cont group received equal amounts of saline ( A)[ ]. After anesthesia, fixed tissue paraffin-embedded sections were used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunofluorescence (IF) experiments. The activities of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT), and Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH-Px) were quantitatively analyzed using commercially available assay kits following manufacturer protocols. Blood samples were clotted and isolated at 2 000 g for 5 min. The serum concentrations of urea nitrogen and creatinine were detected harnessing specific kits. 

## Ethics statement
The animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Agricultural University (Approval no. SDAUA-2021–038).

## Determination of Cd concentration
Cadmium concentrations in blood and kidney tissues were quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Briefly, 1 g kidney tissue samples and 1 mL blood collected from each experimental group were digested with 5 mL concentrated nitric acid and 1 mL hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) using microwave-assisted digestion at 800 W. Then, samples were diluted with ultrapure water to appropriate concentrations for analysis. Cadmium levels were then determined using a PerkinElmer NexION 300 plasma mass spectrometry (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT).

## Cell culture and treatment
Rat renal tubular epithelial cells (NRK-52E) were from the American Type Culture Collection. NRK-52E cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2 and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). CdCl2 was dissolved in sterile ultrapure water, with untreated cells cultured in normal medium serving as the control for CdCl2 exposure. NRK-52E cells were treated with different levels (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) of CdCl2 for 12 or 24 h when confluence rate reached 70–80% in 6-well plates. A phase-contrast microscopy was used to assess the morphological changes in NRK-52E cells. Mito-TEMPO and Mdivi-1 were dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions, which were filtered, stored at −20°C, and diluted to working concentrations prior to use. To assess the protective role of Mdivi-1 (Drp1 inhibitor) on mitochondrial oxidative stress, cells were pretreated with 10 μM Mdivi-1 for 2 h and then incubated with Cd. Cells were pretreated with 10 μM Mito-TEMPO (MT) for 4 h before Cd exposure to remove excessive mitochondrial ROS (mtROS). For these treatments, control cells received medium containing 0.001% DMSO as a vehicle control.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was detected by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kits following a previously established protocol[ ]. Cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells per well. 10 μL of the CCK-8 solution was added into each well and cells were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance value at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 51119000). Six independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## LDH release assay
Cell death rate was performed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol[ ]. Briefly, the cells were inoculated on 96-well plates with a density of 104 cells/well. After Cd exposure, the cell supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plates and added to LDH detection reagent, then cell supernatant was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance was recorded at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Six independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Evaluation of cell necrosis
Cellular necrosis was evaluated with an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) double-staining assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol[ ]. After Cd treatment, about 1 × 105 cells were harvested and dyed with 10 µL mixture of Annexin V-FITC and PI solution (1:1) for 15 min in the dark at 4℃. The rates of cell apoptosis/necrosis were assessed by flow cytometry after double staining. Three independent experiments were done for biological replicates.

## Transmission electron microscopy
The ultrastructure of NRK-52E cells was photographed by Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, HT7800). Cells were collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes after treatment. Then cells were centrifuged to discard the supernatant. Deposited pellets were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h and then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, followed by graded ethanol dehydration and resin embedding. Ultrathin section was stained by 1% uranyl acetate and 0.1% lead citrate, and observed with the TEM at 80 kV.

## Assessment of mitochondrial morphology
Mitotracker stock solution (200 μM) was diluted to working solution (200 nM) using serum-free medium. Cells were cultured onto a confocal dish in a diameter of 15 mm. After treatment, the media was removed and added working solution, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 30 min. The staining solution was replaced and cells were observed using a laser confocal microscope equipped with × 100 oil objective.

## Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay
The changes of MMP were examined using 5,5,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1,3,3-tetraethylimidacarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) dye. NRK-52E cells were stained at 37°C for 30 min, and the details of the procedure are in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction. Fluorescence images of JC-1 aggregates (red) and monomers (green) were observed under fluorescence microscope. MMP was expressed as the fluorescence intensity ratio of aggregates to monomers. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Mitochondrial ROS assay
Cells were inoculated on 24-well plates for growth, and then exposed to Cd for 24 h when the confluence rate of 70–80%. Cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator at 37°C for 10 min to detect mtROS generation. The intensity of red fluorescence, which represents the accumulation of mtROS, was observed under excitation wavelength 510 nm and emission wavelength 580 nm. Three independent experiments were done for biological replicates.

## Assessment of intracellular NADPH/NADP+ ratio, GSH/GSSG ratio, and ATP levels
Cells were transfected with negative control (NC) or MLKL siRNA at 37°C for 6 h on 6-well plates, and then exposed to 10 µM Cd for 24 h. The glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) assay kit, reduced/oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH/NADP+) assay kit, and reduced/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) assay kit were utilized to detect the activity of GSH-Px and assess the ratios of NADPH/NADP+ and GSH/GSSG according to the manufacturers’ guidelines.
Cellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) level was detected following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, firefly luciferin requires energy from ATP to catalyze luciferin to produce fluorescence. Intracellular ATP concentration is proportional to the fluorescence value measured by a fluorescent microplate. A standard curve was established to calculate the cellular ATP levels, which was expressed as the fold of control cells. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Intracellular Ca2+ labeling
For the assessment of mitochondrial Ca2+ ([ Ca2+]mit) and cytosolic Ca2+ ([ Ca2+]c), cells were incubated with 0.5 mM TPEN for 10 min to eliminate perturbation of calcium measurement caused by presence of Cd ion. After incubation, cells were stained with 2 μM Fluo-4, AM or 5 μM Rhod-2, AM at 37°C for 30 min, both of which contained Pluronic F-127. Fluorescence images were acquired by a laser confocal microscope. Flow cytometry was also applied to evaluate the changes of [ Ca2+]mit and [ Ca2+]c. The fluorescence intensity was observed using FlowJo software. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Assessment of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening
mPTP opening was detected by the calcein/cobalt chloride (CoCl2) method as mentioned previously with appropriate modifications [ ]. Briefly, cells were incubated with 500 µL Calcein AM staining solution (1 μM) in presence of CoCl2 (1 mM) for 30 min in the dark after treatment. The staining solution was replaced and incubated again for 30 min. The fluorescence images were captured by fluorescence confocal microscopy at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission wavelength of 505 nm. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection
The designed siRNAs (listed in ) were from GenePharma Co. Ltd. The most suitable sequences of MLKL siRNA (si-MLKL-2) were screened by immunoblotting and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). Cells were plated on 6-well plates or 24-well plates close to 50% confluence and transfected with a NC or MLKL siRNA sequences mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX at a final concentration of 50 nM for 6 h. After siRNA transfection, cells were exposed to 10 µM Cd for another 24 h.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis
Cells were transfected with negative control (NC) or MLKL small interfering RNA at 37°C for 6 h in glass bottom dishes, and then exposed to 10 µM Cd for 24 h. Then cells were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde for 8 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 14 min. After being blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, cells were incubated with p-MLKL antibody (1:200 dilution) or Drp1 antibody (1:200 dilution) at 4°C overnight and incubated with Alexa Fluor ® 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at RT. After washing three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cells were incubated with Tom 20 antibody (1:200 dilution) or p-MLKL antibody (1:200 dilution) at 4°C for 12 h, then incubated with the Alexa Fluor ® 555-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Nuclei were marked by DAPI for 6 min, followed by washing with PBS again. Finally, images were visualized using the Leica TCS SPE confocal microscope. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## qRT-PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA following a previously described method[ ]. Total cDNA was amplified by qRT-PCR reactions in a LightCycler 96 system with SYBR Green I Master. All primers were designed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. and shown in . The relative levels of mRNA were normalized to house-keeping gene GAPDH, and data were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCT method. Six independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates. 

## Immunoblotting and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
The detailed method of western blot assay was performed as previously described [ , ]. Whole proteins of kidney tissues and cultured cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) for 1 h. After the protein concentrations were determined, equivalent samples were run on 10–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels, and proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After 1 h blocking with 5% skim milk at RT, PVDF membranes were immunostained with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) at 4℃ overnight. Subsequently, membranes were inducted with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000 dilution) for 50 min at RT. Bands were visualized on Chemidoc XRS by using the ECL Kit. Quantitative analysis of protein levels was measured by computer-assisted densitometric analysis and normalized for loading control. For co-immunoprecipitation assays, cell lysates were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation, followed by the addition of protein A/G agarose beads for an additional 2 h incubation. Antibody-bound complexes were collected by centrifugation at 5,000 g for 5 min at 4°C and subsequently washed three times. Finally, immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling at 95°C for 5 min and subsequently subjected to immunoblot analysis. Three independent experiments were carried out for biological replicates.

## Molecular docking
Molecular docking assay was performed by the HADDOCK 2.4 webtool (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4). The structure files of MLKL and Drp1 were downloaded from the Uniprot website (https://www.uniprot.org) as the input molecules. The docking result was visualized using the Pymol software (https://pymol.org), and the interface area between two molecules was calculated and shown in the stick model.

## Transcriptome sequencing
The RNA isolated from rat kidneys tissues of Cont and Cd groups was used for RNA-Seq. Then samples were purified by the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). The cDNA size and total level were qualified and quantified on Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA-seq was carried out using a NextSeq500 with a min depth of 10 M reads. Differential expression and visualization were yielded by Cufflinks (version v2.2.1) and R package cummeRbund (version v2.22.0).

## Statistical analysis
Data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA and LSD post hoc tests were used for data conforming to normal distribution, and SPSS 26 software package was used for statistical analysis. For data with non-normal distribution, Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to compare the differences. Graphs were established using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software), and P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

## Results

## Necroinflammation is involved in Cd-induced acute kidney injury
Male rats were injected intraperitoneally with CdCl2 to clarify the effect of Cd on renal damage. The analysis revealed a statistically significant elevation of Cd concentrations in the Cd-exposed group compared to the control group, with values measuring 17.59 ± 2.27 μg/L in blood and 2.25 ± 0.26 μg/g in kidney tissues. Next, serum biomarkers of kidney damage were detected in vivo. As shown in B and C, evident increases in levels of creatinine (CRE) and urea nitrogen (BUN) were observed in Cd group compared to control rats. Western blot analysis results also showed that the protein expressions of kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) were evidently up-regulated in Cd-exposed rats ( D), suggesting that Cd exposure induced acute kidney damage. Meanwhile, the damage of Cd-exposed kidney tissues was noticed in histological H&E staining results. The clarity of tubular structure was decreased, and the lumen was dilated. Some cells were subject to nuclear pyknosis and degeneration and cytoplasmic vacuolar degeneration, and some necrotic cells of the renal tubules were degenerated and detached ( E). Next, RNA-seq was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes during Cd-induced acute kidney damage ( ). KEGG pathway enrichment analyses suggested that inflammatory response and necroptosis process were involved in this process. Moreover, a set of genes coding inflammatory response including IL-1β, IL-18, and NLRP3 was consecutively upregulated and key indicators of necrosis (RIPK3 and MLKL) were also significantly enriched during Cd exposure ( F–H). Western blot analysis was performed to validate the RNA-seq data, and the data demonstrated that Cd induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and initiated RIPK3/MLKL-mediated necroptosis, triggering necroinflammation in acute kidney damage ( I–J). Moreover, necroptosis was further validated by increased expression of phospho-MLKL using immunofluorescence in vivo ( K). In addition, western blot analysis suggested that the expressions of cleaved caspase-8, cleaved caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-9 were down-regulated in Cd groups compared with Cont groups, indicating that apoptotic pathways may not play a predominant role in Cd-induced AKI ( L). All together, these data suggested that necroinflammation is the key molecular event in Cd-induced nephrotoxicity.

## MLKL is an executioner of Cd-induced cytotoxicity in NRK-52E cells
Next, NRK-52E cells were exposed to 0, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 μM CdCl2 for 12 or 24 h, then cell viability and LDH release were firstly detected. The results showed a dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity induced by Cd, and significant cytotoxicity occurred after 10 μM Cd for 12 h ( A). Meanwhile, release of LDH demonstrated that exposure to 10 μM Cd for 24 h caused the rupture of cell membranes ( B). Combined with the results of cell viability and LDH release, NRK-52E cells were exposed to 0, 5, 10 and 20 μM Cd for 24 h to design the next experiments. As shown in C, a number of cells shed from petri dishes in Cd treatment groups, and Cd induced a dose-dependent increase of cell death. Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry results demonstrated that low-dose Cd (5 μM) caused a significant increase in apoptotic cells, while high-dose Cd (10 or 20 μM) increased the percentage of necrotic cells ( D). The results of E further showed that 10 or 20 μM Cd treatment significantly increased PI uptake by NRK-52E cells. Notably, these findings were also supported by TEM analysis of Cd-treated cell morphology, and typical characteristics of necrotic cells were observed, such as cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture (multiplication symbol), nuclear fragmentation (asterisk), and loss of mitochondrial cristae (black arrows) after 10 or 20 μM Cd treatment for 24 h ( F). Therefore, these results demonstrated that necrosis was induced in renal tubular epithelial cells during 10 or 20 µM Cd treatment. 
Necroptosis serves as a novel mode of programmed cell death, and caspase-8 is a key switch for apoptosis and necroptosis [ ]. Thus, the expressions of caspase-8 were firstly examined in cells. Results showed that the expressions of caspase-8 were significantly increased after 5 μM Cd treatment, while decreased at 10 or 20 μM Cd treatment, suggesting that apoptosis was initiated by low-dose Cd and converted to necroptosis at high dose. In this regard, although the protein level of RIPK1 did not change considerably, RIPK3, another key protein in necroptosis pathway, dose-dependent increased after Cd treatment ( G). Meanwhile, MLKL acts as an executioner of necroptosis, and the phosphorylation levels of MLKL were increased with the enhancement of dose ( H). In addition, the expressions of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-9 were also examined in cells. Results showed that the expressions of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-9 were significantly increased after 5 μM Cd treatment, while slightly decreased at 10 or 20 μM Cd treatment ( I), confirming that apoptosis was initiated by low-dose Cd (5 μM) and converted to necroptosis at high dose (10 or 20 μM). To investigate the role of MLKL in Cd-induced cell death, three siRNA sequences of MLKL were next synthesized and applied ( ) to knockdown the level of MLKL in NRK-52E cells. Immunoblotting and qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the levels of MLKL were decreased after knockdown by three siRNA primers compared to control group. Moreover, transfection with siRNA targeting MLKL (si-2) markedly attenuated the Cd-induced activation of MLKL ( J–K). Noticeably, data showed that decreased cell viability and increased LDH release due to Cd exposure were reversed by transfection with siRNA targeting MLKL ( L–M). Collectively, these data showed that Cd-induced necroptosis activated by RIPK3/MLKL axis is required for Cd-triggered cytotoxicity in NRK-52E cells. 

## MLKL activation is responsible for Cd-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome cascade in vitro
Inflammatory response is considered as a major pathological event in Cd-triggered nephrotoxicity. NLRP3 and high-mobility group box protein-1 (HMGB1) are pivotal upstream mediators of inflammatory cytokines and play key roles in the progression of inflammation. Firstly, NLRP3 inflammasome pathway was examined, and the results demonstrated that Cd treatment induced dose-dependent up-regulation of NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, caspase-1, pro-interleukin-1β (pro-IL-1β) and IL-1β expressions ( A). Similarly, the protein level of HMGB1 markedly increased with exposure dose of Cd ( B). In addition, the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6 and IL-18 were also detected. As shown in C, the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6 and IL-18 were markedly increased with exposure dose of Cd. Thus, these results suggested that NLRP3 inflammasome cascade is triggered in Cd-induced nephrotoxicity. 
Necroptosis has been involved in different inflammatory diseases [ ]. However, whether MLKL knockdown can mitigate Cd-induced inflammatory response needs to be further investigated. To answer this question, western blot and qRT-PCR assays were performed to detect inflammation-related indicators in Cd-triggered NRK-52E cells after transfection with MLKL siRNA. Results showed that MLKL knockdown not only inhibited activation of NLPR3 inflammasome, but also reduced the protein level of HMGB1 and the mRNA levels of tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), IL-2, IL-6, IL-1β and IL-18 in Cd-treated NRK-52E cells ( D–F), suggesting that MLKL knockdown can alleviate Cd-evoked NLRP3 inflammasome cascade. Overall, these results suggested that MLKL activation is responsible for Cd-evoked NLRP3 inflammasome cascade in NRK-52E cells.

## MLKL-mediated mitochondrial oxidative stress accounts for Cd-induced necroinflammation
As shown in and F, decreased ATP levels in vivo and mitochondrial structural damage in vitro was observed. Interestingly, Cd exposure promoted the translocation of p-MLKL (green) on mitochondria (Red) in a time-dependent manner ( A), indicating the potential role of MLKL in Cd-evoked mitochondrial dysfunction. To further investigate the role of MLKL in this process, relevant mitochondrial indicators were examined. In this regard, MLKL knockdown prevented cellular ATP decline and mitochondrial depolarization caused by Cd in vitro ( B and C). Further, as evidenced by fluorescence microscopy, the red fluorescence detected by MitoSOX Red staining was obviously enhanced by Cd treatment, which was weakened in the cells transfected with MLKL siRNA ( D). Thus, data showed that activated MLKL contributed to Cd-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and mtROS overproduction. To clarify the relationship between MLKL-dependent mtROS accumulation and necroinflammation trigged by Cd, Mito-TEMPO (MT) was used to remove Cd-induced excessive mtROS. Data showed that decreased cell viability and increased LDH release due to Cd exposure were reversed by MT treatment ( E and F). Furthermore, the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6 and IL-18 were also down-regulated by MT, compared with Cd group ( G). Correspondingly, Cd-activated NLRP3 inflammasome pathway was partially suppressed by MT treatment ( H). Based on the above results, it can be concluded that excessive mtROS production due to MLKL activation aggravates Cd-triggered necroinflammation in NRK-52E cells. 

## Drp1 is a target of MLKL in Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress
Based on the results of cross-enrichment of genes associated with the regulation of necroptotic process and the mitochondrial fission, Drp1 may be a key target of MLKL-induced mitochondrial dysfunction ( A). Moreover, data of RNA sequencing showed that the expression of Drp1 was significantly increased by Cd exposure ( B). Increased protein expression levels of Drp1 and fission-1 (Fis1) induced by Cd were inhibited by MLKL knockdown compared with the cells transfected with scrambled siRNA ( C). While the protein level of p-Drp1 (Ser637) did not change considerably, the protein level of p-Drp1 (Ser616), another regulatory site governing mitochondrial fission, was significantly increased after Cd treatment. And increased protein expression levels of p-Drp1 (Ser616) induced by Cd were inhibited by MLKL knockdown ( D). In this regard, Drp1 may be implicated in MLKL-mediated mitochondrial oxidation stress during Cd exposure. Meanwhile, molecular docking was applied to assess the binding capacity involving MLKL and Drp1. The protein–protein docking of MLKL-Drp1 complex showed that residues of MLKL protein e.g. Trp-195, Lys197, and Arg-357 were interacting with residues Ser-371, Glu-369, and Glu723 of Drp1 protein, respectively. Moreover, the docked complex MLKL-Drp1 had attained high binding energy of −156.6  kcal/mol. Cd activates MLKL, exposing its pseudokinase and 4HB domains. MLKL may recruit Drp1 via residues like Arg-357 and Trp-195, forming a stable complex. This interaction hyperactivates Drp1′s fission activity, leading to excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, ROS overproduction, and necroptotic cell death ( E). To further validate the relationship between Drp1 and p-MLKL, analyses of IF and Co-IP assays were performed to show that the binding between MLKL and Drp1 was enhanced in Cd-treated NRK-52E cells. Data showed that mitochondrial MLKL promoted mitochondrial fission by enhancing the interaction with Drp1 ( F and G). Therefore, these results suggested that Drp1 acts as a target protein interacting with MLKL to trigger Cd-induced mitochondrial redox imbalance. 

## Inhibition of Drp1 can alleviate Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidation stress
To explore whether Cd-induced mitochondrial dysfunction is ascribed to Drp1-mediated mitochondrial dynamic imbalance, NRK-52E cells were pretreated with Mdivi-1, a specific Drp1 inhibitor. The results showed that Mdivi-1 pretreatment reversed the high expressions of Drp1 and Fis1 ( A). And increased protein expression levels of p-Drp1 (Ser616) induced by Cd were inhibited by Mdivi-1 pretreatment ( B). Moreover, mitochondrial morphological staining also demonstrated that Mdivi-1 pretreatment could restore mitochondrial dynamic homeostasis, as shown by the reestablishment of mitochondrial network connections ( C). Next, the levels of MMP and ATP were measured to explore the role of Mdivi-1 on Cd-evoked mitochondrial dysfunction. As expected, Mdivi-1 pretreatment alleviated Cd-caused the loss of MMP and the decrease of ATP levels ( D and E). Meanwhile, Cd-induced excessive mtROS generation was also attenuated by Mdivi-1 pretreatment ( F). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of Drp1 significantly attenuated Cd-induced inflammation response ( ). To sum up, these results offered us solid evidences that the Drp1-mediated mitochondrial dynamic imbalance facilitates mitochondrial oxidative stress in Cd-treated NRK-52E cells. 

## MLKL-decreased antioxidant capacity aggravated Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress
The balance of oxidant/antioxidant system is necessary for the normal homeostasis of cell. An up-regulation in pro-oxidation levels or a down-regulation in antioxidant capacity can cause excessive ROS production. The present investigation revealed a diminished antioxidant capacity in vivo, as demonstrated by decreased activities of SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px enzymes, with GSH-Px exhibiting the most pronounced alteration among these biomarkers ( ). To further investigate whether decreased antioxidant capacity also accounts for Cd-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress, GSH-Px activity and the ratios of GSH/GSSG and NADPH/NADP+ were determined in vitro. As shown in A–C, GSH-Px activity and the ratios of GSH/GSSG and NADPH/NADP+ were reduced in the Cd-treated group. Moreover, MLKL knockdown prevented Cd-reduced GSH-Px activity and the ratios of GSH/GSSG and NADPH/NADP+, suggesting that Cd-induced MLKL activation disrupted the balance of oxidant/antioxidant system. Activation of NADPH oxidase is dependent on the levels of calcium ion (Ca2+). Then, Ca2+ fluorescence probes were applied to evaluate the concentration of [ Ca2+]mit (Rhod-2, AM) and [ Ca2+]c (Fluo-4, FluO-4). Data of flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy demonstrated that Cd exposure caused a down-regulation in [Ca2+]mit and an up-regulation in [ Ca2+]c, which was rescued by MLKL knockdown, suggesting that Cd-induced MLKL activation disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, promoting mitochondrial calcium outflow ( D and E). Mitochondrial permeability transition pore plays an important role in regulating the process of Ca entering and leaving mitochondria. Thereby, in this study, mPTP was detected by fluorescence microscopy. Data showed that Cd exposure decreased green fluorescence signal (Calcein), while MLKL knockdown abolished Cd-decreased green fluorescence signal, suggesting that Cd-induced MLKL activation disrupted mPTP to release [ Ca2+]mit and decreased antioxidant capacity ( F). These results demonstrated that Cd-induced MLKL activation is involved in [ Ca2+]mit efflux by inducing mPTP opening, resulting in the imbalance of oxidant/antioxidant system and resultant ROS accumulation in NRK-52E cells. 

## Discussion
Environmental exposure to Cd is a major risk factor for global health. Nephrotoxicity is considered to be one of the most common health problems of Cd exposure and an early warning of greater damage. Here, data demonstrated that necroinflammation was responsible for Cd-triggered nephrotoxicity and clarified that activated MLKL-Drp1 axis-mediated mitochondrial oxidative stress was involved in Cd-induced necroinflammation. Our findings first demonstrate that necroinflammation due to activated MLKL-Drp1 axis acts as an important pathogenetic mechanism in Cd-induced kidney damage.
Programmed cell death mechanisms, particularly apoptosis and necroptosis, play distinct yet interconnected roles in kidney disease. Apoptosis occurs through an ordered proteolytic cascade where initiator caspases (e.g., caspase-8) activate executioner caspases (e.g., caspase-3), leading to cellular death. Conversely, necroptosis represents a caspase-independent, pro-inflammatory mode of programmed cell death, involving sequential activation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and ultimately MLKL to execute cell death. Emerging evidence has shown that necroptosis serves as an alternative cell death pathway when apoptosis is inhibited [ ]. Studies have demonstrated a key role for necroptosis in different renal pathologies, especially in the field of inflammatory diseases [ , ]. Cd exposure can cause necroptosis in various organs of different species, such as chicken liver, duck ovaries and pig epididymis [ , , ], but current insights into the association between Cd exposure and necroptosis in kidney tissues are still limited. In the present study, our results unraveled that necroptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation were involved in Cd-induced AKI. Further studies showed that Cd initiated RIPK3/MLKL-mediated cell necroptosis under the inhibition of caspase-8, confirming that necrosis instead of apoptosis was induced in Cd-evoked nephrotoxicity. However, the exclusive use of male rats in this study represents a limitation, as it precludes the assessment of potential sex-based differences in the experimental outcomes. Sex can sometimes significantly influence physiological responses and disease mechanisms. While this study focused on male rats to reduce variability, future investigations should incorporate both sexes to evaluate the generalizability of findings. Emerging evidence showed that Cd exposure at lower concentrations (2.5–5 μM CdCl2) for 12 or 24 h predominantly induced apoptosis and autophagy in renal tubular cells[ ]. Similarly, our data observed that apoptosis was initiated by low-dose Cd (5 μM) and converted to necroptosis at high dose (10 or 20 μM), which is also consistent with previous report in human renal cells[ ]. Notably, Cd-induced nephrotoxicity could be reversed by MLKL siRNA-mediated knockdown, an effect was consolidated by an earlier finding [ ], favoring the significant role of MLKL-mediated necroptosis in Cd-evoked nephrotoxicity.
Excessive inflammation response is a major pathological event in Cd-triggered nephrotoxicity [ ]. Our studies confirmed that Cd activated NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and increased the levels of inflammatory cytokines, clarifying that MLKL activation was responsible for Cd-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome cascade in vitro. In this regard, previous studies have shown that Cd-evoked ferroptosis contributed to inflammation response in pancreatic β-cells [ ], while the exact mechanisms of necroptosis in Cd-triggered inflammation response remain unclear. Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a major driver of inflammation response. Notably, the targeting of mitochondria and the resultant disruption of redox homeostasis have been identified as pivotal mechanisms underlying heavy metal toxicity, particularly in Cd exposure [ , , ]. Data in this study showed that Cd-triggered necroinflammation was significantly mitigated by mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MT, suggesting the key role of mtROS overproduction in Cd-induced necroinflammation. Thus, further research is required to completely clarify the complex mechanisms between activated MLKL and mtROS accumulation.
Emerging evidence has shown the dysregulation of mitochondrial Ca2+ cycling in the pathologic process of necroptosis, and necrotic signals can destroy mitochondrial quality control system, contributing to reduced ATP synthesis and increased ROS accumulation [ , ]. Here, data showed that Cd disrupted mitochondrial dynamic and induced more mtROS release in NRK 52E cells. Recent studies have suggested that MLKL can recruit not only cell membrane but also different organelle membranes, including lysosomes, mitochondria, and nuclei[ , , ]. Thus, we hypothesized that MLKL-mediated necroptosis might be connected with Cd-evoked mitochondrial damage. Here, data observed that MLKL was first activated and translocated to mitochondria during Cd exposure in NRK 52E cells. Knockdown of MLKL restored mitochondrial dysfunction and resultant mtROS accumulation, suggesting that activated MLKL was involved in mtROS overproduction. Drp1 is a highly relevant molecular for mitochondrial dynamics and researchers found Drp1-mediated mitochondrial dynamics homeostasis was associated with necroptosis [ , , ]. However, the mechanistic relationship between Drp1-driven mitochondrial fission and MLKL/p-MLKL remains unexplored, representing a critical gap in understanding organelle-specific necroptotic regulation. In the present study, through cross-enrichment of genes associated with the regulation of necroptotic process and the mitochondrial fission, Drp1 may be a key target of MLKL in Cd-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Protein-protein docking of structure models further showed high binding capacity of MLKL and Drp1 protein. To further validate the regulatory mechanism of MLKL in this process, proteins interacting with MLKL by Co-IP assay and immunofluorescence colocalization assay were detected. Data showed that the binding between MLKL and Drp1 was enhanced in Cd-treated NRK-52E cells. Specifically, MLKL activation promotes Drp1 translocation to mitochondrial membranes, where site-specific phosphorylation triggers its activation to execute mitochondrial fission. In this regard, targeted mutagenesis of MLKL’s putative Drp1-binding domains will be required in future studies to obtain structural and functional proof of this interaction mechanism. Although necroptosis execution primarily involves RIPK3-mediated MLKL activation, accumulating evidence indicates that RIPK3 possesses non-necroptotic functions. Previous evidence found that RIPK3 can drive Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission and subsequent oxidative stress generation in acute lung injury models[ ]. This bifurcation of RIPK3 signaling creates a compelling rationale to investigate whether MLKL collaborates with RIPK3 to regulates Drp1-mediated mitochondrial dynamics. Pharmacological inhibition of Drp1 with Mdivi-1 effectively attenuated Cd-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and resultant mtROS accumulation in NRK-52E cells. While Mdivi-1′s primary action involves suppressing Drp1 GTPase activity, we observed concomitant reduction in total Drp1 protein levels, consistent with reports in other models[ , ]. This suggests additional regulatory mechanisms may operate, potentially involving proteasomal degradation of Drp1 following prolonged fission inhibition or transcriptional downregulation through ROS-sensitive pathways. Although the exact mechanism requires further investigation, these coordinated effects collectively contribute to improved mitochondrial homeostasis under Cd exposure. In summary, our data revealed that Cd-activated MLKL evoked mitochondrial oxidative stress by strengthening the interaction with Drp1.
Besides, the balance of oxidant/antioxidant system is necessary for the normal homeostasis of cell [ ]. An up-regulation in pro-oxidation levels or a down-regulation in antioxidant capacity can cause ROS overproduction. In the present study, Cd-induced MLKL activation decreased GSH-Px activity and the ratios of GSH/GSSG and NADPH/NADP+, contributing to the imbalance of oxidant/antioxidant system. There are ample evidences that the levels of mitochondrial Ca2+ acted as key regulators in maintaining NADP+ regeneration into NADPH and preventing excessive ROS accumulation [ ]. In the present study, the results have fully demonstrated that Cd-induced MLKL activation contributes to mitochondrial Ca2+ efflux by inducing mPTP opening, resulting in the balance of oxidant/antioxidant system and resultant mtROS accumulation. Thus, the structural disruption leads to mtROS overproduction through two complementary pathways. The impaired mitochondrial morphology directly enhanced mtROS generation. Moreover, MLKL activation also induced mPTP opening, which disrupted Ca2+ homeostasis through uncontrolled mitochondrial Ca2+ efflux.

## Conclusion
Collectively, our study reveals that the MLKL/Drp1 axis drives Cd-induced necroinflammation in renal tubular cells via mitochondrial oxidative stress. These findings offer us a promising strategy for the prevention and treatment of Cd-induced kidney damage via targeting MLKL/Drp1 axis. Limitations include unvalidated MLKL-Drp1 signaling in vivo, necessitating further investigation using pharmacological inhibitors or genetic models in Cd-induced kidney damage. Moreover, to address species-specific conservation, we will conduct complementary experiments employing both CRISPR-mediated MLKL knockout and pharmacological inhibition in human renal proximal tubule (HK-2) cells to guide future translational research.
