## SUMMARY
Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) plays a key role in maintaining vascular endothelial homeostasis. Here, we show that blood flows determine activation and inactivation of VEGFR2 through selective cysteine modifications. VEGFR2 activation is regulated by reversible oxidation at Cys1206 residue. H2O2-mediated VEGFR2 oxidation is induced by oscillatory flow in vascular endothelial cells through the induction of NADPH oxidase-4 expression. In contrast, laminar flow induces the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase and results in the S-nitrosylation of VEGFR2 at Cys1206, which counteracts the oxidative inactivation. The shear stress model study reveals that disturbed blood flow operated by partial ligation in the carotid arteries induces endothelial damage and intimal hyperplasia in control mice but not in knock-in mice harboring the oxidation-resistant mutant (C1206S) of VEGFR2. Thus, our findings reveal that flow-dependent redox regulation of the VEGFR2 kinase is critical for the structural and functional integrity of the arterial endothelium.

## In brief
In this study, Kang et al. show that the activity of VEGFR2, a key receptor tyrosine kinase that maintains endothelial integrity in adult arterial vessels, is reversibly regulated by S-nitrosylation and S-oxidation in response to blood flow patterns. This provides a molecular mechanism underlying disturbed flow-induced arterial occlusion.

## INTRODUCTION
Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) is one of the endothelial receptor tyrosine kinases essential for the maintenance and function of vascular endothelial cells (ECs). , Upon VEGF-A binding, VEGFR2 undergoes tyrosine autophosphorylation, which in turn leads to the activation of mitogenic and angiogenic signaling pathways in the ECs. Since endothelial VEGFR2 was proposed to use reactive oxygen species (ROS) as signal mediators, several studies have shown that nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase (NOX) or lipoxygenase functions as a ROS producer in the VEGF-induced signaling. – However, a molecular mechanism underlying the role of intracellular H2O2 in VEGF signaling has not been clarified.
We have been studying the role of the 2-cysteine peroxiredoxin (2-Cys Prx) family enzymes in receptor-mediated signal transduction. , The 2-Cys Prx enzymes are the natural thiol peroxidases that reduce H2O2 to water by coupling with thioredoxin (Trx) and Trx reductase (TR) redox system. A decade ago, we reported that depletion of Prx type II enzyme (one of the 2-Cys Prx family enzymes) results in the loss of VEGFR2 kinase activity in various types of ECs. Although we demonstrated that Prx II depletion in ECs causes the loss of VEGFR2 activity through the oxidative Cys modification, a physiological significance of the redox regulation of the VEGFR2 complex in the endothelium was not characterized.
In the arterial vasculature, the flow stress of blood stream challenges the vascular endothelium with a frictional force known as fluid shear stress. The prominent endothelial responses to shear stress include nitric oxide (NO) and ROS production. , Thus, we investigated the endothelial VEGFR2 activation in a shear stress-mediated redox environment. Specifically, we characterized the reversible oxidation-reduction cycle of VEGFR2 in association with the Trx system. We proceeded to study the redox cycle of VEGFR2 in both cellular and mouse shear stress models. The results show that the laminar and disturbed shear stresses induce the expression of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) and NOX4, respectively, which differentially regulate the VEGFR2 activation by inducing the nitrosative and oxidative modifications of the critical cysteine residue. This study, therefore, establishes a novel redox mechanism of VEGFR2 activation crucial for maintaining endothelial integrity.

## RESULTS

## VEGFR2 kinase activity is regulated by the oxidation-reduction cycle
We have previously reported that VEGFR2 tyrosine kinase is inactivated by the H2O2-dependent disulfide formation between a pair of cysteine residues (Cys1199 and Cys1206 in mouse sequence) unique among VEGFR gene family members in the cytoplasmic tail of VEGFR2. We subsequently examined the reversibility of VEGFR2 oxidation in ECs. In the human aortic EC (HAEC), treatment with increasing concentrations of H2O2 gradually reduced the activation of VEGFR2 and its downstream effector ERK, as evidenced by the reduced level of activation-dependent phosphorylation ( ). Since VEGF-A can bind to the oxidized VEGFR2, we further addressed whether H2O2 affects the VEGFR2 internalization through immunofluorescence (IF) staining and surface biotin labeling. Both IF staining and surface-labeling experiments showed that VEGF stimulation induces the internalization of VEGFR2, while H2O2 pretreatment blocks the internalization of VEGFR2 WT, but not the C1206S mutant ( ). Thus, we confirm that H2O2-mediated disulfide formation inhibits VEGFR2 autophosphorylation as well as the receptor internalization, thereby preventing activation of downstream molecules such as ERK. We also tested the oxidation sensitivity of VEGFR2 by treating HAECs with submicromolar concentrations of H2O2. Surprisingly, VEGFR2 activation was impaired by H2O2 treatment at concentrations as low as 0.1 μM ( ). In contrast, a millimolar concentration of H2O2 (e.g., 1 mM) rather induced the ligand-independent activation of VEGFR2. However, this condition did not lead to the ERK activation and caused the hyperoxidation of 2-Cys Prx (Prx-SO2/3 form), suggesting the stringent oxidative stress. Moreover, the VEGFR2 auto-phosphorylation induced by 1 mM H2O2 was turned out to be associated with the c-Src kinase among various stress-responsive kinases ( ). Therefore, for technical feasibility and consistency, we used 100 μM H2O2 in the following experiments, as this concentration does not cause the hyperoxidation of 2-Cys Prx, which is a mild stress tolerable to ECs.
 When the HAECs and human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) were treated with 100 μM H2O2 and transferred to fresh culture media for recovery, the VEGFR2 activation was gradually restored in a recovery time-dependent manner ( ). Similar results were obtained in HEK293 cells expressing ectopically introduced full-length mouse VEGFR2 ( ), which indicated that the oxidative-reduction cycle of VEGFR2 is preserved regardless of cell types. Furthermore, the electrophoretic mobility assay under non-reducing conditions clearly showed that the disulfide linkage in the oxidized VEGFR2 was cleaved by a reduction reaction during the recovery times ( ).

## VEGFR2 oxidation is reversed by the Trx system
As VEGFR2 activation was recovered after H2O2 removal, we hypothesized that a natural redox system reduces VEGFR2. Mammalian cells contain two highly conserved redox systems mediated by Trx and glutathione (GSH). To identify the bona fide system, we prepared recombinant proteins for the cytoplasmic domain (CD) of VEGFR2 containing bipartite kinase domains and the C-terminal tail ( ). Subsequent in vitro kinase assays showed that recombinant mouse VEGFR2-CD has auto-phosphorylating kinase activity, which is inactivated by H2O2 treatment in a time-dependent manner ( ). Consistently, the kinase activity of recombinant human VEGFR2-CD was also inactivated by increasing H2O2 levels ( ). We next performed the oxidation-reduction reaction in vitro using VEGFR2-CD as the substrate. The kinase activity of VEGFR2-CD (lane 2), inhibited by H2O2 treatment, was effectively restored by the Trx/TR system but not by the GSH/GR system ( ). Interestingly, the level of kinase activity restored was even higher than that of untreated VEGFR2-CD (lane 1), indicating that recombinant VEGFR2-CD had been slightly oxidized during preparation. The Trx/TR system also restored the kinase activity of inactivated VEGFR2 isolated from H2O2-treated HUVECs ( ). Like the recombinant proteins, reactivation extent of the cellular VEGFR2 by the Trx/TR system was higher than that from the untreated cells (lane 2), which implicates the intrinsic redox sensitivity of cellular VEGFR2. Next, we examined the involvement of the endogenous Trx/TR system in the reactivation of VEGFR2 in HUVECs. Treatment of a TR inhibitor auranofin as well as knockdown of Trx1 expression both impaired the recovery of VEGFR2 activation after H2O2 removal ( and ). These results demonstrate that the Trx/TR system specifically regulates the redox state of VEGFR2.
 

## Oscillatory flow pattern induces VEGFR2 inactivation through NOX4 induction
Since ECs consist of the first-line barrier responding to the blood flow in the arterial walls, the steady laminar flow and disturbed flow protects and damages the endothelial layer, respectively. – Hence, we examined how shear stresses affect the redox state of VEGFR2. Differential flow patterns were set on the EC culture using a cone-and-plate device. , As reported, laminar and disturbed flow patterns guided directional and scrambled EC alignments, respectively ( ). Biomarker assay showed that the laminar flow pattern (designated laminar shear stress [LSS]) induced the expression of anti-inflammatory genes ( ). In contrast, both static culture and disturbed flow patterns (designated oscillatory shear stress [OSS]) triggered the expression of pro-inflammatory genes ( ). Using this in vitro system, we found that LSS preserved the VEGF-induced VEGFR2 activation, which was severely impaired under OSS ( ). Subsequently, we examined the expression of NOX enzymes as potential candidates that can cause the oxidative inactivation of VEGFR2. The qPCR data revealed that NOX4 expression was highly dominant among isoforms in the static culture of HUVECs and HAECs ( ). Thus, we evaluated the effect of shear stress on NOX4 expression. Strikingly, LSS almost completely blocked NOX4 expression, whereas OSS strongly induced NOX4 expression compared with that in the static culture ( ). In contrast, NOX2 expression was flow pattern independent. Then, we examined whether the increase in expression of NOX4 leads to increase of intracellular ROS using an oxidation-sensitive fluorescence probe. Indeed, OSS strongly induced increases in intracellular ROS in HAEC, which was blocked by treatment with a pan-NOX inhibitor (APX-115) ( ). In addition, we examined the protein level of NOX isoforms using the specific antibodies against human NOX2 and NOX4 that have been verified in HUVECs depleted of each NOX isoform ( ). It was noted that the knockdown of NOX2 boosted the NOX4 expression and vice versa. Western blot analysis also showed that NOX4 expression was augmented by OSS but suppressed by LSS ( ). Once again, the NOX2 level remained unchanged, regardless of shear stress. Last, the kinase assay and immunoblot analysis showed that VEGFR2 activation impaired by OSS was completely restored by NOX4 depletion ( ). Thus, we conclude that flow-dependent NOX4 expression is a key factor in the oxidative inactivation of VEGFR2.
 

## NOX4 colocalizes with VEGFR2 in the lipid raft
As NOX4 and VEGFR2 are integral membrane proteins, we presumed that their co-localization in the membrane microdomain is necessary for forming a localized redox integration. Thus, we evaluated their co-localization through subcellular fractionation of detergent-resistant lipid rafts. Immunoblot analysis showed that the low-density lipid raft fractions, including caveolae, contained both VEGFR2 and NOX4 ( ). We also found that the same lipid raft fractions contain Prx II, the thiol peroxidase that protects VEGFR2 by eliminating the adjacent H2O2, and eNOS. Furthermore, immunostaining experiments clearly showed that NOX4 colocalizes with both caveolin-1 and VEGFR2 in the plasma membrane ( ). Proximity ligation assays further supported that NOX4 interacts with caveolin-1 and VEGFR2 in proximity ( ). Collectively, the data demonstrated that microscopic image analyses confirmed that VEGFR2 and NOX4 interact each other within the lipid raft microdomain.
 Next, we assessed the functional interactions of the three redox components colocalized in ECs. First, depletion of Prx II induced an increase in intracellular H2O2, which was abolished by the co-depletion of NOX4, but not NOX2 ( ). Second, Prx II depletion inhibited VEGF-dependent VEGFR2 activation ( , lane 3), which was completely restored by co-depletion of NOX4 ( , lane 5). It should be noted that augmentation of NOX2 expression by NOX4 depletion had no effect on VEGRF2 activation. Therefore, the results demonstrate that the VEGFR2 function is impaired by NOX4-derived H2O2 specifically and in an irreversible manner in the absence of its redox defender Prx II.

## Oxidation and S-nitrosylation reciprocally regulate VEGFR2 activation
2-Cys Prx enzymes are self-inactivated by hyperoxidation during continuous reaction cycles. , As Prx II was colocalized with NOX4, we addressed whether shear stress triggers the hyperoxidation of Prx II. Indeed, the hyperoxidation of 2-Cys Prxs was induced by OSS, not by LSS, in HAECs ( ). This event was well correlated with the NOX4 induction. In contrast, we found that the protein expression of eNOS, which was found to be present in the lipid raft microdomain, was markedly induced by LSS, not OSS ( ). When the LSS-conditioned HAECs were exposed to OSS, all the events were reversed: NOX4 induction was resumed, the 2-Cys Prxs were hyperoxidized, and eNOS expression was repressed.
 Since the eNOS-produced NO plays an anti-atherogenic role in ECs through S-nitrosylation of the signaling proteins, we tested whether VEGFR2 can be a novel NO target in the ECs. To this end, HAECs were treated with an NO donor S-nitroso GSH (GSNO), and S-nitrosylation was evaluated by biotin-switch assay. The biotin-switch assay combined with immunoprecipitation demonstrated that VEGFR2 was indeed S-nitrosylated by GSNO treatment ( ). Subsequent knock-down experiments confirmed that LSS-induced eNOS mediated the laminar flow-dependent S-nitrosylation of VEGFR2 ( ). Furthermore, the knock-down of LSS-induced eNOS expression abolished the VEGF-dependent VEGFR2 activation in the H2O2-treated HAECs compared with that in untreated cells ( ). Together, these results demonstrate that LSS-induced eNOS is responsible for VEGFR2 nitrosylation and plays a key role in constitutively protecting VEGFR2 from oxidative inactivation.
We then designed an experiment to explore the comparative effects of H2O2 and NO on VEGFR2 activation. HAECs were sequentially treated with S-nitroso-N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP) and H2O2. Surprisingly, SNAP pretreatment preserved VEGFR2 activation from H2O2-dependent inhibition ( , lane 4). Conversely, when H2O2 treatment preceded the SNAP challenge, VEGFR2 remained inactive ( , lane 5). In addition, the electrophoretic mobility assay under non-reducing condition showed that SNAP-treated VEGFR2 did not undergo the disulfide formation upon H2O2 treatment ( ). These results indicate that the S-nitrosylation of the receptor blocks formation of the intramolecular disulfide bond.
To define the flow dependency of different redox status of VEGFR2, we performed a modified biotin-switch assay using DTT- or ascorbate-treated lysates. The modified assay revealed that VEGFR2 isolated from OSS-conditioned ECs was biotinylated only in DTT-treated lysates ( , left). In contrast, VEGFR2 isolated from LSS-conditioned ECs was biotinylated in both DTT- and ascorbate-treated lysates ( , right). Thus, this finding suggested that OSS and LSS induce cysteine S-oxidation and S-nitrosylation of VEGFR2, respectively. To visualize the flow-dependent cysteine modifications, we attempted differential labeling of cysteinyl thiols with Cy3 and Cy5 fluorophore-conjugated maleimide reagents. Labeling with Cy3 after ascorbate reduction marks the S-nitrosylated cysteine, and labeling with Cy5 following tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine reduction marks the oxidized cysteine ( , schematic illustration). Sequential cysteine labeling demonstrated that VEGFR2 was S-nitrosylated (Cy3 label) in LSS-conditioned ECs, whereas it was oxidized (Cy5 label) in OSS-conditioned ECs ( ). To identify the S-nitrosylated cysteine residue, we examined the S-nitrosylation state of VEGFR2 using Cys-to-Ser (CS) mutants. Surprisingly, only the C1206S mutant among five CS mutants was defective in S-nitrosylation ( ), indicating that an identical Cys1206 residue is used for both oxidation and nitrosylation, even though entirely distinct enzymatic systems mediate the processes. Collectively, biochemical analyses demonstrate that VEGFR2 kinase activity can be reciprocally regulated by oxidative and nitrosative cysteine modifications.

## Oxidative inactivation of VEGFR2 causes arterial thickening under disturbed blood flow
Next, we demonstrated the physiological significance of the flow-sensitive cysteine modification on VEGFR2 in vivo. To achieve the goal, we utilized a mouse shear stress model in which partial ligation induces disturbed blood flow in the carotid artery. , Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated that partial ligation of the left carotid artery (LCA) effectively leads to reduced and disturbed blood flow compared with the untreated contralateral right carotid artery (RCA) with steady laminar blood flow ( ). We then isolated intimal ECs from carotid arteries and examined the inflammatory gene expression ( ). Indeed, expression of pro-inflammatory genes, such as adhesion molecules (ICAM1 and VCAM1), was induced in the partially ligated LCA, whereas expression of anti-inflammatory genes (KLF2 and eNOS) was suppressed ( ). These results indicate that partial ligation triggers disturbed flow-induced inflammation in the carotid arteries. Importantly, NOX4 expression was markedly upregulated in the intimal layers of partially ligated LCA while NOX2 expression was flow independent ( ), which is consistent with results from the in vitro cone-and-plate culture study.
 To evaluate whether VEGFR2 oxidation is involved in the disturbed flow-induced vascular remodeling in vivo, we generated a knock-in (KI) mouse line harboring the Ser substitution of Cys1206 residue on the VEGFR2 gene ( ). When LCAs were partially ligated, medial thickness of arterial wall was much less in VEGFR2C1206S KI mice compared with that in wild-type (VEGFR2WT) littermate mice ( ). In parallel, the untreated contralateral RCA showed normal arterial vessel in both WT and KI mice. To examine endothelial integrity in the WT and KI mice, we performed en face immunostaining analyses on the lumen surface of carotid arteries. The IF staining for an endothelial marker, CD31, showed the unidirectional alignment of the ECs in untreated contralateral RCAs with laminar flow from both WT and KI mice ( ). However, the endothelial layers in the partially ligated LCAs with disturbed flow were scrambled in WT mice but were still well aligned in KI mice ( ). This result indicates that oxidation-resistant VEGFR2C1206S mutant maintains endothelial integrity under disturbed flow. More important, the level of VEGFR2 auto-phosphorylation was equally high in the contralateral RCAs of both WT and KI mice, which means that VEGFR2 is constitutively in an activated state under laminar flow, regardless of S-nitrosylation. In contrast, the VEGFR2 auto-phosphorylation was greatly reduced in the partially ligated LCA of WT mice, not KI mice. In addition, it was noted that the disturbed flow did not affect the level of VEGFR2 (see RCA versus ligated LCA in WT mice). Hence, the result demonstrates that VEGFR2C1206S mutant was sufficiently activated and maintained endothelial integrity under disturbed blood flow.
Since autocrine activation of VEGFR2 is critical for endothelial maintenance in adult arterial vessels, we examined the EC viability in the partially ligated LCAs. The IF staining for caspase-3 activation using anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175) antibody revealed massive endothelial apoptosis in the partially ligated LCAs of WT mice, but not KI mice ( ). This indicates that oxidative inactivation of VEGFR2 by disturbed flow triggers EC death. To further check endothelial dysfunction in vivo, we performed an Evans blue extravasation assay in the partially ligated LCA. In WT mice, vigorous leakage of Evans blue dye was observed in the partially ligated LCAs, not in the untreated contralateral RCAs. In contrast, the dye leakage was greatly reduced in the partially ligated LCAs of KI mice ( ). Overall, the in vivo data concluded that defective VEGFR2 activation under disturbed blood flow is a critical cause of EC death and dysfunction.

## DISCUSSION
Atherosclerosis is the major arterial disease that often leads to myocardial and cerebral infarctions. Typically, this arterial disease progresses for many years until the arterial vessel thickens and ruptures. Of note, endothelial damage due to disturbed blood flow in the vessel curvature or branchpoint is one of the main reasons for the initiation of atherosclerosis. , Numerous studies have proven that laminar blood flow has an antiatherogenic effect, whereas disturbed blood flow acts as the source of OSS that damages the endothelium and induces inflammation in arterial walls. Complex signaling pathways, including those involved in embryonic vessel development, respond to shear stress in the mature arterial vasculature. Among signaling events involved, the interaction of VEGFR2 and ROS has been implicated in shear stress-mediated endothelial impairment. , However, the molecular mechanism underlying the redox regulation of the VEGFR2 complex has not been fully characterized to date. In this study, we show that the redox state of the critical cysteine residue (Cys1206) proximal to the kinase domain of endothelial VEGFR2 is flow sensitive and is involved in the regulation of VEGFR2 activity. On the one hand, the disturbed flow induces the expression of NOX4 as the major H2O2-producing enzyme in both cell lines and mouse models. Moreover, NOX4 and VEGFR2 are colocalized in the caveolar microdomain of the plasma membrane. Since NOX4 is a constitutively active integral membrane enzyme, , it is anticipated that NOX4-producing H2O2 causes oxidative inactivation of VEGFR2 in ECs exposed to disturbed flow, as evidenced by the knockdown experiment in the study. We also confirmed the existence of Prx II acting as a redox defender of VEGFR2 in the plasma membrane microdomain. However, its co-localization in the caveolar microdomain seems no longer effective under disturbed flow. The conceivable reason is because the NOX4-producing H2O2 concomitantly resulted in the inactivation of Prx II, as seen in the OSS-conditioned ECs. To explore the effect of VEGFR2 oxidation under disturbed flow in vivo, we generated VEGFR2C1206S KI mutant mice. Surprisingly, the oxidation-resistant C1206 KI mutation provides anti-atherogenic effect by preserving VEGFR2 activity under disturbed flow. On the other hand, laminar flow induces the expression of eNOS, and its product NO preemptively modifies the same H2O2-sensing C1206 cysteine on VEGFR2. We show that the SNO group blocks the H2O2-mediated formation of the disulfide bond on VEGFR2 in cellular system. However, the effect of C1206S KI mutation related to S-nitrosylation could not be explored at this time in a mouse model because laminar and disturbed flows are distinctive in vivo. Nonetheless, the immunostaining of CD31 and VEGFR autophosphorylation indicated that VEGFR2 is constitutively in an activated state under laminar flow, regardless of S-nitrosylation. Thus, it is likely that S-nitrosylation of VEGFR2 under laminar flow may provide preemptive protective action against other forms of oxidative stress. Taken together, the differential enzyme systems constitute the flow-responsive redox signaling axes targeting VEGFR2 in the arterial endothelium: eNOS in laminar flow versus NOX4 in disturbed flow ( ).
In addition to the NOX4-mediated VEGFR2 inactivation in the disturbed flow-challenged ECs, we also observed that NOX4 depletion in cultured HAECs causes a change in cell morphology to a shrunken shape ( ). This in part suggests a potential role of NOX4-derived H2O2 in the regulation of cellular morphology or adhesion beyond VEGFR2, which is a newly proposed role of ROS to be explored further down the road.
Although eNOS induction by LSS has been known for long, the S-nitrosylation of endothelial VEGFR2 has not been reported or characterized so far. In this study, we show for the first time that LSS-induced eNOS leads to S-nitrosylation of VEGFR2. More surprisingly, we found that the S-nitrosylated site is the same Cys1206 residue as the one modified by the disturbed flow-induced oxidation. Thus, it is of interest if the physiological role of S-nitrosylation related to laminar flow can be explored in VEGFR2C1206S KI mutant mice. Overall, our findings reveal a full set of redox signaling and enzymatic mechanisms by which blood flow patterns determine the endothelial homeostasis through the activation and inactivation of VEGFR2.

## Limitations of the study
In this study, we generated the VEGFR2C1206S KI mouse to investigate the effect of VEGFR2 S-oxidation under disturbed blood flow. Since a disturbed flow pattern is easily established by partial ligation operation in carotid artery, we demonstrate that VEGFR2 S-oxidation is crucial for endothelial damage under disturbed flow. In addition, we see that the C1206S mutation has no effect on normal carotid endothelium with laminar blood flow. This result suggests that S-nitrosylation is not essential for autocrine VEGFR2 activation under laminar flow. Because of the limitation of the partial ligation model, the anti-atherogenic effect of the S-nitrosylation cannot be investigated in KI mice. However, it will be interesting to study the role of VEGFR2 S-nitrosylation in atherosclerosis by using the KI mouse in combination with the ApoE knockout mouse.

## EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT PARTICIPANTS DETAILS

## VEGFR2C1206S knock-in mouse model
All animal experiments were performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. The animal study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Asan Institute for Life Sciences, Republic of Korea.
VEGFR2C1206S knock-in (KI) mice were generated using the conventional Cpf1-mediated gene targeting method, as described. For the generation of crRNAs and Cpf1 mRNAs, mouse genomic DNA sequences were analyzed and target sequences were selected using a web tool, Benchling (https://benchling.com/). Donor DNA and single-strand oligodeoxynucleotides (158 bp) were designed as follows: 5′-AATGTCAGAGACACTGAGCATGGAAGAGGATTCTGGACTCTCCCTGCCTACCTCACCTGTTTCCTGTATGGAAGAA GAAGAGGTCTCCGACCCCAAATTCCATTATGACAACACAGCAGGAATCAGGTACTGGAGATAGCCAGCATCCCCTCCAAGGC -3′, where the target locus is underlined.
Zygotes were obtained after mating B6N males and superovulated females and injected with a mixture of 50 ng/μL AsCpf1 mRNA, 100 ng/μL crRNA, 1 mM Scr7, and 200 ng/μL single-strand oligonucleotide. The injected zygotes were then transferred into the uterus of pseudo-pregnant ICR females. Genotyping of founder mice was performed through the direct sequencing of PCR products (405 bp) obtained from tail DNA. The PCR primers used were as follows: 5′- CCTTGAAGGACAGCAGCCAT −3′ (WT forward-L) and 5′- AACATGGGCCACAGATGGG −3′ (WT reverse-L).
WT and KI pups were identified by genomic PCR using a 4-primer mixture with an additional primer set as follows: 5′-GAGGAAG AGGAAGTGTGCGAC-3’ (WT forward-S) and 5′-GTCGGAGACCTCTTCTTCTTC-3’ (KI reverse), both of which produce different size of PCR products (256 bp and 167 bp in WT and KI, respectively).

## Mouse partial carotid ligation model
The mouse study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ewha Womans University, Republic of Korea, and conforms to the ARRIVE guidelines. The 6-week-old male VEGFR2C1206S knock-in and wild-type control mice were bred in a pathogen-free animal facility. The partial ligation of the LCA was performed as described. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane inhalation. The neck was disinfected with 70% ethanol and epilated. After the ventral midline incision (4–5 mm) was made, the LCA was exposed by blunt dissection. Three of four caudal branches of the LCA (external carotid, internal carotid, and occipital artery) were ligated with the 6–0 silk suture, whereas the superior thyroid artery was left intact. The contralateral RCA was not ligated and used as an internal control. On the first day and one week after ligation, blood flow velocity was measured using the VEVO 2100 high-resolution microimaging ultrasound system with the MS400 transducer (VisualSonics).

## Cell lines and cell culture condition
HAECs and HUVECs were purchased from Clonetics-Bio Whittaker (Venders, Belgium) and grown at 37◦C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 in Endothelial Basal Medium (EBM-2) SingleQuotes with full supplements (Clonetics-BioWhittaker; Cat no. CC-4176). The cells in passage number 5 were mainly used for the study.

## METHOD DETAILS

## Recombinant VEGFR2 production
The cytoplasmic domain of mouse VEGFR2 (amino acids 788–1345) was PCR amplified using forward and reverse primers (5′-CATGCCATGGATAAGCGGGCCAATGAAGG-3′ and 5′-CCGGCGAGTTAAACAGGAGGTGAGCGCAG-3′) and subcloned into a Bac-to-Bac HT vector (pFastBac-HT A) at the NcoI and XhoI sites. Recombinant baculovirus was produced using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus system (Invitrogen). Briefly, the subcloned pFastBac plasmid was transformed into E. coli DH10Bac to generate Bacmid DNA. Sf9 insect cells were transfected with the recombinant Bacmid DNA to produce the recombinant baculovirus. Viral titer was determined by a plaque assay. High Five Sf9 cells (Invitrogen) were infected with recombinant baculovirus (1 × 108 pfu/mL) to produce the recombinant protein. The cells were harvested after 48 h of infection at 27◦C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 3 mL lysis buffer (40 mM Tris [pH7.4], 110 mM NaCl, 2.2 mM KCl, 20% glycerol, 3 mM TCEP, 1% DDM, 1:1,000 Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 160 μg/mL benzamidine, 2.5 μg/mL leupeptin, and 20 mM imidazole) per mL of cell pellet and lysed by rotating for 1 h at 4◦C. Nickel-NTA resin was added to the supernatant (500 μL/25 mL) and the suspension was shaken for 2 h at 4◦C. The resin was first washed in a rinse buffer containing 40 mM Tris [pH7.4], 110 mM NaCl, 2.2 mM KCl, 20% glycerol, 3 mM TCEP, 0.1% DDM, 1:1,000 Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 160 μg/mL benzamidine, 2.5 μg/mL leupeptin, and 20 mM imidazole, followed by washes in the secondary rinse buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH7.4], 110 mM NaCl, 2.2 mM KCl, 1:1,000 Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 160 μg/mL benzamidine, and 2.5 μg/mL leupeptin) containing 20 mM imidazole. The protein was then eluted with the elution buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH7.4], 110 mM NaCl, 2.2 mM KCl, 1:100 Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 160 μg/mL benzamidine, and 2.5 μg/mL leupeptin) containing 400 mM imidazole. The protein was further purified by size exclusion chromatography. The peak fractions were collected, concentrated in the storage buffer containing 20% glycerol, and stored at − 80◦C before use.

## Retrovirus production
The pQ retroviral plasmid containing the full-length mouse VEGFR2 (mVEGFR2) cDNA with the hemagglutinin (HA) tandem epitope tag in-frame at the carboxyl terminus is described. Briefly, 293T cells (7 × 106 cells) were seeded on a 15-cm culture dish and cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were then co-transfected with 20 μg each of gag/pol, VSV-G, and pQ-mVEGFR2 plasmids using the calcium phosphate method. Approximately 6 h after transfection, the serum-free culture medium was changed for viral collection. The retrovirus-containing supernatant was collected, filtered using the 0.45-μm syringe filter to remove cell debris, and stored at − 80◦C until use.

## Real-time quantitative PCR
Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DN was synthesized using MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega). For real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), the cDNAs were diluted 10 times and mixed with the SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and primers (10 μM each). The PCR reactions were performed in triplicate for 40 cycles (95◦C for 15 s and 60◦C for 1 min) using the 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). After completing the reaction cycles, the melting point was checked for specificity. PCR data were quantified as 2—ΔΔCt values relative to that of endogenous β-actin. The gene-specific primers for human and mouse NOX isoforms were obtained from Qiagen: hNOX1 (QT00025585), hNOX2 (QT01751869), hNOX3(QT00044737), hNOX4 (QT00057498), hNOX5 (QT00021924), hDUOX1 (QT00038346), hDUOX2 (QT00012236), mNOX2 (QT00139797), and mNOX4 (QT00126042). The β-actin primers were (sense) 5′-TGGATCAGCAAGCAGGAGTAT-3′ and (antisense) 5′-GCATTTGCGGTGGCAGAT-3′. All other primers are listed in Supplementary Table I.
To isolate total RNA from carotid arteries, the lumens of the left and right carotid arteries obtained from eight-week-old mice were flushed with 200 μL of QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen, USA). The eluent and remaining tissue were used for intimal and medial RNA isolation, respectively, using the miRNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, USA).

## Shear stress studies
HAECs were grown to confluent monolayers in 60-mm culture dishes and subsequently adapted to the arterial level of shear stresses for 48 h in a cone-and-plate viscometer with a few modifications. LSS and OSS were generated through unidirectional flow at 25 dyn/cm2 and by bidirectionally rotating a cone at ± 5 dyn/cm2, respectively.

## siRNA and transfection
The siRNA sequences for knocking down human genes were as follows: 5′-CGCUUGUCUGAGGAUUACGUU-3′ for PrxII, 5′-CCAACGUGAUAUUCCUUGATT-3′ for Trx1, 5′-GAACAGGCUAAGGAUACUATT-3′ for NOX2, and 5′-AACGAAGGGGUUAAACACCUCTT-3′ for NOX4. The siRNA for the firefly luciferase gene was purchased from Dharmacon. Unless otherwise stated, the HAECs or HUVECs were transfected with the siRNA duplexes for 24 h using lipofectamine RNAi MAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, the cells were serum-starved for an additional 18 h in media containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum before VEGF stimulation.

## Immunoprecipitation, in vitro kinase assay, and immunoblot analysis
The cells were lysed in TEB buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 5 mM Na3VO4, 5 mM NaF, 1 mM AEBSF, aprotinin (5 μg/mL), and leupeptin (5 μg/mL). After centrifugation, the protein content of the clear supernatants was quantified using the Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad). For immunoprecipitation, the supernatant (120 μg protein) was pre-cleaned with 10 μL of protein-A/G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads (Amersham Biosciences) for 1 h and incubated with 2 μg of primary antibodies overnight. The immune complexes were precipitated by incubating with 20 μL of protein-A/G Sepharose beads for 3 h at 4◦C. The beads were washed three times with 1 mL of TEB buffer and subjected to either the in vitro kinase assay or immunoblotting.
For the in vitro kinase assay, the precipitated immunocomplexes were incubated with 30 μL of kinase buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 20 mM MgCl2, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 200 μM Na3VO4) containing 5 μCi of γ−32[P]-ATP for 10 min at 30◦C. The reaction was stopped by adding 5 × SDS sample buffer. The samples were boiled and separated by SDS-PAGE. The gel was vacuum-dried and subjected to autoradiography.
For immunoblotting, the cell lysates or immunoprecipitates were mixed with 5× SDS sample buffer and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes for immunoblotting. For reprobing with another antibody, the membranes were stripped by shaking in 67 mM Tris (pH 6.7), 2% SDS, and 100 mM 2-mercaptoethanol for 1 h at 60◦C and washed three times with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.2% Tween 20.
Rabbit polyclonal antisera against Prx II and hyperoxidized 2-Cys Prx (Prx-SO2/3) were produced and affinity-purified using agarose beads conjugated with an antigenic peptide or recombinant protein.

## Surface biotin labeling
To assess the VEGFR2 internalization, HUVECs starved overnight in EBM2 with 0.5% FBS and incubated with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin (0.5 mg/mL, Thermo Scientific) at 4◦C for 1 h and rinsed with 50 mM glycine in PBS to quench the unbound biotin. Cells were briefly rinsed with EBM2 media and separated to two parts. A part of the cells was harvested and used to determine total biotinylated cell surface protein. The other part of the cells were pretreated with or without H2O2 (100 μM) for 5 min and stimulated with VEGF (25 ng/mL) for additional 10 min in 37◦C incubator. Cells were then rinsed and incubated for 20 min on ice with the membrane-nonpermeable reducing agent GSH (50 mM) in a buffer containing 75 mM NaCl, 75 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% BSA to remove biotin moiety from surface proteins. Finally, cells were rinsed twice with PBS containing iodoacetamide (5 mg/mL) to quench remaining GSH and lysed in a lysis buffer. Cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with 30 μL of streptavidin agarose beads (Millipore) at 4◦C overnight. The beads were rinsed and subjected to immunoblot analysis.

## Intracellular ROS assay
HAECs were rinsed with Endothelial Basal Medium without phenol red (EBM, Lonza) and immediately incubated with 5 μM 5,6-chloromethyl-2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (CM-H2DCFDA) (Molecular Probe) for 5 min at 37◦C. The cells were briefly rinsed with EBM, and fluorescence images were taken using the Axiovert 200 Basic standard microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Relative DCF fluorescence was calculated by averaging the fluorescence intensities from 60 to 80 cells after subtracting background fluorescence.

## Lipid raft isolation
HAECs (5 × 107) were washed twice with ice-cold MBS buffer (25 mM 2-(N-morpholino)-ethane sulfonic acid [pH 6.5] containing 0.15 M NaCl) and collected by scraping in 2 mL MES. The cell suspension was adjusted to 0.2% Triton X-100 by adding 2% Triton X-100 solution and incubated for 10 min at 4◦C. The cell suspension was homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenates were mixed with 2 mL of 80% sucrose in MBS buffer and added to the bottom of an ultracentrifuge tube. Then, 5% and 30% sucrose solutions (4 mL each in MBS) were gently overlaid stepwise. Ultracentrifugation was performed for 18 h at 100,000 ×g in an SW41 rotor (Beckman Instruments, USA). Twelve fractions (1 mL each) were collected and subjected to immunoblot analysis.

## Proximity ligation assay
Fixed HAECs were incubated with a blocking solution for 30 min at 37◦C and then incubated with plus and minus antibodies in the blocking solution with 1× Duolink reagent overnight at 4◦C. PCR was performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and images were taken using the 510 Meta confocal microscope equipped with argon and helium-neon lasers (Zeiss, Germany).

## Fluorescence labeling of reactive cysteine thiols
HUVECs were cultured in the shear stress device for 48 h. The cells were rinsed with ice-cold PBS, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transferred to an anaerobic chamber. The cells were lysed in an extraction buffer containing 200 μM of N-ethylmaleimide and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to block the free cysteine thiols. The alkylated samples were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-FLK1 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The immunoprecipitates were reduced by incubating them with 5 mM sodium ascorbate for 30 min and washed three times with TEB buffer. The immunoprecipitates were labeled with 100 μM Cy3-maleimide for 30 min, washed three times with TEB buffer, reduced using 5 mM TCEP for 30 min, and washed three times with TEB buffer. Subsequently, the immunoprecipitates were labeled with 100 μM Cy5-maleimide for 30 min and washed three times with TEB buffer. All labeling reactions were performed at room temperature. The double-labeled immunoprecipitates were boiled in 2× SDS sample buffer and separated by SDS-PAGE. The fluorescence images were taken using the Typhoon 9400 variable mode imager (GE Healthcare) and fluorescence levels were quantified using ImageQuant version 5.2 (Molecular Dynamics).

## Biotin switch assay
Protein S-nitrosylation was detected using the biotin switch assay. HAECs or HEK293T cells were transfected with a retroviral vector encoding HA-tagged mouse VEGFR2 and cultured in the shear stress device. The cells were lysed in HENS buffer (25 mM HEPES [pH 7.8], 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.01 mM neocuproine, and 1% SDS) containing 20 mM methyl-methanethiosulphonate or 30 min to block free cysteine thiols. After rinsing three times with HENS buffer, VEGFR2 was immunoprecipitated with an anti-FLK1 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The immunoprecipitates were reduced by 5 mM ascorbate and incubated with 500 μM pyridyl dithiol-biotin in HENS buffer for 1 h. Biotinylated VEGFR2 was separated by SDS-PAGE and blotted using HRP-conjugated streptavidin.

## Morphometric analyses
The experimental mice were anesthetized by 2% isoflurane inhalation and the common carotid arteries were excised after transcardiac perfusion-fixation with heparinized saline containing 3.7% formaldehyde. The vessels were paraffin-embedded and sectioned using a rotary microtome (Leica HistoCore MULTICUT). Two serial sections (4-mm thick) were obtained from the middle of common carotid arteries and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The luminal, internal elastic laminal, and external elastic laminal areas were quantified using NIH ImageJ v1.62. The intimal and medial areas were determined by subtraction of the luminal area from the internal elastic area and by subtracting the internal elastic area from the external elastic area. The values from the two serial sections per rat were averaged for analysis.

## Immunofluorescence staining
HAECs were fixed using 3.7% paraformaldehyde on a glass slide and washed with PBS. The fixed cells were permeabilized and blocked with 5% normal donkey serum in PBST (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. The fixed cells were incubated with primary antibodies (1:200 dilution for caveolin-1, human NOX4, and VEGFR2; 1:500 dilution for α-tubulin) overnight at 4◦C and with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG antibodies at 1:400 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were labeled with DAPI.
For en face immunofluorescence staining of common carotid arteries, the arterial vessels were removed from paraformaldehyde-perfused mice and split longitudinally to expose the endothelium. The spread vessels were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in TBS and blocked with 10% normal goat serum in TBST (2.5% Tween 20 in TBS). The arteries were incubated with primary antibodies (1:200 dilution for CD31 and VEGFR2-pY1175; 1:200 dilution for VEGFR2; 1:50 dilution for cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175)) overnight at 4◦C and then incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-hamster and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were obtained for three random fields per tissue section at a screen magnification of 100×, using the 880 Meta confocal microscope equipped with argon and helium-neon lasers (Zeiss, Germany).

## Vascular permeability test
Mice were injected intravenously with 100 μL of 5% Evans blue dye for 30 min and perfused with PBS for 5 min. Untreated contralateral RCA and partially-ligated LCA were removed, opened longitudinally, and examined using a phase contrast microscope at 20×. For quantification, the extravasated Evans blue dye was extracted from the vessel fragments with formamide at 55◦C overnight and centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 rpm. The absorbance of the collected supernatant was measured at 620 nm and normalized by subtracting background values at 740 nm.

## QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Quantification of microscopy images and immunoblots were performed using ImageJ. Data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism v5.0. Data in the graphs represent mean ± SD or SEM. p values were calculated using a Student’s t-test for comparison between two groups or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for multiple groups containing two variables. p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
