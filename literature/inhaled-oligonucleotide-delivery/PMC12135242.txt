## Background
The poor viability and paracrine function of transplanted mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hamper their therapeutic efficacy in ischemic heart injury treatment. Protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) mediate arginine methylation and have important functions in cellular responses. However, the role of PRMTs in MSC-based therapies for ischemic heart injury remains unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of PRMT1 on MSC function and therapeutic efficacy using a mouse myocardial infarction (MI) model.

## Methods
We isolated, cultured, and identified mouse adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ADSCs). We used Furamidine, a PRMT1 inhibitor, and adenoviral shPRMT1 to study the effects of PRMT1 inhibition on ADSC proliferation, migration, and viability. We performed RNA sequencing and biochemical experiments to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of PRMT1 in ADSCs. Finally, we infected ADSCs with adenoviral shPRMT1 and administered an intramyocardial injection after MI. Cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography and pathological staining, and ADSCs survival rates, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and capillary density were evaluated using immunofluorescence staining.

## Results
We found that PRMT1 was highly expressed in ADSCs and markedly upregulated in response to H2O2 challenge. Inhibition of PRMT1 by Furamidine or adenoviral shPRMT1 promoted ADSC proliferation and migration and reduced H2O2-induced ADSCs apoptosis. Mechanistically, PRMT1 inhibition significantly increased the expression of matrix metallopeptidase (MMP)-10, placenta growth factor (PlGF) and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β2 in ADSCs through transcription factor RUNX1. Blockage of RUNX1 abolished the proliferative and migratory effects of PRMT1 inhibition in ADSCs. Compared to ADSCs infected with the adenovirus-control, PRMT1 knockdown adenovirus markedly increased the survival and retention of transplanted ADSCs in injured hearts after MI. ADSCs with PRMT1 knockdown markedly improved cardiac function and alleviated cardiac fibrosis in mice after MI by reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and increasing capillary density.

## Conclusions
PRMT1 inhibition enhances the cardioprotective effect of ADSCs against MI through RUNX1-mediated production of MMP-10/PlGF/TGF-β2. Overall, PRMT1 inhibition is a promising strategy for augmenting MSCs therapeutic efficacy in ischemic cardiomyopathy.

## Introduction
Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of severe morbidity and mortality worldwide [ , ]. Among these diseases, coronary artery occlusion-induced myocardial infarction (MI) leads to heart failure and even death [ , ]. After MI, oxidative stress and cardiac tissue death caused by ischemia activate various cell signaling pathways that can cause inflammation, angiogenesis, and fibrosis [ – ]. The loss of cardiomyocytes in an infarcted area is a key pathogenic factor in the development of MI. Moreover, adult mammalian cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated cells that generally have very limited regenerative capacity [ ]. Thus, reducing the loss of cardiomyocytes and promoting angiogenesis are of great significance for improving the recovery of cardiac function after MI [ ]. Although considerable progress has been made in the exploration of MI treatments, exploring innovative and effective treatment strategies is still of great significance.
Stem cell-based therapies have emerged as promising alternative treatments for MI. Among the candidate stem cells that have been studied, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are the most widely used for cardiac repair because of their unique properties, such as lack of ethical issues, easy isolation and expansion, lack of immunogenicity, and secretion of regenerative cytokines [ , ]. Among all types of MSCs, adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) are readily available and expanded and are increasingly favored in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases [ , ]. Although they do not differentiate into new cardiomyocytes, the engrafted ADSCs exert cardioprotective effects by reducing cardiomyocyte death and inflammatory responses and stimulating local angiogenesis in the infarcted heart [ , ]. However, the biggest limitation in the treatment of MI with ADSCs is their poor viability and inadequate paracrine capacity, which is due to the pathological ischemic niche caused by ischemia in infarcted cardiac tissues, such as oxidative stress and inflammation [ ]. Pre-adaptation of stem cells to hypoxia or alteration of the stem cell microenvironment through biomaterials can promote the survival rate of stem cells and improve therapeutic efficacy for MI [ – ].Increasing evidence shows that pharmacological or genetic interventions can improve the survival rate and paracrine effects of implanted MSCs, thereby inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of stem cells in MI [ – ]. Therefore, enhancing the myocardial survival of injected ADSCs, increasing the protective paracrine capacity of ADSCs, and improving the therapeutic effect of ADSCs on MI require further study.
Methylation of arginine residues by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) is an epigenetic marker involved in the regulation of many fundamental cellular processes [ ]. Nine PRMTs are encoded in the mammalian genome and are divided into three types based on the catalyzed protein products: Type I, which includes PRMT1, PRMT2, PRMT3, PRMT4, PRMT6, and PRMT8, is responsible for asymmetric dimethylarginine on their substrates; Type II, which includes PRMT5 and PRMT9, is symmetric dimethyltransferase; and Type III, which includes PRMT7, can only monomethylate arginine [ ]. PRMTs play pivotal roles in stem cell biology and regenerative medicine. A recent study demonstrated that PRMT3 promotes MSC osteogenic differentiation and represents a potential therapeutic target for treating bone regeneration and osteopenia disorders [ ]. In intestinal stem cells, PRMT5 is a critical regulator of intestinal epithelium development and intestinal homeostasis [ ]. In glioblastoma stem cells, PRMT6 sustains tumorigenicity through methylation of the regulator of chromatin condensation 1 (RCC1), while PRMT6 inhibition enhances radiotherapy-induced cytotoxicity in tumor xenografts, highlighting therapeutic potential [ ]. PRMT7 is implicated in schizophrenia risk, its inhibition in neural progenitor cells reduces proliferation while promoting neuronal differentiation and neurite elongation in differentiated neurons [ ]. Notably, PRMT1, the primary type I PRMT, accounts for over 90% of asymmetric dimethylarginine in mammalian cells and is required for embryogenesis, cell cycle progression, cell migration, inflammatory responses, and cell fate determination [ , ]. PRMT1 knockout leads to embryonic lethality in mice, stalled tadpole development, and lethality in Xenopus tropicalis, suggesting the evolutionary importance of PRMT1 in the development and heterogeneity between different species [ ]. Moreover, PRMT1 is highly expressed in neural stem cells and plays a key role in the survival and proliferation of embryonic neural stem cells [ ]. Interestingly, inhibition of PRMT1 significantly inhibits leukemia stem cell growth and survival [ ], while loss of PRMT7 selectively eradicate leukemia cells in chronic myeloid leukemia through reprogramming glycine metabolism [ ]. Recent studies have determined that inhibition of type I PRMTs enhances the proliferative capacity of muscle stem cells, thereby promoting muscle regeneration after injury [ ]. However, the role of PRMT1 in ADSCs for MI remains poorly understood.
This study aimed to explore whether PRMT1 inhibition modulates the fate of implanted ADSCs in the post-infarcted heart and to elucidate the intrinsic mechanism. We found that PRMT1 was markedly upregulated in ADSCs in response to H2O2. PRMT1 inhibition promoted ADSC proliferation and migration, attenuated H2O2-induced apoptosis, and conferred cardioprotective effects against MI in mice. PRMT1 inhibition increased the expression of MMP-10, PlGF, and TGF-β2 through RUNX1 in ADSCs. These results demonstrate that PRMT1 inhibition enhances the cardioprotective effects of ADSCs via RUNX1-mediated cytokine production.

## Results

## PRMT1 expression is upregulated in ADSCs under H2O2-induced oxidative stress
First, we isolated and expanded ADSCs from mouse inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissue and used passage 2 ADSCs to characterize their surface molecular markers and differentiation ability. The results showed positive expression of stroma-associated markers such as CD29 (100%), CD90.2 (99.7%), and Scal1 (95.5%), but low expression of the monocyte marker CD11b, endothelial cell marker CD34, hematopoietic marker CD45, and red cell marker Ter119 (Supplemental Fig.  A, B). In addition, we evaluated the multilineage differentiation potential of the ADSCs by incubating them in adipogenic or osteogenic media. Oil Red O and Alizarin Red S staining confirmed the ability of ADSCs to undergo adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation (Supplemental Fig.  C). These results indicate that ADSCs are a population of multipotent MSCs and are not of hematopoietic origin.
To explore whether PRMTs were involved in the functional regulation of ADSCs, we examined the mRNA levels of all PRMTs in ADSCs under H2O2-induced oxidative stress. The levels of PRMT1, PRMT5 and PRMT6 significantly increased in response to oxidative stress (Fig.  A). In addition, PRMT1, PRMT2 and PRMT3 were abundantly expressed in ADSCs, whereas PRMT8 was barely detectable (Fig.  A). Next, we explored the changes and functions of PRMT1 in ADSCs under oxidative stress. The mRNA expression of PRMT1 increased under the stimulation of H2O2 at both 100 µM and 200 µM conditions (Fig.  B). Interestingly, H2O2 increased PRMT1 mRNA expression in a time-dependent manner (Fig.  C). Similarly, western blotting results showed that PRMT1 protein expression increased in ADSCs under oxidative stress conditions (Fig.  D, E). These findings suggest that PRMT1 may play a role in ADSCs.

 

## PRMT1 Inhibition promotes proliferation and migration, and reduces H2O2 induced apoptosis in ADSCs
To investigate the role of PRMT1 in ADSCs, we detected the effect of PRMT1 specific inhibitor Furamidine on cell proliferation, migration, and survival under oxidative stress in ADSCs. Cell numbers at four continuous time points, 0 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h after Furamidine treatment, were detected using the CCK-8 assay. Compared to the vehicle group, the Furamidine-treated group showed increased ADSCs proliferation (Fig.  A and Supplemental Fig.  A). The migratory ability of ADSCs pretreated with or without Furamidine was evaluated using wound healing and transwell migration assays. After 16 h of scratching, ADSCs migration was significantly enhanced after Furamidine treatment compared to that in the vehicle group (Fig.  B, C). Similarly, the transwell assay further verified that Furamidine promoted the migration of ADSCs (Fig.  D, E). To investigate whether PRMT1 is related to ADSCs survival under oxidative stress, we directly detected the activation of caspase-3, the main effector caspase, in Furamidine-treated ADSCs under H2O2 stress. The PRMT1 specific inhibitor Furamidine markedly reduced cleaved caspase-3 protein levels induced by H2O2 (Fig.  F, G).

 

Next, we verified the role of PRMT1 in ADSCs using a PRMT1-short hairpin RNA recombinant adenovirus (Ad-shPRMT1) and a control adenovirus (Ad-control). We determined a suitable multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 200 for Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs (Supplemental Fig.  B-D). Consistent with PRMT1 inhibitor results, Ad-shPRMT1 infection promoted cell proliferation and migration (Fig.  H-J), and reduced H2O2-induced caspase-3 activation in ADSCs (Fig.  K, L). Thus, PRMT1 inhibition promoted proliferation and migration, and reduced H2O2-induced apoptosis in ADSCs.

## PRMT1 Inhibition increases the expression of MMP-10, PlGF and TGF-β2 in ADSCs
To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which PRMT1 functions in ADSCs, we evaluated differences in genomic expression using RNA sequencing (-seq) in ADSCs after treatment with Furamidine. Compared to vehicle group, 300 up-regulated and 517 down-regulated genes were detected in the Furamidine treated ADSCs group (Fig.  A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that after Furamidine treatment, the differentially expressed genes were enriched in growth factor activity and cytokine activity (Fig.  B). Furthermore, we analyzed the top 40 differentially expressed genes and found three typical proliferation/migration/survival-related genes [Mmp10, encodes protein matrix metallopeptidase (MMP)-10; Plgf, encodes protein placenta growth factor (PlGF); Tgfb2, encodes protein transforming growth factor (TGF)-β2] were markedly upregulated after Furamidine treatment (Fig.  C), which was also confirmed by qRT-PCR in Furamidine-treated ADSCs (Fig.  D). Moreover, both the mRNA and protein levels of MMP-10, PlGF and TGF-β2 were increased in Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs (Fig.  E-G). These results suggest that PRMT1 inhibition enhanced proliferation, migration, and survival ability of ADSCs by increasing the expression of MMP-10, PlGF, and TGF-β2 in ADSCs.

 

## PRMT1 Inhibition promotes proliferation and migration of ADSCs through RUNX1
To further elucidate how PRMT1 regulates the expression of Mmp10, Plgf and Tgfb2, we predicted the common transcription factors (TFs) of these genes using various databases. Based on two data analysis sites, AnimalTFB4.0 and JASPAR, we identified a common transcription factor, RUNX1, and predicted site sequence logos for possible transcription factor binding (Fig.  A, B). Co-immunoprecipitation results showed that PRMT1 and RUNX1 interacted with each other in ADSCs (Fig.  C, D). Moreover, the RUNX1 inhibitor Ro5-3335 abolished PRMT1 inhibitor induced upregulation of MMP-10, PlGF and TGF-β2 expression in ADSCs (Fig.  E, F). Importantly, Ro5-3335 abrogated the Furamidine-promoted proliferation and migration ability of ADSCs (Fig.  G-I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PRMT1 inhibition enhanced the proliferation and migration of ADSCs through RUNX1-mediated MMP-10, PlGF and TGF-β2 expression.

 

## PRMT1 knockdown increases the retention and survival of ADSCs in the infarcted heart
Next, to verify the PRMT1 inhibition-mediated functions of ADSCs in vivo, we transplanted Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs with Red Fluorescent Protein (RFP)into infarcted heart and evaluated the ADSCs retention rates on days 1, 3, and 7 after MI (Fig.  A). We first tested the feasibility of a method for the myocardial injection of ADSCs. The morphology of ADSCs isolated from RFP transgenic mice was observed, demonstrating characteristic spindle-shaped morphology with 100% RFP-positive (Supplemental Fig.  A). To validate the effect of microsyringe transplantation on ADSCs, we assessed the impact of microsyringe-mediated delivery on adherent properties, morphology and cellular viability of ADSCs. Compared to the non-injected cells, the cells were completely adherent 6 h after injection, and there was no change in cell morphology and viability before and after microsyringe injection, indicating that the microinjection did not damage the cells (Supplemental Fig.  B, C). Immediately after coronary ligation, ADSCs were injected into the peri-infarct region at three sites, and immunofluorescence staining showed ADSCs retention at all three injection sites on day 3 after MI (Supplemental Fig.  D).

 

The mice were randomly divided into the MI + ADSCs-control (injected with Ad-control infected ADSCs into infarcted hearts) and MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1(injected with Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs into infarcted hearts) groups. The number of transplanted ADSCs was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig.  B, the transplanted ADSCs formed clumps with a large number of cells on day 1 after MI, began to disperse and the number of cells decreased on day 3 after MI, whereas the number of transplanted ADSCs decreased significantly on day 7 after MI. Interestingly, compared to the MI + ADSCs-control group, the number of RFP-positive ADSCs in the MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1 group increased at all three time points (Fig.  B, C). These results suggest that PRMT1 knockdown increased the retention rate of ADSCs in infarcted hearts.

## Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs therapy reduces cardiomyocytes apoptosis and promotes angiogenesis after MI
To determine whether knocking down PRMT1 enhances the cardioprotective effects of ADSCs against MI, cardiomyocyte apoptosis and angiogenesis were detected on day 3 after MI. Apoptotic cardiomyocytes were detected by TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining in the peri-infarction area, and no apoptotic cardiomyocytes were observed in the sham heart (Fig.  A, B). Compared to the MI + ADSCs-control group, the number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cardiomyocytes was markedly decreased in the infarcted heart of MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1 group (Fig.  A, B). Consistently, CD31 immunofluorescence staining showed that intramyocardial delivery of Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs significantly enhanced angiogenesis compared to that in the MI + ADSCs-control group (Fig.  C, D). These results suggest that Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs therapy reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and promoted angiogenesis after MI.

 

## Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs therapy improves cardiac function and reduces myocardial fibrosis after MI
Finally, we further explored the long-term therapeutic effect of Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs on MI by evaluating postoperative cardiac function and myocardial fibrosis (Fig.  A). Mice were randomly divided into four groups: sham (only needle puncture, no ligation, and no injection), MI + vehicle (injected with vehicle without ADSCs), MI + ADSCs-control (injected with Ad-control infected ADSCs into infarcted hearts), and MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1 (injected with Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs into infarcted hearts). Compared to the MI + vehicle and MI + ADSCs-control groups, the MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1 group showed significantly reduced MI-induced mortality (Fig.  B). In addition, we used echocardiography to visualize the cardiac structure and evaluate cardiac function, and found significantly decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) in MI mice (Fig.  C-E). The heart to body weight ratio was higher in the MI + vehicle group than the sham group (Fig.  F). Histological analysis showed that MI mice had left ventricular hypertrophy and massive interstitial fibrosis (Fig.  G, H). Most importantly, the MI + ADSCs-shPRMT1 group exhibited improved cardiac function, lower heart to body weight ratio, and reduced myocardial fibrosis compared to the other two MI groups (Fig.  C-H). Taken together, these results indicate that the inhibition of PRMT1 in ADSCs enhanced the cardioprotective effects of ADSCs against MI by improving the survival rate, cardiac function and reducing myocardial fibrosis.

 

## Discussion
Owing to their easy availability, lack of ethical concerns, moderate differentiation potential, and paracrine properties, ADSCs have shown promise for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases [ , , ]. However, the low implantation rate and survival rate of ADSCs limit their application in ischemic heart disease, which requires optimization strategies to enhance the myocardial retention and survival of injected ADSCs [ ]. In this study, we observed that both mRNA and protein levels of PRMT1 were upregulated in H2O2-treated ADSCs. PRMT1 inhibition markedly promoted migration/proliferation and survival in ADSCs through RUNX1-mediated MMP-10/PlGF/TGF-β2 expression. In vivo, PRMT1 knockdown increased the retention and survival of engrafted ADSCs in the ischemic heart and enhanced the protective effects of ADSCs against MI. Therefore, PRMT1 is a crucial molecule in engrafted ADSCs for the treatment of ischemic heart injury.
PRMTs perform important biological functions in MSCs. PRMT4 and PRMT7 are essential for the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs as they activate bone morphogenetic protein signaling [ , ], whereas PRMT5 inhibition promotes the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs by repressing basal interferon-stimulated gene expression [ ]. Interestingly, PRMT3 regulates human MSCs osteogenesis by increasing miR-3648 [ ]. PRMT1 is the predominant type I protein arginine methyltransferase, and previous studies have focused on embryonic development, DNA damage repair, and tumor cell biology [ , ]. The PRMT1-p53 pathway is required for ventricular morphogenesis and coronary vessel formation during heart development via modulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [ ]. Here, we found that PRMT1 was also highly expressed in ADSCs, and both the mRNA and protein levels of PRMT1 increased in response to H2O2 challenge. Inhibition of PRMT1 with Furamidine or PRMT1 siRNA in ADSCs promotes cell proliferation, migration, and survival via RUNX1 signaling. Paradoxically, Furamidine inhibits the proliferation and in vivo tumor growth of U87MG-derived glioblastoma stem cells by blocking STAT3 signaling [ ]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in signal transduction between the different cell types. Consistent with our findings, PRMT1 knockout increased cell proliferation in adult mouse crypts [ ].
Although our investigation demonstrates the therapeutic efficacy of PRMT1-inhibited ADSCs in a mouse MI model, the translational potential for clinical applications in humans requires critical evaluation. Compelling evidence supports the adaptability of this strategy to human MSCs (hMSCs). Notably, recent studies reveal that PRMT1 enhances survival and serial replating capacity in human primary leukemia stem cells, while pharmacological PRMT1 inhibition (e.g., MS023) effectively eradicates leukemia stem cells in preclinical models [ ]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of clinical trial data demonstrates that hMSCs transplantation post-acute MI significantly improves cardiac function [ ]. While these findings are encouraging, it is important to recognize that there are fundamental differences between mouse and human ADSCs in terms of paracrine signaling and immunomodulatory responses, that acute myocardial infarction models cannot replicate the pathophysiology of chronic ischemia in patients, and that the long-term biosafety of PRMT1 regulation needs to be further evaluated.
Increasing evidence has shown that MSCs exert their therapeutic effects mainly through paracrine effects by producing growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines [ – ]. Here, we found that PRMT1 inhibition promoted the paracrine function of ADSCs and thereby increased the production of MMP-10/PlGF/TGF-β2, thus significantly promoting the proliferation/migration ability and reducing H2O2-induced cell apoptosis in ADSCs. The expression of MMP-10, MMP-13, and HGF is the main factor that enhances the protective effects of ADSCs against ischemic heart failure [ ]. As a member of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family, PlGF plays an important role in cardiovascular diseases, such as MI [ ]. Interestingly, in a pig model of MI, administration of recombinant human PlGF improved post-infarction cardiac remodeling [ ]. Moreover, stem cell-derived TGF-β2 promotes angiogenesis and inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the infarcted heart [ , ]. These results further support our results that upregulation of MMP-10/PlGF/TGF-β2 promoted by PRMT1 inhibition creates a favorable microenvironment for ADSCs survival and migration.
The transcription factor RUNX1 is known for its important role in hematopoiesis, and its activity is regulated by numerous post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation [ ]. PRMT1 methylates RUNX1 thereby dissociating its association with the corepressor SIN3A and enhancing the transcriptional activity of RUNX1 [ ]. Interestingly, genetic ablation of PRMT1-mediated RUNX1 methylation leads to resistance to apoptosis and a survival advantage of hematopoietic stem cells under stressful conditions [ ]. We observed that the interaction between RUNX1 and PRMT1, and inhibition of RUNX1 eliminated the proliferation and migration of ADSCs promoted by PRMT1 inhibition by decreasing the expression of MMP-10, PlGF, and TGF-β2. RUNX1 is essential for MSC proliferation, and inhibition of RUNX1 resulted in cell cycle arrest and reduced proliferation of bone marrow-derived MSCs [ ]. These results suggest that PRMT1 plays a role in ADSCs by interacting with the transcription factor RUNX1; however, whether PRMT1 methylates arginine at specific sites of RUNX1 in ADSCs requires further investigation.
This study has several limitations. First, the study failed to take advantage of multiple cell types to verify its results. Second, the underlying mechanism of the novel molecular interaction of PRMT1 and RUNX1 is unknown. The data presented are still preliminary and need to be fully explored with further experimental validation. Finally, the effectiveness and applicability of Ad-shPRMT1 infected ADSCs should be validated in other experimental animals or ischemia models.

## Conclusions
We identified that inhibition of PRMT1 promoted the proliferation and migration, and reduced apoptosis under H2O2 stress of ADSCs via RUNX1-mediated expression of MMP-10, PlGF, and TGF-β2. Knockdown of PRMT1 in ADSCs from the engrafted ischemic myocardium improved cardiac function after MI by reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and increasing angiogenesis (Fig.  ). Therefore, inhibition of PRMT1 signaling in ADSCs using appropriate approaches may be a potential strategy to enhance the adaptability of ADSCs to the ischemic myocardial milieu, suggesting a novel strategy for improving the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-based therapy.

 

## Materials and methods

## Animals
All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J background in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin Medical University. Adult male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Red fluorescent protein (RFP) transgenic mice with C57BL/6J background were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. All mice were fed in cages with a 12 h light/ 12 h dark cycle, were free to drink water, and had a standard diet during the study period.

## ADSCs isolation, culture and oxidative stress model in vitro
ADSCs were isolated from the inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissue of adult male C57BL/6J mice or RFP transgenic mice, as previously reported [ ]. All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after being completely anesthetized with isoflurane (R510; RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) in accordance with the Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Laboratory Animals (GB/T 39760 − 2021). Briefly, subcutaneous adipose tissue was stripped from the groin of mice, washed several times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fully cut into a paste. The cut adipose tissues were transferred to preheated 0.2% collagenase I (C0130-1G; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) in water bath at 37 ℃, violently shaken every 5 min, and digested for 60 min. When there was no visible precipitated mass, the digested adipose tissues were terminated by the complete medium, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min (4 ℃), then the supernatant was removed. The cells were re-suspended and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (C11330500BT, DMEM/F12; Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141 C, FBS; Gibco, NY, USA). After being placed in a 5% CO2 cell incubator at 37 ℃ overnight and changing the culture medium, the adherent cells obtained were the primary ADSCs for division and expansion. An oxidative stress model was established using hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).When the ADSCs reached 80–90% confluence, they were treated with H2O2 (100 or 200 µM) for the specified time (indicated in the specific figure legend), and then follow-up experiments were performed. None of the experimental cells exceeded passage 2.

## Flow cytometry
ADSCs were digested with 0.05% trypsin (25200056; Gibco, NY, USA), terminated by the addition of complete medium, and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The cells were collected and washed twice with 1× PBS. The suspended cells were allocated in equal volume, and the corresponding flow antibody was added as follows: anti-CD29‐PEcy7 (102221; BioLegend, CA, USA), CD90.2-PEcy7 (105326; BioLegend, CA, USA), anti‐ SCA1-PE (122507; BioLegend, CA, USA), CD11b-APC (101211; BioLegend, CA, USA), anti‐CD34‐Percp‐cy5.5 (128607; BioLegend, CA, USA), anti‐CD45‐FITC (103108; BioLegend, CA, USA) and anti‐Ter119‐APC‐cy7 (116223; BioLegend, CA, USA), and then stained at 4 ℃ for 30 min. The stained cells were washed with 1× PBS, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and suspended in 1× PBS. Cell surface marker expression was detected using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

## Adipogenesis and osteogenesis
For adipogenesis, ADSCs were inoculated in 12-well plates and differentiation was induced when the cell density reached more than 80%. ADSCs were cultured in the composition solution containing 10% FBS low-glycemic DMEM (11885084; Gibco, NY, USA), 1 µM dexamethasone (D4902; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 0.5 mM 1-methyl-3-isobutylxanthine (I7018; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 0.5 µg/mL insulin (I3536; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 1 µM rosiglitazone (R2408; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and 100 µM L-ascorbate acid (261556; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). The control group was cultured in low-glycemic DMEM containing 10% FBS. The cell culture medium was changed every 3 days. The cells were cultured for approximately 14 days and stained with Oil Red O solution (G1262; Solarbio, Beijing, China).
For osteogenesis, ADSCs were inoculated on 6-well plates. When the cell density was close to 100%, the cell culture medium was added with high-glucose DMEM (D6429; Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% FBS,100 nM dexamethasone, 200 µM L-ascorbate acid, and 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate (G9422; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). The cell culture medium was changed every 3 days, the cells were cultured for 21 days, and stained with Alizarin-Red Staining Solution (TMS-008; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA).

## Adenovirus construction and infection
Empty plasmid (control) adenoviruses and shPRMT1 adenoviruses were constructed by Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were infected using 1/2 small volume method and multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 200. ADSCs were supplemented with 10% FBS medium to total volume after 4 h of adenovirus infection. The medium was replaced with fresh complete medium after 16 h of infection and the cells were cultured in an incubator for 24 h.

## Myocardial infarction, intramyocardial ADSC injection and evaluations
The mouse myocardial infarction (MI) model was established as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1.2% tribromoethanol (T161626; Aladdin Biotech, Shanghai, China) and intratracheally intubated. The thoracic cavity was opened successively and fixed using a chest expander to fully expose the surgical area, and the pericardium was gently separated. The chest was opened in the 3–4 intercostal space and secured with a chest expander to fully expose the surgical area. The left anterior descending (LAD) of coronary artery was permanently ligated with an 8 − 0 polypropylene suture at 2 mm below the left auricle. The sham control group underwent all procedures, except coronary artery ligation and intramyocardial injection. The anterior wall of the left ventricle was pale at the distal end of the ligation line. Immediately after MI, 2 × 105 ADSCs suspended in 20 µL cold PBS containing 0.2 mM EDTA were injected intramyocardially at three different sites in the infarct border zone area by a Hamilton microsyringe (1710; Reno, NV, USA) with 30G needle. The mice in the MI + vehicle group were injected with the same volume of PBS. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.
For histological observations, including RFP immunostaining (on days 1, 3 and 7 post-MI), TUNEL staining (on day 3 post-MI), and CD31 staining (on day 3 post-MI), 28 mice were randomized as follows: (1) sham (n = 4), (2) MI + ADSC-control (2 × 105 ADSCs infected with ad-control were intramyocardially injected, n = 12), and (3) MI + ADSC-shPRMT1 (2 × 105 ADSCs infected with ad-shPRMT1, n = 12).
For long-term observation, including survival curves (the entire experimental cycle), echocardiography (on days 3, 7,14 and 28 post-MI), and Masson staining (on day 28 post-MI), 69 mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) sham (n = 10), (2) MI + vehicle (n = 22), (3) MI + ADSC-control (n = 23), and (4) MI + ADSC-shPRMT1 (n = 14).

## Echocardiography
Mice were anaesthetized by inhaling 1–2% isoflurane (R510, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Cardiac function was evaluated via echocardiography using the VisualSonics Vevo 2100 high-resolution ultrasound system (VisualSonics, Inc., Toronto, Canada). The MS400 (30 MHz) probe transducer was selected to obtain the M-mode images of the left ventricular parasternal long axis. Left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESd) were measured during three consecutive cardiac cycles. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) were calculated automatically by the following formula: LVEF(%) = [(LVIDd3 − LVIDs3)/LVIDd3]×100%; LVFS(%) = (LVEDd − LVESd)/LVEDd×100% [ ].

## Immunofluorescence staining
All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after inhalation of isoflurane (1–2%), as previously described. Mouse hearts were obtained according to the experimental time points, and fully perfumed using PBS after intraperitoneal injection of anesthesia. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P1110; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 24 h, dehydrated with sucrose (S8271; Solarbio, Beijing, China), then underwent OCT embedding (4583; Sakura, CA, USA) and placed in the refrigerator at -80 ℃. Cut the frozen slices into 6-µm-thick slices, paste them on the carrier slices, and balance at room temperature. Sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-100 (ST797; Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 15 min and sealed with 3% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. The tissue was incubated with primary antibody at 4 ℃ overnight, and the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 2 h away from light. Nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (GB1012, DAPI; Servicebio, Beijing, China) and sealed with a cover glass. All immunostained sections were stored in the dark, and micrographs were acquired using an Axio-Imager_LSM-800 (Carl Zeiss AG, BW, Germany).
The primary antibodies used were anti-Troponin T mouse monoclonal antibody (MA5-12960; ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) (1:300), and anti-CD31 antibody (GB13063; Servicebio, Beijing, China) (1:300).

## TUNEL staining
Each heart tissue was cut into 6 μm thick slices at -20 ℃. Apoptotic cardiomyocytes were detected using terminal deoxynucleotide transferase-mediated dUTP nickel end labeling (TUNEL) staining (40306; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Apoptotic cells were analyzed and counted using ImageJ software.

## Masson’s trichrome staining
Hearts were extracted after perfusion, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and then embedded in paraffin. Each heart was sliced 4 μm thick and Masson’s trichrome staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (G1340; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to assess cardiac fibrosis and structural changes. The fibrotic area was determined using ImageJ software (Media Cybernetics, MD, USA), which was the average ratio of the fibrotic area to the left ventricular area.

## Cell viability assay
ADSCs cell suspension was calculated with 4000 cells per well and 100 µL was uniformly inoculated into the 96-well plate. The next day, 100 µL of completely treated cells containing the drug were replaced and continued to be cultured for 24–72 h. Cell viability was measured over time by cell counting kit 8 (CK04, CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by microplate reader.

## Wound healing assay
The wound healing assay was performed by generating a uniform scratch in a confluent monolayer of ADSCs using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, fresh medium with or without Furamidine (10 µM) was administered. Scratch closure was monitored at 0 h and 16 h using phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Japan). Migration distance was quantified with ImageJ software through measurement of residual wound width at three predetermined points per well.

## Transwell assay
The serum-free 2 × 104 ADSCs were vertically added to the upper cavity of a modified two-compartment plate (3422; Corning, NYC, USA) with a pore size of 8 μm. Complete media containing the drug was added to the lower chamber and cultured in an incubator for 24 h. The upper chamber was removed using tweezers, washed twice with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The cells that penetrated the membrane were stained with 0.1% Crystal Violet (C8470; Solarbio, Beijing, China), photographed under a microscope and counted.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from ADSCs using TRIzol reagent (15596018; Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesised from 1 µg of total RNA using a Hifair® III First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (11141es60; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed via SYBR Green (11184es08; Yeasen, Shanghai, China), and samples were analyzed in triplicate 10 µL reactions. Gapdh served as the housekeeping target. The primers used are listed in supplemental Table.

## Western blot analysis
Total proteins were isolated from ADSCs with 1 × lysis buffer supplemented by a protease inhibitor cocktail (C0001; TargetMol, MA, USA), and determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The proteins were denatured at a high temperature, separated by gel electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010; Merck Millipore, MA, USA), which were blocked with 5% milk for 2 h and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary anti-mouse antibody (7076 V, 1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) or anti-rabbit antibody (7074 V, 1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) at room temperature for 2 h, and exposed to enhanced chemiluminescence.
The primary antibodies used in this study included: anti-PRMT1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (2449 S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), anti-cleaved caspase-3 rabbit polyclonal antibody (9664 S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), anti-MMP-10 rabbit polyclonal antibody (A3033, 1:1000; Abclonal Technology, Wuhan, China), anti-TGF-β2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (19999-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-PlGF rabbit polyclonal antibody (10642-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-RUNX1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (25315-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-GAPDH rabbit polyclonal antibody (10494-1-AP, 1:10000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China).

## Co-immunoprecipitation
The cells were collected, added to IP cell lysis buffer (P0013; Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitors, and placed on ice. After centrifugation at 4 ℃, 12,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was obtained. Anti-PRMT1 antibody was added to the cell lysate and incubated at 4 ℃ overnight; then, protein A + G agarose (P2012; Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) was added to the cell lysate. After centrifugation at 4 ℃, 3000 × g for 5 min, the protein A + G agarose was located at the bottom of the tube, washed 5 times with 1 mL lysis buffer, dissolved with 2 × SDS buffer, and boiled in a metal bath at 100 ℃ for 10 min. Western blotting was performed, as previously described.

## RNA sequencing analysis
After stimulating ADSCs with DMSO (D8371; Solarbio, Beijing, China) or Furamidine (19121; Cayman Chemical, MI, USA), RNA was collected for RNA sequencing analysis at LC-Bio Technologies Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Differential gene expression was analyzed based on the sequencing results. The promoter sequence is selected as the upstream 2000 bp of the 5’ ends and downstream 100 bp of the 3’ ends of the target gene sequence, which is obtained by National Center for Biotechnology Information. Using Animal TFB4.0 (http://bioinfo.Life.hust.edu.cn/AnimalTFDB4/) [ ] and the JASPAR website (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) [ ] to predict transcription factors in light of the promoter.

## Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). If the data does not follow a normal distribution, then increase the sample size. For normally distributed data, an unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was performed to compare different groups. For multiple group comparisons with two variables, two-way ANOVA was used. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
