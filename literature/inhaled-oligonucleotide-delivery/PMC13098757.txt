## ABSTRACT

Burkholderia mallei is the causative agent of glanders, a serious zoonotic disease of major concern for both animal and human health. This highly contagious and potentially fatal bacterium is always classified as a biothreat agent. In this review, we covered a comprehensive overview of glanders, including the biology of the pathogen, epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, control strategies and ongoing eradication programs in Eurasia. We also summarized human case reports, differential diagnosis, treatment and organism's antimicrobial properties. The section on vaccine development is among the most detailed in this article, covering various experimental approaches and vaccine types evaluated to date. Glanders mainly affects horses, donkeys, mules and carnivorous predators and can be transmitted to people by close contact with glanderous animals. Currently, no specific therapy or licensed vaccine exists for either animals or humans. It should be noted that live‐attenuated vaccines represent one of the most promising approaches for future prevention. An integrated collaboration between veterinary and human health sectors is essential to control the incidence and re‐emergence of glanders.

## Pathology
6.1.1

## Equids
Donkeys and horses usually manifest the acute and chronic form of glanders respectively, while both forms could be seen in mules (Khan et al. ). There are three manifestations of glanders, including nasal, pulmonary and cutaneous (also called Farcy) forms, in equids. The nasal form comprises turbinates, concha and nasal mucosal ulcers replaced with stellate scar tissue, which clarifies that the animal has passed a period of suffering from glanders (Khan et al. ). This stellate scar tissue is the key to easily diagnosing the equids exposed to B. mallei. In this type of disease, purulent nasal discharge can be unilateral or bilateral (Gilad ).
The pulmonary form is characterized by grey to yellow, hard miliary nodules approximately 3 mm in diameter that sometimes coalesce and are surrounded by peripheral connective tissue. The centre of these nodules may be caseous or calcified. The formation of pyogranuloma in pulmonary tissue with degenerated neutrophils in the centre and macrophages, epithelioid cells, giant cells, lymphocytes and fibroblasts in the periphery are also possible (Erdemsurakh, Purevdorj, et al. ). Pulmonary oedema is an important cause of death in horses as they are obligate nasal breathers, and airway obstruction is associated with pulmonary oedema (Lopez et al. ).
The cutaneous form or Farcy is mainly characterized by ruptured discharging skin nodules, which are the consequences of lymphangitis and lymphadenitis. They are in the route of lymphatics and are sometimes called ‘farcy pipes’ (Singha, Shanmugasundaram, Tripathi, et al. ). This form may be a result of direct skin inoculation (Gilad ). These pus‐discharging nodules cause an enormous number of B. mallei organisms to be released in the surrounding area. Muscle abscesses have also been reported in horses (Lopez et al. ).

6.1.2

## Laboratory Animal Models (Nonhuman Primates)
Gross lesions could be found in the liver, spleen, lymph nodes and nasopharynx, in common marmoset—a monkey species (Jelesijevic et al. ). It has been reported that experimental glanders exhibited splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, lung consolidation, pale and mottled lungs and liver discoloration as gross lesions in common marmosets (Nelson et al. ). It has been reported that the diameter of the foci of necrosis is about 5–12 mm in rhesus macaque (Yingst et al. ). Abscesses were developed in the epididymis and gonad of an African green monkey following the aerosol glanders challenge (Waag et al. ). Histopathological evaluation of a swollen lymph node indicated an enlarged cortex and medulla in which follicles were infiltrated by lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells in common marmoset and rhesus macaque (Jelesijevic et al. ; Yingst et al. ).

6.2

## Differential Diagnosis and Possible Misdiagnoses
Symptoms and manifestations of glanders could be mistaken with other diseases that affect mucous membranes of the upper respiratory tract and sinuses and produce mucopurulent discharge or result in skin lesions. These diseases may include Strangles (Streptococcus equi), Ulcerative Lymphangitis (Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis), Pseudotuberculosis (Yersinia pseudotuberculosis), Epizootic Lymphangitis (Histoplasma capsulatum var. farciminosum), Tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis), Botryomycosis (Staphylococcus aureus) and Sporotrichosis (Sporothrix schenckii).
As often mentioned, glanders should be accurately differentiated from Melioidosis, a disease caused by B. pseudomallei, in equine cases (Gonzalez‐Medina et al. ). Melioidosis is primarily a disease involving sheep, goats and humans, but there is possibility that horses manifest this disease. Its clinical signs in horses are pneumonia, septicaemia and lymphangitis. Gastrointestinal symptoms like colic or diarrhoea could be diagnosed (L. Sprague and Neubauer ). Compared to glanders, melioidosis in horses can be more acute with a shorter course. The death of horses because of superacute melioidosis may occur within few hours or days, but glanders in horses usually develops its chronic form and a glanderous horse can survive up to several months (Khan et al. ; L. Sprague and Neubauer ). Integumentary manifestations resemble fungal eczema at first but later turn into papula without formation of abscesses. Neurological and ocular symptoms as meningoencephalitis and keratitis could be among clinical signs (L. Sprague and Neubauer ).

## Treatment
The aetiologic agent of glanders has a unique characteristic that enables it to fight against conventional antibiotics, bacteriostatics and especially bactericidal compounds. That is an intrinsic antibiotic resistance mechanism, leading to the lack of a specific therapy against it. The organism is highly contagious and leads to high mortality rates. If the disease remains untreated, treatment will be complicated, and the disease may even relapse after a period of treatment (Khakhum et al. ; Tapia et al. ). Since the organism is a facultative intracellular bacterium, antibiotics such as aminoglycosides that cannot infiltrate cells cannot be effective against B. mallei (Van Zandt et al. )
The treatment of animal glanders is not encouraged routinely, as the zoonotic aspect of transmission, intrinsic resistance of the agent, relapsing nature of the disease and the risk of developing carriers will counteract an effective and reasonable treatment (Kettle and Wernery ; Torres ). Thus, euthanasia of the animal is suggested by international instructions (EFSA Panel on Animal Health and Welfare et al. ; Torres ). However, through intensively monitored studies, prolonged animal treatment may be applied. Prolonged administration (12 weeks) of parenteral Enrofloxacin, Trimethoprim‐Sulfa (Trimethoprim‐Sulfadiazine) and oral administration of Doxycycline to 23 culture‐positive horses infected with B. mallei treated the infected horses. Random immunosuppression of six horses confirmed complete treatment because the immunosuppressed animals did not experience the recurrence of the disease (Saqib et al. ).
It has also been recommended that co‐trimoxazole (Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole) or co‐amoxiclav (Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid) effectively prevented infection and resulted in post‐exposure prophylaxis when combined with previous vaccination in BALB/c mice animal models of glanders (Waag ). Two antibiotics belonging to the class Fluoroquinolones, Finafloxacin and Moxifloxacin, were evaluated to determine their ability in preventing inhalational glanders in murine animal models. Moxifloxacin appeared to be efficacious, but it has been approved that simultaneous application of vaccination and antibiotic therapy is required for successful treatment (Waag ). Finafloxacin was a bit weaker than Co‐trimoxazole in prophylaxis and survival of animal models, but this difference did not appear significant. In contrast, Finafloxacin was more successful in controlling the load of bacteria in tissues and must be mentioned as a suitable alternative in preventing the glanders disease (Barnes et al. ). It has been shown that administration of cytosine–phosphate–guanine (CpG) containing oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs) is beneficial in the protection and survival of murine animal models (Mott et al. ). Mucosal administration of cationic liposome DNA complexes (CLDCs) efficiently protected the patients by activating innate immunity. Administration of CLDCs before or shortly after bacterial challenge showed complete or nearly complete protection when a lethal dose of B. mallei was applied inhalationally (Goodyear et al. ).

## Glanders in Human
Following inoculation, the acute form of glanders can be manifested clinically in human beings after 1–14 days, and the incubation period is approximately 12 weeks in the chronic form of the disease (Khan et al. ; Van Zandt et al. ). The mortality rate of human glanders is even up to 90%–95% in the pulmonary, septicaemic and cutaneous forms if the disease remains untreated, and it may remain up to 40%–50% even following treatment of acute or chronic glanderous patients (Van Zandt et al. ).
8.1

## Transmission and Infection Routes
The transmission of human glanders occurs mainly through three routes. These routes are percutaneous, inhalational and mucosal. The percutaneous route requires abraded skin, and intact skin does not seem to contribute to the transmission; however, in many cases there are no reports regarding obvious skin abrasions or lacerations. The inhalational route takes place by deep pulmonary disposition and colonization of the agent. Nasal, oral and conjunctival mucous membranes play role in mucosal route of transmission, when bacteria invade them directly (Van Zandt et al. ). Oral mucosal route could also be designated as ingestional route (Gilad ).

8.2

## Clinical Signs
Human glanders appears in different manifestations, including chronic, disseminated, pulmonary and septicaemic forms (Van Zandt et al. ). The clinical signs of glanders in humans include mild afternoon fever and night sweats, malaise, restlessness, fatigue, headache, myalgia, pain in the back and chest, rigors, weight loss, abscesses, osteomyelitis, lymphadenopathy and lymphangitis and when remains untreated, it leads to sepsis and even death (Cetin et al. ; Lupi et al. ; Srinivasan et al. ; Van Zandt et al. ). If transmitted through inhalation, ulcerative necrosis of the upper and lower respiratory tract, purulent nasal discharge and pneumonia can be seen (Gilad ).

8.3

## Diagnosis
The gold standard for the diagnosis of glanders is bacteriological culture and isolation of B. mallei. IHA is a serological test for glanders diagnosis. This test is not glanders specific, but also Burkholderia specific. PCR and CFT are also of diagnostic importance. Diagnostic imaging such as radiography can reveal abscesses in lung, liver and spleen; but these must be well differentiated from other abscess‐forming diseases (Gilad ; Van Zandt et al. ).

8.4

## Differential Diagnosis
The disease should be differentiated and well‐defined from pneumonia by other bacterial (especially melioidosis) and viral agents, malaria, anthrax, plague, smallpox (because of pustular skin lesions) and typhoid fever, in humans (Gilad ; Nasiri et al. ).

8.5

## Case Reports
Eight reports have shown laboratory infections from 1943 to 2013 in US laboratories such as Fort Detrick Center, Maryland (Van Zandt et al. ). A case of human glanders that has recently been reported, demonstrated the disease in an Iranian man who got infected when he buried a horse with the glanders disease history (Nasiri et al. ). A 15‐year‐old male patient with pneumonia, osteomyelitis and abscesses was infected by the glanders bacterium in Türkiye in 2024 (Cetin et al. ). Besides, two case reports in 2020 and 2024 have reported the confirmation of human glanders in an 11‐year‐old boy and a 73‐year‐old man in Brazil (Luz et al. ; Santos Junior et al. ).

8.6

## Treatment
There is limited information available regarding the treatment of human glanders, because the disease is rare and of low incidence. Experimentally, B. mallei is susceptible to a spectrum of antibiotics. A military microbiologist who was diagnosed with glanders in 2000 successfully responded to a treatment protocol with Imipenem and Doxycycline for 2 weeks and was then followed by Azithromycin. Finally, the treatment protocol was completed with Azithromycin and Doxycycline for 6 months. B. mallei was believed to be resistant to Azithromycin when this therapeutic protocol was performed, but improvement after 6 months showed the probable effect of Azithromycin on this organism (Srinivasan et al. ). Antibiotics introduced by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) to test the primary susceptibility of B. mallei are Imipenem, Ceftazidime and Doxycycline (McLaughlin and Sue ). Meropenem is also effective based on susceptibility tests (Cetin et al. ). Sulfonamide antibiotics such as Sulfadiazine are also effective (Lupi et al. ). As mentioned before, Aminoglycosides and other antibiotics that do not enter host cells have no ability to treat the disease. Oral antibacterial therapy should be performed within a time range of 3–12 months. Treatment of abscesses in organs like the liver and prostate gland should be done through surgical removal of the mass (Nasiri et al. ).

## Additional Applications and Misuses of Burkholderia Species
9.1

## 
B. mallei as a Biothreat Agent
Humans constitute a significant part, because of the zoonotic aspect of the disease. This criteria made this organism a potential biothreat agent (Anderson and Bokor ); it plays a vital role in defence considerations (Amemiya et al. ; Broertjes et al. ; Chiang et al. ; Saikh and Mott ; Sarkar‐Tyson et al. ; Wolfe et al. ). Besides misusing and misapplication of the bacterium as a biothreat agent in World War I and II, the Soviet Union also implemented an attack on the Mujahedin and their horses in Afghanistan by this bacterial agent (Alibek ). It has also been included in the Tier 1 select and Class B biothreat agents' list of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Gilad ). Low infectious dose and the organism's aerosolization capacity, together with the organism's viability through this way, always nominate B. mallei as a potential biothreat agent (Khakhum et al. ).

9.2

## 
Burkholderia Species as Antimicrobial Agents
In contrast with the potential capability of Burkholderia species, including B. mallei, to be used as a biothreat agent, it is possible to utilize them as antimicrobial agents. For example, gene clusters for synthesizing Glidobactin A are found in B. mallei. Glidobactins or Cepafungins are compounds comprised of acyl‐peptides. They are mainly produced by B. cepacia and inhibit pathogenic species of yeast and fungi species like Candida, Aspergillus, Microsporum and Trichophyton. A genomic cluster responsible for encoding LlpAs has been found in the B. pseudomallei group, including B. mallei. LlpAs are lectin‐like bacteriocins that structurally have mannose‐binding lectin domains, precipitating mannose‐containing glycoconjugates and polysaccharides to apply their antimicrobial activity (Rodriguez‐Cisneros et al. ).

## Prevention and Control
As a disease of high global animal and zoonotic concern, glanders should be accurately and thoroughly monitored. An increasing demand for establishing integrated global, continental and national surveillance systems is being sensed, and taking concrete actions is indispensable. The best prevention and control strategy is to suppress the disease through a health approach in which testing animals, monitoring suspected human cases, and controlling the population of probable biological vectors such as M. domestica should be addressed (Al‐Ani and Roberson ). Thus, cross‐border measurements for transferring the disease and reducing close contact between humans and horses in endemic areas are fundamental actions to be taken. World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) provided disease‐specific mitigation measures for the control of six WOAH‐listed equine diseases, including African horse sickness, equine influenza, equine infectious anaemia, equine piroplasmosis, glanders and Venezuelan equine encephalomyelitis. These measures are biosecurity, health management, traceability and contingency planning. The disease‐specific mitigation measures for glanders indicate that serological tests must be accomplished in areas free from the disease, two tests for countries in which freedom from glanders for at least the past 3 years has not been approved yet, and a single test for countries that eradicated the disease or are free from the disease generally (Dominguez et al. ).
Studies using decision analysis methods are available that prioritize and sort zoonotic diseases by the emergency and importance of the disease. Glanders is among the 50 zoonotic diseases that the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) prioritized and published in 2023 for continental and country‐level surveillance systems (European Food Safety Authority et al. ). Making these programs applicable relies on the degree of multisectoral collaboration, especially at the national level (Singha, Shanmugasundaram, Saini, et al. ). Glanders is in the top eight diseases of importance for policymaking in Haryana State, India (Thukral et al. ). The control program of glanders through one health approach is also investigated in Malaysia and is included in the five animal diseases that have been worked on to establish early detection systems in Kuwait (Al‐Hemoud et al. ; Mariappan et al. ).

## Vaccine Development
As we face the intrinsic resistance of B. mallei to antibiotics, developing vaccines against this bacterium should be at the centre of attention (Wang et al. ). By the time that this review was written, there was no licensed and commercially available vaccine against pathogenic species of Burkholderia (Grund et al. ). The candidate platforms for developing vaccines against Burkholderia infections are inactivated whole‐cell vaccines, live‐attenuated vaccines, subunit vaccines such as outer membrane protein (OMP) vaccines, recombinant non‐membrane protein vaccines and glycoconjugate vaccines including gold nano‐glycoconjugates (AuNPs). DNA and viral vector vaccines are also on the table (Biryukov et al. ; Grund et al. ; Tapia et al. ; Wang et al. ). The cost‐effectiveness evaluation and measurement of developing vaccines against Burkholderia infections should be based on economic and occupational issues and must be available for low‐income populations since these infections are also called ‘poor man's disease’ (Choh et al. ). Peacock et al. ( ) showed that vaccination against Burkholderia infections is relatively more cost‐effective than other preventive and therapeutic processes.
11.1

## Inactivated Whole‐Cell Vaccines
A study reported a killed whole‐cell vaccine from Russia, using B. mallei strain 11 with formaldehyde inactivation and aluminium hydroxide as adjuvant. The vaccine had passed Phases I and II clinical trials and resulted in a considerable antibody titre in 53.6% of human cases, 6 months post‐vaccination (Feodorova et al. ).

11.2

## Live‐Attenuated Vaccines
Different attenuation procedures that have been applied on B. mallei to elicit an efficient vaccine are mutations in iron transport (tonB) genes (Mott et al. ), amino acid synthesis pathways (ilvl) (Ulrich et al. ), endoproteases (ctpA) (Bandara et al. ) and quorum sensing (bmal3) (Aschenbroich et al. ; Ulrich et al. ). Live‐attenuated vaccines include capsular and auxotrophic mutants of B. mallei (Bondi and Goldberg ). Immunization with a capsular mutant produced a high antibody titre but did not protect mice against high doses of wild‐type organisms by aerosol route because the capsular mutant did not result in a mucosal antibody response. On the other hand, using an auxotrophic mutant of B. mallei led to an IgG response and transient survival protection against the wild‐type challenge, previously done on the capsular mutant vaccine. Auxotrophic mutants are a better choice and more efficacious (Silva and Dow ). The use of B. mallei double mutant (ΔtonBΔhcp1) strain CLH001 as a live‐attenuated vaccine against glanders in murine models appeared to be effective. It also provided reasonable proving that live‐attenuated vaccines are the most effective vaccines for protection against Burkholderia infections including glanders (Khakhum et al. ). A research elucidated that recombinant Salmonella typhimurium SL3261 expressing B. mallei O antigen against intranasal infection with B. thailandensis, a surrogate for Burkholderia species in a murine model that is able to simulate glanders, protected models significantly (Moustafa et al. ).

11.3

## Subunit Vaccines
In comparison to live‐attenuated vaccines, subunit vaccines have a great advantage in reducing safety issues because the antigens and epitopes are used instead of the whole cell. Outer membrane vesicle and OMP vaccines have been analysed and listed to determine whether they are sufficiently immunogenic against the disease by further investigation. Some of these proteins, such as secretins, efflux pumps, metal transport receptors, polysaccharide exporters and proteins with undefined action, are different types of porins. These are probable candidates for developing new vaccines in the future (Schell et al. ). Interestingly, a study has clarified that the B. pseudomallei outer membrane vesicles can cross‐protect mice and nonhuman primate (NHP) models from glanders (Baker et al. ). Recombinant antigenic proteins can play a significant role in immunization against glanders. BopA, LolC and Hcp1 are paid the most attention among these proteins (Titball et al. ). New laboratory examinations introduced a dominant platform that is probably effective against glanders in mice. This platform is a capsule conjugate + Hcp1 subunit vaccine (Biryukov et al. ). The use of BopA protein elicited protection easily comparable to that of a combined vaccine with all B. mallei subunits (Whitlock et al. ). Glycoconjugate vaccines can be effectively antigenic because bacterial polysaccharides are made up of bizarre and uncommon carbohydrates. Still, they should be conjugated and linked to proteins to produce the T‐cell‐dependent pathway and long‐lasting memory unavoidably (Wang et al. ).
Outer surface LPS O‐antigen, a linear polysaccharide formed from a repeating disaccharide with talose residue, is an exciting option to study, focused on vaccine development (Rohokale and Guo ). In an optimized formulation of gold nano vaccines, six highly immunogenic antigens were purified and attached to gold nanoparticles in combination with LPS from B. thailandensis. The OMP and haemagglutinin were then used for optimization and better immunogenicity. This vaccine completely immunized and protected mice models of inhalational glanders from mortality and produced sustained and stable antibody titres in the sera and facilitated opsonization of bacterial cells by macrophages and reduced adherence ability to lung epithelial cells in the surviving and immunized mice (Tapia et al. ). In another experiment, AuNP glycoconjugates developed protection and titres in a good percentage of vaccinated and survived rhesus macaque (Torres et al. ). A research on capsular polysaccharide (CPS)‐based glycoconjugates suggested a strategy of covalently linking carbohydrate antigens to carrier proteins and demonstrated that high titre IgG responses in BALB/c mice against CPS‐based glycoconjugates were reachable (Burtnick et al. ).

11.4

## DNA Vaccines
Immunization by DNA plasmids encoding recombinant proteins is applicable in manufacturing DNA vaccines (Wang et al. ). In a study on open reading frames (ORFs) of B. mallei, 3 out of 12 protective protein‐encoding ORFs produced immunity against experimental glanders (Whitlock et al. ). DNA vaccines must be studied more to evaluate their efficacy and ways to boost their immunogenicity (Wang et al. ).

11.5

## Viral Vector Vaccines
Viral vector vaccines are rooted in encoding the bacterial agent's antigens into viral particles that have passed attenuation. The capability of Parainfluenza virus 5 (PIV5) as a viral vector for immunization against glanders has been evaluated. Despite an acceptable immune response, the genome of the viral vector used in vaccine production has the potential to be integrated into the host genome and be neutralized by the immune system (Lafontaine et al. ; Wang et al. ). It has been shown that the PIV5 vector expressing BatA protein induced immunity in which 74% of mice survived chronic infection after an aerosol challenge of B. mallei lethal dose (Lafontaine et al. ).

## Conclusion
By reading the present review, readers will become familiar with the key aspects of glanders. Effective prevention and control require coordinated global and national strategies through unified, intersectoral measures. Reducing close contact between equids and humans in endemic areas is a critical step. In addition, medical personnel should be well trained to manage and prevent high‐casualty eruptions and re‐emergence of the disease, due to the limited experience, and insufficient preparedness for the glanders disease. Routine surveillance and testing of equids must also be maintained by veterinary organizations. Recent KAP (Knowledge, Awareness and Perception) studies conducted among veterinary and medical professionals revealed that these two groups need to be more educated on different aspects of glanders. Successful control of this zoonotic disease demands close collaboration between veterinary and human medicine, as emphasized decades ago.
Another challenge is antimicrobial resistance, as is a significant issue, and the aetiologic agent of glanders exhibits intrinsic resistance to many antibiotics. Therefore, the development of effective vaccines for both humans and equids should be a research priority, since this approach can reduce disease burden, mortality and the costs of antibiotic therapies. Live‐attenuated vaccines play a leading role in preventing glanders in the future; however, their use may complicate diagnosis. Thus, the development of reliable diagnostic assays capable of differentiating infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA tests) will be essential to ensure accurate surveillance and disease management.
