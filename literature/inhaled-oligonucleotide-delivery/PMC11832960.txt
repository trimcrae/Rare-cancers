## Abstract
This present study carried out a systematic review and meta-analysis of peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management for improved patient outcomes from 2014 to 2024. Different electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ResearchGate, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library, were searched for relevant literature using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and boolean operators. A total of 317 articles were obtained and include PUBMED (39), Scopus (215), ResearchGate (30), Google Scholar (25), and Cochrane Library (8). From these, 186 duplicate entries were eliminated, while 76 articles were dismissed for some reasons. After scanning the titles, abstracts, and contents of the remaining 55 articles for relevance, 22 articles were eliminated. After a full-text screening using inclusion/exclusion criteria, an additional 11 articles were discarded, while 4 were excluded during the data extraction phase. In the end, seven (7) publications were considered relevant based on the eligibility criteria, representing 2.22%. Results showed that sequential exclusion of the studies did not have a significant impact on the effects of peptide nanoparticles on glucose control, insulin delivery, bioavailability, efficacy, safety, and patient outcomes in diabetes management. Also, peptide nanoparticles had positive improvement on glycemic control, insulin levels, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C) levels, and overall patient outcomes. The study concludes that peptide nanoparticles harbour the potential to improve diabetes management through enhanced glucose control, insulin delivery, and patient outcomes. However, there is a significant gap in knowledge. Further research is required to understand the long-term safety and efficacy of many of the enlisted nanoparticles. Additionally, future studies should explore a wider range of peptides and proteins for encapsulation, develop delivery systems for larger and conformationally diverse molecules, and improve the oral bioavailability of encapsulated therapeutics. Long-term clinical trials are needed to validate this approach in humans and elucidate the underlying mechanisms for optimal treatment design. If these knowledge gaps are addressed, peptide nanoparticles will unavoidably become a powerful tool for effective management of diabetes along with traditional methods.
Keywords: Peptide-nanoparticles, Diabetes, Bioavailability, Insulin, Exenatide

## Highlights

The study performed a systematic review and meta-analysis on the use of peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management for the past decade, from 2014 to 2024.
The study adopted PRISMA guidelines and PICOS framework in addressing the research questions.

The results showed that the use of peptide nanoparticles was associated with improved glycemic control, insulin levels, reduced HbA1C, and overall patient outcomes in diabetes management.
The study identified various types of peptide nanoparticles, including silica-based, polymeric, liposomes, dendrimers, inorganic, and carbon-based nanomaterials, that have been used for diabetes management.
The study highlights the need for further research to fully understand the long-term effects, safety, and optimal dosage of peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management.

## Introduction
According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [ ], diabetes is a chronic disease that occurs when the pancreas does not produce enough insulin or when the body cannot effectively utilise the insulin it produces. Insulin is a hormone that regulates the blood glucose levels. Hyperglycemia, or raised blood glucose, is a common effect of uncontrolled diabetes and over time leads to a serious damage to many of the body tissues, especially the nerves and blood vessels [ ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) [ ] reported that the number of people with diabetes rose from 108 million in 1980 to 422 million in 2014. As at 2021, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [ ] reported that approximately 537 million adults (20–79 years) are living with diabetes, constituting 10.5% of the world’s population of the age group. Currently, 540 million people are living with diabetes. In fact, the total number of people living with diabetes was projected to rise to 643 million by the year 2030, and 783 million by the year 2045. Diabetes was responsible for an estimated USD 966 billion in global health expenditure in 2021 [ ]. This rapid increase, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (including African countries), shows that diabetes is an in deed, a growing global health concern.
In Africa, 1 in every 22 adults lives with diabetes—representing about 24 million adults [ ]. The total number of people living with diabetes in Africa is predicted to increase by 129% to 55 million by 2045, the highest increase of all IDF Regions [ ]. Africa has the second lowest diabetes-related expenditure (USD 13 billion), accounting for 1% of the global diabetes-related expenditure. Because over 1 in 2 (54%) people living with diabetes in Africa goes undiagnosed [ ]. Diabetes is a major cause of blindness, kidney failure, heart attacks, stroke, and lower limb amputation [ ]. It can also lead to other serious conditions, affecting nearly every major organ in the body [ ]. Liu et al. [ ] reported a 3% increase in diabetes mortality rates between 2000 and 2019. In fact, in 2019, diabetes and diabetes-associated kidney disease caused an estimated 2 million deaths [ ]. Both type I and II diabetes are increasingly affecting children and teenagers [ ]. This highlights the importance of early prevention and management strategies [ ].
The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) [ ] posited that diabetes management refers to the ongoing process of monitoring and managing the blood glucose levels of individuals diagnosed with diabetes. It involves a combination of lifestyle modifications, dietary adjustments, and medication or insulin therapy. The goal is to maintain blood glucose levels within a target range, prevent complications, and enhance the quality of life. Traditional management strategies, while effective to a certain extent, are not without limitations. They often require strict adherence to medication schedules, lifestyle adjustments, and continuous monitoring, which can be burdensome for patients. Moreover, these conventional treatments do not always provide optimal control of blood glucose levels, leading to long-term complications and impacting the quality of life [ ].
Interestingly, peptide nanoparticles have emerged as a significant area of interest in diabetes management and represents a significant advancement in the field of nanomedicine. Nanoparticles are nano-sized particles which can deliver drugs at targeted sites in a controlled manner, reducing drug toxicity and enhancing drug stability, solubility, absorption, permeation, and bioavailability [ ]. The use of nanoparticles can overcome problems like short half-life, low intestinal permeability, and bioavailability associated with traditional diabetes medications [ ]. The use of peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management has several advantages, including controlled delivering of insulin and other antidiabetic drugs directly to the target cells, maintenance of stable blood glucose levels over a longer period and improving the effectiveness of the treatment [ ]. Moreover, peptide nanoparticles can potentially alleviate the common side effects associated with the regular administration of pure drugs. They can also be used in the development of devices such as microneedles for improved delivery of anti-diabetes drugs. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis were undertaken to assess the use of peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management over the past one decade (2014–2024) using PRISMA method of data reporting and PICOS framework.

## Methodology
The study employs a systematic review and meta-analysis, which are well-established strategies for collecting and synthesizing data relevant to a specific research question [ ]. The research questions were formulated using the PICOS (Participants, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes, and Study Design) framework. To ensure the review is both comprehensive and accurate, the study adopted Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 standards. PRISMA 2020 facilitates high-quality systematic reviews by utilising innovative approaches in identifying, selecting, evaluating, and summarizing studies [ ]. Different electronic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ResearchGate, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library were searched for relevant literature. The search strategy involved using a combination of suitable keywords, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), and Boolean operators. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was employed to assess the quality of randomized controlled trials [ ]. For non-randomized studies, the study adapted the tool to assess the quality of observational studies. The study employs a predefined data extraction form to extract the necessary information.

## Research design
Using the PICOS framework to examine the use of peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management from 2014 to 2024, the following research questions were developed:
Population What is the demographic and clinical profile of diabetic individuals who have been managed with peptide nanoparticles?
Intervention: What is the effectiveness of peptide nanoparticles in managing diabetes in these individuals?
Comparison How do peptide nanoparticles compare with traditional diabetes management approaches in terms of effectiveness, safety, and patient outcomes?
Outcome What are the key factors influencing successful utilization of peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management, and how have these factors evolved over the past 10 years?
In order to address these research questions, a systematic literature review was conducted, through the reading of peer-reviewed articles as data sources. The data collected was subjected to qualitative and quantitative analyses, to better understand the interplay between diabetes, its management, and the roles of peptide nanoparticles.

## Search strategy
A total of 317 articles which aligned with the specified search criteria were discovered through online search, and included PUBMED (39), Scopus (215), ResearchGate (30), Google Scholar (25), and Cochrane Library (8). After eliminating 186 duplicate entries, 131 articles were left, out of which 76 articles were dismissed for various reasons. The remaining 55 articles were assessed for relevance based on their titles, abstracts, and contents. Thereafter, 22 articles were further eliminated. After a thorough full-text screening using inclusion/exclusion criteria, an additional 11 articles were discarded, and 4 were excluded during the data extraction phase (Fig.  ). In the end, seven (7) publications were considered pertinent based on the eligibility criteria, representing 2.22%. The PRISMA flowchart is illustrated in Fig.  [ ].
 To extract information from journal articles, the following keywords were utilized: (“Peptide nanoparticles” OR “Nanotechnology” OR “Diabetes management” AND “Insulin delivery” AND “Bioavailability”) AND (“Efficacy” OR “Safety” OR “Patient outcomes”) AND (from 2014 –2024). Additional keywords like “Effects of peptide nanoparticles on glucose control” AND “Nanoparticles improving insulin delivery” OR “Nanotechnology for diabetes management” were employed to refine the search. The inclusion criteria include articles published within 2014–2024 which are related to peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management, studies with both treatment and control groups. Studies that report outcome measures relevant to the research question (e.g., HbA1c levels, fasting blood glucose levels, etc.) and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Exclusion criteria include articles published before 2014, studies related to peptide nanoparticles but not linked to diabetes management, studies without both treatment and control groups, studies that do not report relevant outcome measures and non-randomized studies or observational studies.

## Study appraisal and synthesis
The methodological quality was executed using the Hoodin and Weber tool [ ]. This tool employed a systematic approach to evaluate the study designs, sample characteristics, sample size, and methodologies of various studies on peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management. The evaluation helped in the classification of the retrieved studies based on methodological qualities to enhance the overall trustworthiness and applicability of the research findings.

## Data analysis
The data analysis in this study involved a comprehensive approach to assess the impact of peptide nanoparticles on glucose control, insulin delivery, bioavailability, efficacy, safety, and patient outcomes in diabetes management. The potential effect on glucose control was analyzed using a binomial distribution, with the variance of peptide nanoparticles calculated through the binomial distribution variance. Each study was allocated a weight using the inverse variance method. Heterogeneity assessment was conducted using Cochrane’s Q test and the I2 index represented in the forest plot Fig.  , categorizing heterogeneity as low (below 25%), moderate (25%–75%), and high (above 75%). The result of the heterogeneity assessment was statistically significant (tau-squared = 5.57, H-squared = 24.89, I-squared = 0.96, Homogeneity: Q = 30.89, df = 6, P-value = 0.0), leading to the utilization of the random-effects model. Furthermore, meta-regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of peptide nanoparticles on diabetes management. Egger’s regression method for funnel plot (Fig.  ) asymmetry was employed to evaluate publication bias. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was carried out through the exclusion of studies one by one, assessing the effects of these exclusions on the pooled prevalence. All data analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS software (v. 28.0.0.1).

## Effectiveness of peptide nanoparticles in managing diabetes
In their studies, Soudry-Kochavi et al. [ ] investigated the pharmacodynamic effects of orally administered Exenatide nanoparticles embedded in gastro-resistant microparticles. The study aims to evaluate the efficacy of this drug delivery system (DDS) in improving glycemic parameters in diabetic ob/ob mice. The DDS successfully lowered blood glucose levels (BGL). The oral bioavailability of Exenatide was significantly increased when using the DDS, leading to improved glycemic control. The DDS improved the relative oral bioavailability of Exenatide, in comparison to Byetta® injection subcutaneously (SC) [ ]. The DDS also raised insulin levels, suggesting an improvement in insulin sensitivity. The DDS was found to be effective in normalizing insulin secretion, which is crucial for maintaining blood glucose levels. The DDS decreased glycated haemoglobin (HbA1C) levels, which is a key marker for long-term glycemic control. It also maintained the body weight of the mice, which is an important aspect of diabetes management.
Also, Pomposelli et al. [ ] evaluated the use of theranostic silencing nanoparticles (MN-siCas-3) in pancreatic islet transplantation, a potential treatment for type 1 diabetes. The study was conducted on a baboon model and aimed to protect the islets from apoptosis (cell death) and inflammation, which often limit the efficacy of islet transplantation. The study found that islet loss during culture prior to transplantation could be significantly reduced by incubating islets with the MN-siCas-3 probe. This was confirmed by counting viable islets before and after 48 h of incubation and by assessing DNA fragmentation using an apoptotic assay. The study demonstrated a remarkable reduction in insulin requirements in all four (4) animals in the experimental group compared to pre-transplant levels. One animal achieved complete insulin independence for the duration of the experiment. Importantly, these animals received a marginal number of islets that otherwise would not be sufficient to maintain normoglycemia. At the endpoint of the study, the livers of all the animals from the experimental group had no remarkable morphological findings, while livers from the control animals showed swelling and vacuolar degeneration of hepatic cells in the pericentral zone of the hepatic lobes. The study suggests that the use of theranostic silencing nanoparticles (MN-siCas-3) can protect and preserve precious islets from damage using RNA interference mechanism leading to a stable control of blood glucose with a marginal number of islet cells and drastic reduction in the amount of exogenous insulin. This approach shows potential for improving the outcome of islet transplantation and reduction of islet donor requirements.
In addition, Ai et al. [ ] identified specific biomarkers for early detection of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) by comparing serum peptide profile differences between GDM patients and healthy controls. The study used Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) in combination with weak cation exchange magnetic bead (WCX-MB). The study found that levels of four peptides were significantly different in GDM patients compared to healthy controls. However, the text does not mention the use or effectiveness of peptide nanoparticles in managing diabetes. Moreover, Burtea et al. [ ] primarily discussed the development of a peptide-functionalized imaging nanoprobe for targeting a specific biomarker of pancreatic beta cells, which are responsible for insulin secretion.
Guo et al., [ ] explored the use of modified nanoparticles, specifically Tat-CS-NPs (nanoparticles with cell-penetrating peptide (Tat) and amphipathic chitosan derivative (a-CS)), for enhanced oral colon absorption of insulin. This approach aims to overcome the barriers against drug diffusion and penetration in the colon, which is considered an ideal absorptive site for oral protein and peptide drug-like insulin. The Tat-CS-NPs were found to be spherical in shape and uniform, with most sizes below 100 nm. This small size enables them to easily cross physiological barriers. The study found that the cellular uptake amount and transcellular transportation performance of Tat-CS-NPs were better than those of CS-NPs and PVA-NPs. This suggests that Tat-CS-NPs could penetrate into Caco-2 cells and pass through Caco-2 cell monolayers more efficiently. The hypoglycemic effect of Tat-CS-NPs was evaluated on diabetic rats. The results showed that Tat-CS-NPs produced the most significant hypoglycemic effect among all the three nanoparticles tested. The hypoglycemic effect of Tat-CS-NPs was 1.79-fold higher than that of CS-NPs and 6.89-fold higher than that of PVA-NPs.
Ji et al. [ ] found that IN-Z-CSA/CS 0.2% nanocomposites, a type of peptide nanoparticle, exhibited higher efficacy in lowering blood glucose levels compared to other forms of insulin delivery. The relative pharmacological bioavailability was 14.12% and relative bioavailability was 15.19% after oral administration. This suggests that the bioavailability of the nanocomposites was improved by the chitosan (CS) coating, which had potential applications in oral delivery of protein therapeutics. The study demonstrated that the IN-Z-CSA/CS 0.2% nanocomposites were transported by both endocytosis and a paracellular route during transport of insulin. This facilitated the uptake of insulin, which allowed better oral absorption and potentially enhanced insulin sensitivity. In addition to improving blood glucose control and insulin sensitivity, peptide nanoparticles also showed potential in other relevant outcomes. For instance, the IN-Z-CSA/CS 0.2% nanocomposites were found to have good cellular compatibility and were suitable candidates for in vivo applications. Furthermore, in vivo toxicity studies showed that these nanocomposites had no toxicity in vivo, suggesting their safety for use.
Senduran et al. [ ] evaluated the effectiveness of orally deliverable nanoformulation of liraglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogue, loaded in Poly lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) nanoparticles (NPs) against type 2 diabetes in a rat model. The findings suggest that the liraglutide NPs could be a promising approach for managing type 2 diabetes. They were found to be effective in reducing blood glucose levels. The liraglutide NPs significantly reduced both fasting blood glucose (FBS) and postprandial blood glucose (PPBS) levels in the rats. The liraglutide NPs significantly increased the insulin secretion in diabetic rats, suggesting an improvement in insulin sensitivity. The liraglutide NPs significantly reduced serum fructosamine and HbA1c levels, which are indicators of long-term blood glucose control, and serum triglycerides and total cholesterol levels. They did not significantly reduce body weight, food intake, and water intake overall, but a significant reduction in food intake was observed at a certain dosage. ## Mechanism of action of peptide nanoparticle
Peptide nanoparticles (NPs) offer a cutting-edge solution for managing diabetes by overcoming key obstacles in delivering peptide-based drugs, including insulin and GLP-1 receptor agonists [ ]. These nanoscale systems employ multiple mechanisms to enhance therapeutic effectiveness. Primarily, they improve the stability of peptides in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract by encapsulating the drugs within protective nanoparticles. This encapsulation shields the peptides from acidic degradation in the stomach and enzymatic activity from proteases like pepsin and trypsin. Advanced materials, such as acid-resistant polymers and enteric coatings, help preserve the structural integrity of the peptides, ensuring they remain intact until they reach the more favourable environment of the intestine for absorption [ ].
Peptide NPs also enhance the ability of drugs to cross the intestinal mucus barrier, a major hurdle in oral drug delivery. Nanoparticles with hydrophilic and neutral surfaces, such as those coated with polyethene glycol (PEG), are designed to penetrate the mucus layer without becoming entrapped. This allows the nanoparticles to reach and interact with the epithelial cells beneath the mucus. Additionally, functionalised nanoparticles, incorporating ligands such as bile acids, transferrin, or folic acid, target specific receptors on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells. These interactions enable receptor-mediated transcytosis, allowing the nanoparticles to bypass tight junctions and improve the absorption of the drug into systemic circulation [ ].
Furthermore, some peptide NPs are designed to leverage glucose-sensitive or pH-responsive properties for precise drug release. For instance, glucose-responsive nanoparticles release their therapeutic payload in response to elevated blood glucose levels, closely mimicking the body’s natural insulin response and reducing the risk of hypoglycemia. Similarly, pH-sensitive coatings dissolve in the mildly alkaline environment of the small intestine, ensuring the drug is released at the optimal site for absorption. Additionally, certain nanoparticles transiently modulate the intestinal barrier by opening tight junctions, thereby enhancing paracellular transport of the peptide drugs. Chitosan-based nanoparticles, for example, interact with negatively charged cell membranes to temporarily relax tight junctions and facilitate drug [ ].
The small size and tailored surface properties of peptide nanoparticles further improve their cellular uptake and transport into the bloodstream. Lipid-based nanoparticles, which resemble cell membranes, efficiently fuse with epithelial cells, facilitating drug delivery. Some formulations incorporate zwitterionic polymers that interact effectively with intestinal transporters, such as the proton-assisted amino acid transporter (PAT1), to optimize absorption. Collectively, these properties make peptide nanoparticles a highly promising approach to improving the efficacy and convenience of diabetes treatments [ ].

## Safety profile of peptide nanoparticles
Soudry-Kochavi et al. [ ] reported a nonsignificant adverse effect during their study, except for some accidental mortalities, which were not associated with the formulations but rather to other unexpected causes. Ji et al. [ ] conducted in vivo toxicity studies to evaluate the safety of insulin-loaded zein-carboxymethylated short-chain amylose (IN-Z-CSA) nanocomposites coated with chitosan (CS). These nanocomposites were designed to improve the oral bioavailability of insulin, a peptide hormone used in the management of diabetes. They conducted both acute and subacute oral toxicity studies in mice. In the acute oral toxicity study, all mice survived the observational period and there were no significant differences in body weights between the treated and control mice. In the subacute oral toxicity study, the body weights of the treated mice were similar to those of the control group during the 28-day observational period. The weights of primary organs (heart, liver, kidneys, and spleen) were found to be normal. The heamatological parameters and blood biochemical values of the treated groups showed no obvious changes from those of the control group. Histological analysis of the primary organs of the mice in all groups also showed no obvious signs of damage. These results suggest that the IN-Z-CSA/CS 0.2% nanocomposites have a good safety profile and are a promising formulation for the oral delivery of insulin.
Senduran et al. [ ] reported that the liraglutide NPs also have a good safety profile. Pomposelli et al. [ ] reported that all animals tolerated the islet infusion well and exhibited a stable appetite and appropriate clinical behaviour. Liver function, kidney tests, and complete blood count (CBC) were overall normal and similar between the two groups throughout the experiment. This suggests that the siRNA as part of the probe had no clinical impact on the animal health after the infusion.

## Commonly used peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management
Different types of nanomaterials (Fig.  ) have attracted a wide range of biomedical applications. Peptide nanoparticles have shown great potential for diabetes management due to their ability to encapsulate and deliver insulin in a controlled manner. These nanoparticles can mimic the function of pancreatic beta cells, providing a more targeted and efficient treatment for diabetes. Recent research by Zashikhina et al. [ ] demonstrated the successful use of peptide nanoparticles for sustained and controlled release of insulin, leading to improved blood glucose control in diabetic animal models. This innovative approach holds promise for the development of advanced therapies for diabetes management. Table gives a summary of these nanoparticles with their efficacies and limitations.
 

## Silica-based nanoparticles
Silica-based nanoparticles have garnered significant attention for their potential to manage glycemic control and enhance the delivery of diabetic medications [ ]. These nanocarriers are particularly effective for oral insulin delivery, addressing the challenges posed by enzymatic degradation and low bioavailability in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Protein corona liposomes, a specialized form of silica-based systems, have demonstrated the ability to overcome mucus and epithelial barriers, facilitating efficient insulin absorption and systemic delivery [ ]. Additionally, these nanoparticles can be functionalized with surface modifications to improve mucoadhesion and interaction with epithelial cells, further enhancing their therapeutic potential insulin, silica-based nanocarriers have been utilized for the delivery of other antidiabetic agents such as metformin and exenatide [ ]. These systems ensure the stability of therapeutic peptides and provide controlled release mechanisms, reducing the frequency of dosing and improving patient compliance. Innovations such as bile-acid-polymer nanocarriers have also shown promise in metabolic and immunomodulatory control, particularly in type 1 diabetes [ ]. By leveraging these advanced formulations, silica-based nanoparticles offer a versatile platform for addressing the limitations of traditional oral peptide therapies, paving the way for more effective diabetes management.

## Polymeric nanoparticles
Polymeric nanoparticles, including PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) and chitosan-based systems, have emerged as versatile tools for drug delivery, particularly in diabetes management [ ]. These nanoparticles are known for their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ability to provide controlled and targeted drug release. PLGA nanoparticles, for example, have been used to encapsulate insulin and other antidiabetic drugs, offering protection against enzymatic degradation in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and improving bioavailability [ ]. Modifications such as surface coating with hydrophilic polymers or the addition of targeting ligands enhance the stability and specificity of these nanoparticles, making them suitable for oral and systemic delivery of diabetic medications.
Chitosan-based nanoparticles exhibit unique properties, including mucoadhesiveness and the ability to transiently open tight junctions in epithelial cells, facilitating paracellular transport [ ]. These nanoparticles can also be functionalized with pH-sensitive coatings, ensuring drug release in the small intestine rather than the stomach. Advanced formulations combining chitosan with other polymers or complexing agents have demonstrated improved insulin bioavailability and sustained hypoglycemic effects in preclinical studies [ ]. Their versatility in encapsulating both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs further underscores their potential in developing innovative treatments for diabetes.

## Liposomes and dendrimers
Liposomes, composed of phospholipid bilayers, have long been explored for their capacity to encapsulate both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs [ ]. In diabetes management, liposomes have been used to deliver insulin and other therapeutic agents through various routes, including oral, nasal, and pulmonary [ ]. Their biocompatibility and ability to enhance drug stability and bioavailability make them particularly effective. Additionally, liposomes can be functionalized with targeting ligands, such as biotin or transferrin, to improve site-specific delivery, thereby minimizing systemic side effects and enhancing therapeutic outcomes [ ].
Dendrimers, on the other hand, are hyperbranched polymeric structures that offer precise control over size, shape, and surface functionality [ ]. Poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers have shown promise in improving the solubility and stability of diabetic drugs. Their ability to mimic hypoglycemic drug actions and provide sustained release has been demonstrated in preclinical models [ ]. Furthermore, dendrimers can be conjugated with ligands to target specific tissues or cells, such as pancreatic beta cells, making them a promising tool for developing targeted therapies for diabetes.

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Inorganic nanoparticles, including gold, silica, and magnetic nanoparticles, offer unique properties that make them valuable in diabetes treatment [ ]. Gold nanoparticles, for instance, can be functionalized with peptides or other ligands to enable targeted delivery of insulin and other antidiabetic drugs. Their biocompatibility and ability to enhance drug stability and absorption have been demonstrated in preclinical studies [ ]. Silica nanoparticles, with their large surface area and tunable pore sizes, provide controlled release of encapsulated drugs and can be modified with mucoadhesive polymers to improve interaction with the GI tract [ ].
Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) stand out for their magnetic properties, which can be harnessed for targeted drug delivery using external magnetic fields. These nanoparticles can be engineered to deliver drugs specifically to the pancreas or other relevant tissues, reducing off-target effects and enhancing therapeutic efficiency [ ]. Their potential for use in non-invasive imaging and monitoring further adds to their value in diabetes research and treatment.

## Carbon-based nanomaterials
Carbon-based nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and quantum dots (QDs), are gaining attention for their unique mechanical, electrical, and optical properties. CNTs have been explored for their ability to deliver drugs, including insulin, directly to target cells due to their high surface area and functional ability [ ]. Their capacity to penetrate cellular membranes makes them suitable for intracellular delivery of therapeutic agents, including siRNA for gene silencing applications in diabetes.
Quantum dots, with their fluorescence properties, are particularly valuable for imaging and monitoring drug delivery in real-time. These nanomaterials can be conjugated with biomolecules, such as peptides or antibodies, to enable targeted delivery and visualization of therapeutic effects [ ]. Their high surface-to-volume ratio allows for the attachment of multiple functional groups, making them versatile carriers for diabetes treatment and research.

## Key factors influencing the successful utilization of peptide nanoparticles for diabetes management

## Peptide and protein structures
The structural and functional properties of peptides and proteins play a crucial role in enhancing the performance of nanotechnology-based treatments for diabetes. The sequences and configurations of peptides provide not only biological stability but also physical resilience to nano-constructs [ ]. These structures ensure that nanoparticles remain functional in challenging environments, such as the acidic and enzymatic conditions of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract [ ]. By leveraging specific peptide sequences, nanoparticles can be engineered to target particular cells or tissues, improving their therapeutic specificity and minimizing off-target effects. Additionally, the interaction of peptide-based nanoparticles with cellular components influences their distribution within tissues, which is essential for achieving effective drug delivery.
Furthermore, peptides and proteins contribute to the self-assembly of nanoparticles, a process that simplifies production and enhances biocompatibility. Polypeptides can form stable nanoparticles through non-covalent interactions, creating delivery systems with precise structural configurations. This self-assembly capability is critical for tailoring the drug release profile, allowing for sustained or targeted drug delivery. For example, peptide-based nanoparticles can induce and regulate specific biological responses, such as triggering insulin release in response to glucose levels. The versatility of peptide structures makes them an integral component in developing innovative and efficient nanotechnological approaches to diabetes management.

## Nanoparticle formulation
The formulation of nanoparticles significantly impacts their efficacy in diabetes treatment. A well-designed nanoparticle system enhances the bioavailability of peptide drugs by protecting them from degradation and facilitating their absorption [ ]. Nanoparticles are engineered to overcome challenges such as poor solubility and enzymatic degradation, ensuring that the encapsulated drug remains stable until it reaches the target site. For instance, solid lipid nanoparticles and nanostructured lipid carriers are known for their ability to encapsulate hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, offering sustained release and improved therapeutic outcomes. By addressing the limitations of traditional drug delivery methods, such as low potency and poor specificity, nanoparticle formulations enhance the therapeutic index of peptide-based treatments [ ].
In addition to stability, the surface properties and size of nanoparticles are critical factors influencing their interaction with biological barriers. For example, nanoparticles with hydrophilic surfaces and optimized size ranges can navigate the mucus layer of the GI tract more effectively. Functionalized nanoparticles, which include ligands for specific receptors, enhance the targeting capability, ensuring that the drug is delivered precisely where it is needed. Furthermore, nanoparticles can be engineered to release their payload in response to environmental stimuli, such as pH or glucose levels, allowing for controlled and sustained drug delivery. These features collectively make nanoparticle formulations a transformative tool in managing diabetes.

## Targeting mechanisms
Targeting mechanisms are a cornerstone of the success of peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management. Active targeting involves modifying the surface of nanoparticles with ligands that bind specifically to receptors expressed on target cells or tissues [ ]. For instance, nanoparticles functionalized with transferrin, insulin, or low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor ligands can achieve precise delivery to pancreatic or hepatic tissues. This targeted approach not only enhances the therapeutic effect but also minimizes systemic side effects, making the treatment safer and more effective. Similarly, the use of cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) allows nanoparticles to traverse cellular membranes, overcoming barriers such as the blood–brain or intestinal epithelium, further enhancing drug delivery efficiency.
Passive targeting, based on the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, also plays a significant role. By tailoring the size, charge, and hydrophobicity of nanoparticles, researchers can improve their ability to accumulate in specific tissues. For example, nanoparticles with a neutral or slightly positive charge can penetrate the negatively charged mucus layer of the intestine, while hydrophobic coatings facilitate interaction with epithelial cells. These targeting strategies are essential for ensuring that peptide drugs, such as insulin or GLP-1 receptor agonists, reach their intended sites of action with minimal loss of activity during transit [ ].

## Peptide modification
Peptide modification is a critical strategy for improving the stability and efficacy of peptide nanoparticles. Modifications such as PEGylation (attachment of polyethylene glycol chains) or conjugation with stabilizing agents help extend the plasma half-life of peptide drugs, reducing their degradation by enzymes in the bloodstream [ ]. These modifications also decrease the frequency of administration, improving patient compliance and convenience. Additionally, altering the peptide sequence or structure can enhance its affinity for specific receptors, boosting the therapeutic effect of the drug. For example, glucose-sensitive peptides can be incorporated into nanoparticles to enable drug release in response to fluctuating blood glucose levels.
Another important aspect of peptide modification is its role in addressing the limitations of traditional peptide therapies. Structural changes can reduce the immunogenicity of peptide drugs, lowering the risk of adverse immune responses [ ]. Furthermore, modified peptides can improve the interaction between nanoparticles and biological barriers, such as epithelial cells or mucus layers. This ensures that the encapsulated drug is absorbed efficiently and reaches its target site intact. These advancements in peptide modification not only optimize the performance of nanoparticles but also pave the way for more effective and patient-friendly diabetes treatments [ ].

## Necessity and characteristics of nanoparticle systems
The necessity for peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management stems from the limitations of conventional therapies, such as insulin injections and oral hypoglycemic agents. Traditional methods often involve invasive administration routes, low bioavailability, and significant side effects [ ]. Nanoparticle systems address these issues by offering non-invasive delivery options, such as oral or nasal routes, and improving the stability and absorption of peptide drugs. For instance, nanoparticles protect peptides from enzymatic degradation in the GI tract and allow for controlled release at the site of absorption [ ]. This improves the therapeutic efficiency and reduces the required dosage, enhancing patient compliance.
The characteristics of effective nanoparticle systems include pH sensitivity, mucoadhesiveness, and biocompatibility. pH-sensitive nanoparticles are designed to remain stable in the acidic environment of the stomach and release their payload in the more neutral pH of the intestine [ ]. Mucoadhesive properties allow nanoparticles to adhere to the intestinal lining, prolonging their retention time and facilitating drug absorption. Biocompatibility ensures that the materials used in nanoparticles are safe and do not cause adverse reactions in the body [ ]. These features, combined with advanced targeting and release mechanisms, make nanoparticle systems an indispensable tool in modern diabetes management.

## Administration routes for peptide nanoparticles in diabetes management
The administration routes for peptide nanoparticles are critical in their design and effectiveness for managing diabetes. Each route presents unique challenges and advantages, influencing patient compliance, bioavailability, and therapeutic outcomes.

## Oral administration
Oral administration is highly favoured for its non-invasive nature and patient convenience. However, delivering peptide-based drugs like insulin orally presents significant challenges due to the harsh gastrointestinal (GI) environment, which includes acidic pH and digestive enzymes that can degrade peptides before they reach systemic circulation [ ]. To address these obstacles, various nanoparticle (NP) systems have been developed to protect peptide drugs from degradation and enhance their absorption. For instance, pH-sensitive nanoparticles remain stable in the stomach's acidic conditions and release their payload in the more neutral pH of the intestine, improving drug stability and bioavailability [ ].
Despite these advancements, achieving efficient oral delivery of peptide drugs remains challenging. The bioavailability of such formulations is often low, necessitating higher doses or frequent administration, which can be impractical. Ongoing research focuses on optimizing nanoparticle characteristics, such as size, surface charge, and mucoadhesive properties, to enhance intestinal absorption and therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Combining nanoparticles with absorption enhancers or enzyme inhibitors is also being explored to further improve oral bioavailability. A comprehensive review of these strategies highlights the potential of nanoparticle-based oral delivery systems in diabetes treatment, though clinical translation requires overcoming significant hurdles.

## Subcutaneous administration
Subcutaneous injection is the traditional route for administering peptide-based therapeutics like insulin, offering rapid absorption and high bioavailability [ ]. However, this method is invasive and can lead to patient discomfort, needle phobia, and poor adherence to treatment regimens. To mitigate these issues, nanoparticle formulations have been developed to provide controlled and sustained release of peptide drugs, potentially reducing the frequency of injections and improving patient compliance [ ]. For example, encapsulating insulin in biodegradable polymer nanoparticles can prolong its release, maintaining therapeutic levels over extended periods.
Despite these benefits, subcutaneous administration of nanoparticle-based formulations faces challenges, including potential immunogenicity and the need for precise control over drug release kinetics. Additionally, the manufacturing complexity and cost of nanoparticle formulations may hinder widespread adoption [ ]. Ongoing research aims to address these challenges by developing biocompatible materials and scalable production methods to enhance the feasibility of nanoparticle-based subcutaneous delivery systems for diabetes management.

## Nasal administration
Nasal delivery offers a non-invasive alternative for the systemic administration of peptide drugs, leveraging the nasal mucosa's rich vascularization for rapid absorption. This route bypasses the GI tract and first-pass metabolism, potentially enhancing bioavailability. Nanoparticle carriers can further improve nasal delivery by protecting peptides from enzymatic degradation and facilitating their transport across the mucosal barrier. Studies have demonstrated that nasal administration of insulin-loaded nanoparticles can effectively reduce blood glucose levels, indicating its potential for diabetes treatment [ ].
However, nasal delivery faces challenges such as limited absorption surface area, mucociliary clearance, and variability in absorption due to nasal pathologies. Formulation strategies, including the use of mucoadhesive materials and permeation enhancers, are being explored to overcome these barriers [ ]. Additionally, patient factors like nasal congestion can affect drug absorption, necessitating consideration in clinical applications [ ]. Further research is needed to optimize nanoparticle formulations for nasal delivery to ensure consistent and effective therapeutic outcomes.

## Pulmonary administration
Pulmonary delivery utilizes the extensive surface area of the lungs for rapid systemic absorption of peptide drugs. Inhalable nanoparticles can deliver insulin directly to the bloodstream, offering a non-invasive alternative to injections. This method has been explored with formulations like Exubera® and Afrezza®, which deliver rapid-acting insulin via inhalation. Nanoparticle carriers can enhance the stability and absorption of inhaled peptides, potentially improving glycemic control in diabetic patients.
Despite its potential, pulmonary delivery faces challenges, including variability in lung deposition due to differences in inhalation techniques, potential respiratory side effects, and the need for specialized inhalation devices. Additionally, long-term safety data are limited, and concerns about pulmonary function have been raised. Advancements in nanoparticle technology aim to address these issues by developing formulations that ensure uniform lung distribution, minimize local irritation, and provide controlled drug release. Ongoing research focuses on optimizing these delivery systems to enhance their efficacy and safety profiles for diabetes management.

## Transdermal administration
Transdermal delivery offers a non-invasive route for systemic administration of peptide drugs, providing sustained release and improved patient compliance [ ]. However, the skin's stratum corneum presents a significant barrier to peptide absorption. Microneedle (MN) technology has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome this hurdle. MN-based systems create transient microchannels in the skin, facilitating the delivery of insulin and other peptide drugs without reaching nerve endings, thus minimizing pain [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of MN-mediated transdermal insulin delivery in achieving glycaemic control.
Despite these advancements, challenges remain in ensuring consistent drug delivery, preventing skin irritation, and developing cost-effective manufacturing processes for MN devices [ ]. Additionally, patient acceptance and proper usage are critical factors for the success of transdermal systems. Ongoing research focuses on optimizing MN design, exploring biodegradable materials, and integrating biosensing capabilities to create responsive transdermal delivery systems that can adjust insulin release based on real-time glucose monitoring, potentially revolutionizing diabetes management [ ].

## Discussion
The demographic and clinical profiles of individuals with diabetes managed using peptide nanoparticles primarily included patients requiring enhanced glucose control and insulin delivery. Studies reviewed encompassed both type 1 and type 2 diabetes populations, with a focus on glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and long-term management outcomes. Additionally, experimental models, such as diabetic mice and baboons, provided foundational insights into the translational potential of peptide nanoparticles for human applications. Peptide nanoparticles demonstrated significant effectiveness in diabetes management. These systems improved glucose control, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and stabilized glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels. Advanced formulations, such as chitosan-coated nanoparticles and glucose-responsive delivery systems, were particularly effective in maintaining long-term glycemic control. Furthermore, these nanoparticles enhanced the bioavailability and stability of therapeutic agents like insulin and exenatide, addressing limitations associated with traditional drug delivery methods.
When compared to conventional diabetes management strategies, peptide nanoparticles exhibited superior outcomes in efficacy, safety, and patient adherence. For instance, nanoparticle-based systems provided more consistent glucose control with reduced dosing frequency. They also minimized adverse effects commonly associated with traditional therapies, such as multiple injections. The targeted delivery offered by nanoparticles improved the therapeutic index while reducing systemic side effects, positioning them as a promising alternative to traditional methods. Key factors influencing the successful utilization of peptide nanoparticles include their ability to encapsulate and stabilize drugs, targeted delivery capabilities, and biocompatibility. Over the past decade, significant advancements have been made in nanoparticle technology, including pH-sensitive coatings, mucoadhesive properties, and ligand-functionalized surfaces, which have enhanced their performance and broadened their applications. Research has also expanded to include a wider range of peptide drugs, improved bioavailability, and reduced manufacturing costs. Despite these advancements, challenges remain, such as the need for long-term safety data and clinical validation, underscoring the importance of continued research in this field.

## Conclusion and further research
In conclusion, peptide nanoparticles hold promise for improving diabetes management through enhanced glucose control, insulin delivery, and patient outcomes. However, significant gaps in knowledge remain. Further research is crucial to understand the long-term safety and efficacy of many of these nanoparticles. Additionally, future studies should explore a wider range of peptides and proteins for encapsulation, develop delivery systems for larger and conformationally diverse molecules, and improve the oral bioavailability of encapsulated therapeutics. Long-term clinical trials are needed to validate this approach in humans and elucidate the underlying mechanisms for optimal treatment design. If these knowledge gaps are addressed, peptide nanoparticles will unavoidably become a powerful tool for effective management of diabetes along with traditional methods.
