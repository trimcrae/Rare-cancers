## Abstract

## Background
Fetal lung development is crucial for neonatal respiratory function, with insufficient maturation contributing to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome. While maternal vitamin D deficiency (VDD) has been linked to adverse offspring outcomes, the impact of paternal VDD on fetal lung programming remains largely unexplored. This study investigates the effects of paternal VDD on fetal lung development and maturation, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for paternal preconception nutritional intervention and highlight the importance of paternal health in offspring respiratory outcomes.

## Methods
Male C57BL/6J mice were gavaged with diets containing vitamin D₃ (0 or 1000 IU kg⁻¹/d) for 90 days. Serum vitamin D concentrations were measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Treated males were then mated with females to generate offspring. Mating outcomes were recorded to assess reproductive success. Fetal lungs were collected at embryonic days 15.5–17.5 for H&E staining, morphometric analysis. Interrogation of a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset delineated cell-type-specific marker gene signatures and prioritized candidate transcription factors, whose expression patterns were subsequently validated in offspring under paternal VDD conditions by quantitative real-time PCR, Western blotting, and multiplexed fluorescence immunohistochemistry.

## Results
In mice, paternal VDD did not significantly affect mating success or pregnancy establishment. However, paternal VDD led to fetal lung dysplasia, accompanied by reduced lung weight and distal alveolar expansion. Alveolar type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) markers were markedly downregulated, reflecting delayed distal epithelial maturation. Guided by analysis of a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset, Tfcp2l1 was prioritized as a candidate transcription factor. Subsequent experimental validation revealed its predominant localization to AT1 and AT2 cells, with downregulation associated with decreased AT2 proliferation, a concomitant reduction in AT1 abundance, and limited AT1 expansion.

## Conclusions
Paternal VDD is associated with altered fetal lung development, characterized by reduced lineage specification of distal alveolar epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2) and diminished alveolar sacculation. The transcription factor Tfcp2l1 may be involved in this process, with its downregulation under paternal VDD correlating with reduced AT2 cell proliferation and decreased AT2-to-AT1 differentiation, ultimately relating to insufficient pulmonary maturation.

## Background
Fetal lung development is closely linked to neonatal respiratory diseases, such as neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS), which is a leading cause of neonatal mortality worldwide, affecting approximately 1% of newborns and accounting for 20–30% of neonatal deaths [ , ]. Beyond postnatal supportive interventions, understanding the intrauterine developmental processes of the fetal lung has become a key focus in perinatal medicine. Traditional studies have highlighted the importance of maternal nutritional status in shaping fetal lung development, with maternal vitamin D deficiency (VDD) shown to impair fetal pulmonary growth and maturation [ , ]. Meanwhile, a growing body of evidence indicates that paternal nutritional status also exerts notable effects on offspring health, influencing metabolic homeostasis, cardiovascular function, and organ development [ – ]. Among key micronutrients, vitamin D (VD) has emerged as a critical factor in intergenerational health, particularly in the context of paternal nutrition [ , ]. This study investigates the impact of paternal VDD on fetal lung development, aiming to elucidate its mechanisms and implications for offspring respiratory health.
The development of the fetal lung is a multistep progression that spans the pseudoglandular, canalicular, and saccular phases [ ]. During these stages, various cellular and molecular mechanisms are at play, ensuring proper lung formation and maturation. Alterations in these developmental events may result in respiratory distress in newborns and predispose individuals to chronic pulmonary disorders later in life. While maternal nutrition has been extensively studied in relation to fetal pulmonary development, the role of paternal nutrition, particularly VD status, remains poorly understood. Recent studies have begun to uncover the importance of paternal dietary factors in programming offspring health, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive understanding of paternal contributions to fetal development [ , ].
VD is a lipid-soluble secosteroid essential for regulating calcium–phosphate homeostasis and maintaining skeletal health. Beyond its classical role, VD has been implicated in immune regulation, metabolic balance, and fetal organogenesis [ – ]. The active form of VD, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)₂D), binds to the vitamin D receptor (VDR), which is expressed in various tissues, including the lungs. VDR influences a broad array of genes linked to growth, lineage specification, and cell survival, positioning it as a crucial determinant of lung development and maturation [ ]. Evidence from previous studies indicates that maternal VDD is linked to compromised fetal lung development and increased susceptibility to respiratory diseases in offspring [ , ]. However, the mechanisms by which paternal VDD influences fetal lung development remain largely unexplored.
VDD is a widespread public health issue, affecting populations across all age groups. While VDD is commonly associated with bone-related disorders, its implications extend to various other physiological processes. In pregnancy, VDD has been linked to multiple unfavorable gestational conditions, notably preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and restricted fetal growth [ ]. Moreover, inadequate maternal VD levels during gestation are associated with a heightened likelihood of respiratory conditions in offspring, including asthma and wheezing [ , ]. Although numerous investigations have examined how VDD affects fetal well-being, research has predominantly centered on maternal status, leaving the paternal contribution insufficiently characterized. Emerging literature has started to address this gap by demonstrating that paternal dietary status can modulate sperm integrity and epigenetic features, thereby shaping subsequent embryonic and fetal developmental trajectories [ ]. Paternal VDD, in particular, has been implicated in altered sperm function and epigenetic modifications that may affect offspring health [ , ]. However, the specific mechanisms by which paternal VDD impacts fetal lung development remain unclear.
To address this knowledge gap, we investigated the impact of paternal VDD on fetal lung growth and maturation. To this end, a male VDD mouse model was established through dietary and ultraviolet (UV) restriction, and fetuses were obtained via paternal inheritance. Mice, as a well-established model for VDD research, share high genetic similarity with humans, and their fetal lung development closely mirrors key stages of human lung maturation [ , ]. This model therefore provides a reliable foundation for examining the impact of paternal VDD on offspring lung development. Furthermore, integration of open-access single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data with in vivo analyses enabled the identification of transcriptional regulators potentially involved in these effects. We hypothesized that paternal VDD is associated with altered fetal lung development, potentially involving changes in distal alveolar epithelial lineage specification, and that the transcriptional regulator may act as a candidate regulatory factor of this process. By uncovering the molecular mechanisms involved, this study provides novel perspectives on the intergenerational influence of paternal nutrition on respiratory health and offers a theoretical basis for targeted interventions to improve offspring outcomes.

## Methods

## Animals and dietary intervention
Experimental workflow and timeline, including dietary intervention, mating procedures, and offspring sampling, are summarized in Fig.  . Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Guangdong Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), and females from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China). All animals were genetically unmodified, immunocompetent, and experimentally naïve. All experimental procedures utilizing laboratory animals were implemented at the Center for Experimental Animals, School of Medicine, Hunan Normal University, which operates under certification from the Hunan Provincial Department of Science and Technology (License SYXK-2020-0012). The project protocol was subjected to rigorous scrutiny and obtained formal authorization from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hunan Normal University (Approval HNUN-2023-502). Execution of all procedures maintained strict conformity with ethical guidelines established at institutional, national, and international levels. The pertinent approval documentation has been archived in the related files.
 Male C57BL/6J mice (4 weeks old, 17–20 g; n = 40) were kept in groups of four under controlled environmental parameters (temperature 18–22 °C, humidity 40–60%, 12 h light/dark schedule) with unrestricted access to food and water. After a one-week acclimation, mice were randomly assigned (n = 20 per group) to receive either a standard AIN-93G control diet containing 1000 IU kg⁻¹/d vitamin D₃ (#P0002) or a VDD AIN-93G diet lacking vitamin D₃ (0 IU kg⁻¹/d, #P0006). Diets were nutritionally matched for calcium (0.5%) and phosphorus (0.4%) and provided by the same vendor (Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Changsha, China). The VDD group was housed under UV A/B-restricted conditions to prevent endogenous VD synthesis. Full dietary compositions are provided (Supplementary Table , Additional File ). Group allocation was performed via a computer-generated randomization sequence using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 (v2506), ensuring allocation concealment and balance.
After 90 days of dietary intervention, serum VD analysis was performed on randomly selected mice (n = 8 per group) to confirm successful induction of VDD. All animals, including those selected for serum analysis, subsequently proceeded to the mating phase. Blood collection was performed from the retro-orbital sinus while mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China, #792632). Samples were transferred into heparin-coated tubes and processed by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min at room temperature. Serum aliquots were then kept at − 80 °C until use. Total serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), including 25(OH)D₃, was quantified by blinded technicians using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS; Haos Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), the gold standard method offering high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy in VD assessment. In accordance with literature guidelines, successful induction of VDD was confirmed by serum 25(OH)D and 25(OH)D₃ levels falling below 15 ng/mL in tested VDD mice [ ]. No adverse events, such as respiratory depression, hypothermia, or reduced intake, were observed. Consistent with the predefined criteria, no animals warranted exclusion, and all 40 males were used for mating.

## Mating procedures
C57BL/6J females (4 weeks old, 17–20 g; n = 80) were paired at a 2:1 ratio with males from either the CON or VDD group and maintained on a standard AIN-93G diet throughout mating. Pairing occurred nightly at 17:00, with males returned to their home cages at 8:00 the following morning to minimize unintended exposure to the standard diet. Using G*Power (v3.1.9.7), the required sample size for a two-way ANOVA (fixed effects, main effects, and interactions) was calculated. An effect size of 0.60, α = 0.05, and a power of 0.87 indicated that 48 pregnancies would be necessary. To reach this endpoint, successful matings were first identified by the presence of vaginal plugs (n = 28). Pairs failing to mate were given a second opportunity after a two-week interval, yielding an additional 20 successful matings (n = 20). All vaginal plugs were designated as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). The procedure continued until 48 successful matings were achieved. Detailed mating outcomes, including mating success rate, number of attempts required, and pregnancy rates, were recorded and subsequently analyzed between groups.
Pregnant dams were housed individually in a dedicated SPF facility and monitored twice daily for health status, including body weight (every 3 days), food and water intake, activity, respiratory pattern, and coat condition. Cage positions were randomized periodically to minimize environmental confounders. Euthanasia was performed at predefined gestational time points (E15.5, E16.5, and E17.5), corresponding to key stages of fetal lung development (pseudoglandular, canalicular, and saccular, respectively), via 3% isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, serving as the humane endpoint.

## Offspring sampling and tissue collection
Fetuses were harvested by cesarean section. To avoid litter effects and ensure statistical independence, one fetus per litter was randomly selected as the experimental unit. Fetuses were classified according to paternal diet (CON or VDD) and assigned to each time point using block randomization (six groups, n = 8 per group). Each fetal sample could be traced back to its biological father, preserving the paternal–offspring correspondence for downstream analyses. Fetal biometric parameters—including body weight, crown–rump length, and lung weight—were measured and assessed by an independent researcher blinded to group allocation and experimental conditions. Lung weight was designated a priori as the primary outcome due to its sensitivity to intrauterine environmental changes and strong support in the literature [ ]. Gross morphology of fetal tissues was photographed using a smartphone camera (Apple, Cupertino, CA, USA) and calibrated with an L-shaped metric ruler placed in the field of view. For histological assessment, lung samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #G1101), whereas tissues intended for molecular analyses were promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

## Serum calcium and phosphate measurement
Serum calcium and phosphate concentrations were determined using the remaining serum aliquots collected for VD analysis. Measurements were performed using commercially available assay kits for calcium (#C004-2-1) and inorganic phosphate (#C006-1-1) obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, working reagents and standard solutions were prepared according to the provided protocols. Serum samples, standards, and working reagents were added to the appropriate wells of a 96-well microplate. After incubation for 10 min at room temperature, absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) at 610 nm for calcium and 660 nm for phosphate. Concentrations were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves.

## Histology and multiplexed fluorescence immunohistochemistry (mIHC)
Paraffin-embedded embryonic lung tissues were initially fixed by immersion in 4% PFA for 24 h and subsequently sliced into 5 μm sections. Histomorphological evaluation was carried out through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following deparaffinization and rehydration in a graded ethanol series. Observations and image acquisition were performed using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an AxioCam 503 digital camera and a Plan-Apochromat 40×/0.95 NA objective lens. Images were captured and processed using ZEN software (v3.6). In the mIHC procedure, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Servicebio, #G1219) using microwave heating. After cooling to room temperature, sections were blocked with 5% BSA (Servicebio, #GC305010) for 1 h. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in a ready-to-apply format (ABclonal, #BR70004) were added for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Signal amplification was achieved using the TSA Fluorescence Triple Staining Plus Kit (ABclonal, #RK05903P), which utilizes HRP-mediated tyramide deposition to enhance fluorescence. To enable sequential labeling, a heat-induced antigen retrieval step was performed between each round to remove previously bound antibodies. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with a DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium (Servicebio, #G1407) prior to imaging. The primary antibodies included TFCP2L1 (Affinity, 1:100), KI67 (Affinity, 1:100), AQP5 (ABclonal, 1:200), HOPX (Proteintech, 1:200), SFTPC (Proteintech, 1:200), ECAD (Huabio, 1:200), and NKX2.1 (Zenbio, 1:100). Detailed antibody information is provided in (Supplementary Table , Additional File ). mIHC slides were digitized using a Pannoramic MIDI II automated slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) configured for multichannel fluorescence imaging. Whole slide mIHC scans were acquired with Pannoramic Scanner software (v1.25) and representative imaging fields were exported using CaseViewer software (v2.4).

## Morphometric and mIHC quantification
Morphometric and mIHC analyses were performed using Fiji (ImageJ, v1.52) on three biological replicates per experimental group, following previously described protocols [ , ]. Briefly, alveolar septal thickness at E17.5, when primary septa first emerged, was measured directly along septal walls within manually selected alveolar regions. Distal airway area was quantified using Fiji’s area measurement function, excluding large proximal bronchioles (Br) and blood vessels, with images focused on alveolar regions. For morphometric evaluation, five random fields per section were captured under a 40× objective.
For mIHC analyses, at least three randomly selected fields per mouse, each comprising on the order of hundreds of cells, were assessed. Cytoplasmic, membrane, and nuclear compartments were analyzed separately to determine the number of positive cells. The proportion of marker-positive cells was calculated relative to the total number of ECAD⁺ epithelial cells. Luminal distance between neighboring AT1 cells was quantified as the intercellular distance between adjacent AQP5⁺ cells. All morphometric and mIHC measurements were performed using a consistent threshold across groups. Images from different experimental groups were presented side-by-side with consistent scale bars, as indicated in the figure legends.

## RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
At designated gestational stages, pulmonary samples were collected and subjected to RNA extraction utilizing TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, #ET101) in accordance with the supplier’s protocol. Subsequent cDNA synthesis was performed with the TransScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, #AT301) using established thermal parameters. Quantitative PCR analyses were conducted on a LightCycler 480 platform (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with PerfectStart Green qRT-PCR MasterMix (TransGen Biotech, #AQ601), employing target-specific oligonucleotides synthesized by TransGen Biotech (Supplementary Table , Additional File ). Relative transcript levels were calculated with LightCycler 480 software (v1.5.1) and normalized to Gapdh as the endogenous reference gene.

## Protein extraction and Western blot analysis
Immunoblotting assays were systematically conducted on pulmonary specimens obtained from CON and VDD neonatal cohorts to interrogate proteomic profiles. Protein lysates (20 µg) were electrophoresed through 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels, followed by electrophoretic transfer onto PVDF membranes (Servicebio, #G6044). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dairy blocking solution (1 h, ambient temperature), then probed with species-specific primary antibodies (Supplementary Table , Additional File ) under refrigerated overnight conditions. Subsequent immunodetection involved 60-minute incubation with HRP-linked secondary antibodies (Huabio, #HA1001, 1:50000). Chemiluminescent visualization was achieved using an enhanced ECL substrate (Servicebio, #G2074). Images were captured with a Tanon 5200 imager (Tanon, Shanghai, China) operated via Tanon imaging software (v4.2), and band intensities were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ, v1.52).

## Quantification of TFCP2L1 protein by ELISA
For TFCP2L1 quantification, a commercial mouse ELISA kit (Fusheng Biotech, Shanghai, China, #A127097) was employed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Experimental workflow involved dispensing 50 µL aliquots of calibrators or tissue lysates into microplate wells, followed by sequential incubation, washing, and chromogenic development steps per standard protocol. A microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) was employed to measure optical density at 450 nm, and analyte concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic model in Gen5 software (v3.10) based on serially diluted standards.

## scRNA-seq data analysis and visualization
To characterize cell type–specific gene expression patterns, open-access scRNA-seq data from C57BL/6J mouse lungs (GEO accession: GSE122332), representing normal lung development, were retrieved and analyzed following established protocols [ ]. Briefly, the distribution of major lung cell populations during normal development was visualized using the LungMAP platform (accession code: LMEX0000004397), integrating principal component analysis (PCA), uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), and heatmap visualization. To identify functionally related gene clusters, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the ToppGene Suite (https://toppgene.cchmc.org/enrichment.jsp) [ ] with an adjusted significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05. The input gene lists, specific to developmental stages and cell types, consisted of classical marker genes and transcription factors (TFs). Marker genes and TFs for epithelial cells were obtained from the LungGENS database (https://research.cchmc.org/pbge/lunggens/default.html) [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v26.0) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). The assumptions of normally distributed data (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogenous variances were examined. If the underlying assumptions were satisfied (P > 0.05), parametric tests were employed; if not, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparing groups, two-tailed independent Student’s t-tests were utilized, and bivariate relationships between paired variables were examined via Pearson’s correlation analysis. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of matings requiring a second attempt between the CON and VDD groups. Two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc adjustment was applied to evaluate quantitative data from fetal biometric measurements, qRT-PCR, and Western blot experiments. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. One fetus per litter was randomly selected as the experimental unit to ensure statistical independence. Each experiment was performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Detailed sample sizes (n) and presentation formats were provided in the corresponding figure legends.

## Results

## Paternal VDD affects fetal lung development
A male C57BL/6J mouse model of VDD was created through dietary intervention to examine the impact of paternal VDD on fetal development. Following 90 days of dietary intervention under UV A/B-restricted housing, serum VD status was assessed. Representative images demonstrated a marked reduction in total 25(OH)D and 25(OH)D₃ levels in the VDD group relative to controls (Supplementary Fig.  A and B, Additional File ). Corresponding quantitative data were reported (Supplementary Table , Additional File ), confirming successful model establishment. To evaluate potential systemic mineral disturbances, we quantified serum calcium and phosphate concentrations. No statistically significant differences were observed between the CON and VDD groups (Supplementary Fig.  C and D, Additional File ; Supplementary Tables – , Additional File ).
Throughout gestation, maternal weight trajectories appeared comparable between groups (Supplementary Fig.  E, Additional File ), which was corroborated by quantitative analysis (Supplementary Table , Additional File ), indicating that paternal VDD did not affect maternal weight gain. Mating and pregnancy outcomes were analyzed to assess the potential impact of paternal VDD on reproductive performance. No significant differences were observed between the CON and VDD groups in mating success rate, the proportion of matings requiring a second attempt, or pregnancy rate (Supplementary Table , Additional File ). To evaluate the impact of paternal VDD on the development of fetal lungs, we evaluated morphological changes and histological features of fetal lungs at three gestational timepoints (E15.5–E17.5) in CON and VDD pups’ group. Initial evaluation during the pseudoglandular phase (E15.5) demonstrated comparable pulmonary architectural profiles between experimental groups, with not statistically discernible histostructural variances. By E16.5, the lungs of VDD fetuses appeared slightly smaller, and at E17.5, they were markedly reduced in size compared to CON (Fig.  A–C). Consistently, at E17.5, VDD fetuses exhibited significantly lower body weight and length, while no notable differences were detected at earlier stages (Fig.  D, E; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Similarly, lung weights and lung-to-body weight ratios (LW/BW) were significantly decreased at E17.5 in VDD fetuses, with no significant changes at E15.5 and E16.5 (Fig.  F, G; Supplementary Table in Additional File ).
 To investigate the inherent connections between the levels of VD in fathers and the developmental outcomes of their offspring, the relationship between levels of paternal serum 25(OH)D and fetal weight was examined, revealing a noteworthy positive association (Fig.  H; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Further analysis revealed a similarly significant positive correlation between paternal serum 25(OH)D levels and fetal lung development indicators, including lung weight metrics (Fig.  I; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). These exploratory correlation analyses reveal a positive association between paternal VD status and fetal lung development parameters, providing complementary observational support for a relationship between paternal VDD and offspring lung development. H&E staining revealed no significant lung defects in VDD pups up to E16.5. At the onset of the sacculation phase (E17.5), VDD pups exhibited markedly compacted distal airspaces and significantly thickened alveolar septa (Fig.  J–L). These structural abnormalities were consistent with delayed lung maturation. Quantitative analysis confirmed that at E17.5, distal airway lumen areas were significantly reduced, while septal wall thickness was notably increased in VDD pups (Fig.  K, L), with detailed data provided (Supplementary Tables and , Additional File ). Collectively, these findings indicate that paternal VDD is associated with altered distal lung development during late gestation, characterized by restricted alveolar expansion and delayed septal thinning, both of which are critical for normal lung maturation.

## Paternal VDD and alterations in the maturation of fetal lung AT1 and AT2 cells
Around E17.5 in mice, pulmonary development enters the saccular stage, marking a shift from earlier branching morphogenesis. This transition is characterized by the enlargement of distal spaces and the initiation of differentiation in alveolar epithelial cells. Specifically, AT2 cells begin forming lamellar bodies and producing surfactants, while simultaneously facilitating the maturation of AT1 cells. AT1 cells subsequently expand to form a thin epithelial barrier essential for efficient gas exchange between alveolar spaces and capillaries. The coordinated maturation of both AT1 and AT2 cell populations is vital for normal alveologenesis during this critical developmental window [ ].
To investigate whether paternal VDD affects alveolar epithelial cell fate commitment in offspring, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from the GEO dataset GSE122332, comprising 35,247 cells from two critical time points, E16.5 and E18.5. PCA and UMAP analyses identified four major cell types during the saccular stage: endothelial cells, immune cells, epithelial cells, and mesenchymal cells (Supplementary Fig.  A and B, Additional File ). Further subclustering of epithelial cells using UMAP identified six subtypes: Sox9⁺ progenitors, secretory and ciliated cells in the proximal airways, AT1/AT2 transitional cells, and distal alveolar epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2) (Supplementary Fig.  C, Additional File ). Heatmap analysis revealed distinct marker gene expression patterns across epithelial subtypes (Fig.  A). GO enrichment analysis indicated that proximal epithelial cells were associated with cilium morphology and motility, whereas distal epithelial cells (AT1 and AT2) were enriched in genes related to alveolar morphogenesis and surfactant secretion (Fig.  B, C). The full list of enriched GO terms was provided (Supplementary Tables and , Additional File ). To further assess the effect of paternal VDD on fetal lung epithelial differentiation, we analyzed the transcriptional profiles of representative markers for proximal and distal epithelial subtypes. At E17.5, expression of proximal airway markers—secretory (Scgb1a1, Scgb3a2, Tubb6) and ciliated (Foxj1)—remained comparable to controls, indicating unaltered differentiation in VDD offspring (Fig.  D; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Nevertheless, both qRT-PCR and Western blot results demonstrated markedly decreased expression levels of cell-specific markers for AT1 and AT2 alveolar epithelial cells in the distal lung regions of VDD pups at E17.5 (Fig.  E–I), with consistent quantitative data presented (Supplementary Tables – , Additional File ). These results suggest that paternal VDD alters the expression profiles of distal alveolar epithelial cells during late embryonic development, potentially interfering with alveolar formation and lung maturation in the offspring.
 

## Paternal VDD suppresses Tfcp2l1 expression in offspring lungs during development
Lung development is a highly dynamic process that is tightly regulated by transcriptional networks, with TFs acting as essential determinants of cell fate [ ]. To investigate the potential mechanisms underlying paternal VDD induced abnormalities in offspring lung development, we hypothesized that paternal VDD diminishes alveolar sac expansion in offspring by affecting key TF, thereby resulting in delayed lung development and functional maturation. To test this hypothesis, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic datasets spanning E16.5 to postnatal day 28 (PND28), comprising 72,674 cells, to identify key TFs with time-dependent expression patterns. UMAP analysis successfully projected various lung cell types into a unified space (Supplementary Fig. A, Additional File ). Further analysis identified 21 critical TF at E16.5 and E18.5 (Supplementary Fig. B, Additional File ). Among these, 11 TF were closely associated with embryonic organ development, epithelial cell differentiation, and transcriptional regulation, as revealed by GO functional enrichment analysis. These TFs exhibited distinct time-dependent expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. C and D, Additional File ), suggesting their potential roles in epithelial cell fate determination during the saccular stage of lung development. Corresponding analysis data were provided (Supplementary Tables and , Additional File ).
To validate the functional relevance of the identified TF, we conducted qRT-PCR analysis, which revealed significantly decreased mRNA levels of Klf5, Tfcp2l1, Irx1, and Cebpa in VDD pups compared to CON pups at E17.5 (Fig. A; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Additionally, dynamic expression profiling across three critical stages of lung development revealed that transcription levels of these TFs markedly increased during the saccular stage (E17.5) in the CON pups’ group, whereas their expression was significantly reduced in the VDD pups’ group (Fig. B–E; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Western blot analysis validated a significant decrease in the protein expression of TFCP2L1 and CEBPA in VDD pups relative to the control group. (Fig. F, G; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). Previous studies have reported that Cebpa deficiency inhibits lung maturation and epithelial differentiation [ , ]. Notably, the pronounced suppression of Tfcp2l1 in the VDD group suggests a potential regulatory role like that of Cebpa in fetal lung development. Moreover, correlation analysis revealed that increases in paternal serum 25(OH)D levels corresponded to elevated expression of TFCP2L1 in fetal lungs at E17.5 (Fig. H; Supplementary Table in Additional File ). These findings demonstrate that paternal VDD leads to downregulation of Tfcp2l1 in offspring lungs.
 

## Paternal VDD-induced Tfcp2l1 downregulation is linked to reduced distal alveolar epithelial lineage formation during early lung sacculation
Previous studies have delineated that Tfcp2l1 governs epithelial homeostasis during lung injury repair in adult mice by coordinating self-renewal and differentiation [ ]. However, its specific function during embryonic lung development remains unclear. To assess whether Tfcp2l1 shapes the development of AT1 and AT2 cells and contributes to the onset of lung sacculation, we initially interrogated scRNA-seq data across the developmental window (E16.5 to PND28) to characterize the cell-type-specific expression pattern of Tfcp2l1. UMAP visualization revealed predominant Tfcp2l1 expression in two distal epithelial clusters corresponding to AT1 and AT2 lineages (Supplementary Fig. A, Additional File ). Building on this, we applied mIHC to follow the expression of TFCP2L1 and the appearance of early AT1 and AT2 cells. At E15.5 and E16.5, staining for both the distal epithelium and TFCP2L1 was absent (Supplementary Fig. B, Additional File ). TFCP2L1 was first detected at E17.5, aligning with the beginning of sacculation, at which time newly forming AT1 and AT2 cells also became visible. In the CON pups, TFCP2L1 was primarily localized to the distal alveolar epithelium regions containing AT1 (HOPX⁺) and AT2 (SFTPC⁺) cells, indicating that Tfcp2l1 may regulate the establishment of distal alveolar epithelial lineages by promoting the generation and maturation of AT1 and AT2 cells essential for normal lung development and function. Although TFCP2L1 exhibited a similar spatial distribution in VDD and CON pups, its expression was markedly diminished in the VDD group (Fig. A). This reduction was accompanied by diminished abundance of AT1 and AT2 cell populations, suggesting that the downregulation of Tfcp2l1 is linked to reduce the formation of distal alveolar epithelial lineages, leading to structural immaturity in alveolar development.
 To further investigate, we evaluated AT2 cell proliferation by co-staining SFTPC and the proliferation marker KI67 and confirmed whether proliferative events occurred within the epithelial lineage using ECAD staining. A marked decline in the percentage of SFTPC⁺KI67⁺ double-labeled cells was detected in the lungs of VDD pups (Fig.  B, C), indicating diminished proliferative capacity among AT2 epithelial cells. Moreover, the overall percentage of AT2 cells was markedly diminished in the VDD group (Fig.  D, E), implying that reduced TFCP2L1 expression adversely affects both the generation and proliferation of AT2 cells. To further evaluate the impact on alveolar epithelial differentiation, we quantified the abundance of AT1 cells. Compared to controls, VDD offspring exhibited a pronounced decline in AT1 cell numbers (Fig.  F, G), consistent with decreased AT2 cell abundance, reflecting reduced AT2‑to‑AT1 differentiation. Furthermore, an analysis of AT1 cell spreading using AQP5 staining revealed that the inter-luminal distance between adjacent AT1 cells was significantly reduced in VDD pups (Fig.  H, I), reflecting decreased AT1 cell spreading ability. These observations were confirmed by quantitative mIHC analysis (Supplementary Table in Additional File ). The results collectively underscore the pivotal role of Tfcp2l1 in guiding the lineage specification and differentiation of distal alveolar epithelial cells during the early phase of alveolar sac formation. Paternal VDD downregulates Tfcp2l1 expression, which is associated with reductions in AT2 cell proliferation, AT2 cell abundance, and AT2-to-AT1 differentiation, together with diminished intercellular spacing between neighboring AT1 cells. Collectively, these changes contribute to structural immaturity and limited sacculation during lung development.

## Discussion
Extensive studies have highlighted the significance of maternal VD levels in fetal lung development, revealing that maternal deficiency is closely related to impaired lung maturation and heightened asthma susceptibility in offspring [ – ]. Nonetheless, the contribution of paternal VDD to this developmental process has been largely neglected. In the present study, we constructed a VDD model in male mice to examine its effect on fetal lung growth. The results highlighted a noteworthy positive relationship between paternal VD levels and fetal parameters, including body weight and lung weight. Morphological analyses at the saccular stage (E17.5) revealed distinct developmental abnormalities in the lungs of offspring, including a reduced number of alveoli, partial alveolar collapse, thickened septa, and restricted lung expansion. These structural alterations suggest that paternal VDD influences the normal formation of distal saccules and primitive alveoli, thereby contributing to a lag in the lung maturation process.
The saccular stage marks a pivotal phase of fetal lung development, during which the establishment of AT1 and AT2 cells forms the structural and functional basis for postnatal alveolarization [ ]. Our study further revealed that paternal VDD significantly reduced the expression of genes associated with AT1 and AT2 cells in the fetal lungs of offspring. This finding provides direct evidence that paternal VDD negatively affects offspring pulmonary development by altering the developmental programming of the fetal lung, particularly through a reduction in distal epithelial lineage specification, leading to constrained primitive alveolar formation. Notably, we observed no significant alterations in the branching morphology of the proximal conducting airways, nor did we detect substantial changes in the expression of corresponding marker genes. This observation is consistent with the temporally coordinated progression of lung development, in which airway branching morphogenesis is largely completed by the canalicular stage, and subsequent development during the saccular stage primarily emphasizes alveolar formation and expansion [ ]. Therefore, our findings suggest that paternal VDD primarily affects the later stages of fetal lung maturation rather than the earlier stages of airway branching morphogenesis.
The molecular basis by which paternal VDD influences fetal lung development has yet to be fully elucidated. Prior research underscores the central involvement of TFs—such as Elf5, Nkx2.1, and Etv5—in directing the specification and differentiation of lung epithelial lineages, with these factors widely acknowledged as critical regulators of alveolar epithelial maturation. These factors not only govern lung morphogenesis but also contribute to the synthesis of surfactants essential for postnatal lung function [ – ]. However, our study did not detect significant alterations in the expression of Elf5, Nkx2.1, or Etv5 in the fetal lungs of paternal VDD offspring, suggesting that the impact of paternal VDD on fetal lung developmental programming may be selective. Through scRNA-seq analysis, we identified several TFs closely associated with the saccular stage, particularly those regulating respiratory epithelial development and differentiation. Among these, Tfcp2l1 emerged as a critical regulator, showing marked downregulation in response to paternal VDD. Correlation analysis further revealed a strong positive relationship between paternal VD levels and TFCP2L1 expression. Consistently, mIHC analysis showed that TFCP2L1 was predominantly localized to distal alveolar epithelial regions containing AT1 and AT2 cells, and its reduced expression in VDD offspring paralleled decreases in these cell populations. These observations collectively imply a potential role for Tfcp2l1 as a candidate factor in the effects of paternal VDD on the development of fetal primitive alveoli.
Building on these observations regarding Tfcp2l1, the mechanistic link between paternal VDD and fetal lung dysplasia can be further contextualized. As outlined in the Introduction, paternal VDD is postulated to exert intergenerational effects through alterations in sperm integrity and epigenetic architecture—processes not directly interrogated in the present study. Unlike maternal VDD, which operates via direct nutrient transfer and placental signaling, paternal influences are more likely conveyed through germline-mediated epigenetic inheritance. This notion is supported by accumulating evidence indicating that VDD can induce persistent epigenetic remodeling in the male germline, including impaired chromatin integrity and widespread DNA methylation reprogramming, particularly at loci enriched for developmental regulators [ , ]. Notably, such alterations have been observed under exposure paradigms comparable in duration to the 90-day VDD regimen employed here, indicating the experimental design could potentially induce epigenetic perturbations. In parallel, emerging data also implicates VD status in shaping sperm small non-coding RNA profiles, further highlighting the responsiveness of the sperm epigenome to dietary modulation and raising the possibility that prolonged exposure may amplify these effects [ ]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which epigenetic modifications in the paternal germline, encompassing both DNA methylation and small non-coding RNA dynamics—serve as plausible vectors for transmitting VDD associated signals to the developing embryo. Within this framework, it is conceivable that such sperm-derived epigenetic alterations converge on key developmental regulators, including Tfcp2l1, thereby attenuating, at least in part, its transcriptional activity in fetal lung tissue, contributing to insufficient distal alveolar epithelial maturation, and ultimately to lung dysplasia. While this proposed pathway provides a coherent conceptual bridge linking paternal nutritional status to offspring lung development, it remains inferential and warrants direct experimental validation in future studies.
Our study sheds new light on the impact of paternal VDD on offspring lung development, particularly during the critical saccular stage (E17.5). We demonstrated that paternal VDD led to reduced lung weight, restricted distal airspace expansion, and significant downregulation of AT1 and AT2 cell markers. While previous studies have focused on maternal VDD, our findings reveal a distinct perspective by which paternal VDD influences lung development, highlighting the importance of paternal nutritional status in offspring health. Notably, our scRNA-seq analysis, validated experimentally, identified Tfcp2l1 as a candidate TF associated with the effects of paternal VDD on lung development (Fig.  ). This is the first study to implicate Tfcp2l1 in this context, providing a novel molecular target for future investigations. Our findings shift the conventional emphasis on maternal nutrition by highlighting paternal nutrition as a significant contributor to offspring developmental trajectories.
 Despite these significant contributions, the study presents certain limitations that require further exploration. First, although paternal nutritional cues are widely proposed to program offspring organ development through sperm-mediated epigenetic inheritance, the present study did not characterize the epigenetic landscape in sperm following paternal VDD, and our scRNA-seq analysis relied on publicly available datasets, which may not fully capture the specific molecular changes induced by paternal VDD. It remains unclear whether dietary VDD induces changes in sperm DNA methylation, histone modifications, small noncoding RNAs, or chromatin accessibility that may persist after fertilization and regulate lung developmental genes such as Tfcp2l1. Second, this study focused on the phenotypic and molecular consequences of paternal VDD on fetal lung development and identified Tfcp2l1 as a key downstream participant, but the upstream intergenerational signaling cascade remains to be fully elucidated. We did not directly determine how paternal VDD is transmitted to the fetus and transcriptionally represses Tfcp2l1 in the developing lung. Third, the current work demonstrates a correlative association between paternal VDD, reduced Tfcp2l1 expression, and associated with insufficient alveolar epithelial maturation, but causal evidence is still lacking. We did not perform rescue experiments to confirm whether restoring Tfcp2l1 expression could reverse the lung dysplasia caused by paternal VDD. Additionally, while our VDD diet was formulated with calcium and phosphorus levels matched to the control diet (0.5% and 0.4%, respectively) to minimize such secondary effects and our direct measurements confirmed no significant differences in serum calcium or phosphate between groups, a rescue experiment involving VD repletion would be the gold standard to definitively isolate VDD-specific effects. We have added this as an important consideration for future studies. Finally, while our findings are robust in a murine model, their direct relevance to human lung development remains to be established; we therefore plan to extend our work to human samples, including cord blood and placental tissues from fathers with VDD. Furthermore, as our study focused on prenatal lung development, future longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate long term pulmonary outcomes and respiratory disease susceptibility in offspring exposed to paternal VDD.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study uncovers a novel intergenerational link between paternal VDD and altered fetal lung development, advancing knowledge of paternal nutritional programming and providing key clinical insights. It highlights the role of paternal preconception nutrition in neonatal respiratory health, suggesting paternal VDD supplementation as a preventive measure (especially in VDD-prevalent populations) and supporting the inclusion of paternal VD screening in preconception checkups. Beyond these clinical implications, this study identifies Tfcp2l1 as a candidate TF potentially involved, lays a foundation for translational research (human cohorts, supplementation trials), and highlights the role of paternal epigenetic inheritance. While future clinical research will deepen understanding of intergenerational health, inform personalized preconception guidance, and reduce the burden of perinatal respiratory diseases.
