## Abstract
Tetraspanins (Tspans) are transmembrane proteins that coordinate life cycle steps of viruses from distinct families. Here, we identify the human Tspan10 and Tspan15, both members of the TspanC8 subfamily, as replication factors for alphavirus Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV) in astrocytoma cells. Pharmacological inhibition and small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated silencing of TspanC8 interactor a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 10 (ADAM10) reduced VEEV infection. Silencing of Tspan10, Tspan15, and ADAM10 did not affect VEEV entry but diminished viral genome replication. We report that Tspan10 is important for VEEV infection of several cell lines, while silencing of Tspan15 diminishes infection with several alphaviruses, but not flaviviruses, in astrocytoma cells. Conversely, we demonstrate that siRNA-mediated silencing of Tspan14, another member of the TspanC8 family, enhances infection with lentiviral pseudoparticles harbouring the envelope proteins of VEEV, identifying it as a restriction factor for VEEV entry. Silencing of ADAM10/Tspan15 substrate neuronal (N)-cadherin reduced VEEV infectivity, suggesting potential roles of ADAM10 substrates in VEEV infection. In sum, our study identifies three TspanC8s and ADAM10 as important modulators of VEEV infectivity.

While Tspans are host factors for several viruses, the role of Tspans in infection with VEEV remains largely elusive. ADAM10 interacts with members of the TspanC8 family including Tspan10, Tspan14 and Tspan15.
Using siRNA-mediated silencing and pharmacological inhibition, the authors identify Tspan10, Tspan15, and ADAM10 as VEEV replication factors and Tspan14 as an entry restriction factor in astrocytoma cells.
This work highlights the role of TspanC8s, ADAM10 and its substrates in VEEV infection contributing to the mechanistic understanding of virus-host interaction.

## INTRODUCTION
Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV) is a mosquito-borne, encephalitic alphavirus that causes disease in humans and equids and is extremely infectious in aerosolized form ( ; ). The enveloped, positive-sense RNA virus has first been isolated in 1938 and has caused epidemic outbreaks primarily in Central and South America ( ; ; ). Upon infection, humans predominantly experience flu-like symptoms such as fever, headaches, and chills ( ; ). In 4–14% of symptomatic cases however, infected individuals develop a neurological disease characterized by symptoms such as convulsions, changes in behavior or disturbed consciousness ( ; ; ). VEEV target cells in the central nervous system (CNS) include neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes ( ; ; ; ). To date, no drug treatment or vaccine has been licensed for use in humans against VEEV ( ), highlighting the necessity for further research. Although some VEEV host factors, such as Fragile X syndrome proteins ( ) or VEEV receptor low-density lipoprotein receptor class A domain containing 3 (LDLRAD3) ( ), have been discovered in recent years, there is still a gap of knowledge regarding the interactions of VEEV with host cells at a molecular level.
Tetraspanins (Tspans) are a family of transmembrane proteins conserved throughout metazoans ( ; ). To date, 33 human Tspans are known, of which a subset is expressed in almost every cell type ( ; ). Characteristics of human Tspans include four transmembrane domains, a small extracellular loop and a large extracellular loop (LEL) containing a conserved cysteine-cysteine-glycine motif. Palmitoylated cysteines are present in intracellular regions proximal to the membrane ( ; ). Tspans form so-called Tspan-enriched microdomains, in which they concentrate interaction partners. These partner proteins include other Tspans, integrins, signaling proteins, and membrane-bound enzymes. Thus, Tspans have important functions in processes such as cell fusion, signal transduction, cell migration, and cell adhesion ( ; ).
The TspanC8 subfamily is characterized by the presence of eight conserved cysteines in the LEL and consists of Tspan5, Tspan10, Tspan14, Tspan15, Tspan17, and Tspan33 ( ). TspanC8s tightly regulate a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 10 (ADAM10) ( ; ; ; ). Subcellular localization of ADAM10 depends on the TspanC8-interaction partner, as Tspan14 and Tspan15 traffic ADAM10 to the cell surface, while expression of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged Tspan10 in HeLa cells directs ADAM10 to a CD63-positive intracellular compartment ( ; ; ). Moreover, ADAM10 is essential for expression of Tspan15 at the protein level ( ). Interestingly, the substrate specificity of ADAM10 also depends on the Tspan interaction partner. It is well established that interaction of Tspan15 and ADAM10 favors cleavage of neuronal cadherin (N-cadherin), while Tspan14/ADAM10 is considered to activate Notch ( ; ; ; ). In osteoclasts, Tspan10 activates Notch2 in an ADAM10-dependent manner ( ).
Recently, our research group identified the Tspan CD81 as a pan-alphavirus replication factor in hepatoma cells ( ). Besides CD81, other Tspans such as CD151, CD63, Tspan9, and Tspan7, have been associated with viral diseases as reviewed by and . To our knowledge, no data is available on the role of all human Tspans in VEEV infection. This prompted us to ask whether Tspans other than CD81 could play a role in VEEV infection of cells of the CNS.
In this study, we identify Tspan10, Tspan15, their interaction partner ADAM10, and substrate N-cadherin as VEEV replication factors in a human astrocytoma cell line and show that Tspan14 restricts VEEV entry. Tspan10 is crucial for VEEV infection of several cell lines, while dependency on ADAM10 and Tspan15 differs among cell lines. In the tested astrocytoma cell line, Tspan15 supports infection with several alphaviruses, but not with flaviviruses.

## RESULTS

## Tspan10 and Tspan15 are VEEV host factors in astrocytoma cells
To elucidate whether VEEV can utilize Tspans as host factors, we performed a small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown screen in the astrocytoma cell line SNB-19. First, we assessed Tspan expression levels by qRT-PCR ( ) and subsequently knocked down the 24 expressed Tspans as well as VEEV receptor LDLRAD3 ( ) using a pool of three siRNAs per target ( ). While none of the tested siRNAs affected cell viability substantially ( ), several altered VEEV infectivity at 24 h post infection (hpi) ( ). Interestingly, neither knockdown of LDLRAD3 nor VEEV host factor CD81 ( ) affected the permissiveness of the tested cell line to VEEV despite reduction of mRNA levels to 13.1% and 5.3%, respectively of control cells ( ). Reduced expression of CD9, which was shown to be able to partially substitute CD81 as a Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) host factor ( ), enhanced VEEV infectivity by 35% as compared with non-targeting (NT) control siRNA at 24 hpi.
 We observed a 3-fold decrease in infection upon knockdown of Tspan10, while silencing of Tspan15 reduced infectivity by 43% at 24 hpi. Interestingly, both proteins belong to the TspanC8 subfamily. Given the apparent role of Tspan10 and Tspan15 as VEEV host factors, we decided to further include Tspan14, another member of the TspanC8 subgroup, in our study. Silencing of Tspan14 enhanced VEEV infectivity significantly at 24 hpi ( ). We found that permissiveness of cells upon knockdown of the three TspanC8 subfamily members was not only altered at 24 hpi but throughout the course of infection ( ). qRT-PCR confirmed successful knockdown of TspanC8 targets ( ). Taken together, our data suggest that Tspan10 and Tspan15 support VEEV infection, while Tspan14 restricts VEEV infection in the tested astrocytoma cell line.

## TspanC8 interaction partner ADAM10 is a VEEV host factor in astrocytoma cells
Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of TspanC8 subfamily members for subcellular localization and substrate specificity of ADAM10 ( ; ) ( ). Therefore, we sought to determine whether ADAM10 is important for permissiveness of cells to VEEV.
 First, we pharmacologically inhibited ADAM10 using matrix metalloprotease (MMP) inhibitor batimastat (BB-94). Inhibitor treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in ADAM10 surface expression ( ) and VEEV infectivity ( ) at 24 hpi compared with DMSO solvent controls. We observed no major effect on cell viability caused by inhibitor treatment with tested concentrations ( ). Notably, treatment with inhibitor concentrations higher than 5 µM did not further reduce VEEV infectivity ( ). This was in line with a comparable reduction of ADAM10 surface expression on cells treated with 5 µM or more BB-94 (Supplemental Figure S1).
Because BB-94 is a broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor, we also used an orthogonal approach to elucidate the role of ADAM10 in VEEV infection. To this end, we knocked down ADAM10 and the closely related a disintegrin and metalloprotease 17 (ADAM17) ( ) in SNB-19 cells. To further elucidate the mechanistic role of TspanC8s and ADAMs in VEEV infection, we also included the downstream targets Notch2 and N-cadherin. Tspan15 and ADAM10 regulate N-cadherin shedding, while Tspan14, Tspan10, and ADAM10 regulate Notch signaling ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ).
Similar to the effect of Tspan15 silencing, knockdown of ADAM10 reduced VEEV infectivity by 45% compared with NT siRNA control at 16 hpi ( ). Silencing of N-cadherin also diminished infection significantly, while silencing Notch2 increased permissiveness of cells by 53%. ADAM17 silencing had no effect on VEEV infectivity. qRT-PCR confirmed successful silencing of targets ( ). Further, we showed that siRNA treatment did not alter cell viability ( ). Taken together, these results suggest a role for ADAM10 and its substrates in VEEV infectivity.

## Tspan10 is a VEEV host factor in multiple cell lines
The identification of TspanC8s and ADAM10 as VEEV host factors in astrocytoma cells led us to investigate whether other cells derived from VEEV relevant organs recapitulated the phenotype. We extended this analysis to Tspans CD81 and CD9, which we identified as alphavirus host factors previously ( ). Specifically, we assessed the role of Tspans and ADAM10 in the glioblastoma cell line U-87 MG and the adenocarcinoma lung epithelial cell line A549.
In glioblastoma cells, silencing Tspan10 reduced VEEV infectivity by 10-fold at 24 hpi, while ADAM10 and LDLRAD3 knockdown less efficiently reduced VEEV infectivity. Interestingly, knocking down Tspan15 and ADAM17 enhanced infection, whereas we did not observe any effect on infectivity caused by other siRNAs tested ( ). Transfection with siRNAs did not induce cytotoxicity and we confirmed successful knockdown at mRNA level by qRT-PCR ( , and ).
 As VEEV is discussed as an aerosol transmitted bioterrorism agent ( ), we next investigated permissiveness of A549 lung carcinoma cells to VEEV upon host factor silencing. A549 Tspan15 knockout (KO) and ADAM10 KO cells showed reduced VEEV infection at 24 hpi by 45 and 41%, respectively, compared with A549 wild-type (WT) cells ( ). Infection kinetics showed that viral replication was not just delayed but overall lower in both A549 KO cell lines than in A549 WT cells ( ). As an orthogonal approach and to also elucidate the role of Tspan10 and Tspan14 in VEEV infection of A549 cells, we performed siRNA-mediated silencing experiments. Interestingly, knockdown of Tspan14, Tspan15, and ADAM10 did not significantly alter the permissiveness of A549 cells to VEEV ( , and ). This was despite successful knockdown of Tspan14, Tspan15, and ADAM10 mRNA expression levels to 20.4%, 12.2%, and 12.8%, respectively ( ). Silencing of Tspan10 reduced infectivity by 4.9-fold compared with NT control siRNA at 24 hpi ( ), although only 46.3% of Tspan10 was knocked down at mRNA level ( ). Neither silencing of CD9 nor CD81 affected VEEV infectivity at 24 hpi despite reduction of mRNA levels to below 10% of controls ( , and ). Knockdown of ADAM10 enhanced cell viability, while none of the other knockdowns affected cell viability ( ). In conclusion, while ADAM10 and Tspan15 may play differential roles in VEEV infection of different cell lines, Tspan10 supports VEEV infection of two CNS- and one lung-derived cell line.

## Tspan15 is a pan-alphavirus host factor in SNB-19 cells
Certain host factors, such as very low-density lipoprotein receptor and apolipoprotein E receptor 2, are used by several alphaviruses ( ). To investigate whether Tspan10, Tspan15, and ADAM10 are pan-alphavirus host factors, we infected siRNA-transfected SNB-19 cells with alphaviruses from different complexes ( ): GFP-tagged Semliki Forest virus (SFV), GFP-tagged CHIKV and firefly luciferase (FLuc)-tagged Sindbis virus (SINV). At 24 hpi, knockdown of Tspan15 and CD9 significantly reduced infectivity of all tested alphaviruses ( , ). Interestingly, silencing of ADAM10 only reduced infection of SFV and SINV (by 32.4% and 69.8%, respectively), but not of CHIKV. Knockdown of Tspan10 pronouncedly reduced CHIKV and SFV infectivity, while it slightly decreased SINV infectivity by 25%. Notably, silencing of CD81 reduced permissiveness of SNB-19 cells to SINV by 3.6-fold and to SFV by 3.9-fold, but did not have a major effect on permissiveness to CHIKV. While reduced ADAM17 expression did not alter CHIKV infectivity, SFV infectivity was highly impaired (34.8% residual infection), whereas SINV infectivity was enhanced by 86.6%. In short, Tspan15 showed pan-alphavirus activity in astrocytoma cells.
 Because Tspans are host and restriction factors for viruses from several families ( ), we decided to test their role in flavivirus infection. Specifically, we focused on the neurotropic flaviviruses West Nile Virus (WNV) and tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV). We infected siRNA-transfected SNB-19 cells with mosquito-borne WNV and tick-borne TBEV, respectively, and subsequently titrated 24 hpi supernatants on BHK-21 cells. We included supernatants from SNB-19 cells treated with interferon-alpha (IFN-α) as a control. IFN-α treatment reduced both WNV and TBEV titres substantially compared with untreated sample, whereas titres did not differ significantly between NT control siRNA and other siRNA-transfected samples ( , and ).
In conclusion, Tspan15 is a host factor for several alphaviruses in SNB-19 cells, whereas the dependency on all other proteins differs between tested alphaviruses. Investigated Tspans and ADAMs do not play a role in flavivirus infection of SNB-19 cells but seem to be specific to alphaviruses.

## Tspan14 restricts VEEV entry
While some Tspans are localized at the plasma membrane of cells, others localize to intracellular compartments such as endosomes. Furthermore, subcellular localization of ADAM10 highly depends on its TspanC8 interaction partner ( ; ; ; ). Accordingly, Tspans and ADAM10 can be involved in several steps of the viral life cycle.
To assess whether studied Tspans and ADAM10 play a role in VEEV entry, we transduced siRNA-transfected SNB-19 cells with lentiviral pseudoparticles harboring the envelope proteins (E proteins) of VEEV strain TC-83 or Trinidad donkey strain (TrD), the donkey isolate, which TC-83 is derived from ( ; ). As positive and negative controls, we used lentiviral pseudoparticles decorated with vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSV-G) or pseudoparticles lacking viral glycoproteins, respectively. Knockdown of Tspan14 significantly enhanced transduction efficiency of lentiviral particles harboring the E proteins of VEEV TC-83 or VEEV TrD by 28.8% and 25.2%, respectively ( , and ). Entry of lentiviral particles decorated with VSV-G was not substantially affected by silencing of Tspan14 ( ). In accordance with results from VEEV infection experiments ( , D and F), silencing of LDLRAD3 did neither alter transduction efficiency of lentiviruses pseudotyped with E proteins of any VEEV strain nor VSV-G ( , ). Silencing other targets had no impact on transduction efficiency of any lentiviral particle tested either.
 In summary, our data suggest that Tspan14 restricts VEEV TC-83 and VEEV TrD entry.

## Tspan10, Tspan15, ADAM10, and N-cadherin are VEEV replication factors
Our findings indicate that neither Tspan10, Tspan15, nor ADAM10 affect VEEV glycoprotein mediated cell entry. Hence, we asked whether these proteins or their substrates could be important for viral genome replication. During alphavirus replication, genomic RNA encoding non-structural proteins (nsPs), that form the viral replicase complex, is translated first. nsPs then drive synthesis of minus-strand RNA and subsequently production of positive-strand genomic and subgenomic RNA, the latter encoding the structural proteins, from minus-strand RNA template ( ). To mimic replication, we here used a VEEV TrD transreplicon system ( ). In this system, VEEV nsPs are expressed from one plasmid (replicon plasmid). Instead of the viral genome, a second plasmid encodes Gaussia luciferase (GLuc) under a subgenomic promotor substituting structural proteins and FLuc substituting nsPs. As a negative control, we used a replicon plasmid containing a GAA mutation in the active site of nsP4, rendering the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase replication deficient ( ; ).
To determine whether TspanC8s, ADAM10, and N-cadherin play a role in VEEV replication, we measured GLuc activity from supernatants of siRNA-transfected SNB-19 cells at 4 h post transfection (hpt), 8 hpt, 24 hpt and 48 hpt. As a control, we used IFN-α treated cells. At 4 hpt, GLuc signals were at background level in all samples (Supplemental Figure S2A). Silencing of Tspan10 and Tspan15 resulted in a 2.4-fold and 2-fold decrease, respectively in replication levels at 24 hpt ( ). Similarly, knockdown of the Tspan10/15 interaction partner ADAM10 led to a 2-fold reduction in GLuc signal ( ). Transfection with siRNAs against the ADAM10 substrate N-cadherin finally diminished VEEV replication by 4.5-fold at 8 hpt ( ). At 48 hpt, we observed effects on replication comparable to effects at 24 hpt (Supplemental Figure S2B). In addition to GLuc indicative of subgenomic RNA production, we measured FLuc activity from whole cell lysate at 48 hpt. We detected a notable reduction in FLuc signal indicative of genomic RNA production upon knockdown of Tspan10, Tspan15, and interestingly, Notch2 (Supplemental Figure S2C). Collectively, our data indicate that Tspan10 and Tspan15, their interaction partner ADAM10 and the ADAM10 substrate N-cadherin are VEEV replication factors.

## DISCUSSION
Tspans organize the cellular plasma membrane by forming Tspan-enriched microdomains, in which they interact with transmembrane partner proteins including metalloproteinases and integrins ( ; ; ; ). Additionally, Tspans also localize to intracellular membranes including those of endosomes and to extracellular vesicles ( ; ). Alphaviruses mainly enter host cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis, replicate in microinvaginations at the plasma membrane termed “spherules” and bud from the plasma membrane as reviewed by . It is therefore reasonable to hypothesize that Tspans have an impact on the infectivity of alphaviruses.
In this study, we identify Tspan10 and Tspan15 as VEEV replication factors and Tspan14 as a VEEV restriction factor in human astrocytoma cells. The aforementioned Tspans belong to the TspanC8 family, a subgroup of Tspans known to interact with ADAM10. It is well established that TspanC8 determine substrate specificity and subcellular localization of ADAM10 ( ; ; ; ). Koo et al. showed that Tspan15 requires ADAM10 for expression at the protein level and trafficking to the cell surface in several cell lines. In the same study, ADAM10 was identified as the main interacting protein of Tspan15 in HEK293T cells ( ). Moreover, cryoelectron microscopy has recently shown how Tspan15 and ADAM10 interact in a heterodimer complex ( ). In line with this, we show that pharmacological inhibition and knockdown of ADAM10 reduce VEEV infectivity in astrocytoma cells.
Our findings suggest that Tspan15 facilitates VEEV replication by regulating ADAM10 subcellular trafficking and ADAM10 substrate specificity. Using proteomics, Kuhn and colleagues identified 91 ADAM10 substrates in neurons and overall, more than 100 substrates of ADAM10 have been discovered in recent years ( ; ). Investigating the role of all ADAM10 substrates in the CNS in VEEV infection would require an independent study. Hence, we focused on two substrates regulated by ADAM10 in complex with the other two host factors identified in this study, namely Tspan10 and Tspan15. Tspan15 is required for ADAM10-dependent cleavage of N-cadherin ( ; ), while Tspan10 is considered to regulate ADAM10-dependent Notch2 activation ( ). Interestingly, Tspan15 negatively regulates Notch activity, probably indirectly by competing with Tspan14 for ADAM10 binding, whereas Tspan14 is important for ADAM10-regulated activation of Notch1 ( ; ).
In line with the fact that Tspan15 bound ADAM10 cleaves N-cadherin, we here demonstrate that knockdown of N-cadherin diminishes VEEV infectivity. Although N-cadherin specifically has not been associated with VEEV infection so far, cadherins have been reported to play a role in alphavirus infection. Gebhart and colleagues observed an enrichment of Molecular Function ontological group Cadherin Binding Involved in Cell-Cell Adhesion (GO:0098641) upon VEEV, CHIKV, and SINV infection of HEK293 cells ( ). Moreover, protocadherin 10 has recently been discovered as a receptor for encephalitic alphavirus Western equine encephalitis virus ( ). Of note, the effect of silencing N-cadherin on VEEV infectivity was less pronounced than the effect of knockdown of ADAM10 and Tspan15. Thus, ADAM10 and Tspan15 might additionally support VEEV infection through mechanisms other than shedding of N-cadherin.
Regarding a possible role of the Tspan10/ADAM10 substrate Notch2 in alphavirus infection ( ), Mudiganti and colleagues discovered that the alphavirus SINV upregulates components of the Notch pathway in Schneider's 2 Drosophila cells. This prompted the authors to suggest that Notch signaling could be important for establishing persistent infection of insect cells ( ). The Notch signaling pathway is also up-regulated in Aedes aegypti infected with CHIKV ( ). In this study, we report that knockdown of Notch2 enhances VEEV infectivity in astrocytoma cells. We conclude that Notch signaling also plays a role in alphavirus infection of mammalian cells, but future studies have to assess the precise role of Notch signaling in VEEV infection.
It is also possible that Tspan10 and Tspan14 influence VEEV infectivity independently of ADAM10 and Notch shedding. This theory is supported by the fact that silencing of ADAM10 and Tspan14 has contrary effects on VEEV infection of SNB-19 cells and impacts different steps of the viral life cycle. In addition, we observed that silencing of Tspan10 reduces VEEV infectivity to a greater extent than knockdown of ADAM10 not only in SNB-19 cells, but also in U-87 MG and A549 cells.
While Tspan10 acts as a VEEV host factor in all tested cell lines, silencing of Tspan15 and Tspan14 has differential effects in different cell lines. In U-87 MG cells, knockdown of Tspan15 enhances VEEV infectivity, while silencing ADAM10 in U-87 MG did not affect VEEV infectivity significantly. One potential explanation for this observation is the expression level of Tspan15. While we could show that Tspan15 is the TspanC8 with the highest expression at the mRNA level in SNB-19 cells ( ), it is the TspanC8 with the lowest expression in U-87 MG cells (Supplemental Figure S3). Therefore, it can be assumed that ADAM10 interacts with other TspanC8s than Tspan15 in U-87 MG cells and is licensed to shed other substrates than in SNB-19 cells.
Expression levels of Tspan15 might also explain our observations in A549 cells. We observed significantly reduced VEEV infectivity upon KO but not knockdown of ADAM10 and Tspan15 in A549 cells. According to proteinatlas.org, expression levels of Tspan15 are substantially higher in A549 cells than in U-251 MG cells (Supplemental Figure S3), a cell line of high similarity and the same origin as SNB-19, on which no data were available ( ; ). Accordingly, Tspan15 expression after silencing still seems sufficient to support VEEV infectivity in A549 cells. Knockdown of Tspan14, which is also highly expressed in A549 cells (Supplemental Figure S3), did not affect VEEV infectivity either. However, this TspanC8 is also expressed to considerably higher levels in A549 than U-87 MG or U-251 MG cells (Supplemental Figure S3). We can therefore not exclude that a complete KO of Tspan14 could affect VEEV infectivity. Thus, differential protein expression levels and a strict threshold level for Tspans to act as VEEV host factor may explain the cell type-specific phenotypes.
It should also be noted that substrate specificities of TspanC8/ADAM10 complexes can differ between cell lines as reviewed by . This could also explain differences in VEEV dependency on Tspans and ADAM10 between cell lines. Similarly, silencing ADAM17 and CD9 had differential effects on VEEV infectivity in different cell lines in this study. Moreover, CD81, a VEEV host factor in hepatoma cells ( ), does not influence VEEV infectivity in the here tested astrocytoma, glioblastoma, and lung carcinoma cells. In a previous study, we reported that pharmacological inhibition of ADAM10 does not affect VEEV infectivity in hepatoma cells ( ). In sum, we conclude that Tspan10 is a VEEV host factor in several cell lines, while dependency on all other proteins investigated in this study is cell-type specific.
Surprisingly, silencing of VEEV receptor LDLRAD3 with siRNAs did not significantly affect VEEV infectivity in tested cell lines, although infectivity was reduced by 51.6% in U-87 MG cells. Complete KO of LDLRAD3 could potentially further reduce susceptibility of U-87 MG cells to VEEV. Importantly, infectivity of VEEV tissue culture adapted strains is promoted by binding to heparan sulfate on the cell surface ( ). The initial CRISPR/Cas9 screen identifying LDLRAD3 as a VEEV host factor was therefore performed in cells lacking heparan sulfate ( ). Presence of heparan sulfate on the cell surface of here tested cell lines might explain the limited effect of LDLRAD3 knockdown on VEEV TC-83 infectivity. Finally, Ma and colleagues observed residual VEEV infection in cell lines lacking LDLRAD3 and concluded that other host factors might additionally promote VEEV entry ( ). In conclusion, residual expression of LDLRAD3 upon knockdown, expression of heparan sulfate and presence of additional, not yet identified entry factors may explain why knockdown of LDLRAD3 did not significantly affect VEEV infectivity in our study.
A limitation of this study is that we could not assess knockdown efficiencies at the protein level due to limited availability of antibodies. Hence, we cannot rule out that differential half-lives of proteins in the tested cells lines account for some of the observed differences.
In SNB-19 astrocytoma cells, we identified Tspan15 as a pan-alphavirus host factor. Interestingly, ADAM10 was dispensable for CHIKV but not SFV and SINV infection, suggesting that Tspan15 may support CHIKV infection in a mechanistically different way than for the other tested alphaviruses. Tspan10 dependency may differ between alphaviruses from different complexes. Specifically, we observed that CHIKV and SFV, both belonging to the Semliki Forest complex, depend on Tspan10, whereas SINV of the western equine encephalitis complex does not ( ). Alphavirus replication factor CD81 and its closest paralogue CD9 ( ) largely support infection with arthritogenic alphaviruses such as CHIKV, SFV, and SINV but not encephalitic alphavirus VEEV in astrocytoma cells ( ). Thus, Tspan usage differs between alphaviruses and cell types.
Flaviviruses, alphaviruses, and VSV are considered to primarily enter host cells through clathrin-mediated endocytosis ( ; ; ). We observed enhanced entry of lentiviral particles decorated with glycoproteins of two VEEV strains but not VSV upon silencing of Tspan14. Additionally, diminished Tspan14 expression does not alter infectivity of neurotropic flaviviruses, suggesting that Tspan14 does not generally affect endocytosis. We conclude that Tspan14 is thus an alphavirus specific entry restriction factor in astrocytoma cells.
Alphaviruses replicate their genome in spherules at the plasma membrane and type-I cytopathic vacuoles (CPV-I) ( ; ; ). Using a VEEV replicon system that distinguishes between subgenomic and genomic RNA replication, we identified Tspan10, Tspan15, ADAM10, and N-cadherin as VEEV replication factors. Tspan15 localizes to the plasma membrane in many different cell lines and could therefore support VEEV replication within spherules ( ; ). Limited data are available on the subcellular localization of Tspan10. Upon overexpression, Dornier et al. reported Tspan10 to be localized in an intracellular compartment in HeLa cells ( ). Assuming that the localization of endogenous Tspan10 in SNB-19 is similar, Tspan10 could potentially support VEEV replication in CPV-I. It must be noted however, that localization of Tspans may differ between cell lines. In addition to cellular localization, Tspan14 and Tspan15 were found in extracellular vesicles ( ; ). Additionally, viral infection can cause relocalization of host proteins. For instance, Yang and colleagues discovered that TBEV infection changes sub-cellular localization of TBEV host factor ADAM15 ( ). Again, due to the lack of specific antibodies, we could not perform (co)localization studies in this study. Interestingly, knockdown of Notch2 also reduces VEEV replication, while overall infection is enhanced. Thus, Notch signaling could differentially influence viral life cycle steps.
In conclusion, we identified Tspan10 as a VEEV host factor in cell lines derived from CNS and lung tissues and Tspan15 as a pan-alphavirus host factor in astrocytoma cells. Tspan15 supports alphavirus infectivity at least partially by promoting shedding of N-cadherin by ADAM10. Further studies are needed to unravel the precise mechanisms by which TspanC8s, ADAM10, N-cadherin, and Notch signaling influence VEEV infectivity. It is also outside the scope of this study, if TspanC8s and ADAM10 could play a role in additional steps of the viral life cycle such as viral budding or cell-to-cell spread and if other substrates of ADAM10 could also influence VEEV infectivity.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Cell culture
All cell lines (SNB-19, HEK293T, U-87 MG, A549 and derivates, Vero E6 and BHK-21 cells) were cultured in DMEM (Life Technologies) at 37°C and 5% CO2 and tested routinely for presence of Mycoplasma. DMEM was supplemented with 10% FCS (Capricorn scientific) for cultivation of SNB-19 cells or 10% FCS, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids (Life Technologies) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Life Technologies) for cultivation of all other cell lines except for Vero E6 cells. Vero E6 cells were cultivated in DMEM with 10% FCS, 2 mM L-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies).
SNB-19 cells were provided by P. Claus, A549 WT cells by N. Arnberg and S. Kunz, U-87 MG cells by O. Keppler and BHK-21 cells by S. Goffinet. HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The A549 Tspan15 KO and A549 ADAM10 KO cell lines were described by . Vero E6 cells were kindly provided by H. Naim.

## Viruses and pseudoparticles

## Viruses
VEEV TC-83 and CHIKV strain LR2006-OPY1 of the East-Central-South-African (ECSA) genotype expressing GFP under a subgenomic promoter have been reported previously ( ; ). SINV Toto 1101 with FLuc insertion in nsP3 has been described by , while SFV4 with enhanced GFP inserted between nsP3 and nsP4 has been reported by .
Plasmids encoding VEEV, CHIKV, and SINV were in vitro transcribed into RNA and RNA was subsequently electroporated into BHK-21 cells. For production of infectious SFV, plasmid DNA was directly electroporated into BHK-21 cells. Supernatants containing infectious virus were collected 48 h after electroporation.
The WNV NY99 infectious clone was harvested from Vero E6 cells, while TBEV Neudoerfl was propagated on A549 cells.

## Lentiviral pseudoparticles
A human codon-optimized version of the VEEV TC-83 (GenBank: L01443.1) or VEEV TrD (GenBank: L01442.2) E3-E1 gene, respectively, was synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies and cloned into the pIRES2-EGFP-CHIKV E3-E1 plasmid ( ) using BamHI restriction sites, thereby replacing CHIKV E3-E1. Successful cloning was confirmed by sequencing.
Lentiviral pseudoparticles harbouring the glycoproteins of VSV, VEEV TrD, VEEV TC-83 or no envelope protein were produced as described by .

## siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments
All siRNAs were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Assay IDs of siRNAs used are listed in . Silencer Select negative control 2 was used as NT control siRNA.
 

## SNB-19
SNB-19 cells were seeded at a cell density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. Twenty-four hours after seeding, cells were transfected with a pool of three siRNAs per target (final concentration of 8.3 nmol per siRNA per well) or single control siRNAs (final concentration of 25 nmol per siRNA per well) using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's recommendation. Medium was replenished 24 hpt and cells were subsequently used for infection experiments, replicon assays, entry assays, cell viability assays or processed for qRT-PCR.
If IFN-α (Schering Corporation) was used as a control, it was added to a final concentration of 1000 units/well 24 h after siRNA-mediated knockdown when the medium was replenished.

## U-87 MG and A549 cells
U-87 MG and A549 cells were seeded at a cell density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and reversely transfected with a pool of three siRNAs (final concentration of 4.2 nmol per siRNA per well) per target or single control siRNAs (final concentration of 12.5 nmol per siRNA per well) using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's recommendation and subsequently used for downstream assays.

## Inhibitor treatment

## BB-94
SNB-19 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates. One day after seeding, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of MMP inhibitor BB-94 or DMSO solvent controls. Twenty-four hours later, cells were either infected with VEEV supplemented with BB-94 or treated with indicated concentrations of BB-94 once more to be used for flow cytometry analysis and cell viability assays. Flow cytometry and cell viability assays were performed 24 h after the second treatment.

## Infection assays
In all siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments, cells were infected 48 h after siRNA transfection with indicated multiplicity of infection (MOIs). In the initial siRNA screen, medium was replenished 2 h after VEEV infection and in flavivirus infection experiments, virus inoculum was removed 1 hpi, cells were washed twice with PBS and medium was replenished. A549 WT, A549 ADAM10 KO, and A549 Tspan15 KO cell lines were seeded at a cell density of 2 × 104 cells/well in 96-well format and infected with VEEV (MOI of 0.1) the following day. As A549 WT cell lines originating from two different sources were available, the arithmetic mean between these cell lines was calculated before comparing them to KO cell lines. SNB-19 cells treated with MMP inhibitor BB-94 were infected with VEEV (MOI of 0.1) supplemented with BB-94 24 h after the first inhibitor treatment.
If not stated differently, VEEV and CHIKV infectivity was estimated as Green Integrated Intensity Per Image/Phase Area Per Image (GCU × µm²/Image/µm²/Image), or simplified as GFP signal over cell density (GCU), using the basic analyzer software of Incucyte S3 imaging platform (Sartorius). VEEV infectivity upon BB-94 inhibitor treatment was estimated as Total Green Object Integrated Intensity (GCU × µm²/Image) using the basic analyzer software of Incucyte S3 imaging platform (Sartorius).
To assess SINV infectivity, cells were lysed at 24 hpi and FLuc signal was estimated as described by (adapted for 96-well format) using Centro XS3 LB 960 (Berthold). SFV infectivity was quantified by flow cytometry at 24 hpi. Infectivity of flaviviruses was determined by titration of supernatants taken at 24 hpi from SNB-19 cells infected with WNV or TBEV. Titrations were performed on BHK-21 cells, cell culture medium with 2% FCS was used.
Except for WNV and TBEV, MOIs were based on titration on the respective cell line used for experiments. MOIs used in WNV and TBEV infection experiments were based on titration on BHK-21 cells. When KO cells were used, titers were based on the respective WT cell line.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed 48 h (SNB-19) or 72 h (A549, U-87 MG) after siRNA transfection, or 24 h after the second treatment with inhibitor BB-94.
Briefly, 5 mg/ml stock solution 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was diluted 1:10 in cell culture medium and added to cells (50 µl/well of 96-well plate). Once cells turned blue, the reagent was exchanged for 50 µl of DMSO. Absorbance was determined at a wavelength of 570 nm using spectrophotometer Multiskan Go (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## qRT-PCR
RNA was isolated from cells using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel) according to manufacturer's recommendations. To assess expression of Tspans and LDLRAD3 and to determine residual transcript expression of Tspans (except for CD9) and LDLRAD3 upon siRNA-mediated knockdown in SNB-19 cells, 500 ng RNA were reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT master mix (Takara Bio). Transcript expression was subsequently estimated by qPCR using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) (Takara Bio). Primers ( ), ordered from Microsynth or Eurofins, were used at a final concentration of 0.6 µM per primer pair; 25 ng of cDNA was used per reaction.
 To determine residual transcript expression upon siRNA-mediated knockdown in all other cell lines and of CD9 in SNB-19 cells, the iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit (Bio-Rad) was used. Per reaction, 25 ng of RNA and primers ( ) at a final concentration of 0.7 µM per primer pair were used.
All qRT-PCRs were performed using LightCycler 96 (Roche). The ∆CT method was applied to determine expression levels relative to reference gene POLR2F and residual transcript expression was calculated using the method ( ).

## Flow cytometry
To test for changes in cell surface detection of ADAM10 upon BB-94 inhibitor treatment, SNB-19 cells were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, supplemented with 1% FCS, and stained for 45 min on ice with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated mouse mAb against ADAM10 (BioLegend, clone SHM14) diluted 1:34 in PBS with 1% FCS. As an isotype control, APC-conjugated mouse IgG1κ (BD Biosciences) diluted 1:10 in PBS with 1% FCS was used.
To assess SFV infectivity, infection was stopped at 24 hpi. Technical replicates were pooled, and cells were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS. Infectivity was estimated as percentage of GFP-positive cells.
All flow cytometric analyses were conducted using Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Invitrogen) and results were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.9.0.

## Entry assay
To assess whether proteins of interest play a role in VEEV entry, SNB-19 cells were transfected with siRNAs as described elsewhere. Forty-eight hours after transfection, SNB-19 cells were transduced with lentiviral pseudoparticles for 72 h and entry of particles was estimated as FLuc activity (protocol adapted for 96-well plate format from ) using Centro XS3 LB 960 (Berthold).

## Replicon assay
To estimate viral replication, SNB-19 cells were transfected with plasmids encoding the VEEV TrD transreplicon system ( ) 48 h after transfection with siRNAs. Per well of a 96-well plate, 0.016 µl of U251 Cell Avalanche Transfection Reagent (EZ Biosystems) were added to 0.65 ng of template plasmid encoding FLuc and GLuc reporter genes and 1.3 ng of either replicon plasmid encoding nsPs1-4 of VEEV TrD or replicon GAA plasmid encoding replication deficient nsPs1-4. OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to a total volume of 0.53 µl, the mixture was vortexed and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 199.5 µl of cell culture medium were added and the entire mixture was applied to the cells. At 4, 8, 24 and 48 hpt, 30 µl of supernatants were taken and stored frozen at −20°C until GLuc assays were performed. Removed supernatant was replaced with 30 µl of cell culture medium.
At 48 hpt, cells were lysed and FLuc signal was estimated as described by (adapted for 96-well format) using Centro XS3 LB 960 (Berthold). GLuc activity was determined in 20 µl of 1:10 diluted supernatant mixed with coelenterazine substrate (40 ng/ml in PBS) using Centro XS3 LB 960 (Berthold).

## Expression of TspanC8s in A549, U-251 MG, and U-87 MG cells
The Human Protein Atlas (proteinatlas.org) was used to compare expression levels of TspanC8s in A549, U-251 MG, and U-87 MG cells ( ). Data were obtained on Nov 1, 2024, 7.30 pm.

## Statistical analyses and illustrations
Data were visualized and analyzed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 or v10.0. Data show the mean ± SD (SD) of biological replicates with each data point representing one biological replicate if not stated differently. To determine statistical significance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's multiple comparison test, or two-tailed, unpaired t tests were performed. Schematic illustrations were created with BioRender (BioRender.com) and Adobe Illustrator version 28.7.1 was used for generation of figure layouts.
