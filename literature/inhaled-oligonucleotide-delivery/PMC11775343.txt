However, roles in thrombopoiesis and platelet function remain poorly understood. We generated megakaryocyte/platelet-specific Ago2-deleted (Ago2 KO) mice and assessed proteomic and functional effects. We predicted platelet hyperreactivity with Ago2 deletion due to large-scale upregulated protein expression. Platelet counts were normal. Mean volumes were increased, associated with larger, though fewer megakaryocytes. Ago2-deleted platelets from male mice showed hyperreactivity to thromboxane but not to other agonists compared to controls, whereas Ago2-deleted platelets from female mice showed normal reactivity. Ago2 KO mice displayed normal hemostasis and clot dynamics. Proteomes of Ago2-deleted and wild type platelets were mostly similar. However, Ago1 - undetectable in wild type platelets - was upregulated in Ago2-deleted platelets in both males and females, confirmed by immunoblotting. Female Ago2-deleted platelets selectively showed downregulation of a protein cohort established in breast cancer cells to be transcriptionally regulated by estrogen receptor-beta coupled to Ago2, whereas male Ago2-deleted platelets did not. Thus, Ago2 is important for platelet development and function, putatively partially rescued by upregulation of Ago1. Platelet reactivity controlled by Ago2 reflects sex-specific regulation of gene expression potentially at both transcriptional and translational levels in megakaryocytes and platelets.

## Introduction
Platelets are highly enriched in miRNAs, 19–22 nt non-coding RNAs that target complementary mRNAs through recognition of a minimal seed sequence, leading to suppression of protein expression (RNA inhibition, RNAi) via blockade of translation, destabilizing transcripts, or possibly a combination of these effects, influencing nearly all biological processes in humans – . Mature miRNAs which can target seed-matched mRNAs are generated from pre-miRNA hairpins by Dicer1 riboendonuclease – which is expressed in MK and platelets. ARGONAUTE2 (AGO2, Ago2 in mice) is the central and essential protein component of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC, miRISC) which docks mature miRNAs to guide effector complexes via recruitment of target mRNAs, driving RNAi via multiple mechanisms including mRNA cleavage and degradation mediated by the unique “slicer” function of AGO2 – .
Rowley et al., first demonstrated that platelets, selectively depleted of miRNAs by Pf4-Cre-driven Dicer1 deletion, have increased mRNA content, at least some increased proteins, and hyperreactivity . Similarly, gene association studies have linked human platelet miRNA expression with platelet production and reactivity – . These prior data suggest that miRNAs are important for platelet protein expression. However, these studies could not separate out effects on platelet production, inherited platelet proteins, and ongoing platelet translation. To investigate specific roles for Ago2 and RISC in platelet gene expression and function, we generated mice with megakaryocyte (MK)-/platelet-specific deletion of Ago2 (Eif2c2fl/fl/Pf4-Cre).
As the principal platelet functional Ago and the only Ago with potent slicing capability, we predicted ablated miRISC activity resulting in increased homeostatic mRNA profile in Ago2-deleted platelets, with potentially distinct effects from Dicer1 deletion . Ago2 and Dicer1 have key functional differences that may alter functional effects in the platelet context. Dicer1−/− platelets are depleted for miRNAs, but not deleted. This may be due in part to the ~ 5-day miRNA half-life. As Pf4-Cre-mediated deletion is in early megakaryopoiesis, murine platelets (~ 5-day lifespan) likely harbor long-lived miRNAs after Dicer1 deletion. Moreover, platelets can internalize RNA from the environment – . Thus, Dicer1−/− platelets in circulation may still support miRNA function. We predicted that Ago2 deletion would ablate miRNA function, regardless of their concentration in platelets. Additionally, Dicer1-/Ago2-independent miRNAs have been described , – , which may modulate platelet function. Finally, Ago2 regulates transcription and pre-mRNA splicing in nucleated cells, which may alter both the platelet mRNAome and proteome , ; these putative functions of Ago2 in MK have not been explored. In this study, we characterized the effects of MK-/platelet-specific deletion of Ago2 on platelet production, protein expression, cellular function, and contributions to hemostasis.

## Results

## Increased mean volume with normal lifespan in Ago2-deleted platelets
To generate mouse models of platelet-specific deletion of miRNA function, we crossed Ago2 (Eif2c2)-floxed (fl.) mice with Pf4-Cre mice (all on C57Bl/6J background) to yield Pf4-Cre + mice heterozygous or homozygous for Ago2fl. (Eif2c2fl/fl/Pf4-Cre). Platelets from Eif2c2fl/fl/Pf4-Cre homozygotes were deleted for Ago2 protein, although other proteins reactive with global Ago antibodies were evident (Supplemental Fig. 1). After multiple generations from het/het crossings, progeny showed normal Mendelian distributions and appeared grossly normal. We observed no significant differences in blood cell counts compared to controls, including platelets, leukocytes, and erythrocytes; blood hemoglobin was also comparable to controls (Fig.  A and Supplemental Fig. 2). In contrast, mean platelet volumes were significantly higher in Ago2 MK-/platelet-specific knockout (Ago2 KO) mice (Fig.  B). Considering effects of sex, we observed no differences in platelet count between Ago2 KO and control male or female mice (Fig.  C). Mean platelet volumes were significantly increased in male Ago2 KO mice compared to male controls and were also increased in female Ago2 KO mice compared to female controls (Fig.  D).

 

We considered whether the observed increase in mean platelet volumes with Ago2 deletion corresponded to altered platelet generation or platelet lifespan. Platelet generation after antibody-mediated platelet depletion with Gp1bα antibodies followed similar rates in Ago2 KO mice as controls (Fig.  E). Ago2 KO platelets showed a trend towards increased mean volumes throughout the experimental time frame, reaching significance by 96 h after platelet depletion (Fig.  F). Platelet lifespan, monitored by pulse-chase labeling with anti-GP1bβ antibodies without platelet depletion, was unchanged between Ago2 wild type and KO platelets (Fig.  G). Thus, MK-/platelet-specific Ago2 deletion results in sex-independent increased platelet volumes but does not alter stress thrombopoiesis or platelet lifespan in mice.

## Fewer, larger megakaryocytes with Ago2 deletion
To determine the basis for the observed increased mean platelet volumes with Ago2 deletion in MK and platelets, we investigated MK development in Eif2c2fl/fl/Pf4-Cre mice. Hematoxylin/eosin staining of femurs and spleens revealed ~ 28% reduction in the number of MK in the femurs of Ago2 KO mice, with no change in the numbers of MK in the spleens (Fig.  A, B). However, Ago2 KO bone marrow MK showed ~ 34% increased size compared to controls, as assessed by mean forward scatter with flow cytometry, using fluorophore-conjugated Cd42d antibodies to identify MK in bone marrow aspirate suspensions (Fig.  C). We counterstained the bone marrow cells with propidium iodide to identify and characterize bone marrow MK by ploidy, and found that MK size was increased across all ploidy groups tested from 2 to 32 N in Ago2 KO mice compared to controls (Fig.  D). As an orthogonal approach to assess MK size, we performed immunohistochemical staining of femoral sections using Cd42d antibodies, and quantified MK area from the images. This analysis showed a similar increase in MK size in femurs from Ago2 KO mice compared to controls (Fig.  E). Interestingly, an overall shift in the median ploidy of bone marrow MK was observed in Ago2 KO mice (median 14.1 N ± 3.3) compared to controls (7.8 N ± 0.18)(Fig.  F).

 

## Sex-selective increased thromboxane reactivity in Ago2-deleted platelets
We investigated the contributions of MK/platelet deletion of Ago2 to platelet function, by testing agonist responses in washed platelets from Ago2 KO mice. We observed a moderate increase in integrin αIIbβ3 (GpIIb/IIIa) activation and α-granule secretion (assessed by surface exposure of P-selectin/Cd62p) in Ago2 KO platelets compared to control platelets following stimulation with thromboxane A2 (TxA2) analog U46619 (Fig.  A). In contrast, responses to low to saturating dose ranges of thrombin, ADP, and convulxin were comparable between Ago2 KO and control platelets (Fig.  B-D). Interestingly, the increase in U46619 reactivity by Ago2 deletion was selective for platelets from male mice, in which U46619 responses were substantially increased in Ago2 KO platelets compared to male controls, whereas platelets from Ago2-deleted female mice showed normal U46619 responses compared to female controls (Fig.  E).

 

## Normal hemostasis and clot dynamics in platelet-specific Ago2-deleted mice
We tested putative effects of MK/platelet deletion of Ago2 on hemostasis using the tail tip amputation model, with 1.0-mm diameter tip amputation followed by monitoring time to hemostasis, rebleeds, and final hemostasis , . Time to primary hemostasis following tail tip amputation was normal in Ago2 KO mice (Fig.  A), as were the number of subsequent rebleeds (Fig.  B) and time to final hemostasis in this model (Fig.  C). Primary hemostasis, rebleeds, and final hemostasis were normal in both males and females, indicating that there were no effects of sex on hemostasis in this model (Fig.  D). We next monitored platelet recruitment to thrombi and clot dynamics after laser injury in cremaster muscle arterioles in Ago2 KO mice , . Thrombus size as assessed by Cd41-positive platelets incorporated into the thrombi, the hemostatic core of P-selectin-positive platelets, and fibrin deposition, were all unaltered compared to controls in this model (Fig.  E-G), and the overall dynamics of platelet accumulation was also normal (Fig.  H, I). Altogether, these results indicate that hemostasis following vascular injury was not significantly altered by MK/platelet deletion of Ago2.

 

## Induced Ago1 expression in Ago2-deleted platelets and megakaryocytic cells
We sought to map the homeostatic proteome of resting Ago2-deleted platelets in comparison to that of control platelets. As detailed above, we predicted a broadly enriched proteome in Ago2-deleted platelets, putatively resulting from de-repressed RISC-mediated silencing in the absence of Ago2. Normalized leukocyte-/erythrocyte-depleted platelet samples isolated from Ago2 KO and control mice were lysed and subject to protein mapping by LC/MS/MS and analyzed by label-free quantitation of derived peptides . We first compared platelet proteomes in cohorts of age-matched female mice. Approximately 2800 unique proteins were identified in each cohort. Although we had predicted substantial enrichment of the platelet proteome by Ago2 deletion, we did not observe broadly increased protein expression in terms of total protein intensity per platelet (Supplemental Fig. 3). We also did not observe broad-spectrum increased expression in the proteomic dataset, although a small number of proteins appeared upregulated in Ago2 KO female platelets compared to controls (Supplemental Table 1). However, we noted with some surprise that Ago1 appeared highly upregulated in Ago2-deleted platelets (14.16-fold; p < 0.00014). Indeed, no Ago1 peptides were detected by LC/MS/MS in platelets from control mice, whereas Ago1-only peptides were highly enriched in Ago2 KO platelets (Fig.  A). In contrast, Ago3, which has been observed previously in megakaryocytic cells , was detected in both platelet cohorts, but Ago3 levels were not significantly different between wild type and Ago2 KO platelets (Fig.  A and Supplemental Table 1). These results indicated that Ago1 expression may be upregulated in MK and/or platelets by Ago2 deletion. We subjected platelet lysates to immunoblotting with Ago1 antibodies, which also partially cross-react with Ago3 due to overlapping epitope sequences. Ago1/3 band intensities were substantially increased in platelets from Ago2 KO mice compared to controls, and this effect was observed in platelets from both male and female mice (Fig.  B, C and Supplemental Fig. 4). We interpret these results to indicate that Argonaute protein expression is important for platelet function, and that lack of the most enriched platelet Argonaute – Ago2 – leads to compensatory upregulation of Ago1 in murine platelets.

 

We sought to investigate whether the apparent induction of Ago1 on a background of Ago2 deletion related to altered Ago1 expression during MK development. For this purpose, we generated AGO2 KO MEG-01 megakaryocytic cell lines using CRISPR/Cas9 targeting of the AGO2 locus (Supplemental Fig. 5A). We selected two independently generated AGO2 KO cell lines for study. Upon induction of these cells to megakaryocytic phenotype with phorbol ester (PMA), as confirmed by positivity for megakaryocyte markers Cd41 and Cd42d, we observed increased AGO1 (AGO1/3) expression in Cd41/42d double-positive platelet-like particles derived from one of the AGO2 KO cell lines after 4 days, whereas by day 5, AGO1 expression in platelet-like particles collected from both AGO2 KO cell lines was double that in control cells (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Platelet-like particle generation was similar between AGO2 KO cells and control cells (Supplemental Fig. 5C). These results indicate that Ago1/AGO1 expression is increased during megakaryopoiesis in the absence of Ago2/AGO2, corresponding to increased Ago1 in Ago2-deleted murine platelets, and platelet generation per se does not appear to be affected by Ago2/AGO2 deletion in murine MK or human megakaryocytic cells.

## Sex-selective modulation of putative ERβ-driven gene expression in Ago2-deleted platelets
To explore further the sex-specific differences in platelet reactivity as a function of Ago2 deletion, we reconsidered differences in the proteomes of male and female Ago2-deleted murine platelets. As noted above, we did not observe broad-spectrum increased protein expression in these platelets compared to controls, although there were some significantly upregulated genes in each cohort. However, we observed that many proteins were downregulated in Ago2-deleted platelets, again in a sex-specific manner – in this case, in platelets from female mice, but not in platelets from male mice. This very surprising result puzzled us for some time, until we considered putative functions of Ago2 beyond RNA-induced silencing of translation. Ago2 also contributes to transcriptional regulation in nucleated cells – an aspect of Ago2 function that has not been investigated in the MK/platelet lineage. Ago2 has been shown to associate with the estrogen receptor beta (ERβ), but not the alpha isoform (in breast cancer cells), and Ago2 association with ERβ regulates binding of this transcription factor to chromatin, and the ability of the ERβ target genes to be transcribed in a manner dependent on estrogen stimulation and Ago2 association . In our platelet proteomics datasets we observed, quite strikingly, that virtually all of the strongly downregulated proteins in female Ago2-deleted platelets compared to controls corresponded to Ago2/ERβ-co-dependent genes regulated at the transcriptional level in breast cancer cells . In contrast, no similar pattern of downregulated proteins in this Ago2/ERβ subset was observed in male Ago2-deleted platelets compared to controls in our proteomics analysis (Table  ). These results may partially explain sex-specific differences in platelet functional response as detailed above, although identification of specific genes involved, and regulatory mechanisms, require further exploration. These results further suggest that Ago2 may have important, gender-specific, hormone-associated roles in regulation of gene expression in MKs, resulting in altered platelet protein expression leading to altered functional response.

 

## Discussion
The results of this study reveal complex roles for Argonaute2 – the principal Argonaute protein in megakaryocytes and platelets – in megakaryocyte development, platelet gene expression, and platelet function. Ago2 ablation in megakaryocytes driven by Pf4-Cre-mediated genomic deletion resulted in normal numbers of enlarged platelets, correlating with fewer but larger megakaryocytes, indicating a role for Ago2 in regulation of megakaryocyte development and control of platelet size. Although platelet function was mostly normal in these mice, sex-specific differences were observed including increased thromboxane reactivity in platelets from male mice, and downregulation of multiple proteins selectively in female mice, pointing to sex-specific roles of Ago2 in platelet gene expression and function. Proteomics analysis followed by immunoblotting of platelet lysates demonstrated that Ago1 - undetectable in control platelets - was highly upregulated in Ago2-deleted platelets, and similar effects were also observed in AGO2-deleted MEG-01 cells, implying compensatory rescue of putatively essential Ago2 functions by induced expression of the Ago1 paralogue, in part via regulation in megakaryocytes. Our findings suggest that Ago2 may support both translational and transcriptional regulatory functions in platelets and megakaryocytes, as further evidenced by downregulated proteins in platelets from female mice representing a cohort of genes shown elsewhere to be coordinately regulated at the transcriptional level by Ago2 in association with estrogen receptor β. Together, these results provide the first characterization of roles of Ago proteins in megakaryocyte development, platelet gene expression, and platelet function.
Increased mean platelet volumes correlated with increased MK size in Ago2 KO mice in a sex-independent manner – although MK were fewer in number - suggesting that Ago2 plays a regulatory role in thrombopoiesis during MK development. Younger platelets, i.e., those formed most recently from MK, are generally considered to be larger in size than older platelets in circulation. These immature platelets have an increased volume at least in part due to their reticulated nature, containing more RNA material than older platelets . Thus, although the overall platelet proteome was not apparently increased in mass with Ago2 deletion as discussed further below, it is conceivable that deletion of Ago2, and in particular loss of Ago2’s unique mRNA slicing capability, may lead to increased MK and platelet volumes in part by increasing total mRNA content. Thus, RISC control of RNA content may be a determinant of MK development regulating platelet size. MK and platelet size and structure are also controlled by intricate coordination of cytoskeletal dynamics, dependent on the actions of many proteins . Whereas we did not observe broad-level alterations in the proteome of Ago2 KO platelets, various proteins were up- or downregulated, and platelet demarcation in developing MK could be strongly dependent on Ago2-driven up- or downregulation of any one or more of these proteins, or proteins of uncharacterized function in regulating the MK or platelet cytoskeleton. Ago2 has also been shown to have slicer-independent roles in hematopoiesis, via control of miRNA maturation , ; however, how these putative functions may affect MK and platelet development remains to be explored. Altogether, our results indicate essential roles for Ago2 in regulation of MK development at least at the level of control of platelet size, through unknown mechanisms that may be manifold.
Platelet activation responses to agonists other than thromboxane were all unaltered in Ago2 KO mice; only moderately increased thromboxane response was observed in male Ago2 KO mice. This overall moderate effect of Ago2 KO on platelet agonist response correlated with unaltered hemostasis after tail tip amputation, and normal clot dynamics in arterioles after laser injury (in male mice). We interpret these results to indicate that deletion of Ago2 in MK and platelets per se was not sufficient to affect major alterations in platelet physiological response in these mice. The modest changes to the proteome observed with Ago2 MK/platelet deletion further support this conclusion; however, additional functions of Ago2 beyond broad-spectrum regulation of the homeostatic proteome may be at play despite the moderate phenotypic responses.
The upregulation of Ago1 expression in platelets in the absence of Ago2 may provide a mechanistic explanation for the moderate effects of Ago2 deletion on platelet protein expression and cellular and physiological function. This result further implies that Ago protein family activity is important for MK and platelet development and function, demanding compensatory rescue by other Ago family proteins in the absence of Ago2. We did not detect Ago1 in wild type platelets in proteomics analyses; however, both wild type and Ago2 KO platelets expressed Ago3 at apparently equivalent levels, consistent with prior studies of AGO3 in human Meg-01 megakaryocytic cells . We also confirmed substantial increases in Ago1/AGO1 protein expression in Ago2 KO murine platelets as well as in AGO2 KO PMA-treated human MEG-01 cells by immunoblotting, pointing to MK as the primary site of upregulation of Ago1 in the absence of Ago2 in the MK/platelet lineage. Ago4 expression has not been seen in murine or human platelets in prior platelet proteomics studies including ours , – , and we did not observe Ago4 in wild type or Ago2 KO platelets in this study. Ago2 is the only mammalian Ago with broad specificity mRNA slicing function, although there is evidence for partial compensation for loss of Ago2 function by Ago3 in other cells , . Like Ago2, Ago3 harbors the residues comprising a catalytic PIWI domain, but Ago3 is held catalytically inactive by additional repressor elements, whereas Ago1 does not possess a catalytic domain , . Hence, Ago3 may partially compensate for loss of Ago2 in upholding RISC function in platelets, as well as in MK, although mechanisms remain to be explored. However, it is important to note in this regard that miRNA-driven RNA inhibition in Ago RISC complexes is thought generally to involve blockade of translation by mechanisms distinct from siRNAs, e.g., not necessarily dependent on target mRNA cleavage , – . The apparent induction of increased Ago1 expression as a result of Ago2 deletion suggests that Ago1 upregulation is important to maintain broad-spectrum RISC suppressor activity in the absence of Ago2. Thus, we predict that Ago1 upregulation in Ago2 KO platelets plays a dominant role in maintaining native RISC function to support proteostasis in megakaryocytes and platelets. The relative contributions of Ago3 and Ago1 to RISC activity in Ago2 KO platelets will require further exploration.
Proteomic analyses revealed interesting and unexpected dependence of some platelet proteins on Ago2, including sex-specific genes that may relate to Ago2 roles in transcriptional regulation in MK. Although we did not observe large-scale changes to the resting proteomes in Ago2-deleted platelets as explored further above, some proteins were upregulated suggesting specific dependence of regulation of these protein levels on Ago2; however, there was little overlap among these proteins between male and female mice. The mechanisms by which regulation of these specific proteins is dependent exclusively on Ago2, whereas that of other proteins was not, requires further investigation. Ago2 may potentially drive roles for RNAi in circulating platelets, as we recently showed that resting platelets in circulation undergo constitutive translation driven by growth factors and hormones in plasma . Platelet mRNA and protein profiles may also relate in significant part to Ago2 roles in mRNA expression in MK, which moreover may be modulated by Ago2 in regulatory processes independent from miRNAs, e.g., Ago2-mediated transcriptional regulation via chromatin binding, and alternative pre-mRNA splicing. As noted above, we observed a striking correlation between a cohort of proteins that only appeared downregulated in platelets from female mice, but not in platelets from male mice, with dependent expression of their cognate mRNA transcripts on ERβ in coordination with Ago2 in breast cancer cells, due to direct interaction of Ago2 with ERβ affecting chromatin binding and potentially also pre-mRNA splicing . ERβ is the more prominent estrogen receptor in the MK/platelet lineage . A connection between estrogens and estrogen receptor expression and platelet function and risk of cardiovascular disease including thrombosis has long been recognized and is still not fully understood – . The correlation of proteins selectively downregulated in platelets in female MK/platelet-deleted Ago2 mice with ERβ/Ago2 co-dependent transcriptional activation of these cognate genes in nucleated cells suggests the attractive hypothesis that Ago2 plays an important role in regulation of expression of these proteins in a sex-specific manner via coordinating estrogen receptor-dependent transcription of estrogen-dependent genes. Ago2 expression levels have recently been shown to stratify breast cancer subtypes, including by ER status , providing further support for Ago2 as an important regulator of ER-dependent gene expression and cellular function. However, we have not tested contributions of ER to Ago2-dependent sex-specific gene expression in MK directly. Further studies on this axis in the megakaryocyte/platelet lineage are warranted.
Altogether, this study reveals new modes of sex-specific regulation of gene expression and potential outcomes for platelet physiology. These results demonstrate important roles for Ago protein family function in megakaryocyte development, platelet gene expression, and platelet function. The overall roles of RISC in these processes, and putative connections between Ago2 and sex-selective gene regulation, will require further investigation such as with compound Ago family deletion.

## Methods

## Mice
Mice deficient in megakaryocyte/platelet Ago2 were generated by breeding Eif2c2flox/flox mice on the C57Bl/6J background (Jackson Lab strain #016520), containing loxP sites flanking Ago2 exons 9–11 (“floxed”), with C57Bl/6J Pf4-Cre mice (Jackson Lab strain #008535). Genotypes were determined from tail clippings taken at the time of weaning, typically at 3–5 weeks of age. Clippings were digested with 25 mM NaOH with 0.2 mM EDTA at 95ºC for 1 hour, at which point an equivalent volume of 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4 was added; samples were then centrifuged at 800xg for 5 minutes at room temperature to isolate DNA. PCR was performed using GoTaq master mixes (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) to determine the presence of Cre and the zygosity of the Ago2 floxed allele. Genotyping primers are as follows, shown 5’ - 3’: Cre FWD, CCCATACAGCACACCTTTTG; Cre REV, TGCACAGTCAGCAGGTT; Ago2 FWD, TGATCATGGTTGAGGTCTGA; Ago2 REV, GTGAGCCACTCACTGTGCAC. 6-12-week-old littermates were used throughout to compare MK/platelet Ago2 KO (Eif2c2flox/flox mic/Pf4-Cre) with controls (Eif2c2WT/WT/Pf4-Cre). All mouse protocols and procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Thomas Jefferson University. This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. For survival studies such as blood draw, mice were returned to the cages for at least one month before being used in subsequent experiments. For non-survival studies, mice were euthanized with CO2 followed by cervical dislocation. No unexplained mortality was observed in these studies. All experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

## Complete cell count analysis, platelet biogenesis and platelet lifespan
Whole blood was collected retro-orbitally into EDTA capillary tubes and transferred to microvette tubes (Sarstedt, Germany). Samples were analyzed using a Hemavet 950 analyzer (Drew Scientific, Miami Lakes, FL, USA). For platelet generation, mice were injected retro-orbitally with depleting α-GpIbα antibodies (R300, Emfret, Germany) at 2 µg/g body weight. Blood was collected every 24 h for five days by retro-orbital bleeding, no more than two consecutive eye bleeds for each eye, and platelet counts and volumes were analyzed. For.
platelet lifespan, mice were injected with non-depleting DyLight™488-conjugated α-GpIbβ antibodies (X488, Emfret, Germany) via tail vein at 0.1 µg/g body weight. Blood was collected retro-orbitally every 24 h for five days. Fluorescence intensity was measured by on a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA, USA).

## Bone marrow isolation and megakaryocyte analysis and histology
Femurs were excised from euthanized mice and surrounding connective tissue was removed. Both ends of the femurs were removed with a razor blade, the femurs were placed in microfuge tubes, and bone marrow cells were collected by centrifugation at 6000xg for 1 min. Bone marrow aspirates were fixed with 1% formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline overnight. Aliquots of fixed aspirates were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated α-Cd42d antibodies (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and propidium iodide (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA). Size and ploidy were measured as a function of forward side scatter (FSC) and mean fluorescence intensities, respectively, in Cd42d(+) populations. For histology, spleens and femurs were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a 3-µM thickness. Paraffin was removed and samples were rehydrated and stained with Harris hematoxylin solution and Eosin Y (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), or with fluorophore-conjugated α-Cd42d antibodies. Images were captured on an EVOS M5000 microscope (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and analyzed using ImageJ.

## Platelet activation
Blood was collected retro-orbitally into EDTA capillary and added to a microfuge containing 500 µL containing 3.8% sodium citrate. Citrated blood was transferred to 15-mL conical tubes containing 2.5 mL of Tyrode’s buffer plus 1 mg/mL BSA and 0.1 µg/mL PGE1. Samples were centrifuged at 1200x g for 10 min at RT. Platelet pellets were gently resuspended in Tyrode’s/BSA, incubated with agonists in the presence of saturating amounts of fluorophore-conjugated α-Cd41 (clone 133913, BioLegend), Jon/A α-activated Cd41/61 (clone 023 − 2, Emfret, Germany), and α-Cd62p (P-selectin, clone 553744, BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) for 15 min at 37 °C, and analyzed on a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer. The platelet population was gated based on FSC/side scatter (SSC) and Cd41 positivity.

## Hemostasis
Mice aged 8 weeks maintained under anesthesia throughout the procedure using aerosolized isoflurane. Mice were placed in a prone position and a 1-mm diameter distal segment of the tail was amputated using a razor blade. The tail was immediately submerged in saline warmed to 37ºC held below the body level. The mouse was monitored for 20 min to determine the time of the initial and final cessation of bleeding and to record any re-bleeding events.

## Clot dynamics following laser injury in cremaster muscle arterioles
8-12-week-old WT and Ago2 KO mice were used. AlexaFluor568®–conjugated α-Cd41 antibody F(ab)2 fragments and AlexaFluor488®–conjugated α–P-selectin (Cd62p) antibodies (BD Bioscience), and AlexaFluor647®–conjugated fibrinogen (a generous gift from Dr. Hartmut Weiler, Medical College of Wisconsin) were administered via the retro-orbital plexus to visualize Cd41 + platelet accumulation, P-selection(+) platelet accumulation and fibrin deposition. Injuries were produced using a pulsed nitrogen dye laser (Ablate! photoablation system, 3i) in arterioles limited to 30–50 μm in diameter. Thrombus growth was measured over a course of 3 min. Videos were analyzed to measure peak and total platelet accumulation, as well as P-selectin and fibrin area, at the site of injury.

## Proteomics (LC/MS/MS & data analysis)
Proteomics data was generated at the Wistar Institute (Philadelphia, PA, USA) from two sets of murine platelet samples, separated by sex, as described previously . Briefly, platelets were isolated from whole blood obtained from the retroorbital plexus and depleted of red and white blood cells. Platelet numbers were normalized across all samples, then platelets were flash frozen using a slurry of dry ice and 100% ethanol for 30 s and stored at -80ºC prior to being sent to Wistar for LC/MS/MS analysis. 10 µg of each sample was reduced with TCEP, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin. Tryptic digests were processed using a single-shot extended 4-hour LC gradient on a Thermo Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. Analysis of MS data began by searching with full tryptic specificity against the UniProt mouse proteome database and a common contaminant database using MaxQuant 1.6.17.0 with protein and peptide false discovery rate set at 1%. Protein quantification was performed using Razor + unique peptides, with Razor peptides being shared, non-unique, peptides assigned to the protein group with the most other peptides. Intensity values reflect the non-normalized sums of the peptide MS peak intensities for a given protein. Unsupervised hierarchical cluster plots were generated using intensity data and range from a minimum of -2 to a maximum of + 2. Fold changes were calculated based on log2 transformation of intensity values and replaced missing proteins with the minimum value of the dataset; p values were calculated using the log2 imputed intensity values.

## MEG-01 cells and AGO2 deletion
Wild-type MEG-01 cells (ATCC line CRL-2021) were cultured in RPMI medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% FBS (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), 1% non-essential amino acids (Cytiva, Amersham, United Kingdom), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (GE HealthCare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA) in a humidified, 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 C. Cells were cultured in EasYFlasks (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, ME, USA) and treated with 50 ng/mL of phorbol myristate acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) to induce differentiation and platelet-like particle formation.
AGO2 knockout MEG-01 cells were generated via CRISPR/cas9 genome editing with the help of Dr. Xianguo Kong using a plasmid purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA; plasmid #48138), as detailed previously , . Briefly, pspCas9(BB)-2A-GFP(pX458) was modified to utilize the EF1α promoter in place of CMV, and to include a second guide RNA insertion site, separated from the first insertion site by 442 nucleotides. Guide RNAs were designed using the open source CRISPOR program to target exon 1 of both AGO2 transcript variants; AGO2 deletion screening primers were designed flanking exon 1, to produce a 385 bp amplicon if AGO2 was successfully deleted, 95 bps shorter than the amplicon generated by wild-type AGO2. Both the guide RNAs and PCR primers are shown in Table 4. gRNA and PCR primers are as follows, listed 5’ ◊ 3’: Set #1 gRNA FWD, CCGTAATTTGATTGTTCTCCCGG; Set #1 gRNA REV, AAACCCGGGAGAACAATCAAATTA; Set #2 gRNA FWD, ACCGTCAAGCCAGAGAAGTGCCCG; Set #2 gRNA REV, AACCGGGCACTTCTCTGGCTTGA; AGO2 KO screen FWD, CCTGGACGTGAATGGAAACA; AGO2 KO screen REV, TCAATCCGTAGGTTTGACTCTG.
