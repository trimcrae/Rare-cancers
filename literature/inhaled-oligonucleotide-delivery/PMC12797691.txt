## Aim
During the tooth-root elongation phase, dental epithelial stem cells (DESCs) cluster together and release various signaling molecules, facilitating the differentiation of stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs) into odontoblasts. This study aimed to investigate the regulatory mechanism of secretory proteins from DESCs cultured in a spherical system mimicking the in vivo microenvironment on the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs during root development, providing theoretical foundations and potential targets for dentin regeneration therapy based on epithelial-mesenchymal interactions.

## Methodology
DESCs were isolated from the root tips of rodent incisors and cultured using low-attachment surface to mimic their in vivo clustering. The effects of spheroid culture (versus monolayer culture) on SCAPs viability and odontoblastic differentiation potential were systematically compared. Label-free mass spectrometry and bioinformatics analyses were employed to characterize the secretory protein profiles of DESCs under different culture conditions. In vitro and in vivo experiments validated the role of a key differential protein Lumican and its downstream signaling mechanisms.

## Results
Compared to monolayer culture, spheroid culture significantly enhanced the secretion of Lumican by DESCs. Lumican demonstrated the ability to significantly promote dentin differentiation of SCAPs in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic analysis revealed that Lumican binds specifically to integrin β1 (ITGβ1) on the SCAPs membrane, thereby activating the downstream ERK signaling pathway to mediate its pro-differentiation effects.

## Conclusion
This study illustrates that spheroid culture can restore the epithelial microenvironment of DESCs, leading to the release of Lumican, which targets SCAPs. This research uncovers the crucial roles of Lumican and ITGβ1 in DESCs-SACPs interactions and paves the way for potential therapeutic strategies in dentin regeneration.

## Introduction
Pulpal-periapical diseases, primarily caused by cariogenic infections, dental trauma, and developmental abnormalities, represent a prevalent clinical challenge in dental practice. The disease progression may lead to progressive alveolar bone resorption and cemental defects, with severe cases involving the dentino-cemental junction. Such pathological changes can compromise the structural integrity of the tooth root, ultimately resulting in tooth mobility or even exfoliation [ ]. Currently, the common treatment combines microscopic endodontic therapy with apicoectomy, which effectively controls infection through hermetic root canal obturation. However, the inherent limitations of mechanical debridement preclude functional regeneration of dental hard tissues [ ]. Consequently, tissue engineering-based regeneration of the apical dentin complex has emerged as a pivotal research direction in odontogenic tissue regeneration [ ].
Stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs), a unique population of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived from immature root apical papillae, have garnered significant attention in odontogenic tissue regeneration due to their exceptional proliferative capacity, self-renewal potential, and multilineage differentiation capabilities [ , ]. Dentin formation is fundamentally dependent on the differentiation and functional activity of odontoblasts, with SCAPs serving as the principal precursor cells for root odontoblasts, thereby playing a pivotal role in periapical tissue regeneration [ ]. Existing studies on SCAP microenvironments have employed diverse models to explore pulp-dentin complex regeneration, including in vivo animal models, in vitro 2D cultures, and 3D platforms like microfluidic “tooth-on-a-chip” system [ ]. However, the current protocols for efficiently directing SCAPs differentiation into odontoblasts remain suboptimal, necessitating further refinement of induction strategies and elucidation of the underlying molecular mechanisms.
Epithelial-mesenchymal interactions play a pivotal role in the development of ectodermal structures, including hair follicles and tooth germs [ ]. During tooth development, these interactions facilitate the precise temporal and spatial expression of various signaling molecules. For instance, growth factors and signaling molecules released by ameloblasts promote the differentiation of mesenchymal cells in the papilla into odontoblasts, thereby contributing to dentin formation [ , ]. However, during the advanced stages of human tooth development, the progressive deterioration and apoptosis of the dental epithelium, particularly ameloblasts, lead to a significant reduction in the release of essential signaling factors [ ]. In contrast, the root apex of rodent incisors features a perpetually expanding specialized epithelial structure that resembles the dental epithelial cell layer observed during the early stages of tooth germ development. This structure harbors dental epithelial stem cells (DESCs), which have the capacity to differentiate into ameloblasts and facilitate the continuous differentiation of SCAPs at the incisor root apex, thereby supporting ongoing tissue formation [ ]. As a result, DESCs in rodent incisors serve as an excellent model for investigating the epithelial cells critical for tooth development and dentin formation.
During tooth development, DESCs aggregate to form a layered structure comprising the inner epithelial layer (IEE), outer epithelial layer (OEE), sheath cell layer (SR), and stratum intermedium (SI). These stratified structures are essential for the formation and functionality of epithelial cells. However, traditional monolayer culture conditions fail to reestablish this complex epithelial hierarchy, making it challenging to fully replicate the physiological characteristics and developmental processes of the dental epithelium [ , ]. On the other hand, spheroid culture offers a three-dimensional (3D) environment that promotes the formation of spherical structures by DESCs, more accurately mimicking the in vivo context and enhancing epithelial-mesenchymal interactions. The primary objective of spheroid culture is to modify the cellular microenvironment, thereby better replicating the 3D spatial organization and intercellular interactions found in vivo. This altered microenvironment provides enhanced growth conditions for seed cells, such as SCAPs, promoting their differentiation into odontoblasts. Consistently, recent studies have demonstrated that 3D graphene/silk fibroin scaffolds also support the biological behavior of dental stem cells, as they can maintain hDPSC viability, upregulate the expression of osteo/odontoblastic markers, and facilitate extracellular matrix mineralization, further confirming the superiority of 3D culture systems in dental tissue regeneration [ ]. Additionally, the 3D culture system fosters gene and protein expression profiles that more closely resemble the cells’ native state, thereby enhancing their physiological functions [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that spheroid cultures of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath (HERS) globules, dental germ cells, and dental mesenchymal stem cells exhibit increased efficacy in generating dentin-like tissues [ – ]. Notably, small leucine-rich proteoglycans (SLRPs)—a family of extracellular matrix molecules critical for mineralized tissue differentiation—have been widely implicated in bone and dentin formation, but their distinct roles in epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk for odontoblastic differentiation remain underdefined. Existing studies highlight that classical SLRP members, such as Decorin and Biglycan, primarily regulate dentin development by sequestering growth factors (e.g., TGF-β, BMPs) to fine-tune collagen fibrillogenesis and mesenchymal stem cell differentiation [ , ].In contrast, Lumican—a less studied SLRP subclass member with keratan sulfate chains—has recently been linked to tissue morphogenesis and matrix mineralization, but its mechanism of action in mediating epithelial-mesenchymal interactions for odontoblastic differentiation remains unknown [ , ]. Unlike Decorin/Biglycan, Lumican’s reported functions in osseous tissues do not rely on growth factor sequestration. Instead, it may exert direct effects on cell surface receptors [ , ]. This distinction underscores the need to investigate Lumican’s unique role in DESCs-driven SCAPs differentiation. Nevertheless, the precise signaling mechanisms underlying these processes remain to be fully elucidated.
This study aims to investigate the biological effects of conditioned media derived from DESCs under monolayer culture (ML-DESCs-CM) and spheroid culture conditions (SPH-DESCs-CM) on SCAPs. We will assess the proteomic differences between ML-DESCs-CM and SPH-DESCs-CM using mass spectrometry analysis, identifying Lumican as a key protein that facilitates odontoblastic differentiation. Furthermore, we will explore Lumican’s role in the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs through both in vitro and in vivo experiments, elucidating the associated mechanisms. Our findings aim to provide novel insights and potential applications for dentin regeneration.

## Materials and methods
All animals were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (China). All animal experiments were reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and approved by the Ethics Committee for Experimental Animal Welfare of Central South University (XMSB-2024-0381).

## Primary cell isolation and culture
Prior to tissue collection, neonatal pups were euthanized by decapitation. Mandibular incisors were collected from 5 to 7 days-old C57BL/6 male mice, for cell isolation. Cervical loop tissues were carefully dissected under a stereomicroscope and digested at 37 °C for 40 min using a solution containing 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Solarbio, China) and 4 mg/mL dispase type II (Solarbio, China). The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet, which contained a mixture of DESCs, SCAPs, and tissue fragments, was resuspended and plated into culture flasks pre-coated with 2 µg/cm² rat-tail tendon collagen type I (Corning-Costar, USA). Cells were maintained in epithelial cell culture medium (Shanghai Pumao, China). After 3 to 5 days of incubation, fibroblast-like and epithelial-like cells emerged around the tissue fragments. DESCs and SCAPs were subsequently isolated using differential digestion. DESCs were maintained in the original epithelial cell culture medium, while SCAPs were cultured in mesenchymal stem cell medium (MSCM; Sciencell, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

## Flow cytometry analysis
DESCs and SCAPs were detached using 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) to generate single-cell suspensions. For DESC analysis, cells were labeled with phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated CD49f antibody (12-0495-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 1:500), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated CD29 (11-0291-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 1:500), and CD90 antibodies (ab25672, Abcam, UK, 1:500). For SCAP analysis, cells were labeled with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated CD24 antibody (APC-65121, Proteintech, China, 1:300) as well as FITC-conjugated CD133 (11-1331-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 1:500), CD90, and CD45 (MCD4501, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 1:500) antibodies. All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Cell populations were analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter Life Science, USA), and the data were processed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, USA).

## Immunofluorescence staining
For immunofluorescence analysis, DESCs were plated into culture dishes suitable for confocal microscopy and cultured overnight. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China), and blocked with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the primary antibody Krt14 (ET1610-42, HUABIO, China, 1:100) followed by treatment with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China, 1:200). The nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Biosharp, China). SCAPs (n = 3) were treated with 10 nM 6*His-tagged Lumican (MCE, China) for 30 min, then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Biosharp, China). After fixation, permeabilization, and blocking, the cells were incubated with anti-6*His antibody (66005-1-Ig, Proteintech, China, 1:200) and anti-integrin β1 (ITGβ1) antibody (12594-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:200) at 4 °C for 16 h. The cells were rinsed with PBS and treated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (AF488, Beyotime, China, 1:200) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (A0473, Beyotime, China, 1:200) at room temperature for 1 h. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, the cells were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany).

## Preparation of DESCs spheroids and conditioned media
To prepare a low-attachment surface, 0.67 g of DMEM powder (Gibco, USA) and 0.185 g of sodium bicarbonate powder (Sigma, USA) were dissolved in 25 mL of sterile water and reserved for later use. Separately, 0.25 g of agarose powder (Life Technologies, USA) was dissolved in 25 mL of sterile water and heated to boiling to ensure complete dissolution. Equal volumes of the DMEM solution and agarose solution were mixed, dispensed into a 24-well plate, and subjected to UV irradiation for 1 h. The mixture solidified to form a low-attachment surface. The specific experimental workflow is shown in Figure S1a: A total of 3 × 10⁴ DESC cells per well were seeded into conventional 24-well plates and pre-coated low-attachment 24-well plates. After 24 h of culture, cells in the standard plates adhered and grew as a monolayer, while those in the low-attachment plates spontaneously formed spheroids. The medium was then replaced with serum-free DMEM, and the cultures were maintained for an additional 24 h. Finally, the conditioned medium (CM) from each group was collected and designated as ML-DESCs-CM (monolayer-cultured DESCs-CM) and SPH-DESCs-CM (spheroid-cultured DESCs-CM), respectively.
For subsequent functional assays (CCK-8, EdU, ALP, ARS) requiring extended incubation, the serum-free CM was diluted in basal medium supplemented with 10% FBS. This supplementation was critical to maintain SCAPs in a healthy, proliferative state and to prevent the confounding effects of cellular stress or starvation that could otherwise compromise the interpretation of differentiation outcomes.

## Assays of multilineage differentiation potentials
For osteogenic differentiation, SCAPs were cultured in Osteo/Odontoblastic induction medium (OIM) consisting of 10 nM dexamethasone, 2 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 µM ascorbic acid (all purchased from Solarbio, China). This culture was maintained for 14 days to promote the differentiation of SCAPs into osteoblasts. Following differentiation, the cells were stained with Alizarin Red to assess the formation of the mineralized matrix. For chondrogenic differentiation, SCAPs were grown in commercial chondrogenic induction medium (iCell, China) for 14 days. Afterward, the cells were stained with Alcian Blue to evaluate glycosaminoglycan production. For adipogenic differentiation, SCAPs were cultured in commercial adipogenic induction medium (Abiowell, China) for 14 days. Following differentiation, the cells were stained with Oil Red O to assess lipid accumulation.

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction(RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was isolated from the cells using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, USA). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using the PrimeScript RT kit (Perfect Real Time, Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was conducted with the Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix kit (Yeasen, China) on Real-Time PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) under the following cycling conditions: Initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s, concluding with a final dissociation step. To normalize mRNA expression levels, GAPDH was used as the reference gene. All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3). The relative expression levels of target genes were then calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. The primer sequences employed in this study are listed in Table  . The amplification efficiency data and melt curve analyses for all primers have been included in Fig. S15.

 

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
SCAPs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 103 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For conditioned media experiments, cells were divided into Control, ML-DESCs-CM, and SPH-DESCs-CM groups, all supplemented with 10% FBS; for test Lumican effects, cells were treated with 0, 0.1, 1, 10, or 20 nM Lumican (HY-P70370, MCE, China) under the same culture conditions. After incubation, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, followed by 1 h dark incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3).

## EdU incorporation assay
SCAPs were cultured under identical conditions and grouped as described in Sect.  . Cell proliferation was assessed using the Click-iT™ EdU Imaging Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were pulse-labeled with 10 µM EdU for 2 h, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. EdU incorporation was visualized by adding the Click-iT™ reaction cocktail and incubating for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min and fluorescence images were captured with a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3).

## Alizarin red S staining(ARS)and quantification
SCAPs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were divided into different experimental series, Conditioned media groups: Control, OIM, ML-DESCs-CM, and SPH-DESCs-CM; Lumican treatment: Control, OIM, and Lumican; Pathway inhibition: Control, Lumican, and Lumican + inhibitor (U0126, HY-12031 A; SP600125, HY-12041, MCE, China). All groups (except Control) were maintained in mineralization medium containing 10% FBS, 50 µM ascorbic acid, 2 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 10 nM dexamethasone for 14 days. Following induction, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 1% Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2; Solarbio, China) for 15 min, then visualized under a light microscope (Nikon, Japan). For quantification, mineralized nodules were dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC; Macklin, China) for 20–30 min, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader for quantitative analysis. All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3).

## Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and activity analysis
Following the culturing method outlined in Sect. 2.9, SCAPs were cultured in the designated media for 7 days to assess early odontoblastic differentiation after 7 days of induction. Following induction, cells were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min. ALP staining was performed using the ALP staining kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were treated with the staining solution at room temperature for 10 min and then visualized under a light microscope. ALP activity was quantified using the ALP assay kit (Nanjing Jianjian Biological Engineering, China). The samples were first washed with PBS and then lysed with 0.3% Triton X-100 at 4 °C for 24 h. After incubation at 37 °C for 15 min, absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a microplate reader for quantitative analysis. All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3).

## Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and bioinformatics analysis
Proteins were extracted, digested, and purified from 6 samples (n = 3 for ML-DESCs-CM and n = 3 for SPH-DESCs-CM). Peptide separation and analysis were conducted using a nanoflow LC-MS/MS system. Raw data were matched against the mouse protein database, and protein identification and quantification were performed using Proteome Discoverer software. The reliability of the results was ensured by controlling the false discovery rate at 1%. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified with a Student’s t-test (p-value<0.05) and |log2 FC|≥1.2. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis via InterProScan-5 categorized DEPs into biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis via KOBAS 3.0 identified significantly enriched metabolic and signaling pathways.

## ELISA assay
The level of Lumican protein in the conditioned media was determined using a commercially available ELISA kit (EM1905, FineTest, China). Briefly, 50 µL of conditioned media was added to the ELISA plate and incubated with the detection antibody at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Following plate washing, a biotin-conjugated antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for another hour. After an additional wash, the SABC working solution was applied, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After a final wash, substrate solution was added for color development, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min before adding stop buffer. The colorimetric reaction was analyzed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## Immunohistochemistry
5-7-day-old C57BL/6 mice were euthanized by decapitation, while 3-4-week-old C57BL/6 male mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation prior to tissue collection. Apical tissues were extracted from incisors and molars, and mandibles were harvested immediately after euthanasia. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using a two-step protocol: first, heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was carried out by incubating the sections in 8 M urea (Solarbio, China) solution at 95 °C for 15 min; second, enzymatic retrieval was performed by treating the sections with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. Endogenous enzymes were inactivated by incubating sections with Endogenous Peroxidase Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, China) for 15 min. For negative controls, the primary antibody was omitted and replaced with PBS under identical staining conditions. The primary antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody kit (AWI0629, Abiowell, China, 1:200) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Visualization was achieved using a DAB staining kit (Abiowell, China), and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin (Abiowell, China). Finally, the sections were dehydrated, mounted on slides, and examined using a microscope. Antibodies: Lumican (AWA10224, Abiowell, China, 1:200), DSPP (SC-73632, Santa Cruz, USA, 1: 200), Osteocalcin (SC-365797, Santa Cruz, USA, 1: 100), Collagen 1 (67288-1-Ig, Proteintech, China, 1:200).

## Western blotting
After discarding the supernatant, the precipitate of cells after centrifugation was resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (Abiowell, China) for protein extraction. Prepare 10% separating gel and 5% concentrating gel using the SDS-PAGE gel preparation kit (Beyotime, China). Denature the protein samples and separate them by gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred to a NC membrane (Beyotime, China), which was blocked with 5% skimmed milk before applying the primary antibodies. After incubating overnight at 4 °C, the membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST; Abiowell, China). A second wash with TBST was performed, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody conjugated to HRP. Finally, Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (OI900 Touch, Bio-OI, China). All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates (n ≥ 3). The inhibitors and antibodies used in this study included: U0126 (HY-12031 A, MCE, China, 10 µM), SP600125 (HY-12041, MCE, China, 10 µM), AIIB2 (Ab03450-6.1, Absolute, China, 1ug/ml), RGD (HY-P0278, MCE, China, dissolve in 5% DMSO, 5 µM), DMP1 (SC-73633, Santa Cruz, USA, 1:200), DSPP (SC-73632, Santa Cruz, USA, 1:200), OCN (SC-365797, Santa Cruz, USA, 1:100), ITGβ1 (12594-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:500), ERK (#4695, CST, USA, 1:1000), p-ERK (#4370, CST, USA, 1:2000), JNK (ab179461, Abcam, UK,1:1000), p-JNK (ab124956, Abcam, UK, 1:5000), p-38 (AWA48968, Abiowell, China, 1:1000), p-p38 (AWA48964, Abiowell, China, 1:1000), GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:5000), ACTIN (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, China, 1:5000), HRP-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody (AWS0002, Abiowell, China, 1:5000), HRP-conjugated goat anti-Mouse Secondary Antibody (AWS0001, Abiowell, China, 1:5000).

## Subcutaneous dentin regeneration model and histochemical staining
The study involved eight healthy male SPF-grade immunodeficient nude male mice aged 6–8 weeks (Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd, China). The selection of this specific animal model because the T-cell deficiency in nude mice allows successful engraftment of mouse-derived SCAPs without immune rejection. The animals were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 4 per group) using a random number generator: the Lumican experimental group and the hydroxyapatite (HA, Aladdin, China)/tricalcium phosphate (TCP, Macklin, China) control group. The sample size was determined based on previous studies and the available resources. The group allocation was known to the researcher only during the allocation phase, while the experiment, outcome assessment, and data analysis were all conducted in a blinded manner. To ensure research integrity, we implemented several key measures. Group allocation information was concealed from personnel responsible for outcome evaluation and statistical analysis throughout the study period. The surgical operator was only aware of group assignments during the cell inoculation phase and participated in no subsequent evaluations. Predefined exclusion criteria included surgery-related mortality, wound infection, and graft displacement. No animals required exclusion based on these criteria, and all data were included in the final analysis.
Post-operative analgesia was provided via subcutaneous administration of buprenorphine every 12 h for 48 h. SCAPs were seeded into culture dishes, and when the cells reached approximately 80% confluency, the medium of the experimental group was replaced. After the addition of 10 nM Lumican, the cells were cultured for 24 h. The media were then removed, and the cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and injected into the dorsal region of the mice. No exogenous Lumican was delivered in vivo. For the cell injection procedure, all mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation to ensure minimal pain and distress. To minimize potential confounders, treatment administration, measurements, and cage placement were randomized to avoid biases. The mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, maintained at 22 ± 318 2 °C, and provided with standard rodent chow and water ad libitum. After 4 weeks of cell inoculation, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following deep anesthesia with isoflurane, and the subcutaneous bone tissue masses were harvested. The tissue samples were sectioned and embedded in paraffin, followed by staining with H&E, Masson’s trichrome and immunohistochemistry to evaluate dentin-like tissue. The antibodies used for immunohistochemistry were as follows: DSPP (SC-73632, Santa Cruz, USA, 1:50); OPN (22952-1-AP, Proteintech, China, 1:250); Collagen I (GB11022-3-100, Service1io, China, 1:600); HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (5220 − 0336, SeraCare, UK, 1:500), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (5220 − 0341, SeraCare, UK, 1:500).

## SiRNA interference
The siRNA sequences targeting the mouse Lumican gene (sequence retrieved from the NCBI database) were designed and synthesized by Gene Rulor (Generulor Co., Ltd, China). The specific sequences are listed in Table S2. For transfection, monolayer-cultured DESCs were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at approximately 80% confluence. The cells were transfected with Lumican-siRNA at final concentrations of 30, 50, and 100 nM using Lipo 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. A fluorescently labeled siRNA was used as an indicator to monitor transfection efficiency.

## Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
SCAPs were divided into two groups: one treated with Lumican and the other untreated. Cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer, scraped using cell scrapers, and subjected to ultrasonication followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Protein extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either 6*His antibody (66005-1-Ig, Proteintech, China, 1:100) or control IgG (B900620, Proteintech, China, 1:100), followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads (C600689-0020, Sangon, China). After washing the beads, bound proteins were eluted with loading buffer. For Western blot analysis, samples were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to NC membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: 6*His antibody (1:5,000) and anti-ITGβ1 antibody (1:5,000), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (AWS0002, AWS0001, Abiowell, China, 1:5000). Finally, protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.), unless otherwise noted. For comparisons between two groups, a paired or unpaired Student’s t-test was used where data followed a normal distribution. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated and included to provide a measure of the magnitude of the observed effects. For multiple-group comparisons, statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

## Results

## Isolation and characterization of scaps and DESCs
The apical tissues of mandibular incisors from 5-7-day-old C57BL/6 mice were cultured using an enzymatic tissue explant method (Fig. a). After 3–5 days of adherent culture, primary cells migrated from the tissue edges, forming a mixed population exhibiting both short spindle-shaped and cobblestone-like morphologies (Fig. b). Two distinct cell populations were isolated and purified through differential digestion based on their adhesion capacity [ ]: one displaying fibroblast-like spindle morphology (Fig. c) and another exhibiting polygonal, tightly-connected pavement-like morphology (Fig. g). The fibroblast-like population demonstrated trilineage differentiation potential when cultured in chondrogenic, osteogenic, and adipogenic induction media, as confirmed by positive staining with Alcian Blue, Alizarin Red, and Oil Red O (Fig. d). Flow cytometric analysis revealed this population expressed MSCs markers CD133 and CD90, while being negative for hematopoietic marker CD45. Notably, the percentage of cells expressing 11.4% CD24, a specific marker for SCAPs, was higher than the previously reported 7.56% [ ] (Fig. e, f). These results confirmed the MSCs characteristics of these SCAPs. Immunofluorescence staining of the pavement-like population showed specific expression of basal epithelial cell marker cytokeratin 14 (K14) (Fig. h). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated high expression of epithelial stem cell markers CD49f and CD29, with absence of CD90 expression (Fig. i, j). This expression pattern was consistent with previous studies: CD49f is a specific marker for DESCs and shows stronger stemness characteristics in CD49f-positive cell populations from Malassez epithelial rests [ , ], while CD29 expression positively correlates with proliferative capacity and serves as a relatively specific marker for epithelial stem cells [ ]. These findings confirmed the epithelial stem cell characteristics of these DESCs.

 

## SPH-DESCs-CM promotes viability, proliferation, and odontoblastic differentiation of scaps
During tooth development, epithelial-derived signaling molecules play a crucial regulatory role in the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that 3D culture technology can effectively mimic the in vivo cellular microenvironment by promoting the formation of cell aggregates, spheroids, or organoids, thereby enhancing the biological functional properties of stem cells [ , ]. As a scaffold-free culture system, spheroid culture facilitates the self-assembly of cells into 3D organoid structures, which not only reconstructs intercellular tight junctions and apical-basal polarity but also better maintains the physiological state of cells in vitro [ ]. Martin Binder et al. established a spheroid culture system that significantly enhanced the stemness maintenance capacity and plasticity of DESCs [ ]. In this study, we used agarose-coated culture plates to create low-adhesion surfaces, which enabled DESCs to aggregate into uniform spheroids (Fig. S1b). Live/Dead staining followed by fluorescence microscopy revealed that the vast majority of cells within the spheroids remained viable after formation (Fig. S1c). This confirms that this spheroid culture model maintains excellent cell health and is suitable for long-term culture and. Immunofluorescence images showed strong positive expression of the key epithelial junction protein E-cadherin the spheroids, providing direct evidence that the spheroids maintain a well-organized epithelial phenotype, characterized by functional cell-cell adhesions (Fig. S1d). Besides, compared to ML-DESCs, the SPH-DESCs showed a significant upregulation of HIF-1α, which provided evidence for the establishment of a physiological hypoxic environment within our spheroid model (Fig. S1e, f). The collected conditioned media from both ML-DESCs-CM and SPH-DESCs-CM were used for subsequent experiments (Fig. S1a).
To evaluate the effects of SPH-DESCs-CM on SCAPs, we compared the impacts of SPH-DESCs-CM and ML-DESCs-CM on SCAP viability, proliferative capacity, and odontoblastic differentiation potential. CCK-8 assays revealed that SCAPs treated with SPH-DESCs-CM exhibited significantly higher viability on days 1, 2, and 3 (Fig.  a). In EdU incorporation assays, under the same field of view, the SPH-DESCs-CM group showed increased numbers of EdU-positive SCAPs, indicating enhanced DNA synthesis and proliferative capacity (Fig.  b). After 7 days of odontoblastic induction, ALP staining demonstrated stronger staining intensity in the SPH-DESCs-CM group (Fig.  c), suggesting enhanced early differentiation, which was confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig.  d). Following 14 days of induction culture, ARS staining revealed more pronounced mineralized nodule formation in the SPH-DESCs-CM group (Fig.  e), with semi-quantitative calcium analysis further supporting this conclusion (Fig.  f). RT-qPCR analysis showed that SPH-DESCs-CM treatment significantly upregulated the expression of odontogenesis-related genes, including Dspp, Dmp1, and Ocn, in SCAPs, while no significant change was observed in the expression of the osteogenic-related gene Bsp compared with the OIM group (Fig.  g). Western blot analysis and quantification confirmed these findings at the protein level (Fig.  h). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S5. These results demonstrate that compared with conventional odontoblastic induction medium and ML-DESCs-CM, SPH-DESCs-CM promotes SCAPs viability, proliferation, and odontoblastic differentiation potential.

 

## Proteomic and bioinformatics analyses of ML-DESCs-CM and SPH-DESCs-CM
To elucidate the mechanism by which SPH-DESCs-CM enhances the odontoblastic differentiation potential of SCAPs, we performed label-free quantitative proteomic analysis comparing the protein profiles of ML-DESCs-CM and SPH-DESCs-CM. Our proteomic profiling identified 576 proteins, with 364 achieving reliable quantification (Fig.  a). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated high similarity in protein composition between the two groups (Fig.  b). Differential expression analysis revealed 72 upregulated and 68 downregulated proteins in SPH-DESCs-CM compared to ML-DESCs-CM (Fig.  c).
GO enrichment analysis (Fig.  d) of DEPs showed significant enrichment in cellular components (CC) related to extracellular regions, including extracellular matrix (ECM) and vesicular structures, suggesting that spheroid culture may enhance cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions by remodeling the extracellular microenvironment. Molecular function (MF) analysis indicated predominant involvement in signaling receptor binding, histone binding, and collagen binding. Biological process (BP) terms were significantly enriched in embryo development, cell population proliferation, and vesicle-mediated transport, implying complex ECM-mediated intercellular interactions in signal transduction and protein trafficking. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig.  e) revealed DEPs enrichment in disease-related pathways, particularly proteoglycans in cancer, indicating that spheroid culture may remodel functional microenvironments through ECM homeostasis regulation.
Through filtering of DEPs, we focused on the top 20 most differentially expressed proteins (Fig. f). It is noteworthy that the list included several serum-derived proteins (e.g., Coagulation Factor II, Carboxypeptidase B2, Hemolytic complement), whose presence is likely attributable to incomplete removal of residual serum components during the collection of SPH-DESCs-CM, as centrifugation may not fully eliminate proteins from the initial culture phase. While the top four proteins, including the aforementioned, showed marked upregulation, their biological functions were primarily confined to coagulation, immunity, and basic metabolic regulation [ – ]. Notably, Lumican, ranked fifth among DEPs, is a secreted ECM proteoglycan crucial for ECM organization and tissue morphogenesis [ ]. Emerging evidence demonstrates Lumican’s capacity to promote osteoblastogenesis while inhibiting osteoclastogenesis [ ], prompting our selection of this molecule for further investigation as a potential mediator of SCAPs odontoblastic differentiation. ELISA validation confirmed the proteomic findings (Fig. g).
Immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. h) revealed Lumican expression in the OEE and IEE of young incisors, spatially coinciding with DESCs localization patterns from single-cell sequencing studies [ ]. In contrast, HERS of young molars showed minimal Lumican expression, with complete absence upon root apex closure. These findings suggest that spheroid culture may recapitulate in vivo Lumican expression patterns through enhanced DESCs secretion.

 

## Lumican promotes viability and odontoblastic differentiation of scaps in vitro and in vivo
To investigate the functional role of Lumican in odontoblastic differentiation, SCAPs were treated with exogenous recombinant Lumican protein. In our preliminary experiments, we excluded any potential confounding effects from Polymyxin B (Fig. S2). The working concentration was determined through concentration gradient experiments. CCK-8 assays revealed a concentration-dependent increase in SCAPs viability within the 0–10 nM range, though no significant difference was observed between 1 nM and 10 nM groups (Fig.  a). Following 7 days of odontoblastic induction, the 10 nM Lumican-treated group exhibited significantly enhanced ALP activity (Fig.  b), establishing this concentration for subsequent experiments.
To evaluate the effects of 10 nM Lumican on the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs in vitro, after 7 days of odontoblastic induction, ALP staining showed the Lumican-treated group exhibited a level of early differentiation comparable to or exceeding that of the OIM group (Fig.  c), and quantitative analysis of activity supported this conclusion (Fig.  d). After extending the induction period to 14 days, ARS staining revealed comparable to or greater mineralization than OIM group (Fig.  e), which was further supported by quantitative calcium deposition measurements (Fig.  f). At the molecular level, both RT-qPCR (Fig.  g) and Western blot analysis (Fig.  h) showed significant upregulation of key odontoblastic differentiation markers, including DSPP, DMP1, and OCN in Lumican-treated SCAPs. Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S6.
While the nude mouse subcutaneous transplantation model cannot fully replicate the apical microenvironment, its controlled nature provides an optimal system for isolating Lumican-specific effects. In vivo validation involved co-transplantation of untreated or Lumican-treated SCAPs with HA/TCP particles. After 4 weeks: H&E staining and quantitation showed enhanced mineralization and thicker odonto-dentin-like tissue layers on HA/TCP surfaces; Masson’s trichrome staining revealed thicker, more uniformly organized blue-stained matrix deposition (Fig.  i); Immunohistochemistry demonstrated intensified staining for odontoblastic markers DSPP, OPN, and Collagen1 (Fig.  j), supported by quantitative analysis (Fig.  k).

 

## Knockdown of lumican inhibits odontoblastic differentiation of scaps
To further verify the functional contribution of Lumican to the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of Lumican in DESCs followed odontoblastic function characterization. Initial transfection optimization at 50 nM concentration achieved > 90% cellular uptake efficiency while maintaining cell viability (Fig.  a). Compared to the control group, the siLum3 group demonstrated a superior knockdown efficiency, effectively reducing both Lumican mRNA and protein expression levels (Fig.  b, c), and was therefore selected for all subsequent loss-of-function experiments. ELISA was used to measure the relative level of Lumican in the collected CM after knockdown of Lumican in SPH-DESCs, which further confirmed its significant downregulation (Fig.  d).
To examine the effects of Lumican knockdown in DESCs spheroids on odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs in vitro, compared with the control group (SPH-siNC-CM), ALP staining showed attenuated differentiation in the Lumican-knockdown group (SPH-siLum3-CM) after 7 days of odontoblastic induction. Extending the induction period to 14 days, ARS staining also revealed markedly decreased mineralization (Fig.  e). At the protein level, Western blot analysis and quantification (Fig.  f, g) showed significant downregulation of key odontoblastic differentiation markers, including DSPP, DMP1, and OCN in SPH-siLum3-CM-treated SCAPs. RT-qPCR results also confirmed the downregulation of these odontoblastic differentiation markers and odontoblastic marker Nestin.

 

## ITGβ1/ERK pathway involved in Lumican-mediated odontoblastic differentiation of scaps
The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, a key regulator of cell proliferation and differentiation, plays a critical role in odontoblastic differentiation [ – ]. To investigate whether MAPK signaling is involved in Lumican-induced odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs, we examined phosphorylation changes of ERK, JNK, and p38 after Lumican stimulation by Western blot. Results showed that Lumican activated both ERK and JNK pathways within 5 min, with sustained enhancement of phosphorylation over time, whereas p38 phosphorylation exhibited only a mild increase without persistent activation (Fig. a, b). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S9. Fluorescence microscopy revealed a significant increase in p-ERK fluorescence intensity in both the cytoplasm and nucleus following Lumican treatment compared to the control group, further confirming the activation of ERK signaling by Lumican (Fig. c). The ERK-specific inhibitor U0126, the JNK inhibitor SP600125, and the p38 inhibitor SB203580 were utilized in functional inhibition assays of Lumican. Both ALP and ARS staining demonstrated that among the three inhibitors, U0126 most significantly reduced Lumican-induced ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation (Fig. d). Consistent with these findings, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that the U0126 treatment group exhibited the most substantial downregulation in mRNA expression of odontoblastic markers (Fig. e). Moreover, western blot analysis and quantification also demonstrating that Lumican-induced upregulation of odontogenesis-related markers DSPP, DMP1 and OCN at protein levels was reversed by U0126 treatment (Fig. f, g). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S10. These findings demonstrate that Lumican promotes SCAPs odontoblastic differentiation primarily through ERK phosphorylation.
Previous studies reported that Lumican activates ERK signaling via ITGβ1 binding to facilitate tissue repair in corneal epithelial cells or osteogenic differentiation [ , ]. To verify whether this mechanism operates in SCAPs, we first identified Lumican’s binding sites. Western blot confirmed ITGβ1 expression in SCAPs, with upregulated levels at 24/48 hours post-Lumican treatment (Fig. h, i). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S11. Immunofluorescence revealed membrane colocalization of Lumican and ITGβ1 (Fig. j), while Co-IP validated their physical interaction (Fig. k). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S12. To assess ITGβ1’s necessity for ERK activation, we performed rescue experiments using the the specific β1-blocking antibody AIIB2 [ ] Western blot analysis and quantification showing AIIB2 treatment did not affect ITGβ1 expression in SCAPs, but attenuated Lumican-enhanced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. l, m). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S13. ALP and ARS staining showed that AIIB2 pretreatment counteracted the enhancing effect of Lumican on both early differentiation and late-stage mineralization. Moreover, RT-qPCR, Western blot analysis and quantification further demonstrated that AIIB2 pretreatment counteracted the Lumican-induced upregulation of odontogenesis-related markers. Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. S14. Additionally, the competitive ITGβ1 antagonist RGD peptide was employed to inhibit its function [ ]. RGD pretreatment suppressed ITGβ1 expression, whereas exogenous Lumican partially reversed this inhibition (Fig. S3a). At the same time, ERK phosphorylation decreased after RGD treatment, while Lumican supplementation partially restored these levels, and the changes were consistent with ITGβ1 expression (Fig. S3b).These results indicate that Lumican promotes SCAPs odontoblastic differentiation via the ITGβ1-ERK signaling axis.

 

## Discussion
Tooth development is a complex process that relies on reciprocal signaling interactions between epithelial and mesenchymal cells [ , ]. From the bud stage to the bell stage, each phase of tooth germ development demonstrates spatiotemporal regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal interactions [ ]. During the bud stage, the epithelium invaginates to form the dental bud, while neural crest-derived mesenchymal cells condense around this invagination. Upon progression to the cap stage, the epithelium enveloping the mesenchyme differentiates into a multilayered epithelial structure, whereas the surrounding mesenchyme differentiates into dental papilla and dental follicle tissues under the guidance of local signaling cues. By the bell stage, the inner enamel epithelium-derived DESCs polarize and differentiate into ameloblasts. These DESCs not only serve as the morphological foundation for enamel deposition but also regulate the differentiation of outer dental papilla cells into odontoblasts [ , ]. This hierarchical architecture requires 3D culture systems for its maintenance, as conventional monolayer cultures fail to recapitulate the spatial organization, thereby impairing the inherent functions of DESCs in epithelial-mesenchymal interactions [ ].
In low-adhesion environments, cells gradually enhance interactions through physical aggregation, leading to alterations in their biological properties. These changes ultimately result in the formation of mature spheroids with hierarchical organization, mediated by integrin-dependent ECM binding, upregulated cadherin expression, and actin stress fiber contraction [ ]. Compared to conventional monolayer adherent cultures, spheroid culture systems may modify the cellular secretome by reinforcing cell-cell interactions and polarity remodeling [ , ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that 3D cultured HERS spheroids and tooth germ cell-CM significantly enhance the proliferation, mineralization, and angiogenesis of dental pulp stem cells, forming physiologically relevant pulp-like structures [ , ]. In this study, we established a 3D DESCs spheroid culture model using agarose-coated low-adhesion plates. By partially restoring their in vivo morphology, we observed functional differences between monolayer- and spheroid-cultured DESCs in modulating biological behavior of SCAPs. Our findings reveal that CM from spheroid-cultured DESCs enhances SCAPs cell viability and promotes their odontoblastic differentiation potential.
To elucidate the mechanism by which SPH-DESCs-CM enhances the odontoblastic differentiation potential of SCAPs, we performed label-free LC-MS/MS analysis to compare the secretory proteome of DESCs under monolayer and spheroid culture conditions. GO enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed proteins in SPH-DESCs-CM were primarily enriched in the extracellular region and ECM components, suggesting that spheroid culture may drive alterations in the extracellular microenvironment through cell-cell contact-mediated mechanotransduction [ ]. KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant activation of proteoglycan-related signaling pathways in cancer following spheroid culture. These findings indicate that ECM remodeling induced by spheroid culture not only modifies physical properties such as matrix stiffness and fiber alignment but may also regulate cell fate through proteoglycan-mediated signaling networks.
Through differential secretory protein screening, we identified significantly elevated expression of Lumican, which drew our attention due to its potential involvement in collagen assembly and hard tissue differentiation [ ]. Lumican belongs to the SLRP family and functions as a secretory protein [ , ]. SLRPs are structurally classified into two categories: those bearing chondroitin/dermatan sulfate glycosaminoglycan chains (e.g., Decorin and Biglycan) and those containing keratan sulfate chains, with Lumican being a representative member [ , ]. Current evidence has established Lumican’s functions in both tumorigenesis and normal multicellular development, particularly through its participation in epithelial-mesenchymal transition signaling pathways, as demonstrated by its specific expression at epithelial-mesenchymal interfaces during lung development [ , , ]. In osseous tissues, Lumican promotes matrix mineralization and supports odontoblast differentiation/osteogenesis [ ]. Despite these multifunctional roles, Lumican’s involvement in tooth development remains underexplored. In vivo studies reveal its initial epithelial localization during early tooth development, followed by subsequent mesenchymal translocation [ , ]. Our immunohistochemical observations align with these findings, showing prominent Lumican expression in young incisor epithelium that decreases in mature molars. This pattern suggests Lumican may mediate epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk in root formation.
To further validate the effects of Lumican on odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs, we performed both gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments. In vitro experiments demonstrated that Lumican treatment significantly upregulated both mRNA and protein expression of key odontogenesis-related markers. In vivo assessments using a nude mouse subcutaneous transplantation model revealed that the Lumican-treated HA/TCP scaffolds developed a stratified structure featuring continuous mineralization layers and well-aligned collagen-mineralized matrix at the interface, suggesting its potential bidirectional regulation of differentiation through coordinated ECM remodeling and mineral deposition. These findings are consistent with Lumican’s known roles in binding collagen via its keratan sulfate chains and protecting collagen matrices in mineralized tissues by inhibiting MMP14, thereby facilitating oriented mineralization [ , ]. Conversely, knockdown of Lumican in SPH-DESCs markedly reduced the expression of odontoblastic markers and impaired mineralization in SCAPs, confirming its essential role in the odontoblastic differentiation potential of the secretome. Collectively, these findings establish that Lumican promotes the odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs. The demonstration of Lumican’s pro-odontoblastic effect in this murine model establishes a proof-of-concept, paving the way for subsequent studies about clinical applicability. It should be noted that the subcutaneous model does not fully replicate the native apical root niche [ ], and the single Lumican concentration used may not reflect physiological conditions. Additionally, while our proteomic analysis identified both cellular and serum-derived proteins due to minimal FBS carry-over, our functional validation strategically focused on cellularly-derived factors Lumican, to ensure biological conclusions reflect stem cell secretory activity. Future studies employing orthotopic models and dose-response experiments will be valuable to further validate these findings.
Odontoblastic differentiation is a biologically precise process regulated by sophisticated signaling networks. In addition to classical pathways such as BMP, Wnt, and Notch, the MAPK signaling pathway has attracted considerable attention due to its pivotal role in dental pulp development and dentin formation [ , ]. As core members of the MAPK family, ERK, JNK, and p38 participate in odontoblastic differentiation by responding to growth factors, stress signals, and mechanical stimuli [ ]. Lumican, as a member of the SLRP family, exerts its biological functions through specific binding to cell surface receptors, including Toll-like receptors, growth factor receptors, and integrins, to initiate intracellular signal transduction [ ]. ITGβ1, the most widely expressed integrin β subunit, can pair with various α subunits to form receptors specific for ECM components such as fibronectin and laminin [ ]. Seomun et al. demonstra›ted that in corneal epithelial cells, Lumican activates the ERK pathway via ITGβ1 binding, thereby promoting tissue repair [ ]; Lee et al. identified ITGβ1 as a critical regulator in osteogenic differentiation [ , ]; Hsp27 was shown to modulate the biological behavior of the mouse odontoblast cell line MDPC-23 through ITGβ1-mediated signaling [ ]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that Lumican might bind to ITGβ1 on cell membrane of SCAPs, activate the intracellular MAPK pathway, and ultimately regulate the transcriptional expression of odontoblastic differentiation markers.
In this study, we confirmed that Lumican activates the ERK pathway in SCAPs. Functional inhibition experiments using the ERK-specific inhibitor U0126 revealed that blocking ERK signaling counteracted Lumican’s pro-odontoblastic effects. Further protein-level validation demonstrated that Lumican physically interacts with ITGβ1, and this binding could be disrupted by the specific β1-blocking antibody AIIB2 and the competitive ITGβ1 inhibitor RGD peptide, which attenuated Lumican-induced ERK phosphorylation. These results collectively indicate that ERK is mainly involved in Lumican-mediated odontoblastic differentiation of SCAPs, and further support the involvement of ITGβ1 in this regulatory process. Notably, we observed that Lumican upregulates ITGβ1 expression, a phenomenon analogous to the mechanism reported by Lai et al., wherein BMP2 enhances osteoblast differentiation by upregulating αvβ integrins [ ]. This intriguing parallel, combined with the known partnership of ITGβ1 with several α-subunits, leads us to hypothesize that the functional heterodimer involved might be αvβ1 [ ]. However, future studies utilizing specific blocking antibodies or genetic approaches against individual α-subunits are essential to conclusively identify the specific αβ heterodimer that partners with Lumican. This proposed a potential bidirectional signaling crosstalk between ECM components and integrin receptors: ECM molecules not only transmit downstream signals upon receptor binding but may also feedback-regulate receptor expression levels, forming a self-reinforcing signaling loop that amplifies cellular responses. It should be noted that the sample size (n = 3) for these experiments, while consistent with initial investigations in the field, represents a limitation, and future studies with larger cohorts will be valuable to further solidify these findings.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study established a 3D DESCs spheroid culture model to investigate how secretory factors derived from spheroid-cultured DESCs regulate SCAPs proliferation and odontoblastic differentiation. Our findings demonstrate that CM from spheroid-cultured DESCs significantly enhanced SCAPs proliferation and promoted their differentiation into odontoblast-like cells. Through proteomic analysis, we identified Lumican as a key secretory protein that plays an essential role in stimulating SCAPs odontoblastic differentiation and dentin-like tissue regeneration. Mechanistically, we revealed that Lumican exerts its pro-odontoblastic effects by binding to ITGβ1 on SCAPs surface and subsequently activating the ERK signaling pathway. These discoveries not only highlight the crucial roles of Lumican and ITGβ1 in mediating DESCs-SCAPs crosstalk, but also provide a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing dentin regeneration. The elucidation of this Lumican-ITGβ1-ERK signaling axis offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying dentin regeneration.
