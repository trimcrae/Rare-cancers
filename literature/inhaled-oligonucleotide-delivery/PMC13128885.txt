Understanding the key pathways affecting cancer progression is crucial for the development of therapeutic strategies. Here, we reveal a pivotal role of Prolyl 3-hydroxylase 1 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma using transcriptome sequencing, proteomic analyses and engineered mouse model. Mechanistically, our findings indicate that this effect is, at least in part, through the regulation of Polo-like kinase 1 and Polo-like kinase 1-mediated β-catenin signaling. Restoration of either Prolyl 3-hydroxylase 1 or Polo-like kinase 1 expression in Prolyl 3-hydroxylase 1-deficient cells reverses the defects of β-catenin signaling, facilitates tumor cell proliferation and elicits macrophage infiltration. In addition, pharmacological inhibition of Polo-like kinase 1 strongly increases the therapeutic efficacy of chemotherapeutic response against pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, alleviating tumor burden in mice. Our findings suggest a promising therapeutic strategy for treating pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

This study identifies Prolyl 3- hydroxylase 1 (P3H1) as a critical regulator of PDAC progression through PLK1-β-catenin signaling, driving tumor-associated macrophage recruitment and tumor growth.

## Introduction
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the deadliest cancers worldwide with over 467,005 death cases in 2022 . More concerningly, it is estimated that PDAC will surpass colorectal cancer by 2040 as the second leading cause of cancer-related death , . The overall 5-year survival rate for pancreatic cancer is approximately 10%, primarily due to the absence of reliable biomarkers for early detection, the lack of effective late-stage treatments and widespread resistance to chemotherapy , . Despite revolutionized advances in immunotherapy and targeted treatments improving outcomes in various cancers, these approaches have achieved limited success in PDAC . Gemcitabine-based chemotherapy has been the primary chemotherapeutic treatment for pancreatic cancer patients since 1997, and current clinical guidelines still recommend either gemcitabine alone or in combination therapies , . Unfortunately, combination chemotherapy generally results in only short-term, partial remission in newly diagnosed PDAC patients . Given these limitations, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and to develop more effective and durable treatment strategies for PDAC.
PDAC has a unique tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) with numerous immunosuppressive factors . These factors include infiltrating immune cells and cytokines that create an immunologically “cold” environment, hindering the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions. Among the immune cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are closely related to worse prognosis, distant metastasis, and drug resistance in pancreatic cancer , . TAMs are recruited to the tumor site by tumor-secreted cytokines such as CXCL1, CXCL5 and CXCL12 . Therefore, targeting TAMs offers a promising strategy for enhancing immune-based therapies and potentially impeding tumor progression , .
Post-translational modifications are fundamental drivers of cancer pathogenesis by influencing protein functionality . The prolyl-hydroxylase family are involved in the post-translational modification of target proteins, particularly in the hydroxylation of proline residues . Prolyl 3-hydroxylase 1 (P3H1), belonging to the P3Hs family, was identified as a basement membrane-associated proteoglycan with a core protein of 804 residues (90 kDa) . It contains an RGD attachment site for cell-matrix interactions and a KDEL sequence for endoplasmic reticulum retention, indicating its roles in both extracellular matrix interactions and intracellular secretory pathways . Previous studies indicate that P3H1 is crucial for bone development and skin integrity through regulating intracellular modification of type I collagen – . Recently, limited evidence suggests that P3H1 is upregulated in various cancers and may play roles in cancer progression according to a large of bioinformatics analyses – . However, to date, the role of P3H1 on PDAC progression and TIME regulation and the related mechanisms have rarely been investigated.
Serine/threonine kinases are key players in the development of pancreatic cancer, impacting cell proliferation and metastasis by phosphorylating their substrate proteins – . Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), a highly conserved serine/threonine protein kinase, has been reported to be overexpressed in various cancers – and contribute to tumor initiation, migration and resistance to therapy , . PLK1 promotes tumorigenesis and drug resistance through phosphorylating various substrates which are involved in numerous cellular events, including DNA replication, transcription, translation, chromosome dynamics, microtubule dynamics, apoptosis and organogenesis . Therefore, characterization of the potential PLK1 substrates will provide valuable insights into understanding the PLK1-mediated cellular events and help to develop anti-cancer agents.
Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has been associated with tumor progression and metastasis of pancreatic cancer – . As a core component of Wnt signaling, β-catenin is tightly regulated by various post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation , . Previous studies reported that the C-terminal phosphorylation of β-catenin at Ser191 and Ser605 controls its nuclear translocation . After entering into the nucleus, β-catenin binds to transcriptional factors of the T-cell factor (TCF)/lymphoid enhancer factor (LEF) family, thereby inducing the transcription of oncogenic genes, such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1, for tumor initiation and progression – . In addition, β-catenin is a critical regulator of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by influencing immune cell infiltration and modulating cytokine secretion, including CXCL1 – . More importantly, growing evidences show Wnt/β-catenin signaling contributes to gemcitabine resistance in PDAC . Therefore, the role of β-catenin played in tumor progression, TIME remodeling and drug resistance highlights its therapeutic value in PDAC.
In this study, we demonstrate that P3H1 is overexpressed in PDAC and is associated with poor patient prognosis. Genetic ablation of P3H1 delays PDAC progression and reduces tumor-associated macrophage infiltration by inhibiting the PLK1-β-catenin pathway. Targeting P3H1 or pharmacologically inhibiting PLK1 enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy in PDAC. These results highlight the role of the P3H1-PLK1-β-catenin axis in PDAC progression and suggest a promising therapeutic strategy for PDAC treatment.

## Results

## P3H1 is upregulated and associated with poor prognosis of PDAC
Due to the critical role of tumor-secreted proteins in influencing tumor growth and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), our study aimed to identify secreted proteins that drive the pathogenesis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). On the basis of our recent published the glycosylated secreted and membrane protein (S-PM) database resource , we conducted a re-analysis from a perspective and applied a more stringent filtering criterion (log2FC > 0, FDR < 0.05) to identify high-confidence subsets of 226 upregulated secreted proteins in the human S-PM protein PDAC dataset and 233 upregulated secreted proteins in the LSL-KrasG12D/+; p53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mouse S-PM protein dataset (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, we curated a focused screening set of 227 ECM-annotated proteins from the human S-PM protein PDAC dataset (Fig.  ). The intersection of these three independent subsets yielded a consensus set of 34 candidate proteins whose expressions were significantly and consistently elevated in both human and mouse samples (Fig.  ). One of the identified candidates, Prolyl 3-hydroxylase 1 (P3H1), an essential enzyme implied in collagen metabolism and bone homeostasis , , was chosen for further investigation due to its consistent inducible expression patterns observed throughout the development of human and mouse pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) (Fig.  ). In addition, P3H1 displayed higher differential expression in human PDAC versus normal tissues, as evidenced by the human S-PM protein dataset (Fig.  , P = 3.96e-13) and the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) cohort RNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Figs.  and , P = 9.0e-17). It also showed a stronger positive correlation with stromal abundance (Supplementary Figs.  and , ρ = 0.42, P = 1.96e-8) and a higher hazard ratio for disease-free survival (DFS) (Supplementary Figs.  and , 2.07; 95% CI 1.39 ~ 3.09) compared to other candidates (Fig.  ). To further validate the elevated expression of P3H1 in human PDAC, we conducted P3H1 immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis on an independent cohort of human tissue microarray (TMA) containing 72 PDAC and 39 non-tumor tissues. Consistent with the S-PM proteome analysis, P3H1 protein expression was found to be minimal in non-tumor human pancreatic tissues, while significantly upregulated in human PDAC samples (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). These observations were further confirmed by staining mouse pancreatic tumor samples from KPC mice (Fig.  ). Next, we assessed the clinical relevance of P3H1 expression in the development of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) by analyzing the association between P3H1 protein levels and clinicopathologic parameters of pancreatic cancer patients, such as gender, age, tumor node and metastasis (TNM) stage, and lymph node involvement. Our findings revealed a significant correlation between P3H1 expression and TNM stage (P = 0.0216), but no significant associations observed with other clinical parameters (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we investigated the impact of P3H1 mRNA expression on the clinical outcomes of human patients with PDAC using Kaplan-Meier plots from the dataset in KM plotter web server . The analysis revealed that higher level of P3H1 was associated with a shorter disease-free survival (Fig.  ). Concurrently, Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients exhibiting higher P3H1 protein expression experienced poorer prognoses compared to those with lower P3H1 levels (Fig.  ). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest a crucial role of P3H1 in the pathogenesis of pancreatic cancer. 

## P3H1 deletion hinders PDAC progression
To experimentally examine whether P3H1 contributes to PDAC malignancy, we generated pancreatic cell-specific P3H1 knockout mice by crossing P3H1fl/+ mice with Pdx1-Cre mice. First, we investigated the impacts of P3H1 deletion on normal pancreas development. Histologic analysis revealed that the morphology of endocrine and exocrine glands from 1-year-old P3H1 cKO mice (P3H1fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre) did not exhibit any abnormalities as compared with WT control mice (P3H1+/+; Pdx1-Cre), suggesting depletion of P3H1 did not impair normal pancreatic tissue development (Supplementary Fig.  ). Next, we intercrossed P3H1fl/+; Pdx1-Cre mice with LSL-KrasG12D/+;p53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mice to generate two experimental groups of mice: LSL-KrasG12D/+;p53fl/fl;Pdx1-Cre;P3H1+/+ (KPC;WT) and LSL-KrasG12D/+;p53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre;P3H1fl/fl (KPC; P3H1 cKO) littermate mice (Fig.  ). Both tail genome PCR analysis and immunohistochemistry staining of mouse pancreatic tumor sections indicated that KPC;P3H1 cKO mice were successfully established (Fig.  ). Immunoblotting analysis of isolated mouse primary pancreatic cancer cells (PCCs) confirmed that P3H1 was specifically diminished in tumor cells from KPC;P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). Then, we examined the gross phenotypes of KPC;WT and KPC;P3H1 cKO mice. Consistent with the previous reports , , the median survival of KPC;WT mice was around 64 days. Whereas KPC;P3H1 cKO mice had a remarkably extended median survival of 76 days. Exceeding 15% survival extension are considered significant and clinically relevant in aggressive PDAC mouse models – . The observed 12-day median survival increase (from 64 days to 76 days) in KPC P3H1 cKO mice represented approximately a 20% extension in lifespan and should be considered a marked survival benefit, suggesting P3H1 deletion in KPC mice greatly prolonged survival (Fig.  ). 
To determine the cause of prolonged survival in KPC; P3H1 cKO mice, we isolated the pancreas from 7-week-old KPC mice, a time point when the pancreatic tissues are occupied by both low- and high-grade pancreatic tumors . The results showed that the weight of pancreatic tumors isolated from KPC; P3H1 cKO mice was significantly reduced compared with that from KPC; WT mice (Fig.  ). Moreover, histological analysis revealed that a predominant increase of normal pancreatic histology in KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). Approximately 92% pancreas were replaced by pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (PanINs) or PDAC in 7-week-old KPC; WT mice, whereas only 66% pancreas with altered acinar architecture in KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). Accordingly, the immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of Cytokeratin 19 (CK19) and Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM), two well-known markers for epithelial tumors, showed that a dramatic reduction of tumor areas in KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). The fibrotic areas, measured by α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and type I collagen, were also decreased in tumor sections from KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these results strongly suggested P3H1 deletion significantly alleviates PDAC progression in KPC mice.

## P3H1 deletion inhibits pancreatic cancer cell proliferation
Pancreatic cancer cells exhibit excessive proliferation abilities . To dissect whether P3H1 depletion delayed PDAC progression through influencing pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, we performed Ki-67 staining. The results showed that a significant decrease in the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells in KPC;P3H1 cKO tumor lesions compared to KPC;WT tumors (Fig.  ). Consistently, staining of another proliferative marker protein, proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) also showed a reduction of the percentage of proliferative cells in KPC;P3H1 cKO tumor lesions (Fig.  ). To confirm these findings in human PDAC, we knocked down P3H1 in MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells, two widely used human-derived PDAC cells (Fig.  ), and performed in vitro cell proliferation assays. Results indicated that pancreatic cancer cells lacking P3H1 displayed lower proliferation rate and lower frequencies of colony formation, indicating that loss of P3H1 has a strong inhibitory effect on pancreatic cancer cell proliferation (Fig.  ). Collectively, these results indicated that loss of P3H1 significantly impedes pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and delays PDAC progression.

## P3H1 deletion decreases CXCL1/CXCL5/CXCL8-mediated M2-like TAMs infiltration in PDAC
Growing evidences indicate that cancer cells could reshape the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), especially the tumor-infiltrating immunosuppressive cells, to facilitate their growth , . To clarify whether the reduced PDAC progression, upon P3H1 deficiency, were associated with changes in TIME, we analyzed the relationship between P3H1 expression and tumor-infiltrating immunosuppressive cells in pancreatic cancers through the TIMER2.0 database . The results showed that P3H1 expression exhibited positively correlated with the infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), but was not correlated with other immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells in PDAC (Supplementary Fig.  ). We then validated the correlation between P3H1 and the abundance of macrophages and neutrophils by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and found a strong reduction of F4/80+ macrophages, but not Ly6G+ neutrophils infiltration in tumor sections in KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). Previous studies reported that macrophages in PDAC are primarily composed of immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages , . Indeed, IHC analysis revealed that KPC;P3H1 cKO tumors exhibited much fewer M2-like macrophages (represented by CD68 and Arginase I) as compared to KPC;WT tumors (Fig.  ), suggesting that loss of P3H1 remodels the TIME, especially by inhibiting the number of M2-like TAMs. Given that M2-like TAMs facilitate cancer cell proliferation by secreting tumor-promoting factors such as PDGF and FGF2 , , we investigated whether P3H1 deletion could modulate the functional state of M2-like TAMs and thereby impact tumor growth. Notably, tumor sections from KPC; P3H1 cKO mice demonstrated a significant reduction in PDGF-B and FGF2 expression compared to KPC; WT controls (Fig.  ). To validate this, macrophages treated in vitro with conditioned media from P3H1-deficient MIA PaCa-2 cells showed decreased transcriptional levels of PDGF-B and FGF2 (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings suggested that P3H1 depletion not only decreased the abundance of M2-like TAMs but also diminished their pro-tumorigenic activity. 
To comprehensively uncover how depletion of P3H1 in tumor cells inhibits TAMs abundance and pro-tumorigenic activity, we conducted RNA sequencing analysis with P3H1 knockdown or control pancreatic cancer cells. The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis conducted on down-regulated genes in P3H1-depleted cells revealed significant enrichment for gene sets related to cell cycle, regulation of protein phosphorylation, and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that PDAC with a low level of P3H1 exhibited downregulation of cytokine production (Fig.  ). Considering the important role of secreted cytokines from tumor cells in the regulation of immune cell infiltration and activities, we performed the secretome proteomics approaches to unveil the difference of secreted cytokine levels between P3H1-deficient pancreatic cancer cells and wild-type cells (Fig.  ). Very interestingly, the results showed that depletion of P3H1 in MIA PaCA-2 cells significantly reduced the secretion of a series of cytokines, including CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL8 and TGF-β (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ), which are well-known for their capability to induce TAMs infiltration and polarization – . These findings were confirmed by analyzing the total and secreted CXCL1/CXCL5/CXCL8 proteins from P3H1-depleted pancreatic cancer cells using immunoblotting analysis and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig.  ). However, P3H1 knockdown did not significantly alter total TGF-β protein level and only modestly decreased secreted TGF-β (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that TGF-β may not be the primary cytokine involved in P3H1-mediated regulation of TAMs abundance. Concordantly, P3H1 ablation led to a pronounced decrease of CXCL1 and CXCL5 in mouse tumor tissues isolated from KPC; P3H1 cKO mice than that from KPC; WT mice (Fig.  ). The mRNA levels of CXCL1, CXCL5 and CXCL8 in tumor cells were also diminished by P3H1 ablation (Supplementary Fig.  ). The next question to investigate is the role of CXCL1, CXCL5 and CXCL8 in P3H1-mediated regulation of TAMs abundance. Given CXCL1, CXCL5 and CXCL8 are key cytokines responsible for TAMs recruitment, we first explored whether P3H1-deficiency-induced inhibition of TAMs recruitment was dependent on these cytokines. To do this, we conducted chemotaxis assays and found that the culture media from P3H1-deficient cells exhibited reduced ability to recruit macrophages compared with that from wild-type cells (Fig. 3S). Importantly, forced increase of CXCL1, CXCL5 or CXCL8 levels by adding the recombinant cytokines in the culture media from P3H1-deficient cells effectively reversed the P3H1-deficiency-induced inhibition of macrophage migration (Fig. 3S). In addition to recruiting TAMs to the tumor site, CXCL1, CXCL5, and CXCL8 are known to play critical roles in inducing TAMs polarization – . Therefore, we next assessed whether P3H1 depletion influenced TAMs polarization, and whether these effects were mediated by CXCL1, CXCL5 or CXCL8. The results demonstrated that P3H1 knockdown in tumor cells decreased the proportion of M2-like TAMs (CD14+CD163+ TAMs), while M1-like TAMs (CD14+CD86+) remained unaffected, consistent with our previous in vivo observations (Fig.  , and Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, reintroducing recombinant CXCL1, CXCL5 or CXCL8 into the culture media from P3H1-depleted cancer cells restored M2-like TAMs polarization, and modestly enhanced tumor cell proliferation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these findings strongly indicate that P3H1 loss reshapes the TIME by inhibiting CXCL1, CXCL5/CXCL8-dependent M2-like TAMs infiltration in PDAC.

## P3H1 deletion reduces PLK1 protein expression
To elucidate the mechanisms by which P3H1 regulates pancreatic cancer progression and resets TIME, we performed proteomic analysis to identify potential downstream molecules regulated by P3H1. The results showed that 172 proteins were up-regulated and 110 proteins were down-regulated in wild-type MIA PaCa-2 cells compared to P3H1 siRNA#1 cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). We also confirmed these observations in P3H1 siRNA#2 MIA PaCa-2 and in another human pancreatic cancer cells PANC-1 (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). Of note, analyses of both cell lines revealed a significant down-regulation of five proteins, including P3H1, in P3H1 knockdown pancreatic cancer cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). Among these candidates, Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) was selected for further study because it was one of the most significantly down-regulated proteins in P3H1 knockdown cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, PLK1 is a serine/threonine kinase that phosphorylates a number of substrates to facilitate cell proliferation . Our above-mentioned RNA-seq data indicated that the differentially expressed genes in P3H1 knockdown cells were highly related to those involved in the regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity (Fig.  ). Moreover, as previously acknowledged , , higher level of PLK1 expression was associated with poorer clinical outcomes and shorter survival in PDAC patients. Consistently, PLK1 knockdown exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect on pancreatic cancer cell growth than other candidates (Supplementary Fig.  ), implying that PLK1 plays a critical role in P3H1-mediated PDAC progression. To test this hypothesis, we explored the effect of P3H1 on PLK1 expression level. Consistent with the knockdown results (Fig.  ), overexpression of P3H1 increased PLK1 protein expression in both MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells (Fig.  ). We validated these findings in mouse pancreatic tumor tissues and obtained similar results. Depletion of P3H1 resulted in a pronounced decrease of PLK1 expression in tumor sections isolated from KPC; P3H1 cKO mice (Fig.  ). Furthermore, human PDAC samples evidenced significantly higher expression levels of both P3H1 and PLK1 compared to normal pancreatic tissues (Fig.  ). Quantitative analysis indicated a strong positive correlation between P3H1 and PLK1 expression levels in human PDAC (R = 0.53, P = 1.4e-5) (Fig.  ). Although our findings confirmed that P3H1 regulates PLK1 expression, the mechanism by which P3H1 affects PLK1 protein levels remains unclear. To explore this, we tested whether P3H1 stabilized PLK1 through direct hydroxylation, considering P3H1’s prolyl hydroxylase activity . However, the results demonstrated that P3H1 did not physically interact with PLK1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, PLK1 lacked the specific proline residues within the Gly-Pro-Hyp repeating motif, which is known to be targeted by P3H1 , , indicating that P3H1 is unlikely to hydroxylate PLK1 directly (Supplementary Fig.  ). To further assess whether P3H1 indirectly affected PLK1 protein stability, a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was conducted. The results indicated no significant difference in the degradation rate of PLK1 between P3H1 knockdown cells and wild-type cells after CHX treatment (Supplementary Fig.  , D), suggesting that P3H1 did not influence PLK1 protein stability. We then hypothesized that P3H1 regulated PLK1 expression at the transcriptional level rather than by affecting protein stability. Interestingly, P3H1 depletion resulted in a significant decrease in PLK1 mRNA levels, supporting a potential role for P3H1 in modulating PLK1 transcription (Supplementary Fig.  ). 

## P3H1 deletion suppresses PLK1-mediated downstream events
Since depletion of P3H1 reduced PLK1 protein expression, we speculated that P3H1 influenced the phosphorylation of PLK1 substrates. To identify the substrates of PLK1 involved in PDAC progression, we applied the phosphoproteomics analysis. A total of 111 phosphorylated proteins were detected in wild-type MIA PaCa-2 cells compared to that in P3H1 depleted cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). To better delineate PLK1 downstream substrates, we complied these 111 up-regulated phosphorylated proteins in MIA PaCa-2 with 490 PLK1 interactors analyzed by the BioGRID database , and obtained 7 overlapped proteins that might be PLK1 direct substrates (Fig.  and Supplementary Dataset  ). The heatmap confirmed the increased phosphorylation levels of seven selected proteins in control MIA PaCa-2 cells (Fig.  ). These phosphorylated proteins are known to be involved in a variety of biological processes including cell proliferation (AKAP12 and CTNNB1) – , cell cycle (MCM2) , , angiogenesis (AKAP12) , and regulation of TME (CTNNB1) , (Fig.  ). Among these candidates, we prioritized CTNNB1 (β-catenin) for further study because it is the only protein known to be involved in both tumor cell proliferation and cytokine secretion modulation, thereby contributing to TME reshaping , , – . Phosphoproteomic analysis validated that depletion of P3H1 reduced the phosphorylation level of β-catenin at S191 (Fig.  ). Because phosphorylation of β-catenin at S191 has been reported to be essential for its nuclear localization and target gene regulation , we then investigated the impact of P3H1 on β-catenin nuclear expression. The results showed that loss of P3H1 decreased β-catenin nuclear expression analyzed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig.  ). We further discovered that P3H1 deletion inhibited the transcription of β-catenin target genes involved in cell proliferation and cytokine production, such as C-MYC, CCND1, CXCL1, CXCL5 and CXCL8 , , (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, an obvious reduction of c-Myc, Cyclin D1, CXCL1, CXCL5 or CXCL8 protein expression was observed in P3H1 knockdown MIA PaCa-2 cells (Figs.  I, J, ). These findings were further verified in PDAC tumor sections from P3H1 cKO mice (Figs.  K, L, ). In summary, these above data imply a potential signaling axis of P3H1-PLK1-β-catenin in PDAC. 

## PLK1 expression level is crucial for P3H1-regulated PDAC progression and TIME remodeling
To further investigate whether P3H1-regulated PDAC progression is dependent on PLK1 expression level, we restored P3H1 or PLK1 expression in P3H1 knockdown MIA PaCa-2 cells. As expected, re-expression of P3H1 rescued the down-regulation of PLK1 expression in P3H1-deficient cells, confirming the aforementioned results that P3H1 controlled PLK1 protein expression (Fig.  ). Intriguingly, re-introduction of PLK1, like that of P3H1, into P3H1-deficienct cells, promoted β-catenin nuclear location and its target gene transcription, including C-MYC, CCND1, and CXCL1 (Fig.  ). In line with this, P3H1-deficiency-induced inhibition of c-Myc and Cyclin D1 expression was reversed by the overexpression of P3H1 or PLK1 (Fig.  ). In addition, total expression and secreted CXCL1 and CXCL5 were restored by re-expression of P3H1 or PLK1 (Fig.  ). Consistent with these findings, re-expression of P3H1 or PLK1 in P3H1 knockdown cells increased pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and macrophage recruitment (Fig.  ). We further validated these findings in vivo. NSG mice were orthotopically injected with an equal number of control or P3H1 knockdown MIA PaCa-2 cells expressing full-length P3H1 or PLK1 (Fig.  ). Similar to what we observed in vitro, nude mice injected with P3H1 knockdown cells showed less tumor burden compared to mice injected with control MIA PaCa-2 cells, while re-expression of P3H1 or PLK1 led to a significant increase of the weight and volumes of tumor (Fig.  ). Moreover, overexpression of PLK1, like that of P3H1, increased tumor cell proliferation (Fig.  ) and induced β-catenin-mediated target gene expression, including c-Myc, Cyclin D1, CXCL1 and CXCL5 (Fig.  ). Taken together, these in vitro and in vivo data demonstrate that the effects of P3H1 on PDAC progression are mediated, at least in part, through control of PLK1 protein expression. 

## P3H1-PLK1 axis inhibition enhances the responsiveness of PDAC to chemotherapy
Gemcitabine (Gem) is a clinical used drug for the treatment of PDAC patients, either alone or in combination with other drugs . However, the treatment efficacy is quite low because of Gem resistance . As previously acknowledged, activation of β-catenin signaling and increased TAMs infiltration heavily contribute to the development of Gem resistance , , . Because our results suggested that the P3H1-PLK1 axis is crucial for β-catenin activation and TAMs infiltration in PDAC, we postulated that combining P3H1 or PLK1 inhibitor with Gem may improve the efficacy of PDAC therapy. Since no P3H1 inhibitor is available, we first tested the efficacy of combining PLK1 inhibitor BI2536 with Gem in the orthotopic xenograft pancreatic tumor mouse model used in Fig.  (Fig.  ). Considering that BI2536 has been reported to induce dose-dependent toxicity and efficacy when combined with other drugs , , we evaluated the toxicity of various doses of BI2536 in combined with Gem in this study. Orthotopic pancreatic tumor-bearing mice were treated with Gem combined with varying doses of BI2536 at 5, 15, and 25 mg/kg. All mice treated with Gem combined with low-dose (5 mg/kg) or middle-dose (15 mg/kg) BI2536 remained viable (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, mice treated with high-dose BI2536 (25 mg/kg) plus Gem (10 mg/kg) died starting from day 5 onward (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, mice treated with low-dose and middle-dose BI2536 (5 and 15 mg/kg) combined with Gem showed no adverse effects on body weight or main organ structure (Supplementary Fig.  , C). In contrast, high-dose BI2536 (25 mg/kg) combined with Gem caused a significant reduction in mouse body weight and severe histopathological damage to major organs such as the liver and lungs, indicating that high-dose of BI2536 exhibits notable organ toxicity in mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Next, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of low-dose and middle-dose BI2536 combined with Gem in inhibiting PDAC progression. The results showed that combining Gem with middle-dose (15 mg/kg) of PLK1 inhibitor significantly increased the therapeutic efficacy compared to that of the single Gem treatment or Gem with low-dose (5 mg/kg) of BI2536 (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , E). Considering both drug toxicity and efficacy, we selected the middle dose (15 mg/kg) of BI2536 for subsequent studies and will refer to it as BI2536 hereafter (Fig.  ). Staining of mouse tumor tissues with Ki67 indicated largely reduced cancer cell proliferation in the Gem/BI2536-treated mice (Fig.  ). In addition, the combination treatment of Gem and BI2536 remarkably inhibited TAMs infiltration in mouse tumor sections compared to that of the single drug treatment (Fig.  ). To enhance the translational relevance of our findings, we evaluated whether PLK1 inhibition could improve the efficacy of other standard PDAC chemotherapies, such as FOLFIRINOX or Gem/nab-paclitaxel. Similar to Gem therapy, combined treatment of BI2536 with FOLFIRINOX or Gem/nab-paclitaxel resulted in significantly greater reductions in tumor volume and weight compared to monotherapies (Supplementary Fig.  ). To further validate the potential synergistic effect of this combination therapy in human patients, we assessed the response of three distinct patient-derived PDAC organoids (PDOs) to the combination of BI2536 with Gem/nab-paclitaxel. PDOs displayed histological features consistent with the original patient tumor samples (Fig.  ). Similar to the observations in the orthotopic xenograft mouse model, the drug combination demonstrated superior efficacy in eliminating all three PDAC organoids compared to either agent alone (Fig.  ).
More importantly, to determine whether the additive effect of Gem/nab-paclitaxel and BI2536 depended on P3H1, we assessed the therapeutic response of Gem/nab-paclitaxel in P3H1-depleted MIA PaCa-2 cell lines. The treatment led to a greater reduction in tumor burden in mice xenografted with P3H1-knockdown MIA PaCa-2 cells compared to control cells. (Fig.  ). Pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and TAMs infiltration were significantly reduced in mice engrafted with P3H1-knockdown cells following Gem/nab-paclitaxel treatment (Fig.  ). To validate the findings from PDAC cell lines and to recapitulate PDAC heterogeneity, we then evaluated the impact of P3H1 on cancer cell proliferation in three PDOs. Consistent with the results in MIA PaCa-2 cells, P3H1 depletion in PDOs markedly suppressed organoid growth (Fig.  ). Furthermore, P3H1 knockdown caused a marked decrease in the expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 and the downstream effector PLK1 (Fig.  ). Importantly, Gem/nab-paclitaxel therapy exhibited enhanced effectiveness in eliminating PDOs with P3H1 deficiency (Fig.  ). Collectively, these results support the notion that targeting the P3H1-PLK1 axis might serve as an effective therapeutic strategy for treatment of PDAC and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy. 

## Discussion
P3H1 is a member of the prolyl 3-hydroxylases (P3H) family that catalyzes the 3-hydroxylation of proline residues in collagen and stabilizes the collagen structure , , . In vivo, P3H1 knockout mice displayed defects in collagen-enriched tissues, including bone, tendon and skin , . Very interestingly, we revealed that in addition to collagen-enriched tissues, P3H1 was overexpressed in human and mouse pancreatic tumor tissues and was associated with poor clinical outcomes in PDAC patients, strongly implying a crucial role of P3H1 in PDAC progression. As described in the current paper, we have performed a series of experimental studies that provide strong evidence for a pivotal role of P3H1 in promoting pancreatic cancer proliferation and shed light on the signaling pathway by which P3H1 regulates this process. Besides on tumor cells, our results also indicated a crucial role of P3H1 on modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Depletion of P3H1 in tumor cells led to a significant reduction of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) infiltration.
How does P3H1 promote PDAC progression and modulate TIME? In this study, we have attempted to identify downstream effectors regulated by P3H1 through a proteomics approach. The results indicated PLK1 expression was positively associated with P3H1 expression. PLK1 is a serine/threonine kinase to catalyze the phosphorylation of its substrates. Herein, our studies suggest that PLK1 modulates the phosphorylation of β-catenin at serine residues S191 to facilitate β-catenin transcriptional activities and induce its target gene expression, which helped cell growth and reset the tumor microenvironment. It is worth noting that previous studies have indicated that phosphorylation of β-catenin at S191 and S605 regulates its nuclear translocation . However, in our phosphoproteomic dataset, the β-catenin phosphorylation site S605 was not identified in either P3H1 knockdown or wild-type MIA PaCa-2 cells (Supplementary Dataset  ). In addition, we were unable to validate the S605 phosphorylation level due to the unavailability of a specific commercial antibody targeting this site. Therefore, our data strongly suggest that the P3H1-PLK1 axis regulates β-catenin nuclear localization primarily through phosphorylation at S191. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that this axis may also influence β-catenin nuclear expression by modulating phosphorylation at other sites, such as S605 . Although our studies have confirmed the function of P3H1 in regulating PLK1 expression at the transcriptional level (Supplementary Fig.  ), how P3H1 affects PLK1 transcription remains elusive. Potentially, P3H1 facilitates collagen maturation and remodels ECM assembly, activating integrin-FAK/Src-Akt/ERK signaling and thereby enhancing FOXM1/E2F-driven PLK1 transcription. Alternatively, P3H1 may perturb cellular oxygen/ROS homeostasis or collagen-HIF crosstalk, indirectly modulating HIF-Myc programs that regulate PLK1 transcription – . Further studies are required to clarify the precise molecular mechanisms by which P3H1 regulates PLK1 transcription.
Our current studies strongly suggest that P3H1 promotes PDAC progression through regulation of the PLK1-β-catenin axis, albeit we do not exclude the possibility that other P3H1-mediated processes may also contribute to PDAC progression. For example, P3H1 is well-known to modulate collagen stability. Here, we indeed observed P3H1 deletion reduced collagen expression and deposition in the tumor microenvironment (Supplementary Fig.  ), which likely also contributed to the PDAC progression. However, re-expression of PLK1 in P3H1-deficient cells significantly reversed the inhibition of tumor proliferation and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling, strongly indicating that PLK1 expression level and its-mediated downstream events is critically involved in P3H1-regualted PDAC progression.
Gemcitabine (Gem) is used as a standard first-line drug for treating pancreatic cancer patients, but its benefits are limited due to its chemoresistance . β-catenin activation and TAMs infiltration are known as the major causes for driving PDAC chemoresistance to Gem , . As described here, our findings support a crucial role of P3H1-PLK1-β-catenin signaling in prompting PDAC progression and inducing TAMs infiltration. Therefore, it is attractive to test whether combining Gem with P3H1/PLK1 inhibition can overcome the chemoresistance problem. Due to lack of P3H1 specific inhibitor, we combining Gem with BI 2536, a PLK1 inhibitor. The results showed that combining Gem with PLK1 inhibition indeed more effectively alleviated PDAC progression compared to Gem monotherapy.
In summary, our findings identify a role of P3H1 in the regulation of PDAC progression and provide a P3H1-PLK1-β-catenin signaling axis (Fig.  ). Moreover, our study reveals a promising therapeutic strategy for combination chemotherapy with Gem for alleviating PDAC progression.

## Methods

## Animal studies
The P3H1fl/fl mice (C57BL/6 J background) were from Cyagen Biosciences. LSL-KrasG12D/+; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mice were obtained from Jackson laboratory and crossed with P3H1fl/fl mice to generate P3H1fl/+; LSL-KrasG12D/+; Trp53fl/fl and P3H1fl/+; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre mice. Offspring were then interbred to produce P3H1fl/fl; LSL-KrasG12D/+; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPC; P3H1 cKO) and P3H1+/+; LSL-KrasG12D/+; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPC; P3H1 WT) littermates. Both female and male mice in 7-week-old were utilized in the described experiments. All experimental mice were fed in the standard animal cages and provided with regular water and food, without any special treatment except for the indicated experimental procedures. CO2 inhalation was used to euthanize all experimental mice.
Tail genotyping was performed by standard PCR protocol. PCR primers were used as follows: (1) P3H1-Forward: 5’-CTCATACACAGTACACACGCTAAG-3’; P3H1-Reverse: 5’-ATCTCCCTTGAAGC AATGTCTCAC-3’. (2) Kras-Forward: 5’-CGTAGGGTCATACTCATCCACA-3’; Kras-Reverse: 5’-CCATGGCTTGAGTAAGTCTGC-3’. (3) p53-Forward: 5’-GCTAACACTCTTGGGATGCTG-3’; p53-Reverse: 5’-TTGTCCCAACTCTAAGAAGCC-3’. (4) Cre-Forward: 5’-CCTGGACTACATCTTGAGTTGC-3’; Cre -Reverse: 5’-AGGCAAATTTTGGTGTACGG-3’.

## Orthotopic transplantation and drug treatment
The NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice (Cat. NO. NM-NSG-001), aged at 4 ~ 5 weeks, were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. 2 × 106 MIA PaCa-2 cells were orthotopically injected into the pancreas of anesthetized NSG mice. Mice were monitored for one week post-surgery for signs of abnormal behaviors. Then, NSG mice were randomized to the various treatment groups. Gemcitabine (Tocris Bioscience, 122111-03-9) was dissolved in ddH2O at 2 mg/mL, BI2536 (Selleck, S1109) in corn oil (5% DMSO) at 1 mg/mL, 3 mg/mL or 5 mg/mL, nab-Paclitaxel (Selleck, E1068) in ddH2O at 1 mg/mL, irinotecan (Selleck, S1198) in corn oil (7% DMSO) at 4 mg/mL, oxaliplatin (Selleck, S1224) in 5% glucose solution at 0.4 mg/mL, 5-fluorouracil (Selleck, S1209) in saline (10% DMSO) at 3 mg/mL, leucovorin (Selleck, S5136) in ddH2O at 4.8 mg/mL for injection. Tumor-bearing mice were injected by intraperitoneal injection with Gemcitabine at 10 mg/kg, BI2536 at 5 mg/kg, 15 mg/kg or 25 mg/kg, irinotecan at 20 mg/kg, oxaliplatin at 2 mg/kg, 5-fluorouracil at 15 mg/kg and leucovorin at 24 mg/kg. Nab-Paclitaxel was administered with intravenous injection at 5 mg/kg. At the end of the treatment period, NSG mice were sacrificed, and pancreatic tumor weight and volume were measured. Tumor volumes were calculated using the following formula: volume = (length × width2)/2.

## Cell culture
The HEK 293 T (CRL-3216), THP-1 (TIB-202), MIA PaCa-2 (CRL-1420) and PANC-1 (CRL-1469) cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were authenticated and tested for mycoplasma contamination. HEK 293 T and PANC-1 cells were routinely maintained in DMEM (Invitrogen, 11995-500) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen, 10099-141) and 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin (Invitrogen, 15140-122). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, C11875500BT), supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin. MIA PaCa-2 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 2.5% horse serum (Gibco, 26050088), as well as 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin. All cells were incubated under 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C.

## Tumor microarrays of pancreatic cancer patients
Human pancreatic cancer tissue microarrays (TMA) were purchased from the Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company. The correlation between P3H1 expression and clinical variables, including gender, age, TNM stage, and lymph node count, was assessed using TMA (OD-CT-DgPan01-006) with corresponding demographic and clinical data. The samples were selected based on the availability of clinical and pathological data (e.g., gender: 72 cases, age: 71 cases, TNM stage: 68 cases, lymph node count: 65 cases). Nine PDAC Samples were excluded due to insufficient tissue available. The correlation between P3H1 expression and PDAC patients’ survival rates was assessed by TMA (HPan-Ade180Sur-01, OD-CT-DgPan01-006, 119 cases in total) and determined by Kaplan-Meier analysis. The expression of P3H1 and PLK1 was detected in TMA (HPanA060CS04, 59 cases) and measured by the IHC toolbox of ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA).

## Organoid culture
Tumor samples were collected from pancreatic cancer patients who received surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All anatomy donors gave their informed and written consent to use their clinical data and surgical specimens for research. Sex was not considered in this study because no reports indicated that P3H1 function is sex-biased, and the study was not designed to investigate sex-based research. This study was conducted in accordance with the national guidelines and complied with all relevant ethical regulations. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval number: IIT20230001C-R1). The organoid culture protocol was described previously . In brief, tissues from patients were washed three times with PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Then, pancreatic cancer tissues were minced into 1 mm3-fragments using surgical scissors and digested with Collagenase Type 1 (1.5 mg/ml, Worthington, WBC-LS004197) at 37 °C for a maximum of 2 h. The supernatant cells were collected and digested with TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 10 min. For inactivating digestive enzymes, cells were washed with 1640 basic medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Isolated cells were resuspended with Matrigel (Corning, 356231) and human PDAC medium for PDAC organoids. Organoid culture medium: basic media (advanced DMEM/F12, 10 mM HEPES, 1 × GlutaMAX-I, 100 µg/mL Primocin, 1 × penicillin/streptomycin solution) and complete medium (advanced DMEM/F12, 10 mM HEPES, 1 × GlutaMAX-I, 100 µg/mL Primocin, 1 × penicillin/streptomycin solution, 30% Wnt3A conditioned medium, 2% R-spondin conditioned medium, 4% Noggin conditioned medium, 500 nM A83-01, 10 µM Y-27632, 1.56 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, 10 ng/mL FGF10, 1 × B27 supplement, 10 µM forskolin). The established organoids were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

## Drug treatment assay on organoids
For the drug treatment assay, the organoids were digested with Tryp-LE (GIBCO), which was subsequently inactivated with 1640 basic medium containing 10% (v/v) FBS. The cells were washed with 1 × PBS and then resuspended in 50% Matrigel medium and seeded into 96-well plates (10,000 cells per well). When organoids reached approximately 100 ~ 150 µm, Then, Gemcitabine, nab-paclitaxel and BI2536 with different concentration gradients and 0.1% DMSO as the vehicle control were added using the Mosquito workstation. After 3 days of drug treatment, cell viability was detected CellTiter-Glo 3D (CTG) assay. Calculate IC50 curves with GraphPad Prism (100 % and 0 % viability constraints) by adding 25 μL of Cell Counting-Lite 2.0 (Vazyme), gently shaking for 10 min at room temperature before luminescence signal detection using the Envision plate reader. The average inhibition rates were calculated from three independent experiments.

## Histology and immunohistochemistry staining
Mouse pancreatic tumor tissues were fixed in 10% formalin at 4 °C overnight and then embedded in paraffin. Pancreatic formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) blocks were sectioned at 4.5 μm intervals and stained with pancreatic Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining according to the standard procedures. The analyses of histological and histopathological were categorized as 4 grades as the standard described previously, including normal pancreas, early PanIN (ADM and PanIN-1), late PanIN (PanIN-2 and PanIN-3) and PDAC . Five independent tumor samples in each group were analyzed.
Immunohistochemistry staining was perform by the MaxVisionTM HRP-Polymer anti-Mouse/Rabbit IHC kit (Maxim, Fuzhou, China) as described previously . Primary antibodies were listed as follows (Detailed in Supplementary Dataset  ): rabbit anti-P3H1 (Abcam, ab244326, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-P3H1 (Novus, NBP2-30424, 1:200), rabbit anti-PLK1 (HUABIO, HA722118, 1:100), rabbit anti-Cytokeratin19 (CK19, Abcam, ab52625, 1:10,000), rabbit anti-PCNA (Abclonal, A12427, 1:500), mouse anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, sigma, A2547, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Collagen I (Abcam, ab260043, 1:1000), rabbit anti-F4/80 (CST, 70076S, 1:500), rabbit anti-Ly6G (CST, 87048 s, 1:200), rabbit anti-CD68 (Proteintech, 28058-1-AP, 1:1000), rabbit anti-ARG1 (CST, 93668 T, 1:200), rabbit anti-CXCL1 (Abcam, ab86436, 1:500), rabbit anti-CXCL5 (HUABIO, HA722561, 1:500), rabbit anti-c-Myc (Abcam, ab32072,1:100), rabbit anti-CyclinD1 (HUABIO, ET1601-31, 1:1000), mouse anti-Ki-67 (Maxim, MAB-0672), rabbit anti-EpCAM (Cell signaling, 93790S, 1:500), rabbit anti-FGF2 (HUABIO, ET1703-18, 1:200), rabbit anti-PDGF-B (HUABIO, HA722003, 1:100). Targeting proteins were detected with peroxidase staining (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin. For IHC analysis, whole-tissue slides were scanned by NanoZoomer S60 (Hamamatsu) at 40 × magnification and viewed by NDP.Viewer software (version 2.9.25). At least ten section fields for each slide were randomly selected for further analysis. Image analysis was performed by thresholding brown pixels for positive staining and normalizing to intra-field tissue area using the IHC tool box of ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA) .

## Lentiviral infection
Plasmids psPAX2 (#12260) and pMD2.G (#12259) were acquired from Addgene. Plasmid pLVX-Flag-Hyg was modified from pLVX-IRES-Hyg (Clontech, 632185). HEK 293 T cells were transfected with pLVX-P3H1-Hyg or pLVX-Flag-PLK1-Hyg together with psPAX2 packing plasmid and pMD2.G envelope plasmid by Lipofectamine 3000 kit (Invitrogen, L3000015) to generate P3H1 or PLK1 overexpression cell lines. Lentivirus-containing medium was collected from the HEK 293 T after 48 h transfection. Lentivirus was precipitated by filtration (pore size 0.45 μm) and ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 2 h). MIA PaCa-2 cells were cultured in a 6-well plate to around 50% confluency and then transduced with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 mixed in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene. Transduction of the MIA PaCa-2 cell line was confirmed by immunoblotting. For organoid lentiviral infection, organoids were dissociated with TrypLE (2 min, 37 °C), resuspended in lentivirus (MOI 50 ~ 100) in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene, incubated overnight and then re-embedded in Matrigel. The infection efficiency was confirmed by qPCR.

## Cell viability and colony formation assay
Cell viability was measured with 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. MIA PaCa-2 cells were seeded as 2000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. At the indicated incubation time points, the culture medium was removed, and 200 μL media containing 10 μL MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. After an additional 3 h incubation, the culture media was aspirated, and 200 μL dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured with microplate reader (BioTek, Epoch2).
For the colony formation assay, 500 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. After continued incubation for 7 days, the cell colonies were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 minutes at room temperature, stained with Modified Giemsa Staining Solution (Beyotime, C0131) for 45 min. Digital images of the cell colonies in each well were obtained and counted.

## Immunofluorescence staining
MIA PaCa-2 cells were seeded onto fibronectin-coated cover slides placed in 6-well plates and cultured overnight. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min, followed by permeabilizing with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Then cells were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and incubated with a primary antibody against rabbit anti-β-catenin (Abcam, ab6302,1:2,000) 4 °C overnight. ## Cycloheximide chase assay
A cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was conducted to assess protein stability. Briefly, cells were plated in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was then removed, and the cells were treated with 2 mL of DMEM containing 10 μM CHX (dissolved in DMSO). At indicated time points (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 hours) after CHX treatment, cells were harvested for protein extraction and subsequent immunoblotting analysis.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis was performed as the following procedure. Briefly, MIA PaCa-2 cells were harvested and lysed in cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) containing 1% proteinase inhibitor cocktail and 1 mM PMSF for 30 minutes at 4 °C. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 minutes, and the supernatant was collected. After centrifugation, the supernatant was pre-cleared with Protein A/G Plus-Agarose (Santa Cruz, sc-2003) for 1 h at 4 °C. Then, equal amounts of cell lysates (2 mg) were incubated with 2 μg rabbit anti-P3H1 antibody (Abcam, ab244326), rabbit anti-PLK1 antibody (CST, 4513S), or control rabbit IgG antibody (Invitrogen, MA5-56524) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubating with Protein A/G Plus-Agarose for another 2 h. Beads were rinsed three times in pre-cold 1 × PBS buffer with 1 mM PMSF. Proteins were then eluted from beads by 1 × SDS PAGE loading buffer containing 10% β-mercaptoethanol and were subsequently subjected to immunoblotting.

## ELISA
ELISA assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The concentration of released CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL8 or TGF-β1 in culture medium of MIA PaCa-2 cells was determined using the human CXCL1 ELISA kit (HUABIO, EH0054), human CXCL5 ELISA kit (UpingBio, SYP-H4127), CXCL8 ELISA kit (HUABIO, EH0014) or TGF-β1 ELISA kit (Bioswamp, HM10060). The absolute concentration of CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL8 or TGF-β1 in the MIA PaCa-2 culture medium was calculated from the standard curve.

## Chemotaxis assays
A chemotaxis assay was performed to detect the macrophage recruitment capacity. An 8-μm pore diameter chamber (Corning, 3422) was used in this transwell assay. THP-1 cells were pretreated with 100 ng/mL Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, MCE, HY-18739) for 48 h. Then THP-1 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and the lower chamber contained medium supplemented with the indicated treatment. After 2 days, cells were fixed using 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature and subsequently stained with crystal violet solution. ImageJ software was used to quantify the number of migrated cells.

## Flow cytometry
Analysis of macrophage surface markers on THP-1 cells was performed by flow cytometry. THP-1 cells were stimulated by CM from MIA PaCa-2 cells, together with or without recombinant chemokines, for 72 h. After that, the cells were collected and washed with PBS for two times at 4 °C (300 × g, 5 min). Then the cells were stained with PE anti-human CD14, Pacific Blue™ anti-human CD86, and APC anti-human CD163 antibodies (301850, 305417, and 333609 respectively, BioLegend, 1:100) for 30 min in the dark, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on Cytoflex Analyzer (Beckman) and analyzed using FlowJo (V.10.9.0).

## RNA sequencing
MIA PaCa-2 cells were extracted by TriPure™ Isolation Reagent (Roche, 11667157001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and purity of RNA were examined by a NanoDrop™ One/OneC spectrophotometer and Life Invitrogen Qubit RNA BR (Broad-Range) Assay Kit. RNA integrity was analyzed using Agilent 4200 TapeStation system. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed to create a mRNA-Sequencing library using the ABclonal Poly(A)mRNA Capture Module and ABclonal Fast RNA-seq Lib Prep Kit V2. After verification of the library quality by Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit, high-throughput transcriptome sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform (Illumina, Inc, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’ s instructions. In brief, for the NovaSeq X, 56 µL of the pooled libraries with a concentration of 1.05 nM were loaded into a lane of a NovaSeq X Series 25B Reagent Kit (300 Cycle) flow cell for a final loading concentration of 125 pM. The pools were sequenced paired-end 150 on the NovaSeq X. Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format was processed using Fastp (version 0.23.0), an ultra-fast FASTQ preprocessor with efficient quality control and data-filtering features . The clean reads were mapped to hg38 human reference genome by HISAT2 software (version 2.1.0) . A sequencing depth of approximately 40 million mapped reads was sufficient to achieve 90% transcriptome coverage. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (version 1.18.1), which performs internal normalization of raw counts with filtering criteria of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 .
Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed via the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.8.2) in R version 4.2.2. Genes used for the analysis were filtered by the criteria of log2(fold change) | > 1 and FDR < 0.05 and analyzed by the “enricher” function with the following size filters: minGSSize = 10 and maxGSSize = 500. The other parameters were set at their default settings. Enrichment results were visualized via the “ggplot2” R package. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) in R version 4.2.3. Genes were ranked according to their log2(fold change) values and analyzed using the “gseGO” function with the following size filters: minGSSize = 3 and maxGSSize = 2000. The other parameters were maintained at their default settings. Enrichment results were visualized using the “gseaplot2” function.

## Proteomic analysis
Proteomics samples were prepared with the SISPROT kit (BayOmics, cat. no. BO-006) . The kit consists of solid-phase extraction pipette tips packed in tandem with C18 membrane layers and a mixed-bed of strong-cation/strong-anion exchange (SCX  +  SAX) beads, enabling a fully integrated workflow for proteomic sample processing from protein preconcentration, reduction, and alkylation to digestion, reversed-phase (RP)-based desalting and high-pH RP-based peptide fractionation. Firstly, MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells were lysed in the Extract buffer with sonication (5 cycles at 4 °C with 30 s on and 30 s off) and collected by centrifuging at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, 23227). 5 μg cell lysates were collected and acidified by adding Acidize buffer. Before sample loading, the SISPROT tip was assembled and equilibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The acidified sample was loaded onto the SISPROT tip by centrifugation, reduced by adding Reduce buffer and digested with Enzymatic Digest Solution. The obtained peptides were transferred to a C18 disk and desalted by adding the Desalt buffer before elution. Finally, the peptides were eluted by adding Elute buffer, lyophilized using a SpeedVac machine (Thermo Fisher) and resuspended by adding Dissolve B buffer for the following LC-MS/MS analysis.

## Secretome profiling
Secretome analysis was performed as described previously . MIA PaCa-2 cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes at approximately 80% confluence. Cells were washed three times with PBS and then starved in culture medium without fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 24 h. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min to remove the cell debris and then concentrated with a 3 kDa cut-off filter (Millipore). The concentrated medium was replaced with PBS using the same filter and subsequently dried using a Speed-Vac. Proteins were reconstituted in 8 M urea lysis buffer containing 50 mM ABC, reduced, and alkylated. Subsequently, the urea concentration was diluted to 1 M with 50 mM ABC, and the proteins were subjected to overnight digestion with trypsin. Peptides were desalted on a home-made C18 StageTip for the following LC-MS/MS analysis.

## Phosphoproteomics analysis
Phosphoproteomics analysis was performed as previously described . MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells underwent lysis in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 7 M Urea, 1% Triton X-100, 10 nM NaF, 1 Mm Na3VO4, protease inhibitor mixtures (Roche, 4693132001) and phosphatase mixtures (Roche, 4906837001). Protein concentration was determined by the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo fisher, 23227). 1 mg cell lysates were used to perform chloroform-methanol precipitation. Collected protein precipitates were resuspended in urea buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) with mild sonication. The soluble proteins were reduced using dithiothreitol (DTT), alkylated using iodoacetamide (IAA) and digested with trypsin. After SPE column desalting, the collected peptides were enriched by the Ti4+-immobilized-metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) tip , . Then the obtained peptides were further desalted on a home-made C18 StageTip for the following LC-MS/MS analysis.

## LC–MS/MS analysis
Global proteomics analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer coupled with an UltiMate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Secretome and phosphoproteomics analyses were conducted on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For all analyses, peptides were dissolved in solvent A (0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water) and separated on a 100 μm i.d. × 20 cm capillary column in-house packed with C18 beads (1.9 μm, 120 Å, Dr. Maisch GmbH) at a constant flow rate of 250 nL/minute. The effective gradient increased linearly from 8% to 25% mobile phase B (0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 90% (v/v) ACN) over 50 minutes (for phosphopeptides) or over 100 min (for secretome samples), and then increased to 40% over 10 min (for phosphopeptides) or 20 min (for secretome samples), respectively. MS/MS scans were performed in a data-dependent mode, parameters were set as previously described . Full MS scans covering m/z of 350 ~ 1550 were acquired using an Orbitrap resolution of 120,000, an AGC target of 3 × 106, and a maximum injection time (MIT) of 60 milliseconds. The top 50 (15 for phosphopeptides) most abundant precursors from each full MS1 scan were selected and isolated through a 1.2 Da window (0.7 Da for phosphopeptides), and fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) at normalized collision energy (NCE) of 27% (32% for phosphopeptides). MS/MS spectra were scanned with a resolution of 7500 (45,000 for phosphopeptides), an AGC target of 1 × 105 and an MIT of 25 milliseconds (86 milliseconds for phosphopeptides).
The raw files were searched by the MaxQuant software (Version 1.6.17) and searched against the Uniprot human fasta database . The carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as the fixed modification. Oxidation (M), deamidation (N/Q) and phospho (STY) were set as the dynamic modifications for the phosphopeptide identification; A maximum of two missed cleavages was permitted for trypsin and Lys-C digestions. The results were filtered to a 1% FDR. The “match between runs (MBR)” function was enabled to reduce missing values. Then label-free quantification (LFQ) data was used to evaluate protein abundances and analyzed by the Perseus software (Version 1.5.5.3) as s previously described .

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis and related visualization were performed using Perseus software (version 1.5.5.3), GraphPad Prism 10 and R Studio (version 4.1.0). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. 2-tailed Student’s t test was performed for comparison between the two groups. One-way ANOVA was performed for a multiple comparison test. The log-rank test was performed for survival analysis. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For proteomics data, significance was calculated by a two-tailed Student’s t test with a P value < 0.05 and a fold change > 2.
