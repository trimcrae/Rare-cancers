## Abstract
Malignant mesothelioma (MM) is a rare but aggressive cancer originating from mesothelial cells, which presents significant challenges to patients’ physical and psychological well-being. The gut–lung axis underscores the connection between gut microbiota and respiratory diseases, with emerging evidence suggesting a strong association between gut microbiota and the development of MM. In this study, we conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the potential causal relationship between gut microbiota and MM, while also exploring the underlying mechanisms through bioinformatics approaches. Gut microbiota summary data were obtained from the MiBioGen consortium, while MM data were sourced from the FinnGen R11 dataset. Causality was examined using the inverse variance weighted method as the primary analysis. Additional methods, including the weighted median, simple mode, MR-Egger, and weighted mode, were also employed. The robustness of the findings was validated through sensitivity analyses, and reverse causality was considered to further strengthen the MR results. Moreover, bioinformatics analyses were conducted on genetic loci associated with both gut microbiota and MM to explore potential underlying mechanisms. Our study suggests that genetically predicted increases in class.Bacilli, family.Rikenellaceae, genus.Clostridium innocuum group, and order.Lactobacillales were suggestively associated with a higher risk of MM, whereas increases in genus.Ruminococcaceae UCG004, genus.Flavonifractor, phylum.Firmicutes, genus.Anaerofilum, genus.Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and genus.Lactobacillus appeared to confer protective effects. Bioinformatics analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes near loci associated with gut microbiota might affect MM by modulating pathways and the tumor microenvironment. The results of this study point to a potential genetic predisposition linking gut microbiota to MM. Further experimental validation is crucial to confirm these candidate microbes, establish causality, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
Keywords: bioinformatics analysis, gut microbiota, malignant mesothelioma, Mendelian randomization, tumor microenvironment

## 1. Introduction
Malignant mesothelioma (MM) is an aggressive tumor that originates from the mesothelial cells lining the pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal cavities.[ ] Among its various forms, pleural mesothelioma is the most prevalent, accounting for 80% to 85% of cases, while peritoneal mesothelioma constitutes 10% to 15%, with other sites representing <5%.[ ] Asbestos inhalation is the predominant cause of MM, with around 70% of cases linked to documented asbestos exposure, and the disease is characterized by a long latency period of up to 50 years, suggesting an anticipated increase in incidence over the coming decades.[ , ] Annually, MM is responsible for up to 40 deaths per million people worldwide and can present significant clinical features in advanced stages, including dyspnea, often resulting from malignant pleural effusion, systemic symptoms, and chest pain.[ , ] Surgical treatment options primarily include pleural decortication and extrapleural pneumonectomy; however, there is no consensus on the role of surgery in managing MM, largely due to a lack of prospective studies.[ ] The standard first-line regimen comprises cisplatin and pemetrexed, which offers a median overall survival (OS) of only 12 months and a median progression-free survival of <6 months.[ ] In 2020, the United States Food and Drug Administration approved nivolumab in combination with ipilimumab for the first-line treatment of advanced mesothelioma, yet the median OS for patients with unresectable disease remains just 18 months.[ ] The limited efficacy of current treatment strategies underscores the urgent need to identify novel mechanisms in MM pathogenesis and progression to develop more effective therapies for this aggressive malignancy.
The human gastrointestinal tract is home to trillions of symbiotic bacteria that maintain a reciprocal relationship with the body, playing critical roles in sustaining health.[ ] These gut microbiota are involved in numerous physiological processes, contributing to the regulation of metabolic balance, immune system development, infection defense, and the production of essential neurotransmitters.[ ] Microbial dysbiosis and impaired epithelial barrier function, leading to bacterial translocation, are considered key drivers of neoplastic transformation.[ ] Both preclinical and clinical studies have identified specific gut microbiota as pivotal regulators of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in various cancers.[ ] Thus, the gut microbiota composition may indirectly affect MM progression by altering immune cell activity and inflammatory factors in the TME, potentially impacting patient responses to immunotherapy. Modulating the gut microbiota has become a new strategy for preventing and treating specific diseases.[ ] Recent research has highlighted that gut microbiota may not only affect MM progression but also modulate patients’ responsiveness to therapy by shaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). For instance, Pentimalli et al[ ] analyzing data from 86 mesothelioma patients via the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, identified 107 microbial genera linked to survival, suggesting that gut microbiota could serve as a prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target to enhance immunotherapy outcomes. Additionally, Gattlen et al[ ] demonstrated that a novel prebiotic microbiome modulator significantly altered the TIME and improved tumor control in a malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) model, potentially by increasing CD3⁺CD8⁺GRZB⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration in tumors. Moreover, the phase II MIST4 clinical trial investigating atezolizumab and bevacizumab in relapsed mesothelioma patients demonstrated that gut microbiota may influence treatment responses.[ ] Despite the growing recognition of the role of gut microbiota in the development, progression, and treatment of MM, their full potential remains underexplored. Thus, further investigation is essential to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the causal relationship between gut microbiota and MM, as well as its effects on the TIME and treatment.
Although evidence increasingly indicates a link between gut microbiota and MM, determining a clear cause-and-effect relationship is still difficult. Additional research is required to confirm this causal relationship and clarify the mechanisms through which gut microbiota affect disease progression, potentially paving the way for novel microbiota-targeted treatments. Mendelian randomization (MR) utilizes genetic variants from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables (IVs) to robustly evaluate the causal effects of environmental exposures on health outcomes. Since an individual’s genotype is established at conception and remains constant throughout life, MR reduces the risks of reverse causation and confounding bias in genotype-disease associations.[ ] MR simulates a randomized controlled trial environment, mitigating the limitations of observational studies and addressing randomized controlled trial challenges.[ ] This study employed two-sample MR analysis to explore the causal link between gut microbiota and MM incidence, complemented by bioinformatics methods to examine potential underlying mechanisms.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Study design
This research sought to identify a causal relationship between gut microbiota and MM and investigate the genes potentially involved in this process. Figure A presents a flowchart illustrating the MR analysis and Figure B depicts the directed acyclic graph that guided the design of the current MR and bioanalytical study. The validity of causal estimates from MR studies hinges on 3 key assumptions[ ]: (1) the IVs must be strongly associated with the exposure phenotypes and are mutually independent; (2) the IVs should not be linked to any confounders that might affect both the exposure and the outcome; and (3) the IVs must influence the outcome only through their effect on the exposure, without any direct association with the outcome itself.
 

## 2.2. Data sources
The aggregated statistical data for MM were sourced from the Finnish database R11 (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results), which encompasses 390 MM cases and 345,118 controls (excluding all cancers). This database, containing 453,733 participants, has been used to study rare medical mutations via gene sequencing. The human gut microbiota composition was examined using summary-level data from a GWAS meta-analysis,[ ] encompassing 24 population-based cohorts with 18,340 participants from Europe, North America, and East Asia (https://mibiogen.gcc.rug.nl/). The 211 taxa were systematically categorized into 5 biological groups: 9 phyla, 16 classes, 20 orders, 35 families, and 131 genera. We excluded 15 ambiguous bacterial traits lacking potential for meaningful biological insights into disease causality, and incorporated well-defined taxonomic units: 9 phyla, 16 classes, 20 orders, 32 families, and 119 genera, as classification units for the bidirectional MR study. Table provides an overview of the data sources utilized in the current MR study.
 

## 2.3. Selection of IVs
Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with significant associations to gut microbiota, identified by a threshold of P < 1 × 10-5, were chosen as potential IVs to adequately represent microbiota traits. To ensure SNP independence and minimize linkage disequilibrium effects, an LD threshold of R² < 0.001 and a clumping distance of 10,000 kb were used. To maintain consistent orientation between exposure and outcome data, any ambiguous, duplicated, or palindromic SNPs were excluded. The strength of the IVs was evaluated using the F-statistic, calculated as F = beta2/se2.[ ] An F-statistic >10 suggests a reduced risk of weak instrument bias. Finally, potential confounding IVs were managed using the LDtrait tool from the LDlink database (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=ldtrait).[ ] This tool assesses SNP associations with different phenotypes, enabling the exclusion of IVs significantly associated with MM-related phenotypes, such as asbestos.

## 2.4. MR and sensitivity analysis
This study utilized high-efficiency methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger,[ ] weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode, to investigate the potential causal relationship between human gut microbiota composition and MM risk. IVW, which performs a weighted regression of the outcome effects of IVs on the exposure effects, provided a precise overall estimate of the impact of the gut microbiota on MM risk, while enhancing statistical power by constraining the intercept of the IVs to zero. Distinct from IVW, the MR-Egger method, relying on the InSIDE assumption, primarily captured the dose–response relationship between IVs and outcomes, while accounting for potential pleiotropy. The weighted median method complemented MR-Egger and offered valid estimates when over 50% of the information came from SNPs that were valid IVs. Similarly, the weighted mode method identified SNPs without pleiotropic effects as those with estimates near the mode.
The sensitivity analysis investigated the impact of horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity on the IVs.[ ] We employed Cochran Q test[ ] to evaluate heterogeneity, utilizing both the IVW and MR-Egger approaches. A P-value below 0.05 from the Q test indicated significant heterogeneity among the IVs. To address potential horizontal pleiotropy, we applied MR-PRESSO,[ ] which identifies and removes outliers that could distort results. Additionally, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed, systematically excluding individual SNPs to test the stability of our findings. We also visualized the results of the sensitivity analysis through scatter and funnel plots, providing a clear depiction of data distribution and potential biases, thereby enhancing the reliability of our conclusions. All analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.6) and the MR-PRESSO package (version 1.0) in R (version 4.4.1). Circos heatmaps were created with the ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.20.0) and circlize (version 0.4.16).

## 2.5. Reverse MR
This study conducted a reverse MR analysis using gut microbiota identified as causally linked to MM. By addressing the risk of reverse causality, we aimed to improve the robustness and credibility of our research results.

## 2.6. Bioinformatics analysis
We conducted a bioinformatics analysis using RStudio and online databases to identify genes enriched with genetic loci highly correlated between gut microbiota and MM, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms influencing MM development. We accessed the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database to identify candidate genes associated with SNPs common to both gut microbiota traits and MM. Microarray profiles of surgically resected MPM tumors and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (dataset: GSE51024), which included 96 samples: 55 MPM samples and 41 normal lung parenchyma tissues. Using the GEO2R tool, we analyzed and compared expression profiles to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and generated a volcano plot for visualization. Subsequently, we matched SNPs associated with gut microbiota linked to a high risk of MM with the DEGs, displaying the overlapping genes using a Venn diagram. We employed STRING database to predict gene functions, visualizing the functions and regulatory networks of high-risk MM-related genes and generating predicted protein–protein interactions (PPIs). To explore the biological functions and mechanisms of pathogenic genes associated with MM, we conducted gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the Sangerbox database (https://sangerbox.com/Tool), with enrichment significance evaluated at a P-value threshold of < .05, and results depicted using a bubble plot. We then assessed the potential relationship between overlapping genes and clinical prognosis using the “Survival Map” and “Survival Analysis” modules of GEPIA2, constructing Kaplan–Meier curves for OS and disease-free survival (DFS) by dividing patients into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression levels of the overlapping genes.
The relative abundance of 22 immune cell types was estimated using CIBERSORT through the SangerBox platform. Subsequently, the correlation between these immune cell levels and the expression of DEGs in MM was analyzed. Additionally, StromalScore in the ESTIMATE module was calculated via the SangerBox platform to evaluate immune reactivity. The relationship between DEGs expression and immune-related biomarkers levels in MM was further investigated using the TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/). Additionally, the GSCA platform, which integrates genomic, pharmacogenomic, and immunogenomic analyses of cancer gene sets, was utilized for a thorough evaluation. The drug module of the GSCA online tool (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) was employed to assess the relationship between DEGs and drug sensitivity.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Selection of IVs for MR analysis
The directed acyclic graph representing the MR framework for assessing the causal association between gut microbiota and MM is presented in Figure . Using a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 1 × 10-5), LD testing (with an LD threshold of R² < 0.001 and a clumping distance of 10,000 kb), and validating F-statistics, we identified 2603 SNPs as IVs for 196 distinct gut microbiota taxa. All selected SNPs had F-statistics >10, indicating no significant evidence of weak instrument bias. Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O731 provides a detailed and meticulous presentation of the IVs categorized across different taxonomic groups of the gut microbiota.

## 3.2. Causal influence of gut microbiota on MM
A systematic two-sample MR analysis was carried out to explore the potential causal link between distinct categories of gut microbiota and the occurrence of MM. The preliminary associations between 196 gut microbiota of various levels of classification derived from 5 MR methods and MM was presented in Figure . By applying the IVW method, we observed suggestive evidence of causal associations between genetically predicted increases in class.Bacilli (OR = 2.496; 95% CI: 1.309–4.761; P = .005), family.Rikenellaceae (OR = 2.175; 95% CI: 1.104–4.285; P = .025), genus.Clostridium innocuum group (OR = 1.670; 95% CI: 1.046–2.664; P = .032), and order.Lactobacillales (OR = 2.907; 95% CI: 1.348–6.268; P = .006) with increased risk of MM. In contrast, genetically predicted increases in genus.Ruminococcaceae UCG004 (OR = 0.510; 95% CI: 0.26–0.998; P = .049), genus.Flavonifractor (OR = 0.367; 95% CI: 0.14–0.966; P = .042), phylum.Firmicutes (OR = 0.466; 95% CI: 0.237–0.92; P = .028), genus.Anaerofilum (OR = 0.584; 95% CI: 0.362–0.943; P = .028), genus.Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (OR = 0.343; 95% CI: 0.152–0.772; P = .010), and genus.Lactobacillus (OR = 0.554; 95% CI: 0.321–0.956; P = .034) were linked to protective effects against MM (Fig. ). Additionally, the MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode analyses yielded causal effect estimates consistent in both magnitude and direction with those from the IVW method (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O731 and Fig. ).
 

## 3.3. Sensitivity analysis
Cochrane Q test, MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO were employed for sensitivity analyses to evaluate heterogeneity and pleiotropy (Table ). The findings revealed no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy, apart from class.Bacilli (P = .016). The leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that no individual IVs significantly impacted the results (Fig. ). Forest and funnel plots were used to demonstrate a symmetrical pattern, enhancing the reliability of the findings (Figure S1, S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O733).
 

## 3.4. Outcome of the reverse MR analysis
In consideration of cross-validation, we performed reverse MR analyses to examine the relationship between gut microbiota and MM. Eleven IVs were identified using a locus-wide significance threshold (P < 1 × 10-5), an LD threshold (R² < 0.001, clumping distance of 10,000 kb), and an F-statistic >10 (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O731). However, the IVW method revealed no statistically significant associations between MM and these bacterial traits (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O731).

## 3.5. Analysis of differential gene expression in proximity to genetic loci
We conducted a comprehensive analysis of genetic loci associated with both gut microbiota and MM to enhance understanding of their relationship (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/O731). Through the analysis of RNA-seq data from 55 MPM samples and 41 normal paired lung parenchyma tissues, sourced from the GEO databases (GSE51024), we identified substantial differences in the expression of 1651 genes (Fig. A). Then, 305 genes from SNP-adjacent loci of MM-associated gut microbiota were compared, and a total of 30 overlapping genes were found. The genes include SOX7, WWC2, PAPOLA, AK7, HIGD1B, ANLN, FZD2, SMAD9, ALDH1A2, KIF14, SORBS2, FAM149A, TPR1, TNFRSF19, RAPGEF4, FBXO15, SFXN1, TNPO1, CCNB2, FGF9, TENM3, JAM2, SERTM1, RNASE1, MSLN, SPATA13, CLEC11A, ERRFI1, CCDC78, and RNASE4 (Fig. B).
 

## 3.6. PPI network, GO, and KEGG pathway analysis
To examine functional and pathway distinctions, we conducted a comparative analysis of overlapping gene sets through PPI network construction, along with GO and KEGG pathway analyses. The PPI analysis revealed limited interactions among the identified potential causal proteins. Specifically, interactions were found between RAPGEF4 and ITPR1, among TNPO1, ANLN, CCNB2, and KIF14, and among WWC2, FAM149A, and SORBS2 (Fig. A), which were involved in the apoptosis, cell cycle, and cell adhesion pathways, respectively. We conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the 30 genes to identify shared regulatory pathways. The GO analysis indicated their involvement in small GTPase binding, ribonuclease A activity, endoribonuclease activity, and cytidylate kinase activity (Fig. B). KEGG analysis revealed their participation in pathways including cellular senescence, apoptosis, proteoglycans in cancer, Rap1 signaling, and p53 signaling (Fig. C).
 

## 3.7. Overlapping genes and prognosis
The prognostic value of overlapping genes was explored in the GEPIA2 database. As shown in Figure , higher expression of 5 genes was associated with poor prognosis for both OS and DFS in mesothelioma. For DFS, TENM3 (P = .046, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.8), CCNB2 (P = .0017, HR = 2.6), SFXN1 (P = .043, HR = 1.8), KIF14 (P = .029, HR = 1.9), and ANLN (P = .0098, HR = 2.2) were significant. For OS, TENM3 (P = .036, HR = 1.7), CCNB2 (P = 1.6e-07, HR = 4.1), SFXN1 (P = .0053, HR = 2), KIF14 (P = 4.7e-07, HR = 3.9), and ANLN (P = 2.4e-06, HR = 3.4) showed significant associations.
 

## 3.8. Analyses of cancer immune reactivity and drug sensitivity
To assess the impact of DEGs on the tumor immune microenvironment during MM progression, we performed an analysis of immune cell infiltration. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we evaluated the correlation between DEGs and 22 immune cell types. Our analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between all DEGs and both plasma cells and activated natural killer (NK) cells. Conversely, KIF14 and SFXN1 were positively correlated with M2 macrophages and M0 macrophages, respectively (Fig. A). The relationship between DEGs and the StromalScore was assessed using SangerBox in MM data from The Cancer Genome Atlas database. A higher StromalScore indicates a greater proportion of stromal cells in the tumor core and invasive margins,[ ] which may promote tumor migration and invasion. We found that TENM3 expression was significantly and positively correlated with the StromalScore in MM (R > 0.3, P < .05), as illustrated in the scatter plots (Fig. B). To further understand how DEGs influence immune responses, we analyzed their association with immune-related biomarkers molecules. Notably, the immunoactivating molecules TGFBR1 and TGFB1 were positively correlated with the expression of all DEGs. Additional immunoactivating molecules showing positive correlations included TIGIT, PDCD1LG2, IL10RB, HAVCR2, CSF1R, CD274, and ADORA2A. In contrast, LAG3, KDR, and CD160 were negatively correlated with the expression of ODZ3 (TENM3), CCNB2, and SFXN1, respectively (Fig. A). We also performed a drug sensitivity analysis using the GDSC database to identify potential drug–gene interactions related to gut microbiota and MM. The analysis revealed that ANLN expression was positively correlated with sensitivity to several drugs, including 5-Fluorouracil, BHG712, AR-42, BMS345541, BX-912, CAY10603, I-BET-762, Ispinesib Mesylate, JW-7-24-1, KIN001-102, KIN001-260, Navitoclax, NG-25, NPK76-II-72-1, PHA-793887, PIK-93, SNX-2112, TG101348, THZ-2-102-1, TL-1-85, TPCA-1, Tubastatin A, UNC0638, Vorinostat, WZ3105, XMD13-2, FK866, and Methotrexate, while exhibiting negative correlations with 17-AAG and Docetaxel. Interestingly, the results for KIF14 showed an almost opposite pattern of drug sensitivity (Fig. B).
 

## 4. Discussion
MM is a relatively rare cancer, characterized by its aggressiveness and resistance to treatment, typically resulting from asbestos exposure.[ ] Late diagnosis, low survival rates, and the absence of reliable diagnostic and prognostic markers remain significant challenges in the clinical management of MM.[ ] With its resistance to traditional oncological therapies, MM is known for having one of the poorest survival rates of any cancer type. The complex gut microbiota, comprising various microorganisms and their ecological interactions in the human gastrointestinal tract, is increasingly recognized as a potential precursor, facilitator, or even instigator of numerous malignancies when it becomes dysbiosis.[ , ] Previous studies have shown that gut microbiota regulates the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapies, presenting an opportunity to target specific microbes to boost anticancer efficacy and reduce toxicity in microbial therapies.[ ] Preclinical and clinical research has revealed that certain gut microbiota act as regulators of the TME in a range of cancers, such as epithelial tumors and melanoma.[ ] Additionally, a recent Phase II study demonstrated the predictive value of gut microbiota in determining the response to immunotherapy in mesothelioma patients.[ ] However, the existing literature lacks a thorough analysis of the causal link between gut microbiota and MM. Using GWAS data, we began with a two-sample MR analysis to investigate the potential causal link between gut microbiota and MM. This approach offers fresh insights into MM pathogenesis and highlights new opportunities for prevention and intervention.
While the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts are anatomically distinct, they arise from the same embryonic tissue and share structural similarities,[ ] allowing for possible interactions that influence both health and disease. The gut microbiota interacts with the host immune system through bacterial components and metabolites, regulating immune responses in the gastrointestinal tract and influencing distant organs like the lungs, while pulmonary diseases can alter gut microbiota via lymphocyte migration and inflammatory mediators, leading to gut disorders. This bidirectional communication between the gut and lungs is known as the gut–lung axis.[ ] Although research on the gut–lung axis is still in its early stages, it holds great potential as a novel therapeutic approach for pulmonary diseases.[ ]
This two-sample MR study explored potential causal links between ten bacterial genera and MM risk. Our results provide suggestive evidence that genetically predicted increases in the abundances of class.Bacilli, family.Rikenellaceae, genus.Clostridium innocuum group, and order.Lactobacillales are associated with a higher risk of MM. Conversely, increases in genus.Ruminococcaceae UCG004, genus.Flavonifractor, phylum.Firmicutes, genus.Anaerofilum, genus.Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and genus.Lactobacillus appear to offer protective effects against MM. While the importance of gut and intratumor microbiota in cancer is gaining attention, their presence and effects in MM have been notably understudied.
Elevated levels of order.Lactobacillales are linked to Helicobacter infection and accelerated gastric cancer progression. This may be due to lactate serving as an energy source for tumor cells, promoting ATP production and inflammation.[ ] Yan et al[ ] identified a link between the genus.Clostridium innocuum group and a higher risk of insomnia. Although its association with cancer has not been reported, research suggests that fluctuations in sleep duration may intermittently influence cancer development.[ ] During the development of colorectal cancer, microbial species with higher relative abundance in the early stages of tumor formation are gradually replaced by family.Rikenellaceae. This shift may be linked to the role of family.Rikenellaceae as opportunistic pathogens, contributing to inflammation or producing mutagenic toxins.[ ] In a prospective study aimed at identifying microbiota and metabolites in biliary tract cancer across different immunotherapy response groups, class.Bacilli was identified as potential predictive biomarkers for survival and immunotherapy efficacy, which may be involved in multiple immune response processes via glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism pathways.[ ]
Taxonomic composition analysis shows that phylum.Firmicutes is a dominant microbiota phylum in both the gut and lungs across all groups.[ ] A reduction in phylum.Firmicutes population leads to a decrease in butyrate production in the intestine, which in turn reduces mucin synthesis. Insufficient mucin on the intestinal membrane increases intestinal permeability,[ ] triggering chronic inflammation and exacerbating intestinal inflammatory responses. These harmful mechanisms are believed to be associated with reduced butyrate production and a weakened intestinal barrier. This aligns with our research findings, which also demonstrate that phylum.Firmicutes plays an inhibitory role in MM formation. Genus.Lactobacillus is generally recognized as a commensal bacterium and is commonly included in probiotic formulations. In a syngeneic model of triple-negative 4T1 breast cancer, oral administration of genus.Lactobacillus led to a reduction in tumor growth, an effect thought to be mediated through the modulation of immune cells.[ ] Although genus.Ruminococcaceae UCG004 has not been extensively studied in tumors, it may play a significant role in regulating protection against all-grade immune-related adverse events.[ ] The genus.Flavonifractor, a Gram-positive anaerobic bacterium from the Clostridium family, has the ability to metabolize catechins.[ ] We identified a potential association between genus.Flavonifractor and a decreased risk of this cancer. However, in other cancers, such as esophageal cancer,[ ]
genus.Flavonifractor has been identified as a pathogenic species contributing to tumor initiation and progression. Furthermore, our analysis also identified genus.Anaerofilum and genus.Clostridium sensu stricto 1 as protective microbial genera, suggesting their potential role in mitigating the risk of MM through mechanisms of gut homeostasis and immune regulation.
In total, 30 hub genes were identified, with 5 (TENM3, CCNB2, SFXN1, KIF14, and ANLN) showing a significant association with DFS and OS. Research has indicated that these genes are involved in the pathogenesis of certain tumors. As a member of the conserved teneurin family, TENM3 encodes transmembrane receptors that are expressed during nervous system development.[ ] Teneurins, conserved glycoprotein receptors, have been associated with tumor progression and resistance to drugs.[ ] Research has revealed that TENM3 is markedly overexpressed in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Patients with elevated TENM3 expression are linked to worse outcomes, indicating that TENM3 may act as an oncogene in ESCC.[ ] SFXN1, part of the sideroflexin family (SFXNs),[ ] has been documented as a potential biomarker for lung adenocarcinoma, where it also mediates immune cell infiltration and tumorigenesis in lung adenocarcinoma.[ , ] Studies have found that SFXN1 is significantly upregulated in triple-negative breast cancer and correlates with poor patient outcomes. Mechanistically, SFXN1 drives triple-negative breast cancer progression by suppressing toll-interacting protein-mediated autophagic degradation of CIP2A.[ ] KIF14, belonging to the kinesin-3 family of microtubule-dependent molecular motors, contains both a kinesin motor domain and a forkhead-associated domain, playing an essential role in the final stages of cytokinesis.[ ] Its overexpression is linked to cancer progression and resistance to chemotherapy in several malignancies, identifying it as an oncogene.[ – ] On the other hand, downregulation of KIF14 inhibits tumor growth by increasing p27Kip1 levels and causing cytokinesis failure.[ ] The ANLN gene encodes the protein Anillin, a critical actin-binding protein involved in regulating cell division, migration, and proliferation.[ ] ZHANG et al identified increased ANLN copy number, reduced CpG island methylation, and elevated histone hypermethylation in hepatocellular carcinoma, which correlates with poor patient prognosis.[ ] Furthermore, ANLN knockdown using small interfering RNA in bladder urothelial carcinoma cells significantly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, causing G2/M phase arrest and a reduction in cyclin B1 and D1 levels.[ ] CCNB2 encodes Cyclin B2, a protein belonging to the cyclin family, which regulates cyclin-dependent kinase activity. Different cyclins function spatially and temporally at distinct phases of the cell cycle.[ ] Recent studies have shown that CCNB2 is overexpressed in various cancers, including breast carcinoma,[ ] non-small cell lung cancer,[ ] and nasopharyngeal carcinoma.[ ] This aberrant expression of CCNB2 disrupts spindle checkpoint regulation, causing chromosomal instability CIN, a key feature of most cancers.[ ]
Further, following GO and KEGG analysis of these related genes, our research has revealed that class.Bacilli, family.Rikenellaceae, genus.Clostridium innocuum group, and order.Lactobacillales may promote the formation of MM by regulating the cellular senescence, proteoglycans in cancer and Rap1 signaling pathways. Cellular senescence refers to a condition of permanent growth cessation, triggered by multiple stress factors, such as telomere attrition, DNA damage, and oxidative stress.[ ] Senescent cells exhibit a distinct senescence-associated secretory phenotype,[ ] characterized by the release of various cytokines, chemokines, and growth regulators. These secretions can have a wide range of effects on cancer development. Persistent senescence-associated secretory phenotype can alter the TME, fostering chronic inflammation and promoting tumor advancement. This includes enhancing blood vessel formation in tumors,[ ] facilitating the spread and migration of cancer cells,[ ] and triggering epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in nearby cells to support tumor invasion.[ ] Proteoglycans, crucial components of cell surface and pericellular environments, play diverse roles in cancer progression. Proteoglycans such as syndecans[ ] and glypicans[ ] can directly influence cancer development by regulating key signaling pathways. Although altered expression of certain proteoglycans is observed in numerous cancers, whether they function as promoters or suppressors of tumors remains ambiguous. This largely depends on the specific type of proteoglycan, its posttranslational modifications, and the nature and stage of the cancer.[ ] The Rap1 signaling pathway plays multiple roles in cell migration, invasion, and metastasis across various cancers. However, its specific functions and effects on metastasis in MM remain an area of ongoing research.[ ] According to the GO analysis, several key enriched functions were identified, particularly “small GTPase binding” and “GTPase binding,” which suggest a significant involvement in signal transduction. Additionally, “ribonuclease activity” was notable, indicating potential roles in RNA metabolism. These outcomes strongly suggest that gut microbiota and related-genes play critical roles in various cellular and physiological processes and exerts functional effects on tumor progression.
Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes reside within the TME, which surrounds tumor cells and plays a critical role in tumor progression through various secreted factors.[ ] The TME creates conditions favorable for cancer initiation and immune evasion, which ultimately leads to drug resistance, especially with immunotherapy.[ ] Previous evidence suggests that the gut microbiota regulates cancer at various stages, including initiation, genetic instability, susceptibility to the host immune response, progression, and response to treatment.[ ] In our study, we preliminarily investigated how genetic loci influence the gut microbiota, particularly concerning tumor immune cell infiltration. However, the mechanisms driving this process still need further clarification. Plasma cells are found in various cancers and are known for their capacity to produce significant quantities of antibodies and cytokines.[ ] Their presence is often linked to antitumor immunity and a positive response to immunotherapy.[ ] However, plasma cells can also exhibit tumor-promoting properties, which are influenced by factors such as their phenotype, antibody isotype, secretion profile, tumor type, TME, and localization.[ ] NK cells play a pivotal role in the innate immune system, recognizing aberrant MHC class I expression and cell stress markers on self-cells.[ ] They exhibit antitumor cytotoxicity without requiring prior sensitization and release cytokines that regulate immune activity.[ ] However, the TME, comprising stromal cells, infiltrating immunosuppressive cells, and tumor cells, can inhibit NK cell function through direct interactions or secretion of soluble factors.[ ] To overcome these suppressive mechanisms, various therapeutic strategies have been developed, including adoptive cell transfer, cytokine-based treatments, and monoclonal antibodies targeting NK cell receptors and TME elements.[ ] The ESTIMATE algorithm using StromalScore was employed to evaluate the TME, revealing that TENM3 is positively correlated with these scores in MM, which suggests that TENM3 is likely involved in regulating the immunological landscape by influencing stromal components in the TME. Our study also found that the expression of TENM3, CCNB2, SFXN1, KIF14, and ANLN was significantly correlated with the upregulation of immune-related biomarkers, particularly TGFB1 and TGFBR1. The TGFB1 gene encodes TGF-β, a pleiotropic cytokine expressed in most cells and tissues.[ ] While the TGF-β pathway serves as a tumor suppressor in early cancer, it promotes immune evasion and metastasis in advanced stages.[ , ] During TGF-β signaling, TGFBR1 plays a critical role of activating downstream R-SMADs (SMAD2/3) and co-SMAD (SMAD4).[ ] Our findings reveal the potential role of these genes in modulating tumor immunity and provide important clues for future targeted therapies and biomarker development.
In investigating MM treatment, we identified 5 genes: TENM3 (rs111552159), CCNB2 (rs77845139), SFXN1 (rs77558518), KIF14 (rs57872228) and ANLN (rs78938557), near a genetic locus linked to the gut microbiota order.Lactobacillales and genus.Clostridium innocuum group, all positively correlated with MM. ALAN expression was positively correlated with sensitivity to 17-AAG and docetaxel, but indicated resistance to vorinostat. Vorinostat, a histone deacetylase inhibitor that modifies gene expression and protein activity, sensitizes malignant pleural mesothelioma cells to apoptosis by downregulating BCL-XL and XIAP,[ , ] as demonstrated in preclinical studies. However, in a phase 3 trial comparing vorinostat with placebo in patients with advanced malignant pleural mesothelioma, vorinostat used as second- or third-line therapy failed to improve OS.[ ] As a taxane derivative, docetaxel acts by binding to microtubules, blocking androgen receptor nuclear translocation, and inducing apoptosis via B-cell lymphoma phosphorylation.[ ] Although Maria and Ioannis et al[ , ] reported that the gemcitabine–docetaxel combination may offer benefits for patients with malignant pleural mesothelioma, docetaxel has not been widely adopted as a standard therapy. The HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG significantly inhibited cell proliferation and survival. HSP90 inhibition was shown to decrease phosphorylation of downstream signaling intermediates (AKT, MAPK, and S6), enhance cell cycle checkpoints (p53, p21, and p27), cause G2 phase arrest, activate caspase 3/7, and elevate the sub-G1 apoptotic population.[ ] The results indicate that 17-AAG could be a promising treatment strategy for human mesothelioma.
Few studies have explored the relationship between gut microbiota and MM. This is the first study using MR to analyze this causality between gut microbiota and MM. By leveraging GWAS data from the FinnGen R11 database, we enhance the statistical power for causal associations. However, several limitations exist. First, the GWAS data were exclusively from European populations, requiring validation in other ethnic groups. Second, the small sample size, due to MM’s rarity, may introduce bias, necessitating larger studies. Third, the use of summary statistics from FinnGen database limited our ability to analyze MM subtypes like pleural and peritoneal mesothelioma. Finally, a relaxed SNP threshold (P < 1 × 10-5) was used, which may include weaker correlations. Our findings reveal potential therapeutic targets and provide new insights into MM development. However, validation across diverse ethnic groups is essential before global clinical application. Certain gut microbiota identified as protective or risky are consistent with previous research, underscoring their significance as reference points for future studies. Further clinical and in vivo studies are needed to explore the causal link between gut microbiota and MM, improving our understanding of the gut–lung axis.

## 5. Conclusion
This two-sample MR study indicates causal links between certain gut microbiota and MM risk. Genetically predicted increases in class.Bacilli, family.Rikenellaceae, genus.Clostridium innocuum group, and order.Lactobacillales were linked to a higher risk of MM, while increases in genus.Ruminococcaceae UCG004, genus.Flavonifractor, phylum.Firmicutes, genus.Anaerofilum, genus.Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and genus.Lactobacillus seemed protective. Bioinformatics analysis suggests that the gut microbiota may influence the development of MM by regulating cellular senescence, cancer-associated proteoglycans, and the Rap1 signaling pathways. Furthermore, the identified DEGs may influence immune cell infiltration and the expression of immune-related biomarkers, affecting the tumor microenvironment. Additional studies are required to establish causality and elucidate the mechanisms connecting gut microbiota and MM, potentially guiding microbiome-focused prevention and treatment approaches.
