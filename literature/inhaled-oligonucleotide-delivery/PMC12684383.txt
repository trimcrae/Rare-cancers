## Abstract
Programmable RNA cytidine deaminase tools have been developed to convert cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) using CRISPR systems with guide RNAs. These tools, however, have limitations such as low editing efficiency, limited targetable sequence flexibility, and off-target RNA editing. Here, we present a novel guide-free C-to-U editing tool, named RECODE (RNA Editor for C-to-U with an Optimized DYW Enzyme), based on the RNA-binding pentatricopeptide repeat proteins, naturally fused to a C-terminal DYW cytidine deaminase domain. The RECODE specificity domain was engineered to enable retargeting, while its length and sequence were optimized to reduce off-target effects. Further optimization of the C-terminal catalytic region increased both the editing activity and the translation of the edited RNA. We showed that RECODE efficiently edits a wide range of targets in human cells, without affecting adjacent cytidines. It achieved over 50% editing efficiency for most sites, except those with an upstream guanine. Furthermore, we showed that RECODE is functional in mice, with high editing efficiency observed in specific tissues such as skeletal muscles using an AAV delivery system, suggesting its therapeutic potential for various diseases.

## Introduction
RNA editing is a transient and safer approach for human therapeutics compared to permanent DNA editing. Several tools have been developed for adenosine-to-inosine [A-to-I (G)] and cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) RNA editing. Current C-to-U RNA editing technologies using guide RNAs (gRNA), such as CRISPR-Cas13 fused to APOBEC or ADAR domains [ – ], often suffers from limitations including low editing efficiency, low range of targetable sequences, local off-target and residual A-to-I editing (for ADAR). To address these issues, a gRNA-free technology based on Pumilio proteins has been explored, but faces challenges such as targeting only short sequences (8∼10 nucleotides) and local off-target within the editing window [ ].
Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins offer an alternative solution. These proteins form a large family of RNA-binding proteins in plants, essential for post-transcriptional modifications in chloroplasts and mitochondria. PPR proteins involved in RNA editing possess an N-terminal recognition domain and a C-terminal catalytic domain. The recognition domain consists of tandemly repeated PPR motifs that specifically recognize the target RNA by following a PPR code, which connects two amino acid positions (5 and last) in a PPR motif to a nucleotide [ ]. Two types of PPR domain are commonly found in plants: the P-type solely composed of canonical PPR motifs and the PLS-type, which contains two additional variants: L, for long and S, for short [ ]. The PLS domain, specific to the PPR-editors, contains repetitions of P1-L1-S1 triplets, with the last PLS triplet in the C-terminus, differing in length and amino acid composition, named P2-L2-S2. Following the PPR domain is an extension including two PPR-like motifs (E1 and E2) and a cytidine deaminase-like domain (DYW) [ ]. DYW domains are classified into three subgroups: DYW:KP involved in U-to-C editing [ ], DYW:PG in C-to-U [ ] and DYW:WW predicted to also catalyse the C-to-U reaction [ ].
Previous attempts to engineer PPR–DYW proteins to target sites naturally edited by plant PPR-editors have shown limited success, requiring cofactors, exhibiting low editing efficiency, or resulting in the C-terminal PPR motifs losing their nucleotide specificity [ – ]. Furthermore, natural DYW:PG proteins require specific neighbouring nucleotides, limiting their applications [ , ]. Therefore, we developed here new approaches, using PPR–DYW proteins, to edit any custom RNA sequence in cells or mice with high efficiency and without affecting the translation of the edited mRNA or increasing the off-target activity.

## Materials and methods

## Design of RECODE
PLSv1 and P domains were designed in previous studies: PLSv1 (PV443078) corresponds to the PLS domain in [ ], while P[TN] and P[NS/TD/ND] to PPR3.0 and PPR2.0 in [ ], respectively. To avoid homologous recombination within the viral vector sequence during the in vivo study, the nucleotide sequence homology of each P-motif was reduced within the PPR gene sequence. PLSv2 (PV443079) designed for this study is composed of three identical triplets of P1, L1, and S1 PPR motifs and was designed based on PPR motifs found in hornwort, lycophyte and fern transcriptomes (https://ppr.plantenergy.uwa.edu.au/onekp/) [ ]. After selecting all the motifs of a specific size (35 aa for P1 and L1 and 31 aa for S1), a consensus sequence for each PPR motif was derived from EMBOSS cons [ ] with a plurality value of 0.
C-terminal domains containing the five PPR-like motifs (P2, L2, S2, E1, and E2) and a DYW domain were added downstream of the PPR domains. To design the C-terminal domain, the non-DYW:KP proteins identified in hornwort, lycophyte and fern transcriptomes [ ] were aligned using MAFFT in L-INS-i mode (v7.407) [ ]. Alignments were then trimmed using TrimAL (v1.4.rev15) [ ] with a minimum conservation threshold and a gap threshold of 20%. A tree was built for the trimmed alignment using IQ-TREE (v.2.0.3) [ ] with the JTT + F + I + G4 substitution model, selected using the in-built automated test [ ]. The branch support was computed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was visualized using iTOL [ ]. Based on this tree, we designed a DYW:PG C-terminal domain specific to lycophytes (PV443080) and a DYW:WW based on a subclade of proteins found in hornworts (PV443083). For the selected clades of proteins, a consensus sequence was generated for each motif of equal length (35 aa for P2, 36 aa for L2, 32 aa for S2, 34 aa for E1 and E2, and 136 and 137 aa for DYW:PG and DYW:WW, respectively) using EMBOSS cons with a plurality value of 0 [ ].
The C-terminal domains synthesized by Azenta were cloned by Golden Gate Assembly with Esp3I in the mammalian expression vector PM18033 carrying the human cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early promoter, the human β-globin chimeric intron, and SV40 polyadenylation signal. For Hepa1-6 cells study, a PM18033 expression vector with an additional T7 promoter and translation enhancer was used. The P and PLS domains synthesized by Azenta were then inserted by Golden Gate Assembly with BpiI. The mutations were introduced into the RECODE (RNA Editor for C-to-U with an Optimized DYW Enzyme) proteins by site directed mutagenesis polymerase chain reaction (PCR).
After the initial development step, we designed and constructed RECODE vectors using a two-step Golden Gate Assembly strategy. In the first step, we cloned the N-terminal region and C-terminal PPR-like motifs (P2, L2, S2, and E1) and E2-DYW domain into PM18033 vector using Esp3I ( ). The N-terminal and P2 motifs were designed to include an additional inward BpiI recognition sequence to enable the second cloning step. The 3′ extension of the N-terminal fragment determines the total number of PPR motifs inserted in the following step. The second step involves the insertion of the N-terminal PPR-P motifs. These were provided as individual fragments, each containing a pair of PPR motifs flanked by inward-directed BpiI recognition sequences ( ).
For the in vivo study, the AAV9-RECODE vectors were produced by VectorBuilder. All primers used in this study are listed in .

## Mammalian cell culture
HEK293T (RIKEN, RCB2202) and Hepa1-6 (ATCC, CRL-1830) cells were maintained in culture using DMEM supplemented with high glucose, glutamine, phenol red, and sodium pyruvate (Wako), and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Capricorn) and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Fujifilm). Cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 to ensure optimal growth. Passaging was performed every 2–3 days upon reaching ∼80%–90% confluency.

## Transfection
For the design of the PG and WW C-terminal domains and the analysis of several PPR code combinations (at the exogenous rpoA editing site), HEK293T cells were plated in 24-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a density of ∼8.0 × 104 cells/well 24 h prior to transfection. A mixture containing 500 ng of transfection plasmid combined with Opti-MEM® I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1.5 µl of FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega) was prepared in a total volume of 25 µl per well. This mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min before being added to the cells. After transfection, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 24 h.
For the development of RECODE proteins (at the endogenous CTNNB1-T41I site), HEK293T cells were plated in 24-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a density of ∼1.0 × 105 cells/well 24 h prior to transfection. A mixture containing 700 ng of RECODE plasmid combined with Opti-MEM® I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2.5 µl of FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega) was added to the cells. After transfection, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 48 h.
For the comparison between RECODE and RESCUE-S, HEK293T cells were plated in 96-well plates coated with poly-L-lysine (IWAKI) at ∼104 cells/well 24 h prior to transfection. 140 ng RECODE plasmid or 90 ng Cas-ADARRESCUE-S/180 ng gRNA plasmids were transfected with 0.5 µl of FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega). The amounts of plasmid DNA for RECODE and RESCUE-S are based on . After transfection, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 48 h.
For western blotting and RNA-seq analysis, 700 ng RECODE plasmid or 450 ng Cas-ADARRESCUE-S/900 ng gRNA plasmids were transfected with 2.5 µl of FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega) to cells plated in 24-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 48 h.
Hepa1-6 cells were seeded into a well of 24-well plate at ∼1.0 × 105 cells/well the day before transfection. Five hundred nanograms of RECODE plasmid was transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C.

## Analysis of in vitro RNA editing by Sanger sequencing
Cell sorting was not performed prior to RNA extraction as it did not significantly increase the editing efficiency for RECODE and RESCUE-S targeting CTNNB1-T41I site ( ).
Total RNA was extracted from transfected cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Maxwell® RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with 300 ng of RNA, ReverTra Ace® (TOYOBO) and random primers. The editing site was PCR-amplified using PrimeSTAR Max DNA® polymerase (TaKaRa) with 1 µl of cDNA and primers listed in . PCR products were purified with ExoSAP-ITTM Express PCR Cleanup Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sequenced (Azenta) using forward or reverse primers specific to the gene. RNA editing was quantified from raw sequencing chromatograms using EditR (EditR software: http://baseeditr.com) [ ]. Editing efficiency was determined as the ratio of thymidine (T) to the sum of T and cytidine (C), based on the percentage of their respective peak areas after trimming low-quality data (P-value cutoff: 0.01). Areas not significantly different from background noise were assigned a value of 0.
To ensure the reliability of this quantification method, Sanger sequencing results were compared to those obtained from amplicon-seq and RNA-seq in mouse and human cells, respectively. Comprehensive amplicon-seq and RNA-seq studies in both mouse and human cells (detailed in the ‘Analysis of in vivo RNA editing by amplicon-seq’ and ‘Off-target analysis by RNA-seq’ sections) showed high correlations ( ; R2 values of 0.98–0.99) with editing efficiencies determined by Sanger sequencing.

## Protein analysis for in vitro samples
Total protein was extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (ATTO) then adjusted to 1 mg/ml. Western blotting was performed with Abby Simple Western system (ProteinSimple) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the 12–230 kDa Separation Module, RePlex Module, and the Anti-Mouse or Anti-Rabbit Detection Module (ProteinSimple). Primary antibodies against β-catenin (sc-59737, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:250), KRAS (12063-1-AP, Proteintech; 1:200), and BRG1/SMARCA4 (21634-1-AP, Proteintech; 1:100) were used. Antibodies were diluted with Can Get Signal Solution 1 (TOYOBO). Total protein was detected using the Total Protein labeling reagent (ProteinSimple). The peak area values of target proteins were normalized to the total protein values using Compass software (ProteinSimple).

## 
In vivo study
The study using wild-type mice was outsourced to Shin Nippon Biomedical Laboratories, Ltd. which approved the study protocol. The breeding and use of animals was carried out in accordance with the animal experiment regulations of the outsourcing company. The mice were kept under the non-SPF (specific pathogen free) condition and were maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle at the controlled room temperature of 21.2–23.0°C with humidity of 40%–57% during experiments. The FVB/NJcl strain mice used in this study were purchased from CLEA Japan Inc.
AAV9 packaged RECODE (RECODE-WW[F75F] and RECODE-PG[I83I]) were prepared by VectorBuilder. The study included three groups of three 8-week-old male WT mice (FVB/NJcl): a control substance group [phosphate buffered saline (PBS group)] and two test substance groups (AAV9-RECODE-WW[F75F] and AAV9-RECODE-PG[I83I]). Mice in each group were administered a single intravenous dose of the test substance at a dose of 3 × 1014 vg/kg or the same volume of PBS as the AAV preparation as the control substance. Eight weeks after administration, the mice were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane inhalation anaesthesia (2.0%–4.0%; Isoflurane Inhalation Anesthetic Solution “VTRS”, Mylan EPD LLC), and various tissues were collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

## RNA extraction from in vivo samples
Ten milligrams of piece of organs were homogenized in 250 µl of the 1-thioglycerol/homogenization (supplied by Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit; Promega) using a BioMasher tube (Funakoshi). The homogenate was centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and 200 µl of supernatant were collected. Two hundred microlitres of Lysis buffer were added before extracting total RNA using Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega).

## Protein extraction from in vivo samples
Total proteins were extracted from 10 mg of piece of organs with 120 µl RIPA buffer (ATTO) supplemented with 1% Proteinase Inhibitor Cocktail. After homogenization in BioMasher tube (Funakoshi) and centrifugation by 14 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, 80 µl supernatant and 20 µl 5 × sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) buffer were mixed and heated at 95°C for 5 min. Total protein and standard protein were quantified using Protein Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 0.05 g/ml Ionic Detergent Compatibility Reagent. The protein concentration of the sample was adjusted to 3 mg/ml with 1 × SDS buffer and protein samples were analysed using Abby Simple Western system (ProteinSimple). Anti-HA monoclonal antibody (clone C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:500) and Anti-Rabbit Detection Module (ProteinSimple) were used to detect RECODE protein. Anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (clone 4F8-2, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:2000) and Anti-Mouse Detection Module (ProteinSimple) for Gapdh protein detection. Antibodies were diluted with Can Get Signal Solution 1 (TOYOBO). Total protein was detected with Total Protein labeling reagent (ProteinSimple) after detection of Gapdh protein on the same blot. These total protein values were then used to normalize both Gapdh protein and RECODE proteins, the latter of which were run on a separate blot using the same samples. The signal intensity (area) of the bands corresponding to RECODE or Gapdh protein was quantified and normalized to total protein using Compass software (ProteinSimple).

## mRNA quantification

Gapdh mRNA was quantified using TaqMan® Gene Expression Assays, using a FAM-MGB probe primer set for mouse Gapdh (Mm9999999915_g1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a VIC-MGB probe primer set for 18S rRNA (Hs99999901_s1, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The QIAcuity Probe Kit (QIAGEN) was used as the reaction reagent. Reaction solutions containing cDNA from nine different organs and tissues from three individuals were amplified using QIAcuity One (QIAGEN). The number of copies of Gapdh mRNA was calculated based on the number of positive partitions.

## Analysis of in vivo RNA editing by amplicon next-generation sequencing
DNA fragments including Gapdh F75 and I83 positions were amplified by PCR using primer sets containing barcode sequences to identify each sample. Forward primers specified the organs, while reverse primers, the individual ( ). PCR products were pooled in equal concentration, purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN) and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Azenta).
Paired-end sequencing reads were merged using FLASH2 (v2.2.00) to generate extended fragments. The resulting merged reads were saved in FASTQ format and used as input for a custom Python script ( ) to quantify site-specific RNA editing.
In the first step, only reads with an exact length of 159 nucleotides were retained. To ensure consistent orientation of all sequences for downstream analysis, each read was examined for the presence of a conserved reference sequence, CAAATTCAAC, located at positions 16–25. If this sequence was not found in the specified region, the reverse complement of the read was computed and the same position in the reversed sequence was checked again. Reads that did not contain the conserved sequence in either orientation were considered to have an ambiguous strand origin and were excluded from further analysis.
For each retained read, two barcode regions were used to classify the tissue type and the individual. The tissue barcode, located at positions 4–12, identified reads originating from one of the following samples: CTAAGTACT (QF), CTACATCTG (GA), CTAGGACTC (TA), CTATACTAT (Heart), CTAACCTTA (Diaphragm), CTACCAATC (Brain), CTACCGCTC (Liver), CTAGTGTTA (Kidney), and CTACTGAGT (Testis). The individual barcode, located at positions 148–156, distinguished between injected reagents and replicate groups: TATGTGACG (PBS, rep. 1), ACTCCAACG (PBS, rep. 2), CCTGTGACG (PBS, rep. 3), GTTAGTACG (RECODE-WW, rep. 1), TCAATCACG (RECODE-WW, rep. 2), AGCGGAACG (RECODE-WW, rep. 3), CGTAGCACG (RECODE-PG, rep. 1), TGGCTCACG (RECODE-PG, rep. 2), and ATGGTGACG (RECODE-PG, rep. 3).
RNA editing was quantified by analysing the nucleotide present at two defined positions in each read: position 85, corresponding to the F75F site, and position 109, corresponding to the I83I site. Only cytidine (C) and thymidine (T) were considered valid at these positions. For each barcode combination (tissue × individual), the number of reads containing either a C or a T at each site was counted. The editing ratio was calculated as the proportion of T-containing reads relative to the sum of C and T reads at each position.

## Next-generation sequencing libraries preparation
The next-generation sequencing (NGS) libraries were prepared from total RNA (as described in ‘RNA extraction from in vivo samples section’) using poly(A) enrichment and Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencing was performed by Azenta.

## Read mapping
Reads were mapped to a customized reference sequence including GRCm39 (Ensembl) and each AAV9-RECODE sequences for in vivo study and GRCh38.105 (Ensembl) and the RECODE sequences for the in vitro study, using STAR (v2.7.10) [ ]. Alignment parameters included: –quantMode TranscriptomeSAM –outFilterType BySJout –outFilterMultimapNmax 1 –outSAMstrandField intronMotif –outSAMattributes All. The resulting alignments were subsequently used for off-target and gene expression analyses.

## Off-target analysis by RNA-seq
PCR duplicates were removed from the alignment generated by STAR (as described in ‘Read mapping’ section) using Picard MarkDuplicates tool (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), RNA editing candidate sites were identified using REDItools (v1.3) [ ] using the following parameters -t 17 -e -d -l -U [AG, TC, CT, GA] -G path/to/gtf -p -u -m 30 -T 6–0 -W -v 10 -n 0 -g 2 -s 1 removing the sites with a read count <10 and minimum mapping quality score of <30. Base frequencies were counted at all positions in the transcript sequences. Positions with significant differences in base frequency compared to the reference were identified using Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (P-value <.01).

## Analysis of transcriptome-wide expression changes
Estimated counts for all the transcripts were generated using RSEM (v1.2.28) [ ] following STAR alignment (as described in ‘Read mapping’ section). Genes were then pre-filtered to include only those with a counts per million >0.5 in at least half of the samples. Differential gene expression analysis was subsequently performed using DESeq2 (1.46.0) [ ] with default parameters. MA plots were generated by plotting the log2 fold change against the mean expression (baseMean).

## Cytotoxicity assessment using lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay
Cytotoxicity was assessed using the Cytotoxicity LDH Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s nonhomogeneous assay protocol. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded in collagen-coated 24-well plates (IWAKI) at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, 24 h prior to transfection. Cytotoxicity was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection. At each time point, 100 µl of cell culture supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate and mixed with 100 µl of Working Solution. The plate was protected from light and incubated at room temperature for 30 min, after which the reaction was stopped by adding 50 µl of Stop Solution to each well. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Cells transfected with an empty vector under the same conditions served as the control, and wells without cells were used as the background. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The remaining cells from the transfected plates were subsequently used to assess editing efficiency and β-catenin protein accumulation (as described in the ‘Analysis of in vitro RNA editing by Sanger sequencing’ and ‘Protein analysis for in vitro samples’ sections).

## Reverse Transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
For the quantification of CTNNB1, KRAS, SMARCA4, and GAPDH mRNA, total RNA was extracted from transfected cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Maxwell® RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega). cDNA was synthesized using ReverTra Ace® (TOYOBO) with 300 ng RNA and 25 pmoles of random primers. Real-time RT-qPCR was performed on QuantStudio™ 3 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and with gene-specific primers ( ). Complete thermocycling conditions were as follows: 95°C for 2 min followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. The data were analyzed according to the ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as a reference gene for normalization. The RT-qPCR was performed using three technical replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. The data shown correspond to the means and standard deviations from three biological replicates.

## Protein expression and purification
The DNA sequences encoding His6-Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) tag (synthesized by Eurofins Genomics) and RECODE’s C-terminal domain were amplified by PCR using primers with Esp3I restriction site. The resulting DNA fragments were subcloned into a modified pET21b + PA vector by Golden Gate assembly with Esp3I. The PPR domain was subsequently cloned using BpiI (as described in ‘Design of RECODE’). The His6-SUMO-RECODE fusion protein was expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) at 15°C overnight following induction with 0.1 mM IPTG and 0.4 mM ZnSO4. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, and 1 mM dithiothreitol) supplemented with 5 mg/ml lysozyme, then stored at −80°C until use. The cells were disrupted by sonication followed by centrifugation to remove cell debris. The soluble fraction was applied to Ni-NTA beads (Qiagen) and thoroughly washed with lysis buffer. The SUMO fusion protein was eluted with lysis buffer containing 500 mM imidazole and then cleaved overnight with 5 µg of Ulp1 protease and dialyzed against buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, and 1 mM dithiothreitol). The cleaved protein was subsequently loaded onto a HiTrap Q column (Cytiva), and peak fractions containing the target protein were pooled. Further purification was performed using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva), equilibrated with 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine. The purified protein was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter unit (Merck).

## RNA electrophoretic mobility shift assay
Cy3- or Cy5-labeled synthetic 31-nt RNA oligonucleotides, CTNNB1-T41I (5′-Cy3-UCUGGAAUCCAUUCUGGUGCCACUACCACAG, the editing site is underlined), and SMARCA4-P88L (5′-Cy5-CAUGAGAAGGGCAUGUCGGACGACCCGCGCU, negative control), were used for RNA electrophoretic mobility shift assay (REMSA). RNA probes (10 nmol) and purified protein at the indicated concentrations (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 nM) were mixed in 10 µl reaction buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 8.0 and 1 mM dithiothreitol) to a final reaction volume of 20 µl. The reactions were incubated at 25°C for 20 min, after which 2 µl of 80% glycerol was added. A 10-µl aliquot of each reaction was loaded onto a 5%–20% e-PAGEL (ATTO) and electrophoresed in 1 × Tris Borate EDTA buffer at 4°C. Gels were imaged using an iBright Imaging System (Invitrogen), and the fraction of oligonucleotide bound was quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

## Prediction of the E1–E2–DYW domain in complex with RNA
The structure of the complex between the E1–E2–DYW domain of RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW targeting CTNNB1-T41I site and its target (5′-CUACC-3′, with the editing site at the second cytosine) was predicted using Protenix (v0.6.0) on the server (https://protenix-server.com/login) [ ] with default parameters. The resulting models were then visualized using PyMOL (https://github.com/schrodinger/pymol-open-source).

## Statistics
Two-tailed unpaired t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test were performed by GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., https://www.graphpad.com/). For all statistical tests, the threshold was set at P <.05.

## Results

## Design of programmable guide-free cytidine deaminases
Synthetic PLS–DYW proteins based on seed plants require cofactors for editing efficiently the target [ ]. In contrast, recent studies have demonstrated that two natural PLS–DYW proteins from moss, an early-branching land plant clade, are functional in nonplant contexts without the need for plant-specific cofactors [ – ]. To engineer cofactor-independent C-to-U PPR-editors, we first constructed a phylogenetic tree using non-DYW:KP C-terminal domains from three early-branching land plant clades (hornworts, lycophytes and ferns) as previously identified [ ] ( ). Subsequently, we designed two P2–L2–S2–E1–E2–DYW domains based on consensus sequences for independent DYW:PG and DYW:WW subclades (hereafter referred to as PG and WW) ( and ). These domains were then fused to a previously designed PLS domain (PLSv1) [ ] ( ) to create functional PPR–DYW proteins.
As our engineered PPR-like motifs lacked a pre-defined PPR code, we adopted a strategy demonstrated in a previous study [ ]. This prior work successfully retargeted a designer PLS–DYW protein to edit the Arabidopsis rpoA editing site by modifying the PPR code amino acid positions in the C-terminal PPR-like motifs based on its natural editor, CLB19 [ ]. To evaluate the editing efficiency and functionality of our PPR–DYW proteins in human cells, we incorporated the exogenous rpoA editing site into the 3′-UTR of the PPR–DYW mRNA (Fig.  ) and transiently expressed PLSv1-PG and PLSv1-WW for 24 h in HEK293T cells. Both proteins showed an editing efficiency exceeding 80% (Fig. ).
 To retarget PG and WW C-terminal domains, we indirectly analysed nucleotide preferences of P2, L2, S2, and E1 motifs as the PPR code is primarily defined for P, P1, and S1 and little is known about the mode of recognition of the other PPR-like motifs. Given that the design of the C-terminal domains is based on early-branching land plant clades, we focused on the 10 most common PPR code combinations found in those plants for each motif ( ). We hypothesized that high editing efficiency for a specific nucleotide with a given PPR code combination suggests a nucleotide preference for the targeted PPR motif using that specific code. Conversely, similar editing efficiencies for multiple nucleotides suggest that the amino acid combination has low or no nucleotide preference. In this experiment, we replaced PLSv1 with PLSv2, a PLS domain composed of three identical P1L1S1 motifs ( ). PLSv2–DYW proteins had lower editing efficiencies than PLSv1–DYW (Fig. ) making it easier to detect variations in nucleotide preference as the presence of the target downstream the stop codon of the PLS–DYW protein could artificially amplify the editing efficiency. We obtained a PPR code for most, but not all the PPR motifs of the two C-terminal domains. For example, none of the tested PPR code combinations for PG:S2 specifically targeted pyrimidines, nor did those for PG:E1 and WW:E1 specifically target guanine. Additionally, PG:L2 recognized its target nonspecifically (Fig. and  , and and ). The PPR code combinations presenting the best balance between editing efficiency and nucleotide preference were selected for further studies.
Engineering the PLS domain is challenging due to limited knowledge about the mode of action of the L1 motif. To bypass this problem, we swapped the PLS domain with a P domain ( ), resulting in only a small decrease in editing efficiency (Fig.  ). The modular architecture of the P and the C-terminal domains enables the design of RNA Editor for C-to-U with an Optimized DYW Enzyme (RECODE) capable of targeting any RNA-sequence, by simple modular assemblies of the motifs ( ). Editors built with PG and WW domains are hereafter referred to as RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW.

## RECODE efficiently edits its target in mouse
To evaluate in vivo RNA editing by RECODE, we designed RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW targeting synonymous substitution sites in mouse Gapdh mRNA (F75F and I83I), to avoid affecting mouse survival by not altering the function of the target protein. The editing activity of these proteins, composed of 16 P-motifs, was first verified in mouse Hepa1–6 cells on the endogenous Gapdh mRNA ( ). RECODE-WW had higher editing efficiency than RECODE-PG at F75F site, whereas the opposite was observed at I83I site. Based on these results, we decided to perform in vivo analysis of F75F and I83I sites, using adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) vectors to deliver RECODE-WW[F75F] and RECODE-PG[I83I], respectively.
Two months after tail vein infection of wild-type mice with AAV9-RECODE-WW[F75F] or AAV9-RECODE-PG[I83I], various organs were collected and analysed for on-target RNA editing activity by amplicon NGS. Both RECODE variants showed high editing efficiency without local off-target (ca. 72%–85%) in skeletal muscle, heart, and diaphragm, but lower efficiency in the brain (16%) and liver, kidney, and testis (0.8%–3.6%) (Fig. ). Western blot analysis in each organ was performed to determine if low RECODE expression levels explained the observed low editing efficiency. A correlation was observed with high RECODE protein expression in organs with high editing activity and low expression in organs with low activity (Fig.  and  ). Nevertheless, the brain, despite similar protein expression levels to skeletal muscles, had moderate RNA editing, suggesting that high RECODE expression is not sufficient for high RNA editing activity. However, basic enzyme kinetics suggest that the abundance of the target RNA could also influence the editing rate. While AAV9-RECODE administration had no significant effect on the expression of the target mRNA or protein in any tissue or organ (Fig.  and  ), a correlation was observed between Gapdh mRNA expression and editing efficiency across different organs (R2 = 0.61 for RECODE-WW[F75F]; R2 = 0.69 for RECODE-PG[I83I]) (Fig.  and  ) suggesting that in addition to RECODE protein expression, the abundance of target RNA is a critical factor for efficient editing in vivo.
 Because natural PPR proteins are prone to off-target effects, we profiled global C-to-U substitution events via whole transcriptome RNA-sequencing. The analysis was performed on total RNA isolated from quadratus femoris (QF), a skeletal muscle sample with high RNA editing activity (84% and 74% in RECODE-WW[F75F] and RECODE-PG[I83I] infected mice, respectively) ( ). The number of C-to-U substituted positions and their median of frequencies were 1931, 4.7% in RECODE-WW[F75F] and 104, 4.8% in RECODE-PG[I83I] (Fig.  ). On-target editing frequency was highest in RECODE-WW[F75F] (87%) and second highest in RECODE-PG[I83I] (70%). However, multiple Gapdh pseudogenes with upstream sequences similar to the target sites were expressed, including the highest frequency site in RECODE-PG[I83I] (74%) ( ). Sequence logos generated from the sequences upstream of the off-target editing sites showed a stronger sequence conservation from nucleotide position −10 to −3, especially at position −3 (0 is the editing site) (Fig. ), suggesting that base substitution is more likely to occur when this region matches the target. Additionally, similarly to other deaminase enzymes, a greater preference for pyrimidine at position −1 was observed. These off-target analyses suggest that potential off-target editing is more likely to occur with RECODE-WW compared to RECODE-PG, and that off-target editing is likely influenced by the C-terminal recognition sequence.
To confirm that the expression of RECODE proteins does not cause a widespread change in RNA stability, we performed a differential expression analysis using DESeq2. Only minimal global changes were observed, and specifically, only five transcripts edited by RECODE-WW[F75F] showed significant changes (Fig.  ). These results suggest that despite triggering off-target editing, any change in gene expression would not be a direct effect of RECODE expression.

## Improved RECODE with lower off-target effects
To develop RECODE with reduced off-target effects, we designed RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW to edit a cytidine in CTNNB1 mRNA in human HEK293T cells, resulting in the T41I substitution in β-catenin, the protein coded by CTNNB1. This specific substitution is known to inhibit a crucial phosphorylation step that leads to β-catenin degradation, thereby causing the protein to accumulate in the cell [ ]. The editing activity of RECODE was evaluated 48 h after transfection by measuring the editing efficiency at the endogenous CTNNB1 on-target site and nine endogenous potential off-target sites (Fig.  ). This experimental design allowed us to correlate the editing efficiency with a successful β-catenin protein expression. Measuring protein expression is crucial, given that some PPR proteins are known to bind strongly to their targets and can consequently interfere with downstream processes [ ]. Editing without a corresponding increase in β-catenin protein would imply a lack of translation of the edited RNA, while an increase in protein levels would indicate successful editing and translation.
 Since longer PPR arrays have been linked to off-target effects [ ], we analysed the influence of four PPR domain lengths (10, 12, 14, and 16 P-motifs) on the editing activity of RECODE-PG (Fig. and  ) and RECODE-WW (Fig.  and  ). The on-target editing efficiency of RECODE-PG reached a plateau with 14 P-motifs, while RECODE-WW plateaued at a shorter length of 12 P-motifs (Fig.  and  ). The off-target editing activity increased significantly with 14 P-motifs for few tested off-target sites and 16 P-motifs for all sites. These results suggest that 12–14 P-motif length is optimal for achieving efficient on-target editing while minimizing off-target effects. A discrepancy was observed between RNA editing and protein accumulation. Although the editing efficiency of the 14 P-motif RECODE variants was significantly higher than the 10 P-motif variants, we observed a similar or significantly lower β-catenin protein accumulation (Fig. and  ). This suggests that only a small portion of the edited RNA is efficiently translated after editing by a 14 P-motif RECODE.
Despite the observed discrepancy, we decided to optimize the translation of the RNA edited by a 14 P-motif RECODE variant, as longer PPR arrays are required to target specific sequences in the nuclear transcriptome. Previous research has shown that natural PPR editors have a lower affinity for a U at the editing site compared to a C [ ], suggesting that improving the editing mechanism itself could facilitate RNA release. The function of the DYW domain is regulated by an autoinhibition mechanism that blocks its catalytic site [ ]. The upstream E1 and E2 motifs have been suggested to contribute to the formation of this catalytic site by completing the deaminase architecture [ ]. Given these observations, we hypothesized that an optimized amino acid sequence in the E-DYW region could modulate this autoinhibition mechanism and influence conformational changes that promote the release of the edited RNA, thus improving translation. We chose to modify the more conserved E1 and E2 motifs because the highly variable amino acid sequence of the DYW domain makes targeted modifications challenging. Specifically, we targeted the first helix (located at the RNA interface) of E1 and E2 motifs in a 14 P-motif RECODE-PG or RECODE-WW. These mutations were designed to match a consensus sequence derived from natural PPR editors (Fig.  ). Amino acid position 5 in the E1 motif was excluded from this study as it could be involved in direct RNA recognition. P3A mutation in the E2 motif of RECODE-PG significantly increased its editing efficiency and the accumulation of β-catenin in the cells (Fig.  and  ). Similarly, D13A mutation in the E2 motif of RECODE-WW also led to a significant increase in β-catenin accumulation (Fig.  and  ). Based on these results, E2:P3A and E2:D13A mutations were selected for further development on RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW, respectively (hereafter referred to as RECODE-PG2 and RECODE-WW2).
 To verify that the positive effect of these mutations on β-catenin translation is independent of the length of the PPR domain, we again tested the influence of four PPR domain lengths on the activity of RECODE-PG2 and RECODE-WW2. Both new RECODE variants showed an optimal combination of editing efficiency and β-catenin translation for the 14 P-motif variants (Fig.  – ). Surprisingly, while editing efficiency and β-catenin protein accumulation generally increased with the mutated RECODE variants, the 16 P-motif variants showed only minor improvements. Although off-target editing efficiency increased with on-target efficiency, the 14 P-motif RECODE-PG2 and RECODE-WW2 showed a decrease in the number of off-target sites that were significantly edited differently from the 10 or 12 P-motif variants compared to the RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW variants (Fig.  and  versus Fig.  and  ). Designs with 14 P-motif domains were selected for further development, as they offered the best balance between editing efficiency and β-catenin protein accumulation.
Since natural P proteins, unlike PLS proteins, stabilize mRNA or block enzyme activity [ ], we hypothesized that off-target effects arise from strong PPR domain binding. To reduce binding affinity and consequently off-target effects, we mutated the conserved Lys13 in P-motifs, involved in PPR/RNA complex stability [ ], to serine (K13S) or glutamine (K13Q), predominantly found at position 13 in L1 and P1 motifs, respectively [ ]. To assess the functional impact of these mutations across different regions of the PPR-P domain, we selected RECODE-WW2, the variant that presented the greatest off-target effects. We performed an initial screen of single K13S and K13Q mutants on a few selected PPR motifs, by choosing the seven odd-numbered motifs to facilitate the experimental design and provide coverage across the entire PPR array. The editing efficiency of these mutants was analysed on eight off-target sites in addition to the CTNNB1-T41I on-target site. Single mutations in motifs 11 and 13 showed the most significant decrease in off-target effects without affecting on-target activity (Fig.  ). To further mitigate off-target effects, we then generated RECODE-WW2 mutants combining mutations in these key motifs with mutations in only PPR motifs 5, 7, and 9 to minimize potential disruption of initial N-terminal RNA binding (Fig. ). RECODE-WW2 variants with a K13S mutation in motifs 7 and 13 and a K13Q mutation in motifs 9 and 13 showed the most significant decrease in off-target effects at the selected sites and are hereafter referred to as RECODE-WW2-PPRm1 and RECODE-WW2-PPRm2, respectively.
 To investigate the transcriptome-wide impact of K13S and K13Q mutations on off-target effects, we performed NGS with RECODE-WW2-PPRm1 and RECODE-WW2-PPRm2 variants. Their C-to-U editing activity was compared to RECODE-PG, RECODE-PG2, RECODE-WW and RECODE-WW2 (Fig.  and ). Except RECODE-WW, all RECODE variants had an on-target editing efficiency above 50% (Fig.  ). RECODE-PG variants edited fewer off-target sites than RECODE-WW variants. The majority of the sites edited by RECODE-PG variants were also edited by RECODE-WW variants ( ). Mutations in RECODE-WW2 PPR domain (RECODE-WW2-PPRm1 and RECODE-WW2-PPRm2) reduced off-target effects by over 80%, reaching levels similar to RECODE-PG (Fig.  and ). Surprisingly, these variants showed less sequence conservation upstream of the off-target sites than RECODE-PG variants (Fig.  ). Finally, RECODE-PG, RECODE-WW2-PPRm1, and RECODE-WW2-PPRm2 showed a higher specificity for the C-to-U editing reaction, lower off-target effects and higher editing efficiency than RESCUE-S, a CRISPR-Cas C-to-U editing tool [ ] (Fig.  and  ).
 We then verified that the RECODE variants did not affect overall gene expression by performing a differential gene expression analysis. The expression level of the CTNNB1 target transcript itself was not significantly affected by any of the variants ( ). Only a small number of transcripts showed differential expression following RECODE editing ( ). For most variants, this amounted to <1% of the total edited genes, with the exception of RECODE-WW2, which showed a slightly higher number of affected transcripts (1.6%). The overall transcriptomic impact of the RECODE variants was comparable to or even lower than that of the well-established RESCUE-S editor, with the exception of RECODE-WW2. This suggests that the observed changes are a direct consequence of the RNA editing event and not due to nonspecific effects such as protein stacking, confirming the previous gene expression results in mice.
To further study the potential toxicity of the RECODE variants and assess their temporal stability, we performed a time-course analysis over 24, 48, and 72 h and used RESCUE-S as a control. Cell death, relative to cells transfected with an empty vector, was low but showed a slight increase over time, reaching 7%–11% at 24 h and 12%–25% at 48 h (Fig. ). The 72-h time point showed a further increase in cell death and greater variability (12%–40%) likely due to cell confluency. The editing efficiency at the CTNNB1-T41I site, which was already high after 24 h, plateaued after 48 h (Fig.  ). Following this editing, β-catenin protein accumulation is observed across all variants, with levels normalized to the empty vector control (Fig.  ). This increase in protein, in the absence of any significant differences in total CTNNB1 mRNA expression across the three time points compared to the empty vector control (Fig.  ), confirms that RECODE variants do not affect overall gene expression. Furthermore, at all three time points, RECODE-PG2 and the three RECODE-WW2 variants showed greater accumulation of β-catenin than in cells transfected with RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW, confirming the beneficial effect of these mutations on the translation of the edited RNA (Fig.  ).
 To investigate the relationship between the observed functional changes (increased β-catenin protein accumulation in some variants and reduced off-target editing in others) and the binding affinity of RECODE proteins, we conducted RNA electrophoretic mobility shift assays (REMSA) using the six RECODE variants developed previously ( ) and their target (CTNNB1-T41I). Our results showed that all RECODE variants have a Kd ranging between 1.6 and 6 nM (Fig.  ). The RECODE-PG and RECODE-PG2 variants presented the strongest and weakest affinities, respectively, among the series, suggesting that the E2:P3A mutation results in a slight but measurable decrease in binding affinity. In contrast, the RECODE-WW variants showed very similar Kd values (4.4–5.4 nM), which suggests that the subtle amino acid changes in these variants have no detectable effect on the overall binding affinity to the on-target RNA.
 

## Highly active RECODE with broad sequence recognition
In nature, PPR-editors present specific nucleotide restrictions upstream and downstream of their editing sites [ ]. To characterize the sequence restriction of RECODE-PG, RECODE-PG2, RECODE-WW, and RECODE-WW2, we analysed their editing efficiency on fifteen endogenous targets. These targets were selected to represent a broad diversity of nucleotides at position −6 to + 5 relative to the editing site. Their activity was directly compared to RESCUE-S, for which an optimal gRNA was previously designed for most sites [ ] ( ). RECODE consistently outperformed RESCUE-S, with at least one RECODE variant exceeding 50% editing efficiency at all sites, except PPIB-A19V (Fig.  and ). Only RECODE-WW2 edited both targets with G at position −1, but with low efficiency for PPIB-A19V. This observation aligns with the off-target analysis on CTNNB1-T41I, showing that only 10% of the off-target sites have G at position −1 (Fig. ). Target editing efficiency variations might be attributed to nonoptimal PPR codes or adjacent PPR code combinations. Finally, we confirmed that the stability and the translation of the edited RNA were not affected by the RECODE variants targeting KRAS-D30D and SMARCA4-P88L ( ).
 While no adjacent cytidines were edited ( ), RECODE-WW2 edited other off-target cytidines on the amplicons. To decrease this off-target editing, we designed RECODE-WW2-PPRm1 and RECODE-WW2-PPRm2 variants targeting KRAS-Q25X and SMARCA4-P88L. These variants significantly decreased RECODE-WW2[KRAS-Q25X] and RECODE-WW2[SMARCA-P88L] off-target effects, although RECODE-WW2-PPRm2 [KRAS-Q25X] showed a significant decrease in on-target editing efficiency (Fig.  ). These results demonstrate the ability of RECODE to edit specifically any RNA sequence and confirms that mutations in the PPR domain can modulate the off-target activity.

## Discussion
We developed RECODE, a new gRNA-free C-to-U editing tool effective in mammalian cells and mice. A major concern with editing tools is the risk of off-target effects leading to deleterious mutations. To limit this, we optimized RECODE by focusing on the specificity of the PPR motifs, its architecture, and the stability of the PPR–RNA complex.
Each PPR motif recognizes a nucleotide via a PPR code, where the 5th and last amino acids differentiate purines/pyrimidines and amino/keto nucleotides, respectively [ ]. While the PPR code has been established for the P, P1, and S1 motifs, we confirmed its applicability to the P2 and S2 motifs (Fig. ). However, PG:S2 motif preferentially recognized only purines, suggesting that an amino acid within the S2 motif or adjacent PPR motifs in PG (e.g. position 2) [ , ] may not be optimal for specifically recognizing pyrimidines. The role of the L1 and L2 motifs as spacers or in specific recognition remains unclear [ , ]. While WW:L2 appears to contribute to specific recognition, PG:L2 seems to work as a spacer. This spacer activity might be important for structural flexibility and enhancing target specificity, potentially explaining why PG (RECODE-PG) has fewer off-target editing sites than WW (RECODE-WW). The E1 motif, instead of following the PPR code, seems to select nucleotides through steric hindrance, with for example large basic amino acids at position 5 favouring pyrimidines.
An in vitro bind-n-seq study showed an increasing 3′-end mismatching tolerance in longer P-array proteins [ ]. This increased tolerance might be attributed to the fact that designer P-motifs, unlike those in natural proteins, prioritize nucleotide recognition over optimal motif interactions, potentially causing structural constraints. However, in our study, reducing the number of P-motifs decreased on-target editing efficiency and is therefore not ideal for reducing off-target effects. Instead, mutating Lys13 in two PPR motifs successfully reduced off-target editing (Fig.  ), although without an improvement in specificity (Fig.  ). This likely reflects reduced nucleotide selectivity at the targeted or downstream positions [ , ], making it a potential method for decreasing off-target effects in longer PPR tracks. The choice of substitution pattern and amino acid is crucial, as suggested by the contrasting effects on the editing efficiency of K13Q and K13S mutations in the fifth and ninth PPR motifs (Fig. ) and the significant decrease in the editing activity of RECODE-WW2-PPRm2 on KRAS-Q25X (Fig.  ). While probabilistic studies correlating amino acids with recognized nucleotides have primarily focused on PLS domains, the variability within the P domain has been largely overlooked [ ]. Future research focusing on the P domain and the amino acids involved in nonspecific recognition will facilitate the design of strategies for reducing off-target effects.
The RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW results in mice, showing similar GAPDH protein levels in PBS and RECODE infected mice (Fig.  ), suggest that the translation of the edited RNA is not affected by RECODE. However, these results appear to contradict the β-catenin results in human cells (Fig.  and  ) where no or a slight increase in protein accumulation was detected despite successful RNA editing. The expression of RECODE-PG and RECODE-WW for only 48 h may reduce the potentiality of β-catenin accumulation even without translation inhibition of the edited RNA. This hypothesis is supported by the conservation of KRAS and SMARCA4 protein levels after editing, even though an editing efficiency between 40% and 80% was observed for most of the targets ( ).
The increase in β-catenin protein accumulation observed in RECODE-PG2 and RECODE-WW2 is directly linked to an increased amount of edited RNA, which could be due to either a decrease in target affinity or an increase in the speed of the editing reaction. The REMSA results support the former hypothesis for RECODE-PG2. Structural predictions of RECODE-PG suggest that position 3 in the E2 motif interacts with the upstream E1 motif ( and ). A mutation at this position could alter the position of the E1 and E2 motifs, potentially affecting the affinity to the target and increasing RNA release. On the contrary, position 13 in the E2 motif could interact with the gating domain of the DYW domain, which regulates its active site [ ] ( and ). Modifying this interaction could facilitate the function of the gating domain and accelerate the editing reaction.
Previous attempts to engineer PPR–DYW proteins, while promising, have faced limitations. Early designs, mimicking the natural architecture of the PLS–DYW proteins, required cofactors, restricting their application to plant systems [ ]. Cofactor-independent editing was achieved by replacing the PLS domain with an S domain (containing an S-motif variant) [ ], but this resulted in reduced editing efficiency on exogenous target in bacteria (50%). A more recent strategy, substituting the designer PLS array with a designer P array and incorporating the E1–E2–DYW domain from a natural PPR–DYW protein, improved the editing efficiency in plant organelles [ ]. Because no PPR code was predefined for the P2, L2, and S2 motifs, another team proposed substituting those PPR-like motifs with P motifs. This approach, however, led to a decrease in nucleotide preference at positions −6 to −4 [ ]. Furthermore, incorporating the E1, E2, and DYW domains of a natural PPR–DYW protein in these latest designs introduces a nucleotide preference at positions −2 and −1, limiting the range of targetable sites [ , ], contrary to RECODE (Fig.  ).
Delivering RNA editing tools to specific tissues in vivo is a critical challenge for their development as therapeutic agents. AAV vectors, with various serotypes developed for targeted tissue delivery, are a suitable technology for efficiently delivering target genes with a low risk of genomic integration. However, their limited packaging capacity necessitates strategies like component minimization or dual-vector delivery for larger systems like CRISPR [ – ]. In contrast, RECODE, with its compact size (a single gene of 2.6 kb) can be easily packaged within a single AAV vector, offering a significant advantage. In this study, we demonstrated that RECODE gene (including 16 P-motifs) can be delivered using an AAV9 vector, achieving high editing efficiency in mice. These features, including programmability, high editing efficiency, reduced neighbouring nucleotide preference, gRNA-free design, and compatibility with AAV vectors, establish the RECODE technology as a versatile platform with potential applications beyond cellular systems, such as therapeutic approaches for repairing disease-causing mutations and modifying protein functions to enable novel treatments.
