Cr(VI) exposure impacts millions of people around the world, primarily through inhalation or ingestion. Such exposures are best known for contributing to cancers of the lung, sinus, and nasal passage, damage to kidneys and liver, and contact dermatitis. Growing evidence indicates exposure contributes to neurological conditions, but knowledge gaps persist regarding Cr(VI) neurotoxicity. Human studies report Cr(VI) contributes to autism spectrum disorders, motor neuron disease, olfactory dysfunction, and impaired memory. However, knowledge of specific targets for Cr(VI) in the brain is limited to reports of regional accumulation (greatest effects reported in the rodent hippocampus, hypothalamus, and pituitary gland), while cell-specific effects remain unknown. Caenorhabditis elegans is a useful high-throughput model, frequently used for metals neurotoxicity. Here, we characterize a C. elegans model to address knowledge gaps in Cr(VI) neurotoxicity. All Cr(VI) concentrations tested were sublethal, and we estimate bioaccumulation of Cr in worms was ~1e10−8% of the administered dose after a 24-hour exposure. Cr(VI) initially targeted GABAergic neurons after a 6-hour exposure, while cholinergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic neurons were affected following a 24-hour exposure. Impacts on behaviors were consistent with neurodegeneration. Further, Cr(VI) exacerbated gut autofluorescence in worms, indicative of accelerated biological aging. These data collectively address key knowledge gaps, identifying key neuronal targets and informing potential mechanisms of neurotoxicity.

## Introduction
Chromium (Cr) is a naturally occurring metal highly sought after in industry for its bright colors, hardness, and anticorrosive properties. Demand for Cr is increasing due to its ability to replace rare earth metals in green energy ( ; ). Cr predominantly exists in two valence states – trivalent [Cr(III)] and hexavalent [Cr(VI)]. While Cr(III) is considered relatively innocuous to human health and potentially pharmacological, Cr(VI) is recognized as a potent toxicant and significant health threat ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). The health threats of Cr(VI) are widely recognized and Cr(VI) is currently ranked as the 17th greatest potential chemical threat to human health on the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry’s Substance Priority List ( ). Environmental pollution of Cr(VI) originates from numerous industries and a couple of prominent geogenic sources, leading to widespread contamination and frequent human exposures ( ; ; ; ). Inhalation exposure is often the primary concern of Cr(VI) toxicological assessments, as Cr(VI) is a known human lung carcinogen and reports show more than 220,000 kg of Cr(VI) are released into the atmosphere annually in the U.S.A. ( ). Dermal exposure may occur through occupational exposures in cement workers or contact with contaminated consumer products, water, soil, or particulate matter, but Cr(VI) absorption across the skin is limited ( ). Ingestion, primarily through drinking water, is a primary concern for public health, given Cr (VI) contamination in drinking water is widespread and a recent report identified it as the #1 chemical hazard in U.S. public drinking water ( ; ; ; ; , , ).
Monitoring and reporting of Cr(VI) in drinking water is limited due to the challenges of distinguishing valence states; however, multiple studies report a near 1:1 correlation between total Cr and Cr(VI) in drinking water ( ; ; ). Notably, U.S. drinking water levels are predominantly below the U.S. EPA maximum contaminant level of 0.1 mg/L, with some exceptions. One report found 5.41 mg/L from a well measured in 2014 from Midland, Texas (highest contamination reported in the U.S.A.), while the highest Cr(VI) level with reported human health effects was in Jinzhou, Liaoning, China at 20 mg/L following a chemical spill incident that started in the 1950s ( ; ). Other sites with exceptionally high groundwater Cr(VI) concentrations include contamination from a chromite ore processing plant reported at 3425 mg/L in Glasgow, UK, from chromium plating operations in Spain at 2692 mg/L, from a chromite ore dump in Ranipet, India at 1976 mg/L, from a chromium producing operation with improper sludge disposal in China at 1000 mg/L, and from a deactivated industrial waste landfill in Brazil at 500 mg/L ( ; ; ; ; ). High concentrations of Cr (VI) are found in drinking water around the world, with drinking water sources in the U.S.A., Greece, China, Iran, and Pakistan having the highest reported Cr(VI) concentrations ( ; ; ; ; ).
Cr(VI) toxicity is well-described in many peripheral tissues, with a significant amount of focus on its role as a human lung carcinogen; however, many knowledge gaps remain regarding its neurotoxic effects ( ; ; ; ; ). Human studies suggest Cr(VI) induces neurological disorders in exposed human populations, such as autism spectrum disorder, motor neuron disease, acute schizophrenia, impaired olfactory discrimination, impaired memory, and increased risk of alcoholism ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Cr(VI) neurotoxicity is reported across several taxa (e.g., rats, mice, fish, Drosophila, birds). Animal studies report behavioral deficits, such as locomotor deficits, anxiety, and impaired memory ( ; ; ; ; ; , ). Various animal studies have identified brain regions that accumulate Cr(VI) – such as the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and pituitary gland – but a complete understanding of affected brain regions, cell types, and changes in neurochemistry remain significant knowledge gaps and limit translation to human health ( ; ; ; ). Cell culture studies from multiple groups report Cr(VI) neurotoxicity (e.g., oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis) across multiple models, such as primary mouse cerebellar neurons, rat hypothalamic neurons, and rat pituitary neurons ( ; ; ). While cell culture models are highly useful to assess mechanisms, limitations in identifying specific targets are difficult and measuring functional outputs (e.g., behaviors) is not feasible. Animal models allow for behavioral translation, relevant physiological exposures, and evaluation of regional and cell-specific targets, but techniques to assess these endpoints are labor-intensive due to the possibly hundreds or thousands of distinct targets across the brain. Caenorhabditis elegans provide a useful high-throughput model that can generate data as quickly as cell culture, while also providing key mechanistic insights into neurological targets and linking these to behaviors that can be translated to other species for more focused research.
C. elegans is a nematode species (colloquially referred to as worms) that is 1 mm in length, with an ~21-day lifespan, quick reproduction cycles, short experimental timeframes, and simplicity of maintenance, making them useful for high-throughput research ( ; ; ; ; ; ). Toxicologists have used C. elegans to characterize general and tissue-specific toxicity for hundreds of chemicals – with mechanisms of metals neurotoxicity (e.g., Mn, Ni, Hg, As, Pb, Cd) being a large focus of C. elegans toxicology studies ( ; ; ). The worm nervous system is well-characterized and entirely conserved across populations, meaning all hermaphroditic worms have 302 neurons. Further, the number of neurons for each respective neuronal phenotype (i.e., cholinergic, dopaminergic, GABAergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic) are highly conserved across worms, facilitating analysis of cell-specific neurotoxicity. The worm cuticle and epidermis are transparent, allowing for neurodegeneration to be observed in vivo with commercially available transgenic strains expressing GFP-tagged neurons ( ; ; ). In addition to its great benefits for neurotoxicology studies, C. elegans has provided substantial mechanistic data ( ; ). Worms share 60–80 % gene homology with humans, transgenic strains can be purchased or generated with relative ease, and knockdowns can be generated using siRNA, making them ideal for detailed investigation of mechanisms and allowing them to work within translational frame-works. Adding to their translational potential, many neurobehavioral phenotypes are directly linked to neuronal subtypes, as demonstrated by specific pharmacological inhibitors, genetic mutations, and laser ablation of individual neurons ( ; ; ; ).
Despite the utility for C. elegans as a model to characterize metal toxicity, worms are not frequently used as a model for Cr(VI), and no published studies have directly assessed Cr(VI) neurotoxicity ( ; ; ; ; ). Here, we characterize Cr(VI) neurotoxicity in a C. elegans model, using wildtype worms (Bristol N2) to assess lethality, Cr accumulation, neurobehavioral effects, and accelerated aging, alongside transgenic reporters (LX929, BY200, eat-4::GFP, CZ13799) to assess neuronal targets. Establishing this model will facilitate more detailed mechanistic investigations and identify key neuronal targets for neuro-anatomical analyses in other models.

## Methods
2.1.

## Strain maintenance and handling
C. elegans strains were maintained on NGM agar supplemented with Escherichia coli strain OP50 at 20 °C. Bristol N2 C. elegans were used as wildtype strains for lethality, Cr accumulation, behavior, metallomics, and autofluorescence assays. The following strains express GFP-tagged neuronal phenotypes and were used for neurodegeneration assays: LX929, vsls48 [unc-17::GFP]; BY200, pdat-1::GFP(vtIs1); CZ13799, juls76 [unc-25p::GFP + lin-15(+)] II; and eat-4p::GFP. These strains are GFP reporters for cholinergic, dopaminergic, GABAergic, and glutamatergic neurons, respectively. All strains were gifted by Dr. Michael Aschner’s lab (Department of Molecular Pharmacology, Albert Einstein College of Medicine).

2.2.

## Sodium chromate preparation
Sodium chromate (Na2CrO4) was the representative Cr(VI) compound used in this study. Sodium chromate (98 % purity) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (307831–100 G). Stocks were prepared at 0.5 mg, 10 mg, and 40 mg Na2CrO4/mL in ddiH2O. Stock solutions were stored at −20 °C in 1 mL aliquots and thawed immediately before exposures.

2.3.

## Sodium chromate exposure and lethality assay
Worms were synchronized by treating with sodium hypochlorite and allowed to mature to the L4 stage at 20 °C on NGM agar. L4 worms were collected, pelleted by centrifugation at 2000 RPM for 3 min, and resuspended in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20 at a density of 1000 worms per tube. Exposures were performed on mixed sex populations. Sodium chromate was added from stock solutions to reach the desired concentration (0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L). We performed 6-hour exposures axenically, whereas 24-hour exposures were supplemented with 20 μL E. coli strain OP50 grown in 2XYT media. The volume of 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20 was adjusted to final volumes of 1 mL for all experimental groups. Tubes were placed on a rotator during exposure to ensure constant suspension throughout exposure. At the end of exposure, worms were pelleted by centrifugation at 2000 RPM for 3 min and washed 3x with 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20. Exposure parameters and collection procedure were kept consistent for all endpoints.
Lethality was assessed immediately following exposure; 20–30 worms were transferred to a microscope slide and scored. Worms were determined to be alive if they responded to a harsh touch by a worm pick. Harsh touches were applied for each worm up to three times, approximately 15 s apart, until a response to the stimulus was observed. Scoring was performed in triplicate for each experimental group, within each experiment.

2.4.

## Cr uptake measured by atomic absorption spectrometry
Intra-worm chromium concentrations were measured by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). Worms were exposed to Cr(VI) as previously described, before being washed three times with 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20 to remove trace Cr from the exposure media; 500 worms were then transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Worms were pelleted and sodium chloride was aspirated before samples were frozen at −20 °C until digestion. For analyses, worms were digested in 70 % nitric acid for 3 h at 85 °C. Worms were then diluted in 7 mL ddiH2O and filtered through 32 mm syringe filters with 0.45 μm Supor membranes (Pall Corporation, 4656) into 15 mL tubes.
Internal Cr concentrations were measured using a Perkin Elmer 900Z graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrometer with Syngistix Software. Total Cr in ng was calculated for each sample and divided by 500 to determine Cr load/worm. A calibration standard was performed using Perkin Elmer Cr Standard (#N9300173) before analyzing the samples. Blanks were implemented every 5–16 samples. The Cr detection limit was 0.004 μg/L and all data points were within the range of detection. Three independent experiments were performed.

2.5.

## Metallomics assessment by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry
To assess the effects of Cr(VI) on worm essential metals homeostasis, we performed metallomics analyses by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Worms were exposed to Cr(VI) for 24 h and digested, as described for AAS.
Samples were analyzed on an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS in the Metallomics Core Facility at the University of Louisville. Initial calibration blank (ICB) and initial calibration verification (ICV) standards were run at the start of the analysis, after every 10 samples, and at the end of the analysis. An internal standard was included in all sample runs to account for any matrix effects. Metallomics data are expressed as fold-change relative to control.

2.6.

## Neurodegeneration assay
Worms were exposed to Cr(VI), washed, and collected as described for lethality assays (6 h and 24 h exposures). Worms were then briefly suspended in 50 μL 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20; 5 μL of worm suspension (approximately 100 worms) was transferred onto 4 % agar pads and anesthetized in levamisole hydrochloride at a final concentration of 5 mmol. Worms were imaged at 20x (CZ13799, eat-4::GFP) or 40x magnification (LX929, BY200) using an Olympus BX51 microscope, Olympus mercury burner (BH2-RFL-T3), and Teledyne Lumenera INFINITY ANALYZE software (v.7.1.1.66). GABAergic (CZ13799) and glutamatergic (eat-4::GFP) reporters were imaged at 20x magnification and neuronal morphology was scored across the whole worm. Cholinergic (LX929) and dopaminergic (BY200) reporters were imaged at 40x and neuronal morphology was only scored in the head region, as previously described ( ; , ).
We modified a semi-quantitative neurodegeneration scoring procedure from to assess the relationship between Cr (VI) exposure and progression of neurodegeneration ( ). Neurodegeneration was scored on the following scale: score = 0 for normal morphology, score = 1 if dendrites exhibited kinks, score = 2 if puncta are present along dendrites, score = 3 if dendrites are broken, score = 4 if neuron somas exhibit altered morphology (e.g., blebbing, shrinking, missing). Each worm received a single score to describe the worst stage of degeneration observed (e.g., a worm exhibiting kinks in dendrites and broken dendrites received a score of 3). 10 worms were imaged and analyzed per exposure group for each experiment. Representative images were taken using confocal microscopy. Confocal images were captured with an A1 confocal inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope, at 20x or 40x magnification. Images were captured as Z-stacks (Z-step = 0.5 μm), denoised, and saved as maximum intensity projections using NIS-Elements (v.5.20.02).

2.7.

## Behavior assays
2.7.1.

## Thrashing assay
We assessed thrashing behavior in C. elegans as a measure of generalized neuronal dysfunction ( ). After washing, worms were briefly suspended in 50 μL of 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20. 5 μL of worm suspension was transferred to a 35 mm dish containing NGM agar inoculated with OP50. Worms were then individually transferred to a 1 μL droplet of M9 buffer on a microscope slide. Thrashing behavior was recorded at 2x magnification using an Olympus BX43 microscope and Olympus cellSens Standard software (v.1.18). Videos were blinded and slowed to 0.25x speed for manual scoring. A worm was determined to have “thrashed” if the internal angle of its body reached a threshold of ~140° during a body bend. Cessation of a “thrash” was determined when the internal angle of the worm’s body exceeded the ~140° threshold. 10 worms were scored per exposure group for each experiment.

2.7.2.

## Curling assay
We assessed curling behavior in C. elegans as a measure of dopaminergic function ( ). Videos of worms were recorded as described for thrashing assays. Videos were blinded and slowed to 0.25x speed for manual scoring of time spent curling. A worm was determined to be “curling” if its head and/or tail cross its body. 10 worms were scored per exposure group for each experiment.

2.7.3.

## Levamisole sensitivity assay
We assessed levamisole sensitivity in C. elegans as a measure of GABAergic function ( ). After washing, worms were briefly suspended in 500 μL of 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20; 10 μL of worm suspension was transferred to a 35 mm plate containing NGM agar without OP50. Once the droplet dried, the plate was flooded with 1 mL of 200 μM levamisole hydrochloride and incubated at 20 °C. We assessed paralysis after 1 h. Using a worm pick, up to 3 harsh touch stimuli were applied to worms challenged by levamisole hydrochloride. If worms failed to respond to at least 1 harsh touch stimulus, approximately 15 s apart, they were scored as paralyzed. 20 worms were assessed per exposure group for each experiment.

2.7.4.

## Aldicarb sensitivity assay
We assessed aldicarb sensitivity in C. elegans as a behavior related to cholinergic function ( ). After washing, worms were briefly suspended in 500 μL of 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20. 10 μL of worm suspension was transferred to a 35 mm plate containing NGM agar, supplemented with 1 mmol aldicarb, without OP50. Once the M9 droplets dried, plates were incubated at 20 °C. After 4 h, paralysis was determined as described for the levamisole sensitivity assay.

2.8.

## Autofluorescence assay
After washing, worms were briefly suspended in 50 μL of 85 mmol sodium chloride + 0.001 % Tween-20; 5 μL of worm suspension (approximately 100 worms) was transferred onto 4 % agar pads and anesthetized in levamisole hydrochloride at a final concentration of 5 mmol. We measured autofluorescence after excitation at a 421 nm wavelength. Worms were imaged using an Olympus BX51 microscope, Olympus mercury burner (BH2-RFL-T3), and Teledyne Lumenera INFINITY ANALYZE software (v.7.1.1.66). Autofluorescence region of interest (ROI) analysis was performed using FIJI (v.1.54 f). Briefly, images were imported to FIJI and converted to 8-bit black and white images. ROIs were identified using the “freehand selection” tool and the average intensity for that area was recorded for analyses; 10 worms were imaged and analyzed per exposure group for each experiment.

2.9.

## Statistics
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v.10.0.0). Normality was assessed using an Anderson-Darling test (α = 0.05). Significance was assessed using a Student’s T-test or Mann-Whitney U test, for parametric and non-parametric data respectively (α = 0.05). Significance was assigned for comparisons where p < 0.05.

## Results
3.1.

## Lethality and metallomic analyses
Sodium chromate did not significantly impact worm survival after 6-or 24-hour exposures, even at high concentrations. Survival was reduced to 93 % and 88 % in worms exposed to 500 and 1000 mg/L for 24 h, respectively ( ). We assessed Cr accumulation in C. elegans using AAS and observed extremely limited uptake at both timepoints; however, uptake was dose-dependent ( , ). Cr load per worm increased with exposure, and we estimate ~1e10−9% of the administered concentration accumulated after 6-hour exposure to 1000 mg/L (Cr load = 0.0002 ± 0.0002, 0.007 ± 0.003, 0.011 ± 0.003, 0.035 ± 0.019, and 0.028 ± 0.008 ng/worm for 6-hour exposure to 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L, respectively). Cr uptake was ~1e10−8% of the administered concentration after 24-hour exposure to 1000 mg/L (0.0002 ± 0.0001, 0.040 ± 0.014, 0.050 ± 0.014, 0.091 ± 0.015, and 0.105 ± 0.002 ng/worm for 24-hours exposure to 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L, respectively). Lethality relative to Cr load (ng/worm) is shown in .
We previously reported Cr(VI) induced essential metals dyshomeostasis in the rat brain after a 90-day drinking water exposure ( ). We measured essential metal levels in worms exposed to sodium chromate for 24 h using ICP-MS. Essential metal levels after a 24-hour exposure to sodium chromate were expressed as fold-change relative to control ( ). We did not observe any significant differences in essential metal levels; however, some patterns were apparent. Magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), zinc (Zn), and molybdenum (Mb) exhibited generally decreased levels relative to controls, while iron (Fe) and cobalt (Co) levels were slightly increased; copper (Cu) was the only essential metal apparently unchanged.

3.2.

## Sodium chromate-induced neurodegeneration exhibited specificity for GABAergic, cholinergic, and dopaminergic neurons
After 6 h, sodium chromate did not induce neurodegeneration in cholinergic or glutamatergic neurons ( , ). We observed a slight but non-significant increase in dopaminergic neurodegeneration after exposure to 50, 500, or 1000 mg/L sodium chromate ( ). Sodium chromate induced significant neurodegeneration in GABAergic neurons of C. elegans exposed to 500 mg/L (p = 0.0241), with slight increases in neurodegeneration after exposure to 1000 mg/L (mean score + SEM = 1.1 ± 0.2, 1.4 ± 0.2, 1.0 ± 0.2, 1.8 ± 0.2, and 1.6 ± 0.2 for 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L exposed groups, respectively) ( ).
After 24 h, glutamatergic neurons remained largely unaffected by sodium chromate exposure ( ). Sodium chromate significantly increased neurodegeneration in cholinergic neurons of worms exposed to 500 (p = 0.0208) and 1000 mg/L (p = 0.0024) for 24 h (mean score = 0.4 ± 0.1, 0.6 ± 0.1, 0.5 ± 0.1, 1.1 ± 0.2, and 1.4 ± 0.3 for 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ). In dopaminergic neurons, sodium chromate induced significant neurodegeneration after 24-hour exposure to 5 (p < 0.0001), 500 (p < 0.0001), and 1000 mg/L (p < 0.0001) (mean score = 0.3 ± 0.2, 1.2± 0.2, 0.5 ± 0.1, 1.6 ± 0.2, and 1.5 ± 0.2 for 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ). Sodium chromate induced neurodegeneration in GABAergic neurons after 24-hour exposure to 500 (p < 0.0001) and 1000 mg/L (p = 0.0001) (mean score = 0.5 ± 0.1, 0.5± 0.1, 0.8 ± 0.2, 1.9 ± 0.2, and 1.9 ± 0.3 for 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ). shows representative images for cholinergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic neurons in control worms ( , , ) and worms exposed to 1000 mg/L ( , , ) for 24 h.

3.3.

## Sodium chromate altered behaviors related to neuronal targets
After 24 h of sodium chromate exposure, we assessed thrashing behavior, coiling behavior, levamisole sensitivity, and aldicarb sensitivity in N2 worms. Sodium chromate significantly reduced thrashing behavior in worms exposed to 50 (p = 0.0015), 500 (p < 0.0001), and 1000 mg/L (p < 0.0001) (mean body bends = 128.8 ± 8.7, 116.5 ± 8.2, 88.4 ± 9.3, 38.5 ± 6.8, and 45.5 ± 7.8 for 0, 5, 50, 500, 1000 mg Cr (VI)/L exposed worms, respectively) ( ). Sodium chromate only significantly increased curling behavior, a behavior related to dopaminergic function, after exposure to 1000 mg/L (p = 0.0167) (mean time curling = 3.2 ± 0.7, 2.7 ± 0.7, 2.9 ± 0.6, 4.6 ± 0.8, and 5.9 ± 1.0s for 0, 5, 50, 500, 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ). Aldicarb sensitivity challenges cholinergic function in C. elegans, where decreased aldicarb sensitivity indicates impaired cholinergic function ( ). After 24 h, sodium chromate reduced aldicarb sensitivity at 5 and 50 mg/L (significantly after 50 mg/L, p = 0.0423); however, we observed no effect on sensitivity in worms exposed to 500 and 1000 mg/L (mean percent paralyzed = 86.7 ± 4.4 %, 63.3 ± 9.3 %, 40.0 ± 11.6 %, 88.3 ± 4.4 %, and 86.7 ± 4.4 % for 0, 5, 50, 500, 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ). Levamisole sensitivity challenges GABAergic function in C. elegans, where increased levamisole sensitivity indicates GABAergic dysfunction ( ). Sodium chromate slightly increased levamisole sensitivity after exposure to 5 mg/L, and significantly increased sensitivity after exposure to 50 (p = 0.031), 500 (p = 0.002), and 1000 mg/L (p = 0.003) (mean percent paralyzed = 10.0 ± 5.8 %, 23.3 ± 13.6 %, 38.3 ± 1.7 %, 73.3 ± 6.0 %, and 58.3 ± 7.6 % for 0, 5, 50, 500, 1000 mg/L, respectively) ( ).

3.4.

## Autofluorescence
We assessed worm gut autofluorescence (421 nm) as a measure of biological aging ( ). After 6 h, sodium chromate significantly increased autofluorescence in worms exposed to 50 mg/L (p < 0.0001) ( ). After 24 h, elevated gut autofluorescence was more pronounced, with significant increases after exposure to 50 (p < 0.0001), 500 (p = 0.0001), and 1000 (p = 0.0002) mg/L (relative intensity = 1.0 ± 0.1, 1.0 ± 0.1, 1.5 ± 0.1, 1.6 ± 0.2, 1.4 ± 0.1 for 0, 5, 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L exposed worms, respectively) ( ).

## Discussion
Nervous system effects are poorly characterized in Cr(VI) toxicity, though mounting evidence suggests Cr(VI) is neurotoxic across multiple taxa regardless of exposure route. We previously reported exposure to low concentrations of Cr(VI) in drinking water altered behavior, led to selective Cr accumulation in the hippocampus, and altered essential metal homeostasis in the hippocampus of rats with age- and sex-specific effects ( , , ). The ability to investigate sex differences is an inherent advantage of rodent models over the C. elegans model established here. While not extensively reported in the literature, we have previously shown Cr(VI) neurotoxicity manifests distinctly across sexes in a rat model – a phenomenon that is difficult to study in worms ( , , ). C. elegans are primarily a hermaphroditic species with male offspring produced at a minuscule frequency (<0.002 %), limiting the ability to study sex differences in this model ( ). While sex differences in C. elegans behavior are reported in the literature, manually scored data in this study (i.e., neurodegeneration assays, behavior assays, gut autofluorescence quantification) were exclusive to hermaphroditic worms ( ). Thus, we did not consider sex differences. While we recognize the lack of consideration for sex differences is a limitation of this study, the data generated here provide a foundation for more detailed investigations in rodent studies where sex differences may be studied more readily.
Other groups have assessed Cr(VI) neurotoxicity in rodents, providing evidence that Cr(VI) altered rodent behavior and metal levels in the brain ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). While we previously reported Cr selectively accumulated in the hippocampus after a drinking water exposure, other studies report Cr accumulation primarily in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland following intraperitoneal injections ( ; ; ). Animal studies assessing Cr(VI) neurotoxicity are incredibly useful to the field, as they can describe specific regional and subregional effects across the brain. However, these studies require intensive and costly analyses to determine effects specific to a cell population or neuronal phenotype, as there are hundreds to thousands of rodent brain regions composed of several cell types and mixed neuronal populations. To address the knowledge gap of neuronal vulnerability to Cr(VI), we characterized Cr(VI) neurotoxicity in a C. elegans model.
Data describing Cr(VI) toxicity in C. elegans are scarce, but the literature suggest Cr(VI) can increase ROS production and expression of heat shock proteins, impair metabolism, and decrease cell signaling ( ; ; ). Regarding data specific to Cr(VI) neurotoxicity in C. elegans, the literature are not in agreement and reports are limited to simple behaviors. Wu et al. demonstrated Cr(VI) reduced head thrashing, body bends, turning frequency, and pharyngeal pumping after Cr(VI) exposure (13 μg/L, 10-days exposure) ( ). However, Saikia et al. suggest Cr (VI) did not affect pharyngeal pumping (up to 1 mmol Cr(VI), 5-days exposure) ( ). One study considered a Cr(III) compound: Du and Wang reported GABAergic neurodegeneration after an axenic 24-hour exposure to 75 μM CrCl2 ( ). While altered C. elegans behaviors suggest neurotoxicity in worms and data suggest a vulnerability in GABAergic neurons to Cr(III), our study is the first to describe specific neuronal targets for Cr(VI) in a C. elegans model.
Further, our data support a hypothesis that Cr(VI) exposure induced progressive neurodegeneration, targeting distinct neuronal populations in a temporal manner. While a 6-hour exposure only induced eurodegeneration in GABAergic C. elegans neurons, prolonging exposure to 24 h induced significant neurodegeneration in cholinergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic neurons ( , ). These data strongly suggest Cr(VI) initially targeted GABAergic neurons in a C. elegans model; however, greater investigation is needed to determine if effects in cholinergic and dopaminergic neurons are a direct result of Cr(VI) neurotoxicity or if damage to these neuronal populations is a downstream effect of GABAergic neurodegeneration. Death of GABAergic neurons alters excitation/inhibition (E/I) balances in neurotransmission, an effect reported to contribute to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinsons’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Huntington’s disease ( ; ). Though we did not explicitly address E/I imbalances in this study, we propose significant loss of GABAergic inhibitory neurons after a 6-hour exposure may contribute to overexcitation and neurodegeneration in subsequently affected neuronal populations. Thus, the role of E/I imbalance in Cr(VI) neurotoxicity should be investigated more thoroughly in future publications.
Behavioral data presented here support the neurodegenerative changes in neurons, translating data from neurodegeneration assays to a functional output. Data gathered from thrashing assays suggest general neurotoxicity occurred in worms after 24 h sodium chromate exposure, when we also observed neurodegeneration in three neuronal phenotypes. Sodium chromate-induced changes in curling behavior, aldicarb sensitivity, and levamisole sensitivity demonstrate altered function specific to dopaminergic, cholinergic, and GABAergic neurons, respectively ( ; ; ; ). Impaired thrashing, curling, and levamisole sensitivity reflect neurodegenerative changes we reported for dopaminergic and GABAergic neurons; however, we report a non-monotonic effect in cholinergic-related behavior (aldicarb sensitivity). To explain these contrasting data, we suggest mechanisms for Cr(VI) neurotoxicity change between lower (5 or 50 mg/L) and higher concentrations (500 or 1000 mg/L). The literature suggests resistance to aldicarb does not negate neurotoxicity in cholinergic neurons, but reflects a shift from presynaptic to postsynaptic neurotoxicity ( ). Future investigations will attempt to confirm localization of effects in neurons while considering how mechanisms for Cr(VI) neurotoxicity may shift with concentration.
In our characterization of this model, we also assessed the effects of sodium chromate on gut autofluorescence, a known marker of biological aging in C. elegans ( ). Wu et al. reported significantly increased gut autofluorescence (approximately 1.5x control) after a 10 day exposure to levels as low as 13 μg/L Cr(VI) ( ). Here we suggest Cr(VI) also increases gut autofluorescence in more acute exposures, as 50 mg/L increased autofluorescence after 6 h exposure and 50, 500, and 1000 mg/L increased autofluorescence after 24 h. These data support our hypothesis that Cr(VI) accelerates biological aging in a C. elegans model (i.e. acts as a gerontogen) – similar to some effects we previously reported in rats – and future experiments will interrogate mechanisms by which Cr(VI) may accelerate aging ( , , ). For more information on interactions between chemicals and aging, see publications by Sorrentino et al., Vielee and Wise, and Martin ( ; ; ).
Literature describing Cr(VI) toxicity in C. elegans are somewhat limited, as data are reported with a variety of conditions. The breadth of experimental designs used limits translation between publications, thus we will compare and discuss key differences between published models for Cr(VI) toxicity in C. elegans. While the study we present here is the first to describe specific neuronal targets for Cr(VI) in worms and translate morphological changes to specific behavioral effects, we should first recognize our model is limited in scope. We have not considered a chronic exposure, explored molecular details underpinning a neurotoxic mechanism, or assessed developmental neurotoxicity – which are key considerations made in other studies. Two groups considered Cr(VI) toxicity after chronic exposure to lower concentrations, but concentrations used in these studies likely would have been too low to induce neurotoxicity under our study parameters ( ; ). Other models have assessed Cr(VI) toxicity after 24 h exposures, but data regarding lethality of Cr(VI) appear different from ours ( ; ; ). Saiki et al. reported an LD50 of 158.5 mg/L in their study, while our model suggests Cr(VI)-induced lethality only reached 12 % after a 24 h exposure to 1000 mg/L ( ). Zhu et al. reported 10 mM K2Cr2O7 resulted in 60.8 % lethality, whereas Jeong et al. reported a 24-hour LC10 of 266 μM K2Cr2O7 ( ; ). While these studies suggest greater sensitivity than reported in our study, our 24 h experiments did not apply an axenic exposure, as was done in these studies. Axenic exposures are a critical consideration when comparing models, as starvation may exacerbate toxicity in worms and skew interpretation of data ( ). Fajardo et al. provided a food source for worms during exposure, similar to our model, but provided a much larger food source than our study (0.5 mL vs. 20 μL) and did not assess toxicity of Cr(VI) alone – impairing our ability to extrapolate from this study ( ). Further, the age at the start of exposure is largely inconsistent across studies. Our study exposed L4 worms; whereas Jeong et al. exposed L1 worms to Cr(VI), Wu et al. and Zhu et al. exposed 3 day old adult worms ( ; ; ). Exposing worms to Cr(VI) at an earlier life stage may explain differences in lethality, and this discrepancy makes comparisons and interpretations of data across worm models difficult – ultimately limiting translation to more complex toxicological models ( ).
While there is significant variability within worm models for Cr(VI) toxicity, comparisons can also be made to other metals where effects are more thoroughly established. Dhawan et al. demonstrated greater susceptibility (lethality, behavior) of worms to Al, Zn, Cu, Cd, and Pb, compared to those reported in our study; however, differences of absorption must be considered when extrapolating between metals ( ). The metals studied by Dhawan et al. may exhibit greater absorption in worms than Cr(VI), whereas our data with high Cr (VI) concentrations resulted in miniscule internal Cr loads (worm load = ~1 ×10−9 Cr of applied dose) ( ). Differences between metals and the concentration needed to induce neurotoxicity are also discussed by Cai et al., who described substantially lower concentrations of Mn, Pb, and As induced similar effects in worm neurodegeneration and behavior ( ). Future studies interrogating Cr(VI) neurotoxicity should consider intra-worm Cr concentrations to better extrapolate across studies.
Despite inconsistencies in published models and distinctions between metals, the data clearly indicate Cr(VI) neurotoxicity in C. elegans. We have addressed key knowledge gaps by demonstrating Cr(VI) exhibits specificity for GABAergic, cholinergic, and dopaminergic neurons, altered C. elegans behaviors consistent with specific neuronal function, and accelerated biological aging. Future work will investigate chronic exposures and underlying mechanisms to explain this neuronal specificity.
