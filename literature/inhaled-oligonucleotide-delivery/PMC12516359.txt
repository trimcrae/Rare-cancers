## Objective
Several decades of medical research have shown an intricate and definitive connection between dysfunctional endothelium and cardiovascular disorders, including atherosclerosis and peripheral artery disease (PAD). Initial investigations into endothelial cell (EC) physiology highlighted excretion of protein-based growth factors and their signaling pathways with highly specific targets. However, more recent research has focused on nonprotein metabolic signaling.

## Methods
A narrative review methodology was used. The review involved keyword searches of electronic databases, including Medline and ScienceDirect, conducted in March through October 2022. Review search terms included “endothelial cell metabolism,” “peripheral artery disease metabolism,” “angiogenesis metabolism,” and “endothelial cell metabolic regulation.” The search included primary research articles and subject matter narrative reviews. Abstracts were reviewed for English-language articles published between 2003 and 2022 and supplemented with targeted reference tracing.

## Results
Small-molecular-weight metabolites have been found to regulate key EC functions such as angiogenesis directly. More specifically, they impact EC behavior through control of energy production, de novo biomass synthesis, redox homeostasis, and production of gases like nitric oxide and hydrogen sulfide. Recent investigations targeting these metabolic pathways have yielded preliminary success in correcting undesirable endothelial dysfunction in atherosclerosis and PAD.

## Conclusions
Further investigations into therapeutic targeting of EC metabolism may yield novel approaches for treating PAD.

EC metabolism encompasses many different biochemical processes, including energy production, metabolic generation of gaseous metabolites like NO and H2S, de novo biomass generation of amino acids, lipids, and nucleic acids, regulation of the endothelial glycocalyx, and redox homeostasis ( ). ## Endothelial metabolic regulation
Like many cell types, ECs possess extraordinary plasticity. This allows for specialization based on vessel location, as well as for switching from a baseline metabolically quiescent state to a proliferative state. This switch facilitates expansion from highly oxygenated environments near blood flow toward hypoxic tissue. , In addition, many disease processes such as tumorigenesis hijack and degrade control of EC angiogenesis, leading to dysregulated blood flow and nutrient delivery. , Angiogenic signaling initiates a sprouting process whereby ECs change into two distinct phenotypes known as tip and stalk cells. Tip cells are found at the distal end of the sprouting ECs, extending outward toward hypoxic tissue with filopodial structures, whereas stalk cells remain behind forming lumen. These processes are extremely energy intensive and require an intricate regulation of energy production dependent on both O2 and nutrient availability.

## Bioenergetics
ECs demonstrate an atypical predilection for glycolysis, while maintaining a great deal of plasticity in their bioenergetic profile. While the majority of human cells prioritize adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production through funneling tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates through mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), ECs inefficiently prioritize glucose metabolism through glycolysis (2 ATP vs 36 ATP with OXPHOS) in a manner similar to cancer cells known as the Warburg effect. One main instigator of this may be due to the fact that the total amount of mitochondria found in ECs, although variably dependent on cellular function, is generally less compared with other highly metabolically active tissues. Early work has shown that mitochondria from murine ECs only occupy 2% to 6% of the cytoplasm compared with 32% found in myocytes from murine cardiac tissue. Additionally, murine ECs at the blood-brain barrier have more plentiful mitochondria than murine ECs found in capillary beds. , 
Because of this dependency on glycolysis, much recent research has illuminated how interrupting sugar metabolism can directly disrupt EC metabolism. 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), an activator of PFK in the second rate-limiting reaction of glycolysis, has direct impact on EC metabolic function ( ). PFKFB3 knockout in human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) not only impaired new blood vessel formation, but also reduced EC proliferation and disrupted tip cell migration. In the extreme, glycolysis produces up to 85% of ATP in tip cells. In choroid murine ECs, laser-induced neovascularization resulted in significant enrichment of genes regulating proliferation, glycolysis, glutamine metabolism, and extracellular matrix remodeling by single-cell RNA sequencing, supporting a robust phenotypic metabolic plasticity. This biochemical flexibility is crucial to sustain EC viability in the dynamic vascular environment.
One aspect exemplifying this vascular dynamism is EC adaptability to hypoxia, a crucial adaptation required to signal angiogenesis in poorly perfused tissue. Hypoxic stress upregulates both genes controlling glycolysis as well as glucose transport by increasing glucose transporter 1 expression and translocation to the plasma membrane in HUVECs under a hypoxia-induced depletion of ATP. In addition to glycolysis and glucose transport, glycogen synthesis and glycogenolysis seem to be critical for healthy EC metabolism. In HUVECs exposed to high concentrations of glucose, glycogen synthesis, and glycogen content were increased. Others have found that, when cultured in low glucose media, HUVECs exhaust their glycogen supplies under normoxic conditions while preserving glycogen stores under hypoxic conditions. Small molecule inhibition of glycogen phosphorylase, the catalyzing enzyme for the rate-limiting step in glycogenolysis, was found to also reduce HUVEC migration and proliferation. 
In conclusion, EC prioritization of glucose metabolism over both OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) is a cell-specific adaptation to the uniquely oxygen-dynamic environment of the vasculature. Future investigations into the biochemical intersections between PAD and metabolic comorbidities such as dysregulated glucose metabolism in diabetes mellitus, already highly associated with PAD, could lead to breakthroughs in glycolysis targeted EC therapies.

## Biomass synthesis
Biomass synthesis, that is, the de novo production of amino acids, nucleic acids, and lipids as building blocks for macromolecules such as proteins and DNA, is a crucial aspect of cell biochemistry. Building blocks for these macromolecules are derived from the original carbon sources, including the aforementioned glucose and glutamine. Although ECs do not seem to generate significant energy production through OXPHOS, the mitochondrially located metabolic processes are crucial for production of metabolites used in the aforementioned generation of biomass. This demonstration of variability in mitochondrial number and location in ECs suggests specificity in role and function and justifies a closer examination of mitochondrial enzymes.
Although the previously discussed single-cell RNA sequencing in ECs showed an upregulation of genes related to glycolysis, genes regulating and controlling OXPHOS were also enriched in proliferative ECs. Although glycolysis provides much of the EC cellular energy, as mentioned other biochemical processes are also activated to allow for expansion and proliferation. EC metabolic flexibility extends also to the use of the amino acid glutamine. Glutamine catabolism results in intermediates that act as carbon sources to enter into the TCA cycle, aiding in protein and nucleotide synthesis. However, EC-specific deletion of glutaminase 1 (GLS1), the first enzyme responsible for glutamine catabolism, significantly decreased EC proliferation, but did not impair motility. Loss of GLS1 results in a precipitous decrease in TCA intermediates that cannot be counteracted by the addition of glucose. These intermediates are necessary for cellular biomass accumulation in preparation for division. If a cell is unable to prepare for this process, it will limit the regenerative capacity of ECs when exposed to injury. 
A multifunctional transcription factor and master regulator for mitochondrial biogenesis, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), is expressed in many cell types. However, its protective role in EC health is unclear and has been shown to be deleterious at both high and low levels of expression. In one study using human aortic ECs (HAECs), PGC-1α expression promoted nitric oxide (NO) production through the upregulation of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS). These authors additionally demonstrated that an endothelial-specific eNOS knockout resulted in both hypertension and EC dysfunction. In contrast, transgenic overexpression of endothelial specific PGC-1α in a different mouse model resulted in both reduced EC migration in vitro and reduced vasculogenesis in vivo by inducing Notch signaling and inactivation of Rac/Akt/eNOS. Additionally, an endothelial-specific knockout model showed an increase in wound healing and amelioration of previously seen dysfunction. These two investigations suggest our understanding of PGC-1α regulatory function is incomplete, with both decrease or increase beyond basal expression, resulting in EC dysfunction. Although mitochondria may not be involved in energy production for ECs significantly, they clearly regulate other crucial metabolic processes essential to EC fitness and function.
An additional source of biomass generation in ECs, besides glucose metabolism through glycolysis and feeding TCA intermediates into the electron transport chain for OXPHOS, is FAO. In HUVECs and HAECs, silencing of both long-chain acyl-coenzyme A (CoA) dehydrogenase, an enzyme that catalyzes the first step of mitochondrial beta-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids, and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A, a rate-limiting enzyme of FAO diminished de novo nucleotide synthesis and reduced pathological angiogenesis in a mouse model of retinopathy of prematurity. In other experiments exploring the roles of mitochondria in ECs, ablation of Q-cytochrome c oxidoreductase, commonly known as complex III of the electron transport chain, resulted in a significant increase in the NAD+/NADH ratio concomitant with a decrease in nearly all amino acids in HUVECs. Increased NAD+ production in PAD patients treated with nicotinamide riboside exhibited increased mobility. Patient improvement could be traced to decreased oxidative stress and improved mitochondrial function. This evidence suggests that a primary role of mitochondria in ECs involves the de novo generation of both amino acids and nucleic acids. Highly regulated control of generating new amino acids, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids is crucial for ECs to ensure either a quiescent or angiogenic state. Future investigations into pathways regulating biomass synthesis will be crucial in developing novel drug therapies for improving perfusion in PAD patients.

## Redox homeostasis
One of the more unique aspects of EC physiology originates from their unique location as a barrier between circulating blood and the surrounding tissue, saturated with hundreds of distinct proteins and high concentrations of O2. , , Because of this, ECs have developed potent mechanisms related to metabolic processes to both alleviate oxidative stress by prioritizing glycolytic ATP production while still using reactive oxygen species (ROS) for cell signaling processes. This results in minimizing ROS owing to the 1% to 2% of electrons that leak from the mitochondrial electron transport resulting in peroxide, superoxide, and hydroxyl radicals. 
Beyond this, FAO seems to regulate and focus on redox homeostasis. For both in vitro HUVECs and an in vivo mouse model, quiescent ECs upregulate FAO almost 300% to sustain anaplerotic feeding of the intermediates citrate and malate to the TCA cycle to sustain NADPH concentrations. NADPH is a crucial cofactor for glutathione reductase, which reduces oxidized glutathione to reduced glutathione, ensuring that a large millimolar pool of reduced glutathione is available for ROS scavenging. , This work also showed quiescent ECs upregulating other genes for NADPH production including NADP synthesis and the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), as well as upregulation of NADPH-dependent enzymes like prostaglandin G/H synthase 1 and glutaredoxin, both redox-sensitive and ROS signaling proteins with peroxidase and cyclo-oxygenase activity. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A, the first rate-limiting step of FAO, has been found to be crucial for healthy EC function through the maintenance of endothelium permeability via regulation of intracellular calcium trafficking. , A common reduction mechanism of NADP+ back to NADPH is through isocitrate dehydrogenase 2, which catalyzes decarboxylation of isocitrate to 2-oxoglutarate in the third step of the TCA cycle. In a HUVEC small interfering RNA knockdown model, a decrease in isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 expression also led to a decrease in complexes I, II, and IV of the electron transport chain, as well as reduced O2 consumption. This illustrates that ECs primarily use FAO for redox homeostasis compared with other cells, such as cardiac muscle cells, that use FAO generating the majority of their ATP.
Recent research has demonstrated in HUVECs and HAECs experiencing inhibition of glycolysis induced angiogenesis through shunting metabolites into the PPP. Shunting results in increased expression PPP related enzymes including glucose 6 phosphate dehydrogenase and phosphogluconate dehydrogenase. Increased activity of these enzymes leads to elevated levels of NADPH, which reduces ROS levels and improves EC function. Further, it was demonstrated in a mouse model of PAD exposed to activator of PPP shunting microRNA (miR) that miR-93 exhibited increased perfusion. miR-93 exposure improved vascular endothelial function and decreased permeability. 
One of the most common mechanisms for ROS amelioration in cells is scavenging through thiol functional groups. These include glutathione or a catalytic transition metal center like the Zn/Cu complex in superoxide dismutase 1. , In ECs, these ROS scavenging processes are crucial. Both glutamine starvation and inhibition of GLS1 impair glutathione synthesis leading to increased ROS levels with impaired vessel sprouting and a reduction in EC proliferation. This effect is driven by the loss of glutamine-derived nitrogen necessary for asparagine synthesis. Generation of asparagine jump started protein synthesis and restarted cell-cycle progression. When glutathione biosynthesis is interrupted through the deletion of endothelial-specific glutamate-cysteine ligase, basal and stimulated concentrations of ROS were increased, followed by the impairment of endothelium-dependent vasodilation and increased fibrosis. In an aerobic exercise model in sedentary human males, isolated human microvascular ECs had a 2.4-fold increase in both superoxide dismutase 1 and glutathione peroxidase levels with concomitant decreases in ROS emission from ECs. Additionally, glutathione peroxidase deficiencies in microvascular ECs increased intracellular adhesion molecule-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, known inducers of atherosclerosis by monocyte accumulation. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of thiol functional groups in moderating ROS in ECs.

## NO
One of the most important functions of ECs in vivo is the production of NO to signal nearby smooth muscle cells to relax resulting in vasodilation. Historically, this was a well-described process involving a family of NOSs catalyzing the production of citrulline and NO from L-arginine. NO then diffuses from ECs to smooth muscle cells to stimulate guanylyl cyclase activity. Recent evidence suggests more complex chemistry involving other enzymatic and nonenzymatic mechanisms for reduction of nitrates into nitrites and subsequently nitrites into NO. Nevertheless, disruptions in NO metabolism can lead to endothelial dysfunction with loss of UbiA prenyltransferase domain-containing protein 1, a prenyltransferase that produces coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), leading to the modulation of eNOS activity. Additionally, factors outside growth factor signaling, such as wall shear stress-induced activation of ECs, lead to increases in eNOS expression and NO production ( ). Manipulation of cofactors for eNOS activity can also lead to endothelial dysfunction in NO production, with tetrahydrobiopterin oxidation, leading to a simultaneous increase in ROS production and decrease in NO production. Finally, the necessity of NO production within ECs may suggest a reason for a lower reliance on mitochondrial respiration, owing to eNOS competing for cellular oxygen with cytochrome C oxidase, the terminal point of the electron transport chain. Derivatives of NO have been shown to block cytochrome c activity, and in some cases result in the release of cytochrome C from the mitochondria. These outcomes impair respiratory chain function and reduce mitochondrial energy production. , , This likely contributes to the low mitochondrial respiration evident in ECs. , Together, these findings demonstrate EC regulation of NO production is tightly controlled to regulate vasodilation strictly. 

## Hydrogen sulfide and reactive sulfur species
Although NO’s physiological effect has been studied extensively and was even awarded the 1998 Nobel Prize in Medicine, alternative gasotransmitters such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S) are still being characterized. H2S has been deemed a gasotransmitter like NO owing to its small-molecular-weight gas-like form, lipophilic permeability to biological membranes, both enzymatic and nonenzymatic endogenous production, and involvement in physiological function. Several enzymes such as cystathionine-β-synthase, cystathionine-γ-lyase (CSE), and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfotransferase have been found to generate H2S through several different mechanisms depending on tissue and intracellular location. H2S restriction owing to amino acid depletion has induced CSE to potentiate angiogenesis in ECs. H2S production by CSE inhibits complex IV reducing ATP production through OXPHOS. Further CSE can augment vascular endothelial growth factor signaling and increasing glucose use through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) compensatory glycolysis. , Recent work has also demonstrated that the integration of H2S into a metabolic framework directly through mitochondrially located sulfide quinone oxidoreductase. This enzyme is located near complex II of the electron transport chain and transfers electrons from H2S to CoQ forming persulfides (H2S2) and reduced CoQ, implicating H2S as an inorganic substrate for OXPHOS in humans. , 
These persulfides and other sulfane sulfur metabolites like polysulfides (H2Sn, n = 3-8), cysteine persulfides/polysulfides (Cys-SSH, Cys-SSnH), glutathione persulfides/polysulfides (GSSH, GSSnH), and sulfenic/sulfoxylic acids (HSOH/HOSOH) have been charactered as reactive sulfur species (RSS) owing to their increased reactivity and interconversion compared with H2S ( ). In addition to the previously described sulfide-to-persulfide mitochondrial electron flux being integrated into a central metabolic pathway, small thiol metabolites are hypothesized to be responsible for post-translational modifications to cysteine residues named persulfidation. Similar to phosphate modulation of serine and threonine resides in the well-known process of phosphorylation, cysteine persulfidation can change the conformation of a protein, thus modifying its functional activity. 
Exogenous application of sulfides, sulfide donors, and sulfane prodrugs have provided much of the evidence related to studying H2S metabolism. Systemic NaHS reduced rat mean arterial pressure, and exogenous administration to HUVECs reduced EC dysfunction through activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated PPARδ and eNOS, resulting in an increase in NO production. Rat inhalation of 80 ppm H2S in a cardiac arrest inducement model had a reduction in blood-brain barrier permeability, decreased brain edema, and improved neurological function. In mouse primary brain microvascular ECs exogenously added sulforaphane, a proposed H2S/RSS prodrug from broccoli sprouts, upregulated >50 genes related to glycolysis, OXPHOS, amino acid metabolism, and mitochondrial biogenesis. In contrast with H2S inhalation, another purported H2S/RSS donor diallyl-trisulfide, a plant metabolite from garlic extract, paradoxically increased EC permeability in mouse aortic ECs, whereas CSEKO and CSE small interfering RNA restored EC barrier functionality. Finally, in in vitro studies in both aortic and coronary ECs, H2S and its oxidized metabolites were found to be extruded extracellularly in concentrations exponentially smaller than corresponding aortic/coronary smooth muscle cells, drawing questions about extracellular cross-reactivity between RSS and NO involved in paracrine signaling. Clearly, our understanding of the fundamental connection between simple H2S administration and complex mixtures of RSS from prodrugs is lacking, and future metabolomic work will help to clarify this relationship.
As previously discussed, the canonical mechanism of action of H2S/RSS signaling is through post-translational modification of cysteine residues. Although this is a new field with many unanswered questions and difficulties related to method development, recent research has shown an importance in EC metabolic regulation through cysteine persulfidation. In some of the first work to examine global cysteine regulation in ECs, datasets were established from three different groups of ECs, including human ECs isolated from plaque-free mesenteric arteries vs plaque-containing carotid arteries, HUVECs in vitro kept under static conditions or fluid shear stress mimicking atherosclerosis conditions, and HUVECs exposed to shear stress with and without sulfane donor SG-1002. Overall, >1500 different proteins had differential persulfidation status change in atherosclerotic-like shear stress conditions with the integrin family, transmembrane receptors that facilitate cell-to-cell communication, being the most affected. Additionally, the exogenous application of sulfane sulfur in atherosclerotic human and mice through SG-1002 ameliorated EC dysfunction. At this time, our understanding of the axis between small thiol metabolism in ECs and cysteine modification is a new field with many hurdles, but future investigations will slowly define how these two systems intersect.

## EC pertubations in atherosclerosis and PAD
Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease in which lipid-driven plaques accumulate in blood vessels causing narrowing and deleterious reductions in blood flow. EC dysfunction is a central perpetrator of PAD and a clinical entity resulting from diseased arteries in the legs. PAD symptoms range from intermittent pain and cramping to severe lack of blood circulation requiring limb amputation. Along with a decrease in blood flow and obvious hypoxia-related pathophysiology, recent evidence also suggests that disturbance to normal laminar blood flow induces metabolic reprogramming in ECs and increases their dysfunction and inability to regulate normal vascular function. With atherosclerotic lesions preferentially forming at arterial branches, an understanding of EC metabolism on exposure to variable hemodynamic forces is crucial to design in vitro experimental conditions to mimic in vivo conditions more closely. 
Examination of HUVECs and mouse aortic ECs under dynamic fluid conditions has begun to unravel how cells adapt and change vs traditionally static in vitro conditions. In HUVECs, mouse aortic ECs, and in a mouse model exposed to disturbed flow dynamics, it was shown that protein kinase adenosine monophosphate-activated/AMPKs exerted an atherosclerotic-protective effect through regulation of glycolysis. In a partial carotid ligation mouse model, ECs activated by disturbed flow increased DNA methyltransferase-dependent hypermethylation of Kruppel-like factor 3 and homeobox A5, both genes regulating cyclic AMP response elements ( ). Beyond changes in metabolic profiles, ECs from carotid arteries in a partial carotid ligation model examined using single-cell RNA sequencing showed ECs transitioning into mesenchymal cells, hematopoietic stem cells, endothelial stem/progenitor cells, and immune cell-like phenotypes, as well as shifting from quiescent and antiatherogenic to proinflammatory and proatherogenic phenotypes. 
Owing to the high variability of blood flow and solute composition of vasculature, EC cell-to-cell communication is highly regulated. The glycocalyx is an extracellular polysaccharide layer of heparan sulfate, hyaluronan, and chondroitin sulfate, as well as many other glycoproteins, glycolipids, and proteoglycans. These coat the EC plasma membranes and regulate many functions, including endothelial permeability, shear stress sensing, and lipid migration. In vivo and in vitro models show Krüppel-like factor 2 regulation of hyaluronan expression changes depending on shearing condition by shuttling glucose intermediates into other biosynthesis pathways. Additionally, impaired sugar metabolism owing to hyperglycemia, a major factor in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, has been shown to deteriorate the endothelial glycocalyx. Owing to the many connections between the macromolecules composing the endothelial glycocalyx and the necessary amino acid, sugar, and lipid metabolic pathways required to synthesize these components, metabolite-specific interventions in glycocalyx physiology may yield atherosclerosis-targeted therapeutics. 
Atherosclerosis and PAD are associated with several metabolically relevant enzymes. Work examining inhibition of peptidylarginine deiminase 4 showed amelioration of EC dysfunction and restoration of endothelium-dependent vasodilation through decreased neutrophil extracellular traps in an obesity-induced EC dysfunction mouse model. Other highly conserved metabolic pathways like autophagy, a set of cellular pathways prioritizing catabolism and recycling of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, have been shown to stimulate angiogenesis in a mouse hindlimb ischemia model. The current evidence of dysfunctional EC metabolism in PAD and atherosclerosis is limited with substantial possibilities for advancement. Further investigations of disrupted lipid, nucleic acid, amino acid, and sugar metabolism in dysfunctional ECs will give clinicians and scientists new strategies for these disease processes with limited nonsurgical treatment options, as well as an expected future increase in the number of patients in both developed and developing nations.

## Therapies targeting EC metabolism
One of the most common metabolic interventions for high cholesterol and the prevention of cardiovascular disease are 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl (HMG)-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins), which work to target cholesterol synthesis, with total drug sales surpassing US$1 trillion. Although the canonical statin mechanism of action is attributed to a decrease in plasma lipids, recent work has explored how the targeting of cholesterol metabolism through the interruption of cholesterol synthesis in ECs may be partly responsible for the improvements in cardiovascular health seen from statin use. For example, recent work has shown that atorvastatin treatment in HUVECs and rabbits decreased Dickkopf-1, a regulator of the WnT pathway, owing to an interruption of nonsteroidal isoprenoid intermediates normally sourced from HMG-CoA activity. Other investigators have found treatment of HUVECs with simvastatin affected their immunoregulatory phenotype by downregulating proinflammatory genes while also upregulating immunoregulatory genes. This success showcases a unique strategy of reexamining current therapeutics for metabolic interactions in lipid, amino acids, or nucleic acid biochemistry. Statin treatment in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived ECs in diabetic conditions, a common comorbidity to PAD, was able to reduce EndMT. It was determined that this occurred by inhibition of YAP/TEAD and improved secretion of NO, improving EC function through statins. 
Another strategy to intervene in metabolism is the targeting of enzymes that share metabolites crucial to EC function. For example, the inhibition of arginase, a competitor with eNOS for L-arginine that has been found to be upregulated in atherosclerosis and PAD, has been shown to reduce EC dysfunction. These types of therapies present opportunities for the generation of new drugs and intellectual property while simultaneously improving patient health. In addition to drug administration, the previously discussed mitochondrial regulator PGC-1α regulates both NO production and antioxidant defense and improves EC health through PGC-1αs, known upregulation by both calorie restriction and exercise. 
Although the discovered unanticipated targets of statins suggest a possible repurposing toward other therapeutic uses, they also highlight an important caveat underlying a strategy of intervening in metabolism: the reality of the hyper-interconnectivity of metabolic pathways. As established, NO production through L-arginine catabolism by eNOS is a crucial function of ECs in the regulation of vascular homeostasis. Therefore, one might rationally expect that feeding more L-arginine into a system would result in greater NO production and a reduction in atherosclerotic symptoms. However, ample work has shown that L-arginine supplementation in a variety of models does not improve vascular function. , , For example, in a mouse model L-arginine supplementation failed to reduce arterial lesions; conversely, increased lipid oxidation actually led to more lesion formation. Although counterintuitive at first, an understanding of the connected nature of metabolite pathways and which pathways specifically use L-arginine can provide possible explanations. L-arginine can react with L-glycine to produce guanidinoacetate through the enzyme L-arginine:glycine amidinotransferase. S-adenosyl-l-methionine:guanidinoacetate N-methyltransferase can then methylate guanidinoacetate to form creatine and S-adenosylhomocysteine. Finally, S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine hydrolase can convert S-adenosylhomocysteine into homocysteine. Thus, simple administration of L-arginine for the purpose of boosting NO production may inadvertently lead to an increase in concentrations of both creatine and L-homocysteine, the latter of which is well-connected to cardiovascular disease ( ). Additionally, methylation demands of the trans-sulfuration pathway of recycling homocysteine back into methionine may be starved of labile methyl cofactors because creatine synthesis accounts for ≤70% of methylation use, resulting in both an increase in homocysteine from L-arginine catabolism as well as an inability to recycle excess homocysteine back into methionine. This cautionary tale of Le Chatelier's principle where reaction equilibria shift unexpectantly should be a central concern and rigorously tested while developing strategies that target metabolic pathways involved in atherosclerosis and PAD. 

## Conclusions
EC dysfunction is a crucial step in disease progression for many cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis and PAD. Although early research focused on protein growth factor regulation of EC physiology, unique aspects of EC metabolism have generated significant interest recently. Recent work exploring the unique aspects of EC metabolism ranging from glycolysis, glutamine metabolism, TCA cycle/OXPHOS, and redox homeostasis to complex NO/H2S/RSS biochemistry have yielded many different avenues of generating new therapies. A combination of changing food habits and a decrease in daily activity in developing countries are expected to continue the current trend of increased rates of obesity, diabetes, atherosclerosis, and PAD. Generation of new therapies will be crucial to counter this expected increase in disease, and targeting of metabolic pathways in ECs should provide new insight and strategies.
