The aim of the present study was to identify the genes or signaling pathways involved in the neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity of 1-bomopropane in two inbred strains of rats. F344 rats and WNA/NUM rats were exposed to 1-bromopropane or filtered air by inhalation for either a single 8-hour session, or 8 h daily for 4 weeks. Motor nerve conduction velocity and distal latency were measured in the tail. At the end of the experiment, the animals were decapitated, and the hippocampus, liver, and blood were collected. Exposure to 1-bromopropane for 4 weeks increased distal latency in the tail nerve significantly in F344 and marginally in WNA/NUM. It also increased total and direct bilirubin in both rat strains. Eight-hour exposure increased metallothionein 2A, metallothionein 1 and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 expression in the liver of both rat strains, whereas 4-week exposure upregulated glutathione S-transferase alpha 3 and CD36 molecule in the liver of both strains. KEGG analysis showed that 8-hr 1-bromopropane upregulated pathways of apoptosis, NOD-like receptor signaling and colorectal cancer, in both the hippocampus and liver, whereas 4-week exposure upregulated NOD-like receptor signaling pathway both in the hippocampus and liver of the two rat strains. Insulin signaling pathway was upregulated persistently in the hippocampus of the two rat strains. Our results suggest the involvement of immune system/inflammation-related pathway in the neuro- and hepato-toxicity of 1-bromopropane and the involvement of insulin signaling pathway in the neurotoxicity of 1-brromopropane.

## Introduction
1-Bromopropane was introduced into workplaces as part of global initiatives aimed at reducing the use of solvents that contribute to ozone depletion or global warming. However, experimental and epidemiological studies subsequently identified the neurotoxicity of 1-bromopropane in both humans and experimental animals. These studies confirmed that exposure to 1-bromopropane induced degeneration of peripheral nerves in human and rodents , , while human cases suggested that 1-bromopropane-induced depressive mood and cognitive dysfunction , . Furthermore, experimental studies showed that inhalation of 1-bromopropane induced degeneration of noradrenergic axons and microglial activation in the brain of rats and necrosis in the liver of mice , . However, the molecular mechanisms of 1-bromopropane-induced neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity remain elusive.
The aim of the present study was to identify the genes or signaling pathways involved in the neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity of 1-bomopropane using two strains of rats. We envisage that identification of such genes and molecular pathways can help generate a hypothesis on the molecular underlying mechanism of 1-bromopropane toxicity. Two inbred strains were used with the objective of identifying genes and signaling pathways commonly affected beyond individual genetic differences. In addition, this approach could be extended in future studies by crossbreeding the two strains to determine genes associated with susceptibility.

## Materials and methods

## Experimental protocol

## Eight hour-single exposure
Six Wistar Nagoya/NUMs (WNA, 5-week-old specific pathogen free) rats were obtained from the Division for Research of Laboratory Animals, Center for Research of Laboratory Animals and Medical Research engineering, Nagoya University School of Medicine. This inbred strain of rats was established by Prof. Kitoh through sister-brother mating from Wistar rats maintained at Nagoya University without special selection. The WNA/NUM rats used in the present study were the 59’s generation offspring. Age-matched F344/NSlc rats were obtained from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). Differently from mice, the number of inbred rat strains is limited. Among them, F344/N is the most well-known and is frequently used in carcinogenicity studies, whereas other inbred strains are not commonly available. In the present study, WNN/NUM rats were used as a counter strain. All rats were housed in stainless steel cages and acclimated to the new laboratory environment for five weeks and subsequently divided into two groups of WNAs each and two groups of F344s each, before the start of exposure to 1-bromopropane. Rats of one group of WNAs and one group of F344s were exposed to a single dose 1000 ppm of 1-bromopropane for eight hours while housed in a stainless-steel chamber. Rats of the other groups of eight WNAs and eight F344s were exposed to fresh air only while housed in the same type of chamber (Fig. ). A case report of human 1-bromopropane intoxication estimated an exposure level of 553 ppm as the mean time-weighted average . The ambient concentration of 1- bromopropane measured in front of a washing tank containing the solvent was approximately 1500 ppm. Therefore, the exposure concentration of 1000 ppm used in the present study reasonably represents the levels that can occur in poorly controlled workplaces associated with reported cases of 1-bromopropane-induced neurotoxicity. Furthermore, previous studies showed exposure to 1- bromopropane at 1,000 ppm for 4 or 6 weeks did not induce serious debilitation – . 

## Four-week exposure
Fifteen 6-week-old WNA rats and the same number of age-matched F344/NSlc rats were obtained from the same sources described above. After acclimation, WNAs and F344/NSlcs were exposed to 1-bromopropane or fresh filtered air for eight hours per day (from 13:00 to 21:00), seven days a week for 28 consecutive days while housed in the above-mentioned inhalation chambers (Fig. ).
The inhalation system used in the present study was described previously in detail , . In brief, a regulated volume of the solvent was evaporated and mixed with a larger volume of clean air to achieve the desired concentration. Vapor concentration was measured every 10 s by gas chromatography and controlled digitally to within ±5% of the target concentration. Rectification boards containing numerous small holes were placed at the chamber inlet for uniform distribution of the vapor. Preliminary studies confirmed that the concentrations of 1-bromopropane at nine points within the chambers were approximately ±3% of the value at the central point. 1-Bromopropane (99.81% purity in the chart area of a capillary gas chromatograph with flame ionization detector [FID]) was kindly supplied by Tosho Co. (Tokyo, Japan). The structure of 1-bromopropane was confirmed with proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). 1-bromopropane used throughout the experiment was from the same lot. Repeated gas chromatographic analyses showed no change in the composition of 1-bromopropane in storage bottle or its vapor in the chambers throughout experiments. The environment was maintained on a 16 h light-8h dark cycle (lights on at 0600 h and off at 2200 h) and was held at 23–25ºC and 57–60% humidity. Food and water were provided ad libitum from 22:00 to 13:00, although food was removed from the beginning of exposure to one hour after the end of exposure, from 13:00 to 22:00 to avoid contamination of food with 1-bromopropane vapor.
All rats received the standard of care in accordance with Japanese law concerning the protection and control of experimental animals and the “Guide of Animal Experimentation,” by Nagoya University School of Medicine. The protocol and experimental design of the study were approved by the animal experimentation committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (Experiment approval number 25398). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).
The measured gas concentration of 1-bromopropane was 997.03±21.52 ppm (mean±SD) for the single eight-hour experiment and 1000.65±24.41 ppm (mean±SD) for 4-week experiment.

## Tail nerve electrophysiological measurements
In the 28-day experiment, motor nerve conduction velocity (MCV) and distal latency (DL) were measured in the tail nerve just before the beginning of exposure and at 2 and 4 weeks after the start of the exposure, using the methods described by Ono et al . and Takeuchi et al .. During these measurements, the rat was gently wrapped in a soft towel to keep it semi-immobilized without anesthesia. Electrode A was placed 3 cm distal from the anus, electrode C was 3–4 cm proximal from the tail end, and electrode B was 5 cm proximal to electrode C. After insertion of the electrodes, the tail was immersed in liquid paraffin kept at 37.0°C using a thermostat. MCV and DL were measured after at least 4 minute immersion, and completed within 20 minutes of immersion. The tail nerve was stimulated at electrode A or B and the waves were at electrode C. Electric stimulation and recording were performed with a Neuropack evoked potential/electromyogram measurement system (model MEB-9102; Nihon Kohden, Co., Tokyo).

## Tissue preparation
The rats were euthanized without anesthesia by instant decapitation using a guillotine, performed by a trained experimenter, at the end of both the single 8-hour exposure and 4-week exposure experiments. This method was chosen because anesthetic agents have been reported to alter mRNA expression in the brain . Moreover, a previous study demonstrated that consciousness vanishes within seconds after decapitation, indicating that this is a rapid and humane method of euthanasia . In the single 8-hour exposure experiment, the rats were euthanized between 16 hr and 18 hr after the termination of exposure. The hippocampus and liver were collected immediately, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80ºC until use. In the 4-week exposure experiment, body weight was measured between 12:00 AM and 1:00 PM, once a week. The rats were euthanized 16-18h after the termination of the last exposure, and the hippocampus and liver were removed and placed in liquid nitrogen before storage at −80ºC.

## Preparation of RNA and microarray designs
Tissue total RNA was extracted using RNase mini kit (Qiagen Inc, MD) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The purity of the total RNA concentration was assessed by the A260/280 ratio using a spectrophotometer (ND-1000, NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE). The integrity of the 28 s and 18 s bands was checked by electrophoresis of 1 µg of total RNA in 1.2% agarose gel.

## Microarray analysis
Microarray analysis was performed to identify genes with expression levels affected by 1-bromopropane exposure. For this purpose, the brain and liver RNA samples were labeled, linearly amplified by Low Input Quick Amp Labeling Kit and RNA Spike-In Kit, then analyzed with oligonucleotide microarray (Whole Rat Genome Microarray Kit 22 K, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA). Signal intensity was measured with laser confocal scanner and Feature Extraction software (Agilent Technologies Inc.). The brain and liver samples of the following pairs were compared: 1) 1-bromopropane-exposed F344/NSlc (n=3) and fresh air-exposed F344/NSlc (n=3), 2) 1-bromopropane-exposed WNA/NUM (n=3) and fresh air-exposed WNA/NUM (n=3)in the single 8-hour experiment, and 3) 1-bromopropane-exposed F344 (n=3) and fresh air-exposed F344 (n=3), 4) 1-bromopropane-exposed WNA (n=3)and fresh air-exposed WNA (n=3) in the 4-week exposure experiment. All datasets were uploaded on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE301010).

## Bioinformatics analysis
Gene expression profiling was conducted on 24 samples using R (v3.5.1) and the corresponding version of Bioconductor. Raw microarray data were normalized using the two-color Loess method via the affycoretools package, with an offset of 30. Probe-level values were aggregated by gene symbol. Batch effect correction and differential gene expression analysis were conducted using the limma package, in which Bayesian t-tests were applied with thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1.0. Functional enrichment analyses for KEGG pathways were performed using adjusted p-values based on z-scores by Stouffer’s method .

## Results

## Effect of 1-bromopropane on body weight and electrophysiological parameters
Exposure to 1-bromopropane for 2 and 4 weeks significantly decreased body weight in both F344/NSlc and WNA/NUM rats. Exposure to 1-bromopropane for 4 weeks significantly prolonged the distal latency in F344/NSlc rats and marginally (p=0.11) in WNA/NUM rats (Table ). 

## Effects of 4-week exposure to 1-bromopropane on liver weight and blood biochemistry
Exposure to 1-bromopropane at 1,000 ppm for four weeks significantly increased total and direct bilirubin levels in both strains (Table ). In WNN/NUM rats, liver weight and total protein were significantly increased, whereas total cholesterol and ALT levels were significantly decreased. 

## Effects of 1-bromopropane on gene expression in the liver
Neither 8 hour nor 4 weeks exposure to 1-bromopropane altered the expression of the same genes in the hippocampus in both rat strains (Table ). On the other hand, the single exposure to 1-bromopropane for eight hours resulted in upregulation of Mt2A, Mt1 and NqO1 and downregulation of Serpina3c, Serpina1, Bhmt, Ahsg, Scp2, Gc, Pck1 and Cyp2d2 in the liver of both rat strains (Table ). Furthermore, Table lists the top 10 genes whose expression in the liver was altered in both rat strains exposed to 1-bromoproane for 4 weeks. Interestingly, upregulation of both Gsta3 and Cd36 in the liver was noted in both strains exposed to 1-bromopropane for 4 weeks. 

## 1-bromopropane induces upregulation of immune system or inflammation-related KEGG pathway in the hippocampus and liver
Figures - and Supplementary Tables 1–4 identify the pathways in the hippocampus or liver of both rat strains that were significantly upregulated or downregulated in the 8-hour and 4-week exposure groups. 
The single exposure to 1-bromopropane for 8 hours upregulated various immune system and inflammation-related pathways, including chemokine signaling, antigen processing and presentation, RIG-I-like receptor signaling, natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, T cell receptor signaling, B cell receptor signaling, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis in the hippocampus, and TGF-beta signaling, toll-like receptor signaling, cytosolic DNA-sensing signaling, leucocyte transendothelial migration in the liver, and apoptosis and NOD-like receptor in both the hippocampus and liver. Furthermore, exposure to 1-bromopropane for 4 weeks also resulted in upregulation of immune system and inflammation-related pathways, including chemokine signaling, TGF-beta signaling, RIG-I-like receptor signaling, natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, B cell receptor signaling, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis and primary immunodeficiency in the hippocampus as well as NOD-like receptor signaling both in the hippocampus and the liver.

## 1-bromopropane induces persistent upregulation of insulin signaling-related KEGG pathway in the hippocampus but not in the liver
Fig. shows upregulated pathways in the hippocampus of both rat strains in the 8-hour and 4-week exposure group. Other than immune system/inflammation-related pathways, insulin signaling pathway was upregulated both in the 8-hour and 4-week exposure group. This upregulation was not observed in the liver (Fig. ). 

## 1-bromoropane induces colorectal cancer pathway in the hippocampus and liver
1-bromopropane induces colorectal cancer pathway in the hippocampus of both rat strains in the 8-hour and 4-week exposure group and in the liver of both rat strains in the 8-hour exposure group (Fig , and , Suppl Table , 2 and 3). 

## Discussion
The present study provided experimental evidence for the involvement of the immune system/inflammation-related pathways in the neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity of 1-bromopropane. NOD-like receptor signaling was commonly upregulated both in the hippocampus and the liver of rats exposed to 1-bromopropaen for 8 hours and 4 weeks. These results add support to the summary statement published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) regarding the involvement of chronic inflammation and/or immunosuppression in 1-bromopropaen toxicity . Furthermore, upregulation of genes involved in NOD-like receptor signaling pathway is in agreement with our previous study, which showed that exposure to 1-bromopropane induced microglial activation in the brain of rats , suggesting involvement of innate immunity in -induced 1-bromopropane toxicity.
The present study demonstrated the involvement of the insulin signaling pathway in the neurotoxicity of 1-bromopropane. This pathway is also known to play a role in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease . Notably, the insulin signaling pathway was not upregulated in the liver, suggesting that its activation may be specific to the neurotoxic effects of 1-bromopropane. This observation raises the hypothesis that a common mechanism involving insulin signaling may underlie both 1-bromopropane-induced axonal degeneration and neurodegenerative diseases.
Our finding of upregulation of pathway involved in apoptosis is in agreement with the results of our proteomic study , although another study from our laboratories found no apoptotic cells in the brain of rats exposed to 1-bromopropane at 200, 400 and 800 ppm for 12 weeks or at 1000 ppm for 5 to 7 weeks , . It is possible that apoptosis signaling is involved in axonal degeneration, which was demonstrated in our previous study . In this regard, we have recently reported the involvement of gene expression of capase-8, a key enzyme that initiates the extrinsic pathway of apoptosis, in the neurotoxicity of acrylamide, which is known to induce neurotoxicity similar to 1-bromopropane .
Upregulation of pathway involved in colorectal cancer is interesting, as 1-bromopropane is carcinogenic in experimental animals , . It was reported previously that exposure to 1-bromopropane for 108 weeks increased colorectal tumors in female rats compared to the control group and induced colorectal tumors exceeding the historical control range for males and females , .
Upregulation of antioxidant genes, such as Nqo1 or Gsta3 in the liver, is also in agreement with our previous study in mice which shows 1-bromopropane-induced upregulation of Nqo1 and increase in GST activity , , suggesting the involvement of transcription factor Nrf2 in 1-bromorpopane-induced alteration of gene expression in the liver. In relation to these antioxidant genes, upregulation of metallothionein 2 A and 1 should be noted. Metallothionein is a sulfhydryl-rich and metal-binding protein which can protect against the toxicity of metals . Further studies are needed to clarify how metallothionein plays a role in toxicity of 1-bromopropane.
Our results showed that exposure to 1-bromopropaane for 4 weeks prolonged distal latency (DL) in the rat tail nerve, which was significant in F344/NSlc and marginal in WNA/NUM, whereas it did not affect motor nerve conduction velocity (MCV). These findings are in agreement with our previous study, which showed that exposure to 1-brompropane at 800 ppm for 4 weeks increased DL but did not change MCV in the tail nerve of rats, suggesting that DL is more sensitive than MCV to 1-bronmoproane exposure. The findings suggest that F344/NSlc rats exhibit slightly higher susceptibility than WNA/NUM rats. However, the present data do not allow a clear interpretation of how the observed differences in gene expression contribute to this variation in susceptibility. Further studies, including analyses of F1 offspring derived from inbred strains with differing susceptibilities, are needed to identify the genes associated with susceptibility to 1-bromopropane.
Our results showed no increase in hepatobiliary enzymes, such as ALT, AST, LDH and ALP, following exposure to 1-bormopropane, which is in agreement with our previous study in rats . Human cases also did not report liver injury – . Compared to mice, which exhibit 1-bromopropane-induced hepatic necrosis at 50 ppm , rats and humans seem to be less susceptible to 1-bormopropane-related hepatotoxicity. However, the present study showed an increase in direct bilirubin, suggestive of cholestasis, which is slowing or stalling of bile flow through the biliary system. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanism of increase in direct bilirubin induced by 1-brompropane.
In conclusion, we have demonstrated in the present study that exposure to 1-bromopropane alters the expression of pathways related to immune system/inflammation, such as NOD-like receptor signaling, thus suggesting the involvement of immune response in the toxicity of 1-bromopropane. The study also suggests the involvement of insulin signaling pathway in the neurotoxicity of 1-bromopropane.
