## Abstract
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic lung disorder with a low survival rate. Pulmonary fibrosis is one of the complications of COVID-19 and has a high prevalence in COVID-19 patients. Currently, no effective therapies other than lung transplantation are available to cure IPF and post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis. MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that mediate the development and progression of pulmonary fibrosis, thus making them potent drug candidates for this serious disease. MicroRNA-21 (miR-21) promotes not only the differentiation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts but also epithelial-mesenchymal transition, both of which have been proposed as fundamental processes in pulmonary fibrosis development. Delivery of anti-miR-21 to block the miR-21-associated fibrogenic pathways represents a promising therapy for pulmonary fibrosis. However, microRNA treatment is challenged by quick degradation of RNA in blood, poor cellular uptake, and off-target effects. To overcome these challenges, we developed a lung-targeted, cationic liposome formulation to encapsulate anti-miR-21, enhance its delivery efficiency, and improve the therapeutic efficacy. We optimized the liposome formulation and demonstrated the anti-fibrotic effects using both in vitro and in vivo lung fibrosis models. Our results showed that anti-miR-21 delivered by cationic liposomes suppressed myofibroblast differentiation, reduced the synthesis of extracellular matrix, and inhibited fibrosis progression.
Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, miR-21, liposomes, pulmonary fibrosis, COVID-19, microRNA, drug delivery

## Graphical abstract
 
Wu and colleagues developed a new lipid nanodrug for post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which lack effective therapy except lung transplantation. This new lipid nanodrug enabled lung-targeted delivery of RNA therapeutics (anti-microRNA-21) and showed strong anti-fibrotic effects, representing a potent treatment for pulmonary fibrosis.

## Introduction
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic interstitial lung disease resulting from an abnormality in lung wound repair. It usually occurs in patients aged 50–70 years. The medium transplant-free survival time of IPF patients is 4.5 years after diagnosis. The mechanism of this disease remains elusive. It is widely accepted that predeposited genetic mutations and some external factors (radiation, toxins, etc.) are fundamental in the initiation of IPF. Besides, pulmonary fibrosis is considered as one of potential long-term consequences of COVID-19. It is estimated that the incidence rate of pulmonary fibrosis will increase rapidly considering that the wide spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) affects hundreds of millions of people. Lung transplant surgery is the most effective clinical therapy, but lung transplantation is only appropriate for patients aged <70 years and limited by available lung sources. Two new drugs, pirfenidone and nintedanib, , have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat IPF and have been used to treat post-COVID-19 lung fibrosis. However, these drugs only slow down the disease progression and show no help in improving the survival rate. Thus, there is an urgent need for effective anti-fibrotic therapies.
MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNAs with sequences of 19–22 nt that regulate gene expression. They work by binding to the 3′ UTR of targeted mRNA, thereby promoting mRNA degradation or inhibiting the translation of mRNA into proteins. MicroRNAs have been found to actively participate in the pathogenesis of many diseases, including fibrosis, , , cancers, , , , and diabetes. , , To date, many microRNAs have been reported to be involved in different stages of IPF. miR-21, let-7d, miR-200, miR-26a, , , miR-375, miR-503, and miR-18a are related with the regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). miR-21, , miR-155, , miR-26a, miR-27a-3p, miR-29, , miR-96, miR-101, miR-326, and miR-9-5p , are reported to mediate the fibrogenesis of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. Studies about the profibrogenic activity of microRNAs in macrophages revealed that miR-155, miR-29a, miR-185, miR-142-5p, and miR-130a-3p may play important roles in IPF. Regulating the expression of microRNAs represents a promising therapeutic strategy for pulmonary fibrosis.
The development of microRNA-based therapeutics for pulmonary fibrosis has just begun. Liu et al. showed that the treatment of anti-miR-21 suppressed the production of ECM in fibroblasts and thereby reversed the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Montgomery et al. reported that synthetic miR-29 duplexes successfully reversed the fibrosis induced by bleomycin in mice. Zhang et al. used lentivirus as the carrier to deliver miR-18a-5p to inhibit sub-pleural pulmonary fibrosis. Dai et al. utilized a bacteria-derived plasmid, pEGFP, to deliver miR-9 mimic to treat bleomycin-induced fibrosis in mice. Xiao et al. complexed a sleeping beauty transposon system with polyethyleneimine (PEI) to induce miR-29 in the lungs of bleomycin-treated mice, which inhibited the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Zhang et al. investigated the potential of miR-30a as an IPF therapeutics and used riboFECT CP reagents to facilitate the delivery of miR-30a. Liang et al. used Lipofectamine 2000 to deliver cholesterol conjugated miR-26a and observed promising anti-fibrotic effects. These studies have demonstrated the great promise of using microRNAs for pulmonary fibrosis therapy. However, none of these reported microRNAs-based therapies offers lung targeting capability, and the use of delivery system such as PEI and Lipofectamine 2000 has limited translational potential to clinical settings.
In this study, we developed a liposome formulation of anti-miR-21 that provided both lung targeting capability and profound anti-fibrotic effects. miR-21 was selected as the therapeutic target because the expression of miR-21 is upregulated in the lungs of IPF patients and mice with bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis, , and the intratracheal instillation of anti-miR-21 attenuated fibrosis in the lungs of bleomycin-treated mice. However, the direct delivery of naked, unmodified RNA molecules is challenged by quick degradation in blood, poor cellular uptake, and off-target effects. To overcome these limitations, we developed lung-targeted, cationic liposomes to encapsulate anti-miR-21 (LP-anti-miR-21) through electrostatic interaction. We showed that LP-anti-miR-21 significantly improved the accumulation of anti-miR-21 in the lung, increased cellular uptake of anti-miR-21, and thus enhanced the anti-fibrotic efficacy of anti-miR-21 in vitro and in vivo. Our results demonstrated that systemic delivery of anti-miR-21 via lung-targeted liposomes is a potent strategy for pulmonary fibrosis treatment.

## Results

## Preparation and characterization of LP-anti-miR-21
As shown in A, empty liposomes were first prepared by the ethanol dilution method. Then the anti-miR-21 was mixed with empty liposomes at the lipid to anti-miR-21 mass ratio of 10 to prepare LP-anti-miR-21 through electrostatic interaction between cationic liposomes and negatively charged anti-miR-21. The size and size distribution of LP-anti-miR-21 were measured by dynamic light scattering. A representative size distribution is shown in B. The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity of the LP-anti-miR-21 were 101.24 ± 4.80 nm and 0.234 ± 0.106, respectively. The zeta potential of LP-anti-miR-21 was +29.4 ± 5.53 mV. Cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) images indicated that LP-anti-miR-21 had multilamellar structure ( C). The encapsulation efficiency of anti-miR-21 was 99.49% ± 0.18%.
 

## In vitro lung fibrosis model
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of LP-anti-miR-21, an in vitro lung fibrosis model was established by treating normal human lung fibroblasts (NHLFs) with transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1, which is a multifunctional cytokine that plays an important role in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. We incubated NHLF cells with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h. The expression of miR-21, fibronectin (FN1), collagen (COL1A1) and alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) mRNAs was analyzed by reverse transcription followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to confirm the establishment of the lung fibrosis cell model. The protein expression of FN1, COL1A1, and α-SMA was also evaluated by western blot. As shown in , the expression of miR-21, FN1, COL1A1, and α-SMA mRNAs was 1.8-fold, 3.6-fold, 4.9-fold, and 7.4-fold higher than untreated cells at 24 h post TGF-β1 treatment. The increased expression of FN1, COL1A1, and α-SMA at protein level was also confirmed by western blot. The upregulation of these fibrotic markers demonstrated that the in vitro lung fibrosis model was successfully established.
 

## Cellular uptake of liposomes
To evaluate the cellular uptake of liposomes, NHLF cells were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h to induce fibrosis. Then the cells were treated with vehicle control, free Cy5.5-labeled oligodeoxynucleotides (Cy5.5-ODN), and liposomes containing Cy5.5-ODN (LP-Cy5.5-ODN) at the Cy5.5-ODN concentration of 50 nM. At 8 h post treatment, the cells were harvested for confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analyses. Confocal microscopy images showed that the LP-Cy5.5-ODN were successfully taken up by the NHLF cells ( A). Flow cytometry was used to quantitate the cellular uptake efficiency. A representative flow cytometry dataset is shown in B. The averaged fluorescence intensity of Cy5.5 in cells treated with LP-Cy5.5-ODN was 435.5-fold higher than the vehicle control group, and 3.0-fold higher than cells treated with free Cy5.5-ODN ( C). These results demonstrated that liposomes are an effective nanocarrier system for nucleic acids delivery to NHLF cells.
 

## Therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21 in in vitro lung fibrosis model
To evaluate the therapeutic effects of LP-miR-21 in lung fibrosis, NHLF cells were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h and then treated with LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 concentration of 50 nM for 8 h. The concentration of anti-miR-21 was selected based on the toxicity of liposomes ( ). Vehicle control, free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC (scrambled miRNA) and LP-anti-miR-SC were used as control groups. At 48 h post treatment, the expression of miR-21 and fibrotic markers including COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 at mRNA and protein levels were measured by qRT-PCR and western blot, respectively. As shown in A, after the treatment of LP-anti-miR-21, the levels of miR-21, COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 mRNAs were reduced by 50.7%, 37.3%, 50.2%, and 33.9% respectively compared with vehicle control group. However, the free anti-miR-21 treatment showed little effect in the downregulation of miR-21, COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 mRNAs. A downregulation of miR-21 (11.0%) was observed in NHLF cells treated with free anti-miR-SC; however, the t test did not show a significant difference between this group and vehicle control. For the levels of COL1A1, FN1, and α-SMA mRNAs, no significant changes were observed in the anti-miR-SC treated group when compared with vehicle control. For NHLF cells treated with LP-anti-miR-SC, we observed no significant change in the expression of miR-21 (2.9%). For COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 mRNAs, only 22.6%, 19.5%, and 12.9% reduction in their expression was observed. The slight downregulation of COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 at mRNA level may be due to non-specific silencing effect. At protein level, compared with vehicle control group, the expression of COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 was downregulated in the cells treated with LP-anti-miR-21 ( B). For other treatment groups, only slight decrease in the expression of FN1 was observed in cells treated with anti-miR-21 and anti-miR-SC, but no significant changes were observed for the expression of COL1A1 and α-SMA in these two groups. LP-anti-miR-SC did not induce any changes in the expression of COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1. Besides, to investigate the dose-dependent response, NHLF cells were treated with a lower dose of LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 concentration of 25 nM. As expected, lower anti-miR-21 concentration was inferior to the higher concentration (50 nM) in inhibiting the expression of miR-21, COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 ( ). These results demonstrated that liposomes effectively delivered anti-miR-21 and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of anti-miR-21, and the anti-fibrotic effect was dose dependent.
 The toxicity of liposomes was evaluated using LP-anti-miR-SC. NHLF cells were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 to induce fibrosis and then treated with LP-anti-miR-SC at anti-miR-SC concentration of 50 nM. The cell viability was measured by alamarBlue assay at 48 h post treatment. As shown in C, LP-anti-miR-SC showed no toxicity in NHLF cells, suggesting the liposomes were a safe delivery system.

## Effect of LP-anti-miR-21 on the contractile force of lung microtissues
Elevated contractile force generation by differentiated myofibroblasts is a hallmark of tissue fibrosis. The elevated contractile force generation was modeled using a novel lung microtissue array model treated by TGF-β1 ( A). , To evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of LP-anti-miR-21, lung microtissues were simultaneously treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 and LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 concentrations of 10 nM and 20 nM for five continuous days. Untreated microtissue was used as the control group. Microtissue contractile force was measured every day. The deflection of the micropillar head in TGF-β1-treated microtissues ( B) was substantially larger than that in LP-anti-miR-21-treated microtissues ( C), suggesting elevated contractile force generation. As shown in D, compared with highly elevated contractile forces under TGF-β1 treatment, the contractile forces under LP-anti-miR-21 treatment at both concentrations were greatly reduced, and higher concentration outperformed the lower concentration, suggesting a dose-dependent response. The forces remained relatively stable from day 2 to day 5. At day 5, the force under 20 nM LP-anti-miR-21 treatment reduced by 66.2% compared with TGF-β1 treatment, and these force values were almost the same as that of untreated sample. Compared with results from our previous study, LP-anti-miR-21 (20 nM) was as effective as pirfenidone (1.6 mM) and nintedanib (3 μM) in reducing contractile force and maintaining contractile force at same level as untreated control. Together, these data showed that LP-anti-miR-21 treatment significantly reduced the contractile force of the lung microtissue, thus serving as a potential therapy for pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## Biodistribution of liposomes in bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis mouse model
The biodistribution of liposomes was investigated using LP-Cy5.5-ODN. C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were treated with bleomycin (1.5 U/kg body weight) to induce pulmonary fibrosis. At 7 days post bleomycin treatment, mice were given LP-Cy5.5-ODN through intravenous injection at the Cy5.5-ODN dose of 1.5 mg/kg body weight. Vehicle and free Cy5.5-ODN were used as the control groups. At 4 h post injection, the mice were sacrificed and the Cy5.5 fluorescence signals in major organs were measured using the IVIS in vivo imaging system. As shown in , compared with free Cy5.5-ODN, a significantly higher amount of LP-Cy5.5-ODN accumulated in the lungs (40.09% vs. 10.59%), demonstrating the lung targeting capability of liposomes. As expected, the remaining LP-Cy5.5-ODN was observed in liver, kidney, and spleen. Because free Cy5.5-ODN is a small molecule, much smaller than LP-Cy5.5-ODN, most of it accumulated in kidney and liver.
 

## Therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21 in bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis mouse model
The anti-fibrotic efficacy of LP-anti-miR-21 was then evaluated in bleomycin-induced fibrosis mouse model. Considering pulmonary fibrosis affects a large population of COVID-19 patients, a combination of prophylactic and therapeutic treatment strategies was used in the in vivo efficacy study. With this treatment strategy, we expect to demonstrate the potential application of LP-anti-miR-21 in an early phase of COVID-19 infection to effectively prevent or treat COVID-19 induced pulmonary fibrosis. C57BL/6 mice were administered with LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 dose of 2 mg/kg body weight through tail vein injection. The anti-miR-21 dose was selected because this dose was well tolerated in mice and no significant toxicity was observed. Two days later, the mice were treated with bleomycin through intratracheal intubation at the dose of 1.5 U/kg body weight to induce fibrosis. Then the mice were treated with LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 dose of 2 mg/kg body weight through tail vein injection at 3, 6, 9, and 12 days post bleomycin intubation. Mice were then sacrificed at 2 days after the last injection. Vehicle, free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC were used as the control groups.
The change of mouse body weight was recorded. The body weight of mice treated with anti-miR-21 and LP-anti-miR-21 did not change significantly during the study. However, the mice treated with vehicle, anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC started to lose weight from day 3 post bleomycin intubation. Approximately 30%, 55%, and 40% of mice treated by vehicle, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC were removed from the study before the endpoint due to >20% loss of body weight. The survival curve is shown in .
The right and largest lobes of mouse lungs were harvested for qRT-PCR and western blot analyses of the expression of miR-21, FN1, COL1A1, and α-SMA. The remaining lobes were fixed in 10% formalin and used for H&E staining to observe the lung structures and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining to examine α-SMA expression. For mice treated with LP-anti-miR-21, the expression of miR-21, FN1, COL1A1, and α-SMA mRNAs decreased by 40.2%, 69.1%, 66.7%, and 35.0% compared with the vehicle control group ( A). The anti-fibrotic effects of free anti-miR-21 were less obvious. No significant downregulation of miR-21 (−5.5%) and α-SMA mRNA (−8.9%) was observed. The expression of FN1 and COL1A1 mRNAs was reduced by 38.4% and 36.1%, which was less significant than LP-anti-miR-21. These results demonstrated that LP-anti-miR-21 more efficiently delivered anti-miR-21 to mouse lungs than free anti-miR-21, therefore enhancing the therapeutic effects of anti-miR-21. For mice treated with free anti-miR-SC and LP-anti-SC, we observed little anti-fibrotic effect. Only 19.0% and 35.0% reduction in FN1 levels was observed in mice treated with free anti-miR-SC and LP-anti-miR-SC, respectively; however, no significant downregulation in the expression of miR-21, COL1A1, and α-SMA mRNAs was found in these two groups of mice. The suppression of fibrotic markers was confirmed by western blot and IHC staining. Mice treated with LP-anti-miR-21 showed much more significant downregulation of FN1, α-SMA, and COL1A1 proteins than those treated with free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC ( B and ). Results from H&E staining showed that bleomycin induced significant accumulation of ECM in the lungs of mice treated with vehicle control compared with healthy mice ( C). LP-anti-miR-21 treatment significantly attenuated the accumulation of ECM in the lungs, while free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC groups only showed slight reduction of ECM in the lungs. Ashcroft score was used to evaluate the degree of pulmonary fibrosis ( C and ). LP-anti-miR-21 treatment greatly reduced the severity of fibrosis and outperformed free anti-miR-21. Free anti-miR-SC and LP-anti-miR-SC showed no effects on alleviating fibrosis. The toxicity of LP-anti-miR-21 in major organs including heart, liver, spleen, and kidney was evaluated by H&E staining. Compared with healthy controls, no significant toxicity was observed ( ). These results collectively demonstrated that LP-anti-miR-21 has great anti-fibrotic performance and represents an effective therapy for pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## Discussion
IPF is a serious disease with poor survival of 2–5 years from the time of diagnosis. Pulmonary fibrosis is found to be one of the complications of COVID-19. A recent meta-analysis study reported that the prevalence of post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis in COVID-19 survivors was 44.9%. Currently there are no effective treatments to cure IPF and post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis except lung transplantation. Therefore, new therapies are urgently needed. MicroRNAs have been reported to be actively involved in the development of pulmonary fibrosis, which makes them promising therapeutics for this serious disease. Nevertheless, the therapies using naked microRNAs usually suffer from quick clearance of microRNAs and poor delivery efficiency to disease sites. Once entering circulation system, microRNAs can be quickly removed by renal filtration and degraded by nucleases in blood. To overcome these limitations, various carrier systems, such as lentivirus, PEI, and Lipofectamine 2000, were used to protect microRNAs and facilitate the delivery. However, these carrier systems lack lung targeting capability, and the off-targeting microRNAs may cause unexpected side effects in patients. Besides, many microRNA therapies were given through respiratory system. , , Patients with pulmonary fibrosis suffer from dyspnea, especially at the late stage of the disease, which may make the drug delivery quite challenging. All these issues limit the clinical translational potential of current microRNA therapies.
We developed a lung-targeted, cationic liposome-based nanocarrier system to effectively deliver microRNA therapeutics via the intravenous route for pulmonary fibrosis treatment. We selected miR-21 as the model therapeutic target because it plays important roles in pulmonary fibrosis. Liu et al. reported an upregulation of miR-21 in fibroblasts isolated from lungs of IPF patients and demonstrated that miR-21 induced excessive deposition of ECM in human primary fibroblasts treated with TGF-β1. Besides fibroblasts, miR-21 was also associated with the EMT process. Yamada et al. transfected EMT-induced mouse alveolar type II cells with a miR-21 inhibitor and observed that the EMT of alveolar type II cells was prevented. 
The cationic liposomes consisted of 1,2-di-O-octadecenyl-3-trimethylammounium propane chloride salt (DOTMA), cholesterol, and D-α-tocopheryl polyethyleneglycol 1,000 succinate (PEG). DOTMA is a commonly used cationic lipid for nucleic acid delivery. It not only provides positive charges for liposomes to complex with nucleic acids but also enhances the interaction between liposomes and negatively charged cell membrane and thus improves cellular uptake efficiency. Cholesterol is used to improve the stability of liposomes. PEG shields liposomes from early clearance from the blood system. The LP-miR-21 had multilamellar structure with the mean diameter by volume of 101 nm and zeta potential of +29.4 mV ( ). The cellular uptake and therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21 were first investigated in a TFG-β1-induced in vitro lung fibrosis model. Results from flow cytometry and confocal microscopy imaging analyses indicated that cationic liposomes are an effective nanocarrier system and increased cellular uptake by 435.5-fold compared with vehicle control ( ). LP-anti-miR-21 successfully downregulated the expression of miR-21, COL1A1, α-SMA, and FN1 in NHLF cells, demonstrating great anti-fibrotic effects in vitro ( ). In a lung microtissue array model, LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 dose of 20 nM reduced the contractile force and maintained the contractile force similar to untreated control ( ), showing anti-fibrotic efficacy comparable with pirfenidone and nintedanib. 
Next, the therapeutic efficacy of LP-anti-miR-21 was evaluated in vivo. The cationic liposomes protected anti-miR-21 from clearance and enabled targeted delivery to the lung. Approximately 40% of liposomes accumulated in the lung at 4 h post intravenous injection, demonstrating a very good lung targeting capability ( ). The lung targeting capability may be due to the type and composition of lipids used in the liposome formation. In our pilot screening study, the biodistributions of different liposome formulations in mice were investigated. Among these formulations, the composition of lipids was adjusted (for example, PEG amount was increased from 1% to 10%), or cholesterol was replaced with another lipid, L-α-phosphatidylcholine (Egg PC). However, increasing PEG content and using Egg PC in the formulation significantly reduced the lung targeting capability ( ), suggesting that lipid type and composition play important roles in determining the lung targeting capability. In a bleomycin-induced fibrosis mouse model, LP-anti-miR-21 significantly reduced the expression of miR-21, COL1A1, FN1, and α-SMA in mouse lungs, and more effectively inhibited the progression of fibrosis compared with vehicle control, free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC ( ). Lung targeting capability and high anti-miR-21 delivery efficiency are two unique properties of LP-anti-miR-21 that make it an effective therapy for pulmonary fibrosis.
In pulmonary fibrosis, activated TGF-β1 signaling pathway induces the expression of miR-21. SMAD7 was found to be the direct target of miR-21, and the upregulation of miR-21 suppressed the expression of SMAD7. As a result of SMAD7 inhibition, the downstream signaling events of TGF-β1 are enhanced, which activate fibroblast migration, proliferation, and differentiation into more fibrogenic myofibroblasts. These myofibroblasts aberrantly secrete large amounts of extracellular matrix that remodel the lung architectures and lead to fibrosis. In this study, LP-anti-miR-21 was shown to downregulate miR-21 expression ( ) and upregulate SMAD7 levels ( ). This was accompanied by the inhibition of the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts (as demonstrated by decreased α-SMA expression) and the reduction in the levels of ECM such as FN1 and COL1A1 ( and ), suggesting the LP-anti-miR-21 inhibited the fibrosis through the SMAD7 and TGF-β1 pathway.
miR-21 was also reported to participate in pulmonary fibrosis by inducing EMT in lung epithelial cells. However, we did not observe significant differences in the expression of EMT markers, including CDH1 and VIM, between vehicle control and various treatment groups ( ). This may be due to the controversial role of EMT in lung fibrosis, with some studies supporting it and others opposing it. It is possible that the EMT markers measured here do not directly reflect the therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21. However, the anti-fibrotic effects of LP-anti-miR-21 were validated using more broadly accepted fibrosis markers such as α-SMA and collagen. The other potential reason may be because we measured the levels of CDH1 and VIM in whole lungs instead of epithelial cells isolated from the lung tissues. In the future, more detailed investigation will be performed to examine the anti-fibrotic effect of LP-anti-miR-21 in lung epithelial cells.
In summary, we developed a lung-targeted, cationic liposome formulation to effectively deliver anti-miR-21 through intravenous route for lung fibrosis treatment. Our results demonstrated that LP-anti-miR-21 is a promising therapy for pulmonary fibrosis. In the future, we will continue optimizing the formulation of liposomes by adding targeting molecules on the surface to further improve its lung targeting capability. We will determine the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of LP-anti-miR-21 and investigate its dose-dependent anti-fibrotic response in vivo. In this study, we used the combination of prophylactic and therapeutic treatment strategies. As the next step, we will investigate the prophylactic treatment strategy to assess the effectiveness of LP-anti-miR-21 in preventing pulmonary fibrosis. We will also investigate the therapeutic treatment strategy to examine the efficacy of LP-anti-miR-21 in treating pulmonary fibrosis when the disease has fully developed. We will combine LP-anti-miR-21 with FDA approved drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib to investigate the synergistic effects of combined therapy in the treatment of IPF and post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis.

## Materials and methods

## Materials
miRIDIAN hsa-miR-21-5p hairpin inhibitor (anti-miR-21, IH-300492-05) and non-targeting miRIDIAN microRNA hairpin inhibitor negative control #2 (anti-miR-SC, IN-002005-01) were purchased from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO). Cy5.5-labeled oligodeoxynucleotide (Cy5.5-ODN, 5′-Cy5.5-CAAGCTGATTTACACCCGGTGA-3′) was purchased from Alpha DNA (Montreal, QC, Canada). DOTMA (890898P) was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. Cholesterol (C3045), PEG (57,668), and TGF-β1 (45-T5050) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). NHLFs (CC-2512, Lonza), FGM-2 fibroblast growth medium-2 BulletKit (CC-3132), which contains FBM fibroblast growth basal medium (CC-3131), and FGM-2 SingleQuots supplements (CC-4126) were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MA). Ham’s F-12K (Kaighn’s) Medium (21127-02), fetal bovine serum (FBS, 16000-044), penicillin-streptomycin (PS, 15140-122), N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES, BP310-100), UltraPure diethypyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water (750023), and Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA assay kit (R11490) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA).

## Cell culture and in vitro fibrosis model
NHLF cells were cultured in FBM fibroblast basal medium supplemented with FGM-2 SingleQuot supplements at 37°C with 5% CO2. The cells were passaged when the confluence reached 70% to 80% for a total of seven passages. The fourth or fifth passage was used for in vitro experiments. The in vitro lung fibrosis model was established by treating NHLF cells with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h in Ham’s F-12K medium containing 2% FBS and 1× PS.

## Preparation of LP-anti-miR-21
DOTMA, cholesterol, and PEG were mixed at the molar ratio of 49.5:49.5:1 in ethanol. The lipid mixture was injected into 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH = 7.4) to reach 10% ethanol and 90% aqueous in the final solution to prepare empty liposomes. Anti-miR-21 was mixed with empty liposomes at the anti-miR-21: lipid mass ratio of 1:10 and then sonicated at room temperature (RT) for 10 min to prepare liposomes containing anti-miR-21 (LP-anti-miR-21).

## Characterization of LP-anti-miR-21
The size, size distribution, and zeta potential of LP-anti-miR-21 were measured using Malvern ZetaSizer NanoZS (Malvern Panalytical, Westborough, MA). The LP-anti-miR-21 was diluted in 1,000 μL of filtered deionized water for all measurements. The measurements were performed at 25°C. The detection angle was 173°. The wavelength of the laser was 632.8 nm. The encapsulation efficiency of anti-miR-21 in liposomes was measured using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA assay kit. Three measurements were carried out for each sample. The mean ± SD was reported. The morphology of LP-anti-miR-21 was characterized by cryo-TEM. Briefly, LP-anti-miR-21 (5 μL) was applied to lacey-carbon-coated copper grids (400 mesh, Pacific Grid-Tech, San Francisco, CA). The grids were snap-frozen in liquid ethane using an automated vitrification device (FEI Vitrobot Mark IV, FEI, Hillsboro, OR). Then the morphology of LP-anti-miR-21 was examined using the FEI Tecnai G2 F20 ST TEM (FEI, Hillsboro, OR).

## In vitro cellular uptake
To measure the cellular uptake efficiency, Cy5.5-ODN was encapsulated in liposomes to prepare LP-Cy5.5-ODN at lipid to Cy5.5-ODN mass ratio of 10. NHLF cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates and cultured in F-12K medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1× PS. After overnight incubation, the cells were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with vehicle control, free Cy5.5-ODN, and LP-Cy5.5-ODN at the Cy5.5-ODN concentration of 50 nM. At 8 h post treatment, the cells were harvested and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for cellular uptake analysis.
The cellular uptake was characterized by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. The flow cytometry analysis was carried out by a BD Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA). The fluorescence intensity of the cells was measured using the APC channel (λex = 640 nm and λem = 670 nm). A total of 10,000 events were collected, and the mean fluorescence intensities were reported. For confocal microscopy imaging, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Then, the cells were mounted on glass slides. LSM 710 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) was used to visualize the cell nuclei and LP-Cy5.5-ODN using the DAPI channel (λex = 405 nm and λem = 460 nm) and the Cy5.5 channel (λex = 640 nm and λem = 670 nm), respectively.

## In vitro therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21
To characterize the therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21, NHLF cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates and cultured in F-12K medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1× PS overnight. The cells were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h to establish the in vitro lung fibrosis model. Then the cells were treated with vehicle control, free anti-miR-21, LP-anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, and LP-anti-miR-SC at anti-miR concentration of 50 nM in serum-free F-12K medium. At 8 h post treatment, the cells were transferred to F-12K medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1× PS. At 48 h post treatment, the cells were harvested for further analysis of therapeutic effects.

## Cytotoxicity of LP-anti-miR-SC
To evaluate the toxicity of the liposomes and microRNA hairpin inhibitors, negative control anti-miR-SC was encapsulated in liposomes at lipid to anti-miR-SC mass ratio of 10 to prepare LP-anti-miR-SC. The NHLF cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured in 200 μL of F-12k medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1× PS overnight. The cells were then treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h. Next, the cells were transferred to serum-free F-12k medium and treated with LP-anti-miR-SC at anti-miR-SC concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 nM. At 6 h post treatment, LP-anti-miR-SC was removed and the cells were cultured in F-12k medium with 2% FBS and 1× PS. At 48 h post treatment, alamarBlue cell viability assay (DAL1025, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to evaluate the cell viability of NHLF cells following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 10 parts of cell culture medium was mixed with one part of alamarBlue reagent. The mixture was then added to cells and incubated for 3 h. The fluorescence signals of the mixed cell culture medium were measured by TECAN microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland) with the excitation wavelength of 560 nm and the emission wavelength of 612 nm.

## Microtissue fabrication and contractile force measurement
The effect of LP-anti-miR-21 on the contractile force of NHLF microtissues was evaluated. An array of NHLF cell-populated microtissues were formed in a microdevice fabricated using multilayer microlithography, as described previously. , Briefly, SU-8 master pattern of an array of microwells, each containing eight micropillars, was created through spin coating, alignment, exposure, and baking of multiple layers of SU-8 photoresists. Next, the micropillar array pattern was transferred to polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Sylgard 184; Dow-Corning) through replica molding. To seed microtissues in the micropillar array, rat tail collagen type I (Corning) was neutralized by NaOH (1 M), mixed with NHLF cells (3 × 105 cells per microdevice), and immediately seeded on the device. Microtissues formed at the neck region of the micropillars, and fibroblast-generated contractile forces caused the micropillars to deflect. Micropillar deflection was measured every 24 h by taking images at both the top and bottom of the micropillars using a 10× objective on an Olympus IX81 microscope. The deflection of micropillar heads from the micropillar base was measured using ImageJ software and multiplied by the micropillar’s spring constant to calculate the contractile force based on cantilever theory. Microtissues were treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 with and without LP-anti-miR-21 at anti-miR-21 concentrations of 10 and 20 nM for 5 days. The contractile forces of untreated microtissues, microtissues treated with TGF-β1 alone, and microtissues treated with both TGF-β1 and LP-anti-miR-21 were measured.

## Experimental animals
The mice used in this study were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at University at Buffalo. Prior to the experiments, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the environment for at least 1 week.

## Establishment of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model
To establish a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model, C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg body weight xylazine (NDC 59399-110-20, Akorn, IL) and 90 mg/kg body weight ketamine (NDC 50989-161-06, Vedco, MO) in 0.9% saline. Atropine (0.04 mg/kg body weight, NDC 50989-075-12, Vedco) was injected subcutaneously to relax muscles. Then the mice were treated with bleomycin at the dose of 1.5 U/kg body weight in 50 μL of PBS by intratracheal intubation.

## In vivo biodistribution of LP-Cy5.5-ODN
Cy5.5-labeled liposomes (i.e., LP-Cy5.5-ODN) were used to evaluate the biodistribution of liposomes. C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were treated with bleomycin at the dose of 1.5 U/kg body weight by intratracheal intubation to induce fibrosis in the lungs. At 7 days post bleomycin treatment, C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into three groups and injected intravenously with vehicle control (n = 3), free Cy5.5-ODN (n = 3), and LP-Cy5.5-ODN (n = 4) at a Cy5.5-ODN dose of 1.5 mg/kg body weight. At 4 h after the injection, the mice were euthanized to harvest major organs including brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. The fluorescence intensity of Cy5.5 (λex = 675 nm and λem = 720 nm) in major organs was measured by the IVIS Lumina II in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) to determine the biodistribution of LP-Cy5.5-ODN.

## In vivo therapeutic effects of LP-anti-miR-21
C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were randomly divided into six groups and intravenously injected with vehicle control, free anti-miR-21, free anti-miR-SC, LP-anti-miR-21, and LP-anti-miR-SC at anti-miR dose of 2 mg/kg body weight (n = 10 per group). The first injection was performed 2 days before bleomycin treatment as described above. An additional four injections were given to mice at day 3, 6, 9, and 12 post bleomycin intratracheal intubation. The mice were sacrificed at 2 days post last injection; i.e., at 14 days post bleomycin treatment. Healthy mice receiving no bleomycin and any treatment were negative controls. The right, largest lobes of the lungs were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for qRT-PCR and western blot analysis. The remaining lobes of the lungs were preserved in 10% formalin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and embedded in paraffin, followed by H&E staining.

## qRT-PCR
The total RNA was extracted by TRIzol, precipitated by isopropanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and reconstituted in DEPC-treated water. The reverse transcription was performed using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (4368814, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression of miR-21 was measured using the TaqMan microRNA assay for hsa-miR-21 (assay ID: 000397, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). RNU48 (assay ID: 001006) and snoRNA135 (assay ID: 001230) were used as the endogenous control for human and mouse samples, respectively. For mRNAs, gene expression assays for alpha smooth muscle actin (ACTA2, assay ID: HS00426835_g1), fibronectin (FN1, assay ID: Hs01549976_m1), collagen (COL1A1, assay ID: Hs00164004_m1), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, assay ID: Hs02758991_g1) were used for real-time PCR analysis of human samples. Gene expression assays for ACTA2 (assay ID: Mm01546133_m1), FN1 (assay ID: Mm01256744_m1), COL1A1 (assay ID: Mn00801666_g1), SMAD7 (assay ID: Mm00484742_m1), and GAPDH (assay ID: Mm99999915_g1) were used for mouse samples.

## Western blot
The cells or mouse tissues were lysed by RIPA buffer (R0278, Sigma-Aldrich) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 10 min. Then the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The total protein concentration in the supernatants was measured by Bio-Rad protein assay (500,006, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The proteins were separated based on molecular weight using SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (10600023, GE Healthcare, IL). The membrane was blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at RT and then incubated with the primary antibodies against COL1A1 (1: 500, sc-293182, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), fibronectin (1: 500, sc-8422, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), α-SMA (1:100, ab7817, Abcam, Waltham, MA), β-tubulin (1:3,000, T8328, Sigma-Aldrich) and β-actin (1:100, sc-47778, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking. The membranes were then washed with Tris-buffered saline (1706435, Bio-Rad, CA) with 0.05% Tween 20 (1610781, Bio-Rad) (TBS-T) three times. The membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody, m-lgGk BP-HRP (1:1,000, sc-516102, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h at RT. The membranes were washed with TBS-T three times. Chemiluminescent substrate mixture (34579, Thermo Fisher) was added to the membrane. The chemiluminescence signals were measured by ChemDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

## IHC staining
IHC staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections. The FFPE tissue sections were de-paraffinized and rehydrated. After antigen retrieval and blocking, the FFPE tissue sections were incubated with the primary antibody against α-SMA (1:1,000, ab7817, Abcam), followed by the secondary antibody (HRP.mouse, K400111-2, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). After DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining, the FFPE tissue sections were imaged using ScanScope XT (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA).
