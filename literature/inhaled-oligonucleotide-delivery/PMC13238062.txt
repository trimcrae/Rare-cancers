## Background
Medicinal animals are integral to traditional healthcare worldwide, yet ethnozoological knowledge in many Ethiopian regions remains under-documented. This study documented the diversity of medicinal animals, associated cultural knowledge, preparation methods, and conservation implications among communities in Andracha District, Ethiopia.

## Methods
A cross-sectional ethnobiological survey was conducted from January to August 2025 using semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions (FGDs), and field observations. Sixty-five informants participated, including 25 key informants purposively selected and 40 general participants randomly chosen. FGDs refined data collection instruments and validated traditional knowledge. Quantitative indices Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), Fidelity Level (FL), and Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index (ZEI) summarized knowledge patterns, species importance, and cultural reliability. Jaccard Similarity Index (JSI) and Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI) assessed cross-community similarity. Statistical analyses, including t-tests, ANOVA, and linear regression, explored variations across gender, age, education, and experience using R software.

## Results
A total of 52 medicinal animal species were documented in Andracha District. Bos taurus, Apis mellifera, and Halictus scabiosae exhibited the highest RFC values. ICF ranged from 0.60 to 0.97, with the strongest agreement observed for respiratory and gastrointestinal ailments. The highest FL was recorded for Capra aegagrus hircus (FL = 83.3%), followed by Apis mellifera (FL = 77.8%). Knowledge of medicinal animals varied significantly with age, gender, literacy, and healer experience (P < 0.05). Major threats to medicinal fauna included agricultural expansion, overgrazing, and overexploitation, while indigenous conservation practices such as sacred forest protection and community stewardship were actively maintained.

## Conclusion
Medicinal animals are crucial for healthcare, cultural identity, and livelihoods in Andracha District. Environmental pressures and generational gaps threaten both species and associated knowledge. Strengthening community-based conservation, sustainable harvesting, and intergenerational knowledge transmission is essential to safeguard this biocultural heritage. The study documents traditional practices but does not endorse ingestion or application of potentially hazardous animal products.

## Background
Ethnozoology, a branch of ethnobiology, examines the complex and evolving relationships between humans and animals [ , ]. These interactions are particularly important in traditional healthcare systems, where animals and their products contribute not only to medicine but also to livelihoods, cultural identity, and daily survival. Globally, animals have long served as accessible and culturally trusted remedies for human and livestock ailments [ , ], with the World Health Organization (WHO) estimating that nearly 80% of the global population relies on traditional medicine, including animal-based ingredients, as a primary healthcare source [ , ].
In Ethiopia, traditional healing practices that integrate both plant and animal resources remain widely practiced and deeply embedded in community life [ , ]. Approximately 80% of Ethiopians rely on traditional medicine, particularly in rural areas with limited access to modern healthcare [ , ]. Within this system, zootherapy the therapeutic use of animals and their derivatives represents a vital but under-documented aspect of indigenous knowledge. Related concepts include medicinal animals (species used in healthcare) and zootherapeutic practices (preparation, application, and culturally informed use of animal products), which are operationally defined in the Materials and Methods section.
Recent reviews report at least 112 animal species in Ethiopia used to treat over 167 ailments, with mammals, birds, reptiles, and arthropods among the most commonly utilized groups [ , ]. For instance, studies from Kucha District, southern Ethiopia, highlight the continued importance of animal-based remedies alongside herbal treatments in local healthcare [ ]. Despite this cultural and medical significance, ethnozoological knowledge faces threats from biodiversity loss, erosion of intergenerational knowledge transfer, and increasing reliance on modern medicine, undermining community identity and sustainable resource management.
Andracha District, located within the Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve (SFBR), hosts diverse ethnic communities and rich wildlife that support long-standing medicinal traditions. However, zootherapeutic practices in this area remain poorly documented. Systematic recording of this knowledge contributes to cultural preservation and supports several United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs): SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), SDG 15 (Life on Land), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 4 (Quality Education), and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production).
This study aims to document and evaluate ethnozoological knowledge of medicinal animals in Andracha District, Southwest Ethiopia, with the following objectives: (i) identify medicinal animal species and their products used to treat human ailments, including the animal parts, preparation methods, administration routes, and associated cultural beliefs; (ii) examine patterns of knowledge variation across socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, education, and healing experience, as well as among different ethnic groups in the district; (iii) assess conservation and sustainability issues, including major threats to medicinal fauna, indigenous protective measures, and the long-term viability of zootherapeutic practices; (iv) compare and contextualize medicinal animal use, evaluating similarities and unique contributions relative to previous Ethiopian ethnozoological studies using similarity indices.
The study tests the hypotheses that: (i) medicinal animal diversity in Andracha District differs from other Ethiopian regions; (ii) socio-demographic factors strongly influence ethnozoological knowledge; (iii) frequently cited species hold greater cultural importance and availability than rarely cited species; (iv) ethnic background shapes medicinal animal knowledge, leading to identifiable cross-group similarities and differences; and (v) local zootherapeutic traditions support sustainable healthcare, strengthen cultural continuity, and contribute to biodiversity conservation.
Documenting this indigenous knowledge is therefore crucial for safeguarding biocultural heritage, promoting responsible use of animal resources, and supporting national and international initiatives focused on biodiversity protection and the achievement of SDGs.

## Materials and methods

## Study area
Andracha District, in the Sheka Zone of Southwest Ethiopia, lies between 7°24′–7°52′ N latitude and 35°13′–35°35′ E longitude (Fig.  ). The district covers approximately 102,000 hectares and spans altitudinal ranges from about 1,001 to 3,000 m above sea level. Administratively, Andracha is divided into 17 kebeles, which are the smallest local government units in Ethiopia. Gecha serves as the only urban center in the district and is situated approximately 714 km southwest of Addis Ababa. Andracha shares boundaries with Masha District to the north, Gambela Region to the southwest, Oromia Region to the northwest, Yeki District to the south, and Kafa Zone to the east [ , ].

 

According to recent demographic reports, the district has a population of approximately 36,495 people, including 18,318 males and 18,177 females [ ]. The majority of residents rely primarily on subsistence agriculture combined with small-scale trading activities. Andracha lies within a biosphere reserve, receiving an average annual rainfall of about 2,074 mm, and monthly temperatures range from 12.6 °C to 31.2 °C, with a mean of approximately 20.6 °C [ ].
The Sheka ethnic group constitutes the dominant community, with Shekinono widely spoken as the main local language. In terms of religion, the majority of residents practice Protestant Christianity (68.55%), followed by Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity (19.96%), traditional belief systems (5.83%), and Islam (1.21%). The district is also home to other ethnic groups, including the Amhara, Oromo, and Tigrie, contributing to its cultural diversity.
Andracha District is located within the Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve, a region known for its rich biological resources. The reserve supports a diverse range of fauna, including 25 mammal species, 21 amphibians, 8 reptiles, 246 bird species, 49 butterfly species, and 19 epiphytic orchid species. The local economy is closely linked to agricultural production, particularly coffee and enset farming, as well as livestock rearing, including cattle, sheep, goats, and poultry [ ].

## Study design
This research was conducted as a cross-sectional ethnobiological survey employing a mixed-methods approach to document and analyze the use of medicinal animals in traditional healthcare in Andracha District, Ethiopia. The study integrated qualitative methods, including focus group discussions (FGDs) and participant observations, with quantitative individual interviews, capturing both the breadth and consistency of ethnozoological knowledge across communities.

## Reconnaissance, informant selection, and study site selection
A preliminary reconnaissance survey was carried out from 16 November to 29 December 2024 to identify suitable study sites within Andracha District and evaluate the effectiveness of data collection instruments. During this phase, 12 informants (7 men and 5 women), recognized by local elders as knowledgeable about medicinal animals, were consulted to provide initial insights into species used, local terminology, and traditional practices. This step ensured that the main survey instruments were culturally appropriate and comprehensive.
Following the reconnaissance, 8 kebeles were selected from the total 17 in Andracha District. Selection criteria included the presence of well-known traditional healers, accessibility of animal-based remedies, recognition by community members, and recommendations from local elders and administrative authorities. This approach ensured that study sites with active zootherapeutic practices were represented while maintaining relevance to the broader district context.
For the main survey, 65 informants (34 men and 31 women), aged 20–78 years, were recruited using a combination of purposive and random sampling. Twenty-five key informants, primarily experienced traditional healers, were identified through recommendations from community elders, development agents, and local authorities. The remaining 40 participants were selected randomly using a lottery method from households within the selected kebeles, capturing broader community-level knowledge about medicinal animals.
In terms of age distribution, the majority of highly knowledgeable informants belonged to the 51–78-year age group (32 individuals, 49.2%), followed by participants aged 31–50 years (21 individuals, 32.3%), and the youngest group, 20–30 years (12 individuals, 18.5%). Regarding educational status, most informants were non-literate (39 participants, 60%), whereas 26 individuals (40%) had attended primary, secondary, or higher education (Table  ).
This combined purposive-random sampling approach enabled documentation of both specialized and general ethnobiological knowledge, providing a robust basis for descriptive analyses of medicinal animal use. Given the limited sample size and single-district focus, inferential statistics were applied cautiously, focusing primarily on descriptive trends rather than broad generalizations.

 

## Ethnozoological data collection
Ethnozoological data were collected between January and August 2025 from 65 informants using the local Shekenono language. Individual interviews recorded participants’ gender, age, residence, common human and livestock illnesses, local names of medicinal animals, species used, animal parts or products utilized, preparation techniques, routes of administration, conservation practices, and non-therapeutic uses [ – ]. Participants were also asked about threats to medicinal animals and other cultural practices associated with these species.
Data collection was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, focus group discussions (FGDs) were carried out with purposively selected traditional healers and knowledgeable community members recognized for their experience in ethnomedicine. A total of eight FGDs were conducted across selected kebeles. Each group consisted of 6–10 participants (mean ± SD: 8.1 ± 1.3 participants per group), ensuring representation of both genders and a range of experience levels. Participants were selected based on their reputation as traditional healers, recommendations from local elders, and willingness to share knowledge. FGDs aimed to document medicinal animal species, treated ailments, parts used, preparation methods, and application practices, while also capturing shared cultural perspectives.
Insights from FGDs were used to refine and develop a semi-structured checklist and interview guide in Shekenono. In the second stage, this checklist was administered through individual interviews across all study sites. FGD data were used exclusively for qualitative purposes and were not merged with individual interview data for the calculation of quantitative ethnozoological indices. Only data obtained from individual interviews were used for quantitative analyses.
To assess informants’ knowledge, a knowledge score was calculated as the total number of distinct medicinal animal species cited per participant, normalized by the maximum number of species recorded across all participants. These scores were used for descriptive and inferential statistical analyses, including independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA, to examine variations by gender, age, and education level.
Data reliability was ensured by re-interviewing participants after an average interval of 7 ± 2 days between the initial and follow-up sessions. Responses were cross-checked for consistency, and 12 statements deemed unreliable or inconsistent with verified information were excluded from quantitative analyses. All interviews and FGDs were conducted at convenient times, with informed consent obtained from participants. Informants were assured that their indigenous knowledge would remain confidential, and the study was conducted solely for documentation purposes without promoting the use of potentially harmful animal-derived remedies.

## Specimen collection and Identification
Medicinal animal specimens were collected with the support of community elders and local authorities, adhering to ethical, institutional, and national research regulations. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Mizan Tepi University (Permit No. MTU/IRB/2024/07). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to specimen collection.
Where feasible, sample specimens were collected, labeled, assigned a voucher number, and preserved appropriately for taxonomic classification (Supplementary Table S1). When physical collection was not possible, high-resolution photographs were taken. Local names, reported uses, and identifying features were meticulously documented. Specimens requiring further verification were transported to Mizan Tepi University (MTU) under research permit MTU/RC/2024/03, authorized by the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority.
Taxonomic identification at MTU was performed using standard keys, illustrations, and reference collections for Ethiopian fauna, including mammals, birds, reptiles, and arthropods. Formal verification was completed by Abel Mandefro. Voucher specimens were deposited at the Department of Biology, Mizan Tepi University under the code MTU002025, ensuring future accessibility.

## Ethical considerations
All research activities adhered to the ethical guidelines of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics. Formal ethical clearance was obtained from the MTU Institutional Review Board (Permit No. MTU/IRB/2024/07), and official permission for fieldwork and specimen collection was granted by the Andracha District Administration (Permit No. ANDR/ETH/2024/05) and the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority (Permit No. EWCA/2024/12).
Informed consent was secured from all participants, who were explicitly informed that the study’s objectives were purely academic, aiming to document the cultural and medicinal significance of animal-based remedies, with no commercial or exploitative intent. Participants voluntarily shared their knowledge after understanding the study’s aims, risks, and benefits.
The study strictly documents traditional practices and does not endorse the ingestion or topical application of potentially hazardous animal products, including raw meat, blood, or human excreta. All fieldwork and specimen handling were conducted to minimize harm to animals and the environment, ensuring compliance with local and national conservation regulations.

## Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index
The Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index (ZEI) was applied to measure both the depth and distribution of ethnozoological knowledge among participants, allowing meaningful comparisons between groups living in similar ecological and faunal contexts [ ]. This index considers several factors, including: (i) the total number of animal species reported by each group, (ii) the average number of species cited per participant, (iii) the average number of citations per species, (iv) the number of participants in each group, and (v) the total number of species documented across all groups in the study [ ].
ZEI values range from just above 0 to a theoretical maximum of 2. Higher values reflect a broader and more detailed understanding of medicinal animal use within a group, while values approaching 2 though uncommon typically indicate particularly specialized or culturally distinct knowledge relative to other groups. ## Preference ranking
Preference ranking was conducted with eight key informants to evaluate the perceived effectiveness of medicinal animals in treating specific human and livestock health conditions. Each informant assigned a score to each species on a scale of 1 to 5, where 5 indicated the highest perceived efficacy and 1 the lowest.
The individual scores for each species were then summed across all informants to determine its overall ranking. This approach allowed the identification of medicinal animals most valued by the community for particular ailments, reflecting local ethnozoological priorities and therapeutic preferences [ – ].

## Data analysis
Data collected from semi-structured interviews, field observations, and ranking exercises were analyzed using a combination of qualitative and quantitative approaches. Qualitative data were examined through thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns and key themes regarding the use of medicinal animals. Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and ranges. In addition, specialized ethnobiological indices, including Fidelity Level (FL), Informants’ Consensus Factor (ICF), Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index (ZEI), Jaccard Similarity Index (JSI), and Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI), were applied to explore usage patterns and cultural similarities across communities. These indices were specifically chosen for their relevance to the study objectives [ , – , ].
All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances was assessed with Levene’s test. Given the qualitative nature of most ethnobiological data and the limited sample size (n = 65), inferential statistics were applied cautiously and primarily to explore descriptive trends rather than to draw broad generalizations.
For inferential analyses, the dependent variable was informants’ ethnobiological knowledge, operationalized as the knowledge score (i.e., the number of medicinal animal species reported per participant, normalized by the maximum number recorded in the study). The independent variables included socio-demographic factors such as gender, educational level, healing experience, and age group.
Gender differences in medicinal animal knowledge (independent variable: gender; dependent variable: knowledge score) were assessed using independent samples t-tests (or Welch t-tests where variance homogeneity was violated), with Cohen’s d reported as the effect size and 95% confidence intervals provided. Similarly, differences in knowledge based on educational level (independent variable: education category) and healing experience (independent variable: healer vs. non-healer or experience category) were evaluated using independent t-tests (or Welch t-tests), including effect sizes and confidence intervals.
For comparisons among multiple age categories (independent variable: age group; dependent variable: knowledge score), one-way ANOVA was applied (or Welch ANOVA when homogeneity of variance assumptions were not met), followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests with 95% confidence intervals.
Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between age as a continuous variable (independent variable: age in years) and ethnobiological knowledge (dependent variable: number of medicinal animal species reported), with correlation coefficients (r) and associated 95% confidence intervals reported.
To reduce the risk of Type I errors due to multiple testing across variables and indices, Bonferroni correction or false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was applied as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. This analytical approach provided a careful balance between descriptive reporting and cautious inferential analysis, respecting both the sample size and the qualitative nature of ethnobiological data, while ensuring robust and reliable insights into the use of medicinal animals in the study area.

## Results

## Distribution and overlap of traditional medicinal animal knowledge among ethnic groups
A total of 52 medicinal animal species were documented across the three ethnic groups in Andracha District. The number of informants contributing data from each group was as follows: Sheka (n = 24), Kaffa (n = 23), and Amhara (n = 18). The distribution of medicinal animal knowledge varied among these groups, reflecting both unique and shared traditional practices.
In this study, unique species were defined as medicinal animal species reported exclusively by informants from a single ethnic group, regardless of the specific use. Conversely, shared species were defined based on species presence across groups, meaning that a species was considered shared if it was reported by two or more ethnic groups, irrespective of differences in use, preparation, or application.
Based on this definition, the Sheka ethnic group reported 10 species used exclusively within their community, while the Kaffa and Amhara groups identified 6 and 4 unique species, respectively (Fig.  ).

 

Analysis of overlapping knowledge revealed notable intersections among the ethnic groups. The Sheka and Kaffa groups shared 7 species, Sheka and Amhara shared 5 species, and Kaffa and Amhara shared 3 species. Additionally, 17 species were commonly reported by all three groups. These overlaps suggest that, alongside distinct knowledge systems, there is considerable exchange of ethnozoological knowledge, likely facilitated by geographical proximity, inter-community interactions, and shared ecological resources.
Among the groups, the Sheka exhibited the highest number of unique medicinal species, indicating a comparatively richer or more specialized ethnozoological knowledge base. The 17 species shared across all groups highlight common traditional practices that may represent culturally conserved knowledge with potential relevance for pharmacological research and biodiversity conservation.
The Venn diagram illustrating these relationships (Fig.  ) was generated using R software (version 4.3.2), employing the VennDiagram package to visualize the distribution and overlap of medicinal animal species among the three ethnic groups.

## Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index across study sites
As shown in Table  , the adjusted ZEI values ranged from 0.061 to 0.289, indicating notable variation in the depth and distribution of knowledge related to medicinal animals across the study sites. The highest ZEI was recorded in Modi (0.289), suggesting that participants in this site reported a relatively higher number of species per individual and demonstrated more extensive ethnozoological knowledge.
Similarly, Gebina (0.262), Getiba (0.210), and Beshifa (0.170) exhibited comparatively higher ZEI values, reflecting substantial ethnobiological knowledge within these communities.
Lower ZEI values were recorded in Gemadro (0.126), Gey (0.108), Shebena (0.079), and Chegacha (0.061), indicating groups where participants cited fewer species per individual and had relatively more limited knowledge of ethnozoological resources.

 

## Species diversity of zootherapy animals in the study area
In total, 52 medicinal animal species were recorded in the study area, including 41 vertebrates and 11 invertebrates, as reported by traditional healers and local community members (Fig.  ). Among the vertebrates, mammals were the most commonly used group, followed by birds, reptiles, and fish (Table  ).

 

The invertebrate species were primarily arthropods, representing 10 species, alongside a single annelid species. This diversity highlights the broad range of animal resources utilized in traditional healthcare practices within the Andracha District and reflects the extensive ethnozoological knowledge held by the local communities.

 

## Medicinal animals and their parts or products used for treating human ailments
The documented animal products were used to address over 45 different human ailments (Table  ). Remedies were prepared from a variety of animal parts and products, reflecting the rich and diverse ethnozoological knowledge maintained by local communities.
Among the materials used, animal products such as honey, eggs, milk, blood, and teeth were the most commonly utilized, representing 26.9% of the total. Meat accounted for 25% of the remedies, while the use of whole animals contributed 13.5%. Animal excreta, including feces and urine, made up 15.3% of the treatments, and external body parts such as skin, hair, fur, feathers, legs, soles, horns, spurs, and eyelashes were used in 7.6% of cases. Less frequently employed materials included visceral organs (5.7%), musk (1.9%), leech heads (1.9%), and combinations of meat and bone (1.9%).

## Methods of remedy preparation
In the study area, traditional remedies were prepared using a variety of techniques, showcasing the rich and diverse approaches practiced by local communities. Overall, nine distinct preparation methods were recorded.
The most commonly employed method was the direct use of animal parts or products, which accounted for 24.8% of the remedies. This was followed by methods involving drying, powdering, and mixing with other ingredients (16.2%), and drying alone (17.9%). Cooking, as well as preparing soups or stews, each contributed 11.1% of the remedies. Less frequently applied techniques included drying and powdering on its own, or warming/melting materials, each representing 5.98% of the preparations. The least common approaches were drying combined with smoking or burning, and the use of fresh or raw materials mixed with other ingredients, with each method making up 3.4% of the remedies.

## Preferred condition of medicinal animals
Traditional healers in the study area demonstrated distinct preferences regarding the condition of animal materials used in remedies. Most remedies were prepared using fresh animal parts or products, accounting for 66 instances. Dried materials were employed in 29 cases, while a combination of both fresh and dried materials was used in 22 cases (Fig.  ).

 

## Indigenous knsowledge on the mode of application of medicinal animals
In Andracha District, the way traditional remedies derived from animals are applied depends on both the ailment being treated and the specific animal part or product used. Our study documented 11 different methods of administration, highlighting the rich indigenous knowledge surrounding the delivery of these treatments.
Oral administration was the most commonly practiced route, particularly for fresh solid and liquid remedies. This included eating (42.3%) and drinking (15.4%) animal-based medicines. Topical or dermal application was the second most frequent approach, involving techniques such as anointing, tying, smearing, massaging, and fumigation, which allow the medicinal compounds to act directly on the skin. Less commonly used methods included inhalation and other specialized practices such as dropping, bathing, snuffing, cleaning, and smoking (Fig.  ).

 

## Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) of medicinal animals in Andracha District
To understand the significance of different animal species in traditional healthcare, the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) index was used. This index measures how often each species was mentioned by local informants during ethnomedicinal interviews, providing insight into the prominence of specific animals in community practices.
Among the documented species, cattle (Bos taurus) were the most frequently cited, with an RFC of 0.97, indicating near-universal recognition among respondents. Bees also played a central role in traditional remedies, with the honey bee (Apis mellifera) cited by approximately 89% of informants (RFC = 0.89) and stingless bees (Halictus scabiosae) mentioned by 80% (RFC = 0.80). Other commonly cited animals included goat (Capra aegagrus hircus, RFC = 0.69), hen (Gallus gallus domesticus, RFC = 0.63), and the spotted hyena (Crocuta crocuta, RFC = 0.52) (Table  ).
Less frequently cited species included humans (Homo sapiens, RFC = 0.43), crested porcupine (Hystrix cristata, RFC = 0.34), fish species (Oreochromis niloticus, RFC = 0.29), and bat (Myotis morrisi, RFC = 0.23).

 

## Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) of medicinal animal use in Andracha District
The study revealed that the importance of medicinal animals varies across different disease categories in Andracha District. The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), which measures the degree of agreement among informants regarding the use of animal species for specific ailments, ranged from 0.60 to 0.97 (Table  ).
To ensure consistency and transparency in disease classification, all reported ailments were grouped into standardized categories using a predefined category dictionary (Supplementary Table S2). This dictionary specifies the criteria for assigning local illness terms to broader biomedical categories and was developed based on commonly used ethnobiological classification frameworks and contextual adaptation to local terminology.
Among the disease categories, respiratory diseases and gastrointestinal disorders exhibited the highest consensus (ICF = 0.97), indicating strong agreement among informants on the animal species used to treat these conditions. This high level of consensus suggests well-established and widely shared traditional knowledge within the community. Similarly, relatively high ICF values were observed for dermatological conditions (0.93), infectious and febrile diseases (0.90), and musculoskeletal disorders (0.88), further reflecting consistency in ethnomedicinal practices.
Moderate levels of agreement were recorded for neurological and mental disorders (0.84), ophthalmological disorders (0.80), and injuries and external conditions (0.77). These values may indicate a broader range of animal species used or variation in knowledge among informants for these categories.
In contrast, poisoning and bites (ICF = 0.71) and socio-cultural and spiritual uses (ICF = 0.60) showed the lowest consensus values. The relatively low agreement in socio-cultural and spiritual applications may be attributed to the secretive nature of traditional beliefs, limited knowledge sharing among healers, and the use of diverse animal species for such purposes.
It is important to note that the number of use reports (Nur) in several categories appears high relative to the total number of informants (n = 65). This is because multiple use reports per informant were permitted and systematically recorded. Each instance in which an informant cited a specific animal species for treating a particular ailment was counted as a separate use report. Consequently, a single informant could contribute multiple reports across different disease categories and species, or even multiple uses of the same species for different ailments. This approach is standard in ethnobiological research and allows for a more comprehensive assessment of knowledge distribution and use diversity, thereby contributing to higher Nur values without inflating the number of participants.

 

## Fidelity Levels (FL) of medicinal animals in Andracha District
Fidelity Level (FL) is used to assess the relative importance of a medicinal animal species for treating a specific ailment, by measuring the proportion of informants who agree on its use for that particular condition. In this study, FL values ranged from 31.4% to 83.3%, indicating varying levels of agreement among informants for different species–ailment combinations (Table  ).
The highest FL value was recorded for Capra aegagrus hircus (goat) (FL = 83.3%) for a specific principal ailment, suggesting a strong preference and high consensus among informants regarding its use for that condition. Similarly, Apis mellifera (honey bee) showed a relatively high FL (77.8%), indicating that it is consistently used for a particular ailment and is widely recognized for its therapeutic importance.
Moderate FL values were observed for species such as Halictus scabiosae (60.0%), Oreochromis niloticus (61.9%), and Bos taurus (55.0%). These values suggest that although these species are important in traditional medicine, they are used for a broader range of ailments, resulting in lower consensus for any single condition.
Lower FL values were recorded for Gallus gallus domesticus (40.7%), Crocuta crocuta (38.5%), Hystrix cristata (36.4%), and Myotis morrisi (31.4%). These lower values indicate less agreement among informants, which may be attributed to the multipurpose use of these species or variability in traditional knowledge and practices.

 

## Preference ranking of medicinal animals for asthma treatment in Andracha District
To understand local perceptions of therapeutic efficacy, the study assessed how eight key informants ranked selected medicinal animals used for managing asthma. Informants assigned scores ranging from 1 (least preferred) to 5 (most preferred), reflecting their assessment of each species’ effectiveness (Table  ).
The results indicated that bee-derived products were highly valued in asthma treatment. Halictus scabiosae (stingless bees) and Apis mellifera (honey bees) received the highest total scores, showing strong consensus among informants regarding their effectiveness.
Several mammalian species were also recognized for their medicinal value. Hystrix cristata (crested porcupine) ranked third, followed by Phacochoerus africanus (common warthog) in fourth place. Moderately ranked species included Procavia capensis (rock hyrax) and Pternistis clappertoni (Clapperton’s francolin), reflecting their acknowledged, though relatively lower, role in asthma management.
Other species, such as Equus africanus asinus (donkey), were ranked lower, suggesting limited use or perceived lower efficacy. Overall, these rankings illustrate a clear pattern of local preference and knowledge regarding the therapeutic potential of different animal species for asthma treatment.

 

## Traditional methods of preserving medicinal animals and the use of additives
In the Andracha District, local communities employ a variety of techniques to preserve medicinal animals and their products. According to key informants, wrapping in cloth sheets emerged as the most commonly used method, followed by hanging under roofs and storage in clay containers. These practices reflect practical strategies for maintaining the efficacy and longevity of animal-based remedies.
Additives are frequently incorporated into traditional remedies to improve taste, reduce potential side effects, or enhance therapeutic effects. The choice of additive often depends on the species, the specific animal part or product used, and the ailment being treated. Commonly used additives include water, butter, honey, oils, spices, garlic, cereals, and select medicinal plants, illustrating a nuanced understanding of how these substances interact with animal-derived remedies.

## Preference ranking of medicinal animal preservation methods
The study also explored local preferences for different preservation methods. Ten key informants ranked methods based on practicality and effectiveness, using a scale from 2 (less used) to 5 (best).
Cloth sheets were ranked highest, with a total score of 44, likely due to their accessibility, ease of use, and ability to maintain the medicinal qualities of animal products (Table  ). Roof hanging was the second most preferred method, scoring 39, reflecting its widespread acceptance as a reliable preservation approach. Clay containers ranked third (total score = 31), offering protection against moisture and pests while preserving the stored materials.
Methods such as plastic bags and sealed bottles were less preferred, ranking fourth (score = 20) and fifth (score = 17), respectively. This lower preference may stem from limited availability, cultural practices, or perceptions of reduced effectiveness in maintaining the therapeutic properties of medicinal animals.

 

## Jaccard Similarity Index
To place the ethnomedicinal knowledge recorded in Andracha District in context, the Jaccard Similarity Index (JSI) was used to compare the current list of medicinal animal species with findings from 13 previous studies across Ethiopia (Table  ). The resulting JSI values varied, highlighting differences in the degree of overlap in traditional zoological knowledge among regions.
The highest similarities were observed with Wolaita (37.21%), followed by Dangila (36.00%) and Assosa (33.33%). These relatively high values suggest that communities in these areas share comparable traditions in the use of medicinal animals, likely influenced by cultural connections, geographic proximity, or shared ethnomedical practices. This pattern indicates that southwestern and northwestern Ethiopian communities maintain notable consistency in medicinal animal utilization.
Moderate similarity values were found with national-scale studies in Ethiopia (32.53%), Kucha (30.00%), Metema (29.09%), and Hulet Eju Enessie (25.00%). These results suggest partial overlap, where some widely recognized therapeutic species are used across regions, while local cultural or ecological factors shape distinctive practices.
Lower similarities were documented with Gamo (20.00%), West Gojjam (14.81%), Kafta-Humera (12.00%), and Leka Dullecha (11.76%), indicating that these areas have more unique medicinal animal traditions with minimal overlap with Andracha. Such differences likely reflect ecological variation, cultural divergence, or different levels of reliance on animal-based remedies within these communities.

 

## Rahman’s Similarity Index
To gain a deeper understanding of how ethnomedicinal animal knowledge in Andracha District aligns with other regions of Ethiopia, Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI) was applied. Unlike the Jaccard Index, which only considers shared species, RSI examines the overlap in how these animals are used for specific therapeutic purposes, providing a more nuanced view of common practices.
The RSI values across the comparative studies were generally low, ranging from 3.28% to 11.11% (Table  ), suggesting that while some medicinal animals are found in multiple regions, their uses and cultural significance often differ. The highest similarity was observed with Hulet Eju Enessie (10.00%), followed by Metema (8.75%), Gamo (8.62%), and Dangur (8.33%). These higher values indicate some shared ethnomedicinal knowledge, likely influenced by cultural connections, ecological similarities, or comparable health challenges that shape local practices.
Moderate similarity was found with Dangila (7.50%), Assosa (7.14%), Wolaita (7.14%), and Kucha (6.67%), reflecting partial overlap in species and their applications. This suggests that while certain widely recognized medicinal animals are used across these regions, local preparation methods and specific indications often remain unique to each community.
At the lower end, Ethiopia’s national-level dataset (5.38%), West Gojjam (4.48%), Kafta-Humera (3.33%), and Leka Dullecha (3.28%) showed minimal overlap. These low RSI values highlight substantial differences in therapeutic practices and the cultural traditions surrounding animal-based remedies, likely due to ecological diversity, ethnic variation, and differing reliance on local faunal resources.

 

## Distribution of medicinal animal knowledge among informant groups
The study examined variations in knowledge of medicinal animals among different informant groups in Andracha District, considering factors such as gender, literacy, experience, and age (Table  ).
Results revealed a pronounced gender difference in ethnomedicinal knowledge. Male participants demonstrated significantly higher familiarity with medicinal animals, with a mean knowledge score of 5.1 ± 1.4, compared to females, who had a mean score of 2.2 ± 1.2 (t = 8.3, P < 0.05).
Literacy was associated with differences in knowledge distribution. Informants without formal education (illiterate) defined as individuals who had not received formal schooling and were unable to read or write in any language reported higher levels of medicinal animal knowledge (mean = 4.1 ± 1.9) compared with literate informants (mean = 2.2 ± 1.2), suggesting that traditional medicinal knowledge is more deeply retained among non-literate community members (t = 4.7, P < 0.05).
Experience emerged as another key determinant of knowledge. Key informants, selected for their expertise in traditional healthcare, exhibited substantially greater knowledge (mean = 12.8 ± 2.8) than general informants (mean = 2.2 ± 1.3), reflecting the importance of specialized experience and long-term engagement in ethnozoological practices (t = 14.1, P < 0.05).
Age was strongly associated with medicinal animal knowledge. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship (r = 0.67, P < 0.001), suggesting that older community members generally possess more extensive knowledge of ethnozoological practices (Fig.  ). Linear regression analysis (B₀ = -0.79, B₁ = 0.1) further confirmed that medicinal animal knowledge tends to increase with age, highlighting the cumulative nature of traditional knowledge acquired through experiential learning.

 

Additionally, a one-way ANOVA comparing knowledge across age groups showed significant differences (F = 36.72, P < 0.05), reinforcing the conclusion that age is a major factor influencing the depth of ethnomedicinal knowledge in Andracha District (Table  ).

 

 

## Transmission of indigenous knowledge on medicinal animals
Traditional animal-based medicine continues to be a cornerstone of primary healthcare among indigenous communities in Ethiopia. In Andracha District, medicinal animals are relatively accessible, and traditional healers are widely available, making these remedies a popular choice due to their affordability, availability, and deep cultural significance compared to modern pharmaceuticals.
However, this invaluable knowledge is at risk of being lost. Much of ethnozoological knowledge is passed down orally from one generation to the next, and most traditional healers are elderly, often over 55 years old. Globalization, cultural change, and religious influences have further disrupted the transmission of these practices. These factors underscore the urgent need to systematically document indigenous medicinal knowledge to ensure its preservation for future generations.

## Medicinal animal popularity and bio-cultural significance
In Andracha District, medicinal animals hold substantial bio-cultural value beyond their therapeutic roles. Communities utilize these animals in various aspects of daily life, social activities, and cultural traditions.
Animal products are frequently employed in clothing and ceremonial contexts. Skins, hides, and other parts are fashioned into garments used during festivals, mourning rituals, and other culturally significant events, serving both practical and symbolic purposes.
Medicinal animals are also integral to music, art, and household practices. Skins are used to construct drums and other instruments, while some parts function as protective covers for tools or as tableware. This highlights the multifunctional nature of many species, fulfilling therapeutic, artistic, and practical needs.
Certain animal parts also contribute to household decoration and ritual practices. Products are used to adorn traditional homes, participate in religious or cultural ceremonies, or serve as charms and talismans, further reflecting their cultural importance.

## Threats and conservation of medicinal animals
Key informants identified a range of anthropogenic and environmental threats affecting medicinal animals in Andracha District (Table  ). Although the district is part of the Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO-recognized conservation landscape, medicinal fauna face multiple pressures.
To assess the relative importance of these threats, a priority ranking method was applied. Ten key informants (R1–R10) independently scored each threat on a scale of 1 to 5, where higher scores indicate greater perceived severity or impact. Total scores for each threat were calculated by summing individual informant rankings, and threats were subsequently ordered, with higher total scores reflecting higher perceived risk.
According to this ranking, agricultural expansion posed the greatest threat (total score = 46), followed by overgrazing (41), overexploitation (36), climate change (26), and construction activities (23). Expansion of small-scale and commercial coffee farming was highlighted as the primary driver of habitat loss and fragmentation, reducing crucial habitats for medicinal species. Overgrazing by livestock further degrades forest edges, while overexploitation through hunting or collection for medicinal or subsistence use represents a moderate threat. Climate variability, including irregular rainfall and temperature fluctuations and construction activities contribute additional pressures, although these were ranked lower.
Despite these challenges, medicinal animals in Andracha District remain relatively secure, largely due to indigenous conservation strategies employed by the Shekacho community. These include sacred forest protection, taboos against unnecessary killing, and community-based forest management practices, which collectively help maintain medicinal animal populations and mitigate human–wildlife conflicts.
For transparency and reproducibility, the informant-level ranking matrix (individual scores from R1–R10) is provided in Table  , allowing verification of total scores and rankings.

 

## Implications of medicinal animal use in Andracha District
The widespread use of medicinal animals in Andracha District highlights their vital role in supporting the primary healthcare of local communities. These remedies remain highly valued because they are accessible, affordable, and culturally accepted, often serving as either an alternative or a complement to modern medicine especially in areas where formal healthcare is limited. This reliance underscores the need to preserve traditional ethnozoological knowledge to maintain community well-being.
Medicinal animals also carry deep cultural and socio-economic significance. Many species are central to rituals, ceremonies, and everyday life, fulfilling symbolic, spiritual, and practical roles. Their use strengthens cultural identity, fosters social cohesion, and ensures the transmission of knowledge across generations. Recognizing these values is essential for developing culturally sensitive conservation strategies that respect both local needs and traditions.
At the same time, the growing demand for medicinal animals raises important conservation concerns. Overharvesting, habitat loss, and pressures from agricultural expansion and settlement can threaten species survival. Without careful management, the decline of key medicinal species could disrupt ecological balance and compromise traditional healthcare practices.
This study emphasizes the importance of documenting indigenous knowledge and incorporating it into wider conservation and public health efforts. By identifying priority species and promoting sustainable practices—such as alternative preservation techniques, controlled harvesting, and habitat protection—policymakers and conservationists can help safeguard both biodiversity and cultural heritage. Such measures are crucial to ensure that medicinal animals remain available for future generations while maintaining the health and cultural richness of local communities.

## Discussion

## Distribution, overlap, and diversity of traditional medicinal animal knowledge in Andracha District
The variation in the Zoological Ethnoknowledge Index (ZEI) across kebeles (the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia) in Andracha District highlights differences in the retention and transmission of traditional medicinal-animal knowledge. Kebeles such as Modi and Gebina recorded the highest ZEI values, likely reflecting both ecological availability and cultural continuity, including proximity to relatively intact forests and reliance on wild fauna for healthcare. In contrast, lower ZEI in Chegacha, Shebena, Gey, and Gemadro may reflect habitat degradation, reduced ecological opportunity, or weaker intergenerational knowledge transmission, a pattern observed elsewhere in Ethiopia [ , , , ]. Cultural norms, such as taboos, sacred sites, and customary forest management, appear to safeguard both biodiversity and ethnomedicinal knowledge [ ].
Across the district, medicinal animal species were documented among the Sheka, Kaffa, and Amhara ethnic groups, comprising both vertebrates and invertebrates. The Sheka communities reported the highest number of unique species, followed by the Kaffa and Amhara groups, while several species were shared across all groups, illustrating both localized knowledge systems and interethnic knowledge exchange. Mammals dominated, followed by birds, reptiles, and fish, whereas invertebrates were mainly arthropods. This taxonomic distribution mirrors patterns in other Ethiopian and East African studies, reflecting therapeutic, symbolic, and practical considerations [ , , , , , – ]. Birds, reptiles, and fish addressed respiratory, gastrointestinal, dermatological, and spiritual ailments [ , , , , – ], while invertebrates, particularly bees, beetles, and spiders, were important for infections and inflammation [ – ].
Forest-adjacent kebeles preserved broader medicinal-animal repertoires, whereas agriculturally dominant areas exhibited contracted knowledge. The 17 widely recognized species suggest convergence due to ecological availability, perceived efficacy, and cultural importance, while unique species indicate localized ecological niches and specialized knowledge.
The study raises important conservation and ethical considerations. Many species are harvested through hunting or use of body parts, potentially contributing to overexploitation and habitat loss. While communities preserve animal by-products using techniques such as cloth wrapping, roof hanging, and clay storage, the conservation status of the species themselves is often overlooked. Sustainable practices, including prioritizing abundant species, non-lethal harvesting, and respecting traditional taboos, are essential for balancing healthcare needs with biodiversity conservation [ , , – ].
Thus, the findings demonstrate the interconnection of biodiversity, culture, and health in Andracha District. Rich ethnozoological knowledge highlights pharmacological potential, guides conservation planning, and underscores the importance of protecting both habitats and species. Preserving traditional practices ensures safe, effective, and culturally appropriate remedies while supporting ecological and social sustainability [ – ].

## Medicinal animal knowledge among informant groups and transmission of indigenous knowledge
The distribution of medicinal animal knowledge in Andracha District is strongly shaped by social, cultural, and experiential factors. Male informants exhibited significantly greater knowledge than females (P < 0.05), reflecting their more active roles in collecting, preparing, and administering animal-based remedies. This pattern aligns with ethnozoological studies from Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, where men often serve as primary healers, while women, despite managing household healthcare, have less direct engagement with animal products [ , , , , , , ].

Traditional knowledge is primarily transmitted orally, and formal education can inadvertently reduce reliance on these practices, emphasizing the need for community-based strategies to preserve indigenous knowledge.
Experience and age were critical determinants of expertise. Key informants and elders, who are more actively engaged in traditional healing, showed substantially greater knowledge (P < 0.05), with a strong positive correlation between age and medicinal animal knowledge (r = 0.67, P < 0.001). Similar trends have been observed in Ethiopia, South Asia, and China, reflecting the cumulative nature of ethnozoological knowledge acquired over prolonged experience [ , , , , , , ].
The transmission of indigenous knowledge remains critical for sustaining traditional healthcare. Communities rely on animal-based remedies for their accessibility, affordability, and cultural acceptance, often as first-line treatments for common ailments [ , , , , ]. However, the oral mode of transmission, coupled with an aging healer population and declining engagement among younger generations due to formal education, urban migration, and exposure to modern medicine, threatens continuity [ , , , , , , ]. Cultural and religious shifts further exacerbate knowledge erosion by reducing reliance on traditional remedies and limiting apprenticeship opportunities [ , , , , ].
Comparative studies indicate that participatory documentation, community-based training, and local knowledge repositories effectively safeguard ethnomedicinal practices while promoting sustainable use of medicinal animals [ , , ]. Applying such interventions in Andracha District could preserve indigenous knowledge as a dynamic resource, supporting healthcare, cultural identity, and biodiversity conservation. The findings underscore that the loss of medicinal animal knowledge would not only erode cultural heritage but also compromise local communities’ capacity to access affordable, ecologically grounded healthcare solutions.

## Medicinal animal popularity and bio-cultural value
In Andracha District, medicinal animals serve multifaceted roles that extend beyond healthcare to encompass cultural, artistic, and ritual dimensions. Their bio-cultural significance reflects the deep interconnection between communities and the environment, where animals contribute to health, social identity, and cultural expression. Similar multifunctionality has been reported in Wolaita, Gamo, and Dangila districts, highlighting the integration of animals into ceremonial and ritual practices [ , , ].
Animal-derived materials, including skins, hides, and feathers, are employed in festivals, mourning rituals, and other culturally significant events, demonstrating both symbolic and functional importance. Comparable patterns are documented across East Africa, where animal parts reinforce social cohesion and cultural identity while serving therapeutic purposes [ , , ]. Beyond ceremonial use, animals provide materials for musical instruments, protective coverings, and household items, illustrating sophisticated resource multifunctionality, a trend also observed in India and China [ , , ].
Animals also hold spiritual and symbolic significance as talismans, ritual objects, or household decorations. Certain species or their products are considered sacred or protective, indicating that therapeutic value is inseparable from cultural meaning. This aligns with studies in Europe and Africa, where medicinal animals function as cultural markers and protective agents in traditional communities [ , , , , ].
Hence, the popularity and bio-cultural value of medicinal animals in Andracha District underscore their holistic role in society, linking healthcare, cultural identity, and environmental stewardship. Recognizing these intertwined functions is critical for biodiversity conservation and cultural heritage preservation, supporting the sustainable use of medicinal animals for future generations [ , , ].

## Threats and conservation practices of medicinal animals
Despite being located within the UNESCO-recognized Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve, medicinal animals in Andracha District face multiple anthropogenic and environmental pressures. Agricultural expansion, particularly coffee plantations, was identified as the most severe threat, fragmenting habitats and reducing ecological niches. This trend mirrors findings across Ethiopia and East Africa, including Uganda and Tanzania, where farmland expansion compromises both biodiversity and ethnomedicinal practices [ , , , , , ]. Overgrazing along forest edges further degrades undergrowth and alters vegetation, diminishing the availability and quality of species used in traditional medicine, consistent with reports from Dangila and West Gojjam districts [ , , ].
Overexploitation through hunting and collection represents a moderate but persistent pressure, highlighting the tension between cultural use and conservation priorities a pattern observed elsewhere in Africa and Asia [ , , , ]. Emerging climate change impacts, including irregular rainfall and temperature shifts, further threaten species distribution, echoing challenges documented in India and China [ , , ]. Although construction activities were ranked lower, their cumulative effects contribute to habitat degradation.
Local conservation practices, however, demonstrate the potential for sustainable management. The Shekacho community enforces sacred forest protection, taboos against unnecessary killing, and community-based management, effectively safeguarding medicinal species. Comparable culturally embedded strategies in Wolaita, Gamo, and Assosa districts highlight how traditional ecological knowledge can preserve both biodiversity and ethnomedicinal resources [ , , ].
From a broader conservation perspective, reliance on lethal resources such as meat, whole bodies, and internal organs raises ethical and ecological concerns, including overexploitation and habitat loss [ , , ]. Conversely, non-lethal products like honey, milk, and eggs, particularly when combined with preservation techniques, offer sustainable alternatives that balance human health needs with biodiversity protection [ , , , ]. Integrating indigenous management practices with formal conservation frameworks can strengthen both ecological integrity and the continuity of traditional knowledge, emphasizing the importance of culturally informed biodiversity policy [ , , ].

## Implications of medicinal animal utilization in Andracha District
Medicinal animals in Andracha District play a central role in primary healthcare, valued for accessibility, affordability, and cultural acceptance, particularly where formal medical services are limited. This reliance parallels patterns reported in Gamo, Wolaita, and Assosa districts of Ethiopia, as well as in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and globally in India, China, and Pakistan, where zootherapeutic practices remain integral to local healthcare [ , , , , , , , ].
Beyond therapeutic use, these species possess significant bio-cultural value, contributing to rituals, ceremonies, clothing, musical instruments, and household items. Skins, hides, and feathers are fashioned into garments or instruments, while some parts serve as charms or ritual objects. Comparable multifunctionality has been documented across Dangila, West Gojjam, Kafta-Humera, Africa, Europe, and Asia, illustrating the integration of practical, spiritual, and symbolic knowledge into daily life [ , , ].
The extensive utilization of medicinal animals, however, raises conservation concerns. Informants identified agricultural expansion, overgrazing, hunting, climate change, and construction as major threats, with agriculture posing the greatest risk. These pressures reflect trends across Ethiopia and East Africa, where land-use change drives habitat loss and species decline [ , , , ]. Overexploitation of meat, whole bodies, and internal organs further underscores the ethical and ecological challenges associated with traditional medicine [ , , ].
Encouragingly, indigenous conservation practices mitigate some risks. The Shekacho community protects sacred forests, enforces taboos, and practices community-based stewardship, preserving both species and ethnomedicinal knowledge. Similar strategies in Wolaita and Assosa districts highlight the potential of culturally embedded approaches to support biodiversity alongside traditional healthcare [ , , , , ]. Integrating these practices with formal conservation and public health initiatives offers a pathway to safeguard both ecological and cultural resources.
Finally, the study emphasizes the urgent need to document and transmit indigenous knowledge, which remains largely oral and concentrated among elders. Younger generations are increasingly disengaged due to urban migration and modern lifestyles, a challenge noted across Ethiopia, India, China, and Pakistan [ , , ]. Targeted documentation, education, and culturally sensitive conservation strategies are critical to maintaining traditional healthcare practices and the biodiversity that sustains them [ , ].
Hence, medicinal animals in Andracha District exemplify the intersection of health, culture, and biodiversity, providing accessible healthcare, reinforcing cultural identity, and supporting livelihoods. Sustainable management strategies are essential to balance utilization with conservation, ensuring these resources endure for future generations [ , , , , , , ].

## Limitations of the study
This study relied on oral interviews and self-reported information, which may be affected by recall bias or selective reporting, especially for culturally sensitive practices. Identification of medicinal animals was based on local names, introducing potential taxonomic uncertainty or misidentification. Data collection was limited to accessible communities, potentially overlooking knowledge from more remote areas.
No ecological assessments were conducted, limiting evaluation of species sustainability, and no pharmacological validation was performed to verify therapeutic efficacy. Some practices may carry safety risks, including exposure to toxic or zoonotic animal products.
As a cross-sectional study, findings capture knowledge at a single point in time and do not account for seasonal or generational variation. Future research should combine taxonomic verification, ecological surveys, laboratory testing, and longitudinal approaches to provide a more accurate and safe understanding of medicinal animal use.

## Conclusion and recommendations
This study documents 52 medicinal animal species used by indigenous communities in Andracha District, revealing a rich body of zootherapeutic knowledge embedded in local healthcare and cultural practices. Quantitative analyses, including RFC, FL, ICF, and ZEI, highlight species of high cultural and use significance, such as Bos taurus, Apis mellifera, stingless bees (Halictus scabiosae), Capra aegagrus hircus, and hyenas. Knowledge distribution among the Sheka, Kaffa, and Amhara groups shows both substantial interethnic sharing and group-specific practices, reflecting dynamic biocultural interactions.
Despite the protection offered by the Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve and local conservation norms, medicinal fauna remain vulnerable to agricultural expansion, overgrazing, habitat loss, and unsustainable harvesting, while knowledge transmission faces challenges due to modernization and reduced intergenerational transfer.
Based on these findings, several recommendations are proposed. Community-based conservation should formally integrate indigenous ethnozoological knowledge into local biodiversity management plans, recognizing cultural practices such as sacred forest protection and taboos as tools supporting sustainable species management. Sustainable use and monitoring guidelines are needed for high-RFC species and vulnerable taxa, promoting non-lethal practices (e.g., beekeeping) and systematic monitoring to prevent overexploitation. Knowledge transmission can be strengthened through school-based cultural programs, workshops, and community-led initiatives facilitated by traditional healers. Alternative livelihoods, including apiculture, ecotourism, and sustainable livestock production, can reduce pressure on wild fauna.
Future research should focus on ethnoecological surveys to assess species population status and habitat requirements, examine climate vulnerability, and carry out comparative cross-cultural studies to understand knowledge distribution dynamics. Socioeconomic studies may clarify how gender, poverty, and healthcare access influence zootherapeutic practices. By emphasizing cultural significance, ecological sustainability, and knowledge preservation, these measures can support the continued availability of medicinal fauna, strengthen community resilience, and safeguard the biocultural heritage of Andracha District.
