Our review will be a valuable resource for researchers and clinicians interested in the latest developments at the intersection of molecular biology, nanotechnology, and infectious disease management.

## Introduction
The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, emerged in late 2019 and rapidly spread worldwide, resulting in a global health crisis [ ]. The virus, highly transmissible and capable of causing severe respiratory illness, overwhelmed healthcare systems and led to unprecedented social and economic disruptions. COVID-19 manifests with a wide range of clinical symptoms, from asymptomatic or mild respiratory illness to severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multi-organ failure. The disease disproportionately affects older adults and individuals with underlying health conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [ ]. As of January 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that the cumulative number of confirmed infections has reached 777 million, with millions of deaths attributed to the disease, highlighting the urgent need for effective diagnostics, therapeutics, and preventive measures. Effective treatments are essential to reduce mortality rates and alleviate severe symptoms, improving patient outcomes. The development of therapeutics not only addresses the immediate challenges posed by COVID-19 but also enhances preparedness for future pandemics. Moreover, innovative therapeutic solutions can significantly reduce the socio-economic impacts of viral outbreaks [ , ].
Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the virus responsible for COVID-19, is composed of structural proteins such as the spike (S) protein, nucleocapsid (N) protein, envelope (E) protein, and membrane (M) protein, along with its RNA genome (Fig.  a) [ – ]. Among these, the N protein is the most abundant and relatively conserved in the virus, playing a crucial role in viral RNA replication and packaging [ ]. The S protein, particularly its receptor-binding domain (RBD), mediates the virus's attachment to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors on host cells, which is the first step in viral entry and infection [ , ]. The virus's RNA genome is about 30,000 nucleotides long and encodes multiple open reading frames that are essential for its life cycle. The SARS-CoV-2 virus is highly mutable, which has led to the emergence of several variants of concern such as Alpha, Delta, and Omicron. These variants often exhibit increased transmissibility and may have altered virulence or immune evasion capabilities. The S and N proteins are usually used as characteristic domain targets for affinity reagents due to their pivotal roles in the virus's life cycle [ ]. Accurate neutralization of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, including its structural proteins such as the S proteins, is essential for effective COVID-19 therapeutics, highlighting their significance in the development of advanced treatment strategies. 
COVID-19 not only causes acute respiratory distress but also leads to long-term complications, such as lung fibrosis and other immunological issues. These complications can significantly impact the quality of life and long-term health outcomes for patients who have recovered from the acute phase of the disease. For instance, lung fibrosis, characterized by the excessive deposition of collagen and other extracellular matrix proteins, can lead to reduced lung function and impaired gas exchange [ ]. This condition often results from the body's inflammatory response to the viral infection, which can cause tissue damage and subsequent fibrosis. Other immunological issues, such as the development of autoantibodies and chronic inflammation, can also persist long after the initial infection has resolved. These issues highlight the complexity of COVID-19 and the need for comprehensive therapeutic strategies that address both the acute and long-term effects of the disease.
In response to the urgent need for effective COVID-19 treatments, the screening of FDA-approved inhibitors as repurposed drugs has been a critical approach. Several FDA-approved drugs have been identified and repurposed for COVID-19 treatment through comprehensive screening and evaluation [ ]. For instance, Remdesivir (Veklury; developed by Gilead Sciences) is an intravenous nucleotide analog prodrug that received full approval from the FDA in October 2020 for the treatment of COVID-19 in patients requiring hospitalization [ ]. Remdesivir has demonstrated efficacy in reducing viral load and improving clinical outcomes in hospitalized patients. Another notable example is Molnupiravir (Lagevrio; developed by Merck), an oral ribonucleoside prodrug that received Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) in December 2021. Molnupiravir has shown significant efficacy in reducing the risk of hospitalization and death in high-risk patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 [ ]. However, these examples don’t show satisfied COVID-19 treatment specificity and efficiency, which calls to new therapeutics to address the issue [ ].
Aptamers have emerged as promising tools due to their high specificity and affinity for viral components. Typically composed of 10–100 nucleotides, aptamers are single-stranded oligonucleotides (ssDNA or ssRNA) that can form distinct secondary or tertiary structures, such as stem-loops, hairpins, and G-quadruplexes [ – ]. The binding mechanisms of aptamers to their targets primarily involve surface recognition and three-dimensional interactions, including van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces, enabling them to selectively bind to target molecules [ ]. Compared to antibodies, aptamers offer several advantages, including a smaller size. The small size of aptamers (approximately 2–3 nm in diameter) helps overcome spatial hindrances in molecular recognition and allows the identification of antigenic epitopes that might be inaccessible to antibodies [ ]. Aptamers can penetrate tissues or cells and bind to small, “hidden” domains within SARS-CoV-2 targets [ ]. Additionally, aptamers are easy to synthesize and store, allowing for room temperature storage and transportation, and they can withstand harsh environmental conditions, making them more suitable for SARS-CoV-2 detection and treatment application [ , ]. They also have lower production costs, minimal batch-to-batch variation, and reduced immunogenicity. Their chemical properties permit easy modification and integration with other technologies to enhance stability and functional applications. These single-stranded nucleic acid molecules can be engineered to selectively bind to SARS-CoV-2 targets, demonstrating significant potential in the treatment of COVID-19. Apart from single-stranded aptamers, we and other researchers in this field have developed multivalent aptamers and various nucleic acid nanotechnologies to sense targeting molecules, execute precise molecular imaging, detect pathogenic biomarkers and treat diverse diseases [ – ]. Hence, it is expected to hold great therapeutic development potential to treat SARS-CoV-2 by integrating aptamers into nanomedicine [ – ].
To enhance the therapeutic efficacy for SARS-CoV-2, the integration of nanotherapeutics and aptamers offers significant advancements. Nanotherapeutics utilize nanomaterials such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and dendrimers, which can encapsulate and deliver therapeutic agents to specific cells or tissues with precision [ , ]. These nanomaterials improve the pharmacokinetics, stability, and bioavailability of drugs, providing better-targeted delivery and reducing side effects. In the context of COVID-19, nanomaterials have been employed in various forms, such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), for the delivery of mRNA vaccines. These nanoparticles protect the genetic material from degradation and facilitate its efficient delivery into cells, allowing for sustained and targeted therapeutic effects. Furthermore, nanoparticles are being explored for their potential in combination therapies, where they act as vehicles for both diagnostic agents and therapeutic molecules. Nanocarriers, designed to encapsulate drugs, offer enhanced stability and allow for controlled release, ensuring sustained therapeutic action [ , ].
This review summarizes the technique principles, molecular strategies, and other advancements in the application of aptamers in the treatment of COVID-19, with a particular focus on aptamers and aptamer-based nanotherapeutics. First, we briefly introduce the selection process, recognition principles, and other unique properties of aptamers in binding to various targets and provide a comprehensive summary of representative reported aptamers targeting SARS-CoV-2 [ – ]. Next, as shown in Fig.  b, we mainly discuss different therapeutic principles and various molecular strategies of aptamers and related nanomedicine, emphasizing their treatment potential for COVID-19. In this review, in addition to discussing the widely recognized RBD-dependent and non-RBD-dependent neutralization strategies [ , ], we also place particular emphasis on therapeutic approaches targeting the SARS-CoV-2 N protein as a potential intervention. Finally, we delve into the challenges and prospects of aptamers and nucleic acid nanomedicine in the context of COVID-19 therapeutics.

## Aptamers used in COVID-19 therapeutics
Various representative aptamers have been employed in the therapeutic strategies for COVID-19. These aptamers typically bind with high specificity and affinity to key viral components of SARS-CoV-2, such as the S protein, or to human cell receptors, such as ACE2, thereby inhibiting viral attachment and subsequent infection. Aptamers are commonly utilized as antiviral agents or neutralizing antibodies in the treatment of COVID-19. Their ability to be chemically synthesized, modified, and engineered for enhanced stability and specificity renders them an attractive alternative to conventional monoclonal antibodies in the fight against COVID-19. Aptamers specific to different antigenic sites of SARS-CoV-2 are selected through a process known as Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment (SELEX) [ ]. This in vitro selection process, akin to natural selection, involves isolating nucleic acid aptamers from a large, random sequence library [ ]. SELEX is an iterative process of selection and amplification. To provide further clarity on the aptamer selection process, we have included a detailed explanation using cell-SELEX as an example (Fig.  ) [ ]. Initially, a random library, typically containing up to 1015 unique nucleic acid molecules, is incubated with the target cells under defined conditions, such as specific temperature and salt concentrations. Nucleic acid molecules that bind to the target cells are separated from the random sequence pool and amplified to generate a new population of molecules. This cycle of selection and amplification is repeated until the optimal binding ligands dominate the sequence population, resulting in nucleic acid aptamers that can be cloned, isolated, and characterized. After the initial selection, further optimization of the aptamers is often necessary to enhance their binding affinity and specificity. This can involve truncation, which involves removing non-essential nucleotides from the aptamer sequence to reduce its size while retaining its binding properties, and mutagenesis, which introduces specific or random mutations into the aptamer sequence to explore and enhance its binding affinity and specificity. These optimization steps ensure that the selected aptamers not only have high affinity and specificity for their intended targets but are also robust and suitable for various applications, including diagnostics and therapeutics. 
Aptamers are single-stranded nucleic acid molecules that fold into complex three-dimensional structures to mimic antibodies, utilizing shape and charge complementarity to bind specific targets [ – ]. While some aptamers naturally exist as ligand-binding elements of riboswitches, most aptamers are generated in vitro and can be tailored for specific targets. Single-stranded nucleic acid molecules have a strong propensity to fold into discrete structures, driven by their tendency to bind through π-π stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions. The highly flexible phosphodiester backbone, with five rotatable bonds, facilitates this folding. In addition, a range of sugar puckers and rotatable glycosidic bonds allow bases to interact in various conformations. The success of the SELEX technique lies in the fact that a random sequence library of nucleic acid molecules represents a vast repertoire of shapes, enabling complementary recognition of other molecules [ ]. Aptamers are capable of adopting various secondary structural forms, such as hairpin structures, G-quadruplexes, and others. Among these structures, the G-quadruplex is a unique secondary structure formed by guanine (G)-rich nucleic acid sequences through hydrogen bonding interactions. In this structure, four guanine bases are held together by Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds, forming a square planar structure known as the G-tetrad. Multiple G-tetrads are stacked to stabilize the overall structure of the G-quadruplex, and the presence of monovalent cations, such as K+ or Na+, further enhances the stability of this structure. Due to its exceptional stability, the G-quadruplex plays a critical role in the functionality of many aptamers. Particularly in the SELEX process, aptamers that form G-quadruplexes offer additional stability compared to traditional double-stranded structures or conventional secondary structures. This enhanced stability enables G-quadruplex aptamers to stand out during the selection process, demonstrating higher affinity and specificity when binding to target molecules [ ]. 

## Application of aptamers in COVID-19 therapeutics
The infection process of SARS-CoV-2 encompasses a series of highly coordinated and precise molecular events, each involving specific host and viral factors. An in-depth understanding of these processes not only aids in elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of the virus but also provides critical targets and theoretical foundations for the application of aptamers and nucleic acid nanomedicine in SARS-CoV-2 treatment. Current therapeutic strategies primarily focus on blocking the recognition and fusion of SARS-CoV-2 with host cells, inhibiting viral replication post-infection, and suppressing inflammatory pathways to prevent excessive inflammatory responses and cytokine storms [ ]. Additionally, therapeutic approaches targeting the inhibition of N protein-mediated liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), although less frequently reported, represent an emerging strategy for intervention [ ].

## Aptamers and aptamer-based nucleic acid nanostructures strategies blocking viral recognition and fusion with host cells
The infection process of SARS-CoV-2 begins with the specific binding of the virus's surface S protein to the host cell surface receptor, typically ACE2 on human respiratory epithelial cells. This step is a critical determinant in the infection process, as it dictates the virus's ability to successfully enter host cells [ ]. Consequently, the S protein has become a pivotal target in COVID-19 therapy, with strategies aimed at blocking viral recognition and fusion with host cells as well as inhibiting replication. In particular, disrupting the specific interaction between the S protein and the ACE2 receptor has shown promise in preventing viral entry. S protein is a multifunctional structural protein that plays a critical role in the viral entry process into host cells. The protein consists of two subunits, S1 and S2, which function at different stages of viral fusion with the host cell. The S1 subunit contains two important structural domains: the Receptor Binding Domain (RBD) and the N-terminal Domain (NTD), each of which performs distinct yet complementary functions during the viral infection process. The RBD is responsible for mediating the interaction between the virus and the host cell receptor, ACE2, initiating the viral entry process. The binding of RBD to ACE2 is a critical step in the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2 and serves as the primary target for neutralizing antibodies. Studies have shown that the RBD exhibits significant conformational flexibility, switching between “open” and “closed” states, which allows the ACE2 binding site to either be exposed or hidden. This conformational flexibility provides the virus with a mechanism to evade host immune surveillance and contributes to its high infectivity. In contrast, the NTD, located at the N-terminus of the spike protein, does not directly engage with ACE2 but plays a key role in viral immune evasion and stability. The NTD promotes viral attachment to host cells through interactions with cell surface glycans, facilitating the initial viral attachment. The high glycosylation of the NTD not only enhances its affinity for host cell surface glycans but also acts as a shield, reducing immune system recognition and aiding in immune evasion. Although the NTD does not directly participate in ACE2 binding, recent studies indicate that it stabilizes the spike protein and enhances its interaction with host cell receptors, further increasing the efficiency of viral entry and fusion. The NTD is also a potential target for neutralizing antibodies, which can bind to the NTD and interfere with the conformational changes required for viral entry. This provides an alternative or complementary antiviral strategy to targeting the RBD. Despite their distinct structural and functional roles, the RBD and NTD work synergistically to ensure efficient infection by SARS-CoV-2. The RBD is primarily responsible for receptor recognition and binding, while the NTD enhances viral attachment and stabilizes the conformational transitions of the spike protein during viral fusion. This interplay enables SARS-CoV-2 to effectively evade host immune monitoring and promotes viral transmission. Aptamers can bind to the RBD and NTD of the S protein, effectively obstructing the virus's attachment to host cells and thereby neutralizing its infectivity [ ]. By blocking this key interaction, aptamers inhibit viral entry, reducing subsequent replication and spread within the host.

## RBD-dependent aptamer strategies
Initial efforts in developing nucleic acid aptamers against SARS-CoV-2 focused on targeting the RBD of the S protein. The RBD is crucial for the virus's attachment to the host cell's ACE2 receptor, making it a primary target for neutralization strategies. Aptamers that specifically bind to the RBD can effectively block this interaction, preventing viral entry and subsequent infection. This approach has shown promise in neutralizing the virus and reducing its infectivity.
Liu et al. exploited the specificity and affinity of aptamers Aptamer-1 and Aptamer-2 to target the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein. This targeting disrupts the crucial S/RBD-ACE2 interaction necessary for the virus to enter host cells. Notably, the selected aptamers were able to neutralize viral particles expressing the S protein, thereby preventing infection of host cells (Fig.  a) [ ]. 
Although strategies based on monovalent aptamers are relatively straightforward to implement, most aptamers exhibit affinities for their targets in the nM to μM range, with IC50 values typically at the nM level. To enhance the virus-neutralizing efficacy of aptamers, Sun et al. developed an aptamer-based neutralization strategy and specifically designed a circular bivalent aptamer CoV2-6 C3. This aptamer binds with high affinity to the SRBD and effectively blocks the interaction between the virus and the ACE2 receptor. CoV2-6 C3 shows promise as a candidate for intranasal administration and other forms of delivery due to its superior binding properties and potential therapeutic efficacy (Fig.  b) [ ].
The use of covalent aptamers is a promising strategy, as it enhances the binding capability of aptamers to viral targets, thereby improving their therapeutic effectiveness. Our group reported a robust covalent aptamer strategy that enhanced the inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 proteins [ ]. By integrating electrophilic groups into aptamers, we created stable and irreversible covalent complexes with the S protein RBD of SARS-CoV-2. Even within the vascular circulation environment, covalent aptamers are capable of maintaining high-affinity binding to the S protein and effectively exerting neutralizing activity (Fig.  c). Qin et al. further advanced the field by developing a chemically modified RBD aptamer containing sulfur(VI) fluoride exchange (SuFEx) modifications, which blocks the RBD-ACE2 interaction through a covalent bonding mechanism. This modification resulted in more than a 25-fold enhancement in pseudovirus neutralization efficacy compared to the original aptamer. The covalent bonding aptamer 6 C3-7SF demonstrated highly specific and efficient binding to key RBD residues, offering a robust approach to inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig.  d) [ ].
While DNA aptamers have shown impressive capabilities, RNA aptamers offer additional advantages in terms of their chemical flexibility and ease of modification. Valero et al. developed a serum-stable RNA aptamer that specifically targets the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein, effectively neutralizing viral entry. The aptamer, modified with 2′-fluoro pyrimidines to enhance stability and resistance to nucleases, binds with high affinity to the RBD, preventing its interaction with the host ACE2 receptor. A trimerized version of the aptamer further increased binding affinity to the low picomolar range, significantly enhancing its neutralization efficiency against SARS-CoV-2 in cell culture experiments. The aptamer maintained a high binding affinity to S proteins from various circulating SARS-CoV-2 strains, highlighting its potential for widespread therapeutic applications (Fig.  e) [ ].

## RBD-dependent nucleic acid nanostructures strategies
While individual aptamers have shown promise in targeting and neutralizing SARS-CoV-2 by blocking the RBD-ACE2 interaction, their small size often limits their binding affinity and stability [ ]. This limitation can be addressed by incorporating aptamers into nucleic acid nanostructures, which offer several advantages. Nucleic acid nanostructures can provide a scaffold for multivalent binding, enhancing the overall binding strength and stability of the aptamers. These larger structures can also introduce steric hindrance, effectively blocking the virus from binding to host cell receptors more efficiently than individual aptamers alone.
For instance, Sun et al. developed a spherical cocktail neutralizing aptamer-gold nanoparticle (SNAP) conjugate. By combining multiple aptamers on a gold nanoparticle scaffold, they achieved exceptional binding affinity and potent neutralization of SARS-CoV-2 (Fig.  a) [ ]. Furthermore, they utilized SNAP to block the interaction between the Omicron RBD and the host ACE2 receptor. This innovative strategy allows the SNAP conjugate to bind tightly to the Omicron RBD with a dissociation constant of 13.6 pM. The SNAP conjugate effectively blocked the infection of Omicron pseudovirus, achieving an IC50 of 35.9 pM (Fig.  b) [ ]. The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants, particularly those with multiple mutations like Omicron, necessitates new strategies to maintain the efficacy of aptamer-based treatments. Wan et al. addressed this challenge by developing a spatial- and valence-matched neutralizing DNA nanostructure, termed MATCH-4. This tetrahedral DNA framework precisely organizes four neutralizing aptamers to match the spatial arrangement of the S trimer’s RBDs. The MATCH-4 structure not only enhances binding affinity through multivalent interactions but also provides a nanoscale barrier that prevents the S trimer from undergoing the conformational changes required for viral fusion with host cells (Fig.  c) [ ]. 
Further advancing this approach, Zhang et al. advanced the development of aptamer-based nanostructures by designing a spatially patterned neutralizing icosahedral DNA nanocage (IDNA-30) to block SARS-CoV-2 infection efficiently. This icosahedral DNA framework assembles up to 30 neutralizing aptamers in a spatially organized manner, topologically matching the SARS-CoV-2 S trimer. The multivalent binding provided by the IDNA-30 structure enhances its neutralization efficacy by inducing virus aggregation, creating a steric barrier that prevents the virus from binding to host cells and limiting the conformational changes of the S protein required for viral fusion (Fig.  d) [ ]. In a study by Zhang et al. an innovative approach was developed using DNA origami to organize RBDs on a nanoscale framework, mimicking the natural viral presentation. This research supports the transition from traditional aptamer-based strategies to more sophisticated nucleic acid nanostructure approaches. The integration of aptamers with DNA origami and nanostructures offers a promising approach to creating potent antiviral agents capable of targeting SARS-CoV-2 and its variants (Fig.  e) [ ].
Moreover, Chauhan et al. present an innovative approach utilizing net-shaped DNA nanostructures to enhance the detection and inhibition of SARS-CoV-2, including its variants. These DNA nanostructures are designed to selectively capture and neutralize intact SARS-CoV-2 virions by exploiting spatial pattern-matching and multivalent interactions between aptamers and viral S proteins. The study highlights the efficacy of these DNA nets in significantly enhancing the binding affinity and specificity toward the virus, thereby providing a rapid, sensitive, and cost-effective therapeutic tool (Fig.  f) [ ].
Building upon traditional aptamer-based approaches, Lee et al. developed a novel category of hACE2-mimicking hybrid ligands, which incorporate critical binding-site peptides with aptamer scaffolds. These hybrid ligands mimic the host cell receptor by integrating key hACE2 binding regions with aptameric structures, resulting in enhanced binding affinity to SARS-CoV-2 variants, including Omicron. The peptide motifs, derived from high-affinity sites on hACE2, synergize with the aptamer scaffold, which provides structural stability and adaptive binding. This innovative strategy yields ligands with approximately a 500% increase in binding affinity for Omicron, demonstrating robust binding resilience across all variants of concern. By closely mimicking the natural receptor, these ligands present a promising therapeutic avenue, potentially adaptable to evolving SARS-CoV-2 mutations [ ].
In conclusion, integrating aptamers with nucleic acid nanostructures significantly enhances their antiviral efficacy, offering a versatile and potent approach to combating SARS-CoV-2 and its emerging variants. This synergy not only improves binding affinity and neutralization potency but also provides a robust and inspiring platform for developing new therapeutic agents against a broad spectrum of pathogens.

## RBD-independent nucleic acid strategies
Despite the success of RBD-targeted aptamers, the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants with mutations in the RBD poses a significant challenge to this strategy. Mutations can reduce the efficacy of RBD-targeting therapies by altering the aptamer binding sites, leading to immune escape [ , ]. Consequently, there is a growing need to develop RBD-independent strategies that can provide broader protection against viral variants. These strategies focus on targeting other conserved regions of the S protein or different viral components altogether, ensuring sustained efficacy even as the virus evolves.
Schmitz et al. have developed a DNA aptamer that exhibits neutralizing capabilities against SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus without relying on the RBD. Although the precise neutralization mechanism remains unclear, the authors hypothesize that the aptamer's efficacy may stem from preventing the initial attachment to the ACE2 receptor, inhibiting the S2 cleavage, or disrupting the pre-fusion conformation of the S protein. These alternative mechanisms suggest that RBD-independent aptamers can offer robust antiviral activity against diverse viral variants by targeting conserved regions of the S protein or other structural features critical for viral fusion and entry (Fig.  a) [ ]. Silwal et al. developed an aptamer, S2 A2 C1, that exhibits high binding affinity to the relatively conserved S2 domain of the S protein. Recognizing the potential of targeting multiple sites on the S protein, the researchers combined S2 A2 C1 with a previously reported anti-RBD aptamer, S1B6 C3, to design a fusion aptamer. This novel fusion aptamer enhances inhibitory efficacy against the S protein of various SARS-CoV-2 variants, including Delta and Omicron (Fig.  b) [ ]. 
In addition to the RBD and S2 domains of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein, NTD is another effective target for neutralization due to its significant antigenic epitopes [ ]. Chen et al. developed a DNA aptamer targeting the NTD supersite, similar to NTD-neutralizing antibodies, to inhibit SARS-CoV-2. Through molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, the aptamer was validated for its binding affinity and specificity to the NTD. While the monomeric aptamer showed inhibitory effects against wild-type pseudovirus, the research further led to the design of a multivalent topology-matching nanocrown. This DNA nanocrown, engineered to topologically match the S protein’s trimeric form, demonstrated superior neutralization efficiency against various SARS-CoV-2 variants, including D614G, N501Y, and Δ69–70. The multivalent approach, allowing simultaneous binding to multiple NTD domains, effectively blocked the virus’s entry into host cells by capping the S protein in its prefusion state, showcasing a promising strategy for broad-spectrum COVID-19 therapies (Fig.  c) [ ].

## Aptamer-based strategies targeting glycan shields of SARS-CoV-2
The extensive glycosylation of viral proteins facilitates immune evasion by covering immunogenic protein surfaces with dense, host-derived glycan coatings. These glycan shields, particularly high mannose glycans, are characteristic features of many viral envelopes, including SARS-CoV-2. High mannose glycans provide an ideal target for viral recognition and inhibition due to their prevalence and conservation across viral strains [ , ]. Targeting high-mannose glycans, Li et al. developed an unprecedented antiviral strategy employing mannose-decorated nanoparticles to target viral glycan shields. These nanoparticles exhibited a distinctive dual-action mechanism: not only blocking virus-receptor interactions but also inducing viral aggregation, thereby inhibiting viral entry and facilitating phagocytosis. This glycan-shielding approach opened new avenues for broad-spectrum viral inhibition. Building upon this strategy, the authors further designed aptamers in multivalent configurations to shield viral glycans [ ]. They introduced a novel strategy using molecularly imprinted magnetic nanoparticles (MIPs) to screen high mannose-specific aptamers. These aptamers exhibited high affinity for the S protein of SARS-CoV-2 and demonstrated the ability to inhibit pseudovirus infection. By engineering the aptamers into multivalent forms, such as dendritic polymers and tetrahedral DNA nanostructures, their binding capabilities were significantly enhanced. This approach highlights the potential of high mannose-specific aptamers in broad-spectrum antiviral applications by targeting the glycan shields that facilitate viral immune evasion (Fig.  ) [ ]. 

## Aptamer-based strategies targeting SARS-CoV-2 S protein and inflammatory pathways
The SARS-CoV-2 S protein is central to the virus's ability to induce severe inflammatory responses, contributing to the pathogenesis of COVID-19. It is well-documented that the S protein can interact with Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on immune cells, particularly monocytes and macrophages, leading to the activation of the inflammatory signaling pathway [ – ]. This interaction mimics an anti-bacterial response, triggering the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 through the MyD88 and NF-κB signaling pathways [ ].
Furthermore, the S protein can also initiate inflammation via Fcγ receptor-mediated pathways. The antibody-dependent enhancement of infection occurs when antibodies facilitate viral entry into monocytes through Fcγ receptors, bypassing the ACE2 receptor. This process results in abortive infection, where the virus does not replicate fully but still activates inflammasomes such as NLRP3 (Clinical trial number: not applicable) and AIM2, leading to pyroptotic cell death and the release of inflammatory mediators. The pyroptosis of these immune cells can lead to systemic inflammation and cytokine storms, which are hallmarks of severe COVID-19 cases.
Given these inflammatory mechanisms, nucleic acid aptamers present a promising therapeutic approach. Aptamers can be designed to bind specifically to the S protein, potentially blocking its interaction with TLR4 and Fcγ receptors, thereby reducing inflammatory signaling and cytokine release. This targeting can mitigate the severe inflammatory responses and cytokine storms associated with COVID-19, offering a strategic advantage over traditional therapeutics that may not address these specific pathways [ ].
The adaptability and specificity of aptamers make them suitable candidates for inhibiting the S protein’s interactions, providing an innovative and targeted approach to managing COVID-19. By preventing the S protein from activating inflammatory pathways, aptamers can serve as both antiviral agents and modulators of the immune response, addressing both viral replication and inflammation simultaneously. This dual action underscores the potential of aptamer-based therapies in the comprehensive management of COVID-19 and its complications.
Yang et al. developed a DNA aptamer, ST-6, which demonstrates high binding affinity and specificity for the SARS-CoV-2 S protein. This aptamer effectively inhibits the interaction between the S protein and TLR4, which is responsible for triggering inflammatory responses in COVID-19. Through 12 rounds of selection, ST-6 was optimized to form stable G-quadruplex structures that enhance its binding capabilities. The aptamer selectively targets the S protein of SARS-CoV-2 without affecting other viral or host proteins, thus reducing off-target effects. ST-6 effectively reduces cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6, in immune cells stimulated by the S protein from both wild-type and variant strains of SARS-CoV-2, highlighting its potential as a targeted anti-inflammatory therapeutic in COVID-19 treatment (Fig.  a) [ ]. 
In addition to traditional aptamer approaches, Yang et al. also introduced a novel dual-function circular aptamer-ASO chimera, termed circSApt-NASO, designed to combat SARS-CoV-2 by targeting both viral replication and inflammation. The chimera combines an aptamer that binds specifically to the S protein with an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting the N gene of the virus. The circular design enhances the stability of the chimera in serum, ensuring prolonged activity. The aptamer component of circSApt-NASO inhibits S-induced inflammation by blocking the interaction between the S protein and TLR4, thereby preventing the cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokine production that leads to cytokine storms in severe COVID-19 cases. Simultaneously, the ASO component silences the expression of the N gene, a critical element in viral replication, effectively reducing the viral load and associated inflammation (Fig.  b) [ ]. In another approach, Saify Nabiabad et al. developed a targeted treatment strategy utilizing aptamer-functionalized lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to deliver RNA interference (RNAi) specifically against SARS-CoV-2. This approach leverages aptamers’ high specificity and the gene-silencing capacity of RNAi. The aptamers bind to the RBD of the S protein, blocking viral entry into host cells. Concurrently, the RNAi molecules target the highly conserved N protein, essential for viral replication, thus inhibiting the replication cycle at a molecular level. By reducing viral load and preventing cell entry, this dual-action strategy not only directly inhibits viral proliferation but also attenuates inflammation, potentially reducing cytokine storm risk associated with severe infection (Fig.  c) [ ]. Recently, Kim et al. identified a DNA aptamer with a specific binding affinity for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. This aptamer was found to effectively inhibit the interaction between the spike protein and ACE2, while simultaneously suppressing spike protein-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine production in ACE2-overexpressing HEK293 cells [ ].
In summary, these innovative aptamer-based strategies highlight the potential of nucleic acid therapeutics in effectively managing COVID-19 by targeting and inhibiting inflammation pathways associated with SARS-CoV-2. By blocking key protein interactions and silencing viral gene expression, these aptamers and aptamer conjugates offer the dual benefit of reducing viral replication and modulating the host's inflammatory response, paving the way for comprehensive treatment options against COVID-19.

## Aptamer-based strategies targeting SARS-CoV-2 enzymes
Targeting viral enzymes is a promising approach for inhibiting viral infections, particularly after the virus has entered host cells. In the context of SARS-CoV-2, the main protease (Mpro) plays a crucial role in processing polyproteins that are essential for viral replication and transcription. This enzyme is responsible for the generation of non-structural proteins (NSPs) from NSP4 to NSP16, which are indispensable for the viral life cycle. By inhibiting Mpro and other key viral enzymes, it is possible to disrupt the virus's replication machinery and reduce its pathogenicity [ – ]. Aptamers have emerged as a novel therapeutic strategy to target these enzymes with high specificity and affinity, offering new solutions for COVID-19 treatment.
Recent research efforts have identified aptamers as potent inhibitors of viral replication and entry by targeting specific viral enzymes. One such aptamer, BC 007, has shown a high affinity for the RBD of the S protein and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) of SARS-CoV-2. By binding to these critical proteins, BC 007 inhibits the virus's ability to replicate and enter host cells, leveraging its quadruplex structure to enhance binding specificity and effectiveness. This aptamer is currently undergoing repurposing for clinical testing due to its excellent safety profile and non-immunogenic nature, positioning it as a promising therapeutic tool against SARS-CoV-2 and its variants (Fig.  ) [ ]. 
Haberland et al. further demonstrated that the aptamer BC 007 is unique in its ability to distinguish between specific virus-neutralizing antibodies and those that cross-react with host tissues, potentially leading to autoimmunity. This distinction is crucial in addressing the long-term effects of COVID-19, such as Long-COVID, where cross-reactive antibodies are implicated. By selectively binding to less specific antibodies, BC 007 minimizes the risk of neutralizing the beneficial, highly specific antibodies that are part of the body’s natural defense against SARS-CoV-2. Furthermore, BC 007 extends beyond viral neutralization to modulate autoimmune responses triggered by SARS-CoV-2, particularly in cases where COVID-19 has led to the development of autoantibodies, contributing to severe and prolonged symptoms [ ].
Building on this approach, another study focused on developing de novo ssRNA aptamers targeting Mpro of SARS-CoV-2, an enzyme essential for viral replication. Using an in-silico design approach, researchers identified aptamers that disrupt the dimerization of Mpro, thereby inhibiting its enzymatic activity. Among these, the aptamer MAptapro-IR1 demonstrated the strongest binding affinity, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for COVID-19. These findings underscore the versatility of aptamers in targeting viral enzymes and their adaptability to emerging variants [ ].
The development of aptamers targeting viral enzymes such as the S protein, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, and main protease represents a significant advance in the field of nucleic acid therapeutics for COVID-19. By specifically inhibiting key viral enzymes while preserving the host’s immune response, aptamers like BC 007 and MAptapro-IR1 offer novel approaches to viral inhibition and address the autoimmune complications associated with the disease. This dual-action capability positions aptamers as potent therapeutic candidates in the ongoing battle against COVID-19 and its variants.

## Aptamer-based strategies targeting SARS-CoV-2 N protein and disrupting the LLPS
The N protein of SARS-CoV-2 is a highly conserved structural protein with significant potential as a therapeutic target for inhibiting viral replication. The N protein plays a critical role in recognizing viral RNA and packaging it into ribonucleoprotein complexes. Additionally, through interactions with both viral and host proteins, it is involved in key biological processes, including viral transcription, replication, and immune regulation [ , ]. During virion assembly, the N protein forms complexes with the viral genomic RNA and interacts with other viral structural proteins. Simultaneously, it plays a pivotal role in evading host innate immunity by suppressing interferon production during viral infection.
LLPS is a process by which biomolecules form high-concentration droplets within cells through non-covalent, weak interactions. These droplets exhibit characteristics similar to membrane-less organelles and are involved in a variety of essential cellular functions. LLPS represents a pervasive and crucial phenomenon in eukaryotic cells, where membrane-less organelles (also referred to as biomolecular condensates or droplets) are formed. During this process, components within a homogeneous solution spontaneously separate into two or more coexisting phases: a dilute phase and a dense phase. Studies have shown that these biomolecular condensates can exist in different material states, including viscous liquids, gels, and even solid aggregates, with transitions between these states being crucial to their functional roles. Notably, these condensates play significant roles within the cell, such as forming biochemical reaction centers, signaling hubs, and supporting structures [ ].
LLPS is primarily driven by multivalent interactions between biomolecules such as proteins and RNAs, which leads to the formation of high-concentration droplet-like structures. These structures are highly dynamic, capable of rapid assembly and disassembly in response to changes in the cellular environment. Within the cell, LLPS participates in numerous key biological processes, including RNA processing, transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, protein synthesis, and cellular stress responses. Through this phase separation mechanism, cells are able to concentrate different molecules in a localized manner, without relying on membrane-bound compartments, to carry out specific functions [ ].
Recent studies have shown that the N protein of SARS-CoV-2 interacts with the viral genomic RNA and undergoes LLPS, forming droplet-like structures. These structures play pivotal roles during the viral life cycle, particularly in RNA packaging, assembly, and replication.[ , ] During viral infection, LLPS facilitates the initiation of viral replication and promotes the assembly of viral structural proteins [ ]. Consequently, LLPS induced by the N protein plays an essential role in viral RNA packaging, the regulation of stress granules, and the modulation of host antiviral innate immune pathways.
In a related study, Huang et al. identified an aptamer named N-Apt17, which exhibited high affinity and specificity for the N protein and effectively disrupted its LLPS (Fig.  ) [ ]. To further enhance the stability and molecular recognition capabilities of N-Apt17, the researchers designed a circular bivalent aptamer, termed cb-N-Apt17. The results demonstrated that cb-N-Apt17 possessed superior molecular stability, enhanced binding affinity, and exceptional LLPS-inhibiting activity, ultimately leading to significant suppression of viral replication. As research continues, these aptamer-based strategies hold the potential to become integral components of comprehensive treatment regimens for COVID-19, providing hope for more effective management of the disease and its emerging variants. 
Aptamer-based therapeutic strategies offer distinct advantages over traditional approaches, particularly in the context of COVID-19 treatment. One of the key benefits of aptamers is their high specificity and affinity for target molecules, such as the SARS-CoV-2 S protein and ACE2, which enables them to effectively block viral entry, often with greater efficiency than conventional small-molecule drugs or monoclonal antibodies. Unlike traditional therapies, aptamers can be rapidly synthesized and modified, allowing for the enhancement of stability and specificity. This flexibility facilitates precise targeting of viral components while minimizing off-target effects, a significant advantage over antibodies, which are typically more complex and costly to produce. Moreover, aptamers generally exhibit lower immunogenicity compared to monoclonal antibodies [ ]. Their smaller size, synthetic nature and easy compatible modifications make them less likely to provoke immune responses, thereby reducing the risk of immune-related side effects, which are often associated with antibody-based therapies. This characteristic is particularly advantageous in long-term treatments, where the potential for immune escape and inflammatory responses represents a critical concern. Aptamers also offer greater versatility in binding to multiple targets simultaneously. When incorporated into nucleic acid nanostructures, aptamers can significantly enhance their binding affinity, stability, and multivalency. These nanostructures enable aptamers to be used in combination therapies, allowing for the concurrent targeting of multiple viral components, thereby improving overall therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, traditional therapeutics often focus on a single target, which may become ineffective against viral mutations or evolving strains such as Omicron [ , ]. Overall, aptamers'customizability, lower production costs, reduced immune risks, and broad application potential make them a promising alternative to conventional therapies in the fight against COVID-19 and other infectious diseases.

## Conclusions and outlook
This review provides an overview of the types of SARS-CoV-2 aptamers and nucleic acid-based nanotherapeutics, with a particular focus on their applications in antiviral therapy research. It highlights recent advances in targeting key viral proteins, optimizing drug delivery systems, and enhancing therapeutic precision. Selected through SELEX technology, aptamers targeting SARS-CoV-2 exhibit high affinity and specificity, demonstrating considerable potential as targeted recognition elements. Aptamers, due to their nucleic acid properties, offer the advantages of low immunogenicity and low toxicity. Furthermore, they can be chemically synthesized, providing cost-efficiency and ease of modification. This lays a foundation for the development of SARS-CoV-2-specific therapeutic tools. Aptamers are capable of not only recognizing SARS-CoV-2 but also of inhibiting viral infection of host cells. Moreover, aptamers targeting the SARS-CoV-2 N protein have demonstrated potential pan-coronavirus antiviral activity [ ]. Their function as “neutralizing aptamers” represents a significant potential for the development of aptamer-based therapeutic strategies.
Despite the significant advancements and promising potential of aptamers in SARS-CoV-2 antiviral research, several challenges and limitations remain to be addressed to facilitate their translation from bench to bedside. The following discussion highlights these obstacles and explores strategies to overcome them, thereby paving the way for the clinical application of aptamer-based therapeutics.
Firstly, it should be noted that aptamers, particularly those that have not undergone any modifications, are susceptible to rapid degradation by nucleases and are swiftly eliminated by glomerular filtration. This results in a relatively short half-life in vivo. Chemical modifications or conjugation with lipid nanoparticles or DNA nanostructures can enhance stability; however, further optimization of these approaches is necessary to ensure both safety and efficacy. It has been demonstrated that aptamers can be integrated with a range of therapeutic carriers and technologies, thereby markedly enhancing their specificity and drug delivery efficiency in anti-SARS-CoV-2 applications. The combination of aptamers with lipid nanoparticles, DNA nanostructures, and other carriers not only enhances their in vivo stability but also increases targeting specificity and reduces off-target toxicity. Furthermore, co-delivery systems comprising aptamers and siRNA or other nucleic acid drugs can more effectively obstruct pivotal stages in viral infection, thereby markedly inhibiting viral replication. The development of multi-target aptamer systems represents a promising avenue for the development of comprehensive antiviral and anti-inflammatory therapies. The implementation of innovative labeling techniques, such as low background noise and efficient reporter molecules, serves to enhance the therapeutic sensitivity of aptamers, thereby rendering them more effective during the early stages of viral infection.
Secondly, although aptamers present a broad range of potential targets for the treatment of SARS-CoV-2, their clinical applications remain limited. It is imperative to develop efficient and high-throughput aptamer screening methods to obtain high-affinity, highly specific aptamers that meet diverse clinical requirements. Bioinformatics tools are of critical importance in the context of aptamer screening, structural prediction, and affinity optimization. Through auxiliary analysis, bioinformatics enables the precise prediction of aptamer structures and their interactions with targets, thereby providing a solid foundation for subsequent therapeutic design. Moreover, bioinformatics facilitates advanced data analysis and processing, thereby enhancing the efficiency and precision of the selection and design of aptamers for use in drug development.
Thirdly, although aptamer-based therapies have demonstrated substantial efficacy in vitro, there is a paucity of in vivo studies and clinical data. The transition from laboratory research to clinical trials presents considerable challenges, necessitating comprehensive preclinical evaluations, and standardized efficacy and safety assessments. In fact, in addition to the acute symptoms and immediate complications of COVID-19, long-term effects, often referred to as “long COVID”, have been observed in many individuals who have recovered from the initial infection. These long-term effects can include persistent symptoms such as fatigue, respiratory issues, cognitive dysfunction, reproductive health and other systemic problems that can significantly impact quality of life and daily functioning [ ]. These findings highlight the diverse and sometimes unexpected impacts of COVID-19 infection and treatment on various aspects of health, emphasizing the need for continued research and comprehensive management approaches. Hence, further investigation is required to ascertain the safety and toxicity of nucleic acid aptamers in clinical treatment. In a pioneering study, Tan et al. employed dynamic PET scanning and pharmacokinetic modeling to investigate the distribution and metabolism of radiolabeled aptamers in humans, thereby furnishing indispensable data for the prospective clinical deployment of aptamers [ ]. To facilitate the broader implementation of aptamers in the treatment of SARS-CoV-2, it is imperative to conduct comprehensive investigations into their in vivo toxicity, drug stability, and safety profile. The development of suitable drug delivery systems and effective aptamer designs will significantly enhance the potential for clinical application. Future research should concentrate on enhancing the in vivo efficacy of aptamers to facilitate their clinical implementation in antiviral therapy and broader application in molecular and translational medicine.
In conclusion, aptamers have considerable potential for use in the treatment of SARS-CoV-2. Their high specificity and flexible chemical modifiability render them uniquely advantageous for combating emerging infectious diseases and viral mutations. Further research should be conducted to improve the targeting and stability of aptamers, optimize combination therapy strategies, and validate the safety of aptamers in a variety of clinical settings. Ultimately, these advances will facilitate the widespread application of aptamers in antiviral therapy.
