## Abstract
Reducing the secondary inflammatory response, which is partly mediated by microglia, is a key focus in the treatment of spinal cord injury. Src homology 2-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2), encoded by PTPN11, is widely expressed in the human body and plays a role in inflammation through various mechanisms. Therefore, SHP2 is considered a potential target for the treatment of inflammation-related diseases. However, its role in secondary inflammation after spinal cord injury remains unclear. In this study, SHP2 was found to be abundantly expressed in microglia at the site of spinal cord injury. Inhibition of SHP2 expression using siRNA and SHP2 inhibitors attenuated the microglial inflammatory response in an in vitro lipopolysaccharide-induced model of inflammation. Notably, after treatment with SHP2 inhibitors, mice with spinal cord injury exhibited significantly improved hind limb locomotor function and reduced residual urine volume in the bladder. Subsequent in vitro experiments showed that, in microglia stimulated with lipopolysaccharide, inhibiting SHP2 expression promoted M2 polarization and inhibited M1 polarization. Finally, a co-culture experiment was conducted to assess the effect of microglia treated with SHP2 inhibitors on neuronal cells. The results demonstrated that inflammatory factors produced by microglia promoted neuronal apoptosis, while inhibiting SHP2 expression mitigated these effects. Collectively, our findings suggest that SHP2 enhances secondary inflammation and neuronal damage subsequent to spinal cord injury by modulating microglial phenotype. Therefore, inhibiting SHP2 alleviates the inflammatory response in mice with spinal cord injury and promotes functional recovery postinjury.

## Introduction
Spinal cord injury (SCI), an extreme trauma that affects the central nervous system (Ahuja et al., 2017), can lead to sensory, motor, and autonomic dysfunction below the injury level (Mothe and Tator, 2012; Nakamura and Okano, 2013). Damage to spinal cord tissues triggers the activation of resident immune inflammatory cells (DiSabato et al., 2024), especially astrocytes and microglia, through a sequence of pathological processes (O’Shea et al., 2017; Bellver-Landete et al., 2019; Bradbury and Burnside, 2019; Dias et al., 2021). This activation leads to the release of chemokines, which attract peripheral inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages (Beck et al., 2010). These cells infiltrate the injury site (Kumar et al., 2018), generating substantial amounts of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (Kumar et al., 2018), as well as inflammatory factors (Nguyen et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2023). In addition, macrophage inflammatory protein-1, macrophage chemoattractant protein-1, and interleukin (IL)-8 are released, further recruiting inflammatory cells to the injury site (Mietto et al., 2015) and inducing inflammatory cell polarization to the M1 inflammatory subtype (Gensel and Zhang, 2015), which secretes proinflammatory factors (Gensel and Zhang, 2015). The reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, reactive enzymes, and inflammatory cytokines produced during the secondary inflammatory response further damage neurons and other cell types (Schwartz, 2001). Even if only 5% to 10% of neurons in the injured spinal cord region remain intact, patients can still exhibit controllable muscle activity (Reynolds et al., 2004). Consequently, inhibiting post-SCI inflammation and reducing secondary damage have emerged as crucial strategies for protecting remaining nerve tissues and enabling recovery of spinal cord function (Beattie, 2004).
SHP2 works in tandem with protein tyrosine kinases to orchestrate the dephosphorylation and phosphorylation of protein tyrosines (Song et al., 2021). SHP2 is involved in several inflammatory pathways, such as the cytokine receptor signaling pathway and the nuclear factor-κB pathway signaling pathway (Schoenborn and Wilson, 2007; O’Neill, 2008; Wang et al., 2014). SHP2 regulates the activity of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, a component of the IL-6 signaling pathway, by promoting its dephosphorylation (Ernst and Jenkins, 2004). Furthermore, SHP2 regulates signaling by other inflammatory mediators, thereby having a significant effect on inflammation initiation and progression (Howard et al., 2002). Inhibiting SHP2 participation in the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway results in excessive generation of inflammatory mediators, intensifying the inflammatory response (O’Neill, 2008). However, the specific role that SHP2 plays in the immunoinflammatory microenvironment following SCI remains unclear.
Microglia are the resident immune cells in the central nervous system, and activated microglia can develop into either the proinflammatory M1 or the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Cowan and Petri, 2018; Wang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023). Activated microglia can both promote and inhibit the repair of damaged tissue (Hu et al., 2015; Devanney et al., 2020): M2 microglia release trophic factors and ingest and eliminate cell debris (Gensel et al., 2009; Lourbopoulos et al., 2015), while M1 microglia generate large amounts of proinflammatory mediators, which aggravate the injury (David and Kroner, 2011). Recent investigations have revealed a close relationship between microglial polarization and glucose metabolism. Following activation by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or interferon-gamma, microglia adopt the M1 phenotype, which is characterized by a metabolic shift toward glycolysis. Conversely, M2 polarization is more reliant on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Orihuela et al., 2016; Viola et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023).
In summary, SCI is a severe neurological injury, and secondary inflammation is an important factor leading to further damage and functional impairment of neural tissue. The purpose of studying the role of SHP2 in the secondary inflammatory response following SCI is to gain a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying inflammation. By gaining a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of SHP2 in secondary inflammation, we can identify novel targets and develop innovative strategies for treating neuroinflammation-related diseases. Additionally, this knowledge has the potential to enhance the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions and rehabilitation for SCI patients, thereby improving their quality of life and recovery outcomes.

## Methods

## Animals
Both male and female mice can be used to establish SCI models, but male mice tend to exhibit a more severe response to SCI compared with female mice (Devivo, 2012). For this study, 40 male adult (6–8 weeks of age) C57BL/6 mice, weighing 25 ± 5 g, were obtained from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China; license No. SCXK 2019-0004). The mice were housed in a specific pathogen–free facility at a constant temperature (24 ± 2°C) and humidity (55%–65%). All mice were acclimated to a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle for 14 days prior to experiments and provided with food and water ad libitum. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center at the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (approval No. 2023-N (A)-183, May 2023). The surgical procedure was conducted strictly in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Chinese Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, ensuring the well-being and minimal suffering of the animals. The mice were randomly divided into three groups: Sham, SCI, and SCI + NSC87877 (n = 6/group). Anesthesia was induced with sevoflurane gas inhalation (RWD, Shenzhen, China) at a concentration of 3% and maintained at a concentration of 1%, and the mice were placed on a thermostatic electric blanket to maintain their temperature at 37°C throughout the surgical procedure and recovery period. Each mouse was immobilized, and a laminectomy was performed at the T10–T11 level to expose the dorsal surface of the spinal cord without harming the dura mater. Subsequently, a 5-g weight was dropped from a height of 30 mm to strike the exposed spinal cord, inducing traumatic SCI. Subsequently, the muscle and skin layers were sutured using 4-0 silk thread as described by Zhong et al. (2019) and He et al. (2024). The mice in the Sham group underwent anesthesia and laminectomy but were not subjected to SCI. Every 3 days after the surgery for 14 days the mice in the Sham and SCI groups received intraperitoneal injections of 0.1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), while the SCI + NSC87877 group received intraperitoneal injections of 0.1 mL NSC87877 (an SHP2 inhibitor, 2 mM, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA, Cat# HY-18756). A shows a detailed timeline of the specific in vivo experimental procedures.
 

## Intrathecal adenovirus injection
The experimental mice were randomly assigned to two groups: the SCI group and the AAV-PTPN11 group (n = 6/group). Following successful anesthesia as described above, the mice were injected with PBS or a viral suspension as follows: the lumbar vertebrae were palpated using the index finger of the dominant hand to identify the spinal midline, while the mouse was lifted with the non-dominant hand to create space between the vertebrae for needle insertion. With the beveled surface of the microinjection needle oriented towards the mouse’s head, the needle was inserted at a 70° angle into the space between two lumbar vertebrae along the midline of the spine. Once the needle made contact with the bone, the angle was adjusted to 30°, and the needle was inserted into the subarachnoid space. The plunger of the syringe was then gently depressed to inject 10 μL of adeno-associated virus (AAV) viral suspension (TSINGKE, Beijing, China; serotype AAV9, titer 2.00E+13 vector genomes per mL (VG/mL)) for the AAV-PTPN11 group or PBS for the SCI group. Following injection, the needle was rotated 180° once or twice before being removed from the spine. One week later, the mice in both groups were subjected to SCI as described earlier.

## Spinal cord function assessment

## Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan score
The behavior of the mice was observed for 2 weeks after SCI, including gait, limb coordination, limping, and limb movement. These observations were quantitatively scored using the Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan (BBB) scoring system (Chen et al., 2022), which ranges from 0 to 21. A score of 0 indicates no motor function, while a score of 21 represents normal motor function. The recorded scores were then statistically analyzed to compare the rehabilitation progress among different groups of mice at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 14 days post-SCI.

## Assessment of residual urine volume
Bladder function was assessed by measuring the amount of urine stored in the bladder following SCI (Li et al., 2022). Residual urine volume was monitored daily. To determine the urine volume in SCI mice at various time points postsurgery, 2 mL of PBS was injected into the tail vein. One hour later, the urine was collected to measure the residual urine volume. This process was repeated for mice in different groups at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days after SCI. Statistical analysis of the recorded residual urine volumes was conducted to assess bladder function recovery.

## Rotarod test
Each animal was placed on a rotating rod (Mouse Rotarod Fatigue Meter, Model: ZF54-YLS-4D, Ca# M407469, Beijing Haifidelity Technology Co., LTD, Beijing, China) facing away from the direction of rotation, requiring it to walk forward to maintain its position on the rod (Shan et al., 2023). The speed of rotation was increased slowly, and the latency to fall, as well as the rotational speed at the time of the fall, was recorded. Both measures were used to assess motor function.

## Footprint analysis
Footprint analysis is used to evaluate the neuromotor function of mice after SCI. The hind limbs of mice in different groups were dipped in red and blue ink, and the mice were allowed to walk on a white track to record their movement trajectories. The stride width and stride length were measured. Stride width refers to the distance between the footprints, while stride length refers to the distance between two consecutive footprints (Zeng et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2021).
All behavioral evaluations were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

## Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analyses
Transcriptomic analysis of the SCI and SCI + NSC87877 groups (n = 3 mice/group) was performed. Samples were collected 14 days after SCI. After being anesthetized with 3% sevoflurane, the mice were immediately euthanized by cervical dislocation. Then, the SCI site was fully exposed, and the spinal segment ranging from 0.5 cm above to 0.5 cm below the SCI site was collected. Total RNA was extracted from fresh spinal cord tissue, and mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads carrying oligonucleotides (dT). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to generate a single-stranded circular cDNA library, which was further amplified using phi29 DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to generate DNA nanoballs.
Single-end 50 bp reads were then generated using a BGISEQ500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) with a sequencing depth of 30× for each sample. Bioinformatics analysis of all raw reads of six samples from the SCI and SCI + NSC87877 groups was performed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those exhibiting a fold change > 1.4 and P < 0.05. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/) was used to perform pathway enrichment analysis to identify metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways significantly enriched in the DEGs compared with the whole-genome background. The Gene Ontology (GO) database (http://www.geneontology.org/) (Ashburner et al., 2000) is an internationally standardized system for classifying gene functions. It provides a dynamically updated controlled vocabulary and strictly defined concepts to comprehensively describe the properties of genes and their products across all organisms. GO comprises three main ontologies: molecular function, cellular component, and biological process. The fundamental unit of GO is the GO-term, each of which belongs to a specific ontology. Enrichment analysis is a method that identifies GO terms that are significantly enriched in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared with the genome background. Initially, all DEGs were mapped to GO terms in the GO database. The number of genes associated with each term was calculated, and significantly enriched GO terms among the DEGs were determined by performing a hypergeometric test relative to the genome background.

## Cell culture
The PC12 neuronal cell line (Cat# CL-0481, RRID:CVCL_0481), and the BV2 mouse microglia cell line (Cat# CL0493, RRID: CVCL_0182) were purchased from Procell Life Science and Technology Co. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C in high-sugar Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Procell, Wuhan, China, Cat# 319-005-CL) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, Corning, NY, USA, Cat# 35-081-CV), 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (100X, Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# C0224-100ml).

## In vitro inflammation model
BV2 cells were plated in a six-well plate and treated with increasing concentrations of LPS ranging from 0.2 to 1 μg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat# L3129) for varying lengths of time up to 24 hours. Following treatment, total protein was extracted and subjected to western blot analysis to detect relevant inflammatory markers and determine the levels of SHP2 protein expression.

## Co-culture experiments
Before co-culturing with PC12 cells, BV2 cells were seeded into tissue culture plate inserts (Corning, 0.4 μm pore size, Corning, ME, USA) and cultured overnight. Subsequently, each group of cells was pretreated with PBS, LPS, or LPS + NSC87877 (5 and 10 μM) for 4 hours, then washed with PBS. The inserts containing the pretreated BV2 cells were then added to the lower chambers seeded with PC12 cells. After 24 hours of co-culture, western blotting and immunofluorescence were performed to assess neuronal morphology and apoptosis.

## Cytotoxicity assay
BV2 cells pre-seeded in a 96-well plate were treated with NSC87877 (0–100 μM) for 24 hours at 37°C. Subsequently, 10 μL of Cell Count Kit-8 solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# C0038) was added to each well and incubated again for 30 minutes at 37°C. The absorbance value of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan MK3).

## Cell viability assessment
A Calcein-AM/PI live/dead cell double staining kit (Servicebio, Cat# G1707-100T, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to label live and dead cells. BV2 cells were treated with NSC87877 (0–100 μM), trypsinized, collected, and centrifuged at 200–400 × g for 3 to 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were then washed two or three times with PBS or another appropriate buffer solution to eliminate residual esterase activity. Next, the cells were resuspended in the Calcein-AM/PI detection working solution to a cell density of 1 × 105 to 1 × 106 cells/mL. The cells were incubated in a light-protected cell culture incubator for 15 to 30 minutes before being observed under an inverted microscope (ZEISS Axio Observer7, Jena, Germany).

## Transfection with small interfering RNAs
The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were designed and synthesized by Anhui General Biologicals Co. (Chuzhou, Anhui, China) ( ). To prepare the transfection mix, 5 μL Lipo3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat# L3000001) was diluted in 125 μL of Opti-MEM (Invitrogen, Cat#31985070), and 5 μL of the siRNA was diluted in 125 μL of Opti-MEM + 10 μL of P3000. The two solutions were then combined, and the mixture was incubated for 15 minutes at 37°C before being added to a six-well plate pre-seeded with BV2 cells. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blotting were employed to assess the knockdown efficiency of the siRNA.
 

## Western blotting
Total protein extracted from BV2 cells, PC12 cells, or spinal tissue was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), followed by blocking at room temperature for 1 hour in blocking buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China). The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 20145-1-AP, RRID: AB_10699877), mouse anti-pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 60268-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2881388), rabbit anti-glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 20403-1-AP, RRID: AB_10694437), rabbit anti-hexokinase-1 (HK-1) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 19662-1-AP, RRID: AB_10859778), rabbit anti-lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) (1:2000, Proteintech, Cat# 19987-1-AP, RRID: AB_10646429), rabbit anti-CD163 (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 16646-1-AP, RRID: AB_2756528), rabbit anti-CD86 (1:1000, Affinity, Liyang, Jiangsu, China, Cat# DF6332, RRID: AB_2838296), rabbit anti-CD206 (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 60143-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2144924), mouse anti-B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 68103-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2923635), rabbit anti-Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 50599-2-Ig, RRID: AB_2061561), rabbit anti-arginase-1 (Arg-1) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 66129-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2881528), rabbit anti-cleaved-caspase-3 (c-caspase3) (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat# ab32042, RRID: AB_725947), rabbit anti-iNOS (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab178945, RRID: AB_2861417), rabbit anti-Caspase-3 (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab32499, RRID: AB_777433), and mouse anti-β-actin (1:10,000, Immunoway, Plano, TX, USA, Cat# YM3028, RRID: AB_2629465). The next day, the membranes were incubated at 25°C for 2 hours with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies: HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-mouse IgG(H+L) (1:5000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, RRID: AB-2722565) and HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-rabbit IgG(H+L) (1:5000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2, RRID: AB-2722564). The protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (KF8003, Affinity, Liyang, Jiangsu, China), and images were obtained using a chemiluminescent gel imaging system (Proteinsimple, FluorChem R, San Francisco, CA, USA). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software (ImageJ2, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012). All experiments were repeated three times, and the results were normalized to blank group.

## qPCR
Total RNA was isolated from treated BV2 or PC12 cells using Trizol reagent (Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, the RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix reagent kit (Vazyme, Cat# R122-01, Nanjing, China) to provide a template for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). qRT-PCR was performed using AceQ-qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q111-02) and a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland, LightCycler480 II). The primer sequences used to detect SHP2, iNOS, CD86, IL-1β, TNF-α, Arg-1, CD206, LDHA, HK-1, PKM2, GLUT3, and β-actin are provided in . The relative expression levels of the target genes were normalized to the reference gene β-actin using the 2–ΔΔCt method (Gilyazova et al., 2023).
 

## Immunofluorescence staining
For cell immunofluorescence staining, BV2 or PC12 cells were seeded onto coverslips and cultured in a 24-well plate. After pretreatment, the coverslips were collected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100. Paraffin-embedded spinal cord tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed using ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid antigen retrieval buffer (pH 8.0). The cells grown on coverslips or the tissue sections treated as described above were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Iba-1 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat# 10904-1-AP, RRID: AB_2224377), rabbit anti-NeuN (1:100, Proteintech, Cat# 26975-1-AP, RRID: AB_2880708), rabbit anti-SHP2 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat# 20145-1-AP, RRID: AB_10699877), mouse anti-PKM2 (1:400, Proteintech, Cat# 60268-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2881388), rabbit anti-CD163 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat# 16646-1-AP, RRID: AB_2756528), rabbit anti-CD86 (1:200, Affinity, Cat# DF6332, RRID: AB_2838296), and rabbit anti-c-caspase3 (1:250, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat# ab32042, RRID: AB_725947). The following day, the sections or coverslips were incubated with the following secondary antibodies for 30 to 60 minutes at room temperature: CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG+L (1:400, Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-1, RRID: AB_2810983) and CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:400, Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-4, RRID: AB_2810984). Afterward, the cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Servicebio, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China, Cat# G1012). Immunofluorescence staining images were then captured using a scanner (Pannoramic DESK P-MIDI P250; 3D HISTECH; Budapest, Hungary). To quantify the fluorescence signals, the intensity and percentage of fluorescence were measured in three distinct regions of interest.

## Histological analysis
Spinal tissue sections from 0.5 cm above and 0.5 cm below the SCI site were subjected to histological analysis. A hematoxylin and eosin stain kit (Cat# G1120) and a Nissl stain kit (Cat# G1434) were sourced from SoraBio (Beijing, China). Both hematoxylin-eosin staining and Nissl staining procedures were carried out strictly following the kit manufacturers’ guidelines. After staining, each tissue section was subjected to ethanol dehydration, clearing in xylene for 10 minutes, and sealing with neutral gum. The stained sections were then visualized using a scanning device (Pannoramic DESK P-MIDI P250).

## Lactate and adenosine triphosphate assays
For the lactate assay (Dojindo, CAT# G270, Shanghai, China), BV2 cells from each group were pretreated with LPS, NSC87877, oligomycin (1 mM), or 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) (25 mM, Dojindo, Cat# G270) for 24 hours. The supernatants were then collected and diluted 10-fold with lactate buffer. Subsequently, 20 μL of each diluted sample and 80 μL of lactic acid working solution were added to each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes, after which the absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The results were normalized to the negative control (cell-free media). For the ATP assay (Dojindo, CAT#G270), BV2 cells were pretreated with LPS, NSC87877, oligomycin, or 2-DG for 1 hour. Then, 100 μL of cell suspension and 100 μL of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) working solution were added to each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at room temperature (25°C) for 10 minutes, after which the relative light units (RLUs) were measured using a microplate reader.

## Selection of SHP2 inhibitors
To evaluate the effect of three distinct SHP2 inhibitors - NSC87877, SHP099 (MCE, Cat# HY-100388), and IACS13909 (MCE, Cat# HY-137092) on microglial SHP2, BV2 cells were pretreated with each these inhibitors at a concentration of 5 μM for 2 hours. Subsequently, LPS was added to the BV2 cells for 4 hours. Finally, qPCR was performed to assess the effect of these inhibitors on SHP2 expression.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed by unpaired t-test and one-way or two-way analysis of variance, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test to determine significant differences between groups. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0 for Windows; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA; www.graphpad.com) was used for statistical analysis and data visualization.

## Results

## SHP2 expression is upregulated in mouse tissue from spinal cord injury sites and in LPS-induced microglia
To verify the cellular localization of SHP2 in SCI and the expression of SHP2 in microglial inflammation model, we performed bioassay in vitro and in vivo. Initially, we performed immunofluorescence staining of mouse spinal cord tissue 14 days post-SCI ( A). The results showed a significant upregulation of SHP2 expression at the site of SCI, with a particularly pronounced increase observed in microglia (identified by labeling with IBA-1) ( B and C). To further investigate the temporal changes in SHP2 expression during the acute phase of SCI in mice, spinal cord tissues were collected at various time points. Western blotting revealed a notable elevation in SHP2 protein expression in SCI mice compared with the sham group, with a gradual increase over 14 days ( D and E). In vitro, we stimulated microglia with LPS and observed a significant elevation in SHP2 expression that correlated with increasing LPS concentration and prolonged stimulation time. Concurrently, the expression of microglial inflammation-related markers, iNOS and CD86, also exhibited a gradual increase ( F–M).

## Downregulating SHP2 attenuates inflammation in an in vitro model of spinal cord injury
Given the observed elevation in SHP2 levels in microglia following LPS stimulation in vitro and SCI in mice, we postulated a potential link between SHP2 expression and microglia-mediated inflammation. To explore this, we first tested the efficacy of several commonly used SHP2 inhibitors and found that NSC87877 was the most effective (Additional Figure 1A and B). Next, we tested the cytotoxicity of NSC87877 toward microglia and found that it had no significant adverse effects when used at concentrations below 25 μM, as demonstrated by cell viability staining and Cell Count Kit-8 assays (Additional Figure 1C–E).
Next, we treated LPS-induced BV2 cells NSC87877 at concentrations of 5 and 10 μM to downregulate SHP2 expression. qPCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in the expression of M1-related inflammatory markers, including iNOS, CD86, TNF-α, and IL-1β, in cells treated with NSC87877 compared with untreated cells. Concurrently, we observed an increase in M2-related anti-inflammatory markers, such as Arg-1 and CD163 ( A). To further validate the role of SHP2 in microglia-mediated inflammation, we used an SHP2-specific siRNA (siSHP2) to knock down SHP2 expression in LPS-induced BV2 cells. The qRT-PCR results showed a notable downregdecrease in the mRNA expression levels of M1-related inflammatory markers and a corresponding increase in M2-related anti-inflammatory markers ( B). Subsequent immunofluorescence staining of NSC87877-treated LPS-induced BV2 cells revealed a relative reduction in CD86 of and a significant increase in CD163 fluorescence intensity ( C–E). Western blot analysis further corroborated these findings, showing a reduction in iNOS and CD86 expression and an increase in Arg-1 and CD163 expression in NSC87877-treated LPS-induced BV2 cells ( F and G). Similarly, western blot analysis demonstrated decreased expression of M1-related markers (iNOS and CD86) and increased expression of M2-related markers (Arg-1 and CD163) when SHP2 expression was knocked down in LPS-induced BV2 cells ( H and I).
 

## SHP2 inhibition reduces tissue damage after spinal cord injury, facilitating functional recovery
In order to verify the specific effects of SHP2 on the recovery of spinal cord tissue and spinal cord function in mice with SCI, we injected SHP2 inhibitor intraperitoneally, and then tested the spinal cord function and tissue repair of the mice. Initially, mice were injected intraperitoneally with NSC87877 on days 1, 4, 7, 10, and 13 following SCI. Immunofluorescence staining of spinal cord tissue sections obtained on the 14th day post-SCI revealed a significant reduction of co-localization of Iba-1 and CD86 in SCI mice treated with NSC87877 compared with untreated groups. Conversely, there was a notable increase in Iba-1 and CD163 co-localization ( A and B). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tissue sections from the SCI + NSC87877 group showed a decrease in the injury area compared with the SCI group ( C). Additionally, the BBB score for hind limb function and residual urine volume demonstrated greater hind limb and function in the SCI + NSC87877 group compared with the SCI group ( D and E). Footprint analysis revealed significant improvement in stride length and stride width in mice from the SCI + NSC87877 group compared with mice from the SCI group ( F–H). Additionally, the Rotarod test demonstrated a significant increase in fall latency in mice treated with NSC87877 compared with the SCI group ( I and J). Collectively, these findings suggest that intraperitoneal administration of NSC87877 promotes functional recovery in mice subjected to SCI.
 

## Transcriptome sequencing indicates that SHP2 is related to inflammation and energy metabolism
Transcriptomic sequencing analysis was performed on spinal cord tissues from mice subjected to SCI and either injected intraperitoneally with NSC87877 or left untreated. In total, 462 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 114 that were upregulated and 348 that were downregulated ( A and B). GO analysis showed changes in immune response-related pathways and fatty acid metabolic processes in the spinal cord microenvironment between the two groups of mice ( C). Specifically, we observed a significant decrease in the immune-inflammatory response at the SCI site, as well as an significant increase neuroblast proliferation and axon choice point recognition, in mice treated with NSC87877 ( D and E). KEGG analysis indicated a significant decrease in energy metabolism, particularly in pyruvate pathways, at the SCI site in mice treated with NSC87877 ( F and G). These results revealed a significant correlation between SHP2 and pathways related to inflammation and energy metabolism.
 

## SHP2 regulates glycolysis/OXPHOS in LPS-treated microglia
Given our finding that SHP2 upregulation in microglia in in vitro and in vivo models of SCI was closely related to changes in energy metabolism, we next explored the effect of SHP2 on microglial energy metabolism in SCI. Immunofluorescence staining of mouse spinal cord tissues 14 days post-SCI revealed a substantial increase in Iba-1 and PKM2 expression and co-localization in microglia at the injury site. Conversely, Iba-1 and PKM2 expression levels in mice subjected to SCI and treated with NSC87877 were significantly decreased (Additional Figure 2A and B). In vitro, SHP2 knockdown in LPS-induced BV2 cells led to reduced levels of glycolysis-related markers, including GLUT3, PKM2, HK-1, and LDHA, compared with the LPS-induced group ( A and B). qPCR analysis of LPS-induced BV2 cells treated with different concentrations of NSC87877 showed reduced mRNA levels of glycolysis markers compared with the LPS-induced group ( C), and these results were confirmed by western blot (Additional Figure 2C and D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that microglia exhibit a predominantly glycolytic phenotype after SCI, and that SHP2 regulates this increase in glycolysis. Inhibiting SHP2 reduces glycolysis in microglia. ATP generated by the glycolytic pathway or the OXPHOS pathway is necessary energy source for cell survival (Jourdain et al., 2021). Treating cells with inhibitors such as oligomycin and 2-DG can induce a switch to glycolysis using alternate metabolic pathways ( D).
 To evaluate the degree of cell dependence on glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), we used a glycolysis/OXPHOS kit to assess the effect of SHP2 on ATP and lactate production by BV2 cells. In LPS-treated BV2 cells, OXPHOS-induced ATP production was significantly reduced by treatment with oligomycin ( E). However, combined treatment with NSC87877 followed by oligomycin led to an increase in OXPHOS-induced ATP production and a reduction in glycolysis-induced ATP production ( E). In the group treated with LPS alone, total microglial ATP production was substantially reduced by treatment with 2-DG ( E). The lactate assay showed an elevated lactate content in the culture medium of BV2 cells treated with LPS alone, while decreased lactate was observed in the culture medium of cells treated with both LPS and NSC87877. Interestingly, the relative lactate content increased after treatment with oligomycin ( F). Overall, BV2 cells treated with NSC87877 exhibited increased ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation and decreased ATP production by glycolysis ( G).

## Glycolysis/OXPHOS regulates the inflammatory phenotype of LPS-induced BV2 cells
BV2 cell markers were detected by western blotting after treatment with different combinations of LPS, oligomycin, and 2-DG. The results showed that, in LPS-induced BV2 cells, expression levels of SHP2 and the M1 markers iNOS and CD86 increased significantly, and this increase was even more pronounced when the cells were treated with oligomycin. In the LPS + 2-DG group, iNOS and CD86 expression levels decreased (though not significantly), while expression of the M2-related anti-inflammatory marker CD163 increased. These findings demonstrate that glycolysis promoted LPS-induced microglia to adopt an M1 phenotype, while OXPHOS promoted adoption of the M2 phenotype. Compared with the LPS group, SHP2 expression was not significantly different in the LPS + 2-DG or LPS + oligomycin group. In the LPS + 2-DG group, treatment with NSC87877 led to upregulation of iNOS and CD86, markers of M1 polarization, and downregulation of CD163, a marker of M2 polarization (Zeiner et al., 2019; H–L). Thus, SHP2 helps mediate LPS-induced glycolysis in microglia to induce M1 polarization.

## SHP2 inhibition of reduces neuronal apoptosis
To verify the effect of SHP2 on neuronal cells, we constructed a mice model of SCI in vivo and a cell co-culture system in vitro. Firstly, we examined the impact of microglial polarization on neuronal cells post-SCI. We collected spinal cord tissues from mice 14 days post-SCI for immunofluorescence staining. This staining revealed a significant decrease in NeuN, a neuronal cell marker (Eriksson et al., 1998), in the area surrounding the SCI lesion, along with an increase in SHP2 expression. In contrast, mice treated with NSC87877 exhibited lower SHP2 expression and higher NeuN expression compared with the SCI group ( A and B). Nissl staining of spinal cord sections supported these findings ( C). Next, a cell co-culture assay was performed to assess the effect of microglial inflammatory polarization on neuronal cells. Microglia pretreated with PBS, LPS, or LPS + NSC87877 were seeded into the upper chamber of the co-culture apparatus, while PC12 cells were seeded into the bottom chamber. After 24 hours of co-culture, PC12 cell apoptosis was assessed by western blot analysis of apoptosis markers. The results indicated a significant increase in C-caspase3 and Bax expression in the LPS group, a significant decrease in the expression of these markers in the LPS + NSC87877 group, and the expression of the antiapoptotic marker Bcl-2 was increased in the LPS + NSC87877 group ( D and E). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of the apoptosis marker C-caspase3 in PC12 cells co-cultured with BV2 cells in the LPS + NSC87877 group ( F and G). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining of spinal cord sections from mice 14 days post-SCI showed a significant reduction in co-localization of NeuN and C-caspase3 immunofluorescence in mice injected intraperitoneally with NSC87877 (Additional Figure 3A and B). Furthermore, western blot analysis of total protein extracted from the spinal cord showed a significant decresse in SHP2, C-caspase3, and Bax expression in mice treated with NSC87877, while Bcl-2 expression displayed an significant increase (Additional Figure 3C and D).
 

## SHP2 overexpression exacerbates tissue damage following spinal cord injury, further impairing neurological function
To further investigate the pathological role of SHP2 in mice subjected to SCI, we used adeno-associated virus (AAV-PTPN11) to overexpress SHP2 in the injured spinal cord region. Immunofluorescence staining showed a significant increase in SHP2 expression near the SCI site in mice injected with AAV-PTPN11 ( A and B), as well as a significant increase in Iba-1 and PKM2 expression, compared with the SCI group. Furthermore, Iba-1 and PKM2 co-localization was significantly increased compared with the SCI group ( C–F), again demonstrating a significant increase in microglial glycolysis after SCI. In the SHP2-overexpressing group, NeuN and C-caspase3 expression and co-localization were significantly increased around the SCI site compared with the SCI group ( D–H), further confirming the role of SHP2 in promoting neuronal apoptosis after SCI. Hematoxylin-esoin staining demonstrated that SHP2 overexpression exacerbated tissue damage following SCI, and Nissl staining results showed a significant reduction in the number of neurons present around the SCI in mice overexpressing SHP2 ( I). Moreover, mice overexpressing SHP2 exhibited significantly lower BBB scores compared with the SCI group, as well as a relative increase in residual urine volume ( J and K).
 Taken together, these findings indicate that SHP2 played an important role in in vitro and in vivo models of SCI. SHP2 promoted microglial polarization toward an inflammatory phenotype by regulating the glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation balance in these cells. Furthermore, SHP2 enhanced the production of proinflammatory factors by polarized microglia, leading to neuronal apoptosis. These findings demonstrate a novel function for SHP2 in the regulation of microglia-mediated inflammation. Further investigation of the regulatory mechanisms and signaling pathways associated with SHP2 will contribute to understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying inflammation after SCI and provide a theoretical basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies. This study offers new insight into and directions for the treatment and rehabilitation of patients with SCI.

## Discussion
SCI is a significant health challenge with limited effective treatments, necessitating a thorough exploration of its pathological mechanisms to develop novel therapeutic strategies. In this study we found that upregulation of SHP2 expression by microglia at the injury site is associated with SCI progressionupreg. SHP2, a tyrosine phosphatase, modulates various signaling pathways through dephosphorylation and plays a key role in immunoinflammatory signaling pathways (Cowan and Petri, 2018). Microglial activation is a recognized hallmark of the secondary inflammatory response following SCI (Devanney et al., 2020). Here we addressed whether SHP2 mediates microglial inflammation, thereby exacerbating SCI damage. Our findings demonstrate that SHP2 downregulation effectively mitigated LPS-induced microglial production of proinflammatory factors. Our findings confirm the regulatory role of SHP2 in inflammatory disease and, for the first time, propose the involvement of SHP2 in secondary microglia-mediated inflammation following SCI.
Microglia-mediated inflammation is closely associated with functional recovery from SCI (Al Mamun et al., 2020). LPS-induced release of destructive proinflammatory mediators from activated M1 microglia adversely affects neuronal cells (Xiong et al., 2016); conversely, activated M2 microglia eliminate cellular debris via phagocytosis and release a variety of trophic and protective substances that may have neuroprotective effects (Xu et al., 2018). Thus, neuronal and functional recovery following SCI are promoted by M2 microglia and inhibited by M1 microglia (Han et al., 2018). The findings of our study demonstrate that SHP2 inhibition promotes microglial polarization from an M1 inflammatory phenotype to an M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype. And the production of inflammatory factors by microglia may contribute to neuronal apoptosis. These findings suggest that SHP2 can inhibit microglia-mediated inflammation thereby rescuing neuronal apoptosis.
Microglial polarization, a process by which microglia acquire different functional phenotypes in response to various stimuli, is intricately linked to metabolic reprogramming, particularly glycolysis (Yu et al., 2023). Proinflammatory microglia show an increased dependence on glycolysis even in normoxic environments. This glycolytic shift supports the rapid production of ATP and provides intermediates for biosynthetic pathways, thus promoting the proinflammatory activities of M1 microglia. Additionally, anti-inflammatory M2 microglia, which promote tissue repair, are largely dependent on OXPHOS for energy. Our findings indicate that glycometabolism in BV2 cells was mainly driven by glycolysis, and that LPS stimulation enhanced glycolysis and promoted microglial polarization to an inflammatory phenotype. SHP2 regulated the balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS in BV2 cells, and inhibiting SHP2 suppressed glycolysis in LPS-induced cells, thereby inhibiting inflammatory polarization of BV2 cells and promoting their polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.
This study had some limitations. The SCI microenvironment undergoes extremely complex changes that cannot be fully simulated by in vitro inflammation models or understood by assessing cell–cell interactions (Norden et al., 2016). In addition, it was difficult to ensure that the impact to the spinal cord was consistently delivered at the same location in the mouse SCI model, which may have led to slight intra-group variations in the data. Moreover, subjective factors have a significant impact when conducting behavioral scoring in mice. Furthermore, SHP2 inhibitors are still in the preclinical stage, and their biotoxicity, cellular targeting, and applications still need to be investigated in depth.
In summary, SHP2 promoted glycolysis, thereby inducing microglial M1 polarization and enhancing inflammation and neuronal damage in the context of SCI ( ). Therefore, we believe that SHP2 inhibitors could represent a novel therapeutic approach for SCI.
