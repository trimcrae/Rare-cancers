## Abstract
Cardiac arrest can lead to severe neurological impairment as a result of inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and post-cardiopulmonary resuscitation neurological damage. Hypoxic preconditioning has been shown to improve migration and survival of bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells and reduce pyroptosis after cardiac arrest, but the specific mechanisms by which hypoxia-preconditioned bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells protect against brain injury after cardiac arrest are unknown. To this end, we established an in vitro co-culture model of bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells and oxygen–glucose deprived primary neurons and found that hypoxic preconditioning enhanced the protective effect of bone marrow stromal stem cells against neuronal pyroptosis, possibly through inhibition of the MAPK and nuclear factor κB pathways. Subsequently, we transplanted hypoxia-preconditioned bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells into the lateral ventricle after the return of spontaneous circulation in an 8-minute cardiac arrest rat model induced by asphyxia. The results showed that hypoxia-preconditioned bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells significantly reduced cardiac arrest–induced neuronal pyroptosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage, whereas knockdown of the liver isoform of phosphofructokinase in bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells inhibited these effects. To conclude, hypoxia-preconditioned bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells offer a promising therapeutic approach for neuronal injury following cardiac arrest, and their beneficial effects are potentially associated with increased expression of the liver isoform of phosphofructokinase following hypoxic preconditioning.

## Introduction
Even after successful resuscitation, the prognosis for cardiac arrest (CA) survivors remains poor, partly because of post-CA syndrome (Helmerhorst et al., 2015). Brain injury is the main driver of poor outcomes in patients who achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC; Wei et al., 2020). Previous experimental and clinical research has shown that therapeutic mild hypothermia improves neurological outcomes after CA (Wang et al., 2019). However, controversy surrounds its application, and there is a lack of consensus regarding the target temperature, timing of hypothermia initiation, and cooling duration (Mody et al., 2019). Thus, there is a crucial need to explore effective neuroprotective strategies for CA patients.
It is now understood that global cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI) is a significant mechanism underlying brain tissue damage after resuscitation (Sandroni et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that various cell death pathways, including apoptosis, autophagy, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis, contribute to the pathophysiological processes involved in cerebral IRI (Martin et al., 1998; Zheng et al., 2019; Tuo et al., 2022). Cell pyroptosis plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of cerebral IRI. It is characterized by cell swelling and pore formation in the cell membrane (Cui et al., 2021). Inflammatory caspases, such as caspase-1 or caspase-4/5/11, are key players in carrying out pyroptosis. They process pro-interleukin (IL)-1β and pro-IL-18 into the mature cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, respectively. These cytokines are then released from the cell through the newly formed pores, triggering an inflammatory cascade (Cui et al., 2021).
In recent years, stem cell–based therapies have been explored as a promising treatment option for neurological diseases (Berlet et al., 2021). Among these, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are multipotent cells that are actively involved in repair processes leading to tissue regeneration. Notably, MSCs have several advantages, including their ability to be derived from diverse sources, low immunogenicity, the capacity to modulate the immune response, and resistance to oxidative stress (Yang et al., 2022a). A previous study demonstrated that MSCs exert a therapeutic effect on brain injuries, such as stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, traumatic brain injury, and Parkinson’s disease (Andrzejewska et al., 2021). Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that the neuroprotective effects of MSCs are largely mediated by paracrine signaling (Cunningham et al., 2018; Gunawardena et al., 2019). MSCs can alter the microenvironment at the injury sites by secreting IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α. They can also inhibit microglial activation and induce IL-10 expression in areas of brain infarction (Darsalia et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2021d, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023c). MSCs may also promote regulatory T cell function, facilitate axonal growth, and decrease the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins and caspases (Li et al., 2021). However, a major challenge in stem cell–based therapy is ensuring the survival of transplanted cells (Regmi et al., 2019). In our previous study, we observed that hypoxic preconditioning (HP) significantly enhanced the anti-pyroptotic capacity of MSCs after cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (Tang et al., 2023). Increasing evidence suggests that HP represents a potential approach for increasing cell survival and treating several diseases, including intracerebral hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, and IRI (Wei et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021b).
Metabolic enzymes play a crucial role in cellular development, function, and the regulation of various biological processes as they provide the energy necessary for these activities (Biswas et al., 2013; Lian et al., 2024). Cellular functions such as proliferation, migration, and apoptosis can trigger metabolic shifts and rely heavily on the activity of these enzymes (Vander Heiden et al., 2009). The metabolic mechanism used by different cells shifts to meet the energy demands in response to changes in the surrounding microenvironment (Yin et al., 2017). This adaptation often involves alterations in the expression levels of specific enzymes. Notably, stem cells rely primarily on glycolysis to generate energy (Shum et al., 2016). However, the precise relationship between glucose metabolism and cellular functions, especially in the context of the reparative function of hypoxia-preconditioned BMSCs (HP-BMSCs) in a CPR model, remains unclear.
Current evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction represents an important pathophysiological process in global cerebral IRI post-resuscitation (Liang et al., 2021). Mitochondria not only play a central role in energy production, but also regulate cell growth, metabolism, cell death, and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Wan et al., 2021). Dysfunctional mitochondria can generate ROS, contributing to oxidative stress, which, in turn, exacerbates mitochondrial damage (Vegh et al., 2021). Previous research has demonstrated that molecules associated with mitochondrial damage can promote assembly and activation of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome (Zhong et al., 2016). In addition, another study showed that MSCs alleviate ischemia and hypoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in PC12 cells (Yang et al., 2020a). Hence, it seems likely that mitigating oxidative stress at the mitochondrial level could help suppress neuronal pyroptosis after stem cell transplantation following CA.
In this study, we postulated that HP-BMSCs could preserve mitochondrial function, thereby protecting neurons from oxidative stress, by suppressing pyroptosis following resuscitation. To test this, RNA sequencing was conducted to identify the molecular changes that occurred during BMSC adaptation to a hypoxic environment. Our findings shed new light on the neuroprotective effects of HP-BMSCs.

## Methods

## Animals
To exclude the effects of the hormonal cycle of female rats, male Sprague–Dawley rats were used to establish the CA model. Male rats were obtained from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Inc. (Beijing, China; animal license No. SCXK (Jing) 2019-0008). Forty adult male rats (8–10 weeks old, 200–250 g) were used for the CPR study, while six younger male rats (3–4 weeks, 70–90 g) were used for BMSC extraction. Additionally, eight timed pregnant rats at day 18 of gestation were used as a source of cortical neurons. All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment with controlled temperature (24 ± 2°C) and humidity ([60 ± 10]%) and a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (approval No. IACUC FJMU 2022-0577, approval date: September 22, 2022). All experiments were designed and reported according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines (Percie du Sert et al., 2020). Every effort was made to minimize animal discomfort and suffering.
The 40 male rats used in the CPR study were randomly divided into five groups as follows: the sham group (in which the rats underwent an identical surgical procedure without CPR or treatment), the CPR + phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group (in which the rats experienced CA/CPR and received an equivalent volume of PBS), the CPR + HP-BMSCs group (in which the rats underwent CA/CPR with HP-BMSC injection), the CPR + si-NC HP-BMSCs group (in which the rats received si-NC HP-BMSCs after ROSC), and the CPR + si-PFKL HP-BMSCs group (in which the rats were transplanted with si-PFKL HP-BMSCs post-resuscitation) (n = 8 per group).

## Isolation, culturing, and identification of BMSCs
Following anesthesia by inhalation of 2% sevoflurane (AbbVie, Osaka, Japan), the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The femurs and tibias were carefully extracted from both legs, and any surrounding soft tissue was meticulously removed. BMSCs were isolated from the bone marrow cavity by flushing with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium-F12 (DMEM-F12, Gibco, New York, NY, USA). The cell suspensions were collected and centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 minutes, then resuspended in DMEM-F12 with 10% fetal bovine serum (Clark, Richmond, VA, USA). The cells were seeded into cell culture flasks (4 mL/flask) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every 48 hours. When the cells reached 90% confluency, they were detached from the culture flask using 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Gibco) and passaged at the same cell density ratio (1:2) for each passage. Third- to fifth-passage BMSCs were used for all subsequent experiments. BMSC-specific surface antigens were detected by flow cytometry (Lin et al., 2020) using CD29-FITC, CD90-PE, CD11b-PE, and CD45-FITC antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Furthermore, differentiated BMSCs were identified through alizarin red staining for osteogenesis and Oil Red O staining for adipogenesis. BMSC differentiation was induced using kits purchased from HyCyte (Haixing Bioscience, Suzhou, China).

## PFKL silencing and overexpression
When the cell density reached 70%, BMSCs and BMSCs expressing luciferase were transfected with a PFKL-specific small interfering RNA (si-PFKL) or a negative control small interfering RNA (si-NC) using a commercial transfection kit (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Seventy-two hours post-transfection, PFKL downregulation was confirmed by western blotting. For the in vivo experiments, si-PFKL BMSCs and si-NC BMSCs were subjected to hypoxia and subsequently injected into the lateral ventricles of rats following ROSC. The PFKL siRNA sequence was 5′-GCC CUU ACA UUC CAG UUC UTT-3′, while the negative control siRNA sequence was 5′-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UTT-3′. Both siRNAs were obtained from GenePharma. To induce PFKL overexpression (oe-PFKL), BMSCs were transfected with a plasmid containing the PFKL gene (oe-PFKL group), and control cells were transfected with an empty plasmid lacking the PFKL gene (oe-NC group); both plasmids were purchased from GenePharma.

## Preparation of HP-BMSCs
BMSCs and si-RNA BMSCs were placed in a hypoxic chamber (Biospherix, Parish, NY, USA) with an atmosphere containing 5% CO2, 3% O2, and 92% N2 at 37°C for 48 hours.

## HP-BMSC transfection with lentiviral vectors encoding luciferase
BMSCs and siRNA BMSCs were seeded into 3.6-mL culture flasks at a density of approximately 2 × 105 cells per flask and transfected with 400 μL of a lentivirus encoding luciferase (1 × 108 TU/mL, GenePharma). Following transfection, the cells were subjected to hypoxia, as previously described (Tang et al., 2023).

## Total RNA extraction
Cellular RNA was isolated using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and any residual DNA was eliminated using DNase I (Takara, Dalian, China). The quality of the extracted RNA was assessed using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and RNA quantity was determined using a ND-2000 instrument (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA).

## mRNA library construction
mRNA libraries were constructed using a TruSeq RNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). In brief, poly(A) mRNA was isolated from the total RNA using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Next, fragmentation buffer was added to cleave the mRNA into short fragments. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the mRNA fragments using a SuperScript Double-Stranded cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen), yielding both first-strand and second-strand cDNA. To finalize the preparation of double-stranded cDNA, end repair was performed using End Repair Mix (Enzymatics, Beverly, MA, USA). Additionally, “A” bases were added to the 3′ ends to construct the Y-form linker. Finally, the cDNA libraries were purified and enriched by PCR. After quantification using a TBS-380 Mini-Fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), the libraries were sequenced with a paired-end read length of 150 bases on a NovaSeq 6000 Instrument (Illumina).

## RNA sequencing analysis and identification of differentially expressed genes
cDNA libraries from BMSCs and HP-BMSCs were sequenced using an Illumina sequencer (Illumina). Adaptor sequences were determined using SeqPrep (https://github.com/jstjohn/SeqPrep) and Sickle software (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle). Next, the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) method was used to obtain transcript level expression estimates, and transcript abundances were quantified using RSEM (https://deweylab.github.io/RSEM/). Finally, the edgerR package (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/2.12/bioc/html/edgeR.html) was used to identify differentially expressed genes. Sequencing data were deposited in NCBI under accession number PRJNA 1033159.

## Gene Ontology functional analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis
All differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mapped to the GeneGo database (http://www.geneontology.org/; Ashburner et al., 2000), and enrichment results with a corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered to be significantly different. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed at three levels, including cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. Similarly, all DEGs were mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and significant enrichment was determined.

## Assessment of cell viability
Cell viability was detected using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) kit (Meilun Biotech, Dalian, China). Briefly, BMSCs, si-NC BMSCs, and si-PFKL BMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Then, the cells were subjected to hypoxia (3% O2) for 48 hours. Four hours prior to the termination of the hypoxic period, CCK8 solution (10 µL/100 µL of growth medium) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 4 hours. The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Richmond, CA, USA).

## Apoptosis assay
Apoptosis was determined using an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection kit (Meilun Biotech). BMSCs, si-NC BMSCs, si-PFKL BMSCs, oe-NC BMSCs, and oe-PFKL BMSCs were subjected to hypoxia. The cells were then detached from the culture flasks using 0.25% trypsin without EDTA, washed with PBS twice, and incubated with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of PI for 15 minutes in the dark. Apoptotic cells were identified using a flow cytometer (BD, Durham, NC, USA), and data were analyzed using Accuri C6 software (BD). These measurements were repeated four times.

## Transwell migration assay
The cell migration assay was conducted using a 24-well insert (Corning, Lowell, MA, USA) with 8-µm pores. Briefly, HP-BMSCs, si-NC BMSCs, and si-PFKL BMSCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells in the upper chamber in 200 μL of serum-free medium, while the lower chambers contained complete media. The cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. After incubation, cells in the upper layer were wiped off with wet cotton swabs, and cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbao, Beijing, China). Finally, the migrated cells were observed using an inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and counted using ImageJ software (version 1.52i, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012).

## Cell cycle distribution analysis
The cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a cell cycle analysis kit (Meilun Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of cells in each phase of the cell cycle was determined with a flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using ModFit software (BD).

## Establishment of the CA/CPR rat model
We next established an asphyxial CA model according to a previously described protocol, with some modifications (Lin et al., 2020). Prior to the surgical procedure, all rats underwent a 12-hour fasting period during which they had unrestricted access to water. Anesthesia was induced with 2% sevoflurane, followed by intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) to maintain a deep level of anesthesia. Subsequently, each rats was intubated with a 14-gauge intravenous catheter (BD) and connected to a small animal ventilator (Alcott Biotech, Shanghai, China). The ventilator parameters were configured as follows: a respiratory rate of 100 times per minute, a tidal volume of 6.5 mL/kg, and an inhaled oxygen concentration of 21%. Additionally, a PE-30 tubing (SDR Scientific, Sydney, Australia) was introduced into the right femoral artery for continuous monitoring of mean arterial blood pressure (MAP). Conventional lead II electrocardiogram readings were monitored continuously. Both MAP and electrocardiogram signals were recorded using a Power Lab data acquisition instrument (AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia). Core body temperature was continuously monitored throughout the surgical procedure using a rectal probe and maintained within a narrow range of 36.5 to 37°C with an automated heating blanket (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China).
Baseline physiological measurements were obtained for 5 minutes. After injection of vecuronium (Xianju Pharmaceutical, Taizhou, China, 1 mg/kg) into the right femoral artery, asphyxia was induced by turning off the ventilator. CA was defined as MAP < 25 mmHg and was maintained for 8 minutes (Additional Figure 1A). Subsequently, rats were mechanically ventilated at 7 minutes 45 seconds post-CA with pure oxygen, while other ventilator parameters were maintained at the previously described settings. At 8 minutes after CA, manual chest compressions were initiated at a rate of 200 compressions per minute. The depth of these compressions was adjusted to maintain the MAP above 25 mmHg. One minute after starting chest compressions, epinephrine (0.05 mg/kg) and unfractionated heparin (0.5 IU in 0.1 mL) were injected into the right femoral artery. ROSC was defined as MAP > 60 mmHg lasting at least 5 minutes. If the spontaneous circulation was not restored, CPR was considered unsuccessful. During the monitoring period, pure oxygen ventilation was provided for 1 hour following initiation of resuscitation. Subsequently, the inhaled oxygen concentration was adjusted to 21% for an additional hour. After 2 hours of ROSC, the endotracheal tube and intra-arterial catheter were removed, and the right femoral artery was ligated, followed by closure of the incision. At the end of the operation, all rats received a 0.4-mL intraperitoneal injection of penicillin (10,000 IU). The entire process was performed by the same investigator for each rat, and none of the rats developed infections at the incision site. Rats were excluded if: 1) ROSC was not achieved within 2 minutes of CPR; 2) mechanical ventilation dependence persisted, with an inability to breathe spontaneously; or 3) death occurred as a result of natural or accidental causes prior to sample collection.

## Cell transplantation following ROSC
Two hours after successful resuscitation, rats in the HP-BMSCs, si-NC HP-BMSCs, and si-PFKL BMSCs groups were injected intraventricularly with 15 μL of cells (1 × 106 cells) suspended in PBS. The PBS group was injected with an equivalent volume of PBS. Subsequently, the rats were re-anesthetized with pentobarbital, and the heads were fixed on a stereo-positioning instrument (RWD Life Science). The skull was exposed, and a 1-mm burr hole (coordinates (Lin et al., 2013): anterior–posterior –0.8 mm, lateral 1.5 mm, dorsal–ventral –4.0 mm) was made using an electric cranial drill (RWD Life Science). The cell suspension was injected into the ventricle using a 25-μL microsyringe (Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland) connected to a microinjection pump (RWD Life Science), with the syringe set to a flow rate of 1 μL/min. Following injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 15 minutes to allow for diffusion of the grafted cells. Finally, the burr hole was sealed with bone wax (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA), and the scalp incision was sutured using 3-0 nylon.

## In vivo imaging of transplanted HP-BMSCs
We used a small animal live imaging system (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA) to assess successful injection and survival of the cells. Twenty-four hours post-resuscitation, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with pentobarbital, followed by intraperitoneal injection with 150 mg/kg D-Luciferin (PerkinElmer). Twenty minutes later, images were acquired of the bioluminescent viable cells using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

## Neurofunctional measurement
Neurological status was evaluated by assigning a neurological deficit score (Geocadin et al., 2000) 24 hours after ROSC. This comprehensive score ranges from 0 to 80, with lower scores indicating greater impairment, and assesses various domains, including general behavior, brainstem function, motor function, sensory function, motor behavior, overall behavior, and twitching.

## Sample collection and storage
Following assessment for neurological deficits, rats were anesthetized with sevoflurane, and blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture. Subsequently, the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, thoracotomy was performed to expose the heart, and the rats were transcardially perfused with precooled sterile saline to remove the remaining blood. Target brain regions were swiftly dissected on ice. The mitochondrial ultrastructure in the right frontal cortex was assessed by transmission electron microscopy. The right parietal cortex was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and reserved for Nissl staining, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence analysis. In addition, the left frontal cortex was used for biochemical indicator testing, ROS level detection, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Target proteins from the left parietal cortex were examined by western blotting. The blood samples were centrifuged for 15 minutes at 1400 × g, and the serum was stored at –80°C for later use in ELISAs.

## Nissl staining
Briefly, brain tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 hours, dehydrated in a graded alcohol series, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were then sliced into 5-μm–thick sections. These specimens were rinsed in distilled water and subjected to staining with toluidine blue (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 60°C for 30 minutes. After staining, the sections were washed with water, differentiated in 95% ethanol for 10 minutes, and cleared in xylene. Following ethanol dehydration, the slides were sealed with neutral gum and examined by optical microscopy (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The average optical density (AOD) of the neurons detected by Nissl staining was calculated using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Bethesda, MD, USA, http://www.mediacy.com/).

## Immunohistochemical assay
The paraffin-embedded brain sections were subjected to dewaxing and hydration, followed by antigen retrieval. Then, the sections were treated with hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 25 minutes to reduce endogenous peroxidase activity. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio), the sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies at 4°C overnight: 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE, rabbit, 1:200, Bioss, Beijing, China, Cat# bs-6313R, RRID: AB_282774) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG, rabbit, 1:200, Bioss, Cat# bs-1278R, RRID: AB_10856120). The next day, goat anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase (1:200, Servicebio, Cat# GB23303, RRID: AB_2811189) was added, and the sections were incubated at 24°C for 50 minutes, followed by the addition of diaminobenzidine to develop the color. Counterstaining was carried out using hematoxylin, and the sections were dehydrated and mounted. Finally, the stained sections were observed under an inverted microscope.

## Immunofluorescence staining
First, paraffin-embedded brain sections were dewaxed and then subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in a microwave oven for 21 minutes in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid buffer (pH 8.0). Subsequently, the tissues were blocked with bovine serum albumin (Servicebio) for 30 minutes. Following this, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-iNOS antibody (rabbit, 1:100, Immunoway, Plano, TX, USA, Cat# YT3169, RRID: AB_3065192) and an anti-NeuN antibody (rabbit, 1:200, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA, Cat# 26975-1-AP, RRID: AB_2880708). Next, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Servicebio, Cat# GB23303, RRID: AB_2811189) and CY3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Servicebio, Cat# GB21303, RRID: AB_2861435) for 50 minutes at 24°C in the dark. Following washing with PBS, the cell nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio). Subsequently, all sections were treated with a fluorescence quenching agent (Servicebio). Finally, the sections were sealed using an anti-fluorescence attenuation sealant (Servicebio) and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon).

## Evaluation of oxidative stress
Oxidative stress was assessed by detecting relevant markers, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), in cortex tissue, using kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Additionally, ROS levels in brain tissue were assessed using a ROS assay kit (Genmed Scientifics, Boston, MA, USA).

## ATP detection and NAD+/NADH ratio calculation
ATP levels in the brain tissues were measured using an enhanced ATP assay kit (Beyotime), while the NAD+/NADH ratio was assessed using an NAD+/NADH assay kit based on WST-8 (Beyotime).

## ELISA
IL-1β and IL-18 expression levels in the left frontal cortex were determined using ELISA kits (Cloud-Clone, Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, the S100B serum concentration was measured using an ELISA kit (Cloud-Clone) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Western blotting
After incubating on ice for 30 minutes, the lysates were centrifuged at 22,500 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant containing the proteins was collected, and the protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, the samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, which was blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk for 30 minutes. Then, the samples were incubated at 4°C overnight with the appropriate primary antibodies: translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20, rabbit, 1:5000, Proteintech, Cat# 11802-1-AP, RRID: AB_2207530), fission 1 protein (FIS1, rabbit, 1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 10956-1-AP, RRID: AB_2102532), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COX IV, rabbit, 1:5000, Proteintech, Cat# 11242-1-AP, RRID: AB_2085278), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF, rabbit, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, Cat# ab108319, RRID: AB_10862052), NLRP3 (rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YT5382, RRID: AB_3095508), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC, rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YT0365, RRID: AB_2750947), cleaved-caspase-1 (rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YC0022, RRID: AB_3095510), gasdermin D N-terminal domain (GSDMD-N, rabbit, Abcam, 1:1000, Cat# ab219800, RRID: AB_2888940), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK, rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YT3513, RRID: AB_3095512), JNK (rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YT2440, RRID: AB_2895034), NF-κB p65 (mouse, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YM3111, RRID: AB_3073611), Bcl-2 (mouse, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YM3041, RRID: AB_2814758), bak (mouse, 1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 29552-1-AP, RRID: AB_2923596), activated-caspase-3 (mouse, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YM3431, RRID: AB_3095515), PFKL (rabbit, 1:1000, Immunoway, Cat# YT3685, RRID: AB_3095516), and β-actin (rabbit, 1:5000, CST, Boston, MA, USA, Cat# 4970, RRID: AB_2223172). Afterward, the membrane was incubated with the following secondary antibodies at 24°C for 1 hour: goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Immunoway, Cat# RS0002, RRID: AB_2943496) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Immunoway, Cat# RS0001, RRID: AB_2943495). Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) ultrasensitive luminescent solution (Meilun Biotech) was used for chemiluminescence. The protein signals were detected using a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, Irvine, CA, USA). Data are expressed as the optical density ratio to β-actin.

## Transmission electron microscopy
Brain tissue collected at 24 hours post-ROSC was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Thereafter, the samples were fixed with 1% osmic acid for 2 hours at 4°C and dehydrated using varying concentrations of alcohol and acetone. Next, the specimens were embedded in 812 resin and polymerized for 48 hours at 60°C. The resin block was subjected to ultra-thin sectioning using an ultramicrotome, generating sections with a thickness of 60–80 nm that were placed onto copper mesh grids. These grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the samples were observed under a transmission electron microscope, and the mitochondrial area was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software.

## Isolation and culture of primary cortical neurons
Primary neurons were obtained from the cerebral cortex of fetal rats (embryonic day 18) (Gu et al., 2021). Briefly, pregnant rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg) and euthanized, and the embryos were retrieved under aseptic conditions. The fetal rat cortical tissue was minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 25 minutes to isolate primary neurons. The tissue was then filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer and resuspended in DMEM-F12 (Gibco). For immunofluorescence and western blotting, 1 × 106 and 1 × 108 cells, respectively, were seeded into cell culture plates coated with polylysine and incubated for 8 hours. Next, the medium was changed to neurobasal medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2% B27 supplement (Gibco), 2 mM glutamine (Meilun Biotech), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Meilun Biotech). Two days later, the cells were treated with 10 μM cytarabine to restrict the growth of glial cells. Finally, mature neurons were identified by staining with the neuronal marker microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2, rabbit, 1:200, Bioss, Cat# bs-1369R; RRID: AB_10853045). Goat anti-rabbit IgG labeled with Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500, Bioss, Cat# bs-0295G-AF594, RRID: AB_2940825) was used as the secondary antibody. Neurons cultured for 7 days were used for oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) or co-culture experiments.

## OGD modeling
The OGD model was established as previously described, with minor modifications (Gu et al., 2021). The neurons were washed twice with PBS and incubated in glucose-free DMEM (Gibco). Then, the cells were placed in an anaerobic chamber (Biospherix) with 5% CO2 and 95% N2 at 37°C for 2 hours to induce OGD. Following OGD, the neurons were returned to regular neuronal culture medium for co-culture experiments.

## Neuron and BMSC co-culture with different oxygen levels
BMSCs were seeded into the upper level of six-well Transwell chambers (Corning) in DMEM at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well 6 days before co-culture. The next day, PFKL was knocked down or overexpressed, and the cells were then cultured under normal conditions for 3 days followed by incubation in a 3% O2 hypoxic chamber for 48 hours. Following this, the culture medium was replaced with neurobasal medium (Gibco), and post-OGD neurons were added to the lower chambers in neurobasal medium. The cells were then co-cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours.
Three experiments were performed using the co-cultured cells. For the first experiment, the cells were divided into seven groups that were left untreated or treated with pathway inhibitors (added to the lower chamber at the beginning of co-culture), as follows: the control group, the OGD group, the OGD + normal-cultured BMSCs (N-BMSCs) group, the OGD + HP-BMSCs group, the OGD + HP-BMSCs + SB203580 group (p38 MAPK inhibitor, 10 mM, APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA), the OGD + HP-BMSCs + SP600125 group (JNK inhibitor, 10 mM, APExBIO) and the OGD + HP-BMSCs + Bay11-7082 group (NF-κB inhibitor, 20 mM, APExBIO). For the second experiment, the cells were divided into five groups that were left untreated or treated with siRNAs, left untreated or treated with 10 μM mitochondria-targeted antioxidant (Mito-TEMPO, TargetMol, Shanghai. China; added to the lower chamber simultaneously with co-culture), as follows: the control group, the OGD group, the OGD + si-NC HP-BMSCs group, the OGD + si-PFKL BMSCs group, and the OGD + si-PFKL BMSCs + Mito-TEMPO group. For the third experiment, the cells were divided into five groups and left untreated or treated with plasmid containing the PFKL gene: the control group, the OGD group, the OGD + oe-NC HP-BMSCs group, the OGD + oe-PFKL BMSCs group, and the OGD + oe-PFKL BMSCs + Mito-TEMPO group.

## Determination of oxygen consumption and ATP production rates
The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of the cells was examined using Seahorse XF HS mini analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ATP production rates were determined using the Seahorse XF Real-time ATP rate assay kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

## Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement in neurons
Mitochondrial damage was assessed using a JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (Beyotime). Following OGD and co-culture, neurons were incubated with JC-1 solution for 20 minutes at 37°C, washed with JC-1 staining buffer twice, and observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica).

## Measurement of intracellular ROS levels
Intracellular ROS levels in neurons were assessed by 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay. Neurons were incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 minutes, following the instructions provided with the ROS assay kit (Beyotime). The DCFH-DA signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

## Measurement of mitochondrial ROS levels
Mitochondrial ROS levels in primary cultured neurons were assessed using MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were incubated with 5 µM of MitoSOX for 10 minutes at 37°C in the dark. Next, neurons were treated with an anti-fluorescence attenuation sealant (with DAPI, Meilun Biotech). The fluorescence intensity was observed and recorded using an inverted fluorescence microscope at excitation wavelengths of 488 nm and 594 nm.

## Statistical analysis
No statistical methodologies were used to predefine sample sizes. Nonetheless, our sample sizes are similar to those used in our previous study (Lin et al., 2020). The investigators who conducted the statistical analyses were blinded to the group assignments. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, http://www.graphpad.com). Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while data with non-normal distribution are expressed as medians (interquartile range). For normally distributed data with equal variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests. For non-normally distributed data, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, was used. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

## HP enhances BMSC-mediated alleviation of neuronal pyroptosis by suppressing the MAPK and NF-κB pathways
Passage 3 BMSCs exhibited an adherent and fibroblast-like morphology ( A). Alizarin red staining confirmed the formation of osteoblasts by BMSCs. Additionally, lipid droplets accumulated and stained red after adipogenic differentiation ( A). Flow cytometric analysis showed high levels of CD90 (96.8%) and CD29 (97.0%) expression and low levels of CD11b (1.3%) and CD45 expression (1.3%) ( B).
 To assess the anti-pyroptotic effects of HP-BMSCs compared with those of N-BMSCs, we mimicked global cerebral IRI in primary cortical neurons by subjecting them to OGD, followed by co-culture with BMSCs. Immunofluorescence staining for the neuronal marker MAP2 in the primary cultures revealed significant MAP2 positivity in a large portion of the cells ( C). As shown in D–H, the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD-N, ASC, and cleaved-caspase-1) increased significantly after 24 hours of oxygen glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) compared with the control group (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, co-culture with HP-BMSCs significantly reduced the expression of pyroptosis-associated proteins in neurons subjected to OGD compared with the OGD group and the OGD + N-BMSCs group (all P < 0.05). As D, I–K show, the expression levels of MAPK and NF-κB pathway–related proteins were significantly higher in the OGD and OGD + N-BMSCs groups compared with the control group (all P < 0.05). However, following co-culture with HP-BMSCs, the expression levels of these proteins in neurons were significantly decreased compared with the OGD and OGD + N-BMSCs groups (all P < 0.05). To further validate the role of the MAPK and NF-κB pathways in mediating the anti-pyroptotic effects of HP-BMSCs, we added SB203580 (a p38 MAPK inhibitor), SP600125 (a JNK inhibitor), or Bay11-7082 (an NF-κB inhibitor) to the co-culture system. When these inhibitors were added separately to the lower layer, the expression levels of pyroptosis-associated proteins in neurons decreased compared with the OGD + HP-BMSCs group (all P < 0.05; Additional Figure 2). Taken together, these in vitro findings demonstrate that HP-BMSCs had a greater anti-pyroptotic effect than N-BMSCs in the OGD/R model, and that this effect may be mediated by the MAPK and NF-κB pathways. Therefore, we investigated the neuroprotective effects of HP-BMSCs further in subsequent experiments.

## BMSC gene expression profiles after HP
RNA sequencing analysis of N-BMSCs and HP-BMSCs showed that, compared with the N-BMSCs group, 125 genes were upregulated and 124 genes were downregulated in the HP-BMSCs group ( A and B). To further understand the function of these differentially expressed mRNAs, GO and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted. GO analysis revealed that the differentially expressed mRNAs were primarily enriched in biological processes such as metabolic processes, biological regulation, and cellular processes ( C). KEGG pathway analysis showed that the mRNAs were mainly enriched in pathways related to fructose and mannose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the pentose phosphate pathway ( D).
 HP alters MSC metabolic pathways, thereby enhancing their tolerance to hypoxia (Yusoff et al., 2022). Notably, changes in glycolytic capacity play a significant role in MSC adaptation to low-oxygen environments (Lee et al., 2019). Among the differentially regulated mRNAs associated with glycolysis regulation, we observed notable changes in PFKL expression ( A). This was confirmed by western blotting, which showed a significant increase in PFKL expression in the HP-BMSCs group, compared with the N-BMSCs group (P < 0.05; E and F). Furthermore, as shown in E and F, the PFKL siRNA effectively knocked down PFKL expression. Given that PFKL is the second rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, catalyzing an irreversible reaction, it was chosen as the presumed regulatory molecule and investigated further in subsequent experiments.

## PFKL regulates the biological characteristics of HP-BMSCs in vitro and in vivo
First, we evaluated the effect of si-PFKL on HP-BMSC proliferation by CCK8 assay (Additional Figure 1B). PFKL knockdown cells showed significantly higher rates of proliferation compared with the untreated and si-NC groups (P < 0.05). Flow cytometric analysis revealed no difference in apoptosis rates between the untreated and si-NC groups (P > 0.05; A and B). However, the apoptosis rate was significantly increased in the si-PFKL group compared with the si-NC group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, Transwell assay ( C and D) showed that HP-BMSC migration decreased following PFKL knockdown. Cell cycle analysis revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of S phase and G2/M phase cells in HP-BMSCs following PFKL knockdown (P < 0.05; E–G). To assess HP-BMSC survival and migration in vivo, we intracerebroventricularly injected HP-BMSCs subjected to different treatments into the CPR rat model 1 hour after CA. We observed no significant differences in physiological parameters among the different groups of rats (all P > 0.05; H and ; the general rat model conditions are shown in Additional file 1). Next, we performed live-animal imaging to track HP-BMSCs transfected with luciferase in vivo and found that HP-BMSCs with low PFKL expression exhibited weak bioluminescence signals, while the untreated and si-NC HP-BMSCs showed strong signals ( I and J). These live imaging results suggest that PFKL positively regulates HP-BMSC viability and migration. Overall, these findings demonstrate that PFKL is involved in regulating HP-BMSC proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and other cellular processes.
 

## PFKL modulates HP-BMSC–mediated neuroprotective and antioxidant functions in the asphyxial CA rat model
We assessed neurological function in our rat CA model 24 hours after ROSC by assigning neurological deficit scores and found that HP-BMSCs effectively reduced post-resuscitation neurological deficits. Notably, this effect was reversed when PFKL was knocked down ( A). S100B is a well-established biomarker of brain injury, and S100B levels in the serum reflect the severity of damage (Zou et al., 2022). As depicted in B, rats subjected to CA/CPR followed by intracerebroventricular administration of HP-BMSCs showed significantly lower serum S100B levels compared with those that received PBS. This neuroprotective effect of HP-BMSCs was markedly reversed by PFKL knockdown ( C). At 24 hours post-resuscitation, Nissl staining of cortical sections indicated a noticeable reduction in the number of surviving neurons after ROSC. However, HP-BMSC transplantation effectively prevented this loss. Importantly, the neuroprotective effect of transplanted HP-BMSCs on post-resuscitation rats was significantly reversed when PFKL was knocked down (P < 0.05; C and D).
 Next, we examined oxidative stress levels, given the importance of this factor in the pathophysiology of CA-induced neuronal injury (Okuma et al., 2021). To do this, we performed immunohistochemical staining for 4-HNE and 8-OHdG ( E–H) and observed a significant increase in 4-HNE and 8-OHdG expression in the CPR + PBS group compared with the sham group, while a decrease was observed in both the CPR + HP-BMSCs and CPR + si-NC HP-BMSCs groups (all P < 0.05). Notably, there was a significant elevation in both 4-HNE and 8-OHdG expression in the CPR + si-PFKL HP-BMSCs group compared with the CPR + si-NC HP-BMSCs group (both P < 0.05). Biochemical tests for SOD, GSH-Px, and MDA confirmed that HP-BMSCs exerted antioxidative activity in CPR rats, but that this activity was abolished by si-PFKL ( I–K). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the number of iNOS/NeuN double-positive cells in the CPR + PBS group compared with the sham group (P < 0.05). MSC transplantation significantly decreased iNOS expression compared with the CPR + PBS group (P < 0.05). However, this effect was reversed by transfection with PFKL-targeted siRNA ( L and M). Similarly, we observed an increase in intracellular ROS levels following CA that was inhibited by intracerebroventricular injection of HP-BMSCs. However, when HP-BMSCs were transfected with PFKL-targeted siRNA, their anti-ROS activity was abolished ( N and O). These findings suggest that HP-BMSCs alleviate oxidative reactions in neurons after CA/CPR in a PFKL-dependent manner.

## PFKL regulates the anti-pyroptotic effects of HP-BMSCs in the asphyxial CA rat model
Inflammasome activation is pivotal in triggering pyroptosis, with the NLRP3 inflammasome serving as the central component in the inflammatory response. Consequently, the NLRP3 inflammasome is intricately linked to the onset of pyroptosis ( A; Shi et al., 2022). Thus, we examined the expression levels of pyroptosis-associated proteins, including NLRP3, GSDMD-N, ASC, and cleaved-caspase-1. Western blotting analysis showed that HP-BMSCs markedly inhibited the expression of pyroptosis-associated proteins in the brains of CA rats ( B–F). However, this inhibitory effect was notably attenuated upon knockdown of PKFL. ELISA analysis showed that PFKL knockdown in HP-BMSCs reversed the inhibitory effect of HP-BMSCs on IL-1β and IL-18 release ( G and H). Next, we detected alterations in cortical neuron cell membranes by transmission electron microscopy. As shown in I, we found that CA/CPR rats exhibited clear signs of neuronal cell membrane rupture that were not observed in the CPR + HP-BMSCs group or the CPR + si-NC HP-BMSCs group. However, transfection with PFKL-targeted siRNA reversed this effect. These findings indicated that PFKL regulated the inhibitory effect of HP-BMSCs on pyroptosis in post-resuscitation rats.
 

## PFKL is involved in HP-BMSC–mediated improvement of mitochondrial function in CA/CPR rats
ROS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction leads to increased ROS production, which in turn exacerbates mitochondrial damage (Brown et al., 2013). Therefore, we next observed and analyzed mitochondrial alterations in the neurons. Mitochondria in neurons exhibit severe damage and significant swelling following CA, while mitochondrial damage was reduced in the CPR + HP-BMSCs group and the CPR + si-NC HP-BMSCs group ( A and B). However, this protective effect was reversed by PFKL knockdown in HP-BMSCs. To further investigate changes in mitochondrial function, we examined brain ATP levels and the NAD+/NADH ratio. The results showed that HP-BMSCs improved neuronal mitochondrial function after ROSC, and this effect was abolished by HP-BMSC transfection with an PFKL-targeted siRNA ( C and D). Western blotting showed that the expression of mitochondrial damage markers (FIS1, COX IV, and TOM20) in the CA group was significantly increased compared with the sham group (all P < 0.05; E–I). Additionally, compared with the CPR + PBS group, the expression of these proteins significantly decreased after HP-BMSC transplantation (all P < 0.05). However, the reduction in the expression of these proteins was significantly reversed by PFKL knockdown in HP-BMSCs. Furthermore, the expression patterns of BDNF, a protein that is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function in neurons (Markham et al., 2014), exhibited opposite trends to those of the mitochondrial injury markers, and the beneficial effect of HP-BMSCs on BDNF expression was reversed when PFKL was knocked down. Taken together, these results indicated that PFKL plays a role in mediating the protective effects of HP-BMSCs on mitochondria in rat neurons after ROSC.
 

## PFKL promotes HP-BMSC–mediated protection against OGD-induced neuronal injury by modulating anti-pyroptotic and antiapoptotic pathways
The brain predominantly depends on aerobic metabolism, and mitochondria play a pivotal role in this energy-producing process (Han et al., 2018). Moreover, ATP production is the main function of mitochondria; therefore, ATP levels serve as a sensitive indicator of mitochondrial function and activity (Wang et al., 2012). As shown in A–J, we evaluated the bioenergetics profile of primary cortical neurons using the Seahorse XF Real-time ATP rate assay kit. The OCR and cellular ATP production rate were decreased in OGD neurons compared with control neurons (all P < 0.05). However, co-culture with si-NC HP-BMSCs improved neuronal OCR and ATP production rate. In the co-culture system, suppression of mitochondrial ROS by treatment with Mito-TEMPO (a mitochondria-targeted ROS scavenger) further restored OCR. Although the addition of Mito-TEMPO to the co-culture system did not have a statistically significant effect on ATP production rate, there was a trend toward increased ATP production. In contrast, when post-OGD neurons were co-cultured with si-PFKL HP-BMSCs, their ability to enhance energy metabolism was significantly diminished compared with co-culture with si-NC HP-BMSCs. Next, we detected neuronal apoptosis by flow cytometry ( K and L) and found that, compared with OGD-damaged neurons, co-culture with si-NC HP-BMSCs significantly decreased the neuronal apoptosis rate (P < 0.05). Addition of Mito-TEMPO to the co-culture system reduced the apoptosis rate, although the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Furthermore, after PFKL knockdown resulted in a significant decline in HP-BMSC anti-apoptotic capacity (P < 0.05). Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the control group, after 24 hours of OGD/R, the expression levels of the apoptosis-related proteins NLRP3, GSDMD-N, ASC, and cleaved-caspase-1 and the pyroptosis-related proteins Bak and activated-caspase-3 increased in primary neurons, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 decreased (all P < 0.05; M–T). Co-culturing post-OGD neurons with si-NC HP BMSCs significantly reversed this effect (all P < 0.05). Moreover, the introduction of Mito-TEMPO to the co-culture system resulted in an even more significant reversal of this effect (all P < 0.05). Conversely, when post-OGD neurons were co-cultured with HP-BMSCs in which PFKL expression had been knocked down, we noted an increase in the levels of pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD-N, ASC, and cleaved-caspase-1) and pro-apoptotic proteins (Bak and caspase-3), as well as a decrease in the levels of an anti-apoptotic protein (Bcl-2), compared with cells co-cultured with si-NC HP-BMSCs (all P < 0.05). In addition, PFKL overexpression enhanced the anti-apoptotic and anti-pyroptotic effects of HP-BMSCs (Additional Figure 3). Taken together, these findings indicate that PFKL plays a crucial role in mediating the protective effects of HP-BMSCs on post-OGD neuronal injury through inhibition of apoptosis and pyroptosis pathways. Furthermore, through the incorporation of Mito-TEMPO in the co-culture system, we found that mitigating mitochondrial oxidative stress is potential mechanism by which HP-BMSCs modulate neuronal cell pyroptosis.
 

## PFKL is involved in HP-BMSC–mediated amelioration of oxidative damage in post-OGD neurons
Neurons are highly dependent upon mitochondrial function (Geden et al., 2021). Hence, we conducted in vitro experiments to further validate the mitochondrial protective effect of PFKL on HP-BMSCs. First, JC-1 dye was used to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential ( A and B): mitochondrial membrane damage shifts the JC-1 signal from red (emitted by JC-1 aggregates that form in the presence of a high mitochondrial membrane potential) to green (emitted by JC-1 monomers that accumulate in the cytoplasm in the presence of a low membrane potential). We found that compared with the OGD group, si-NC HP-BMSCs significantly promoted the recovery of neuronal membrane potential. Adding Mito-Tempo (a mitochondrial-targeted ROS scavenger) to the co-culture system further improved membrane potential compared with the si-NC HP-BMSCs group. However, this improvement was significantly reduced when HP-BMSCs were transfected with PFKL siRNA. Inflammasome activation induces ROS generation, and increasing mitochondrial ROS levels in turn activate the inflammasome (Lin et al., 2019). Therefore, we employed DCFH-DA analysis and the mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX to assess intracellular ROS and mitochondrial ROS levels in primary neurons after OGD ( C–F). The results showed that OGD significantly increased intracellular ROS levels and mitochondrial ROS levels in neurons, while HP-BMSCs significantly reduced intracellular and mitochondrial ROS levels. Furthermore, when co-cultured with HP-BMSCs, the addition of Mito-TEMPO further exacerbated the decrease in ROS levels. This suggests that HP-BMSCs may exert their neuroprotective effects by alleviating mitochondrial oxidative stress. Next, we co-cultured neurons with si-PFKL HP-BMSCs and observed a reduction in the aforementioned antioxidative effects, indicating that HP-BMSCs reduced oxidative stress in neurons after OGD, and that PFKL played a key role in this process. Furthermore, western blotting revealed a significant increase in the expression levels of neuronal mitochondrial damage markers (FIS1, COX IV, and TOM20) following OGD ( G–J). However, these changes were reversed when the neurons were co-cultured with si-NC HP-BMSCs. The addition of Mito-TEMPO further reduced the expression levels of neuronal mitochondrial damage markers. Importantly, when PFKL was knocked down in HP-BMSCs, we observed a significant increase in the expression levels of mitochondrial damage markers in neurons compared with those that were co-cultured with si-NC HP-BMSCs. Taken together, these results indicate that PFKL regulates HP-BMSC–mediated alleviation of mitochondrial ROS accumulation and protects mitochondrial function in neurons following OGD.
 

## Discussion
CA often results from respiratory failure, toxicological mechanisms, or upper airway obstruction. Given the pivotal role of neuronal death in the pathophysiology of CA, stem cell transplantation, as a regenerative therapy, offers significant potential for neuroprotection following this event. However, there has only been limited research on the protective effects of stem cell therapy against oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in this context. Our study provides key insights: (1) HP-BMSCs exhibit a significantly greater capacity to inhibit neuronal pyroptosis than N-BMSCs; (2) attenuation of mitochondrial oxidative stress appears to underlie the anti-pyroptotic effects of BMSCs; (3) PFKL plays a crucial role in mediating the protective function of HP-BMSCs against mitochondrial oxidative stress, thereby alleviating both neuronal pyroptosis and oxidative stress. These findings suggest that PFKL may represent a novel regulatory molecule in BMSCs under hypoxic conditions. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that HP-BMSCs can attenuate cellular pyroptosis by inhibiting the mitochondrial oxidative stress response and preventing oxidative stress damage, potentially offering a novel therapeutic approach for treating brain damage associated with CA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the connections between mitochondrial oxidative stress and neural pyroptosis following resuscitation in the context of BMSC-based therapy ( ).
 Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial function are involved in the initiation and progression of numerous neurological disorders (Bayat et al., 2012; di Giacomo et al., 2020). Pyroptosis is an inflammatory-mediated programmed cell death process that is dependent on caspase-1 (Magupalli et al., 2020) and instigates inflammation. Current evidence suggests that pyroptosis is critically involved in several disorders of central nervous system diseases, including traumatic spinal cord injury, hemorrhagic stroke, and Alzheimer’s disease (Walsh et al., 2014; Mamik and Power, 2017; Chen et al., 2020). Pyroptosis is distinct from apoptosis and other types of cell death in terms of both morphology and mechanism. It is characterized by inflammasome formation and caspase-1 activation, ultimately resulting in generation of the pore-forming N-terminus of GSDMD. The membrane pores formed by GSDMD facilitate lytic cell death and the release of mature forms of IL-1β and IL-18 (Anderson et al., 2021; Paik et al., 2021). Overall, inflammasomes play key roles in regulating pyroptosis, and the NLRP3 inflammasome is a critical component of the inflammatory response (Dufies et al., 2021). Given that neuronal pyroptosis is observed in focal cerebral IRI, we hypothesized that neurons might undergo pyroptosis after CA/CPR-induced global IRI, thus aggravating the neuroinflammation. Our findings aligned with this hypothesis, indicating that neuronal pyroptosis was activated after CA, leading to increased tissue damage and neurological impairment. Western blotting indicated a significant decrease in the neuronal expression of pyroptosis-related proteins in of CPR rats after transplantation with HP-BMSCs, as compared with N-BMSCs. We also found that HP-BMSC therapy attenuated brain damage and reduce serum S100B levels. Western blotting analysis and Annexin V/PI assay confirmed the neuroprotective effect of HP-BMSCs in a neuronal OGD/R model. Taken together, these results suggest that HP-BMSCs are more effective at preventing neuronal pyroptosis than N-BMSCs, and that inhibition of cortical neurons pyroptosis could alleviate brain damage post-CA.
The NF-κB signaling pathway is an important regulatory hub that regulates ROS, oxidative stress, and inflammation (Xu et al., 2019b). Activation of this pathway activates the inflammatory response in neurons, thus contributing to central nervous system dysfunction (Xu et al., 2019a). NF-κB is a downstream effector of the Toll-like receptor 4 signaling pathway, which is regulated by positive feedback. Upon engagement with damage-associated molecular patterns or pathogen-associated molecular patterns, Toll-like receptor 4 triggers the activation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB. This, in turn, induces the expression and release of inflammatory cytokines and mediators, orchestrating an inflammatory response (Nguyen et al., 2020). A previous study showed that NF-κB expression increased significantly following cerebral ischemic injury, indicating its activation (Yang et al., 2022b). NF-κB plays an important role in neuroinflammation (Khan et al., 2020); specifically, aberrant NF-κB activation promotes transcription of NLRP3 inflammasome components (Ye et al., 2019). There is increasing consensus that the NLRP3 inflammasome is involved in NF-κB-mediated inﬂammation in the context of neurological disease, which indicates crosstalk between the NLRP3 inflammasome and the NF-κB pathway (He et al., 2020). Moreover, the MAPK signaling pathway is a critical upstream regulator of the NF-κB pathway (Yang et al., 2020b). In spinal cord injury models, inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway has been shown to ameliorate neuronal pyroptosis and promote motor function recovery (Zhang et al., 2023a). Another study showed that MC4 receptor activation inhibits the MAPK pathway, alleviating intracerebral hemorrhage–induced brain injury by suppressing neuronal pyroptosis (Chen et al., 2019). Therefore, pyroptosis is closely correlated with the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. In our study, we subjected primary neurons to OGD/R to simulate brain injury after CA/CPR. Using SB203580, SP600125, and Bay11-7082, we found that NF-κB and MAPK pathway activation was involved in the cellular pyroptosis after OGD/R, and that this effect could be reversed by co-culturing the neurons with HP-BMSCs. Our findings offer preliminary evidence suggesting that HP-BMSCs are neuroprotective. Importantly, the brain has a high metabolic rate, consuming a significant amount of energy even during normal functioning; however, it lacks sufficient energy reserves. Mitochondria are the primary organelles responsible for both generating ROS and producing the cellular energy substrate ATP in the brain (Holper et al., 2019). Brain tissue is densely populated with mitochondria, which are the major source of brain ROS, and are particularly prone to oxidative damage (Liu et al., 2021c). In mammalian cells, ATP is primarily generated by mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Galera-Monge et al., 2020). During this process, the mitochondrial electron transport chain generates ROS; and indeed, this is a main source of intracellular ROS production under both physiological and pathological conditions (Koncsos et al., 2016). When excessive ROS generation is coupled with decreased ROS scavenging activity, cells become susceptible to oxidative stress damage (Christians and Benjamin, 2012). Mitochondrial ROS have been implicated in several neurological disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Baev et al., 2022). In a rat model of CA, mitochondrial ROS were found to be closely related to neurological dysfunction post-resuscitation (Zhang et al., 2023b). Other studies have suggested a central role for mitochondrial function in post-resuscitation myocardial and cerebral dysfunction (Qin et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022), thus demonstrating the critical role of mitochondria in regulating inflammation. In addition, targeted mitochondrial treatment has been demonstrated to alleviate neuronal pyroptosis (Zheng et al., 2019), and current evidence suggests that suppressing ROS generation substantially inhibits NLPR3 inflammasome activation (Zhang et al., 2018). Consequently, preventing the continuous production of mitochondrial ROS may be an appropriate therapeutic target for the modulation of pyroptosis. To assess changes in brain energy metabolism, we determined the ATP content of and NAD+/NADH ratio in neurons. We found that CA/CPR and OGD/R induced a decline in ATP levels and NAD+/NADH ratio, both of which improved when the neurons were co-cultured with HP-BMSCs. We further detected mitochondrial ROS using MitoSOX Red and found that the high mitochondrial ROS levels induced by OGD/R were also decreased by HP-BMSC co-culture. Moreover, the expression levels of mitochondrial damage markers (FIS1, COX IV, and TOM20) were restored in vivo following injection of HP-BMSCs into the lateral ventricle, indicating that HP-BMSCs improved mitochondrial respiratory chain function following global IRI. Our results show that CA/CPR induced mitochondrial ROS mass production, which further led to structural damage to the mitochondria and mitochondrial dysfunction. These results were in line with increased levels of NLRP3, GSDMD-N, ASC, and cleaved-caspase-1 that we observed, indicating that neuronal pyroptosis had occurred. However, co-culture with HP-BMSCs significantly reduced mitochondrial ROS generation, preserved mitochondrial structure, and restored mitochondrial function, consequently reducing pyroptosis. Moreover, BDNF expression increased following HP-BMSC transplantation, suggesting that HP-BMSCs induce an increase in the expression of protective factors that help maintain mitochondrial function.
Increasing evidence indicates that various mitochondrial enzymes, as well as mitochondrial dysfunction, enhance intracellular ROS production (Huang et al., 2020). Research has demonstrated the involvement of ROS in both lipid peroxidation, leading to the formation of MDA, and DNA damage, specifically the oxidation of guanine residues into 8-OHDG (Mariani et al., 2005). 8-OHdG and 4-HNE are common markers of oxidative stress that are used to evaluate ROS-induced DNA and lipid damage in vivo (Katunga et al., 2015; Fu et al., 2019). Studies have demonstrated that excessive ROS production can lead to oxidative stress injury, such as oxidative damage to DNA, protein oxidation, and lipid peroxidation, ultimately resulting in cell death (Zhang et al., 2020). In addition to ROS, nitric oxide (NO) also plays a complex role in mediating the oxidative stress response (Singh et al., 2019). NO is produced by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which is activated as part of the inflammatory response and is the critical enzyme responsible for abnormally elevated NO levels (Long et al., 2020). Another study confirmed increased iNOS mRNA expression in the cortices of patients with Alzheimer’s disease (Haas et al., 2002). Because the brain is vulnerable to oxidative stress damage, the formation of oxygen free radicals can exacerbate brain damage (Cherubini et al., 2000; Kontos, 2001). Likewise, depletion of natural antioxidants (SOD and GSH-Px), as well as accumulation of excessive MDA, reflect the degree of lipid peroxidation and can thus be used to evaluate the severity of cerebral IRI (Abd El-Fatah et al., 2021). To demonstrate the relationships among mitochondrial ROS, intracellular ROS, and pyroptosis, we cultured subjected primary neurons to OGD conditions in vitro and examined mitochondrial ROS by means of MitoSOX Red, intracellular ROS by DCFH-DA assay, and pyroptosis by western blotting. The results showed that co-culture with HP-BMSCs significantly suppressed mitochondrial ROS production after OGD, leading to lower ROS levels and reduced pyroptosis. Additionally, in our 8-minute CA rat model, we found that CA/CPR activated the oxidative stress pathway, leading to increased levels of ROS, MDA, iNOS, 4-HNE, and 8-OHdG, as well as decreased levels of SOD and GSH-Px. However, these effects were partially blocked by HP-BMSC transplantation. These results showed that the potentially beneficial effect of HP-BMSCs on brain damage might be related to their anti-oxidative stress activity.
Substantial evidence suggests that MSCs have therapeutic effects, including neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, in the context of many diseases (Li et al., 2021). A recent study showed that MSC administration could alleviate neuronal pyroptosis in a swine CA/CPR model (Xu et al., 2021). However, the presence of free radicals and inadequate blood supply to brain lesions can hinder the neuroprotective effects of MSCs (Zhuo et al., 2021). In addition, for stem cells to effectively contribute to tissue repair, they need to be able to survive in a hostile microenvironment (Rodrigues et al., 2013), and the harsh inflammatory microenvironment associated with brain injury can significantly reduce the effectiveness of MSC-based treatments (Hu and Li, 2019). Thus, new approaches are needed to increase the efficacy of MSC-based treatment. Recent investigations have shown that HP significantly improves the effects of MSC-based therapy in models of ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and spinal cord injury (Jiang et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021a). Following brain injury, the brain often experiences decompensated hypoxia, unlike normal brain tissue conditions (McKeown, 2014; Zhang et al., 2021). Another study confirmed that, in comparison with the normoxic group, HP led to enhanced MSC migration into the ischemic penumbra and improved the capacity of cells to tolerate environmental stressors in an experimental stroke model (Hu et al., 2019). A previous study investigated the effects of HP involving oxygen levels ranging from 0.1% to 5%, as well as hypoxic pretreatment durations varying from 24 to 48 hours (Teixeira et al., 2015). There is evidence suggesting that increased cell survival and migration post-HP may involve inhibition of caspase-3 activity and increased hypoxia-inducible factor-1α expression (Chen et al., 2017). Furthermore, HP was reported to have no observable effect on MSC phenotype or differentiation, although it enhanced autophagy through hypoxia-inducible factor-1α up-regulation and AMPK/mTOR pathway activation (Boyette et al., 2014). After HP, transplanted cells migrate to sites of brain injury and promote the recovery of neurological function after stroke by secreting a variety of growth factors and cytokines, including BDNF, vascular endothelial growth factor, and hepatocyte growth factor (Chang et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2015). Moreover, preconditioning MSCs by subjecting them to hypoxic culture conditions demonstrably reduces their tumorigenic potential following engraftment, providing encouraging evidence in support of the safety of MSC-based therapies (Feng et al., 2015). In the CA/CPR model, HP was found to promote the migration of transplanted MSCs, and transplantation of HP-MSCs decreased neuronal apoptosis and inflammation in brain lesions (Wang et al., 2017). The present study used an in vitro approach to examine the possibility that HP could enhance the neuroprotective effect of BMSCs on CA/CPR-induced neuronal damage by increasing BMSC viability and migration.
We demonstrated that HP-BMSCs significantly attenuated OGD/R-induced pyroptosis compared with N-BMSCs. This protective effect was likely mediated by suppression of the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. Furthermore, our findings showed that HP-BMSC–based therapy alleviates mitochondrial oxidative stress, a hallmark of reoxygenation after ischemia. Importantly, we used Mito-TEMPO, a specific mitochondrial ROS inhibitor, to evaluate the role of neuronal mitochondrial ROS in HP-BMSC–mediated neuroprotection. We found that HP-BMSCs treated with Mito-TEMPO reduced OGD/R-induced neuronal pyroptosis in co-culture even more than untreated HP-BMSCs, suggesting that HP-BMSCs protect against pyroptosis following resuscitation by inhibiting mitochondrial oxidative stress. Nevertheless, the detailed mechanism by which HP modulates BMSC function remains unclear.
PFK-1, a key kinase in the glycolytic pathway, catalyzes the phosphorylation of fructose 6-phosphate to fructose 1, 6-bisphosphate (Cowan et al., 2016). While most studies of PFK-1 function have focused on tumor cells, its potential role in MSCs remains unexplored. This is particularly relevant given the capacity of MSCs for self-renewal and their reliance on glycolysis. Notably, no prior reports have investigated PFK-1 in the context of HP-BMSCs. Our RNA sequencing analysis showed that PFKL expression was upregulated in BMSCs following HP, and the glycolytic pathway was also significantly enriched in the KEGG pathway analysis. We further confirmed that HP-BMSCs expressed high levels of PFKL, which were associated with increased cell migration and enhanced cell activity after HP. Therefore, from the perspective of HP-BMSC–mediated inhibition of pyroptosis and mitochondrial ROS management, we analyzed the contribution of PFKL to HP-BMSC–based therapy. The results indicated that siRNA-mediated PFKL knockdown reduced HP-BMSC migration in vivo and in vitro and partially reversed the inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative stress by HP-BMSC, thereby inducing higher levels of pyroptosis. Similarly, PFKL overexpression enhanced HP-BMSC–mediated inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative stress, thus decreasing pyroptosis. These findings indicate that PFKL may be involved in HP-BMSC–mediated neuroprotection post-resuscitation.
This study had some limitations. First, there is no consensus regarding the optimal culture conditions for HP-BMSCs, particularly regarding the duration of hypoxia and the specific oxygen concentration. Future studies should address this issue directly. Second, while hypoxia increased PFKL expression in BMSCs and promoted cell migration, siRNA-mediated PFKL knockdown unexpectedly promoted MSC proliferation. This seemingly paradoxical finding contradicts the negative effects of PFKL knockdown that we observed in HP-MSCs. Further investigation is needed to determine the detailed mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. Finally, although HP-BMSCs appear to protect against neuronal damage following ROSC, it remains unclear whether they have the same effect on other brain cell types, such as astrocytes, oligodendroglia, and microglia. Future studies should explore the relationship between HP-BMSCs and other brain cell types to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their potential applications in preventing post-resuscitation brain damage.
In summary, our findings suggest that HP-BMSCs likely act through multiple mechanisms: suppressing the MAPK/NF-κB pathways and scavenging mitochondrial ROS to inactivate pyroptosis, ultimately promoting functional neurological recovery after resuscitation. These findings suggest HP-BMSCs are a promising strategy to combat mitochondrial oxidative stress injury and could be a valuable therapeutic approach for post-CA neurological injury.
