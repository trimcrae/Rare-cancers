## Background:
This study aimed to identify potential therapeutic targets for sepsis and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, with a particular focus on the liver as a key target organ for experimental validation.

## Methods:
Here, two sequential studies were conducted to uncover and characterize therapeutic targets involved in sepsis-induced liver injury. In Study 1, a proteome-wide Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed using protein quantitative trait loci from deCODE (n = 35 559 Icelanders), UK Biobank (n = 54 219), ARIC (n = 7213 European Americans; n = 1871 African Americans), AGES-Reykjavik (n = 5368 Icelanders), and Fenland (n = 10 708 European ancestry). These datasets were integrated with a sepsis genome-wide association study (GWAS) (FinnGen R12; n = 17 133 cases and 439 048 controls) to identify causal protein candidates. Subsequently, summary-data-based MR was performed. The analysis was based on data from eQTLGen (n = 31 684), GTEx v8 (n = 838 donors), and sepsis GWAS (FinnGen R12; UK Biobank, n = 11 643 cases/474 841 controls). Its goal was to prioritize genes concordant with protein signals. Expression of the candidate was then validated in vivo and in vitro. In Study 2, a metabolite GWAS (n = 8299) was used to investigate metabolic mediation and perform pathway enrichment analysis. Finally, potential small-molecule therapeutics predicted to modulate the prioritized target were identified.

## Results:
Integrated multi-omics analyses identified tubulin-folding cofactor B (TBCB) as a promising therapeutic target for sepsis. Quantitative analyses in both in vivo and in vitro models consistently demonstrated significant upregulation of TBCB during sepsis-induced liver injury. Functional knockdown of TBCB significantly attenuated inflammatory signaling. Mechanistically, TBCB appears to contribute to sepsis progression by modulating intracellular lipid metabolism and metabolic homeostasis. Furthermore, molecular docking and dynamics simulations predicted several small molecules for TBCB, suggesting potential therapeutic value.

## Conclusion:
This study identifies TBCB as a central regulator of sepsis pathogenesis, especially in sepsis-induced liver injury. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights and a promising therapeutic target for intervention.

## Introduction
Sepsis remains one of the foremost causes of mortality worldwide, with its incidence and death rates showing slight improvement over recent decades. Despite advances in intensive care, antimicrobial therapy, and organ support technologies, severe sepsis continues to be associated with a mortality rate of approximately 20–30%[ , ]. As a systemic inflammatory response to infection, sepsis can lead to multiple organ dysfunction, among which liver injury is particularly prominent. In experimental models of sepsis, hepatic inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 are significantly upregulated, activating intracellular signaling cascades that promote hepatocellular injury and apoptosis[ , ]. Moreover, excessive hepatic production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) further exacerbates oxidative stress, aggravating hepatocellular damage and amplifying inflammation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome[ ]. As a result, elucidating the mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced liver injury is essential.
Current clinical management primarily relies on early administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, hemodynamic stabilization, and advanced organ support measures[ ]. However, despite substantial improvements in supportive care, no targeted pharmacological therapies addressing the core molecular mechanisms of sepsis have yet been approved. This underscores an urgent need to identify and validate novel therapeutic targets, alongside a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways driving sepsis pathogenesis, especially sepsis-induced liver injury.

## HIGHLIGHTS
Identification of tubulin-folding cofactor B (TBCB) as a novel causal therapeutic target: Integrative multi-omics Mendelian randomization (MR) and summary-data-based MR (SMR) analyses identified TBCB as a novel protein causally associated with sepsis risk, specifically implicated in sepsis-induced liver injury.
TBCB upregulation drives liver injury and inflammation: In vivo [cecal ligation and puncture and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) mouse models] and in vitro (bone marrow-derived macrophage cells) validation consistently demonstrated significant upregulation of TBCB expression, predominantly localized in hepatic macrophages. Knockdown of TBCB significantly reduced LPS/interferon-γ-induced pro-inflammatory signaling in macrophages.
Mechanistic link to metabolic reprogramming: Metabolome-wide MR and mediation analysis revealed that TBCB acts as an upstream regulator influencing sepsis pathogenesis, establishing a causal protein–metabolite–sepsis axis.
Prediction of therapeutic small molecules: Molecular docking and dynamics simulations identified and evaluated potential therapeutic small molecules targeting the TBCB protein, providing a foundation for future drug development.
Multidimensional validation approach: The study employed a robust translational pipeline combining human genetics (multi-cohort protein quantitative trait loci/expression quantitative trait loci, sepsis genome-wide association study), causal inference methods (MR, SMR, colocalization), metabolomics, and experimental validation (in vivo sepsis models, cellular assays, functional knockdown) to establish TBCB’s role.

Recent advances in genomics and multi-omics technologies have provided powerful tools for deciphering the molecular landscape of sepsis. Integrative approaches combining genetic and biomarker data enable the identification of causal relationships between biological factors and disease risk. Mendelian randomization (MR), which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), provides a robust framework for establishing causality between specific proteins, genes, or metabolites and sepsis susceptibility[ ]. Furthermore, rapid progress in experimental models, including animal and cell, has facilitated the mechanistic validation of candidate targets and pathways involved in sepsis-induced liver injury. These approaches form a genetics-guided strategy that holds promise for uncovering new therapeutic avenues and improving sepsis-induced liver injury outcomes beyond the limitations of current supportive care.
This study integrated multi-omics datasets with experimental validation to identify potential therapeutic targets involved in sepsis-induced liver injury and to elucidate their underlying molecular mechanisms. This work adheres to the TITAN Guidelines 2025, and the detailed item-to-manuscript mapping is provided in Supplemental Digital Content 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G639[ ].

## Methods and materials

## Study design
This investigation consisted of two sequential studies. Study 1 aimed to identify potential therapeutic targets associated with systemic sepsis, and given the liver’s central role in sepsis-associated organ dysfunction and metabolism, focused on the liver as a key organ for experimental validation. Study 2 then focused on conducting further investigations of the identified targets.
Specifically, proteome-wide MR analysis was performed using cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTLs) from five cohorts (ARIC, AGES-Reykjavik, deCODE, UK Biobank, and Fenland) and sepsis genome-wide association study (GWAS) from the FinnGen R12. Proteins meeting suggestive thresholds and demonstrating evidence of colocalization were considered potential causal candidates. At the gene expression level, summary-data-based MR (SMR) and Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) tests were performed across all pairwise combinations of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) datasets (eQTLGen, GTEx v8) and sepsis GWAS (FinnGen R12, UK Biobank). These analyses were used to identify suggestive genes overlapping with candidate proteins. Following the identification of systemic targets, experimental validation was performed to assess the expression of the targets in the liver. Both in vivo and in vitro models were employed, including cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced mouse models, as well as LPS-stimulated cellular assays. Expression patterns of candidate biomarkers were quantified, and their functional roles were further assessed through small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown experiments. After potential targets were identified, a metabolome-wide MR analysis was performed to identify sepsis-associated metabolites, and the mediation proportion was calculated. Finally, molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to screen and evaluate potential small-molecule compounds that modulate the identified therapeutic targets.

## Data sources
Proteomic GWAS data were obtained from five large-scale cohorts: the ARIC[ ], the AGES-Reykjavik[ ], deCODE[ ], the UK Biobank[ ], and the Fenland[ ]. In these cohorts, plasma concentrations of hundreds to thousands of circulating proteins were quantified using high-throughput proteomic platforms such as SOMAscan or Olink. Cis-pQTLs were selected as IVs for MR analysis, as they provide greater biological relevance to protein expression[ ].
The GWAS summary statistics of sepsis were obtained from Release 12 of the FinnGen study, a large-scale research consortium integrating nationwide health registry data with genomic information. Sepsis outcomes were defined using diagnostic codes from ICD-8, ICD-9, and ICD-10. Another sepsis GWAS from the UK Biobank, comprising 11 643 cases and 474 841 controls, was included in the SMR analysis.
At the transcriptomic level, eQTL data were derived from GTEx v8[ ] and the eQTLGen Consortium[ ]. The GTEx v8 dataset provides comprehensive gene expression profiles across 49 different tissues obtained from 838 postmortem donors. The eQTLGen consortium analyzed whole-blood transcriptomes from 31 684 individuals, identifying significant cis-eQTLs for 16 987 genes.
For metabolome-wide MR, GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging[ ], which includes genetic associations for hundreds of circulating metabolites.

## Mendelian randomization analyses
Two-sample MR analyses were conducted to evaluate the causal relationships between candidate targets (proteins, metabolites) and sepsis risk. The study design strictly adhered to the three fundamental MR assumptions[ ]. IVs were selected as independent, genome-wide significant single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure at P < 5 × 10−8. Independence among SNPs was ensured through linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 < 0.001, 10 000 kb). The strength of each IV was evaluated using the F-statistic, with values of F > 10 indicating a low likelihood of weak-instrument bias[ ]. In this exploratory study, associations with nominal P < 0.05 but false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P ≥ 0.05 were considered suggestive; those with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant[ ]. Primary causal inference was primarily assessed using the inverse-variance-weighted method. When only a single IV was available, the Wald ratio was applied to estimate the causal effect. To improve the robustness of the results, several complementary MR methods were employed, including MR-Egger regression, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode. The Cochran’s Q test was performed to assess heterogeneity. A P-value <0.05 was considered significant heterogeneity. To evaluate potential horizontal pleiotropy, we employed the MR-Egger regression method. The intercept term from the MR-Egger regression provides an estimate of the average directional pleiotropy across the genetic variants. A statistically significant MR-Egger intercept (P < 0.05) was taken as evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. All analyses were performed in RStudio using the “TwoSampleMR” package (version 0.6.6). The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines (Supplementary Material: STROBE-MR Checklist; http://links.lww.com/JS9/G681). Ethical approval was not required, as all data were derived from publicly available GWAS summary statistics and did not involve individual-level human data.

## Colocalization analysis
Colocalization analyses were conducted to determine whether genetic associations for targets and sepsis risk shared the same underlying causal variant. The analyses were performed using the “coloc” R package, which employs a Bayesian statistical framework to estimate the posterior probability (PPH4) that both traits share a common causal variant. In this exploratory study, colocalization was interpreted according to predefined thresholds: PPH4 ≥ 0.5 was considered suggestive of colocalization, PPH4 ≥ 0.7 indicated moderate colocalization, and PPH4 ≥ 0.8 was regarded as strong evidence of colocalization[ ].

## Summary-data-based MR analysis
SMR analyses were performed using eQTL summary statistics from the eQTLGen consortium and GTEx v8. These eQTL were integrated with sepsis GWAS summary data from FinnGen and the UK Biobank. Each combination of eQTL and GWAS dataset was tested using the SMR and HEIDI test framework. Associations were considered valid only if they satisfied the HEIDI criterion (P_HEIDI ≥ 0.05). As previously mentioned, results were classified as statistically significant when the FDR-adjusted SMR_P < 0.05, and as suggestive when the nominal SMR_P < 0.05 but the FDR-adjusted SMR_P ≥ 0.05.

## Pathway enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction network
Functional enrichment analyses were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package to investigate the gene ontology biological processes and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with genes identified through the SMR and HEIDI analyses. To further explore metabolic alterations linked to sepsis, metabolite pathway enrichment was performed using the Small Molecule Pathway Database, KEGG, and Relational database of Metabolic Pathways (RaMP-DB), identifying metabolic pathways associated with sepsis[ , ]. For potential protein targets, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses were conducted using the STRING database to elucidate the intracellular interaction landscape and functional relationships among candidate proteins[ ].

## Mediation Mendelian randomization analysis
Mediation MR analyses were performed to investigate the proteins–metabolites–disease axis. The analysis was conducted in three sequential steps. First, MR was applied to assess the causal association between proteins and metabolites (pathway a, βa). Second, a metabolome-wide MR was performed to evaluate the causal effects of metabolites on sepsis risk (pathway b, βb). Third, the total causal effect of each protein on sepsis was defined as pathway c (βᴄ). The proportion of mediation was quantified as the ratio of the indirect effect (βa × βb) to the total impact, estimating the extent to which metabolites mediate the causal relationship between candidate proteins and sepsis.

## Molecular docking and dynamics simulations
Molecular docking and MD simulations were conducted to identify and evaluate potential therapeutic small molecules for selected causal targets. Protein structures [tubulin-folding cofactor B (TBCB)] were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AF-Q99426-F1). Three-dimensional small-molecule structures were downloaded from PubChem. PubChem CIDs are as follows: tocopherol: 14 986, A-tocopherol: 14 985, and tocophero: 91 738 518. Ligands and receptors were prepared using PyMOL, ChemOffice, and AutoDock Tools for structure editing, 3D format conversion, and energy minimization. Docking simulations employed AutoDock Vina to predict binding affinities and optimal binding conformations between ligands and proteins. The best-ranking docking poses were selected based on binding affinity scores for further analysis[ ]. Subsequently, MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS software package. Before initiating the MD simulations, energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods to eliminate unfavorable atomic contacts. After energy minimization, equilibration was conducted under constant number of particles, volume, and temperature (NVT) and constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) conditions. Subsequently, 100 ns MD simulations were carried out for each of the three systems. During the simulations, the CHARMM36 force field was applied for the protein, and the GAFF2 force field with RESP2 charges was used for the ligand[ ]. The TIP3P model was employed for water molecules. A 10 Å cutoff was set for van der Waals interactions, and long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a 1.0 nm cutoff. The simulations were performed with a 2 fs time step, at a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 atm. Stability and dynamics of the protein–ligand complexes were assessed using root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen bond analyses to ensure robust binding interactions[ ].

## Animal experiments
Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the research institution. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Bioethics Committee of the research institution[ ].

## Sepsis model
To ensure accurate estimation of the primary endpoints, C57BL/6 mice were assigned to n = 10 per group [sham, CLP, LPS, and PBS (phosphate-buffered saline)]. This sample size was informed by precedents in sepsis-related liver-injury models[ , ] and aligned with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines[ , ]. Primary endpoints included serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, liver histopathology, and the expression of the target protein. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated allocation sequence, implemented by personnel independent of outcome assessment. Due to the nature of the surgical procedures (sham vs. CLP), blinding the operating surgeon was not feasible; however, all outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded until completion of the primary analysis. To capture inter-animal variability within groups, individual animal values are displayed as scatter points in the figure panels. Environmental and procedural sources of variability, including housing conditions, cage assignment, and perioperative analgesia, were stringently controlled.
Sepsis was induced either by CLP following standardized procedures or by inducing endotoxemia via LPS injection. The CLP model was established according to previously described methods[ ]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and a 1–1.5 cm midline laparotomy was performed to expose the cecum, which was then ligated at approximately 50% of its length distal to the ileocecal valve using 4-0 silk suture. The ligated cecum was punctured once through-and-through with a 25-gauge needle, and a small amount of fecal material was gently extruded to ensure bacterial leakage. The cecum was then repositioned, and the abdominal wall was closed in two layers. All animals received 1 ml of pre-warmed sterile saline subcutaneously for resuscitation and were maintained on a warming pad until fully ambulatory. Sham-operated animals underwent laparotomy and cecal exposure without ligation or puncture to serve as surgical controls.
For the endotoxemia model, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (Sigma), while controls were administered an equivalent volume of sterile PBS. All animals had free access to food and water throughout the study period. Survival was monitored every 6 h, and blood and tissue samples were collected at 6 and 24 h post-procedure. Mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation, and samples were harvested for subsequent biochemical and histological analyses.

## Biochemical analysis and hematoxylin–eosin staining
Serum AST and ALT activities were quantified to assess hepatic injury. Paraffin-embedded liver sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation of liver damage.

## Tissue immunofluorescence (IF)
Following antigen retrieval using EDTA buffer for 30 min, liver sections (5 μm) were permeabilized with Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature and subsequently blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: TBCB (Proteintech, 1:200), F4/80 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:200), and HNF4α (HUABIO, 1:100).
After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional PBS washes, the slides were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

## Cell experiments
In vitro experiments were conducted using Alpha Mouse Liver 12 (AML12)[ ] and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)[ ]. Each experimental condition was performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3), consistent with established standards for mechanistic studies of sepsis-induced liver injury[ ]. AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin–amphotericin B solution. BMDMs were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) containing the same serum and antibiotic concentrations, supplemented with 20 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor.
Septic conditions were simulated by stimulation with LPS at concentrations of 1000 ng/ml for AML12 cells and 100 ng/ml LPS plus 20 ng/ml interferon-γ (IFN-γ) for BMDMs (Fig. A). Following treatment, cells were collected. Proteins were extracted for western blot analysis to assess temporal changes in the expression of the target molecule.

## Western blot analysis
Liver tissues and cultured cells were lysed on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Protein samples were then mixed with 5× loading buffer at a 4:1 volume ratio and heated to 100°C for 10 min to denature.
Equal amounts of protein from each group were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto 0.45 µm polyvinylidene fluorid (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against TBCB (Proteintech, 1:1000), endoplasmic reticulum lectin 1 (ERLEC1) (Proteintech, 1:1000), amyloid beta precursor-like protein 2 (APLP2) (Abclonal, 1:1000), and β-actin (Proteintech, 1:1000). Following three 10-min washes with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with an horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature.
All immunoblots were performed using at least three independent biological replicates. Protein bands were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Advansta) and imaged using the Molecular Imager Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software for densitometric analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.

## Results

## Protein quantitative trait loci Mendelian randomization analysis
Proteome-wide MR analysis identified C-reactive protein (CRP), APLP2, ERLEC1, and TBCB as proteins showing suggestive associations with sepsis risk (nominal P < 0.05 with FDR-adjusted P ≥ 0.05) (Fig. A, Supplemental Digital Content Tables S1–S5, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G636).
 

Genetically predicted levels of TBCB were associated with sepsis risk across multiple cohorts: deCODE (P = 0.048, OR = 1.441, 95% CI: 1.003–2.069), Fenland (P = 0.048, OR = 0.611, 95% CI: 0.375–0.996), and UK Biobank (P = 0.048, OR = 1.268, 95% CI: 1.002–1.604). Similarly, APLP2 demonstrated consistent and suggestive positive associations with sepsis, including AGES (P = 0.016, OR = 1.091, 95% CI: 1.017–1.171), ARIC (P = 0.001, OR = 1.265, 95% CI: 1.096–1.460), deCODE (P = 0.003, OR = 1.383, 95% CI: 1.121–1.706), and UK Biobank (P = 0.003, OR = 1.252, 95% CI: 1.082–1.448). Similarly, ERLEC1 demonstrated comparable genetic effects in the AGES (P = 0.022, OR = 1.249, 95% CI: 1.033–1.510), ARIC (P = 0.045, OR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.002–1.256), and deCODE (P = 0.045, OR = 1.330, 95% CI: 1.007–1.758) cohorts. Heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy statistics for all analyses are summarized in Supplemental Digital Content Tables S6–S8, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G636, Supplemental Digital Content Tables S9–S15, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G637.
CRP, APLP2, and ERLEC1 showed concordant directional effects in at least three cohorts. Although TBCB displayed a discordant direction in one dataset, it was retained for further analysis given its established biological relevance to tubulin biogenesis and microtubule dynamics[ ]. Microtubule-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling is a well-recognized contributor to endothelial barrier dysfunction and organ injury during sepsis[ ]. Considering this strong mechanistic plausibility and the suggestive MR signal, TBCB was retained for experimental validation in this exploratory study. It should be noted that CRP, although identified as a significant marker, primarily serves as a clinical biomarker of inflammation rather than a direct therapeutic target[ , ]. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on the remaining three candidate proteins: TBCB, APLP2, and ERLEC1.
Further colocalization analysis suggested that the genetic signals for TBCB and APLP2 were shared with sepsis susceptibility loci, with PPH4 of 0.70 and 0.68, respectively. These values indicate a moderate/suggestive likelihood that the same genetic locus simultaneously affects protein abundance and sepsis susceptibility. In comparison, ERLEC1 did not demonstrate colocalization evidence (Fig. B, Supplemental Digital Content Table S23, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G638).
Finally, PPI network analysis revealed that TBCB and APLP2 occupy key nodes within an interaction cluster, underscoring their roles in the molecular network potentially governing sepsis pathogenesis (Fig. C).

## Expression quantitative trait loci summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis
The SMR analysis identified TBCB as the only gene showing a suggestive association with sepsis risk (nominal SMR_P < 0.05 with HEIDI_P ≥ 0.05) (Fig. E, Supplemental Digital Content Tables S19–S22, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G638).
 

Pathway enrichment analysis of the SMR-suggestive genes demonstrated significant overrepresentation in the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, and the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily binding (Fig. A–D). These pathways are well established as key mediators of sepsis pathophysiology[ – ], providing strong biological plausibility for the genetic associations identified in our analysis.

## Tubulin-folding cofactor B is upregulated in the liver of sepsis mice
Sepsis is a systemic disorder that affects multiple organs, among which the liver, a central immunometabolic organ, plays a pivotal role and is particularly susceptible to injury. Previous studies have documented extensive transcriptomic reprogramming, inflammatory activation, and metabolic remodeling within hepatic tissue during sepsis[ – ]. The three candidate targets identified in our genetic analyses, TBCB, APLP2, and ERLEC1, are each implicated in biological processes integral to hepatic pathology, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (ERLEC1), cytoskeletal dynamics (TBCB), and lipid trafficking (APLP2). Based on these mechanistic associations, the expression patterns of these targets were assessed in in vivo and in vitro models of sepsis-induced liver injury.
Two models were established as described (Fig. A). The H&E staining revealed classical histopathological features of liver injury, including hepatocellular ballooning, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and necrosis, in both CLP- and LPS-treated mice. These lesions were more severe at 24 h compared to 6 h post-induction (Fig. B). Consistent with these findings, serum ALT and AST levels were significantly elevated, confirming hepatic injury (Fig. C–D).
 
 

To further investigate molecular alterations, WB was performed to assess TBCB, APLP2, and ERLEC1 expression in liver tissues. Among these, TBCB showed pronounced upregulation in both CLP and LPS models, whereas APLP2 and ERLEC1 showed no significant changes (Fig. E–H). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that TBCB expression was increased in macrophages of the disease model compared to those in the control group(Fig. I–J). 
 

## Tubulin-folding cofactor B is upregulated in vitro
The expression profiles of the three candidate targets were further examined in AML12 and BMDMs (Fig. A). WB analysis confirmed the successful induction of a sepsis-like inflammatory state, as indicated by significant upregulation of iNOS and TNF-α (Fig. B–D).
Consistent with the in vivo findings, TBCB expression was significantly elevated in LPS-stimulated BMDMs, reaching a peak at 12 h post-treatment. In comparison, APLP2 and ERLEC1 displayed minimal or no detectable changes in expression under the same conditions (Fig. B–D). However, parallel analyses in AML12 hepatocytes revealed no significant alterations in TBCB expression following LPS exposure (Fig. E–F).

## Knockdown of tubulin-folding cofactor B reduces inflammation in bone marrow-derived macrophages
To elucidate the functional role of TBCB in macrophage-mediated inflammation, TBCB was knocked down in BMDMs using siRNA. Three distinct siRNA sequences targeting TBCB were transfected into BMDMs, and knockdown efficiency was evaluated by western blot at 72 h post-transfection. Among the tested constructs, siTBCB#3 achieved the most effective reduction in TBCB protein expression compared with the non-targeting control (siNC) (Fig. A–B).
 

Upon stimulation with LPS + IFN-γ (12 h), BMDMs demonstrated robust induction of iNOS and key inflammasome-related proteins, including caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-1β (Fig. B–D). TBCB knockdown significantly attenuated this inflammatory response, leading to significant reductions in iNOS, caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-1β levels relative to siNC-treated controls (Fig. C–D).
All three proteins, TBCB, APLP2, and ERLEC1, showed suggestive associations with sepsis risk in the MR analysis; only TBCB was further supported by SMR and colocalization evidence and displayed consistent upregulation in both in vivo and in vitro models. These convergent lines of genetic, biological, and experimental evidence highlight TBCB as the most credible candidate for further mechanistic and therapeutic investigation, whereas APLP2 and ERLEC1 were deprioritized due to insufficient gene-level support and experimental evidence.

## Metabolome-wide and mediation MR analysis
The metabolome-wide MR analysis identified several circulating metabolites that demonstrated suggestive associations with sepsis risk (Fig. A, Supplemental Digital Content Table S16, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G637, Supplemental Digital Content Tables S17 and S18, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G638). Enrichment analysis of these sepsis-associated metabolites revealed prominent involvement of immunometabolic pathways, including the arginine and urea cycle, glutathione metabolism, and bile acid/intestinal lipid absorption (Fig. B–D).
 

To further clarify the mechanistic connections between protein levels and sepsis susceptibility, mediation MR analyses were conducted to evaluate the intermediary role of metabolites. The results demonstrated that alterations in specific downstream metabolites mediated a substantial portion of TBCB’s genetic effect on sepsis risk. The proportion mediated was estimated at 31.78% for docosapentaenoate (22:5n3), 62.77% for stearidonate (18:4n3), 22.44% for 3,5-dichloro-2,6-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and 39.12% for eicosapentaenoate (20:5n3) (Supplemental Digital Content Table S24, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G638, Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G651). These findings support the hypothesis that TBCB may act as an upstream regulator of sepsis-driving metabolic reprogramming.

## Molecular docking and dynamics simulations
RMSD, radius of gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), hydrogen bonds, and RMSF analyses were conducted to assess the stability of protein–ligand complexes. As shown in Figure A, RMSD stabilized after ~60 ns (TBCB-tocophero), ~70 ns (TBCB-tocopherol), and ~60 ns (TBCB-A-tocopherol). After RMSD stabilization, the RMSD values for the main chain (Cα, C, N) were 25.119 ± 0.576, 23.607 ± 0.782, and 23.369 ± 0.500 Å, respectively, indicating the presence of structural changes. To further evaluate the stability of the three complex systems, we simultaneously analyzed the systems’ Rg (Fig. B) and SASA (Fig. C). Rg and SASA both decreased over time and then plateaued, consistent with compaction, reduced solvent exposure, and improved stability. Hydrogen-bond analysis (Fig. D) showed ~1 hydrogen bond maintained on average across complexes, implying a modest stabilizing role. RMSF values (Fig. E) were low (mostly below 8 Å), indicating limited flexibility and high stability.

## Discussion
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated systemic inflammatory response to infection[ ]. Despite advances in fluid resuscitation, antimicrobial therapy, and organ support, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal due to the heterogeneity of sepsis, limited diagnostic specificity, and the absence of effective treatments that target its fundamental mechanisms[ , ]. As a central immunometabolic hub, the liver plays a pivotal role in sepsis pathogenesis. Sepsis-induced liver injury contributes to impaired lactate clearance and gluconeogenesis, cholestasis, coagulopathy, and defective drug metabolism, features closely associated with disease severity and mortality. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced hepatic injury is of substantial scientific and clinical significance.
This study integrated multi-omics analyses with in vivo and in vitro validation to identify novel therapeutic targets involved in sepsis-induced liver injury. Our findings reveal that TBCB is closely associated with the pathogenesis of sepsis and its associated hepatic dysfunction. Experimental silencing of TBCB significantly attenuated inflammatory responses, suggesting that TBCB plays a functional role in modulating macrophage-driven inflammation. Furthermore, metabolome-wide MR analysis indicated that TBCB may act as an upstream causal regulator of metabolic reprogramming that drives sepsis progression. Complementary molecular docking and MD simulations identified candidate small molecules that interact with TBCB, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for sepsis-induced liver injury.
TBCB belongs to the tubulin-specific chaperone (TBC) family, which includes TBCA, TBCB, TBCC, TBCD, and TBCE, a group of cofactors responsible for guiding the correct folding and assembly of α- and β-tubulin monomers into functional αβ-heterodimers[ , ]. These heterodimers are critical components of the microtubule network, which regulates cytoskeletal structure, intracellular transport, and cellular signaling. Emerging evidence links dysregulated TBCB expression to several human diseases. Elevated TBCB levels have been associated with adverse prognosis in acute myeloid leukemia, potentially through modulation of the tumor microenvironment[ ], and with altered immune metabolism in HIV infection[ ]. In neurological disorders, tubulin cofactors, including TBCB, play key roles in synaptic plasticity, and genetic alterations in these cofactors have been implicated in conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis[ , ].
In clinical settings, a TBCB-targeted therapeutic strategy would be best positioned as an adjunctive intervention to standard sepsis management, which includes early source control, prompt administration of antimicrobials, fluid and vasopressor resuscitation, and comprehensive organ support, consistent with Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines[ ]. Initiation of such therapy would ideally occur after hemodynamic stabilization and in parallel with antibiotic treatment.
Given that TBCB plays a critical role in tubulin biogenesis and microtubule homeostasis, systemic modulation of this protein could potentially affect a broad range of microtubule-dependent physiological processes beyond the liver. These include endothelial barrier regulation in the lung and kidney, innate immune cell trafficking and chemotaxis, neuronal and cardiac microtubule dynamics, ciliary epithelial function, and hematopoiesis[ ].
Risk mitigation strategies should prioritize tissue-specific drug delivery, such as liver-targeted formulations, along with short-duration dosing confined to the acute phase of sepsis. Moreover, comprehensive functional safety assessments, including assays that evaluate microtubule-related biological processes in vulnerable organ systems, should accompany routine toxicological evaluations to ensure therapeutic precision and minimize off-target effects[ ].
The results of the metabolomic studies revealed that TBCB-associated downstream metabolites are predominantly enriched in pathways related to intracellular lipid handling and cellular metabolic regulation. The TBCB-binding ligands emerging from docking and MD were three closely related tocopherol-class molecules: A-tocopherol, tocophero, and tocopherol. These compounds share a typical tocopherol backbone; this architecture makes them small, lipophilic, and membrane-permeable. Pharmacologically, tocopherol-derived compounds are well characterized as antioxidants and cytoprotective agents: they terminate lipid peroxidation, scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, preserve intracellular glutathione, and limit hepatocellular oxidative injury[ , ]. In polymicrobial sepsis induced by CLP, A-tocopherol treatment has been shown to reduce serum aminotransferases and decrease hepatic lipid peroxidation, indicating hepatoprotective activity under septic inflammatory stress[ ]. Beyond redox control, vitamin E analogs also exert direct anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing inducible mediators such as TNF-α, COX-2, and iNOS[ ]. Taken together, these data indicate that tocopherol-class compounds have documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hepatoprotective bioactivity in sepsis-relevant settings. This supports their plausibility as pharmacologically relevant starting points for TBCB-directed modulation, consistent with our findings.
This study demonstrates various significant strengths. First, a multidimensional, integrative approach was adopted to identify potential therapeutic targets for sepsis. The experimental studies demonstrated that TBCB modulates the inflammatory microenvironment in sepsis-induced liver injury, providing novel insights into the molecular mechanisms governing hepatic dysfunction during sepsis. Second, the combination of integrative metabolomics, molecular docking, and MD simulations offered unprecedented mechanistic insights into how TBCB may regulate the metabolic and structural pathways contributing to sepsis progression. Finally, the convergence of evidence across in vivo, in vitro, and multi-omics datasets, supported by diverse experimental techniques, substantially reinforces the reliability and translational potential of our findings.
Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the MR, SMR, and colocalization analyses were performed using data primarily from European-ancestry cohorts. Differences in allele frequencies, LD structures, and QTL distributions may limit the generalizability of our findings to non-European populations. Future research should include ancestry-specific replication, involving reanalysis using cross-population heterogeneity tests. Second, this study focused primarily on liver injury, whereas TBCB may also play a role in other organs affected by sepsis, such as the lungs, kidneys, and heart. Further organ-specific investigations will be necessary to clarify the systemic contribution of TBCB to sepsis pathophysiology. Moreover, inter-animal variability in experimental models may introduce biological noise, underscoring the need for large-scale clinical validation. Third, while the FDR correction was applied and both statistically significant and suggestive findings were reported, it was found that the latter category carries a higher risk of false positives. However, subsequent experimental validation of candidate targets mitigates this concern and strengthens the credibility of our results. In addition, all genetic, proteomic, and transcriptomic data used in this study were derived from biobank-level summary statistics, without direct validation using patient-derived liver tissues. As such, our findings should be confirmed in prospective clinical cohorts. Finally, the downstream regulatory metabolites influenced by TBCB remain unvalidated experimentally. Future studies employing targeted metabolomics and functional assays will be critical for delineating these pathways and validating TBCB’s mechanistic role in sepsis at the biochemical level.

## Conclusion
Through an integrated approach combining multi-omics analyses, animal models, and cell-based experiments, this study identified TBCB as a key protein with a genetically supported causal role in sepsis-induced liver injury. These findings provide novel insights into the complex pathophysiology of sepsis, suggesting that disruptions in cytoskeletal organization, metabolic regulation, and inflammatory signaling contribute to disease onset and progression. However, as the genetic analyses were derived primarily from European-ancestry populations, future validation in diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of these results.
