## Abstract

## Background
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a respiratory disease with complex causes; its pathogenesis remains unclear. This study explores the regulatory relationship between NORAD and miR-150-5p to provide novel insights for COPD treatment.

## Methods
Serum from 108 COPD patients and 94 healthy controls were collected. qPCR measured NORAD and miR-150-5p expression, while dual-luciferase assays verified their interaction. The clinical relevance of NORAD was analyzed using ROC, logistic, and Pearson methods. Human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (hPMECs) were treated with 5% cigarette smoke extract (CSE) for 36 h to establish a cell injury model. NORAD was silenced alone or co-inhibited with miR-150-5p in this model. Inflammatory and oxidative stress markers were measured by ELISA, and cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay.

## Results
COPD patients exhibited significantly elevated serum NORAD level (P < 0.001), with higher levels in acute exacerbation than stable phases (P < 0.001). NORAD showed high diagnostic (AUC = 0.867, P < 0.001) and predictive value (OR = 3.271, P = 0.002) for COPD and was significantly correlated with lung function (P < 0.05). Serum NORAD was strongly negatively correlated with miR-150-5p (r = -0.749, P < 0.001), indicating a targeted regulatory relationship (P < 0.001). After CSE treatment, NORAD expression in hPMECs was significantly up-regulated (P < 0.001). NORAD knockdown reduced inflammation (P < 0.001), oxidative stress (P < 0.01), and reversed CSE-induced proliferation inhibition (P < 0.001). Co-inhibition of NORAD and miR-150-5p reversed the protective effect of NORAD knockdown (P < 0.05).

## Conclusion
NORAD can regulate miR-150-5p, and both NORAD and miR-150-5p may serve as potential therapeutic targets for COPD, offering a promising strategy for modulating disease progression.

## Background
COPD is a complex respiratory disorder with multifactorial etiology, clinically manifested by chronic cough, sputum production, dyspnea, and impaired respiratory function [ , ]. Research indicates that COPD patients not only exhibit compromised pulmonary function but also frequently develop extrapulmonary comorbidities, including cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and diabetes mellitus [ ]. In recent years, increasing emphasis has been placed on the early diagnosis of COPD. Spirometric test remains the cornerstone in the clinical evaluation of COPD and holds irreplaceable diagnostic value. However, due to the heterogeneous nature of high-risk group of COPD and the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria [ ], some patients in the early stages do not yet meet the spirometric threshold of FEV1/FVC < 70%, increasing the risk of underdiagnosis when relying solely on this metric. Comorbidities may confound respiratory function assessments and complicate the attribution of clinical symptoms; and it only measures airflow limitation, not underlying inflammation or oxidative stress, limiting its ability to assess severity or predict progression. There is a pressing need to establish reliable methods for disease monitoring and surveillance. The pathogenesis of COPD involves not only dysregulated immune responses mediated by inflammatory factors [ ] but is also closely linked to aberrant expression of non-coding RNAs, particularly long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs) [ ] and microRNAs (miRNAs) [ ]. Notably, LncRNA biomarkers in COPD-such as MALAT1 [ ], H19 [ ], and NEAT1 [ ] have garnered significant research attention. Research on MALAT1 and NEAT1 has mainly reported associative findings: MALAT1 is linked to cigarette smoke-induced airway inflammation, and NEAT1 to COPD susceptibility and severity. These associations support the potential of lncRNAs as biomarkers and provide a basis for exploring their regulatory roles in COPD-related pathways. The role of lncRNAs in regulating signaling pathways has been well established in cancer biology. Given their conserved biological functions in pathway regulation, this knowledge provides a rational framework for hypothesizing their potential regulatory involvement in COPD pathogenesis.
The lncRNA NORAD was initially identified in the DNA damage response, where it plays a pivotal role in cell cycle regulation and the maintenance of genomic stability [ ]. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that increased expression of NORAD is closely associated with the pathological progression of various pulmonary diseases [ ]. In pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), serum NORAD levels are significantly elevated and exhibit high diagnostic value, as they promote macrophage activity and amplify inflammatory responses [ ]. In lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury (ALI), NORAD expression is markedly induced, while its silencing mitigates inflammatory damage and cellular dysfunction [ ]. Notably, NORAD is up-regulated in both lung cancer tissues and patient sera, correlating significantly with disease progression and poor prognosis [ , ]. Furthermore, analysis of gene expression profiles from COPD patients in the GEO database revealed 463 differentially expressed LncRNAs, among which NORAD was identified as one of the ten hub LncRNAs, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for COPD [ ]. However, its specific mechanism of action in COPD remains unclear.
MiR-150-5p serves as a critical biomarker for COPD progression [ ], demonstrating a gradual decline in plasma levels as disease severity worsens, with its expression with lower expression associated with reduced pulmonary function indices and increased inflammatory markers. Notably, miR-150-5p exhibits remarkable prognostic value for COPD patients, with nearly 90% of high-expression patients surviving 54 months compared to only 50% survival among low-expression counterparts [ ]. In cigarette smoke-induced COPD mouse models, miR-150-5p levels were progressively down-regulated in both plasma and bronchial epithelial cells following prolonged exposure. Significantly, overexpression of miR-150-5p was shown to mitigate smoke-induced pulmonary damage, suppress the release of inflammatory cytokines, alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress, and enhance cellular migration capacity [ ]. Bioinformatics predictions show conserved binding sites in NORAD that complement miR-150-5p, supporting their direct interaction. NORAD and miR-150-5p exhibit opposite expression patterns in COPD, consistent with the ceRNA regulatory network. NORAD functions by binding miRNAs to regulate disease, while miR-150-5p has anti-inflammatory and lung-protective effects in COPD. Their functional link in COPD pathogenesis supports a potential regulatory relationship. However, it remains unclear whether NORAD modulates the pathogenesis of COPD through the regulation of miR-150-5p. To address this gap, human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (hPMECs) are an ideal cellular model for investigating this regulatory axis, as they serve as a key interface between the lungs and circulation, maintaining vascular homeostasis, regulating inflammation, and modulating oxidative stress. They are directly involved in core COPD pathologies such as lung injury and vascular remodeling. As a source of circulating non-coding RNAs, hPMECs provide a relevant cellular model for studying the NORAD–miR-150-5p regulatory interaction. It is postulated that NORAD potentially aggravates inflammation and lung function decline in COPD through the negative modulation of miR-150-5p expression, ultimately contributing to disease advancement.
The primary objective of this study is to investigate whether NORAD serves as a biomarker associated with the pathogenesis and progression of COPD, thereby providing novel therapeutic targets for clinical intervention in this disease. Furthermore, this research aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanism of NORAD on miR-150-5p in the disease context, ultimately clarifying its role in the onset and development of COPD.

## Methods

## Study subjects
This research has approved by The Ethics Committee of Suizhou Central Hospital. In addition, informed consent has been obtained from the participants involved. And the study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study enrolled 108 patients diagnosed with COPD, including 66 patients in the acute exacerbation phase (AE-COPD group) and 42 patients in the stable phase from June to December 2020. Furthermore, a cohort of 94 age-matched healthy controls was recruited for comparative analysis. The inclusion criteria were based on the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2020 guidelines for the diagnosis and management of COPD. This study follows standard COPD diagnostic criteria: (1) presence of chronic cough, sputum, and progressive dyspnea with risk factors such as smoking (≥ 10 pack-years) or pollutant exposure; (2) pulmonary function testing performed 30 min after bronchodilator administration, with airflow limitation defined as a post-bronchodilator ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 s to forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) < 70%, with severity staged by percent-predicted FEV1, strictly adhering to the 2020 GOLD guidelines: GOLD 1 (≥ 80% of predicted value), GOLD 2 (50–79% of predicted value), GOLD 3 (30–49% of predicted value), GOLD 4 (< 30% of predicted value); and (3) AE-COPD defined as acute worsening of symptoms requiring treatment change; stable COPD as no exacerbation for ≥ 4 weeks.
Baseline characteristics were collected from enrolled patients, and pulmonary function parameters, inflammatory biomarkers, and clinical scores were assessed. The CAT score is the actual score from the patient’s completion of the COPD assessment test scale, serum C-reactive protein (CRP) is the measured concentration with a measurement unit of mg/L, and peripheral blood white blood cell count (WBC) is the detected value, measured in 10⁹/L.
Lung function tests were performed in strict alignment with the respective disease states of the enrolled patients: for AE-COPD patients, tests were conducted within 24 h of hospital admission (during the acute exacerbation phase); for stable-phase COPD patients, tests were performed during their clinically stable periods, which meet the inclusion criterion of “no acute exacerbation for ≥ 4 weeks”. This timing captures true lung function changes corresponding to each patient’s disease phase, avoiding misrepresentation from inconsistent timing—such as assessing stable-phase patients during acute episodes or vice versa—and eliminating detection bias due to incomplete symptom manifestation in outpatient settings. It ensures that FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC accurately reflect the patient’s respiratory status—such as dyspnea severity and inflammation—during acute episodes. FEV1% was calculated using the Chinese population-specific predicted value equations recommended by the 2018 Chinese Guidelines for Pulmonary Function Testing in Adults. For males, the predicted FEV1 (L) was calculated as: 0.038 × age (years) – 0.015 × height (cm) + 5.489–0.012 × weight (kg); for females, the equation was: 0.026 × age (years) – 0.011 × height (cm) + 4.358–0.009 × weight (kg). FEV1% was derived by dividing the measured FEV1 by the predicted FEV1 and multiplying by 100%. Participants in the control group had no history of respiratory diseases.

## Serum collection
Within 24 h of the patient’s admission, obtain a 5 mL sample of peripheral venous blood and transfer it into a blood collection tube. Vacuum blood collection tubes without anticoagulants were used for serum isolation. Serum was separated by allowing the blood to clot at room temperature for 30–60 min. The obtained blood samples were uniformly distributed and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min using a refrigerated centrifuge maintained at 4 ℃. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully transferred to an enzyme-free aseptic EP tube with a pipette gun, and then stored in a refrigerator at −80 ℃ after numbering.

## Cell culture and CSE-induced model construction
Human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (hPMECs) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in the dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin double antibody (Gibco, USA). Subsequently, the cells were placed in an incubator with a carbon dioxide concentration of 5%. When the cells reached approximately 80% confluence in the culture dish, they were subcultured. Upon completing the designated growth cycle—specifically, continuing to culture the passaged cells in the aforementioned DMEM medium for 24 h to ensure they resume growth and enter the logarithmic growth phase (a state with active cell proliferation and stable cellular status, enabling better response to subsequent CSE induction)—the cells were subsequently treated with cigarette smoke extract (CSE, Pythonbio, China) for experimental induction. This 24-hour post-passage culture step was implemented to avoid potential interference from the stress state of cells immediately after passaging on experimental results.
The cigarette filter was removed, and the cigarette was connected to one end of a gas sampling tube containing 200 mL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS, Beyotime, China), while the other end was connected to a negative pressure device maintaining a constant pressure of 0.02 MPa. The cigarette was ignited, and the smoke was collected and fully dissolved in the PBS until the solution turned light yellow. The resulting suspension was sealed and mixed thoroughly. The solution was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane under sterile conditions to eliminate bacteria and particulates. The CSE was prepared freshly and used immediately. The hPMECs were subsequently cultured in complete medium containing 5% (v/v) cigarette smoke extract for 36 h.

## Cell transfection
First, hPMECs were treated with 5% CSE. Subsequently, transfection was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol of Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Hieff Trans, China). The transfection mixture was prepared at a volume ratio of opti-minimal essential medium (MEM, Thermo Fisher, USA): small interfering RNA (siRNA): Lipofectamine 3000= 1:1:1. Small interfering RNA targeting NORAD (si-NORAD; sense 5′-GGAAGAGAGAAAUGGUAGAAUtt-3′, anti-sense 3′-ttCCUUCUCUCUUUACCAUCUUA-5′) and its negative control (si-NC; sense 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, anti-sense 3′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-5′) were transfected into hPMECs pre-treated with 5% CSE. Both si-NORAD and si-NC were commercially synthesized and validated by RiboBio (China). Concurrently, control groups were established, including untreated cells and cells exposed to 5% CSE treatment alone. Transfection of miR-150-5p was likewise conducted using Lipofectamine 3000, adhering to the same procedure as described above.

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from clinical serum samples and in vitro cultured hPMECs using a commercial kit (TIANGEN, China), followed by cDNA synthesis according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China). The reverse transcription reaction was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: 65 ℃ for 5 min, 37 ℃ for 15 min, and 95 ℃ for 5 min using a PCR thermocycler.
For RT-qPCR, SYBR® Green Realtime PCR Master Mix-Plus (Toyobo, Japan) was employed on a Quant Studio 6 Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 sec and 60 ℃ for 1 min. U6 (forward 5’-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3’ and reverse 5’-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3’) and GAPDH (forward 5’-GTCAACGGATTTGGTCTGTATT-3’ and reverse 5’-AGTCTTCTGGGTGGCAGTGAT-3’) served as internal controls for miR-150-5p (forward 5’-GCTCTCCCAACCCTTGTACC-3′and reverse 5’-CGAGGAAGAAGACGGAAGAAT-3’) and NORAD (forward 5’-CTGGATTGAAGGCAGAGAAGGAAGG-3’ and reverse 5’-TTGTCCACCACATACACAGCACTG-3’), respectively. Relative expression levels were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCT method. The relative expression of NORAD in the COPD group was presented as fold change relative to the mean expression level in the healthy control group. Further stratifies the COPD patients into stable and acute exacerbation phases, and compares NORAD expression levels between these subgroups. NORAD expression was normalized to the mean level in mMRC grade 0 (mildest symptoms) and GOLD stage 1 (least severe airflow limitation) patients, respectively. Fold changes for higher grades or stages were calculated accordingly.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay
All non-time-course experiments were conducted 48 h after cell transfection. ELISA-Based Quantitative Analysis of Inflammatory Factors [including interleukin (IL)−8, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and IL-6] and Oxidative Stress Indicators [including malondialdehyde (MDA), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and superoxide dismutase (SOD)] in Cellular Samples Using Commercial Kit (Mult, China). The experimental procedure was rigorously conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol. Initially, the microplate was coated with specific capture antibodies, followed by blocking with bovine serum albumin (BSA) to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, prepared cellular samples were added and incubated to allow antigen-antibody binding. After thorough washing, enzyme-conjugated detection antibodies were introduced and incubated. The immune complexes were then labeled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. For signal development, tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added to initiate the chromogenic reaction, which was subsequently terminated at the optimal time point. Finally, the absorbance values were measured at 450 nm wavelength using a microplate reader for quantitative determination of target analytes.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
The 5 × 10³ cells/well hPMECs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, which aligns with standard cell culture inoculation practices. Preliminary experiments ensured cell density variation of less than 5% across wells. After seeding, plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 2 h to allow uniform cell adhesion and reduce bias from uneven distribution, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 5% CSE for 36 h, after which the medium in each well was carefully removed. Following treatment with 5% CSE-induced hPMECs and transfection with si-NORAD, hPMECs were further transfected with miR-150-5p inhibitor (in-miR-150-5p) and its negative control (in-miR-NC), respectively. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, a mixture of CCK-8 reagent (Biosharp, China) and complete medium was then added to each well simultaneously, and the plate was incubated for an additional hour. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each group included 5 biological replicates, and the experiment was independently repeated 3 times for statistical reliability.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The binding sites of NORAD and miR-150-5p were predicted using bioinformatics software, followed by the cloning of the NORAD wild-type sequence into the pGL-3 luciferase plasmid to construct the NORAD wild-type reporter vector (NORAD-wt). Subsequently, a mutant NORAD reporter vector (NORAD-mut) was generated by introducing site-directed mutations at the predicted binding sites. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol of Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), the constructed vectors were co-transfected into cells alongside miR-150-5p mimics (miR-150-5p, 5′- UCUCCCAACCCUUGUACCAGUG-3′), miR-150-5p inhibitors (in-miR-150-5p, 5′-CACUGGUACAAGGGUUGGGA-3′), or negative controls (miR-NC, 5′- UUGUACUACACAAAAGUACUG-3′ and in-miR-NC, 5′- CAGUACUUUUGUGUAGUACAA-3′). All non-time-course experiments were conducted 48 h after cell transfection. All constructs were synthesized and validated by RiboBio (China). Finally, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega, USA), with firefly and Renilla luciferase activities quantified using a microplate reader. Normalizing NORAD wt and mut luciferase activity to the blank control (vector without miRNA transfection) enables direct comparison of the same vector across treatments and between vectors under the same treatment, indirectly reflecting the effect of binding site mutations on miR-150-5p binding. This approach simultaneously shows whether the treatment is effective and whether the binding site is essential.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were repeated 3 times with triplicate measurements, and all data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Continuous variables between two groups were compared using the t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups (analysis of variance) were performed with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. The correlations between serum NORAD and miR-150-5p were assessed in the COPD patient population by Pearson linear correlation analysis, focusing on their expression relationships in this disease cohort.
Moreover, multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors associated with COPD occurrence. For this analysis, all continuous variables—including FEV1, FEV1/FVC, CAT score, CRP level, and WBC count—were converted into binary variables (0 = below the median, 1 = above or equal to the median) based on the median values of the dataset. Similarly, NORAD levels (expressed as relative quantities) were categorized using the same median-based criterion prior to inclusion in the regression model. This standardized variable transformation ensures more interpretable and clinically meaningful estimation of effect sizes.
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc.), and the figure was plotted using GraphPad Prism 7.00 (GraphPad Software Inc.). The discriminative performance of serum NORAD in differentiating COPD patients from healthy controls was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Serum NORAD expression levels were used as the diagnostic variable, and the diagnostic accuracy was quantified by calculating the area under the curve (AUC).

## Results

## The general information of COPD patients and healthy controls
The study comprised a total of 108 COPD patients, with the stable phase group consisting of 42 patients (13 females and 29 males) having a mean age of 61.62 ± 7.38 years, while the acute exacerbation phase group included 66 patients (18 females and 48 males) with a mean age of 64.00 ± 6.86 years. The healthy control group contained 94 participants (38 females and 56 males), demonstrating a mean age of 63.14 ± 7.78 years. Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between COPD patients and healthy controls regarding age, gender, BMI, or family history of COPD (P > 0.05) (Table  ). The two subject groups were well matched.
 Compared with the control group, COPD patients had a significantly higher proportion of smoking history (P = 0.001), along with significantly lower lung function parameters (FEV1/FVC and FEV1), and significantly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and WBC) and CAT scores (P < 0.001). In comparison to patients in the stable phase, those experiencing acute exacerbation exhibited significantly higher mMRC (P < 0.001) and GOLD grades (P = 0.040) (Table  ).

## Expression and clinical significance of NORAD in COPD serum
The NORAD levels exhibited a significant up-regulation in peripheral serum of COPD patients compared to normal controls (Fig.  A, P < 0.001). Similarly, a marked increase in NORAD expression was observed in patients during acute exacerbation phases relative to those in stable conditions (Fig.  B, P < 0.001). NORAD expression demonstrated discriminative capacity between clinical states (COPD and healthy) with an ROC curve AUC of 0.867 (95% CI = 0.819–0.915, Fig.  C), with sensitivity of 74.07% and specificity of 88.3%. These values were determined by identifying the maximum Youden index on the ROC curve, corresponding to an optimal value of 0.6237 and a NORAD relative expression level of 1.202 at this threshold.
 The results of the multicollinearity analysis for the variables included in the logistic regression model indicated that all variables had tolerance values ≥ 0.805 and variance inflation factors (VIF) ≤ 1.243, suggesting no significant multicollinearity among the predictors(Supply 1). The primary multivariate logistic regression analysis presented herein excludes these two pulmonary function parameters FEV1 and FEV1/FVC. This adjusted model identified elevated LncRNA NORAD (OR = 3.271, P = 0.002), increased CRP levels (OR = 6.165, P < 0.001), raised CAT scores (OR = 3.963, P < 0.001), and smoking history (OR = 3.501, P < 0.001) as independent risk factors for COPD occurrence, highlighting NORAD’s predictive utility independent of lung function (Fig.  D). For transparency and comprehensive reporting, the original multivariate logistic regression model including FEV1 and FEV1/FVC has been transferred to the supplementary materials (Supply 2).

## Relationship between NORAD expression level and clinical indexes
Figure presents an analysis of the correlation between NORAD expression levels and clinical parameters. NORAD exhibited a negative correlation with pulmonary function indices, specifically FEV1/FVC (%) (Fig.  A, r = −0.630, P < 0.001) and FEV1 (% predicted) (Fig.  B, r = −0.531, P < 0.001). Conversely, NORAD demonstrated positive correlations with established inflammatory indicators, including CRP (Fig.  C, r = 0.571, P < 0.001) and WBC (Fig.  D, r = 0.453, P < 0.001), as well as CAT scores (Fig.  E, r = 0.537, P < 0.001). Subsequent evaluation of NORAD expression patterns across different GOLD grades (Fig.  F, P < 0.05) and mMRC grades (Fig.  G, P < 0.05) revealed statistically significant variations in its expression levels.
 

## The effect of NORAD on inflammation, oxidative stress, and hPMECs proliferation in CSE-induced hPMECs
CSE induction significantly up-regulated the expression of NORAD in the damage model (Fig.  A, P < 0.001). In this model, CSE also elevated inflammatory markers (Fig.  B, P < 0.001), and increased oxidative stress (P < 0.001) while reducing antioxidant enzyme SOD levels (P < 0.01) (Fig.  C). CSE aggravated the suppression of cell proliferation, as evidenced by reduced OD values (Fig.  D, P < 0.001).
 Further silencing of NORAD in the CSE model led to a significant downregulation of NORAD expression compared to the CSE control group (Fig.  A, P < 0.001), with sustained knockdown maintained throughout the entire multi-day detection period (Supply 3 A, P < 0.001). NORAD silencing effectively suppressed the release of inflammatory cytokines—TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 (Fig.  B, P < 0.001)—and alleviated oxidative stress injury in hPMECs, as evidenced by reduced MDA and ROS levels (P < 0.001) and increased SOD activity (P < 0.01) (Fig.  C). Additionally, NORAD knockdown reversed the CSE-induced inhibition of hPMEC proliferation (Fig.  D, P < 0.001).

## Evaluation of the interaction between NORAD and miR-150-5p
From Fig.  A, it can be observed that the relative expression level of miR-150-5p in the serum of COPD patients was significantly lower than that of the control group (P < 0.001) and compared with stable COPD patients, miR-150-5p in the serum is further downregulated in the acute exacerbation phase (Fig.  B, P < 0.001). The expression level of miR-150-5p in the serum of COPD patients was negative correlation with serum NORAD expression levels (Fig.  C, r = −0.749, P < 0.001).
 The schematic diagram of the binding sites between NORAD and miR-150-5p is illustrated in Fig.  D. To further validate the direct, sequence-specific bindin between NORAD and miR-150-5p (the core objective of this dual-luciferase assay), we performed co-transfection of reporter vectors with miR-150-5p mimics, inhibitors, or their negative controls. As shown in Fig.  E, overexpression of miR-150-5p (miR-150-5p mimics) markedly suppressed the luciferase activity of the NORAD wild-type (NORAD-wt) group (P < 0.001), indicated that exogenously miR-150-5p can specifically recognize and bind to the intact binding sites on NORAD, thereby inhibiting the expression of the linked luciferase reporter gene. In contrast, no significant change in luciferase activity was observed in the NORAD mutant (NORAD-mut) group—where the predicted binding sites were disrupted—confirming that the interaction between NORAD and miR-150-5p is dependent on the specific target sequence. Furthermore, treatment with miR-150-5p inhibitors (in-miR-150-5p) resulted in a significant enhancement of luciferase activity in the NORAD-wt group (P < 0.001), which further corroborates the specificity of this regulatory interaction by blocking the endogenous miR-150-5p-mediated suppression. These results collectively validate the direct, sequence-specific targeting relationship between NORAD and miR-150-5p, laying a solid foundation for subsequent ceRNA mechanism exploration.

## LncRNA NORAD promotes CSE-induced hPMEC injury response through negative regulation of miR-150-5p
CSE treatment significantly inhibited the expression of miR-150-5p (P < 0.001), while NORAD silencing significantly restored the expression of miR-150-5p (P < 0.01) (Fig.  A). In the si-NORAD transfection group, NORAD expression was consistently down-regulated (Supply 3 A, P < 0.001) and miR-150-5p was significantly up-regulated (Supply 3B, P < 0.05). These results support the regulatory cascade “sustained knockdown of NORAD → upregulation of miR-150-5p → enhanced cell proliferation,” confirming its role in driving functional cellular changes.
 To further investigate the roles of NORAD and miR-150-5p in regulating inflammatory factors, oxidative stress markers, and hPMEC proliferation, this study employed co-inhibition of NORAD and miR-150-5p. The control group data and results from the NORAD silencing alone group are presented in Fig.  . Transfection with in-miR-150-5p (a miR-150-5p inhibitor) reversed the knockdown effect of NORAD (Fig.  A, P < 0.01). As illustrated in Fig.  B, knockdown of NORAD significantly up-regulated the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (P < 0.001). Concurrently, the levels of oxidative stress markers were markedly elevated (P < 0.001), while the activity of the antioxidant enzyme SOD was substantially reduced (P < 0.05) (Fig.  C). Furthermore, this genetic intervention remarkably enhanced cellular proliferation rates (Fig.  D, P < 0.01).

## Discussion
COPD is a complex systemic disorder involving core pathological processes such as inflammatory [ ], oxidative stress [ ], and cellular dysfunction [ ]. This study investigates the association and potential regulatory mechanisms between lncRNA NORAD and the onset and progression of COPD, focusing on its regulatory relationship with miR-150-5p through integrated analysis of clinical serum samples and in vitro experiments using CSE-induced hPMECs.
Clinical data showed that serum NORAD levels were significantly elevated in COPD patients compared to healthy controls, with higher expression in the acute exacerbation phase than in the stable phase. ROC curve analysis confirmed NORAD’s ability to distinguish COPD patients from healthy individuals, while logistic regression further validated it as an independent risk factor for COPD. Correlation analysis revealed that NORAD expression was negatively associated with pulmonary function indices (FEV1/FVC, FEV1) and positively correlated with inflammatory markers (CRP, WBC) and CAT scores. Notably, FEV1/FVC and FEV1 are not merely key indicators of airway obstruction but the central diagnostic and defining parameters for COPD—their reduction is not only essential for establishing the hallmark pathophysiological feature of persistent airflow limitation but also serves as the primary basis for assessing disease severity, guiding clinical management decisions, and evaluating patient prognosis [ ]. CRP and WBC are classic inflammatory markers, and elevated levels objectively reflect the inflammatory activity in COPD patients, which is closely associated with disease severity and exacerbation risk [ ]. The CAT score, a standard tool for assessing COPD symptoms (e.g., cough, sputum, and dyspnea) [ ], correlates with symptom burden and quality of life. This aligns with previous findings that higher CAT scores are associated with increased disease burden, and the resulting for CAT scores in the multivariate model conforms to valid statistical interpretation logic, thereby supporting the rationality of NORAD’s significant OR > 1 (adjusted for lung function and smoking history). These results suggest NORAD may contribute to COPD pathogenesis by modulating inflammatory responses and pulmonary function. Furthermore, NORAD expression varied significantly across different mMRC [ ] and GOLD classifications [ ], reinforcing its association with disease progression and providing preliminary evidence that NORAD participates in COPD-related pathological processes via regulating inflammatory responses and pulmonary microvascular endothelial cell function. A key limitation is the lack of direct in vivo evidence of NORAD expression in pulmonary microvascular endothelial tissue, as serum results cannot fully reflect tissue-specific levels—future studies will collect lung tissue (e.g., surgical or biopsy samples) and use in situ hybridization to address this. Furthermore, systemic or inhaled corticosteroids (common in COPD treatment) may affect inflammatory regulation and non-coding RNA expression; without medication records, interpreting the NORAD/miR-150-5p axis in clinical contexts is limited. Future research will include full medication histories (especially glucocorticoid use) to enhance analysis depth and clinical relevance.
In vitro experiments were performed using 5% CSE-induced hPMECs to establish a cell injury model that mimics the COPD-associated microenvironment. hPMECs are central to COPD pathogenesis, as they directly participate in core pathological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular remodeling—particularly in emphysema. Acute CSE exposure rapidly and reproducibly induces hPMECs to display COPD-relevant phenotypes (elevated inflammatory factors, oxidative stress imbalance, impaired proliferation), which facilitates targeted investigation of the NORAD/miR-150-5p axis’s regulatory function. Notably, this model is well-validated in the field for studying COPD cellular mechanisms. In the CSE-induced hPMECs model, NORAD expression was significantly up-regulated. Inflammation and oxidative stress are core to COPD progression: persistent inflammation drives progressive pulmonary function decline [ ], while excessive reactive oxygen species (from internal or external sources) cause cellular damage and dysfunction [ , ]. Silencing NORAD reduced CSE-induced inflammatory cytokine levels (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8), decreased oxidative stress markers (MDA, ROS), and increased expression of the antioxidant enzyme SOD. Additionally, hPMEC proliferation and repair are critical for pulmonary endothelial homeostasis—CSE inhibits this proliferation, and NORAD knockdown reversed this effect, indicating NORAD regulates cellular proliferation and may impact pulmonary endothelial repair in COPD (dysregulation of which can further compromise pulmonary function [ ]). While this study focuses on hPMECs, we recognize COPD involves multiple cell types (e.g., lung epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages) and extrapulmonary organs (e.g., liver, via inter-organ signaling such as the lung-liver axis in pneumonia) that may contribute to circulating NORAD and miR-150-5p. Future studies will include these cell types and tissues to confirm their multicellular/multi-organ origins, enhancing the comprehensiveness and rigor of our conclusions.
NORAD was first identified for its role in DNA damage responses and exerts regulatory effects in DNA damage-associated neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ ] and Parkinson’s disease (PD) [ ]. Additionally, NORAD is implicated in key cellular processes—proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis [ , ]—underscoring its multifaceted role in various diseases and potential as a diagnostic biomarker. MiR-150-5p, a key inflammation modulator, is regulated by the lncRNA MALAT1 in osteoarthritis and linked to inflammatory cytokines and cell proliferation [ ]. Here, we found serum miR-150-5p was significantly down-regulated in COPD patients and strongly negatively correlated with NORAD levels, suggesting NORAD modulates miR-150-5p activity to regulate inflammatory responses. Subsequent experiments confirmed NORAD sequesters miR-150-5p: miR-150-5p overexpression markedly reversed the pro-inflammatory, pro-oxidative, and anti-proliferative effects of NORAD overexpression in our in vitro model. These results validate that NORAD acts as a molecular sponge for miR-150-5p, with this interaction mediating the aforementioned biological processes in our experimental system. Furthermore, our co-inhibition experiments revealed that simultaneous inhibition of NORAD and miR-150-5p reverses the pro-proliferative effect of NORAD silencing and abolishes its protective effects against inflammatory and oxidative stress damage, indicating that NORAD might regulate hPMEC injury and COPD progression by sponging miR-150-5p in a targeted manner. These findings offer potential novel therapeutic targets for COPD. However, the mechanistic evidence chain remains incomplete, as current data rely solely on clinical serum samples and in vitro experiments, lacking direct evidence from COPD lung tissues or in vivo models. Future studies will address this gap: in vivo experiments will strengthen validation, Western blot analysis will quantify the expression and phosphorylation of key proteins in inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways, and dual-luciferase reporter assays will validate predicted miR-150-5p targets. These efforts will clarify the molecular pathways through which the NORAD/miR-150-5p axis regulates inflammation and oxidative stress, reinforcing direct mechanistic insights.
However, this study has several limitations that warrant attention in future research. The relatively small sample size may compromise statistical power and generalizability to the broader COPD population; additionally, unevaluated and uncontrolled factors—such as the degree of airway lesions in enrolled COPD patients and the potential biases from lung tissue sampling locations—may introduce variability and affect result interpretation. The cross-sectional design also precludes inferring causality between NORAD and COPD progression; future prospective cohort studies will longitudinally track changes in NORAD expression alongside key progression indicators (e.g., lung function decline rate, acute exacerbation frequency) to clarify its role in disease advancement. Additionally, this study only included COPD patients and healthy controls, excluding those with other lung conditions (e.g., interstitial lung disease). This leaves uncertainty about whether NORAD is specific to COPD or reflects general lung injury, limiting its diagnostic specificity. Future studies will expand the sample to include patients with other lung diseases for direct NORAD expression comparisons, validating its COPD-specificity and clinical utility for diagnosis and differentiation. Furthermore, while we identified the NORAD/miR-150-5p pathway’s association with COPD, we cannot exclude potential crosstalk with other pathways. The in vitro CSE model, though widely used for simulating cigarette smoke exposure, has inherent limitations: its variable composition and static incubation fail to fully replicate in vivo dynamic smoke metabolism and complex lung microenvironment cell interactions. To enhance physiological relevance, we will establish a 12-week chronic smoke-exposed mouse model, collect lung tissue and blood samples, and use single-cell RNA sequencing to compare molecular profiles between CSE-treated hPMECs and mouse pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, validating the in vivo relevance of our in vitro findings. Notably, despite confirming the NORAD/miR-150-5p interaction and its effects on hPMECs, we did not perform transcriptome-wide network analysis or identify specific pathway nodes. Future work will employ RNA-seq to analyze gene expression changes following NORAD knockdown/overexpression and miR-150-5p mimic/inhibitor treatment, identifying differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways. We will also use ChIP-qPCR to validate NF-κB and Nrf2 binding, clarifying the underlying regulatory mechanisms and providing new insights for COPD treatment.

## Conclusion
In summary, NORAD is closely associated with the severity and prediction of COPD and may influence disease development via regulation of miR-150-5p. However, further validation of this regulatory pathway in vivo—through lung tissue analyses and animal models—is required to confirm its role in COPD pathogenesis.
