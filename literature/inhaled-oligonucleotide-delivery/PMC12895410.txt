## Down-selecting Synthesis
and Quality Modules

## Pedagogic Logic
Understanding the
functional capacity
of small molecules enforces the student connection to the power and
application of molecules across diverse fields. In this activity,
students explore data from a real-world, AI-guided functional optimization
campaign toward the discovery of top-in-class organic laser materials. ). Then, students follow along in their lab handout to learn
how an international team of researchers partnered with AI to discover
new, best-in-class lasers. The core concept is that AI was utilized
to rapidly learn how molecular structure relates to molecular function
via rational down-selection of a large chemical space containing 155,520
possible molecules. Students learn the concept of chemical space and
are asked to analyze a chemical space plot to determine where in the
chemical space the researchers found the highest-performing laser
molecules. To familiarize students with the laser structures and enforce
their ability to identify iterations, students first analyzed a previous
gold standard organic laser. Then, students move on to an analysis
of selected lasers from the data set.
 Each organic laser consists of three functional
modules: electron
donor cap, pi-bridge, and lynchpin core. Each module has three building-block
options and a curated list of nine lasers, in which each of the three
options has three representative lasers. Each laser is listed alongside
its experimental emission gain cross section value. Students calculate
the average emission gain cross sections across lasers that share
a common building block to determine which option is the highest performing.
Based on their optimized building blocks, students use iterative synthesis
logic to assemble their highest performing lasers which should result
in students reconstructing the highest performing laser in the recent
report. ). Students identify
iteration on an example OPV to reinforce connecting target molecules
to the iterative synthesis platforms needed. Then, students follow
along in their handouts to learn about how researchers partnered with
AI/ML to rapidly characterize the function via a series of active
learning cycles. After each batch of molecules were synthesized, the
team provided the collected function data to the AI model, which then
proposed the next structures to synthesize. After only 44 total molecules
were synthesized from a possible 2,200 molecules, the team optimized
their desired function. Using AI-predicted function data for the entire
data set, the students enter the DMM to design OPV candidates.
 Students then entered the DMM to design five OPV
candidates. They
do this by dragging and dropping their selected building blocks from
the left-hand block pallet into the workspace. The graph in the upper
right of the interface will live-update with the remaining achievable
function properties. The DMM at the time of implementation was only
accessible on a laptop, but a mobile version is in development. Students
rather quickly were able to pick up how to use the DMM as we saw most
students complete this entire section of the activity within about
30 min. Students tended to quickly understand the goals of the platform.
The most common student concerns regarded small technical challenges
such as errors in case-sensitity in class codes, which we plan to
address in the next round of programming development.
In their
notebooks, they record the predicted Spectral Overlaps
(SO), T-80 lifetimes, and Photostability, defined as the product of
SO and T-80. To visualize the data, students plot their molecules
on a graph for their T-80 lifetime and SO. After designing five molecules,
students predict their highest performing molecule based on maximized
photostability and submit the molecule to the group cart. To complete
the activity, students draw their optimized molecules in their notebooks
and rationalize their selection.

oligopeptide, and oligosaccharide synthesizers, on-resin chemistry
enables excess reagents to be washed away after each coupling. It
has been challenging to use this same approach for iterative carbon–carbon
bond formation. The unique binary affinity of MIDA and TIDA boronates
to silica gel provided an alternative and highly effective solution
by enabling general purification via catch-and-release chromatography

## Conclusions
The goal of this activity
was to introduce automatable blocc chemistry
to undergraduate students prior to significant experience with organic
chemistry. We leveraged real-world examples where chemists utilized
automated, iterative synthesis coupled with AI/ML tools to rapidly
explore functional space. In doing so, we developed a laboratory experience
that introduces automated synthesis platforms, challenges students
to identify repeating chemical subunits, modularize organic lasers
into functional building blocks, and then reconstruct organic laser
candidates with optimized lasing properties, developed an online Digital
Molecule Maker platform for exploration of blocc chemistry, and synthesized
a collection of small molecules amenable to general purification methods.
To accompany the written materials, video instruction enables the
teaching of unfamiliar theory and direct instruction of the activity
goals.
We postulate that blocc chemistry increases accessibility
of organic
chemistry such that anyone, including nonexperts and computers, can
engage with the design and synthesis of functional molecules. Blocc
chemistry simplifies synthesis by design in order to be 1) friendly
to automation, 2) friendly to AI, and 3) friendly to anyone. Blocc
chemistry for iterative carbon–carbon bond formation was designed
based on inspiration from other automated synthesizers that perform
iterative carbon-heteroatom or heteroatom-heteroatom bond formation,
which rely on iteration of a single reaction and general purification
for efficient synthesis of oligomers. These two fundamental characteristics
of automation were developed for blocc chemistry to make small molecule
synthesis friendly to automation. The focus of this lab module is
to highlight the need for general purification strategies for automation.
Blocc chemistry also simplifies synthesis in a way that makes chemistry
inherently friendly to AI. ChatGPT and other large language models
have largely been successful based on the choice to tokenize information
at the word, rather than letter, level. We view blocc chemistry as
an analogous strategy, where molecules are tokenized into molecular
building blocks, each with a predicted contribution to a molecule’s
function, using a new modular Chemical Language Model (mCLM). Through training AI models about the function
of molecular building blocks, new molecules with the desired functions
can be proposed. With continued research in this area, we are writing
a new “language” for molecule making that can be interpreted
by AI and executed by automated iterative synthesis machines. The
Molecule Maker Lab Institute is eagerly working toward a future where
anyone can meaningfully engage with the design of new molecules that
fit their needs, regardless of their background. Analogous to how
block-based coding (e.g., Scratch) made coding accessible to nonexperts,
we aim to make block-based synthesis of functional molecules accessible
to nonexperts. This course was implemented in General Chemistry 2,
and students were expected to have little background in organic chemistry
concepts. Through blocc chemistry, they were able to meaningfully
interact with functional molecules and organic synthesis. Through
the development of this course series, we aim to include undergraduates
in this frontier research space at the interface of small molecule
synthesis, automation, and AI.
To date, over 2,340 students
at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign
have completed a version of the dry lab portion of this activity.
A limitation of the wet lab activity is the need for teaching assistants
to synthesize the custom-building blocks and run the student reactions.
Therefore, we only offer the Suzuki-Miyaura coupling and purification
activities for our accelerated track students, of which over 350 students
have completed. This lab is the second of a three-part series being
developed at UIUC that is illuminating the interface of automation-friendly
blocc chemistry and AI by making research programs and processes accessible
to introductory chemistry students.
