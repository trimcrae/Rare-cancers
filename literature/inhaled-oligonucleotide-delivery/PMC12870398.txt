Among these, glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) stands out as the most prevalent and aggressive form of glioma in adults, carrying a 5-year survival rate of less than 5% [ , ]. At present, surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for malignant gliomas, often complemented by radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy options [ ]. Nevertheless, the overall prognosis remains poor, emphasizing the urgent need to develop theories to refine glioma tumorigenesis and discover new treatment targets and approaches.
The study of glioma tumorigenesis is hampered by the complexity and heterogeneity of cancer cells. However, recent advances unearth a significant link between the development of tumors and gastrulation [ ]. Gastrulation refers to the process during early embryonic development in multicellular animals where the three primary germ layers—ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm—are formed [ ]. These germ layers subsequently differentiate into various tissues and organs. The process of gastrulation involves complex and finely regulated mechanisms, including cell division, migration, and differentiation, which are crucial for establishing the intricate structure and function of multicellular organisms [ – ].
Tumorigenesis shares lots of similarities with gastrulation, such as continued proliferation, invasion and metastasis. Moreover, sets of genes and pathways involved in gastrulation, such as Wnt, Notch, and Hedgehog signaling, play critical roles in cell differentiation and proliferation [ – ]. Precise regulation of gene expression during gastrulation is also essential for proper cell differentiation and tissue formation. Dysregulation of these pathways or gene expression can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation and promote tumor formation [ ], during gastrulation, there are numerous pluripotent stem cells with the potential to differentiate into various cell types. Mutations or abnormal differentiation of these stem cells may also contribute to tumorigenesis [ ]. Notably, gliomas harbor a subpopulation of glioma stem cells (GSCs) that mirror the pluripotency and self-renewal capacity of gastrulation-stage stem cells [ ]. GSCs often reactivate developmental pathways like Wnt and RAS to sustain their stem-like phenotypes [ ], driving tumor recurrence and therapy resistance—yet how these pathways intersect with gastrulation-linked genes in GSC regulation remains poorly understood.
Despite these established parallels between gastrulation and gliomagenesis, systematic analyzes of GLD genes in gliomas are lacking, and the specific molecular mediators bridging GLD pathways to oncogenic processes (e.g., GSC maintenance) have not been fully characterized. This gap limits our ability to translate developmental biology insights into targeted glioma therapies, highlighting the need for focused studies on GLD genes and their key regulators.
MEST (mesoderm-specific transcript), an imprinted gene critical for embryonic cell fate specification and tissue patterning, has been implicated in oncogenesis across multiple cancers (e.g., breast, lung) by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and stemness [ , ]; however, its functional role and regulatory mechanisms in glioma remain largely unexplored, making it a compelling candidate for investigating GLD-associated oncogenic targets.
Here, we performed a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of GLD genes, providing a novel insight into the landscape of the GLD in gliomas, and confirmed MEST as a key oncogenic therapeutic target via machine learning feature selection framework. Furthermore, we have conducted extensive research to elucidate the pivotal role of the identified MEST gene in glioma progression through in vivo and in vitro experiments.

## Methods

## Gathering and pre-processing of transcriptomic data and patient clinical information
Large-scale bulk transcriptomic datasets and corresponding clinical information were acquired from the publicly accessible databanks. Noticeably, the mRNA expression profiles and clinical information of TCGA-glioma (n = 702), including low grade glioma and glioblastoma, were downloaded from the XENA (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). Gathered from Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/), four glioma cohorts such as CGGA-325 (n = 325), CGGA-693 (n = 693), CGGA-1018 (n = 1018) and CGGA-array (n = 301) were processed for further analysis. Survival information and transcriptomic profile of glioma patients (n = 168) with RNA-sequencing data for at least two time points were collected from the Glioma Longitudinal Analysis Consortium (GLASS, https://www.synapse.org/glass). According to the diagnostic time points, the cohorts from GLASS were divided into two cohorts comprising 168 primary glioma patients and 168 recurrent glioma patients, respectively. From the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), the GSE108474 (n = 314), GSE16011 (n = 276), GSE43289 (n = 40), GSE47696 (n = 84), GSE43378 (n = 50), GSE35493 (n = 71) and GSE4290 (n = 180) were obtained. All the cohorts with survival information were integrated into a large glioma cohort, named glioma meta dataset (n = 2549). The meta dataset was divided into the training dataset (n = 2042) and validation dataset (n = 507) in a ratio of 8:2. The profiles with different GBM geometrical region annotation were downloaded from Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas Project (IVYGAP, https://glioblastoma.alleninstitute.org/). Normal brain cortex bulk transcriptomic data and clinical information were obtained from The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/datasets). For the datasets from the Illumina HiSeq platform, transformation of transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) was needed, while for the microarray data, preprocessing included background correction, quantile normalization and log2 transformation. The samples without complete survival information were excluded for survival analysis.
The single-cell RNA sequencing data of glioma were gathered from Synapse (https://synapse.org/singlecellglioma) [ ] or GEO with GSE117891 [ ], GSE84465 [ ] and GSE117891 [ ]. The workflow of single-cell transcriptomic data was referenced to the source literature of the corresponding data. The R package Seurat (v.4.3.0.1) was used for quality control, normalization, batch effect correction, cell clustering, dimension reduction, cluster annotation and so on, the parameters of which were the same as these in the studies, following the guidelines from the reference literature [ – ].

## Collecting the germ layer development (GLD) genes
The GLD related genes were obtained from the publicly accessible databases (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb), with the key words ‘Germ layer development’. 13 GLD related pathways were gathered: GOBP AXIAL MESODERM DEVELOPMENT (n = 9), GOBP CONVERGENT EXTENSION INVOLVED IN GASTRULATION (n = 9), GOBP DETERMINATION OF LEFT RIGHT ASYMMETRY IN LATERAL MESODERM (n = 8), GOBP ECTODERM DEVELOPMENT (n = 22), GOBP ECTODERMAL PLACODE DEVELOPMENT (n = 14), GOBP FORMATION OF PRIMARY GERM LAYER (n = 128), GOBP LATERAL MESODERM DEVELOPMENT (n = 15), GOBP MESODERM DEVELOPMENT (n = 136), GOBP NEURAL CREST FORMATION (n = 15), GOBP NEURAL PLATE MORPHOGENESIS (n = 5) and WP ECTODERM DIFFERENTIATION (n = 142). The “n” means number of genes. After removing the duplicate genes and excluding the genes lacking RNA-seq data in TCGA, 358 GLD genes were finally obtained for further analysis. The details of the GLD genes are displayed in supplementary Table 1.

## Evaluating the activation of the GLD pathway in cancer samples
To describe and provide a specific insight into the comprehensive landscape of the GLD in gliomas, the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) algorithm in the R package GSVA (v.1.40.1) was utilized for computing the enrichment of the GLD pathway in bulk RNA-sequencing datasets. As for single cell transcriptomic data, the R package AUCell (v.1.14.0) was used for identifying the cells with active GLD gene sets. Moreover, the enrichment results of GO for differential expression genes were produced by the R package clusterProfiler (v.4.7.1).

## Markedly elevated expression of GLD genes
The genes with markedly elevated expression were identified using a differential expression analysis and Tukey’s outlier analysis [ ]. The former analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We computed the expression fold-change (FC) values with the mean of two groups including tumor samples and the normal samples. The genes with an adjusted P-value less than 0.05 and an absolute value of logFC greater than 1 were identified as differentially expressed genes in glioma compared to normal brain cortex tissues. The genes were classified as either up-regulated or down-regulated based on the positive and negative values of logFC. Further, a cancer sample was classified as highly expressed (an outlier) if the expression of a specific gene exceeded 1.5 times the interquartile range (25th to 75th percentile) for all samples. In the absence of these criteria, samples are categorized as having the expected range of expression. For Tukey’s outlier analysis, we calculated expression FC values by comparing two groups of cancer samples: those having highly elevated and those having expected GLD gene expression. The median expression values of these two groups were used to calculate the ratio for each gene. Switch-like GLD genes were annotated individually. These genes were defined to have no expression in most samples (i.e., median of 0).

## Developing and evaluating the optimal prognostic GLD model with 117 machine learning combinations
A good predictor of prognosis in glioma patients was needed for improved management and precise treatment of glioma patients. Here, the 56 genes with adjusted P value less than 0.05 and logFC greater than 2 were selected from 358 GLD genes. The training meta-dataset was used to develop the optimal predictive model, while the validation meta-dataset and 11 glioma cohorts were used for validation. The development of the best predictive model was carried out with the R package Mime1, just as in the previous study [ – ]. The developed prediction model was named GLDscore. We then performed a comprehensive evaluation of the GLDscore, with survival analysis, univariate Cox regression analysis, multivariate Cox regression analysis, calculation of C-index and AUC. We also compared our GLDscore prediction model with previously published glioma models using the R package Mime1. GLDscore values in different clinical groups were calculated and presented.

## Identifying patient prognosis-related GLD genes
To identify GLD genes that are significantly associated with the prognosis of patients, we employed a machine learning framework based on several statistical techniques. These included differential gene expression analysis, univariate Cox regression analysis, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso), elastic network (Enet), Boruta, XgBoost, random forest (RSF), CoxBoost, and bootstrap analysis, which were adapted from our previous work [ ]. The model features comprised log1p-transformed expression profiles of 358 GLD genes. The TCGA GBM-LGG cohort was employed for the purpose of feature selection because of its availability of both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) data. The 56 GLD genes with significantly elevated expression in gliomas were included (adjusted P-value < 0.05 and logFC > 2). Following 1,000 iterations of the machine learning combined models fitted to a random subset of 80% of the samples, the GLD genes were prioritized. At each iteration, the process comprised four steps. Firstly, genes associated with patient survival were identified through the pre-selection of features using univariate Cox regression on both continuous and categorical data, with a significance level of P < 0.05. Secondly, the genes were incorporated into combinations comprising six machine learning algorithms. Each algorithm generated a set of features. Thirdly, genes identified by at least half of the algorithms were identified as OS or PFS-related genes. Fourthly, the overlap between OS-related and PFS-related genes was identified as the candidate genes in one iteration. After all iterations, the final GLD genes were selected, as they were identified as optimal survival-related features in over 95% of iterations.

## Additional comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of GLD genes in glioma
The feature selection machine learning framework was employed to identify five prioritized GLD genes for subsequent analysis. Based on the expression profile in single-cell transcriptomic data, we selected the GLD gene MEST as the focus for further analysis, given that only this gene exhibited significantly elevated expression in tumor cells in comparison to the other four GLD genes. To ascertain the prognostic associations, the glioma samples were dichotomised according to the median of the MEST transcriptomic expression. The prognostic difference was evaluated and visualized using Kaplan-Meier plots and a log-rank test. Additionally, the transcriptomic expression of MEST was scaled and displayed using TCGA GBM-LGG, GTEx, CGGA-325, CGGA-693, CGGA-1018, GSE43378, GSE16011, GSE35493, GSE4290 and IVYGAP, with a focus on comparing normal brain cortex tissues and gliomas across different grades and spatial locations. Moreover, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyzes of MEST were conducted using the R package survminer (v. 0.4.9).

## Collection of glioma tissue samples
As described in previous studies, the clinical glioma samples and normal brain tissues used in this study were obtained from patients undergoing surgical treatment at the Neurosurgery Department of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University between 2019 and 2024 [ ]. The samples were classified according to their histological grade, including Grade II (n = 8), Grade III (n = 6), Grade IV (n = 24), and normal brain tissues (n = 8). All patients provided written informed consent for the use of their tissue specimens in research. The Ethical Review Committee of the Xiangya Hospital approved the study protocol and the acquisition of tissue specimens.

## RT-qPCR
RNA extraction from glioma cell lines and human glioma tissue was performed using the Total RNA Extractor kit (Sangon Biotech, China). The extracted RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript® RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). For gene expression analysis, RT-qPCR was conducted on the 7500 Real Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) utilizing SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan) as the PCR master mix. The specific primers used are: GADPH forward primer 5′-CTCCTGCACCACCAACTGCT-3′; GADPH reverse primer 5′-GGGCCATCCACAGTCTTCTG-3′; MEST forward primer TGTGGGTGTGGTTGGAAGTC; MEST reverse primer CCTCAAGGTCAGACCCTTCC.

## Cell lines and cell culture
The glioblastoma cell lines A172, U87, and U251 were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank, while the human normal astrocyte cell line HA1800 was purchased from Science Cell, USA. All cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. The culture medium consisted of high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

## Plasmid construction and lentiviral package
The PLKO.1 vector was digested with EcoRI and AgeI restriction enzymes (#R310S,#R3552L, NEB, USA) and subsequently ligated to a short hairpin structure using T4 DNA ligase (#M0202S, NEB, USA) to construct the shRNA plasmid. Viral particles were produced in 293 T cells using the shRNA plasmid and second-generation lentivirus packaging envelope system with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (#L3000015, Invitrogen, USA). Twenty-four hours post-infection with the lentivirus, the cells were cultured in fresh medium for three days. The knockdown efficiency of the virus was then verified by RT-PCR after a two-day selection with 5 ng/ml puromycin. The MEST shRNA mature antisense sequences are 5′-CGACTGATGAACTTCTTTGTA-3′(#1) and 5′-CTTGAGGTTTCATCGGGTGAT-3′(#2). The nonsense sequence is 5′-CCGCAGGTATGCACGCGT-3′. MEST in pcDNA3.1-3xFlag-C Vector (Human), MEST in pcDNA3.1-EGFP Vector (Human) and VCP-mCherry in pcDNA3.1(+) Vector (Human) were purchased from Applied Biological Materials Inc (B14 National University Science Park, Jingkou District, Zhenjiang, Jiangsu 212009 China).

## Immunocytofluorescence (ICF)
Glioma cells were seeded on cover glass slides (CAT#G6012-1, Servicebio, China) and cultured for 48 h. The cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed in formaldehyde solution for 15 min, followed by three additional PBS washes, and then incubated in blocking solution for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed again three times with PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ki67 (CAT# 9449 S, CST, USA) and MEST (CAT# orb412513, USA). Afterward, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated at room temperature for 1 h with fluorescent secondary antibodies (CAT# A11029, CAT# A11035, Invitrogen, USA). Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI staining solution for 5 min.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
The transfection of the aforementioned plasmids into cancer cells was facilitated by utilizing Lip3000. Following a 72-hour transfection period, the cells were harvested, lysed in IP lysis buffer, and the lysate was washed by centrifugation at 4 °C. The extract was then subjected to incubation with an anti-Flag antibody and protein A/G resin at 4 °C with gentle rotation for a period of one hour. Subsequently, unbound proteins were washed thrice with buffer, and the bead-bound proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE. Antibodies against the following proteins were used for the experiment: VAP (F1019, Selleck, China) and FLAG (F0973, Selleck, China).

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)
Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. U251 and U87 cells (including Non-Targeting shRNA(sh-NT), shMEST#1, and shMEST#2 variants) were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate and cultured for 1–5 days. At each time point, cells were treated with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Biosharp, China) and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax® ABS Plus, USA) to quantify cell viability.

## Transwell assay
Transwell assays were performed using Falcon 24-well insert systems with 8.0 μm pore size. Cells were seeded into the transwell inserts using media without FBS at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well after 6 h of serum starvation. The inserts were then placed into media with 30% FBS. Following incubation for 24 h, the cells were fixed, crystal violet stained, photographed, and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software.

## RNA-seq profiling of MEST knockdown
RNA-seq data was generated in the GBM cell line U87 with shRNA targeting MEST, or non-targeting (NT) shRNA control, as described above. Total RNA was collected 4 days post lentiviral shRNA transduction using Total RNA Extractor kit (Sangon Biotech, China). Lentiviral transduction and RNA extraction were performed in triplicates. RNA sequencing was performed through Illumina sequencing platform by BGI genomics (China). The R package limma (v.3.50.3) was used for differential gene expression analysis. Genes were classified based on significance (adjusted P < 0.05) and fold change (|logFC| > 1). The methods of enrichment analysis have been mentioned above.

## Tumor xenograft
The experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Central South University (China). Following the previous study, six-week-old female BALB/c athymic nude mice were used for generating xenograft mouse model [ ]. These mice were maintained under sterile conditions, including filtered air and sterilized food, water, bedding, and cages. The mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. All mice were anesthetized with isoflurane gas and secured in a stereotaxic head frame. The skull was exposed, and a small incision was made using a sterile dental drill 2 mm lateral to bregma and 2 mm posterior. Using a Hamilton syringe, 5 × 105 U87 cells in 5 µL of HBSS (without Ca2+ and Mg2+) were slowly injected over 3 min to a depth of 2.5 mm from the skull surface at these incisions. All procedures were performed under sterile conditions. Xenograft growth was monitored by bioluminescent imaging using FUSION FX, and was quantified using EvolutionCapt v18.10. software. Mice were imaged 10 min after intraperitoneal injection of luciferin under isoflurane anesthesia. Observation of mouse survival period involved collecting brains from mice that died during the same period, embedding them in paraffin, and subsequently preparing hematoxylin and eosin (HE) stained sections and performing immunohistochemical staining. At the end of the study, all mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation and orthotopic tumors were collected for pathological analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All data analysis and graph generation were performed using R (v4.1.3), Adobe Illustrator and Biorender.com. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test or two-tail unpaired Student’s t test were employed for continuous variables, while the chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. A correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the various variables, employing Spearman’s coefficient. The Benjamini and Hochberg procedure was employed for the adjustment of the P value. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant and all statistical tests were two-sided. ## Results

## Insight into the landscape of germ layer development in glioma
Initially, the activation of GLD was evaluated using the GSVA algorithm across diverse glioma datasets. The enrichment scores for germ layer development in tumors were found to be significantly higher than in normal brain cortex tissues (Fig. A). With regard to the spatial distribution, it was observed that the closer the region is to the tumor core, the higher the enrichment scores (Fig. B). 20 representative GLD genes were selected for analysis, with their expression patterns in space resolved using IVYGAP. The results demonstrated elevated transcriptomic expression of MEST, SOX2, SOX9 and VEGFA in the tumor core relative to the edge. This finding was consistent with the results of previous studies that identified SOX2, SOX9 and VEGFA as key driving factors in the regulation of neural differentiation and cancer progression [ – ] (Fig. C). A further investigation was conducted to ascertain whether GLD was associated with patient survival. Patients with higher GLD level calculated by GSVA algorithm exhibit a poorer prognosis in the majority of glioma cohorts, thereby proving the remarkably negative correlation between GLD and glioma patient prognosis (Fig. D, supplementary Fig.  A). Transcriptomic analysis revealed striking patterns of GLD gene overexpression in individual glioma samples. Initially, based on differential transcriptomic profiles and the prognostic value, the GLD genes were classified into five distinct groups: up in glioma and protective (33, 9.6%), up in glioma and risky (87, 25.4%), down in glioma and protective (13, 3.8%), down in glioma and risky (5, 1.5%), and not significant (205, 59.8%) (Fig. E). Our findings revealed that MMP2, MMP4, MEST, and FN1 were classified as oncogenes with high-risk potential (Fig. F). Some of these genes have previously been identified as playing a role in the malignant progression of gliomas. For example, MMP2, a well-known prprotein involved in invasion, had been confirmed to be involved in tumor invasion, metastasis and angiogenesis of glioma [ ]. Additionally, a typical GLD genes demonstrated upregulation (LGG: 0.95, GBM: 0.87) in a minor subset of samples (LGG: 0.77%, GBM: 0.61%) compared to other samples of the same glioma type (Fig. G-H). The proportion in the upper right quadrant is indicative of the proportion of highly expressed GLD genes among all GLD genes. The proportion was elevated in GBM in comparison with LGG, suggesting that the GLD genes might participate in glioma program. Furthermore, we considered the subset of GLD genes with switch-like activation, defined as switch genes with zero expression in most glioma samples and non-zero expression (not switch genes) in less than half of the samples. The term ‘switch-like genes’ is employed to denote genes that are expressed in a binary ‘on/off’ manner under specific conditions. In summary, the expression state of these genes is either fully active (‘on’) or completely silent (‘off’), exhibiting switch-like behavior. In contrast to the continuous, gradient-like expression exhibited by many genes, the distinctive expression pattern characteristic of switch-like genes plays a pivotal role in determining cell fate [ ]. A total of 20 (6%) and 18 (5%) switch-like GLD genes were identified in LGG and GBM, respectively (Fig. I-J). The identified switch-like genes might be key for glioma program and be potential therapeutic targets. Finally, the GLD pathway activity was scaled in the sc-RNA sequencing data from GSE131928, PRJNA575953, GSE84465, GSE117891 and Verhaak’s research. The data indicated that tumor cells exhibited elevated GLD enrichment scores (Fig. K-L, supplementary Fig. B-D) in GSE131928 and PRJNA575593. In conclusion, our study offered a comprehensive and novel insight into the involvement of GLD landscape genes in gliomas. Our findings revealed a negative correlation between the GLD involvement and patient survival and demonstrated significant differential expression patterns of GLD genes spatially within tumors and between different samples.

 

## Constructing and evaluating the machine-learning based GLD signature
To demonstrate the significance of GLD for patient prognosis and facilitate its clinical application, we used a previously developed R package that integrates multiple machine learning algorithms to build a model capable of accurately predicting patient outcomes. The model, constructed using the ‘StepCox [forward] + plsRcox’ method, was designated as GLDscore, exhibiting the highest mean C index value (Fig.  A). A survival analysis was conducted on all incorporated cohorts to evaluate the survival difference between the high and low GLDscore groups (Fig.  B). A high GLDscore was predictive of an unfavorable prognosis for patients with glioma. Additionally, the C-index values, one-year, two-year, and three-year AUC of previously published predictive models across the glioma cohorts were evaluated (Fig.  C-E, supplementary Fig.  A-C). These results collectively demonstrate the efficacy of GLDscore in prognostic prediction. Furthermore, the results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis in TCGA GBM-LGG, CGGA-325, CGGA-693, and CGGA-1018 provided robust evidence that the GLDscore could be an independent predictor of patient survival (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Further, we established correlation between the GLDscore and various clinical and molecular characteristics in TCGA, CGGA-325 and CGGA-693 cohorts. Particularly, clinical and tumor molecular characteristics we evaluated included comparisons between patient genders, patient age (below and above 50 years), gliomas grades, IDH mutation type, 1p-19q codeletion status and MGMT methylation state. Opposed to patient gender, we found patients with age exceeding 50 years, higher grade gliomas, IDH wildtype, 1p-19q co-deletion and MGEMT methylation exhibited elevated GLDscore values in TCGA, CGGA-325 and CGGA-693 cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3B-D). To improve usability and broaden access, we integrated our model into a nomogram and made it available online at https://mime1234.shinyapps.io/GLAIDPI/. In conclusion, we developed an optimal prognostic model utilizing the GLD genes and machine learning algorithms, which serves as an independent predictor of prognosis for glioma patients and performs considerably better than previously published models.

 

## Machine-learning based feature selection identified MEST as the core oncogenic gene
The feature selection framework, comprising machine-learning algorithms, identified five key GLD genes, including BTK, SMO, COL8A1, SFRP2 and MEST, which were subsequently confirmed as survival-associated genes (Fig.  A). Next, we evaluated the expression of the five genes at the single cell level in the glioma datasets, and this is displayed in Fig.  B and supplementary Fig.  A-I. These results indicated that MEST was the gene with the highest expression in tumor cells compared to the other selected GLD genes. In conclusion, MEST was identified as the core oncogenic and survival-associated GLD gene with a machine learning framework at both the bulk and single-cell level.

 

## High MEST expression associated with poor survival of glioma patients
Considering the findings of previous studies, we undertook an investigation into the expression characteristics of MEST in different glioma datasets. Our findings revealed that across datasets MEST expression levels were markedly elevated in gliomas relative to normal brain tissues, with levels demonstrating a progressive increase with tumor grade (Fig.  A). A higher level of MEST expression was observed in the vicinity of the tumor core in GBM (Fig.  B). This observation was subsequently validated in clinical GBM samples (Fig.  C-D). Furthermore, MEST expression was significantly higher in conventional glioma cell lines than in normal astrocytes (Fig.  E-F). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that overall survival was lower in glioma cohorts stratified by MEST expression (Fig.  G). The AUC of MEST, univariate and multivariate Cox regression, provided evidence that MEST is a good and independent predictor of survival in patients with gliomas (Fig.  H-J). A combined survival analysis of MEST and GLDscore was performed, determining that MEST could enhance the predictive performance of the GLD signature (Supplementary Fig. 5 A). Furthermore, we discovered that patients with high MEST expression experienced improved survival rates following radiotherapy for both glioma and LGG, though not for GBM (Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Higher transcriptomic MEST expression was observed in TMZ-resistant (TMZ-R) cell lines than in TMZ-sensitive (TMZ-S) cell lines (Supplementary Figs. 6B-C). In the CGGA dataset, MEST expression was higher in patients who received chemotherapy or radiotherapy than in those who did not (Supplementary Figs. 6D-E). It can therefore be concluded that the highly elevated expression of MEST was associated with poor survival of glioma patients. These findings prompted us to study the function of MEST in gliomas.

 

 

## MEST enhances glioma proliferation and increases glioma migration
The Pearson’s correlation analysis for expression levels of MEST was performed across all glioma cohorts. Genes positively and negatively related with MEST were counted in each cohort respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7 A). Genes with adjusted P value < 0.01 and absolute R > 0.1 were identified as MEST-related genes. Genes significantly associated with MEST in at least half of the cohorts were used for enrichment analyzes. The enrichment analysis indicated that the MEST might be involved in development, cell growth, DNA replication, cell cycle checkpoint signaling, DNA packaging and chromatin assembly (Supplementary Fig. 7B-C). These observations suggested that the MEST might contribute to the glioma proliferation and migration. Subsequently, we utilized lentiviral interference approach to specifically knock down MEST expression in glioma cell lines U251 and U87, and validated the knockdown efficiency at both RNA and protein levels (Fig.  A-C). Immunofluorescence assays revealed that MEST knockdown significantly reduced the proportion of KI67-positive cells in the cell population, suggesting that MEST knockdown may affect the cell cycle progression of glioma cell lines (Fig.  D-E). Our CCK-8 assay data further demonstrated that MEST knockdown significantly reduced the proliferation capacity of U251 and U87 glioma cells (Fig.  F). Additionally, the transwell assays provided substantial evidence that suppressing MEST expression notably inhibited the migration ability of glioma cells (Fig.  G-H). These results indicated that in vitro knockdown of MEST significantly decreases the proliferation and motility of glioma cells.

 

## MEST knockdown deregulates tumor proliferative potential or tumor proliferation in vivo
GBM is the most common and malignant glioma in adults, with a 5-year survival rate of no more than 5%. We next assessed potential glioma-related impacts from MEST expressed by U87 cells. To do so, we knocked down MEST in U87 cells expressing firefly luciferase and then intracranially injected the shNT or shMEST U87 cells into BALB/c nude mice (Fig.  A). In a mouse glioma xenograft model, we found that the MEST knockdown group exhibited slower tumor progression and significantly increased survival compared to the control group (Fig.  B-D). Collectively, MEST plays an important role in the abnormal growth and proliferation of tumors. Knocking down this gene may either inhibit tumor growth or cause tumor cell death, thereby reducing tumor size.

## MEST knockdown decreases RAS signaling and Wnt signaling
To explore the mechanism underlying the tumorigenesis by MEST, we performed a series of experiments (Fig. A). First, we performed RNA high-throughput sequencing of the U87 cell line with the MEST knock down (Supplementary Table 2). Compared with the control group, there were 118 significantly up-regulated genes and 320 significantly down-regulated genes in U87 cell line with MEST knock down (Fig. B, |logFC| >1 and P value < 0.05). Then, the pathway enrichment analysis of these differential expression genes indicated that the MEST might be associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress, RAS signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, cell growth, DNA packaging, etc. (Fig. C-E). These results were consistent with previous studies that MEST could indeed affect tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion and metastasis through various signaling pathways. In particular, the interaction of MEST and VAP could promote cancer invasion and metastasis through activating NF-κB signaling [ ]. We further investigated the expression of some key genes involved in ER stress, RAS signaling and Wnt signaling (Fig. F). Along with the knockdown of MEST in U87, all these key genes were significantly downregulated. We further validated this in U251 and U87 by quantitating the expression of KRAS, MRAS, RASA4, WNT10B, ADGRA2, FRAT1 and SFPP2 using RT-qPCR (Fig. G-H). Collectively, with the RNA-sequencing and the RT-qPCR, we found that the MEST might promote the rapid proliferation of glioma cancer cells by activating the RAS pathway and Wnt signaling, leading to poor prognosis of patients with glioma.

 

## MEST interacts with VCP to promote glioma progression
The NF-κB pathway was reported to regulate the RAS signaling and Wnt signaling [ , ]. To further elucidate the direct molecular mechanism by which MEST activates RAS and Wnt signaling pathways, we focused on the interaction between MEST and Valosin-containing protein (VCP) and its regulatory role in the NF-κB pathway [ ]. First, spatial interaction evidence was obtained via immunofluorescence co-localization assays in U251 glioma cells. MEST-eGFP (green fluorescence) and VCP-mCherry (red fluorescence) showed significant overlapping signals in the perinuclear area, indicating their spatial proximity for potential interaction (Fig. A-B). Then, transcriptional coordination was confirmed by correlation analysis in TCGA GBM-LGG, revealing a strong positive correlation between MEST and VCP mRNA expression (Pearson’s r = 0.23, P < 0.0001, Fig. C). This suggests that there is coordinated regulation at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, physical binding validation was achieved via co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), confirming the endogenous physical interaction between MEST and VCP (Fig. D). In all, we found that MEST could interact with VCP to promote glioma progression.

 

## Discussion
Gliomas are common primary malignant brain tumors of the central nervous system in adults and are currently considered incurable. Despite the adoption of aggressive combined treatment strategies, including maximal safe surgical resection, adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy, patient prognosis remains poor [ , ]. Investigating the molecular changes involved in the development and progression of gliomas and identifying new molecular targets are crucial for improving glioma diagnosis and treatment.
Over fifty years ago, Dr. Pierce proposed that the development of tumors is closely related to developmental biology [ ]. Tumor development shares several characteristics with embryonic development, such as rapid cell division, high cellular plasticity, and a dense vascular microenvironment. Additionally, the processes of embryo implantation and tumor cell invasion and migration are highly correlated. Furthermore, in terms of stemness maintenance, proliferation, and differentiation regulation, tumor cells are significantly related to embryonic cells. Some proteins that are only present during fetal development also appear in certain adult cancer tissues; these are known as oncofetal proteins. Examples include the serum marker CA19-9 in pancreatic cancer and alpha-fetoprotein in liver cancer. Here, we performed a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of GLD genes, providing a novel insight into the landscape of the GLD involvement in gliomas, and confirmed the MEST as a key oncogenic therapeutic target via machine learning feature selection framework.
MEST has been regarded as an imprinted gene involved in mesoderm development [ ]. However, emerging evidence indicates that it plays a significant role in the progression of various cancers. For instance, MEST is significantly upregulated in highly invasive lung cancer [ ] and breast cancer [ ], with its expression inversely correlated with patient survival. It promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through the activation of the NF-κB pathway [ ] and the induction of STAT3/Twist-1-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Additionally, research by Chen et al. shows that the zinc finger protein ZFP57 can inhibit the proliferation of breast cancer cells by downregulating MEST expression, whereas overexpression of MEST can counteract the tumor-suppressive function of ZFP57 [ ]. Other studies have revealed that MEST contributes to the malignant phenotypes of gastric cancer [ ], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [ ], and ovarian cancer [ , ], suggesting that the mechanisms by which MEST operates in various cancers are complex and diverse. These findings underscore the multifaceted role of MEST in cancer biology, providing potential targets for cancer diagnosis and therapy.
The switch-like GLD gene activation concept we introduced refers to binary, on/off expression changes in a subset of GLD-related genes during glioma progression—these genes transition abruptly between expressed and non-expressed states, acting as molecular switches for cell fate decisions. However, MEST does not exhibit this switch-like behavior. This pattern aligns with MEST’s known role in regulating cell differentiation plasticity (not binary fate determination), distinguishing it from switch-like GLD genes. The gradient expression of MEST correlates with clonal selection, with higher levels observed in therapy-resistant subclones. The plasticity-supporting expression of MEST may enable glioma cells to adapt to treatment stress (e.g. chemotherapy) without undergoing binary fate changes, thereby contributing to residual disease.
GLDscore is a multi-gene prognostic signature derived from GLD-related genes, not a single molecular marker. Its core advantage lies in integrating heterogeneous GLD gene expression patterns to capture glioma’s molecular complexity—this distinguishes it from traditional single-marker indicators (e.g., IDH mutation, MGMT methylation) and enables more precise risk stratification. Compared to previous prediction models, it has very good predictive performance (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C). Based on the user-friendly predictive website we developed, doctors can predict patients’ risks, achieving precise medical care and prognosis monitoring.
Based on current literature, MEST’s association with RAS and Wnt signaling pathways offers novel therapeutic possibilities [ , ]. Firstly, the regulation of MEST expression plays a critical role in the stabilization of Wnt signaling pathways and pathological signal cascades [ , ]. Studies have demonstrated that modulating MEST expression can significantly reduce the maintenance of cancer stem cell characteristics through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [ ]. Secondly, the development of RAS inhibitors, such as Sotorasib and Adagrasib, which target KRAS mutations, and modulatory drugs for the Wnt pathway, such as Erlotinib, provide potential strategies for regulating these signaling pathways [ , ]. Moreover, combining small molecule inhibitors (targeting promoter methylation levels or directly inhibiting upstream regulatory factors), siRNA technologies, and joint regulation of RAS and Wnt signaling pathways will undoubtedly reinforce the feasibility of MEST as a therapeutic target. Future research should leverage proteomics and high-throughput screening techniques to elucidate MEST’s potential in early disease diagnosis and personalized treatment applications.
In our study, we observed elevated expression levels of MEST in glioma tissues, which correlated positively with tumor grade. Analyzing molecular genetics, epigenetic features, and histopathological characteristics revealed significant associations between MEST expression levels and invasive biomarkers of glioma aggressiveness, as well as shorter patient survival. Using in vitro and in vivo glioma models, we confirmed that downregulation of MEST significantly attenuates malignant proliferation and migration of glioma cells by suppressing the activity of the RAS and WNT pathways. Specifically, in vivo knockdown of MEST reduced tumor size in xenograft mouse glioma models, thereby prolonging overall survival in tumor-bearing mice.
We recognize the limitations of our study. Firstly, the mechanism by which MEST affects the adverse prognosis of gliomas requires further verification through experimental means. Primarily, this study serves to illustrate the potential pathways through which MEST may be involved, as evidenced by the RNA-sequencing data and RT-qPCR. This conclusion awaits validation at the protein level and in vivo. Secondly, the glioma cell lines employed in this study were U251 and U87. The capacity of these cell lines to accurately reflect the true condition of the patient is a topic of some contention. To ensure experimental rigor and reliability, we intend to use patient-derived cell lines. This approach will yield results and conclusions that are more reflective of the clinical representation in the patient. Thirdly, our research has identified a significant function for the GLD-related gene MEST in gliomas through a range of bioinformatics analyzes and the utilization of two different animal models. However, the role of MEST in glioma tumor development and its interaction with other targets remains to be fully investigated and demonstrated experimentally. Fourthly, the GLD level was defined based on the GSVA score at the bulk level and the AUCell score at the single-cell level of the GLD genes. The question of whether an alternative algorithm in future could provide a more accurate description of the overall level of GLDs in the real world is one that requires further studies. Additionally, in the absence of prospective clinical trials, we employed retrospective data to evaluate the validity and applicability of our predictive model. Furthermore, despite the comprehensive data set gathered (including in-house samples), more samples and data are required to substantiate these findings and to facilitate its use in the clinic.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, using rigorous bioinformatics, we have provided a comprehensive description of the landscape of GLD in gliomas. Additionally, we have established a prognostic model capable of accurately predicting GLD-related outcomes. Furthermore, we have identified the core gene MEST and have conducted extensive research to elucidate its pivotal role in glioma progression through in vivo and in vitro experiments.
