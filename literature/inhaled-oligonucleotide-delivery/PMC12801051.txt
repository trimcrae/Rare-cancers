## Abstract
Influenza A virus (IAV) infections continue to represent a significant global health concern, both in terms of severe individual cases of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and the potential for the emergence of pandemics. Despite decades of research, therapeutic options remain limited and the pathogenesis of severe disease is not yet fully understood. One critical yet underappreciated aspect is how IAV reprogrammes the ribosomal landscape of the host to facilitate viral replication and evade immune responses. Ribosomes take centre stage during infection, as both the immune response and viral propagation depend on the protein synthesis machinery. Recent studies have shown that the ribosome is not a static structure but can undergo dynamic changes in composition and function (ribosomal heterogeneity), which may influence the balance between viral propagation and host defence. Additionally, cancer research has remarkably demonstrated the feasibility of targeting the ribosome therapeutically. In this review, we summarise emerging evidence on how IAV hijacks the ribosomal landscape, including ribosomal biogenesis, ribosomal proteins, translation factors and associated signalling pathways, and how these changes may shape the course of infection, immune response and lung injury. Drawing parallels with cancer biology, we explore whether components of this reprogrammed landscape could serve as therapeutic targets in severe IAV infection and ARDS. By connecting molecular mechanisms with clinical relevance, we aim to highlight a novel perspective on host–virus interaction that could open avenues for future treatment strategies.

## Introduction
Influenza viruses continue to cause severe infections in humans and pandemic outbreaks, remaining a major threat to global public health [ ]. The World Health Organization states that annual epidemics result in up to 5 million cases of severe illness and approximately 500 000 deaths worldwide [ ]. The majority of pandemic outbreaks are attributed to influenza A virus (IAV) [ , ]. Those usually arise when a new subtype emerges as a result of antigenic shift and are of zoonotic origin [ ]. For example, the 2009 pandemic, the first of the twenty-first century, was caused by an H1N1 subtype of IAV, defined by the two antigenic proteins on the surface of the viral envelope, haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) [ ]. It is estimated that approximately 285 000 individuals worldwide died from the cardiopulmonary consequences (including myocardial infarction and myocarditis) of the H1N1 infection, 80% of whom were younger than 65 years of age [ , ]. Severe influenza illness is typically the result of virus-induced pneumonia or a secondary bacterial superinfection in the lower respiratory tract and may present as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) with a possible fatal outcome [ ].
At the cellular level, the outcome of a severe infection depends greatly on the control of the protein synthesis machinery of the host, the ribosomes. Both the cell and the virus require ribosomes; the cell needs them to initiate immune responses and maintain cellular functions, while the virus requires them for synthesising new viral proteins [ ]. IAV has developed numerous mechanisms to control the ribosomal landscape, primarily by suppressing cellular gene expression and mRNA translation (host shutoff), but also by hijacking key regulatory pathways [ – ]. To counteract this, the cell initiates a fundamental stress response that also has a broad impact on the ribosomal landscape. In this review, the term “ribosomal landscape” is used not only to describe the ribosome and its components, but also the ribosomal biogenesis, translation mechanisms, the cellular mRNA pool and important associated signalling pathways.
In recent years, the view of the ribosome has evolved from that of a static factory to that of a heterogeneous, specialised manufacturing facility that can be reprogrammed depending on requirements, a phenomenon known as “ribosomal heterogeneity” ( ) [ – ]. Furthermore, cancer research has demonstrated that the ribosomal landscape can serve as a pharmacological target [ , ]. Of note, viral infections and cancer largely overlap in mechanisms of ribosomal landscape reprogramming, immune response evasion and manipulation of cellular signalling pathways [ – ]. Regarding IAV, there is a clear necessity for the development of novel therapeutic modalities that are specifically designed to target maladaptive processes within the host organism [ ].
 This review focuses on how reprogramming the ribosomal landscape may influence immune response and IAV infection severity and whether components of this landscape could serve as therapeutic targets. To this end, we first summarise the current state of research on IAV and the ribosomal landscape, as well as the variables that can be influenced (mal)adaptively during infection, describing fundamental concepts, such as ribosomal heterogeneity and host shutoff. We then discuss clinical relevance and potential pharmacological targets, ranging from pro-viral ribosomal proteins and components of ribosomal biogenesis to the manipulation of associated signalling pathways, such as the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) and NF-κB pathways. Furthermore, we aim to highlight gaps in our knowledge, thereby paving the way for further research and encouraging scientists to delve deeper into fundamental ribosomal processes and learn from other research fields.

## Methodology
This review employs a narrative approach, recognising the absence of homogeneous data in the field, which precludes the possibility of conducting a meta-analysis. Furthermore, several reviews have been published focusing on the progression of IAV infections, the mechanisms of IAV-induced host shutoff, ribosomal heterogeneity and the initiation of translation. We focus on the role of the ribosomal machinery and the associated signalling pathways during IAV infection. A comprehensive literature search of English-language publications in peer-reviewed journals was conducted, employing the following search terms in PubMed and Google Scholar: (“influenza” OR “virus”) AND (“ribosome” OR “translation”), (“ribosomal heterogeneity”), (“cancer”) AND (“ribosome” or “translation”). To ensure the relevance and actuality of the findings, the search period extended from 1970 to 2025. No additional strict inclusion criteria were applied, but non-English articles were excluded. First, the abstracts of the best-matching publications were screened to identify false-positive search results. Then, the publications were individually reviewed for relevance, including analysis of references to identify additional works.

## The ribosomal landscape in health and disease

## The ribosome: structure, biogenesis and function

## Ribosomal biogenesis
Eukaryotic ribosomes are molecular cytosolic protein synthesis machines composed of a small 40S subunit for binding and scanning mRNA and a large 60S subunit for peptide bond formation. The mature 80S ribosome comprises over 5500 rRNA nucleotides and 80 ribosomal proteins (RPs) [ , ].
Ribosome biogenesis in the nucleolus is one of the most complex and energy consuming cellular processes [ , ]. It involves pre-rRNA synthesis, various endo- and exonucleolytic processing steps and subsequent assembly with RPs, requiring RNA polymerase I, II and III, over 200 auxiliary factors and 80 small nucleolar RNAs [ ]. After further maturation steps, the subunits are exported into the cytosol [ ]. RPs fulfil a variety of functions during the maturation of the subunits, contributing to rRNA synthesis and the nuclear export of pre-40S particles [ , ]. This complexity requires stringent regulation and coordination with cellular signalling pathways, including growth, cell death and ageing, making it a focus of cancer research in recent years [ – ]. For instance, it has been demonstrated that the ribosomal biogenesis factor is overexpressed in lung adenocarcinomas, and that its inhibition has been linked to a substantial reduction in tumour growth in both in vitro and in vivo models [ ]. Moreover, ribosomal biogenesis can also be targeted by viruses, as demonstrated by the example of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) protein Nsp1, which specifically restricts rRNA synthesis [ ].

## Ribosomal (nucleolar) stress
The nucleolus serves as a stress sensor that, when exposed to stressors such as DNA damage, stabilises the tumour suppressor and transcription factor p53 inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [ ]. Such a stressor can also be the impairment of ribosomal biogenesis per se at any stage, leading to increased levels of free RPs (e.g. RPL5, 11 and 23) that are now able to bind the antagonist of p53, murine double minute 2 (MDM2), leading to increased p53 activity [ ]. There is evidence that ribosomal stress contributes to various degenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Parkinson's disease [ , ]. In addition, targeted induction of nucleolar stress may suppress cancer cell growth and represents a potential therapeutic strategy [ ].

## Regulation of translation initiation

## Canonical translational pathways
Translation, the conversion of mRNA into protein-forming amino acid sequences, consists of four steps, including initiation, elongation, termination and ribosomal recycling. The initiation phase is where regulatory influences are primarily exerted and the velocity is determined [ , ]. Canonical initiation is eukaryotic translation initiation factor (eIF) 4E-dependent. Initially, the 43S pre-initiation complex (PIC) is formed by the association of the 40S ribosomal subunit and various initiation factors (eIF1, eIF1A, eIF3, eIF5) with the so-called ternary complex of eIF2/initiator methionyl-transfer RNA (tRNA)/guanosine triphosphate [ ]. The 43S PIC is then recruited to the capped, untranslated 5' end of the mRNA, mediated by the cap-bound factor eIF4E, which together with eIF4G and eIF4A forms the eIF4F complex, and by the interaction between eIF3 and eIF4G [ ]. Consequently, the new 48S complex initiates scanning along the mRNA and translation starts [ ].

## Regulating signalling pathways
Regulatory mechanisms help cells adapt translation, an energy-intensive process, to stressors such as viral infection. Central are eIF2α and the eIF4F complex. The phosphorylation of eIF2α is a key step in the integrated stress response (ISR), reducing global translation while increasing expression of protective transcription factors [ ]. The ISR is activated by four different eIF2α kinases depending on stressors [ ], namely haem-regulated inhibitor for haem deficiency, general control nondepressible 2 (GCN2) for amino acid deprivation, protein kinase R (PKR) for viral infection and PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) for endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [ – ]. GCN2 can also be viewed as a link between translation initiation and elongation because it is activated when ribosome stalling cannot be resolved [ , ]. ER stress, in turn, arises as a result of an increased number of unfolded proteins in the ER, leading to the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) [ ]. The UPR aims to restore ER function and involves inhibition of mRNA translation by phosphorylating eIF2α, as well as increasing the degradation of misfolded proteins [ , ]. If ER stress continues, apoptotic pathways may be initiated [ ]. Sustained UPR activation has been implicated in diverse pathological states, including sepsis-associated lung injury and pulmonary fibrosis [ , ].
The mTOR and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) (MEK) also regulate translation. mTOR induces translation following stimulation by growth factors within the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt signalling pathway via phosphorylation of the eIF4E-regulating protein 4E-BP [ , ]. Moreover, mTOR elicits analogous outcomes by activating the kinase S6K1, which then phosphorylates RPS6 [ ]. In response to stressors, the activation of mTOR is reduced and canonical translation initiation is inhibited [ ]. MEK, an MAPK, is a central component of the RAF (rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma)–MEK–ERK pathway. The activation of this pathway due to stress or proliferative stimuli results in the activation of MAPK-interacting kinases (MNKs) leading to the phosphorylation of eIF4E [ , ]. This, in turn, exerts a decisive influence on the translation of mRNA subsets encoding proteins that are particularly essential for cellular survival [ ].
For a more detailed description of translation initiation, we refer to previous reviews [ , , ].

## Noncanonical translational pathways
Alternative (noncanonical) pathways of translation include modifications of the mRNA and mRNA-binding proteins and variations of the translation machinery itself (ribosomal heterogeneity) [ , ]. This includes alternative translation factors, such as eIF3 that can interact directly with mRNAs and regulate their translation, both positively and negatively, depending on the presence of cis-elements in the transcripts [ ]. Another example of the use of the special secondary structure of RNA cis-elements are internal ribosomal entry sites (IRES), which catalyse the formation of a functional ribosome independent of 5′ cap binding. These were first described in viruses and allow easy access to the translation machinery, not only to increase viral protein synthesis but also to significantly impair cellular integrity [ ].

## Ribosomal heterogeneity and the specialised ribosome theory
The ribosome has long been disregarded as a potential regulator of translation due to its classification as a nonspecialised factory with a limited capacity for turnover. Although functional differences in the composition of bacterial ribosomes were first identified in the 1970s [ ], the “ribosomal filter hypothesis” was first proposed in 2002 by Mauro and Edelman [ ]. This hypothesis suggests that ribosomes control translation rates of distinct mRNAs based on their composition and interaction with extraribosomal factors, thereby acting as a filter for translation [ ]. The theory was further corroborated in subsequent years, leading to the recognition of ribosomal heterogeneity as a (patho)physiological concept [ , – ].
Ribosomal heterogeneity occurs at multiple levels: variation in RPs, rRNA, translation factors and tRNA [ , , ]. RPs can vary in stoichiometric composition, undergo post-translational modification or are replaced by paralogues [ , ]. Furthermore, the transcriptional regulation of RPs varies significantly across different tissues, including the lung [ – ]. Heterogeneous composition of RPs has also been found at the protein level in various organs in mice [ ]. Additionally, there is a notable diversity in RPs within actively translating ribosomes, which is accompanied by preferential translation of mRNAs that code for cell proliferation and survival proteins [ ]. Further evidence indicates the presence of distinct ribosomes within a cell, extending beyond those that are tissue-specific [ – ]. This gave rise to the theory of the specialised ribosome, which posits that ribosomes are capable of exerting preferential influence on translation through a range of mechanisms, including initiation, mRNA selectivity and speed, due to their specialised composition [ ]. In cancer research, the term “onco-ribosomes” has been proposed [ ]. This includes a general increase in the synthesis of oncogenic proteins, extraribosomal functions of RPs and ribosomal changes that promote genomic instability [ ]. For instance, in lung adenocarcinoma, RPL11 may be overexpressed and its knockdown inhibits cancer growth in vitro, in part by regulating autophagy [ ]. RPS6 and phosphorylated RPS6 have been observed to be overexpressed in nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cases, functioning as both a potential target for treatment and a negative predictor of survival [ ]. In contrast, RPL22, which is not essential for protein synthesis, is downregulated in NSCLC and thus may act as a tumour suppressor, presumably by controlling the growth-promoting casein kinase 2α [ , ]. Similarly to onco-ribosomes, the role of specialised ribosomes in viral infections merits consideration [ ]. In this context, RPs are typically ascribed proviral functions, whereby they are hijacked by viruses to facilitate the synthesis of viral proteins [ ]. The interaction of RPS19 and the N protein of the hantavirus can serve as an example [ ].
Other evidence links RPs to nontumourous lung diseases. Global RP downregulation in fibroblasts characterises early idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [ ] and inhibition of RPS3 protects against acute damage caused by cigarette smoking, likely through its impact on the immune-regulating transcription factor NF-κB [ ].
Despite the abundance of recent findings, the fundamental functions of numerous RPs remain ambiguous. In an important study, Luan
et al. [ ] dissected the consequences of the specific knockdown of various RPs on cellular functions, including cell cycle, metabolism and signal transduction. Their findings illustrate that the effects can vary significantly among the RPs. For example, while knockdown of RPS8 was associated with increased apoptosis, knockdown of RPL13 led to enhanced cell senescence [ ].
An example of rRNA variation is the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, which exerts a regulatory influence on rRNA synthesis and impacts ribosomal biogenesis [ , ]. With regard to tRNAs, tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs should be mentioned [ ]. These are fragments of tRNA produced under cellular stress that are able to inhibit translation by affecting eIF4 components [ ].
In summary, the ribosome is a dynamic, highly regulated structure adapting composition and function to environmental changes, including viral infections. This heterogeneity plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of malignant, infectious and fibrotic diseases, offering new opportunities for bench-to-bedside clinicians.

## Remodelling of the ribosomal landscape during IAV infection
Like all viruses, IAV is entirely dependent on the cellular protein synthesis apparatus. In the absence of functional ribosomes, the synthesis of viral proteins and the progression of infection would be impossible. Consequently, multiple interactions have been identified between IAV and the components of the transcription and translation machineries [ ], shaping a proviral ribosomal landscape ( ).
 

## How IAV hijacks host protein synthesis

## Host shutoff
As early as the 1970s and 1980s, IAV infection was linked to reduced protein synthesis and reduced cytosolic mRNA levels [ , ]. Various mechanisms contribute to this host shutoff, including suppression of RNA polymerase II, inhibition of RNA processing and degradation of host mRNA [ , – ]. In vitro, the viral endoribonuclease polymerase acidic X (PA-X), identified in 2012, is considered the major contributor to the depletion of the cellular mRNA pool [ , ]. However, shutoff is the consequence of an interplay among multiple viral proteins (e.g. PA-X, nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP)) and varies depending on viral strain, infection stage and host [ – , ]. Of note, expression of PA-X and NS1 is strain-dependent [ , – ]. Shutoff dampens antiviral immune responses while limiting immune cell recruitment and leads to reduced competition for viral mRNA, thereby facilitating viral replication and translation [ ]. Unlike many viruses, IAV replicates in the nucleus, utilising host-like mRNAs with 5′ caps and 3′ poly(A) tails, making them more stable and accessible to translation [ ]. The underlying process is called cap-snatching, where viral RdRP hijacks host RNA-Pol II transcripts for viral transcription, and was once thought to be the primary shutoff mechanism given that noncapped mRNAs are degraded by exonuclease enzymes [ ]. However, evidence indicates that cap-snatching primarily involves the utilisation of noncoding RNAs, which suggests that this may not be the primary mechanism responsible for the reduced gene expression [ ].
Khaperskyy and McCormick's [ ] model divides shutoff into two phases: the initial nuclear phase in which cap-snatching and NS1-mediated mRNA processing ensure the export and translation of viral mRNA with minimal competition, and the late cytoplasmic phase in which preferential access to the cell's translation machinery is ensured [ ].

## Noncanonical translational pathways during IAV infection
In light of these observations, it is evident that IAV does not simply switch off canonical translation, as is the case with many other viruses, such as the poliovirus and picornaviruses (e.g. rhinovirus, enterovirus) [ , ]. This would compromise its own replication, even though IAV is capable of translating its proteins in cells with impaired eIF4E-dependent translation [ ]. In fact, several mechanisms of selective, noncanonical viral translation have been identified. Li
et al. [ ] synthesised these into four models, as follows 1) NS1 interacts with the 5′-untranslated region (UTR) of viral mRNA to recruit poly(A)-binding protein 1 (PABP1) and eIF4GI [ , ]. 2) Viral polymerase PB2 (polymerase basic protein 2) replaces eIF4E function by directly interacting with eIF4GI [ , ]. In contrast to eIF4A and eIF4G, eIF4E may not be a requisite factor for viral propagation [ , ]. 3) NS1 binds eIF4GI and recruits the 43S complex to viral mRNA without interaction with the 5′-UTR [ ]. 4) PABP1 interacts with both the 3′ poly(A) tail and the 5′ UTR due to high adenosine concentrations and subsequently recruits eIF4G without NS1 [ ].
Additionally, selective translation can be initiated by non-translation factors that are reprogrammed by IAV. The antiviral protein, IFIT2 (interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 2) is repurposed during infection to enhance viral translation by binding directly to viral mRNA and preventing ribosomal pausing [ ]. Although IRES-mediated translation initiation is largely attributed to single-stranded RNA viruses, no such mechanism is known for IAV, possibly due to the negative polarity of the genome as well as the fact that IAV possesses 5′ caps, whereas IRES mediate cap-independent translation [ , ]. Furthermore, IAV selectively alters the tRNA population on translating ribosomes in favour of its codons, which are clearly different from those of the cell [ ].

## Cellular stress responses
An important cellular response is the induction of translational arrest via the ISR (see the Regulating signalling pathways section), which serves to restrict the synthesis of viral proteins and initiates cytoprotective pathways. It must be distinguished from the shutoff initiated by IAV.
During infection, the ISR is activated by the PKR-mediated phosphorylation of eIF2α, which results, among other things, in the accumulation of stress granules (SGs) [ – ]. SGs contain a number of key components, including the 40S subunit, initiation factors, mRNAs and RNA-binding proteins, consequently leading to inhibition of global translation [ ]. However, SGs also lead to the prioritisation of the translation of specific mRNAs, which has an overall antiviral effect [ ]. IAV inactivates PKR through NS1 and minimises the formation of SGs independently of PKR by NP and PA-X [ ]. Furthermore, IAV manipulates the UPR to its advantage. Viral glycoprotein production causes ER stress, primarily activating the IRE1 pathway of the UPR [ ]. This pathway bypasses the induction of the ISR via PERK and appears to promote viral propagation [ ]. Additionally, the shutoff functions of NS1 restrict an adequate UPR [ ], which promotes the degradation of the viral glycoprotein haemagglutinin [ ]. Moreover, persistent ER stress contributes to the destruction of epithelial barriers and predisposes to bacterial superinfections [ ].
Another fundamental cellular protective mechanism in response to stress is autophagy [ ], which plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by degrading cellular components in lysosomes and facilitating cell renewal [ ]. Disruption of autophagy results in cell death [ ]. Autophagy is generally essential for IAV propagation, as viral replication and translation are considerably restricted in autophagy-deficient cells [ ]. However, autophagy is also essential for the cell's antiviral defence [ ]. The initial inhibition of autolysosome formation by M2, which leads to an accumulation of autophagosomes, can be seen as decisive [ ]. The viral regulation of mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), the essential negative regulator of autophagy, is a highly dynamic process. During the early course of infection, mTORC1 is activated downstream of the PI3K–Akt pathway, which is upregulated by NS1, leading to reduced autophagy [ , ]. However, this activation may decline in the course of the infection, leading to increased autophagy and induction of apoptosis [ , , ]. Moreover, highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (H5N1) induces autophagy by inhibiting the mTOR signalling pathway, thereby contributing to the development of ARDS [ ]. Of note, severe lung injury can be prevented through pharmacological inhibition of autophagy in mice [ ].
Additionally, the phosphorylation of RPS6 by S6-kinases (RSKs), which are distal players of the mTOR signalling pathway, may play an important role in this setting [ ]. It is assumed that IAV attempts to stimulate the cap-dependent translation of viral proteins by this mechanism; however, the pathophysiological relevance of this remains to be elucidated. In contrast, the activation of RSKs can also be regarded as a physiological response to stress and might stimulate the antiviral immune response [ , ].

## Putative roles of ribosomal biogenesis and RPs

## Ribosomal biogenesis
The structure of the nucleolus, the site of ribosomal biogenesis, is significantly affected by IAV [ ], with quantitative proteomics revealing targeted alterations of nucleolar protein expression rather than global effects [ ]. Individual proteins regulating ribosomal biogenesis, such as RPL24 and UTP23, showed increased expression in a strain-dependent manner [ ]. These changes are probably targeted and promote viral replication [ ]. Interestingly, regulatory proteins of ribosome biogenesis (such as RRS1 and RRP1) were found enriched in insoluble perinuclear aggregates during infection, together with viral proteins and proteins associated with RNA metabolism and the ribosome [ ]. Those aggregates are produced as part of the IAV-induced UPR and appear to be essential for virion assembly [ ]. Central to the regulation of ribosomal biogenesis is mTORC1, controlling the expression and composition of ribosomal components [ ]. While the early activation of mTORC1 is crucial for viral propagation, supporting viral entry and delaying premature apoptosis, the specific effect on ribosomal biogenesis remains unclear [ ].
Ribosomal biogenesis is linked directly to cell cycle regulation via p53–MDM2. The interactions between IAV and p53 are crucial but complex, resulting in pro- or antiviral outcomes. In most cases, p53 is attributed antiviral activity, which is mediated by its stabilisation by NS1 [ , ]. However, NS1 can inhibit p53 transcriptional activity as well, with specific effects depending on the time-course of infection [ , ]. Of note, MDM2, despite its designation as a negative regulator of p53, has been demonstrated to possess antiviral properties, which it is presumed to execute independently of p53 [ ]. These observations can be interpreted as follows: IAV aims to delay apoptosis of the host cell during the early stages of infection to enable multiple replication cycles and the formation of numerous virions. In addition, NS1 triggers nucleolar stress by interacting with nucleolin, suppressing rRNA synthesis and impairing ribosomal biogenesis [ , ]. This is accompanied by increased binding of free RPs with MDM2, resulting in the accumulation of p53 [ ]. However, the precise extent to which ribosomal biogenesis is influenced remains uncertain. Furthermore, it has been shown that p53 is essential for the early type I interferon (IFN) response and that p53-negative mice develop more severe disease, indicating a central role for ribosomal stress induction [ , ]. Interestingly, induction of nucleolar stress through rRNA accumulation stimulates human cytomegalovirus replication by inhibiting viral double-stranded DNA sensor HMGB2 (high mobility group box 2) expression and consequently reducing IFN- β induction [ ], a mechanism that is unclear for IAV.
The rRNA-processing protein RRP1B positively regulates IAV mRNA transcription by interacting with RdRp in the nucleus and promoting its association with capped mRNA [ ]. In addition, the nuclear DEAD-box RNA helicase 5 (DDX5), which promotes rRNA synthesis and is associated with increased ribosomal biogenesis [ , ], supports the function of viral polymerases [ ] and interacts with NP to inhibit antiviral factors, including IFN-β, interleukin 6 and DHX58 [ , ]. Mechanistically, DDX5 promotes methylation of antiviral transcripts, leading to their subsequent nuclear export and increased degradation by YTHDF2, a regulatory protein recognising m6A-containing mRNA [ – ]. Here, a further association with tumourigenesis can be established, as DDX5 is also known to promote invasive growth and metastasis in various malignancies [ ]. illustrates how changes in ribosomal biogenesis may interfere with the course of IAV infection.
 

## RPs
Recent studies investigated whether RPs undergo alterations during IAV infection. It was observed that the entire mRNA cluster of RPs remained stable, suggesting this is a prerequisite for the virus to access functioning ribosomes [ ]. With regard to individual RPs, an elevated expression of RPS21 and RPL29 was described using quantitative proteomics [ ], whereas Khaperskyy
et al. [ ] demonstrated a downregulation of RPS18 and RPS6 mRNAs, likely attributable to PA-X. Other studies also suggest that IAV selectively affects the expression of individual RPs, depending on the cell type and subcellular localisation [ , ]. Multiple screenings have been conducted to identify crucial host factors that promote the progression of the infection. In a large-scale study, co-immunoprecipitation was employed to demonstrate that numerous RPs interact with viral proteins [ ]. In a subsequent RNAi screening, initial evidence for the influence of individual RPs (such as RPL26, RPL27A, RPS12 and RPS15) on the course of the infection was found. The study also establishes a role for RPL26 in the composition of viral particles during the late phase of infection [ ]. A previous RNAi screen in Drosophila indicated crucial roles for small subunit RPs in viral propagation [ ]. This is supported by the results of two groups which employed genome-wide RNAi screens in A549 cells [ , ]. Their findings demonstrate the significance of genes involved in cellular mRNA translation upon IAV infection, as suggested by gene ontology analyses, in comparison to other viruses [ , ]. The small ribosomal subunit was described as the third most crucial cellular complex necessary for infection [ ]. Notably, evidence was provided for specific host factor roles for several RPs, including RPL35, RPS10, RPS14, RPS16 and RPS27A [ ]. Consistent with these findings, it was demonstrated that small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown of RPS27A results in a significant reduction in viral load [ ]. Mechanistically, RPS27A was identified as a possible interacting partner for multiple ribosome-independent host factors that contribute to the assembly of viral ribonucleoproteins (vRNPs) into virions [ ]. A genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindrome repeats (CRISPR) screen identified RPL6 and RPLP0 as critically important for the viral life cycle [ ]. Recent work describes how the suppression of RPS16 inhibits infection, attributed to an increased expression of antiviral type I IFNs following RPS16 knockdown [ ]. In turn, the expression of RPS16 is negatively controlled by microRNA let-7, which is specifically targeted by IAV [ ]. A similar RP–IAV relationship was described in an RNAi-based study investigating factors that interact with NS1 and NS2, as knockdown of RPL13A resulted in reduced infection and increased type I IFN interferon levels [ ]. summarises the most important findings on specific RPs.
 Furthermore, IAV leads to relocalisation of RPs into autophagosomes together with viral mRNA, potentially converting them into alternative viral translation sites [ ]. These autophagosomes accumulate early in infection, as discussed above. In a separate study, it was shown that exosomes (small extracellular vesicles) secreted by IAV-infected cells contain upregulated RNA of RPs (including RPS6, RPS27 and RPL18) [ ]. This might express reprogrammed translation during infection or represent a way of intercellular communication [ ]. Thus, exosomes may function as mediators during viral infections [ ].
In summary, IAV affects the ribosomal biogenesis significantly, inducing ribosomal stress and manipulating the p53–MDM2 apoptosis axis. Furthermore, individual RPs may act as crucial host factors hijacked by IAV. Similar to onco-ribosomes, this viral reprogramming may lead to the formation of specialised IAV-shaped ribosomes promoting viral propagation ( ) and contributing to clinical deterioration.
 

## Clinical consequences of the reprogrammed ribosomal landscape during IAV infection

## Ribosome-driven regulation of immune responses and ARDS
The antiviral immune response is a double-edged sword, necessary to interrupt viral propagation, yet an excessive immune response in the form of a cytokine storm can contribute significantly to ARDS disease states and mortality [ – ]. From the standpoint of the specialised ribosome theory, ribosomes act as filters influencing the balance between pro- and antiviral responses, as well as between an adequate and an excessive immune response [ ]. The following is an overview of the factors that may be relevant in this context.
Viral RNA in the cytosol activates signalling pathways that initiate the innate antiviral immune response. These include RIG-I (retinoic acid-inducible gene i), a cytosolic pattern recognition receptor and RNA helicase, as well as Toll-like receptor proteins and the inflammasome [ , ], resulting in the induction and secretion of type I and type III IFNs and the expression of hundreds of IFN-stimulated genes, which also switch neighbouring cells to an antiviral state [ ]. Moreover, proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including tumour necrosis factor, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 and interleukin 6, are released [ , ]. However, in addition to combating the virus, these factors contribute to injury of the epithelial–endothelial barrier and further immune cell recruitment [ , ]. There is evidence that some RPs and impaired ribosomal biogenesis (ribosomal stress) influence the course of the antiviral immune response in general [ , ]. RPL13A is able to restrict the pro-inflammatory IFN-γ (type II) response as part of a negative feedback mechanism [ ]. IFN-γ is a crucial mediator of a pathological inflammatory immune response in ARDS pathogenesis, as the absence of the IFN-γ receptor has been linked to reduced virus titres and milder symptoms in murine models [ , ]. Although the mechanisms are unknown, RPL13A was identified as a crucial host factor regulating the type I IFN response during IAV infection [ , ]. Moreover, numerous RPs (e.g. RPS3, RPS27, RPL8 and RPL13) activate NF-κB, illustrating an extraribosomal function of RPs [ ]. During IAV infection, NF-κB is activated and exemplifies the ambiguity of many signalling pathways [ ]. While it acts antivirally by stimulating immune response, it also appears to be essential for viral replication by promoting vRNP synthesis in the early phase and later contributing to the opening of nuclear pores via the activation of proapoptotic factors (tumour necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) and Fas ligand (FasL)) and caspases, thereby promoting nuclear export of newly synthesised vRNPs ( ) [ – ]. NS1 specifically targets NF-κB, inhibiting type III IFN expression [ ]. Furthermore, the small subunit protein RPSA is capable of recognising IAV RNA, subsequently initiating a proinflammatory immune response [ ]. RPSA influences access to proinflammatory genes epigenetically by increasing NF-κB chromatin accessibility to target gene promoters [ ]. However, this appears proviral as siRNA-mediated knockdown of RPSA significantly reduces infection [ ]. Of note, RPS19 functions as an endogenous blocker of macrophage migration and an inhibitor of NF-κB in a glomerulonephritis model [ ], and administration of recombinant RPS19 in mice prevents severe renal dysfunction by acting as an anti-inflammatory agent [ ].
ARDS is characterised by a severe impairment of gas exchange leading to hypoxaemia and in some cases to hypercapnia (elevated CO2 levels in blood and tissue) requiring respiratory support [ ]. While hypoxia is typically compensated rapidly in patients receiving mechanical ventilation, hypercapnia is often further aggravated due to protective low-tidal-volume ventilation strategies required to prevent further lung damage [ , ]. Of note, hypercapnia can significantly impact the ribosomal landscape and immune response and is associated with increased mortality [ – ]. In alveolar macrophages, elevated CO2 partial pressure leads to increased viral replication and suppressed antiviral immune response, presumably mediated by activation of the protein kinase Akt, as these effects were counteracted by Akt1 inhibition [ ]. RPS3, an Akt substrate, is a positive apoptosis regulator inactivated by phosphorylation [ ]. It specifically binds mRNA at the ribosome entry channel, regulating IAV translation in several ways [ ]. In addition, hypercapnia in skeletal muscle cells reduces protein synthesis through impaired ribosomal biogenesis mediated by activation of the stress sensor AMP-activated protein kinase α2 (AMPKα2) [ ]. As IAV infection activates AMPKα1, it remains unclear to what extent these results can be extrapolated to alveolar epithelial cells [ ]. Furthermore, AMPKα mediates endocytosis of the Na-K-ATPase during both hypercapnia and IAV infection, contributing significantly to impaired pulmonary oedema clearance [ , ]. Moreover, the influence of AMPKα on rRNA synthesis and ribosomal biogenesis is widely acknowledged [ ]. Cellular hypoxia also activates AMPK, triggering adaptation mechanisms that ensure cellular survival [ ]. This involves inhibition of the mTOR signalling pathway, restricting cap-dependent translation [ ]. As IAV typically activates mTOR, the overall effects on protein synthesis during ARDS may be complex. In addition, AMPK activation reduces the proinflammatory response by regulating NF-κB and reducing neutrophil activation, which may have a positive effect on ARDS severity [ ]. As local hypoxia and hypercapnia are cancer hallmarks [ , ], this represents another common ground with severe IAV infection.
In summary, numerous RPs like RPSA initiate an antiviral immune response, particularly through the NF-κB pathway. As excessive NF-κB activation promotes a hyperinflammatory state and is associated with development of ARDS [ , ], RPs influencing this pathway may contribute to ARDS pathogenesis. Conversely, certain RPs like RPS16 and RPL13A can be hijacked by IAV to dampen the type I interferon response, highlighting those as potential therapeutic targets.

## Targeting the ribosomal landscape pharmacologically
Although ribosomes are essential components of cellular functionality, cancer research has shown that individual components and associated signalling pathways can be targeted pharmacologically without compromising cellular integrity [ , , ]. For example, a direct inhibitor of eIF4A, zotatifin, is currently being investigated in clinical trials for solid tumours and for pancreatic adenocarcinoma [ ]. Inhibition of eIF4A by rocaglates like silvestrol and zotatifin demonstrates promising antiviral effects against SARS-CoV-2 in vitro [ ]. Furthermore, a phase 1B study testing the safety of zotatifin has been initiated (NCT04632381). Similarly, the inhibition of the eIF4E-eIF4G interaction by the small molecule 4EGI-1 has both anticarcinogenic and antiviral effects [ ], including inhibiting melanoma and breast cancer growth, as well as picornavirus replication [ , ]. Though eIF4A and eIF4G are considered essential for IAV mRNA translation, no studies have investigated the effect of rocaglates or 4EGI-1 during IAV infections.
Another approach to pharmacological intervention at the ribosome level is to modulate upstream signalling by inhibiting mTOR or MNKs [ ]. However, inhibiting mTOR presents significant challenges, given its crucial role in the initiation of antiviral immune responses while also exhibiting proviral activity in certain circumstances [ ]. Regarding IAV infections, the administration of the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus in infected mice resulted in adverse effects, including weight loss and increased lung inflammation [ ]. Nonetheless, treatment with sirolimus in addition to corticosteroids improved certain outcome parameters, including hypoxia, organ dysfunction, and duration of mechanical ventilation, in a small group of severely ill mechanically ventilated patients with H1N1 influenza [ ]. This implies a possible effect in severe immune dysregulation, indicating that the timing of therapy is crucial. In addition, the MEK–MNKs cascade is an example of how analogies between viral infections and cancer can be successfully exploited pharmacologically [ ]. This cascade represents a foundational signalling pathway for cell differentiation and cell growth. As IAV hijacks such cascades to facilitate its own replication, promising results with MEK inhibitors in IAV infections in vitro will now be followed by clinical trials [ , ]. A phase 2 study has already provided proof-of-concept for the benefit of the MEK inhibitor zapnometinib in COVID-19 [ ].
Modulating ribosomal biogenesis has demonstrated initial success. Its inhibition through the use of selective RNA polymerase I inhibitors like CX-5461 or BMH-21, exerts anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, and anti-carcinogenic effects [ , , , ]. In viral infections, these effects were investigated during human cytomegalovirus infection, where CX-5461 impaired the synthesis of viral DNA and acted synergistically with known antiviral agents [ ]. The antibiotic toyocamycin inhibits the maturation of the 40S subunit by binding to the atypical protein kinase Rio1. It also exhibits antiviral properties, although this has not yet been studied in the context of IAV [ ]. Using classic chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin or cisplatin in modified doses to attack the nucleolus has also shown antiviral effects [ ].
The pathophysiological relevance of RPs is particularly evident in the group of ribosomopathies like Diamond-Blackfan anaemia, a genetic disease caused by mutations in various RPs, including RPS19 [ ]. Malfunctioning RPs result in nucleolar stress and p53 activation in a tissue-specific manner, as well as a reduction in functional ribosomes [ ]. In severe cases, this leads to bone marrow insufficiency [ ]. Preclinical research, particularly in RP-based gene therapy, indicates advancements in this area, coinciding with new insights into viral infections [ ]. The regulation of NF-κB by RPs such as RPSA or RPS3 may be particularly important, since the pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB by a soluble, inhalable derivative of acetylsalicylic acid (D,L-lysine-acetylsalicylate·glycine (LASAG)) has already shown initial success in vitro and in vivo [ ]. In a phase 2 study, faster resolution of symptoms of severe influenza infection was observed after LASAG inhalation [ ].
Theoretically, specific inhibitors could be developed for almost every ribosome-associated factor. Several translation initiation factors are currently undergoing preclinical testing against various carcinomas [ ]. However, potential systemic cytotoxicity with off-target effects must be addressed and may best be avoided by local administration with the highest specificity possible [ ]. For respiratory infections, local administration of potential compounds is crucial. This includes the local application of siRNA against essential RPs, such as RPL13A or RPS27A, and seems technically possible, given that siRNA packaged in lipid nanoparticles has been successfully administered by inhalation to treat bacterial pneumonia in mice, for example [ ]. Using selective drugs, such as small-molecule inhibitors of ribosomal biogenesis or RPs and their interaction with viral proteins, could be highly effective in treating viral infections and complications such as ARDS and corresponding immune dysregulation. In severe infection, the strength of these therapeutic interventions may become apparent, circumventing the adverse effects of nonspecific medications such as inhibitors of mTOR or NF-κB. Such host-directed strategies may prove especially valuable against antiviral resistance or emerging pandemic strains.

## Future directions

## Methodological challenges
Although compelling evidence exists for the critical role of ribosome components during IAV infection, currently, the lack of more complex functional studies precludes any definitive conclusions about the biological significance of individual RPs. Moreover, the results of the numerous screening studies that have been conducted diverge significantly in certain instances. One potential explanation is the use of different viral strains and disparate outcome parameters in the various studies, including reporter, plaque and cell viability assays, as well as phenotypic readouts, such as haemagglutinin integration into the cell membrane. As the results regarding RPs are heterogeneous, one should consider that RPs also represent functional groups among each other and that the knockdown of individual RPs can lead to the knockdown of others [ , ]. Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate whether such functional clusters play a role with respect to the specialised ribosome during IAV infection.
Applying novel methodologies developed in recent years for the study of the translatome, such as multiplexed enhanced protein dynamics, may offer greater insight [ ]. The combination of tandem mass tags and pulsed stable isotope labelling in cell culture allows near real-time study of changes in the translatome, particularly during cellular stress such as viral infections [ , ]. Indeed, the use of these methods has already expanded our knowledge regarding the role of the translatome during SARS-CoV-2 infection, emphasising the significance of the cellular translation apparatus [ ]. This led to the observation that using translation inhibitors, such as emetine, which inhibits RPS14, restricts the propagation of SARS-CoV-2 in vitro [ ].
Given the methodological discrepancies among the aforementioned studies, it may be advantageous to use human primary alveolar epithelial cells, which are currently undergoing rigorous characterisation for experimental applications [ , ]. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to examine the impact of RPs in more complex systems, such as precision-cut lung slices [ , ]. These have previously demonstrated utility in investigations into the pathophysiology of IAV infections [ ].

## Future research
Future research should pursue a two-pronged approach. First, to specifically target relevant elements of the IAV-shaped ribosomal landscape, it is key to further characterise the mechanisms of dynamic alterations of its composition and function upon viral infections. Second, it is essential to monitor other research areas that are assessing the practical applicability of pharmacological agents that modulate the ribosomal landscape in relation to potential side-effects. These two approaches may ultimately help to improve the outcomes of patients with severe influenza infections.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our knowledge of the ribosomal landscape in the context of IAV infection has exploded in the last few years ( ). Looking back on this progress, it is clear that understanding how the ribosomal landscape contributes to the severity of IAV infection is not just an academic exercise. It has direct implications for identifying new therapeutic approaches. However, as detailed above, important aspects remain incompletely understood. In light of annual influenza epidemics and the lack of targeted therapies, further research is warranted. As dynamic alterations to the ribosomal landscape during IAV infection may actively drive key features of severe disease, including immune dysregulation and ARDS, it is important to characterise this landscape further and validate findings in complex models that more closely resemble the human organism. Bridging the gap between bench and bedside will require interdisciplinary efforts, including studies in patient-derived cells and translational models of ARDS. Yet the potential payoff is substantial; by looking beyond the virus and focusing on the host, we may uncover new tools to prevent or treat severe IAV and its complications. This is particularly relevant since experience from cancer research demonstrates that it is feasible to pharmacologically target components of the ribosomal landscape.
 

## Questions for future research
What are the relevant changes in the ribosomal landscape during the different stages of severe IAV infection? Which specific components of the specialised ribosome, such as RPs, play a crucial role in the progression of the infection?
What role do maladaptive ribosomal adjustments play in the development of a hyperinflammatory state (cytokine storm) during the course of the infection?
To what extent is it possible to target the ribosomal landscape using pharmaceutical methods? Can compounds be repurposed from cancer research or research on other respiratory viruses, such as SARS-CoV-2?
