## Abstract

## BACKGROUND:
DRP1 (dynamin-related protein 1) mediates mitochondrial fission and permits rapid cell cycle progression in hyperproliferative cells by coordinating nuclear and mitochondrial division, a process called mitotic fission. However, DRP1 alone appears insufficient to complete fission, and the link between fission and cell cycle progression is unknown. We hypothesize that DNM2 (dynamin 2) interacts with DRP1 to complete mitochondrial fission and regulate cell cycle progression. We show that DNM2 is upregulated in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) in human and rodent pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), contributing to disease pathophysiology.

## METHODS:
Mitochondrial morphology, protein colocalization, and fission were assessed using stimulated emission depletion microscopy, protein interactions by immunoprecipitation, and transcriptomics by RNA sequencing. DNM2 was quantified in PASMC and lungs from patients with PAH and rats with pulmonary hypertension (PH), induced by monocrotaline or sugen5416/hypoxia. siDNM2’s effects on cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis were assessed by flow cytometry. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on publicly available data sets. siDNM2 was nebulized to monocrotaline- and sugen5416/hypoxia-PH rats, and disease regression was quantified by cardiac catheterization and histology.

## RESULTS:
DNM2 is increased in PAH PASMC. DNM2 interacts with DRP1 via its GTPase domain, permitting mitochondrial translocation and promoting fission. siDNM2 inhibits fission and cell proliferation and increases apoptosis. siDNM2 causes G1/G0 blockade by downregulating RGCC (regulator of cell cycle) with downstream effects on CDK (cyclin-dependent kinase) 4, cyclin D1, and p27kip1. Conversely, augmenting DNM2 in normal PASMC induces fission and accelerates proliferation. Upregulation of DNM2 in PAH is due to decreased miR-124-3p (microRNA-124-3p) and activation of STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3). An miR-124-3p-STAT3-DNM2-DRP1-RGCC pathway accelerates mitotic fission and is upregulated in PASMC, airway epithelium, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages in PAH. Nebulized siDNM2 regresses established PH in vivo in rats of both sexes.

## CONCLUSIONS:
DNM2 is a mediator in the terminal steps of DRP1-dependent fission and constitutes a novel therapeutic target in PAH.

Keywords: endoplasmic reticulum; mitochondrial dynamics; myocytes, smooth muscle; phosphorylation; STAT3 transcription factor

## Novelty and Significance

## What Is Known?
Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) features pathologic pulmonary vascular remodeling that is characterized, in part, by hyperproliferative, apoptosis-resistant, pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) that exhibit a pseudoneoplastic phenotype associated with enhanced mitochondrial fission.
This increased mitochondrial fission is mediated by DRP1 (dynamin-related protein 1), which alone cannot complete mitochondrial fission, suggesting that there is a missing effector of mitochondrial fission in PAH PASMCs.

## What New Information Does This Article Contribute?
This study addresses a key gap in the fundamental understanding of the terminal steps of mitochondrial fission, revealing that DRP1 recruits DNM2 (dynamin 2), a large GTPase, to the mitochondria, where together the 2 proteins mediate the terminal steps of mitochondrial fission.
In PAH, DNM2 is epigenetically and transcriptionally upregulated in PASMC and other lung cells, promoting excessive mitotic fission leading to pulmonary vasculopathy through regulation of the cell cycle protein, RGCC (regulator of cell cycle).
DNM2 is a potential therapeutic target in PAH, and siDNM2 is effective in regressing pulmonary hypertension in 2 rodent models of experimental pulmonary hypertension.
PAH is a progressive cardiopulmonary disease marked by pulmonary vascular obstruction caused in part by hyperproliferative, apoptosis-resistant, PASMCs. A key feature of this abnormal behavior is increased mitochondrial fission, which is largely controlled by the protein DRP1. However, to complete mitochondrial fission, DRP1 requires assistance from the GTPase DNM2. In this study, we show that DRP1 recruits DNM2 to the mitochondria, where together they execute the terminal stage of mitochondrial fission. We further show that DNM2 is epigenetically and transcriptionally upregulated in PASMC and other lung cells in PAH. This increase in DNM2 promotes mitotic fission and supports cell proliferation via regulating the expression of the cell cycle regulator, RGCC. Silencing DNM2 regresses pulmonary hypertension in 2 rodent models, highlighting DNM2 as a potential therapeutic target.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a lethal syndrome characterized by pulmonary vascular obstruction and stiffening, partially due to a cancer-like hyperproliferation of apoptosis-resistant pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). This pseudoneoplastic phenotype is driven, in part, by acquired mitochondrial disorders that promote cell proliferation and impair apoptosis in PAH PASMC, including (1) increased uncoupled glycolysis; (2) disrupted mitochondrial oxygen sensing; (3) impaired mitochondrial calcium uptake, resulting in reduced glucose oxidation; and (4) an imbalance between fission and fusion, favoring fission. This shift in the fission/fusion ratio regulates cell cycle progression, apoptosis, metabolism, and mitophagy. Approved PAH-targeted therapies are primarily vasodilators, and despite offering symptomatic benefits, functional impairment persists, and death from right ventricular failure occurs in 40% of patients within 5 years of diagnosis. This highlights a need to explore therapeutic targets in other pathways involved in the pathogenesis of PAH, such as PAH’s mitochondria-mediated, pseudoneoplastic phenotype.
In rapidly dividing cells, there is an obligatory, but incompletely understood, coordination between mitosis and mitochondrial division. This mitotic fission requires activation of DRP1 (dynamin-related protein 1), a large GTPase, and ensures equitable distribution of mitochondria between daughter cells. If mitotic fission is inhibited, whether by inhibiting fission or enhancing fusion, cell cycle arrest and apoptosis occur. Coordination of fission and mitosis is thought to be mediated, in part, by a shared dependence of both processes on similar kinases, including cyclin B-CDK (cyclin-dependent kinase) 1, which activates DRP1 by phosphorylation at serine 616, and also triggers mitosis. Upon posttranslational activation, DRP1 translocates from the cytosol to the outer mitochondrial membrane at a site of narrowing created by contact with the endoplasmic reticulum and actin. There, DRP1 interacts with its binding partners (MiD49 [mitochondrial dynamics protein of 49 kDa], MiD51 [mitochondrial dynamics protein of 51 kDa], MFF [mitochondrial fission factor], and Fis1 [fission protein 1]) and multimerizes, forming a macromolecular fission apparatus that further narrows the mitochondria. , 
In PAH, a mitochondrial-mediated proliferation/apoptosis imbalance, favoring proliferation, occurs in PASMC, endothelial cells (ECs), and fibroblasts. Mitochondrial fission is increased in PAH, partly due to upregulation and activation of DRP1. An imbalance between fission and fusion, caused by pathological DRP1 activation and downregulation of mitofusin-2, favors mitochondrial fission in PAH and cancer cells. These acquired mitochondrial abnormalities are permissive of pathological rates of cell proliferation and prevent apoptotic removal of abnormal cells. Mitochondrial abnormalities in PAH result from both activation of transcription factors (HIF-1⍺ [hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha], NFAT [nuclear factor of activated T cells], and STAT3 [signal transducer and activator of transcription 3] ) and epigenetic mechanisms (dysregulation of DNA methyltransferases and microRNAs ). We have successfully targeted increased mitotic fission to regress PAH and cancer, , using small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting DRP1, small molecule inhibitors of DRP1 GTPase (eg, mdivi-1 [mitochondrial division inhibitor 1] and Drpitor1a [dynamin-related protein 1 inhibitor 1a], a novel DRP1 inhibitor ), or downregulating DRP1’s binding partners, MiD49 and MiD51. 
However, the diameter of a fission apparatus comprised solely of DRP1 and its known binding partners appears insufficient to complete fission, , and the molecular mechanisms linking fission to cell cycle progression are incompletely understood. This study explores a putative, terminal step in DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission, which we propose is mediated by the large GTPase, DNM2 (dynamin 2). We further examine the role of DNM2 in mitochondrial dynamics and cell cycle regulation in normal human PASMC, describe the causes and consequences of DNM2 upregulation in PAH (in patients and multiple rodent models), and exploit DNM2 as a therapeutic target to regress PAH in 2 rodent PAH models.

Immunofluorescence staining of the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded rat lung tissues was performed as described. See the Supplemental Material.

## Western Blotting
See the Supplemental Material.

## Immunoprecipitation
Immunoprecipitation was performed as described in the Supplemental Material.

## siRNA and Plasmid Transfection
PAH PASMCs were grown to 60% to 80% confluence and then transfected with 25 picomoles of siRNA, as described. Knockdown efficiency was assessed after 48 hours using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) and immunoblotting. For plasmid overexpression, the cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of the specified plasmid, as described ; also, see the Supplemental Material.

## Cell Cycle Analysis
The DNA content of PAH PASMCs was analyzed with propidium iodide 72 hours post-transfection using flow cytometry (Sony SH800S, San Jose, CA). See the Supplemental Material.

## Cell Proliferation Assay
Cell proliferation was assessed 72 hours following transfection with either siRNA or plasmids, using the Click-iT EdU kit following manufacturer’s instructions (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) or by xCELLigence cell proliferation assay (300600910, Agilent Technologies, Mississauga, ON, Canada) as previously described.

Cell migration assay was performed by using the xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis DP system (Agilent Technologies) and Real-Time Cell Analysis CIM Plates (5665817001). See the Supplemental Material.

Apoptosis was determined in PAH PASMC 72 hours following transfection by the Annexin V+/propidium iodide method, using flow cytometry ; see the Supplemental Material.

## Whole Cell Micropolarimetry
Micropolarimetry of PAH PASMC was performed using a mito stress test assay, using a Seahorse XFe24 (Seahorse Biosciences, North Billerica, MA) after 48 hours of transfection with either ctrl-siRNA or siDNM2 (Supplemental Material).

## Confocal and Stimulated Emission Depletion Super-Resolution Microscopy of Live Cells
Live cells were transfected with specified plasmids, siRNAs, or microRNAs and imaged with a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), as described. For details, see the Supplemental Material.

## Confocal and Stimulated Emission Depletion Imaging of Fixed Cells
For detailed methods, see the Supplemental Material.

## Image Analysis
Image analysis was performed using ImageJ and Leica Application Suite X software. The Pearson Correlation Coefficient was quantified using ImageJ and Leica Application Suite X software.

## Quantification of Mitochondrial Morphology and Networking
Mitochondrial fission was quantified by 3 techniques: (1) measuring mitochondrial fragmentation count (MFC), with reduced mitochondrial fragmentation count indicating a more fused mitochondrial network , ; (2) a machine learning algorithm, which is trained to independently classify mitochondria as punctate, intermediate, or filamentous , ; and (3) mitochondrial networking analyses, in which cells are transfected with a mito-PA-GFP (mitochondrial-targeted, photoactivatable, green fluorescent protein). The diffusion of GFP (green fluorescent protein) is tracked along the mitochondrial lumen from the point of photoactivation, a metric called the MNF (mitochondrial networking factor), which increases in proportion to fusion, as described. , 

## miR-124-3p and STAT3 Binding Luciferase Reporter Assays
The binding of miR-124a-3p (microRNA-124-3p) to the 3′ untranslated region (3’-UTR) of DNM2’s mRNA was validated using a luciferase binding assay, as described. , The binding of STAT3 to DNM2’s promoter region was validated using a promoter binding assay (Supplemental Material).

## Transcriptomic Analyses
Single-cell RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data from lungs of patients with PAH (n=5; Gene Expression Omnibus [GEO] ID: GSE296823) and healthy controls (n=12; GEO ID: GSE227136), generated by Natri et al, were obtained via the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GEO Archive. Libraries were constructed using the Lexogen 3′ kit (Lexogen, Austria), quantified (Qubit 4; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), pooled equimolarly, and sequenced (75-cycle single end) on an Illumina NextSeq550. Reads were processed using standard pipelines, including quality control (FastQC and MultiQC), adapter and quality trimming (BBDuk), alignment (STAR), and read quantification (HTSeq). – Differential expression and Gene Ontology Biological Process and enrichment were performed using DESeq2 and gprofiler2.

## Monocrotaline-PH Rat Model
PAH was induced in adult, 200 to 250 g, male and female, Sprague-Dawley rats (Charles River, NY) by a single subcutaneous injection of monocrotaline (60 mg/kg), as described. PBS was used as a control vehicle.

## Su/Hx-PH Rat Model
Pulmonary hypertension (PH) was induced in male and female Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g) by Sugen5416 (20 mg/kg, SC) followed by 3 weeks of hypoxia (10% O2) and 3 weeks of normoxia, as described previously. PBS served as a control (Supplemental Material).

## Sugen5416/Hypoxia Mouse Model
Mice from both sexes were placed in a normobaric chamber with an incremental decrease of oxygen levels to 10% over 3 days. They were maintained for 3 weeks at 10% FiO2 without returning to normoxia. Animals were given sugen5416 (20 mg/kg, SC) on days 0, 7, and 14 of hypoxia. Echocardiography was performed on day 21 with right heart catheterization and tissue collection on day 22. The development of PH was confirmed (Figure S1B through S1E).

## In Vivo siRNA Treatment
The siRNA used for in vivo treatment contained 2′-O-methyl modifications to increase stability (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA). For each nebulization, 1 nanomole of siDNM2 or ctrl-siRNA in 200-μL saline was administered to anesthetized rats using an aerosol nebulizer to achieve lung-specific delivery (Kent Scientific cat neb-1200).

## Monocrotaline-PH Rat Model
siDNM2 or ctrl-siRNA for the monocrotaline-PH was administered beginning on day 11 and was repeated on days 14, 17, and 20 post-monocrotaline injection. The siRNA treatment was initiated after PH had been confirmed using Doppler echocardiography.

## Su/Hx-PH Rat Model
siDNM2 or ctrl-siRNA for the Su/Hx-PH rats started on day 29 and was repeated on days 32, 35, and 38 post-sugen5416 injection. Development of PH was confirmed on day 28, using echocardiography.

## Echocardiography
Echocardiography was performed at day 10 post-monocrotaline and day 28 post-Su/Hx using a Vevo 2100 system, as described. Pulmonary artery acceleration time was measured and used for randomization (Supplemental Material).

## Pulmonary Hemodynamics
Pulmonary hemodynamics were assessed by right heart catheterization on day 24 post-monocrotaline injection and day 42 post-sugen5416 injection via a closed-chest approach. Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and cardiac output were determined as described. Subsequently, left-ventricular end-diastolic pressure was measured via a closed-chest, retrograde catheterization via the right carotid artery. Cardiac index (CI), mean pulmonary arterial pressure, and pulmonary vascular resistance index (PVRI) were calculated (Supplemental Material).

## Statistical Analysis
Data are presented as the mean ±SEM. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For 2-group (unpaired or paired) comparisons, a Student t test, a Wilcoxon Rank-sum test, or a Mann-Whitney U test was used, depending on the results of the normality testing and sample size. For sample sizes ≥6, parametric testing was used. For paired analyses with small sample sizes, we acknowledge that the Wilcoxon signed-rank test yields a minimum possible 2-sided P value of 0.25 (n=3/group), limiting statistical resolution. Similarly, for unpaired analyses with very small sample sizes, the Mann-Whitney U test also has limited resolution, with a minimum attainable 2-sided P value of 0.10 (n=3/group). Accordingly, these analyses should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and statistical inference is constrained by sample size. For analyses of groups with n=6 to 10 with normally distributed data, we acknowledge that violations of the assumptions of the test may not have been detected due to low statistical power. One-way ANOVA with the Šídák, Dunnett, or Tukey multiple comparisons tests was used, as appropriate. For experiments involving 2 independent variables, 2-way ANOVA followed by the Dunnett multiple comparisons test was applied when comparing multiple groups to a single control. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (San Diego) and R (version 4.4.0). The RNA-seq analysis was conducted with the DESeq2 package (version 1.44) in R (version 4.4.0), which uses a negative binomial, generalized linear model for differential expression analysis of RNA-seq count data (Supplemental Material).
No experiment-wide multiple test correction was applied in this study. In the in vivo studies for comparisons among 3 groups, 1-way ANOVA was used. The Dunnett multiple comparisons test was applied for all experiments in which treatment groups were compared with a single control group. All tests were 2-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Representative images were selected to best reflect the response observed across multiple independent experiments supported by the quantitative analyses.

## Results

## DNM2 Expression in PASMC Is Increased in Human and Rodent PAH
Increased DNM2 expression occurred in PASMC from patients with PAH versus control subjects (Figure A and B). DNM2 is also increased in the media of small pulmonary arteries (PAs) and macrophages in patients with PAH, in the small PAs of rats with monocrotaline-PH or Su/Hx-PH, and in PAs of mice with Su/Hx-PH (Figure C through F; Figure S1A through S1E). DNM1 (dynamin 1) and DNM3 (dynamin 3) were not upregulated in human PAH PASMC (Figure S2A and S2B).
 

## Silencing DNM2 Inhibits Mitochondrial Fission in PAH PASMC, While DNM2 Overexpression Causes Fission in Normal PASMC
siDNM2 reduced DNM2 mRNA and protein expression by 84% and 71%, respectively (Figure S3A and S3B). siDNM2 in PAH PASMC inhibits mitochondrial fission, reducing mitochondrial fragmentation count and increasing filamentous mitochondria, as detected by a machine learning algorithm (Figure A). siDNM2 knockdown also significantly increased MNF, increasing diffusion of mito-PA-GFP throughout the mitochondrial matrix (Figure S4).
 Conversely, overexpressing DNM2 in normal PASMC increased mitochondrial fission, recapitulating a PAH phenotype (Figure B). Similar to human PAH PASMC, mitochondria in rat monocrotaline-PH PASMC and Su/Hx-PH PASMC (studied within passage 1-2) exhibit increased mitochondrial fission. Likewise, siDNM2 inhibited mitochondrial fission and increased fusion in rat monocrotaline-PH PASMC and Su/Hx-PH PASMC (Figure C and D). Silencing DNM2 did not affect mitochondrial morphology in normal human or rat PASMC (Figure S5A and S5B).
siDRP1 does not alter the expression of DNM2 and vice versa (Figure S6A), indicating that the effects of siDNM2 are not indirectly mediated by DRP1 suppression. In addition, knocking out DRP1 did not alter DNM2 expression in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (Figure S6B). Furthermore, silencing DNM2 in DRP1-silenced PAH PASMC did not inhibit mitochondrial fission or cell proliferation more than when DNM2 or DRP1 was individually silenced (Figure S6C and S6D). This indicates that DNM2’s profission and proproliferative effects are DRP1-dependent. Silencing DNM2 in PAH PASMC did not alter the expression of other fission and fusion mediators, indicating that siDNM2’s effect on PASMC is due to inhibition of DNM2-mediated mitochondrial fission, rather than off-target effects (Figure S7A through S7D). Furthermore, the mitochondria of PAH PASMC displayed an increased invagination at evolving mitochondrial fission sites, as evidenced by a decrease in length and residual thickness of these mitochondrial constriction sites (Figure E; Figure S7E). Further demonstrating DNM2’s role in creating short and tight areas of constriction and silencing DNM2 in PAH PASMC produced significant elongation of mitochondrial constriction sites (Figure F).

## DNM2-Induced Mitochondrial Fission Is DRP1-Dependent and Requires an Intact DNM2 GTPase Domain
Stimulated emission depletion (STED) imaging shows increased DRP1-DNM2 colocalization on the mitochondria in PAH PASMC (Figure A-i), evident as a reduced DRP1-DNM2 intermolecular distance (Figure A-ii). Similarly, live-cell STED also revealed interaction between DRP1 and DNM2, evident from the close molecular proximity of these proteins (Figure S8). Increased DRP1-DNM2 colocalization at mitochondrial constriction sites is mainly associated with elevated DNM2, rather than DRP1, expression (Figure A-iii). The proximity of DNM2 and DRP1 on the mitochondrial membrane was further quantified by placing a linear region of interest along the long axis of the mitochondrion. The distance between DRP1 and DNM2 molecules is decreased in PAH versus control PASMC (Figure B), using STED, which has a 50-nm resolution threshold. Consistent with this, coimmunoprecipitation studies confirmed the direct interaction between DNM2 and DRP1 (Figure C and D; Figure S9).
 In addition, silencing DRP1 inhibited the translocation of DNM2 to mitochondria (Figure E), consistent with DRP1 being involved in DNM2 trafficking, which is relevant because DNM2 lacks a mitochondrial-targeting sequence. Further supporting an obligatory role of DRP1 in DNM2-mediated mitochondrial fission, augmenting DNM2 increased mitochondrial fission in conditional DRP1-knockout mouse embryonic fibroblasts (without adenovirus expressing Cre recombinase) in the control state; however, DNM2-induced fission was eliminated in this cell line when DRP1 was deleted, using adenovirus-delivered Cre (Figure F and G).
A DNM2 construct lacking the GTPase domain (ΔGTPase) largely fails to localize to mitochondria or induce mitochondrial fission (Figure H and I). Conversely, a construct containing the DNM2’s GTPase domain interacted with DRP1 and localized to the mitochondria (Figure J and K; Figure S10A and S10B). Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of DNM2’s GTPase activity inhibited DNM2’s mitochondrial localization (Figure S11). These 3 findings demonstrate that the GTPase domain of DNM2 is critical for both its DRP1-dependent mitochondrial localization and its profission properties and support an essential role for DNM2 in fission.

## DNM2 Regulates Cell Proliferation, Cell Cycle Progression, and Apoptosis in PASMC
Silencing DNM2 inhibited the proliferation of human PAH PASMC (Figure A; Figure S12) and caused G1/G0 phase cell cycle arrest (Figure B). Furthermore, siDNM2 downregulated the expression of the cell cycle protein CDK4 and its regulator cyclin D1, and upregulated the inhibitor of CDK4, p27Kip1 (Figure C through E), identifying one potential inhibitory mechanism by which reducing DNM2 expression affects slowing of cell proliferation. siDNM2 did not affect G2/M phase in PAH PASMC, evident as unchanged expression of CDK1, a key regulator of the G2 phase (Figure S13). Thus, DNM2 knockdown affects the G0/G1 transition without affecting the G2/M phase of the cell cycle.
 Silencing DNM2 showed a marked trend toward apoptosis induction under basal, unstimulated conditions, indicating that depletion of DNM2 alone is sufficient to activate intrinsic cell death pathways in PAH PASMCs (Figure F). This finding is associated with increased expression of the apoptotic mediator Bak (BCL2 antagonist/killer; Figure G). Conversely, augmenting DNM2 expression in normal PASMC induced proliferation, recapitulating a PAH phenotype (Figure H; Figure S14). However, silencing DNM2 in normal PASMC did not reduce proliferation, suggesting that inhibiting DNM2 selectively affects the hyperproliferative state of PAH PASMC (Figure S15A). Similar to human PAH PASMC, monocrotaline and Su/Hx rat PASMC maintained a hyperproliferative phenotype in culture, which was reversed by silencing DNM2 (Figure S15B and S15C). Silencing DNM1 also inhibits the proliferation of human PAH PASMC, whereas silencing DNM3 did not (Figure S16A and S16B); however, because DNM1’s expression is unchanged in PAH PASMC, it was excluded from further study. Silencing DNM2 in PAH PASMC at 48 hours did not significantly affect oxidative mitochondrial respiration or PDH activity (Figure S17A through S17J), indicating that its effects on cell proliferation, which were evident at this time point, were not primarily metabolically regulated. In contrast, at 96 hours post-siDNM2, there was reduced basal glycolysis and compensatory glycolysis, and a strong trend toward decreased basal respiration, proton leak, and ATP-linked oxygen consumption rate (Figure S17K through S17S). In addition to inhibiting PASMC proliferation, silencing of DNM2 inhibited contraction of PAH PASMC; however, siDNM2 had no effect on PASMC migration (Figure S18A and S18B).

## DNM2 Regulates the Cell Cycle Through the Regulation of Regulator of Cell Cycle
Next, we investigated the mechanistic pathway that connects DNM2 to the cell cycle. We treated human PAH PASMC cell lines with siDNM2 and used RNA-seq to identify the most DNM2-regulated cell cycle mediators. 48 genes passed the filtering threshold (an adjusted P value of 0.05), indicating differential expression after siDNM2 (Figure A). DNM2 showed a log2 (fold change) of −1.257 and a −log10 (adjusted P value) of 8.464, which indicates the expected silencing of DNM2. The top 5 most downregulated genes post-siDNM2 were EHBP1-AS1, PEF1-AS1, GTPBP2, KCNJ2, and RGCC (regulator of cell cycle; Table S2). RGCC, the fifth most downregulated gene, is a known regulator of cell cycle progression. , Gene Ontology analysis revealed that RGCC is the only gene in the top 5 most downregulated genes, which results in Gene Ontology term enrichment referencing cell cycle (Figure B; Table S3). Therefore, we selected RGCC for further investigation as a putative link between DNM2 and cell cycle regulation.
 siDNM2 reduced RGCC expression in PAH PASMC, validating the RNA-seq result (Figure C). Consistent with the biologic relevance of RGCC in PAH, RGCC was upregulated in PAH PASMC (Figure D; Figure S19A). Furthermore, RGCC’s mitochondrial localization and total cellular expression were increased in PAH PASMC (Figure S19B). In addition, RGCC was markedly increased in the nucleus in PAH PASMC (Figure S19B) and siRGCC in PAH PASMC caused G1/G0 cell cycle blockade (Figure E). Moreover, DNM2 augmentation failed to induce proliferation in normal PASMC in which RGCC had been silenced (Figure F). RGCC expression is regulated by Akt1 (protein kinase B1), which is activated in PAH PASMC (as confirmed in our PAH PASMC; Figure S20). We show that silencing DNM2 inactivates Akt1, whereas silencing Akt1 downregulates RGCC expression. Thus, downregulation of RGCC by siDNM2 is Akt-mediated (Figure G and H). In addition, like siDNM2, siRGCC reduced cyclin D1 expression, indicating cell cycle blockade at the G1/G0 phase (Figure I).

## Downregulation of miR-124-3p Increases DNM2 in PAH in Part by Regulating STAT3
We next addressed whether upregulation of DNM2 at the protein level is due to reduced degradation or increased production. DNM2 mRNA expression is higher in PAH PASMCs, suggesting increased transcriptional activity (Figure S21A). To determine whether a change in RNA stability contributes to this increase, we conducted an actinomycin D chase assay. DNM2 mRNA decay rates were comparable between normal and PAH PASMCs (Figure S21B). Thus, elevated DNM2 expression in PAH is not due to increased RNA stability. This is likely due to the fact that actinomycin D inhibits the de novo synthesis of miR-124-3p in both normal and PAH PASMCs, thereby eliminating the effect of miR-124-3p regulation on DNM2 mRNA in both cell types during the assay. In addition, a cycloheximide chase study demonstrates that DNM2 protein is less stable in PAH versus normal PASMC, indicating that the observed DNM2 upregulation is not due to impaired proteasomal degradation (Figure S22A).
Next, we considered whether increased DNM2 in PAH results from decreased regulatory microRNAs and increased transcription. In silico analysis identified 12 microRNAs that putatively bind DNM2’s 3’-UTR (Figure S22B). miR-124-3p and miR-204-5p were considered most promising because they are downregulated in PAH and regulate cell proliferation and apoptosis. , Most of the other microRNAs predicted to bind DNM2 are either upregulated or not reported to be dysregulated in PAH, for example, miR-130-3p, miR-301-3p, miR-454-3p, miR-17-5p, miR-20-5p, and miR-211-5p. Augmenting miR-204-5p in PAH PASMC does not downregulate DNM2 expression, inhibit cell proliferation, or induce apoptosis (Figure S22C through S22E). Thus, we focused on miR-124-3p and 4 other candidate microRNAs that are reported to be downregulated in PAH (miR-93-5p, miR-106a-5p, miR-133a-3p, and miR-519-3p ). Because expression of miR-93-5p, miR-106a-5p, and miR-133a-3p in our study did not differ between normal and PAH PASMCs, they were excluded from further investigation (Figure S22F through S22H). miR-519a-3p was downregulated in PAH PASMC but was excluded from further study because augmentation failed to downregulate DNM2 (Figure S22I and S22J). In contrast, miR-124-3p was decreased in human PAH PASMC (Figure A), and augmenting miR-124-3p decreased DNM2 expression, inhibited mitochondrial fission and proliferation, and induced apoptosis in PAH PASMC (Figure B through E).
 The interaction between miR-124-3p and the 3’-UTR of DNM2 mRNA was confirmed by a luciferase reporter assay (Figure F). These data strongly support the regulation of DNM2 by miR-124-3p.
However, miR-124-3p also negatively regulates STAT3, and STAT3 can positively regulate DNM2 expression. Importantly, serine 727 phosphorylation of STAT3 is markedly increased in PAH PASMCs, supporting a role for enhanced STAT3 activation in the DNM2 pathway and in PAH in general (Figure S23A). Therefore, we assessed whether increased expression of STAT3 in PAH also increases DNM2. siSTAT3 downregulated DNM2 and inhibited mitochondrial fission in PAH PASMC (Figure G and H), while augmenting STAT3 in normal PASMC upregulated DNM2 (Figure I; Figure S23B). Thus, STAT3 is a positive regulator of DNM2. Next, we investigated whether pathologically reduced miR-124-3p levels directly increase DNM2 expression or do so via augmenting STAT3 (or both). Our results show that augmenting miR-124-3p downregulates both STAT3 and DNM2 expression in PAH PASMC (Figure J). Furthermore, cotransfection of HEK293A cells with STAT3 plasmid and a luciferase reporter construct fused to the promoter region of DNM2 resulted in increased luciferase activity, confirming the binding of STAT3 to DNM2’s promoter region (Figure K). Thus, expression of DNM2 in PASMC is regulated by both miR-124-3p and STAT3, and the reduced miR-124-3p levels account for at least some of the observed STAT3 upregulation.
We next examined the cellular localization of expression of DNM2, RGCC, DNM1L, and STAT3 in human lungs using 2 human, single-cell sequencing data sets comprised of 5 PAH and 12 randomly selected control samples, respectively (GEO accession: GSE227136 and GSE296823 ). There was significant regulation of the DNM2 pathway in airway epithelium and ECs, at the P<0.05 significance level. DNM2 and DNM1L mRNA (DRP1) were significantly upregulated in airway epithelium, while DNM2 and RGCC mRNA were significantly upregulated in ECs (Table S4). Due to the limited quantity of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and lack of markers to distinguish PASMC from other SMC (mean=12.1; SD=17.83), no evidence of upregulation or downregulation was observed in the SMC by single-cell sequencing. Therefore, for PASMC DNM2 expression analysis, we relied on PASMC cultures and lung tissues derived from patients with PAH (Figure ). Examining the single-cell sequencing plots (Figure L and M; Figure S24A), we see a global increase in expression among the 4 genes of interest in a wide variety of lung cells, including airway epithelium, ECs, fibroblasts, and macrophages in addition to PASMC.
Likewise, to independently assess our findings in rodents, we examined a publicly available rat single-cell RNA-seq data set. Whereas all components of the STAT3-DRP1-DNM2-RGCC pathway were upregulated in most human lung cells (Figure M; Figure S24A), we were unable to assess the pathway in rat PASMCs due to the limited number of SMC captured in the available study (70 total SMCs; ≈0.2% of total cells; Figure S24B). It appears that the study methods favored macrophage isolation (as macrophages constituted 43% of all cells).
We also tested whether DNA methyltransferases regulate DNM2 expression in PAH. Silencing DNMT1 (DNA methyltransferase 1), DNMT3A (DNA Methyltransferase 3A), and DNMT3B (DNA methyltransferase 3B) in PAH PASMC cell lines did not significantly change DNM2 expression. Thus, increased DNM2 expression in PAH PASMC is not driven by changes in DNA methyltransferase expression (Figure S25A and S25B).

## Silencing DNM2 Regresses PH in Monocrotaline- and Su/Hx-PH Rats
Rats were allowed to develop monocrotaline-PH or Su/Hx-PH before administration of siDNM2 or control siRNA by nebulization (Figure A and H; Figure S26). The knockdown efficiency of siDNM2 in small PAs was confirmed by immunofluorescence (Figure B and I). In monocrotaline-PH rats (male and female), siDNM2 treatment regressed vascular obstruction, as quantified by a decrease in PA medial thickness (Figure C). Furthermore, siDNM2 treatment improved hemodynamics as evidenced on right heart catheterization by decreased RVSP (Figure D), a significant increase in CI and a fall in PVRI and regressed RVH, evident as a decrease in the Fulton index (Figure E through G). Similarly, in Su/Hx-PH rats, siDNM2 regressed vascular obstruction (Figure J) and improved pulmonary hemodynamics as evidenced by a significant decrease in RVSP, an increase in CI, and a reduction in PVRI (Figure K through M). However, siDNM2 treatment did not improve the Fulton index in the Su/Hx-PH rats (Figure N). In the monocrotaline-PH model, male rats showed higher RVSP, PVRI, and Fulton index than females, indicating more severe PH and RVH. However, siDNM2 was effective in both sexes and improved CI more in females, suggesting better therapeutic effects on right ventricular function. In the Su/Hx model, although RVSP and Fulton index were similar between sexes, siDNM2 improved CI more in females than males, while PVRI (though reduced by siDNM2) was higher in males (Figure S27A through S27H).
 siDNM2 treatment did not result in any hematologic, hepatic, or renal toxicity in monocrotaline-PH rats (Figure S28A through S28K). DNM2 expression levels are increased in both rodent PH models. However, there was no sexual dimorphism as DNM2 expression in either model, either in controls or rodents with PH (Figure S29A and S29B). In addition, DNM2 expression was increased in fibroblasts, ECs, and macrophages in both monocrotaline and Su/Hx-PH rats, and siDNM2 treatment decreased DNM2 expression in all cell types (Figure S30A and S30B). Thus, the DNM2 pathway is upregulated in diverse lung cell types in human PAH and experimental PH both at the mRNA level (by single-cell sequencing in patients) and protein level (by immunofluorescence in rodent PH models and patient lungs).

## Discussion
This study reveals a role for DNM2 in mediating the terminal phase of DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission and reveals a new link between mitotic fission and the cell cycle, namely, RGCC. Moreover, the study has translational implications, revealing a contribution of DNM2 to the pathophysiology of PAH and demonstrating its value as a therapeutic target.
This study offers 6 important findings: (1) DNM2 enhances mitochondrial fission but, nonetheless, depends on DRP1 to move to mitochondria, and once there, DNM2 interacts with DRP1 to narrow mitochondrial segments at sites of impending fission; (2) pathological upregulation of DNM2 occurs in human PAH and rodent PAH models; (3) molecular downregulation of DNM2, using siDNM2 in PAH PASMC, inhibits mitochondrial fission, blocks cell cycle progression, and exhibits a strong trend toward apoptosis induction; (4) DNM2 exerts positive regulatory effects on RGCC, a cytosolic activator of CDKs, which constitutes a molecular link between mitotic fission and cell cycle progression; DNM2 regulates the expression of RGCC by positively regulating Akt1; (5) miR-124-3p deficiency and STAT3 activation together upregulate DNM2 in human PAH; this DNM2 pathway is upregulated in PASMC, ECs, airway cells, and macrophages; and (6) nebulized siDNM2 regresses established monocrotaline- and Su/Hx-induced PH models in rats of both sexes. Thus, DNM2 is not only relevant to fundamental mechanisms of mitochondrial fission but also links fission and cell cycle regulation. On a translational level, these findings have relevance to PAH, as DNM2 upregulation contributes to the pathogenesis of this hyperproliferative vascular disease.
Prior studies of DNM2’s role in mitochondrial dynamics are limited in number, and their findings are nonconcordant, with one report supporting and another refuting a role of DNM2 in mitochondrial fission. Neither of these earlier studies examined DNM2’s role in human disease. Lee et al concluded that DRP1 lacks the dynamic constriction range to complete mitochondrial fission. They proposed that the final division event is performed by DNM2, which can constrict lipid membranes to <10 nm. Lee et al showed that DNM2 is recruited to endoplasmic reticulum-induced mitochondrial fission sites and, in HeLa (human cervical cancer) cells, found that siDNM2 causes mitochondrial hyperfusion, similar to cells lacking DRP1. Contradicting Lee et al’s findings, Fonseca et al found that triple knockdowns of all dynamin isoforms (1–3) did not alter mitochondrial fission. Our study supports and extends the work of Lee et al.
Currently, the established function of DNM2 is the scission of plasma membrane vesicles during endocytosis. DNM2 also forms vesicles from the Golgi apparatus and regulates vesicle trafficking and the dynamics of microtubules and the actin cytoskeleton. – Furthermore, inhibiting DNM2 reduces cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in cervical cancer, prostate cancer, non–small-cell lung cancer, glioblastoma, and chronic myeloid leukemia. While inhibitors of mitochondrial fission (or enhancers of fusion) regress human cancers of various types, , prior studies of DNM2 in cancer did not investigate DNM2 as a mediator of mitochondrial fission, nor did they implicate mitochondrial biology in the preclinical efficacy of DNM2 inhibition as an antiproliferative therapy.
We demonstrated that DNM2’s role in executing mitochondrial fission (Figure A through D; Figure S4) is achieved through a partnership with DRP1 (Figure A through G). This conclusion is supported by our observation of increased length of the constriction sites following DNM2 silencing, indicating that mitochondrial fission is impaired by loss of DNM2 (Figure F). Furthermore, in comparison to normal PASMCs, PAH PASMCs exhibit tighter constrictions at fission sites, as evidenced by a decrease in the length and residual thickness of the mitochondria at constriction sites on STED microscopy. Thus, greater mitochondrial DNM2 expression promotes the excess fission and more severe mitochondrial narrowing seen in PAH PASMC (Figure E). Our observation of DRP1-DNM2 interaction (by immunoprecipitation) and closer proximity of these molecules at fission sites in PAH versus normal PASMC (by STED microscopy) is consistent with the findings of Lee et al, who reported incomplete DRP1-marked fission (stalled super-constrictions) at mitochondrial fission sites in DNM2-depleted cells.
There is no marked difference in maximal mitochondrial diameter between normal and PAH PASMC (each ≈400 nm on conventional confocal imaging; Figure S7E); however, the true diameter of mitochondria (measured using STED, which has 5-fold higher resolution than confocal microscopy) is ≈200nm (for both normal and PAH mitochondria), attesting to the importance of super-resolution imaging in assessment of mitochondrial dynamics (Figure E). Therefore, the observed reduced length and thickness of the mitochondrial constriction sites in PAH versus control PASMC are not due to any preexisting differences in baseline mitochondrial thickness; rather, it reflects the enhanced narrowing that results when DNM2 interacts with DRP1 on the PAH PASMC mitochondria (Figures E and A).
Like DRP1, DNM2 lacks mitochondrial-targeting domains. Interestingly, DRP1 conveys DNM2 to the mitochondria. DNM2 interacts with DRP1 in the cytosol and then translocates to the OMM at the site of mitochondrial fission (Figure A through D). Silencing DRP1 inhibits translocation of DNM2 to the mitochondria (Figure E). Furthermore, augmenting DNM2 increased fission only when DRP1 was present (Figure F and G), indicating that DNM2-mediated mitochondrial fission is DRP1-dependent.
A structure-function study revealed that a DNM2 construct lacking the GTPase domain (ΔGTPase) failed to localize to mitochondria or interact with DRP1 and did not induce mitochondria fission. These findings highlight the critical role of DNM2’s GTPase domain in binding DRP1 and targeting DNM2 to mitochondria (Figure H through J). Conversely, the constructs carrying the GTPase domain of DNM2 were sufficient to colocalize and interact with DRP1 and translocate to mitochondria (Figure J and K; Figure S10B), highlighting the crucial role of this domain in DNM2’s mitochondrial-targeting and fission. These findings emphasize an orchestrated action of the DRP1-DNM2 complex in executing mitochondrial fission.
The basal expression of DNM2 on normal mitochondria is low, and normal PASMC can be rendered hyperproliferative by heterologous overexpression of DNM2. However, in human PAH and rodent PH, there is pathological upregulation of DNM2 (Figure A through F; Figure S1A), which increases mitotic mitochondrial fission, leading to hyperproliferation and apoptosis resistance of PAH PASMC (Figures A through D and ). Interestingly, among the isoforms of the dynamin family, only DNM2 is upregulated in PAH, highlighting its unique role in disease pathogenesis (Figure A through F; Figures S1A, S2A, and S2B). This preferential importance of the DNM2 isoform in PAH is consistent with the elevated expression of DNM2 in many cancers. 
The importance of DNM2 in the pathogenesis of PAH was further supported by our observation that silencing DNM2 restored mitochondrial network fusion, inhibited proliferation by causing a cell cycle blockade at G1/G0 phase, and demonstrated a strong trend toward unstimulated apoptosis in PAH PASMCs (Figures A through D and through G). Conversely, augmenting DNM2 mimicked a PAH phenotype in normal PASMC by inducing mitochondrial fission and cell proliferation (Figures B and H). Consistent with the idea that DNM2 inhibition causes cell cycle arrest at the G1/G0 phase, siDNM2 reduced the expression of the cell cycle proteins CDK4 and cyclin D1 (Figure C and D) and increased the expression of the CDK4 inhibitor, p27kip1 (Figure E). Notably, the expression of CDK1, a G2/M phase regulator, remained unchanged, indicating that DNM2 inhibition primarily affects the G1/G0 transition rather than the G2/M phase (Figure B; Figure S13). We previously reported that a similar inhibition of CDK4 and upregulation of p27kip1 occurs when DRP1 receptor protein MiD51 was silenced in human PAH PASMC. This suggests that inhibiting mitochondrial fission, whether through inhibition of DRP1, DRP1 binding partners, or DNM2, induces changes in cell cycle mediators (downregulation of positive regulators and upregulation of inhibitors), which mediate cell cycle blockade. 
Our findings further explain the complementary roles of DRP1 and DNM2 in mitochondrial fission and their impact on cell proliferation in PAH PASMCs. DRP1 assembles at mitochondrial constriction sites to initiate narrowing of the membrane, while DNM2 mediates the terminal scission event that completes mitochondrial division. DNM2 does require DRP1, both to translocate to the mitochondria and to induce fission. Thus, DRP1 and DNM2 act sequentially within the same pathway rather than as independent regulators. Consistent with this, dual silencing of DRP1 and DNM2 did not produce additive effects on mitochondrial fusion or PASMC proliferation, as each protein contributes to successive steps of the same fission process. Thus, therapeutic strategies targeting both DRP1 and DNM2 are unlikely to provide added benefit beyond inhibition of either protein alone. Instead, therapeutic strategies may focus on modulating the DRP1-DNM2 axis as a single coordinated pathway.
In contrast to prior reports that DRP1 silencing improved respiration, induction of mitochondrial fusion by silencing DNM2 did not improve mitochondrial respiration at 48 hours (Figure S17A through S17I). This suggests that DRP1 regulates mitochondrial metabolism through a DNM2-independent mechanism. Ultimately, 96 hours following DNM2 silencing, DNM2 caused a global reduction in cellular metabolism, as evidenced by decreased basal oxygen consumption rate, proton leak, ATP-linked respiration, and glycolytic rates. This late reduction in metabolism may reflect the fact that these cells were in cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase, caused by silencing DNM2 or RGCC. In the G0/G1 phase, cells are more quiescent and less metabolically active (Figure S17K through S17S). Notably, this differs from DRP1 silencing, which causes cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, suggesting that the distinct phases of cell cycle blockade may differentially influence metabolic demand.
In addition, we identified a new mechanism that connects DNM2 to cell cycle progression. After silencing DNM2 in PAH PASMC, we found that RGCC is the most downregulated gene (Figure A; Table S2) that is associated with cell cycle progression. RGCC (also known as RGC-32 [response gene to complement 32]) is a cytosolic protein that activates cyclin-dependent kinases, potentially playing a key role in regulating the cell cycle. RGCC is involved in SMC proliferation and migration, angiogenesis, and cancer development. , Like DNM2, RGCC expression is upregulated in the mitochondria and nucleus of PAH PASMC (Figure D; Figure S19A and S19B), and silencing RGCC causes cell cycle arrest in PAH PASMC at the G1/G0 phase (Figure E). Given that RGCC functions as a cell cycle regulator and that its expression is positively regulated by DNM2, this redistribution toward the nucleus provides a plausible explanation for the hyperproliferative phenotype observed in PAH PASMC. Of note, some CDKs, particularly CDK1-cyclin B, localize to mitochondria during the G2/M transition, where they enhance mitochondrial respiration and phosphorylate DRP1, as well as components of the electron transport chain, resulting in coupling of cell cycle progression to mitochondrial respiration. , This supports the concept that mitochondrial localization of conventional cell cycle regulators, such as RGCC, may represent a broader mechanism coordinating mitochondrial dynamics with proliferative signaling. The mitochondrial localization of RGCC remains an intriguing finding, and its functional implications warrant further investigation (Figure S19B). Moreover, in RGCC-silenced PASMC, DNM2 failed to induce proliferation, indicating that DNM2 regulates cell proliferation through RGCC (Figure F). This is the first demonstration that RGCC is a key downstream mediator in DNM2-mediated regulation of cell cycle progression. Furthermore, Akt1, which regulates RGCC expression, is activated in PAH PASMC (Figure S20), and silencing DNM2 reduces Akt1 activation (Figure G). Given that the expression of RGCC is regulated by Akt1 (Figure H), and Akt1 itself is regulated by DNM2, we conclude that DNM2 regulates the expression of RGCC by regulating Akt1. One of the other highly downregulated genes following DNM2 silencing is KCNJ2, which encodes the Kir2.1 (inwardly rectifying potassium channel subfamily J member 2) protein, a subunit of an inward-rectifier potassium channel (Table S2). Although Kir2.1 has been implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation, its role as a regulator of cell cycle progression remains unestablished.
The increase in DNM2 expression in PAH PASMC is due both to reduced miR-124-3p and increased expression of STAT3. miR-124-3p is downregulated in PAH, which can also increase STAT3 activity and, thereby, promote STAT3-dependent transcription of DNM2. In addition, miR-124-3p directly targets DNM2’s 3’ UTR to repress its expression. Thus, downregulation of this microRNA partially explains the upregulation of DNM2. Thus, both STAT3 activation and miR-124-3p downregulation combine to reinforce DNM2 upregulation in PAH (Figure ). In PAH PASMC, restoring miR-124-3p downregulates DNM2 expression, inhibiting mitochondrial fission, suppressing proliferation, and inducing apoptosis (Figure B through E). Further molecular evidence showing that miR-124-3p regulates DNM2 is the direct binding of miR-124-3p to the 3’-UTR of DNM2 (Figure F). Dysregulation of miR-124-3p has also been implicated in causing metabolic and proliferative abnormalities in pulmonary artery ECs of patients with PAH via PTBP1 and the PKM1/PKM2 glycolytic, Warburg metabolic pathway. How the miR-124/PTBP1/PKM axis and this new miR-124/DNM2 pathway interact merits future study.
 Elevated DNM2 protein in PAH PASMCs could theoretically result from either increased production or reduced turnover of the transcript or protein. Our data support the former. RNA stability remains largely similar between diseased and control cells (Figure S21B). Likewise, differences in proteasomal degradation rate do not explain the observed increase in DNM2 expression, as DNM2 protein stability is reduced, not increased, in PAH cells (Figure S22A). Thus, enhanced transcriptional signaling, rather than impaired protein degradation, drives pathological upregulation of DNM2 in PAH PASMCs.
DNM2 expression is also positively regulated by STAT3, a regulator of cell proliferation and apoptosis, which is constitutively activated in PAH (Figure S23A). Silencing STAT3 in PAH PASMC downregulated DNM2 and inhibited mitochondrial fission, while augmenting STAT3 in normal PASMC increased DNM2 expression (Figure G through I). Moreover, we demonstrated a direct interaction of STAT3 with DNM2’s promoter region (Figure K), indicating that STAT3 can directly control DNM2 transcription in PAH PASMC; however, miR-124-3p can also negatively regulate STAT3 expression. Thus, pathologically low miR-124-3p levels can directly increase DNM2 expression (Figure A, B, F) or do so indirectly by increasing the expression of STAT3 (Figure J). We conclude that the elevated DNM2 expression in PAH PASMC is a combined consequence of an epigenetic mechanism (decreased miR-124-3p expression) and a transcriptional mechanism (increased STAT3). Although altered DNA methylation occurs in PAH and several DNMTs are upregulated in PAH cells and tissues, our data failed to demonstrate a role for DNMTs in the upregulation of DNM2 in PAH (Figure S25A and S25B).
Single-cell RNA-seq data in human lungs showed that DNM2 is broadly expressed across multiple lung cell types, with increased expression in the ECs and airway epithelial cells of PAH lungs (Figure L and M). DNM2 is upregulated in patient-derived PASMC, but we could not assess SMCs in the available single-cell RNA-seq data due to the small portion of SMCs in the total cell population. Specifically, SMCs are only 1% to 2% of total cells, and there are no markers to differentiate among the types of SMC found in the lung, which include airway, pulmonary artery, bronchial artery, and pulmonary venous SMCs. Importantly, DRP1, RGCC, and STAT3 increased globally across the human and rodent PAH lungs in multiple cell types (Figure S24), perhaps explaining why nebulized siDNM2 therapy in rodent PH models, which would affect multiple lung cell types, resulted in significant regression of PH. Thus, the DNM2 pathway is widely upregulated in many disease-relevant cells in human PAH and rodent PH models.
Creating a persistent state of mitochondrial fusion, whether by inhibiting DRP1, augmenting mitofusin-2, or downregulating expression of DRP1 adaptors (MiD49/51), suppresses cell proliferation and regresses PAH and cancers. , , , Consistent with this, we now show that inhibiting DNM2 causes sustained mitochondrial fusion in PAH PASMC, resulting in the suppression of cell proliferation, and tends to increase unstimulated apoptosis, offering a strong rationale for therapeutically targeting DNM2 in PAH in vivo. Indeed, nebulizing siDNM2 in either monocrotaline- or Su/Hx-induced PH rat models regressed PH and improved some dysregulated hematologic, hepatic, and renal parameters (Figure A through N; Figure S28A through S28K). Furthermore, consistent with the comparable responses of siDNM2 treatment–observed benefits between sexes (Figure S29A through S29H), DNM2 expression did not differ significantly between male and female rats under control conditions, after monocrotaline or Su/Hx treatments (when it was elevated) or after siDNM2 treatment (when it was reduced; Figure B and I; Figure S30A and S30B). siDNM2 effectively reduced overall DNM2 expression to a similar extent in both sexes (Figure B and I; Figure S29A and S29B), suggesting that DNM2 regulation in experimental PH is sex-independent. Interestingly, siDNM2 treatment decreased DNM2 expression in other cell types such as ECs, fibroblasts, and macrophages, potentially explaining why nebulized siDNM2 therapy was so effective in rodent models. Specifically, the siRNA therapy may be beneficial to the many lung cell types in which components of the DRP1-DNM2-STAT3-RGCC pathway are upregulated in PAH (Figure S30A and S30B).

## Limitations
This study has limitations. First, the diagnostic and prognostic values of DNM2 and miR-124-3p in PAH were not evaluated in a patient cohort, warranting future investigation. Second, the potential therapeutic benefits of augmenting miR-124-3p or silencing RGCC were not evaluated in vivo. Third, site-directed mutagenesis of miR-124-3p binding sites within the DNM2 3’ UTR was not performed; however, we did not aim to explore the precise mechanism by which miR-124-3p binds DNM2 mRNA, and therefore, site-directed mutagenesis of miR-124-3p to identify DNM2 mRNA binding sites was not performed. Fourth, there are limited metadata associated with the collected human PASMC and lung tissues. We lack detailed information regarding patient ethnicity, clinical history, comorbidities, including smoking history, and the use of immunosuppressive medications, which may limit clinical contextualization.

## Conclusions
DNM2 participates in the terminal step of mitochondrial fission (Figure ). Epigenetic and transcriptional upregulation of DNM2 increases mitotic fission in PAH PASMC and promotes pulmonary vasculopathy. The underlying mechanism that connects DNM2 and fission to cell cycle progression involves RGCC. The miR-124-3p-STAT3-DNM2-DRP1-RGCC pathway offers potential therapeutic targets in PAH.
