## Introduction
In the last decade, nanomedicine has opened new doors in cancer treatment and bioimaging. Covalent organic frameworks (COFs), with their nanoscale structures, remarkable porosity, adjustable functionality and impressive biocompatibility, have emerged as leading-edge biomedical materials that support a range of therapeutic applications [ ]. Millions of people die each year from diseases associated with cancer, making it one of the biggest health risks in the world. Treatments like chemotherapy, radiation and surgery are still the main approaches; however, they come with severe side effects, high risks of recurrence, and a frustrating tendency for tumors to resist multiple drugs unpredictably [ ]. Understanding the biological barriers that limit treatment effectiveness is vital for advancing drug delivery and biomedical applications. These barriers, present in various disease states, limit the efficacy of therapeutic agents, particularly in cancer and neurological diseases. COFs represent a promising class of nanomaterials designed to overcome these barriers due to their tunable structural properties, high surface area and biocompatibility. One of the most significant barriers is the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which is a highly selective semipermeable membrane that protects the brain, but also prevents the passage of many therapeutic agents. This limits the treatment options for diseases such as brain tumors and neurodegenerative disorders. COFs can be engineered to enhance drug transport across the BBB through surface functionalization, which allows targeting of specific receptors and facilitates drug entry into the brain. Because of their biocompatibility and ability to protect drugs from enzymatic breakdown, COFs are especially suited for targeted delivery to the central nervous system (CNS). In cancer treatment, the tumor microenvironment (TME) presents a major obstacle. Abnormal blood vessels, elevated interstitial pressure and dense extracellular matrix within tumors limit drug penetration, often leading to less effective outcomes. COFs, with their high porosity and adaptable functions, are well-suited to navigate these barriers by enabling controlled drug release, improving retention within tumors, and promoting deeper drug penetration into the tumor. FCOFs functionalized with targeting ligands can actively target tumor cells, while their porous structure allows for a high drug-loading capacity, facilitating sustained drug release within the tumor. Similarly, nasal and skin barriers challenge drug delivery, especially for non-invasive methods. The nasal route, which is frequently used for systemic drug delivery, faces challenges from mucociliary clearance and enzymatic breakdown. COFs can be functionalized with mucoadhesive properties to enhance drug retention in the nasal cavity, allowing for improved drug absorption and direct delivery to the brain through the olfactory nerve​. Similarly, COFs can be engineered to penetrate the stratum corneum of skin or deliver drugs through transdermal patches, offering non-invasive treatment options for skin-related diseases or systemic drug delivery. By addressing these biological barriers, COFs provide a promising platform for improving the delivery of therapeutic agents across various disease contexts. Their tunable mechanical properties and functional versatility allow them to bypass these barriers, enhancing drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy in diseases such as cancer, neurological disorders and respiratory conditions.
In order to enhance cancer treatments, experts have worked hard in the field of nanomedicine. For this purpose, many polymeric micelles, carbon and metal-organic based nanomaterials have been prepared, but they have some limitations including long term toxicity and low drug loading capacity [ ]. Therefore, to overcome the limitations of conventional nanomedicine—such as low drug loading capacity, long-term toxicity and suboptimal biocompatibility—researchers are developing next-generation nanomedicines with enhanced performance [ ]. COFs are one of the most innovative and versatile groups of porous organic materials (POMs), formed from building blocks linked by covalent bonds through reticular chemistry. The crystalline conjugated topological network structure of COFs shows a large number of accessible channels, functional groups and holes [ ]. With an organized network topology and multiple active sites, COFs offer a broad adhesion surface, making them distinctly superior to two-dimensional (2D) graphene nanosheets [ ]. In contrast to zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) and three-dimensional (3D) metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), COFs can be rationally designed by selecting appropriate building blocks according to specific requirements. On the other hand, COFs are more stable and biocompatible when metal ions are absent. The versatility of COFs, attributed to their large surface area, customizable structures, ease of functionalization and high chemical stability, has facilitated their broad application across fields such as gas storage, adsorption, catalysis and sensing [ , ]. To create 2D and 3D polymeric architecture in COFs, crystalline extended organic frameworks are built from the organic building units. The light elements, carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), boron (B) and nitrogen (N), which are bonded together by strong covalent bonds, make up the core structure of the COFs [ ]. The primary ways in which COFs differ from other kinds of covalent organic polymers are their ordered and controllable porosity, functionalization and structural regularity. In contrast to the traditional POMs composed of amorphous polymeric components joined by irreversible covalent links, COFs are formed through reversible condensation of molecular unit, which confer both crystallinity and biodegradability [ ]. The scientific world has recently become more interested in COFs due to their peculiar characteristics and the promise they have shown for the atomic-scale, spatially precise synthesis of highly targeted, particular materials [ ]. The directionality and strength of covalent bonds enable the formation of previously unheard-of organic structures polymer chemistry, leading to materials that are stiff, low in density, highly stable, extremely resistant, and possess well-defined and permanent porosity [ ]
This review provides an in-depth look at COF-based strategies to address challenges in drug delivery across diverse biological barriers, and also explores COFs' roles in various therapeutic approaches, including immunotherapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), chemotherapy, oral medications, and photothermal therapy (PTT). This work aims to shed light on the potential of COFs as novel drug carriers to cross the barriers such as BBB, nasal, skin mucosa and mouth mucosa by examining the most recent research and findings in this field. A deeper understanding of COFs' potential and constraints will open the door to the the development of more effective and targeted delivery strategies in this challenging area.

## Structural properties, composition and biocompatibility of COFs
2.1

## Structural properties and applications
Through partially reversible condensation reactions, organic linkers assemble into COFs. This reversible setting enables the formation of a crystalline structure rather than an amorphous polymer, allowing for error correction and reorganization via bond breaking and reforming. This process occurs even while the covalent bonds within the framework ensure high thermal stability [ , , , ]. Typically, COFs are synthesized through reversible condensation of molecular building block. The reversibility of the condensation reaction facilitates error repair. High crystallinity and excellent thermal stability are conferred upon these 2D COFs by the covalent bonding character and the capacity for error correction while forming lattices. This section provides a brief overview of the various linkages and molecular components that constitute 2D COFs [ , , ]. Yaghi and colleagues reported on the the pioneering synthesis of 2D COFs based on boronic acid derivatives [ ]. 1,4-benzene diboronic acid (BDBA) self-condenses to form boroxine-based COF network (COF-1) as shown in . These boronic acid-based 2D COFs exhibit high thermal steadiness (500–600 °C) and highly ordered structure due to the reversible condensation processes involving -B-O- links [ , , ]. Different topologies can be used to create 2D COFs; polygon skeletons with regular (isotropic) and irregular (anisotropic) topologies have been developed for COF design. Each time, there is a discrete pore and a highly organized lattice structure. The foundation for the structural diversity of 2D COFs is the topological diversity. There are massive COF members with various construction components and linkers behind every topology [ , ]. 
COFs, an emerging class of porous, covalently bound organic structures organized into crystalline 2D or 3D frameworks, have attracted significant attention as potential nanoplatforms for cancer treatment. Light elements like H, C, silicon (Si), B, N and O are assembled via various dynamic covalent bonds, including azo (N=N), boronate ester (B-O-C), benzosiloxaborole (B-O-Si), boroxine (B-O-B), and imide (C=N) [ ]. The distinct characteristics of these materials include their modular design, which permits customization and control over the morphology; porosity, which offers increased loading capability and regulated payload release; conjugated chains, whose π-conjugated structure enhances photocatalytic action; ease of functionalization or modification, which enables specific tumor targeting through the incorporation of particular ligands; stability, which offers superior cell internalization through enhanced dispersibility and chemical stability in aqueous solution; and metal based free structures, which contribute to biocompatibility [ , , ].
The primary structural components of COF materials include woven networks, 2D and 3D structures. There have been reports of COF morphologies in a variety of shapes, including sheets, spheres, foams, cubes, rectangular prisms and more [ ]. Generally, 2D planar COFs have stiff structures and low water dispensability. Typically, non-planar building blocks with some flexible properties constitute 3D COFs. A range of 2D COFs based on boronate estershowed good thermal steadiness (500–600 °C). However, they’re quite vulnerable to acids, alkalis, alcohol and certain other substances, which can cause rapid degradation. Test findings revealed a 50% degradation rate in 11 s and a 90% degradation rate in 1 min. Researchers have explored several ways to adjust the mechanical properties of boric acid-based COFs, such as altering porosity and crystallinity to enhance their stability in water-rich environments [ ]. The mechanical properties of COFs, such as their structural rigidity, flexibility and porosity, are critical factors influencing their biomedical applications, especially in drug delivery and cancer therapy. Recent studies have demonstrated the ability to fine-tune these mechanical properties through controlled synthesis and functionalization, which directly impacts their performance in therapeutic contexts. For example, Shi et al. [ ] reviewed the relationship between the mechanical strength of 2D COFs and their efficacy in drug delivery. The study demonstrated that adjusting the pore sizes and linker flexibility in COF structures led to improved mechanical stability and higher drug loading capacity, which in turn enhanced their therapeutic efficacy in vitro cancer models​. This was further supported by Sun [ ], who showed that immobilizing enzymes in COF with tunable mechanical rigidity significantly improved enzymatic stability and activity under harsh physiological conditions. Additionally, research on polyethylene glycol (PEG)-functionalized COF nanoparticles (NPs) demonstrated their capability to cross biological barriers, like the BBB, thanks to their adjustable mechanical properties and stability in the body. Zhang et al. [ ] demonstrated that COFs with optimized mechanical properties were able to maintain structural integrity during circulation and effectively deliver anti-cancer agents across the BBB, achieving superior therapeutic outcomes in brain metastasis models. These examples underscore the importance of mechanical property optimization in enhancing the performance of COFs for biomedical applications. Customizing the rigidity, flexibility and porosity of COF structures allows them to bypass certain biological barriers, enhancing drug loading, controlled release and targeted delivery in cancer treatment and other therapies. Water-dispersible COF-1 as a decomposable drug delivery vehicle for in vivo environments. By adding PEG-enhanced nonfunctional curcumin derivatives, amino functionalization and chemotherapeutic drugs, this nanosystem improves stability, reduces inflexibility and pore size, and effectively targets cancer cells for strong anti-tumor effects [ ].
The geometry and connectivity of the linkers in a COF lattice dictate the geometry of the framework. Trigonal planar linkers have the ability to condense into sheets with hexagonal pores, whereas tetragonal linkers with linear building blocks can condense into tetragonal pores. After that, these sheets stack to create a framework with one-dimensional (1D) channels. In contrast to 3D COFs, which are formed from building blocks with connectivity in 3D to form a 3D net, 2D COFs are typically produced from stacked 2D layers. displays a few sample COF networks (2D and 3D COFs) to demonstrate these construction ideas [ , , ]. In summary, synthesizing COFs through reversible condensation reactions provides exceptional structural flexibility and thermal stability, making COFs ideal candidates for drug delivery and biomedical applications. However, challenges such as synthesis complexity and scalability need to be addressed to fully exploit their potential in practical applications. 

2.2

## Composition and advantages
COFs are highly ordered crystalline structures composed of lightweight elements such as C, H, O, B and N. COFs are created by linking organic building blocks, like aromatic rings, with strong covalent bonds to form structured 2D or 3D networks. The composition of COFs is highly versatile, allowing for the inclusion of different functional groups to modify their chemical and physical properties. Scientists use reticular chemistry to carefully design and control COF structures, creating highly ordered, porous materials with customizable pore sizes and shapes. This adaptability in design makes COFs highly valuable in applications requiring specific pore sizes, such as drug delivery, catalysis, gas storage and separation. One of the most remarkable features of COFs is their high porosity and surface area, which allows for the storage and transport of large amounts of therapeutic molecules or other substances. The well-defined pores in COFs can be tailored to accommodate molecules of different sizes, ensuring efficient drug loading and release. For instance, COFs can hold chemotherapy drugs or proteins within their pores, shielding them from degradation and allowing for controlled release triggered by changes in pH or temperature. This makes COFs particularly promising for targeted drug delivery in cancer therapy, where controlled and sustained release of drugs is crucial for enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects.
In terms of biocompatibility, COFs offer a significant advantage over other porous materials, such as MOFs which often contain metal ions that may lead to toxicity concerns. COFs, being composed of organic elements, avoid the risk of metal ion toxicity and thus offer a safer platform for biomedical applications, such as drug delivery, where biocompatibility is paramount​. Additionally, COFs can be further functionalized with biocompatible polymers such as PEG to improve their stability in biological environments and enhance their circulation time within the bloodstream, thus optimizing their efficacy in therapeutic applications​. Another key advantage of COFs is their structural tunability. The building blocks of COFs can be carefully selected to modify the pore size, shape and overall framework properties. This allows for the creation of COFs that can respond to specific stimuli, such as changes in pH or temperature, making them ideal for stimuli-responsive drug delivery. For instance, COFs can be engineered to release drugs only in the acidic environment of tumors, thereby reducing damage to healthy tissues and increasing drug efficacy at the target site. COFs are very stable and can withstand a range of conditions, including exposure to moisture, heat and various chemicals. This stability ensures that COFs can maintain their structural integrity during the drug delivery process, allowing for sustained release of therapeutic agents over extended periods. In comparison to other drug delivery platforms, such as liposomes or polymeric NPs which may degrade or lose stability under physiological conditions, COFs offer superior robustness and durability. COFs have been used to deliver chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin (DOX). The drug can be encapsulated within the porous network of COFs and released in a controlled manner, reducing systemic toxicity and enhancing the accumulation of the drug at the tumor site [ ]. COFs functionalized with nucleic acids can enhance the stability of these molecules, protecting them from enzymatic degradation. This makes COFs excellent carriers for gene therapy applications, where stability and controlled delivery of nucleic acids are essential for therapeutic success. COFs also can be functionalized with photosensitizers to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon exposure to light, killing cancer cells through PDT. This approach provides a non-invasive option for treating localized tumors.
In summary, COFs’ unique makeup—with lightweight organic building blocks and strong covalent bonds—provides several clear advantages for biomedical applications. Their high porosity, biocompatibility, structural tunability and mechanical stability make them a superior choice for drug delivery, gene therapy and cancer treatments. Moreover, their ability to be functionalized for specific tasks, such as targeting tumor cells or responding to environmental stimuli, further enhances their potential in nanomedicine​.

2.3

## Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency
COFs load drugs mainly through their highly porous structure, which enables therapeutic molecules to be stored securely within their well-defined pores. The tunable pore sizes and surface functionalities provide a customizable environment where drugs of varying sizes and properties can be stably loaded. This allows COFs to carry a larger drug payload than other nanomaterials, such as liposomes or polymeric NPs. One major advantage of COFs is their robust framework, which significantly minimizes premature drug leakage. The strong covalent bonds in COFs prevent the structure from collapsing under bodily conditions, ensuring drugs stay safely encapsulated during circulation. Surface modifications, such as the addition of PEG or other functional groups, further stabilize COFs and prevent leakage by creating a steric barrier, which hinders the premature release of drugs. Additionally, encapsulated drugs can be engineered for pH-sensitive or stimuli-responsive release, ensuring that the drugs are released only in the targeted environment, such as within acidic TME.
The encapsulation efficiency of COFs is generally high due to their extensive porosity and high surface area. Reports have demonstrated that COFs can achieve drug loading capacities of up to 65% weight for certain therapeutic agents, such as insulin. Precise control over pore size and functionalization in COFs allows for optimal loading of both small-molecule drugs and larger biomolecules. The uniform pore structure promotes even drug distribution, ensuring a consistent release over time. Additionally, COFs can be customized with targeting ligands, which boosts the accuracy of drug delivery, reduces unintended effects, and enhances therapeutic effectiveness. While COFs offer excellent encapsulation efficiency and stability, there are limitations regarding the physicochemical properties of the drugs being loaded. For instance, hydrophobic drugs may face challenges in uniform distribution within hydrophilic COF frameworks unless surface modifications are applied to enhance compatibility. Additionally, large-molecule drugs may not fit into the smaller pores of some COF structures, limiting their use for certain biological agents like large proteins or nucleic acids. This can be addressed by adjusting the pore size and modifying the COF structure, but this also increases the complexity of synthesis.

2.4

## Surface activity and pore size
The biocompatibility of NPs for organic use is also determined by their surface characteristics. For instance, in biological mediums, the dispersibility is increased by hydrophilic chains on the exterior [ ]. Biocompatibility is strongly affected by the surface charge of NPs; charged NPs tend to be more harmful to cells than neutral ones. Neutral NPs also circulate in the bloodstream for longer periods, making them ideal for biological research. While they aren’t soluble, their ability to disperse well still makes them valuable in various biological applications [ ]. For biological applications, smaller NPs (10–200 nm) with hydrophilic functionalities often have enough dispersibility. To adorn the surface of hydrophilic side chains, hydrophobic nano COFs (nCOFs) must undergo PEGylation or other changes [ ].
The mechanical characteristics of COFs materials are also influenced by their pore size [ ]. The porosity of COFs can be adjusted by modifying the length of the linkers used in their synthesis. Typically, COFs exhibit two types of pores: mesopores (2–50 nm) and micropores (<2 nm). For example, COF-6 has one of the smallest pore sizes at 8.6 oA, while porous chiral COF (PC—COF) features larger pores, measuring up to 5.8 nm [ , ]. In addition to pore size and shape, the organization of pores within the framework which is further influenced by layer stacking also determines the porosity of COFs [ ]. Overlapping (AA) stacking happens when all atoms in neighboring layers align directly on top of each other. In contrast, staggered (AB) stacking occurs when three vertices of a hole align with the centers of the pores in the adjacent layers [ , ]. COFs have also emerged as a highly studied class of porous materials, with extensive research exploring their potential in drug delivery, photocatalysis, molecular sensing, gas storage, and membrane technologies. In applications involving medication delivery, porosity and pore geometry are critical. By contrasting the drug molecules' sizes with the pore geometry, one can evaluate the potential for drug loading. A larger surface area, combined with suitable pore geometry, enhances drug loading efficiency. The pore geometry of COF NPs can be easily adjusted by selecting appropriate building blocks [ , ].

## Biocompatibility
COFs, with their adaptable structures and extensive surface areas, have attracted significant interest in material science, catalysis and biomedicine, particularly for their potential in drug delivery systems (DDSs), biosensors and bioimaging technologies [ ]. The material’s tendency to elicit a suitable host reaction in a particular presentation is frequently referred to as biocompatibility. The term "biocompatibility" also refers to a material's capacity to elicit a biological reaction and undergo full clearance without producing intolerable toxicity [ , ]. The biocompatibility of NPs depends on several factors, including their size, shape, surface properties, pore structure and overall porosity. A material is considered biocompatible if it doesn’t cause intolerable toxic, carcinogenic, or immune reactions. To determine the biocompatibility of a nanomedicine in biological applications, it’s essential to carefully evaluate these characteristics, as they influence how NPs interact with blood and cell organelles [ , ]. We evaluated the intrinsic properties and biocompatibility of COFs in the context of biomedical applications given that COF‐based theranostic systems remain in their early stages and our understanding of their long–term biocompatibility is still evolving [ ].
Certain COFs, such as imine-based COFs and boronate ester-linked COFs, have been designed to be biodegradable. These COFs offer controlled degradation, making them particularly useful in DDSs where timely and targeted release of therapeutics is essential. Biodegradable ensures that the material can be metabolized and eliminated from the body without causing long term harm [ , , ]. Strong host–guest interactions between loaded organic drug molecules and COF carriers have been shown in COF-based DDSs to occasionally make it impossible for payload release under physiological settings in a condensed amount of time [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. Additionally, surface modifications like PEGylation and biocompatible polymer coatings are used to make COFs even safer by improving their dispersibility in biological environments. These modifications not only reduce the chances of immune reactions but also extend circulation time and limit unwanted interactions with healthy tissues. COFs can also be tailored to interact specifically with biological molecules like proteins, nucleic acids and cell membranes, making them ideal for targeted drug delivery or sensing applications. To improve their compatibility with biological systems, COFs can be added to biocompatible matrices or coatings. These coatings may also have additional functions, such as enhancing cellular adherence or stimulating particular biological reactions [ , , , , , ]. A multitude of biomedical applications could benefit from the use of COFs; however, in order to fully assess their biocompatibility, extensive research addressing aspects like chemical stability, biodegradability, toxicity, interactions with biological molecules, immunogenicity, and in vivo behaviour must be conducted. Overall, the biocompatibility of COFs makes them suitable for biomedical applications, particularly in drug delivery. Although initial studies show low toxicity, ongoing research is required to address long-term safety concerns and optimize surface modifications for enhanced biocompatibility.

## Biomedical applications of COFs
The development of nano-DDS has progressed rapidly in recent decades, leading to numerous breakthroughs. The way nanomedicines move through the body—in blood circulation, tumor targeting, deep tissue penetration, cellular uptake, and drug release—is strongly influenced by the mechanical properties of NPs [ , , ]. This section primarily focuses on current developments in the biomedical applications of COFs with various functional properties for protein and nucleic acid delivery, cancer therapy modification and vaccine development as shown in and . 
3.1

## Specific proteins and nucleic acid delivery
Protein therapy is a controversial topic in biomedical research. Unlike traditional small-molecule treatments, protein therapy offers high efficiency, reliable protection and strong targeting capabilities. However, nearly 70% of proteins encoded by our genome struggle to cross the cell membrane, creating significant challenges in developing new protein-based therapies and functions [ ]. Scientists were able to address this problem because of the unique properties of COFs, such as their high surface region, non-toxicity, programmable structural design and lipid solubility. The unique fusion-based delivery strategy for effective protein administration inside the cells is achieved by grafting protein on COFs and then attaching polymers with strong biological affinity to the surfaces of COFs. One benefit of this protein delivery method is that the protein is neither chemically altered nor denatured while being delivered [ ]. COFs have shown great promise in delivering specific proteins, such as enzymes and therapeutic proteins. For instance, horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and cytochrome c (Cyt-c) are two model proteins commonly used to demonstrate COFs' potential for enzyme immobilization and delivery [ , ]. These proteins maintain their bioactivity after being encapsulated within the COF structure, making them suitable for catalytic and therapeutic applications. Additionally, insulin, a critical protein for diabetes management, has been successfully encapsulated in COFs, where it remains stable and is released in a controlled way to help manage blood glucose levels [ ]. This illustrates COFs’ ability to preserve protein integrity while providing controlled, stimulus-responsive release. COFs have also shown potential for delivering nucleic acids, such as plasmid DNA (pDNA), small interfering RNA (siRNA) and messenger RNA (mRNA). These nucleic acids play essential roles in gene therapy and RNA interference (RNAi) strategies for treating genetic disorders and cancers. COFs offer critical protection against enzymatic degradation, helping to maintain the stability and effectiveness of nucleic acids during delivery.
The advancements of COFs in protein and nucleic acid delivery are notable, allowing for high loading capacity, stability under environmental conditions, and precise and responsive release profiles based on factors like pH, temperature or light. By protecting against degradation and enabling targeted and controlled release, COFs have shown promising therapeutic results in preclinical models. Targeted gene expression can be effectively inhibited by nucleic acids, which are potent molecular tools. However, the practical use of nucleic acid medications is severely constrained by their properties, which include low transmembrane absorption, quick breakdown, poor targeting and significant safety risks. Nucleic acids added to the surface of COF NPs may improve their colloidal constancy and interface forces by preventing aggregation through steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion [ ]. On the exterior of COF NPs encumbered with DOX, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) labelled with Cy5 dye for mRNA appreciation was adsorbed. While the densely coated ssDNA could stop DOX from leaking from COF NPs and perform flexible properties, the DOX encumbered in the holes of COF based NPs could reinforce the interaction force and improve their mechanical and stable properties. Additionally, in vitro studies demonstrated a delivery impact specific to tumor cells. provided a potential method for controlling the automated force between COFs and biomolecules, as well as a widespread nano platform for cancer theranostics. 
COFs can also be functionalized with proteins or nucleic acids, to facilitate targeted interactions with biological entities. This process is known as bio-targeting. For imaging, medication delivery or therapeutic purposes, COFs can be targeted to certain cells or tissues by attaching antibodies that recognize particular cell surface markers or proteins overexpressed in particular illnesses [ ]. COFs can be linked to short peptides that have a natural affinity for specific molecules or receptors. These peptides act with remarkable precision, identifying and attaching to their target molecules, which helps guide the COFs directly to their intended biological targets [ , ]. By adding proteins and nucleic acids to COFs to make them functional, these materials may be precisely targeted to particular biological entities. This opens new possibilities for advanced biomedical applications. Furthermore, these functionalization techniques can be adjusted to match the particular needs of various biomedical applications, enabling flexible and adaptable bio-targeting systems. COFs can bind to or encapsulate specific proteins, supporting targeted protein therapy or enzyme immobilization, which are particularly effective for targeted applications. For instance, methoxy‑functionalized COF (COF–OMe) structures have been used to link with proteins, helping to preserve their structure and improve delivery efficiency [ ].

3.2

## Cancer cells/Tumor
When used as carriers, COFs can deliver chemotherapy drugs to cancer cells with controlled release and precision. Functionalizing COFs with targeting ligands, like antibodies or peptides, allows them to actively target tumors or accumulate specifically in tumor tissue through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Thanks to their porous structure and large surface area, COFs can release drugs gradually, which may reduce overall toxicity and boost therapeutic effectiveness. For PDT, which is a non-invasive cancer treatment method that uses light to activate photosensitizing chemicals and cause the production of ROS and consequent death of cancer cells, COFs can be employed as photosensitizers [ ]. Strong light-absorbing COFs can be used in PTT, a treatment that uses light-absorbing materials to produce localized heat in response to light irradiation, which causes the thermal ablation of cancer cells. When exposed to near-infrared (NIR) light [ , ], COFs can be functionalized with molecules or NPs that absorb NIR light, enabling efficient conversion of light to heat that causes localized hyperthermia and tumour elimination [ , ]. In the TME, functionalized COFs can stimulate immune cell recruitment and activation, strengthening anti-tumor immune responses and boosting the effectiveness of immunotherapy techniques [ ]. A water-dispersible n COF system, created through a cyanine-assisted exfoliation technique, features adjustable mechanical strength and a negative charge, which help target tumors through enhanced retention and extended blood circulation. This COF-based nanocomposite shows potential as a delivery system for the anticancer pro-drug cis-aconityl-DOX (CAD). In both lab and animal studies, these nanocomposites demonstrated strong anticancer effects and high drug-encapsulation efficiency [ ]. Functionalized COFs can serve as contrast agents for imaging techniques like fluorescence imaging, computed tomography (CT) scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and more. This allows for accurate cancer detection and helps in tracking treatment progress over time. Theranostic applications—where therapy and diagnostics are combined on a single platform—and multimodal imaging are made possible by COFs that can be customized to include imaging agents or NPs [ ].
2D COF itself has anticancer properties. For example, TrzCOF, an imine-based 2D COF has anticancer action in HCT-116 human colon cancer cells [ ]. TrzCOF can be endocytosed by cancer cells and has a nanorod-like morphology made up of self-assembling nanoflakes that are between 40 and 50 nm in size. TrzCOF is tested in a range of concentrations from 1 to 80 µg/ml for its cytotoxicity against human liver cancer cells HepG2, human colon cancer cells HCT-116, and mouse melanoma cells B16F10. With a 50% inhibitory potential (IC50) of ∼8.31 µg/ml, TrzCOF exhibits the best cell slaying efficacy in HCT-116 cells. This is equivalent to 5-fluorouracil's IC50 of ∼10.27 µg/ml in the similar cells [ ].

## Antimicrobial activity
Applications for COFs in antimicrobials are possible. Mitra and colleagues [ ] synthesised self-exfoliated ionic covalent organic nanosheets (iCONs) using guanidinium halide and 1,3,5-triformylphloroglucinol. Guanidinium units have the ability to create hydrogen bonds with phosphate oxoanions, which allows positively charged iCONs to disrupt the negatively charged phospholipid bilayer formed by bacteria. These nanosheets showed outstanding antimicrobial efficiency against both bacteria of Gram-positive and Gram-negative, according to antibacterial investigations. ## Enzyme catalysis
Biocatalysts have recently attracted a lot of attention as a sustainable, eco-friendly way to produce chemicals on a large scale. Researchers are exploring the use of COFs as molecular containers to store specific enzymes. In a fascinating study, Sun et al. investigated the use of an imine-based, 2D COF as a molecular container for enzyme immobilization, as shown in [ ]. The original precursor material employed by the writers was COF-OMe, which was made via condensation of 1,3,5-tris (4-aminophenyl)-benzene and dimethoxy terephthaldehyde. With a hole size of 3.3 nm, the mesoporous (1D) channel of COF–OMe is capable of immobilizing amino lipase PS, act as an enzyme that has a small length of around 3.2 nm, while preserving its activity [ ]. As shown in , the COF biocomposite demonstrated superior catalytic activity compared to free enzymes and other biocomposites made from materials like mesoporous silica and MOFs. Additionally, researchers found that the COFs’ reticular chemistry remains intact, even when adjusting the pore environment and modifying the activity of the immobilized enzymes. To further improve the enzyme's activity, durability, and reusability, an innovative method was developed using a hierarchically porous, 2D COF as the host material for enzyme immobilization [ ].

## Targeted and mechanisms for COF-based DDSs
Cancer Therapy: COFs have shown great promise in the field of oncology by improving drug delivery to tumors. Due to the EPR effect, COFs can accumulate in tumor tissues and bypass the challenges posed by the dense extracellular matrix and irregular tumor vasculature. COFs have been used to load chemotherapeutic drugs such as DOX and paclitaxel, allowing for controlled and sustained drug release, leading to reduced systemic toxicity and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, COFs can be functionalized by targeting ligands, such as antibodies or peptides, to specifically bind to receptors overexpressed on cancer cells, further enhancing selectivity and minimizing off-target effects. These systems have been successfully used in breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer models, showing significant tumor inhibition and improved drug bioavailability [ , ].
Neurological Disorders: The BBB presents a significant challenge for drug delivery to the CNS. COFs can be engineered with surface modifications to facilitate receptor-mediated transcytosis, enabling them to cross the BBB. For instance, COFs functionalized with transferrin or other ligands that target brain endothelial cells have shown success in delivering therapeutic agents into the brain. This method has been explored for treating neurodegenerative diseases like AD and Parkinson’s disease, as well as brain tumors. COFs loaded with neuroprotective drugs or chemotherapy agents have demonstrated improved effectiveness by allowing these drugs to reach the CNS, which is usually challenging due to the selective nature of the BBB [ , ].
Respiratory Diseases: In the treatment of respiratory diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, COFs have been explored for their ability to enhance drug delivery to the lungs. COFs can be engineered to deliver anti-inflammatory agents, bronchodilators, or gene therapy vectors directly to the lungs, improving treatment outcomes by reducing inflammation and tissue damage. Additionally, COFs have been shown to protect encapsulated drugs from enzymatic degradation, ensuring their stability during inhalation therapy. Their high surface area allows for high drug loading, while the controlled release mechanisms provide sustained therapeutic effects, reducing the need for frequent dosing [ ].

## Use of COF in crossing biological barriers
Compared with MOFs, COFs are built from robust covalent linkages rather than coordination bonds, resulting in enhanced chemical stability even under harsh conditions. In addition, their extended π–π conjugation endows COFs with distinctive photoelectrical properties and high charge–carrier mobility. These attributes make COFs promising candidates for advanced biomedical applications, including drug delivery, radiotherapy, immunotherapy and phototherapy [ ]. Furthermore, by adjusting their pore structure and surface functionality, COFs can be engineered to modulate or disrupt microbial biofilms, thus opening new possibilities in anti–infective therapies while retaining the inherent advantages of a stable, covalently bonded network.
4.1

## BBB
Within the CNS, the BBB is a highly selective semipermeable membrane that divides the extracellular fluid of the brain from the blood that is circulated. It controls how substances enter and exit the brain, which is essential for preserving brain homeostasis [ ]. The BBB, however, also poses a serious obstacle to medication delivery to the brain since it blocks the entry of numerous therapeutic substances, such as medications and NPs [ ]. The customizable features of COFs—including pore size, surface chemistry and biocompatibility—make them ideal candidates for delivering therapies to the brain and crossing the BBB. Acting as carriers, COFs can encapsulate therapeutic compounds and transport them across the BBB effectively [ ]. COFs enable for targeted delivery of drugs to specific areas of the brain through by loading drugs or imaging agents into their pores or attaching them to their surface. Ample room is available for drug loading on COFs due to their high surface area and porosity, and the integrity of the encapsulated payload is guaranteed during transit across the BBB due to their stability under physiological conditions [ ]. Thanks to their lipophilicity, structural flexibility and biocompatibility, COFs hold great potential for delivering drugs to the brain. However, research on using COFs for targeting the brain in living systems is still in its early stages, and COF-based medicines for brain delivery are just beginning to be developed [ ]. For example, BBB crossing and the treatment of brain metastasis from renal cancer (BMRC), a class of water dispersible PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF-1 nanocomposite that targets the BBB was created. Compared to rats given with an identical quantity of free pazopanib (PA,a multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor) [ ], BMRC mice treated with PA-loaded PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF-1 Nano composites substantially suppressed angiogenesis and tumor growth, extending their life time within cancer lesions as shown in . The pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, brain accumulation and effectiveness of PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF-1 were further evaluated in the BMRC mouse model. When injected intravenously, PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF-1@PA nanocomposites increased PA concentration in the brain’s parenchyma by 4.7 times compared to other treatment groups [ ]. To track the movement of the nanocomposites in the brain, researchers used ex vivo fluorescent imaging, and LC-MS/MS was used to monitor PA kinetics simultaneously. These findings suggest that external fluorescence imaging of nanocomposites could be a quick, efficient way to track brain drug delivery, confirming that PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF-1 nanocomposites effectively transport PA to the brain [ , ]. While no activation of system-associated toxicity was observed in vivo, the combination delivery of PA with the nanocomposites demonstrated great superiority in terms of prolonged survival time, decreased renal cell number in brain tumor lesions, improved antiangiogenesis and apoptosis effects synergistically at the low drug dose, and better modulation of the TME. Furthermore, the PA loaded amphiphilic PEG350-CCM@APTES-COF nanocomposite exhibits promising promise as a therapeutic strategy against a diverse array of CNS disorders [ , ]. 
AD is the most common neurological disorder worldwide. The choice of NPs for AD treatment depends on several factors, including their biocompatibility, pharmacokinetics, adjustability and ability to prevent β-amyloid (Aβ) fibrillation. COFs are a promising new class of NPs with strong bonds formed from C, O, N and H atoms, arranged in 2D or 3D structures [ , ]. Moreover, an energetic perspective can be used to examine the COF compounds' anti-β-amyloidogenic properties. Numerous research on the regulation of other attributes, such as the agent's hydrophobicity as the crucial component of the inhibitory effect, have been reported to date. The majority of the contacts between the COFs and Aβmonomer were evaluated to be unbounded interactions, such as van der Waals (vdW), electrostatic and hydrophobic collaborations. Electrostatic energy is the driving force behind effective interactions between nanomaterials and Aβ proteins. The overall interaction energy between COFs and Aβ is influenced by both vdW and electrostatic forces. However, in JUC 510 COF/Aβ and TPA-COF/Aβ interactions, electrostatic forces play a bigger role, while vdW forces are more important in Tp PaSO3Li COF and CH3 Li ImCOF interactions with Aβ. This suggests that the surface charges of both Aβ proteins and COFs significantly impact Aβ’s ability to self-assemble, as shown in . 
The BBB membrane is the primary obstacle that needs to be crossed by NPs in order to target specific tissues and deliver drugs. The penetration of COF NPs with various functional groups and sizes through the BBB membrane was simulated in order to examine the impact of surface charge and aspect ratio. Because glycosaminoglycan molecules are present in both the underlying basement membrane and the glycocalyx layer of the luminal side of endothelial cells, the BBB charge is regarded as negative. Remarkably, COFs functionalized with the amine group (positive charge) pass across the BBB membrane more readily than non-functionalized COFs do because of the BBB membrane's negative charge [ , , ].

## Nasal delivery barriers
The nasal cavity provides a promising route for systemic drug delivery, particularly due to its large surface area, high vascularization, and the potential for rapid drug absorption into the bloodstream. However, several obstacles limit the effectiveness of this route. Mucus, rapid mucociliary clearance, enzymatic activity and tight epithelial junctions all reduce the time drugs can remain and their ability to permeate the nasal mucosa. COFs can address these challenges by providing a platform for mucoadhesive delivery systems. Functionalizing COFs with hydrophilic groups or using surface modifications such as PEGylation can enhance their retention time in the nasal cavity by interacting with the mucus layer. Additionally, COFs can be designed to encapsulate drugs within their porous structure, protecting them from enzymatic degradation and enabling controlled release. COFs modified with chitosan or other mucoadhesive polymers have shown the ability to extend drug retention in the nasal cavity and improve drug permeability across the nasal barrier. Recent studies have also demonstrated that COF NPs can be functionalized with peptides or ligands targeting specific receptors on the nasal epithelium, making it easier for drugs to cross this barrier. This is especially relevant for brain-targeted therapies, as the nasal route provides a non-invasive way to bypass the BBB and deliver drugs directly to the CNS.
Acute lung injury (ALI) represents a significant economic and social burden, with an extraordinary incidence and frightening morbidity or mortality rate (30%–40%). Through a number of pathways, including record factors, pathways of protein kinase, and the genomic countenance of pro-inflammatory mediators, ROS is linked to inflammatory reactions and can trigger an overactive immune response. Furthermore, growth factor level of vascular endothelial in the lungs have been linked to the advancement of ALI, and this could be lowered by the increased ROS [ , ]. The biodegradable polymer OC—COF, which can eliminate H2O2, has been successfully developed as a promising dry powder inhaler (DPI) carrier for treating ALI. An anti-inflammatory agent was later added to create LIG@OC—COF, which demonstrated excellent aerodynamic properties and enhanced anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. This was due to the combined action of H2O2-scavenging bonds in OC—COF and the release of LIG, as shown in . Inhalation therapy with LIG@OC—COF significantly reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in ALI rats, allowing for a 5-fold reduction in LIG dosage [ ]. Moreover, LIG@OC—COF shielded ALI animals by controlling the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-Κb) signaling pathways' activity, as shown by Western blot analysis. All things considered, this study's findings strongly encourage the creation of ROS-approachable materials as DPI exporters for more effective ALI treatment. Due to its beneficial features, which include biodegradability, favorable biocompatibility, and admirable anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, OC—COF shows promise not only for the respiratory delivery of energetic pharmaceutical mediators for the cure of ALI but also for other ROS-related diseases like wound remedial, periodontitis, COPD and COVID-19. In addition, the synthesis of OC—COF is straightforward, repeatable, environmentally benign, and has a lot of potential for use in medicine.

## Skin mucosa barriers
The skin's outermost layer, the stratum corneum, acts as the main barrier against foreign substances, including drug molecules. While transdermal drug delivery has the advantage of being non-invasive with sustained drug release, it is often limited by the skin’s impermeability, especially for larger or hydrophilic drugs. Open skin wounds can result from burns, injuries, surgeries or conditions like diabetes. To tackle bacterial skin infections, various wound dressings have been developed recently, including films, electrospun nanofibers, membranes, sponges and hydrogels [ ]. Antibiotics, organic products (like chitin), and NPs (like Ag NPs) are among the antimicrobial agents added to the dressing material to provide or improve its antibacterial activity [ , , , ]. COFs present a novel approach to overcoming the skin barrier, involving using COFs engineered to deliver drugs by either penetrating the stratum corneum or releasing drugs in response to triggers like pH, temperature or light. With adjustable pore sizes and surface properties, COFs can improve the diffusion of therapeutic agents through skin layers. COF-based NPs can also be added to hydrogels, creams or films for sustained drug release at the application site. In wound healing, COFs functionalized with antimicrobial agents, such as Ag NPs or photosensitizers for PDT, can help prevent infection and support tissue regeneration. Studies have shown that COF-based hydrogels can maintain a moist environment that aids healing while releasing drugs in a controlled way. Furthermore, COFs can be designed to respond to environmental triggers at the wound site, such as increased temperature or pH changes due to infection, allowing for the on-demand release of antimicrobial agents. PDT is a potent treatment modality that destroys target pathogens by converting molecular oxygen into ROS. PDT has several key advantages over conventional antibiotic and radiation therapies: it provides localized treatment to minimize side effects, and it can be combined with other therapeutic approaches (such as photothermal therapy) for synergistic effects. In PDT, photosensitizers (PSs) like porphyrin species generate singlet oxygen (¹O₂) with a very short half-life (<0.4 µs) and limited diffusion distance (<0.02 µm), ensuring that its cytotoxic action is confined to the targeted area [ , , , , , ]. Importantly, COFs can serve as ideal carriers for PSs due to their robust, well-defined porous structures and high stability. By immobilizing PSs within the COF, the system can maximize ¹O₂ production exactly where needed, enhancing the overall therapeutic efficacy ( ). This integration of PDT with COF-based platforms directly links the unique properties of COFs to improved treatment outcomes. 
Phototherapeutic agents can be directly incorporated into COFs to create nano-agents for phototherapy, guided by advanced material design principles and engineering insights. Additionally, due to their natural porosity, COFs can serve as hosts to load drug molecules, like the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) ibuprofen (IBU, an anti-inflammatory drug), to function as an effective DDS. This allows for the reasonable integration of combination antibacterial and anti-inflammatory therapy on COF-platforms to obtain a therapeutic effect that is not achievable with immunotherapy.
COFs with antimicrobial agents can be used wound dressing to prevent infection by inhibiting the growth of pathogenic bacteria and fungi. COFs promote tissue regeneration and reduce inflammation to avoid infection. They can also reduce bleeding and accelerate wound closure.

## Mouth mucosa
In light of the unique challenges in treating infections on the mouth mucosa—where moist conditions and complex microbial communities can hinder conventional therapies—nanomaterial-based PDT offers a promising solution. COFs serve as ideal platforms for this approach because they are constructed from compounds with inherent photosensitizing and redox-active properties. Their planar and rigid structures facilitate the efficient generation of ROS when exposed to external light, while their microporous networks help deliver additional oxygen, thereby enhancing the photodynamic inactivation of oral pathogens [ ]. Moreover, by incorporating traditional photosensitizers such as porphyrins, COFs can be tailored as effective nanoscale agents for antibacterial PDT specifically aimed at the mouth mucosa [ ]. Complementing this strategy, iCONs—which resemble ultrathin nanofilms—have also been explored for oral applications. For instance, researchers developed an iCONs@PSF mixed matrix membrane by combining PSF with exfoliated guanidinium halide linker-based iCONs (TpTGCl, TpTGBr and TpTGI). The exceptional antibacterial performance of this membrane was attributed to the electrostatic attraction between the positively charged guanidine groups on the membrane surface and the negatively charged phospholipid bilayers of bacteria such as gram-positive (S. aureus) and gram-negative (Escherichia coli), common culprits in oral infections [ ]. Together, these innovative materials and strategies underscore the potential of COF-based platforms in achieving effective antibacterial PDT on the mouth mucosa.

## Gastrointestinal barrier
Oral insulin delivery remains a major challenge in diabetes treatment because of the harsh environment in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Insulin, being a protein, is easily broken down by enzymes in the stomach and intestines, and its large size makes it difficult to absorb through the intestinal lining. Despite these challenges, oral insulin delivery holds great promise as a noninvasive option that could transform diabetes care, reducing the need for frequent injections and making insulin therapy more appealing [ , ]. Natural insulin is first delivered to the liver, closely mimicking its biological release; in contrast, subcutaneously injected insulin bypasses this hepatic pathway, which may lead to peripheral hyperinsulinemia and associated complications [ , , , ].
Due to their biocompatibility and tunable properties, COFs have emerged as promising carriers for oral insulin delivery. Their stable, porous structures encapsulate insulin, shielding it from enzymatic degradation in the stomach while enabling controlled release in the intestines. Moreover, COFs can be functionalized to respond to pH variations along the GI tract, ensuring that insulin is released where absorption is optimal. This strategy not only improves insulin stability and bioavailability but also enhances its therapeutic efficacy. For instance, Benyettou et al. [ ] showed that TTA-DFP-nCOF—a COF built from 4,4′,4″-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)trianiline and 2,6-diformylpyridine—achieves 65 wt% insulin loading, excellent biocompatibility, and robust protection of insulin under gastric acidity. By exploiting the spatially precise pore architecture of nCOFs, subsequent work used a seeded-growth protocol to prepare highly crystalline imine-linked nanoparticles that, when given orally to T1D rats, fully normalized blood–glucose levels without systemic toxicity. These studies underscore the potential of COF platforms for controlled, glucose-responsive oral insulin delivery [ ].
The highly organized structure of nCOFs enables controlled loading and release of large molecules like drugs proteins, and enzymes, thanks to their spatially precise, 2D or 3D pore design. Additionally, nCOFs offer great flexibility in molecular structure and functional design, making them adaptable and highly responsive to their surroundings [ , ]. In contrast, illustrates a distinct COF application: the DahTph-membrane. This ibuprofen-loaded antimicrobial membrane (IBU@DahTph-membrane) generates reactive oxygen species under visible light and simultaneously releases ibuprofen, yielding synergistically enhanced antibacterial efficacy. COFs have emerged drug molecules to encapsulate insulin molecules and protect them from enzymatic degradation in GI environment or track. The porous structure of COFs allows for entrapment of insulin and release insulin in controlled manner [ , ]. COFs can be modified to improve insulin absorption across the intestinal lining by adding ligands that interact with receptors on intestinal cells. These modifications help COFs transport insulin into the bloodstream, imitating the natural release of insulin by the pancreas. In studies with diabetic models, COF-based insulin delivery systems showed better blood sugar control, lowering glucose levels and providing sustained insulin release over time.

## COF-Enabled strategies for overcoming biological barriers in drug delivery
Biological barriers such as TME, BBB and mucosal surfaces present significant challenges to effective drug delivery. For instance, the TME is characterized by abnormal vasculature—with disorganized, twisted blood vessels and incomplete endothelial junctions—that generates an EPR effect, yet also leads to uneven drug distribution, hypoxic regions, and nutrient deprivation [ ]. Furthermore, the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) composed of collagen, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans sequesters therapeutic agents and alters their pharmacokinetic profiles, while elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) and impaired lymphatic drainage further hinder drug penetration [ , ]. In addition, immune cells within the TME, such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), can actively internalize NPs, reducing the payload that reaches cancer cells [ ].
COFs have emerged as promising nanocarriers to address these challenges due to their tunable porosity, structural adaptability, and ease of surface modification. Their robust, porous architectures enable the encapsulation and protection of therapeutic agents, ensuring enhanced bioavailability and controlled release. For brain-targeted delivery, for example, COFs can be functionalized with targeting ligands and coated with PEG to cross the BBB, delivering chemotherapeutic agents directly to brain tumors while minimizing systemic toxicity. In the TME, COFs can exploit the EPR effect to achieve higher drug accumulation; their adjustable structures permit the conjugation of ligands for active targeting and facilitate steady, controlled drug release—for instance, COFs loaded with DOX have demonstrated improved penetration and sustained therapeutic effects in deep tumor tissues.
Moreover, for non-invasive routes such as nasal and transdermal delivery, COFs can be engineered with mucoadhesive properties to enhance retention and absorption. Nasally delivered COFs have shown potential in bypassing enzymatic degradation, allowing drugs to reach the brain via the olfactory pathway—a promising strategy for treating neurological disorders. Similarly, COF-based transdermal patches have improved drug absorption through the skin, offering a convenient alternative for systemic delivery.
By integrating an in-depth understanding of these biological barriers with advanced COF design, researchers are developing more effective DDSs that hold great promise for clinical applications across a range of challenging therapeutic contexts.

## Roles and mechanisms of COFs in cancer treatment
COFs have gained significant attention as multifunctional platforms for cancer therapy due to their exceptional properties, including high porosity, structural tunability, and the ability to be functionalized with therapeutic and imaging agents. These features make COFs highly effective in drug delivery, PDT, PTT and combined therapeutic and diagnostic (theranostic) applications. Below are the key achievements and mechanisms through which COFs contribute to advancements in cancer treatment:
5.1

## Drug delivery and chemotherapy
COFs make excellent carriers for chemotherapy drugs, thanks to their large surface area and well-defined pores. They can encapsulate a range of anticancer drugs, like DOX, paclitaxel, and cisplatin, and provide controlled release directly at the tumor site. Their porous structure helps protect the drugs from breaking down too soon, ensuring they remain stable during circulation. This controlled release not only reduces toxicity in the body but also improves the effectiveness of chemotherapy [ ]. Additionally, COFs can be modified with targeting ligands like folic acid or antibodies to achieve active targeting of cancer cells. This allows COFs to selectively accumulate in tumors through the EPR effect, which exploits the leaky vasculature of tumors. For example, a COF-based system loaded with cisplatin and modified with folic acid demonstrated enhanced accumulation in ovarian cancer cells, leading to a marked increase in therapeutic efficacy compared to conventional drug delivery methods [ ]. Furthermore, the adjustable pore size of COFs ensures that drug molecules can be released in a controlled manner, making them ideal for sustained drug delivery in cancer therapy [ ].

5.2

## PDT and PTT
COFs show great promise as carriers for photosensitizers in PDT, a non-invasive cancer treatment that uses light-activated compounds to produce ROS, which cause targeted cancer cell death. The porous structure of COFs allows them to carry a high amount of photosensitizers, like porphyrins or phthalocyanines, while protecting them from degradation until they are activated by light [ ]. One key advantage of using COFs in PDT is their ability to facilitate precise light-triggered ROS generation at the tumor site. For instance, COFs functionalized with zinc phthalocyanine, a well-known photosensitizer, exhibited enhanced ROS generation and selective tumor cell death when exposed to NIR light. This not only increased the efficacy of the treatment but also minimized damage to healthy tissues [ ]. The biocompatibility and tunability of COFs make them suitable candidates for PDT, allowing for improved therapeutic outcomes with minimal side effects.
In PTT, COFs can be functionalized with light-absorbing molecules to convert NIR light into heat, effectively ablating cancer cells through localized hyperthermia. COFs’ high thermal stability and capacity for functionalization make them ideal platforms for PTT [ ]. When exposed to NIR light, functionalized COFs can generate enough heat to destroy cancer cells while sparing nearby healthy tissue. For instance, a COF functionalized with gold NPs showed high efficiency in converting light to heat, resulting in successful tumor elimination in live models [ ]. This approach offers a non-invasive alternative to conventional cancer treatments, such as surgery or radiation, and can be combined with other therapies, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, to enhance overall treatment efficacy. The ability of COFs to maintain structural integrity under high temperatures ensures their long-term stability during the PTT process [ ].

5.3

## Tumor targeting and overcoming biological barriers
One of the major challenges in cancer treatment is the ability to efficiently target tumor cells while minimizing off-target effects. COFs can be engineered with ligands such as antibodies, peptides, or aptamers that recognize specific biomarkers overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells. This active targeting mechanism ensures that COFs selectively bind to tumor cells, increasing drug accumulation at the tumor site and reducing side effects [ ]. COFs are effective at overcoming biological barriers in the TME, like the dense extracellular matrix and high IFP, which often limit drug penetration. Thanks to their strength and flexible structure, COFs can penetrate deeper into tumor tissues than traditional DDSs. Plus, their modular design allows for multi-functionalization, enabling COFs to carry both therapeutic agents and targeting ligands, which boosts their ability to home in on tumors.

5.4

## Theranostics and imaging
Theranostic applications, which combine therapeutic and diagnostic functions on a single platform, have become a key area of interest in cancer research. COFs can be functionalized with contrast agents for imaging techniques, such as MRI, fluorescence imaging, or CT, enabling real-time tracking of drug delivery and monitoring of treatment progress [ ]. COFs functionalized with gadolinium, a common MRI contrast agent, have demonstrated excellent imaging capabilities, allowing for precise visualization of tumor sites while simultaneously delivering chemotherapy drugs. This dual functionality not only improves the accuracy of treatment but also reduces the need for multiple separate procedures [ ]. Beyond imaging, COFs can carry radionuclides for targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT), where radioactive isotopes are delivered directly to tumors for localized radiation treatment. This combined 'theranostic' approach improves both diagnostic precision and treatment effectiveness, offering a well-rounded solution for cancer care [ ].

5.5

## Immunotherapy and COF-Induced immune activation
Recent studies have explored the role of COFs in enhancing the immune response against cancer. Functionalized COFs can stimulate immune cell recruitment and activation within the TME, boosting the effectiveness of immunotherapy. By presenting tumor antigens to immune cells, COFs can enhance the immune system's ability to recognize and destroy cancer cells [ ]. In combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, COFs have shown potential in overcoming immune suppression in the TME, leading to improved outcomes in cancer immunotherapy [ ].

## Conclusion
In this review article, we have provided a thorough analysis of the various 2Dand 3D COFs that are pertinent to biological applications. The wide and accessible pores, high surface area, ordered structure, biodegradability, and tailor able surface of these porous organic polymeric materials COFs make them particularly advantageous for biological applications. Furthermore, COFs are a great option for bio sensing and bio imaging due to their huge surface area and high π-electron density. With their enormous surface areas, porosity, and customizable mechanical properties, COFs are a newly developed class of highly controlled produced porous materials that hold great promise for use in drug delivery and anticancer applications. It is expected that nCOFs will soon play a significant role in the effective targeted delivery of medicines and biomolecules that are multi-Nano sized. Numerous COF-based Nanomedicines are being investigated for use in the diagnosis and treatment of cancer in vivo. nCOFs have the potential to improve their capacity for drug loading and cancer cell targeting even more. The promising applications of COF in photo-immunotherapy and pyroptosis offer great promise for more in-depth study in this field. Because COFs can be easily transformed via stable covalent bonds, pre- and post-synthetic alterations of nCOFs are continuously improving biocompatibility and various therapies. COF-based NPs offer strong biocompatibility and are highly effective for drug delivery. With their porous organic structure, COFs play a vital role in various treatments, including chemotherapy, PDT, immunotherapy and PTT. They help overcome challenging biological barriers, such as the BBB, barriers in oral drug delivery, skin mucosa, and nasal pathways.
