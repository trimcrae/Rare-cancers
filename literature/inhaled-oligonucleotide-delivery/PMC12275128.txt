## Introduction
With the accelerating pace of global population aging, central nervous system degenerative disorders, brain tumors, cerebral inflammation, and other neurological diseases have become grave threats to human health. As research on novel drug delivery systems—including liposomes, nanoparticles, micelles, and other nanocarriers—advances, brain-targeted drug delivery systems (BTDDS) have emerged as a promising strategy to address the therapeutic challenges of brain diseases. These systems hold significant promise for facilitating efficient trans-blood-brain barrier (BBB) delivery of existing therapeutics, thereby overcoming a key bottleneck in central nervous system drug development ( ).
Studies have demonstrated that brain-targeted liposomes enhance drug permeability across the BBB via receptor-mediated active transport ( ). However, single-receptor-mediated liposomes often fail to escape lysosomal entrapment, a limitation that restricts their utility in brain drug delivery ( ). Beyond abundant receptor expression, the BBB exhibits a highly negative charge profile ( ). Positively charged cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) facilitate cell membrane translocation through electrostatic interactions and possess lysosomal escape capability ( ; ). Nevertheless, CPPs lack tissue and cell selectivity, and their use as monotherapeutics is associated with substantial toxicity, thereby limiting their application in the construction of brain-targeted liposomes.
Dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes designed via the integration of receptor-mediated and adsorption-mediated mechanisms are expected to surmount the limitations of single-receptor or single-adsorption-mediated systems, thereby substantially enhancing blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability. The construction of such dual-mediated liposomes primarily involves two strategies: 1) conjugating targeting ligands and cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) separately onto the liposomal surface to form biligand-modified liposomes; 2) pre-conjugating targeting ligands to CPPs followed by liposomal surface modification to generate monoligand liposomes ( ; ; ).
Kumar et al. reported conjugating the positively charged R9 peptide to the C-terminus of rabies virus glycoprotein 29 (RVG29) to facilitate siRNA delivery across the blood-brain barrier, achieving efficient gene silencing in the brain ( ). However, critical knowledge gaps persist in the following areas: 1) the feasibility of direct conjugation between targeting peptides and cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs); 2) whether such conjugation compromises targeting efficiency; 3) whether the BBB transport mechanisms of monoligand-modified liposomes differ from those of biligand liposomes; and 4) whether BBB permeability varies between dual-mediated liposomes constructed via these two strategies. These questions demand urgent investigation to advance the field.
In this study, leveraging the high expression of N-acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) and the negative charge profile of the BBB ( ; ), we selected RVGP (a mimic of RVG29) as the brain-targeting peptide and the positively charged R9 as the CPP ( ; ). We fabricated two types of dual-mediated liposomes: monoligand-modified liposomes (RVGPR9-SSL) and biligand-modified liposomes (RVGP-R9-SSL). Through comparative analyses, we aim to characterize their BBB permeability, transport mechanisms, and in vivo distribution, with the goal of identifying the optimal dual-mediated liposome for brain targeting. This work provides critical data to guide the rational design and translational application of dual-mediated brain-targeted delivery systems.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Materials
1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol)-1000 (DSPE-PEG1000), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol)-2000 (DSPE-PEG2000), and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[succinimidyl (polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000-NHS) were purchased from Corden Pharma (Eichenweg, Switzerland). Soybean phospholipids and cholesterol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The RVGP peptide (n-CDGFCSSRGKR-c), R9 peptide, and RVGPR9 peptide (n-CDGFCSSRGKRRRRRRRRR-c) were synthesized by Xinpeptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanning, China).

2.2

## Cell lines
Brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs), HeLa cells, Neuro 2a cells, and C6 cells were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. BMVECs were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin (Invitrogen), and 100 U/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen). HeLa cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium 1640 (RPMI-1640; Gibco, Invitrogen) containing 10 % FBS (Gibco, Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. Neuro 2a cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10 % FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin.

2.3

## Preparation and characterization of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL
First, the key intermediate compounds DSPE-PEG1000-R9, DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP, and DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 were synthesized. At pH 7.4, DSPE-PEG-NHS (molecular weights 1000 and 2000) underwent nucleophilic substitution with R9 peptide, RVGP peptide, or RVGPR9 peptide in the presence of DMAP as a catalyst. Reaction products were characterized by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS, linear mode).
For the preparation of RVGPR9-SSL, predetermined quantities of soybean phosphatidylcholine (SPC), cholesterol (Chol), DSPE-PEG2000, and DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 were weighed and added to a round-bottom flask. The molar ratio of components was set as follows: SPC: Chol: DSPE-PEG2000: DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 = 100: 50: (8 − X): X, where the variable X denoted the molar percentage of DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 (all ratios in mol/mol). By adjusting X, liposomes with different molar fractions of DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 were prepared. For example, X = 2 corresponded to a 2 mol% density of DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9 in RVGPR9-SSL. The mixture was dissolved in chloroform, and the solvent was evaporated under vacuum to form a lipid film. Following hydration with an aqueous buffer, the liposomes were extruded through 200 nm polycarbonate membranes to yield RVGPR9-SSL.
RVGP-R9-SSL was prepared following the same procedure as described above. The molar ratio was optimized as SPC: Chol:DSPE-PEG2000:DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP:DSPE-PEG1000-R9 = 100:50:(8 − X − Y):X:Y, where X and Y denote the molar percentages of DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP and DSPE-PEG1000-R9, respectively (all ratios in mol/mol). By varying X and Y, liposomes with different molar fractions of DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP or DSPE-PEG1000-R9 were prepared. The sum of X and Y was maintained below 8 mol%.
Fluorescently labeled liposome formulations were prepared using the same protocol, including those loaded with coumarin-6 (Cou-6), 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO), 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI), and 1,1-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR).

2.4

## Investigation of blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability of the two dual-mediated liposomes and evaluation of BBB integrity
BMVECs were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well into the upper chamber of transwell plates. A 1.5 mL cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 2 mL of BMVEC-specific medium was added to the lower chamber. The transwell plates were then incubated in a 5 % CO₂, 37 °C incubator for 3–7 days to establish the blood-brain barrier (BBB) model, with medium replacement every 2 days. Model integrity was validated using three methods: a 4-h fluorescent leakage assay, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of intercellular tight junctions.
Following successful establishment of the BBB model, the original medium in the upper chamber of transwell plates was discarded, and 1.5 mL of RVGPR9-SSL or RVGP-R9-SSL solution was added to the respective upper chambers. Subsequently, 2 mL of phenol red-free basal medium was added to the lower chambers. The liquid levels in the upper and lower chambers were maintained equal to eliminate the effect of hydrostatic pressure differences on permeability. At 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h post-incubation, 2 mL of solution was withdrawn from the lower chambers, followed by replacement with 2 mL of phenol red-free basal medium. The blood-brain barrier permeability (Pe) of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL at each time point was calculated using the following formula:Pe%=Ct×V+V∑CiVdonor×C0×100%where Ct is the concentration of the sample in the receiving pool at time t, and C0 is the initial concentration of the sample in the donor pool. Vdonor is the sample volume in the donor pool, and V is the sample volume in the receiving pool.
Following the BBB permeability experiment, 2 mL of samples were collected from each transwell receiver pool for subsequent analysis. The receiver pool samples and fluorescently labeled RVGPR9-SSL/RVGP-R9-SSL formulations [RVGPR9-SSL(DiO), RVGPR9-SSL(DiI), RVGPR9-SSL(DiO + DiI), RVGP-R9-SSL(DiO), RVGP-R9-SSL(DiI), RVGP-R9-SSL(DiO + DiI)] were co-incubated with BMVECs, respectively. Fluorescence spectroscopy and laser confocal microscopy were employed to evaluate fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) effects, thereby assessing the structural integrity of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL post-BBB traversal.

2.5

## Transport mechanisms of the two dual-mediated liposomes in BMVECs
SSL(Cou-6), RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6), and RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6) were prepared using the methods described above. Brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs) were pre-incubated with endocytosis inhibitors (chlorpromazine, filipin III, sodium azide, or RVG29) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with inhibitor-containing liposome solutions (SSL(Cou-6), RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6), or RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6)) for 1 h. Control groups were treated identically but without inhibitors. Flow cytometry was employed to quantify cellular uptake of each liposome formulation.
Subsequently, BMVEC lysosomes were labeled with the fluorescent probe Lyso-Tracker Red, and endoplasmic reticulum was labeled with ER-Tracker Red, following the manufacturers' protocols. SSL(Cou-6), R9-SSL(Cou-6), RVGP-SSL(Cou-6), RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6), and RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6) were then coincubated with the fluorescently labeled BMVECs. At 1, 2, and 4 h post-incubation, laser confocal microscopy was employed to visualize the colocalization of each liposome formulation with BMVEC lysosomes and endoplasmic reticulum.

2.6

## Transport mechanisms of the two dual-mediated liposomes in C6s
SSL(Cou-6), RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6), and RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6) were prepared as described above. C6 cells were pre-incubated with endocytosis inhibitors (chlorpromazine, filipin III, or sodium azide) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with inhibitor-containing liposome solutions (SSL(Cou-6), RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6), or RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6)) for 1 h. Control groups were treated identically but without inhibitors. Flow cytometry was employed to quantify the cellular uptake of each liposome formulation by C6 cells.
Lysosomes of C6 cells were labeled with the fluorescent probe Lyso-Tracker Red, and endoplasmic reticulum was labeled with ER-Tracker Red, following the manufacturers' protocols. Colocalization analysis of each liposome formulation with C6 cell lysosomes and endoplasmic reticulum followed the same protocol as described in Section 2.5.
Doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded liposomes [SSL(DOX), RVGPR9-SSL(DOX), RVGP-R9-SSL(DOX)] were prepared as described above. C6 cell nuclei were labeled with the fluorescent probe Hoechst 33342 following the manufacturer's protocol. Colocalization analysis of SSL(DOX), RVGPR9-SSL(DOX), and RVGP-R9-SSL(DOX) with C6 cell nuclei was performed using the same laser confocal microscopy protocol described in Section 2.5.

2.7

## Cellular uptake of the two dual-mediated liposomes in a BBB/C6s co-culture model
C6 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well into six-well plates. Following uniform adherent growth, transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size) were placed into the wells, and brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs) were seeded onto the upper chambers of the transwells. The C6 cells and BMVECs were co-cultured for 12 h to establish a BBB/C6 co-culture model. Flow cytometry was employed to quantify the cellular uptake of liposome formulations by C6 cells in the co-culture model.

2.8

## Investigation of distribution in vivo
SSL(DiR), R9-SSL(DiR), RVGP-SSL(DiR), RVGPR9-SSL(DiR), and RVGP-R9-SSL(DiR) were prepared as described above. All nude mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facilities maintained at a constant temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, 50 % ± 10 % humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Individually ventilated cages (IVCs) were used for housing. For biodistribution studies, liposomes were administered via tail vein injection (200 μL per mouse) in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The biodistribution was observed using the in vivo optical imaging system at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72 h post-liposome injection. Nude mice were sacrificed 72 h after injection, and the major organs were harvested for ex vivo imaging. Euthanasia was performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, involving deep anesthesia by inhalation of isoflurane (5 % induction, 2 % maintenance) until loss of the righting reflex, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure humane endpoints. Images were processed using Living Imaging software.

2.9

## Neurotoxicity evaluation of RVGPR9-SSL (DOX)
Doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded liposomes [SSL(DOX), RVGPR9-SSL(DOX)] were prepared as described previously. Twelve male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200 ± 10 g) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 3) using a random number generator. Animals were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facilities maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10 % humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. The experimental groups received SSL(DOX) or RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) via tail vein injection at 5 mg DOX/kg body weight, administered every other day for 7 days. The negative control group received saline, while the positive control group was treated with colistin (20 mg/kg/day) via the same route for 7 consecutive days. The rats were sacrificed 24 h after the last tail vein administration. At the experimental endpoint, euthanasia was performed in accordance with animal welfare guidelines. Specifically, rats were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (5 % induction, 2 % maintenance) until loss of righting reflex, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure rapid and painless death. Brain tissues were immediately dissected, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and processed for Nissl staining to assess neuronal integrity.
Concurrently, blood samples were collected via retro-orbital venous plexus puncture under isoflurane anesthesia. Serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C) and analyzed using commercial kits for alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), and acetylcholinesterase (AChE) according to the manufacturers' protocols.

2.10

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as ⁎P < 0.05, ⁎⁎P < 0.01, and ⁎⁎⁎P < 0.001. Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 8.0.1).

## Result and discussion
3.1

## Characterization of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL
In order to prepare two dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes (RVGPR9-SSL, RVGP-R9-SSL) ( a, b), we first synthesized three leading compounds, DSPE-PEG1000-R9, DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP and DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9, through nucleophilic reactions. The products were detected and analyzed by MALDI-TOF-MS, and the results were shown in Fig. S1. The MALDI-TOF-MS spectra displayed molecular ion clusters centered at m/z 3279.96, 4213.55, and 5482.50, corresponding to the characteristic peaks of DSPE-PEG1000-R9, DSPE-PEG2000-RVGP, and DSPE-PEG2000-RVGPR9, respectively. 
The number and spatial distribution of cell membrane receptors are dynamic ( ). Upon approach of specific ligand molecules, receptors undergo lateral movement within the membrane, leading to a “clustering” phenomenon ( ; ; ; ). Studies have demonstrated that the surface ligand modification density of liposomes is critical for target cell uptake, necessitating its optimization. In this study, we optimized the ligand modification densities of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL using BMVECs, nAChR-expressing Neuro 2a cells, and nAChR-negative HeLa cells. The results showed that the optimal RVGPR9 modification density for RVGPR9-SSL was 2 % ( c,d), and the optimal RVGP density for RVGP-SSL was 2 % ( e,f). To minimize non-specific uptake, the R9 modification density on RVGP-R9-SSL was also optimized to balance maximal uptake in BMVECs with minimal uptake in HeLa cells. This optimization identified 4 % as the optimal R9 density ( g-i).
The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL are summarized in Table S1. Both liposomes exhibited a mean particle size of ∼100 nm, zeta potential near −4.5 mV, and PDI values below 0.2, indicating homogeneous size distributions and excellent dispersibility. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging ( j) revealed that both liposomes displayed well-defined spherical morphologies. Notably, RVGP-R9-SSL exhibited increased surface roughness compared to RVGPR9-SSL, likely attributed to the conjugation of PEG chains with distinct lengths on its surface. AFM measurements showed particle sizes of ∼97 nm for RVGPR9-SSL and ∼ 100 nm for RVGP-R9-SSL, which were in good agreement with dynamic light scattering data obtained using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical; j).
Subsequently, the targeting capabilities of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL were investigated using brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs, nAChR-expressing components of the BBB), Neuro 2a cells (positive control, high nAChR expression), and HeLa cells (negative control, nAChR-negative) as model systems. The cell targeting factor (CTF)—defined as the ratio of liposome uptake in target cells (BMVECs/Neuro 2a) to non-target cells (HeLa)—was used to quantify targeting efficiency. As shown in Fig. S2a-g, SSL and R9-SSL exhibited CTF values ∼1, indicating non-specific uptake with minimal target selectivity. In contrast, RVGP-SSL, RVGPR9-SSL, and RVGP-R9-SSL displayed CTF values >1, demonstrating that RVGP peptide modification significantly enhanced cell targeting. Notably, dual-mediated liposomes (RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL) showed higher CTF values than single-ligand RVGP-SSL, evidencing a synergistic targeting effect of RVGP and R9 peptides. The R9 on RVGPR9-SSL is conjugated to the RVGP peptide at the end distal to the liposome, while the proximal end is linked to a PEG chain, resulting in no direct exposure. This design helps avoid non-selective interactions of R9. As demonstrated by the results in Fig. S2 a-g, RVGPR9-SSL exhibits significantly higher targeting specificity than R9-SSL. The mean cellular uptake of RVGPR9-SSL in target cells is higher than that of RVGP-SSL, indicating that R9 promotes cellular uptake. This may be attributed to the dynamic conformational adjustment of the lipid bilayer and PEG chains ( ). When RVGP on RVGPR9-SSL interacts with nAChR on the target cell surface, it shortens the distance between positively charged R9 and the negatively charged cell membrane. Concurrently, dynamic conformational adjustments of the lipid bilayer and PEG chains enable electrostatic interactions between R9 and the cell membrane, thereby promoting the cellular uptake of RVGPR9-SSL. Future studies will further explore the relationship between these conformational changes and cellular uptake. Notably, despite both being dual-mediated liposomes, RVGPR9-SSL exhibited a significantly higher cell targeting factor (CTF) than RVGP-R9-SSL, indicating that distinct ligand modification strategies (i.e., single-chain dual-ligand conjugation in RVGPR9-SSL vs. separate PEG-conjugated ligands in RVGP-R9-SSL) lead to divergent targeting efficiencies. This discrepancy may be attributed to differential intracellular trafficking pathways induced by their structural configurations. Additionally, RVGP-SSL, RVGPR9-SSL, and RVGP-R9-SSL showed higher uptake rates in Neuro 2a cells than in BMVECs, likely due to the higher surface expression of nAChR in Neuro 2a cells (Fig. S2a-d, g).

3.2

## Investigation of blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability of the two dual-mediated liposomes and evaluation of BBB integrity
An in vitro blood-brain barrier (BBB) model was first constructed ( a). A 4-h leakage test revealed that the liquid level difference in each BBB model group remained at 0.5 cm, with trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) values exceeding 200 Ω·cm2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was employed to characterize the cellular morphology of each BBB model group ( b, Fig. S3a), demonstrating that BMVECs formed densely packed monolayers with well-defined tight junctions between cells. Collectively, these results confirmed the successful establishment of the in vitro BBB model, which was suitable for evaluating the BBB permeability of liposome formulations. 
The integrity of the in vitro BBB was evaluated after 4-h incubation with RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL. Following incubation, the BBB model maintained intact structural integrity, as evidenced by well-defined tight junctions between BMVECs, indicating that the targeted liposomes did not compromise BBB integrity over the 4-h incubation period (Fig. S3b,c).
The BBB permeability and post-transport integrity of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL are essential for evaluating their brain-targeted drug delivery efficacy. To assess the BBB permeability rates and structural integrity of dual-mediated liposomes post-transport, FRET fluorescent probe pairs (DiO and DiI) were encapsulated in RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL. The results showed that the 4-h cumulative BBB permeability of RVGP-R9-SSL was 7.9 %, whereas that of RVGPR9-SSL was 10.3 %—1.3-fold higher than RVGP-R9-SSL ( c). These findings suggested that distinct ligand modification strategies influence the BBB permeability of brain-targeted liposomes, potentially attributable to differences in transmembrane transport pathways.
Upon co-incubation of RVGPR9-SSL(DiO + DiI) and RVGP-R9-SSL(DiO + DiI) with BMVECs, FRET signals were maintained, indicating that both liposomes retained structural integrity after cellular uptake ( d, e). Following 4-hourours co-incubation with the BBB model, samples from the transwell receiving pool were incubated with BMVECs, and FRET effects were observed in both RVGPR9-SSL(DiO + DiI) and RVGP-R9-SSL(DiO + DiI) ( f, g, Fig. S3d, e). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL maintain structural integrity after crossing the BBB, highlighting their potential to deliver encapsulated drugs to brain lesion sites.

3.3

## Transport mechanisms of the two dual-mediated liposomes in BMVECs
The above results demonstrated that RVGPR9-SSL exhibited significantly higher CTF values and BBB permeability than RVGP-R9-SSL. This discrepancy is likely attributed to divergent transmembrane transport mechanisms of the two dual-mediated liposomes. Thus, this study aimed to elucidate the endocytic pathways of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL in BMVECs. Emerging evidence indicates that the transmembrane transport of nanoparticles is closely associated with the type of targeting ligand. Nanoparticles primarily enter cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and non-clathrin/ non-caveolin-dependent pathways ( ). The endocytic mechanism of nanoparticles directly dictates their intracellular trafficking trajectories.
In this study, the transport mechanisms of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL in BMVECs (the primary cellular component of the BBB) were investigated using uptake inhibition assays and organelle colocalization analyses. Chlorpromazine (a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), filipin III (a caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), RVG29 (an nAChR antagonist), and sodium azide (an energy metabolism inhibitor) were employed to assess their inhibitory effects on liposome internalization in BMVECs. The results demonstrated that SSL(Cou-6) primarily entered BMVECs via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, whereas RVGPR9-SSL(Cou-6) utilized receptor- and caveolae-mediated pathways. In contrast, RVGP-R9-SSL(Cou-6) relied on receptor- and clathrin-mediated endocytosis ( b). Notably, all three liposomes exhibited energy-dependent uptake ( b), indicating that modifications in liposomal surface ligand conjugation significantly alter cellular internalization mechanisms. 
The internalization of RVGPR9-SSL primarily involved receptor- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, likely attributable to the synergistic effect of the RVGPR9 peptide. In contrast, RVGP-R9-SSL uptake predominantly occurred via receptor- and clathrin-mediated endocytosis. This mechanistic discrepancy may be attributed to the distinct conjugation modes in RVGP-R9-SSL: RVGP linked to a long-chain PEG first binds to nAChR, triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis, while R9 conjugated to a short-chain PEG approaches the cell membrane upon receptor engagement, initiating clathrin-mediated endocytosis driven by R9's cationic charge ( ). The distinct uptake pathways observed between RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL are primarily attributable to their conformational differences. Studies indicate that during receptor-ligand interaction (receptor-mediated cellular uptake), concomitant dynamic conformational changes in the PEG chains can induce caveolin aggregation and stabilize caveolin structures, thereby triggering the caveolae-mediated uptake pathway. The interaction of RVGPR9-SSL with nAChR on the BMVEC surface brings the positively charged R9 into proximity with the negatively charged membrane. This proximity induces conformational alterations in the PEG chains, consequently activating the caveolae-mediated pathway ( ; ; ). In contrast, within RVGP-R9-SSL, the RVGP and R9 are conjugated to distinct PEG chains. Upon interaction with nAChR on BMVEC cells, electrostatic interactions occur between R9 and the membrane. However, this configuration does not elicit dynamic PEG chain conformational changes. Consequently, uptake proceeds primarily via electrostatic interaction-triggered clathrin-mediated endocytosis and fails to initiate the caveolae-mediated pathway ( ; ).
Studies have shown that the uptake mechanism of nanoparticles influences their intracellular transport pathways. Nanoparticles entering cells via clathrin- or caveolae-mediated endocytosis primarily undergo intracellular transport through lysosome- or endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-mediated pathways ( ; ). Next, we investigated the transport mechanisms of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL in BMVECs. Colocalization assays of liposomes with lysosomes or ER in BMVECs revealed that RVGPR9-SSL exhibited a significantly higher colocalization rate with the ER than with lysosomes, indicating preferential transport via the ER pathway. In contrast, RVGP-R9-SSL primarily followed a lysosomal transport pathway ( a, c, d and Fig. S4). Additionally, compared with SSL, ligand-modified RVGP-SSL showed enhanced accumulation in lysosomes, yet no lysosomal escape was observed within 4 h ( a, c and d). In contrast to SSL and RVGP-SSL, liposomes modified with the cell-penetrating peptide R9 (R9-SSL, RVGPR9-SSL, and RVGP-R9-SSL) displayed discernible lysosomal escape capability ( a, c and d).
The uptake inhibition and colocalization experiments revealed the transport mechanisms of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL in BMVECs, as illustrated in . RVGPR9-SSL enters BMVECs via receptor- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, preferentially trafficking through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) pathway to bypass lysosomal degradation. This mechanism effectively avoids degradation by lysosomal hydrolases. In contrast, RVGP-R9-SSL enters cells via receptor- and clathrin-mediated pathways, primarily utilizing lysosomal transport pathways while exhibiting lysosomal escape capability. 
Combined with the integrity investigation experiment after crossing the BBB ( d-g), both liposomes are transported out of BMVECs in intact form, enabling their potential delivery to brain lesion sites (e.g., glioma) to exert therapeutic effects. Collectively, RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL either bypass lysosomes or possess lysosomal escape ability, allowing intact crossing of the BBB, qualifying both as promising brain-targeted drug delivery systems. Notably, RVGPR9-SSL demonstrates higher BBB permeability ( c), attributed to its ER-mediated transport pathway that circumvents lysosomes, thereby enhancing trans-BBB efficiency.

3.4

## Transport mechanisms of the two dual-mediated liposomes in C6s
Glioma is a primary brain tumor predominantly characterized by invasive growth. Surgical resection often fails to completely remove the lesion, while radiotherapy and chemotherapy exhibit poor efficacy, leaving a critical need for effective therapeutic agents. The two dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes developed in this study exhibited high BBB permeability, with RVGPR9-SSL demonstrating a 4-h cumulative permeability of 10.31 %—a finding that holds promise for glioma therapy. Tumor tissue harbors an acidic microenvironment (pH ∼6.5) ( ), distinct from BMVECs that express nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChR). Notably, glioma cells express M2-type muscarinic acetylcholine receptors on their surface ( ). Given the tumor's acidic milieu and receptor specificity, the transport mechanisms of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL in C6 cells may differ from those in BMVECs. Thus, to further explore the therapeutic potential of these dual-mediated liposomes in glioma, we investigated their uptake mechanisms and intracellular transport pathways in C6 cells. The results showed that SSL, RVGPR9-SSL, and RVGP-R9-SSL were all internalized by C6 cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Furthermore, the uptake of the three liposomes was energy-dependent ( a). 
Colocalization assays between liposomes and lysosomes or the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in C6 cells revealed that both RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL exhibited significantly higher colocalization rates with lysosomes than with the ER, indicating that these dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes primarily traverse the lysosomal pathway in C6 cells ( b-e). Notably, both liposomes demonstrated lysosomal escape capability in C6 cells ( b-e), which may be attributed to the enhanced positive charge on their surfaces within the tumor's acidic microenvironment (Table S2). In this context, RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL primarily enter tumor cells via R9-mediated uptake mechanisms and undergo lysosomal pathway transport in C6 cells.
Subsequently, the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin (DOX) was encapsulated in RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL. The colocalization of RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) and RVGP-R9-SSL(DOX) with C6 cell nuclei was investigated. Results showed that the colocalization rates of both liposomes with nuclei increased gradually over 1, 2, and 4 h post-administration, indicating liposomal accumulation in the nuclear region (Fig. S5a, b). Moreover, the nuclear colocalization rates of RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) and RVGP-R9-SSL(DOX) were significantly higher than that of SSL(DOX). This discrepancy may be attributed to the lysosomal escape capability of the dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes, which facilitates their nuclear accumulation and lays the foundation for therapeutic action (Fig. S5a, b).
In conclusion, both RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL traverse the lysosomal pathway in C6 cells, exhibiting the capability to escape lysosomes and accumulate in the nucleus to exert therapeutic effects. Considering the prior BBB permeability findings ( c), RVGPR9-SSL may exhibit superior therapeutic efficacy in glioma treatment owing to its higher BBB permeability (10.3 % cumulative permeability at 4 h).

3.5

## Cellular uptake of the two dual-mediated liposomes in a BBB/C6s co-culture model
To further compare the therapeutic potential of RVGPR9-SSL versus RVGP-R9-SSL for glioma, we constructed a BBB/C6 co-culture model to characterize their differential roles via uptake assays in this model. An in vitro BBB/C6 co-culture model was first established ( a). A 4-h leakage test showed that the liquid level difference in each BBB/C6 co-culture group remained at 0.5 cm, with trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) values all exceeding 200 Ω·cm2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe cellular morphology in each co-culture group ( b), demonstrating that BMVECs were densely arranged with distinct tight junctions. These results indicated that co-culturing C6 cells with the BBB did not compromise BBB integrity. The BBB/C6 co-culture model was thus suitable for investigating the uptake of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL by C6 cells after crossing the BBB, with uptake outcomes reflecting the drug delivery potential of the two liposomes for glioma therapy. 
The results showed that the mean cellular uptake of RVGPR9-SSL in the BBB/C6 co-culture model was significantly higher than that of RVGP-R9-SSL, demonstrating superior glioma targeting potential for RVGPR9-SSL ( c, d). This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher BBB permeability of RVGPR9-SSL. During the same incubation period, RVGPR9-SSL penetrated the BBB at a higher rate, leading to elevated concentrations in the glioma region and thereby enhancing C6 cell uptake.

3.6

## Distribution of the two dual-mediated liposomes in vivo
We further investigated the in vivo distribution of RVGPR9-SSL and RVGP-R9-SSL. Experimental results showed that 72 h after administration, SSL, R9-SSL, and RVGP-SSL exhibited negligible accumulation in the brains of nude mice ( a, b). The fluorescence intensity of RVGPR9-SSL in mouse brains was significantly higher than that of RVGP-R9-SSL at 72 h post-administration ( a, b). These findings are consistent with in vitro results, confirming the superior brain-targeting efficacy of RVGPR9-SSL. 
Nude mice were sacrificed 72 h after administration, and the heart, liver, lungs, spleen, kidney, and brain were dissected and collected. Ex vivo imaging was performed under identical conditions, with results shown in Fig. S6. RVGPR9-SSL (DiR) exhibited distinct fluorescent signals in brain tissues, whereas SSL(DiR), R9-SSL(DiR), RVGP-SSL(DiR), and RVGP-R9-SSL(DiR) showed negligible fluorescence in the brain. These findings are consistent with prior results, further confirming the superior brain-targeting efficacy of RVGPR9-SSL.

3.7

## Neurotoxicity evalution of RVGPR9-SSL(DOX)
Neurotoxicity evaluation is a critical component of safety assessments for brain-targeted drug delivery systems. Here, we evaluated the neurotoxicity of RVGPR9-SSL—the liposomal system with optimal brain-targeting potential—using Nissl staining and biochemical indices. Normal saline was used as the negative control, and colistin served as the positive control. This neurotoxicity evaluation aimed to provide comprehensive supporting data for the translational application of RVGPR9-SSL.
Nissl staining results showed that colistin administration induced significant neurotoxicity, evidenced by reduced size and number of Nissl bodies, indicating diminished cellular protein synthesis capacity ( a). In contrast, administration of SSL(DOX) and RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) showed no significant changes in the number or morphology of Nissl bodies compared to the normal saline group ( a). These findings indicate that RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) does not elicit neurotoxicity following repeated administration. 
The normal serum values for male SD rats were as follows: ALT 20.28–45.71 U/L, AST 85.62–196.98 U/L, AchE 67.12–80.79 U/L, and TP 58.87–76.98 g/L ( ). Following colistin administration, rat serum ALT and ASTs levels were significantly elevated beyond normal ranges, while TP levels were markedly decreased, indicating evident hepatotoxicity ( b). In contrast, administration of normal saline, SSL(DOX), and RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) maintained ALT, AST, AchE, and TP values within normal ranges. These results further confirm that RVGPR9-SSL(DOX) exhibits favorable safety profiles ( b).

4

## Conclusion
In this study, we constructed novel dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes by modifying the blood-brain barrier (BBB)-targeting peptide RVGP and cell-penetrating peptide R9 on liposome surfaces via two distinct strategies. First, RVGP and R9 were conjugated to form the RVGPR9 polypeptide, which was then modified onto liposomes to create single-ligand modified liposomes (RVGPR9-SSL). Alternatively, RVGP and R9 were separately conjugated to liposomes to generate double-ligand modified liposomes (RVGP-R9-SSL). We optimized the peptide modification density for both liposome systems and investigated their transport mechanisms across the BBB, cellular internalization pathways, and in vivo targeting efficacy. Additionally, the neurotoxicity and brain-targeting capabilities of these liposomes were evaluated in animal models. Results indicated that different ligand modification modes profoundly influenced the cellular internalization mechanisms and transport pathways of dual-mediated liposomes, thereby affecting their BBB permeability and brain-targeting efficiency. RVGPR9-SSL demonstrated superior BBB traversal efficiency and brain-targeting ability, bypassing lysosomal degradation to target tumor cell nuclei. Notably, RVGPR9-SSL exhibited favorable biological safety profiles in toxicity assays. These findings establish RVGPR9-SSL as an ideal brain-targeted drug delivery system with promising therapeutic potential for glioma treatment. Furthermore, this study offers critical insights and experimental data to guide the rational design of next-generation dual-mediated brain-targeted liposomes, highlighting the importance of ligand conjugation strategies in optimizing neurotherapeutic delivery.
