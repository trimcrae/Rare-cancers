## Abstract
In the context of stroke and revascularization therapy, brain ischemia-reperfusion injury is a significant challenge that leads to oxidative stress and inflammation. Central to the cell's intrinsic immunity is the cGAS-STING pathway, which is typically activated by unusual DNA structures. The involvement of oxidized mitochondrial DNA (ox-mtDNA)—an oxidative stress byproduct—in this type of neurological damage has not been fully explored. This study is among the first to examine the effect of ox-mtDNA on the innate immunity of neurons following ischemia-reperfusion injury. Using a rat model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion and a cellular model of oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation, we have discovered that ox-mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway in neurons. Importantly, pharmacologically limiting the release of ox-mtDNA into the cytoplasm reduces inflammation and improves neurological functions. Our findings suggest that targeting ox-mtDNA release may be a valuable strategy to attenuate brain ischemia-reperfusion injury following revascularization therapy for acute ischemic stroke.
Keywords: Brain ischemia-reperfusion injury, cGAS-STING pathway, Inflammation, Neuronal immunity, Ox-mtDNA

## Introduction
Stroke is defined as a sudden neurological deficit caused by acute focal ischemia or hemorrhage in cerebral, spinal, or retinal vessels. Most strokes are ischemic due to impaired blood flow [ ]. Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide, and the incidence and mortality of stroke have been increasing for decades [ ]. Reperfusion therapies, including thrombolysis and endovascular treatment, have advanced significantly for ischemic stroke. These therapies can restore blood flow to the ischemic penumbra (ischemic but potentially salvageable brain regions) and improve outcomes after ischemic stroke. However, reperfusion therapies are limited by their narrow time window of efficacy, strict patient selection criteria, and the risk of hemorrhagic complications [ ]. The main source of these limitations for the therapies is ischemia-reperfusion injury. Therefore, in the current era of great developments in thrombolysis and endovascular treatment, inhibiting ischemia-reperfusion injury may greatly improve efficacy and overcome the restrictions of reperfusion therapies [ ].
Oxidative stress and inflammation are the main mechanisms of ischemia-reperfusion injury [ ]. The oxygen carried by the blood in brain reperfusion can produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the disruption of the blood-brain barrier caused by ischemic injury may lead to local overoxidation in the brain [ ]. Oxidative stress may damage cells mitochondria and affect mitochondrial function [ ]. The mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) is the primary pathway for ROS bursts following ischemia-reperfusion injury [ ]. It comprises a series of multisubunit complexes (I-IV) coupled to mobile carriers, coenzyme Q and cytochrome c, within the mitochondrial membrane. During physiological conditions, tricarboxyl acid cycle intermediates in the mitochondrial matrix supply Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) with protons and electrons, forming Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and reduced FAD (FADH2), which donate electrons to the ETC. As electrons traverse the ETC, they release energy used to pump protons into the intermembrane space, generating the membrane potential (Δψ). Δψ drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP, utilizing the proton motive force through ATP synthase [ , ]. During ischemia-reperfusion injury, impaired function of complexes I and III of the ETC can lead to electron leakage, resulting in the formation of superoxide (O2−), a major ROS. This leakage may be exacerbated by the uncoupling of the ETC, which further disrupts electron flow and enhances ROS production. The generated ROS can continue to damage the ETC complexes, creating a detrimental feedback loop that impairs mitochondrial function. The rate of O2− production is directly proportional to the magnitude of Δψ [ ]. Excessive O2− activates uncoupling proteins (UCPs), which dissipate the proton gradient across the membrane without producing ATP. Superoxide generated by the ETC is converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by superoxide dismutase (SOD). Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) then catalyzes the conversion of H2O2 to H2O, a reaction that requires glutathione (GSH) [ ].
Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is more susceptible to damage from DNA-damaging agents compared to nuclear DNA due to the absence of protective histone packaging and a more limited array of DNA repair systems. Furthermore, mtDNA's location near the electron transport chain within the mitochondrial matrix exposes it to a high concentration of reactive oxygen species (ROS), significantly increasing the risk of oxidative damage [ ]. The oxidative damage to mtDNA involves a complex interplay of processes and enzymes. Flap Endonuclease 1 (FEN1) plays a crucial role in the repair and maintenance of mtDNA by recognizing and cutting oxidized mtDNA fragments, thus preventing the accumulation of mutations [ ]. Meanwhile, 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1) is responsible for excising the oxidized base 8-oxoguanine (8-oxoG), a common lesion resulting from oxidative stress, and initiating the base excision repair (BER) process to restore mtDNA integrity [ ]. The expression of Lon Peptidase 1 (LONP1), a mitochondrial protease, is associated with the regulation of oxidized mtDNA levels, as it helps maintain mitochondrial protein quality control and influences mtDNA copy number [ ]. 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) serves as a biomarker for oxidative DNA damage, including mtDNA, reflecting the cellular levels of oxidative stress and the extent of mtDNA damage [ ]. Studies have confirmed that ox-mtDNA fragments are cut into 500–600 Bp fragments by FEN1 and then released from mitochondria into the cytoplasm through mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP)and voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) calcium-dependent channels, activating downstream inflammatory pathways, including cGAS-STING [ ]. However, the mechanism of action of ox-mtDNA in brain injury-related research is still unknown.
STING is a key molecule of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-STING pathway, an important component of the innate immune system. cGAS can recognize and bind to abnormal double-stranded DNA, catalyzing the synthesis of the second messenger 2′,3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) from ATP and GTP. cGAMP binds to STING in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and STING translocates to the Golgi apparatus. STING recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and transcription factor interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) to form a complex that undergoes post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation of related molecules. Finally, phosphorylated IRF3 forms a dimer and enters the nucleus to induce the transcription of various interferons (IFNs) and IFN-stimulated genes. Nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) is also activated by the STING complex and enters the nucleus to regulate the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. The reports on the cGAS-STING pathway are mainly focused on infectious diseases [ ], tumor immunity [ ], and other immune-related diseases. In recent years, with increasing attention being paid to immune inflammation after brain injury, reports on the mechanism of action of this pathway in nervous system damage, including stroke, have also gradually increased. These studies have mainly focused on the cGAS-STING pathway of microglia, which can inhibit the activation of microglia and inflammation through its suppression [ , , ].
For the first time, this study focused on the cGAS-STING pathway in neuronal cells, a key innate immune pathway, and demonstrated the proinflammatory role of ox-mtDNA in ischemic reperfusion injury after stroke.
This study aimed to find a link between two key elements of ischemic reperfusion injury: oxidative stress (causing mitochondrial DNA oxidative damage) and inflammation (cGAS-STING pathway, cytokine release, and microglial activation). In summary, oxidative damage causes mitochondrial shrinkage in neurons and the release of ox-mtDNA. cGAS senses and binds to ox-mtDNA in the cytoplasm, activating the cGAS-STING pathway; key molecules are upregulated, and STING, TBK1, IRF3, and P65 are phosphorylated; downstream cytokines IFNβ, TNFα, and IL6 expression are increased; cytokines and ox-mtDNA activate microglia, triggering a cascade of inflammatory responses, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction. Inhibiting the release of mitochondrial DNA can alleviate activation of the cGAS pathway and inflammation, thereby improving neurological functions.

## Material and Methods

## In vivo methods

## Animals and housing, establishment of the transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) model, and drug treatment
Healthy male Sprague-Dawley, specific pathogen-free rats weighing approximately 250 ​g were purchased and housed at the Henan Animal Center of Zhengzhou University. All experiments were approved by the Department of Science and Technology of Henan Province and the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University (Grant number: V3A02022127017). This study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations of the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Animals were housed under controlled conditions with temperatures maintained between 22 and 25 ​°C and a 12 ​h light/dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to food and water throughout the study. The euthanasia protocol was carefully designed to adhere to humane practices and consisted of CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. This method was used at the end of the experiment or when humane endpoints were reached, which included signs of severe distress, inability to access food or water, or significant neurological deficits.
We induced transient cerebral ischemia, as described in a previous article [ ]. The sham operation group underwent the same procedure but did not insert a filament.
Cyclosporine A (CsA) is a mPTP inhibitor that binds to cyclophilin D (CyD), which mediates Ca2+-stimulated mPTP opening [ ]. To investigate the role of ox-mtDNA release in the inflammatory response after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion, we administered CsA to tMCAO-model 24 ​h rats. CsA was administered at a dose of 5 ​mg/kg per day, dissolved in corn oil, by intraperitoneal injection starting 2 weeks before the induction of tMCAO and ending with the last injection after the model was established. The other animal groups without CsA treatment received equal volume of corn oil.
In the experimental design, the tMCAO groups were categorized according to the time elapsed after reperfusion: 2, 4, 8, and 12 ​h, with each group consisting of 10 rats. Within these groups, 4 rats were allocated for Western Blot analysis, another 4 for tissue sectioning, and 2 served as backups. The study also included a Sham Group, a tMCAO group with 24 ​h of reperfusion, and a group that received CsA treatment followed by 24 ​h of reperfusion post-tMCAO; each of these groups comprised 14 rats. The allocation for these groups was as follows: 4 rats for MRI/TTC assessments, 4 for behavioral testing in conjunction with Western Blot analysis, 4 for behavioral testing followed by immunofluorescence studies, and 2 as backups.

## Behavioral tests
Open field test: Measuring the locomotor activity 24 ​h after the tMCAO model. The rats were individually placed in a corner facing the wall of open space (100 ​× ​100 ​cm) and allowed to explore freely for 5 ​min. The movement distance, average speed, resting time, and moving time were recorded using Smart v3.0.

## Infarction area measurements
Twenty-four h after modeling, rats were anesthetized and underwent Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI; MR Solutions, England). Anesthetic Regime for MRI: Anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane in a mixture of 70% N2O and 30% O2. Maintenance of anesthesia was achieved with 1.5–2% isoflurane delivered via a nose cone throughout the imaging procedure.T2 Acquisition Parameters: MRI scans were performed on a 4.7T MRI scanner.T2-weighted images were acquired using the following parameters: TR: 5000 ​ms ​TE: 51 ​ms slice thickness:1 ​mm.Measurement of Infarct Volume: Infarct volume was measured using manual region of interest (ROI) delineation on T2-weighted MRI images. The software used for analysis was RadiAnt DICOM Viewer. The total infarct volume was calculated by summing the infarct areas across slices and multiplying by the slice thickness.
Twenty-four h after modeling, the brains were sliced to a thickness of approximately 2 ​mm and stained with 2, 3,-5-triphenyl tetrazole (TTC) solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 37 ​°C for 30 ​min. Images were captured using a camera, and ImageJ software was used to analyze the infarct size.

## Immunofluorescence
Rats were perfused with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde, and the brains were post-fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 ​°C. Paraffin embedding and coronal sectioning of the striatum were performed. Sections were deparaffinized with xylene and graded alcohol and then subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After blocking with 5% BSA for 30 ​min at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ​°C. Secondary antibodies were applied for 1 ​h at room temperature, followed by DAPI staining for 5 ​min. The slides were washed and mounted. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) at ​× ​100 magnification. The ischemic penumbra in the cerebral cortex of each rat brain tissue specimen subjected to ischemia-reperfusion was selected and images of them were captured.
The antibodies used were: Phospho-TMEM173/STING Antibody (AF7416, Affinity), NEUN Mouse mAb (94403, Cell Signaling Technology), Mouse monoclonal IgG1 to IBA1 (EPR16589, Abcam), GFAP Mouse mAb (3670, Cell Signaling Technology), Rabbit Anti-8-hydroxy-2-deoxyguanosine (8-OhdG) antibody (bs-1278R, Bioss), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Cy3 ®) (6939, Abcam), and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150113, Abcam).

## In vitro methods

## Cell culture, oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model, and drug administration
HT22 ​cells were obtained from NEWGAINBIO (Wuxi, China) and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Gibco, 11995065) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 12483020) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, SV30010) in a humidified incubator at 37 ​°C in the presence of 5% CO2 in the air.
To explore the mechanism of the neuronal cGAS-STING pathway and the activation and transmission of inflammation after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, we established an in vitro HT22 ​cells OGD/R model. The method of the OGD/R model was the same as described in a previous paper [ ].
To explore the mechanism of ox-mtDNA-induced secondary inflammatory injury after ischemia-reperfusion, we established a mtDNA-depleted HT22 ​cell model by adding ethidium bromide (EtBr) to the culture medium at 450 ​ng/mL for 4 days [ ]. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to OGD/R for 24 ​h to simulate ischemia-reperfusion injury. The experimental groups were organized as follows: non-conditioned control group (NC): Cells that received no treatment. OGD/R Model Group (OGD/R): Cells subjected to OGD/R 24 ​h without prior EtBr treatment. EtBr Treatment Group (EB): Cells treated with EtBr for 4 days without subsequent OGD/R. OGD/R Model with EtBr Culture Treatment (OGD/R ​+ ​EB): Cells first treated with EtBr for 4 days to deplete mtDNA and then subjected to OGD/R for 24 ​h.

## Immunofluorescence
Cell slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 ​min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 ​min at room temperature, blocked with BSA for 30 ​min at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ​°C, incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 ​h at room temperature, stained with DAPI for 5 ​min in the dark, and mounted. Ultra-high-resolution laser confocal microscopy (LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Germany) was used for observation.
The antibodies used were: Phospho-TMEM173/STING Antibody, Rabbit Anti-8-OhdG antibody, Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Cy3 ®), and Goat Anti- Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488). (ab150077, Abcam).

## Mitochondrial fluorescence observation
A working solution of MitoTracker Red CMXRos (C1035, Beyotime) was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were incubated with the working solution for 15 ​min at 37 ​°C, washed, resuspended in a normal medium, and observed under a microscope.

## JC-1 staining
The JC-1 staining working solution (Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1, C2006, Beyotime) was prepared according to the manufacturer's recommended concentration and volume and added to the culture medium at a 1:1 ratio. Cells were incubated for 20 ​min at 37 ​°C in a cell culture incubator. The supernatant was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer. The cells were resuspended in a cell culture medium and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

## Immunoprecipitation
To investigate the mechanism of cGAS-STING pathway activation in neuronal ischemia-reperfusion injury, we analyzed the DNA bound to cGAS in OGD/R HT22 ​cells. Cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 15 ​min to crosslink proteins and DNA. Glycine was added, and the mixture was incubated for 5 ​min at room temperature. Cells were lysed with SDS Lysis Buffer (containing 0.1% dithiothreitol and 0.5% Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) on ice for 30 ​min. Micrococcal Nuclease (#10011; Cell Signaling Technology) was added and incubated at 37 ​°C with shaking for 20 ​min. EDTA was added, and the mixture was placed on ice for 1–2 ​min to stop the reaction. An immunoprecipitation antibody, cGAS rabbit monoclonal antibody (31659, Cell Signaling Technology), was added. Equal amounts of Normal Rabbit IgG (2729; Cell Signaling Technology) were added as a negative control. The IP samples were rotated overnight at 4 ​°C. Protein G Magnetic Beads (9006, Cell Signaling Technology) were added to each IP reaction and incubated with rotation for 2 ​h at 4 ​°C. The tubes were placed on a Magnetic Separation Rack while waiting for the solution to clear, and the supernatant was removed. A low-salt wash buffer was added and then washed with rotation for 5 ​min at 4 ​°C. Magnetic separation and washing were repeated three times, and a high-salt wash buffer was used to wash the beads once. ChIP elution buffer was then added and incubated for 30 ​min at 65 ​°C. The tubes were placed on a Magnetic Separation Rack while waiting for the solution to clear, and the supernatant was collected. 5 ​M NaCl and Proteinase K were added to the supernatant to reverse crosslinking and incubated for 2 ​h at 65 ​°C. DNA was purified using silica gel chromatography. The samples were then subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis.

## PCR and detection of mtDNA
Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using a QuautStudio-5 (Thermo Scientific) instrument and SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix qPCR Kit Ⅳ (AG11746, Accurate Biology), following the instructions for system preparation and reaction time. The results were calculated according to the following formula: percentage of total input chromatin ​= ​2% ​× ​2(C[T] of 2% input sample-C[T] of IP sample).
The following primers were used for mtDNA: COX1 Forward, AACCATTATCTTCCTAGGACCCCT, and Reverse, GTGGATAGGATGCTCGGATTCATA; COX2 Forward, AATCCCAACAAACGACCTAAAACC, and Reverse, GAAGTTCTATTGGCAGAACGACTC. Nuclear DNA: SimpleChIP® Mouse RPL30 Intron 2 Primers (7015, Cell Signaling Technology), β-actin Forward, GTGACGTTGACATCCGTAAAGA, and Reverse, GTAACAGTCCGCCTAGAAGCAC.

## Cytoplasmic oxidized DNA detection
The NC group and the 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 ​h OGD/R HT22 ​cells group were treated with the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027, Beyotime) to obtain cytoplasmic proteins, which were immediately assayed for 8-OHdG content in the cytoplasm using a Mouse 8-OHdG ELISA Kit (Abmart AB-2957) to determine the oxidation status of the cytoplasmic DNA.

## GSH detection
Quantitative assessment of GSH in the NC group and the 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 ​h OGD/R HT22 ​cells was conducted using the GSH Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime). The assay utilizes the DTNB method, wherein oxidized glutathione (GSSG) is enzymatically reduced to GSH. The resultant GSH reacts with DTNB, yielding a yellow chromophore. The assay protocol initiates with the measurement of total GSH, followed by the quantification of GSSG post-GSH depletion. The net concentration of reduced GSH is inferred by the differential between total GSH and GSSG.

## ROS detection
ROS levels in HT22 ​cells were quantified using the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) ROS Assay Kit (S0033S, Beyotime). The assay employs DCFH-DA, which permeates the cell membrane and is hydrolyzed to DCFH monomers. These monomers are subsequently oxidized by ROS to form the fluorescent DCF, retained within the cells. Fluorescence intensity, indicative of ROS levels, was measured using flow cytometry. The experimental groups included the NC group and HT22 ​cells treated with OGD/R at the following time points: 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 ​h.

## In vitro and in vivo methods

## Western blotting(WB)
Cells or rat brain ischemic penumbra tissues were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 ​h and incubated with the primary antibody diluted in TBST overnight. The membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody diluted in TBST for 1 ​h and detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrate solution.
The antibodies used were as follows: anti-GAPDH antibody (ab9485, Abcam), STING Antibody (2922D, Novus Biologicals), cGAS (31659, Cell Signaling Technology), IRF-3 Rabbit mAb (4302, Cell Signaling Technology), Rabbit Anti-NFKB p65 antibody (0465R, Bioss), TBK Rabbit mAb (38066, Cell Signaling Technology), Phospho-TMEM173/STING Antibody (AF7416, Affinity), Phospho-IRF-3 Rabbit mAb (29047, Cell Signaling Technology), Phospho–NF–κB p65 Rabbit mAb (3033, Cell Signaling Technology), Phospho-TBK1 Rabbit mAb (5483, Cell Signaling Technology), LONP1 Antibody (TD12119, Abmart), OGG1 Polyclonal Antibody (PA1-16505, Invitrogen), and flap FEN1 Polyclonal Antibody (PA5-27518, Invitrogen).

## Data analysis
All the research data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the results showed that the data did not deviate significantly from the normal distribution. The software SPSS 21.0 and GraphPad Prism 8 were used for data analysis and graphing, and p ​< ​0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Brain ischemia-reperfusion injury induces cGAS-STING pathway activation in the ischemic penumbra
The activation of the cGAS-STING pathway was observed in the brain ischemic penumbra after rat brain ischemia-reperfusion injury. Western blot analysis confirmed a time-dependent increase in the expression levels of key cGAS-STING pathway molecules in the ischemic penumbra within 24 ​h post-tMCAO( A). At 24 ​h, there was a significant upregulation of cGAS (P ​< ​0.0001), STING (P ​< ​0.01), IRF3 (P ​< ​0.001), TBK1 (P ​< ​0.0001), and P65 (P ​< ​0.001) compared to the sham-operated group ( B). Additionally, phosphorylation levels of STING (P ​< ​0.01), TBK1 (pTBK1, P ​< ​0.001), and P65 (pP65, P ​< ​0.01) were also significantly elevated( A–C). Phosphorylated STING (pSTING) indicates the formation of a functional complex that activates IRF3 and NFκB [ ]. Immunofluorescence analysis after tMCAO showed a significant increase in pSTING expression in neurons and microglia within the ischemic penumbra at 4, 8, 12, and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001). Notably, microglial activation, marked by pronounced protrusions, was evident at 12 and 24 ​h. Moreover, microglial pSTING expression progressively escalated at 4 and 8 ​h, culminating in near-complete expression by 12 and 24 ​h ( D–E,G, See supplementary file 1 for pSTING expression images at 8 and 12 ​h). No significant increase in pSTING expression was observed in the astrocytes at either time point ( F and G). These results indicate that the cGAS-STING pathway is first activated in neurons and then in microglia, showing temporal and spatial differences after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury in rats.
 

## Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in neuronal cells after OGD/R in vitro
Post-OGD/R, HT22 ​cells showed a time-dependent increase in cGAS-STING pathway activation, with significant expression and phosphorylation of pathway proteins. Notably, compared to the control group: cGAS expression rose significantly at 12 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05). TBK1 expression increased at 8 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.01), continuing to rise through 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001). P65 expression began to elevate at 4 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05), peaking at 12 ​h, and remained high at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001). IRF3 expression significantly increased at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.01). STING expression was significantly higher at both 12 ​h and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) ( A and B). Phosphorylated proteins pSTING, pTBK1, pIRF3, and pP65 also showed marked increases, with pSTING and pIRF3 notably higher at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.01 and P ​< ​0.05, respectively), and pP65 significantly elevated at 12 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05) and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.001). pTBK1 showed a significant rise from 8 ​h onwards, remaining elevated at 12 ​h and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) ( A–C). Inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IFN-β were significantly higher at 12 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05) and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001), while IL6 levels increased at 8 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.01), peaked at 12 ​h, and continued to be significantly elevated at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) ( A–D). Immunofluorescence also confirmed that, compared to the NC group, the expression of pSTING increased in HT22 ​cells 24 ​h after OGD/R ( E). This verified that the in vitro model was representative of the in vivo cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, and further confirmed that the activation and phosphorylation of the cGAS-STING pathway in neuronal cells led to the upregulation of downstream inflammatory cytokines after reperfusion.
 

## Neuronal mitochondrial DNA oxidation following OGD/R and tMCAO
Following OGD/R, HT22 ​cells exhibited an increase in ROS levels within 0.5 ​h, which remained elevated (P ​< ​0.0001) ( A. Flow cytometry data are presented in ). Concurrently, GSH levels initially surged at 0 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) but fell to levels indistinguishable from the control group at 0.5 ​h, reaching a low at 4 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.01) and staying reduced thereafter ( B). Oxidized DNA levels began to significantly rise 1 ​h post-OGD/R (P ​< ​0.05), further increasing at 2 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.001), and maintaining high levels at subsequent time points (P ​< ​0.0001) ( C).
 In the ischemic penumbra of rat brains after tMCAO, an early accumulation of oxidized DNA was noted in neurons at 4 ​h, significantly increasing by 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) ( D). Microglia showed a rise in oxidized DNA levels at 4 ​h in less than half of the cells, which substantially increased at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001) ( E). Astrocytes had fewer cells positive for oxidized DNA at 4 ​h, but a notable increase was observed at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.001) ( F). A statistical analysis of 8-OHdG-labeled oxidized DNA damage in these cell types within the ischemic penumbra is depicted graphically ( G).

## Ox-mtDNA released into the cytoplasm and binds to cGAS in HT22 cells post-OGD/R
Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy revealed that, compared to before injury ( A), mitochondria shrank from linear to spherical, and ox-mtDNA was released into the cytoplasmic matrix and scattered around the mitochondria ( B). In the context of OGD/R-induced neuronal injury, Western blot analysis revealed a time-dependent increase in the expression of key mitochondrial DNA repair and maintenance proteins. Specifically, LONP1 expression was significantly elevated at 8 ​h and continued to rise at 12 and 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001). FEN1 levels notably increased starting at 12 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05) and further escalated at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.0001). OGG1 expression peaked at 8 and 12 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.001) and, although it decreased slightly, remained significantly higher at 24 ​h ​(P ​< ​0.05) compared to the control group. Collectively, these results indicate an upregulation of LONP1, FEN1, and OGG1 in HT22 ​cells following OGD/R, highlighting the cellular response to oxidative mitochondrial DNA damage( C and D). At 24 ​h post-OGD/R, chromatin immunoprecipitation revealed a significant increase in mitochondrial DNA (COX1, COX2) binding to cGAS (P ​< ​0.0001), with negligible nuclear DNA interaction ( E). These results confirmed that after ischemia-hypoxia injury, neuronal mitochondria go significant shrinkage and deformation, and mtDNA is oxidized and released into the cytoplasmic matrix, where it binds to cGAS molecules and further activates the downstream cGAS-STING pathway, inflammatory response, and microglial activation. This oxidized DNA may also directly cause microglial activation and neuroinflammation through corresponding pathways.
 

## Inhibition of ox-mtDNA downregulates repair proteins and the cGAS-STING pathway in neurons
We established an HT22 cellular model of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) depletion using EtBr treatment. This intervention significantly reduced mtDNA content, as confirmed by PCR (P ​< ​0.0001) ( A). Following OGD/R, a 24-h analysis revealed a substantial reduction in the expression and phosphorylation of cGAS-STING pathway-related proteins—TBK1 (P ​< ​0.05), cGAS (P ​< ​0.05), IRF3 (P ​< ​0.001), and STING (P ​< ​0.01)—in mtDNA-depleted HT22 ​cells, indicating a dampened inflammatory response. Concurrently, phosphorylated proteins pTBK1 (P ​< ​0.01), pP65 (P ​< ​0.05), pSTING (P ​< ​0.0001), and pIRF3 (P ​< ​0.01) also exhibited significant decreases compared to untreated controls ( B–D). Moreover, the mtDNA-depleted cells showed a notable decrease in the expression of FEN1 (P ​< ​0.05), OGG1 (P ​< ​0.0001), and LONP1 (P ​< ​0.0001) relative to normal HT22 ​cells ( E and F). Additionally, mtDNA depletion appeared to ameliorate mitochondrial membrane potential damage post-OGD/R ( G), underscoring the therapeutic potential of modulating mtDNA release in the context of neuronal ischemia-reperfusion injury.
 

## Ox-mtDNA release inhibition improves neurological function after tMCAO
Treatment with CsA significantly reduced the levels of 8-OHdG labeled oxmtDNA in various cell types within the ischemic penumbra of rats subjected to tMCAO 24 ​h ( A). Immunofluorescence labeling and microscopic analysis confirmed this reduction and also demonstrated a substantial decrease in the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, as indicated by the pSTING along with neuronal and glial markers ( B). This reduction was accompanied by a notable downregulation in the expression and phosphorylation of key molecules in the cGAS-STING pathway, including TBK1 (P ​< ​0.01), P65 (P ​< ​0.001), cGAS, IRF3, pTBK1, pSTING, and pIRF3 (P ​< ​0.0001), compared to the sham-operated group ( C and D). Behavioral assessments conducted 24 ​h post-tMCAO indicated that CsA-treated rats showed significantly improved locomotor activity in the open field test, with an increased movement distance within 5 ​min (P ​< ​0.01). However, despite these improvements, there was still a significant difference in motor performance between the CsA-treated rats and those without cerebral infarction (P ​< ​0.0001) ( E). Furthermore, CsA treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the volume of cerebral infarction, as evidenced by T2-weighted MRI coronal sections (P ​< ​0.01) ( F–H) and triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining (P ​< ​0.001) ( F and G). These findings highlight the potential of CsA treatment in reducing oxDNA levels, attenuating the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, and consequently diminishing the inflammatory response, which contributes to the alleviation of ischemic brain injury and promotes neurological recovery.
 

## Discussion
As mentioned earlier, ischemia-reperfusion injury is the main cause of secondary damage after cerebral infarction and reperfusion therapy. The role of inflammation in this process is crucial and cannot be overlooked. Abnormal double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm is a major inflammatory mediator that activates the cGAS-STING pathway of innate immunity and causes inflammation. After cerebral infarction, double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm activates the cGAS-STING pathway and induces inflammation. Inhibiting cGAS, knocking out microglial cGAS, or silencing STING molecules with siRNA can reduce inflammation after cerebral infarction, reduce cell death, reduce infarct volume, and alleviate neurological deficits [ , ]. Mitochondrial DNA-induced activation of the cGAS-STING pathway has also been observed in the brains of rats with ischemia-reperfusion injury. Knocking out microglial cGAS [ ] and inhibiting STING [ ] improved neurological function and reduced ischemia-reperfusion injury after infarction. This is supported by the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in the rat brain after ischemia-reperfusion injury observed in this study. The cGAS-STING pathway, one of the core pathways of innate immunity, is the oldest pathway and can be traced back to the bacterial immune response. This pathway is widely present across various species, from prokaryotes to mammals, so its function should also be preserved in non-immune neurons [ ]. Neuronal vulnerability is well-known in brain tissue. Neurons are undoubtedly the first victims of tissue damage caused by hypoxia-ischemia and reperfusion injury. In this study, we found that the oxidative DNA released by neurons at after ischemia-reperfusion injury may cause subsequent innate immune responses. Suppression of hypoxia-hypoglycemia-induced neuronal mitochondrial oxidative stress damage, ox-mtDNA release in the ultra-early stage, and immediate intervention to inhibit innate immunity in the early stage may suppress the cascade reaction of neuroinflammation and rescue a large number of damaged but not dead neurons in the ischemic penumbra after reperfusion injury [ ]. We believe that the neuronal cGAS-STING pathway may be the precursor of the brain injury immune response, and exploring the innate immune pathway of neurons may be the key to improving neuronal ischemia-reperfusion injury.
mtDNA is regarded as an important mediator of innate immunity in noninfectious inflammation, as monitored by cGAS and other sensors. The mitochondrial origin may be derived from prokaryotic symbiosis, and the “foreign” mtDNA with a different structure from the nuclear genome may be considered a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) [ ]. mtDNA is a covalently closed circular genome that can easily form special structures and become a DAMP upon immune stimulation [ ]. Moreover, mtDNA maintains a high copy number and forms a large amount of DAMP under damage [ ], stimulating the cytoplasmic DNA-induced immune pathway and triggering an immune response. cGAS is one of the most important DNA sensors. Previous studies suggested it was located in the cytoplasm and isolated from nuclear and mtDNA by membranes. Recent studies have found that cGAS is also located in the nucleus, mitochondria, and cell membrane [ ], which is related to its signal-specific function. Located on the plasma membrane, it can effectively identify pathogen DNA [ ]. However, nuclear cGAS maintains low activity due to chromatin structure limiting DNA accessibility or nucleosomes inhibiting GAS catalysis, thus maintaining low antigenicity of chromatin DNA [ ]. In vitro and in vivo experiments, mitochondrial cGAS inhibits ROS and cell death in cancer cells, whereas cGAS detached from the mitochondrial membrane increases ROS and cancer cell death [ ]. These indicate that cGAS mainly recognizes mtDNA and activates the downstream pathway when it is free in the cytoplasm. Under normal conditions, mtDNA is protected in mitochondria with a double membrane; however, the special environment of mitochondria also makes mtDNA prone to transformation into DAMP. After oxidative stress, mitochondria are easily deformed, and mtDNA is mislocalized in the cytoplasmic matrix, acting as a center of inflammation induction through proteins such as toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and cGAS, causing corresponding cytokine elevation [ ]. This is consistent with the results of this study. After ischemia-reperfusion injury, ox-mtDNA leaked into the cytoplasm, and cGAS bound to mtDNA but not to nuclear DNA, which may be related to the fact that the cell nucleus did not completely rupture after glucose-oxygen recovery and the chromatin structure may not have been completely destroyed, as shown in . Targeting cGAS may affect the normal immune system throughout the body, affect the subcellular localization of cGAS with protective effects, and aggravate immune damage. The immune system in brain tissue at the subacute and late stages after ischemia-reperfusion injury may have a protective effect on neurons [ ]. Therefore, targeting mtDNA is likely to be the preferred strategy to improve neuronal damage and functional impairment in the ultra-acute phase after ischemia-reperfusion injury.
The role of ox-mtDNA in the activation of the immune system has been verified in previous studies. The injection of 8-OHdG into mice transplanted with tumors increased the content of 8-OHdG in DNA, leading to DNA strand breaks, inducing apoptosis, and reducing tumor incidence [ ]. Chemotherapy for mouse tumors can induce mtDNA oxidation, trigger effective anti-tumor T-cell immunity, and promote tumor cell regression [ ]. A previous study showed that after radiation therapy, ROS and 8-OHdG content increased in tumor cells, and mitochondrial DNA oxidative damage activated the STING-TBK1-IRF3-IFN-β pathway in dendritic cells, which then presented antigens to CD8+T cells and induced anti-tumor immunity [ ]. In autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and chronic granulomatosis, mitochondrial ROS is the key to inducing neutrophil activation and forming Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Injection of ox-mtDNA into mice can activate the cGAS-STING pathway, and inhibition of mitochondrial ROS can improve lupus severity in mice [ ]. There have been studies to detect ox-mtDNA to judge the immune status of patients. Neutrophils in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma contain high levels of mitoROS and form NETs enriched with ox-mtDNA. These changes are related to clinical manifestations and can trigger inflammatory mediators that promote metastasis [ ]. In peripheral blood plasma samples of patients with myelodysplastic syndrome, the ox-mtDNA can be used as a biomarker to judge the severity of the disease [ ]. In patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms, urine analysis of the ox-mtDNA marker 8-OHdG revealed that mitochondrial oxidative damage related to 8-OHdG was associated with the presence of gene mutations in chronic myeloproliferative neoplasms [ ]. In patients with abdominal aortic aneurysms, nuclear and mtDNA were elevated in plasma samples and associated with neutrophil activation and NET formation [ ]. Patients with sepsis had increased urine levels of oxidatively damaged nuclear and mtDNA and RNA. In addition, sepsis also caused an increase in free damaged cell mtDNA in plasma [ ]. Patients with metabolic syndrome had elevated plasma ox-mtDNA levels and TLR9 expression levels. Additionally, antioxidant treatment in rats and cells could reduce ox-mtDNA-induced inflammation [ ]. In summary, ox-mtDNA has broad clinical prospects and has been studied as a biomarker in patients with infections, tumors, and autoimmune diseases. Unlike tumor research, brain ischemia-reperfusion injury requires minimizing ox-mtDNA release as an antigen in the ultra-early stages. Preventing ox-mtDNA from entering local brain tissue or cytoplasm may improve ischemic penumbra treatment.
In response to the susceptibility of mtDNA to damage, mitochondria have a corresponding BER mechanism in which OGG1 can remove oxidized bases and quickly restore mtDNA oxidative damage [ ]. Upregulating mtOGG1 levels in mouse mitochondria can improve age-related neuroinflammation and function, and cells expressing mtOGG1 in vitro can reduce mtDNA release into the cytoplasm and reduce the release of inflammatory cytokines through the cGAS-STING pathway . Lonp1 regulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation metabolism, and knocking out Lonp1 can inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis, while upregulation can prevent protein and lipid oxidative damage, thus maintaining the mitochondrial redox balance [ ]. However, it has been reported that when LONP1 is overexpressed, oxidized mtDNA is released into the cytoplasm and activates the cGAS-STING pathway, transporting mtDNA outwards through extracellular vehicles [ ]. However, LONP1 expression levels can be considered to be related to mitochondrial ROS and ox-mtDNA production. In this study, we found that OGD/R induced LONP1 and OGG1 expression, which increased in response to oxidative damage in HT22 ​cells. Therefore, BER is a promising drug target for ox-mtDNA after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion. Another target was the release of ox-mtDNA. Studies have confirmed that STING can bind to VDAC, affecting CA2+ influx into mitochondria and mitochondrial ROS production [ ]. In addition, ox-mtDNA can promote Gasdermin D oligomerization, which has been proven to be a necessary pore-forming protein for the neutrophil release of mtDNA and cell death [ ]. Cytoplasmic mtDNA upregulates MHC class I antigen presentation through the cGAS/STING pathway, activating downstream effector cells of immunity [ ]. These reactions may trigger a cascade of events in the cytoplasm, becoming irreversible; therefore, blocking ox-mtDNA from crossing the mitochondrial membrane is a good strategy to prevent inflammation.
CsA is a pharmacological inhibitor of mPTP, which effectively prevents mPTP opening, preserves mitochondrial function and membrane potential, reduces intracellular mtDNA accumulation, and suppresses downstream cGAS-STING pathway activation [ ]. In this study, we successfully inhibited ox-mtDNA release into the cytoplasmic matrix with CsA, alleviated neuronal and microglial inflammation in vitro and in vivo, and improved behavioral impairment caused by ischemia-hypoxia reperfusion in rats. Previous studies have indicated that CsA may reduce M1 polarization [ ], and as shown by the results of this study, pSTING was downregulated in both neurons and microglia, reducing the possibility of inflammation spreading.
Our in vivo assessment did not include baseline open field testing for comparison with post-stroke outcomes, and evaluations were conducted at 24 ​h post-stroke, potentially influenced by surgical and anesthetic effects. In vitro, our use of mouse hippocampal neurons lacks the cellular diversity of cerebral IR injury, omitting astrocytes and microglia, which may limit the translation of our findings to human conditions. In conclusion, we demonstrated that neuronal mitochondrial oxidative stress caused by ischemia-reperfusion injury releases ox-mtDNA into the cytoplasm, which binds to cGAS, activates the pathway to release cytokines, and activates microglia, triggering a cascade of inflammatory reactions and linking the two mechanisms of inflammation and oxidative stress in ischemia-reperfusion injury, ultimately leading to neurological dysfunction. By controlling the release of ox-mtDNA, we were able to downregulate the cGAS-STING pathway and inflammatory factors, reduce inflammation, and improve neurological function.
