## Abstract
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a fatal interstitial lung disease where macrophages drive fibrogenesis, yet Hdac11’s role is unclear. We first identify pronounced Hdac11 upregulation in IPF lungs, which is associated with an enrichment in alveolar macrophages (AMs). Genetic ablation of Hdac11 or adoptive transfer of Hdac11-deficient macrophages markedly attenuates fibrosis. Specifically, Hdac11 deficiency significantly reduces M2 macrophage polarization in vivo and vitro and is associated with reduced macrophage-myofibroblast transition (MMT) like phenotypic reprogramming, thereby decreasing myofibroblast accumulation and profibrotic gene expression. Mechanistically, impaired mitophagy mediates Hdac11-mediated M2 macrophage polarization and is associated with MMT-like changes. Hdac11 regulates mitochondrial quality control by deacetylating Parkin at lysine 76, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, which impairs mitophagy and drives profibrotic macrophage activation. Pharmacological Hdac11 inhibition effectively reverses bleomycin-induced fibrosis. Taken together, our work identifies Hdac11 as a target of Parkin-mediated mitophagy in macrophages, establishing Hdac11-Parkin axis disruption as an important mechanism in IPF and highlighting Hdac11 inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy.
Subject terms: Cystic fibrosis, Innate immunity, Monocytes and macrophages

Epigenetic dysregulation is a central driver of the progressive remodeling seen in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis with specific factors to be further explored. The authors here show Hdac11 drives lung fibrosis by repressing Parkin-dependent mitophagy, which is associated with enhanced macrophage M2-type polarization and myofibroblast trans-differentiation.

## Introduction
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and irreversible lung disease characterized by the destruction of alveolar architecture and the aberrant accumulation of myofibroblasts, resulting from excessive deposition of extracellular matrix. Due to limited effective therapies and unclear pathological mechanisms, IPF is associated with a poor prognosis, with an average survival time of only 3-5 years after diagnosis . Therefore, exploring key pathological mechanisms and identifying effective drug targets are of great significance for the treatment of IPF.
Abnormal wound-healing has been shown to drive the progression from lung injury to fibrosis . Macrophages are the most abundant immune cells in lung tissue and play a crucial role during the wound-healing process . With high plasticity, macrophages can polarize into classical (M1) and alternative (M2) activation phenotypes in response to Th1 and Th2 stimulation, respectively. M1 macrophages initiate and amplify the inflammation by expressing pro-inflammatory factors and chemokines such as TNF, IL-1β, IL-12, IL-23, and CCL2 , and M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory mediators, including CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1, facilitating inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling . Numerous studies have demonstrated that fibrotic regions in the lungs of IPF patients and experimental mouse models are infiltrated with abundant M2 macrophages. These macrophages drive fibroblast proliferation and differentiation, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop that perpetuates abnormal tissue repair processes and exacerbates fibrotic progression – . Macrophage–myofibroblast transition (MMT) has been increasingly recognized as a cellular process associated with fibrotic progression , . Emerging evidence suggests that, under profibrotic conditions, a subset of M2-like macrophages can acquire myofibroblast-like characteristics, including expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), collagens, and fibronectin, and may contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling as well as local amplification of profibrotic signaling such as TGF-β activation . MMT-like phenomena has been reported in rodent models of pulmonary fibrosis, underscoring its pathophysiological relevance . Thus, intervention of macrophage M2 polarization and MMT-associated phenotypic changes may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for IPF.
Recent studies have shown that mitochondrial quality control is closely linked to macrophage polarization . Mitophagy, the selective autophagy of mitochondria, eliminates dysfunctional organelles through a key regulatory axis involving PINK1 (PTEN-induced putative kinase 1) and Parkin (an E3 ubiquitin ligase) . Emerging evidence demonstrates that disrupting mitophagy promotes macrophage reprogramming toward a pro-fibrotic M2 phenotype in kidney fibrosis , but this mechanism remains poorly understood in IPF.
Histone deacetylases (Hdacs) are a family of enzymes that regulate gene expression by modifying chromatin structure through histone deacetylation and directly removing acetyl groups from nonhistone proteins . Based on structural and functional homology, Hdacs are classified into four categories: Class I (1, 2, 3 and 8), II (4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10), III (SIRT1-7), and IV (Hdac11) . Growing evidence underscores Hdacs as pivotal regulators in fibrotic diseases , . Notably, the pan-HDAC inhibitors, including vorinostat (SAHA) and panobinostat (LBH589), which are FDA-approved for cancer therapy, have demonstrated potent anti-fibrotic effects in preclinical models of IPF , . However, Hdac isoforms exhibit functional heterogeneity in subcellular localization and biological roles, rendering pan-Hdac inhibitors prone to adverse effects , . Understanding isoform-specific mechanisms and developing selective inhibitors thus represent key approaches to advance IPF therapeutics.
Hdac11 is the sole member of the recently identified class IV Hdac subfamily . With only 347 amino acids, Hdac11 retains a conserved catalytic domain and has been found highly expressed in brain, lung and immune cells, playing important roles in tumorigenesis and immune response , . Within the respiratory system, elevated Hdac11 expression is observed in various pathological processes. Hdac11 dysregulation is associated with poor prognosis of lung cancer and confers antiviral resistance against influenza while weakening bacterial clearance in the lung , . In allergic asthma models, Hdac11 shows marked upregulation during allergen-induced eosinophilic airway inflammation, exacerbating type 2 inflammation through IL-4/STAT6 hyperactivation . Mounting evidence delineates the important role of Hdac11 in macrophage function through epigenetic modulation . Inhibition of Hdac11 enhances IL-10 transcription in macrophages by increasing histone 3 acetylation at the IL-10 promoter ; Hdac11 deficiency also results in increased production of NO and reactive oxygen species, which potentiate antifungal immunity in macrophages . The overexpression of Hdac11 in fibrotic lung tissues, coupled with its established role in immune homeostasis, prompted us to hypothesize that Hdac11 may orchestrate IPF pathogenesis within the spectrum of inflammatory lung diseases. However, the mechanistic contribution of Hdac11 to IPF progression remains unexplored.
In the current study, we identify Hdac11 as a critical regulator of pulmonary fibrosis, showing its upregulation in lung tissues of IPF patients and a mouse model. Hdac11 drives macrophage M2 polarization and promotes MMT-like features by impairing Parkin-mediated mitophagy, linking epigenetic regulation to mitochondrial quality control in fibrogenesis. Pharmacological inhibition of Hdac11 attenuates fibrosis in preclinical models. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized Hdac11-mitophagy axis and highlight Hdac11 as a promising therapeutic target for IPF.

## Results

## Hdac11 expression is elevated in lungs and macrophages of IPF patients and mice subjected to pulmonary fibrosis
To investigate the potential role of Hdac11 in pulmonary fibrogenesis, we systematically evaluated its expression in clinical lung tissues, experimental murine models, and lung organoids. Initial analysis of human transcriptomic datasets (GSE150910, GSE99621, GSE231693) from the NCBI GEO repository showed no significant difference in Hdac11 expression between healthy controls and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) lungs (Fig.  ). Given the crucial role of immune cells in IPF pathogenesis, we further analyzed Hdac11 expression specifically in pulmonary immune populations. Strikingly, re-analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE135893, GSE227136) revealed that Hdac11 expression was significantly upregulated in macrophages, but not in other immune cell types, in IPF lungs compared to healthy controls (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). This macrophage-specific upregulation was further confirmed by immunohistochemistry (Fig.  ).
 In murine fibrosis models, bleomycin (BLM) challenge significantly elevated pulmonary Hdac11 transcript and protein levels compared to saline controls (Fig.  ). IHC analysis similarly identified abundant Hdac11-positive macrophage-like cells in fibrotic lungs (Fig.  ). To further verify Hdac11 upregulation in lung macrophages, dual immunofluorescence co-staining was performed in clinical lung tissue specimens and experimental murine models. Hdac11 exhibited preferential macrophage expression, confirmed by CD68/Hdac11 immunofluorescence co-localization analysis (Fig.  ). In addition, we established a human lung organoid model containing macrophages from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). This was achieved through a three-stage differentiation process-lung progenitor buds, lung branching morphogenesis, and lung maturation-by modulating hematopoietic signaling during pulmonary epithelial differentiation, as validated by stage-specific marker expression across developmental stages (Supplementary Fig.  ). Subsequently, we exposed the organoids to bleomycin (BLM) to generate a model of pulmonary fibrosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Using this model, we also found that Hdac11 expression was significantly upregulated in macrophages within the fibrotic organoids (Fig.  ). Together, these findings indicate that Hdac11 expression is elevated in lung macrophages under fibrotic conditions.
To dissect temporal dynamics, we isolated alveolar macrophages (AMs) and interstitial macrophages (IMs) at serial time points post-BLM. Immunofluorescence quantification revealed progressive Hdac11 upregulation in both macrophage subsets, showing significant induction at 7 days post-BLM administration that further intensified by day 21 (Fig.  ). Complementary transcriptomic and proteomic analyses via RT-qPCR and immunoblotting confirmed this temporal amplification of Hdac11 expression throughout fibrotic progression (Fig.  ). These findings suggest that Hdac11 expression is primarily upregulated in macrophages during the fibrogenesis phase of pulmonary fibrosis.

## Hdac11 deficiency attenuates BLM-induced lung fibrosis
Building upon the above findings, we investigated the functional role of Hdac11 in pulmonary fibrogenesis using Hdac11-knockout (Hdac11-/-) and wild-type (Hdac11+/+) mice subjected to BLM challenge. Micro-CT revealed that BLM administration induced pronounced increases in pulmonary parenchymal density and heterogeneous loss of aerated lung volume in Hdac11+/+ mice, whereas Hdac11-/- mice exhibited significantly attenuated severity of these radiological abnormalities, three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions further confirmed that Hdac11deficiency reduced bleomycin-induced lung volume loss (Fig.  , a finding supported by quantitative analysis (Fig.  ). Consistent with imaging findings, H&E and Masson’s staining revealed that BLM treated Hdac11+/+ mice developed multifocal fibrotic lesions characterized by robust myofibroblast accumulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and aberrant collagen deposition. In contrast, Hdac11 ablation markedly mitigated these BLM-driven architectural distortions (Fig.  ). Fibrosis quantification using a validated, double-blind multi-field Ashcroft scoring system further indicated a significantly reduced lung injury score in Hdac11-/- cohorts (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, the RT-qPCR analysis indicated sharply decreased expression of fibrogenic markers α-smooth muscle cell actin (α-SMA), fibronectin and collagen Ⅰ in BLM induced Hdac11-/- mice compared to that in BLM-induced Hdac11+/+ mice (Fig.  ). Complementary biochemical assays revealed significantly lower hydroxyproline content in lung homogenates of Hdac11-/- mice, further corroborating the antifibrotic phenotype (Fig.  ). Taken together, these findings indicate that Hdac11 deficiency protects mice against BLM-induced lung fibrosis.
 

## The protection conferred by Hdac11 deficiency depends on macrophages
Having confirmed the involvement of Hdac11 in lung fibrosis, we then investigated whether the effect of Hdac11 on pulmonary fibrosis is dependent on macrophages. To delineate macrophage-specific contributions, we delivered 5 × 105
Hdac11+/+ or Hdac11-/- AMs into the trachea of anesthetized recipient mice that were pre-treated with clodronate liposomes to deplete lung resident macrophage population (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Recipient mice adoptively transferred with Hdac11+/+ or Hdac11-/- macrophages were subjected to BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis and analyzed. Adoptive transfer of Hdac11+/+ macrophages to Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- groups of mice exposed to BLM-induced IPF exhibited severe lung tissue damage, along with elevated fibroblast aggregation, extracellular matrix deposition and collagen production assessed by H&E and Masson’s staining (Fig.  ). Quantitative assessment using the Ashcroft scoring system confirmed substantially exacerbated fibrosis severity in these groups compared to controls (Fig.  ). Consistent with histological findings, molecular analysis revealed distinct fibrotic profiles between experimental groups. RT-qPCR quantification demonstrated significantly elevated expression of established fibrosis markers - α-SMA, fibronectin and collagen Ⅰ-in mice reconstituted with Hdac11+/+ AMs compared to recipients of Hdac11-/- AMs following adoptive transfer (Fig.  ). This molecular pattern was paralleled by biochemical evidence, as hydroxyproline assays revealed markedly elevated collagen deposition in mice delivered with Hdac11+/+ AMs compared to the mice that received Hdac11-/- AMs (Fig.  ). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the loss of Hdac11 protects mice against BLM-induced lung fibrosis depending on the macrophages.
 

## Hdac11 drives macrophage M2 polarization and promotes MMT-like phenotypic changes in a deacetylase-dependent manner to promote pulmonary fibrosis
Macrophage polarization has been extensively studied in IPF, where it critically regulates disease progression by modulating the inflammation-fibrosis balance . To investigate whether Hdac11 participates in this process, we examined its role in regulating macrophage polarization in an injury-related microenvironments. Based on our prior work demonstrating that interleukin-33 (IL-33) contributes to IPF initiation and progression , , we compared IL-33-induced macrophage polarization in Hdac11+/+ versus Hdac11-/- AMs. Our results revealed that Hdac11 deficiency significantly reduced expression of M2-associated markers (CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1) following IL-33 stimulation for 24 hours (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), which was further confirmed by ELISA showing a significant decrease in CD206 protein expression (Fig.  ). In contrast, Hdac11 deficiency had no effect on M1-associated cytokines (TNF, IL-1β, and IL-6) (Supplementary Fig.  ), a finding consistent at the protein level as measured by ELISA for TNF and IL-6 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, Hdac11 deficiency markedly suppressed IL-4/IL-13-induced M2 polarization (Supplementary Fig.  ). In murine IPF models, BLM-challenged Hdac11-/- mice exhibited significantly lower CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1 expression versus Hdac11+/+ controls (Fig.  ), with ELISA data specifically corroborating the reduced CD206 protein levels in the corresponding groups (Fig.  ). Adoptive transfer of Hdac11+/+ macrophages into Hdac11+/+ or Hdac11-/- recipients undergoing BLM-induced IPF elevated M2-like cytokine production relative to Hdac11-/-macrophage recipients (Fig.  ), supporting a macrophage-autonomous role for Hdac11.
 To assess macrophage Hdac11’s role in fibroblast differentiation, we co-cultured AMs from BLM-treated Hdac11+/+ or Hdac11-/- mice with primary pulmonary fibroblasts (Fig.  ). Comparative analysis revealed that Hdac11-competent AMs from fibrotic mice drove significant myofibroblast differentiation, marked by pronounced upregulation of α-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen Ⅰ expression in co-cultured fibroblasts. Strikingly, AMs from BLM-treated Hdac11-/- mice attenuated this profibrotic response, demonstrating significant reductions in these myofibroblast markers compared to wild-type controls under identical experimental conditions (Fig.  ). Western blot quantification corroborated these findings, showing substantially enhanced α-SMA and collagen Ⅰ protein levels in fibroblasts co-cultured with AMs from BLM-treated Hdac11+/+ mice relative to those exposed to AMs from BLM-treated Hdac11-/- mice (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Emerging evidence suggests that M2 macrophages undergo MMT, acquiring myofibroblast-like characteristics that enable active ECM component secretion and exacerbate fibrotic responses , . To examine whether Hdac11 regulates this process, we extended IL-33 stimulation of alveolar macrophages (AMs) to 48 hours. Our analysis revealed that IL-33-stimulated AMs exhibited MMT-like features, evidenced by significant upregulation of profibrotic markers (α-SMA, fibronectin, collagen Ⅰ) (Fig.  ), and dual CD68/α-SMA positivity via immunofluorescence (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Complementary in vivo experiments suggested a potential involvement of Hdac11 in reducing myofibroblasts via immunofluorescence to show CD68/α-SMA positive expression and significant upregulation of profibrotic markers (α-SMA, fibronectin, collagen I) (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). To assess whether this pathway is solely IL-33-dependent in vivo, we administered an IL-33-neutralizing antibody in the bleomycin model. Although IL-33 blockade attenuated lung injury, M2 polarization, and MMT-like phenotypic features, Hdac11-/- mice remained significantly protected compared with IL-33-inhibited Hdac11+/+ controls. These data indicate that Hdac11 inhibition provides broader antifibrotic effects than targeting IL-33 alone (Supplementary Fig.  ).
We further investigated whether Hdac11 is sufficient to initiate profibrotic macrophage activation. Overexpression of Hdac11 in AMs, without exogenous profibrotic stimuli, significantly upregulated M2 and profibrotic markers consistent with MMT-like features (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating its role as an upstream driver. Importantly, this effect depended on the catalytic activity of Hdac11: reconstituting Hdac11-/- macrophages with wild-type Hdac11 restored M2 polarization and MMT-like phenotypic features upon IL‑33 stimulation, whereas a catalytically inactive mutant of Hdac11 (Hdac11-H143A) failed to do so (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ).
In summary, these data demonstrate that Hdac11 promotes bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in a deacetylase-dependent manner by enhancing M2 polarization and facilitating MMT-like phenotypic changes, thereby accelerating fibrotic tissue remodeling.

## Hdac11 governs IL-33-mediated mitophagy dysfunction to orchestrate macrophage M2 polarization and MMT-like phenotypic changes
We next investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying IL-33-induced macrophage M2 polarization and MMT-like phenotypic changes. Macrophages stimulated with IL-33 for varying durations (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, or 96 hours) underwent transcriptome sequencing. Transcriptomes from TGF-β-stimulated primary fibroblasts differentiated into myofibroblasts were analyzed in parallel. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) analysis of M1/M2 polarization markers, pan-macrophage activation signatures, and myofibroblast markers (as displayed in the heatmap) suggested that IL-33-induced macrophages exhibit a temporal shift from M1 to M2 phenotypes, followed by a progressive acquisition of profibrotic features consistent with MMT-like phenotypes (Fig.  ).
 Based on the observed phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 polarization initiating at 24 h, we performed differential gene expression analysis comparing IL-33-stimulated macrophages (24 h) to unstimulated controls (0 h). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed predominant enrichment in mitochondrial pathways, including mitochondrion outer membrane organization, morphogenesis, and membrane potential maintenance (bar graphs, Fig.  ). Enrichment was also observed in macrophage/monocyte polarization pathways and protein regulatory processes (post-translational modification, histone acetylation/deacetylation, kinase activity, peptidyl-threonine modification). To determine whether Hdac11 regulates mitochondrial dynamics, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of macrophages. IL-33 stimulation induced severe mitochondrial damage in Hdac11+/+ cells, featuring cristae disorganization and outer membrane rupture. These pathologies were significantly attenuated in Hdac11-/- macrophages (Fig.  ). Mitophagy is a lysosome-dependent process critical for eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria to maintain cellular homeostasis . We thus quantified mitophagic flux in IL-33-stimulated AMs from Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- mice. Western blot analysis revealed that Hdac11 deficiency increased mitochondrial retention of mitophagy-initiating proteins PINK1 and Parkin (Fig.  ), concurrent with elevated LC3-II accumulation-a hallmark of autophagosome formation, and decreased autophagy substrate p62/SQSTM1 (Fig.  ). Complementary confocal microscopy demonstrated significantly increased colocalization of MitoTracker-labeled mitochondria with LysoTracker-stained lysosomes in Hdac11-/- AMs compared to wild-type controls under IL-33 stimulation (Fig.  ), indicative of restored mitophagic flux. Consistent with the in vitro findings, Hdac11 deficiency similarly improved mitochondria-lysosome colocalization in macrophages isolated from in vivo models (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these findings suggest Hdac11 as a key mediator of IL-33-induced mitochondrial injury and mitophagy suppression.
To assess whether defective mitophagy mediates Hdac11-dependent M2 polarization and MMT-associated changes, AMs from Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- mice were pretreated with the mitophagy inhibitor mdivi-1 or vehicle control, followed by IL-33 stimulation for 24 hours. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that pharmacological inhibition of mitophagy with mdivi-1 reversed the Hdac11 deficiency-induced suppression of M2-associated markers, including CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1 (Fig.  ), which was further confirmed at the protein level for CD206 by ELISA (Fig.  ). Crucially, treatment with mdivi-1 abrogated the anti-fibrogenic effects of Hdac11 ablation, as evidenced by restored expression of fibrogenic markers α-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen Ⅰ (Fig.  ), and increased co-localization of α-SMA and CD68 in confocal microscopy images (Fig.  ). These results establish Hdac11-dependent mitophagy impairment as a critical driver of IL-33-induced M2 polarization and fibrotic reprogramming.
To investigate how mitophagy dysfunction primes macrophages for M2 polarization and fibrotic reprogramming, we analyzed the interplay between Hdac11, mitophagy, and key signaling pathways, focusing on STAT6, a master transcriptional regulator of M2 polarization , and SMAD2/3, central mediators of fibrogenic signaling , in IL-33-stimulated macrophages. The results show that IL-33 robustly enhanced phosphorylation of STAT6 and SMAD2/3, whereas Hdac11 knockout significantly attenuated these activation events (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, pharmacological inhibition of mitophagy reversed the suppressive effect of Hdac11 ablation on STAT6 and SMAD2/3 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results demonstrate that Hdac11 promotes STAT6- and SMAD2/3-dependent signaling through mitophagy impairment, thereby driving both M2 polarization and MMT-like features in macrophages.
Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the IL-33 stimulation results in mitophagy impairment, Hdac11 inhibits mitophagy to induce M2 polarization and MMT-like features via activating STAT6 and SMAD2/3 in macrophages.

## Hdac11 represses Parkin to induce mitophagy dysfunction-mediated macrophage reprogramming in pulmonary fibrosis
Parkin is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that plays a pivotal role in mitophagy . Upon mitochondrial damage, Parkin is recruited to the outer mitochondrial membrane to promote the proteasomal degradation of mitochondrial proteins and the selective elimination of damaged mitochondria by autophagy . To explore the molecular mechanism of Hdac11-involved mitophagy, we investigated the effect of Hdac11 on the expression of Parkin in IL-33-stimulated AMs. Analysis by western blot revealed that Hdac11 deficiency effectively restored Parkin expression that reduced by IL-33 stimulation (Fig.  ). To delineate the functional interplay between Parkin and Hdac11 in mitophagy regulation, we performed Parkin knockdown experiments using small interfering RNA (siParkin) in Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- AMs. Successful silencing of Parkin expression (approximately 50% knockdown) was confirmed through complementary RT-qPCR and immunoblotting analyses (Supplementary Fig.  ). Following IL-33 stimulation, Parkin deficiency markedly attenuated the mitochondrial accumulation of PINK1 and Parkin, while concurrently suppressing cytoplasmic LC3-II formation and p62 degradation in Hdac11-deficient cells (Fig.  ). Crucially, Parkin knockdown abolished the IL-33-induced colocalization of mitochondrial (Mito Tracker) and lysosomal (Lyso Tracker) markers in Hdac11-/- AMs (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), demonstrating the requirement of Parkin for complete mitophagic flux. Consistent with the phenotypic modulation observed, genetic knockdown of Parkin significantly diminished IL-33-driven upregulation of M2-type anti-inflammatory markers, including CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1 in Hdac11-/- macrophages (Fig.  ), which was further confirmed at the protein level for CD206 by ELISA (Fig.  ). Moreover, Parkin knockdown in Hdac11-/- AMs substantially reversed the suppressive effects of Hdac11 deficiency on MMT-like phenotypic changes induced by IL-33. This reversal was evidenced by elevated protein expression of α-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen Ⅰ (Fig.  ), accompanied by enhanced co-localization of CD68 with α-SMA in dual immunofluorescence assays (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings indicate that Parkin serves as a critical modulator of Hdac11-dependent regulation in macrophage plasticity during IL-33-driven fibrotic responses.
 Consistent with in vitro findings, in vivo genetic knockdown of Parkin reversed the protective effects of Hdac11 deficiency against bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Parkin siRNA or control siRNA was administered via intratracheal instillation to Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- mice with established bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. The intervention effectively knocked down Parkin mRNA levels in alveolar macrophages (AMs) (Supplementary Fig.  ). As expected, Parkin knockdown in Hdac11-/- mice rescued M2-like mediator production, including CD206, Arg1, Fizz1, and YM-1 (Fig.  ) and MMT-associated markers as quantified by qPCR and immunohistochemistry (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated increased myofibroblasts accumulation and collagen deposition in Parkin-depleted Hdac11-/- lungs compared to Hdac11-/- controls (Fig.  ). Critically, hydroxyproline assays confirmed that Parkin knockdown abolished the Hdac11 deletion-mediated suppression of collagen accumulation (Fig.  ). These data establish Parkin as an essential downstream effector of Hdac11 in mitigating fibrotic progression, by coordinately restraining M2-like and phenotypic changes associated with MMT.

## Hdac11 deacetylated Parkin-K76 to facilitate its ubiquitin-mediated degradation in macrophages
Next, we sought to elucidate the specific molecular mechanism by which Hdac11 regulates Parkin. Initial assessment of Parkin levels in HEK293T cells (Procell system. Wuhan. China) expressing HA-Hdac11 versus vector control revealed that HA-Hdac11 overexpression significantly decreased Parkin protein (Fig.  ), but not its mRNA levels (Fig.  ), suggesting Hdac11 regulates Parkin protein stability. Since a dynamic acetylation equilibrium regulates Parkin , we investigated if Hdac11 modulates Parkin through acetylation. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) using an anti-acetyl-lysine antibody in HEK293T cells co-expressing Flag-Parkin and HA-Hdac11 or vector control showed significantly reduced acetylated Parkin levels in Hdac11-overexpressing cells (Fig.  ), indicating direct Parkin deacetylation by Hdac11.
 Given the established finding that Parkin is dually regulated at lysines K76/K220 through acetylation and ubiquitination modifications, we present its domain architecture supporting these regulatory functions. The N-terminus contains the ubiquitin-like Ubl domain (3-76) for substrate interactions, followed by catalytic elements: RING0 domain (146–229; E3 ligase scaffold), ZnF-RING domain (228–316; zinc-dependent ubiquitin transfer), BRcat domain (301–379; ubiquitin charging), and Rcat domain (409–463; conjugation catalysis). Critically, residues K76 and K220 (arrows) in Ubl/RING0 domains serve as substrates for acetylation (K76-ac/K220-ac) and ubiquitination (K76-ub/K220-ub), collectively governing Parkin’s activity-stability equilibrium , (Fig.  ). We postulate that Hdac11-mediated deacetylation of Parkin releases lysine residues from acetylation-ubiquitination antagonism, ultimately accelerating Parkin degradation. Indeed, ubiquitinated Parkin levels were significantly increased in HA-Hdac11-expressing cells versus controls (Fig.  ). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays confirmed that Hdac11 overexpression significantly shortened Parkin’s half-life (Fig.  ), indicating reduced stability. Furthermore, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 blocked Hdac11-mediated Parkin degradation (Fig.  ). These data collectively establish the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) as the pathway executing Hdac11-dependent Parkin turnover.
To identify key lysine residues targeted by Hdac11 for ubiquitin-mediated degradation, we modeled the Hdac11-Parkin complex using ZDOCK. Docking revealed lys76 and lys220 as prominent interaction sites (Fig.  ), residues implicated in both acetylation and ubiquitination , . Strong binding energies (− 8.31 kcal/mol for lys76; − 8.7 kcal/mol for lys220) indicated high-affinity interactions. Parkin mutants with lysine residues 76 and 220 substituted to arginine (K76R and K220R) were generated. Subsequent co-immunoprecipitation (IP) and Western blot (WB) analysis revealed that the K76R mutation markedly attenuated Hdac11-mediated Parkin ubiquitination (Fig.  ). Furthermore, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays demonstrated that Hdac11 overexpression no longer significantly shortened Parkin’s half-life following the K76R mutation (Fig.  ). Consistent with this, treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 confirmed that the K76R mutation substantially blocked Hdac11-induced proteasomal degradation of Parkin (Fig.  ), underscoring K76 as a critical residue for Hdac11-regulated Parkin turnover.
We next asked whether Hdac11’s deacetylase activity is required for Parkin regulation. Reconstitution of Hdac11-deficient macrophages with wild-type Hdac11, but not a catalytically inactive mutant, reduced Parkin protein and acetylation levels while increasing its ubiquitination, confirming the enzyme activity dependence of this regulation (Fig.  ). To further substantiate the physiological relevance, we evaluated the effect of the Hdac11-specific inhibitor SIS17. In cells expressing wild-type Parkin, SIS17 increased Parkin acetylation and ubiquitination, leading to protein stabilization. Moreover, under Hdac11-overexpressing conditions, SIS17 effectively reversed Hdac11-induced Parkin degradation and concurrently enhanced Parkin acetylation and ubiquitination. Importantly, this rescuing effect was abolished in cells expressing the K76R mutant (Fig.  ).
Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Hdac11 specifically promotes the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway-mediated degradation of Parkin by facilitating ubiquitination at K76 following its deacetylation, and that pharmacological inhibition of Hdac11 by SIS17 stabilizes Parkin protein levels specifically by modulating its acetylation and ubiquitination at K76.

## Inhibition of Hdac11 effectively protects against pulmonary fibrosis
To assess the therapeutic potential of targeting Hdac11, BLM-induced IPF mice were treated with Hdac11 specific inhibitor SIS17 or Solvent control via i.p. injection every 3 days, starting 7 days after the establishment of BLM-induced. The mice in different groups showed no marked changes in the behavior, weight, and respiration rate. Lung samples were analyzed after 21 days post-BLM (Fig.  ). As expected, H&E staining and Masson staining showed BLM worsened pulmonary alveolar structure remodeling and accumulated fibrotic lesions, as well as increased collagen deposition (blue area), while SIS17 treatment markedly reduced the above fibrotic conditions and collagen deposition in a dose dependent manner (Fig.  ), with significantly reduced hydroxyproline content (Fig.  ). Consistently, RT-qPCR assay revealed that SIS17 treatment notably reduced the expression of M2-like macrophage markers including CD206, Arg1, Fizz1 and YM-1 (Fig.  ) and fibrogenic genes a-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen Ⅰ (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence staining of lung tissues further confirmed that SIS17 notably reduced the co-localization of α-SMA and CD68 (Fig.  ). Moreover, in line with our proposed mechanism, SIS17 treatment downregulated Parkin expression in lung-infiltrating macrophages (Fig.  ).
 Collectively, these data establish that selective Hdac11 inhibition with SIS-17 effectively mitigates BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

## Discussion
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive chronic interstitial lung disease with high mortality rates. The treatment of IPF remains a significant clinical challenge due to the complexity of its pathogenesis . Emerging evidence implicates epigenetic dysregulation as a central regulator of fibrotic remodeling . In this study, we identify Hdac11 as an epigenetic driver through its dual role in promoting macrophage M2 polarization and myofibroblast differentiation, mediated by the suppression of Parkin-dependent mitophagy. Our findings not only uncover a previously unrecognized pathway connecting mitochondrial quality control to fibrotic remodeling but also position Hdac11 as a potential therapeutic target for IPF.
Histone deacetylases (Hdacs) constitute a family of are enzymes that play pivotal roles in transcriptional repression through chromatin remodeling. Hdac dysregulation is implicated in the pathogenesis of IPF . Hdac11, the sole member of the class IV Hdac family, has garnered significant attention as a potent epigenetic modulator in immune regulation and tumorigenesis . Elevated Hdac11 expression is observed in lung cancer tissues and is associated with poor prognosis . Hdac11 overexpression inhibits influenza A virus (IAV) infection but paradoxically impairs bacterial clearance and promotes long-term Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection in the lungs , . Whether and how Hdac11 regulates the pathogenesis of IPF remains unclear. In the present study, we identified significantly elevated Hdac11 expression in lung macrophages from both IPF patients and bleomycin-induced murine fibrotic lungs. Single-cell analyses suggest that this upregulation is largely restricted to lung macrophages, with minimal changes observed in fibroblasts or macrophages from other organs, highlighting a tissue-specific response during pulmonary fibrosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Strikingly, Hdac11-/- mice exposed to bleomycin demonstrated attenuated pulmonary fibrosis, as evidenced by reduction in α-SMA+ myofibroblast accumulation and decrease in ECM deposition compared to Hdac11+/+ littermates. Importantly, macrophage-specific ablation and adoptive transfer experiments confirmed that macrophage-derived Hdac11 is essential for bleomycin-induced fibrogenesis. Our study focuses on how Hdac11 intrinsically programs macrophage function within the fibrotic niche. Notably, Hdac11 deficiency was associated with a reduction in total cell numbers in bronchoalveolar lavage following bleomycin injury, while the relative proportions of major immune cell subsets remained largely comparable (Supplementary Figs.  , ) is decrease may reflect both attenuated tissue damage and changes in macrophage-derived signals that secondarily influence leukocyte accumulation, consistent with reported roles of H11 in modulating immunoregulatory pathways , . While we cannot rule out an additional effect on macrophage recruitment, our data establish that Hdac11 expression is critical for dictating a pro-fibrotic macrophage phenotype. Although the upstream signals remain to be fully elucidated, preliminary promoter analyses and luciferase assays suggest that transcription factors such as EGR1 may contribute to Hdac11 upregulation under fibrotic conditions (Supplementary Fig.  ). This macrophage-restricted expression pattern is consistent with recent reports that Hdac11 plays a critical role in macrophage regulation. For instance, Hdac11 was initially identified as a key modulator of IL-10 cytokine release in macrophages . Further studies revealed that its deficiency promotes acetylation of histones H3 and H4 at the Nos2 promoter, thereby enhancing Nos2 transcription and upregulating inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) levels in macrophages . This concept is supported by prior work establishing Hdac11 as a critical regulator of macrophage immune function, for instance, through the modulation of IL-10 production and the epigenetic regulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) via histone acetylation at the Nos2promoter (citations). Therefore, beyond merely affecting cell numbers, Hdac11 likely shapes fibrotic progression by programming the pathogenic activity of macrophages within the alveolar environment.
Mechanistically, Hdac11 deficiency suppressed M2 polarization and reduced MMT-like features following IL-33 stimulation. As a damage-associated molecular pattern released during alveolar epithelial injury, IL-33 maintains elevated levels in both pulmonary fibrosis patients and murine models , . Recent studies have made significant progress in understanding IL-33-mediated regulation of macrophage polarization . Specifically, IL-33 promotes M2 polarization by upregulating prototypical M2 markers (Arg1 and YM-1) and enhancing anti-inflammatory responses and tissue-repair functions , . Complementary studies in parasitic infection models further demonstrate that IL-33 amplifies type-2 immunity by inducing IL-4/IL-5/IL-13 production . Our investigation revealed that Hdac11 deficiency significantly attenuated IL-33-induced expression of M2 markers (CD206, Fizz1, Arg1, YM-1) while concurrently suppressing myofibroblast-associated proteins (α-SMA, fibronectin, collagen Ⅰ). Although the role of MMT is an area of ongoing investigation, emerging evidence from renal, cardiac, and pulmonary fibrosis models suggests that it may be associated with the expansion of myofibroblast populations , , – . Our data align with this concept, suggesting that Hdac11 is a regulator of macrophage plasticity associated with MMT-like features. This dual regulatory effect on both M2-like and profibrotic phenotypic changes in macrophages highlights the role of Hdac11 in shaping a pro-fibrotic immune environment. These findings extend prior work showing that IL-33-ST2 signaling promotes pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization through STAT6-dependent pathways , our study reveals Hdac11 as an upstream epigenetic checkpoint controlling IL-33 responsiveness.
A striking finding of our study is the identification of Hdac11 as a direct regulator of mitochondrial quality control in macrophages. While Hdac11 possesses potent deacylase activity that can surpass its deacetylase function for certain substrates , , we provide compelling evidence that its deacetylase activity toward Parkin is primary in IPF pathogenesis. Specifically, we demonstrate that Hdac11 represses Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase essential for mitophagy, through site-specific deacetylation at lysine residues K76. This post-translational modification accelerates ubiquitin-mediated degradation of Parkin, leading to impaired mitochondrial clearance and metabolic reprogramming-a cascade culminating in pro-fibrotic macrophage activation. While recent studies have implicated Hdac11 in mitophagy regulation in other contexts (e.g., bacterial infection and auditory cell models) , , our work uniquely establishes a direct post-translational mechanism wherein Hdac11 deacetylates Parkin at K76 to drive its destabilization, specifically within macrophages in pulmonary fibrosis. The role of Hdac11’s deacylase activity in fibrosis remains an important question for future research, while our findings align with emerging paradigms of mitochondrial dysfunction in fibrogenesis . For instance, impaired mitophagy in alveolar epithelial cells promotes senescence-associated secretory phenotypes that drive fibroblast proliferation . However, our data uniquely implicate Hdac11-mediated macrophage mitochondrial stress as a critical contributor to fibrotic remodeling. This metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk mirrors recent findings that inhibition of Hdacs suppresses cervical cancer cell proliferation via activating mitophagy through mediating Parkin acetylation, but to our knowledge, this is the report of Hdac11-driven mitophagy dysfunction in fibrosis.
The mechanistic link between Hdac11 and Parkin degradation also suggests combination therapeutic strategies. For example, Hdac11 inhibitors could be paired with mitophagy enhancers like urolithin A that stabilize Parkin . Furthermore, our identification of K76 as a critical acetylation site provides a structural basis for designing small molecules to protect Parkin from Hdac11-mediated deacetylation. Our pharmacological studies using the Hdac11-specific inhibitor SIS17 demonstrate striking attenuation of BLM-induced fibrosis, paralleling findings in genetic knockout models. These results are particularly significant given the limited efficacy of pan-Hdac inhibitors (e.g., SAHA), likely due to opposing roles of different Hdac classes. The selectivity of Hdac11 inhibition may circumvent toxicity associated with broader Hdac blockade while preserving beneficial epigenetic regulation. Although Hdac11 inhibition demonstrates promising antifibrotic effects, the possibility of compensatory activity by other Hdac isoforms warrants consideration. Our transcriptional analyses show that Hdac1-10 expression is not significantly altered in Hdac11-deficient macrophages, indicating minimal compensation at the mRNA level (Supplementary Fig.  ). Building upon these findings, we will leverage our research team’s expertise in lung organoid fibrosis modeling to investigate lead compounds and small-molecule entities targeting Hdac11 for the prevention and treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.
Our work establishes Hdac11 as a master regulator of macrophage mitochondrial quality control and fibrotic responses. By coupling epigenetic control with mitophagy, Hdac11 promotes macrophage M2-like polarization and is associated with profibrotic phenotypic changes, including MMT-like features, which may contribute to fibrotic remodeling in the lung. These findings open an avenue for targeting Hdac11 and mitophagy in fibrotic diseases and reinforce the importance of macrophage plasticity in tissue remodeling.

## Methods

## Mice, clinical samples and human lung organoids
Hdac11-/- mice (C57BL/6N-Hdac11em1cyagen, stock #KOCMP-232232-Hdac11-B6N-VA) purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China) were backcrossed to C57BL/6 mice for 11 generations. C57BL/6 (stock #B204) mice were obtained from Slac Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Jiangnan University. Control mice were littermates of the experimental mice and were housed under a stable 12-hour light/dark cycle at a constant temperature of 25 °C and 55% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were performed using 8-10-week-old male mice, with 6–8 mice per group. Experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangnan University (JN. No 20211130m1720615[501]). Human lung tissues, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis samples and healthy control tissues, were provided by Wuxi People’s Hospital (Jiangsu, China) and approved for research use by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (Approval No. JNU20220310IRB31). The generation and use of human lung organoids derived from iPSCs were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (Approval No. JNU202601RB002). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

## BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis
After anesthesia, 1.4 μ/kg of BLM (#BT-ENZ-018, BioTang, USA) in 50 μL of sterile saline (#CZ0027, LEAGENE, China) was intratracheally (i.t.) delivered into wild-type (WT, referred as Hdac11+/+) and Hdac11-/- mice (8-10 weeks old) via an intratracheal route. Mice administered with the same volume of saline served as controls. On days 3, 7, or 21 after bleomycin or saline administration, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane anesthesia, confirmed by loss of consciousness, for the analysis of pulmonary fibrosis.

## CT imaging
The Quantum GX2 micro-CT enables high-resolution reconstruction of localized regions from the original full-field-of-view (FOV) scan. Following a full-body scan, a target area was selected in region of interest (ROI) mode and reconstructed at higher resolution. CT imaging was performed using the Quantum GX2 micro-CT (PerkinElmer, MA, USA) with the following parameters: voltage, 90 kV, current, 88 μA, scanning resolution range, 1–100 μm, pixel resolution, 12 μm, scanning rotation, 360。, angular step interval, 0.5。. Image reconstruction and analysis were conducted with software provided by AnalyzeDirect (Overland Park, KS, USA).

## Lung histology and immunohistochemistry
Left lungs were intratracheally inflated with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, #DF0111, LEAGENE, China), fixed in fresh 4% neutral-buffered PFA (#BL539A, Biosharp, China) for 48 hr, then processed for paraffin embedding. Sections (4 μm) were stained with H&E (#BL735B, Biosharp, China) or Masson’s trichrome (#D026-1-3, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) using standard protocols. Pulmonary fibrosis was graded via the modified Ashcroft scale (0–8) . For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Hdac11 antibody (PB9630, BOSTER, USA). Negative controls omitted the primary antibody. Antigen-antibody complexes were detected via the ABC method (#AR1022, Boster Biological Technology, USA) and visualized with DAB substrate.

## Hydroxyproline assay
Lungs were hydrolyzed, and total lung hydroxyproline levels were quantified using a hydroxyproline assay kit (A030-2-1, NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Each sample was tested in triplicate. Data are expressed as micrograms of hydroxyproline per gram left lung.

## Cells Isolation and culture

## Alveolar macrophages (AMs)
BALF from control and BLM-treated mice was centrifuged (200 × g, 4 °C, 15min). Cell pellets underwent RBC lysis (#C3702, Beyotime, China), followed by 1 hr adhesion. Adherent AMs were collected by gentle scraping after washing with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free PBS .

## Interstitial macrophages (IMs)
Minced lungs were digested in RPMI 1640 (#C3001-0500, Vivacell, China) /10% FBS (#C04001-500, Vivacell, China)/1 mg/mL collagenase D (#C5138, Sigma, Germany)/30 μg/mL DNase I (#D4263, Sigma, Germany) at 37 °C for 30 min. RBC-lysed cells were plated in serum-free RPMI for 1 hr. Adherent IMs were maintained overnight in complete RPMI. Purity (>95%) was verified by flow cytometry .

## Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)
Bone marrow cells were collected from the femurs and tibia of mice and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, #C3113-0500, Vivacell, China) containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (#BL505A, Biosharp, China), and 10 ng/mL mCSF (#CB34, Novoprotein, China) for 7 days. The culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing mCSF, FBS and penicillin/streptomycin after 3 days and differentiated BMDMs after 7 days of culture were used in cellular assays.
pulmonary fibroblasts: WT mouse lungs were minced and digested in DMEM/5% FBS/collagenase IV (1 mg/mL)/DNase I (30 μg/mL) for 30 min. Washed cells were cultured in DMEM/10% FBS/penicillin-streptomycin for 7–14 days. Tissue fragments were removed pre-experiment.

## iPSC culture
iPSC line ChiPSC7 was obtained from CellArtis (Y00275, created by reprogramming skin fibroblasts, RRID: CVCL_RM98, passage number 20-24) and maintained under feeder-free conditions as previously described1. Briefly, cells were cultured with mTeSR1 medium (#85850, STEMCELL Technologies, China) on six-well plates coated with Matrigel (#354277, Corning, USA) at a 1:50 dilution in an incubator with 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were checked daily for differentiation and passaged every 4 days. Before the initiation of differentiation, the pluripotency of iPSCs was examined by the expression of NANOG and OCT4 using immunofluorescence staining, with a > 90% dual positive proportion indicative of acceptable quality, as previously described2.

## Differentiation of lung organoids and establishment of fibrosis models
Human lung organoids were generated by using a commercial differentiation kit (#3d0010, iORGANtech, China), as previously described . In brief, endoderm and mesoderm were generated by treating the iPSCs with 3-d co-induction medium2, which consisted of RPMI 1640 (#61870036, Gibco, USA), 10% mTeSR1, 2% NB27 w/o insulin (#01033, iORGANtech, China), 10 ng/mL BMP4 (#314-BP, R&D Systems, USA), and 100 ng/mL Activin A (#11348-AC, R&D Systems, USA). SOX17 and KDR staining confirmed endodermal and mesodermal induction. Then, lung bud-like progenitor clusters were generated by sequential treatment with anteriorized medium for 3 d and with ventralized medium for another 3 d. Anteriorized medium was comprised of 100 ng/mL NOG (#6057-NG, R&D Systems, USA) and 5 μM A83-01 (#2939/10, Tocris, UK), while ventralized medium contained 3 μM CHIR99021 (#4423/10, Tocris, UK), 10 ng/mL BMP4, 10 ng/mL FGF7 (#251-KG, R&D Systems, USA), 10 ng/mL FGF10 (#345-FG, R&D systems, USA), 50 nM RA (#0695/50, Tocris, UK), 10 ng/mL VEGF (#298-VS, R&D Systems, USA) and 10 ng/mL SCF (#11010-SC, R&D Systems, USA). NKX2.1 staining verified lung progenitor specification, while CD45 identified emerging hematopoietic-like cells. Then, adherent structures formed at day 9 were mechanically detached with pipette tips, and transferring to an ultra-low attachment plate (#174930, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 7-d suspension culture. Lung bud-like progenitor clusters formed at day 16 were then 1:1 embedded into a Matrigel sandwich assembled on the insert of 12-well transwell (#3460, Corning, USA). Co-localization of NKX2.1⁺ and CD45⁺ populations at day 16 confirmed epithelial–hematopoietic co-specification. The ventralized medium was used until the end of day 35, thereafter supplemented the lung maturation cocktail DCI, which consisted of 0.1 μM dexamethasone (#D4902, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 50 μM 8-Br-cAMP (#B7880, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 50 μM IBMX (#2845/50, Tocris, UK). CD68 immunofluorescence at maturation confirmed macrophage-lineage cells within the organoids.
Fibrotic modeling was established by exposing of the day-60 organoids with 100 μM bleomycin (BLM). BLM was continuously added following medium replacement (every 48 h). On day 21 post-induction, organoids were subjected to macroscopic documentation followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 minutes. Subsequent histological analyses included Masson’s trichrome staining and multiplex immunofluorescence staining.

## Macrophage adoptive transfer
The resident pulmonary macrophages of Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- mice were depleted by i.t delivery of liposomes encapsulating clodronate (50 μL, #CP-005-005, LIPOSOMA, Netherlands). 2 days after clodronate liposomal delivery, Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- recipient mice were administered with Hdac11+/+ and Hdac11-/- AMs (i.t 5 ^105 cells per mouse in 50 μL saline). After 24 h of AMs adoptive transfer, all four groups of mice were subjected to BLM-induced IPF and analyzed.

## Transmission electron microscopy
Electron microscopy was used to assess ultrastructural alterations in the macrophages. AMs were stimulated with IL-33 (10 ng/mL, #C091, Novoprotein, China) for 24 hr, cells were were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde/2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.15 M sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, #C091, Novoprotein, China) overnight, and then commissioned by the Shiyanjia lab (www.shiyanjia.com). A transmission electron microscope (H-7650, Hitachi, Japan) was used for observation.

## Mitophagy analysis
Mitochondria were isolated using a commercial extraction kit (#C3601, Beyotime, China). The mitochondrial pellet was lysed in RIPA buffer and immunoblotted with antibodies against PINK1 (Proteintech, #23274-1-AP) and Parkin (Signalway, #49374). Cytoplasmic fractions were analyzed for LC3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3868) and p62 (Cell Signaling, #23214S).
For organelle imaging, clinorotated cells on coverslips were sequentially stained with 250 nM MitoTracker Deep Red FM (Yeasen, #40743ES50; 30 min, 37 °C) and 50 nM LysoTracker Green (Yeasen, #40738ES50; 45 min, 37 °C), then imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM880).

## Structural modeling
The Hdac11-Parkin complex structure was computationally predicted using HDOCK with default parameters. Resultant models were energy-minimized in AMBER22 and visualized using PyMOL v2.5 (Schrödinger).

Plasmid transfections were carried out by using Lipofectamine TM 3000 transfection reagent (#L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, formerly Mirus), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For immunopreciptation, cells were lysed with 0.1% NP-40 buffer (#BL653A, Biosharpas) described above. In all experiments, absorbance was measured using a Synergy H4 multifunctional microplate reader, and sample concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated on each microplate.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Cells were isolated from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), lysed to remove erythrocytes, centrifuged, and resuspended for surface staining. The cells were incubated with flow cytometry antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing, the samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACS Aria III).

## Statistical analysis
Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. The in vitro data were obtained from at least three independent experiments, and the in vivo data were obtained from experiments by at least 6 mice in each group. Statistical differences between single comparisons were performed by Student’s t test, and the differences between two groups were performed by Mann-Whitney tests, multiple groups by one-way ANOVA or one-way ANOVA non-parametric ANOVA Kruskall-Wallis test using GraphPad Prism 9. All in vitro and in vivo experiments were repeated at least three times independently with similar results.

All data are publicly available and can be freely downloaded and reused. The code used for analysis in this study is based on standard open-source software and has not been uploaded. Access to the code may be obtained from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. All data are included in the Supplementary Information or available from the authors, as are unique reagents used in this Article. The raw numbers for charts and graphs are available in the Source Data file whenever possible.  are provided in this paper.
