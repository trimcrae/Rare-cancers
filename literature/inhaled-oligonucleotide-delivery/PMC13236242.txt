## Abstract

## Background:
Although monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation is essential for innate immune defense, its dysregulation can drive excessive inflammation in sepsis. Reticulocalbin 3 (RCN3) is an endoplasmic reticulum chaperone in the secretory pathway implicated in alveolar epithelial maturation and lung injury repair. This study aimed to investigate the effect of RCN3 on monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation in pneumonia-associated sepsis.

## Methods:
Patients with pneumonic sepsis (n = 10) and age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers (n = 8) were enrolled from Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital. Peripheral blood was collected within 12 h after diagnosis; peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated and monocytes were purified for transcriptional analyses of RCN3 and macrophage markers. For in vivo validation, myeloid-specific Rcn3 knockout mice received intratracheal lipopolysaccharide (10 mg/kg) or saline, and were assessed 16 h later (n = 3–6 per group). Mechanistic studies in THP1 cells, human primary circulating monocytes and mouse bone marrow-derived monocytes used RCN3 knockdown and overexpression to evaluate differentiation markers, intracellular oxidative stress, and phagocytosis assays and autophagy flux evaluation.

## Results:
In patients with pneumonic sepsis, RCN3 was significantly increased in circulating monocytes, but not lymphocytes, and its expression correlated with the macrophage marker CD68. RCN3 was also upregulated during monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation in both human and mouse monocytes. Furthermore, RCN3 knockdown attenuated differentiation, phagocytosis, and the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory response, whereas RCN3 overexpression had opposite effects. In vivo, myeloid-specific Rcn3 deletion decreased lung interstitial macrophage accumulation after intratracheal LPS instillation and alleviated acute lung injury. Mechanistically, the blunted differentiation caused by RCN3 deficiency was associated with reduced autophagy, due to impaired autophagosome formation.

## Conclusions:
The upregulation of RCN3 in sepsis facilitates monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation by modulating autophagy. These findings also suggest that RCN3 in circulating monocytes presents a potential therapeutic target for sepsis.

Keywords: Pneumonic sepsis, Reticulocalbin 3, Autophagy, Monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation

## Introduction
Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of multiple organ dysfunction originating from the deregulated responses to infection, in which pneumonic sepsis has the highest incidence.[ ] Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), characterized by severe spread of alveolar inflammatory injury, is a common and often fatal consequence of severe sepsis.[ ] Suppression of the natural immune response often occurs in earlier stage sepsis, followed by the dysregulation of the inflammatory response.[ , ] During the inflammatory response, activated monocytes migrate to tissues and differentiate into macrophages.[ ] The differentiation of circulating monocytes to inflammatory macrophages is critical for innate immune defenses because their phagocytic function is essential for pathogen evacuation and macrophage-derived cytokines play a vital role in guiding the immune response.[ ] However, uncontrolled differentiation causes extensive macrophage infiltration, leading to local and systemic inflammatory overreaction. In addition, recruited mononuclear macrophages exhibit greater immune reactivity and plasticity than tissue-inhabitant macrophages confined to the lung.[ , ] Harnessing monocyte-derived alveolar macrophages during pulmonary infection is essential to avoid sepsis progression. However, the mechanism of this regulation is far from clear.
Autophagy is a widespread pathway induced by a variety of stresses, including nutrient and energy stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).[ , ] Autophagy maintains cell homeostasis by establishing bilayer membrane vesicles for degradation and recycling of macromolecules. These vesicles encapsulate cytoplasm and organelles, which are then transferred to lysosomes for degradation, defending against the destruction caused by pathogens and microorganisms in the cellular surroundings.[ ] Previous studies have indicated that autophagy markers accumulate and autophagy is activated during the monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation induced by colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1). The suppression of autophagy significantly hinders this differentiation and cytokine production, leading to apoptosis.[ , ] Although these observations indicated that autophagy induction is essential for monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation through survival-associated pathways, the regulatory mechanism of autophagy in monocyte differentiation remains elusive.
Reticulocalbin 3 (RCN3), belonging to the CREC family (Cab45/Reticulocalbin/ERC-55/Calumenin), functions as a chaperone protein localized to the secretory pathway in the ER and plays an essential role in protecting alveolar epithelial cells against ER stress and apoptosis.[ – ] Recent studies have indicated that RCN3 also localizes in the cytoplasm, where RCN3 enhances the alveolar epithelial inflammatory response by regulating nuclear factor-kappa B signaling and promotes lung fibroblast activation by interacting with transforming growth factor beta signaling.[ , ] Given the importance of RCN3 in lung epithelial cells and fibroblasts, RCN3 is involved in acute lung injury (ALI)/ARDS and interstitial lung disease.
Because autophagy is intimately associated with the differentiation of monocytes to macrophages and the RCN3 sequence bears a potential microtubule associate protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) binding sequence, we hypothesize that RCN3 facilitates mononuclear-macrophage differentiation by regulating autophagy. With this study, we aimed to investigate the regulatory effect of RCN3 on autophagy and its subsequent effect on mononuclear-macrophage differentiation during sepsis.

## Methods

## Ethics statement
All studies involving human blood samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (No. 2021-KE-313), and signed informed consent was obtained from all subjects for the research-related use of blood samples. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committees of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (No. 2021-Dong-17) and the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Animal care and experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

## Isolation of PBMCs and CD14+ monocyte
Patients with pneumonic sepsis (n = 10) and age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers (n = 8) were enrolled in Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital between 2021 and 2022. Patients (≥18 years old) met the Sepsis-3 criteria (an acute elevation of sequential organ failure assessment [SOFA] scores ≥2 points on suspected or confirmed infections).[ ] Patients with diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, pregnancy, chronic immunosuppressive therapy, and infection in other systems were excluded. Approximately 10 mL of peripheral whole blood was collected from patients within 12 h after diagnosis. Simultaneously, approximately 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected from the age- and gender-matched healthy controls, volunteers with standard chest computed tomography scan results from the physical examination center of the hospital during the same period. After centrifuge, the blood cells were subjected to red blood cells (RBC) lysis and resuspended in lymphocyte separation medium (MP Biomedical, #50494, USA). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation at 400 × g for 30 minutes. Monocytes were further purified from PBMC by Dynabeads CD14 (Invitrogen, #11149D, USA) and cultured in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (10% FBS–RPMI 1640) with 50 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (Sigma, #H6916, USA) for differentiation.

## Establishment of myeloid-specific Rcn3 deletion and ALI model
By crossing Lyz2-Cre mice carrying Cre recombinase inserted in the lysozyme-M (Lyz2) gene locus (The Jackson Laboratory, USA) and Rcn3flox/flox (Rcn3f/f) mice (generated in our laboratory),[ ] the myeloid-specific Rcn3 deletion mice (CKO mice, Rcn3f/f/Lyz-Cre) were generated and their littermates (Rcn3f/f) served as controls. The intratracheal administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was performed in 8-week-old mice at a dose of 10 mg/kg in 50 μL saline (25 μL × 2), and sterile saline was instilled at the same procedure as a control (n = 3–6 per group). The samples were collected 16 h after LPS administration. All experiments were conducted using male mice. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free system, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle, and kept at 22–23 °C with access to water ad libitum.

## Preparation and differentiation of murine BM cells
To obtain bone marrow (BM)–derived macrophages (BMDMs), BM was flushed from femurs and tibiae of 8-week-old Rcn3f/f and Rcn3f/f/Lyz-Cre mice. The BM cells were cultured for 7 days in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and 20 ng/mL M-CSF (Sigma, #M9170). After 7 days of culture, the cell cultures contained >95% macrophages, as determined by CD11b and F4/80 staining.

## Cells culture and differentiation assay
The THP-1 cell line was obtained from Procell and cultured in 10% FBS–RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 50 μmol/L β-mercaptoethanol and penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (100 μg/mL) under standard cell culture conditions at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment. To induce differentiation into macrophages, THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Abcam, #ab120297, USA) for 24 and 48 h.

## Detection of intracellular oxidative stress
Intracellular oxidative stress was assessed by detecting the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Beyotime, #S0033S, China). The cells were treated with 10 µmol/L DCFH-DA and incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C, avoiding light. Fluorescence intensity was measured with a Varioskan Flash microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm.

## Phagocytosis assay
The phagocytic activity of macrophages was assessed by quantifying the uptake of red fluorescent pHrodo BioParticles (Invitrogen, #P35360). The macrophages were plated into 24-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well. A suspension of Escherichia coli particles was added to the cell culture microplate at a dilution of 1:20. The cell–particle mixture was then incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C to evaluate the uptake activity using fluorescence microscopy.

## Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596018), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, #R045A, China). Subsequently, the cDNA samples were subjected to real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, #RR820) and detected using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In human samples, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal reference, whereas ribosomal protein L19 (RPL19) was used as an internal reference for mouse samples. The relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method (the primer sequences for real-time-PCR are detailed in Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C790).

## Flow cytometry and cell sorting
BMDMs and THP1-derived macrophages were separated using trypsin (Gibco, #25200056), followed by obtaining a single-cell suspension through a 40-µm cell strainer. The single cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline and incubated with an Fc receptor-blocking agent (BD Biosciences, #553141, USA) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were treated with CD14 antibody (BD Biosciences, #557831), CD11b (BD Biosciences, #555388), CD68 (BD Biosciences, #562117), and F4/80 (BD Biosciences, #565410) for 30 minutes at 4 °C. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using fluorescence-activated cell sorting CantoII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).
Lung interstitial macrophage analyses proceeded as follows. After the animals were euthanized, the lungs were perfused through the right ventricle with 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline. The lungs were then dissected into small pieces and digested for 1 hour at 37°C in Hank’s balanced salt solution containing 5% FBS, 1 mg/mL collagenase A (Roche, COLLA-RO, USA), and 0.05 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, #4536282001). To obtain single-lung cell suspensions, after 45 minutes of digestion, the digested suspension was flushed through a 40-µm cell strainer to dissociate aggregates. Subsequently, cells were stained for intracellular protein in 100 μL of fixation/permeabilization buffer (BD Biosciences, #554714) with Fc block for 30 minutes at room temperature. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (BD Biosciences), including anti-CD11b (M1/70) (#552850), anti-CD11c (#557400), anti-CD45 (#550994), and anti-F4/80 (#565410), were applied to identify alveolar macrophages as CD45+F4/80+CD11c+ and interstitial macrophages as CD45+F4/80+CD11b+CD11c–.

## Immunoblotting assay
Protein extracts (30–50 µg) derived from cells were used for the immunoblot analyses. The samples were subjected to Western blotting with specific antibodies against RCN3 (Sigma, #HPA050402), LC3B (NOVUS, #NB100-2220, USA), and Atg7 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2631, USA). Tubulin (Yeasen, #30301ES80, China) or glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Yeasen, #30201ES80) antibodies were used for protein loading controls.

## Cellular immunofluorescence staining
The BMDMs were grown on poly-L-lysine–coated glass coverslips for immunofluorescence staining (IF). After fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fix solution (Beyotime, #P0099), cells were incubated with antibodies specific to RCN3 (1:100, Sigma, HPA050402) and F4/80 (1:50, Santa Cruz, sc-377009, USA). The immune signals were then detected using secondary anti-rabbit antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, ZSGB-BIO, ZF0511, China) and Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500, ZSGB-BIO, ZF0513).

## RCN3 knockdown and overexpression
The lentivirus for the overexpressing RCN3 (NM_020650.3) construct and for the RCN3-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was produced according to our previously published article.[ ] The sequence of RCN3 without signal peptide was constructed into FUGW plasmid: FUGW vector/Flag-FUGW-RCN3. The shRNA construct was used for the PLKO.1 vector bearing the targeting sequence GGACATCGTGATTGCTGAAAC. The lentiviral particles were produced in 293T cells using lentiviral shuttle vectors together with Pspax2 and vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV-G). After 48 h of transfection, the medium was removed, centrifuged, and further concentrated using the Universal Virus Concentration Kit (Beyotime, #C2901M) following the manufacturer’s instructions. THP1 cells were plated at approximately 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates with 10% FBS RPMI 1640, and the cells were transfected by overexpression or knockdown of lentivirus 24 h after transfection; the cells were cultured in FBS-free medium overnight. PMA treatment was performed 3 days after transduction.

## Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B transfection assay
Selected THP1 suspension cells transfected with shRNA control (shC) and shRNA RCN3 (shRCN3) were inoculated into 12-well plates, achieving a density of 1 × 105 cells/500 µL; the adenovirus (Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3) was transfected into cells according to multiplicity of cellular infection (MOI) = 100. Following the observation of virus infection using a fluorescence microscope 24 h after infection, PMA was added to each well to induce differentiation. The cells were then plated onto a 12-well plate containing a slide. After 48 h, the cells were fixed, mounted, and observed under a fluorescence microscope. On microscopic imaging, the combination of red-green fluorescence revealed yellow spots indicative of autophagosomes and red spots indicative of autophagolysosomes.

## Statistical analysis
For normally distributed continuous variables, the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. To compare continuous variables, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test the normality of the data. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Statistical comparisons of three or more groups were performed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Bonferroni’s post hoc test) for normally distributed variables, one-way Kruskal–Wallis tests (Dunn’s post hoc test) for non-normally distributed variables, or two-way analysis of variance (Tukey’s post hoc test). A P value of <0.05 was deemed to indicate a significant difference between groups. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

## Results

## Upregulated monocyte RCN3 and its association with CD68 in pneumonic sepsis
Because the deregulation of the inflammatory response of PBMCs is one of the marked characteristics during sepsis, we first analyzed the transcriptional level of RCN3 in PBMCs in patients with clinically diagnosed pneumonic sepsis. The characteristics of the patients with sepsis and normal subjects are found in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C790. RCN3 was significantly upregulated in PBMCs in study patients (n = 10) compared with healthy controls (n = 8) [Figure A]. To clarify whether RCN3 is upregulated in monocytes or lymphocytes, we isolated the predominant cell types in PBMCs, monocytes and lymphocytes, using CD14+ magnetic beads and evaluated the expression of RCN3 in these cells. Interestingly, RCN3 was significantly upregulated in monocytes but not in lymphocytes in these patients [Figure B]. Monocytes from these patients also had more phagocyte-like phenotypes, including enhanced expression of the surface molecules CD14 and CD68 and the inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), suggesting a high inflammatory response [Figure C]. We further evaluated the correlation between the increases in RCN3 and these indices to explore the potential role of RCN3 in pneumonic sepsis. The increase in RCN3 was statistically correlated with increased phagocytic marker CD68, but not with other inflammatory factors, in circulating monocytes [Figure D]. CD68 is highly expressed in macrophages and other mononuclear phagocytes, and the increased expression of CD68 has been used as a marker to track monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation. The significant correlation between RCN3 and CD68 upregulation suggests the potential importance of RCN3 in promoting monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation during sepsis.
 

## RCN3 upregulation during monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation
To further confirm whether RCN3 expression is increased during monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation, the monocyte cell line THP1 and primary circulating monocytes were subjected to the differentiation induced by PMA and M-CSF, respectively. PMA treatment successfully induced macrophage-like phenotypes in THP1 cells in a time-dependent manner, including morphological changes and upregulated transcriptional expression of the differentiation markers CD14 and CD11b and the macrophage surface marker CD68. Accompanied by these changes, RCN3 expression increased in a similar time-dependent manner within 48 h of PMA treatment despite its low expression in monocytes [Figure A, B]. In isolated primary circulating monocytes from healthy subjects, RCN3 messenger RNA expression was also upregulated in a time-dependent manner accompanied by increases in macrophage markers during M-CSF-induced monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation. Moreover, RCN3 protein expression was markedly increased when the primary circulating monocytes were subjected to differentiation [Figure C, D].
 

## Requirement of RCN3 for monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation
To further investigate the importance of RCN3 upregulation during differentiation of monocytes into macrophages, we evaluated the effects of RCN3 knockdown and overexpression on PMA-induced THP1 differentiation. After 24 h from PMA exposure, we assessed the expression levels of the differentiation markers CD14, CD11b, and CD68 using quantitative PCR and quantified their levels on the cell surface through flow cytometry. RCN3 deficiency significantly suppressed the PMA-induced upregulation of differentiation markers, and their levels on the cell surface were consistently dramatically decreased, especially the macrophage cell surface marker CD68 [Figure A–C], suggesting that RCN3 deficiency suppressed monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation. Conversely, RCN3 overexpression enhanced the PMA-induced upregulation of differentiation markers [Figure D–F]. These findings indicate that RCN3 is critical in monocyte differentiation into macrophages.
 

## Impaired BM monocyte–macrophage differentiation and attenuated LPS-induced ALI in Rcn3-deficient mice
To investigate the potential effect of RCN3 gene deletion on the differentiation process of monocytes into macrophages in vivo, we generated myeloid-specific Rcn3 deletion mice (CKO mice, Rcn3f/f/Lyz-Cre) by crossing Lyz2-Cre mice with Rcn3f/f mice, especially targeting RCN3 in myeloid monocytes. BM monocyte cells were isolated from CKO and control mice, followed by 7 days of M-CSF treatment to induce differentiation to BMDMs. Immunoblot assay indicated a significant decrease in RCN3 in BMDMs from CKO mice compared with controls [Figure A]. RCN3 induction induced by M-CSF was also suppressed in these cells from CKO mice, although M-CSF treatment markedly upregulated RCN3 expression in BM monocytes from control mice [Figure A]. In line with observations in THP1 cells, RCN3 deficiency significantly reduced the M-CSF–induced monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation, as indicated by suppressed expression of the macrophage differentiation markers F4/80 and CD11b, in CKO BMDMs [Figure B]. In addition, flow cytometric assays of the surface markers CD11b and F4/80 revealed that the percentage of CD11b+F4/80+ cells (representative of monocyte-derived macrophages) was attenuated in CKO BMDMs [Figure C]. Furthermore, immunofluorescence assay of the surface marker F4/80 revealed significantly reduced fluorescence staining of CKO BMDMs [Figure D]. Moreover, it has been established that circulating monocytes significantly contribute to pulmonary interstitial macrophages during pneumonic sepsis. Thus, we challenged the mice with LPS by intratracheal administration to induce acute lung inflammatory injury that mimicked clinical pneumonic sepsis. Along with the amelioration of LPS-induced lung injury as indicated by attenuated lung pathological changes [Figure E] and inflammatory cytokine induction [Figure F], CKO mice manifested reduced lung interstitial macrophages as indicated by reduced F4/80 immunohistochemical staining [Figure G]. Flow cytometric analyses also displayed a marked diminution in total lung macrophages (CD45+F4/80+) accompanied by a marked decrease in the proportion of lung interstitial macrophages (CD45+F4/80+CD11b+CD11c−) in CKO lungs vs. control lungs after LPS exposure [Figure H]. These findings suggest that RCN3 is critical for monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation during pneumonic sepsis, which likely contributes to inflammatory lung injury.
 

## RCN3 deficiency impairs the functional maturation of monocyte-derived macrophages
To explore whether RCN3 promotes functional maturation of macrophages, we evaluated the indices for activation of macrophages in CKO BMDMs and RCN3-shRNA–targeted THP1 cells, including phagocytosis, ROS production, and inflammatory response to LPS. The capability of macrophages to engulf and eliminate microorganisms was evaluated by quantifying the uptake of labeled E. coli particles (pHrodo™ E. coli BioParticles). RCN3-shRNA–targeted THP1 cells exhibited a significantly decreased number of ingested particles compared with the control group [Figure A]. Furthermore, ROS play a vital role in the innate immune response against intracellular bacteria and serve as important mediators for activating proinflammatory signaling pathways. RCN3 knockdown in THP1 cells reduced the levels of cellular ROS after PMA-induced differentiation [Figure B]. In addition, the depletion of RCN3 dramatically suppressed the inductions of two essential inflammatory genes, IL-1β and TNF-α, by LPS exposure in both BMDMs and THP1-derived macrophages, suggesting a reduced inflammatory response [Figure C and D]. Conversely, RCN3 overexpression significantly enhanced the inductions of IL-1β and TNF-α in LPS-treated THP1-derived macrophages [Figure E]. In summary, RCN3 is essential to the functional maturation of monocyte-derived macrophages.
 

## RCN3 facilitates differentiation by regulating autophagosome generation
Given the indispensable role of autophagy in macrophage differentiation, we evaluated the hypothesis that RCN3 facilitates differentiation by regulating autophagy. In accordance with previous studies, we also observed enhanced autophagy during the M-CSF-induced differentiation of human primary monocytes to macrophages, as indicated by increases in autophagy-related gene 7 (Atg7) and LC3B protein levels [Figure A]. During the differentiation of monocytes to macrophages, the levels of Atg7 and LC3B both increased at 24 hours and 48 hours after PMA exposure [Figure B]. Of note, RCN3 depletion in both human and mouse monocytes significantly suppressed such increases in Atg7 and LC3B during differentiation, suggesting reduced autophagy [Figure B and C]. Furthermore, RCN3 overexpression in THP1 cells enhanced autophagy during PMA-induced differentiation accompanied by enhanced expression of Atg7 and LC3B [Figure D]. To validate autophagic enhancement by RCN3 overexpression as causally associated with differentiation, we used the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3MA) to treat these cells. Importantly, 3MA significantly inhibited the inductions of CD14 and CD68 in RCN3-overexpressing THP1 cells after PMA exposure [Figure E and F].
 The accumulation of autophagy markers reflects two possibilities: an increase in autophagosome formation or impaired lysosomal function leading to decreased autophagosome degradation. RCN3 knockdown and overexpression in THP1 cells decreased and increased the accumulation of autophagy markers, respectively, suggesting that RCN3 presence promotes the formation of autophagosomes or affects lysosomal function. To address this hypothesis, we applied the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) to block autophagosome degradation in RCN3-deficient THP1 cells. Intriguingly, CQ treatment failed to reverse the decreased accumulation of LC3B-II by RCN3 deficiency, suggesting that RCN3 deficiency did not affect lysosomal function [Figure A]. In addition, LC3B accumulation was enhanced by RCN3 overexpression, and this increase was also observed in CQ presence [Figure B]. Furthermore, we observed that LC3B accumulation was lower in the knockdown group than in the control group, regardless of the presence or absence of Rapamycin [Figure C]. To provide direct evidence that RCN3 was involved in regulating autophagy, we transfected the mCherry-GFP-LC3B adenovirus into THP1 cells and evaluated autophagic flux on PMA through the following fluorescence changes: yellow dots (red-green merge) indicating autophagosomes and free red dots representing autolysosomes. We ascertained that RCN3 deficiency caused a significant reduction in the number of autophagosomes compared with the ShC controls [Figure D]. These results further support the involvement of RCN3 in autophagosome formation.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we showed that RCN3 promotes monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation by enhancing autophagosome formation. The principal findings of this study were as follows. (1) A significant increase in RCN3 was observed in circulating monocytes but not in lymphocytes from patients with pneumonic sepsis, and this was significantly correlated with the increase in CD68 cells. (2) RCN3 upregulation facilitated monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation and functional maturation of both human circulating monocytes and mouse BM monocytes. (3) In vivo studies revealed that Rcn3 deletion in BM monocyte (Rcn3f/f/Lyz-Cre mice) reduced numbers of lung interstitial macrophages in response to LPS inhalation, which was accompanied by an amelioration of ALI. The suppression of differentiation caused by RCN3 deficiency was associated with reduced autophagy due to impaired autophagosome generation. Our schematic summary of the proposed pathogenic mechanisms is presented in Figure E.
Sepsis is characterized as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome in which circulating PBMCs play a critical role in immune responses to infection. The predominant cell types in PBMCs include monocytes and lymphocytes, both of which have marked changes in immune activation during sepsis.[ ] Circulating monocytes are indispensable in the innate immune response, whereas lymphocytes had more advantage in adaptive immune response. On tissue damage or infection, circulating monocytes are recruited to the tissues and then differentiate into macrophages,[ , ] and it has been demonstrated that monocyte-to-macrophage maturation is a critical process in sepsis-induced ALI.[ – ] Importantly, our observations from patients with sepsis revealed that RCN3 was upregulated in monocytes but not in lymphocytes and that the increases in RCN3 were significantly associated with CD68, a mononuclear phagocyte marker. In further in vivo and in vitro studies, the suppression of RCN3 induction markedly blunted circulating monocyte differentiation and proinflammatory functional maturation, and myeloid-specific RCN3 deficiency restricted the number of lung interstitial macrophages in response to an LPS challenge. This direct evidence confirmed the role of RCN3 in the facilitation of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation and functional maturation.
The ability of monocytes to differentiate into inflammatory or anti-inflammatory macrophage subsets is dependent on stimulus microenvironments.[ ] Inflammatory macrophages are crucial to the innate immune defense against infections and tissue damage by phagocytosis, clearance of cellular debris, and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines.[ , ] Herein, RCN3 knockdown significantly reduced phagocytic activity against E. coli and the levels of intracellular ROS, which are fundamental for the elimination of invasive microorganisms by macrophages.[ ] Furthermore, RCN3 knockdown and overexpression significantly decreased and increased proinflammatory cytokine expression in response to LPS stimulation, respectively. RCN3 knockdown did not affect the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokines such as dendritic cell-specific ICAM-3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN) and mannose receptor C-type 1 (MRC-1), which mark M2 macrophages [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C790]. These observations suggested that RCN3 facilitated monocyte differentiation to proinflammatory macrophages. Congruent with our in vitro results, the mice with a myeloid-specific Rcn3 deletion had attenuated LPS-induced acute lung inflammatory injury with decreased interstitial macrophages. These findings suggest that RCN3 facilitation of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation is involved in lung inflammatory injury during sepsis and that targeting RCN3 in circulating monocytes would constitute a potential therapeutic strategy for sepsis-induced ALI/ARDS.
Monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation is an early transitional stage during the inflammatory response, although it is essential for maintaining the immune system, which includes replenishing tissue macrophages in a steady step and recruiting aggressive macrophages against infection.[ ] Earlier transcriptomic and epigenomic studies revealed changes in macrophage function-associated transcription profiles (cell-type specific) during this differentiation, including genes involved in phagocytosis, metabolic processes, and cell cycle.[ , ] The regulatory mechanism involved in this process is far from clear as most studies have focused on the regulation of macrophage polarization.[ ] Recent studies have indicated several signaling pathways that regulate monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation during atherogenesis, including ERE1/2, p38 MAPK, and AMPK signaling, which are classical inflammatory response and cell differentiation signaling pathways.[ – ] Herein, we identified RCN3 as a novel regulator that facilitates monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation and promotes sepsis-induced acute lung inflammatory injury. Intriguingly, our previous study also depicted the importance of RCN3 in the differentiation and functional maturation of alveolar epithelial type II cells (AECII) during lung development, which was associated with functional maturation of the ER and surfactant protein synthesis.[ ] However, this study indicated that the distinct regulatory mechanism underlying RCN3 action in monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation was associated with the promotion of cellular autophagy. This difference in the studies is likely due to their distinct environments: lung development and the innate immune response against infection in adulthood and the different cellular types of differentiation, that is, secretory epithelial cells and phagocytes (phagocytosis requires cooperation with cellular autophagy).[ ]
Autophagy is a vital factor in immune system regulation and is crucial for cellular development, stability, and survival.[ ] Autophagy induction is found to be pivotal for the survival and macrophage differentiation of monocytes. In addition, autophagy inhibition using pharmacologic inhibitors, siRNA approaches, and Atg7-deficient mice (Atg7−/−) impedes differentiation and cytokine production, resulting in cellular apoptosis.[ , ] In our study, the autophagy marker LC3-II was significantly and consistently elevated during the differentiation process in the THP-1 cell line, primary human monocytes, and mouse BM monocytes, whereas inhibition by the autophagy inhibitor 3MA significantly suppressed THP-1 differentiation induced by PMA. Notably, RCN3 deficiency significantly reduced PMA-induced autophagy during differentiation, whereas the enhanced differentiation due to RCN3 overexpression was markedly attenuated by 3MA. Furthermore, RCN3 deficiency reduced the autophagy induced by rapamycin (the mTOR-dependent classical autophagy activator) during monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation. These results suggest that RCN3 promotes this differentiative process by maintaining mTOR-dependent classical autophagy, consistent with a previous study indicating the importance of mTOR-dependent autophagy in monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation.[ ]
Furthermore, after autophagy initiation and phagophore formation, the process of autophagy includes autophagosome formation with sequestered cytoplasmic cargo, autolysosome formation through fusion with the lysosome, and ultimate degradation of cytoplasmic components.[ ]
RCN3 knockdown and overexpression in THP1 cells decreased and increased, respectively, the accumulation of Atg7 and LC3-II molecules involved in phagophore formation.[ ] These results were further confirmed as the lysosomal inhibitor CQ failed to reverse the effect of RCN3 deficiency at the LC3B-II level, suggesting that RCN3 deficiency did not affect lysosomal function. We also observed that the LC3B-II levels were lower in the knockdown group than in the control group, regardless of the presence or absence of rapamycin, supporting the involvement of RCN3 in autophagosome formation. Our previous investigations indicated an essential role of RCN3 as an ER chaperone protein against ER stress.[ , , ] Given that phagophores are derived from the ER, RCN3 might be associated with phagophore initiation and flowing autophagosome assembly. This hypothesis aligns with previous studies that indicate a crosstalk between ER stress and autophagy in inflammation.[ ]
There are some limitations to this study. The clinical portion of this study was a pilot analysis that included data from a small number of patients. This limited sample size would likely not allow for an accurate estimate and would thus produce statistical bias. The detailed mechanisms underlying RCN3 effects in the regulation of the autophagic machinery still need to be clarified; these include the interaction of RCN3 with key components of autophagosomes such as LC3 and Atg.
In summary, our study reveals that RCN3 functions as a novel regulator facilitating monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation by maintaining homeostasis of the autophagy process. Thus, RCN3-facilitated monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation is likely to be involved in inflammatory lung injury during sepsis, and targeting RCN3 in circulating monocytes represents a potential therapeutic strategy for sepsis-induced ALI/ARDS.
