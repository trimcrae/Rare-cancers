## Abstract
Polymeric micelles assembled from amphiphilic block copolymers
represent a versatile platform for mRNA delivery due to their modular
chemical design and tunable self-assembly. Here, we report a library
of ternary mixed micelles composed of amphiphiles displaying a cationic
oligoamine (A7), anionic carboxyethyl acrylate (CEA), and neutral
poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG), engineered to enhance in vitro transfection and enable passive, ligand-free in vivo organ targeting. Systematic blending of these components allowed
modulation of micelle surface chemistry and morphology, which are
key parameters that govern mRNA complexation, stability, and biodistribution.
Increasing incorporation of anionic amphiphiles resulted in a distinct
evolution of morphology from spherical micelles to elongated wormlike
structures, while maintaining uniformly high zeta potential and efficient
mRNA complexation. Top-performing ternary formulations in
vitro achieved over 95% transfection efficiency and cell
viability across multiple cell types, representing a 2-fold performance
enhancement relative to homomicelle controls. These formulations also
exhibited robust mRNA internalization and efficient endosomal escape,
resulting in a 7-fold improvement in GFP mean fluorescence intensity.
Furthermore, by controlling micelle aggregation, we demonstrate that
serum stability can be significantly improved, highlighting the tunability
of this mixed micelle system. Finally, in vivo, increasing
incorporation of anionic amphiphiles consistently shifted mRNA expression
from lungs to spleen. We attribute this shift in organ tropism to
morphology, where spherical micelles sequester in the lungs and wormlike
ternary mixed micelles accumulate in the spleen. Transmission electron
microscopy confirmed the elongated wormlike architecture that correlated
with spleen localization, supporting morphology as the dominant factor
driving organ selectivity. This morphology-driven organ selectivity
marks a departure from traditional charge-centric targeting paradigms
and establishes a tunable, composition-based framework for directing
polymeric micelles to specific organs. Together, these findings introduce
a simple yet powerful strategy for enhancing in vitro mRNA delivery and achieving organ-specific mRNA delivery using a
blended micelle platform.
Keywords: mRNA delivery, micelle, blends, polymer, lungs tropism, spleen tropism, organ tropism

## Introduction
The emergence of nucleic acid therapeutics
has revolutionized modern
medicine, offering highly precise interventions for the treatment
of both genetic and acquired disorders, including thalassemia, sickle
cell anemia, cystic fibrosis, and cancer.
−

 With 33 current
FDA-approved genetic therapies and thousands more in clinical trials,
the potential for curative treatments continues to expand. Among these, messenger RNA (mRNA)-based therapies
have attracted significant attention due to their ability to drive
transient protein expression without necessitating genomic integration,
thus mitigating the risks of insertional mutagenesis and other genetic
perturbations.
,
 Despite its widespread clinical
adoption, including the development of vaccines, mRNA-based therapeutics
face significant barriers, primarily stemming from their susceptibility
to enzymatic degradation, instability in physiological environments,
and limited cellular uptake.
,
 Consequently, overcoming
these barriers demands the development of advanced delivery systems
capable of effectively protecting mRNA payloads while facilitating
targeted cellular entry and efficient intracellular release. Furthermore,
addressing the challenges associated with organ-specific localization
remains crucial, as precise organ-targeted delivery not only enhances
therapeutic efficacy but also minimizes off-target effects and systemic
toxicity.
To address these challenges, nanoparticle-based delivery
platforms
have been extensively explored, with viral vectors and lipid nanoparticles
(LNPs) leading the field.
,
 However, while effective,
these systems can present notable drawbacks, particularly challenges
related to room temperature storage stability and effective manufacturing
scale-up.
−

 Consequently, polymeric nanocarriers have gained
traction as versatile alternatives, offering broad chemical tunability,
scalable synthesis, and the ability to precisely modulate their physicochemical
properties for enhanced therapeutic performance.
,
 Additionally, polymeric delivery systems provide access to less
encumbered intellectual property space, as many LNP formulations are
patented, which can hinder broad adoption and rapid translation. Among these, cationic polymeric micelles have
emerged as particularly promising candidates, formed through the self-assembly
of amphiphilic block copolymers to generate stable, nanoscale carriers
with well-defined architectures.
−

 These micelles exhibit
remarkable potential in gene delivery by electrostatically condensing
nucleic acids into micellar nanoparticles (MNPs) or micelleplexes,
ensuring cargo protection, controlled release, and allowing access
to a diverse range of structural motifs.
−

 Furthermore,
polymeric micelle platforms offer a compelling route toward effective
extrahepatic targeting, whereas LNPs
exhibit a strong hepatic bias, along with accumulation in other organs,
driven by their cholesterol- and phospholipid-rich compositions.

Beyond their role in nucleic acid complexation,
the physicochemical
properties of cationic micellar nanoparticles, particularly surface
charge and corona composition, critically influence their biodistribution,
cellular interactions, and therapeutic efficacy.
,−

 The positive surface charge of these nanoparticles is well-known
to trigger protein opsonization, leading to serum fouling, immune
activation, and diminished delivery efficiency.
,
 In contrast, neutral or mildly anionic formulations tend to exhibit
extended circulation times and lower systemic toxicity.
−

 Given these charge-dependent effects on biodistribution and cellular
uptake, a particularly promising approach to achieve optimal charge
balance and structural control is the engineering of mixed micelle
formulations. By coassembling amphiphiles with distinct physicochemical
properties, mixed micelles provide a dynamic platform for optimizing
corona charge balance and modulating micelle size and morphology.
Notably, incorporating neutral or anionic amphiphiles alongside cationic
components has been shown to modulate nanoparticle-cell interactions,
thereby affecting stability, cellular uptake, and release.
,,,
 For instance, the incorporation of PEGylated and cationic polymer
blocks has been shown to facilitate siRNA release while simultaneously
mitigating premature aggregation. Likewise,
integration of anionic functionalities within mixed micelle formulations
has led to enhanced pDNA delivery efficiency and improved nanoparticle
serum stability in vitro, underscoring the critical
role of charge modulation in improving therapeutic performance.

Importantly, modulation of nanoparticle
physicochemical properties
has demonstrated considerable promise in achieving passive targeting
for organ-selective delivery.
−

 Several findings have demonstrated
charge-dependent organ-selective LNP systems, where cationic components
preferentially promoted lung tropism, while anionic components facilitated
spleen targeting.
,−

 Recently, Li et al. demonstrated that altering the charge ratio
in polymer–nucleic acid complexes modulates not only the surface
zeta potential but also particle morphology, resulting in distinct
patterns of organ localization. Specifically, nanoparticles formulated
with higher cationic charge densities maintained strongly positive
surface potentials at physiological pH, which enhanced serum protein
adsorption and promoted pulmonary accumulation. In contrast, formulations
with near-neutral charge profiles favored splenic uptake. While these
studies have emphasized surface charge as a determinant of organ tropism,
other findings suggest that nanoparticle morphology and size may play
an equally critical role in directing biodistribution. Studies from DeSimone and co-workers
−

 and Mitragotri and co-workers
−

 have shown that tuning particle
shape, curvature, and mechanical deformability can redirect organ
accumulation by altering how nanoparticles navigate vascular, interstitial,
and cellular barriers. Taken together with insights from other related
studies,
,,
 efficient
and selective lung delivery appears to depend primarily on nanoparticle
composition, where variations in surface chemistry, and the resulting
protein corona, govern pulmonary accumulation more strongly than particle
size. In contrast, spleen targeting is influenced predominantly by
both particle morphology and size, as rigid nanoparticles exceeding
200 nm are physically restricted from passing through the splenic
reticuloendothelial system, leading to preferential retention within
the spleen.
,−

 Together, these
reports highlight that both surface characteristics and morphology
synergistically determine nanoparticle biodistribution, challenging
the notion that surface charge alone governs organ selectivity.
A previous work from our group demonstrated that cationic micelles
formulated from an amphiphilic diblock polymer incorporating oligoamine
(A7 amphiphile) as the cationic functionality exhibited robust in vitro transfection performance (∼70% delivery
efficiency) and precise lung-specific mRNA delivery in vivo. In the present study, we advance the
A7 homopolymer micelle platform by systematically generating a diverse
library of binary and ternary mixed micelles through controlled incorporation
of neutral and anionic amphiphilic counterparts in variable proportions
( ). Specifically,
polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based amphiphilic diblock copolymers are
introduced as neutral components to mitigate cytotoxicity, and improve
overall biocompatibility, whereas anionic carboxyethyl acrylate (CEA)-based
amphiphiles are employed to modulate micelle charge, improve in vitro transfection efficacy, and induce tunable organ-selective
biodistribution. For these ternary mixed micelles, we observe a unique
morphological evolution wherein increasing the fraction of anionic
amphiphile results in a progressive shift in micelle morphology from
spherical to worm-like structures in a dose-dependent manner. Comprehensive in vitro transfection assays reveal that the ternary micelles
comprising A7, CEA, and PEG exhibit remarkably high transfection efficiencies
(∼95%), representing a significant advancement over A7 homomicelles.
Additionally, the incorporation of PEG and CEA results in an impressive
7-fold enhancement in GFP mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), coupled
with a substantial reduction in cytotoxicity, thus effectively doubling
the overall effective efficiency (defined as transfection efficiency
* cell viability). These improvements are particularly pronounced
in serum-containing conditions, where we introduce a controlled aggregation
strategy to achieve a remarkable 10-fold increase in GFP expression
relative to native A7 micelles. Importantly, our in vivo investigations demonstrate, for the first time, that the compositional
mixing–driven tuning of micelle morphology through noncationic
amphiphile incorporation ultimately dictates precise tuning of organ
tropism. Specifically, systematically varying the anionic component
ratio, induces predictable changes in micelle morphology and surface
presentation, which in turn enable targeted biodistribution from lung-specific
to spleen-specific delivery, all without relying on ligand-based targeting
strategies. Collectively, this study constitutes the first reported
instance of polymeric micelle-based nucleic acid delivery systems
exhibiting inherently morphology dependent tunable organ selectivity,
underscoring the transformative potential of rationally engineered
mixed micelle architectures for precision gene therapy applications.
 

## Results and Discussion

## Amphiphile Synthesis and Mixed Micelle Formulation
The mixed micelle formulations were prepared through the synthesis
and assembly of three distinctly charged diblock copolymers: the cationic
oligoamine amphiphiles (hereafter referred to as A7 amphiphiles),
anionic carboxyethyl acrylate (CEA)-derived amphiphiles, and neutral
poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG)-based amphiphiles ( A-B). Leveraging our previously established
postpolymerization modification methodology, the cationic A7 amphiphiles were synthesized from modular scaffolds
comprising poly­(pentafluorophenyl acrylate)-b-poly­(n-butyl acrylate) (PPFPA-b-PnBA). These
scaffolds were obtained through RAFT polymerization, subsequently
functionalized via amidation with the A7 amine derivative. Selectivity
in modification was enhanced through Boc protection of amines in A7,
and quantitative conversion was confirmed by the complete disappearance
of poly­(pentafluorophenyl acrylate) characteristic peaks, monitored
via 19F NMR spectroscopy, as reported previously (Figures S1 and S4–S6). Furthermore, to promote stable micelle self-assembly, the hydrophobic
PnBA block and cationic block lengths were fixed at 118 and 230 repeat
units respectively, affording a hydrophilic fraction (f
hydrophilic) of 0.66, which exceeds the ∼0.5 criterion
associated with spherical micelle assembly. The anionic CEA amphiphiles were similarly synthesized through RAFT-mediated
chain extension of carboxyethyl acrylate monomer on a poly­(n-butyl acrylate) backbone used for synthesis of the A7
amphiphile, ensuring hydrophobic block consistency across amphiphiles
(Figures S3A and S8). Finally, for the
neutral amphiphile, a PEG (10K) macro chain transfer agent was first
synthesized via acid-alcohol coupling of polyethylene glycol with
DDMAT chain transfer agent (CTA). This PEG-functionalized macro-CTA
was subsequently used for the RAFT polymerization of n-butyl acrylate, yielding the neutral PnBA-b-PEG
amphiphile with matched hydrophobic chain length relative to the cationic
and anionic amphiphiles (Figure S2). Each
noncationic amphiphile possess hydrophilic blocks exhibiting consistent
lengths of approximately 240–260 repeat units, ensuring uniformity
in hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic ratios across the polymer library. Following
synthesis, all amphiphiles underwent purification via dialysis, and
purity was assessed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
(Figures S6–S11). Furthermore, potentiometric
titration of these amphiphiles revealed distinct ionization behaviors;
notably, the A7 amphiphile exhibited dual pK
a values at 5.1 and 8.6, while the CEA amphiphile showed an
acidic character with a single pK
a around
5.2 (Figure S12).
The assembly of
the amphiphiles to generate “mixed” micelles (micelles
containing 2 or 3 different block copolymers) was accomplished via
an ultrasound-assisted thin-film hydration procedure ( B). To ensure the uniform composition
and incorporation of the amphiphiles, each copolymer was individually
dissolved in methanol and mixed at specified molar ratios to generate
homogeneous mixtures. Subsequently, the solvent was evaporated under
a nitrogen stream to yield a polymer film, that was hydrated with
deionized water, followed by ultrasound sonication to yield the micelle
formulations. To methodically explore ternary amphiphile compositions,
we adopted the strategy of anchoring the internal molar ratio between
the cationic A7 and neutral PEG amphiphiles at defined proportions
(where each number represents the mole percentage): A7(100):PEG(0),
A7(95):PEG(5), A7(90):PEG(10), and A7(85):PEG(15) ( ). Each of these binary mixtures
was incrementally doped with 10%, 20%, and 30% molar ratios of the
CEA amphiphile. All components for a given formulation were combined
in a single mixing step according to these final molar percentages
yielding a comprehensive library of 16 distinct micellar formulations
encompassing, 1 purely cationic homomicelle, 3 PEG containing binary
mixtures, 3 CEA containing binary mixtures and 9 ternary mixtures
( ). Importantly,
practical limitations on amphiphile mixture solubility constrained
maximum CEA doping to 30 mol %, as higher incorporation led to precipitation
upon hydration due to potential charge neutralization. Similarly,
increased PEG incorporation beyond 15 mol % was limited by aggregation-induced
precipitation, thus establishing an upper bound for the A7:PEG ratio
at 85:15. The nomenclature adopted for mixed micelle formulations
is detailed in along with the respective molar composition ratios for the amphiphiles.
 

## Micelle and Micelleplex Biophysical Characterization
Following the preparation of mixed micelles, dynamic light scattering
(DLS) was employed to assess their hydrodynamic size distribution
(Table S1). As shown in A, a clear trend emerged, wherein
hydrodynamic radii increased progressively with the incremental incorporation
of PEG and/or CEA amphiphiles. PEG-containing binary formulations
(APC 5/0, 10/0, and 15/0) exhibited a progressive increase in particle
size, with hydrodynamic diameters increasing from ∼100 nm for
APC 0/0 homomicelles to ∼180 nm for APC 15/0, corresponding
to an approximately 2-fold increase. Similarly, CEA-containing binary
formulations (APC 0/10, 0/20, and 0/30) displayed a more pronounced
size expansion, reaching ∼250 nm for APC 0/30 micelles, representing
an approximately 2.5-fold increase relative to APC 0/0. Ternary mixed
formulations showed the largest size increases, with diameters reaching
up to ∼400 nm for APC 15/30, corresponding to an approximately
4-fold increase compared to APC 0/0 homomicelles. The increase in
size upon PEG incorporation likely results from neutral PEG chains
displacing cationic amphiphiles within the micelle, thereby reducing
electrostatic repulsion among neighboring chains in the corona. Literature
reports have shown that strong charge–charge repulsion within
highly cationic assemblies limits packing efficiency and typically
yields smaller particles.
,
 Thus, the reduction
in interchain repulsion upon PEG incorporation permits denser amphiphile
packing and greater aggregation, leading to larger micelles (∼150–200
nm). Additionally, the strong hydrophilicity of PEG enhances corona
hydration and induces swelling, further expanding the hydrodynamic
radius. In contrast, the size increase observed with CEA incorporation
appears counterintuitive, as electrostatic complexation between anionic
and cationic species should result in structural compaction. However,
prior studies have shown that partial charge neutralization between
anionic and cationic polymers can lead to the formation of a charge-neutral
intrapolyelectrolyte complex (IPEC) layer surrounding the hydrophobic
core rather than collapse of the assembly ( D). In our system,
the molar ratio of cationic to anionic amphiphiles was maintained
above 1 for all formulations ( ), resulting in an excess of cationic amphiphiles.
Under these conditions, we propose that the ternary micelles adopt
a core–shell–corona architecture in which electrostatic
pairing between cationic and anionic amphiphiles forms an intermediate
IPEC shell. Importantly, this charge-neutral IPEC layer is highly
hydrated and can swell significantly in aqueous media, providing a
plausible explanation for the increased hydrodynamic radii observed
for CEA-containing formulations. In addition, DLS measurements revealed
negligible changes in hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index after
two years of storage (Table S2), demonstrating
excellent long-term stability of the mixed micelles. Notably, incorporation
of oppositely charged amphiphiles did not result in long-term aggregation,
underscoring the structural robustness and formulation stability of
these assemblies for nucleic acid delivery applications.
 To elucidate the underlying morphological transformations
responsible
for the observed size increase, transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
was utilized to visualize the micellar structures directly. TEM imaging
of a representative subset of formulations (APC 0/0, 10/0, 10/10,
10/20, and 10/30), selected to span the compositional space of the
full library, revealed a progressive morphological evolution with
increasing CEA incorporation ( E). Initially, the native cationic homomicelles (APC
0/0) and binary A7-PEG micelles (APC 10/0) predominantly exhibited
spherical morphologies, consistent with previous reports
,
 ( E (i)-(ii)).
The APC 10/10 micelles containing all three different amphiphiles,
retained a uniformly spherical shape; however, they displayed almost
two times the size compared to the APC 0/0 homomicelles ( E (iii)). Furthermore,
they displayed a distinct dark border around the core, indicating
the likely formation of the core–shell-corona architecture
( E (i), (iii)).
Interestingly, APC 10/20 micelles exhibited subtle micelle–micelle
associations, manifested as chaining of spherical particles, consistent
with the onset of higher-order assembly rather than discrete particle
behavior ( E
(iv)). We attribute this behavior to diminished corona charge–charge
repulsion resulting from increased anionic incorporation and consequently
reduced cationic density at the micelle surface, which increases the
probability of micelle–micelle associations. In the APC 10/30
formulation, higher-order micelle architecture became strikingly pronounced,
driving a distinct morphological transition from discrete spherical
structures to elongated cylindrical/wormlike assemblies ( E (v)). We posit
that this transformation is driven by increased incorporation of anionic
amphiphiles, which enhances charge neutralization and expands the
volume of the charge-neutral intermediate shell. Swelling of this
shell increases the effective packing parameter and introduces internal
strain within the micellar assembly, thereby destabilizing the spherical
morphology. Simultaneously, the diminishing cationic surface charge
density weakens electrostatic repulsions, further encouraging micelle–micelle
fusion into extended wormlike structures.
,
 Similar curvature transitions have been described in complex coacervate
micelles (C3M), where modulation of electrostatic interactions alters
packing efficiency and increases the effective packing parameter,
resulting in a shift from spherical to wormlike geometries. Although our micelles differ in architecture,
the underlying principle applies: increasing anionic incorporation
reduces cationic surface density, expands the intermediate shell,
and decreases the effective headgroup area, all of which push the
assembly toward lower interfacial curvature. In our system, this results
in evolution of compact spherical micelles into extended wormlike
structures as the balance of charge neutralization, corona packing,
and intermicellar association shifts with composition.
In addition,
because the micelles were prepared by thin film rehydration,
the resulting assemblies may represent kinetically trapped or metastable
structures rather than fully equilibrated curvature-driven morphologies.
Prior studies of block copolymer micelles have shown that preparation
pathway and slow chain exchange kinetics can produce nonergodic assemblies
whose structure depends on formation history rather than thermodynamic
equilibrium. Under such conditions, polymer
chain exchange between micelles may be restricted, leading to long-lived
metastable morphologies that reflect precursor film organization or
early aggregation events. Accordingly, the morphological evolution
described here may arise from a combination of equilibrium self-assembly
and kinetic trapping during the rehydration process. Furthermore,
the thin film rehydration method may produce partially aggregated
or interconnected structures, which could contribute to the large
hydrodynamic radii observed for the ternary mixed micelles in DLS.
Collectively, the transition from spherical to wormlike architecture
appears to arise from composition-dependent modulation of electrostatic
interactions, corona packing, and intermicellar association, while
potential kinetic trapping during thin film rehydration may also influence
the resulting structures. These findings highlight the importance
of both compositional design and assembly pathway in determining the
final architecture of polymeric micelles.
The proposed core–shell-corona
architecture suggests that,
due to the charge balancing effects and the presence of residual cationic
and neutral PEG moieties, the micelle corona is likely to bear a net
positive charge. To validate this hypothesis, zeta potential measurements
were conducted across all formulations. Despite varying levels of
anionic amphiphile incorporation, all micelles exhibited consistently
high positive surface charges (∼+45–55 mV) (Figure S13), suggesting that residual cationic
and PEG dominate the micelle surface, while anionic CEA is primarily
confined to the neutralized intermediate shell. Thus, the zeta potential
data complements DLS and TEM findings and underscores the need to
interpret surface charge measurements within the broader context of
micelle architecture and physicochemical environment.
Finally,
we investigated the impact of mixed micelle composition
on micelle complexation with mRNA cargo. Complexes were formulated
at a fixed nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) molar ratio of 10, representing
the micelle nitrogen (N) content relative to mRNA phosphate groups
(P). Upon complexation in aqueous conditions, DLS measurements revealed
a consistent decrease in the hydrodynamic diameter of the micelle-nucleic
acid particles (micelleplexes or MNPs) when compared to respective
native micelles ( B, Table S2). This reduction in size can
be attributed to efficient cargo compaction within the micellar corona,
thereby resulting in more compact structures. TEM analysis of the
micelleplexes revealed morphologies consistent with the native micelles
( E (vi)-(x)).
APC 0/0 and APC 10/0 micelles retained the spherical architecture
upon complexation with mRNA while APC 10/30 MNPs displayed wormlike
morphologies. However, dilution of the complexes into the transfection
medium OptiMEM led to a pronounced increase in particle size for the
ternary mixed micelles, suggestive of MNP aggregation mediated by
ionic interactions with charged species present in the medium ( C). This aggregation
phenomenon was particularly exacerbated in formulations containing
higher levels of anionic amphiphile incorporation (APC XX/20 series
and APC XX/30 series), as evidenced by the formation of aggregates
extending into the micrometer scale. This behavior likely arises from
increased charge neutralization, which reduces electrostatic repulsion
and enhances susceptibility to salt-induced aggregation.
Crucially,
successful mRNA delivery is immensely influenced by
the micelle-mRNA binding affinity and the corresponding dynamics of
cargo release. To investigate these parameters, two complementary
dye exclusion assays employing RiboGreen dye were implemented (Figure S14). The RiboGreen binding assay aids
quantification of the relative nucleic acid complexation by measuring
the reduction in fluorescence that occurs when RiboGreen dye is excluded
from RNA upon binding with the micelle. The initial RiboGreen assay
conducted in pure water and at an N/P ratio of 10, indicated consistently
high mRNA complexation efficiencies (∼98%) across all micelle
formulations, independent of amphiphile composition (Figure S14A). Similarly, upon dilution into OptiMEM, an increase
in fluorescence intensity, indicative of partial mRNA release, was
observed (Figure S14B). However, this release
did not exhibit clear compositional dependence, maintaining an average
binding efficiency of approximately 80–85% across the library.
To further assess the relative strength of micelle–mRNA interactions,
we introduced heparan sulfate, an anionic glycosaminoglycan abundant
on cell surfaces that competitively displaces nucleic acid cargo,
into the RiboGreen assay. Its competitive binding with mRNA offers
an indirect measure of MNP stability. However, despite the introduction
of this competitive agent, no discernible trends emerged regarding
amphiphile composition and binding strength (Figure S14C). These results corroborate earlier zeta potential observations,
which demonstrated similar positive surface charges (∼+ 45
mV) across all micellar formulations, suggesting the surface charge
plays a critical role in determining mRNA binding affinity. Thereby,
the incorporation of PEG and anionic amphiphiles within the micelle
architecture does not significantly compromise mRNA binding stability
or complexation dynamics due to consistent surface charge, highlighting
the robust and consistent biophysical behavior of these mixed micelle
formulations.

## Anionic Amphiphile Incorporation Enhances In Vitro mRNA Delivery
To elucidate the impact of mixed micelle
composition on in vitro mRNA delivery efficiency,
we employed a reporter gene assay with green fluorescent protein (GFP)-encoding
mRNA. Flow cytometry was used to quantify two key delivery outcomes
- transfection efficiency, defined by the percentage of GFP-positive
cells, and geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), indicative
of functional mRNA expression per transfected cell. While transfection
efficiency provides a global perspective on the overall ability of
micelles to mediate cellular entry and transfection, GFP MFI offers
deeper insights into intracellular mRNA release efficiency and subsequent
translation efficacy, directly correlating to the amount of functional
payload delivered per cell. Experiments were conducted across a range
of therapeutically relevant cell lines, including HEK293T (kidney),
A549 (lungs), and HuH7 (liver), at a fixed N/P ratio of 10, as previously
we have shown A7 homomicelle to perform the best at this ratio. Since serum proteins commonly present in complete
growth media can significantly influence MNP stability through aggregation
or premature decomplexation, transfection was initially performed
in serum-free OptiMEM for 4h. Postincubation, cells were supplemented
with complete growth medium to maintain cellular viability. This experimental
design enabled a clear assessment of micelle-specific delivery performance
without interference from serum proteins.
Analysis of transfection
outcomes across all three cell types demonstrated a clear composition
dependent impact on micelle performance. For clarity, subsequent discussion
focuses on HEK293T cells, as comparable trends were observed across
all cell lines ( and S16–S17). Notably, incremental
incorporation of the anionic CEA amphiphile in binary mixed formulations
(APC 0/10, 0/20, and 0/30) displayed a strong positive correlation
with GFP expression, with APC 0/30 formulation achieving 95% transfection
efficiency ( A). In contrast, increasing incorporation of the PEG-based amphiphile
in binary formulations (APC 5/0, 10/0, and 15/0) led to reduced transfection,
with GFP-positive cells decreasing to ∼50% compared to ∼70%
for APC 0/0 homomicelles ( A). Remarkably, ternary mixed formulations containing
30 mol % CEA (APC 5/30, 10/30, and 15/30) displayed significantly
elevated transfection efficiency, achieving GFP-positive populations
exceeding 90%, representing a substantial enhancement (∼30%
improvement) compared to the APC 0/0 (∼70% GFP+ve) micelle
control. Overall, across both binary and ternary formulation subsets,
a clear stepwise increase in GFP expression was observed as CEA incorporation
increased from 10 to 30 mol %.
 Interestingly, GFP MFI analysis revealed even more
pronounced performance
enhancements attributable to micelle composition ( B). Incremental incorporation
of the anionic amphiphile produced a consistent increase in GFP MFI
across both binary and ternary formulations, with APC 5/30 exhibiting
the highest values (∼3500), compared to ∼500 for the
APC 0/0 homomicelle control, corresponding to approximately 7-fold
enhancements in MFI. Conversely, increasing PEG amphiphile content
negatively impacted GFP MFI, with values decreasing from ∼500
for APC 0/0 to ∼250 for binary mixed formulations (APC 5/0,
10/0, and 15/0), reinforcing the inverse relationship between PEG
incorporation and mRNA transfection efficiency. Consequently, ternary
compositions mirrored these trends, demonstrating reduced GFP MFI
values with elevated PEG amphiphile content, yet maintaining a positive
correlation with CEA incorporation. Importantly, formulations APC
0/30 and APC 5/30 not only exhibited superior performance relative
to other micellar formulations but also markedly outperformed the
commercial standard, JetPEI, in terms of both transfection efficiency
and GFP MFI. Consistent trends were observed in A549 and HuH7 cells
(Figures S16–S17), where mixed formulations
containing 30 mol % anionic CEA again exhibited the highest GFP expression,
underscoring the robustness and broad applicability of these ternary
mixed MNPs for efficient mRNA delivery across multiple cell lines.
Since all the evidence thus far points to the importance of anionic
incorporation, we set out to investigate the impact of anionic amphiphile
identity on delivery performance. This was done by replacing carboxyethyl
acrylate (CEA) with other anionic amphiphiles, each featuring distinct
chemical functionalities ( A). We synthesized three new amphiphiles from the following
monomers: succinic ethyl acrylate (SEA), benzoic acid ethyl acrylate
(BEA), and 2-(phosphonooxy)­ethyl acrylate (PEA) via RAFT polymerization
as described before (Figures S3 and S9–S11). Given the superior delivery performance previously demonstrated
by the APC 5/XX micelle formulation incorporating CEA, analogous ternary
micelle formulations were prepared with SEA, BEA, and PEA at an identical
A7:PEG molar ratio of 95:5 with different amounts of anion doping
(10–30%). Transfection assays in HEK293T cells revealed that
SEA-containing ternary formulations exhibited transfection efficiency
and GFP MFI trends closely resembling those observed with CEA, while
formulations incorporating BEA or PEA demonstrated no significant
performance enhancement over the APC 0/0 homomicelle control ( A-B). Potentiometric
titration assays revealed that the distinct ionization properties
of the anionic amphiphiles were critical to the performance observed.
SEA (pK
a ∼ 5.9) exhibited a similar
acidic profile to CEA (pK
a ∼ 5.2),
whereas BEA (pK
a ∼ 7.2) and PEA
(pK
a ∼ 7.7) possessed substantially
higher, near neutral pK
a values (Figure S12). These findings strongly suggest
that amphiphiles with acidic pK
a values
may play a key role in modulating pK

cellular mechanisms, which helps in improving delivery performance.
Together, these results highlight the critical role of micelle composition
in modulating transfection outcomes, with acidic amphiphile doping
emerging as a key driver of enhanced gene expression. Overall, this
establishes a tunable formulation strategy that can be broadly leveraged
to optimize polymer-based delivery systems for high-efficiency, cell-type
agnostic mRNA transfection.
 

## Mechanistic Understanding of the Role of Anionic Amphiphile
in Improving Performance
To probe the underlying mechanistic
drivers governing the delivery performance trends, we conducted cellular
internalization assays using Cy5-labeled GFP mRNA ( A). Uptake studies at 4 h postincubation
demonstrated consistently high Cy5 positive populations (>90%)
across
all micelleplex formulations, which was sustained even at 24 h (Figure S15A,C). Despite comparable uptake percentages,
Cy5 MFI measurements at 4 h revealed pronounced composition-dependent
differences in internalization efficiency ( B and S15B). To
facilitate direct comparison of mRNA uptake and intracellular consumption
across formulations, Cy5 MFI values in B are presented relative to the APC 0/0 homo
micelle control at each time point, while absolute MFI values are
reported in the Supporting Information (Figure
S15B,D). Increasing anionic amphiphile incorporation led to systematic
increases in Cy5 MFI across both binary and ternary formulations.
Specifically, APC 0/30 exhibited an approximately 3-fold increase
in Cy5 MFI (∼3000 units) relative to the APC 0/0 control (∼1000
units), while ternary formulations APC 5/30, 10/30, and 15/30 showed
approximately 2-fold higher Cy5 MFI. In contrast, PEG-only binary
formulations (APC 5/0, 10/0, and 15/0) did not enhance internalization,
exhibiting Cy5 MFI values (∼800–1000 units) comparable
to APC 0/0, thus correlating directly with their reduced transfection
performance previously observed. The enhanced uptake observed for
anion-containing mixed formulations is attributed to increased aggregation
in OptiMEM, as supported by DLS measurements discussed above, which
likely promoted accelerated MNP sedimentation onto the cell surface
and facilitated internalization. At 24 h postincubation, absolute
Cy5 MFI increased for all formulations (with Cy5MFI for APC 0/0 MNPs
increasing 4× to ∼4000 units), reflecting continued cellular
internalization of mRNA. Notably, CEA-containing binary formulations
(APC 0/10, 0/20, and 0/30) maintained Cy5 MFI levels comparable to
APC 0/0 (∼4000 units). In contrast, ternary formulations exhibited
substantially lower Cy5 MFI (∼2000 units), corresponding to
a 30–50% reduction relative to APC 0/0. This pronounced loss
of Cy5 signal at later time points is indicative of more efficient
intracellular release and subsequent translation-coupled catabolism
of the delivered mRNA. Together, these results suggest that enhanced
early internalization, followed by rapid intracellular payload utilization,
underpins the superior GFP expression levels and elevated GFP MFI
observed for ternary mixed micelle formulations.
 Given that cationic nanoparticles predominantly
utilize endosome-mediated
pathways for cellular internalization,
−

 successful nucleic acid
delivery critically hinges upon overcoming endosomal entrapment.
,
 A key strategy to facilitate efficient endosomal escape involves
enhancing the buffering capacity of delivery vehicles, thereby triggering
the proton sponge effect. In this process,
protonatable amine groups within nanoparticles buffer against endosomal
acidification, resulting in counterion influx (primarily Cl–) and osmotic swelling, ultimately compromising endosomal integrity
and enabling cargo release into the cytosol. For the mixed micelles,
the combination of acidic CEA amphiphiles with A7 amphiphiles (already
display broad buffering capacity)
,
 likely enhances
endosomal escape via the proton sponge effect. To experimentally validate
this hypothesis, we employed bafilomycin-A1 (Baf-A1), an inhibitor of V-ATPase proton pumps, to block endosomal
acidification ( C). Treatment with Baf-A1 substantially reduced GFP MFI across all
tested formulations, confirming the crucial role of endosomal acidification
in mediating efficient cytosolic delivery ( D). While all micelles containing anionic
amphiphiles exhibited dramatic reductions (>90%) in GFP MFI following
Baf-A1 treatment, detailed analysis further emphasized this effect,
revealing that the top-performing ternary micelle formulations (e.g.,
APC 0/30 and APC 5/30) experienced approximately 7-fold greater reductions
in GFP MFI relative to APC 0/0 homomicelle control. On the other hand,
binary formulations containing only PEG as the neutral amphiphile
demonstrated considerably lower sensitivity to Baf-A1 treatment, experiencing
only about a 50% reduction in GFP MFI. These results suggest that
incorporating anionic amphiphiles significantly enhances the buffering
capacity of mixed micelles, synergizing with the intrinsic buffering
capabilities of polyamine-rich A7 amphiphile to amplify proton sponge-mediated
endosomal escape. This enhanced buffering directly correlates with
the substantial increase in functional mRNA delivery, as evidenced
by the pronounced elevation in GFP expression (MFI) observed for anionic-containing
formulations.
Overall, the exceptional performance of anion-doped
mixed micelles
can be attributed to a synergistic mechanism involving enhanced cellular
uptake and endosomal escape. We propose that the anionic amphiphile
drives micelle aggregation and sedimentation in culture media, promoting
rapid and efficient cellular uptake. Once internalized, its low pK
a (∼5.2) value complements the broad
buffering capacity of A7 (pK
a = 5.1, 8.6),
synergistically enhancing the proton sponge effect to facilitate endosomal
escape. This is evidenced by the pronounced Baf-A1 sensitivity and
sharp drop in Cy5 signal at later time points, indicating efficient
and rapid mRNA translation. Altogether, these findings establish that
anion-driven modulation of micelle assembly, uptake, and endosomal
trafficking is key to achieving potent and broadly effective mRNA
delivery with ternary mixed micelle systems.

## PEG Amphiphile Incorporation Enhances In Vitro Viability
To evaluate how amphiphile composition influences
cellular viability and overall delivery performance, we performed
CCK-8 viability assays concurrently with the transfection experiments.
The CCK-8 assay is based on the enzymatic reduction of a tetrazolium-based
salt by NADH-dependent oxidoreductases present in viable cells. This
generates a formazan product, which is detectable by its strong absorbance
at 450 nm, allowing the determination of relative viability by comparing
MNP treated cell populations against untreated controls. Analysis
of the CCK-8 results ( A) indicated that incorporation of anionic amphiphiles into
binary mixed micelles (APC 0/10, 0/20, and 0/30) negatively impacted
cellular viability relative to APC 0/0 homomicelles, indicating potential
cytotoxic effects associated with increased anionic incorporation.
Since higher anionic content necessitates the addition of more total
micelle to maintain the N/P ratio at 10, this inadvertently increases
the overall polymer dose resulting in increased toxicity. Conversely,
binary mixed micelles containing PEG amphiphiles (APC 5/0, 10/0, and
15/0) demonstrate enhanced viability compared to both APC 0/0 homomicelles
and anion containing binary mixtures (APC 0/10, 0/20, and 0/30). This
observation aligns with existing literature, suggesting that PEG incorporation
mitigates cytotoxicity through its ability to effectively shield excessive
positive surface charges, thereby reducing detrimental cellular interactions. Finally, all ternary mixed micelles containing
both PEG and anionic amphiphiles exhibited viability trends aligning
closely with PEG-containing binary micelles, suggesting that PEG’s
protective effects persist within these ternary mixtures, mitigating
the detrimental effects of increased polymer load.
 To integrate the competing impacts of transfection
efficiency and
viability on overall micelle performance, we introduced the concept
of effective efficiency (Eeff), which is defined as the
product of transfection efficiency and cell viability ( B). Examining Eeff across different formulation subsets, three distinct trends emerged:
First, the incorporation of anionic amphiphiles alone (APC 0/10, 0/20,
and 0/30) enhanced transfection efficiency but concurrently decreased
viability, resulting in effective efficiencies comparable to APC 0/0
homomicelles. Second, PEG incorporation alone (APC 5/0, 10/0, and
15/0) improved cell viability but correspondingly reduced transfection
efficiency, again yielding effective efficiencies similar to APC 0/0
homomicelles. Finally, ternary formulations (APC XX/YY series) uniquely
demonstrated a synergistic benefit from incorporating both anionic
and PEG amphiphiles. Anionic amphiphile presence improved transfection
efficiency, while PEG incorporation preserved or improved viability,
cumulatively driving a marked increase in effective efficiency. Among
these ternary formulations, the APC 15/10, 15/20, and 15/30 series
displayed the highest Eeff, achieving nearly a 2-fold enhancement
(∼0.75 Eeff) over native APC 0/0 homomicelles (∼0.4
Eeff). This enhancement in Eeff far outperforms
the commercial transfection standard JetPEI (∼0.6 Eeff). Collectively, these findings underscore the necessity of balancing
amphiphile composition to achieve optimal transfection performance,
highlighting that strategic integration of PEG and acidic anionic
amphiphiles synergistically enhances the overall efficacy and biocompatibility
of polymeric micelle-based delivery systems.

## Ternary Mixed Micelles Show Tunable Organ Tropism In
Vivo

To assess the in vivo performance
and biodistribution of the mixed micelle formulations, we employed
luciferase-encoding mRNA as a bioluminescent reporter. MNPs were formulated
at an N/P ratio of 10 and administered intravenously to immune-competent
BALB/c mice at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg via tail-vein injection. Bioluminescence
imaging was performed to quantify luciferase expression, serving as
a direct readout of mRNA delivery and translation efficiency across
anatomical sites. Whole-body luminescence analysis revealed a composition-dependent
decrease in total signal upon incremental incorporation of PEG or
anionic amphiphiles ( A,B). However, despite this global attenuation in signal in
mixed formulations ( B), striking shifts in organ-specific biodistribution emerged,
underscoring the impact of amphiphile composition on nanoparticle
tropism ( A).
Homomicelles composed solely of the cationic amphiphile (APC 0/0)
exhibited strong pulmonary accumulation, with strong luminescence
localized to the thoracic region. Binary A7-PEG micelles (like APC
5/0) maintained this lung targeting ability but showed a stepwise
reduction in intensity with increased PEG content. In contrast, ternary
micelles incorporating anionic amphiphiles demonstrated a pronounced
redistribution of organ tropism. Intermediate anionic content (like
APC 5/10 and 10/20) produced dual-site localization in both thoracic
and splenic regions, while the highest anionic formulation (APC 10/30,
15/30) exhibited predominant splenic accumulation ( A, C). To quantify this shift,
formulations showing elevated splenic signal (e.g., APC 0/30, 5/30,
10/30, and 15/30) were selected for follow-up organ-level biodistribution
studies ( C,D).
APC 0/0 homomicelles demonstrated strong pulmonary targeting, with
approximately 2 orders of magnitude higher luminescence in the lungs
(2 × 105 p/s/cm2/sr) compared to the spleen
(1 × 103 p/s/cm2/sr) ( C). In contrast, formulations
with increasing anionic content progressively inverted this distribution.
Notably, APC 0/30 yielded equivalent luminescence in both lungs and
spleen (∼4 × 104 p/s/cm2/sr), whereas
APC 15/30 exhibited a pronounced splenic preference, with almost 1.5
orders of magnitude higher signal in the spleen (∼6 ×
104 p/s/cm2/sr) relative to the lungs (∼2
× 103 p/s/cm2/sr) ( C). The spleen-targeting capability of these
ternary mixed micelles is illustrated in D, which shows pronounced spleen-localized
luminescence in contrast to the lung-specific luminescence observed
for the APC 0/0 homomicelle control. These results establish that
oppositely charged amphiphile blending enables tunable reprogramming
of organ specificity from lung to spleen.
 Similar trends in biodistribution upon anionic
doping have been
reported for lipid nanoparticle systems, suggesting that changes in
surface charge may mediate organ targeting.
,
 However, zeta potential measurements in our system consistently
demonstrated a uniformly high positive surface charge (∼+45–55
mV) across all formulations, indicating that net surface charge of
micelles is not the dominant driver of tropism in this context. Furthermore,
all MNPs were formulated at the same N/P ratio, which is defined exclusively
by the stoichiometric ratio of the cationic amphiphile to anionic
mRNA and is therefore independent of the amounts of neutral or anionic
amphiphiles. This maintains a constant overall charge balance across
the formulation library. Taking all these factors together, we hypothesize
that nanoparticle morphology, together with concurrent changes in
particle size and deformability, may play a central role in dictating
the observed organ selectivity. Several reports have demonstrated
that particle shape, size and deformability strongly impact in vivo splenic distribution: rigid or irregular particles
are often trapped within the spleen, whereas softer, more deformable
structures can evade the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and circulate
more broadly.
,,
 Our own TEM and DLS analyses revealed a transition from small spherical
micelles to larger, elongated wormlike structures with increasing
anionic amphiphile incorporation, accompanied by an increase in particle
size (up to ∼400 nm by DLS). Furthermore, TEM analysis of the
mRNA-bound micelleplexes confirmed the persistence of these morphological
differences, where lung-targeting APC 0/0 homomicelles formed spherical
aggregates ( E­(vi)), while APC 10/30 micelleplexes displayed wormlike or tubular
structures ( E­(x)), indicating that micelle morphology is retained upon complexation
and represents the form encountered by organs during biodistribution.
We propose that this morphological and size heterogeneity dictates
the observed shifts in biodistribution: smaller spherical micelles
(APC 0/0) preferentially localize to pulmonary capillaries, whereas
larger, anisotropic worm-like structures (like APC 10/30 and APC 15/30)
are more readily sequestered by the splenic reticuloendothelial system.
This model also explains the dual-site localization observed in APC
10/10 and APC 10/20 formulations, which likely consist of mixed morphologies,
where smaller spherical micelleplexes readily slip through the RES
and accumulate in the lungs and larger, elongated aggregates are preferentially
retained and cleared by the splenic RES ( E­(viii-ix)). Collectively, these results
underline the complex interplay between amphiphile composition, nanoparticle
morphology and size, and resultant biodistribution patterns. They
further emphasize the importance of morphological control as a key
factor in optimizing targeted organ-specific delivery. The ability
to precisely modulate nanoparticle morphology through strategic amphiphile
selection and incorporation represents a potent approach for enhancing
the precision and efficacy of systemic nucleic acid delivery platforms,
paving the way for tailored therapeutic strategies.

## Optimizing in Vitro Delivery Performance in
Serum Presence
While the observed organ tropism can be rationalized
by morphology-dependent biodistribution, the ternary mixed micelles
also exhibited a global attenuation in overall signal in vivo, prompting further investigation. One likely contributor is interaction
with serum proteins, which are known to reduce the performance of
cationic polymer–nucleic acid complexes by inducing premature
disassembly, reducing payload protection, and limiting effective cellular
uptake.
,
 To assess how mixed micelle composition
influences serum tolerance and functional mRNA delivery under physiologically
relevant conditions, we performed in vitro transfection
studies in HEK293T cells using OptiMEM supplemented with 10% fetal
bovine serum (FBS) as a controlled proxy for serum exposure in vivo ( A­(i)). While these experiments evaluate functional delivery
outcomes in the presence of serum, they do not directly measure particle
integrity or RNA protection, and conclusions regarding physicochemical
stability are therefore limited. For delivery under serum conditions
((−) OptiMEM maturation), MNP formulations were directly diluted
3× in OptiMEM containing 10% FBS rather than OptiMEM alone, while
the remaining transfection protocol described in the section was unchanged. Flow cytometry
analysis revealed significant differences in performance under serum
conditions compared to serum-free conditions ( B–C, (−) OptiMEM maturation).
Incremental incorporation of PEG amphiphile into binary micelles (APC
5/0, 10/0, and 15/0) did not yield additional performance improvements
relative to APC 0/0 homomicelles. However, binary and ternary micelles
containing anionic amphiphiles exhibited improved transfection efficiency
(∼70–80% GFP+ve cells) over APC 0/0 homomicelles (∼50%
GFP+ve cells). Notably, this improvement was maximal at intermediate
levels of anionic doping (10–20 mol % CEA) and diminished at
higher CEA content (30 mol %), where transfection efficiency declined
back to ∼50% GFP+ve. Analysis of GFP MFI revealed a pronounced
serum-dependent attenuation across all formulations ( C). Relative to serum-free
conditions, where GFP MFI spanned approximately 500–4000 across
the formulation library, MFI values decreased by ∼80% under
serum conditions to ∼100–350. This reduction was particularly
severe for ternary mixed formulations, which exhibited a decrease
from 3000 to ∼300 MFI, compared to a more modest reduction
for APC 0/0 homomicelles (∼500 to ∼250 MFI). These results
suggest reduced functional intracellular payload delivery in the presence
of serum. Collectively, these results suggest that excessive incorporation
of PEG and anionic amphiphiles may lower effective micelle charge
density, which could promote premature decomplexation in the presence
of serum proteins. While micelle morphology governs organ-selective
biodistribution in vivo, the magnitude of the overall expression signal
appears to be dictated by micelle complexation and decomplexation
dynamics under serum-rich physiological conditions.
 To address this serum-associated reduction in functional
mRNA delivery in vitro, we employed an innovative
strategy leveraging
controlled micelleplex aggregation. Previously, we observed rapid
aggregation of mixed micelles upon dilution into serum-free OptiMEM.
Leveraging this behavior, we developed a novel strategy where micelles
were first allowed to aggregate in serum-free OptiMEM, after which
serum-supplemented medium was introduced ( A­(ii)). For this OptiMEM maturation strategy,
MNP formulations were first diluted 2× in OptiMEM and incubated
for 15 min to allow preaggregation, followed by further dilution with
OptiMEM containing 20% FBS to achieve a final 3× dilution and
10% total FBS concentration, while the remaining transfection protocol
was unchanged. By initially inducing micelle aggregation in serum-free
conditions, followed by subsequent serum introduction, we hypothesized
that serum proteins would preferentially opsonize micellar aggregates
rather than individual micelles, thereby improving functional mRNA
delivery. Implementing this strategy, transfection outcomes demonstrated
performance trends closely resembling those of serum-free experiments,
with significant improvements in transfection efficiency and GFP MFI
observed for formulations incorporating higher levels of anionic amphiphiles
( B–C,
(+) OptiMEM maturation). Particularly, the APC 10/30 formulation exhibited
superior performance, surpassing both other tested formulations and
the commercial JetPEI control. Viability remained high (>70%) across
all formulations, though slight reductions were noted for formulations
with higher anionic incorporation that can be attributed to MNP aggregate
sedimentation (Figure S18). DLS characterization
of serum-induced aggregate proved challenging, as interference from
serum protein size distributions produced unreliable autocorrelation
measurements. Nevertheless, robust transfection outcomes strongly
support the effectiveness of our aggregation-based strategy (referred
to as OptiMEM maturation), underscoring its potential to mitigate
serum-associated reductions in transfection performance. Collectively,
these findings emphasize the critical interplay between micelle composition,
serum interactions, and controlled aggregation. This strategic aggregation
of micelles prior to serum exposure presents a compelling approach
for optimizing polymeric micelle-based nucleic acid delivery in physiologically
relevant in vitro environments.

## Conclusion
In summary, this work establishes amphiphile
composition as a central
design parameter governing the structure, stability, and biological
performance of micellar polymeric nanoparticles for mRNA delivery.
By systematically varying cationic oligoamine amphiphile, acidic anionic
amphiphile (CEA, SEA, BEA, PEA), and neutral PEG amphiphile mixing
ratios, we delineated clear structure–function relationships
linking micelle composition to physicochemical properties, cellular
interactions, and in vivo biodistribution. Progressive
incorporation of anionic amphiphiles drove a dose-dependent morphological
transition from spherical to elongated, wormlike micelles, revealing
morphology as an emergent and tunable feature of ternary mixed systems. In vitro, incorporation of acidic anionic amphiphiles markedly
enhanced transfection efficiency and GFP expression across multiple
cell lines (HEK293T, A549, HuH7), driven by increased uptake, efficient
intracellular release, and improved endosomal escape. Notably, amphiphiles
with lower pK
a values (CEA and SEA) consistently
outperformed near-neutral analogues (BEA and PEA), underscoring the
importance of amphiphile acidity in enhancing mRNA delivery and expression.
Concurrent PEG incorporation mitigated cytotoxicity through charge
shielding, yielding optimized ternary formulations (APC 10/30 and
APC 15/30) that achieved the highest effective efficiencies by balancing
transfection performance with cell viability. Furthermore, during in vitro transfection in serum presence, introducing controlled
preaggregation significantly improved serum resistance, preserving
cargo stability, and ultimately displaying superior transfection efficiency
and GFP expression levels comparable to serum-free conditions, highlighting
a practical strategy for improving serum robustness. Finally, in vivo biodistribution revealed a clear morphology-dependent
shift in organ tropism from lung to spleen as noncationic amphiphile
content increased. While spherical, cationic micelles preferentially
displayed thoracic luminescence, mixed micelles with elongated wormlike
morphology showed splenic luminescence. Notably, this transition occurred
despite comparable positive zeta potentials across formulations, indicating
that micelle morphology, rather than surface charge, governs organ
selectivity. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the correlation
between wormlike morphology and splenic accumulation, establishing
morphology as the key determinant of biodistribution and highlighting
a tunable, composition-driven strategy for directing polymeric micelles
to specific organs. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that
deliberate control over amphiphile composition and careful manipulation
of micelle morphology enables simultaneous optimization of in vitro performance and in vivo organ
selectivity. This blended micelle platform provides a versatile framework
for designing polymeric mRNA delivery systems with enhanced efficacy
and programmable biodistribution, advancing the rational development
of next-generation nucleic acid therapeutics.

## Materials and Methods

## Synthesis of Poly­(nBA)-b-poly­(A7 acrylamide)
Amphiphilic Diblock Polymers
All reagents were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich or BocSciences and used without further purification.
Pentafluorophenol acrylate was synthesized and used as previously
reported. Poly­(nBA)-b-poly­(A7 acrylamide) was synthesized following previously reported
procedures. Briefly, the poly­(nBA) macro-chain
transfer agent (poly­(nBA)-DDMAT) was prepared by bulk RAFT polymerization
of n-butyl acrylate (nBA) using 2-(dodecylthiocarbonothioylthio)-2-methylpropionic
acid (DDMAT) as the chain transfer agent. After purification and characterization,
poly­(nBA)-DDMAT macroCTA (1 equiv) was weighed into a 10 mL scintillation
vial, and pentafluorophenyl acrylate (PFPA, 300 equiv) was added.
The mixture was dissolved in 1,4-dioxane to achieve a final monomer
concentration of 2 M, yielding a yellow homogeneous solution. Azobisisobutyronitrile
(AIBN, 0.05 equiv) was then introduced, and the solution was sparged
with nitrogen gas for 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen. The reaction
mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 18 h under a nitrogen atmosphere.
The resulting diblock copolymer, poly­(nBA)
n
-b-poly­(PFPA)
m
, was
purified by precipitation in methanol. Block composition was confirmed
by integration of diagnostic signals in the 1H NMR spectrum
(CDCl3): 4.04 ppm (2H, poly­(nBA)), 3.08 ppm (1H, poly­(PFPA)),
and 0.94 ppm (3H, poly­(nBA)).
To introduce the functional amine
group, 200 mg of purified poly­(nBA)-b-poly­(PFPA)
was dissolved in 5 mL of anhydrous DMF and stirred until fully homogeneous.
Hydroxyethyl acrylate (20 equiv) was added as a capping agent, followed
by addition of 2n equivalents of Boc-protected A7 amine, where n corresponds
to the number of PFPA repeat units. The reaction vial was sealed,
purged with nitrogen, and stirred at 40 °C for 18 h. Upon completion,
the reaction was cooled to room temperature, and an aliquot was analyzed
by 19F NMR to confirm the disappearance of PFPA signals,
indicating successful substitution. For Boc deprotection, the crude
polymer was dissolved in trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at a ratio of
1 mL TFA per 100 mg of polymer and stirred open to air for 4.5 h.
The reaction mixture was subsequently dialyzed against deionized water
using a SnakeSkin dialysis membrane (MWCO 3.5 kDa) to remove excess
acid and byproducts. The deprotected A7-functionalized amphiphile
was recovered by lyophilization, and purity was verified via 1H NMR spectroscopy.

## Synthesis of Poly­(nBA)-b-poly­(anionic) Amphiphilic
Diblock Polymers
The anionic amphiphiles were prepared via
chain extension on poly­(nBA)-DDMAT macroCTA using RAFT polymerization.
poly­(nBA)-DDMAT macroCTA was synthesized by the bulk polymerization
of n-butyl acrylate (nBA) with 2-(Dodecylthiocarbonothioylthio)-2-
methylpropionic acid (DDMAT) CTA as previously reported. The anionic monomer (Carboxyethyl acrylate (CEA),
or 4-(3-(Acryloyloxy)­propoxy)­benzoic acid (BEA), or Mono­(2-acryloyloxyethyl)
Succinate (SEA), or 2-(phosphonooxy)­ethyl acrylate­(PEA)) (300 Eq,
purified on an alumina plug), and poly­(nBA)-DDMAT macroCTA (1 equiv)
were first weighed into a 20 mL scintillation vial, dissolved in DMF
(1M), and AIBN (0.05 Eq) was added to it. The reaction mixture was
degassed by sparging with N
2(g) for 30 min and subsequently immersed in an oil bath that was preheated
at 70 °C. The reaction was stirred at 70 °C for 18h under N
2(g) atmosphere and subsequently
quenched by immersing the flask in liquid nitrogen and opening to
air simultaneously. The polymer was purified via dialysis in methanol.
After drying under vacuum, the polymer was recovered as an off-white
powder.

## Synthesis of Poly­(nBA)-b-poly­(ethylene glycol)
Amphiphilic Diblock Polymers
The PEG-based chain transfer
agent (PEG macroCTA) was synthesized following previously reported
procedures with minor modifications. In
a typical reaction, PEG–OH (M
n =
10 kDa, 1 equiv), DDMAT (2 equiv) and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP,
1 equiv) were dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM)
and cooled in an ice bath to 0 °C. Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC,
2 equiv), predissolved in DCM, was added dropwise under stirring.
After 2 h at 0 °C, the reaction mixture was allowed to warm to
room temperature and stirred for an additional 70 h. The crude product
was precipitated using cold diethyl ether and collected by filtration.
The resulting PEG­(10K)-DDMAT macroCTA was dried under high vacuum
overnight to afford a yellow solid (yield: 95%).
RAFT polymerization
of n-butyl acrylate (nBA) was then carried out using PEG­(10K)-DDMAT
macroCTA. Specifically, PEG-CTA (1 equiv), n-butyl acrylate (140 equiv,
prepurified by passage through a neutral alumina plug), and azobisisobutyronitrile
(AIBN; 0.05 equiv) were weighed into a 20 mL scintillation vial. The
reaction mixture was degassed by nitrogen sparging for 1 h and subsequently
transferred to a preheated oil bath at 70 °C. Polymerization
was allowed to proceed for 45 min under a nitrogen atmosphere before
quenching by immersion in liquid nitrogen while simultaneously exposing
the reaction to air. The resulting block copolymer was purified by
dialysis in methanol, followed by vacuum drying to yield a yellow
solid.

## Mixed Micelle Formulation and Characterization
Micelles
were prepared via the thin-film hydration method. First, individual
stock solutions of each amphiphile were prepared by dissolving the
dry powder in methanol to achieve 1 mg/mL concentration. Appropriate
amounts of each amphiphile stock (A7, PEG, and Anionic (CEA, SEA,
BEA, or PEA)) were mixed in a vial to achieve the desired mixing ratio.
Subsequently, these mixtures were dried under N2 stream
to yield polymer films. Finally, the polymer films were hydrated with
deionized RNase and DNase free water to obtained 1.5 mg/mL polymer
dispersions. The dispersions were stirred at room temperature for
3 days and sonicated for 30 min at every 24-h interval using an ultrasonic
bath. During the sonication process the temperature of the bath was
monitored and never exceeded 30 °C. The size distribution of
the mixed micelles was measured with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (λ
= 633 nm) with a scattering angle of 173° at a temperature of
25 °C.
For TEM imaging, images of polymer micelles were
obtained using a FEI Tecnai Spirit Bio-Twin TEM. Carbon coated mesh
300 copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) were glow-discharged
using a PELCO glow discharged. 10 μL of polymer micelles or
micelleplexes (MNPs) at a concentration of 0.25 mg/mL were deposited
on the grid. The grid was blotted after 30 s to remove excess sample.
μL of 2% uranyl formate solution were deposited on the grid
and blotted after 30 s. The grid was washed using Milli-Q water to
remove excess UA. The grid was left to dry for 30 min. The grid was
then mounted on a single tilt sample holder. The microscope was operated
at 120 kV with a magnification of 10,000–120,000x to assess
particle morphology and size distribution.

## Micellar Nanoparticle Formulation and Characterization
MNP solutions were prepared by diluting micelle and mRNA components
in DNA/RNase free water. All micelle stocks were diluted down to a
final concentration of 3 nMol of nitrogen per μL. These stocks
were then diluted to appropriate concentration necessary so that upon
mixing (equal volumes) with diluted mRNA solution, the appropriate
N/P is achieved. For example, 5 μL of the polymer stock (3 nMol
N/ul) is necessary for every 0.5 μg of plasmid at an N/P 10.
To make a polyplex sample containing 1.5 μg of plasmid at N/P
10 for a transfection, 15 μL of polymer stock was diluted in
60 μL of PBS to yield 75 μL of micelle stock. For mRNA
at 1 mg/mL concentration, 1.5 μL of mRNA stock was added to
73.5 μL PBS. To form the MNPs, 75 μL of micelle stock
was added to 75 μL mRNA stock, to total of 150 μL, mixed,
and then allowed to incubate at room temperature for 45 min prior
to use.
The hydrodynamic diameter (D
h) of the thus formed MNPs in water was determined via DLS using a
DynaPro Plate Reader at a temperature of 25 °C. For determining
their size distribution upon dilution in cell culture media, the MNPs
were first formed in water, their size distributions recorded and
then diluted (3X) with OptiMEM. The OptiMEM diluted MNPs were incubated
further for 2 h prior to DLS measurements. The interval of time between
the addition of media and sample measurement was kept constant for
all samples.

## RiboGreen Dye Exclusion Assay
Dye exclusion assays
were performed using Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA reagent (ThermoFisher
Scientific). mRNA solutions were prepared by diluting the primary
mRNA stock (1 mg/mL) into RNase free water containing RiboGreen to
obtain a final dye concentration of 0.5% v/v. Equal volumes of this
RiboGreen-labeled mRNA solution and micelle dilutions were mixed to
form micelle–mRNA polyplexes (MNPs), following aforementioned
protocols. The N/P ratio was maintained at N/P 10 for all the MNPs
in the library. Controls included mRNA with RiboGreen in the absence
of polymer and RiboGreen in water alone. Aliquots (100 μL) of
each formulation were dispensed in triplicate into black, flat-bottom
96-well microplates. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate
reader (λex= 485 ± 10 nm, λem = 528 ± 10 nm). Raw fluorescence values were background-subtracted
using dye-free controls and normalized to the polymer-free sample
(N/P = 0). Each well was subsequently diluted 3-fold with serum-free
OptiMEM, and fluorescence end points were recorded 30 min postdilution.
To assess mRNA release, 10 μL of heparan sulfate (1.875 mg/mL
in water; target S/N = 10) was added to each well (containing 100
μL of MNPs), and fluorescence increase was recorded using the
same parameters. The difference in normalized fluorescence intensity
before and after heparin treatment was used to quantify mRNA release
from the MNP complexes.

## Delivery Reporter Assay
GFP-encoding mRNA was utilized
as a reporter to assess in vitro delivery efficiency
in HEK293T, HuH7, and A549 cell lines. Unless otherwise indicated,
identical procedures were followed for all three cell types. Cells
were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 25,000 cells per well
24 h prior to transfection. Micelle-based nanoparticles (MNPs) were
prepared as previously described and diluted 3-fold in serum-free
OptiMEM before transfection. The culture media was aspirated, and
150 μL of the diluted MNP formulation was added to each well
in triplicate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. As a
representative formulation, 75 μL of A7 micelle stock was mixed
with 75 μL of mRNA solution (containing 1.5 μg of mRNA),
incubated at room temperature for 45 min, and subsequently diluted
with 300 μL of OptiMEM to reach a total volume of 450 μL.
This solution was then distributed across three wells (150 μL
per well), yielding a final mRNA dose of 0.5 μg per well. Following
the 4 h incubation, 500 μL of cell-type-specific complete growth
medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added to
each well. A positive control using jetPEI (Polyplus) was included
and prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol at an
N/P ratio of 5. All experimental formulations were tested at an N/P
ratio of 10 with an mRNA dose of 0.5 μg per well. All biological
experiments were performed in triplicate, and error bars in all figures
represent the standard deviation.
Twenty-four hours post-transfection,
cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay (Dojindo). For this,
CCK-8 reagent was diluted into FluoroBrite DMEM supplemented with
10% FBS to a final concentration of 10% (v/v). The media in each well
was aspirated and replaced with 500 μL of the CCK-8 working
solution, followed by 2 h incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently,
150 μL of media from each well was transferred to a clear-bottom
96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy
H1 Hybrid microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT). Cell viability
was calculated by normalizing absorbance values to untreated control
wells.
Following viability assessment, cells were harvested
for flow cytometry.
Media was removed, and adherent cells were trypsinized and transferred
to 2 mL deep-well plates. Cells were washed with PBS, centrifuged
at 1000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in PBS containing
2% FBS and Calcein Violet AM to label live cells. GFP-positive cell
populations were quantified using a ZE5 flow cytometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules,
CA) equipped with 405 and 488 nm lasers. Data was analyzed using FlowJo
software (BD Biosciences); representative gating strategies are provided
in Figure S19.

## Delivery Assays in Serum
For delivery in the presence
of serum the methodology outlined above was followed with two different
modifications. For (−) OptiMEM maturation strategy, the MNP
formulations were 3X diluted with OptiMEM containing 10% FBS instead
of normal OptiMEM. For (+) OptiMEM maturation strategy, the MNP formulations
were 2X diluted with OptiMEM and allowed to incubate for 15 min, followed
by addition of 1X OptiMEM containing 10% FBS, to a total of 3X dilution.
The rest of the protocol was kept the same.

## Cellular Uptake Assay
For cellular uptake studies,
Cy5-labeled GFP-encoding mRNA (EZ Cap Cy5 EGFP mRNA [5-moUTP], ApexBio
Technology) was employed as the fluorescent reporter. The transfection
protocol was identical to that described above, with the exception
that, during flow cytometry sample preparation, cells were washed
three times with CellScrub buffer (Amsbio) to facilitate removal of
cell surface-associated MNPs. Internalization was assessed at 4 and
24 h post-transfection by quantifying Cy5 signal via flow cytometry.
Specifically, the percentage of live cells positive for Cy5 fluorescence,
as well as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), was measured using
a ZE5 flow cytometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) equipped with a 640 nm
laser. Representative gating strategies used to define live and Cy5-positive
populations are provided in Figure S19.
All uptake experiments were conducted at an N/P ratio of 10 with an
mRNA dose of 0.5 μg per well.

## Bafilomycin-A1 Inhibition Assay
Transfections for the
bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) treatment group followed the same workflow
as previously described, with minor modifications. For the Baf-A1
(+) condition, culture medium was removed and replaced with 500 μL
of DMEM containing 200 nM Baf-A1 10 min prior to transfection. After
the brief preincubation period, the Baf-A1-containing media was aspirated,
and transfection was performed as outlined above. Cells were harvested
and analyzed via flow cytometry 24 h post-transfection. The positive
control jetPEI was included at an N/P ratio of 5. All experimental
conditions were carried out at an N/P ratio of 10 with a final mRNA
dose of 0.5 μg per well.

## 
In Vivo Studies
All animal studies
were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC,
protocol number -2207-40246A) at the University of Minnesota. Mice
were housed within the University of Minnesota’s Research Animal
Resource facilities, an AAALAC accredited program. Specific Pathogen
Free female Balb/C mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory
(Strain #:000651). Animals were dosed at 8 weeks old with either PBS,
MNP formulations, or JetPEI carrying 10 μg luciferase mRNA in
a total of 200 μL via tail vein injection. Six hours after injection,
delivery was assessed by in vivo bioluminescence
imaging on the IVIS Spectrum 2 (University of Minnesota Imaging centers,
SCR_020997, NIH grant 1S10OD032261). Briefly, animals are intraperitoneally
injected with D-luciferin substrate (100 μL total volume, 28
mg kg–1). Animals are then anesthetized with inhaled
isoflurane and placed in the imager where luminescent signal is measured
5 min after substrate injection. Image acquisition is set to 5 min
exposure with fStop = 1 and Binning = 2.
For organ specific
expression determination, mice were intraperitoneally injected with
D-luciferin substrate, then euthanized by CO2 inhalation
for sample collection. The animals were perfused with PBS, and organs
including the lungs, thymus, liver, spleen, heart, and kidneys were
collected (6 h). Organs were imaged immediately after collection on
the IVIS Spectrum 2. Bioluminescence data was analyzed using Living
Image software (version 4.7.3).
