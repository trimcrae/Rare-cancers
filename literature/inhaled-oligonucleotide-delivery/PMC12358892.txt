## Significance
This study presents MiniCasUltra, a next-generation compact genome editing tool with improved activity, low off-target effects, and broader genome-targeting capacity compared to earlier compact editors. Its small size enables packaging of both the nuclease and dual guide RNAs into a single adeno-associatedvirus (AAV) vector, allowing efficient simultaneous editing of two disease-relevant genes in vivo. In a mouse model of choroidal neovascularization (CNV), MiniCasUltra delivered by AAV significantly reduced lesion size by targeting vascularendothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), demonstrating therapeutic potential for CNV, a key feature of neovascular age-related macular degeneration. MiniCasUltra bridges the gap between compactness and efficacy, offering a powerful platform for treating complex diseases involving multiple genetic factors.
Keywords: MiniCasUltra, Un1Cas12f1, compact programmable nucleases, genome editing, neovascular age-related macular degeneration

## Abstract
Compact programmable nucleases provide versatile genome editing tools with therapeutic potential, particularly when delivered via adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors. However, their limited editing efficacy and stringent protospaceradjacent motif (PAM) requirements impose significant limitations in practical application. Here, we engineered MiniCasUltra, an optimized Un1Cas12f1 variant, through rational mutagenesis. MiniCasUltra exhibits sixfold higher editing activity than Un1Cas12f1, minimal off-target effects (on/off-target ratio > 10), and an expanded PAM preference (5′-WBTR). Using a single AAV vector encoding MiniCasUltra and two single-guide RNAs, we achieved simultaneous editing of two disease-causing genes (Pten and Fah) in mouse liver, with indel rates of 15.82% and 29.39%, respectively- significantly surpassing CasMINI V3.1 (3.45% and 10.98%). Furthermore, AAV delivery of MiniCasUltra targeting a noncanonical 5′-TCTG PAM site in human vascularendothelial growth factor A reduced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) lesions in a laser-induced CNV mouse model of neovascular age-related macular degeneration, a leading global cause of blindness. The broad and effective targeting capabilities of MiniCasUltra, coupled with its compact size, highlight its potential for in vivo genome editing and therapeutic interventions.

The CRISPR-Cas system, originally evolved as an adaptive immune system in archaea and bacteria, has been repurposed into a groundbreaking genome-editing platform ( – ). Despite its transformative potential, a critical limitation hinders its therapeutic application: The large size of commonly used Cas nucleases. For instance, Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), Francisella novicida Cas12a, and Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 each exceed 1,000 amino acids in length, approaching the maximal packaging capacity of adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors. AAV is the preferred delivery vehicle for in vivo gene therapy due to its nonpathogenicity, high transfection efficiency, diverse organ tropism and specificity, and low risk of genomic integration ( ). However, the bulky size of conventional Cas protein leaves inadequate space within AAV particles for essential components like single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) or regulatory elements ( , ), severely limiting their utility in clinical settings.
To address this challenge, compact CRISPR nucleases such as Cas12f (formerly Cas14) ( , ), and transposon-derived nuclease TnpB/IscB, putative ancestors of Cas12 and Cas9, respectively—have emerged as promising alternatives ( – ). Cas12f characterized by a single RuvC nuclease domain and protospacer adjacent motif (PAM)-dependent DNA cleavage was the first compact nuclease shown to enable programmable double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) editing in vitro ( ). Subsequent engineering efforts have expanded the Cas12f family to include variants like uncultured archaeon Cas12f1 (UnCas12f1) ( , ), Acidibacillus sulfuroxidans Cas12f1 (AsCas12f1) ( – ), Oscillibacter sp. Cas12f1 (OsCas12f1) ( ), Ruminiclostridium herbifermentans Cas12f1 (RhCas12f1) ( ), and Clostridium novyi Cas12f1 nuclease (CnCas12f1) ( ), all of which support AAV-delivered genome editing in mammalian cells and murine models ( – , ). However, these systems suffer from critical drawbacks: Their editing efficiencies remain markedly lower than those of Cas9 or Cas12a, and their strict PAM requirements (e.g., T-rich motifs for UnCas12f1) drastically restrict targetable genomic loci ( ). Parallel limitations plague TnpB and IscB nucleases, including suboptimal activity and narrow targeting scope ( , – ), underscoring the urgent need for improved compact editors.
In this study, we rationally engineered Un1Cas12f1 to develop MiniCasUltra, a hypercompact nuclease with enhanced editing efficiency, expanded PAM compatibility, and minimal off-target effects. Capitalizing on its small size, we achieved simultaneous dual-locus editing of two disease-associated genes in mouse liver via a single AAV vector.
Furthermore, MiniCasUltra’s ability to recognize noncanonical PAMs enabled efficient targeting of human vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) in a choroidal neovascularization (CNV) mouse model—a key pathological feature of neovascular agerelated macular degeneration (nAMD), the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. AAV-mediated delivery of MiniCasUltra to the retina robustly suppressed CNV progression, demonstrating its therapeutic potential. These advances establish MiniCasUltra as a versatile, next-generation genome editor that overcomes the critical limitations of existing compact systems.

## Results

## Engineered Variants of Un1Cas12f1 Exhibit Improved Activity in Mammalian Cells.
To improve the gene-editing efficiency of Un1Cas12f1, we designed mutants based on structural analyses of its ternary complex (sgRNA: Un1Cas12f1: DNA) ( , ). Key residues in three functional regions—the PAM-interacting duplex, catalytic RuvC domain, and target nucleic acid binding domain—were substituted with amino acids bearing bulky or charged side chains to optimize DNA interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). To evaluate dsDNA cleavage efficiency, we utilized a Gaussia luciferase-based reporter system (pSSA) where successful editing restores the luciferase reading frame via single-strand annealing (SSA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) ( ). Screening of initial mutants revealed that substitutions M427F and F487W individually increased editing efficiency by >twofold compared to wild-type Un1Cas12f1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).
Next, we introduced the RRA mutation cluster (D143R/T147R/E151A), previously shown to enhance CasMINI activity ( ) and generated combinatorial variants (RRA, RRAF, RRAW, RRAFW). All constructs were paired with the engineered Ge4.1 sgRNA (129 nucleotides), which enhances Un1Cas12f1 performance in mammalian systems ( ). SpCas9 was included as a benchmark editor. The pSSA assay revealed progressive improvements in editing efficiency across combinatorial mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The RRAFW variant achieved a sixfold increase over wild-type Un1Cas12f1, matching the activity of SpCas9 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We named this optimized nuclease MiniCasUltra to reflect its ultracompact size and superior performance. To quantify nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) efficiency, we employed a Green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter where indels restore the fluorescent protein’s reading frame ( ). MiniCasUltra achieved NHEJ rates of 13.31 ± 2.10%, statistically indistinguishable from SpCas9 (13.05 ± 0.37%) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Using a fluorescence conversion reporter (FCR) where homology-directed repair (HDR) converts BFP to GFP, MiniCasUltra demonstrated measurable HDR activity (4.28 ± 1.76%), though lower than SpCas9 (9.77 ± 1.32%), was substantially higher than that of Un1Cas12f1 and CasMINI V3.1 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and C).
 We next compared MiniCasUltra, Un1Cas12f1, CasMINI V3.1, and SpCas9 at four endogenous loci in human cells. All these loci contain a 5′-TTTR Un1Cas12f1 PAM located upstream of the target and a 5′-NGG SpCas9 PAM positioned at the 3′ end of the target sites. Although editing efficiency varies significantly among different loci, MiniCasUltra consistently outperformed Un1Cas12f1 and CasMINI V3.1, achieving editing efficiency closer to that of SpCas9 ( ). Deep sequencing of two loci revealed distinct indel profiles: MiniCasUltra, Un1Cas12f1, and CasMINI V3.1 predominantly caused large deletions (10 to 20 bp), whereas SpCas9 induced short indels (1 to 2 bp) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). MiniCasUltra’s cleavage sites were located 15 to 30 bp downstream of the PAM, distal to the sgRNA binding region—a pattern consistent with CasMINI V3.1 ( ). In contrast, SpCas9 generated indels 2 to 3 bp upstream of the PAM, within the sgRNA-targeted region (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B).

## Minicasultra Achieves Broadened PAM Compatibility.
To determine whether MiniCasUltra’s engineering altered its PAM specificity, we developed Single-Nucleotide Variant PAM Determination Assay (SNV-PAMDA). This method leverages the pSSA reporter system to systematically test all single-nucleotide variants of Un1Cas12f1’s canonical 5′-TTTA PAM ( ). We generated 12 mutant PAM sequences including 5′-aTTA/cTTA/gTTA/TaTA/TcTA/TgTA/TTaA/TTcA/TTgA/TTTc/TTTg/TTTt and quantified editing activity in HEK293T cells. Only PAMs compatible with the nuclease’s binding pocket supported detectable editing ( ). Sequence logos generated with ggseqlogo visualized PAM preferences ( ). SNV-PAMDA faithfully reproduced Un1Cas12f1’s known 5′-TTTR (R = A/G) PAM preference ( ) ( , ). Strikingly, MiniCasUltra exhibited relaxed specificity: The first PAM nucleotide tolerated A/T (W = A/T), and the second position accepted T/C/G (B = T/C/G), defining an expanded 5′WBTR motif ( ). This represents a sixfold increase in theoretical PAM diversity compared to Un1Cas12f1.
 We validated MiniCasUltra’s expanded PAM by targeting 124 endogenous loci (5′WBTR PAM) in human HEK293T and mouse NIH/3T3 cells. Editing efficiencies ranged from 7.22% to near 100% across loci, with 82/124 (66.13%) sites showing >20% activity ( ). Furthermore, when adapted for adenine base editing (ABE), MiniCasUltra-ABE demonstrated an expanded coverage to 5′-TCTG and 5′-ATTG sites with much improved efficiency from CasMINI (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).
Bioinformatic analysis revealed that MiniCasUltra’s 5′-WBTR PAM increases targetable sites in the human genome from 11.81 (Un1Cas12f1) to 47.15% (SI Appendix, Table S1). Importantly, altered PAM of MiniCasUltra complements to that of other compact CRISPRCas systems including AsCas12f1 variants, enOsCas12f1, RhCas12f1, and CnCas12f1, all of which have demonstrated effective editing capabilities when targeting human genomic regions ( – ). Consequently, in theory, the collective use of these newly identified compact CRISPR-Cas systems as a toolbox is capable of providing comprehensive coverage of the human genome.

## Minicasultra Maintains High Specificity with Minimal Off-Target Effects.
To investigate the specificity of MiniCasUltra, we first conducted an examination of in silico predicted off-target sites, from Cas-OFFinder ( ), followed by deep sequencing of five sgRNAs targeting mouse Trp53, Pten, Fah, and human VEGFA. MiniCasUltra achieved robust on-target editing (40 to 60%) while limiting off-target indels to 1.22 to 3.48% across all loci ( ). Analysis of 20 predicted off-target sites revealed MiniCasUltra’s specificity ratio (on-target/off-target activity) was 11.39 to 48.05, significantly surpassing Un1Cas12f1 (0.16 to 8.88) and CasMINI V3.1 (3.32 to 13.48) ( ).
 Next, we performed genome-wide double-strand break (DSB) mapping via GUIDE-Seq ( ) at three loci (NLRC4, NUDT16, KLHL29) targeted by all four nucleases. This approach eliminates bias from variable target sites. At the NLRC4 locus, MiniCasUltra showed the higher on-target reads (53,306 versus 43,731 for Un1Cas12f1) and the lowest total off-target reads (1,832), comparable to CasMINI V3.1 (1,449) but markedly lower than SpCas9 (7,862) ( ). Strikingly, MiniCasUltra generated only one high-frequency off-target site (≥100 reads), whereas Un1Cas12f1 and CasMINI V3.1 produced 6 and 2 such sites, respectively ( ). A similar trend was observed at the NUDT16 and KLHL29 loci, where MiniCasUltra consistently exhibited high on-target editing efficiency coupled with minimal off-target activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These results further underscore the robust specificity of MiniCasUltra across diverse genomic environments.
Third, we applied primer-extension-mediated approach for sequencing (PEM-Seq) ( ) to profile chromosomal rearrangements and indels at NLRC4 in HEK293T cells. MiniCasUltra predominantly caused large deletions ( ), consistent with prior observations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Critically, MiniCasUltra induced only one chromosomal translocation versus seven for SpCas9 ( ), confirming its superior specificity. Collectively, these results indicate MiniCasUltra as a high-fidelity editor with minimal off-target risks.

## Single AAV-Delivered MiniCasUltra Enables Efficient In Vivo Genome Editing.
To evaluate the in vivo genome editing capability of MiniCasUltra, we packaged it with dual sgRNAs targeting Pten (phosphatase and tensin homolog) and Fah (fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase) into a liver-tropic AAV8 vector ( ). In vitro validation in NIH/3T3 cells showed 57% and 26% editing rates at Pten and Fah, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). GUIDESeq in Hepa1-6 cells revealed only five off-target sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), supporting its specificity.
Intravenous delivery of AAV8-MiniCasUltra ( ) achieved 15.82 ± 2.57% (Pten) and 29.39 ± 2.85% (Fah) editing in mouse liver—4.6- and 2.7-fold higher than CasMINI V3.1 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) confirmed significant protein reduction for both targets versus Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) controls ( ).
 To evaluate off-target effects in vivo, we amplified and deep-sequenced 18 high-confidence off-target sites predicted by Cas-OFFinder and identified via GUIDE-Seq in vitro ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), using genomic DNA from AAV-treated mouse livers. Targeted deep sequencing followed by CRISPResso analysis revealed that all tested loci exhibited indel frequencies below 0.75%, with no statistically significant difference compared to PBS-treated controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). Notably, MiniCasUltra displayed a fivefold higher on-target/off-target ratio relative to CasMINI V3.1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 C and D), highlighting its superior genome-wide specificity in vivo. PEMSeq analysis detected only one chromosomal translocation event resulting from on-target cleavage (MiniCasUltra: 0.006%; CasMINI V3.1: 0.00015%; SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). Translocation events include sgRNA-dependent translocations (both on-target and off target) and sgRNA-independent translocations. The overall translocation rates were at a low level of 0.017% or below for MiniCasUltra and CasMINI V3.1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C).
Histological examination of liver samples 14 d postinjection by using Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no signs of inflammation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Serum levels of alanineaminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured 3 d before and 3 d after AAV or PBS injection. AST levels did not differ significantly among the groups, ALT levels showed a slight increase following MiniCasUltra treatment while the observed levels stayed within the normal range and did not indicate hepatocellular damage (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 B and C) ( ). No significant differences were observed in the other groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 B and C). These data confirm MiniCasUltra’s high specificity and biocompatibility in vivo.

## MiniCasUltra Rescues Pathology in a VEGFA-Substituted Mouse Model of CNV.
We next tested MiniCasUltra in a CNV model where mouse Vegfa is replaced by human VEGFA via homologous recombination in mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Laser-induced choroidal injury in these humanized mice triggers VEGFA-driven CNV ( ), mimicking nAMD—a leading cause of blindness characterized by Bruch’s membrane rupture, retinal fluid exudation, and hemorrhage. Unlike transient anti-VEGF antibody therapies ( ), MiniCasUltra offers durable editing to suppress VEGFA.
To this end, taking advantage of the relaxed PAM requirement of MiniCasUltra, a sgRNA targeting a 5′-TCTG PAM site was employed to knockout VEGFA ( ). We designed an sgRNA that achieved 35% VEGFA editing in HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). The sgRNA and MiniCasUltra were copackaged into a single AAV9 vector with retinal tropism and delivered into the mouse eye via subretinal injection ( ). Pathological CNV was induced by laser injury to model nAMD. The therapeutic effect of MiniCasUltra was evaluated by comparing mice treated with MiniCasUltra to those receiving AAV-GFP (CNV GFP) or PBS (CNV PBS). An additional group receiving PBS without laser injury (No CNV PBS) served as a healthy control ( ). Then, 18.36 to 31.81% of GFP expression was determined in AAV-GFP expression of the retina (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Deep sequencing analysis of mouse retina revealed indel rates of 26.95% targeting VEGFA ( ). Only one off-target site was observed according to the GUIDE-Seq analysis when the same plasmid was delivered to HEK293T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Consistently, significant reduction of VEGFA protein level was observed upon MiniCasUltra administrations, as seen from comparative analysis of samples harvested from the whole eye ( ).
 Fundus fluorescent angiography (FFA) showed MiniCasUltra reduced leakage compared to CNV PBS, correlating with improved visual outcomes ( ). Consistent conclusions could be drawn from reduced isolectin IB4-stained endothelial cells ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S17). This was further confirmed by optical coherence tomography (OCT) scan images showing a significant decrease in CNV width and height in the MiniCasUltra group, as indicated by arrowed lines ( ).
Electroretinography (ERG) revealed MiniCasUltra restored inner retinal function. ERG-a wave as a measure of photoreceptor cell potential and the ERG-b wave to reflect the function of the inner plexus layer, encompassing Müller cells, bipolar cells, and ganglion cells ( ). Quantitative evaluation of scotopic ERG responses ( ) showed no significant improvements in a-wave signals in the MiniCasUltra group relative to the CNV PBS or CNV GFP groups. However, substantially restored wave amplitudes were observed in b-wave signals, suggesting that MiniCasUltra enhanced the inner retinal electrophysiological function. Taken together, these results demonstrate that single AAVdelivered MiniCasUltra achieves phenotypic rescue in CNV by suppressing VEGFA.

## Discussion
In this study, we report MiniCasUltra, a hyperactive variant of Un1Cas12f1 with superior genome editing capabilities. Compared to CasMINI V3.1, MiniCasUltra exhibits a 4.6-fold improvement in editing efficiency ( ), while maintaining high specificity ( ) and PAM flexibility ( ). Its compact size (529 aa) enables dual-sgRNA delivery via a single AAV vector ( ), facilitating efficient in vivo editing (~32% in mouse liver; ) and achieving therapeutic efficacy in a CNV disease model ( ). These features position MiniCasUltra as a powerful tool for next-generation genome engineering and gene therapy.
MiniCasUltra builds on Un1Cas12f1, a compact CRISPR nuclease with a canonical 5′TTTR PAM ( ). Initial efforts, including Ge4.1 sgRNA optimization ( ) and protein engineering in CasMINI ( ), enhanced transcriptional activation and genome editing in mammalian cells. However, these variants fell short of achieving efficient in vivo editing, thereby limiting their translational applications ( , ). More recently, circular guide RNAs were shown to enhance Cas12f-mediated activation and base editing in vitro ( ). Collectively, these findings highlight the limited efficiency of wild-type Cas12f and underscore the need for combined RNA and protein engineering to unlock its full genome editing potential.
MiniCasUltra overcomes these limitations through rational engineering of five key residues that synergistically optimize both DNA binding and cleavage activity. Cryo-Electron Microscopy structural analyses (PDB: 7L49, 7C7L) revealed that the REC, RuvC, and NUC domains collaboratively mediate PAM recognition and DNA cleavage ( , ). Specifically, the reported structure of Un1Cas12f1(PDB: 7L49) provides a DNA substrate with a 5-nt polyCytosine model. Residues M427 and F487 form stabilizing interactions with cytosine bases at positions 3 and 4 of the DNA substrate (SI Appendix, Fig. S18A) ( ).
Building on these insights, substitutions M427F and F487W were designed to introduce bulkier side chains predicted to enhance base stacking-a mechanism distinct from the DNA backbone-binding mutations (D143R/T147R/E151A) in CasMINI (SI Appendix, Fig. S18B) ( , , ). Strikingly, although individual substitutions (M427F or F487W) weakened transcriptional activation in dCasMINI (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), their combination synergistically enhanced cleavage activity in MiniCasUltra by threefold compared to wild-type Un1Cas12f1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). These results highlight the context dependency of structural mutations and emphasize the critical balance between DNA affinity and catalytic efficiency in nuclease design.
The expanded PAM flexibility of MiniCasUltra may arise from the electrostatic interactions between the positively charged arginine residues (D143R and T147R) and the negatively charged DNA backbone at T (-3) and T (-2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S18) ( ). Consistent with our reasoning, CasMINI (RRA) also showed an altered PAM preference, allowing A or T at the first position and A, C, or T at the third position (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). PAM profiling further demonstrated that MiniCasUltra tolerates deviations from the canonical TTTR motif while maintaining specificity—a critical advantage over Un1Cas12f1 and CasMINI V3.1 ( and ). Additionally, MiniCasUltra’s expanded genomic coverage, combined with advances in other compact nucleases (SI Appendix, Table S1) ( , , , – , ), could reduce dependence on bulkier CRISPR-Cas systems. Notably, MiniCasUltra’s expanded PAM compatibility enabled the targeting of several sites ( ), one of which was validated in vivo ( ).
This combination of enhanced PAM compatibility and catalytic efficiency underscores MiniCasUltra’s potential for multitarget therapeutic applications. For example, when MiniCasUltra is codelivered with dual sgRNAs in a single AAV vector, it demonstrates efficient in vivo editing ( ). These advances establish MiniCasUltra as a transformative addition to the programmable nuclease toolkit, surpassing existing compact nucleases such as enAsCas12f ( – ), which show promise but remain limited by T-rich PAM constraints, and enIscB ( ), which lacks robust in vivo validation.
Building upon previous work ( – ), we employed a mouse model of laser-induced CNV to evaluate the therapeutic potential of MiniCasUltra by targeting VEGFA. In this model, MiniCasUltra-mediated genome editing achieved robust therapeutic outcomes, including reduction in IB4-stained lesion area, decrease in endothelial cell infiltration by fluorescein angiography and improvement in photoreceptor function by ERG ( ). These results position MiniCasUltra as a promising compact genome editor for gene therapy applications.
Although targeting human VEGFA yielded significant therapeutic benefits, the use of a murine system limits a comprehensive assessment of off-target effects in a human genomic context. Moreover, the limited yield of genomic DNA from retinal tissue precluded PEM-Seq-based off-target profiling, representing a technical limitation of the current study. In addition, although the orthogonal substitution with human VEGFA in a mouse model enabled the use of human targeting sgRNAs, this model does not recapitulate the full pathology and genetics of human AMD. To further evaluate the translational potential of MiniCasUltra-based therapeutics, genetically defined models, such as monogenic retinal dystrophies or human organoid-based systems, will be essential for assessing allele-specific editing and therapeutic precision in future investigations.
In conclusion, MiniCasUltra represents a transformative advance in compact genome editing tool engineering. More importantly, its dual-targeting capability and AAVcompatibility make it uniquely suited for treating polygenic diseases—such as metabolic syndromes or retinal disorders—that demand multiplexed gene regulation. By achieving balanced optimization of editing activity, specificity, and delivery efficiency, this system not only establishes a foundation for next-generation CRISPR technologies but also accelerates their translation into both biomedical research and clinical therapeutics.

## Materials and Methods

## Plasmid Construction.
CRISPR plasmids were cloned to generated pCMV_NLS-CasNLS_PA_pU6_sgRNA scaffold. The Un1Cas12f1 (Cas14a1) sequence and the sgRNA backbone fragments were amplified from the Cas12f-GE ver4.1 (Addgene Plasmid #176544). Un1Cas12f1 protein mutant plasmids were generated by PCR and Gibson assembly methods using YEASEN Universal One Step Cloning Kit (10922ES20). The CasMINIV3.1 sequence was amplified from Addgene Plasmid #176271. SpCas9 was cloned from Addgene Plasmid #422302. Targeted sequences used in this study are available in Dataset S1. All primers used in this study are available in Dataset S2. MiniCasUltra sequence is available in SI Appendix, Table S2. MiniCasUltra-ABE sequence is available in SI Appendix, Table S3. pSSA construct is available in SI Appendix, Table S4.

## Cell Culture and Transfection.
NIH/3T3 and HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were trypsinized and plated in 24-well plates in appropriate concentrations 1 d before transfection (8 × 104 cells per well for NIH/3T3 and 1.5 × 105 cells per well for HEK293T). Then, 800 ng of total plasmids were transfected using PEI (Polysciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfected cells were harvested 48 h posttransfection for deep sequencing analyses or flow cytometry analyses.

## pSSA Gaussia Luciferase Reporter Assay.
Before transfection, 2.5 × 104 cells for HEK293T were seeded per well in a 96-well plate. Per well in a 96-well plate was transfected with a total of 200 ng DNA. Equal amounts of each construct were used for transfection. Fresh medium was changed 5 h after transfection, and cells were incubated for another 48 h before analysis. Gaussia readout was collected using Vigorous Biotechnology T004 kit by microplate reader.

## Transcriptional Activation Assay.
Before transfection, 2.5 × 104 cells for HEK293T were seeded per well in a 96-well plate. Per well of 96-well plate was transfected with a total amount of 200 ng DNA. Each plasmid, containing target-miniP2-Gaussia, dCasVP64(V)P65(P)HSF1(H) and targeting sgRNA, was transfected in equal amount. A fresh medium was changed 5 h after transfection and cells were incubated for another 48 h before analysis. Gaussia luciferase readout was collected using Vigorous Biotechnology T004 kit by a microplate reader.

## Flow Cytometry Analyses.
GFP-positive cells were analyzed using Calibur cell analyzer (BD Coulter) to evaluate gene-editing efficiency by flow cytometry analyses. The Fluorescein isothiocyanate (GFP) channel was used without compensation. A minimum of 10,000 cells from each sample were assessed.

## Indel Efficiency Analysis.
Genomic DNA was extracted using AllPure Cell Micro Kit (Magen Biotech). Target sites were PCR-amplified using KOD-Plus-Neo Polymerase (TOYOBO). The PCR products were subjected to sequencing on Illumina platforms (PE150 by Annoroad). The data were analyzed using CRISPResso2 ( ).

## PEM-seq Analysis.
PEM-seq in HEK293T cells was conducted as previously reported ( , ). Plasmids encoding CRISPR components targeting the NLRC4 site were delivered into HEK293T cells using PEI. 72 h later, cells were collected for genomic DNA isolation. A total of 20 μg of genomic DNA was sheared into fragmented ranging from 300 to 700 bp. DNA fragments were biotin-labeled. Then, primers were eliminated, and the fragments were purified. A bridge adapter was then joined to the single-stranded DNA bound to streptavidin beads. Nested PCR was carried out to amplify DNA fragment harboring the bait DSB and incorporate illumine adapter sequences. The constructed sequencing library was sequenced using a Hiseq 2500 platform configured for a 2 × 150 bp run. Genomic DNA of the mouse liver was extracted and performed PEM-seq following the above procedures.

## GUIDE-seq Analysis.
HEK293T cells or Hepa1-6 cells (2 × 105) were transfected with 1 μg CRISPR plasmids and 10 pmol of preannealed GUIDE-seq oligonucleotides by Lonza 4d nucleofector, followed by culturing in 12-well plates. Cells were treated with puromycin for selection and harvested 72 h after transfection. Genomic DNA was isolated and the target sites were amplified using nested PCR, followed by purification with the Gel DNA Recovery Kit (ZYMO Research) for deep sequencing. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using two-tailed, paired Student’s t tests, while ANOVAs were employed for comparisons involving multiple groups.

## AAV Package and Dilution.
As previously described in the literature ( ), AAV9 and AAV8 were produced by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with pAAV-Helper and pAAV-Rep-Cap. Once collected, the viral particles were purified using a GE Healthcare Biosciences heparin column and concentrated with an Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter unit with a molecular weight cutoff of 100,000. qPCR was applied to quantify the viral particle titers.

## Mice and Intravenous Injection.
All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Animal Care and Use Committee at Shenzhen University, Shenzhen, China. The mice were kept in a controlled environment maintained at 23 °C with 55 ± 5% humidity and a 12-h light– dark cycle. Specifically pathogen-free (SPF)-grade C57BL/6J male mice (aged 6 wk, 22 ± 1 g) were restrained in a rodent restrainer (KW-XXY, KEW BASIS), their tails were warmed using a heat lamp and disinfected by 70% ethanol, and 200 μL of AAV8 (1 × 1013GC/mL, 2 × 1012GC/mouse) or 200 μL PBS was administered through the tail vein of each mouse. All mice survived the procedure.

## Histology and Serum Analysis.
Blood collection was performed via the orbital vein, with a maximum volume of 200 μL collected per mouse. The blood was left to coagulate at room temperature, followed by centrifugation to separate the serum, which was then stored at −20 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis. Mice were killed through carbon dioxide inhalation in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. After the mice were killed, cardiac perfusion was conducted using 30 mL of PBS to remove residual blood from the circulatory system. Liver tissue was subsequently harvested, with the left lobe preserved in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and the remaining lobes snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C for future DNA and protein extraction. For histological evaluation, liver samples fixed in formalin were embedded in paraffin, sliced into section, and stained with H&E for microscopic examination. Serum AST and ALT levels were quantified using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS-420), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## ELISA for Liver Protein.
Liver tissue was mechanically homogenized in precooled PBS to prepare tissue lysates. The homogenate was then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for protein quantification. Total protein concentration was determined using the OmniRapid™ Protein Assay Kit (ZJ103, Epizyme Biotech), and protein concentrations were adjusted to 1.5 µg/mL for subsequent analyses. PTEN levels in the liver homogenate were quantified by ELISA based on the manufacturer’s instructions for the Mouse PTEN (JL49300, JONLNBIO). FAH levels were assessed by ELISA using the Mouse FAH ELISA Kit (BR5212924, Bioleaper).

## Mice and Subretinal Injection.
The humanized-VEGFA mice (6 to 8 wk old; male; Cat. NO. NM-HU-2000028) purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. were housed in standard conditions with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, at a temperature of 23 °C and 55% ± 5% humidity. They were provided with ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital, Fudan University, in compliance with the guidelines established by the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) (Number: IRBEENT-20210301b). The mice were randomly allocated into three groups. Prior to the injections, bupivacaine hydrochloride and a moisturizer were topically applied to suppress local reflexes. Subsequently, the mice were transferred to a microscopy setup where a coverslip was affixed to the cornea for stabilization. Using tweezers to secure the eyeball, an initial incision was created behind the limbus, followed by the insertion of a needle carrying the prescribed medication at a 60° angle. The injection was deemed complete upon visual confirmation of the needle penetrating the retina, and 2 μL AAV9 (2.904E+13 vg/mL) or PBS was injected. Following a 6-wk duration, laser-induced CNV was induced in the mice to proceed with the investigation.

## Laser-Induced CNV.
The mice were anesthetized using avertin, and their pupils were dilated with a 1% tropicamide solution. Once the pupils had fully dilated after approximately 2 min, Carbomer was applied to the cornea to maintain moisture. Subsequently, four rounds of laser photocoagulation were performed around the optic nerve in each eye, preventing the primary retinal blood vessels. The laser parameters used were as follows: a wavelength of 532 nm, power of 150 mW, duration of 50 ms, and a fixed diameter of 50 μm. The appearance of bubbles during the laser photocoagulation procedure indicated the rupture of the Bruch membrane.

## FFA.
One week after establishing the CNV mice model, real-time FFA images were captured following the intraperitoneal injection with 20 μL of a 10% sodium fluorescein solution (Alcon). The CNV leakage was assessed using a previously validated methodology, with grading criteria established based on dynamic FFA images: Grade I indicated no hyperfluorescence, Grade II required hyperfluorescence without leakage, Grade III necessitated hyperfluorescence in early or mid-transit images along with late leakage, and Grade IV denoted intense hyperfluorescence in early or mid-transit with late leakage extending beyond the borders of the treated area.

## OCT.
OCT images were obtained by using Spectralis system (Heidelberg, Germany). The spindle-shaped hyperreflective areas correspond to CNV lesions. The width and height of the lesion were determined by measuring a scan passing through the center of the lesion. The long axis of the lesion aligns with the level of the Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).

## ERG.
Before conducting the ERG recording, all mice were acclimated to the dark for 12 h. Following anesthesia, the mice’s eyes were dilated, and the ground electrode was placed under the skin of the thigh while the reference electrode was positioned on the nose following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Using a set of 3 mm gold S9 ring electrodes placed on the cornea, ERG responses were stimulated and recorded using the Espion E3 device (Diagnosys LLC). During dark adaptation, responses from rod and mixed cone/rod cells were evoked by 10 cd*s/m2 flashes. The recorded signals were averaged for analysis, and the amplitudes were determined using the Espion E3 instrument (Diagnosys LLC).

## Choroidal-Retinal Flat Mount Preparation and Immunostaining.
After undergoing laser photocoagulation for a week, the male VEGFA mice were anesthetized with Avertin. Their eyeballs were then removed and promptly frozen in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. Following three rinses in PBS, the RPE–choroid–sclera complex was subjected to four radial incisions and subsequently placed in 5% BSA + 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h. The tissue was then incubated with isolectin IB4 (Alexa Fluor™ 568 isolectin GS-IB4 conjugate, Cat NO. I21412, Invitrogen) (1:50) at 4 °C overnight, rinsed, mounted flat, and covered. Flat mounts were imaged using a Zeiss Aiox506 fluorescence microscope with 488 nm excitation wavelengths at ×5 magnification. The proportional CNV area [(CNV area)/(total choroid area)] was calculated using ImageJ, with IB4 images serving as quantitative references.

## ELISA for Quantification of VEGFA Protein.
Whole eyes were harvested 6 wk postAAV injection for ELISA analysis. Each entire eyeball was placed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing two grinding beads (SUS304, Diameter = 2 mm) and 200 µL of lysis buffer. The samples were homogenized using a Tissue Homogenizer (XFSTPRP-CLN, Jingxin) at 4 °C and 50 Hz for 300 s. After centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, the protein was extracted from the supernatant. VEGFA protein levels were quantified using a Human VEGFA ELISA Kit (ab119566, Abcam) in accordance with the manufacturer’s assay protocol.
