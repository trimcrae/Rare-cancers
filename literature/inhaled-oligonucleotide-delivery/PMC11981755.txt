## Abstract
This study aimed to develop and evaluate a CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG ODN)-adjuvanted trivalent inactivated Salmonella vaccine including S. enterica subsp. Enterica serovar Typhimurium, Salmonella enterica subsp. Enterica serovar Enteritidis, and Salmonella enterica serotype Infantis, for its immunogenic efficacy in chickens. The immunomodulatory effects of various CpG ODNs were assessed based on proinflammatory cytokine secretion and the expression levels of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II in the chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40. According to the results, CpG ODNs D35 3CG PO, D35 3CG MB, 1466 Acore PO, 1466 Acore MB, and K3 which exhibited non-cytotoxicity in both HD11 and DT40 cell lines, were selected for vaccine formulation. To evaluate their effects under in vivo conditions, chicks (n = 25) were randomly assigned to fourteen groups (G1: only sterile pyrogen-free saline solution, G2: only inactivated vaccine, G3: inactivated vaccine with 150 mg/dose of ALUM, G4: commercial Salenvac T vaccine, G5-G14: various experimental vaccine formulations which included different CpG ODNs combined with inactivated bacterial strains, with or without ALUM). Immune responses were analyzed through serological assays for antigen-specific antibody titers and ex vivo splenocyte cultures for cytokine secretion. Flow cytometry was performed to assess T-cell activation and IFN-γ production. The results demonstrated that the CpG ODNs-adjuvanted vaccine formulations significantly enhanced both humoral and cellular immunity compared to the commercial vaccine. Specifically, the Vac#5+ ALUM formulation, which included the K3 CpG ODN, induced robust antibody responses against Salmonella antigens and significantly increased IFN-γ secretion, nearly two-fold higher than the commercial vaccine. This effect was primarily mediated by CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. These findings highlight the potential of CpG ODNs as effective vaccine adjuvants in poultry. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the use of CpG ODNs as adjuvants in inactivated Salmonella vaccine formulations. Future studies should focus on evaluating the long-term protective efficacy of this vaccine formulation and its ability to provide cross-protection against a broader spectrum of Salmonella serovars.

## Introduction
Salmonella is one of the most significant foodborne pathogens and remains a global public health concern ( ). Worldwide, Salmonella infections are estimated to cause approximately 93 million cases of gastroenteritis and 155,000 deaths annually ( ). In the United States alone, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that Salmonella enterica serovars account for approximately 1.4 million cases of foodborne illness and 420 associated deaths each year ( ). Among the Salmonella enterica serovars classified as non-typhoidal pathogens, S. Typhimurium (ST), S. Enteritidis (SE), and S. Infantis (SI) are the most prevalent, being responsible for the majority of Salmonella infections. Notably, SE and ST serovars are implicated in 17.4 % and 34 % of poultry-related Salmonella infections, respectively ( ). While various transmission vehicles exist, poultry and poultry products are considered the primary reservoirs. Furthermore, Salmonella infections can contribute to the persistence of environmental contamination through asymptomatic carriers, leading to cross-contamination of carcasses and subsequent human infections ( ).
Over the past few decades, vaccination programs have been regarded as crucial for reducing Salmonella contamination in poultry and poultry products, thereby supporting modern poultry production and ensuring its sustainability ( ). Vaccination strategies effectively limit the spread of specific Salmonella serotypes by enhancing the immune resistance of poultry against Salmonella exposure. Consequently, vaccination emerges as a reliable intervention method, contributing to Salmonella-free poultry and poultry products, reducing environmental contamination, and indirectly minimizing the risk of Salmonella-related foodborne illnesses ( ). Multiple vaccines have been developed over the years to combat avian salmonellosis. Commercially available Salmonella vaccines for poultry can be broadly categorized into killed or inactivated vaccines and live attenuated vaccines ( ). Nobilis® Salenvac T (Merck Animal Health, France) is a bivalent inactivated vaccine containing of S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis strains, which demonstrates excellent protective efficacy against both infections in chickens following multiple immunizations. These killed, or inactivated vaccines are generally preferred due to their safety, as they do not pose the risk of reverting to a virulent strain ( ). However, these vaccines primarily stimulate humoral immunity and are less effective at eliciting strong cell-mediated immune responses (Sing, 2009). In contrast, live attenuated vaccines can induce both cell-mediated and humoral immune responses and typically do not require boosters. Several commercial attenuated S. Typhimurium vaccines are available, including AviPro® Megan® Vac 1 (Lohmann, Germany). POULVAC® ST (Zoetis, USA), a modified-live S. Typhimurium vaccine, provides a high-level protection against S. Typhimurium infections and a cross-protection against S. Heidelberg through spray or oral administration. AviPro Salmonella Duo (Elanco, United Kingdom) is a live freeze-dried vaccine containing two attenuated strains, S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium. Despite their effectiveness, a major concern with live attenuated Salmonella vaccines is the potential for the vaccine strain to revert to its virulent form ( ). Therefore, vaccination strategies in poultry still requires adjuvants and booster immunization strategies for optimal efficacy ( ).
Adjuvants play a pivotal role in vaccine formulations by providing co-stimulatory signals that enhance the immunogenicity of antigens ( ). Aluminum salts are the most widely used adjuvants in the vaccine industry. Although these adjuvants can elicit a strong antibody response, they are ineffective in triggering cellular immunity or inducing immune responses at mucosal surfaces ( ). Therefore, there has been growing interest in the identification of novel adjuvants. Among the promising candidates, oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs) containing unmethylated cytosine–guanine dinucleotide (CpG) motifs have emerged as a potent option. At least four distinct types of CpG ODNs exist, each characterized by unique backbones, sequences, and immunostimulatory properties ( ). D-type CpG ODNs typically consist of a single palindromic CpG motif with a phosphodiester (PO) backbone which are susceptible to nuclease degradation, and a phosphorothioate (PS) poly(G) tail which is modified with a replacement of non-bridging oxygen with sulfur in phosphate linkages to prepare the nuclease-resistant forms. Besides, Mixed Backbone (MB) ODNs have a combination of both PO and PS linkages in their backbone which allows for the benefits of both stability (from PS) and a more natural backbone (from PO). D-type CpG ODNs primarily activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) to produce significant amounts of IFN-α. However, they do not induce pDC maturation or B-cell activation. In contrast, K-type CpG ODNs which also have a PS backbone, contain nonpalindromic multiple CpG motifs, strongly activate B cells to produce IL-6, as well as stimulate pDC maturation. However, they induce minimal IFN-α production (Verthelyi et al., 2009). C- and P-type CpG ODNs which contain one and two palindromic CpG sequences, activate B cells similar to K-type ODNs and pDCs similar to D-type ODNs ( ). In avian species, ODNs with unmethylated CpG motifs mimic the immunostimulatory properties of viral or microbial DNA through the activation of toll-like receptor (TLR) 21 ( ). Previous studies using chicken models have investigated the immunoprotective effects of CpG-ODN against Escherichia coli
 ) and S. Typhimurium infections (Taghavi, et al. 2018). Further research has demonstrated that CpG-ODN treatment accelerates immune development by enriching immunological niches in chicks ( , , ). Therefore, CpG-ODNs, with their various CpG motifs, hold significant potential as vaccine adjuvants and immunotherapeutic agents for combating infectious diseases ( ). Despite this potential, the ability of CpG-ODNs to enhance immune responses in poultry remains underexplored.
The objective of this study is to develop a novel inactivated trivalent Salmonella vaccine adjuvanted with CpG ODNs for stimulating innate immune responses in poultry. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to combine inactivated triple Salmonella strains with CpG ODNs. This approach aims to present a new vaccine formulation that enhances immune responses in poultry.

## Materials and methods

## Bacterial strains and media
Salmonella enterica subsp. Enterica serovar Typhimurium (ST, ATCC 700408), Salmonella enterica subsp. Enterica serovar Enteritidis (SE, ATCC 25928), and Salmonella enterica serotype Infantis (SI, ATCC MZ1479) were utilized for vaccine formulation and cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) medium (BD, USA) at 37°C for 24 h. The cultures were stored as frozen stocks in LB broth containing 80 % glycerol at −80°C until use.

## Preparation of trivalent inactivated Salmonella vaccine
SE, ST, and SI were cultured separately in iron-restricted broth medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL iron chelator 2,2′-bipyridine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to induce expression of the antigenic determinants ( ). After incubation at 37°C for 24 h, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (4.000×g for 10 min). The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with physiological saline and resuspended. Prior to inactivation, each cell suspension was serially diluted and pour-plated onto LB agar plates. After incubation, colonies were counted and expressed as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFUs/mL). Each bacterial strain was adjusted to the final concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/dose and incubated at 60°C for 90 min in a water bath ( ). To verify complete inactivation, cell suspensions were cultured on LB broth and agar plates and incubated for 48 h. Finally, a cocktail of the strains was prepared by mixing equal volumes of each inactivated cell suspension, both with and without adjuvants. Nobilis Salenvac T (MSD Animal Health; batch S376ZA), a commercially available inactivated and aluminum hydroxide-gel adjuvanted bacterin containing SE (strain PT4) and ST (strain DT104) grown under iron-restricted conditions, was used as the control vaccine.

## Cell culture studies
To evaluate the immune stimulatory activity of the 22 CpG ODNs (Alpha DNA, Canada) listed in , chicken macrophage HD‐11 and B‐lymphocyte DT40 cells were used. HD-11 cell line was generously provided Prof. Dr. Ihsan Gursel (THORLAB, IBG, Izmir, Turkey) and cultured in high glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 4 mM l-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), 1 % non-essential amino acid (Gibco, USA), 5 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 5 % chicken serum (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). DT-40 cells isolated from the bursa of Fabricius of chicken, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC CRL-2111) and cultured in the same medium with the addition of 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). All cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 and 95 % relative humidity ( ). The culture medium was changed regularly. Once cells reached 80 % confluence in a 25 cm² culture flask, they were rinsed with 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, UK), and harvested by treatment with 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After centrifugation at 200×g for 5 min, cells were stained with trypan blue (Merck Millipore, Germany) and counted by an Automated Cell Counter (TC20, Biorad, USA).
 

## Measurement of immunological markers
HD-11 and DT40 cells were sub-cultured at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in 96-well tissue culture plates (Sarstedt, Germany) and stimulated with CpG ODNs (1 µM) and peptidoglycan from Staphylococcus aureus (PGN, 5 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. After incubation, the concentration of chicken proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines including interleukin (IL)−1β (Creative Biolabs, USA), IL-6 (Kingfisher Biotech Inc., USA), IL-12p40 (Kingfisher Biotech Inc., USA), IFN-interferon (IFN)-γ (Mabtech, Sweden), and IFN-α (Mabtech, Sweden) in cell supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.
Markers of immune activation, including CD80, CD86, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II were analyzed by flow cytometry. Following stimulation, cells were washed with 1X PBS and resuspended in 100 µL of a FC buffer containing 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.05 % Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 1X PBS. Antibodies against CD80 (PE, Bio-Rad, USA), CD86 (APC, Bio-Rad, USA), and MHC class II (FITC, Bio-Rad, USA) were added at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL and incubated for 30 min. The stained cells were subsequently analyzed using an Agilent NovoCyte 2006R flow cytometer (USA). In all experiments, K23 flip, along with its active ODN, K23, was used as a control.

## Cell viability assay
The effects of adjuvants, selected based on their immune activation capacity, and inactivated bacteria on the proliferation and viability of HD-11 and DT40 cells were also determined. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and incubated with either CpG ODNs (1 µM) alone or the respective vaccine formulations containing inactivated bacteria. After 24 h of incubation, cell proliferation was assessed using the SRB Assay Kit (Abcam, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Additionally, apoptosis was evaluated by Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, USA) and analyzed with an Agilent NovoCyte 2006R flow cytometer. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 1 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) used as the positive control.

## In vivo studies

## Ethical statement
This study was conducted at the Experimental Poultry Facility of Ankara University, Ankara, Turkey. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ankara University (2021-16-142) and carried out in compliance with the current legislation of the Ministry of Agriculture of Turkey.

## Animals, housing, and experimental design
A total of 350, one-day-old male Ross 308, broiler chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery. Upon arrival, the chicks were randomly allocated to 14 floor pens. Each bird was individually weighed and wing-tagged at initial placement. The chicks were raised in a controlled environment under commercial standards for 42 d. Starter, grower, and finisher diets based on corn-soybean meal were provided from 0 to 10, 11 to 24, and 25-42 d of age, respectively. All diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient recommendations outlined by Aviagen (2022). Each pen was equipped with a bucket feeder and a nipple drinker line. Water and experimental diets (in mash form) were provided ad libitum. Broiler chickens were weighed individually, and body weight (BW) was recorded at weekly intervals. Mortality was monitored and documented.
The experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with recommended vaccination schedule of the commercial vaccine as outlined in . On 0 d, chicks were separated and randomly assigned to fourteen groups (G1-G14), each consisting of 25 animals. On 7 d, the first vaccination was administered. Briefly, G1 received only sterile pyrogen-free saline solution as the negative control. G2 was administered the inactivated vaccine containing SE + ST + SI at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/dose for each bacterial strain, which is the same concentration used for Salenvac T. G3 received the inactivated vaccine formulated with ALUM (150 mg/dose). G4 was designated as the control group and received the commercial Salenvac T vaccine. G5-G14 were administered various experimental vaccine formulations (D35 3CG PO for Vac#1, D35 3CG MB for Vac#2, 1466 Acore PO for Vac#3, 1466 Acore MB for Vac#4, and K3 for Vac#5) which included different CpG ODNs (20 µg/dose) combined with inactivated bacterial strains (SE, ST, and SI), with or without ALUM, via intramuscular injection. Blood samples were collected, and a booster dose was administered on d 21. On 35 d, the second set of the blood samples were collected. The experimental cycle was finalized with euthanasia of the chickens via carbon dioxide inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation on d 42.
 

## Bacteriological assays
The Salmonella-free status of the chickens during the experimental procedures was verified using bacteriological culturing, following the protocol described by . Each week, cloacal swabs were randomly collected from ten chickens per group and suspended in Tetrathionate broth (TB) supplemented with Brilliant Green (Oxoid, Germany). The samples were incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Following incubation, the tubes were vortexed and subcultured onto XLD agar (Difco, USA) supplemented with 0.46 % Tergitol 4 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (XLDT4). The presence of Salmonella spp. was assessed after the incubation period.

## Measurement of serum IgY levels in chickens
The levels of anti-Salmonella IgY in the serum were quantified using an ELISA assay. Briefly, high protein binding 96-well plates were coated with either a mixed bacterial suspension of SE + ST + SI at a concentration of 3 × 107 CFU/mL, or with SI alone at 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. Following incubation at 37°C for 2 h, the plates were washed three times for 5 min with washing buffer (0.5 % Tween 20 in PBS), and blocked with 200 μL blocking solution (5 % BSA and 0.05 % Tween 20 in PBS) at 37°C for 2 h to prevent non-specific binding. Subsequently, serially diluted serum samples were added to the plates to capture Salmonella-reactive IgY antibodies. After three additional washes, alkaline phosphatase-conjugated anti-IgY antibodies (Mabtech, Sweden) were added, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Substrate was then added to initiate the enzymatic reaction, which was stopped by the addition of 0.5 M H₂SO₄. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader, and IgY concentrations were determined by comparison with a standard curve.

## Ex vivo studies
Three weeks after the booster vaccination, the spleens were aseptically removed from the chickens and crushed with the back of a syringe in 3 mL of culture media. The resulting splenocyte-containing media was centrifugated at 400×g for 5 min, after which the splenocytes were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in culture media. For the analysis of the cellular immune response, splenocytes were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 4 × 106 cells per well and incubated with 2 × 106 CFU/mL inactivated bacteria in each well for 72 h. During the last 4 h of stimulation, a Cell Stimulation Cocktail containing Brefeldin A (Cytek, Netherlands) was added according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, the cells were stained with antibodies specific to CD45 (SPRD), CD3 (FITC), CD4 (PE/Cy7), CD8 (APC) (Southern Biotech, USA), and IFN-γ (PE) (Mabtech, Sweden) at a concentration of 10 µg/mL. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using an Agilent NovoCyte 2006R flow cytometer. During analysis of the data acquired to eliminate non-specific binding of antibodies isotype controls with the same conjugated fluorochrome as used. Additionally, the levels of IgY and IFN-γ in the cell supernatants were quantified by ELISA, as previously described.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences between treatment groups.

## Results

## CpG ODNs selected for in vivo studies
The immunomodulatory effects of 22 CpG ODNs were evaluated based on proinflammatory cytokine secretion and the expression levels of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II in the chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40. After 24 h of CpG ODN stimulation, proinflammatory cytokine secretion from HD11 (IL-6, IL-12 p40, IL-1β, and IFN-γ) and DT40 (IL-6, IL-8, IL-12 p40, and IFN-α) cells was quantified using ELISA. Among the 22 CpG ODNs tested, 1466 pG6 PS significantly enhanced the IL-6 synthesis of HD11 cells compared to PGN (p < 0.0001). Additionally, D35 3CG MB induced the highest IL-12 p40 secretion, outperforming all other CpG ODNs when compared to PGN (p < 0.0001) ( A). Furthermore, stimulation with 1466 Acore PO and K3 resulted in increased IFN-γ levels (p < 0.05). When the results for DT40 cells were evaluated ( B), it was observed that K3 significantly increased IL-6 and IL-8 synthesis (p < 0.0001), while IL-12 p40 production was most strongly stimulated by1466 pG6 PS compared to PGN (p < 0.0001). Taken together, a rating system was used to select the most effective CpG ODNs, based on comparison with PGN data: [(1): cytokine secretion level equal to PGN; (2): 1-1.5-fold increase in cytokine secretion compared to PGN; (3): >1.5-fold increase in cytokine secretion compared to PGN]. Based on this rating system, CpG ODNs D35 3CG PO, D35 3CG MB, 1466 Acore PO, 1466 Acore MB, 1466 pG6 PS, and K3, which exhibited more effective immunomodulatory effects with a score of 3 compared to PGN, were selected for further analysis.
 Subsequently, the expression levels of MHC Class II, CD80, and CD86 in response to selected CpG ODNs was determined in chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40 using flow cytometry. The gating strategy for this analysis was presented in C. Among the CpG ODNs selected, K3 displayed a more pronounced effect compared to PGN in both cell lines. D35 3CG PO, D35 3CG MB, and 1466 Acore MB also significantly increased MHC Class II and CD80 (ICAM-1) expression on the surface of HD 11 cells (p < 0.05) ( D). Additionally, D35 3CG PO and D35 3CG MB induced the expression of MHC Class II and CD80 in DT40 cells (p < 0.05) ( E). Interestingly, CD86 expression was generally reduced following CpG ODN stimulation in both cell lines. Overall, CpG ODNs D35 3CG PO, D35 3CG MB, 1466 Acore PO, 1466 Acore MB, and K3 were selected for vaccine formulation due to their effective immunomodulatory effects on the chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40.

## Effects of selected CpG ODNs on HD11 and DT40 chicken cell viability
The cytotoxicity of selected CpG ODNs on chicken cells, HD11 and DT40, was assessed using apoptosis and cell proliferation (SRB) assays. Flow cytometry analysis following Annexin V and PI staining ( A) indicated that none of the CpG ODNs induced apoptosis. In contrast, H₂O₂, used as a positive control, induced significant levels of apoptosis in both HD11 and DT40 cells. Additionally, the assay was performed with CpG ODN formulations combined with inactivated bacteria to determine whether a potential vaccine combination could have adverse effects on immune cells. Our results demonstrated that neither CpG ODNs nor their combinations with inactivated bacteria induced apoptotic effects on chicken cells, at least under in vitro conditions ( B, C). Similarly, no cellular toxicity of selected CpG ODNs on chicken cells was observed in SRB assay. However, the combination of inactivated bacteria with CpG ODNs significantly enhanced the proliferation of HD11 and DT40 cells compared to the CpG ODN-only groups (p < 0.0001) ( D, E).
 

## Effects of trivalent inactivated Salmonella vaccines formulated with CpG ODNs under in vivo conditions

## Mortality, weight change, and bacterial contamination
Mortality and BWs were recorded on 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 d. The results indicated that no significant differences in BW or mortality rates were observed among the treatment groups during any of the time points or throughout the entire experimental period (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, Salmonella-free status was confirmed through microbiological assays. No colonies were detected on XLDT4 agar plates from individual cloacal swabs taken weekly from each group, indicating the absence of bacterial contamination.

## Humoral immune response induced by adjuvanted vaccines
The in vivo immunization study was conducted using inactivated bacteria (SE + ST + SI) combined with selected CpG ODNs formulations (D35 3CG PO for Vac#1, D35 3CG MB for Vac#2, 1466 Acore PO for Vac#3, 1466 Acore MB for Vac#4, and K3 for Vac#5) with or without ALUM. The results were compared with groups injected with sterile pyrogen-free saline solution (negative control) and vaccinated with Salenvac T (positive control). The humoral immune response induced by the vaccination was assessed by measuring anti-Salmonella antibody levels in the serum using ELISA after each bleeding. The OD values at the titration of 1:6250 were used to compare humoral immune response between groups. Following the primary injection, only Vac#5 + ALUM induced significantly higher antibody levels compared to the commercial vaccine (p < 0.005, A). Formulations Vac#2 + ALUM and Vac#3 + ALUM also significantly increased the antibody levels (p < 0.05). After the booster injection, all groups exhibited similar immunization levels to the commercial vaccine, except for Vac#5 + ALUM, which resulted in significantly higher antibody levels (p < 0.05, B). Similar results were observed between the second and third (final) bleedings ( C). Anti-SI antibody levels were subsequently determined by coating ELISA plates with inactivated SI. The results indicated that the Vac#2 + ALUM (p < 0.05), Vac#4 + ALUM (p < 0.05), and Vac#5 + ALUM (p < 0.0005) groups induced significantly higher anti-SI antibody levels compared to the commercial vaccine during the third bleeding ( D). Consistent results were observed for anti-Salmonella ( E) and anti-SI antibody ( F) secretion in the ex-vivo challenge assay.
 

## Cellular immune response mediated by CpG ODNs
As IFN-γ secretion from immune cells is considered a key indicator of the cellular immune response induced by vaccines, this study analyzed IFN-γ levels upon antigen challenge and identified the cellular source of the cytokine across vaccination groups. Splenocytes of each group were first challenged with inactivated trivalent bacteria or inactivated SI alone. Following co-incubation, the supernatants were collected and IFN-γ levels were assessed using sandwich ELISA. As shown in A, splenocytes from Vac#2 + ALUM (>1.5-fold) and Vac#5 + ALUM (>2-fold) groups secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-γ (p < 0.0001) upon recognition of SE + ST + SI antigens compared to the commercial vaccine. Non-ALUM formulation of Vac#5 also induced significantly higher IFN-γ levels (p < 0.05) than commercial vaccine. However, when splenocytes were challenged with only inactivated SI ( B), the commercial vaccine failed to elicit comparable IFN-γ levels relative to the CpG ODN-containing groups. Notably, all CpG ODN-containing groups, regardless of ALUM inclusion, significantly increased IFN-γ secretion. Among these, Vac#2 + ALUM and Vac#5 + ALUM emerged as the most potent formulations. To further examine the cellular immune response, antigen challenges were conducted with Brefeldin A to inhibit intracellular trafficking of IFN-γ. Splenocytes were subsequently stained for CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, and IFN-γ antigens and analyzed by flow cytometry. T lymphocytes secreting IFN-γ in response to SE + ST + SI or SI antigens alone were identified using the gating strategy shown in C. The results revealed that the Vac#5 + ALUM group elicited a significantly higher number of CD8+ T cells, or cytotoxic T cells, inducing IFN-γ secretion almost 2-fold higher than the commercial vaccine (p < 0.0001, D). Similarly, cytotoxic T cells were predominantly activated in all CpG ODN-containing groups when challenged with SI antigens compared to the commercial vaccine ( E). Taken together, Vac#5 + ALUM was identified as the most potent formulation, resulting in an 8-fold increase in cytotoxic T cells compared to the commercial vaccine. Comparable trends were also observed for CD4+ T cells ( F) and helper T cells ( G) in the Vac#5 + ALUM group.
 

## Discussion
Despite the established immunogenicity of current vaccines, several pathogens continue to impose significant economic burdens on the global poultry industry. To address these challenges, researchers have developed strategies to enhance the generation of cross-reactive antibodies and robust T-cell-mediated immune responses through vaccination. These strategies frequently include adjuvants, such as chemical agents, microbial components, or proteins, which enhance the immune response to vaccines ( ). However, many commercial and experimental adjuvants face limitations, including suboptimal efficacy, systemic toxicity, production complexities, instability, and high costs ( ). Therefore, there is a growing need for alternative adjuvants that can stimulate both humoral and cellular immune responses in vaccines, providing a foundation for improved disease control and economic sustainability ( ).
CpG ODNs as next-generation adjuvants, have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing humoral and cellular immune responses and are increasingly used in human vaccines. Notably, the synthetic CpG ODN 1018 which specifically activates TLR-9 induces robust Th1-type cellular responses and cytotoxic T cell activation by modulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons ( ). This adjuvant was initially approved for use in hepatitis B vaccines ( ) and has recently been used in the CpG ODN 1018-adjuvanted COVID-19 vaccine, named SCB-2019, evaluated for emergency use ( ). Similarly, CpG ODN 7909 has been employed in malaria vaccines, where phase I clinical trials have shown its dose-sparing effects in protein-based malaria vaccine formulations ( ). Another CpG ODN candidate, IC31, has undergone extensive evaluation in preclinical and clinical trials for various subunit vaccines ( ; ). These findings highlight CpG ODNs as promising adjuvants for clinical translation in human vaccine development ( ). In poultry, CpG ODNs have also demonstrated immunostimulatory effects against E. coli ( ; , ), Salmonella Typhimurium ( ; ), and Campylobacter spp. ( ) by enhancing cytokine production and activating cytotoxic T cells. These effects are mediated through the activation of avian-specific TLR-21, positioning CpG ODNs as valuable tools for infection control in poultry ( ). Despite these advances, the role of CpG ODNs as adjuvants in poultry vaccine formulations remains poorly understood. This study investigates the immunological effects of a trivalent inactivated Salmonella vaccine formulated with CpG ODNs, with and without ALUM, on cellular and humoral immune responses in poultry.
Within the aim of this study, firstly, the immunomodulatory effects of 22 CpG ODNs were evaluated based on their ability to stimulate proinflammatory cytokine secretion and upregulate the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II in the chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40. The results revealed diverse immunological responses depending on the specific class of CpG ODN used. These findings align with previous studies, which have demonstrated that chicken macrophages respond differently to CpG ODNs in a sequence-specific manner ( ). For instance, K3, a synthetic class B CpG ODN, strongly stimulate the production of IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 while inducing lower levels of IFN-α compared to class A CpG ODNs (D-type) ( ). Consistent with our observations, it was also reported that class B CpG ODNs increased the mRNA expression of IL-1β and IL-6 in chicken macrophages ( ) but did not significantly induce IFN-α production ( ). Furthermore, we determined that the selected CpG ODNs activated the chicken cells and upregulate the expression of CD80, CD86 and MHC-II while class B CpG ODN K3 only up-regulated CD86. These molecules, which function as co-stimulatory markers critical for antigen presentation to the adaptive immune system, also serve as indicators of macrophage activation. Thus, our findings demonstrated that stimulation with selected CpG ODNs, acting as TLR21 ligands, led to macrophage activation, as evidenced by increased surface expression of CD80 and CD86 and the corresponding elevation in proinflammatory cytokine levels ( ). Moreover, to assess the safety profile of different CpG-ODN formulations, cellular toxicity studies were performed. Our results showed that the selected CpG ODNs did not exhibit cytotoxicity in the chicken cell lines HD11 and DT40. In fact, they significantly promoted the proliferation of both cells line. This finding aligns with the results of . They demonstrated that CpG-A1585, CpG-B1555, CpG-BK3, and CpG-C2395, except for CpG-AD35, significantly increased the chicken cell proliferation index of chicken cells compared to PBS treatment, suggesting enhanced mitogenic activity. In contrast, Xie et al. (2004) observed that ODN 2006 treatment arrested HD11 cell growth in a dose-dependent manner.
Subsequently, we investigated the humoral immune responses induced by various injection groups by measuring anti-Salmonella antibody levels in the serum using ELISA after each bleeding. Our results demonstrated that Vac#5 + ALUM elicited significantly higher antibody levels compared to the commercial vaccine following the primary injection, demonstrating its ability to induce a more rapid immune response. This early increase in antibody titers suggests that Vac#5 + ALUM may provide early-stage protection, which is critical for preventing pathogen establishment during the initial stages of exposure. Similar to our results, evaluated a new, cochleate-based, trivalent injectable vaccine against S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium and S. Infantis. They described the vaccine's safety profile and its ability to induce specific anti-Salmonella serovar IgY antibodies in chickens. In our study, the ELISA results also revealed that Vac#5 + ALUM induced significantly higher antibody titers against all three Salmonella strains compared to the commercial vaccine, with the most pronounced difference observed for SI. While the commercial vaccine exhibited minimal antibody response against SI, Vac#5 + ALUM generated a robust humoral immune response, highlighting its superior immunogenicity against this serotype. The inclusion of SI in our formulation induced immunity against one of the most prevalent strains that the commercial vaccine fails to cover, thereby increasing the overall efficacy of our vaccine and providing enhanced protection against Salmonella infections in poultry.
CpG ODNs have been shown to elicit robust IFN-γ production and cytotoxic T-cell activation, both of which are essential for long-term immunity against intracellular pathogens such as Salmonella. Both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses have been implicated in the protective immune response to Salmonella infections in chickens (Plym et al., 2007). Systemic Salmonella-specific antibodies generated post-vaccination play a pivotal role in controlling the early stages of infection by enhancing bactericidal activity prior to the pathogen's entry into host cells ( ). In our study, the formulation Vac#5 + ALUM exhibited superior efficacy, inducing significantly higher levels of IFN-γ and activating both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations compared to the commercial vaccine Salenvac T. Similar to our results, recently reported that an increased IFN-γ production and CD4+/CD8+ ratio in vaccinated birds challenged with three different Salmonella serovars. Considering that CD4+ T cells orchestrate cytokine production, including IFN-γ, and initiate the immune response by activating other immune cells such as NK cells, macrophages, and CD8+ T cells, the increase in the CD4+/CD8+ ratio following vaccination indicates a strengthened immune response ( ). Taken together, Vac#5 + ALUM demonstrated the ability to elicit both robust humoral and cellular immune responses, resulting in elevated antibody titers and enhanced T-cell activation, particularly against SI. This combination of immediate antibody-mediated protection and sustained T-cell immunity highlights the potential of our vaccine formulation. Notably, the findings highlight the potential of CpG ODNs to overcome the limitations of traditional aluminum-based adjuvants, which primarily enhance humoral immunity but are less effective in stimulating robust cellular responses. These results underline the promise of CpG ODNs as advanced adjuvants for developing vaccines with comprehensive immunogenicity against Salmonella infections.

## Conclusion
Developing adjuvants specifically tailored for poultry vaccines is crucial to addressing current limitations such as stability, cost, and the requirement for cross-protection against diverse pathogens. Ongoing research into innovative adjuvant combinations and formulations remains vital for overcoming these challenges and advancing the management of poultry health. This study provides robust evidence supporting the inclusion of CpG ODNs as effective adjuvants in trivalent inactivated Salmonella vaccines. Notably, the Vac#5 + ALUM formulation, incorporating K3 CpG ODN, demonstrated remarkable potential as an advanced vaccine strategy in poultry. This formulation significantly enhanced both humoral and cellular immune responses, notably increasing antibody production and T-cell activation, which are key components for robust protection against Salmonella infections. Future investigations should focus on assessing the long-term protective efficacy of this vaccine formulation and evaluating its capacity to confer cross-protection against a broader spectrum of Salmonella serovars. Such studies will be instrumental in determining its feasibility for large-scale application in the poultry industry. Ultimately, this could lead to significant advancements in public health by mitigating the risk of zoonotic Salmonella transmission and enhancing food safety standards globally.
