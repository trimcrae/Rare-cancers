## INTRODUCTION
Circadian rhythm is an autonomous and self-sustainable variation that governs extensive biological processes in anticipation of solar cycles. These rhythms are primarily driven by a biological clock in the hypothalamus, an internal pacemaker comprising a transcriptional-translational feedback loop that orchestrates organismal metabolism, physiology, and behavior ( ). The circadian clock is also expressed in peripheral organs, wherein the timekeeping system engages in cyclic genomic programs in a cell type– and tissue-specific manner ( ). The central clock conveys temporal information to the peripheral clock through hormones, the autonomic nervous system, and body temperature, thereby establishing a phase alignment among the clocks in each tissue. Peripheral clocks are also subject to food, exercise, and hypoxia, among other cues, and thus can be uncoupled by mistimed environmental perturbations.
Salt handling and blood pressure (BP) exhibit daily physiological fluctuations. For example, the increase in food and water intake during the dark phase is synchronized with increased urine volume and urinary sodium and potassium abundance ( ). Circadian rhythms of renal sodium excretion are attenuated in systemic Clock knockout mice ( ). Per1 regulates the expression of renal epithelial sodium channels in a serum and glucocorticoid-inducible kinase 1 (Sgk1)–independent manner ( ). Supporting this notion, Per1 knockout mice exhibit reduced BP ( ). These findings underscore the importance of the kidney clock in regulating renal salt handling and BP. While salt excretion through the kidney under the finely tuned regulation of endocrine and neuronal factors is central to dietary salt handling, the intestine also plays an important role in salt homeostasis, as it is the primary site where dietary salt is absorbed into the systemic circulation ( ). However, it remains unclear whether the gut clock directs the temporal patterns of dietary salt absorption and BP. Moreover, while the circadian misalignment of feeding rhythms with the light-dark cycle causes obesity and phase uncoupling of peripheral clocks, it remains unclear whether the desynchronization of salt intake with photic rhythms influences daily sodium handling and BP variations through the peripheral clocks ( – ).
Endocrine sensing of the extracellular fluid volume affects salt handling and BP regulation. One such hormonal pathway is the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), the activities and concentrations of which display diurnal variations ( ). The genomic action of aldosterone, such as the Sgk1 and ENaC gene induction, is mediated by the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) ( ). The functional contribution of the intestinal MR to sodium absorption and BP is substantiated by the gut-specific deletion of MR, which protects against high-salt diet– and deoxycorticosterone-induced hypertension by blunting sodium absorption ( ). While sodium absorption is constitutive and linked to cotransport with glucose and amino acids in the small intestine, aldosterone exerts predominant effects in the large intestine, indicating that colonic sodium absorption is homeostatic and regulated on the basis of temporal BP and body fluid levels through the RAAS ( , ). Functional interplay between the circadian clock and MR has been implicated. Aldosterone induces Per1 gene through MR and synchronizes circadian clocks in cardiomyoblasts ( ). Conversely, the adrenal clock is fundamental for shaping the circadian rhythm of aldosterone production through cyclic Hsd3b6 expression ( ). Given the abundant expression of Bmal1 and MR genes in the mice colon ( ), it could be posited that the circadian clock–aldosterone circuit may contribute to the diurnal rhythms of the colonic transcriptome, aldosterone concentrations, and BP in response to the circadian timing of dietary sodium loading or restriction.
To interrogate whether the colonic circadian clock mediates the temporal response to dietary salt intake in concert with the MR, we assessed the impact of the clock and MR on the diurnal rhythms of genes encoding sodium handling in the colon and BP in nocturnal mice. Aldosterone and MR target genes were synchronized and oscillated in a BMAL1- and MR-dependent manner. Ablation of BMAL1 and MR in the gut reduced sodium absorption and BP. Chromatin immunoprecipitation of BMAL1 and MR, followed by deep sequencing, revealed a considerable overlap of occupancy on a genome-wide scale. Time-restricted feeding clarified the phase entrainment and amplitude control of the colonic circadian oscillator and diurnal BP rhythms in response to the circadian timing of salt loading or restriction, along with the minimal phase response of aldosterone to temporal feeding restriction. These observations unveiled a previously unappreciated cross-talk between the circadian clock and MR to regulate daily sodium handling and underscored the importance of circadian timing of salt intake and BP fluctuations.

## RESULTS

## Colonic sodium absorption exhibits daily variations
To investigate whether sodium absorption follows diurnal fluctuations in the colon, we fed 8-week-old C57BL/6J male mice an ad libitum normal-salt chow diet under a 12-hour light/dark (LD) cycle ( ). In parallel with the increased food and water intake, as well as stool weight during the dark phase, the fecal sodium content decreased, suggesting possible daily rhythms of sodium absorption in the gut ( and fig. S1A). While the urinary sodium content and amount were virtually unchanged between day and night despite increased food intake during the dark phase, fractional excretion of sodium (FENa), the percentage of sodium filtered by the renal glomerulus that is excreted in the urine, was higher during the dark phase than during the light phase ( and fig. S1, B to D) ( ). The result suggested that the higher percentage of the sodium filtered by the kidney was excreted in the urine during the active phase, which was consistent with the daily rhythms of urinary sodium excretion in the rat ( ). Expression of core clock genes such as Bmal1 and Dbp [D site of albumin promoter binding protein, a transcription factor whose gene expression is primarily driven by circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK)/BMAL1] was cyclic and synchronized between the colon and kidney, which reflected the successful daily sampling of those tissues ( ). Expression of genes involved in colonic salt absorption and renal sodium reabsorption under the control of the aldosterone-MR axis, such as Sgk1, Scnn1g [epithelial sodium channel subunit γ (ENaCγ)], Fkbp5 (FK506 binding protein), Slc9a3 [sodium/hydrogen exchanger-3 (NHE3)], and Atp1a1 (sodium/potassium-transporting adenosine triphosphatase subunit α-1), oscillated and tended to be high during the active phase in the colon and kidney ( ). This pattern aligns with the potential diurnal rhythms of colonic sodium absorption but is incongruous, showing an opposite phase, with those of renal sodium excretion ( ). This suggests that temporal sodium excretion in the urine is primarily influenced by the dietary sodium intake rather than by the expression levels of renal sodium reabsorption genes. The diurnal expression of some of those genes [Scnn1g (ENaCγ), Slc9a3 (NHE3), and Atp1a1 in the colon and Sgk1, Scnn1a (ENaCα), and Fkbp5 in the kidney] peaked at zeitgeber time (ZT) 12, which was virtually synchronized with that of the core clock genes (Dbp and Per1 in the colon and Per1 and Per2 in the kidney), along with daily variations in serum aldosterone and corticosterone concentrations ( ). Overall, expression of genes encoding dietary sodium absorption and excretion appears to follow a daily rhythm in synchrony with the circadian clock and aldosterone ( ).
 

## Colonic circadian clock and MR shape daily sodium absorption and BP
Given the phase alignment of genes encoding sodium absorption, the circadian clock, and aldosterone, intestinal epithelial cell (IEC)–specific Bmal1 knockout (BKO) and MR knockout (MRKO) mice were generated and subjected to ad libitum normal-salt chow diet under a 12-hour LD cycle to clarify the functional contribution of the clock and aldosterone to the expression and rhythmicity of the genes encoding colonic sodium absorption ( ). Bmal1 and MR were deleted in the IEC at both the gene and protein expression levels ( , and fig. S2, A to D). Bmal1 deletion had little effect on MR gene expression ( ). Likewise, MR ablation minimally influenced the core clock gene expression ( ). Expression of genes encoding sodium absorption was repressed and dampened in rhythmicity by ablation of Bmal1 and MR, indicating that both BMAL1 and MR engage in temporal transcriptional regulation of these genes ( ). Notably, while the circadian timing of collecting organs was validated by the expression profile of core clock genes such as Bmal1 and Dbp, daily expression peaks of genes such as Cry1 and Slc9a3 (NHE3) somewhat differed between wild-type (WT) and Bmal1flox/flox (BCT) mice, which may be potentially attributed to animal genetic background ( and ). Although plasma corticosterone and aldosterone levels were not altered, fecal sodium and potassium amount showed a trend toward an increase in BKO mice compared to WT mice, albeit not to a statistically significant degree ( ). Water intake, urinary volume, and electrolyte levels were comparable between WT and BKO mice (fig. S2, E to G). In contrast, fecal sodium and potassium levels were substantially increased in MRKO mice despite similar food intake and somewhat increased aldosterone production in MRKO mice ( ). Supporting this observation, urinary sodium excretion tended to be reduced, while water intake was increased in MRKO mice (fig. S2, E and G). Notably, the difference in fecal Na+ amount between and is likely attributed to the types of diet. Specifically, a grain-based chow diet with 0.32% of Na+ was used in , while a purified diet with 0.24% of Na+ was given in . Together with the previous report that MR-mediated transcriptional regulation of genes encoding sodium absorption is predominant in the colon but not in the small intestine ( ), these results suggested that both BMAL1 and MR contribute to colonic sodium absorption. In support of this notion, BP was reduced in both BKO and MRKO mice with similar daily activity profiles compared to that in WT mice, suggesting the functional contribution of colonic BMAL1 and MR to daily BP variations ( , and fig. S2H). While there might be significant changes in fecal sodium amount in BKO mice if the number of replicates is increased, the degree of fecal sodium excretion in those mice is not as increased as that in MRKO mice. This suggests that colonic sodium handling and reduced BP in BKO mice likely represent processes independent of sodium retention, including sympathetic nervous activity, although clear evidence supporting their mechanisms is lacking. These findings indicate a transcriptional mechanism by which both BMAL1 and MR are responsible for diurnal sodium absorption in the intestine and fluctuations in BP.
 

## Colonic circadian rhythms largely rely on the tissue-intrinsic clock and MR
To comprehensively understand the impact of the intestinal clock and MR on diurnal gene expression in the colon, we performed transcriptome analysis using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (data S1). Of the 24,883 coding transcripts, 8366 were cyclic in at least one of the three groups ( ). Counterintuitively, the number of rhythmic transcripts in the BKO group was the highest among the three groups, and cycling transcripts in the BKO mice predominantly peaked at ZT4 ( ). This suggests that the colonic clock normally overrides and suppresses the rhythmic expression of a considerable number of genes that may be driven by LD cycles and/or behavioral rhythms ( ). The phase distribution of the oscillatory genes was different in each group ( ). The colonic epithelial cells undergo rapid turnover, which is linked to their structure and function, such as intestinal barrier and nutrient absorption ( ). In support, disruption of the circadian clock promotes colorectal cancer through the c-Myc induction and heightened glycolysis ( , ). Notably, common pathways such as cell cycle and DNA repair were enriched in all groups, likely reflecting the proliferative propensity of large IECs affected by BMAL1 and MR ( ). The common rhythmic transcripts in all three groups exhibited distinct phase and amplitude distributions, indicating that colonic BMAL1 and MR profoundly influence the daily genomic signature of the colon (fig. S3, A to D).
 To better understand the impact of BMAL1 on normally cyclic genes in the WT colon, we explored common rhythmic transcripts between WT and BKO mice and compared to the genes that were exclusively oscillatory in WT mice ( ). Gut-specific ablation of Bmal1 ceased the oscillation of 1136 genes (46.0%) in the WT. In contrast, 1335 genes remained rhythmic following Bmal1 deletion, although their phase and amplitude were substantially redistributed in the absence of Bmal1 ( ). These results suggest that all rhythmic genes in the WT were more or less regulated by Bmal1. Bmal1-dependent rhythmic genes were overrepresented in processes such as protein folding and DNA repair, as well as rhythmic process and circadian regulation of gene expression as expected ( ). In contrast, 75.6% of rhythmic genes in the WT stopped oscillation as a result of MRKO, suggesting that the MR also plays a fundamental role in driving rhythmic gene expression in the colon ( ). Commonly rhythmic 603 transcripts between WT and MRKO mice displayed relatively similar amplitudes, with phases slightly advanced by MR deletion ( ). The MR-dependent oscillating genes in the intestine were enriched with annotations such as cell cycle and cell division according to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis ( ). The genes that oscillated regardless of MR in the intestine were enriched with rhythmic process such as Per1, Dbp, and Nr1d1, reinforcing the finding that the impact of MR on the core clock machinery is minimal ( and ). Notably, nearly 16% of the sodium ion transport genes (GO:0006814) and aldosterone-induced genes in the colon organoid ( ) exhibited daily rhythmicity (fig. S3, E and F). While the down-regulated genes following MR deletion were enriched with GO annotations such as regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, sodium ion transmembrane transport, and cellular response to aldosterone, the up-regulated genes were overrepresented by the immune response, which implies the derepression of glucocorticoid receptor (GR) target genes (fig. S3, G and H). Because the MR modulates the GR-mediated response to glucocorticoids, genomic alterations resulting from MR ablation may partially reflect changes in glucocorticoid signaling in the colon ( ). Collectively, these results unraveled that BMAL1 and MR govern daily genomic signatures in the colon.

## Cistrome analysis reveals the substantial overlap of genome-wide occupancy between BMAL1 and MR
To gain insight into the transcriptional mechanisms by which spatiotemporal cues of sodium abundance are integrated through the colonic circadian clock and the aldosterone-MR axis, we sought to determine genome-wide binding patterns using chromatin immunoprecipitation, followed by deep sequencing (data S2). The peak regions of both BMAL1 and MR throughout the genome were clustered around the promoters and gene bodies ( and fig. S4, A and B). BMAL1 and MR binding sites overlapped considerably, while their genomic distributions were distinct; BMAL1 was more recruited to the proximal promoters, whereas MR occupied more distal intergenic regions ( and fig. S4C). This is exemplified by the binding of BMAL1 and MR to Per1, Bhlhe41 (basic helix-loop-helix family, member e41), and Slc6a18 (solute carrier family 6, member 18) genes ( ). Motif analysis of the cis-regulatory elements of BMAL1 confirmed the enrichment of known motifs of transcription factors that recognize E-box sequences, such as CLOCK and NPAS2 (neuronal PAS domain-containing protein 2), which form complexes with BMAL1 to activate circadian transcription ( ). In contrast, genomic regulatory elements of MR were enriched with previously described transcription factors, such as AP-1 (activator protein 1), ETS1 (ETS proto-oncogene 1), THRb (thyroid hormone receptor beta), ZNF189 (zinc finger protein 189), and GR, a nuclear receptor belonging to the same subfamily and group as MR that interacts with MR ( ) ( , – ). Heatmaps of BMAL1 and MR binding at genes that were rhythmically expressed exclusively in WT; commonly in WT and BKO; commonly in WT and MRKO; and commonly in WT, BKO, and MRKO mice displayed similar occupancy, indicating that colonic BMAL1 and MR coordinate rhythmic transcription ( and ).
 To further establish the potential coordinated transcription by BMAL1 and MR, we probed the number of genes targeted by the two transcription factors. Notably, 5255 genes were targeted by both BMAL1 and MR, supporting the notion that colonic BMAL1 is involved in salt absorption, in addition to MR ( ). These dually targeted genes included protein phosphorylation, core clock genes, and genes associated with sodium ion transport ( ). Genes bound exclusively by BMAL1 were enriched with regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, and regulation of cell cycle ( ). In contrast, genes that are targeted exclusively by MR were overrepresented by an inflammatory response and defense response pathway, which is consistent with the induction of genes related to the immune response upon MR ablation ( and fig. S3H). GR and MR can form heterodimers and mutually influence the response to glucocorticoids in tissues where both receptors are coexpressed ( , , , ). Hence, it is conceivable that the enrichment of the immune response and inflammatory pathways at least partially represents GR/MR-mediated glucocorticoid action in the colon. Gene set enrichment analysis verified that BMAL1 and MR binding were associated with the induction of target gene expression, implying that the BMAL1 and MR binding event is likely functional ( ). Collectively, BMAL1 and MR contribute to extensive transcriptional control of daily gene expression, and cobinding of BMAL1 and MR regulates sodium absorption genes in the colon ( ).

## Timed salt loading or restriction influences the diurnal rhythms of sodium absorption
To explore the impact of temporal dietary sodium abundance on daily BP variations, we fed WT nocturnal mice a normal chow diet ad libitum, followed by 2 weeks of nighttime feeding with a low-salt diet, nighttime feeding with a high-salt diet, daytime feeding with a high-salt diet, and daytime feeding with a low-salt diet ( ). Under normal chow diet conditions, BP displayed a daily rhythm ( and fig. S5A). Nighttime feeding with the low-salt diet lowered BP, particularly during the light phase ( and fig. S5A). The light-phase BP decrease was blunted by daytime feeding of the low-salt diet ( , and fig. S5A). Nighttime feeding with a high-salt diet elevated BP during the dark phase, as expected ( , and fig. S5A). Notably, the light-phase BP reduction was virtually abolished by daytime feeding of the high-salt diet ( , and fig. S5A). Heart rate also exhibited daily rhythms under ad libitum and nighttime feeding conditions, while daytime feeding regimens slowed down the heart rate, leading to the loss of diurnal rhythms (fig. S5, B to E). The activity cycles were virtually independent of the temporal feeding behavior ( ). These observations suggest that daily BP and heart rate fluctuations are affected by temporal patterns of dietary sodium abundance and that the combination of mistimed eating and a high-salt diet substantially influences the daily BP rhythmicity.
 To interrogate the circadian timing of salt loading and restriction impacts on the temporal patterns of sodium absorption in the gut, we randomly assigned 8-week-old WT mice and subjected to time-restricted feeding of high- or low-sodium diets for 4 weeks. Daytime feeding increased the water intake, feces, and urine during the light phase in both the high- and low-salt diet groups to the same degree as those during the dark phase in the nighttime feeding groups, except for the fecal weights in the low-salt diet and the urine volume in the low- and high-salt diets (fig. S5, F to I). Despite the previously reported increase in body weight as a result of daytime-restricted feeding, the body weight of mice subjected to light-phase–restricted feeding of a high-salt diet was lower than that of mice subjected to dark-phase–restricted feeding of the same diet under comparable calorie intake and water consumption across the groups (fig. S5, F, J, and K).
Daytime feeding phase advanced the expression of colonic clock genes and proteins in the low-salt diet ( , and fig. S6A). This phase shift in the circadian clock was also observed in the kidney, albeit to a lesser degree, with a more damped amplitude (fig. S6B). The tissue-specific response of peripheral clocks to daytime feeding is in line with a previous report ( ). In parallel, genes encoding sodium transport were also phase advanced by daytime feeding under low-salt diet conditions ( and fig. S6C). Conversely, daytime feeding did not alter the peak phase of serum aldosterone levels in the low-salt diet group ( ). Likewise, serum concentrations of corticosterone, a well-described synchronizer of peripheral clocks, retained their phase with a dampened amplitude ( ). These results suggest that light acts as a more predominant zeitgeber than food for corticosteroid and mineralocorticoid hormone synthesis in the adrenal gland and that the colonic-intrinsic clock but not the MR dictates the phase of genes encoding sodium absorption.
 Nighttime feeding with a high-salt diet attenuated the oscillatory expression of the colonic clock genes compared to with a low-salt diet, while the core clock proteins were accumulated in nighttime feeding with a high-salt diet ( , and fig. S6D). In parallel, genes encoding sodium absorption along with serum aldosterone levels were also dampened by the high-salt diet, suggesting that the MR dictates the daily amplitude of sodium ion transport–related genes ( ). This blunted rhythmicity due to nighttime feeding of a high-salt diet was also present in some renal genes, albeit to a lesser degree (fig. S6, E and F).
Conversely, daytime feeding with a high-salt diet severely disrupted the rhythmic expression of some clock genes, such as Dbp, Nr1d1, and Per1, as compared to nighttime feeding with a low-salt diet in both the colon and kidney ( , and fig. S7, A and B). Notably, Per2 expression appeared to be primarily driven by timed feeding, which is consistent with previously described system-driven Per2 gene expression in the liver ( and fig. S7B) ( ). While the genes encoding sodium absorption and serum aldosterone concentrations were prone to down-regulation as a result of daytime feeding of the high-salt diet compared to nighttime feeding of the low-salt diet ( , and fig. S7C), the degree of suppression tended to be attenuated compared to nighttime feeding of the high-salt diet (fig. S7, D to F). In parallel, daytime-restricted high-salt diet feeding did not alter the diurnal phase of the serum aldosterone and corticosterone concentrations compared to nighttime feeding of the low-salt diet ( ) but augmented the amplitude of aldosterone and repressed that of corticosterone in comparison to nighttime feeding of the high-salt diet (fig. S7E). This suggests that insufficient suppression of aldosterone secretion from the adrenal gland under the daytime-restricted high-salt diet condition may contribute to the blunted down-regulation of the colonic sodium absorption genes and loss of daily BP variations. Overall, these results indicated that mistimed salt intake, particularly with high sodium abundance, substantially disrupts the daily rhythms of the colonic and renal clocks and fails to sufficiently attenuate those of the aldosterone-MR axis, leading to the insufficient suppression of sodium absorption and reabsorption and the loss of diurnal BP variations ( ).

## Temporal alterations in the circadian clock in response to timed salt loading or restriction dictate daily genomic signature in the colon
Temporal patterns of feeding behavior and diet types evoke circadian reprogramming, giving rise to de novo oscillations of a unique subset of genes ( – ). Transcriptome analysis was performed on large intestine samples using RNA-seq to determine how colonic diurnal genomic rhythms are reprogrammed by the timing of salt loading or restriction (data S3). Of the 24,883 annotated transcripts, 10,480 oscillated under at least one of the four dietary conditions ( ). A substantial number of transcripts exhibited shared rhythmicity across the different groups ( ). However, the common oscillatory transcripts in all four groups were confined and displayed distinct phase and similar amplitude distributions (fig. S8, A to C). This group of transcripts was enriched in core clock genes, reflecting the resilient nature of the circadian clock (fig. S8D). In contrast, the phase distribution of rhythmic transcripts was specific to each dietary condition ( ). Dietary sodium abundance and temporal patterns of food intake invoked different alternative pathways in the colon ( ). GO analysis revealed that the circadian rhythm and cellular response to aldosterone were enriched pathways in the cyclic genes under nighttime feeding of low-salt diet conditions, reaffirming that robust oscillations were established by the colonic clock and aldosterone ( ). Similarly, mRNA processing and immune system process were overrepresented in rhythmic genes on the high-salt diet under nighttime and daytime feeding restrictions, respectively. The GO annotations related to these pathways were also noted in the GO analysis of MR-bound genes ( ), which is consistent with the suppression of the aldosterone-MR axis as a result of the high-sodium diet. As glucocorticoids repress immune system processes, it is tempting to speculate that high salt–induced aldosterone-MR inhibition alters GR/MR-mediated glucocorticoid signaling outcomes, including the immune response. The peak phase distribution of cyclic genes in the high-salt diet group during the light phase was concentrated at ZT4, which was similar to the daily genomic profile observed in BKO mice ( and ). This recapitulation conceivably indicates that daytime feeding of a high-salt diet heavily disrupts peripheral clocks and hence manifests a somewhat similar state to the genetic loss of Bmal1 in the large intestine.
 To delineate the impact of daytime feeding on daily rhythmic gene expression, we compared the common oscillatory transcripts between daytime and nighttime feeding with a low-salt diet ( ). While the amplitude distributions were comparable, the peak phases were extensively redistributed ( ). These genes were enriched in rhythmic process and circadian rhythm ( ). This particular group of genes, with peak phases altered by daytime feeding, was predominantly targeted by BMAL1, indicating that food entrainment of the colonic clock directs the daily genomic profile in the colon (fig. S8E).
 Nighttime feeding of the high-salt diet ceased the oscillation of 478 genes in the colon compared to nighttime feeding of the low-salt diet, while the remaining 314 rhythmic genes were repressed by the high-salt diet, particularly during day-night transition, leading to peak phase redistribution with a similar amplitude ( ). The genes that lost oscillation upon a high-salt diet included those involved in sodium ion transport, such as Scnn1b and Scnn1g, and were repressed by a high-salt diet, reconfirming that the colonic clock and MR attenuation impinged on the damped gene oscillation at the genome-wide level ( ). In contrast, rhythmic genes, regardless of dietary sodium abundance under night-restricted feeding, were overrepresented in circadian rhythm, which was also noted in the previous quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) results ( and ). Both groups of genes were primarily targeted by BMAL1, implying that attenuation of clock function underlies the dampening of daily expression as a result of high-salt diet ( ).
Daytime feeding of the high-sodium diet prominently diminished the number of oscillatory transcripts compared with nighttime feeding of the low-sodium diet and virtually inverted the phase distribution of the common rhythmic genes with a similar amplitude ( ). This group of genes was enriched in GO pathways, such as circadian rhythm, regulation of cholesterol metabolic process, and gluconeogenesis, and was predominantly controlled by BMAL1, while the genes that lost oscillation as a result of daytime feeding of the high-salt diet were enriched with GO pathways, such as cell division and cellular response to aldosterone, and were also targeted by BMAL1 ( ). As daytime feeding of the high-salt diet disrupted the colonic clock, cyclic genes were compared between BKO and daytime feeding of the high-salt diet conditions. A total of 561 transcripts was commonly rhythmic under both conditions with similar phase distributions, albeit with higher amplitudes in the BKO colon (fig. S9, A to D). A higher number of genes were oscillatory under the BKO condition than in daytime feeding under high-salt diet conditions (fig. S9A). GO analysis identified the enrichment of cell division and DNA repair in genes exclusively cyclic in the BKO group; autophagy, intracellular protein transport, and lipid metabolic process in those oscillatory in both groups; and response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, protein folding, and innate immune response in those rhythmic only in the daytime feeding of the high-salt diet group (fig. S9E). Because genes related to the immune response are associated with changes in MR-mediated gene expression, the genes cyclic exclusively under the daytime-restricted high-salt diet condition are presumably more influenced by MR than by BMAL1. Together, these results suggest that the alteration of the colonic clock and the aldosterone-MR axis through temporal sodium abundance impinges on the diurnal genomic signature in the colon.

## DISCUSSION
Daily salt handling is an integral aspect of mammalian homeostasis and is established by a number of regulators and their effects on target organs. Multiple layers of regulation underlying the physiology relevant to sodium balance, such as BP, RAAS, and renal sodium excretion, follow daily variations, with peaks at the onset of the active period ( , , ). Circadian misalignment caused by experimental forced desynchrony and clock mutations is associated with blunted BP dipping and salt-sensitive hypertension ( , ). To date, the impact of mistimed salt intake or sodium loading on daily sodium handling, BP variations, and the peripheral circadian clock has been largely unexplored. In this study, we revealed that salt absorption in the intestine and BP dynamics display diurnal variations under the control of peripheral clocks and the aldosterone-MR axis. Crucially, these two factors responded to temporal sodium availability in a distinct fashion: Timed food intake somewhat overrode photic cues and entrained the colonic circadian clock, while rhythmic aldosterone production remained aligned with LD cycles. The circadian rhythm of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein, a rate-limiting enzyme of adrenal steroid hormone synthesis, is under circadian control and driven by LD cycles ( ). As peripheral clocks integrate various input signals in a tissue-specific manner ( – ), mistimed salt intake uncouples clocks across different tissues, thereby losing the cohesiveness of circadian networks within an organism and contributing to the dampening of BP variations. Moreover, the addition of a high-salt diet to daytime feeding aggravated clock disruption, which was comparable to that observed in BKO mice. Daytime feeding of a high-salt diet caused the disappearance of diurnal variation in BP without altering body weight ( ). In other words, a nondipping-type BP may be attributed to underlying mistimed sodium-eating behaviors and resultant peripheral clock misalignment. Conversely, the daily BP rhythm was not fully entrained by time-restricted feeding, suggesting that LD cycles and/or circadian pacemakers likely direct BP rhythms through various pathways, including the autonomic nervous system, RAAS, natriuretic peptides, and microbiome ( , ). For instance, sleep apnea is associated with circadian disruption, which may contribute to the attenuation of daily BP variations ( , ). The circadian mechanisms of nondipping BP fluctuations need to be further delineated in future investigations.
 In the present study, daytime-restricted feeding of a high-salt diet tended to increase serum aldosterone levels and the expression of MR target sodium-related genes compared with nighttime-restricted feeding of a high-salt diet, in parallel with the loss of diurnal BP variations. This observation implies that mistimed high sodium intake fails to appropriately suppress aldosterone, leading to an increase in fluid retention and salt-sensitive BP elevation. Supporting this notion, the MR antagonists restore daily BP fluctuations in the pathological states of MR activation, such as metabolic syndrome in rhesus macaques and obstructive sleep apnea in humans ( , ). The MR in the central nervous system regulates sodium channels in the brain and contributes to increases in sympathetic nerve activity, BP, and heart rate ( , ). Because the heart rate during daytime feeding of the high-salt diet lost diurnal variation, it is conceivable that aberrant activation of MR resulted in the activation of the sympathetic nervous system during the light phase and possibly contributed to the loss of BP fluctuations under the daytime-restricted high-sodium diet condition.
A prior study in rats showed the increased urinary sodium excretion during the active phase, which was not replicated in mice subjected to normal chow diet, although the FENa exhibited diurnal fluctuations with an increase during the dark phase ( ). The difference might be attributed to the animal types and relatively inadequate dietary sodium contents. Specifically, we observed an increased urinary sodium excretion in alignment with increased food intake under time-restricted feeding of a high-salt diet in which 8% of sodium was contained in fig. S5H. In contrast, there was merely 0.32% of sodium in normal chow diet in fig. S1C, which might obscure the daily rhythm of urinary sodium excretion. Hence, dietary sodium abundance seems to predominantly influence sodium excretion in the urine. In addition, we observed induction of genes encoding renal sodium reabsorption during the active phase, albeit with virtually constant renal sodium excretion in normal chow diet. A possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the increased sodium filtered by the glomeruli during the active phase is counterbalanced by enhanced tubular sodium reabsorption, leading to urinary sodium excretion comparable to that of the resting phase. It could be deduced that such a diurnal counteraction in the kidney represents an anticipatory and adaptive mechanism to compensate for the heightened sodium loss in the urine in response to the increase in dietary salt load during the active phase.
Overall, our study highlights that temporal sodium sensing and handling through the circadian clock and aldosterone-MR axis in the colon affect daily BP variations. Advances in our understanding of the circadian mechanisms of salt handling and BP regulation will open new avenues for preventive approaches against hypertensive diseases.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Experimental design
Eight-week-old C57BL/6J male mice (CLEA Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were fed with a standard chow (CE-2; CLEA Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and bred under a temperature-controlled, 12-hour LD cycle. Bmal1flox/flox mice (007668, the Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA) and Nr3c2 (MR)flox/flox mice ( ) were bred with Vil-Cre+/− mice (B6.SJL-Tg[Vil-Cre]997Gum/J; the Jackson Laboratory) over three generations to obtain Bmal1flox/flox; Vil-Cre+/− (BKO) and MRflox/flox; Vil-Cre+/− (MRKO) mice under the LD condition, respectively. Eight- to 12-week-old male BKO and MRKO mice were fed a chow diet. Following 2 weeks of habituation, samples were collected over a circadian cycle at ZT0, ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, and ZT20 (N = 4 biological replicates per time point for each group).
With regard to the time-restricted feeding experiment, eight-week-old male mice were imposed on temporal feeding restriction of high-salt diet (D21121401, Research Diets, New Brunswick, USA) and low-salt diet (D21121402, Research Diets) exclusively during 12 hours of dark phase or light phase using automatic feeding cages (Melquest, Toyama, Japan) for 4 weeks. Samples were collected at ZT0, ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, and ZT20 (N = 3 to 4 biological replicates per time point for each group).
Nine-week-old male WT, BKO, and MRKO mice (N = 3 biological replicates) fed an ad libitum normal-salt diet (D19120901N, Research Diets) were implanted with a telemetry transmitter (DSI, Minnesota, USA) under anesthesia (medetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol) following isoflurane inhalation. Antisedan (atipamezole hydrochloride) was administered for reversal, and postoperative analgesia was provided using buprenorphine. BP, heart rate, and activity were measured under the arranged dietary regimens switched every 2 weeks as follows: nighttime feeding of a low-salt diet, nighttime feeding of a high-salt diet, daytime feeding of a high-salt diet, and daytime feeding of a low-salt diet. All animal experiments were designed and conducted in accordance with the animal experimentation guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use committee at Keio University School of Medicine.

## IEC isolation
The colon was opened longitudinally and washed vigorously in wash solution [15 mM Hepes, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and penicillin/streptomycin]. The samples were placed in 10 mM EDTA and 15 mM Hepes in PBS with 5% fetal bovine serum and incubated in the shaker at 200 rpm for 20 min at 37°C. The supernatant containing IECs was centrifuged (720g for 5 min at 4°C). The pellet was resuspended in PBS and recentrifuged (2000g in 5 min at 4°C). Subsequently, it was frozen and stored at −80°C.

## Metabolic cage analysis
WT, BKO, and MRKO mice, aged 8 to 12 weeks, were individually placed in metabolic cages with free access to food and drinking water for 24 hours. Fecal samples were collected, resuspended in 0.75 M nitric acid, incubated overnight at 4°C, and centrifuged as described previously ( ). The supernatants were used for the fecal assays. The urinary and fecal content of sodium and potassium along with the urinary creatinine concentrations were determined at SRL Inc., Japan. Serum sodium and creatinine levels were determined using a DRI-CHEM NX500V analyzer (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). The urinary and serum creatinine levels were measured to calculate the FENa. In all figures, fecal Na+ and K+ content denotes Na+ and K+ content per 1 g of feces, respectively; fecal Na+ and K+ amount denotes Na+ and K+ abundance in feces collected during 12 or 24 hours, respectively; urine Na+ and K+ content denotes Na+ and K+ content per 1 ml of urine, respectively; and urine Na+ and K+ amount denotes Na+ and K+ abundance in urine collected during 12 or 24 hours, respectively.

## Fractional excretion of sodium
FENa is defined as the ratio of sodium excreted in the urine to that filtered at the renal glomerulus and was calculated using the following equationFENa= [Na]Urinary × Urinary volume[Na]Serum × Time[Cr]Urinary × Urinary volume[Cr]Serum × Time × 100=[Na]Urinary × [Cr]Serum[Na]Serum × [Cr]Urinary × 100where [Na] is the sodium concentration and [Cr] is the creatinine concentration.
Physiologically, most sodium filtered at the glomerulus is reabsorbed in the kidney tubule, and only ~1% of sodium is excreted in the urine. In addition to constitutive reabsorption of sodium, the degree of sodium reabsorption is controlled on the basis of the temporal sodium load in the body to maintain homeostasis of the extracellular volume. Hence, FENa is an indicator of tubular absorptive function and urinary sodium excretion. Theoretically, the calculation of FENa accounts for variation in the glomerular filtration rate and urine concentration. Creatinine serves as a filtration marker, as it is produced at a relatively constant rate while undergoing minimal tubular secretion or reabsorption, independent of sodium handling. In addition, sodium concentrations are determined by the solute-free water abundance and renal response to the hydration status. Hence, the ratio of urine creatinine to serum creatinine is used to normalize FENa.

## Aldosterone and corticosterone quantitation
Serum or plasma corticosterone and aldosterone levels were quantified using a Corticosterone enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Kit (ADI-900-097, Enzo Life Sciences Inc., NY, USA) and an Aldosterone ELISA Kit (ADI-900-173, Enzo Life Sciences Inc.), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Protein extraction and Western blot
Frozen IECs of colon were homogenized in modified radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 15 mM MgCl2] supplemented with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany), NaF, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, sonicated briefly and rocked to lyse cells at 4°C. Samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were stored as whole-cell protein lysates. Subsequently, 5 to 60 μg of protein lysates was separated on 6 to 8% gels by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for an hour and visualized using a chemiluminescent horseradish peroxidase substrate (WBKLS0500, EMD Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). The blots were detected by chemiluminescence and scanned using an imaging system (LAS-4000 mini, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

## Antibodies
The antibodies used for Western blotting were as follows: MR (ab64457, Abcam; 1:1000), BMAL1 (ab93806, Abcam; 1:10,000), REV-ERB (#13418, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), PER2 (PER21-A, Alpha Diagnostic International; 1:1000), CRY1 (A302-614A, Bethyl Laboratories; 1:2000), β-ACTIN (#4967, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:5000), and secondary antibody (12-348, EMD Millipore; 1:10,000). Anti-MR (ab64457, Abcam) and BMAL1 (ab93806, Abcam) antibodies were used for chromatin immunoprecipitation, followed by deep sequencing.

## RNA extraction and reverse transcription
Total RNA was extracted from IECs of the colon and kidney using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), followed by precipitation with isopropanol and ethanol. cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription of 1 μg of RNA using iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, California, USA).

## Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
cDNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR using fluorescent dye reagent (SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix, Bio-Rad Laboratories, California, USA) and QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (A34322, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The mRNA levels were measured and normalized to those of 18S ribosomal RNA. The primer sequences are listed in data S4.

## RNA sequencing
Total RNA was monitored for quality control using an Agilent Bioanalyzer Nano RNA chip and NanoDrop absorbance ratios of 260/280 and 260/230 nm, respectively. Library construction was performed using the Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA protocol. The input quantity of total RNA was within the recommended range, and mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. Enriched mRNA was chemically fragmented. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random primers and a reverse transcriptase. Following second-strand synthesis, the double-stranded cDNA was cleaned using AMPure XP beads, and the cDNA was end repaired. Subsequently, the 3′ ends were adenylated. Illumina dual-index adapters were ligated at the ends, and the adapter-ligated fragments were enriched using 9 cycles of PCR. The resulting libraries were validated by qPCR and sized using an Agilent Bioanalyzer DNA high-sensitivity chip. The concentrations in the libraries were normalized and multiplexed. Multiplexed libraries were sequenced using paired-end 150 cycles chemistry on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The FASTQ files were passed through the Tuxedo protocol using TopHat and Cufflinks ( , ). TopHat was used to align reads to the mouse genome assembly (mm10) with Bowtie2, and Cufflinks, with max-bundle-frags set to 500,000, calculated gene expression levels. The protocol provided fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads values for each gene in every replicate.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

## 
Chromatin immunoprecipitation

The samples were sent to Active Motif (Carlsbad, CA, USA) for chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq). The Active Motif was used to prepare the chromatin, perform ChIP reactions, generate libraries, sequence the libraries, and perform basic data analysis. Briefly, the mouse colon epithelial tissues were homogenized, fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min, and quenched with 0.125 M glycine. Chromatin was isolated by adding lysis buffer, followed by disruption using a Dounce homogenizer. Lysates were sonicated, and the DNA was sheared to an average length of 300 to 500 base pairs (bp) using an Active Motif’s EpiShear probe sonicator (catalog no. 53051). Genomic DNA (input) was prepared by treating aliquots of chromatin with ribonuclease (RNase), proteinase K, and heat for de–cross-linking, followed by cleaning with SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter) and quantification using CLARIOstar (BMG Labtech). Extrapolation to the original chromatin volume allowed the determination of the total chromatin yield.
An aliquot of chromatin (30 μg) was precleared using Protein G agarose beads (Invitrogen). Genomic DNA regions of interest were isolated using 6 μl of antibody against BMAL1 (Abcam, catalog no. ab93806) and 8 μl of antibody against MR (Abcam, catalog no. ab64457). The complexes were washed, eluted from the beads with SDS buffer, and treated with RNase and proteinase K. Cross-links were reversed by incubation overnight at 65°C, and ChIP DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.

## 
ChIP-seq (Illumina)

Illumina sequencing libraries [a custom type, using the same paired read adapter oligonucleotides described by Bentley et al. ( )] were prepared from the ChIP and input DNAs on an automated system (Apollo 342, Wafergen Biosystems/Takara). After the final PCR amplification step, the resulting DNA libraries were quantified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq [75–nucleotide (nt) reads, single end]. The reads were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) using the BWA algorithm (default settings). Duplicate reads were removed, and only uniquely mapped reads (mapping quality ≥ 25) were used for further analysis. Alignments were extended in silico at their 3′ ends to a length of 200 bp, which is the average genomic fragment length in the size-selected library, and assigned to 32-nt bins along the genome. The resulting histograms (genomic “signal maps”) were stored in bigWig files. Peak locations were determined using the MACS algorithm (v2.1.0) with a cutoff P value of 1 × 10–7. Peaks that were on the ENCODE blacklist of known false ChIP-seq peaks were excluded. Signal maps and peak locations were used as input data for the proprietary analysis program, Active Motifs, which creates Excel tables containing detailed information on sample comparisons, peak metrics, peak locations, and gene annotations. For the heatmaps, the data were clustered using the k-means algorithm (default = 5 clusters; indicated by C1 to C5) and sorted by decreasing average values inside each cluster. Graphs were generated with the SeqPlots Bioconductor R package using the commands plotAverage and plotHeatmap.

## Statistical analysis

## 
Circadian rhythmicity determination

The daily rhythmicity of transcripts from RNA-seq was assessed using the nonparametric JTK-CYCLE algorithm, incorporating a 24-hour period ( ). Transcripts with no detectable expression/abundance in more than six biological replicates were filtered out before rhythmicity was tested. Genes were considered rhythmic over the circadian cycle if their permutation-based adjusted P values were <0.01.

## 
Bioinformatic analysis

The Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery pathway analysis tool was used to identify the GO terms related to biological processes (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) ( ). Differential analysis of gene expression in individual ZTs was performed using Cyber-T ( , ). Gene set enrichment analysis was performed to determine whether a specific subset of genes is enriched in WT, BKO, or MRKO mouse colon ( ). Gene sets with false discovery rate of less than 25% were considered significantly enriched. Transcripts with no detectable expression/abundance in more than six biological replicates were filtered out before the downstream analysis was performed. The analysis was performed using pipelines implemented in the CircadiOmics ( – ) database and a web portal (circadiomics.ics.uci.edu).

## 
Other statistical analyses

Experimental data are presented as means + SEM. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using paired t test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons between three groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA.The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the three or four groups of amplitude distributions in the diurnal transcriptome. Two-way ANOVA was performed to compare groups of two different variables (GraphPad Prism 10). Heatmaps of the rhythmic transcripts were generated using the R package “gplots,” whereas polar histograms were created using the R package “ggplot2.” The telemetry data were obtained per heartbeat and plotted as an average of every 30 min. In qPCR, any samples of threshold cycle value with “undetermined” were defined as outliers and were excluded from the analysis. Graphs were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 10, and RStudio.
