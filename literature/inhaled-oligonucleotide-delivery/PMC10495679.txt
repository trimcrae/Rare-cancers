## Highlights
•There are abnormal accumulation and dysfunction of MDSCs in AAA patients and mouse models; The abnormal accumulation of MDSCs is related to the increase of Th17 cells. Depletion of circulating MDSCs reverses the proportion of circulating Th17 cells and inhibits the development of AAA.
•MDSCs regulate the proliferation of Th17 cells in vitro and in vivo through ICOSL-ICOS axis in response to the stimulation of IL-3. Adoptive transfer of ICOSL-defects MDSCs to Ang II-induced ApoE−/− mice can inhibit the development of AAA.
•IL-3 promotes the proliferation of MDSCs in vitro, mediates their abnormal immune function, and upregulates the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs by inhibiting the activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Th17 cells are powerful inflammation promoters in the pathogenesis of abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs). Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) can promote the differentiation of Th17 cells in chronic inflammatory autoimmune injury. Here, we aim to examine whether MDSCs regulate the differentiation of Th17 cells to participate in the development of AAA. We demonstrated an abnormal accumulation of MDSCs in AAA patients, which was positively associated with Th17 cells. We established angiotensin II-induced apolipoprotein E knockout mice and found the impaired immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs. After systemic injection of anti-Gr-1 antibody in AAA mice to deplete circulating MDSCs, AAA formation and the differentiation of Th17 cells were abolished, and the overexpression of inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS) on Th17 cells was reversed accordingly. Regulating the expression of ICOS ligand (ICOSL) on MDSCs affects the differentiation of Th17 cells. The adoptive transfer of ICOSLlowMDSCs in AAA mice inhibited the differentiation of Th17 cells and the development of AAA. Meanwhile, rIL-3 promoted the survival and immunosuppressive dysfunction of MDSCs, upregulated ICOSL expression on MDSCs by inhibiting activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and regulated MDSCs to promote the differentiation of Th17 cells via the ICOSL-ICOS axis. An increase in serum IL-3, ICOSL+MDSCs, and ICOS+Th17 cells was detected in AAA patients, and IL-3 levels were positively correlated with the proportion of ICOSL+MDSC cells. In conclusion, we uncovered a pivotal role of MDSCs in promoting the differentiation of Th17 cells through the IL-3-ICOSL-ICOS axis during AAA, providing an important theoretical basis for understanding the pathogenesis of AAA.

## Introduction
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is defined as focal weakening and dilatation of the abdominal artery, wider than 30 mm or 1.5 times the normal diameter . The prevalence of AAA has been reported to range from 2% to 12%, while that among Asian persons is 1.3% . Asymptomatic aortic rupture is the main complication of AAA and is responsible for approximately 200,000 deaths worldwide annually . Clinically, surgical repair is the only current treatment for AAA, although it could lead to perioperative complications and does not apply to AAAs smaller than 55 mm in men (or 50 mm in women) , , . There are currently no established treatments for AAAs smaller than this, but up to 67% slowly grow in size and ultimately undergo surgical repair [ , ]. Targeting the pathogenesis of AAA's early stages is desirable to recommend medications and decelerate disease progression.
The pathogenesis of AAA involves chronic inflammation, upregulation of proteolytic pathways, oxidative stress, and loss of arterial wall matrix , , . A variety of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) inhibitors are being studied to slow aneurysm growth , , , . However, there are no pharmacological treatments available to either prevent or reverse the development of AAA. Inflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells have been investigated for their contribution to the progression of AAA in the last decade . In the aortic tissue of AAA patients, interleukin-17 (IL-17) secreted by Th17 cells increases significantly, promoting the local arterial inflammatory response[17]. IL-17 deficiency protects against angiotensin II (Ang-II) -induced aortic expansion and AA rupture . In addition, inhibiting Th17/IL-17A-related inflammatory responses with digoxin or a p210 vaccine confers protection against experimental AAA . However, the regulation of AAA circulating immune cells on Th17 cells is still unclear.
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of myeloid cells with potent immunosuppressive functions generated during pathologic conditions that can could be divided into two subtypes: monocyte-MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) , , . In mice, MDSCs are defined as CD11b+Gr-1+ cells in the flow cytometry plots, whereas M-MDSCs are defined as CD11b+Ly6ChiLy6G– cells, and PMN-MDSCs can be defined as CD11b+Ly6ClowLy6G+ cells. The markers on the surface of human MDSCs are complicated. Currently, human total MDSCs are defined as HLA-DR−CD11b+/CD33+, M-MDSCs are defined as HLA-DR−CD11b+/CD33+CD14+/CD15−/CD66b−, and PMN-MDSCs are defined as HLA-DR−CD11b+/CD33+CD14−/CD15+/CD66b+ [ , ]. Recently, MDSCs have been identified as potent initiators of several inflammatory diseases, including diabetes mellitus, atherosclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease, and heart failure , , . Our previous studies showed that aberrant MDSCs in systemic lupus erythematosus regulated the differentiation and inflammatory response of Th17 cells, which was consistent with other chronic inflammatory diseases , , . However, whether MDSCs mediate the differentiation of Th17 cells in AAA is still unclear.
Inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS) is the third member of the CD28 family of receptors and is mainly expressed on the surface of activated T cells. ICOS costimulatory signals are essential for Th17 cell differentiation, development and cytokine secretion . It has been reported that ICOS costimulation can activate the transcription of c-Maf, transactivate the production of IL-21 and upregulate the expression of the IL-23 receptor to amplify the Th17 response, inducing Th17 cells to secrete higher levels of IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22 and IFN- γ, exerting strong antitumor activity , while loss of ICOS costimulation leads to defects in Th17 cell expansion . ICOS ligand (ICOSL) is the transmitter of the ICOS costimulatory signal, which is expressed on various antigen-presenting cells (APCs) , yet whether ICOSL is expressed on MDSCs remains unknown, as does the role of ICOSL+ MDSCs in the differentiation or inflammatory response of Th17 cells in AAA.
In this study, we identified the phenotype and function of MDSCs both in AAA patients and in Ang-II perfusion-induced apolipoprotein E knockout (ApoE−/−) mice and demonstrated that MDSC depletion was resistant to Th17-related inflammation, delaying AAA development. We further clarified that interleukin-3 (IL-3) could upregulate the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and then exacerbate the differentiation of Th17 cells via the ICOS/ICOSL axis. Adoptively transferred ICOSL-deficient MDSCs in mice inhibited the differentiation of Th17 cells and the development of AAA. These findings provide an important theoretical basis for understanding the pathogenesis of AAA.

## Materials and methods

## Human subjects
In this study, 30 patients with AAA and 30 normal volunteers were included from Nanjing University Medical School Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital. The patients were diagnosed with AAA (aortic diameter greater than 30 mm) by transabdominal aortic ultrasound, abdominal computed tomography (CT) or aortography. AAA patients with severe infectious diseases, connective tissue diseases, rheumatic immune diseases, rare heritable disorders (Marfan syndrome, Loeys‒Dietz syndrome, or Ehlers‒Danlos syndrome), malignant tumors, Takayasu arteritis, thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA), and abdominal aortic dissection (AAD) were excluded. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (ID: 2022–059), and all studies were conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All volunteers provided written informed consent. Clinical information regarding human patients is provided in Table S1.

## Animal model and treatment
Specific pathogen-free male C57BL/6 and apolipoprotein E knockout (ApoE−/−) mice aged 10–12 weeks were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China). All animals were housed in an environment with a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 1%, and a light/dark cycle of 12/12 h. All animal studies (including the mouse euthanasia procedure) were performed in compliance with the regulations and guidelines of Nanjing University institutional animal care and conducted according to the ARRIVE guidelines.
To evaluate the role of MDSCs in the development of AAA, we established Ang II-induced mouse AAA. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2.0% isoflurane (vol/vol) in 600 cc/min oxygen flow via inhalation throughout surgery, and respiration was artificially controlled during surgery. Then, 14-week-old male ApoE−/− mice were implanted with Alzet osmotic pumps (Model 2004; Alzet Scientific Products) for continuous 28-day delivery of angiotensin II (Ang II; Sigma Aldrich, Inc) or normal saline (control) at a dosage of 1.44 mg/kg/day [ , ].
To evaluate the effect of MDSCs on AAA, anti-Gr-1 antibodies (clone: RB6–8C5, Biolegend, USA) were used to specifically deplete the circulating MDSCs in AAA mice; the method was performed as previously described [ , ]. Briefly, anti-Gr-1 antibodies were injected (200 μg, i.p.) once every 4 days, while the control group mice received anti-IgG antibodies (clone: RTK4530, Biolegend, USA) at the same frequency.
To determine the role of ICOSL+MDSCs in mouse AAA development, siRNA specifically targeting mouse ICOSL and negative control siRNAs (NC) were transfected into MDSCs using RFectPM small nucleic acid transfection reagent (BAIDAI, Changzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S3. Twenty-four hours later, ICOSLlow MDSCs (5 × 106 cells/injection) were obtained and transferred to ApoE−/− mice by intravenous (i.v.) injection on postoperative Days 9 and 18 . AAA mice treated with NC siRNAs transfected with MDSCs were used as controls.
All the above mice were fed standard water and a high-fat diet, and the health of the mice was monitored each day. Twenty-eight days after surgery, animals were euthanized with pentobarbital (200 mg/kg), an accepted method by the Panel on Euthanasia of the American Veterinary Medical Association, and then aortic tissue samples were obtained for the following analysis.

## Quantification and histological analyses of aneurysms
The aorta was dissected under an anatomical microscope and then photographed. ImageJ software was used to measure the maximum external diameter of the infrarenal abdominal aortic segment. AAA was defined as a 50% increase in the external diameter of the abdominal aorta .
Separated abdominal aortas were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at 5 μm and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson trichrome, and Elastica van Gieson (EVG) for morphometric analyses . H&E staining was used to analyze the morphology, and aortic intima-media thickness as the mean value from the endothelial surface to the adventitia was recorded from ten different locations spanning the entire cross-section using ImageJ software. EVG staining was used to evaluate elastin degradation, and the pathological score of elastin degradation in the aortas was determined in a double-blind manner according to the following rules: a score 1 of for degradation less than 25%; score of 2 for degradation ranging from 25% to 50%; score of 3 for degradation ranging from 50% to 75%; and score of 4 for degradation greater than 75%. Ten discontinuous sections in each specimen were used to quantify elastin degradation, and average scores were obtained from the mean value of all specimens in each group.

## Immunohistochemistry
Samples were blocked using 10% normal goat serum for 30 min at 37 °C and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-F4/80 antibodies (Abcam, Cat No. ab100790). The slices were then incubated with the horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, and DAB chromogenic reagent was added until the nucleus showed a brown‒yellow color under a microscope. Samples were then counterstained with hematoxylin staining solution to label the nucleus, washed and mounted onto glass slides with a resin mounting medium. Images were processed and analyzed with ImageJ software.

## Flow cytometry
In our previous study, we described the steps of flow cytometry detection in human blood . Briefly, human blood samples were treated with an FC blocker for 15 min, and then MDSCs were stained with anti-HLA-DR-Alexa Fluor®488, anti-CD11b-PE-Vio®770, anti-CD14-Percp-efluor710, anti-CD33PE/Cy5, anti-CD66b-APC/Cy7, and anti-ICOSL-PE-efluor610 for 30 min. Th17 cells were stained with the surface markers anti-CD3-percp-efluor710, anti-CD4-Alexa Fluor 488, and anti-ICOS-PE/Cy7. Th17 cells were then fixed and permeabilized. Next, intracellular staining was performed with anti-RORγt-APC for another 30 min in the dark.
To detect mouse MDSCs and T cells, cell suspensions isolated from blood, spleens, and bone marrow were incubated with Fc-blocker for 15 min. MDSC cells were stained with anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-Gr-1-PE/Cy7, anti-Ly6G-PE, anti-Ly6C-APC, and anti-ICOSL-eFluor 660 for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Th17 cells were surface labeled with anti-CD3-Alexa Fluor 532, anti-CD4-PE/Cy5, and anti-ICOS-APC for 30 min, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and intracellular staining with anti-RORγt-PE for another 30 min in the dark. Finally, all cells were resuspended in FACS buffer for flow cytometry detection on a flow cytometer (CYTEK NL-2000, CA). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, OR, USA). Information on the antibodies used in flow cytometry is listed in Table S2. In this study, we determined threshold gating for individual markers using multiparameter flow cytometry and combined single marker staining using a fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) approach, as well as the gating strategy of MDSCs, M-MDSCs, G-MDSCs and Th17 cells in human and mouse models, as shown in Fig. S1A, S2J and S5E-G.

## Isolation of MDSCs and MDSC suppression assay
MDSC sorting and T-cell proliferation were described previously [ , ]. An MDSC isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions to obtain splenic PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs. Normal splenocytes were incubated with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) stain (Invitrogen, USA), and activated with anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL, eBioscience, CA) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/mL, eBioscience, CA) antibodies in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI) 1640. Then PMN-MDSCs/M-MDSCs were cocultured with splenocytes (MDSCs: splenocytes = 1:2, 1:4 and 1:8) in 96-well flat-bottom plates. CFSE is divided equally among daughter cells with each division; therefore, T-cell proliferation could be assessed by flow cytometry 72 h later. All experiments were run in triplicate.

## Bone marrow-derived MDSC (BM-MDSC) culture
BM-MDSCs were cultured as described previously . In short, the tibiae and femora were removed from 12-week-old C57BL/6 J male mice to flush BM cells with PBS, after which clean BM cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 40 ng/mL murine IL-6 and GM-CSF (Miltenyi Biotec, USA).

## Lentivirus or siRNA transfection
We customized an ICOSL-overexpressing lentivirus from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. According to the manufacturer's instructions, BM-MDSCs were transfected with ICOSL-lentivirus and vector lentivirus. Twenty-four hours later, the cell medium was replaced with regular culture medium. After 48 h, the transduction efficiency of ICOSL was confirmed by real-time PCR (RT‒PCR) and flow cytometry.
For siRNA transfection, BM-MDSCs were transfected with ICOSL or negative control (NC) siRNA by RFectPM small nucleic acid transfection reagent (BAIDAI, Changzhou, China) following the manufacturer's protocols. Twenty-four hours later, the knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT‒PCR and flow cytometry. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S3.

## Th17 cell differentiation and coculture with BM-MDSCs
Naive CD4+ T cells from WT mice were extracted by magnetic bead negative selection using the mouse naive CD4+ T-cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, USA) . CD4+ cells were stimulated with anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/mL), 2.5 ng/mL tissue growth factor-β (TGF-β), 20 ng/mL IL-6, 10 μg/mL IL-4 and IFN-γ in 24-well plates to induce Th17 cells. Meanwhile, pretreated BM-MDSCs were added to the culture system on Day 0 at a ratio of 1:1. Three days later, Th17 cells were stained with anti-CD4-FITC and anti-RORγt-APC antibodies for flow cytometry detection . The experiments were independently repeated three times.

## RNA sequencing analysis
BM-MDSCs were incubated with recombined IL-3 (50 ng/mL, Bioworld, USA) for 24 h, and total RNA from BM-MDSCs was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme Biotech, China). RNA sequencing analysis of MDSCs was performed by NovelBio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The fold value represents the degree of differential expression between MDSCs of the IL-3-induced group (IL-3-MDSCs) and control group (Control). The standard used to judge differential expression was as follows: the gene expression from the IL-3-MDSCs with the Control was used as a valid gene. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was used to identify significantly differentially expressed genes within each cluster using the logistic regression test for significance and an average log fold-change. Only genes with a positive average log fold-change value (greater than 0.58) and an adjusted P value lower than 0.05 were selected for further analysis.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)
Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer's protocols and reverse-transcribed using HiScript II Q Reverse Transcriptase SuperMix (Vazyme, China). qRT‒PCR was performed using SYBR green dye on a sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative mRNA expression was normalized to that of GAPDH based on the assays in triplicate and evaluated by the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt). The primers are listed in Table S4.

## Western blot
Western blotting was performed as previously described . Briefly, using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used collect all proteins. The protein concentrations in the extracts were detected using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of the protein sample were loaded with sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE). Then, the protein sample was electrotransferred to 0.22 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (PVDF). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA dissolved in TBST for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies: anti-MMP2, anti-MMP9, anti-PI3K, anti-p PI3K, anti-AKT, anti-p AKT, and anti-β-actin overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. After washing with TBST buffer, protein bands were visualized using ECL plus Western blotting detection reagents (Bio-Rad, USA). The strips were scanned and quantified using a computer image analysis system. Information on the antibodies used in the Western blot is listed in Table S5. All results are representative of three independent experiments.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Plasma IL-3 levels were measured using ELISA kits (RayBiotech, USA) following the manufacturer's protocols, drawing a standard curve, and performing statistical analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

## Gelatin zymography
Gelatin zymography assays were performed to detect gelatinase activity (MMP2 and MMP9) after SDS‒PAGE electrophoresis separation . Aliquots of 30 µg of cell lysates from abdominal aorta tissues were separated by electrophoresis on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel containing 0.1% gelatin under nonreducing conditions. After electrophoresis, the gels were washed for 18 h with a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 µM ZnCl2 and 2.5% Triton X-100 and then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 0.02% Brij35 and 1 µM ZnCl2. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G 250 for 3 h and destained with decolorizing solutions A, B, and C for 0.5, 1, and 2 h, respectively.

## Statistical analysis
For the animal, cell culture, and human blood sample studies, values are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical evaluation was performed using GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). The experiments were usually conducted at least three times independently to ensure the reproducibility of the data and for statistical analysis. Comparisons between two means were made by Student's t-test; comparisons between three or more means were made by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple comparisons tests. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman's correlation test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## MDSC accumulation is associated with AAA formation
To explore the potential relevance of MDSCs to the pathophysiology of AAA, we obtained peripheral blood samples from a total of 30 AAA patients and sex- and age-matched normal volunteers as controls (Normal) (Table S1) to detect the proportion of MDSCs and their subsets by flow cytometry (Fig. S1A, B). We found that the proportions of total MDSCs, PMN-MDSCs, and M-MDSCs in AAA patients were significantly higher than those in Normal (P < 0.0001, A, B). Among them, the proportion of M-MDSCs increased by nearly 5 times, while PMN-MDSCs increased by approximately 3 times. 
We established an Ang II-induced AAA mouse model (Fig. S2A-C), and the aortic wall showed obvious damage, collagen deposition, elastin loss, and inflammatory macrophage infiltration (Fig. S2D-I) . The proportions of total MDSCs, PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in the bone marrow, blood, and spleen tissues of AAA mice were significantly increased (P < 0.001, C, Fig. S2J). Annexin V+ MDSCs were significantly decreased in the blood and spleen of AAA group mice (P < 0.05, Fig. S3), suggesting that the apoptosis of MDSCs in AAA mice was reduced, which might be the reason for the abnormal accumulation of MDSCs. Furthermore, we sorted splenic total MDSCs and then detected the expression of immunosuppressive effectors. The quantitative PCR (qPCR) results showed that the expression of Arginase 1 (Arg-1), ROS-related genes (gp91), and IL-6 was upregulated in the AAA group, while inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and IL-10 were reduced ( E), suggesting that the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs was disordered. Then, we separately sorted PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs from treated groups and cocultured them with CFSE-labeled normal splenocytes in the presence of anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation ( F). We found that the inhibitory effect of M-MDSCs from AAA mice on CD4+ T-cell proliferation was significantly weakened ( G), while that of PMN-MDSCs showed no difference (Fig. S4). These results indicate that the accumulation of MDSCs and the reduced immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs might be involved in AAA formation.

## MDSC depletion reduces Ang II-induced AAA formation
Eighteen years ago, the RB6–8C5 antibody was proven to reduce AAA induced by aortic elastase perfusion due to the depletion of neutrophils . However, RB6–8C5 was also proven to deplete MDSCs (including PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs) in recent years, suggesting that MDSCs might be an effective target by which RB6–8C5 relieves AAA. To evaluate the effect of MDSCs on AAA, we injected (i.p.) anti-Gr-1 antibodies every 4 days to deplete the circulating MDSCs and verified the exhaustion effect (Fig. S5A-D). After 28 days of Ang II infusion, the incidence of AAA induced by Ang II infusion was 66.7% (10 of 15) and 20% (3 of 15) in the anti-IgG group mice and the anti-Gr-1 group mice, respectively ( A, B). The maximal diameter of the abdominal aorta from mice in the anti-Gr-1 group was significantly smaller than that of mice in the anti-IgG group ( C). Histological analysis of the aortic tissues revealed reduced damage to the arterial wall and decreased elastin fragmentation and adventitial collagen deposition after MDSC depletion ( D-F). In addition, the infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages in the aortic wall was downregulated in the anti-Gr-1 group ( G, Fig. S5D) . Aortic mRNA expression of inflammatory cytokines was decreased in the anti-Gr-1 group ( H), while protein levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9 and the enzymatic activities of pro-MMP2, MMP2, pro-MMP9 and MMP9 decreased significantly in the abdominal aorta of anti-Gr-1 group mice ( I, Fig. S5E). Furthermore, Th17 cells in the blood and spleen of mice in the anti-Gr-1 group were both significantly decreased ( J-K, Fig. S5F, G). We subsequently investigated whether the expansion of Th17 cells in AAA patients increased significantly ( L, Fig. S5G, H) and whether it was significantly positively correlated with MDSCs and their subsets ( M). The above results indicate that depletion of MDSCs could significantly inhibit the differentiation of Th17 cells and subsequently attenuate Ang II-induced AAA formation. 

## MDSCs drive Th17 cell differentiation through the ICOSL-ICOS axis in AAA
ICOS/ICOSL costimulatory signals are essential for the differentiation, development and cytokine secretion of Th17 cells , , . Our results showed that the mRNA levels of ICOS in the blood, spleen, and aorta of AAA mice increased significantly (Fig. S6A), especially the expression of ICOS on Th17 cells in AAA mice ( A, B), while the upregulation of ICOSL on MDSCs was verified in AAA mice ( C, D). Depletion of MDSCs significantly reduced the expression of ICOS on Th17 cells in AAA mice ( E). Next, bone marrow-induced MDSCs (BM-MDSCs) were transfected with lentivirus to overexpress ICOSL expression in vitro ( F). On this basis, ICOSLhi MDSCs were cocultured with CFSE-labeled splenocytes, and the results showed that the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs decreased, accompanied by the enhancement of ICOSL expression ( G). Then, we activated coculture system cells under Th17 polarizing conditions and found that the differentiation of Th17 cells cocultured with ICOSLhi MDSCs significantly increased ( H). In contrast, in vitro, the differentiation of Th17 cells was significantly suppressed by the downregulation of ICOSL on MDSCs (Fig. S6B-D). All the above results suggest that MDSCs might regulate the differentiation of Th17 cells through the ICOSL-ICOS axis. 
To explore the role of ICOSL+ MDSCs in the proliferation of Th17 cells in vivo and the formation of AAA, we adoptively transferred ICOSLlow MDSCs into AAA mice (Fig. S6E). Macroscopic examination of the aortas revealed that adoptive transfer of ICOSLlow MDSCs significantly mitigated AAA damage ( A, B), while the maximum diastolic diameter of the abdominal aorta decreased prominently ( C). Furthermore, dilatation in the abdominal aortic lesions decreased, adventitial collagen deposition was reduced and fragmentation of the elastin layer was attenuated after adoptive transfer of ICOSLlow MDSCs in Ang II-induced AAA mice ( D-F), while infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages in aortas was markedly lower ( G), and the mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokines decreased significantly (Fig. S6F). Meanwhile, flow cytometry analysis revealed that the adoptive transfer of ICOSLlow MDSCs significantly inhibited Th17 cells in AAA mice ( H, I). The above results indicate that reducing the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs blocked the differentiation of Th17 cells in AAA mice, leading to the remission of AAA. 

## IL-3 is required for promoting ICOSL+MDSCs and mediating Th17 cells differentiation
It has been reported that IL-3 is involved in the development of atherosclerosis, AAD, and TAA and is considered to be a risk factor for cardiovascular disease [ , ]. To determine the effect of IL-3 on MDSCs, we treated BM-MDSCs with rIL-3 in vitro and found that rIL-3 promoted the proliferation of MDSCs and inhibited their apoptosis ( A, B) while intensely upregulating the mRNA expression of Arg-1 and IL-6 in MDSCs ( C). In addition, the ability of MDSCs to inhibit the proliferation of CD4+ T cells was impaired after rIL-3 treatment ( D), and the differentiation of Th17 cells increased significantly after coculture with rIL-3-induced MDSCs ( E), while rIL-3 induction alone did not directly promote the percentage of Th17 cells (Fig. S7A). In addition, IL-6 induces the development of Th17 cells from naive T cells, whereas upregulation of IL-6 mRNA expression was observed in MDSCs stimulated with rIL-3. We further inactivated IL-6 in BM-MDSCs by siRNA transfection and surprisingly found that rIL-3 could still promote the differentiation of Th17 cells (Fig. S7B-D). These data suggested that there might be other factors besides IL-6 inducing the differentiation of Th17 cells in the rIL-3-primed BM-MDSC coculture system. 
Interestingly, we observed that both the mRNA and protein levels of ICOSL were upregulated during rIL-3 induction in MDSCs ( F, G). To determine whether IL-3-mediated MDSCs promote the differentiation of Th17 cells via the ICOSL-ICOS axis, we added an anti-ICOSL antibody into the coculture system of IL-3-induced MDSCs and Th17 cells. The results showed that blocking the ICOSL-ICOS axis significantly inhibited the pro-proliferative effect of MDSCs on Th17 cells after rIL-3 induction ( H). The above results indicated that IL-3 mediated the immune dysfunction of MDSCs and promoted the differentiation of Th17 cells by upregulating the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs.

## IL-3 upregulates ICOSL expression on MDSCs by blocking the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
To further explore the underlying mechanism of rIL-3 in the upregulation of ICOSL on MDSCs, we performed RNA-seq detection and analysis. The results showed that a total of 83 mRNAs were elevated, and 23 mRNAs were decreased after IL-3 stimulation (fold change ≥ 0.58 and P ≤ 0.05) ( A). Among them, IL-4, IL-13 and IL-6 were upregulated and amplified inflammatory responses, while col6a2 and col6a4, which encode collagen IV, were downregulated ( B, Fig. S7E). Furthermore, pathway analysis showed that rIL-3 significantly activated the function of MDSCs to promote the Th17/IL-17 signaling pathway, which further supports our previous discovery ( C). In addition, the RNA-Seq analysis results showed that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was changed after IL-3 induction ( C). We verified that the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT in BM-MDSCs was decreased after IL-3 treatment ( D). It has been reported that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway plays an important role in the immune regulation involved in the ICOSL-ICOS axis . Then, we pretreated MDSCs with LY294002, an inhibitor of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, following treatment with rIL-3 ( E). The results showed that after LY294002 treatment, rIL-3 could further upregulate the expression of ICOSL ( F, G). All the above results indicate that IL-3 might upregulate the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs by inhibiting the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. 

## Increased IL-3 was positively correlated with the accumulation of ICOSL+ MDSCs in patients with AAA
Finally, we verified that plasma IL-3 levels in AAA patients were significantly increased and were 5 times higher than those in Normal ( A). Correlation analysis showed that the proportions of total MDSCs, M-MDSCs, and PMN-MDSCs were significantly positively correlated with plasma IL-3 levels ( B). In addition, the expression of ICOSL on total MDSCs and M-MDSCs of AAA patients was significantly increased ( C), and plasma IL-3 levels were positively correlated with the expression of ICOSL on M-MDSCs ( D). Moreover, plasma IL-3 levels showed a positive correlation with the proportions of Th17 cells ( E), while Th17 cells were positively correlated with the expression of ICOSL on M-MDSCs ( F). Finally, we observed that the expression of ICOS on Th17 cells was upregulated in AAA patients compared with that in Normal ( G). 

## Discussion
Although the role of MDSCs in tumors, autoimmune diseases, infections, and other diseases has received extensive attention, the role of MDSCs in cardiovascular diseases is still ambiguous. It has been reported that PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs significantly increase hypertension, which is an important risk factor for AAA . In addition, MDSCs are amplified in atherosclerosis, presenting a suppressive function on the proliferation of T cells in an IFN-γ-dependent manner . In this study, we found that MDSCs abnormally accumulated in both AAA patients and AAA mice, while the ability to inhibit CD4+ T-cell proliferation of spleen-sorted M-MDSCs from AAA mice was impaired, suggesting that MDSCs participate in the development of AAA.
Conventionally, MDSCs are identified as Gr-1+CD11b+ cells, with immune-suppressive functions, which is different from other Gr-1+ cells . The Gr-1-specific RB6–8C5 Ab is known to bind both Ly6G and Ly6C molecules, and two subsets of MDSCs sequentially lose their suppressive capacity after Gr-1 Ab treatment . Based on the above characteristics, Gr-1-specific RB6–8C5 Ab is widely used to study the role of MDSCs in multiple types of tumors, inflammatory diseases, and autoimmune diseases [ , , ]. In this study, to reveal the role of MDSCs in AAA, we depleted circulating MDSCs in AAA mice with an anti-Gr-1 antibody and found that depletion of MDSCs significantly reduced the incidence of AAA and reduced arterial wall rupture and elastin loss. In the development of AAA, the infiltration of exogenous immune cells triggers a sustained inflammatory response, in which macrophages have an irreplaceable role. Our results showed that the depletion of circulating MDSCs reduced the infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages in the arterial wall. The above results indicated that the depletion of MDSCs in AAA mice significantly improved the symptoms of AAA.
Currently, MDSCs and Th17 cells have been the focus of immunological research, and the interaction between MDSCs and Th17 cells has been studied in autoimmune disease and cancer settings. It has been reported that MDSCs promote Th17 differentiation and aggravate disease progression by producing interleukin-1β (IL-1β) or Arg-1 to metabolize arginine . For example, in Ehrlich ascites carcinoma, MDSCs could regulate the Th17 immune response by secreting various cytokines, such as IL-6, TGF-β, and IFN-γ . However, MDSCs could promote Th17 cell polarization and differentiation in arthritic mice or rheumatoid arthritis patients in an IL-1β-dependent manner . In an autoimmune encephalomyelitis model, MDSCs enhance the differentiation of naive CD4+ T-cell precursors into Th17 cells and increase IL-17A production . However, there is little research on how MDSCs affect Th17 cell differentiation in AAA. In this study, we found that the abnormal accumulation of MDSCs in AAA patients showed a significant positive correlation with an increase in Th17 cells, which was validated in mice with AAA. In addition, we observed that Treg cells were decreased in AAA patients, and the ratio of Th17/Treg cells in the peripheral blood of AAA patients was significantly increased (Fig. S8A, D). Depleting MDSCs reduced the proportion of Th17 cells in AAA mice, reversed the reduction in Treg cells, and decreased the ratio of Th17/Treg cells (Fig. S8B, E), suggesting that MDSCs probably promote the proliferation and differentiation of Th17 cells in AAA.
It has been reported that the ICOS/ICOSL pathway acts in a complex manner in different subsets of immune cells, and can produce different or even opposite effects . For example, the interaction of ICOS expressed on Treg cells with ICOS-L that is expressed on ILC2s causes immune suppression and tolerance, while Th2 cells interact with ILC2s through the ICOSL-ICOS axis to improve inflammatory responses . In addition, the combination of ICOS and ICOS-L promotes the differentiation and function of Tfh cells . It is not yet known whether the ICOSL-ICOS interaction is involved in Th17 cell differentiation. Previous studies have shown that ICOS has a decisive role in the development of human Th17 cells, and Th17 cells costimulated with ICOS produced more IL-17A, IL-17F, and IFN-γ than CD28 costimulation [ , ]. The Th17/IL-17 response in ICOS−/− mice was significantly impaired, and the degree of the Th17/IL-17 response was positively correlated with the expression of ICOS . This prompted us to explore the role of the ICOSL-ICOS axis in Th17 cell differentiation in AAA. Fortunately, our results show an expansion of Th17 cells in AAA patients, and the expression of ICOS on Th17 cells accordingly increased. ICOSL is expressed on the surface of APCs and mediates the occurrence of various diseases by combining with ICOS on the surface of T cells. For example, the receptor ICOS on T cells interacts with ICOSL on dendritic cells (DCs) and can induce the Th17 cell phenotype ; ICOSL+ bone marrow-induced mast cells (BMMCs) can secrete various cytokines to promote Treg differentiation toward RORγt+ Tregs . This study first reported increased expression of ICOSL on MDSCs in patients and mice with AAA. In vitro studies suggest that the upregulation of ICOSL on MDSCs could correspondingly enhance the differentiation of Th17 cells, while its deregulation could reverse this process, suggesting that MDSCs could mediate the differentiation of Th17 cells through the ICOSL-ICOS axis. Adoptive transfer of MDSCs with low ICOSL expression not only inhibited the abnormal differentiation of Th17 cells, reversed the reduction in Treg cells, and decreased the ratio of Th17/Treg cells in AAA mice (Fig. S8C, E) but also relieved AAA symptoms and inhibited the occurrence of AAA.
IL-3 has been recognized as the upstream coordinator of an inflammatory response to amplify the cytokine storm in mouse sepsis and other inflammatory diseases. IL-3 can also promote monocyte differentiation or recruit inflammatory cell infiltration to aggravate local vascular inflammation. In model mice, IL-3 upregulated and stimulated macrophages to overexpress MMP-12 to promote the formation of TAA/AD, while IL-3 knockdown reduced macrophage infiltration and the inflammatory response in the aorta . Our results also showed higher plasma IL-3 levels in AAA patients than in normal volunteers, and the content of IL-3 was positively correlated with the MDSC proportion. Previous studies have confirmed that the secretion level of IL-3 in AAA tissue culture medium was significantly higher than that in normal tissue . In addition, the infiltration of MDSCs and Th17 cells has also been observed in the aortic tissue of AAA patients . Therefore, IL-3, MDSCs, and Th17 cells coexist in arterial tissue, providing a venue for their interaction. As a multicolony-stimulating factor, the main physiological source of IL-3 is activated T lymphocytes , while its main function is promoting hematopoiesis by triggering hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation into myeloid cell subsets . Our research has shown that recombinant IL-3 can promote the proliferation of MDSCs and induce high expression of ICOSL but does not affect the differentiation of Th17 cells. In addition, the coculture of ICOSL+MDSCs with Th17 cells promoted the differentiation of the latter, while blocking the ICOSL-ICOS axis offset the proliferation of Th17 cells. In summary, we speculated that IL-3 might be produced by activated T cells in arterial tissue, where it upregulates ICOSL on MDSCs, which promotes Th17 cell differentiation through the ICOSL-ICOS axis.
Previous studies have shown that IL-3 is the main factor that stabilizes ICOSL expression in mast cells. In addition, IL-3 could also stimulate ICOSL expressed on pDCs, while anti-ICOSL antibodies significantly blocked pDC responses induced by IL-3 [ , ]. Our results showed that IL-3 induces and stabilizes the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs. Plasma IL-3 was also significantly positively correlated with the expression level of ICOSL on M-MDSCs in AAA patients. This is consistent with the attenuated immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs observed in AAA mic. Furthermore, we reported that IL-3 inhibited the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in MDSCs. It has been reported that the activation of immunosuppressive function in MDSCs is often accompanied by the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway . We found that after pretreatment with an inhibitor of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, rIL-3 further promoted the expression of ICOSL on MDSCs, suggesting that IL-3 could upregulate the expression of ICOSL by inhibiting the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

## Conclusion
In the present study, we first reported an abnormal accumulation of MDSCs in AAA. Moreover, MDSCs could promote the differentiation of Th17 cells through the IL-3-ICOSL-ICOS axis during AAA, while depletion of MDSCs or adoptive transfer of ICOSLlowMDSCs reduced the formation of AAA. Our study provides an important theoretical basis for further understanding the pathogenesis of AAA.
