## ABSTRACT

## Background
Eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (eCRSwNP) is characterized by persistent sinonasal inflammation and marked eosinophilic infiltration. Although the relationship between eosinophils and NP formation has been extensively studied, the mechanisms governing eosinophil transepithelial migration into the nasal mucosa remain poorly understood. Cadherin‐26 (CDH26), an epithelial adhesion molecule that binds to integrins α4 (ITGA4) and αE, has been implicated in eosinophilic inflammation and may play a critical role in eosinophil recruitment and tissue infiltration.

## Objective
This study aimed to investigate the role and underlying mechanism of CDH26 in facilitating eosinophil transepithelial migration in patients with eCRSwNP.

## Methods
Single‐cell ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed on nasal tissues from patients with eCRSwNP, non‐eCRSwNP, chronic rhinosinusitis without nasal polyps (CRSsNP), and healthy controls to evaluate CDH26 expression. Human primary nasal epithelial cells (hPNECs) were stimulated in vitro with interleukin‐4 (IL‐4) or IL‐13, and CDH26 expression was assessed via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR), western blotting, and IHC. The effect of IL‐4 receptor blockade using Dupilumab was evaluated in monolayer cultures. ITGA4 expression in EoL‐1 eosinophil‐like cells was measured by flow cytometry, and their interaction with recombinant CDH26 was evaluated using cell adhesion and Transwell migration assays.

## Results
CDH26 expression was significantly upregulated in eCRSwNP tissues compared to that in other groups. IL‐4 and IL‐13 stimulation induced CDH26 expression in hPNECs, which was dose‐dependently inhibited by Dupilumab. EoL‐1 cells expressed ITGA4 and adhered to recombinant CDH26 in vitro. Th2 cytokine stimulation enhanced EoL‐1 cell transepithelial migration, which was significantly reduced by CDH26 knockdown or Dupilumab treatment.

## Conclusion
Th2 cytokine‐induced upregulation of epithelial CDH26 facilitates eosinophil transepithelial migration, potentially via ITGA4 interaction. Thus, CDH26 may represent a novel therapeutic target for managing eCRSwNP.

## Introduction
Eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (eCRSwNP), a subtype of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), is a refractory and intractable disease believed to result from systemic eosinophilic dysregulation [ , ]. Characterized by tissue eosinophil infiltration, patients with eCRSwNP primarily experience nasal congestion and loss of smell, accompanied by thick mucus production and recurrent secondary bacterial infections, all of which significantly impair their quality of life [ ]. Furthermore, patients with eCRSwNP exhibit more severe sinus inflammation on computed tomography (CT) and nasal endoscopy compared to patients with non‐eCRSwNP. Additionally, elevated tissue eosinophil counts are indicative of nasal polyp (NP) recurrence [ , ].
Eosinophils, which play an important role in eCRSwNP, are produced in the bone marrow and migrate to target tissues via the bloodstream in response to Type 2 reactions [ ]. The complex processes of eosinophil migration and tissue retention are tightly regulated by cell adhesion molecules, such as integrins and Th2 cytokines, along with other inflammatory mediators that control the expression and activation of these adhesion molecules [ ]. In eCRSwNP, eosinophil transepithelial migration into the airway lumen is a key step in the pathogenesis of airway inflammation, facilitating their entry into the nasal mucus layer, where they degranulate and release cytotoxic proteins [ ]. However, although the mechanisms underlying eosinophil transendothelial migration are well‐characterized, their transepithelial migration—essential for eosinophil accumulation in the airway lumen and contribution to inflammation—remains poorly understood [ , ].
Cadherin‐26 (CDH26), a member of the cadherin family, is a calcium‐dependent adhesion molecule with restricted expression on epithelial cells that binds to αE and α4 integrins [ ]. Several studies have linked CDH26 expression with eosinophilic inflammation. For example, CDH26 is upregulated in human gastrointestinal tissues exhibiting active eosinophilic inflammation [ ]. Furthermore, CDH26 knockdown has been reported to be associated with reduced immune cell infiltration in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of an asthma mouse model, indicating its potential role in eosinophil recruitment or retention [ ].
Given that eosinophil transepithelial migration is suspected to be a major contributor to epithelial damage in eCRSwNP, and considering the potential interaction between CDH26 and integrin α4 (ITGA4), we hypothesized that CDH26 may facilitate this process. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the role of CDH26 and its underlying mechanisms in eosinophil transepithelial migration within the nasal mucosa of patients with eCRSwNP.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Patients and Specimens
Normal uncinate process (UP) tissues were obtained from patients undergoing septoplasty, whereas both UP and NP tissues were collected from patients with CRS during endoscopic sinus surgery. CRS diagnosis was based on histopathological, endoscopic, radiological, and CT findings, following the 2020 European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps (EPOS) guidelines. eCRSwNP was defined as the presence of ≥10 eosinophils per high‐power field (HPF, ×40) in the nasal mucosa, as specified by the EPOS 2020 criteria.
All tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Otolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery, Korea University Guro Hospital. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Medical Center (approval number: 2024GR0180), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Table  summarizes the clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients.
 
2.2.

## Human Primary Nasal Epithelial Cells (hPNECs) and Air–Liquid Interface (ALI) Culture
The hPNECs were obtained from individuals without signs of inflammation, allergy, asthma, or aspirin sensitivity by brushing the middle portion of the inferior turbinate during rhinoplasty. Cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) with PneumaCult‐EX Plus medium (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). At 80%–90% confluence, the cells were detached using the Animal Component‐Free Cell Dissociation Kit (ACF Kit; Stemcell Technologies) and reseeded into 75T flasks or 12‐well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. Cultures were incubated for an additional 3–4 days.
The ALI culture was performed as previously described by Park et al. [ ]. hPNECs were seeded onto Transwell inserts with a 0.4‐µm pore size (Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well. After 3–4 days of submerged culture in PneumaCult‐EX Plus medium, the medium in the upper chamber was removed to establish ALI conditions, whereas the lower chamber was replaced with PneumaCult‐ALI medium (Stemcell Technologies). The cells were maintained for at least 21 days, washed with Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (D‐PBS; Gibco, NY, USA), and the medium was changed every other day.
To assess the effect of Dupilumab on CDH26 expression, hPNECs were treated with Dupilumab at 1, 10, or 50 µg/mL for 24 h under submerged culture conditions. In addition, ALI‐cultured hPNECs were treated with 50 µg/mL Dupilumab for 24 h, and CDH26 mRNA levels were measured by quantitative PCR.

2.3.

## EoL‐1 Cell Culture and Differentiation
EoL‐1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute‐1640 (RPMI‐1640) medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in 75T flasks. After 3–4 days, cells were differentiated for 5 days in RPMI‐1640 medium containing interleukin‐3 (IL‐3) and IL‐5 at 1 ng/mL each, granulocyte‐macrophage colony–stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) at 0.5 ng/mL (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and sodium butyric acid at 0.5 mM (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) [ ].

2.4.

## Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‐seq)
The scRNA‐seq data were analyzed using two publicly available datasets: HRA000772 (control and eCRS samples) and GSE121600 (Dupilumab‐treated samples) [ , ]. Quality control procedures excluded genes expressed in fewer than three cells, cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, and cells with mitochondrial RNA expression exceeding 15%. Data normalization was performed using the LogNormalize method, and highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using RunPCA, and batch effects were corrected with RPCA integration. Cell clustering was achieved using the FindAllMarkers function and the SingleR package. Cell clusters were visualized using t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and subjected to functional enrichment analyses, including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology pathway analyses. Visualization was performed using volcano plots.

2.5.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining and Sirius Red Staining
Paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin, followed by eosin for H&E staining. For selective staining of eosinophilic granulocytes, Sirius Red staining was performed using Direct Red 80 (Sigma‐Aldrich). After staining, the slides were dehydrated and mounted using a commercial mounting medium (Sigma‐Aldrich).

2.6.

## Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining
Tissue sections and ALI cultures were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval and permeabilization. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody targeting CDH26 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Signal detection was conducted using either 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB; Zytomed Systems, Berlin, Germany) or an Alexa Fluor 568‐conjugated anti‐rabbit secondary antibody. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin or 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany).

2.7.

## Immunocytochemical (ICC) Staining
EoL‐1 cells were attached to glass slides using a cytospin centrifuge (1000 × g, 5 min) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After permeabilization, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ITGA4 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and IL‐5 receptor alpha (IL‐5Rα; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated anti‐mouse or Alexa Fluor 568‐conjugated anti‐rabbit secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal LSM.

2.8.

## Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Analysis
Total RNA was extracted from the cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus (M‐MLV) reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed with the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex System (Applied Biosystems) using the gene‐specific primers (Table  ). Gene expression levels were normalized to those of glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).
 
2.9.

## Western Blotting
Total protein was isolated using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and quantified using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against CDH26 (Invitrogen) and GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, South Korea).

2.10.

## Flow Cytometry
EoL‐1 cells were fixed with cold 2% PFA, permeabilized, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with fluorescence‐conjugated antibodies against human ITGA4 or IL‐5Rα (R&D Systems). Flow cytometric analysis was conducted using a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

2.11.

## Cell Adhesion Assay
Ninety‐six‐well enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay plates were coated overnight at 4°C with 3% BSA or recombinant CDH26 protein (Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan). After blocking with 3% BSA, differentiated EoL‐1 (diEoL‐1) cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded and allowed to adhere at 37°C for 1 h. The adherent cells were fixed with cold methanol and stained with crystal violet (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature. Staining was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 590 nm.

2.12.

## siRNA Transfection
hPNECs were cultured in PneumaCult‐EX Plus medium until 60% confluency. Before transfection, the medium was replaced with Opti‐MEM reduced serum medium (Gibco). Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to transfect CDH26‐targeting siRNA or negative control siRNA (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea), according to the manufacturer's protocol (Table  ). Transfection was performed at 37°C for 6 h. The efficiency of CDH26 gene knockdown by siRNA was confirmed using quantitative real‐time PCR (Figure ).

2.13.

## Transwell Migration Assay
hPNECs were seeded on the underside of 8‐µm pore size Transwell inserts (24‐well format; Corning) at 1 × 105 cells/well. After 4 h of attachment, the inserts were inverted to their upright orientation and cultured overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. The cells were treated with si‐CDH26 or Dupilumab and/or stimulated with IL‐4 or IL‐13. Subsequently, diEoL‐1 cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were added to the upper chamber and incubated for 4 h. The migrated cells in the lower chamber were collected, cytospun on glass slides, and stained with Diff‐Quik for visualization.

2.14.

## Statistical Analysis
All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical differences between the two groups were evaluated using an unpaired two‐tailed t‐test. One‐ or two‐way analysis of variance was used to compare multiple groups. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.4.2) and relevant packages.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Analysis of Epithelial Cell Subtypes and Cell Adhesion Molecules Expression Specific to Eosinophilic Chronic Rhinosinusitis
scRNA‐seq was performed on nasal tissue samples from 11 participants (five controls and six eCRSwNP). The t‐SNE dimensionality reduction identified five cell clusters: immune, epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial, and smooth muscle (Figure  ). In eCRSwNP tissues, the proportion of epithelial cells was reduced, whereas that of immune cells was elevated compared to those in the controls (Figure  ). Further classification of epithelial cells revealed distinct subtypes (Figure  ). Notably, DLK2/WNT10A⁺ basal cells and CST1/CD36⁺ secretory cells were increased, whereas CFAP/DNAH⁺ ciliated cells and SMR3A/MUC7⁺ goblet cells were decreased in eCRSwNP (Figure  ).
 Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis indicated the activation of pathways related to cellular junctions, including focal adhesions, tight junctions, and adherens junctions (Figure  ). Genes, such as IGHG4, POSTN, CST1, CD44, and CDH26, were significantly upregulated in eCRSwNP epithelial cells (Figure  ). Given the altered cellular junction pathway in eCRSwNP, we analyzed the expression of adherens/tight junction and focal adhesion genes (Figure  ). Although most adhesion‐related genes remained unchanged, the expression of CDH3 and CDH26 was notably elevated in the epithelial subtypes of patients with eCRSwNP (Figure  ).

3.2.

## CDH26 Expression Is Elevated in Epithelial Cells of eCRSwNP
CDH26 expression was further examined using t‐SNE and violin plot analyses (Figure  ). The expression of CDH26 was significantly higher in epithelial cells from eCRSwNP compared to controls, particularly within the DLK2/WNT10A⁺ basal and CST1/CD36⁺ secretory cell subsets (Figure  ). CDH26‐expressing cells also showed increased expression of inflammation‐related genes, including ALOX15 and POSTN (Figure  ), alongside enrichment in leukocyte cell–cell adhesion pathways (Figure  ), suggesting a potential role for CDH26 in immune cell recruitment.
 Furthermore, additional scRNA‐seq analyses revealed that CST1 expression was predominantly increased in CST1/CD36⁺ secretory cells, whereas POSTN was enriched in DLK2/WNT10A⁺ basal cells. Correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive associations between CDH26 and CST1 (Pearson r = 0.734, p = 0.0001) and between CDH26 and POSTN (Pearson r = 0.673, p = 0.0001), supporting their coordinated upregulation in eCRS epithelium (Figure ).
We also examined whether CDH26 expression is associated with epithelial barrier remodeling, we assessed the expression of mucins (MUC5AC and MUC5B) and epithelial‐derived cytokines (IL‐25, IL‐33, and TSLP). MUC5AC and MUC5B were not elevated in ECRS samples and showed no clear correlation with CDH26 expression. IL‐25 was undetectable. IL‐33 and TSLP were increased in DLK2⁺/WNT10A⁺ basal epithelial cells. CDH26 expression showed a modest positive correlation with IL‐33 (Pearson r = 0.311, p < 0.0001), but not with TSLP (Pearson r = 0.092, p < 0.0001) (Figure ).

3.3.

## Eosinophil Infiltration and CDH26 Expression in eCRSwNP Nasal Polyps
Eosinophilic infiltration was assessed by staining nasal tissue sections with H&E and Sirius Red (Figure  ). The eCRSwNP samples exhibited significantly higher numbers of epithelial eosinophils, averaging 7/HPF, compared to the other groups (p < 0.03). IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) staining confirmed elevated CDH26 expression in the nasal epithelium of patients with eCRSwNP, with a significantly stronger staining intensity than that in non‐eCRS tissues (Figure  ).
 
3.4.

## IL‐4 and IL‐13 Induce CDH26 Expression in hPNECs and ALI Cultures
To identify the cytokines capable of inducing CDH26 expression, hPNECs were treated with various inflammatory mediators. IL‐4 (100 µg/mL) and IL‐13 (100 µg/mL) produced the most robust response, increasing CDH26 mRNA expression by 27‐ and 24‐folds, respectively (p < 0.0001), peaking at 24 h (Figure  ). Western blot analysis revealed a corresponding increase in protein levels (Figure  ). Other cytokines (e.g., interferon‐gamma [IFN‐γ], tumor necrosis factor‐alpha [TNF‐α], IL‐5, and transforming growth factor‐beta 1 [TGF‐β1]) did not significantly affect CDH26 expression.
 Under ALI conditions mimicking the in vivo airway environment, IL‐4 (100 µg/mL) and IL‐13 (100 µg/mL) again induced a >5.5‐fold increase in CDH26 mRNA and a 1.6–2.0‐fold increase in protein expression (Figure  ). IHC and IF staining of ALI‐cultured cells confirmed that CDH26 expression broadly increased throughout the epithelium following IL‐4/13 stimulation (Figure  ).
In line with these findings, correlation analysis demonstrated that CDH26 expression was positively associated with Type 2 cytokines, including IL‐4 (Pearson r = 0.664, p = 0.0259), IL‐13 (Pearson r = 0.720, p = 0.0124), and IL‐5 (Pearson r = 0.680, p = 0.0001), further supporting the close link between CDH26 and Type 2 inflammatory responses in ECRS (Figure ).

3.5.

## CDH26 Expression Is Regulated by the IL‐4R Signaling Pathway
To investigate whether CDH26 expression is regulated via IL‐4Rα, hPNECs were treated with Dupilumab, an IL‐4Rα antagonist. After 24 h of treatment, CDH26 mRNA levels were not suppressed by 1 or 10 µg/mL Dupilumab but were significantly inhibited at 50 µg/mL (p < 0.0001) (Figure  ). At this concentration, CDH26 protein expression was reduced to control levels after 48 h (Figure  ).
 In ALI cultures, IL‐4 (100 µg/mL) and IL‐13 (100 µg/mL) increased CDH26 mRNA levels by 9.8‐ (p = 0.0002) and 7.5‐fold (p < 0.0001), respectively, at 24 h. Dupilumab treatment significantly suppressed this upregulation, although the levels remained higher than the baseline levels (Figure  ). CDH26 protein expression was also significantly reduced by Dupilumab at 48 h in IL‐4‐ and IL‐13‐treated groups (p = 0.01) (Figure  ), confirming IL‐4Rα signaling as a key regulator of CDH26 induction.
To assess whether Dupilumab regulates CDH26, hPNECs were treated with Dupilumab alone at different concentrations. No significant changes in CDH26 expression were observed under either submerged or ALI conditions, confirming that Dupilumab does not affect CDH26 levels (Figure ).
To validate the effect of Dupilumab, we analyzed a publicly available single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset of nasal epithelial cells. CDH26 and POSTN were upregulated in ECRS samples and suppressed following Dupilumab treatment. CST1 remained largely unchanged, whereas IL‐33 and TSLP showed no significant changes, and IL‐25 was not detected (Figure ).

3.6.

## CDH26 Binds to ITGA4 and Promotes Eosinophil Adhesion
We examined whether CDH26 interacts with ITGA4 in eosinophils. Flow cytometry and mRNA analyses confirmed ITGA4 expression in both undifferentiated and diEoL‐1 cells, with higher expression observed in differentiated cells (90.49% vs. 78.21%) (Figure  ). diEoL‐1 cells also expressed IL5RA and exhibited smaller cell and nuclear sizes.
 A cell adhesion assay demonstrated that diEoL‐1 cells adhered to recombinant CDH26‐coated wells in a dose‐dependent manner during a 1‐h incubation, with 1.8‐ (p = 0.03) and 2.2‐fold (p = 0.002) increases at 2 and 5 µg/mL, respectively, compared to BSA‐coated controls (Figure  ), supporting a functional interaction between CDH26 and eosinophil ITGA4.

3.7.

## CDH26 Facilitates Eosinophil Transepithelial Migration
To evaluate whether CDH26 mediates eosinophil transepithelial migration, a Transwell migration assay was performed using IL‐4‐ or IL‐13‐stimulated hPNEC monolayers (Figure  ). The migration of diEoL‐1 cells from the upper to the lower chamber across the hPNEC monolayers increased 3.8‐ (p < 0.0001) and 6‐fold (p < 0.03), respectively, under IL‐4 and IL‐13 stimulation (Figure  ). This enhancement was abolished by si‐CDH26 transfection or Dupilumab treatment, with migration returning to baseline levels (p < 0.02). These results indicate that CDH26 upregulation facilitates eosinophil migration across the airway epithelium, and its inhibition effectively reduces this process.
 

4.

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that CDH26 plays a crucial role in the transepithelial migration of eosinophils in patients with eCRSwNP. Through scRNA‐seq, we initially observed a marked upregulation of CDH26 expression in the nasal epithelium of patients with eCRSwNP. This observation was reinforced by histological analyses that showed increased eosinophil infiltration and CDH26 expression in the NP epithelium. Functional in vitro assays further supported a direct mechanistic link between CDH26 and eosinophil migration, as CDH26 potentially promotes eosinophil adhesion through ITGA4 binding and facilitates their transmigration across the epithelial barrier. Importantly, CDH26 expression was potently induced by Th2 cytokines (IL‐4 and IL‐13) and was attenuated by IL‐4Rα blockade using Dupilumab, suggesting that CDH26 functions as a downstream effector in the Th2‐inflammatory signaling cascade. Collectively, these results identify CDH26 as a previously underrecognized but pivotal mediator in eCRSwNP pathogenesis.
Eosinophils are immune cells that are primarily involved in parasitic infections and allergic reactions. Once matured in the bone marrow, eosinophils enter the bloodstream and are activated by cytokines, such as IL‐5 [ , ]. Upon peripheral circulation, eosinophils undergo transendothelial migration and move across the epithelial barrier toward the targeted tissues, where the epithelial surfaces come into contact with the external environment [ , ]. Eosinophils contribute to eCRSwNP by accumulating in the nasal mucosa and releasing cytotoxic granules and cytokines that disrupt the epithelial barrier function [ , , , , , ]. These mediators downregulate adhesion molecules, such as E‐cadherin and claudins, promoting further eosinophil recruitment and activation through autocrine and paracrine signaling. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of eosinophils and their mediators in impairing epithelial barrier function. However, despite their well‐characterized effector functions within tissues, the molecular mechanisms regulating their transepithelial migration remain inadequately understood.
The significance of the transepithelial migration of immune cells under inflammatory conditions is well recognized. Neutrophil transepithelial migration has been extensively studied in various organs, including the intestines and lungs, where organ‐specific differences have been observed [ , ]. In respiratory syncytial virus infections, neutrophils migrate toward airway epithelial cells, adhere to them, form clusters, and subsequently exacerbate epithelial damage, thereby worsening disease severity [ ]. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), both chronic and acute inflammation increase epithelial permeability, resulting in persistent diarrhea [ ]. The severity of IBD is closely associated with neutrophil transepithelial migration, which contributes to pathological features such as cryptitis and crypt abscesses [ ].
These findings underscore the pivotal role of immune cell migration across epithelial barriers in initiating and exacerbating mucosal inflammation. Although eosinophil transepithelial migration is less well‐characterized than neutrophil migration, this process likely plays a critical role in the pathogenesis and progression of inflammatory diseases.
Previous studies on cadherins have primarily focused on their roles in cell–cell adhesion and the maintenance of cytoskeletal structures [ ]. However, recent research suggests that cadherins also participate in signaling pathways involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses [ , ]. Although the specific role of epithelial adhesion molecules, such as CDH26, in guiding eosinophils across epithelial barriers remains poorly understood, our findings build upon previous reports highlighting CDH26's predominant expression in epithelial tissues and its upregulation in eosinophil‐associated diseases, such as eosinophilic gastritis. Additionally, CDH26 has been implicated in eosinophil recruitment in asthma models [ , ]. These findings imply that CDH26 may play a broader role in maintaining epithelial integrity, particularly in eosinophil adhesion and migration.
The airway epithelium serves as a physical barrier against inhaled pathogens and allergens, while simultaneously interacting with underlying immune and mesenchymal cells [ ]. Inflammatory conditions, such as eCRSwNP, compromise epithelial integrity, leading to increased permeability and downregulation of junctional proteins. However, some adhesion molecules, including those involved in tissue remodeling and fibrosis, may be compensatory upregulated during chronic inflammation [ ]. This context provides a plausible mechanism for the increased expression of CDH26 in response to Th2 inflammation, not only as a structural component but also as a mediator of immune cell interactions and barrier modulation.
Notably, we confirmed that CDH26 expression was particularly enriched in DLK2⁺ basal and CST1⁺ secretory cell subsets in eCRSwNP tissue, which are the very subtypes that showed an increased proportion in our scRNA‐seq dataset of eCRSwNP. IHC and IF staining showed that CDH26 expression increased throughout the epithelium, and this widespread distribution could be attributed to the expansion of secretory cell populations and the strong expression of CDH26. This indicates that both quantitative and cell type–specific shifts in the epithelium may contribute to the overall elevation of CDH26 levels, highlighting the convergence of cellular remodeling and molecular regulation in eCRSwNP. Furthermore, the distinct expression pattern of CDH26 in eCRSwNP compared to other CRS subtypes highlights its potential as a biomarker for disease classification and severity stratification. Because the functional role of CDH26 is better understood, it may offer a novel therapeutic target for patients with eosinophilic inflammation.
In summary, our findings demonstrated that CDH26 upregulation in nasal epithelial cells, driven by Th2 cytokines, facilitates eosinophil adhesion and promotes transepithelial migration via interactions with ITGA4. This mechanism contributes to mucosal eosinophilic inflammation in eCRSwNP. By identifying CDH26 as a novel mediator of eosinophil trafficking, this study provides new insights into eCRSwNP pathogenesis and highlights CDH26 as a potential therapeutic target. Further in vivo investigations are warranted to validate its role in eosinophilic inflammation and to assess whether the therapeutic modulation of CDH26 can attenuate NP development.

(B) hPNECs cultured under airliquid interface (ALI) conditions were treated with 50 µg/mL Dupilumab for 24 h. CDH26 mRNA expression remained unchanged relative to control cells.
