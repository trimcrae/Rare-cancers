MOCOS gene, leading to dual deficiency of xanthine dehydrogenase and
aldehyde oxidase. To establish a robust animal model for this condition, we generated
Mocos knock-in (KI) rats carrying the Arg419Ter nonsense mutation

anemia, and reduced survival, with all individuals dying by 14 weeks of age. Biochemical
analyses revealed elevated levels of hypoxanthine and xanthine, along with decreased uric
acid in both serum and urine, confirming xanthinuria. Homozygous KI rats also showed
increased blood creatinine (CRE) and urea nitrogen (UN), and decreased urinary CRE and UN,
indicating renal dysfunction. Histopathological examination showed obstructive nephropathy
characterized by tubular atrophy, crystal deposition, and inflammation. Compared to
existing mouse models, Mocos KI rats demonstrated extended lifespan,
enabling more detailed investigation of disease mechanisms. This rat model provides a
valuable tool for studying the pathogenesis of xanthinuria type II and exploring potential
therapeutic strategies.

## Introduction
Purines are important components of nucleotides. During nucleotide catabolism, phosphate
and ribose are recycled, while purines are broken down by oxidization and finally excreted
as uric acid. In this metabolic process, xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH, EC:1.17.1.4) has a
crucial role in the oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Defects
in XDH lead to the accumulation of hypoxanthine/xanthine and depletion of uric acid in
urine, resulting in xanthinuria.
Xanthinuria is a rare hereditary disorder with unknown prevalence, but about 150 cases have
been described so far [ ]. Xanthinuria is divided into
two forms: type I and type II. The xanthinuria type I is caused by XDH deficiency alone due
to mutations in the XDH gene. Type II is caused by a dual deficiency of XDH
and aldehyde oxidase (AOX1, EC: EC:1.2.3.1). The deficiency results from mutations in a
single gene, MOCOS [ ], because
MOCOS (molybdenum cofactor sulfatase, EC:2.8.1.9) catalyzes the
sulfurization of molybdenum cofactor of XDH and AOX1, which is required for their
activities.
Both forms of xanthinuria lead to an identical clinical phenotype: accumulation of xanthine
and depletion of uric acid in blood and urine. Although about two-thirds of affected
individuals are asymptomatic, the remaining third form xanthine stones in the kidney, which
result in varying degrees of nephrolithiasis, hydronephrosis, and renal failure [ ]. There is no curative treatment, but a low purine diet
and a high fluid intake are recommended.
To date, four variants associated with xanthinuria type II have been identified in human
MOCOS gene. An Arg419Ter nonsense variant has been identified in two
unrelated Japanese patients [ ]. An Ala57Pro missense
variant has been identified in a Japanese family [ ].
An Arg776Cys missense variant has been identified in a Bedouin Arab family with xanthinuria
[ ]. This variant has also been found in an
unrelated woman of Jewish descent who harbors a frameshift mutation (Gln347fsTer379),
suggesting she is compound heterozygous for both mutations.
In addition, MOCOS gene variants associated with xanthinuria have been
identified in several domestic animal species. A frameshift mutation was first described in
the Tyrolean Grey breed of cattle [ ], and the same
mutation was later detected in Brown Swiss cattle [ ],
suggesting that this variant is prevalent among certain European breeds. A homozygous
missense variant (Asp303Gly) has also been reported in goats [ ]. In dogs, multiple variants have been identified: a splice variant leading to
exon 2 skipping in Manchester Terriers, a missense variant (Leu46Pro) in Dachshunds, and a
frameshift variant (Ala128Glyfs*30) in Cavalier King Charles Spaniels and English Cocker
Spaniels [ ]. All of these mutations have been
detected in homozygous form in affected animals or as compound heterozygotes, supporting an
idea that MOCOS variants causing xanthinuria result in loss of
function.
To test this hypothesis and develop a model for xanthinuria type II, Mocos
knockout (KO) mice have been generated [ ]. They
exhibit xanthinuria and obstructive nephropathy, with a significantly shortened lifespan of
less than 4 weeks. Although the Mocos KO mice are potential models of
xanthinuria, their short lifespan limits their availability in experiments with
intervention.
Similarly to mice, rats are widely used in biomedical research, but their physiological
characteristics differ from those of mice. Aiming to extend the lifespan and establish a
better model of xanthinuria type II, we attempted to generate Mocos
gene-edited rats with CRISPR/Cas9 system. To mimic a human case, we focused on the Arg419Ter
nonsense variant, which has been identified in Japanese patients with xanthinuria type II
[ ]. We introduced a knock-in (KI) allele, AGG (Arg)
to TGA (Ter), using CRISPR/Cas9 with long single-stranded oligonucleotide (lssODN). The
Mocos KI rats exhibited growth retardation, anemia, and xanthinuria.
Obstructive nephropathy was also observed. The Mocos KI rats survived up to
14 weeks of age, which enhances their utility for investigating the pathogenesis and
developing curative treatments for xanthinuria.

## Materials and Methods

## Animal experiment
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Tokyo University
of Agriculture (Approval number: 2022130) and The University of Tokyo (Approval number:
A23-34), and were conducted in accordance with their regulations on animal
experimentation.

## Generation of Mocos KI rats
Mocos KI rats were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 system as previously
described [ ]. Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed by
Optimized CRISPR Design (crispr.mit.edu) and synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies,
Inc. (Coralville, IA, USA). LssODNs were prepared using a LsODN Preparation Kit
(Biodynamics Laboratory Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Cas9 protein was purchased from Integrated
DNA Technologies. Pronuclear-stage embryos of F344/Jcl rats were obtained through natural
mating. Female rats with vaginal plugs were euthanized by CO2 inhalation
followed by cervical dislocation, and their oviducts were collected. Embryos were flushed
out from the ampullae using culture medium. Cas9 protein, lssODNs, and gRNAs were
introduced into the embryos using a super electroporator NEPA 21 (NEPA GENE Co., Ltd.,
Ichikawa, Chiba, Japan). The sequences and positions of the induced mutation, lssODNs and
gRNAs are presented in A and B . Embryos that developed to the two-cell stage were transferred into the oviducts of
pseudopregnant female rats anesthetized with isoflurane. Genotyping of the offspring was
performed using the following primer set: 5′-GTGGCCAATGTGAAGGAGTT-3′ and
5′-AATGCCTCCTACCAACAACG-3′ ( ). Founder
rats were mated with F344/Jcl rats and F1 heterozygous rats were intercrossed to obtain F2
progeny.

## RNA preparation and quantitative PCR
Total RNA was isolated using ISOGEN II (NIPPON GENE Co., Ltd., Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan)
according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized
using FastGene Scriptase II (NIPPON Genetics Co., Ltd.). Real-time PCR was performed using
the Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (Takara Bio, Inc.) with the KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR
Kit (Roche Diagnostics K.K., Minato, Tokyo, Japan). Primer sequences for quantification of
Mocos were 5′-CAGTAGCATCCAGCCGATCC-3′ and
5′-GTCACCTCAAACGCAGCACAG-3′.
The number of target molecules in each test sample was analyzed by monitoring the
amplification curves of both test and reference samples, each containing 101 to
106 molecules of the gene of interest, as previously described [ ]. The number of target molecules was normalized to
that of peptidylprolyl isomerase A (Ppia), which served as an internal
control.

## Genotyping of the Arg419Ter mutation
The CycleavePCR method was used to genotype the Arg419Ter mutation. This method detects
fluorescent signals from allele-specific DNA-RNA chimera probes that are digested by
thermostable RNaseH following hybridization with amplicon [ ]. The primer sequences to obtain amplicon are 5′-CACAACCAGACGTTTCTGAC-3′ and
5′-ACTACAGGGTCTTGCACTGA-3′. The probe for detecting the wild-type allele was
5′-CACCTG(A)GG-3′ with 3′ end labelled with FAM. The probe for detecting the mutant allele
was 5′- CAGTTC(A)CAG-3′ with 3′ end labelled with ROX. RNA bases in the probes are
indicated in parentheses. Real-time PCR was performed with the CycleavePCR Reaction Mix
(Takara Bio, Inc., Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan).

## Body weight measurement and survival rate
Body weights of homozygous (male: n=8, female: n=5), heterozygous (male: n=6, female:
n=6), and wild-type (male: n=6, female: n=5) rats were measured weekly from 4 to 16 weeks
of age. Rats were provided with standard chow (CE-2, Japan CLEA, Tokyo, Japan) and tap
water ad libitum. Survival rates were calculated by recording the number
of rats that died during the experimental period. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests
were generated using the EZR (Easy R) plugin for R Commander [ ].

## Hematology and Urine analysis
Blood samples were collected in EDTA-2K tubes (Capiject II, Terumo Corporation, Tokyo,
Japan) and analyzed using an automated veterinary hematology analyzer, XN-1000V (Sysmex
Corporation, Kobe, Japan). To collect urine and feces, rats at 7 weeks of age were
individually housed in metabolic cages (Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for
three nights, with free access to standard chow and tap water. Urine and feces were
collected every morning, and their volumes were measured. Food and water intake were also
recorded. Urine biochemical analysis was performed at the Nagahama Institute of Bioscience
(OrientalBio Co., Ltd., Nagahama, Japan). Xanthine and hypoxanthine levels were determined
using the Xanthine/Hypoxanthine Assay Kit (ab155900; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), following the
manufacturer’s instructions.

## Serum biochemistry
Blood samples were collected from the abdominal vein of male rats (9–12 weeks of age)
under isoflurane anesthesia and were coagulated in Venoject II tubes (Terumo Corporation,
Tokyo, Japan). Serum was collected after centrifuge at 10,000 × g for 5 min. Blood urea
nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CRE) were measured at Nagahama Institute of Bioscience
(OrientalBio Co., Ltd.).

## Histological and immunohistochemical analysis
Rats (7–8 weeks of age) were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia. Tissue samples
harvested from the kidney, liver, adrenal glands, spleen, and brain were fixed in 10%
neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections 4 µm thick
were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). After pretreatment by autoclaving in
citrate buffer (pH 6.0), immunohistochemistry for ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule
1 (IbaI) was performed on 4 µm paraffin sections using a HISTOSTAINER 36A
(Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). Sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature
with a rabbit anti-Iba1 polyclonal antibody (1:750; Code No. 019-19741, FUJIFILM Wako Pure
Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Following incubation in 3% H2O2
for 15 min at room temperature, bound antibodies were detected using Histofine Simplestain
Rat MAX-PO(R) (Nichirei Biosciences) and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB Substrate Kit;
Nichirei Biosciences). Images were acquired using an Olympus VS-120 virtual slide scanner
(Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical comparisons between experimental groups were performed using one-way or
two-way ANOVA. The post-hoc testing was performed by the Mann-Whitney U
test. Survival analyses were conducted using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were
conducted using the EZR package [ ]. A
P value <0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Development of Mocos KI rats harboring the Arg419Ter nonsense
mutation
To develop a rat model of xanthinuria and investigate the underlying mechanisms, we
generated Mocos KI rats harboring the Arg419Ter nonsense mutation using
the CRISPR/Cas9 system ( ). The mutation
(AGG>TGA) was introduced by transferring lssODNs along with gRNAs to embryos ( ). We obtained two
Mocos-mutated rat strains. One strain,
F344-Mocosem1Tua, harbored the Arg419Ter mutation. The
other strain, F344-Mocosem2Tua, carried a 434 bp deletion that
included the target site. For further investigation, we used the
F344-Mocosem1Tua strain, as it harbored the Arg419Ter
mutation identical to the variant found in Japanese patients [ ]. Mocos mRNA was significantly reduced in the liver
of the KI line. The relative expression levels compared to wild-type F344 rats was 0.56 ±
0.08 (P<0.01) in F344-Mocosem1Tua ( ). The significant reduction in mRNA levels
is likely due to nonsense-mediated decay.

## Growth retardation and reduced survival in Mocos KI rats
Rats homozygous for the nonsense mutation exhibited reduced body size from the time of
weaning ( ). Their body weight was significantly lower than that of heterozygous or wild-type
rats in both sexes. Notably, homozygous rats showed impaired weight gain after 5 weeks of
age, indicating severe growth retardation ( ). All homozygous rats died between 7 and 14 weeks of age, with an average
lifespan of 12.6 ± 2.0 weeks. In contrast, heterozygous rats did not differ in body weight
or lifespan from wild-type rats ( B and
C). There was no apparent phenotypic difference observed between males and females.

## Polydipsia and polyuria in homozygous Mocos KI rats
Obstructive nephropathy has been reported in Mocos-deficient mice [ ]. Impaired renal reabsorption under such conditions
can lead to polyuria and dehydration. To assess these symptoms, we measured water and food
intake, as well as urine and feces output. No significant differences were observed in
food intake or feces output among homozygous, heterozygous, and wild-type rats ( A and B ). In contrast, water intake and urine volume normalized to body weight were
markedly elevated in homozygous rats compared to heterozygous and wild-type controls
( C and D). These findings indicate that
homozygous Mocos KI rats exhibit polydipsia and polyuria.

## Biochemical evidence of xanthinuria in homozygous Mocos KI
rats
Urine hypoxanthine/xanthine concentrations were significantly higher in homozygous
Mocos KI rats compared to heterozygous and wild-type rats ( ). Consistently, uric acid–the final metabolite of purine metabolism–was
significantly lower in homozygous rats than in heterozygous or wild-type rats ( ). We also observed significantly elevated
hypoxanthine/xanthine and reduced uric acid levels in the serum of homozygous rats
compared to heterozygous and wild-type controls ( C and D). These findings indicate that homozygous Mocos KI rats
developed xanthinuria.

## Hematological abnormalities in Mocos KI rats
As anemia is common in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [ ] and Mocos KO mice have been reported to exhibit
anemia [ ], we performed hematological analysis of
Mocos KI rats. Red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB), and
hematocrit (HCT) values were significantly decreased in Mocos KI
homozygous rats compared to heterozygous and wild-type rats ( ). Interestingly, RBC, HGB, and HCT values in heterozygous rats were also
significantly lower than wild-type controls.
White blood cell (WBC) count was significantly increased in homozygous KI rats. Notably,
neutrophil (NEUT) counts were elevated, suggesting systemic inflammation in homozygous
rats ( ).

## Obstructive nephropathy in homozygous Mocos KI rats
Urine biochemistry demonstrated significantly higher albumin concentration and
significantly lower levels of CRE and urea nitrogen in homozygous Mocos
KI rats compared to heterozygous and wild-type rats, indicating impaired glomerulus
function ( A–C ). Additionally, homozygous rats exhibited significantly lower concentrations of
electrolytes, including sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, and phosphate ( D–H), suggesting impaired reabsorption of
water and electrolytes in the proximal tubule. In addition to urine biochemistry, serum
biochemistry demonstrated significantly higher levels of BUN and CRE in homozygous rats
compared to heterozygous and wild-type controls, indicating the nephropathy in
Mocos KI homozygous rats ( ).
Consistent with urine and serum biochemistry, we observed significant renal abnormalities
in homozygous rats. At necropsy, the kidney of wild-type rats appeared normal ( A and C ), whereas those of homozygous rats showed a rough and pale surface and appeared
atrophic, with thinner cortex ( B and D).
Histopathological examination revealed numerous renal tubules exhibiting atrophy or cystic
dilation in the renal parenchyma of homozygous rats, accompanied by interstitial fibrosis
and infiltration of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages ( E and F). From the cortex to the medulla, the dilated
tubular lumens contained characteristic deposits of various sizes, including round to
irregularly shaped lamellated eosinophilic materials with a basophilic rim and/or
unstained crystal-shaped clefts ( E and
F). These findings were associated with tubular disruption, detachment or loss of tubular
epithelium, accumulation of cellular debris with neutrophils, and formation of urinary
casts ( E and F). In addition,
infiltration of foreign body-type multinucleated giant cells phagocytizing deposits or
crystals was frequently observed ( ),
which was rarely seen in other rat nephropathy models, including Alport syndrome and
cystic fibrosis [ , ]. The number and size of glomeruli were reduced, with expansion of
Bowman’s space and thickening of Bowman’s capsule, although sclerotic glomeruli were
rarely observed. Other organs appeared normal in homozygous rats. No significant
histopathological changes were observed in any organs of heterozygous rats. Taken
together, these findings indicate that homozygous Mocos KI rats suffered
from obstructive nephropathy.

## Discussion
In the present study, we generated Mocos KI rats carrying Arg419Ter
nonsense mutation which was identified in Japanese patients with xanthinuria. The
gene-edited homozygous rats exhibited severe growth retardation and failed to survive beyond
14 weeks of age. They showed clear biochemical features of xanthinuria, including elevated
hypoxanthine/xanthine and reduced uric acid levels in both serum and urine. Additionally,
lamellate deposits were observed in the renal tubules. These findings demonstrate that the
Mocos KI rats recapitulate the phenotype of xanthinuria type II.
The Arg419Ter nonsense variant was identified in two unrelated Japanese xanthinuria type II
patients [ ]. Both patients exhibited hypouricemia and
undetectable urinary uric acid. Their serum xanthine levels were 2.1 mg/dl and 0.17 mg/dl,
respectively, compared to 0.077 ± 0.032 mg/dl in healthy individuals. Oxypurinol was not
detected in the serum or urine of either patient following allopurinol loading, leading to a
diagnosis of xanthinuria type II. Although our Mocos KI rats were not
subjected to an alloprinol loading test, they mimicked the serum and urinary phenotypes of
the patients, supporting that the Arg419Ter variant was causative and validating the
homozygous KI rats as a model of the xanthinuria type II.
Nonetheless, the clinical symptoms in Mocos KI rats were more severe than
those observed in human patients. Homozygous rats showed marked growth retardation after
weaning and did not survive beyond 16 weeks. Similarly, mouse models of xanthinuria,
including Xdh KO (type I) and Mocos KO (type II) mice,
exhibited stunted growth and die before 6 weeks of age [ , ]. In constant, human patients with
xanthinuria are often asymptomatic and rarely progress to renal failure [ ]. These phenotypic differences suggest that the
development and severity of xanthinuria may vary among species. Indeed, purine metabolism
has been known to differ between human and rodents. Hypoxanthine phosphoribosyl transferase
(HPRT), which converts hypoxanthine into inosine monophosphate and reuses it in the purine
salvage pathway, exhibits higher activity in humans than in mice [ , ]. Therefore, hypoxanthine
may accumulate more readily in rodents. Since hypoxanthine inhibits the synthesis of
nicotinamide mononucleotide, a precursor of NAD+, low HPRT activity may lead to
NAD+ depletion [ ] and potentially
contributing to the shortened lifespan observed in xanthinuria model mice such as
Xdh and Mocos KO mice [ ]. We hypothesize that differences in lifespan among mouse, rat, and human
MOCOS mutants may reflect species-specific variations in HPRT
activity.
Mocos KI rats displayed significantly reduced RBC, HGB, and HCT values
compared to heterozygous and wild-type rats. These erythrocyte-related values were outside
the normal range established across approximately 200 rat strains [ , ], indicating that
homozygous Mocos KI rats were anemic. Anemia is common in patients with CKD
and is typically caused by reduced erythropoietin (EPO) production, inflammation, and
oxidative stress in the kidney. It is likely that renal dysfunction in homozygous rats led
to decreased EPO production, resulting in anemia.
Interestingly, heterozygous Mocos KI rats also showed significantly lower
RBC, HGB, and HCT values than wild-type rats, despite not exhibiting xanthinuria.
MOCOS deficiency inactivates XDH, which plays a key role in regulating
reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ], and ROS are
critical mediators of hematopoiesis [ ]. We
hypothesize that ROS activity may affect hematopoiesis in heterozygous rats, or that EPO
production may be impaired despite the absence of overt histopathological changes in the
kidney. Further investigations, including measurement of ROS and EPO levels, are needed to
elucidate the mechanisms underlying the reduced erythrocyte parameters in both heterozygous
and homozygous rats.
We observed various deposits in the renal tubules of homozygous rats. The deposits were
surrounded by the lamellated eosinophilic material with a basophilic rim, which closely
resemble those reported in Mocos KO mice [ ]. Given that elevated hypoxanthine/xanthine concentrations in the urine of
Mocos KI rats, these deposits are likely xanthine crystal
precipitates.
Crystal deposition in the kidney can cause injury when formed or retained within the renal
structures, leading to acute and chronic kidney disorders referred to as crystalline
nephropathies [ ]. Crystal nephropathies have been
classified based on the location of crystal deposition: renal parenchyma (type 1), nephron
(type 2), or urinary tract (type 3) [ ]. The type 2
crystalline nephropathy involves endogenous metabolites such as xanthin and it is
characterized by acute necroinflammation and tubular obstruction, often accompanied by
multinucleated giant cells. Our morphological data showed that the deposits were located
within the tubules, appeared to obstruct them, and were associated with multinucleated giant
cells and widespread renal inflammation. These findings suggest that Mocos
KI rats are classified as type 2 crystal nephropathy.
In summary, we generated Mocos KI rats harboring the Arg419Ter nonsense
variant. These rats exhibited severe growth retardation, anemia, and xanthinuria. In
addition, Mocos KI rats exhibited obstructive nephropathy, in which
xanthine crystals were often phagocytized by multinucleated macrophages. Notably,
Mocos KI rats demonstrated extended survival compared to the mouse model,
providing a valuable platform for more precise and comprehensive investigations into the
molecular mechanisms underlying xanthinuria and obstructive nephropathy.
