## Abstract

## Background:
Radiotherapy (RT) is a crucial treatment for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Recent pre-clinical studies highlight the potential of combining RT with immunotherapy due to RT-induced activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP-synthase (cGAS)/Stimulator of Interferon (IFN) Genes (STING) pathway, yet the clinical implications remain unclear.

## Methods:
In this study, we utilized a cGAS-mNeongreen knock-in tumor model, human HNSCC cell lines and serial tumor biopsies from HNSCC patients to investigate RT-induced cGAS activation and re-localization. cGAS positive micronuclei and IFN stimulated gene (ISG) induction were quantified. In HNSCC patient biopsies, cGAS positive micronuclei were identified in different cellular compartments and IFN/ISG and NF-κB serum levels were correlated to survival. Differences were tested using paired t-tests, Wilcoxon test, One-way and two-way ANOVA or Kaplan Meier curves.

## Results:
Here we show that cGAS positive foci present in micronuclei increase post-RT in both mouse tumor models (P = 0.0305; t-test) and HNSCC patient biopsies (P = 0.0012; Wilcoxon test), concurrently with higher ISG expression. Notably, cGAS re-localization in HNSCC patient biopsies is most prominent in immune cell-infiltrated tumor regions (P = 0.0061; Wilcoxon test). Enhanced systemic type I IFN inducing activity is observed post-RT (P = 0.0040; mixed-effects analysis) and correlates with improved survival (OS P = 0.0146; log-rank test; DFS P = 0.0256; Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test), particularly in HPV positive patients (OS P = 0.0017; log-rank test; DFS P = 0.043; log-rank test).

## Conclusions:
These findings suggest that cGAS re-localization and systemic IFN responses may serve as biomarkers for RT efficacy, providing a basis for optimizing HNSCC treatment protocols, especially when integrated with immunotherapy.

Subject terms: Head and neck cancer, Radiotherapy, Tumour immunology, Cancer microenvironment

Mekers et al. examine how the immune sensor cGAS responds to radiotherapy in mouse models, human cancer cell lines, and patient tumor samples. Radiotherapy enhances cGAS activity and immune signaling, which is linked to improved survival, particularly in HPV positive patients.

## Plain language summary
Radiotherapy (RT) is a common treatment for head and neck cancer, but patients do not all benefit equally. Research suggests that RT can also stimulate the immune system, which may improve the effectiveness of treatment. In this study, we examined how a protein involved in the immune system called cGAS behaves after RT, using mouse models, human cancer cells, and samples from people with cancer. We found that RT increased cGAS activity and boosted the activity of the immune system in both mice and patients. Patients who showed stronger immune responses after treatment tended to live longer. These results indicate that measuring immune activity could help guide treatment and improve outcomes, especially in combination with immunotherapy.

## Introduction
Radiotherapy (RT), next to surgery, is a cornerstone of treatment for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), exerting its cytotoxic effects by damaging the DNA of dividing cancer cells. However, it has become increasingly evident that the benefits of RT extend beyond its direct impact on tumor cells. A growing body of evidence points towards an intriguing phenomenon known as a systemic “RT-induced anti-tumor immune response” or “abscopal effect”, wherein local irradiation not only eliminates the targeted tumor cells but also evokes systemic immune-mediated tumor control – . HNSCC is often caused by risk factors such as smoking and alcohol abuse or human papillomavirus (HPV) infection . The success of RT varies with site and stage of the tumor . For advanced stages, other treatment modalities combined with RT are necessary. In HNSCC, immunotherapy (IT) in combination with RT is being explored, however, such treatment options are not yet successful, and further knowledge is needed .
Clinical radiation schedules are generally conventional with relatively low dose (1.8–2 Gy) per fraction, delivered every weekday, to a total dose of 66–70 Gy in 6.5–7 weeks. For palliative RT radiation schedules, the dose per fraction is higher . Lately, moderate hypofractionated RT, consisting of less fractions, with a higher dose per fraction, is also more often employed in curative settings , . Preclinical mouse models have shown that hypofractionated RT (e.g., 2 fractions of 5 Gy or single dose 8 Gy fraction) can enhance immune activation, including recruitment of dendritic cells, CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and type I IFN induction, compared to conventional fractionation – . Furthermore, combinations of hypofractionated RT with immunotherapies elicit stronger CD8⁺ T cell responses and systemic abscopal effects in murine models, often via type I IFN-dependent mechanisms , . However, in some tumor contexts, hypofractionated RT may paradoxically enhance immunosuppression, suggesting that tumor intrinsic features in the TME critically influence outcomes . In patients, evidence on the immunogenic potential of hypofractionated RT remains scarce , with existing studies limited to small cohorts of advanced-stage cases, underscoring the need to refine dosing strategies according to tumor immunogenicity.
RT results in the activation of different biological and cellular processes, which can impact immune activation, as shown preclinically when RT and IT are combined – . With the introduction of IT in the clinic, gaining more insight in the mechanisms behind the success or failure of RT-induced anti-tumor immune responses is necessary.
The cyclic GMP-AMP Synthase (cGAS)/Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) pathway is a key pathway linking RT to immune induction. Through its ability to sense double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and cytosolic DNA species derived from pathogens or damaged genomic material, cGAS serves as a critical mechanism against infections and cellular stress . The recognition of dsDNA leads to the formation of cGAS dimers, which undergo a conformational change enabling the synthesis of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) from ATP and GTP . This second messenger cGAMP then binds to the adapter protein STING, located on the endoplasmic reticulum, activating downstream signaling pathways that either lead to the expression of type I interferons (IFNs) followed by the stimulation of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) or other inflammatory cytokine pathways (via the transcription factor Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB)) . These soluble factors serve as powerful orchestrators of immune responses . cGAS/STING pathway-associated genes are affected in numerous types of malignancies, showing its important role in cancer . In addition, aberrant cGAS/STING signaling can lead to autoimmunity, which emphasizes the role of cGAS in maintaining a proper immune balance , . Multiple murine studies have demonstrated that intact cGAS/STING signaling is required for RT to elicit systemic anti-tumor immunity, whereas STING-deficient tumors or hosts fail to mount a durable response . In human tumors, observational studies link higher cGAS/STING pathway activity with improved prognosis in certain cancers, but variability in detection methods and tumor heterogeneity limit direct clinical translation . As activation of cGAS is a crucial first step in this process, more insight into the optimal conditions of cGAS activation could possibly predict the induction of an RT-induced immune response.
Different mechanisms trigger cGAS activation. Activation can occur in response to the presence of bacterial or viral DNA , , the introduction of mitochondrial DNA into the cytoplasm , the damage and subsequent release of nuclear DNA into micronuclei or the cytoplasm or the intercellular transfer and release of DNA into the cytoplasm . Interestingly, the activation of cGAS appears to be closely tied to its subcellular localization. Specifically, cGAS located within the nucleus is restricted by chromatins and is likely inactive , , while cGAS found in the cytoplasm, especially upon re-localization to micronuclei and subsequent multimerization, can exhibit potent activity . The process of cGAS activation is dynamic and highly sensitive to numerous factors including cell type, cell cycle stage, overall cellular condition, and levels of stress – . Earlier cell culture and xenograft studies have suggested that nuclear retention of cGAS after RT can limit downstream signaling, whereas cytoplasmic relocalization is associated with stronger IFN induction , . However, these studies often relied on cGAS overexpression systems, which may not reflect physiological regulation and can lead to artefacts in activation kinetics. These limitations make it challenging to accurately study endogenous signaling dynamics. The increasing accessibility of CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing has enabled precise insertion of fluorescent tags at the endogenous locus, allowing for unbiased analysis of protein localization and function under endogenous regulatory control.
Preclinical studies show that robust activation of cGAS plays a pivotal role in achieving optimal immune activation induced by RT . However, how cGAS localization and activation are influenced by RT in the clinical setting, and how these factors are interconnected with the production of ISGs within the context of tumor-immune interactions, is largely unknown. This knowledge will provide valuable insights into designing RT regimens that maximize immune stimulation, thereby enhancing their efficacy in cancer treatment, also in combination with IT.
In recent years, the incidence of HNSCC has been increasing among a younger subgroup (age <45 years), which is predominantly linked to HPV infection . HPV positive HNSCC cells are often more sensitive to RT compared to HPV negative cells . Additionally, HPV-associated proteins, such as viral protein E7, are known to inhibit the cGAS/STING pathway , . Moreover, HPV positive tumors present with a different immune microenvironment compared to HPV negative tumors , . Although both animal and patient studies have examined immune responses in HPV positive versus HPV negative HNSCC, most have focused on general immune infiltration or checkpoint expression , with few assessing cGAS/STING activation specifically during RT, leaving a clear gap. Although a large number of HNSCC cell lines have been established and well-characterized , translational relevance is highest when using patient-derived material. While tumor biopsies are routinely obtained for diagnostic purposes, serial sampling during or after RT is not commonly performed. Paired pre- and post-treatment biopsies from the same patient remain particularly scarce, as such procedures are not part of standard care and pose a higher burden for patients, especially when compared to less invasive alternatives such as imaging.
In this study, we develop a cGAS-mNeongreen (-mNG) knock in (KI) murine tumor model and use serial biopsies of HNSCC patients to study RT-induced cGAS re-localization and associated immune effects. This CRISPR/cas9-engineerd model enables unbiased assessment of cGAS localization and activation at a physiological gene-expression level both in vitro and in vivo. We further translate these findings to human HNSCC cell lines and serial HNSCC patient biopsies obtained before and during RT to explore immune effects and prognostic value. We find that RT increases cGAS positive micronuclear foci in both mouse tumors and patient samples, accompanied by elevated ISG expression. cGAS re-localization is most pronounced in immune cell-infiltrated tumor regions, and RT enhances systemic type I IFN-inducing activity. Importantly, stronger RT-induced immune activation correlates with improved survival, particularly in HPV positive patients.

## Methods

## Sex as a biological variable
Our animal study exclusively examined female mice. Sex was not considered as a biological variable in the animal experiment. It is unknown whether the findings of the animal experiment are relevant for male mice. Our patient study included both sexes (male and female), and similar findings are reported for males and females.

## Cell lines
MC38 murine colon carcinoma cells (obtained from Kerafast; cat. no. ENH204-FP), human HNSCC cell lines Fadu (kindly provided by Michael Baumann, German Cancer Research Center, Heidelberg, Germany), UT-SCC5 and UT-SCC40 cell lines (kindly provided by Prof. Grenman, University of Turku, Turku, Finland) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/ml penicillin (Gibco), 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Gibco), 1x MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco). THP1 dual cells were obtained from Invivogen (cat. no. thpd-nfis) and maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FCS, 2 mM L-glutamine (Lonza), 100 units/ml penicillin (Gibco), 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Gibco). After two passages, cells were cultured with 10 µg/ml blasticidin (Invivogen) and 100 µg/ml zeocin (Invivogen) to select for dual reporter expressing cells and 50 µg/ml of antimicrobial agent normocin (Invivogen). Cells were maintained under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma.

## Generation of cGAS-mNG KI cell line
HDR template (obtained from IDT integrated DNA technologies) was designed containing the last 500 bp of the last exon of murine Cgas removing the stop codon, the sequence for mNG, and 500 bp of the 3’ UTR region of Cgas (sequence provided in Supplementary data  ). This template was cloned into a pSMART vector plasmid (CloneSmart HCKan Blunt Cloning Kit; Lucigen; cat. no. 40704-2). Active sgRNAs (Sigma) directed at the C-terminus of murine Cgas were selected using Guide-it™ Mutation Detection Kit (Takara Bio; cat. no. 631443; guide RNA sequence top: caccgCTTAATTGACAAAAGAAGTA, bottom: aaacTACTTCTTTTGTCAATTAAGc) and sequences were cloned into a px459 plasmid (Addgene), containing the sequence for the cas9 protein.
MC38 cells were plated in T75 culture flasks at a concentration of 375.000 cells/flask and allowed to adhere overnight. Next day, cells were transfected using 75 µl/flask metafectene (Biontex; cat. no. T020-2.0), 1 µg/flask plasmid containing HDR template, 1 µg/flask plasmid containing cas9 and guideRNA sequences (or empty vector as negative control). First, for 15 min, complexes were formed, and added to the flasks. Next day, puromycin (5 µg/ml) and 10× diluted Flt3l+ dendritic cell-derived IFNα-rich supernatant were added to each flask to stimulate expression of cGAS-mNG. The next day, cells were fluorescently sorted on the FACSAria (BD) based on endogenous mNG signal. mNGhigh cells were plated single cell and allowed to clonally grow out. Clones were screened using genomic PCR. Clones were dedicated mNg+/+ (homozygous), mNg+/− (heterozygous), mNg−/− (negative) or unclear based on the PCR results. Once grown out, a selection of clones was further analyzed on fluorescence using flow cytometry and protein expression using western blot. Ultimately, two clones (one mNg+/+; one mNg+/−) were selected to further explore cGAS/STING pathway functionality.

## Animal studies
C57BL/6 mice were purchased when 6–8 weeks old, as animals have then a fully functional immune system. After two weeks of acclimatization, 0.5*106 syngeneic MC38, or MC38-derived cGAS-mNG expressing cells were s.c. injected on the right hind leg under anesthesia (2–3% isoflurane inhalation) to ensure correct tumor location in a volume of 50 µl. Before and after cell injection, viability of cells in suspension was confirmed using trypan blue staining and was >95% viable cells. The investigators were blinded for tumor cell type injected. Tumor growth was monitored 3 times a week using calipers. Mice were block-randomized over treatment groups when tumors reached a volume of ±150 mm3 (~12 days after inoculation), no animals were excluded. This experiment was designed as a pilot experiment, in which 3 mice were included per group. No sample size calculation was performed. Irradiation took place under anesthesia (2–3% isoflurane inhalation) to ensure the tumor was located in the radiation field. Tumors were irradiated with 8 Gy single dose or were left untreated (0 Gy). After 24 h, mice were sacrificed using cervical dislocation and tumors were harvested, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Confounders were not controlled.

## HNSCC study
Human HNSCC tumor sample collection is described in ref. . In this observational study in which biological samples (tumor biopsies and blood) were collected at defined timepoints during patients’ routine treatment schedules, 49 patients with HNSCC were included in a study officially titled “Early treatment-induced changes in the tumor microenvironment and potential consequences for biology-based predictive tests in head and neck cancer”, here called “The second biopsy study”, with a time period between September 2007 and December 2013. They underwent standard care (IMRT, dose of 1.48 Gy, total dose of 50.3 Gy, with a boost to the tumor of 2 Gy, total dose of 68 Gy, in 34 fractions in total), with or without cisplatin or cetuximab. Patient characteristics are stated in Table  . This cohort is as far as we know unique. As no previous data is available from such a patient cohort, power analysis is impossible to perform as no data exists on differences and/or variations. For this study, biopsies were taken at distinct timepoints (before (T0) and after (T1) 5 daily 2 Gy fractions of RT). Tumor biopsies were snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C. Blood withdrawal was done at distinct timepoints (T0, T1, and T2 (after 34 fractions)). Blood was collected in serum tubes, centrifuged at 956 × g for 10 min. Serum was collected and stored at 80 °C. Supplementary Fig.  shows the sample overview. Clinical endpoints were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), which included local failure, locoregional failure, and metastasis. Candidate variables in the study included sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, tumor stage, HPV status, and systemic therapy. HPV status determination was not standard routine at the time of the study, therefore, of patients for whom the HPV status was unknown, a PCR determination was performed to assess HPV positivity, followed by HPV variant determination, as described before .
 

## PCR on genomic DNA
Cells from outgrown clones were pelleted during culturing and frozen at −20 °C. Genomic DNA was isolated using the Nucleospin Tissue kit (Macherey-Nagel) following manufacturers’ protocol. A PCR mix was made containing MyTaq reaction buffer, forward primer (CCAACCTGAGCTAATGAGACC), reverse primer (TCATGGTGTCTGTTCATAGCAG), MyTaq polymerase and 100 ng genomic DNA. Products were loaded on a 2% agarose gel and the gel was analyzed using the Chemidoc (Biorad). For mNg+/+ cells a single band at 1862 bp was expected, for mNg+/− cells a double band at 1862 bp and 1154 bp was expected and for mNg−/− cells a band of 1154 bp was expected. Whole uncropped gel can be found in Supplementary Fig.  .

## Flow cytometry and FACS
For flow cytometry analysis of in vitro cultured cells, cells were washed in PBS, then incubated for 20 min, in the dark at 4 °C in eFluor780 fixable viability dye (1:4000 in PBS; eBioscience). Cells were washed and resuspended in PBS and measured on the FACSCanto II (BD) and mNG level was determined in FITC channel. Gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig.  .

## Western blotting
Murine tumor cells were stimulated for 6 h, 24 h or 72 h and human HNSCC for 16 h with cGAMP (2'3'-cGAMP; 10 ug/ml; Bioconnect; cat. no. tlrl-nacga23-1), which was premixed with metafectene (15 min) before adding it to the cells to stimulate phosphorylation of STING. To generate cell lysates, medium was removed, cells were washed and lysis buffer (1% SDS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC, cOmplete, Roche; cat. no. 04693132001) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Sigma; cat. no. 10837091001) for cGAS and mNG detection, RIPA supplemented with PIC, PhosSTOP (Roche; cat. no. 11699695001) and 1 mM PMSF for pSTING detection) was added to each well and wells were scratched with a cell scraper. The solution was transferred to an Eppendorf tube. Lysates were mixed with sample buffer, heated at 95 °C for 5 min and cooled on ice. A 10% bis-acrylamide SDS-PAGE gel was used to separate the proteins. Amersham Protran Nitrocellulose membranes (Sigma; cat. no. GE10600001) were used to transfer proteins on. Blots were pre-stained with Ponceau S (Sigma; cat. no. P7170) staining to ensure equal protein loading and transfer to blot. Membranes were incubated with 5% milk in phosphate-buffered saline supplied with 0.1% Tween-20 (Merck; cat. no. 8.22184) (PBS-T) for mNG and cGAS detection, in 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline supplied with 0.1% Tween-20 (Merck; cat. no. 8.22184) (TBS-T) for pSTING detection and incubated 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were washed 3 times with wash buffer (PBS-T for mNG and cGAS or TBS-T for pSTING) followed by incubation with a-mNG (mouse; Chromotec; clone 32F6; cat. no. 32f6; 1:1000 in 5% milk in PBS-T staining buffer), a-mcGAS (rabbit; CST; clone D3O8O; cat. no. 31659S; 1:1000 in 5% milk in PBS-T staining buffer), a-pSTING (rabbit; CST; clone D8F4W; cat. no. 72971S; 1:1000 in 5% BSA in TBS-T staining buffer) and co-incubated with either a-actin (mouse; Sigma; clone AC-40; cat. no. A4700-.2 ML; 1:5000) or a-tubulin (mouse; Calbiochem; clone DM1A; cat. no. CP06; 1:5000) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the membranes were washed 3 times 5 min using wash buffer, followed by incubation with IRDYe 800CW goat-anti-rabbit (Westburg; cat. no. LI 926-32211; 1:5000) for mcGAS or pSTING, IRDye 680RD goat-anti-mouse (Westburg; cat. no. LI 926-68070; 1:5000) for actin or tubulin, and anti-mouse-HRP for mNG (CST; cat. no. 7076; 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature in staining buffer. The membranes were washed 3 to 5 times 5 min in wash buffer. For cGAS and actin visualization, membranes were scanned using the Odyssey CLx (Licor). For mNG, SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate kit (Thermofisher Science) was used to develop signal. Protein was visualized using the Chemidoc (Biorad). Whole uncropped western blots can be found in Supplementary Fig.  .

## Irradiation
Irradiation of murine cells and mice tumors was performed using an XRAD-320 X-Ray irradiator (Precision) at a dose rate of 3.1 Gy/min. Human HNSCC cells were irradiated using an Elekta Versa HD (Elekta) with a 10-MV photon beam at a dose rate of 6 Gy/min. Distinct samples were used for unirradiated control cells/tumors and irradiated cells/tumors.

## cGAMP ELISA
ELISA kit for 2’3’-cyclic GAMP detection was purchased from Arbor Assays (cat. no. K067-H). Murine tumor cells were irradiated with 8 Gy and harvested 24 h or 72 h after RT. Human HNSCC cells were irradiated with 2 Gy or 8 Gy and harvested 48 h after RT. RIPA lysates were generated of all cell lines and generated as mentioned in the section “Western Blot”. The intracellular cGAMP concentration was determined following manufacturer’s guidelines. Absorbance was measured on an iMark microplate reader (Biorad).

## RNA isolation and quantitative PCR
Murine tumor cells were irradiated with 8 Gy and harvested 72 h after RT. Human HNSCC were irradiated with 2 Gy or 8 Gy and harvested 72 h after RT. RNA of cell lines was isolated using a Quick-RNA miniprep kit (Zymo research; cat. no. R1055) following manufacturer’s instructions. For murine tumors and HNSCC patient biopsies, samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Tumor tissue was embedded into Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound (Sakura), and 10 cryosections of 10 µM were cut, which were put into TRIzol and resuspended thoroughly using a p1000 pipette. TRIzol isolation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with minor modifications. RNA quantity and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (DeNovix). RNA was DNAse I (Invitrogen) treated. Reverse transcription into cDNA was started with equal amounts of RNA per sample using random hexamers and Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). The Faststart SYBR green mastermix (Roche; cat. no. FSSGMMRO) and gene-specific oligonucleotide primers (Table  ) were used to determine mRNA levels of ISGs (Cxcl10 (IP10), Rigi, Ifih1 (MDA5), Hmbs for mouse; CXCL10 (IP10), RIGI, IFIH1 (MDA5), IFIT3, OAS1, HMBS for human) using a CFX96 sequence detection system (Biorad). Quantitative PCR data were analyzed with CFX maestro software (Biorad). mRNA levels of ISGs were normalized to mRNA levels of Hmbs (mPBGD)/HMBS (hPBGD), and relative expression is depicted as 2−ΔCt. For the ISG signature, a pooled Ct level was calculated from human IP10, RIGI, MDA5, IFIT3, OAS1.
 

## Immunofluorescence
Murine MC38, cGAS-mNG KI cells or human HNSCC cell lines were plated in chamber slides and treated with RT (for murine lines 8 Gy, for human lines 2 Gy or 8 Gy) or left untreated. Fixation took place 48 h after RT using 4% PFA (10 min at room temperature), followed by permeabilization using 0.1% triton (10 min at room temperature) and blocking with 5% normal goat serum (NGS; Jackson Immuno Research) diluted in Primary Antibody Diluent (PAD; Biorad) for a-mcGAS and a-hcGAS or 4% BSA in PBS for a-mNG (1 h at room temperature). Primary antibody was diluted in PAD as followed: a-mcGAS (rabbit; CST; clone D3080; cat. no. 31659S; 1:200), a-hcGAS (rabbit; CST; clone D1D3G; cat. no. 15102S; 1:50) a-mNG (mouse; Chromotec; clone 32F6; cat. no. 32f6; 1:500) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Isotype control (CST; clone DA1E; cat. no. 3900S) for a-mcGAS and a-hcGAS, or buffer without antibody for a-mNG were taken along as negative control. Secondary antibody chicken-anti-mouse AF647 (Invitrogen; 1:400 in PAD) or goat-anti-rabbit Cy3 (Jackson Immuno Research; 1:300 in PAD) was incubated 30 min at 37 °C. Hoechst (5 min, 1:2000 in PBS) was used to stain nuclei. Slides were mounted in Fluoromount-W (SERVA Electrophoresis).
For murine MC38 or cGAS-mNG KI tumors, cryosections of 5 µM were cut. For mNG analysis, slides were directly incubated with Hoechst (5 min, 1:3000 in PBS), followed by mounting in Fluormount-W. Analysis of cGAS positive micronuclei was performed within 48 h after mounting.
Antibody staining of murine tumors was performed as followed: cryosections of 5 µM were incubated with 4% PFA (30 min at room temperature), quenching with 0.1 M glycine (in PBS) was performed (10 min at room temperature), followed by incubation in 10% NGS supplied with 0.1% triton in PBS (15 min at room temperature). First, a-mNG fluotag-AF647 (alpaca; Nanotag Biotechnologies; cat. no. N3202; 1:500 in 3% NGS + 0.1% triton in PBS) or buffer without antibody was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with a-mcGAS (rabbit; CST; D3080; cat. no 31659S; 1:200 in 3% NGS + 0.1% triton in PBS) or isotype (rabbit; CST; DA1E; cat. no. 3900S) for 30 min at 37 °C. Secondary donkey-anti-rabbit Cy3 (Jackson Immuno Research; 1:100; in 3% NGS + 0.1% triton in PBS) was incubated for 45 min at 37 °C followed by 5 min incubation with Hoechst (1:3000 in PBS). Slides were mounted in Fluoromount-W (SERVA Electrophoresis).
Cryosections of 5 µM were cut from patient biopsies. Slides were fixed using 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature followed by permeabilization with 0.1% triton (10 min at room temperature) and incubation of 5% normal donkey serum (NDS; Jackson Immuno Research) diluted in PAD (30 min at room temperature). First, a-hcGAS (rabbit; CST; D1D3G; cat. no. 31659S; 1:50 in PAD) or corresponding isotype (rabbit; CST; DA1E; cat. no. 3900S) was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by incubation donkey-anti-rabbit Cy3 (Jackson Immuno Research; 1:100 in PAD) for 45 min at 37 °C. Next, a-hCD326 (Epcam; mouse (biotin labeled); Miltenyi Biotec; cat. no. 130-113-262; 1:50 in PAD) or corresponding isotype control (mouse; BioLegend; cat. no. 400104; MOPC-21) was incubated for 45 min at 37 °C, followed by incubation of chicken-anti-mouse AF647 (BioLegend; 1:400 in PAD) for 45 min at 37 °C. Last, a-CD45-AF488 (directly labeled; BioLegend; cat. no. 3040006; 1:40 in PAD) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by 5 min Hoechst (1:3000 in PBS) incubation and mounting in Fluoromount-W (SERVA Electrophoresis).
The H&E staining was performed as followed. Sections were fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature, followed by 3x washing in PBS. Slides were incubated with hematoxylin (DAKO, Agilent) for 6 min at room temperature, followed by rinsing in running tap water for 30 min. Slides were incubated for 3 min in eosin Y 1% aqueous solution (VWR) for 3 min, and rinsed with tap water. Slides were put in 70% ethanol, 90% ethanol and 100% ethanol, and lastly incubated in neo-clear (Merck). Slides were mounted with Neo-mount Mounting medium (Merck).
All images were obtained using the Zeiss Axio Scope A.1. For cGAS positive micronuclei quantification in murine tumors, 10 randomly taken images per tumor were analyzed blinded for treatment group and tumor cell type. Areas were chosen in viable tumor tissue (based on H&E evaluation of consecutive section). Thresholds for the fluorescent signals were interactively set above the background and converted into binary images. Number of foci per image were quantified using Fiji/ImageJ software. For analyzing HNSCC biopsies, one total biopsy section was scanned for H&E and one consecutive section was multicolor stained for Epcam, CD45, cGAS and Hoechst and scanned. H&E-stained sections were used to delineate viable tumor tissue and exclude necrosis or surrounding normal tissue. Epcam, CD45, and cGAS positive signal was determined blinded for patient number and timepoint, thresholds for the fluorescent signals were interactively set above the background for each individual marker and converted into binary images. cGAS foci in total image (total section area), Epcam positive region (tumor area), CD45 positive region (immune area), Epcam/CD45 positive region (infiltrated tumor area) and Epcam/CD45 negative regions (stromal area) were quantified using Fiji/ImageJ software.

## sFIS assay
THP1-dual cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (30.000 cells/well in 100 µl supplemented RPMI culture medium). Cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C at 5% CO2. Next day, cells were treated with 50 µl/100 µl normal human serum (NHS; Merck) or patient derived serum for 24 h. Stimulation with LPS (1 µg/ml), cGAMP and TNF-α concentrations diluted in NHS or medium was taken along as positive controls. IFN/ISG signal level was quantitively measured using Luciferase (bioluminescence) using QUANTI-Luc™ (Invivogen) on a Victor3 multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer) and NF-κB signal level with SEAP (colorimetric) QUANTI-Blue™ (Invivogen) on an iMark microplate reader (Biorad). Patient serum levels were divided by NHS and plotted as serum level relative to NHS, or induction of serum level was plotted as change or induction to T0. Patients were stratified based on NF-κB relative to NHS low (<median level) or high (>median level), IFN/ISG low (<median level) or high (>median level), NF-κB induction at T1 (>1) or no induction (<1), IFN/ISG induction at T1 (>1) or no induction (<1). In addition, HPV status was also taken into account when stratifying for NF-κB or IFN/ISG level or induction.

## Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc.). First, normality was tested using a Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significant difference was tested as followed: cGAS positive micronuclei induction and mRNA ISG induction in mice using a two-tailed unpaired t-test, cGAS positive micronuclei induction and ISG/Cgas/Sting mRNA induction in HNSCC patients using a two-tailed paired t-test, Wilcoxon test or two-way ANOVA using post-hoc Tukey correction for multiple testing, difference in IFN/ISG and NF-κB serum signal induction at T1 or level at T0/baseline was tested using a two-tailed unpaired t-test, and DFS or OS difference was tested with a Gehan-Breslow-Wilcox (DFS) or log-rank (OS) test. DFS and OS was cut-off at 60 months at the end of follow-up, fewer than 50% of patients had experienced an event, and therefore the median overall survival was not reached. The mean survival time was 39.0 months (SD 17.9). To test significant changes of IFN/ISG signals over T0, T1 and T2 in H patients, first outliers were identified and removed using a ROUT outlier analysis, then a mixed-effects analysis or one-way ANOVA was performed using a post-hoc Tukey correction for multiple testing. To test the difference in IFN/ISG and NF-κB serum level or induction in patients with a shorter or longer OS, patients were divided in patients with event (dead) before 4 years of follow-up, and patients without event (alive) with at least 4 years of follow-up. For this comparison, patients without an event (alive) but dropout before 4 years of follow-up were excluded. Data represent mean ± SEM. Significance is indicated in figures as followed: * represents p = <0.05; ** represents p = <0.01; *** represents p = <0.001; **** represents p = <0.0001, exact P values are presented in supplementary data  . Sample sizes and number of replicates are indicated in the figure legend.

## Results

## Generation of cGAS-mNG KI tumor cells endogenously expressing functional fluorescent cGAS
cGAS localization studies have shown that different antibodies and immunohistochemical staining procedures yield different cGAS staining patterns , . In addition, transient transfection of fluorescent cGAS encoding DNA plasmids will automatically activate the cGAS/STING pathway, thereby affecting cGAS localization. To study the endogenous cGAS expression and re-localization, the fluorescent protein monomeric Neongreen (mNG) was knocked-in at the C-terminus of the Cgas gene using CRISPR/cas9 in the MC38 murine tumor cell line (Supplementary Fig.  ). mNG was selected as a fluorescent tag due to its superior brightness compared to conventional GFP variations, making it more suitable for live cell and in vivo imaging . Additionally, mNG matures more rapidly than GFP, enabling earlier and more reliable detection of tagged proteins . This model allows for high-resolution visualization of cGAS localization dynamics in response to RT, under physiologically relevant expression levels.
Genomic characterization of clonally expanded CRISPR/cas9 subjected cells identified homozygous (mNg+/+), heterozygous (mNg+/−) and negative (mNg−/−) clones (Supplementary Fig.  ). cGAS-mNG protein expression was readily detected in mNg+/+ and mNg+/− cells, with the absence of WT cGAS protein expression in mNg+/+ cells, confirming fully homozygous KI of mNG at both cGAS alleles (Supplementary Fig.  ). Heterozygous mNg+/− cells did express both cGAS-mNG and WT cGAS. Flowcytometry analysis further confirmed auto-fluorescence of both mNg+/+ and mNg+/− cells, while mNg−/− cells and parental MC38 cells were negative (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Next, the functionality of the fluorescent cGAS-mNG fusion protein was tested by irradiation (8 Gy) of the cGAS-mNG expressing cell clones (mNg+/+ and mNg+/−). Irradiation produced equal levels of the second messenger cGAMP in cGAS-mNG and WT cells as well as comparable phosphorylation of the downstream signaling molecule STING (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, cells were able to induce similar mRNA expression of ISGs C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (Cxcl10; commonly known as IFN gamma-induced protein 10 (IP10)), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (Rigi) and interferon-induced with helicase C domain 1 (Ifih1, commonly known as melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5)) upon RT (Supplementary Fig.  ). Altogether, these results show accurate genomic insertion of the mNG construct at the C-terminus of the cGAS gene, resulting in endogenous cGAS-mNG fusion protein expression in both mNg+/+ and mNg+/− cells, without compromising the functionality of the cGAS/STING pathway.

## cGAS localization in murine cGAS-mNG KI cells and human HNSCC cell lines in vitro
Microscopic analysis of the mNG signal in cGAS-mNG expressing cells showed a low-level nuclear cGAS-mNG signal in some cells which was absent in WT cells, confirming the specificity of the signal. Optimized antibody staining procedures were performed with a-cGAS (CST; clone D3O8O; mouse specific) and a-mNG, and antibody signal colocalized with endogenous cGAS-mNG signal in foci in mNg+/+ and mNg+/− cells. These cGAS-mNG positive foci were positively stained for Hoechst/DNA, indicative for cGAS positive micronuclei (Fig.  ; Supplementary Figs.  and ).
 In addition, upon 8 Gy irradiation, cGAS-mNG cells showed a strong induction of mNG positive micronuclei compared to unirradiated cells, also robustly co-localizing with a-cGAS and a-mNG antibodies. WT cells showed similar cGAS positive micronuclei upon RT, when stained with a-cGAS antibodies. The murine model endogenously expressing cGAS-mNG shows the cGAS positive micronuclear staining pattern obtained using the optimized a-cGAS antibody staining protocol is one-on-one comparable and can therefore be appointed as true cGAS localization pattern when stained with the selected antibody.
To extrapolate our murine data to the human setting, human cGAS staining protocols were optimized and cGAS localization was determined in three human HNSCC cell lines. Staining of Fadu cells, UT-SCC5 cells and UT-SCC40 cells with a-cGAS antibody (CST; clone D1D3G; human equivalent of D3O8O) revealed human cGAS localization patterns very similar to the findings in the cGAS-mNG model. cGAS can be present in both cytoplasm and nuclei . We further observed an increase in cGAS positive foci in all cell lines, which colocalized with Hoechst/DNA positive micronuclei, 48 h after irradiation with 2 Gy or 8 Gy (Fig.  ). Unirradiated UT-SCC5 and UT-SCC40 cells did not show cGAS positive micronuclei. In Fadu cells some cGAS was already detected in non-irradiated cells; however, the number of cGAS foci increased after RT. Cytoplasmic staining was detected in both irradiated and non-irradiated conditions, particularly in Fadu cells. The similarity in cGAS positive micronuclei staining between the HNSCC cells and the murine cGAS-mNG KI model ensured the validity of the human cGAS staining protocol, allowing for proper human cGAS positive micronuclei detection and re-localization quantification.
 Next, we assessed whether cGAS re-localization to micronuclei in the human HNSCC cell lines was associated with cGAMP production, STING phosphorylation and ISG expression. Hereto, the cells were irradiated with either 2 Gy, 8 Gy or left untreated. After irradiation, the intracellular cGAMP concentration was significantly increased in Fadu cells (P = 0.0131; Two-way ANOVA with Tukey correction for multiple testing), and UT-SCC5 and UT-SCC40 showed a similar trend (Fig.  ). To further study the capability of these cells to induce STING signaling and ISG induction, cells were stimulated with cGAMP. Phosphorylation of STING was readily observed in Fadu and UT-SCC40 cells and effective cGAS/STING pathway activation in these cells was accompanied by a potent increase in multiple ISG in a RT dose-dependent manner (Fig.  ). In contrast, cGAMP stimulation and different RT doses were unable to trigger STING phosphorylation or clear induction of the ISGs IP10, RIG-I, MDA5, Interferon Induced Protein with Tetratricopeptide Repeats 3 (IFIT3) and 2’−5’-oligoadenylate synthetase 1 (OAS1) in UT-SCC5 cells (Fig.  ). These data imply that despite effective cGAS re-localization and cGAMP production in UT-SCC5 cells, the cGAS/STING signaling cascade is aberrant in these cells. Of note, the Fadu, UT-SCC5, and UT-SCC40 cell lines are all HPV negative, whereas a substantial subset of HNSCC patients presents with HPV positive tumors. As HPV status could influence cGAS/STING signaling , two HPV positive cell lines were also subjected to 2 Gy and 8 Gy RT (Supplementary Fig.  ). These experiments show that these HPV positive cell lines both exhibit very similar responses following RT when compared to the HPV negative lines, suggesting similar RT-induced cGAS/STING activity in the context of HPV positive disease.
Collectively, these data show the diverse RT response of HNSCC lines, emphasizing the heterogeneity in cGAS/STING signaling among different cell lines from an HNSCC origin.

## RT induced cGAS-mNG positive micronuclei are associated with increased ISG response in vivo
To study cGAS re-localization and appearance of cGAS positive micronuclei in situ, mNg+/+ tumor cells, expressing cGAS-mNG protein, were injected into mice and established tumors were irradiated with 8 Gy (Supplementary Figs.  , ). The mNG signal is exclusively expressed by cGAS-mNG engineered tumor cells, not in WT MC38 tumor cells in vivo (Supplementary Fig.  ). Similar to the in vitro setting, the cGAS-mNG positive foci were located at selective Hoechst/DNA positive micronuclei structures, a localization pattern associated with cGAS/STING activation. Radiation was able to elicit cGAS-mNG positive micronuclei in cGAS-mNG expressing tumors (Fig.  ).
 A significant increase in cGAS positive micronuclei was readily observed upon quantification (Fig.  ; P = 0.0305; t-test). Of note, next to cGAS-mNG positive micronuclei, also some nuclear mNG signal was present. In addition, enhanced mRNA expression of ISGs IP10 (P = 0.0223; t-test), Rigi (P = 0.0147; t-test) and MDA5 (P = 0.0595; t-test) was readily detected 24 h after 8 Gy irradiation (Fig.  ). In this model, increased cGAS-mNG positive micronuclei were thus associated with an increased ISG response. These data confirmed, independently from antibody or staining procedure, the expression pattern of cGAS after RT in vivo.

## RT induced cGAS positive micronuclei in HNSCC patients are associated with ISG induction
To study cGAS behavior in vivo in a human setting, we analyzed serial biopsies and serum samples obtained from patients with HNSCC receiving RT (Fig.  ) in the so-called “Second biopsy study” (n = 49). From 42 patients, tumor sample biopsies were successfully obtained prior to (T0; baseline) and during radiation, after completing 5 daily fractions of 2 Gy (T1). Patient details and sample overview can be found in Table  and Supplementary Fig.  . Out of these biopsies, 13 paired biopsy samples (before vs during RT) of high quality were selected to examine the presence and localization of cGAS (Fig.  ). Paired biopsies from other patients were not suitable for high quality microscopic analysis of cGAS re-localization due to various issues such as necrosis interfering with staining quality or quantification reliability. In the 13 informative paired samples, microscopic analysis revealed predominant localization of cGAS in micronuclei. In addition, some nuclear and cytoplasmic staining was occasionally observed. cGAS positive micronuclei were quantified in different areas of the tumor prior to and after 5 daily fractions of 2 Gy (Fig.  ). Compared to T0, on T1 the number of cGAS positive micronuclei in the total tumor section increased in 12/13 patients analyzed (P = 0.0012; Wilcoxon test). In tumor/Epcam rich areas (P = 0.0109; paired t-test), immune infiltrated tumor areas (both CD45 and Epcam rich; P = 0.0061; Wilcoxon test) and stromal areas (both CD45 negative and Epcam negative; P = 0.0171; Wilcoxon test), this increase was significant, while in the area containing mainly infiltrated immune cells (CD45 positive) it was not (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 Interestingly, while cGAS positive micronuclei were detected in all areas, the highest density and number of cGAS positive micronuclei/mm2 were detected in regions where both tumor and immune cells were present, both at T0 (Infiltrated tumor versus Total section area P = 0.0343, Infiltrated tumor versus Stroma P = 0.0172; two-way ANOVA, Tukey correction for multiple testing) and T1 (Infiltrated tumor versus Total section area, Tumor or Stroma all P = < 0.0001; two-way ANOVA, Tukey correction for multiple testing) (Fig.  ). These data show similar results on the presence of cGAS positive micronuclei compared to the cGAS-mNG KI model and human HNSCC cell lines, validating the robustness of these clinical findings.
To analyze the effect of RT on cGAS/STING pathway activation, mRNA expression profiling of multiple ISGs (IP10, RIGI, MDA5, OAS1, IFIT3) was performed on a selection of 25 paired biopsies, of which high-grade mRNA was successfully obtained. Comparable to the in vitro analysis of the human HNSCC cell lines, we observed heterogenous activation of the ISGs following RT in this patient cohort. Patients could be readily divided into two groups showing distinct responses in ISG induction following RT. Specifically, 10 patients exhibited an increased mRNA level of an ISG signature (pooled induction IP10, RIGI, MDA5, OAS1, and IFIT3) after RT (ISG responder group), while 15 patients did not (ISG non-responder group) (Fig.  ). Interestingly, cGAS expression, which is a type I IFN responsive gene itself, is also significantly higher (P = 0.0004; t-test) induced upon RT in ISG responder patients compared with patients that were ISG non-responders (Fig.  ).
 In “The second biopsy study”, also serum samples were taken prior to (T0; baseline), during radiation after completing 5 daily fractions of 2 Gy (T1) and when radiation was ended (T2) (Fig.  ). As a RT-induced type I IFN response is crucial for the generation of a systemic immune response, we next employed the serum-functional immunodynamics status (sFIS) assay to assess RT-induced serum changes in sera of “The second biopsy study”. This assay utilizes THP1 dual reporter cells to evaluate serum factor-induced IFN/ISG and NF-κB responses. These reporter cells produce luciferase when detecting IFN/ISG-stimuli in the serum and secrete alkaline phosphatase when NF-κB-stimuli are present. Although cGAS/STING is associated most with type I IFN signals, NF-κB signals can be derived from the cGAS/STING pathway-induced factors. Of note, sustained NF-κB activation is also associated with inflammation linked to immunosuppression in the cancer context . At T0/baseline, all patients in this analysis showed IFN/ISG signal levels in serum similar to levels found in commercially available pooled serum from healthy controls. However, increased NF-κB signal levels in serum were detected in HNSCC patients compared to levels found in pooled serum from healthy controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). From 37 evaluable patients, paired serum samples (from T0 and T1) were available. IFN/ISG signal levels significantly increased (P = 0.0040; mixed-effects analysis, Tukey correction for multiple testing) at T1 after 5 fractions of RT compared to T0/baseline (Fig.  ), while NF-κB signal levels did not change over this one-week time interval (Fig.  ). NF-κB signal levels significantly decreased at T2 at completion of RT, after 34 fractions compared to T0/baseline (T0 vs T2; P = 0.0206; mixed-effects analysis, Tukey correction for multiple testing) or after 5 fractions (T1 vs T2; P = 0.0024; mixed-effects analysis; Tukey correction for multiple testing; Fig.  ). A diverse response was observed among the patient population comparing T0 and T1, with the majority of patients increasing in circulating IFN/ISG signals in their serum upon 5 fractions of RT (Fig.  ). This observation was independent of systemic treatment with cisplatin or cetuximab or HPV status (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Interestingly, those patients that failed to induce IFN/ISG signals after RT exhibited high NF-κB levels at T0/baseline while patients with lower-than-median NF-κB signal levels were able to specifically induce IFN/ISG levels at T1 (P = 0.0012; t-test) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), which was independent of HPV status (Supplementary Fig.  ). This implies that the level of NF-κB induction already present before therapy can influence the level of IFN/ISG induction upon RT.
 

## Prognostic impact of IFN/ISG induction during RT in HNSCC patients
To assess the potential impact of RT-induced IFN/ISG activation in HNSCC patients, survival data from “The second biopsy study” were analyzed. Remarkably, patients exhibiting an IFN/ISG induction during RT demonstrated superior overall survival (OS; P = 0.0146; log-rank test) and disease-free survival (DFS; P = 0.0256; Gehan-Breslow-Wilcox test) outcomes in comparison to those lacking this induction (Fig.  ). Notably, this effect was specific to IFN/ISG signals (OS P = 0.0017; DFS P = 0.0049; log-rank test) and not observed in relation to NF-κB signaling (Supplementary Fig.  ). No correlation of IFN/ISG levels prior to RT and survival was found (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, patients with a longer (>4 year) OS showed a tendency towards an IFN/ISG induction during RT (P = 0.0551; t-test), whereas patients with a shorter (<4 year) OS did not (Fig.  ).
 In the HNSCC patient cohort in this study, both HPV positive and HPV negative participants were included. Strong evidence indicates that the HPV status of HNSCC patients is associated with a favorable prognosis, particularly in response to radiation therapy – . Although HPV status itself did not correlate with OS or DFS in this cohort (Supplementary Fig.  ), the survival benefit of an RT induced IFN/ISG activation was only observed in the HPV positive subgroup of the cohort (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
As IFN/ISG induction is associated with a better prognosis, and we observed patients with an elevated NF-κB level before treatment had a diminished IFN/ISG induction, we explored the potential relationship between NF-κB levels and survival outcome. Indeed, patients with a shorter OS had a significantly higher level (P = 0.0300; t-test) of NF-κB signals in their serum prior to therapy (Fig.  ). In addition, although NF-κB signal levels significantly decreased at the T2 time point after 34 fractions, it did not result in a favorable survival (Supplementary Fig.  ).
These results indicate that in HNSCC patient biopsies, the presence of cGAS positive micronuclei increases upon RT, in both tumor-rich and immune-rich regions as well in as stromal areas. In a subset of patients, concurrent induction of cGAS mRNA and an elevated ISG response was observed. Overall, HPV positive patients exhibited a systemic IFN response predictive of RT outcome, which was influenced by the status of the basal inflammatory NF-κB response.

## Discussion
Preclinical murine studies show that RT-induced DNA damage results in the activation of the cGAS/STING pathway, leading to type I IFNs that play a key role in RT-induced immune effects . However, many questions linking RT and immune activation in the clinical setting remain to be explored.
The cGAS-mNG KI model allowed us to resolve detection issues reported with antibody staining and provided reliable insight into localization patterns in in vitro and in vivo settings. However, beyond the localization of cGAS in micronuclei, alternative localization patterns are observed, including nuclear or membranous localization, which are often associated with cGAS suppression, and cytosolic localization, indicative of cGAS activity . Although detectable in several compartments, cGAS was most clearly enriched in micronuclei after RT, consistent with its activation at these sites. The increased detection of cGAS in micronuclei aligns with the concept that cGAS-mNG molecules re-localize to DNA-containing micronuclei, form oligomers and become potently activated , , and are therefore clearly visible as cGAS-mNG signal, compared to more diffuse expression patterns observed in the nucleus or cytoplasm. Using the cGAS-mNG KI model, we circumvent previously reported anti-cGAS staining and detection problems , , and can conclude that the presence of cGAS in micronuclei is increased upon RT. Moreover, the acquired knowledge was essential to select the anti-cGAS antibody most suitable for the detection of human cGAS in vitro and in situ.
This study confirms that in controlled murine settings, cGAS/STING activation after RT is robust and reproducible, in contrast to the more heterogeneous responses observed in human HNSCC cells. All cell lines were capable of forming cGAS positive micronuclei upon RT. However, UT-SCC5 failed to translate this into STING activation, as confirmed by the lack of STING phosphorylation and the induction of ISGs. Interestingly, UT-SCC5 cells express various STING isoforms, including isoforms known to inhibit cGAS/STING pathway activation .
The prevalence of cGAS positive micronuclei aligns with the tendency of tumor cells to accumulate micronuclei due to their higher susceptibility to DNA damage and frequent division (much more than e.g., stromal cells) . The correlation between increased cGASpositive micronuclei and immune infiltration suggests a possible mechanism involving the activation of tumor cell signaling pathways leading to the production of IFNs and other immune chemoattractants, thereby facilitating immune cell infiltration. Despite our efforts to characterize the immune cell composition within the biopsies, the limited number and diversity among patients hampered the identification of clear correlations. Nonetheless, we identified various immune cell populations, including CD3+ T cells, CD11b+ myeloid cells, CD66b+ granulocytic cells, and CD56+ NK cells, across the patient samples.
Only a subset of patients (10 out of 25 patients) mounted an ISG response after RT, highlighting the heterogeneity of cGAS/STING functionality in HNSCC. It is recognized that many cancers develop a dysfunctional cGAS/STING pathway as a survival mechanism, including HNSCC and as we observed in the HNSCC-derived cell lines. In addition, high-level local immune suppression may prevent proper activation and downstream signaling in immune cells following cGAS activation in the tumor . Alternatively, mutations in genes involved in the cGAS or downstream signaling proteins could also prevent proper activation, as they have been frequently reported in many cancers, including HNSCC . In this context, it may be valuable to screen the patient cohort for mutations in the cGAS/STING pathway. As RT is an integral part of the treatment strategy of HNSCC patients, genetic analysis of mutations in the cGAS/STING pathway may be used to stratify patients on forehand to predict response to RT, especially to determine eligibility to add IT to the treatment plan.
This study found no correlation between HPV status and cGAS positive micronuclei formation, ISG induction, systemic IFN induction, or survival. However, it is noteworthy that in the HPV positive subgroup, the positive effect of RT-induced systemic IFN induction on OS and DFS was much more pronounced, whereas this effect was absent in the HPV negative subgroup. HPV-associated proteins are known to antagonize the cGAS/STING pathway , . RT may counteract this inhibitory process by inducing an additional burst of type I IFNs in the tumor microenvironment and beyond, possibly leading to the a (systemic) immune activation required for improved tumor control, reflected in the potential survival benefit observed in this study. Also, the difference in immune composition in HPV positive and HPV negative tumors , could potentially lead to the different effect of IFN/ISG serum signal induction upon RT on survival benefit. Nonetheless, confirmation in larger and more homogeneous cohorts is warranted.
Patients with high baseline NF-κB activity appeared less able to mount systemic IFN responses, suggesting that pre-existing inflammatory states may hinder RT-induced immune activation. Inflammation is a hallmark of cancer , in which transcription factor NF-κB is key . Although cGAS/STING pathway activation itself can lead to NF-κB activation contributing to an anti-tumor response , constitutive activation of NF-κB (via cGAS/STING or other mechanisms) is often more pro-tumorigenic. NF-κB is constitutively activated in a significant number of human cancers , and the elevated NF-κB signal level in HNSCC serum is thus in line with expectations. In cancer, NF-κB is linked to inducing cancer cell proliferation and is associated with poor survival. Others have found that a majority of ovarian cancer patients expressed a NF-κBhigh/IFNlow inflammation , comparable to these RT unresponsive HNSCC patients. This suggests that a more persistent and active NF-κB-mediated inflammatory response could hamper a systemic (RT induced) IFN/ISG activation, which contributes to a survival benefit. The exact mechanisms of how NF-κB signaling in these HNSCC patients plays a role in the RT-induced type I IFN induction are still unclear and should be further investigated.
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. “The second biopsy study” was designed to explore the early local effects of RT at the tumor site and its immune impact at the systemic level. This study offers a valuable opportunity for such investigation, given the logistical challenges and patient burden associated with collecting serial biopsy samples and corresponding patient serum samples during intensive RT. Based on prior studies, HPV positive patients generally demonstrate superior outcomes compared to HPV negative patients – . In our study, although HPV positive patients with IFN/ISG induction in serum appeared to have a survival benefit, we did not observe a significant survival advantage associated with HPV status, indicating possible selection bias. Our cohort was not powered for survival analysis by HPV status or for definitive evaluation of the effect of RT on cGAS localization and IFN/ISG induction. Importantly, our study population represents a highly selected group of patients eligible and willing to undergo serial biopsies during RT, which may reflect a relatively fit subgroup. This, combined with other factors such as potential differences in tumor stage distribution, may have overshadowed prognostic differences. Metastatic disease was an exclusion criterion, limiting the diversity of the cohort. Lastly, the follow-up period may not have been sufficient for every patient to capture later differences in survival. Expanding this, to the best of our knowledge, unique, paired biopsy dataset in future studies could help clarify the prognostic role of HPV status in the context of RT-induced immune responses. In addition, the variability observed in NF-κB and IFN signal activation may be influenced by additional patient-specific factors. For instance, differences in smoking status and baseline immune status can modulate innate immune responses and may confound pathway-specific effects observed in response to RT – . In the current study, such parameters were not systematically accounted for, which may have introduced confounding effects. Future research in larger, well-annotated patient cohorts will be essential to assess the contribution of these variables and to better understand the context in which cGAS/STING-mediated immune activation occurs. Another interesting question is how the local IFN signature in the biopsies relates to the systemic IFN signals detected in serum. Supplementary Fig.  illustrates the limited number of samples in which all analyses could be performed (cGAS relocalization in the TME, ISG induction in the TME, and systemic IFN/ISG and NF-κB activation). The diversity in the patient group (e.g., tumor stage, tumor location, tumor microenvironment present before and after RT) and variations in biopsy sites could introduce considerable variability. It remains, therefore, challenging to draw conclusions regarding local versus systemic effects in this study cohort.

## Conclusions
In summary, assessing the localization of cGAS is pivotal yet challenging. Our findings reveal cGAS located in micronuclei following RT, as evidenced by our engineered cGAS-mNG KI murine cell line. This localization is associated with cGAS activation upon RT in certain cases. ISG responses and the induction of IFN signals following RT vary among human HNSCC cells and HNSCC patients. Interestingly, clinical outcome is significantly associated with this IFN signal upregulation. This discrepancy triggers further investigation into the underlying factors influencing differential responsiveness to RT via the cGAS/STING pathway among patients. These findings highlight the potential of using cGAS localization and downstream signaling as biomarkers for RT-induced immune activation. However, the variability observed across cell lines and patients calls for further investigation into the factors driving this differential responsiveness (such as HPV status and the tumor microenvironment). To validate these findings and assess their clinical utility, further studies in larger, more diverse cohorts with extended follow-up are essential. This will be critical for translating cGAS/STING-mediated immune activation into effective treatment strategies that enhance the efficacy of RT, particularly in combination with IT.
