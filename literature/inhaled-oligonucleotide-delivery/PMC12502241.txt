## Background
The SARS-CoV-2 spike protein is the primary antigen used in most COVID-19 vaccines. To improve protection, especially at mucosal surfaces, a robust and safe delivery system capable of inducing both systemic and mucosal immunity is needed. Bacillus subtilis spores offer a promising platform due to their stability, safety, and capacity for protein display. A study to evaluate the potential acute oral toxicity of recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins was undertaken.

## Results
B. subtilis spore was used to express the SARS-CoV-2 S1 domain spike proteins during the bacterial sporulation. No in vitro cytotoxicity was observed when the spores were used to treat cells. BALB/c mice were given a single oral dose (5 × 1011 CFU/ml) over three consecutive days and monitored for 14 days to assess acute toxicity. BALB/c mice fed with up to 3 × 1011 CFU/mouse of the spores showed no adverse effects. A gross necropsy and histology of the treated mice’s vital organs, kidneys, liver, heart, lungs, and spleen revealed no pathological alterations, and the biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges.

## Conclusion
These findings suggest that the SARS-CoV-2 S1 domain spike proteins expressed on B. subtilis spores is well tolerated in mice when administered orally at a high dose. Findings from this study, hence, support further investigation of the recombinant bacterial spores expressing SARS-CoV-2 S1 domain spike proteins as an oral vaccine candidate against SARS-CoV-2 infection.

## Background
The ongoing circulation of SARS-CoV-2 underscores the persistent nature of COVID-19 transmission in the population. While current vaccines have significantly reduced the severity of the disease, limitations remain in achieving comprehensive mucosal immunity and long-term protection. This underscores the need for improved vaccine strategies capable of stimulating both systemic and mucosal immune responses. Central to this effort is the development of stable, scalable, and safe platforms for antigen delivery. A more robust and effective method to express the spike proteins as antigens for the vaccine is, therefore, much needed.
Bacillus subtilis is a gram-positive bacterium recognized as a Generally Regarded as Safe (GRAS). Due to its well-understood genetics, especially its regulatory mechanisms and ease of genetic manipulation [ ], and ease for large-scale cultivation, it has gained much attention in various fields of research and industrial applications. B. subtilis also has an established safety profile for its wide application as probiotics and in food preparation for human and animal consumption [ ]. One useful characteristic of B. subtilis is that when faced with nutrient deprivation, it undergoes morphological transformation to produce resilient endospores that are highly resistant to extreme conditions [ , ]. The spores allow the bacterium to survive indefinitely in a dormant state that will later germinate into active, vegetative cells when conditions become favorable [ , ].
The stability of B. subtilis endospores is attributed to their intricate multilayered spore coat structure, which includes the inner forespore membrane, cortex, and outer forespore membrane, as well as various protective layers [ ]. Within the spore coat, various coat proteins play essential roles in spore formation [ ]. In a spore-display system, where heterologous recombinant protein is expressed, these coat proteins could serve as anchoring motifs for surface-expressed recombinant proteins, making B. subtilis an ideal vehicle for delivering biologically active proteins, especially to the gut mucosal surfaces [ , ]. Among the bacterial spore coat proteins that have been utilized to display various recombinant proteins are CotB [ ], CotC [ ], CotG [ ], and CotZ [ ]. The spore-display potential of these proteins has been investigated with various target proteins, including tetanus toxin fragment (TTFC) [ ], streptavidin [ ], β-galactosidases [ , ], and more recently, the bacterial spores have been utilized to display the SARS-CoV-2 antigenic protein [ ].
During sporulation, B. subtilis cells divide asymmetrically into a mother cell and a smaller forespore. The mother cell engulfs the forespore, forming a double membrane that becomes the spore coat [ ]. By expressing target proteins in the mother cell compartment, these proteins become adsorbed into the membrane and integrated into normal coat proteins. Subsequently, the peptidoglycan cortex between the inner and outer forespore membranes starts to assemble during the late sporulation phase. This process is accompanied by a large decrease in water content as well as the pH of the forespore, resulting in a hydrophobic and highly electrostatic spore [ ]. These features make them highly suitable as a stable delivery vehicle of target recombinant proteins. The expression of the recombinant proteins in the mother cells is accomplished by using the sporulation-specific promoter cry1Aa, which is derived from the Bacillus thuringiensis crystal toxic proteins [ ]. This promoter is controlled by the mother cell-specific σE and σĸ [ ]. Adopting this strategy, the recombinant proteins are expressed on the spore coat without utilizing the spore anchoring protein motifs.
The recombinant B. subtilis spore delivery platform, administered orally, is designed to deliver antigens to gut-associated antigen-presenting cells, which then stimulate immune cells without allowing the spike protein to circulate systemically. Their ability to survive gastrointestinal transit enables efficient antigen delivery to gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), where they can induce both mucosal and systemic immune responses, as reported in previous studies [ , ]. While B. subtilis spores offer promising potential as delivery vehicles for vaccines and therapeutic proteins, their safety as a delivery vehicle still requires evaluation, particularly when expressing potentially toxic heterologous proteins such as the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. It has been reported that when expressed in cells, the spike protein potentially caused thrombosis [ , ], myocarditis [ ], meningitis [ ], and inflammatory responses in organs expressing the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor [ , ]. Toxicity from the spike protein has also been demonstrated in mice, where intratracheal exposure to a pseudovirus expressing the spike protein caused inflammation and lung damage [ ]. The toxicity is suggested to arise from the protein’s ability to bind to the ACE2 receptor, triggering a chain reaction that eventually leads to cell damage and inflammation [ ]. In this study, the potential acute toxicity of recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the full-length SARS-CoV-2 S1 domain spike proteins was investigated. The recombinant spores were administered to specific-pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice through the oral route. The clinical signs of toxicity including animal condition, behavior alteration, mortality, food consumption, abnormal body weight changes, necropsy findings of vital organs, and biochemical parameters of the kidney, liver, and lipid profile of vital organs were examined to determine for any potential adverse effects. Ultimately, the study aimed to determine if SAR-CoV-2 spike protein expressed on B. subtilis spores would be safe for oral delivery as a potential candidate oral vaccine. By focusing on the safety profile, this study lays the groundwork for future immunogenicity and long-term efficacy studies.

## Materials and methods

## Construction of recombinant plasmid
The antigenic spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 was identified using computational analytics as previously described [ , ]. The target gene sequence, full-length S1 domain of the SARS-CoV-2 D614G spike protein, named S1.1, was codon optimized for the expression in B. subtilis, and synthesized as a full-length oligonucleotide on the cloning vector pUC57 (GenScript, USA). The S1 domain, which contains the receptor-binding domain (RBD), was selected due to its immunogenic properties, which is more suitable for expression on the spore surface. The plasmid was named B. sub/pUC57/S1.1. E. coli/B. subtilis shuttle vector, pHPS9 (ATCC 37816) was used to express the target gene sequence in the spore coat of the B. subtilis spores without the need to anchor the recombinant protein to the spore coat proteins. The B. sub/pUC57/S1.1 and pHPS9 vector plasmids were transformed and propagated using the NEB 5-alpha competent E. coli (NEB, USA), following which plasmids were used to transform into competent B. subtilis WB800N, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol (MoBiTec, Germany) and as previously described [ ]. Positive transformants were screened for the presence of the target gene using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification with the primer sets shown in Table . 

## Sporulation and recovery of B. subtilis spores
B. subtilis WB800N was induced to sporulate by culturing in a nutrient-deprived medium as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the vegetative recombinant B. subtilis was cultured in 2×YT media following which the cultures were then inoculated into GYS sporulating media and incubated at 37˚C with constant shaking at 250 rpm (Inforst HT, Ecotron, Switzerland). Following 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was heated at 90˚C for 30 min to remove the non-sporulating and vegetative bacterial cells. After the heat treatment, the bacterial spores were sedimented at 10 000 × g for 10 min at 4˚ C.

## Purification of B. subtilis spores
The bacterial spores were purified through a two-phase extraction system using polyethylene glycol (PEG) and potassium phosphate buffer as previously described [ ]. The two-phase extraction medium consisted of 11.18% w/v PEG 4000, 34% v/v 3 M potassium phosphate buffer (1.76 M K2HPO4, 1.24 M KH2PO4) and 50% v/v nucleus-free water. The suspension was centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min to separate into the two-phase solution. The bacterial spores were collected from the interphase and diluted in sterile distilled water and then centrifuged at 600 × g for 30 min. The spore pellet underwent five washing cycles with sterile distilled water to remove residual buffers, antibiotics and selection agents. Spore purity was assessed by endospore staining using Malachite green, followed by microscopic observation to verify the presence of spores and the absence of vegetative cells (Additional File 1: Figure S1).

## Detection of recombinant protein within the bacterial spore coat
The spore pellets were sonicated and boiled in lysis buffer. The protein lysates were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking with blocking solution, SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) Spike Non-Neutralizing Antibody, Mouse Mab (1:1000 dilution, Sino Biological Inc., China) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Precision Protein StrepTactin-HRP Conjugate (Bio-Rad, USA) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (1:5000 dilution, Abcam, Fremont, USA). The membrane was reacted with Clarity Western ECL substrate and imaged using the ChemiDoc MP System (Bio-Rad, USA). Non-recombinant spores served as negative controls, and SARS-CoV-2 Spike S1 (D614G) His Recombinant Protein (Sino Biological Inc., China) was used as a positive control.
The expression of the recombinant protein was also detected using immunofluorescent microscopy as described earlier [ ] with slight modifications. After permeabilization in GTE-lysozyme (50 mM glucose, 20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 10 mM EDTA, 2 mg/ml lysozyme), the suspension was immediately distributed onto multichannel microscope slides previously coated with 0.01% (wt/vol) poly-L-lysine. The microscope slide was allowed to air-dry at RT, blocked with 2% BSA-PBS, and incubated at 4 °C with SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) Spike Non-Neutralizing Antibody, Mouse Mab (Sino Biological Inc., China) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150113, Abcam, Fremont, USA). Slides were then viewed, and the fluorescence image was captured using the Zeiss LSM 700 on AXIO Observer Z2 inverted microscope. The same exposure times were used for all samples. Non-recombinant spores were included as the negative control.
The localization of the recombinant proteins on the spore coat of B. subtilis spores were observed using immunogold labeling and electron microscopy (IEM), following previously established protocol [ ]. Briefly, spores were fixed (glutaraldehyde/osmium tetroxide), dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were immunogold-labeled using anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody (Sino Biological Inc., China) and examined with a ZEISS LIBRA microscope. Full protocols are provided in Additional File 1: Supplementary Method S1.

## [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2-(4-sulfophenyl)−2 H-tetrazolium] MTS cytotoxicity
An in vitro cytotoxicity test of the recombinant B. subtilis spores was performed on Vero cells using the MTS assay as previously described [ ]. Cells were treated with the recombinant B. subtilis spores (1 × 1010– 1 × 101 CFU/ml) for 48 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The presence of viable Vero cells was determined at 490 nm absorbance, and results were expressed as the percentage of total viable cells. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-treated cells served as the positive control for cell death and blank wells containing media without cells were included to account for background absorbance. ## Acute oral toxicity
All procedures involving the use of laboratory animals were reviewed and approved by the Universiti Malaya, Faculty of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (FOM-IACUC) with animal ethics reference no: 2022-240110/TIDREC/R/SAB. The acute oral toxicity study was performed according to the OECD Guideline for the Testing of Chemicals No. 423, Acute Oral Toxicity, Acute Toxic Class Method, adopted on December 17, 2001 [ ].
Eighteen SPF female BALB/c mice aged five to six weeks obtained from the Malaysian Institute of Pharmaceuticals and Nutraceuticals (IPharm, Malaysia) were used. The mice were placed in individually ventilated cages (IVC), provided with free access to water and commercial pallets, and housed under twelve hours of light-dark cycles. The mice were randomly divided into three groups, each comprising of six mice according to the study design (Table ). The mice were acclimatized for fourteen days before use. The mice were fasted for four hours prior to the administration of the bacterial spores. A single dose in three consecutive days was given via oral gavage for the study.

 

Mice were observed for acute toxicity signs which included continuous monitoring for the first four hours, periodic checks within the initial 24 h, and subsequent daily assessments for 14 days. The parameters monitored included indications of toxicity, alterations in skin, fur, eyes, and mucous membranes, somatomotor activity, behavioral patterns, presence of tremors and convulsions, salivation, the occurrence of diarrhea, lethargy, sleep patterns, changes in gait, and mortality [ ]. Weight increments or loss at baseline were documented on the first day of the treatment and the seventh and fourteenth days. Daily feed residuals were measured, and the average feed consumption of the mice per group was calculated. On day 15, prior to euthanasia, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) for blood collection via cardiac puncture. Mice were euthanized strictly adhering to the OECD Guideline for the Testing of Chemicals No. 423 by an intraperitoneal overdose of ketamine (240–360 mg/kg) and xylazine (30–48 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death [ ]. Vital organs including kidneys, liver, heart, lungs, and spleen were retained for macroscopic and histopathological examinations, and the relative organ weight to body weight index was measured.

## Preliminary immunogenicity study
To evaluate the preliminary systemic antibody response, a separate group of mice was orally administered recombinant B. subtilis spores (5 × 10¹⁰ CFU/mL) over three consecutive days. Serum was collected two weeks after the final administration, and specific anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG antibodies were measured using a commercial Mouse Anti-2019 nCoV (S) IgG ELISA Kit (Fine Biotech Co., Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum (1:10 dilution) were incubated in precoated 96-well plates, followed by HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG detection and TMB substrate development. Absorbance was read at 450 nm. Negative controls included mice receiving diluent only. The results are presented in Additional File 1: Figure S2.

## Statistical analysis
The data were presented as the mean with the standard deviation (SD). Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, CA, USA) as denoted in the respective figure legends. Normal sample distribution was determined using the Shapiro–Wilk tests. Statistical significance was established when the p-value was below 0.05.

## Results

## Cloning and expression of recombinant SARS-CoV-2 spike protein
The SARS-CoV-2 S1 domain spike protein (S1.1) was cloned, and successful expression of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein was detected using immunoblotting. A polypeptide band of approximately ~ 100 kDa matching the expected size of the recombinant SAR-CoV-2 spike protein was detected (Fig.  A, Lane 3). No band of comparable size was observed in the vector, pHPS9 protein lysate. The expression of recombinant spike proteins was also verified by using immunofluorescence microscopy. The recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing spike proteins exhibited fluorescence signals with no fluorescence signal observed in B. subtilis spores with empty vector (B. sub/pHPS9) negative control (Fig.  B). IEM labeling demonstrated the presence of gold particles, about 10 nm in size, specifically concentrated on the spore coat structures. These gold particles, indicating binding by the SARS-CoV-2 spike-neutralizing antibody, were clearly visible on the spore coat of recombinant B. subtilis spores, confirming successful expression and localization of the target antigen. No gold particles were detected on the non-recombinant spores, further supporting the specificity of the labeling (Additional File 1: Figure S2).

 

## In vitro cytotoxicity assay
The MTS cytotoxicity assay was performed to determine the in vitro Vero cell viability following exposure to various concentrations of B. subtilis spores (Fig. ). At the lowest concentration of 1 × 101 CFU/ml, cell viability was 101.7 ± 4.23% for the recombinant B. sub/pHPS9/S1.1 spores and 106.4 ± 7.31% for the non-recombinant B. sub/pHPS9 spores. At the highest concentration of 1 × 1010 CFU/ml, cell viability remained high at 148.5 ± 20.33% for B. sub/pHPS9/S1.1 spores and 127.6 ± 45.16% for B. sub/pHPS9 spores. Overall, these results showed that Vero cell viability remained high (> 100%) across all B. subtilis spore concentrations (1 × 101– 1 × 1010 CFU/ml) for both B. sub/pHPS9 and B. sub/pHPS9/S1.1 spores, with notable increasing concentrations of spores correlating to higher percentages of viable cells. 

## Acute oral toxicity study
The potential acute toxicity of B. subtilis spores was assessed through oral inoculation in mice. The mice were randomly divided into three groups: Group 1 received a diluent (control), Group 2 received a single dose of 5 × 1011 CFU/ml of non-recombinant B. sub/pHPS9 spores, and Group 3 received a single dose of 5 × 1011 CFU/ml of recombinant B. sub/pHPS9/S1.1 spores. Behavior patterns were observed in all mice during the first 30 min, at the fourth hours, periodically within the first 24 h, and daily thereafter until day 14 [ ]. During the initial 30 min post-administration, mice in Groups 2 and 3 exhibited increased somatomotor activity and demonstrated behavior consistent with itching, which persisted for up to four hours. After this period, all mice presented with normal behavior and showed no signs of distress including tremors, convulsions, diarrhea, gait changes, abnormal respiration, salivation, urination, altered feces consistency, sleep disturbances, lethargy, or mortality (Table ). No Changes were observed in the appearance of the fur, skin, eyes, and mucous membranes. The survival rate of the mice in this study was 100%. These behavioral observations suggested that a single administration of up to 5 × 1011 CFU/ml recombinant B. subtilis expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins did not induce acute toxicity in mice.
The average body weights of the mice during the pre-quarantine period ranged between 15.08 ± 1.194 g and 15.18 ± 1.12 g. The mice were acclimatized for 14 days prior to the commencement of the study. On the day before the administration of the bacterial spores, the mice’s body weights had increased to 16.14 ± 1.5 g, 16.92 ± 0.81 g, and 16.63 ± 0.84 g for Groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Throughout the study period, the body weights of the mice were monitored. On day 7, the body weights were recorded as 16.51 ± 1.43 g for Group 1, 17.57 ± 0.43 g for Group 2, and 17.03 ± 0.58 g for Group 3. By the final day before exsanguination, the body weights had increased to 17.2 ± 1.24 g for Group 1, 17.89 ± 0.66 g for Group 2, and 18.01 ± 0.67 g for Group 3. One-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in endpoint body weight between the mice orally fed with the B. subtilis spores (Groups 2 (p = 0.4201) and Group 3 (p = 0.3217)) and the control group (Group 1) (Fig. A). These findings suggested that oral administration of B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins and the non-recombinant spores did not adversely affect the growth or body weight of the mice. 
On day 15, the mice were humanely sacrificed, and all of the vital organs such as kidneys, liver, heart, lungs and spleens were observed and weighed. Gross macroscopic evaluation of the vital organs showed no macroscopic alteration in the group of mice administered with the B. subtilis spores and the control group (Fig. B). The relative organ to body weight index was measured after necropsy. The relative left kidney to body weight indexes were 0.48 ± 0.15% in the control group, 0.57 ± 0.15% in Group 2 (p = 0.392), and 0.54 ± 0.06% in Group 3 (p = 0.67). The relative right kidney to body weight indexes were 0.5 ± 0.08% in the control group, 0.6 ± 0.14% in Group 2 (p = 0.468), and 0.5 ± 0.11% in Group 3 (p > 0.999). The relative liver-to-body weight indexes were 4.32 ± 0.84% in the control group, 4.37 ± 0.45% in Group 2 (p = 0.993), and 3.39 ± 1.06% in Group 3 (p = 0.119). The relative heart-to-body weight indexes were 0.43 ± 0.1% in the control group, 0.49 ± 0.081% in Group 2 (p = 0.315), and 0.31 ± 0.028% in Group 3 (p = 0.059). The relative lung-to-body weight indexes were 0.8 ± 0.073% in the control group, 1.00 ± 0.07% in Group 2 (p = 0.083), and 0.71 ± 0.21% in Group 3 (p = 0.285). The relative spleen-to-body weight indexes were 0.47 ± 0.21% in the control group, 0.38 ± 0.09% in Group 2 (p = 0.429), and 0.36 ± 0.06% in Group 3 (p = 0.311). Overall, gross macroscopic evaluation of vital organs revealed no visible abnormalities in mice orally fed with B. subtilis spores. The relative organ-to-body weight indices showed no significant differences for all organs, suggesting that oral administration of the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins did not induce significant organ toxicity or pathological changes.
The food intake of the mice was monitored over 13 days (Fig. C). On day 1, the mice of Group 1 consumed 16.1 g, Group 2 consumed 15.62 g, and Group 3 consumed 16.27 g. By day 13, the food intake peaked at 37.49 g for the mice of Group 1, 26.87 g for Group 2, and 37.01 g for Group 3. Overall, the oral administration of the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins did not result in any observable changes in the mice’s appetite as indicated by normal food intake throughout the study.

 

Serum of mice orally fed with a single dose of recombinant B. subtilis spores were obtained on Day 15 before exsanguination. Serum biochemical profiles were analyzed to assess the effects of the administration of the recombinant B. subtilis spores on the mice’s physiology by determining the kidney and liver profiles.
Mice from Group 2 showed an average urea concentration of 10.2 ± 0.79 mmol/L (p = 0.812) and mice from Group 3 showed 10.67 ± 2.61 mmol/L (p = 0.626), compared to mice from the control group, 9.35 ± 1.77 mmol/L. Creatinine levels in mice from Group 2 showed an average of 8.33 ± 1.16 µmol/L (p = 0.211) while mice from Group 3 showed an average of 10.67 ± 1.16 µmol/L (p = 0.967). The creatinine level of mice from the control group was 11 ± 2.82 µmol/L (Fig. A). Overall, urea levels were slightly elevated in mice of both Groups 2 and 3 compared to the control group, but the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Creatinine levels showed a minor decline in mice of Group 2 and Group 3, though these changes were also not significant (p > 0.05). These findings suggested that the recombinant B. subtilis spores posed no significant impact on the kidney function of the inoculated mice. 
Mice from Groups 2 and 3 exhibited an increase in total protein levels, measuring 43 ± 1.73 g/L (p = 0.999) and 43.67 ± 0.58 g/L (p = 0.9982), respectively, compared to the control group (42 ± 1.41 g/L). Albumin levels remained relatively consistent across mice from all groups, with values of 13 g/L, 13.33 ± 0.58 g/L, and 13.67 ± 0.58 g/L observed in Groups 1, 2 (p > 0.9999), and 3 (p = 0.9997), respectively. Globulin levels showed a decrease in mice from Group 2 (29.67 ± 1.16 g/L) (p = 0.9997) and an increase in mice from Group 3 (30 ± 1 g/L) (p = 0.9993), compared to the control group (29 ± 1.41 g/L). Alkaline phosphatase activities were increased to 209.33 U/L in mice from Group 2 (p = 0.774) and 201.33 U/L in Group 3 (p = 0.908), compared to 198.67 U/L in the control group. Alanine aminotransferase levels measured 58.33 U/L in mice from Group 2, 63 U/L from Group 3, and 67 U/L in the control group. Aspartate aminotransferase levels were lower, 159.75 U/L (p = 0.0021) in mice from Group 2 and 252.67 U/L (p = 0.653) in Group 3, compared to 279 U/L in the control group (Fig. B). Overall, total protein levels and albumin levels showed variation across all groups, while globulin levels exhibited a decrease in mice from Group 2 and an increase in Group 3. Alkaline phosphatase activity increased marginally in the spore-treated groups, whereas alanine aminotransferase levels showed a decrease. Aspartate aminotransferase levels were reduced in both treatment groups, more prominently in mice from Group 2. These findings suggested observable variations in the biochemical parameters measured, with no remarkable deviations from normal physiological ranges attributed to the oral inoculation of mice with the recombinant B. subtilis spores.
The potential effects on the lipid profile were also assessed in the serum of mice orally fed with the recombinant B. subtilis spores. The total cholesterol levels were slightly lower in mice from Group 3, showing an average of 1.51 ± 0.99 mmol/L (p = 0.561) and in Group 2, showing 1.53 ± 0.94 mmol/L (p = 0.539), compared to the control group, 1.95 ± 0.07 mmol/L. Triglyceride levels were also lower in the spore-treated groups, with mice from Group 2 showing 0.91 ± 0.4 mmol/L (p = 0.215) and Group 3 showing 1.00 ± 0.68 mmol/L (p = 0.29), compared to the control group, 1.65 ± 0.5 mmol/L. High-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels were lower in mice from Groups 2 and 3 (0.76 ± 0.48 mmol/L (p = 0.935) and 0.75 ± 0.46 mmol/L (p = 0.931), respectively) compared to the control group, 0.90 ± 0.06 mmol/L. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels were higher in mice from Group 2 (0.39 ± 0.26 mmol/L (p = 0.973)) and Group 3 (0.34 ± 0.23 mmol/L (p = 0.995)) compared to the control group (0.31 ± 0.09 mmol/L). Non-HDL cholesterol levels followed a similar trend, with mice from Group 2 at 0.76 ± 0.48 mmol/L (p = 0.752) and Group 3 at 0.76 ± 0.47 mmol/L (p = 0.755), compared to the control group, 1.05 ± 0.07 mmol/L (Fig. C). Overall, the lipid profile analysis revealed unremarkable variations in the total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol levels across the mice.
Histological analysis of the vital organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, kidney, spleen, and small intestines, revealed no abnormalities (Fig. ). The heart displayed normal myocardial structure with no signs of inflammation, fibrosis, or cardiomyocyte damage. The lungs showed preserved alveolar architecture without evidence of inflammation, congestion, septal thickening, alveolar hemorrhage, pneumonitis, fibrosis, edema, or emphysema. The liver exhibited intact hepatic lobular structure with no signs of hepatocyte degeneration, necrosis, inflammatory infiltrates, sinusoidal congestion, steatosis, fibrosis, or cholestasis. The kidneys showed well-preserved glomeruli and renal tubules with no evidence of vascular congestion, vacuolization, tubular injury, interstitial inflammation, fibrosis, or glomerulonephritis. The spleen maintained normal architecture of both white and red pulp without signs of hyperplasia, congestion, lymphoid depletion, hemosiderosis, necrosis, or granulomatous lesions. The small intestine demonstrated intact mucosal architecture, including villi, crypts, and lamina propria, with no evidence of inflammation, epithelial damage, or submucosal thickening. These findings suggested that oral inoculation of mice with the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike protein did not cause any detectable histopathological changes in major organs, indicating a favorable safety profile. 
To assess the immunogenic potential of the recombinant B. subtilis spores, serum IgG responses were measured two weeks after oral administration over three consecutive days. Mice that received recombinant B. sub/pHPS9/S1.1 (5 × 10¹⁰ CFU/mL) showed significantly elevated serum IgG levels (3.85 ± 0.45 ng/ml) compared to those receiving the non-recombinant B. sub/pHPS9 (–0.41 ± 1.24 ng/ml, p = 0.0003) or diluent control (0.22 ± 0.23 ng/ml, p = 0.0018) (Additional File 1: Figure S2).

## Discussion
Bacterial spores have been widely explored in various field applications, including as a spore-based vaccine platform [ , ]. The consumption of B. subtilis has been regarded as safe in the food industry for a long time [ ]. Food delicacies such as Natto, which is made of fermented soybeans, have been safely consumed throughout history with no reported health concerns [ , ]. In particular, the traditional process of making Natto involves using the B. subtilis fermentation [ ]. The long-standing history of its safe use and consumption highlights the exceptional suitability of B. subtilis as a reliable and effective delivery platform for recombinant proteins. We had previously demonstrated the expression of Acinetobacter baumanii proteins on the recombinant B. subtilis spores [ ]. The recombinant spores stimulated antigen-specific immune responses in mice immunized at 1 × 109 CFU/ml. While prior study has demonstrated the immunogenicity of B. subtilis spores displaying heterologous antigens [ ], comprehensive safety assessments, particularly for spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, remain limited. The present study addressed this gap by assessing the potential acute toxicity of B. subtilis spores expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins when administered at a higher dose of 5 × 1011 CFU/ml. The dose given was 250 times higher than the dose recommended by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for human consumption, which ranges from 2 × 109 CFU to 6 × 109 CFU per day [ ]. This is done in spite of earlier studies, which have shown that B. subtilis, even when administered at a higher dose, does not pose safety concerns [ – ]. The high inoculation number of spores used in the present study was intentional with the aim to assess the safety profile of a single dose administration of the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing potentially toxic SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins.
The in vitro cytotoxicity demonstrates that both recombinant and non-recombinant B. subtilis spores, when added to cultured cells, retained high levels of cell viability across a wide range of concentrations, from 1 × 101 to 1 × 1010 CFU/ml. No cytotoxicity was observed, as reported earlier in B. subtilis CU1 on Vero cells [ ], B. subtilis IDCC1101 on HaCaT cells [ ], B. subtilis L2 on PK-15 cells [ ] and B. subtilis WB800N on human PBMCs [ ]; in fact, a mild proliferative or stimulatory effect was noted. The high cell viability observed in this study could be due to several factors including, (i) the spores could be stimulating cellular metabolic activities resulting in cell replication, (ii) the inherent properties of the B. subtilis spores in promoting cell proliferation or reducing cell death, the mechanisms of which are still unknown. However, since the spores are intended for mucosal administration, the use of gastrointestinal epithelial cell lines would provide a more physiologically relevant model for safety assessment. Future studies will therefore incorporate cytotoxicity evaluations using gastrointestinal cell lines such as Caco-2 and HT-29. Notably, the cytotoxicity of B. subtilis-based platforms has previously been evaluated in these cell types without evidence of adverse effects [ , ].
The dose used for the in vivo acute toxicity study was deliberately set higher than the maximum concentration tested in the in vitro cytotoxicity assay. While in vitro assays provide valuable preliminary information on cellular tolerance, they cannot fully replicate the complex physiological environment of a living organism. Therefore, to ensure a good safety assessment, the mice were orally administered with approximately a five-fold higher dose of recombinant spores (5 × 10¹¹ CFU/mL) over three consecutive days. This dose escalation strategy aligns with standard toxicology practices aimed at identifying potential adverse effects. Despite the administration of an exceptionally high dose of the recombinant B. subtilis, no mortality was recorded for all the orally fed mice. The group of mice administered with bacterial spores, however, exhibited transiently increased somatomotor activity and itching behavior within four hours after the administration of the bacterial spores, suggesting a mild acute response to the bacterial spores. While the exact component responsible for these effects remains unidentified, potential mechanisms may include gut-immune activation through mast cell or dendritic cell stimulation, leading to localized cytokine release (e.g., IL-2, IL-13), resulting in an itch behavior [ , ]. Importantly, these effects were self-limiting and not associated with any pathological changes, indicating they represent only transient physiological responses. Although the current study did not investigate these mechanisms in depth, future work could examine acute-phase immune markers (e.g., histamine, mast cell proteases) or neural activation patterns to further characterize these observations. Importantly, the self-resolving nature and lack of associated toxicity suggest these effects are likely negligible for vaccine safety, but dose-optimization studies may further minimize such responses.
Changes in mice’s body weight are often a reliable indicator of an animal’s overall health status and are also considered a key measure in human studies [ ]. A significant decrease in body weight reflects the potentially harmful effects of the substances being evaluated. In the present study, no significant differences in the mice’s body weight were observed in the group of mice orally fed with recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. Food intake was similarly unaffected, supporting the conclusion that the spore treatment did not impair the animals’ appetite or growth. Observations made in the present study are also consistent with the acute toxicity study conducted with other Bacillus strains [ – ] and no evidence of toxicity of spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins.
The relative organ to body weight index is another important indicator of the toxicity effects. It is a sensitive parameter to detect any treatment-related changes in vital organs [ ]. The present study revealed that there were no significant alterations, lesions, or injuries observed in the area of the vital organs. A similar organ-to-body weight index pattern was also observed, suggesting that there was no toxicity associated with acute administration of the recombinant B. subtilis spores. Histopathological examination of vital organs (liver, kidneys, spleen, heart, and lungs) revealed normal architecture with no evidence of inflammation, necrosis, or other pathological alterations. These findings were complemented by serum biochemical analyses showing all parameters (kidney function markers, liver enzymes, and lipid profiles) were not significantly different across all groups except for a minor decrease in AST level in the non-recombinant B. subtilis spore group but the values were still within the normal range as reported in earlier studies [ , ]. These findings are consistent with earlier report that the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein did not induce hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or lipotoxicity and no adverse effects from oral consumption of the B. subtilis spores expressing spike proteins in mice were observed [ ].
Preliminary data from a pilot immunization study demonstrated that oral administration of recombinant B. subtilis spores displaying the SARS-CoV-2 S1.1 fragment elicited significant higher levels of spike-specific serum IgG, two weeks post-administration, compared to control groups (Additional File 1: Figure S2). This suggests that the spore-based oral vaccine platform is also capable of inducing a systemic humoral immune response. While these findings are encouraging, the immunogenicity observed was preliminary and derived from a limited sample size. Further studies are necessary to confirm the magnitude and durability of the immune response.
The potential for antigen shedding associated with the use of live bacterial vectors has raised important biosafety concerns. However, the present study demonstrated that recombinant B. subtilis spores, while capable of surviving gastrointestinal transit, as confirmed by PCR amplification of fecal samples within 72 h post-immunization, did not persist beyond this period. No spores were detectable in fecal samples after day 3, indicating their transient passage through the gut (unpublished data). This temporal restriction suggests that it provides an adequate antigen exposure to stimulate the host immune system, yet limits systemic dissemination or long-term colonization, thereby localizing antigen stimulation to the mucosal surfaces. However, it is worth exploring the localization of the recombinant spores in other parts of body systems.
It is worth noting, however, that in the present study, the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins were expressed within the spore coat of the B. subtilis spore without the anchoring motifs, in comparison to the earlier studies, which utilized additional linkers to present the spike proteins [ , ]. Despite the absence of such motifs, IEM revealed that the spike proteins were predominantly localized on the outer layers of the recombinant spores, particularly within the spore coat (Additional file 1: Figure S3). This suggests successful surface presentation of the antigen, which is crucial for facilitating direct interaction with the host immune system and may contribute to enhanced immunogenicity of the spore-based vaccine platform. Regardless, all of these studies demonstrated that the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins, when presented by the B. subtilis spores, did not cause any acute toxicity even at extreme doses, supporting their further development as a viable oral vaccine delivery platform.

## Conclusion
In the present study, the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins were expressed on the B. subtilis spores without using the fusion to any anchor proteins. The recombinant B. subtilis spores when administered orally up to 5 × 1011 CFU/ml, did not cause mortality or any severe adverse effects on the behavior, body weight, food intake, relative organ weights, or biochemical parameters in mice. Both in vitro and in vivo toxicity studies demonstrated that the recombinant B. subtilis spores expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein are not toxic. These findings establish the preliminary safety profile of this platform for oral vaccine delivery. Given the known ability of spore-based vaccines to stimulate mucosal immunity, as demonstrated by the preliminary IgG responses in this study and supported by previous reports, this platform holds promise as a needle-free booster to enhance systemic and mucosal immunity following conventional parenteral vaccination. While our results are promising, future studies should investigate the long-term toxicity, immunogenicity potential, and dose optimization to advance this platform toward clinical application. This investigation, nonetheless, provides a critical foundation for developing B. subtilis spores as a safe, needle-free vaccine delivery system, particularly for the expression of potentially toxic proteins, like SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins.
