Here, we probe the role of serine metabolism in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb)-infected macrophages. We reveal that Mtb infection induces enzymes associated with the serine synthesis pathway (SSP) and serine transporters. Moreover, inhibition of the key SSP enzyme or restriction of exogenous serine boosts antimycobacterial immunity in both in vitro and in vivo. Depletion of serine reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels by diminishing the levels of reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. This ROS reduction destabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha, impairing glucose uptake and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Consequently, reduced ATP production activates adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase, which inhibits mTOR and induces autophagy, thereby exerting an antimycobacterial effect. These findings underscore serine’s role as a crucial immune metabolite during Mtb infection and propose that manipulating serine metabolism holds therapeutic promise against mycobacterial infections.

## INTRODUCTION
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), the causative agent of tuberculosis (TB), remains a significant global health threat ( ). Mtb exhibits unique characteristics, including residing within macrophages, manipulating phagosome maturation to avoid lysosomal degradation, and inhibiting host cell apoptosis ( ). While Mtb clearly possesses distinct mechanisms to regulate host immunity, the precise methods by which it modulates immune function remain to be fully elucidated. Accumulating evidence suggests that host immunometabolism plays a critical role in immune cell function during TB pathogenesis ( , ). Given that macrophages are the first line of cellular defense against Mtb infection ( ) and their inflammatory functions are directly responsible for controlling the infection ( ), understanding macrophage metabolism has emerged as a central theme in TB pathogenesis research.
Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of inflammatory macrophages ( ). A growing body of research has highlighted the potential of amino acid transporters and catabolic enzymes as immunotherapy targets in inflammatory and infectious diseases ( , , , ). For instance, during bacterial infection, exogenous serine promotes the synthesis of glutathione, which in turn downregulates reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated immune responses ( ). However, how specific metabolic activities, such as amino acid metabolism, participate in antibacterial innate immunity is largely unknown.
Serine, a non-essential amino acid, plays a multifaceted role in macrophage function ( ). Serine can be acquired through both de novo synthesis pathway and uptake from the microenvironment ( ). During de novo synthesis, glycolysis-derived 3-phosphoglycerate is converted to 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate by phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), followed by a series of enzymatic reactions that ultimately generate serine. A recent study has demonstrated that exogenous serine supports macrophage polarization through mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling ( ). Additionally, serine metabolism sustains macrophage inflammatory function via NAD+ protein acetylation ( ). However, serine metabolism antagonizes antiviral innate immunity by preventing lysosomal degradation ( ). These findings suggest that the PHGDH-mediated serine synthesis pathway (SSP) and/or exogenous serine may have a broader and yet unidentified role in macrophages during infection.
Our study investigates the impact of serine metabolism on mycobacterial pathogenesis. We demonstrate that murine macrophages infected with Mtb exhibit enhanced PHGDH-mediated SSP. Inhibiting PHGDH, the rate-limiting enzyme of SSP, with the inhibitor CBR-5884 significantly restricts the intracellular survival of Mtb. Furthermore, limiting both exogenous serine and glycine availability suppresses bacterial survival in both in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, inhibiting both exogenous and endogenous serine metabolism decreases ROS production due to a reduction in NADH levels. This reduction in ROS downregulates GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake, leading to enhanced AMPK activity through limited adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and ultimately promoting autophagy-mediated antimycobacterial immunity. Collectively, our findings elucidate a novel mechanism by which serine metabolism plays critical roles in Mtb pathogenesis and propose a potential therapeutic target for TB treatment.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Mice
All experimental mice were on the C57BL/6 background and housed in individually ventilated microisolator cages under levels 3 biological containment conditions with a 12 hours light/dark cycle at controlled temperature (22°C ± 2°C). Mice had ad libitum access to standard chow diet (Cat #A10021B, Research Diet) and water. For experiments involving serine- and glycine-free diets, mice were pre-fed with either control (Cat #A10021B, Research Diet) or serine- and glycine-free (Cat #A17060601, Research Diet) for 2 weeks before experimentation. All procedures involving mice were conducted in accordance with guidelines set by the Korean Food and Drug Administration.

## Bone Marrow-Derived Macrophages (BMDMs)
Primary BMDMs were isolated from femoral, tibial, and pelvic bones of C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old). BMDMs were differentiated by culturing bone marrow cells on cell culture plates for 4 days in complete DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, WELGENE), 25 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor (R&D Systems), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 250 ng/ml amphotericin B. Mature BMDMs were washed in antibiotic-free DMEM containing 5% FBS and rested overnight before use.

## Bacterial Strains
The Mtb strain H37Rv (ATCC 25618) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. In certain in vitro experiments, an red fluorescent protein (RFP)-expressing strain of Mtb H37Rv was used. Mycobacteria were cultured in Middle-brook 7H9 broth (BD Biosciences) supplemented with 10% OADC (oleic acid, albumin, dextrose, catalase) and 0.2% glycerol. Cultures were maintained at 37°C with agitation at 140 rpm until they reached mid-log phage (OD600 0.6-0.8). Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 30 minutes, washed, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). They were then storage at −80°C until further use.

## In vitro Bacterial Infection
Bacterial suspensions were thawed, briefly sonicated, and immediately used for infection. BMDMs were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:1 by adding single-cell bacterial suspensions to the culture medium for 3 hours. Non-phagocytozed bacteria were removed by washing and cultured in antibiotic-free DMEM containing 5% FBS.

## In vivo Bacterial Infection
Eight-week-old female mice were intratracheally infected with Mtb H37Rv at the indicated colony-forming units (CFU). Mice were sacrificed 2 or 4 weeks post-infection, and blood samples were collected from the heart. Left lung lobes were obtained for intracellular survival analysis, while right lung lobes were homogenized with beads in radio immune precipitation assay buffer using bead homogenizer (BIOAND) for immunoblotting.

## Intracellular Survival Analysis
To quantify intracellular survival bacteria, cells were lysed in sterilized distilled water. Lysates were serially diluted and plated onto Middle Brook 7H10 agar plates (BD Biosciences) supplemented with 10% OADC and 0.5% glycerol. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 3 weeks to enumerate CFUs.

## Determination of Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Value
Synergistic effects of CBR-5884 or siPhgdh with rifampicin or bedaquiline were assessed by the checkerboard method ( ). The fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index was calculated using the formula: FIC = (MICA combination/MICA alone) + (MICB combination/MICB alone). Synergy was defined as FIC ≤ 0.5, indifference as 0.5 < FIC ≤ 4, and antagonism as FIC > 4.

## Immunoblotting
Proteins were extracted from cells by resuspending them in radio immune precipitation assay buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, the total protein concentration was determined using Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad) and BSA standards. Equal amounts (15-20 μg) of total protein were separated by 8%-15% SDS-PAGE gels for immunoblotting. Specific antibodies were used to measure target protein levels, and visualization was performed using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Millipore).

## Cytokine Quantification
Right lung lobes were homogenized with beads in PBS containing 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (ThermoFisher Scientific) using a bead homogenizer and immediately frozen at –80°C until analysis. Homogenates were thawed, centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were analyzed using a Cytometric Bead Array (Mouse Inflammation Kit, BD Biosciences).

## Flow Cytometry
For surface molecule staining, the cells were incubated in specific antibodies in PBS for 20 minutes at 4°C, protected from light. Intracellular ROS was measured using dihydroethidium (DHE, Invitrogen) in PBS for 30 minutes at 37°C. 2-NBDG uptake was quantified by incubating cells sequentially in glucose-free DMEM, followed by incubation with 50 μM of 2-NBDG (ThermoFisher Scientific) in glucose-free DMEM for 1 hour at 37°C. Mitochondrial ROS was assed using 1 μM MitoSOX (Invitrogen) in PBD for 30 minutes at 37°C. Samples were acquired using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent) and analyzed with FlowJo.

## NAD and NADH Quantification
NAD or NADH levels in cell lysates were measured using a NAD/NADH Assay Kit (Abcam).

## ATP Quantification
ATP concentration was quantified with an ATP Assay Kit (Abcam) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## PHGDH Activity Measurement
PHGDH activity in cell lysates was measured using a PHGDH Activity Assay Kit (Abcam) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Cell Viability Assay
Cell viability was measured using a cell counting kit-8 (Dojindo) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Measurement of Serine Concentration
Serine concentration in serum, medium, or cell lysate was determined using a DL-Serine Assay Kit (Abcam). For serum preparation, whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture and centrifuged. The resulting supernatant (serum) was collected, and the cell pellet was discarded. For in vitro experiments, cells and their corresponding culture media were harvested separately. The cells were lysed using assay buffer, followed by 3 freeze-thaw cycles to ensure complete lysis. All samples were deproteinized with the sample cleanup mix, filtered through a 10 kDa spin column, and assayed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RNA Extraction and RT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reversed-transcribed with Hybrid-R (GeneAll). RT-PCR was performed using Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England BioLabs) with specific primers. Results were normalized to β-actin expression in each sample.

## Immunofluorescent Staining and Confocal Microscopy
Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100, and incubated overnight with specific antibodies at 4°C. Secondary fluorescent antibodies were added for 2 hours at RT, protected from light. DAPI staining was used for nuclear visualizing. The samples were analyzed using a confocal microscope (ZEISS).
Colocalization analysis was performed using ZEISS. To quantify the degree of overlap between the green (LC3 or LAMP1) and red fluorescence (RFP-H37Rv) signals, colocalization efficiency was calculated using Mander’s colocalization coefficient, which represents the fraction of the red signal that overlaps with the green signal. A minimum of 30 cells per condition were analyzed across at least 3 independent experiments.

## Mitochondrial Morphology Analysis and Fragmentation Coefficient
Mitochondrial morphology was analyzed using a confocal microscope (ZEISS). Mitochondria were stained using an antibody against TOMM20. For each experimental condition, at least 20-30 cells were randomly selected across at least 3 independent experiments. Image analysis was performed using Fiji software. Within each cell, mitochondria were segmented and quantified using an automated tool, the Mitochondria Analyzer plugin for Fiji. The mitochondrial fragmentation coefficient was calculated for each cell as the ratio of the number of individual mitochondrial segments to the total mitochondrial area within that cell.

## Mitochondrial Membrane Potential
To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were stained with 1 μM of JC-1 dye (Invitrogen) for 30 minutes at 37°C. Fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence plate reader with excitation/emission wave lengths of 550 nm/600 nm for red fluorescence and 485 nm/535 nm for green fluorescence. The ratio of red fluorescence to green fluorescence was calculated.

## Transfection of Small Interfering RNA (siRNA)
Gene knockdown was achieved using siRNA targeting specific mRNAs (Santacruz) or negative control siRNAs (Bioneer), transfected into cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) Analysis
Cells were seeded at 5×104 cells per well in XF Seahorse DMEM medium and subjected to OCR analysis using the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RNA Sequencing
To extract total RNA, cells were lysed with TRIzol reagent. The RNA was quantified assessed for purity by a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). A sequencing library was prepared with 1 μg of total RNA for each sample using the Illumina TruSeq” RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina). Briefly, the total RNA sample was treated with the Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Epicenter) to deplete bacterial and eukaryotic ribosomal RNA (rRNA). The remaining RNA was converted to single-stranded cRNA using reverse transcriptase and random hexamers, and the second strand was synthesized to produce double-stranded cDNA ready for application to TruSeq library construction using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. These cDNA fragments underwent an end repair process, the addition of a single “A” base, and ligation of the indexing adapters. The products were purified and enriched with PCR to create the final cDNA library. Finally, the libraries were quantified and qualified using a qPCR quantification protocol guide (KAPA Library Quantification Kits for Illumina Sequencing platforms) and TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies), respectively. The resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced using the HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina), generating approximately 453 million paired-end reads of 101 nucleotides in length. All raw sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI database with the accession numbers SRX5057205-SRX5057210 under BioProject PRJNA50644.

## Quantification and Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using Prism v8.4 software (GraphPad Software, Inc). The unpaired 2-tailed t test compared 2 independent groups, and 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) analyzed multiple groups across different time points. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for mouse survival studies. Data are represented as the mean ± SEM from at least 3 independent experiments, with sample sizes specified in figure legends. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: *, P ≤ .05; **, P ≤ .01; ***, P ≤ .001; n.s., not statistically significant (P > .05).

## RESULTS

## Mtb H37Rv Infection Activates Serine Metabolism
Serine has been characterized as a critical signaling molecule that antagonizes antiviral innate immunity and regulates immune cell function ( , ). To determine the dynamics of serine metabolism in macrophages after Mtb H37Rv infection, we employed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Genes involved in serine de novo synthesis (Phgdh, Psat1, and Psph) were found to be upregulated in BMDMs responding to infection with H37Rv ( ). We observed increased expression of SSP enzymes in BMDMs at 0-48 hours post-infection with Mtb H37Rv ( and ). Notably, treatment with Mtb secretory proteins ESAT-6 and CFP-10, but not heat-killed H37Rv, induced SSP enzyme expression, suggesting that secretory proteins of Mtb was at least partially involved in SSP induction ( , , , and ). Additionally, we demonstrated a corresponding increase in the protein expression of the SSP enzymes in the lungs of H37Rv-infected mice at 2 weeks post-infection (wpi) ( and ). 
The intracellular serine pool is primarily sustained via 2 coupled processes: extracellular serine uptake through the alanine-serine-cysteine-threonine transporters (ASCTs) and de novo synthesis through SSP ( ). We then investigated whether Mtb H37Rv infection influences exogenous serine uptake and intracellular serine levels. The expression of Slc1a4 and Slc1a5 genes, encoding ASCT1 and 2, respectively, was time-dependently increased in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs ( ). Consistent with the rise in Slc1a4 and Slc1a5 mRNA, Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs exhibited increased consumption of serine from the culture medium ( ). Additionally, we observed a rise in intracellular serine levels upon Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). To elucidate which process contributes to the intracellular serine pool, we used CBR-5884, a selective small molecule enzymatic inhibitor of PHGDH ( ), and serine/glycine-depleted culture medium (–SG). This condition did not significantly increase cytotoxicity and cell death in Mtb-infected BMDMs ( ). Depletion of serine and glycine from the medium significantly decreased intracellular serine levels, and treatment with the PHGDH inhibitor CBR-5884 also resulted in decreased serine during Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). Notably, serine depletion in the medium had a more pronounced effect on intracellular serine levels compared to PHGDH inhibition, indicating that exogenous serine uptake more strongly affects the intracellular serine pool during infection ( ). Taken together, Mtb H37Rv infection upregulates the intracellular serine pool through SSP and ASCTs.

## Serine Depletion Contributes to Host Defense Against Mtb H37Rv Infection
To explore the function of the serine in immunity to Mtb, we assessed the intracellular survival of Mtb H37Rv in serine-deprived macrophages. We observed that both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of PHGDH significantly reduced bacterial growth within BMDMs ( , , ). Similarly, depletion of serine by serine/glycine-depleted culture medium also resulted in decreased survival of Mtb H37Rv in BMDMs ( ). Furthermore, inhibition of ASCTs using the specific inhibitor L-phenylglycine suppressed Mtb H37Rv growth in BMDMs ( ). Next, to explore the potential effect of PHGDH inhibition as a host-directed therapeutic strategy in TB, we examined the combinatorial effects of serine metabolism inhibition with anti-TB drugs. Specifically, we assessed the interaction between pharmacological inhibition of PHGDH using CBR-5884 and genetic suppression of PHGDH using siPhgdh with first-line anti-TB drugs (rifampicin and bedaquiline) against intracellular Mtb in BMDMs. Both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of the SSP demonstrated synergistic interactions with rifampicin and bedaquiline, with FIC indices ranging from 0.19 to 0.5 ( ), indicating clear synergy. These results suggest that inhibition of serine metabolism enhances the activity of conventional anti-TB drugs and supports the potential of serine metabolism targeting as an adjunct host-directed therapeutic strategy. 
To further elucidate the effect of serine restriction on Mtb H37Rv infection in vivo, we maintained mice with control or serine/glycine-free feed for 2 weeks and then infected them with Mtb H37Rv ( ). This dietary intervention resulted in a significant reduction in serine concentration in serum without affecting body weight ( and ). Importantly, mice fed the serine/glycine-free diet displayed a significant reduction in bacterial burden within their lungs at both early (2 weeks post-infection) and later (4 weeks post-infection) ( and ) time points without weight loss (data not shown). Serine-restricted mice also exhibited a marked decrease in pulmonary inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, interleukin (IL)-10, and IL-6, compared to control-fed mice upon Mtb H37Rv infection, but showed no cytokine differences in uninfected controls ( and ). Furthermore, serine-restricted mice displayed significantly improved survival following Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). Collectively, these findings suggest that depletion of serine, either by restricting its uptake or inhibiting its de novo synthesis, can enhance the clearance of Mtb H37Rv.

## Serine Catabolism is a NADH Source During Mtb H37Rv Infection
Serine catabolism reportedly plays a role in providing NADH, a crucial metabolite for cellular energy production, particularly when mitochondrial respiration is impaired ( ). Given that Mtb infection can cause mitochondrial damage ( ), potentially contributing to the development of TB pathogenesis ( ), serine catabolism may be important for NADH during Mtb infection ( ). To determine whether serine catabolism is required for NADH production during H37Rv infection, we measured the expression of SHMT2, an enzyme involved in serine catabolism. We observed that Mtb H37Rv infection increased the expression of SHMT2 ( and ), indicating a potential role for serine catabolism in NADH production. Consistent with this observation, Mtb H37Rv infection resulted in elevated intracellular NADH and NADH/NAD+ ratio, accompanied by decreased intracellular NAD+ ( ). However, serine depletion did not affect NAD(H) abundance during Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). 
We qualitatively assessed mitochondrial dynamics in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs using electron microscopy. Mtb H37Rv infection induced mitochondrial swelling, characterized by morphological alteration, loss of cristae, and the presence of electron-dense particles within mitochondria ( ). Additionally, Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs displayed increased mitochondrial fragmentation compared to uninfected BMDMs ( ). Consistent with this observations, JC-1 staining revealed decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) during H37Rv infection ( ), suggesting mitochondrial damage.
We then explored whether serine depletion affects NADH during H37Rv infection. Interestingly, serine depletion significantly decreased NADH and the NADH/NAD ratio in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs, while having no effect on intracellular NAD+ levels compared to uninfected controls, and no effect on uninfected controls ( , , and ). Since NAD(H) are essential metabolites involved in glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism, we further explored lactate secretion and mitochondrial dynamics. Serine depletion resulted in lower lactate secretion and decreased mitochondrial potential in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs ( , , and ). Furthermore, serine depletion resulted in a decrease in mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in BMDMs during Mtb H37Rv infection, while showing no effect in uninfected controls ( and ). Additionally, extracellular acidification rate was decreased serine-deprived macrophages during Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). These results suggest that intracellular serine contributes to cellular metabolism by affecting NADH levels.

## Serine Depletion Reduces GLUT1-Dependent Glucose Uptake Through ROS-Mediated HIF1α Regulation
The mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) is the primary source of ROS ( ). Mitochondrial NADH levels can modulate ROS generation through the ETC ( ). To investigate whether serine-derived NADH contributes to ROS production, we measured cellular ROS and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels in Mtb-infected macrophages upon serine deprivation. Serine depletion was sufficient to reduce cellular ROS levels in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs compared to controls ( ). Consistent with this observation, serine depletion also decreased mtROS levels ( ), indicating that intracellular serine abundance is closely related to ROS generation. 
Since preventing ROS-mediated stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) represses glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) expression and glucose uptake ( ), we then investigated whether serine depletion affects HIF1α and GLUT1-medited glucose uptake. Our results showed serine depletion decreased HIF1α expression during Mtb H37Rv infection ( and ). Furthermore, serine depletion also lowered GLUT1 expression and glucose uptake in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs ( and ).
To determine if the observation in ROS production interferes with GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake during Mtb H37Rv infection, we incubated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs with β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) or Mitotempo to restrict ROS or mtROS, respectively. Both methods of ROS restriction decreased HIF1α expression ( and ), leading to suppressed GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake ( ). Additionally, to determine the role of HIF1α in GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake during Mtb H37Rv infection, we treated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs with HIF1α inhibitor FM19G11. Our results showed that FM19G11 effectively inhibited HIF1α expression during Mtb H37Rv infection ( and ). Consistent with the observation in serine-depleted cells, inhibition of HIF1α expression with FM19G11 resulted in reduced GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake ( ).
To determine whether the observed decrease in glucose uptake upon serine depletion is dependent on GLUT1, we treated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs and serine-depleted BMDMs with the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876. Our results showed that BAY-876 inhibited glucose uptake of Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs to a similar extent as serine depletion ( ). Consistently, FM19G11 treatment also resulted in a similar reduction in glucose uptake ( ). Collectively, these findings indicate that serine depletion reduces ROS-mediated HIF1α stabilization, leading to a subsequent suppression of GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake.

## Inhibiting Mitochondrial Serine Catabolism Reduces Oxidative Stress and Suppresses GLUT1-Dependent Glucose Uptake
Mitochondrial serine catabolism is a crucial pathway for generating NADH ( ), particularly through pathways that involve specific enzymes ( ). We hypothesized that inhibiting mitochondrial serine catabolism during Mtb H37Rv infection might alter the NADH/NAD ratio and impact ROS levels. To investigate this, we treated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs with either SHIN1 or LY345899, small molecule inhibitors of mitochondrial serine catabolism ( ). Interestingly, while neither SHIN1 nor LY345899 affected NAD+ levels, both treatments significantly reduced the NADH levels and the NADH/NAD ratio in Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs and had no effect on uninfected controls ( and ). These finding suggest that mitochondrial serine catabolism is a key source of NADH during Mtb H37Rv infection. 
Consistent with our observations on serine depletion ( and ), inhibiting mitochondrial serine catabolism resulted in decreased cellular and mitochondrial ROS levels ( and ). Furthermore, these cells exhibited reduced GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake ( ). Notably, inhibiting mitochondrial serine catabolism also led to a significant decrease in the intracellular survival of Mtb H37Rv ( ). These results collectively indicate that mitochondrial serine catabolism plays a critical role in mediating serine’s contribution to Mtb clearance.

## Serine Depletion Activates AMPK and Suppresses mTOR Signaling, Restricting Mtb Survival
Cellular metabolism relies heavily on glucose, which is broken down through glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to generate ATP ( ). Adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a critical metabolic sensor, responding to changes in the AMP/ATP ratio ( ). Increases in this ratio, indicative of cellular stress, trigger AMPK activation, which could enhance antimicrobial responses against Mtb infection ( ). Since our data demonstrated that serine-depleted BMDMs exhibited reduced lactate secretion, glucose consumption and GLUT1 expression compared to controls during Mtb H37Rv infection ( and ), we hypothesized that serine depletion might impair ATP generation. Indeed, we observed decreased ATP levels in serine-depleted BMDMs during Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). Furthermore, serine deprivation of BMDMs increased p-AMPK ( , , , and ). 
To investigate whether GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake affects AMPK activation during Mtb H37Rv infection, we incubated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs with the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876 for 24 hours. This treatment also resulted in a comparable increase in p-AMPK ( and ). It is well established that AMPK phosphorylation leads to the subsequent inhibition of mTOR, and S6K is directly phosphorylated by mTOR ( , ). In line with this, AMPK activation in serine-depleted BMDMs decreased p-mTOR and p-S6K during Mtb H37Rv infection ( , , , and ). Thus, serine deprivation-induced limitation in ATP activates AMPK, leading to a subsequent suppression of mTOR signaling.
Previous studies have suggested that AMPK is involved in the attenuation of proinflammatory cytokine production in Mtb-infected BMDMs ( , ). In line with this observation, our results revealed a decrease in inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, and IL-10, in serine-depleted BMDMs compared to control BMDMs during Mtb H37Rv infection ( ). Notably, cytokine levels were dependent on serine concentration, peaking at control levels (42 mg/L), with both lower and higher serine concentrations resulting in decreased cytokine production ( ). To further elucidate the role of AMPK activation in restricting of the intracellular survival of Mtb H37Rv, we incubated Mtb H37Rv-infected BMDMs with the AMPK activator, AICAR for 24 hours. AMPK activation by AICAR decreased p-mTOR and p-S6K levels while repressing bacterial growth in BMDMs ( , , and ). These results suggest that proper serine levels are crucial for modulating Mtb H37Rv-induced inflammatory responses and that serine-deprivation-induced AMPK activation plays a critical role in suppressing Mtb H37Rv-induced inflammatory responses and limiting bacterial survival.

## Serine Depletion Regulates Intracellular Survival of Mtb M37Rv by Inducing Autophagy
AMPK activation has been shown to enhance autophagic activation through negative regulation of the mTOR pathway, playing a crucial role in antimicrobial responses against Mtb ( ). To investigate the potential role of autophagy in the context of serine depletion, we analyzed autophagy markers both in vivo and in vitro. Serine-restricted mice exhibited decreased levels of the autophagy substrate p62 and increased levels of the autophagosomal marker LC3 in lung tissue during Mtb H37Rv infection ( and ). Similarly, silencing PHGDH by siPhgdh in BMDMs also showed decreased p62 and increased LC3 compared to controls ( and ). In line with this, serine-depleted BMDMs cultured in serine/glycine-depleted medium or CBR-5884 had similar changes in p62 and LC3 expression compared to controls during Mtb H37Rv infection ( , , , and ). Furthermore, treatment with the autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin a1 (Baf) revealed a time-dependent increase in p62 and LC3 expression in serine-depleted BMDMs compared to controls during Mtb H37Rv infection, suggesting that serine depletion increased autophagic flux infection ( , , , and ). 
We next examined the effect of serine depletion on mycobacterial phagosomal maturation. Serine-depleted BMDMs exhibited increased co-localization of LC3, an autophagosomal marker, with RFP-Mtb H37Rv ( ). Additionally, serine deprivation significantly enhanced the co-localization of Mtb phagosomes with LAMP1 ( ). To determine whether autophagy is essential for serine depletion-mediated bacterial regulation, we knocked down Atg5, a key autophagy protein. Atg5 knockdown significantly abolished the serine depletion-mediated restriction of bacterial growth ( ). Taken together, these data suggest that serine depletion promotes autophagy activation, which results in the regulation of the intracellular survival of Mtb H37Rv.

## DISCUSSION
Serine metabolism is established as a pivotal regulator of innate and adaptive immunity, influencing macrophage polarization ( ), effector T cell proliferation ( ), and regulatory T cell function ( ). Notably, serine metabolism is also critical for the intracellular survival of Mtb ( ). During infections, immune cells dynamically modulate serine metabolism ( , , ). Viral infection appears to alter serine metabolism ( , ), whereas bacterial infections activate it to bolster immune responses ( , ). Our data illustrate that Mtb infection specifically activates the SSP and enhances serine uptake by macrophages ( ). Although Mtb is capable of endogenous serine synthesis, it primarily does so by converting host-derived glycine ( ). Therefore, the direct contribution of Mtb-derived serine to the total intracellular serine pool within infected macrophages is likely minimal. Serine is crucial for synthesizing nucleotides, S-adenosyl methionine, NADH, and glutathione via one-carbon metabolism ( ). Our results demonstrated that serine depletion reduces NADH levels and significantly decreases intracellular survival of Mtb ( and A-C), consistent with the study emphasizing NAD(H) homeostasis in host defense against Mtb ( ). Even partial inhibition of the de novo SSPs by genetic and pharmacological approaches has measurable effects on Mtb survival through regulation of NADH levels. These findings underscore serine as a critical immune metabolite in regulating intracellular Mtb survival in macrophages.
Our data suggest that Mtb infection drives serine utilization to maintain NADH levels, possibly through mitochondrial fragmentation and the activation of serine catabolism. Consequently, serine depletion leads to a decrease in NADH and subsequent downregulation of glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism ( A-I). A high NADH/NAD+ ratio inhibits alternative NADH production routes like tricarboxylic acid and fatty acid oxidation but not mitochondrial serine catabolism ( ). Moreover, Mtb disrupts MMP, crucial for NADH to NAD+ conversion via the ETC ( ). These results imply that Mtb infection alters MMP and activates serine catabolism, leading to NADH accumulation. Interestingly, enhancing NAD(H) abundance through the salvage pathway has antimycobacterial effects by promoting glycolysis ( ). However, while serine depletion reduces NADH levels, overall NAD(H) or NAD+ abundance remains unaffected ( C and ), suggesting that serine depletion may partially impair NAD+ salvage but does not inhibit antimycobacterial effects through NAD(H)-mediated glycolysis reprogramming.
Previous studies indicate that factors such as reduced NAD+/NADH ratio or ROS stabilize the HIF1α protein ( , ). Consistent with these findings, our data show that both Mtb infection and serine metabolism deficiency downregulate ROS production and HIF1α expression ( ). Given that higher NADH levels during bacterial infection correlate with increased intracellular ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential ( ), our findings suggest a potential link between serine metabolism and HIF1α stabilization. Furthermore, our study indicates that serine depletion not only reduces glucose uptake but also suppresses glycolysis upon mycobacterial infection ( ). HIF1α regulates glycolytic enzymes and promotes glucose uptake by upregulating GLUT1 expression ( ), suggesting that serine depletion may broadly impact macrophage metabolism via HIF1α regulation.
Although the reduction in 2-NBDG uptake observed under serine-depleted conditions was modest, this effect was consistently reproducible across multiple independent biological replicates and was accompanied by decreased GLUT1 expression in Mtb-infected macrophages. Moreover, serine depletion was associated with reduced extracellular acidification rate and lactate production during infection, further supporting an overall reduction in glucose utilization and glycolytic activity. Together, these findings suggest that serine depletion results in a modest but reproducible impairment of GLUT-1-associated glucose uptake and glycolytic metabolism during Mtb infection.
In addition, pharmacological restriction of ROS using β-mecaptoethanol or the mitochondrial ROS scavenger Mitotempo similarly reduced HIF1α expression and GLUT-1dependent glucose uptake in Mtb-infected macrophages. These observations support a functional role for ROS in linking serine metabolism to HIF1α-mediated metabolic regulation. However, although ROS modulation affected downstream metabolic signaling, the present study did not directly examine whether ROS induction can rescue bacterial burden under serine-depleted conditions. Therefore, the contribution of ROS to the antibacterial phenotype observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously and will require further investigation using ROS-modulating approaches in future studies.
Macrophages play critical roles in acute and chronic inflammatory processes ( , ), with glycolysis being essential for their inflammatory activation ( ). Consistent with this, our data demonstrate that serine deprivation diminishes glycolysis in macrophages, leading to a reduction in inflammatory cytokine levels in vivo and in vitro during Mtb infection ( , , and ). Since excessive cytokine production exacerbates TB-induced immunopathology ( ), our findings suggest that serine could serve as an immunomodulatory agent influencing host immunometabolism and inflammatory responses. Importantly, our studies show that serine depletion decreases not only inflammatory cytokine levels but also survival of Mtb in the lungs ( and ). Given that uncontrolled inflammation correlates with increased mortality in TB patients ( ), these results imply that serine depletion might promote protective immune responses while mitigating detrimental inflammatory responses. While glutamine and leucine primarily activate mTOR ( , ), enhanced serine uptake can also elevate mTOR activity ( , ). Consistent with these findings, we demonstrate that Mtb infection elevates both serine uptake and mTOR activity ( and ). Importantly, serine depletion effectively downregulates mTOR activity ( and ), establishing a connection between serine uptake and mTOR activity during Mtb infection. Furthermore, our data suggest that serine depletion may enhance AMPK activity due to decreased ATP production ( ). AMPK inhibits mTOR activity by phosphorylating the regulatory-associated protein of mTOR and tuberous sclerosis complex 2 ( ), suggesting a serine-dependent mechanism of AMPK-mediated mTOR activity regulation. Previous studies highlight AMPK’s role in antimicrobial autophagy against mycobacteria ( ), with AMPK activators showing potential to enhance intracellular killing of Mtb ( , ). Our data demonstrate that serine deprivation induces AMPK activation and autophagy ( , , ), suggesting that a crucial role for serine metabolism in autophagy-mediated Mtb killing in macrophages.
While our data provide compelling insights into the role of serine metabolism in macrophage responses to Mtb infection, we acknowledge the limitations of our study. Direct quantitative measurement of intracellular serine levels following pharmacological inhibition of PHGDH under -SG conditions was not performed in this study. Accurate assessment of intracellular serine levels under these conditions would require validated metabolomic approaches, such LC-MS or HPLC-based amino acid analysis, to ensure reliable and reproducible quantification of intracellular serine metabolism. Nevertheless, the observed reductions in Mtb burden and immune responses may be due to a combination of factors, including the direct impact of serine depletion on immune metabolism and the indirect effects resulting from reduced bacterial load. Importantly, the relatively modest reduction in bacterial burden observed in vivo (∼2-3 fold) should therefore be interpreted as evidence of immunometabolic modulation rather than a strong standalone therapeutic effect, which is consistent with the incremental outcomes commonly reported in host-directed therapy studies for TB. Future studies utilizing more refined experimental designs, including a broader analysis of molecular pathways, will be essential to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying the effects of serine depletion on Mtb infection.
In conclusion, our findings provide compelling insights into the role of serine metabolism in macrophage responses to Mtb infection through autophagy, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target for TB.

## LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY
Although we observed significant effects of serine depletion on Mtb survival and macrophage metabolism, other pathways may also be influenced by serine regulation that were not explored in this study. The observed reductions in Mtb burden and immune responses may be due to a combination of factors, including the direct impact of serine depletion on immune metabolism and the indirect effects resulting from reduced bacterial load. Future studies utilizing a broader analysis of molecular pathways will be essential to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying the effects of serine depletion on Mtb infection.
In addition, in vivo infections were performed using a high-dose Mtb inoculum to ensure robust and consistent immune responses. However, such experimental dosing may elicit immune dynamics distinct from those elicited by natural low-dose aerosol exposure, potentially limiting physiological relevance. Validation using lower-dose aerosol models will be necessary to confirm these findings under more representative conditions.
Furthermore, the -SG diet used in our study may induce systemic metabolic alterations in vivo that could influence multiple immune cell populations. For example, serine restriction has been reported to restore FoxP3 expression and modulate regulatory T cell function ( ), suggesting that serine depletion may exert immunomodulatory effects beyond macrophages. Accordingly, such systemic immune modulation may have contributed, at least in part, to the anti-mycobacterial effects observed in our study. In addition, the −SG diet resulted in only modest reductions in Mtb burden and did not selectively target Mtb–infected macrophages. Therefore, to strengthen mechanistic conclusions, future studies employing more cell-type–specific approaches or conditional genetic models will be necessary to confirm the macrophages-intrinsic AMPK-mTOR axis in anti-TB immunity while minimizing potential systemic confounding effects.
