## Abstract
Smokers consider that electronic cigarettes are safer than tobacco and are marketed as safe products. Nevertheless, reports show the exposure to high levels of electronic cigarette aerosols (ECA) activates lung cells and triggers inflammation and structural alterations after chronic exposure. In order to assess the potential harmful long term effect of exposure to ECA, we investigated in mice, its effect on lung and systemic inflammation as well as on lung function. To reproduce closely the situation frequently encountered in human, we exposed mice during 1 h/day during 3 or 6 months with two levels of electronic cigarette power in comparison with mice exposed to cigarette smoke (CS). Lung and systemic inflammation was evaluated by measuring cell recruitment and activation or cytokine concentrations. Respiratory function and lung transcriptome and structure were also measured. Our data revealed that chronic exposure to moderate levels of ECA increased specifically lung inflammation, these effects being characterized by the mobilization of conventional dendritic cells in the BAL and the recruitment of T cells in the lungs and by the early secretion of IL-22. Surprisingly, there is no strong overlap between the impact of both ECA and CS exposure on lung transcriptome. Modulation of pro-inflammatory pathways are limited to mice exposed to low power e-cigarette. In contrast, alteration of respiratory function is observed in high-power ECA-exposed mice with a different profile than with CS. Subchronic exposure to ECA might alter the respiratory function independently of the inflammatory response and in a different manner than CS.

## Introduction
Over the past few years, new methods of delivering nicotine have emerged, and many users prefer electronic cigarettes (e-cig) to traditional tobacco cigarettes [ ]. Invented in 2003, e-cigs have revolutionized the tobacco industry. They were introduced as a safe alternative to conventional cigarettes, and designed with the intent to provide smokers the satisfaction of conventional cigarettes without major side effects. E-cig aerosols (ECA) are generated by non-combustion heating and aerosolization of an e-liquid commonly composed of glycerol, propylene glycol, nicotine and flavorings [ ]. In addition to nicotine, ECA contain a variety of substances, depending on the composition of the e-liquids, the duration and volume of the puff and the power of the e-cig [ , ]. In particular, ECA may contain low concentrations of toxic substances, some of which are also present in combustible cigarette smoke (CS) such as carbonyl compounds, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and metals [ – ]. However, the concentration of these compounds is also strongly dependent of the type of e-cig and of the power settings used for the e-cig [ ].
Supporting this, recent data demonstrated that ECA induced an inflammatory reaction both in vitro, on epithelial cells [ , ], and in vivo in mice and in smokers.
An in vivo study conducted in a mouse model of intense exposure (5 h per day for 3 successive days) suggested that ECA increased pro-inflammatory cytokines and diminished lung glutathione levels which are critical in maintaining cellular redox balance [ ]. In another study, e-cig exposure for 2 weeks resulted in immunomodulatory effects similar to those observed after exposure to CS, with impaired pulmonary anti-microbial defenses [ ]. Furthermore, the chronic inhalation of ECV (1-hour Daily for 1 to 4 months) has been reported to induce airway hyper-reactivity and air space enlargement in exposed mice [ , ] as well as mucin production [ ] some of these effects being nicotine-dependent [ ]. In addition, some inflammatory scars and alveolar lesions persists after 2 months of starvation [ , ]. Furthermore, Paoli et al. highlighted sex-specific metabolic and xenobiotic alterations in the lungs of adolescent mice, underlining a potential vulnerability in this developmental stage [ ]. Altogether, these data confirm that acute inhalation of ECA could have an impact on the inflammatory and immune response of the lungs, two stages that could constitute the background for the development of cancers or chronic bronchitis as shown with the conventional cigarette [ ].
Nowadays, a comprehensive understanding of the long-term toxicological and immunologic consequences associated with e-cig use compared with CS are lacking. Long-term effects of ECA and the specific role of nicotine on lung parenchyma have been assessed by Roxlau et al. [ ]. Using an intense whole-body exposure protocol of 6 h per day for 8 months, the authors showed that airway inflammation following ECA inhalation, characterized by lymphocyte recruitment and significant changes in lung structure and function, was close to mild tobacco smoke-induced alterations. In addition, Dai Y et al. reported with a closely related model that ECA with a high dose of nicotine also alters lipid metabolism, cardiac remodeling and arterial oxygenation in addition to the impact on lung function [ ]. However, it remains unclear if a moderate ECA exposure protocol in a more physiologic context might have similar effects. This is why we used a moderate exposure protocol (1 h/day) to low and high power (18 W and 30 W) e-cig in nose-only exposed mice. Using this protocol, we previously reported that moderate exposure to the high-power e-cig aerosol induced oxidative DNA damage in the lung and the liver of exposed mice similarly to the effect of 3R4F cigarette smoke exposure [ ]. In the present study, we compared the impact on lung function and immune response of chronic exposure (either 3 or 6 months) to ECA with that of conventional cigarette (3R4F) smoke (Fig. ).
 

## Material and method

## In vivo model
Our experimental procedure has been reported in accordance of the ARRIVE2.0 guidelines. Experiments were conducted on one hundred and twenty male BALB/c mice (Janvier Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France), 9 weeks old. This mouse strain is described as sufficiently sensitive to the chemical induction of lung cancers [ ]. Mice have been randomly divided into eight group groups (15 mice/group). Animal procedures has been agreed with European directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and obtained the Ethical Committee on Animal Experimentation approval (ref APAFIS 10363-2017062615002072v2). Mice have been euthanized by cervical dislocation.
Animal body weights have been recorded on Monday of each weak while clinical signs were monitored daily.

## E-cigarettes and conventional cigarette
We chose the third generation “ModBox” model from NHOSS® (Innova, Bondues, France), used with the “Air Tank” clearomiser equipped with a 0.5 Ω kanthal coil and with a partially closed air flow. For experiments, we chose two power settings for the Modbox model: a “low” power of 18 W and a “high” power of 30 W. For the e-liquid, we chose the best-selling NHOSS® brand containing 65% propylene glycol, 35% glycerin, 16 mg/mL nicotine and the most common flavor, “blond tobacco”. Conventional 3R4F cigarettes were obtained from the University of Kentucky (Lexington, KY, USA). Under the HCI puffing profile, cigarette delivered 95 µg nicotine/puff while the e-cig delivered 60 µg/puff at 18 W setting and 137 µg/puff at 30 W setting [ ] and the mice received 120 puffs per day. To avoid chemical cross-contamination, two different pieces of equipment (dilution chamber, tubes, exposure towers and pipes) were used for e-cig and 3R4F exposures (Fig. ). Aerosols from e-cigs and 3R4F cigarette were generated with an InExpose e-cigarette extension system on which we adapted the Modbox and a cigarette smoking robot (SCIREQ®, Emka technologies, Montreal, Quebec, Canada), respectively. Mice were exposed to aerosols by a nose-only tower (InExpose system, SCIREQ®, Emka technologies). In order to perform a comparative toxicological study of ECA and CS, we used the Health Canada Intense puff regime (55 mL puff volume, 2 s puff duration, 30 s puff period). Based on data from the literature and our preliminary study after a 4-day subacute exposure (data not shown), two exposure protocols were applied in this study for both e-cig and 3R4F emissions: a 3-month subchronic and a 6-month chronic exposure, 60 min/day and 5 days/week. For each exposure schedule, an additional group of 15 mice was exposed to fresh conditioned air and to restrained condition as mice exposed to ECA and CS (negative control). To estimate the level of exposure, we have measured by ELISA (Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan) the blood concentrations of cotinine 24 h after the last exposure to ECA or CS.

## Sample collection
Mice were euthanized 24 h after the last exposure for both the protocols of 3 or 6 months exposure and after the lung function analysis by flexivent. The procedure was randomized among the different groups in order to avoid bias in the results. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluids, lungs, spleens and blood samples were collected and kept on ice until they were processed. In addition for lungs, one lobe was stored in RNA Later (Ambion, ThermoFisher Scientific, Illkirch, France) for transcriptomic analyses, posterior lobes were fixed with paraformaldehyde (PFA 4%, Labonord, Villeneuve d’Ascq, France) for histopathological analyses, and another lobe was frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at − 80 °C until further analyses.

## Broncho-alveolar lavage procedure, lung and spleen processing
BALs were performed on whole lungs by instilling 5 aliquots of 0.5 mL (final volume 2.5 mL) of sterile PBS. After centrifugation at 400 g for 6 min at 4 °C, supernatants (cell-free BAL fluid) from the first two aliquots of 0.5 ml were stored at − 80 °C for cytokine analysis (ELISA), and cell pellets were used for flow-cytometry analysis.
The left lobe of the lung was mashed with a sterile blade and then digested with collagenase (Collagenase Type VI 17104–019 Gibco by Life technologies, Carlsbad, California United States) at 37 °C. After 15 min of digestion, lungs were homogenized with an 18 G needle and further digested for 15 min. After centrifugation at 400 g for 6 min at 4◦C, the pellets were resuspended in a 30% Percoll solution (Percoll TM GE Healthcare 17–0891-01, Chicago, IL, United States) and centrifuged at 500 g for 15 min. Total spleen cells were also isolated from spleen and centrifuged at 400 g for 6 min at 4 °C. The lung and spleen pellets were resuspended in red blood cells (RBC) lysis buffer during 5 min at room temperature, to remove erythrocytes. The reaction of RBC lysis was stopped with PBS 2% FBS (Gibco by Life technologies, Carlsbad, California United States). After centrifugation at 400 g for 6 min at 4 ◦C, pulmonary and spleen cells were resuspended in PBS 2% FBS, then enumerated and used for flow cytometry.

## Flow cytometry
BAL, lung and spleen total cells were incubated with the appropriate panel of antibodies for 30 min in PBS 2% FCS. Conjugated antibodies were used against mouse CD5 (ref 130–102–574, FITC-conjugated), PBS57-loaded CD1 d Tetramer (NIH facility, PE-conjugated), NK1.1 (ref 130–103–963, PerCp-Cy5.5–conjugated), CD4 (ref 130–102–411, PE-Cy7-conjugated), CD25 (ref 130–102–550, APC-conjugated), CD69 (ref 561–238, Alexa700-conjugated), TCRγδ (ref 130–104–016, APC-Vio770 conjugated), TCR-β (ref 130–104–815, V450-conjugated), CD8 (ref 130–109–252, V500-conjugated), CD45 (ref BLE103140, BV605-conjugated), I-Ab (ref 130–102–168, FITC-conjugated), F4/80 (ref 130–102–422,PE conjugated), CD103 (ref 563–637, PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated), CD11c (ref 558–079, PE Cy7-conjugated), CD86 (ref 560–581, Alexa-700 conjugated), Ly6G (ref 560–600, APC-H7conjugated), CD11b (ref 560–455, V45O conjugated), CD45 (ref 130–402–512, V500 conjugated), Ly6C (ref BLE128036, BV605-conjugated) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, United States; Biolegend, San Diego, United States and Myltenyi Biotech, Paris, France) and CCR2 (ref FAB 5538 A, R&D systems, APC conjugated).
Data were acquired on a LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, United States) and analyzed using FlowJo™ software v10.2 (Stanford, CA, USA). Absolute cell numbers were calculated according to the total cell number and the frequency of CD45+ immune cells. Our previously described gating strategy [ ] allows to define the cell numbers of alveolar macrophages (AM), conventional dendritic cell (cDC), interstitial macrophages (IMa), inflammatory monocytes (IMo), natural killer (NK), Natural killer T cells (, (NKT), CD4 and CD8 T cells (CD4 T and CD8 T) as well as their level of activation by the expression of I-Ab and CD86 expression for antigen presenting cells and of CD25 and CD69 for T lymphocytes. In some cases the BAL from 3 mice were pooled in order to allow the analysis of rare cells by flow cytometry.

## Cytokine assay
Levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-22, IL-23, IFN-γ, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5, CXCL17, TNF-α and Resistin were determined in BAL, lung and serum by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the manufacturer’s recommendation (R&D systems, Biotechne, Minneapolis, MN, United States). In addition, concentrations of IL-17 and IL-23p19 were measured by an ELISA from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, United States).

## Lung histology
Fixed lung lobes were paraffin-embedded and lung sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Lung injury and inflammation were scored based on a scale evaluating bronchial damage, hyperplasia, inflammatory cell influx, alveolar exudates, damage, wall thickness, inflammation, and emphysema, all with a scale from 0 to 4. Moreover, hemorrhage, fibrinoid necrosis, leukocytoclasis and suppuration were noted as present or not (value of 1 and 0, respectively). This allows to generate a global histologic score from 0 to 36. The score was established in a blinded fashion by 2 independent experts (OCV clinical research, Lille).
In order to assess the potential development of emphysema, we evaluated the mean linear intercept (MLI) on lung sections by using the Image J software (NIH). Results were expressed in mean ± SD.

## Lung function
On the day of the sacrifice, each mouse was anaesthetized intraperitoneally with xylazine hydrochloride (15 mg/kg)/ketamine (100 mg/kg), tracheotomized, and cannulated with an 18 G metal cannula (resistance: 0.36–0.40 cmH2O.s/mL). Mice were then connected to a flexiVent FX system (SCIREQ Inc., Montreal, Qc, Canada) and operated by the flexiWare software v7.7. Immediately after connection to the ventilator, set at 150 breaths/min, two deep lung inflations were performed at least 6–12 s apart to recruit lung beyond any closed airway and to standardize lung volume history. This was done by inflating the lungs to 30 cm H2O over 3 s and holding that pressure for another 3 s to allow for the lungs to equilibrate after the inflation. Mice were then submitted to a 300 breaths/min hyperventilation in order to eliminate spontaneous breathing before a 150 breaths/min return. Next, the mechanical properties of the mouse respiratory system were assessed at baseline, i.e. before the construction of a full-range pressure-volume (PV) curve. This was done using a sequence of measurements integrated by default in the flexiVent operating software (FlexiWare v7.7). The Area between the PV loop inflation and deflation limbs (Hysteresis (H)) A static compliance (Cst) was calculated. Cst, the parameters A (estimate of inspiratory capacity) and K (shape constant) can be extracted from the Salazar-Knowles equation. Cst reflects the intrinsic elastic properties of the respiratory system (i.e. lung + chest wall) [ ]. 

## Transcriptomic analyses
Total RNAs were extracted from RNA later treated tissue samples using the miRNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration was measured with the Biospecnano spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Marne-la-Vallée, France). Transcriptomic experiments were carried out using 8 × 60k OneColor microarrays (Agilent Technologies) coupled to 60-mer oligonucleotides covering the entire mouse genome. Labeling, hybridization and lncRNA detection were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Agilent Technologies). For each microarray, Cyanine 3-coupled lncRNAs were synthesized by the QuickAmp Low Input Kit from 50 ng of total RNA. Spike-in RNAs were added to each tube and used as positive controls for the labeling and amplification steps. Labeled cDNAs were purified and 600 ng of each cDNA was then hybridized to the microarrays according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing, the microarrays were scanned and the data exported using Agilent Feature Extraction Software© (FE version 10.7.3.1). Results were then interpreted by selecting those mRNAs whose expression was at least significantly 1.5-fold higher or 1.5-fold lower than that of unexposed control mice (p < 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed with the “linear models for microarray data” (limma) package for R, using moderated t statistics with standardized data. Functional analysis of selected deregulated lung RNAs was performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software (Qiagen) by selecting the top 25 of canonical pathways for each exposure condition.

## Statistical analyses
The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were computed using GraphPad Prism software (5.00 version, GraphPad software, San Diego, USA) in accordance with the size of samples and the nature of the experiment. Values were compared to the controls with a bilateral and non-parametric Mann-Whitney test, or with a one-sample t-test, or a two-way ANOVA test when appropriate. Statistical significance was accepted for an error risk inferior to 5% and is represented as follows: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001.

## Results

## Long term e-cigarette exposure impairs clinical parameters
We first analyzed the impact of ECA on clinical parameters, including body weight and lung function, and compared it to that of CS. As expected, mice exposed to CS showed slower weight gain than control mice exposed to fresh conditioned air [ ]. This effect was also observed when mice were exposed to ECA whatever the power settings (Fig. A). In parallel, we detected cotinine within the sera of mice exposed to both ECA and CS although at 3 months exposure, these levels were lower in ECA 18 W mice as compared with CS-exposed mice (Additional figure ).
 Interestingly, this reduction in weight gain was associated with impaired lung function in mice exposed to ECA 30 W and those exposed to CS for 3 months compared to the control mice (Fig. B). Indeed, the PV-loop curve revealed a shift towards higher volumes for mice exposed to ECA 30 W and to CS-exposed mice although at a lower intensity but not with 18 W ECA-exposed mice. Based on PV-loop curves, we calculated the static compliance (Cst) and the area under the curve (AUC) (Fig. D). The Cst tends to be increased both in ECA 30 W and CS-exposed mice whereas the resistances of the central and lung airways were higher in mice exposed to ECA 18 W (Additional Fig. A). After 6 months of exposure, ECA 30 W but not 18 W-exposed mice exhibited an e the emphysematous-type alteration of lung function in mice (Fig. C). This was confirmed by a significant increase in Cst, A and Area in the ECA 30 W-exposed mice as compared to Air mice (Fig. H) associated with a decreased elastance of the lung tissue (additional Fig. B). Surprisingly, CS-exposed mice displayed a fibrotic-like alteration of the PV loop curve and no significant alteration of the Cst, A and AUC parameters as compared with air-exposed mice.
Afterwards, we analyzed the impact of ECA exposure on morphological lung structure. We observed an extensive lung remodeling in alveolar walls and bronchial epithelium both after 3 and 6 month exposures (Fig. ). Compared to control mice, extensive thickening of respiratory epithelium due to hyperplasia and folding was observed in both ECA- and CS-exposed mice. Atypias like loss of orientation, binucleation or meganucleation were also sporadically observed in ECA 30 W-exposed mice. Some area of cell desquamation within the bronchial epithelium were detected mainly in both ECA 30 W- and CS-exposed mice. Inflammatory cell recruitment was also observed in both peribronchial areas and alveoli. Additionally, all samples of 30 W ECA and CS-exposed mice displayed periarterial edema and alveolar emphysema. MLI measurement exhibited a statistically significant (p < 0.001) alveolar enlargement in both mice exposed for 3 months to ECA 30 W or to CS as compared with Air mice which persisted after 6 months (Fig. G).
 

## E-cigarette aerosols induce pulmonary inflammation

## a- inflammatory cell infiltrate
The inflammatory cell infiltrate was analysed in both lung and spleen by flow cytometry. This study revealed that mice exposed to CS during 3 months exhibited an increased number of cells both within the BAL and the lung tissue (Additional Fig. A). This increase is significant in the BAL of ECA 30 W- and CS-exposed mice and in lung tissue from 18 W ECA- and CS-exposed mice. After 6 months, exposure to both ECA and CS did not alter the total number of cells within the BAL and the lung tissue (Additional Fig. 3B).
Moreover, exposure to CS and ECA induced specific immune cell signatures compared to Air mice (Fig. ). Exposure to ECA have no significant effect on the number of AM and IMa in the BAL although we observed an increased number and expression of I-Ab on IMo and of AM at both 3 and 6 months. Exposure to ECA 18 W increased the number of IMo in the lung tissue at 3 months and their expression of I-Ab at 6 months. At the same timing, exposure to 30 W ECA significantly reduced the number of cDC1 in both the BAL and lungs and of cDC2 in the BAL (Additional Fig. ). Mice exposed to ECA exhibited decreased number of NK (at both 3 and 6 months), CD8 + T (at 3 months) and CD4 T cells (at 6 months) in the BAL. In lung tissue, ECA 18 W induced the recruitment of NK cells, CD8 + and CD4 + T cells after both 3 and 6 months of exposure while exposure to ECA 30 W increased the number of CD8 + and CD4 + T cells at 6 months. This is associated with an increased expression of CD25 on CD4 T and NKT cells at 6 months. Surprisingly, we did not observe the recruitment of neutrophils in both the BAL and the lungs after exposure to either ECA or CS and at both timings (data not shown).
 CS-exposed mice presented reduced number of AM in the BAL after 6 months and lung tissue at both time periods although these cells expressed higher levels of the activation markers I-Ab (Fig. ) and CD86 (data not shown). Furthermore, we observed a significant increased number of both cDC1 and cDC2 in the BAL and the lungs at 3 months but not after 6 months (Additional Fig. ) and this is not associated with cell activation (data not shown). A statistically significant (p < 0.01) recruitment of IMo as well as NKT cells was also reported in the BAL after 3 and 6 months whereas the number of NK was higher only after 3 month (Fig.  A). This is associated with IMo activation in the BAL and in the lung only after 6 months. In contrast, exposure to CS also increased the number of CD8 + and CD4 + T cells (at both 3 and 6 months) in lung tissue (Fig. B). This is associated with an increased expression of CD25 on CD4 T cells after 3 months whereas there is no modulation of the activation marker expression for the other populations (Fig. D and data not shown). Altogether this confirms that exposure to CS activated AM and inflammatory monocytes and increased the recruitment of cDC after 3 months.

## b- cytokine concentrations
In order to decipher the mechanism of the inflammatory cell recruitment, we measured the levels of cytokines in the BAL and lungs of exposed mice (Fig. ). Exposure to ECA 30 W for 3 months was associated with higher levels of IL-22 and the chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2 and in BAL and a trend for CXCL17 concentrations (Fig. A). After 6 months, ECA 18 W-exposed mice produced significantly more CXCL17 in the BAL and lungs as compared with controls (Fig. A-B). In contrast, we observed lower levels of IL-1β and CXCL1 in lungs in mice exposed for 6 months to ECA and CS whereas the others cytokines were not modulated.
 In mice exposed for 3 months to CS, we detected higher concentrations of IL-17 in the lungs compared with control mice (Fig. B). Meanwhile no effect was observed on IL-17 levels after exposure to ECA in either lung tissue or BAL in contrast with CS-exposed mice.
In addition, we further assessed the ability of lung immune cells to produce Th1 and Th17 cytokines with and without in vitro CD3 activation. Exposure to ECA 30 W increased the levels of IL-22 by unstimulated cells but not after anti-CD3 activation while there is no change with ECA 18 W. Surprisingly, lung cells from CS-exposed mice for 3 months produced significantly less IL-22 than those of control mice. After 6 months of exposure, no significant alteration of the Th1 and Th17 cytokine secretion by lung cells was reported in mice exposed to ECA or CS.

## E-cigarette aerosols induce systemic inflammation
To assess the systemic impact of ECA, we examined first, the number and phenotype of immune cells in the spleen, as well as their ability to produce Th1 and Th17 cytokines, and second, cytokine concentrations within the blood.

## a- immune response within the spleen
The total cell number of spleen cells are similar among all the groups after both 3 and 6 months exposure (data not shown). Exposure to ECA 18 and 30 W for 3 months was responsible for a decreased number of antigen-presenting cells (DC and macrophages) and lymphocytes (CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) (Fig. A). However, this was associated with an increased expression in CD25 in CD8+ T cells in mice exposed to ECA 30 W (Fig. B). In contrast, the spleen of CS-exposed mice was characterized by a statistically significant recruitment of iNKT and a lower number of neutrophils. Despite the lack of effect on their numbers, a higher expression of I-Ab was observed in macrophages as well as CD25 in CD4+ and CD8+ T Cells after CS exposure.
 After 6 months, ECA-exposed mice presented a higher number of NKT cells in the spleen, regardless of e-cig power. Moreover, only ECA 18 W increased the recruitment of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells (Fig. A). Both ECA and CS exposures induced a drop in the neutrophil number in the spleen. No effect was observed on the expression of activation markers as well as on the number of other immune cells.
Regarding the ability of spleen cells to produce cytokines (Fig. ), IL-22 production was enhanced in unstimulated and anti-CD3-activated spleen cells from mice exposed for 3 months to ECA 30 W and to CS whereas only spleen cells from ECA 18 W -exposed mice produced more IL-22 after anti-CD3 stimulation. In contrast, IL-17 and IFN-γ production was unchanged (data not shown).
 

## b-Blood cytokine concentrations
Interestingly, ECA 30 W-exposed mice for 3 months had lower concentrations of CXCL1 (Fig. A). Similarly, the levels of CXCL1, CXCL2, IL-6, IL-22 and TNF-α were lower in the blood from ECA 18 W mice but also in ECA 30 W animals for IL-6, IL-22 and CXCL1. Interestingly, CS exposure significantly increased the secretion of resistin.
We have also analyzed the cotinine levels in order to assess the level of exposure of mice to CS and ECA. Our data revealed that the cotinine levels are higher in mice exposed to CS and ECA than in mice exposed to air (Additional Fig. ).
In summary, exposure to ECA has an ambivalent effect on systemic inflammation, with a drop in blood pro-inflammatory cytokine concentration, and, at the opposite, some activation and enhanced capacity to produce IL-22 in splenic T lymphocytes.

## Impact of e-cigarette on lung transcriptome
In order to compare the signaling pathways deregulated by exposure to ECA or CS, lung transcriptome was characterized by pangenomic microarrays in mice exposed for 3 or 6 months.

## a- subchronic exposure (3 months)
Exposure to CS for 3 months induced the deregulation of 248 genes (186 up- and 62 down-regulated). Surprisingly, the number of deregulated genes was much higher in ECA 18 W-exposed mice (197 with 121 up- and 76 down-regulated) compared to those exposed to ECA 30 W (72 with 50 up- and 22 down-regulated) (Fig. A). The list of differentially expressed genes at 3 months is reported in the Supplementary Material S .
 Overall, 25 genes (Table ) were deregulated by exposure to the three types of emissions, including genes involved in cancer development (increase in Pla1a expression and down-regulation of the tumour suppressor genes Cdkn2a and Ppp2r2c) and cell metabolism (e.g. increased levels in Fmo3). Moreover, the expression of 16 genes were specifically altered by both ECA 18 W and 30 W (Fig. B), some of which are involved in the control of oxidative stress (Hmox1, Slc7a11) and cell proliferation (Fosl1).
 Analysis of the pathways affected by CS treatments (Fig. C) revealed that most of the deregulated pathways are involved in the metabolism of xenobiotics (in particular the biotransformation of PAHs with increased expression of Cyp1a1 and Cyp1b1) and oxidative stress (increased levels of Nqo1, Gsta3, Gsto1). Pathways deregulated by ECA 18 W are mainly related to inflammation linked to IL-17 signaling (decreased expression of Ccl2 and Il6) (Fig. A), oxidative stress (deregulation of Abcc1, Chac1, Gclc, Hmox1 and Myc) and lipid metabolism (Pla1a, Pmvk, Plcb1, Plcb4).
 

## b- chronic exposure (6 months)
While we found an increase in the number of deregulated genes after 6 months of exposure to 18 W ECA (431 with 388 up- and 43 down-regulated), the opposite was observed with ECA 30 W, which altered the expression of only 40 genes (12 up- and 28 down-regulated) (Fig. 8C). The fold changes and the adjusted p values were reported in the Additional Table 4. Thirty-four of them were commonly deregulated by the aerosols of the 2 wattages (Fig. D). CS exposures changed the expression of 615 genes (336 up- and 279 down-regulated), including 398 in a specific manner. Furthermore, 215 genes were deregulated in common with 18 W ECA. The list of differentially expressed genes at 6 months is reported in the Supplementary Material S .
Overall, 23 genes (Table ) were deregulated by exposure to the three types of emissions (Fig. D), including genes involved in inflammatory response (decreased expression of Il6 and Tnip3) and in cancer development (down-regulation of Fosl1 and Sfn and up-regulation of Plk5).
 After 6 months, the main pathways affected by CS were related to the inflammatory response (e.g. decreased levels of Il6, Il1b and Mmp9) and xenobiotic metabolism (e.g. modulation of Ahrr, Cyp1a1, Cyp1b1 and Gsta3) (Fig. C). The ECA 18 W mostly affected nucleotide biosynthesis, metabolism of xenobiotics (Aldh18a1, Aldh1l2 et Aldh3a1) and inflammation (Ccl20, Cxcl6, Il6, Muc5ac) (Fig. A).
 Altogether, the transcriptomic data showed that mRNA profiles are very different between 3 and 6 months of exposure, for both ECA 18 W and CS. Moreover, the impact of ECA 30 W on the lung transcriptome appeared to be much lower than that of ECA 18 W.

## Discussion
The use of e-cig has gained significant in popularity as an alternative to conventional cigarette or even as a trend among adolescents. While the well-established health risks associated with CS exposure are widely acknowledged, the potential long-term effects of e-cig usage have only recently received attention. In this study, we aim to investigate the impact of moderate (1 h/day) and chronic exposure to e-cig set at 2 different wattages (i.e. 18–30 W) in comparison to CS. Interestingly, exposure to ECA induced an alteration of lung and metabolic function and these effects are associated with mostly specific responses on transcriptome, inflammation and lesions as compared to the effects of CS.
Exposure to ECA has some major impact on physiological mechanisms including host metabolism and respiratory function although this latter is restricted to ECA 30 W. The impact on host metabolism was evidenced by a lower weight gain in mice exposed to ECA and might be related to an altered lipid metabolism as previously described [ ]. This effect is similar with both low and high power e-cig and does not seem to be related to blood cotinine levels (Additional Fig. 1). Furthermore, the impact of CS on weight gain was similar to that of ECA, although blood cotinine concentrations were higher in these mice. We also observed a strong effect of ECA 30 W on lung function, with a shift in the PV-loop curve towards an emphysematous phenotype associated with a major impact on static compliance and the area of the curve. The presence of emphysema was confirmed by an increase in MLI in ECA 30 W-exposed mice, close to the effect observed in CS-exposed mice. However, our protocol of CS exposure has a very different effect on the PV-loop curve with no significant effect on compliance and area under the curve. In addition, exposure to ECA 18 W provokes airway constriction after 3 months, a result associated with the expression of pro-inflammatory signals. A previous study has reported a similar impact of ECA on lung function with emphysema and alteration of static compliance, an effect that has been shown to be nicotine-dependent [ ]. In parallel, we also reported on the same mice that the ECA exposure induced important remodeling in both ileum and colon, which reveals that ECA-induced lesions are not restricted to respiratory mucosa [ ].
To determine whether a chronic inflammatory response could be responsible for the altered lung function observed in mice exposed to ECA, we assessed pulmonary and systemic inflammation in these mice by measuring leukocyte recruitment and activation, as well as cytokine concentrations. In contrast to a previous study using an intense ECA exposure (5–6 h/day) protocol, we did not detect any effect on neutrophil recruitment within the lung after ECA exposure. This suggest that neutrophil recruitment and persistent chemokine production are not detectable after chronic exposure to moderate doses of ECA, an effect associated with lower levels of neutrophil specific chemokines after 6 month exposure. However, our protocol of exposure using ECA 30 W was associated with higher activation of antigen-presenting cells such as AM and inflammatory monocytes in the BAL and lung tissue although their numbers were similar. This stimulation was associated with the mobilization of NK cells and T lymphocytes within lung tissue even if activation of these cells could not be observed. Altogether, these data showed that exposure to e-cig induced a specific profile of cell recruitment and activation as compared to CS. Moreover, no clear impact was detected on neutrophil count and phenotype in contrast to CS.
Regarding cytokine production, we observed some upregulation of neutrophil-chemotactic cytokines such as CXCL1, CXCL2 and CXCL17 in the BAL of mice exposed to ECA 30 W for 3 months. This transient upregulation was not associated with neutrophil recruitment, probably because a defect in structural cell activation required for leucocyte diapedesis. While IL-17 levels are unaffected by ECA exposure, IL-22 concentrations are higher in the BAL and in the supernatant of unstimulated lung cells from ECA 30 W-exposed mice. Spleen cells from ECA-exposed mice also produced higher levels of IL-22 after anti-CD3 stimulation suggesting that the effects of ECA are also systemic. Although IL-17 has been identified as a key player in the development of CS-induced COPD [ ], IL-22 production seems to be preferentially targeted by ECA. In the present study, the role of IL-22 in the lung injury remains to be determined although some reports show that IL-22 can participate in COPD development [ ]. Exposure to ECA seems to induce a mobilization of both antigen presenting cells and T lymphocyte in both lung and spleen at 3 months, followed by the recruitment of NKT cells and T Lymphocytes at 6 months. Some T cell activation was also detected at 3 months of exposure, as evidenced by modulation of CD25 expression and enhanced capacity to produce IL-22. This suggests that the effect of e-cigarette is not restricted to the lung and could induce some recirculation of immune cells towards the spleen and/or intestinal mucosa. Although the effects observed with our protocol using moderate doses of ECA appear to be close to those following exposure to high concentrations of ECA [ ], the mechanisms underlying these effects are probably different. At high concentrations, ECA induced the recruitment of inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, oxidative stress and the upregulation of metalloproteinases such as MMP9 and MMP12 [ , , ]. At lower concentrations, we reported that chronic exposure to ECA did not promote persistent inflammation and MMP expression whereas this treatment enhances the expression of IL-22, an essential cytokine for the maintenance of the epithelium and remodeling of mucosa [ ]. 
Our transcriptomic analyses revealed that the pathways affected by CS were deregulated more significantly than by ECA. Unsurprisingly, following exposure to CS, the biological functions most affected are the metabolism of xenobiotics and the inflammatory response. In mice exposed to CS, deregulated genes contribute mainly to the AhR pathway, which is characteristic of a response to exposure to PAHs such as those found in CS [ ]. The overexpressed genes code in particular for the cytochromes CYP1A1 and CYP1B1, enzymes that may be involved in the generation of highly reactive intermediate metabolites capable of inducing DNA damage. In contrast, exposure to ECA did not induce gene expression of these two cytochromes, a result consistent with the very low levels of PAHs measured in these emissions [ ]. ECA exposure rather modulated gene expression of aldehyde dehydrogenases (Aldh18a1, Aldh1l2 and Aldh3a1) possibly involved in the metabolism of carbonyl compounds, and in the biotransformation of propylene glycol, one of the main compounds in e-liquids. Chronic exposure to ECA also altered the expression of gene involved in oxidative stress, a mechanism which can explained the oxidative DNA damage in the lung that we previously reported in this experimental model [ ]. The involvement of major genes of LXR/RXR pathway, which are also deregulated following exposure to CS and ECA, has already been involved in macrophage-mediated lung inflammation in cases of CS exposure [ ]. In addition, our data show that the number of deregulated genes was greater after exposure to 18 W than 30 W ECA in opposition with the lower quantity of toxic compounds measured in ECA 18 W. Indeed, we had previously shown that the ECA 18 W generated 2 times lower levels of carbonyl compounds than ECA 30 W [ ]. Several mechanisms could account for this non-monotonic response. First, the aerosol generated at lower power may possess specific physical characteristics—such as smaller particle size or different condensation properties—that facilitate deeper or more efficient deposition on cellular surfaces. Second, it is possible that the higher-power aerosol exerts a more cytotoxic effect, leading to dampened transcriptional responses due to widespread damage or cell death, whereas the low-power exposure may engage adaptive stress-response pathways in activated cells. These hypotheses warrant further investigation to better characterize the physicochemical and biological properties of ECA under varying operational conditions.
In ECA-exposed mice, transcriptomic data revealed deregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism (Pla1a, Pmvk, Plcb1, Plcb4). This effect on host metabolism has been previously reported [ ] and could explain the altered weight gain in exposed mice. These genes have been already described as being affected in regular e-cig users, which could reflect the excessive lipid load provided by the glycerol contained in e-liquids. This excessive load could then disrupt surfactant secretion in the lungs and impact lung function [ ]. The primary limitation of our study is the reliance on a murine model to evaluate the effect of e-cigarette exposure. Indeed, the structure of the respiratory tract in mice is different from human one. As an example, mucus production is differently regulated in both species [ ], and we are not able to detect mucus overproduction in mice exposed to CS in contrast to human. In addition, we did not compare the impact of nicotine-free or low-nicotine e-liquids, despite nicotine is known to have effects on central nervous and immune systems [ ]. We focused exclusively on a single, widely-used tobacco flavor, whereas previous studies reported that different flavors agents can also modify the lung response to ECA [ ]. Accordingly, additional study both in the murine model and in clinical cohorts are required in order to elucidate these different questions.

## Conclusion 
Altogether, our data showed that long term exposure to moderate levels of ECA can induce some alteration of lung function and host metabolism whereas only limited effect on the inflammatory reaction can be exhibited. Il-22 upregulation is associated with these changes suggesting a physiopathologic role for this cytokine. Our transcriptomic analyses highlighted certain signaling pathways common to exposure to CS and ECA (although the number of genes involved is much lower with ECA), including response to oxidative and chemical stress. Overall, the impact on the transcriptome of ECA (particularly with ECA 18 W) revealed some alteration in the expression of genes involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolism of lipids and xenobiotics associated with airway constriction. These data associated with the genotoxic effect of ECA [ ] and their impact on cardiac function [ ] or intestinal mucosa [ ] confirm the urgent need of information in the general population about regular use of e-cigarette even at moderate levels.
