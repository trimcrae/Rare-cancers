## Highlights
•TLN1, a cytoskeletal protein understudied in gliomas, was explored for its role/mechanisms in glioma pathogenesis using databases, clinical samples, and in vitro/in vivo experiments.
•Public datasets and institutional samples consistently showed upregulated TLN1 in gliomas, with high TLN1 linked to poorer patient prognosis.
•TLN1 knockdown in glioma cells reduced proliferation/migration/invasion and enhanced apoptosis, verified via CCK8, Transwell, and flow cytometry.
•GSEA identified TLN1’s enrichment in the TGF-β pathway, further validated by Western blotting and in vivo murine models.
•TLN1 promotes glioma initiation/progression via the TGF-β pathway, highlighting its potential as a novel glioma therapeutic target.

TLN1, a cytoskeletal protein associated with various tumors, remains inadequately studied in gliomas. In this study, we examined the functional role and mechanisms of TLN1 in glioma pathogenesis. Utilizing public databases, we conducted differential expression, survival (Kaplan-Meier/ROC), and regression analyses, which were subsequently validated with institutional datasets and clinical tissues. In vitro experiments demonstrated that TLN1 knockdown in glioma cells resulted in reduced proliferation (CCK8/EDU), migration and invasion (wound healing/Transwell), and increased apoptosis (AO/EB/flow cytometry); these findings were corroborated by Western blot analyses. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) linked TLN1 to the TGF-beta signaling pathway, a connection further validated by Western blot and in vivo murine models. Both public and institutional data indicated that TLN1 was upregulated in gliomas, with elevated expression correlating with poor prognosis. Furthermore, TLN1 knockdown inhibited glioma growth and progression in vitro and in vivo, primarily through the TGF-beta signaling pathway. Our findings establish TLN1 as an oncogenic driver in gliomas and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Introduction
Glioma is the most common primary intracranial malignant tumor type, accounting for approximately 80% of all intracranial malignancies [ , ]. As a highly invasive and heterogeneous brain cancer arising in an organ with complex vital anatomy, gliomas are shielded by the blood-brain barrier and possess a unique immune microenvironment, factors that collectively drive a poor prognosis despite the existence of several multimodal therapies, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and molecular targeted therapy [ , ]. Although molecular pathological diagnostics have improved prognostic stratification and treatment guidance [ ], their clinical impact remains limited. Therefore, identification of novel molecular biomarkers with which to predict prognosis and inform therapy is crucial.
Talin-1 (TLN1), a cytoskeletal protein that binds adhesion molecules and regulates a diverse range of biological processes, is implicated in tumorigenesis and progression across multiple types of cancer [ ]. For example, the disruption of TLN1-integrin beta1 interaction has been shown to suppress invasiveness and metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer [ ], Furthermore, the downregulation of TLN1 has been shown to reduce proliferation, migration, and the invasion of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells [ ]. However, the role of TLN1 in gliomas remains poorly characterized, and its underlying mechanisms of action remain largely unknown. Elucidating the precise function of TLN1 in the initiation and progression of glioma is therefore critical.
Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) is a cytokine that regulates key cellular processes, including immunosuppression, growth inhibition, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and extracellular matrix remodeling [ , , , ]. In particular, TGF-beta upregulates mesenchymal markers and enhances invasiveness in glioblastoma (GBM) cells [ ]. However, the interplay between TLN1 and TGF-beta signaling in gliomas has yet to be fully elucidated. Determining the precise functional role of TLN1 and the associated mechanisms underlying glioma may reveal novel therapeutic targets.
In this study, we investigated the role of TLN1 in gliomas. Initially, we analyzed TLN1 expression patterns in public databases and in patients with glioma from our institution, alongside its association with clinical prognosis. Subsequently, we performed in vitro experiments to evaluate the impact of TLN1 on the malignant phenotypes of glioma cells and validated these results by performing in vivo experiments. 

## Materials and methods

## Data download and differential expression analysis
We downloaded and collated datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), a database held by the National Center for Biotechnology Information and extracted the expression profiles of glioma-related datasets, including GSE4290. Additionally, we acquired clinical information and gene expression data from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Furthermore, we analyzed the expression of TLN1​ in glioma using the online Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Potential underlying mechanisms were investigated by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

## Bioinformatics analysis
RNA-seq data were downloaded, along with the corresponding clinical information. Subsequently, samples lacking complete clinical records were excluded from the analysis. The raw expression values were normalized using the log2(x + 1) transformation. To facilitate survival analysis, we utilized the R programming language (version 4.2.1) with the survival (v3.3.1), surviminer (v0.4.9), and ggplot2 (v3.4.4) packages. To define “High vs. Low Expression Groups”, we established a cutoff threshold based on the median expression level of all selected differentially expressed genes. Genes exhibiting expression levels above this median were classified as the “High Expression Group” whereas those below this threshold were categorized as the “Low Expression Group”.

## Patient samples
Between January 2016 and January 2024, we collected postoperative tumor and corresponding peritumoral tissues from 24 patients with glioma at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University. These tissues were placed in cryovials and stored long-term at -80C. In addition, we acquired pathological tissue sections and clinical information (age, gender, tumor grade, and Isocitrate Dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status) from 94 patients with glioma ( ). Immunohistochemistry was then used to determine TLN1 expression in tumor tissues. 

## Western blot
Cells and tissues were lysed using a 1:100 ratio of protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail to RIPA buffer. Following denaturation, the lysed proteins were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The blots were subsequently blocked using 5% non-fat milk for 1 h and then incubated with a range of primary antibodies overnight at 4°C: TLN1: Proteintech, 1:5000; N-cadherin: Proteintech, 1:5000; E-cadherin: Proteintech, 1:20000; Vimentin: Proteintech, 1:20000; MMP9: Proteintech, 1:2000; Smad3: abcam,1:1000; TGFbeta1: GeneTex,1:1000; GAPDH: Proteintech, 1:10000; beta-tublin: Bioss,1:20000. The next day, corresponding secondary antibodies were incubated with the membrane for 1 h. Then, the membrane was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system. Protein levels were normalized to GAPDH and quantified using ImageJ.

## RNA extraction and quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from exponentially growing cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was then transcribed into cDNA with a Reverse Transcription Kit (YESEN, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, quantitative PCR analysis was performed using a PCR Kit (YESEN, China) and a Bio-Rad qPCR system (USA).
The primer sequences for human TLN1 were F:5´-CAAAAAACGGTGAAGAGAGCTGAT-3´ and R:5´-TGCTGTACCTCGATCTGAATCTG-3´. The primer sequences for murine TLN1 were F: 5´-CACAGCTCAGCTCCTGGTAG-3´ and R: 5´-GCAATGCCCCCAACCATTTT-3´. GAPDH was used as an internal control.

## Immunohistochemical staining and analysis
Tissue sections (4-mm-thick) were dewaxed, hydrated, and underwent antigen retrieval. Next, the sections were blocked with a blocking solution (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The tissues were then incubated with a primary antibody (TLN1, Proteintech, 1:1000) overnight at 4°C. Following treatment with a reaction enhancer solution, secondary antibody, and DAB reaction solution, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, batch analysis was performed using Image J software.

## Cell culture
The human glioma cell lines LN229 and T98G were purchased from the Peking Union Medical College Cell Resource Center (PCRC, China) and U138 and U251 were obtained from the China National Cell Line Resource Infrastructure. The mouse glioma cell lines GL261-luci and CT2A-luci were provided by the Neurology Laboratory of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University. All cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio Life Science, China) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator.

## Gene silencing and sorting by flow cytometry
TLN1 small interfering RNA (siRNA) and a negative control were obtained from Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China). TLN1 siRNA was transfected into U251 and T98G cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. After 48 h, TLN1 protein and TLN1 RNA levels were assessed to validate successful knockdown before performing functional experiments. The TLN1-siRNA sequence was 5´-CAGGGCAATGAGAATTATGCA-3´. The murine glioma cell line GL261 was incubated with primary antibody (TLN1: Proteintech, 1:200) and then a corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody (R488: Proteintech, 1:200) before being sorted by a flow cytometer to distinguish cells expressing high and low levels of TLN1.

## EDU Assay
Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Edu assays were performed with a kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Yeasen, China). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and then stained with 1x Apollo®488. Results were detected using fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and the cell proliferation ratio was calculated as the proportion of Edu-positive to DAPI-positive cells.

## CCK-8 assay
Cells were seeded at a density of 2,000 cells per well in 96-well plates, with five replicates per group. Cell viability was evaluated by CCK-8 assays. The culture medium was replaced daily with CCK-8-containing medium and incubated for 1 h. Optical density values at a wavelength of 450 nm were measured using a Nano Quant (TECAN) system for seven consecutive days.

## Wound healing assays
Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to grow until they reached approximately 70–80% confluence before the experiment. A sterile micropipette tip (200 μl) was then used to scratch the cell monolayer, and the medium was replaced with serum-free and antibiotic-free culture medium for subsequent cell culture. The cells were then observed and photographed under a microscope at 0 h, 24 h and 48 h.

## Transwell assays
After being starved of serum for 24 h, the cells were trypsinized, counted, and placed in the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8-µm pore size) with or without Matrigel (Corning, Lowell, MA, USA). DMEM containing FBS was then added to the lower chambers. The cells were then incubated for 48 h, fixed with methanol, and stained with crystal violet. Cells in the upper chambers were gently wiped away and inserts were observed and photographed under a microscope.

## AO/EB assay
Cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70%–80% confluence before the experiment. A staining reaction solution was prepared (1x buffer: AO staining solution: EB staining solution = 18:1:1), and 100 μL of the reaction solution was added to each well. The cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 3–5 min. Subsequently, 3–5 random fields-of-view were captured by fluorescence microscopy.

## Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis
Cells were digested with trypsin without EDTA, centrifuged, and then resuspended in a small volume of binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL of PI staining solution were added to the experimental groups. The samples were mixed well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. Flow cytometry was then performed within 1 h.

## Establishment of a mouse model
We used GL261 cell lines with verified high and low TLN1 expression, as sorted by flow cytometry. Ten male C57BL/6 mice (approximately 20 g in weight) were acquired from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and randomly divided into two groups. The high-expression group (n = 5) received a subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10⁶ TLN1-High cells while the low-expression group (n = 5) received 1 × 10⁶ of TLN1-Low cells. Mice were euthanized when the maximum tumor volume reached 1500 mm³. Tumor tissues were then isolated and either frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately or fixed in 10% formalin. Tumor size was measured with a vernier caliper, and volume calculated as (length × width²)/2; weight was measured with an analytical balance. Animal experiments followed approved procedures by the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University's Medical Ethics Committee (Approval Letter No.:2025-R804).

## In vivo imaging of mice
Mice were prepared for subcutaneous tumor formation by shaving the relevant area. Each mouse was intraperitoneally injected with a contrast agent (absin, 150 mg/kg) and placed in an inhalation anesthesia induction chamber containing 2.0% isoflurane (RWD). After the mice were fully anesthetized and the contrast agent was fully absorbed, mouse imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA).

## Statistical analysis
All experimental results were statistically analyzed using R software version 4.3.0 and Prism 8 version 8.4.3. After conducting normality tests, either the unpaired t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test (rank-sum test) was used to compare data between the two groups. A Cox proportional hazards model was used for univariate and multivariate analyses. All statistical analyses are reported with effect sizes, 95% confidence intervals and P-values. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## High TLN1 expression predicted a poor prognosis in gliomas
Survival analysis of data from the CGGA and TCGA databases revealed that high TLN1 expression was associated with reduced overall survival in brain tumor patients ( A, CGGA: P < 0.001; E, TCGA: P = 0.048). Receiver operating characteristic curves demonstrated the good performance of TLN1 in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates for these patients ( B, F). These findings were validated using clinical data from a cohort of 94 patients at our center ( M, P = 0.002; N, 1-year AUC = 0.772, 3-year AUC = 0.808, 5-year AUC = 0.733). Variables included in the univariate and multivariate analyses were selected a priori based on their clinical relevance and known associations with outcomes from previous literature. The final models incorporated covariates such as age, gender, grade, and IDH status, as these factors are well-established prognostic confounders in glioma. Univariate analyses conducted on data from the CGGA, TCGA, and our center consistently identified TLN1 as a prognostic factor for patient survival ( C, G, O). Furthermore, multivariate analysis of the CGGA data indicated that TLN1 represents an independent prognostic factor for survival in brain tumors ( D, H, P). First, we verified the expression of TLN1 in all glioma cell lines in our institution. The U251 and T98G cell lines, along with the murine glioma cell line GL261, were subjected to flow cytometry to sort cells with high and low TLN1 expression, followed by validation at the protein and mRNA levels ( A-C. Collectively, these results demonstrated successful knockdown in the U251 and T98G cell lines, as well as successful sorting for the GL261 cell line. Subsequently, these three cell lines were used for the following experiments. 

## TLN1 promoted proliferation and inhibited apoptosis in glioma cells
EDU assay results showed that U251 and T98G cells with TLN1 knockdown exhibited significantly lower proliferation rates than controls. In GL261, High-TLN1 cells proliferated significantly faster than Low-TLN1 cells ( D-F. U251: P = 0.0023 n = 3; T98G: P = 0.0028 n = 3; GL261: P = 0.0483, n = 3.). CCK-8 assay results were consistent ( G-I, n = 5), indicating TLN1 knockdown reduced the proliferation of glioma cells. AO/EB assays further revealed higher apoptosis rates in TLN1-knockdown U251 and T98G cells than controls, while High-TLN1 GL261 cells exhibited significantly lower apoptosis rates than Low-TLN1 cells ( M-O. U251: P = 0.0165 n = 3; T98G: P < 0.001 n = 3; GL261: P = 0.0041, n = 3.). Flow cytometry apoptosis assays yielded similar results ( J-L. n = 3). Collectively, these experiments indicated that TLN1 knockdown inhibited glioma cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis.

## TLN1 promoted migration and invasion in glioma cells
Wound healing assays revealed that TLN1-knockdown U251 and T98G cells migrated slower than controls, while High-TLN1 GL261 cells migrated significantly faster than Low-TLN1 cells ( A-C. U251: 24h: P = 0.0063 48h: P = 0.0036, n = 3; T98G: 24h: P < 0.001 48h: P < 0.001, n = 3; GL261: 24h: P = 0.0051 48h: P < 0.001, n = 3.). Transwell migration assays yielded consistent results, with fewer migrating cells in the low-expression group ( D. U251:P < 0.001 n = 3; T98G: P < 0.001 n = 3; GL261: P < 0.001, n = 3.). Transwell invasion assays revealed that TLN1 knockdown significantly reduced the number of invading cells, indicating inhibited invasiveness ( E. U251:P < 0.001 n = 3; T98G: P < 0.001 n = 3; GL261: P = 0.0038, n = 3). Examination of invasion-related proteins revealed that TLN1 knockdown upregulated E-cadherin and downregulated N-cadherin, vimentin, and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) ( F-H. n = 3). These findings confirm that TLN1 knockdown inhibited glioma cell migration and invasion. 

## Mechanism of TLN1 in the occurrence and development of glioma
To investigate how TLN1 contributes to the initiation and progression of glioma, we first conducted GSEA using CGGA and TCGA data; this revealed a significant association between TLN1 and the TGF-beta signaling pathway ( A-B, P < 0.05, FDR < 0.1). Correlation analysis further revealed a strong correlation between TLN1 and TGF-beta family factors, especially TGFB1 ( C-D, CGGA: r = 0.52, P < 0.001; TCGA: r = 0.684, P < 0.001). Western blot experiments demonstrated that TLN1 knockdown reduced the expression of TGFB1 and its downstream SMAD3 ( E-G. U251: TGFB1: P = 0.0218, SMAD3: P = 0.0447, n = 3; T98G: TGFB1: P = 0.0161 SMAD3: P = 0.0254 n = 3; GL261: TGFB1: P < 0.001 SMAD3: P = 0.0132, n = 3.). Collectively, these results suggest that TLN1 may promote the initiation and progression of glioma by activating the TGF-beta signaling pathway. 

## In vivo experiments
Flow cytometry-sorted GL261 cells exhibiting high and low TLN1 expression ( F-G. WB: P = 0.0410, n = 3; qPCR: P = 0.0357, n = 5) were subcutaneously inoculated into C57BL/6 mice. Daily monitoring revealed significantly larger tumor volumes and weights in the High-TLN1 group than the Low-TLN1 group ( A-E), suggesting that TLN1 promotes the initiation of glioma. Immunohistochemical and western blotting analyses of mouse tumor tissues revealed high expression of TGFB1 and SMAD3 in High-TLN1 tumor tissues ( H-I. WB: TGFB1: P = 0.0410 SMAD3: P = 0.0357, n = 3). These in vivo results confirm that TLN1 may promote the initiation and progression of glioma via the TGF-beta pathway. 

## Discussion
Gliomas represent the most common primary malignant tumors of the central nervous system; of these, GBM stands as the most prevalent and highly malignant subtype, accounting for over half of all glioma cases [ ]. Currently, the standard treatment paradigm for newly diagnosed GBM continues to involve maximal safe tumor resection, followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide. In 2011, the first-generation tumor-treating fields device received approval for use in recurrent GBM [ ], and in 2015, this device was further approved as an adjuvant therapy for newly diagnosed GBM. However, owing to the diffuse growth pattern and extensive invasiveness of these brain tumors, even with such aggressive treatment regimens, recurrence rates remain exceedingly high. Median survival stands at just 12–15 months, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 5%. Although current diagnostic approaches that integrate molecular pathological features are able to provide more precise prognostic insights and therapeutic strategies [ ], their clinical benefits remain limited. Thus, identifying the key molecules involved in glioma initiation and malignant progression, along with their specific mechanisms of action, carries substantial clinical significance. This is particularly valuable for advancing precision treatments and improving survival prognostication in patients with glioma.
TLN1 is a focal adhesion-associated protein that binds to a range of cytoskeletal proteins, and plays a critical role in integrin activation and focal adhesion formation [ ]. The loss of TLN1 impairs EMT and diminishes cell motility [ ], whereas its overexpression correlates strongly with the initiation, progression and development of malignant phenotypes across various tumor types. In one key study, small-molecule inhibitors targeting the interaction between TLN1 and beta1-integrin effectively suppressed tumor progression and lung metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer [ ]. Similarly, a separate investigation demonstrated that BCKDK inhibited breast cancer cell adhesion and metastasis by downregulating TLN1, findings that collectively support the role of TLN1 in driving the progression of breast cancer [ ]. More recently, research has confirmed that TLN1 functions as a potential oncogene in the progression of lung cancer [ ], while also contributing to tumor advancement in colorectal cancer [ , ], acute myeloid leukemia [ ] and prostate cancers [ ], among other malignancies. Previous research conducted by You et al [ ] confirmed that the inhibition of TLN1 can suppress the proliferation, migration and invasion of glioma cells while Jiang et al [ ] verified that targeting TLN1 could reduce the invasiveness of glioblastoma multiforme. However, research on the mechanisms of action of TLN1 remain limited. Thus, in the present study, we investigated the impact of TLN1 on the initiation and progression of glioma, as well as its potential mechanisms of action.
EMT serves as a key phenotypic transition in tumor invasion and metastasis, enabling epithelial cells to adopt a mesenchymal phenotype that facilitates both local tumor progression and distant metastasis. Previous research has documented changes in genes linked to invasive phenotypes during the progression of gliomas, highlighting the molecular plasticity underlying this process. ​In the present study, the knockdown of TLN1 expression resulted in distinct alterations in key molecular markers: specifically, an upregulation in E-cadherin levels and the concurrent downregulation in the expression of N-cadherin, vimentin and MMP9.
Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) is primarily synthesized, secreted and sequestered in the extracellular matrix (ECM) as latent complexes, with only its activated form exerting biological functionality by binding to receptor complexes. Three isoforms namely; TGF-beta1, TGF-beta2 and TGF-beta3 exist in mammals, of which TGF-beta1 exhibited the strongest association with pathway activation [ ]. The TGF-beta pathway plays a pivotal role in early embryonic development and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis in adults [ ]. However, the dysregulated overexpression of this pathway contributes to a spectrum of metabolic disorders and functional impairments, driving EMT, excessive ECM deposition, and subsequent pathological outcomes, including carcinogenesis [ ].Notably, accumulating experimental evidence highlights the dual role of TGF-beta in cancer progression, exerting context-dependent effects by modulating the tumor microenvironment and crosstalk with other cytokines [ ]. Specifically, in the early stages of tumorigenesis, TGF-beta acts as a tumor suppressor in precancerous lesions, inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis to constrain malignant transformation [ ],In later stages, however, TGF-beta promotes tumor progression by orchestrating genomic instability, EMT, angiogenesis, immune evasion, enhanced cell motility and metastatic dissemination. ​In the context of gliomas, studies have emphasized the multifaceted role of TGF-beta in driving disease progression by facilitating glioma cell proliferation, invasion, EMT, and migration while actively suppressing immune effector cells, processes that collectively fuel tumor aggressiveness [ , ].
The canonical downstream pathway of TGF-beta, the SMAD pathway, featuring Smad2 and Smad3 as key mediators, is widely recognized as a central transducer of TGF-beta signaling in tissue fibrosis and tumorigenesis. For instance, Zhang et al [ ] recently demonstrated that PCK2 enhanced invasion and EMT in triple-negative breast cancer by potentiating the TGF-beta/SMAD3 signaling axis. Similarly, Yin et al [ ] showed that RMRP drove proliferation, invasion, and migration in non-small cell lung cancer cells via the TGFBR1/SMAD2/SMAD3 pathway.
Notably, no previous studies have established a functional link between TLN1 and the TGF-beta signaling pathway. In the present study, GSEA enrichment analysis first indicated that TLN1 expression correlated with enrichment in TGF-beta signaling processes, thus suggesting potential involvement in this pathway. Correlation analyses further revealed a significant positive association between TLN1 and the TGFB1 gene within this signaling network.​ Subsequently, immunoblotting analyses across three cell lines confirmed that TLN1 knockdown concomitantly reduced the expression of TGFB1 and its downstream effector, Smad3. Finally, in vivo murine models validated that TLN1 promoted the initiation and progression of glioma; immunoblotting and immunohistochemical analyses detected elevated levels of TGFbeta1 and Smad3 in tumors with high TLN1 expression. ​Collectively, these findings provide preliminary evidence that TLN1 may drive the initiation and progression of glioma by activating the TGF-beta1-Smad3 signaling cascade.
In this study, we observed that TLN1 featured two distinct bands in the histone protein; however, only one in the cellular protein. Apart from the complexity of the tissue samples and the simplicity of cell samples, we conducted a literature review and found that Lina et al [ ] reported the existence of splicing isoforms for TLN1 and noted that these isoforms are regulated by the TGF-beta/SMAD3 signaling pathway. This phenomenon likely accounts for the differential expression patterns we observed between the two sample types. However, evidence at the RNA level did not necessarily prove the existence of these isoforms at the protein level. While we cannot rule out the possibility that post-translational modifications or alternative splicing contributed to this discrepancy, we acknowledge that our current data may not fully capture the molecular complexity of TLN1 in vivo. To address this limitation and ensure the accuracy of our conclusions, we have planned additional experiments for our future studies.
No previous study has clearly demonstrated a functional association between TLN1 and the TGF-beta/Smad3 signaling pathway. However, we hypothesize that TLN1 may function as an upstream mechanical sensor of the TGF-beta pathway. Given that TLN1 is a key component of focal adhesion and directly connects integrins to the actin cytoskeleton, this protein is in an ideal position to convert mechanical signals into biochemical reactions. In the case of tumor microenvironment hardness or integrin aggregation, the mechanical transduction mediated by TLN1 may help to stabilize TGF-beta receptors or promote the release of latent TGF-beta from the extracellular matrix, thereby initiating the downstream SMAD signaling pathway. In future studies, we plan to investigate whether knocking out TLN1 will affect the phosphorylation status of SMAD3 or the expression of TGF-beta target genes (such as E-cadherin, vimentin). In addition, we plan to use drug inhibitors to investigate the potential interaction between the integrin/TLN1 axis and the TGF-beta pathway.
The subcutaneous glioma model used here effectively assessed tumorigenicity and drug response; however, poorly mimicked the invasive behavior of intracranial gliomas due to lacking CNS-specific anatomy and immune microenvironments. Our glioma model cannot recapitulate in-brain invasion, migration or neuron–glia crosstalk, thus only validating the oncogenic potential of TLN1 in a preliminary manner. Future orthotopic and intracranial models will better reflect clinical pathology and improve translational relevance for the pro-invasive role of TLN1 in the CNS niche.
Collectively, our findings demonstrate that TLN1 is closely linked to the progression of gliomas, with patients exhibiting high TLN1 expression levels exhibiting markedly poorer clinical prognoses. Specifically, elevated TLN1 expression enhanced glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasive potential while attenuating their apoptotic capacity. These pro-tumorigenic effects appear to be mediated, at least in part, through the TGF-beta1 signaling pathway, as supported by our preceding experimental evidence.
