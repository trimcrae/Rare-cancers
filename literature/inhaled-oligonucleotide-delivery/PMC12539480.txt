## Highlights
•Atorvastatin inhibits fibrogenic activation of EECs in part by increasing KLF2 expression.
•Atorvastatin administration in neonatal rat hearts reduced EFE thickness and inhibited fibrogenic activation of EECs by down-regulating SNAI2 and up-regulating KLF2.
•Atorvastatin treatment significantly reduced myocardial infiltration of EFE in rat hearts.
•This study suggests that atorvastatin is a potential treatment option for patients with EFE and provides preclinical data needed to move to a clinical trial in patients with EFE.

## Summary
Endocardial fibroelastosis is a condition caused by the fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells via endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the endocardium, which is regulated by the transforming growth factor-β pathway. Atorvastatin, a statin, can protect the vascular endothelium by up-regulating KLF2 and inhibiting the transforming growth factor-β pathway. This study aimed to investigate the effects of atorvastatin on the fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells in the endocardium. The study found that atorvastatin treatment reduced fibrogenic activation of endocardial endothelial cells and increased KLF2 expression in both in vitro and in vivo models of endocardial fibroelastosis–related left ventricular restriction.

Endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE) was first described in 1943 and represents a pathologic response of the endocardium to unknown stimuli. In the presence of congenital heart defects, it is first observed by fetal echocardiography due to a highly echogenic thickened endocardial surface. , In a healthy heart, the endocardium consists of a single cell layer with a "cobblestone-like" morphology throughout the heart. EFE, however, presents as an excessive thickening of the subendocardium composed of organized collagen and elastic fibers, which macroscopically appear as pearly or opaque endocardium. Clinically, EFE restricts the growth potential of the left ventricle (LV) and impairs diastolic function, which is a known risk factor for early death in patients treated with biventricular repair and is associated with adverse outcomes after biventricular conversion. To salvage LV structures and allow for LV growth, Boston Children’s Hospital has instituted the approach of resecting this EFE tissue. Nevertheless, EFE often recurs after resection and takes on a more infiltrative growth pattern into the underlying myocardium. , , Therefore, understanding the stimuli for EFE formation and identifying inhibitors thereof is crucial to exploring alternative and adjunct treatments to prevent its formation.
As we have previously reported, EFE forms through fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells via the transition of endocardial endothelial cells (EECs) to mesenchymal cells, identified by immunohistochemical double-staining of cells with endothelial and fibroblast markers. , , , , This mechanism, known as endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), is the primary source of EFE tissue and is regulated by the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) pathway through the activation of transcription factors such as Snail family transcriptional repressors (formerly known as SNAI2 or SNAI1-3 and SNAIP1) and TWIST1 family BHLH transcription factor via SMAD2/3. The stimuli that induce EndMT in congenital heart disease are currently unknown, but mechanical alterations, such as defective heart valves, which induce flow stagnation or flow turbulence of intracavitary blood flow, , genetic predisposition, , and inflammation, have been proposed.
Little is known about the response of EECs to flow; however, in the vascular endothelium, in areas of increased flow disturbances, atherosclerosis is induced in part via an EndMT-mediated process. The expression of the transcription factor KLF2 is an indicator of the physiological, nonactivated state of the endothelium, and plays an essential role in endothelial cell biology as an antiatherosclerotic factor through inhibition of fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells (ie, EndMT). , , , , TGF-β has been recognized as a major driver of EndMT, and drugs such as statins that up-regulate KLF2 have shown promise in inhibiting the TGF-β pathway through induction of SMAD7 or blocking the TGF-β cofactor activator protein 1, protecting the vascular endothelium from EndMT-mediated diseases. Besides their plasma lipid-lowering effect, statins were also shown to directly target the endothelium, up-regulate KLF2 via the RHOA pathway, and thus, have an atheroprotective effect. , Nevertheless, the effects of statins on endocardial cells are scantily understood. Therefore, the aim of this study was to inhibit EFE formation through the up-regulation of KLF2 with the lipophilic, and thus, cell-permeable statin, atorvastatin, in an in vitro endocardial cell culture model and to confirm its inhibitory effect with an in vivo neonatal heterotopic rat heart transplantation model that resembles human EFE development.

## Cell culture experiments
Healthy endocardial endothelial cells (HEECs) were isolated from lamb hearts. Extracted hearts were rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and the LV was cut open to expose the cavity. The LV was then placed face down in the same digestion buffer, followed by the same isolation procedures described herein. EFE tissue from the LV was obtained directly from the operating room and the tissue was minced, placed in a 50-mL conical centrifuge tube with 23 mL digestion buffer (18 mL Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium, 2.5 ml D10 buffer (500 ml Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium, 50 mL fetal bovine serum, 5 mL penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine [concentration = 250 mg/L], 2.5 mL Ca2+Mg2+, 100 mg collagenase type II, 100 mg collagenase type A, 100 mg dispase) and 20 mL D10 was added. The tubes were then placed on a rocking platform at 37 °C for 2 hours. The dilution was poured through a 40-μm filter and the reaction was quenched with 5 mL D10 buffer, followed by centrifugation at 400g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. Red blood cells were lysed by incubating with 4 mL lysis buffer (3 mL NH4Cl, 1 mL PBS) and 12 mL isolation buffer (450 mL PBS, 2.5 mL bovine serum albumin, 50 mL 6% ACD-A solution (1 L double-distilled H2O, 8 g citric acid, 22 g sodium citrate, 22.3 g glucose) at 4 °C for 5-10 minutes. After washing with 10 mL D10 and 10 mL isolation buffer, and centrifugation at 400g for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL medium and transferred onto a cell culture dish.
All cells were grown on gelatin-coated petri dishes in Endothelial Cell Growth Basal Medium-2 (Lonza) containing 2% fetal bovine serum, hydrocortisone, heparin, ascorbic acid, human fibroblast growth factor-β, vascular endothelial growth factor, human endothelial growth factor, gentamicin sulfate-amphotericin (GA-1000), R3 insulin-like growth factor-1, and penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine (c = 250 mg/L) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. A TGF-β inhibitor (Miltenyi Biotec) at a concentration of 6 mg/L was added to the medium to inhibit fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells during cultivation. The medium was changed every other day until the cells reached 80%-90% confluency. Trypan blue was used to determine the overall number, percentage, and concentration of viable cells in the cell suspension. The cells were diluted with Endothelial Cell Growth Basal Medium-2without TGF-β inhibitor to a concentration of 1,600,000 cells/mL, and 30 μL of this cell dilution was pipetted into the reservoir of an ibidi slide (μ-Slide VI 0.4, ibiTreat—Tissue Culture Treated Coated Polymer Coverslip; ibidi). After 2 hours, the cells were attached to the channel and the reservoirs were filled with an additional 120 μL medium. The experiments were started at 24 hours to allow the TGF-β inhibitor to fade. Before starting the experiments, the medium was changed, and the medium respective to the performed condition was added. For HEEC TGF-β stimulation, 10 μg/mL of recombinant human TGF-β (R&D Systems) was used to mimic the clinical situation of TGF-β–mediated EFE formation. Additionally, these cells were exposed to either atorvastatin (c = 0.5 μmol/L) or vehicle (0.01% dimethyl sulfoxide). We chose the concentration of atorvastatin based on previously published data determining that concentrations up to 0.7 μmol/L are nontoxic for endothelial cell cultures for extended periods of time. For the no-flow (static) experiments, the ibidi slides were placed in an incubator, and the medium was exchanged every 24 hours. For the flow experiments, cells were exposed to 7.5-12.5 dyn/cm2 laminar flow conditions using a commercially available flow pump system for cell culture under flow (ibidi). After 72 hours, the cells were fixed by incubation with 3.7% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 minutes and then washed twice with a washing buffer consisting of 0.1% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS.
For KLF2 forced expression experiments, endocardial fibroelastosis–derived endocardial endothelial cells (EdEECs) were plated on 1% gelatin-coated dishes and cultured in endothelial cell growth medium-2 (Lonza) containing the above-mentioned TGF-β inhibitor at the same concentration, and the cells were allowed to reach confluency. Next, EdEECs were trypsinized and seeded at a density of 36,000 cells/cm2 into 1% gelatin-precoated 6-well dishes in endothelial cell growth medium-2 without a TGF-β inhibitor. Then, 24 hours later, cells were infected with either an adenovirus-expressing green fluorescent protein or murine KLF2 at a multiplicity of infection of 25 for 24 hours. Medium was replaced with fresh medium and cells were cultured for an additional 24 hours before processing for messenger RNA isolation. We further conducted small interfering RNA KLF2 silencing experiments in EdEECs (see and for details) and exposed the cells to atorvastatin treatment to assess its effect on key markers of fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells in the absence of KLF2.

## Animal procedures
Twenty neonatal heterotopic rat heart transplants were performed with 15 animals (75%) surviving until the study endpoint, with a modified Stanford score of ≥3, as illustrated herein. We described the technique used previously. , , Briefly, neonatal rats with a median age of 3 days (IQR: 0.25) were used as donors and anesthetized with ketamine (75 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. The anterior rib cage was removed, and 3 mL of ice-cold modified high-potassium Krebs-Henseleit solution was administered for cardioplegia to the right atrium for retrograde coronary infusion and cardiac arrest. The pulmonary arteries, distal ascending aorta, and all other ligated vessels were transected, and the harvested heart was stored in an ice-cold cardioplegic solution until further use.
Male adolescent rats with a median weight of 150 g (IQR: 40 g) were used as recipients. After induction of anesthesia with ketamine (45 mg/kg), xylazine (5 mg/kg), and heparin (300 IU/kg) intraperitoneal injection, endotracheal intubation, and continuous isoflurane inhalation (0.5%-2%), a median laparotomy was performed, and the donor heart was anastomosed end-to-side aorta to aorta, and end-to-side pulmonary artery to inferior vena cava. After removing the vascular clamps, a sufficiently coronary-perfused, viable, beating donor heart with flow stagnation within the LV was established. Approximately 15 minutes after heart reperfusion, all hearts were visually evaluated for contractility and heart rate to ensure proper organ viability, and a modified score derived from the Stanford score (0-4) was applied, followed by closure of the abdominal wall in 2 layers with resorbable sutures. After subcutaneous injection of meloxicam (1 mg/kg) and Ethiqa XR (0.65 mg/kg), the animals were given ample time to recover before returning to their cages. No restrictions on food or water were applied and no antibiotics were administered. Daily meloxicam was continued until postoperative day (POD) 3.
All surgical procedures were performed using a Zeiss OPMI 6-SFR surgical microscope (12.5; Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and an Advanced Ventilator System for Rodents (SAR-1000; CWE, Inc). Recipient rats were assigned to the untreated or the vehicle group (n = 4 in each group) and were either treated with vehicle (0.5 mL 10% dimethyl sulfoxide in 0.9% NaCl/100 g) or remained untreated, or to the atorvastatin group (n = 7) where atorvastatin was administered daily starting on POD 1 (0.5 mL 10% dimethyl sulfoxide in 0.9% NaCl + 0.3 mg atorvastatin/100 g), respectively. Daily intraperitoneal injections were administered until euthanasia on POD 14.

## Echocardiographic monitoring
Assessment of graft viability and quality was performed by transabdominal echocardiography on POD 1, 7, and 14 using a Vevo 3100 ultrasound device (FUJIFILM VisualSonics Corporation). Rats were lightly sedated with continuous 1%-2% isoflurane inhalation through a nose cone and placed on a heating pad for electrocardiogram monitoring. Heart rate was derived from the electrocardiogram, and LV short-axis and long-axis views were obtained. The modified Stanford score was calculated as follows: 0 = no organ function; 1 = (rest) organ function only minimal contraction; 2 = weak or partial organ function; 3 = contractile rate or intensity reduced, but homogenous organ function; 4 = optimal atrial and ventricular contractions (120-160 beats/min). A score of 3 or 4 was considered successful. Additional Doppler evaluations at the anastomosis sites were performed to confirm the proper flow. Between echocardiographic visualizations, grafts were monitored through palpatory evaluation.

## Histologic EFE assessment
On POD 14, the recipient rats were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, relaparotomy was performed under sterile conditions, and the donor graft was excised. One horizontal, midventricular slice through both ventricles was obtained and embedded in optimal cutting temperature medium before snap-freezing with liquid nitrogen. Sectioning and staining with hematoxylin-eosin, Masson trichrome, and elastin van Gieson were performed.
EFE thickness was measured on Masson trichrome–stained LVs using an inverted microscope (Axio Observer Z1; Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and Zen 3.6 software (ZEISS Group) at 5× magnification. The endocardial layer of each heart was measured using 12 fields of vision with a clockwise pattern within the center of the LV. The images were transferred to Adobe Photoshop Desktop (version 24.6), and the pixels were calibrated for micrometers. All remaining donor LV tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further RNA and protein extraction.

## Fluorescent immunohistochemical staining
After fixation of cells or frozen sections of the midventricular horizontal slices of the transplanted neonatal donor hearts with 3.7% formaldehyde, nonspecific binding was blocked with a buffer containing 10% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS) at room temperature for 45 minutes. The blocking buffer was removed, and the primary antibodies in dilution buffer (1% bovine serum albumin, 1% normal goat serum, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 0.01% sodium azide in 1× PBS) were added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing twice with washing buffer, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies dissolved in dilution buffer at room temperature in the dark for 1 hour. After rinsing twice with washing buffer, cells or tissue sections were incubated with diluted 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole solution at room temperature for 5 minutes and rinsed once with 1× PBS and distilled water, respectively. Finally, 1 drop of mounting medium (Agilent-Dako) was added, and the stained slides were kept at 4 °C in the dark until imaging. Fluorescently stained slides were imaged with a TCS SP8 Confocal (Leica) and 10×, 20×, or 63× objective lens and LasX software, and nonfluorescent images with an Axio Observer Z1 microscope and Zen 3.6 software at 5×, 10×, 40×, or 63× objective lenses. Images were analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) and QuPath software. Negative and positive controls are displayed in and , and information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in and .

## Western blotting
LV tissues from the transplanted neonatal donor hearts were pulverized using a mortar and pestle. Tissue (60 mg) and 200 μL of pre-extraction buffer from a nuclear extraction kit (Abcam) were placed in an Eppendorf tube, homogenized using an electric pellet pestle, and incubated on ice for 15 minutes. Centrifugation was performed for 10 minutes at 12,000 rev/min and 4 °C. The supernatant was skimmed and used as cytosolic protein. To extract nuclear proteins, dithiothreitol solution, protease inhibitor cocktail, and extraction buffer were added to the pellet and incubated on ice for 15 minutes with intermittent vortexing. Sonication was then performed (3× 10 seconds each), and the samples were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 14,000 rev/min and 4 °C before the supernatant was transferred into a new tube and used as nuclear proteins. Pierce 660-nm BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a VersaMax microplate reader (Molecular Devices) were used for protein measurement.
Protein lysate 60 μg per well was loaded into 15 wells of 4%-20% Tris-Glycine gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for cytosolic proteins and 10 wells of 10% Tris-Glycine Gel for nuclear proteins. Electrophoresis and transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were followed by fast transfer with a power blotter cassette (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following the transfer, gels were stained with GelCode Blue Safe Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to control for equal loading. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline for 1 hour at room temperature before incubation with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies against collagen type I, SMAD2/3, and cyclophilin B were used as loading controls for cytosolic proteins. pSMAD2/3 and histone H3 were used as loading controls for the nuclear proteins. Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated antibodies from a Western blot detection kit (Kindle Biosciences) were used at room temperature for 1 hour and an enhanced chemiluminescent detection system was used to detect primary antibodies. The Western blotting antibodies are detailed in .

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction
RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy Mini Kit and the DNAse Kit (Qiagen). LV tissues from transplanted neonatal donor hearts were pulverized using a mortar and pestle. Tissue (20 mg) was weighed and transferred into a 2-mL lysing matrix tube (MP Biomedicals) before adding 700 μL QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen) and homogenized using a FastPrep-24 5G lysis system (MP Biomedicals). Then, 140 μL chloroform (Sigma-Aldrich) was added and the mixture was incubated for 3 minutes at room temperature. Centrifugation for 15 minutes at 12,000g at 4 °C was performed, and the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube before adding 1.5× 100% ethanol. Next, an RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen) was used to extract messenger RNA from lysed tissues according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure equal loading, the RNA concentration was measured at 260 nm using a NanoDrop One Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Multiplex quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction using the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed, which enabled the amplification of up to 4 targets in a single reaction. TaqPath 1-Step Master Mix (No ROX, 4×; Thermo Fisher Scientific), which includes a thermostable Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase, a mixture of deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates (deoxyguanosine, deoxythymidine, deoxycytidine, deoxyadensoine triphosphates), uracyl N-glycosilase, and a thermostable DNA polymerase, was used. As endogenous control genes (housekeeping genes/reference genes), GAPDH for human cells and rat tissue or 18S ribosomal RNA for healthy control cells from lambs were used. All samples were analyzed in triplicate. The resulting Ct values were standardized to that of the housekeeping gene (ΔCt) and further normalized to the ΔCt of the control condition (ΔΔCt). These values were then expressed as ratios (fold change) using the following formula: Ratio = 2−ΔΔCt. The full list of primers used is provided in .

## Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, LLC) and STATA 16 (StataCorp LLC) software. Outliers were identified and subsequently removed using the ROUT (Robust Outlier Test) method, with a Q value of 1%. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM and compared using an unpaired 2-sided t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as applicable in animal samples. Normal Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test were used to assess the normality of continuous data. Based on past experiments, a sample size of 7 per group was estimated to have a >90% probability of detecting a 20% at α = 0.05. Cell data from healthy control cell lines were analyzed using 2-way analysis of variance with post hoc Tukey correction for multiple comparisons. All cell culture experiments were performed in triplicate. All cell data derived from human samples were analyzed using paired 2-sided t-tests, or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to account for variance from humans at the individual patient level (matched pairs). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

## Results

## In vitro

## Healthy endocardial endothelial cells
Evaluation of fibrogenic activation of HEECs at the protein level via immunohistochemistry revealed significant inhibitory effects of atorvastatin and exposure to flow ( , ). Atorvastatin-treated HEECs under no-flow conditions exhibited reduced α-smooth muscle actin expression compared with untreated cells in both unstimulated and TGF-β–stimulated HEECs (both P < 0.001) ( ). Exposure to flow further reduced α-smooth muscle actin expression in both untreated and atorvastatin-treated cells ( ). Additionally, flow, but not atorvastatin, significantly reduced collagen expression in HEECs (P < 0.001) ( ).
 Gene expression analysis using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction revealed that atorvastatin and exposure to flow inhibited fibrogenic activation of HEECs at the transcriptional level. The expression of key genes associated with fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells, including Acta2, Smad2, Col1a1, Klf2, and Snai2, was quantified. In unstimulated HEECs under no-flow conditions, atorvastatin significantly reduced Acta2, Smad2, and Snai2 expression (P < 0.001 for each: Acta2, Smad2, and Snai2) ( ). Exposure to flow also led to a significant reduction in these gene expressions (P < 0.001 for each: Acta2, Smad2, and Snai2) ( ).
 In TGF-β–stimulated HEECs, atorvastatin similarly inhibited Acta2 and Snai2 expression under no-flow conditions ( ). Moreover, this effect was associated with significant Klf2 up-regulation (P < 0.001) ( ). Exposure to flow further enhanced these TGF-β pathway-mediated inhibitory effects on EndMT, despite a lack of sustained Klf2 increase but additional inhibition of Smad2 expression (P < 0.001).
Flow, but not atorvastatin, was associated with a significant decrease in Col1a1 expression in both unstimulated and TGF-β–stimulated HEECs ( ).

## Effects of Atorvastatin in EdEECs Are KLF2 Mediated
In EdEECs, atorvastatin treatment also showed inhibitory effects on fibrogenic activation at the transcriptional level ( ). Under both no-flow and flow conditions, atorvastatin significantly reduced ACTA2 expression (P = 0.027 and P = 0.013, respectively). This effect was associated with an increase in KLF2 expression (P = 0.008), which was sustained under the flow (P = 0.003). Under flow exposure, we additionally detected a decreased expression of its downstream intermediary SMAD2 (P = 0.010) in cells treated with atorvastatin. No significant changes in collagen expression were observed after atorvastatin therapy in EdEECs.
 Adenoviral overexpression of KLF2 in EdEECs resulted in a marked induction of endothelial nitric oxide synthase messenger RNA expression (approximately 21-fold increase; P = 0.006; n = 3), and simultaneously significantly inhibiting the induction of SMAD2 (P = 0.001). Further, KLF2 overexpressed EdEECs showed a trend toward increased PECAM1 (P = 0.095) and reduced ACTA2 (P = 0.097), additionally indicating KLF2-dependent inhibition of fibrogenic activation ( ). In addition, silencing of KLF2 via small interfering RNA in EdEECs resulted in a blunting of atorvastatin’s effects on the PECAM1 and ACTA2 ( ).

## In vivo

## Animal procedures and graft viability assessment by echocardiography
Of the 20 transplanted hearts, 15 survived to the study endpoint at POD 14. There was no significant difference in donor graft ischemia time (mean: 106.3 ± 17.5 minutes vs 96.1 ± 9.8 minutes; P = 0.20) nor postoperative Stanford score (mean: 3.5 ± 0.5 vs 3.1 ± 0.4; P = 0.28) between the untreated/vehicle-treated hearts and the atorvastatin-treated hearts. Echocardiographic assessment of graft viability and function of the donor hearts on PODs 1, 7, and 14 was not different between the groups (POD 1: P = 0.077; POD 7: P = 0.20; POD 14: P = 0.20), indicating transplant success (of note, in this acute early-onset EFE model, characterized by a thin layer of fibrosis, severe cardiac dysfunction would not be expected); however, EFE formation could be visualized in the untreated/vehicle-treated hearts on POD 14 ( ).
 

## Atorvastatin treated neonatal donor hearts showed less EFE thickness
Post mortem histologic analysis of the donor hearts’ LVs after 14 days’ transplantation showed significantly less EFE thickness 24.3 ± 2.5 μm in the atorvastatin-treated group vs 173.2 ± 20.1 μm in the vehicle-treated group (P < 0.001), indicating successful inhibition of EFE formation ( ). Furthermore, atorvastatin prevents the infiltrative growth of EFE in the underlying myocardium, which is a common feature of EFE. Only some patchy areas of thin EFE were visualized by immunohistochemical and Masson trichrome staining.
 Collagen type I cytosolic protein expression was increased in untreated/vehicle-treated compared to atorvastatin-treated rat hearts (P = 0.034) indicating more fibrosis within the LV of nonatorvastatin treated rat hearts. Transcriptional analyses with qRT-PCR gene expression of collagen type I showed a trend toward higher expression in untreated/vehicle-treated hearts, but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.095). Furthermore, Western blotting analysis of cytosolic Smad2/3 showed significantly higher protein expression in untreated/vehicle-treated rat hearts than in atorvastatin-treated rats (P = 0.033). Nuclear protein densitometry of pSmad2 showed significantly higher expression in untreated and vehicle-treated rat hearts (P < 0.001), but pSmad3 did not differ between the 2 groups (P = 0.90) ( ). Gene expression of the transcription factor Slug/Snail was significantly down-regulated in atorvastatin-treated rat hearts (P = 0.028) ( ), which is indicative of inhibition of the fibrogenic activation of EECs. Conversely, Klf2 expression was significantly up-regulated (P = 0.022) in the atorvastatin-treated rat hearts ( ).
 

## Discussion
Our integrated analysis of cellular and animal data provides valuable insights into the potential therapeutic benefits of atorvastatin in inhibiting fibrogenic activation at the endocardial level, thus blocking EFE formation. Fibrogenic activation of endothelial cells via EndMT is a critical process implicated in various pathological conditions including EFE. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of exposure of EECs to each physiological flow and atorvastatin on the fibrogenic activation of endocardial endothelial cells in both in vitro and in vivo models of EFE using HEECs, primary EECs from resected EFE tissue (EdEECs), and an EFE rat heart model. HEECs are responsive to flow, which significantly inhibits the TGF-β pathway and, thereby, fibrogenic activation, and stimulates the up-regulation of KLF2. In contrast, this response was blunted in EdEECs. Our results demonstrate that atorvastatin reduces fibrogenic activation in healthy and diseased EECs, at least in part, by up-regulating KLF2 expression. This is supported by the blunting of atorvastatin’s protective effects on markers of fibrogenic activation after KLF2 silencing. Additionally, adenoviral overexpression of KLF2 confirmed that KLF2 up-regulation plays a significant role in inhibiting fibrogenic activation and maintaining markers of endothelial cell function in EdEECs. Furthermore, atorvastatin inhibited EFE development in vivo and significantly reduced infiltrative growth into the underlying myocardium by reducing collagen production through KLF2 up-regulation and TGF-β pathway inhibition. Interestingly, when considering the observed discrepancies in the effects of atorvastatin on collagen type I expression between our in vitro and in vivo studies, it is important to recognize the inherent differences in experimental conditions. In vitro environments typically lack complex interactions with various cell types and the dynamic biomechanical forces present in living organisms. Specifically, the shear stress variances from pulsatile blood flow that are multidimensional as opposed to the unidimensional shear stress applied in vitro and interactions with cellular components in the blood can modulate endothelial cell functions in vivo, affecting downstream signaling pathways such as TGF-β, which are critical for the regulation of collagen type I synthesis. Additionally, the metabolic transformations that atorvastatin undergoes in the systemic circulation and its effects on adjacent cells can amplify its efficacy, further contributing to the observed variances. The reduction in overall extracellular matrix production observed in vivo, however, suggests a therapeutic role for atorvastatin in preventing fibrogenic activation of EECs during EFE formation and ultimately inhibiting EFE formation and reducing aberrant extracellular matrix production, which may have implications for the cardiovascular health of patients affected by this rare form of cardiac fibrosis, primarily seen in patients with congenital heart disease in the spectrum of hypoplastic left heart syndrome and congenital aortic stenosis.
This study emphasizes the role of flow stagnation as a main contributor to the pathomechanism of EFE. HEECs were rescued from fibrogenic activation through laminar flow exposure at physiological levels within the human heart, whereas the effect of the same flow conditions on EFE-derived cells was blunted. Specifically, at shear stress levels at which, even under TGF-β stimulation, HEECs reacted with reduced fibrogenic activation, we did not observe any improvements in EdEECs. This could be attributed to several factors, including the intrinsic predisposition of EFE patients that has been postulated in several studies. , Miao et al recently shed light on the intrinsic endocardial defects and abnormal EndMT in human hypoplastic left heart syndrome–induced pluripotent stem cells, highlighting the pivotal role of these factors in the pathogenesis of hypoplastic left heart syndrome and EFE. These findings complement our clinical observation of faster recurrence rates in patients with genetic variants in genes ontologically related to cardiac EndMT. Despite this predisposition, our data showed a beneficial effect of atorvastatin in inhibiting fibrogenic activation in EdEECs. These findings underscore the potential significance of targeting these cellular and molecular pathways, rather than just aiming to normalize flow patterns during fetal development to mitigate the pathological changes associated with EFE.
Not only on isolated cell level but also in a neonatal rat heart heterotopic transplantation model, we have shown that flow stagnation is a strong stimulus for fibrogenic activation of endocardial endothelial cells through TGF-β pathway activation and, thus, EFE formation. Similar to our animal model, in which flow stagnation triggered the development of EFE, a recent study introduced a fetal lamb model of left heart hypoplasia through the reduction of left heart inflow. By implanting coils in the left atrium of fetal lambs, the researchers successfully created a scenario of flow stagnation in the LV, leading to significant left heart hypoplasia and thickening of the subendocardial connective tissue, which is histopathologically consistent with EFE. The observed thickening of the endocardial layer and a shift in the cellular composition of the hypoplastic LV toward an increased abundance of fibroblasts and decreased numbers of cardiomyocytes. These features were suggestive of an aberrant and ectopic induction of mesenchymal and profibrotic signaling, including up-regulated expression of extracellular matrix components and dysregulation of fibroblast growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor signaling at the small nuclear RNA level. The findings all point toward EndMT at the endocardial level triggered by flow stagnation as the main contributing factor for hypoplasia of the LV, further providing experimental evidence that abnormal flow patterns during fetal heart development lead to pathological changes in cardiac structure and composition across different species via cellular dysregulation of the endocardium. Our animal model replicated these findings and our isolated cell culture experiments supported these results. It is important to note that neonatal rat hearts resemble a developmental stage comparable to fetal heart in other species. In our model of unloaded neonatal rat hearts, lacking intracavitary flow, significant EFE develops within a short time frame after transplantation, mirroring the developmental characteristics seen in fetal hearts of humans and lambs during EFE development in utero. 
From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that therapies aimed at mitigating molecular alterations in EECs triggered by disturbed flow such as flow stagnation could be used as neoadjuvant fetal treatment to reduce EFE progression early after diagnosis or as adjuvant treatment after EFE resection to prevent EFE recurrence. , Our mechanistic insights indicate that atorvastatin treatment inhibits fibrogenic activation of EECs on a cellular level (in vitro and in vivo), leading to reduced EFE formation, which is at least in part mediated via up-regulation of KLF2 expression. KLF2 is a transcription factor that responds to laminar shear stress and has been shown to be protective against atherosclerosis. Under laminar shear stress, KLF2 is up-regulated in endothelial cells and inhibits TGF-β signaling. TGF-β is a cytokine that promotes fibrogenic activation endothelial cells via EndMT, a process that plays a key role in the development of atherosclerosis. KLF2 inhibits TGF-β signaling through a number of mechanisms, including induction of Smad7, an inhibitory Smad protein that blocks the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of Smad2/3, and suppression of activator protein 1, a transcription factor that is required for TGF-β–mediated gene expression. In our animal model of EFE, we showed the effects of statins on KLF2 up-regulation and down-regulation of the nuclear expression of phosphorylated SMAD2, which was reflected in a significant reduction in EFE.
Our findings confirm that these mechanisms are crucial for maintaining the homeostasis of EECs. KLF2 plays important roles in embryonic development and vascular homeostasis. KLF2-deficient mice die in utero due to defects in cardiac development. KLF2 is required for the maintenance of normal endothelial function in adults. KLF2-deficient mice develop atherosclerosis and other vascular diseases. Our endocardial cell culture model showed intermittent KLF2 up-regulation without downstream effector down-regulation under no-flow conditions, but still mitigated fibrogenic activation of EECs. However, under protective conditions through flow and statin, KLF2 was not significantly up-regulated, but it was accompanied by significant down-regulation of downstream effectors. These adaptive changes point toward an acute response, in contrast to the long-term maintenance effect of flow and statins. EFE is a disease process that starts during fetal development, necessitating the provision of safe therapeutic strategies as early as in utero. A recent paradigm shift in atherosclerosis has led to a US Food and Drug Administration request to remove the “Pregnancy Category X” label to allow for statin therapy in pregnancy because more evidence suggests that theoretical concerns about potential teratogenesis were not held up in clinical trials. , , , This opens the opportunity for statins to become a feasible therapy for EFE patients diagnosed in utero, which may reduce the burden of EFE on the offspring and enable less restrictive growth and improved LV function. Furthermore, statins are already safely used in children with familial hypercholesterolemia and post heart transplantation and are, therefore, readily available for the prevention of EFE.

## Study limitations
Our study is not without limitations, and careful consideration should be given to interpreting the findings. First, the in vitro concentrations of atorvastatin employed in our experiments, which are consistent with those commonly reported in cell studies, are higher than the blood concentrations typically observed in adult humans administered 80 mg/d orally. This discrepancy prompts caution in directly extrapolating our in vitro results to a clinical setting.
Second, previous studies have shown an atorvastatin dosing regimen of 1 min/100 g body weight in rats resulting in a maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of 54.15 ± 29.11 ng/mL. Extrapolating this data, we estimated that at the lower dose of 0.3 min/100 g body weight given in our study would yield a plasma concentration at the upper limit of the range observed in humans receiving 80 mg/d orally. 
Further, while the heterotopic rat heart transplant model employed in this study offers an innovative approach to investigate the acute phase of EFE development under conditions of flow stagnation, it does not fully replicate the chronic, multifactorial aspects of human EFE, particularly given the limited 14-day follow-up period.
Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the understanding of the effect of atorvastatin on the development of EFE, presenting a potential pharmacologic treatment option for this challenging cardiac condition affecting the most vulnerable patients with congenital heart disease.

## Conclusions
In summary, our integrated analysis of cellular and animal data provided comprehensive evidence that atorvastatin treatment effectively inhibits fibrogenic activation of EECs, leading to reduced EFE formation in vivo. Atorvastatin suppresses fibrogenic activation of EECs at both the protein and transcriptional levels at least in part via the up-regulation of KLF2. The results presented here suggest that atorvastatin is a promising drug that impedes EFE formation and infiltration into the underlying myocardium, allowing more efficient ventricular adaptation to achieve biventricular circulation.
PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: EFE restricts the LVs of patients with hypoplastic left heart syndrome variants, complicating biventricular surgical repair, and often leading to single-ventricle palliation and the associated adverse outcomes of Fontan physiology. As part of the staged LV recruitment strategy pursued at Boston Children’s Hospital, EFE resection aims to preserve LV function but is limited by the recurrence and infiltrative progression of fibrosis due to the fibrogenic activation of EECs. This study highlights the potential of atorvastatin as an adjuvant therapy that impedes EFE formation and infiltration into the myocardium. At least in part by up-regulating KLF2 and inhibiting TGF-β signaling, atorvastatin allows for more efficient ventricular adaptation and supports the feasibility of achieving successful biventricular circulation. Understanding these mechanisms provides clinicians with a foundation for considering atorvastatin in comprehensive treatment strategies for EFE.
TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The findings present atorvastatin as a promising candidate for clinical trials targeting EFE in patients with congenital heart defects, such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome and congenital aortic stenosis. This research paves the way for potential neoadjuvant treatments that could be administered in utero or early postnatally to reduce the risk of developing EFE. Future investigations should focus on optimizing atorvastatin dosing and evaluating long-term outcomes, safety, and efficacy in pediatric populations. Additionally, advancements in genetic and mechanistic studies could enable personalized approaches, improving the prevention and management of fibrosis-related complications in congenital cardiology.
