## Abstract

## Purpose:
Hepatic metastasis (HM) is the leading cause of death in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). However, the underlying cellular and molecular programs remain poorly understood, leading to limited therapeutics for this disease.

## Experimental Design:
In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing data from paired primary tumors and HMs, along with bulk RNA sequencing and IHC data from hundreds of patients to elucidate metastasis-associated programs.

## Results:
Our analysis identified a metastasis-prone malignant subpopulation, which is associated with a higher risk of HM and a transitional plastic state. This malignant subpopulation represents a poorly differentiated and highly proliferative phenotype, with H2AFZ potentially contributing to this phenomenon. Moreover, the presence of tumor cells in the liver was accompanied by an increased abundance of M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells, and exhausted T cells (Tex) in HMs compared with adjacent tissues, indicative of a shift toward an immunosuppressive environment. Notably, within the tumor environment of HMs, Tex exhibited elevated expression of PDCD1 and LAG3. The combined therapy targeting these two genes effectively inhibited tumor growth in mouse models of metastatic PDAC.

## Conclusions:
In conclusion, we reveal a metastasis-associated malignant subpopulation and provide a promising therapeutic strategy for metastatic PDAC.

## Translational Relevance
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy, with nearly half of patients developing distant metastases. The liver is the most frequent metastatic site, and hepatic metastasis (HM) is associated with particularly poor outcomes, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate mechanisms that drive metastatic progression. Tumor cells, as the fundamental drivers of metastasis, represent the most critical and central cell population to investigate in metastatic PDAC. By applying single-cell RNA sequencing to paired primary tumors and HMs and integrating bulk transcriptomic and pathologic data from large patient cohorts, we identify a metastasis-associated tumor subpopulation, C2, and its signature gene, H2AFZ, linked to metastatic potential. Targeting H2AFZ markedly suppresses the formation of HMs, highlighting its essential role in metastatic progression and underscoring the translational importance of our findings.

## Introduction
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive and lethal malignancy characterized by the rapid dissemination of tumor cells. Nearly 50% of patients with PDAC present with distant metastases at the time of diagnosis ( ). The lack of effective treatments to inhibit metastasis results in a devastating 5-year survival rate of only 3% for these patients ( ). Despite the development of new chemotherapy regimens ( , ), targeted therapies ( ), and immunotherapies ( )—including NALIRIFOX, olaparib, and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors—the survival benefits for patients with metastatic PDAC remain very limited. This is primarily due to an inadequate understanding of the complex mechanisms governing metastatic pancreatic tumors and their interaction with the surrounding microenvironment.
Among all metastatic organs, the liver is the most frequent site of metastasis, and patients with hepatic metastases (HM) exhibit poorer clinical outcomes ( , ). This underscores the necessity of elucidating the mechanisms underlying HM in PDAC. Our previous work was among the first to explore HMs and their matched primary tumors (PT) via bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) and whole-exome sequencing, revealing key features such as altered microenvironments and metabolic rewiring in metastatic PDAC ( ). However, the bulk sequencing techniques used in this study were unable to track alterations at the cellular level, limiting the ability to dissect the heterogeneity and dynamic states of individual cells.
The emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology offers a great opportunity to explore the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms at an unprecedented single-cell resolution. This technology has been widely utilized to investigate tumor metastasis in several cancers, such as colon ( ), head and neck ( ), and gallbladder cancer ( ). In pancreatic cancer, scRNA-seq has also been applied to metastatic PDAC ( – ). Although these works have advanced our understanding of PDAC metastasis, they primarily emphasize microenvironmental shifts. The intrinsic properties of tumor epithelial cells—the fundamental drivers of metastasis—remain insufficiently explored. In our study, we hope to place a stronger focus on delineating tumor cell subpopulations and their intrinsic roles in driving metastatic progression.
Therefore, we applied scRNA-seq to 12 synchronously resected samples of paired PTs and oligo-HMs. We further integrated bulk RNA-seq and IHC data from hundreds of patients with PDAC to investigate associations between specific cell subsets and metastatic potential. The functional relevance of key cell types and genes was validated through in vitro assays using human PDAC cell lines and in vivo experiments in murine models. Notably, we observed an enhanced antitumor effect of combined anti–PD-1 and anti-LAG3 therapy in metastatic mouse models. In summary, our investigation of paired PTs and HMs at single-cell resolution reveals metastasis-associated gene and cellular programs, providing new insights into metastatic mechanisms and offering a promising therapeutic strategy for metastatic PDAC.

## Materials and Methods

## Sample acquisition
A total of 12 fresh samples were obtained from six patients (P1–P6) via surgical resection at Shanghai Renji Hospital under Institutional Review Board approval. All patients were pathologically diagnosed with PDAC with liver oligo-metastases. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection for research purposes. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Five out of six patients (P1–P3, P5, and P6) had no prior treatment before surgery, whereas P4 had been treated with paclitaxel and gemcitabine before the operation. The detailed clinical information of these patients in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.
The criteria in our study for patients with liver oligo-metastases were based on a previous report ( ) as follows: (i) no extrahepatic metastases; (ii) not combined with venous resection or multivisceral resection, and R0 resection can be performed on the primary pancreatic tumor; (iii) the number of liver metastases ≤3, and macroscopic liver metastases can be completely extirpated by operation or operation combined with radiofrequency ablation; (iv) the general medical condition of the patient was good, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists score ≤III.

## scRNA library preparation and scRNA-seq
Tissues from 12 paired PTs and liver metastases were digested using the dissociation enzyme 0.25% Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #25200-072) and 10 μg/mL DNase I (Sigma, cat. #11284932001). Then, tissues were dissociated at 37°C with a shaking speed of 50 rpm for about 40 minutes. Cell suspensions were filtered using a 40 μm nylon cell strainer, and red blood cells were removed using 1× Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #00-4333-57). Cells were stained with 0.4% Trypan blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #14190144) to check viability on the Countess II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 10× library preparation and sequencing beads with unique molecular identifiers (UMI) and cell barcodes were loaded close to saturation so that each cell was paired with a bead in a gel beads-in-emulsion. After exposure to cell lysis buffer, polyadenylated RNA molecules hybridized to the beads. Beads were retrieved into a single tube for reverse transcription. During complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged on the 5′ end (i.e., the 3′ end of a messenger RNA transcript) with a UMI and cell label indicating its cell of origin. Briefly, 10× beads were then subjected to second-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and universal amplification. Sequencing libraries were prepared using randomly interrupted whole-transcriptome amplification products to enrich the 3′ end of the transcripts linked with the cell barcode and UMI. All the remaining procedures, including library construction, were performed according to the standard manufacturer’s protocol (CG000206 RevD). Sequencing libraries were quantified using a High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Agilent) on a Bioanalyzer 2100 and the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) using 2 × 150 chemistry.

## scRNA-seq data processing
The raw scRNA-seq data were quantified using Cell Ranger (RRID: SCR_017344; version 7.0.0, 10x Genomics) with GRCh38 as the reference ( ). The filtered_feature_bc_matrix files generated by Cell Ranger were filtered and analyzed via Seurat (RRID: SCR_016341; version 4.1.1; ref. ). Cells with detected transcripts fewer than 500, transcripts more than 50,000, and mitochondrial UMI percentages greater than 30% were filtered out. DoubletFinder was used for each sequencing library to further remove potential doublets ( ).
After quality control, the count data were normalized, logarithmically transformed, and scaled by the SCTransform function. Batch effects were corrected by the FindIntegrationAnchors (with the parameter reduction = “rpca”) and IntegrateData functions. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the RunPCA and RunUMAP functions. Unsupervised clustering was conducted to identify major cell clusters using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions with the top 30 principal components and a resolution of 0.4. Canonical markers were used to annotate different cell types (Supplementary Table S2).

## Copy-number variation estimation
The copy-number variation (CNV) profiles of ductal cells were inferred via the inferCNV algorithm (RRID: SCR_021140; ref. ), with endothelial cells and fibroblasts serving as the normal reference. The CNV score (CNVscore) of each cell was calculated as the quadratic sum of CNV levels deviating from the normal level.

## Signature gene identification
Differential expression analysis was performed using the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat R package under different comparisons. The signatures of different ductal subclusters were defined based on the criteria of “avg_log2FC > 0.30, P_val_adj < 0.05”; ribosomal and mitochondrial genes were excluded from signature genes. The signature genes of unipotent cells with higher plasticity were identified based on the criteria of “avg_log2FC > 0.5, P_val_adj < 0.05.” To robustly define signature genes for HM tumor cells, patient-level analysis was applied by comparing HM versus PT cells within each patient. Genes upregulated in HM cells in more than three patients were identified as the HM signatures with “avg_log2FC > 0 and P_val_adj < 0.05.”

## Signature score and pathway score calculation
The signature and pathway scores of different gene sets for the scRNA-seq data were calculated via AUCell (RRID: SCR_021327; version 1.16.0; ref. ). The AUCell_buildRankings function was applied to build gene expression rankings for each cell. Then the AUCell_calcAUC function was performed to calculate the scores of different gene sets across cells.
The signature and pathway scores for bulk RNA-seq data from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)–PDAC cohort were calculated using the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) method (RRID: SCR_021058; ref. ). The log2sfCounts assay was used as the input and analyzed using the gsva function with the parameter kcdf = “Gaussian.”

## Pseudotime trajectory inference
Pseudotime analysis was performed on ductal cells using Monocle2 (RRID: SCR_016339; version 2.22.0; ref. ). The newCellDataSet function was used to generate the cds object. Genes that were highly variable and differentially expressed between different cell clusters were selected to infer the trajectory. The reduceDimension function was used for dimensionality reduction with the parameters reduction_method = “DDRTree” and num_dim = 6. Cells were then arranged along the inferred trajectory using the orderCells function.

## Developmental potential
CytoTRACE2 (version 1.1.0; bioRxiv 2024.03.19.585637) is a computational method for predicting cellular potency categories and absolute developmental potential from scRNA-seq data based on interpretable deep learning. The developmental potential was inferred using the cytotrace2 function with default parameters.

## Survival analysis
The expression data and clinical information of 177 patients diagnosed with pancreatic adenocarcinoma were downloaded from TCGA. To investigate the relationship between our results and metastasis, rigorously curated clinical follow-up information for the TCGA cohort, including the type, site, and date of new tumors, was obtained from a public article ( ). The signature scores of each ductal subcluster were calculated using the GSVA method. Survival curves were fit by the survfit function of the R package survival (RRID: SCR_021137; ref. ) and visualized by the ggsurvplot function of the R package survminer (RRID: SCR_021094). The optimal cutoff point for patient grouping was estimated via the R package maxstat ( ). Additionally, TCGA-PDAC patients were classified into the high metastatic risk (highM) group or the low metastatic risk (lowM) group based on metastatic sites and disease-free survival (DFS) duration. The highM group included patients with documented metastatic sites and a DFS of 365 days or less, whereas the lowM group comprised patients with documented metastatic sites and a DFS greater than 365 days.

## Tissue microarray construction
Two sets of tissue microarrays (TMA) were constructed for different research purposes. The first set of TMAs contained matched HMs and PTs from 22 patients with metastatic PDAC. The second set of TMAs contained 67 PTs from highM patients and 82 PTs from lowM patients.

## IHC
IHC staining was performed on TMAs and formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections at a thickness of 4 μm. An intensity score of 0 to 3 was assigned for the intensity of tumor cells (0, none; 1, weak; 2, intermediate; and 3, strong). A proportional score was given based on the estimated proportion of positive tumor cells in percentage. To assess the average degree of staining within a tumor, multiple regions were analyzed, and at least 100 tumor cells were assessed. The cytoplasmic expression was assessed using the H-score system. The H-score was calculated as H-score = ∑(I × Pi), where I = intensity of staining and Pi = percentage of stained cells. We implemented IHC for H2AFZ (Abcam, abs100991), LAG3 (RRID: AB_2133350; Proteintech, 16616-1-AP), and PD-1 (Servicebio, GB15338). DAPI (Life Technologies, 62247) was used as a nuclear counterstain. Images were obtained using the Zeiss Axioplan 2 Fluorescence microscope.

## Knockdown of H2AFZ in PDAC cells
To achieve targeted gene knockdown of H2AFZ in PDAC cells, small interfering RNA (siRNA) oligonucleotides (Obio Technology) were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #13778150) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells transfected with siRNA were collected after 48 hours for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to assess knockdown efficiency and for further assays. The specific siRNAs for H2AFZ were designed and synthesized by Genepharma; the sequences were shown as follows: siH2AFZ-1, CGUAUUCAUCGACACCUAA(dT)(dT); siH2AFZ-2, CGUCACUUGCAACUUGCUA(dT)(dT); the siRNA control was nonhomologous to any human genomic sequences. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #13778030) was used to conduct siRNA transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA from PDAC cells was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio, cat. #9109) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, cat. #RR037A). Following this, real-time PCR assays were conducted with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio, cat. #RR820A) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), adhering to the recommended thermal cycling conditions (an initial cycle of 10 minutes at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 10 seconds at 95°C and 30 seconds at 60°C). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the 18S RNA levels.

## Cell culture
The human PDAC cell lines AsPC-1 (RRID: CVCL_0152), BxPC-3 (RRID: CVCL_0186), Capan-1 (RRID: CVCL_0237), Mia PaCa-2 (RRID: CVCL_0428), PANC-1 (RRID: CVCL_0480), Patu8988 (RRID: CVCL_1846), SW1990 (RRID: CVCL_1723), and the nonmalignant pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line HPNE (RRID: CVCL_C466) were all purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and preserved at the Shanghai Cancer Institute, Ren Ji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The mouse PDAC cell line KPC1199 was generated from KPC (Pdxcre; LSL- KrasG12D; LSL-TP53R172H) mice as described previously ( – ) and generously provided by Prof. Jing Xue (State Key Laboratory of Oncogenes and Related Genes, Renji-Med X Clinical Stem Cell Research Center, Shanghai Cancer Institute, Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University). Cells underwent high-density SNP typing for identity authentication and were verified to be Mycoplasma-free using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme, D101-01). All experiments shown were performed within a passage of <6 months from the acquisition of all cell lines. All cell lines were cultured in their recommended media as per American Type Culture Collection protocols, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2 at 37°C.

## Cell transwell migration assay
The transwell migration assay was performed using a 6.5 mm chamber with 8 μm pores (Costar, Corning Incorporated) housed in 24-well plates. Briefly, PDAC cells were resuspended in serum-free medium at a concentration of 4 × 104 cells per 200 μL and carefully loaded into the upper chamber. The lower compartment contained 700 μL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 hours of incubation, cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were removed using cotton swabs. Cells adherent to the lower membrane surface were subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for about 15 minutes, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 minutes for visualization. Quantitative analysis was performed by counting stained cells in three randomly selected fields per membrane using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Zeiss AXIOVERT 200), with experiments performed in triplicate for each condition.

## Cell proliferation assay
Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, MedChemExpress, cat. #HY-K0301) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 103 cells/well (n = 5 technical replicates) and allowed to adhere overnight. At 24-hour intervals over a 5-day observation period, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK-8 reagent, followed by 2 hours of incubation at 37°C under 5% CO2. The absorbance values at 450 nm were measured using an automatic enzyme-linked immunosorbent detector (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Growth curves were generated from triplicate independent experiments. For the colony formation assay, cells were inoculated in six-well plates at a density of 1,500 cells per well and cultured for 12 to 14 days. Following this incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, cat. #G1101) for 30 minutes and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Servicebio, cat. #G1014) for another 30 minutes. Photographs were taken, and colonies were counted under a microscope. Three independent experiments were performed for this assay as well.

## Liver metastasis model for evaluating the function of H2AFZ

H2AFZ-targeting short hairpin RNAs were designed and synthesized by GenePharma. To produce lentiviral particles, HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cells were cotransfected with the psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, cat. #11668027), in accordance with established protocols. Target cells KPC1199 were then transduced with 1 × 106 infectious lentiviral units in the presence of 6 mg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #H9268). Twenty-four hours after transduction, selection was initiated by adding 5 μg/mL puromycin (Gibco, cat. #A1113802) to the culture medium. Stable puromycin-resistant clones were enriched over a 5-day selection period under continuous puromycin pressure (5 μg/mL). These selected cells were subsequently expanded through passaging, and the efficiency of H2AFZ knockdown was confirmed by both qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis.
C57BL/6J mice were used to establish a liver metastasis model of PDAC. Each mouse received an intrasplenic injection of 2 × 106 KPC1199 PDAC cells—both stably expressing luciferase—suspended in 25 μL of PBS. Prior to injection, animals were anesthetized using isoflurane. Bioluminescent imaging was conducted on days 14 and 28 after injection to track hepatic tumor burden. Mice were first given an intraperitoneal dose of 150 mg D-luciferin (Promega; cat. # P1043) and then anesthetized again with isoflurane and imaged using the IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). At the study endpoint (day 28), deeply anesthetized mice were humanely sacrificed via cervical dislocation. Their livers were harvested, fixed, and processed for histopathologic analysis.

## Liver metastasis model construction and immunotherapy treatment
All male C57BL/6 background mice were acquired from East China Normal University. Seven-week-old mice were used in this study. Mice were anesthetized by inhalation anesthesia (2% isoflurane). Intrasplenic injections of KPC1199 cells were performed for the liver metastatic model, with each mouse receiving 1 × 106 cells. After the injection, drugs (anti–PD-1 and anti-LAG3) were administered intraperitoneally every 3 days at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Five mice per group were randomly selected for bioluminescence imaging under 2% isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. Three weeks after the injection of KPC1199 cells, the mice were sacrificed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde.
Animals were housed in an isolated ventilated cage barrier facility at Shanghai Jiao Tong University Laboratory Animal Center. Mice were maintained on a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle at 22°C to 26°C with sterile pellet food and water ad libitum. All animals received humane care according to the criteria outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” prepared by the National Academy of Sciences and published by the NIH. All manipulations were performed under approved protocol number 20141204 assigned by the Research Ethics Committee of East China Normal University.

## Identification of treatment-induced genes by bulk RNA-seq
Tumor tissues from immunotherapy-treated mice were subjected to bulk RNA-seq. The raw reads of tumor tissues from immunotherapy-treated mice were first processed using Trim Galore (RRID: SCR_011847; version 0.6.7; ref. ), and the low-quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using STAR (RRID: SCR_004463; version 2.7.10; ref. ). The read counts and Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) of each gene were calculated by featureCounts (RRID: SCR_012919). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the biological replicates. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were identified by the DESeq2 (RRID: SCR_015687) R package (Padj < 0.1, fold change >0 for DEGs_up and fold change <0 for DEGs_down; ref. ). Based on the hypergeometric test, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed to screen the significantly enriched terms.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses used in this study included the t test, Wilcoxon test, and Fisher’s exact test, among others, as described in the figure legends. The Kaplan–Meier method was applied for survival analysis.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (LY2024-064-B), and informed consent was obtained from each participant.

## Results

## Single-cell transcriptomes of paired PTs and liver metastases in PDAC
To elucidate the cellular and molecular programs underlying PDAC HM, we enrolled 28 patients who underwent synchronous resection of PTs and paired oligo-metastatic hepatic lesions (HM). scRNA-seq was applied to 12 matched fresh-frozen PT and HM samples from six patients with PDAC ( ; Supplementary Table S1). PT and HM samples from the remaining 22 patients were used to construct TMAs to validate key findings from the scRNA-seq data analysis. Additionally, TMAs constructed from PTs of 149 patients with PDAC with follow-up information, along with bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-PDAC cohort, were leveraged to assess the prognostic significance and metastatic potential of the identified cell populations or genes of interest.
 In total, 58,262 cells from 12 paired PT and HM samples were retained after stringent quality control. Upon data integration, unsupervised clustering, and marker-based annotation (Supplementary Table S2), 16 major cell types were identified, including ductal cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, B cells, hepatocytes, stellate cells, mast cells, plasma cells, neutrophils, myeloid cells [mono/macro/dendritic cell (DC)], fibroblasts, endothelial cells, proliferative cells, acinar cells, tuft cells, and endocrine cells ( and ). Acinar cells were exclusively detected in PTs, whereas hepatocytes were present only in HMs, confirming the reliability of our cell type annotation. Ductal cells, fibroblasts, stellate cells, and tuft cells were more abundant in PTs, whereas T and NK cells showed higher abundance in HMs (P < 0.05; and ). These results indicate that tumor cells from PTs and HMs reside in different microenvironments, which may result in distinct tumor–TME cross-talk.

## Cellular composition and functional analysis reveal increased tumor plasticity in metastatic PDAC
To interpret how malignant cells contribute to metastasis, we extracted 7,150 ductal cells for subsequent analysis. Five ductal subclusters (C0–C4) were identified through unsupervised clustering ( ). Ductal cells of different subclusters represented distinct expression profiles, biological functions, and cellular states ( and ; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). C3 exhibited a state resembling normal ductal cells based on the lowest CNVscore among all subclusters and its functions related to the normal pancreas, such as exocrine and pancreatic secretion ( and ; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). All subclusters except C3 were identified as malignant based on their increased CNV burden and functional annotations ( ). C0 was associated with cell junction assembly and the cell surface receptor signaling pathway. C1 displayed biological functions related to antigen processing and presentation and T-cell activation. C2 exhibited stronger oxidative phosphorylation, DNA repair activity, and the MYC signaling pathway. C4 showed functions related to the cytoskeleton and cell migration, but the number of cells in C4 was too low for a robust analysis.
 The PTs and HMs exhibited distinct cellular compositions. HMs contained more C2 and C0 subclusters, whereas PTs contained more C1, C3, and C4 subclusters. When analyzing the cellular compositions of ductal subclusters within each patient, we observed that C2 was more prevalent in HMs in five out of six patients ( ). Additionally, we employed a public dataset containing three additional pairs of PT and HM samples and observed the same trend of increased C2 abundance in HMs in two out of three patients (Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B). Integrating our private dataset with this public dataset, seven out of nine metastatic patients exhibited a trend of increased C2 tumor cells in HMs (Supplementary Fig. S1B). In addition, C2 formed a separate developmental branch compared with other subclusters ( ). We then evaluated the clinical relevance of each subcluster in the TCGA-PDAC cohort. Notably, C2 was the only subcluster that was negatively associated with both overall survival (OS) and DFS (P < 0.01; ). Furthermore, when looking into nonmetastatic tumors that later developed HMs during follow-up, the PTs at high risk of HM (highHM, DFS ≤365 days) exhibited significantly higher C2 signature scores than those at low risk of HM (DFS >365 days; P = 0.026; ).
Partial epithelial–mesenchymal transition (pEMT), rather than complete EMT, has been reported to play a more vital role in tumor progression and metastasis. A recent study employed lineage tracing to track and classify the EMT process into six states, including complete epithelial, hybrid EMT (H1∼H4), and complete mesenchymal states ( ). We estimated the EMT state scores across tumor cells and found that C2 showed higher hybrid EMT scores than other tumor subclusters ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1C). Given that the six EMT states were identified in mouse models, we performed a parallel analysis using gene sets derived from a human pan-cancer study ( ). Similar to the above results, C2 exhibited higher pEMT scores compared with other subclusters while having lower mesenchymal scores ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1D). pEMT or hybrid EMT manifests a transitional state of higher cell plasticity. To quantify this property, we used CytoTRACE2 (bioRxiv 2024.03.19.585637) to calculate a cellular potency score, in which higher values indicate a less differentiated, more plastic state. Our analysis confirmed that C2 possessed a significantly higher potency score (P < 0.001; and ). Interestingly, this plastic state seems to be associated with metastatic progression. When comparing tumor cells from different sites, those in HMs exhibited markedly higher potency scores than cells in PTs (P < 0.001; ).
In summary, these results collectively indicate that C2 is associated with higher HM risk and worse prognosis. C2 tumor cells reprogram the expression profiles and transition into a plastic state to facilitate HM.

## Identification of H2AFZ as a major driver of malignant cell proliferation and HM
To robustly identify the potential driver genes of the C2 subpopulation, we intersected the signature genes of C2, HM, and unipotent tumor cells with higher plasticity ( ; Supplementary Table S5). This analysis yielded six common genes, among which H2AFZ and PFN1 showed significant prognostic value for both OS and DFS ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1E). Given that the role of PFN1 in tumorigenesis is contentious and highly context-specific ( , ), we focused our subsequent investigation on H2AFZ, a histone variant consistently implicated in promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis in multiple cancers ( , ). However, the role of H2AFZ in PDAC metastasis remains largely unexplored.
 The strong association between H2AFZ expression and the C2 subpopulation was further confirmed in the independent TCGA-PDAC cohort (R = 0.68; P < 2.2e−16; ). To directly link H2AFZ to the metastatic phenotype, we performed IHC on paired primary and metastatic tumors from an additional 22 patients. This confirmed that H2AFZ protein levels were significantly elevated in metastatic lesions ( ). Moreover, analysis of 149 PDAC PTs showed markedly higher H2AFZ levels in the highM group than in the lowM group ( ), further supporting its association with metastatic potential. Importantly, elevated H2AFZ expression was again found to correlate with poor prognosis in our private dataset, showing negative associations with both OS and DFS ( ).
Given H2AFZ’s reported function in cell proliferation ( , ), we next examined its relationship with proliferative activity in PDAC. Correlation analysis in the TCGA-PDAC cohort showed that H2AFZ expression positively correlated with the cell-cycle score ( ). In our metastatic PDAC samples, HM samples with elevated H2AFZ expression also exhibited increased MKI67 levels, a well-known proliferation marker ( ). Functional experiments demonstrated that the knockdown of H2AFZ in two human PDAC cell lines, Patu8988 and SW1990, significantly reduced colony formation and impaired the proliferative capacity of PDAC tumor cells ( and ).
Beyond proliferation, we explored H2AFZ’s role in tumor cell migration, a critical step in metastatic dissemination. The knockdown of H2AFZ substantially suppressed tumor migratory ability in the transwell migration assay ( ). We also performed in vivo experiments to evaluate the impact of H2AFZ on liver metastasis using mouse metastatic models. The results showed that H2AFZ knockdown significantly repressed tumor growth in the liver compared with the control group ( ). Taken together, these findings indicate that H2AFZ plays a vital role during HM, potentially by regulating both tumor cell proliferation and migratory capacity.

## PDAC metastasis remodels an immunosuppressive hepatic microenvironment
To understand the immune microenvironment of HMs, we collected public scRNA-seq data for healthy liver (HL) samples ( ) and compared the myeloid and T-cell compositions between HMs and HLs (Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2F). Clustering analysis of 3,894 myeloid cells identified eight myeloid subclusters: Mye0 CD16+ monocytes, Mye1 CD14+ monocytes, Mye2 SPP1+ macrophages, Mye3 C1QC+ macrophages, Mye4 CLEC9A+ conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s), Mye5 CLEC10A+ cDC2s, Mye6 LAMP3+ cDCs, and Mye7 LILRA4+ plasmacytoid DCs ( and ). SPP1+ and C1QC+ macrophages exhibited higher proportions in HMs than HLs ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2C). These two myeloid subclusters have been associated with tumor progression in multiple malignancies ( ). We scored all myeloid cells with signature genes of previously reported M1 and M2 macrophages ( ), which represent the pro- and anti-inflammatory states, respectively (Supplementary Table S6; refs. , ). M1 scores were highest in CD14+ monocytes and LAMP3+ cDCs, whereas M2 scores were highest in C1QC+ macrophages and SPP1+ macrophages ( ). Pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2 revealed that monocytes differentiated along two distinct branches, one leading to SPP1+ macrophages and the other to C1QC+ macrophages ( ). These results suggest that myeloid cells exhibited a shift from proinflammatory toward anti-inflammatory states, raising the possibility that macrophages in HMs may suppress immune activity and facilitate tumor growth although further investigation is needed.
 T cells also constitute a critical part of the TME in metastatic PDAC. To characterize them, we conducted clustering analysis on 26,858 T cells in metastatic PDAC. Three CD4+ T-cell subclusters (T6–T8), five CD8+ T-cell subclusters (T0–T4), and one CD4−CD8− NK-like T (NKT) cell subcluster (T5) were identified ( and ). Among them, T6 and T7 were recognized as naïve T cells due to the low expression of genes related to Th cell functions and relatively high expression of naïve T cell–related genes such as LEF1 and CCR7 ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2D). T8 was identified as regulatory T cells (Treg) based on the expression of FOXP3, CTLA4, and other relevant markers. T0 and T1 were identified as CD8+ cytotoxic effector T cells, with CCL4 highly expressed in T0 and AOAH highly expressed in T1. T2 exhibited higher expression of genes related to the IFN signaling pathway, suggesting a response to IFNs. T3 represented T cells in an exhausted state, characterized by the expression of PDCD1 and HAVCR2. T4 was classified as mucosal-associated invariant T cells, an innate-like T-cell subset that is overabundant in the liver ( ). T5 was identified as NKT cells due to high expression of both T-cell and NK cell markers. HMs exhibited more Treg and Tex compared with HLs, suggesting a more immunosuppressive state in hepatic metastatic lesions than in uncolonized livers ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2F).
Next, we explored the cell–cell communications between tumor cells and immune cells in the HMs using CellChat ( ). We observed the strongest MDK–SDC2 interaction between HM-promoting C2 tumor cells and suppressive SPP1+ macrophages compared with other tumor subclusters ( ). Notably, the MDK–SDC2 interaction has previously been associated with M2 macrophages ( ), which are known to support an immunosuppressive TME. The interaction between C2 tumor cells and exhausted T cells (Tex; T3) was probably mediated by LAMA3–(ITGA1 + ITGB1) ( ). In turn, SPP1+ macrophages interacted with C2 tumor cells through multiple ligand–receptor pairs, including LGALS9–CD44, RETN–CAP1, and SPP1–CD44, several of which have been implicated in promoting tumor cell stemness, invasion, and metastasis, thereby reinforcing the protumoral microenvironment in HMs ( ; refs. , ). Together, these findings highlight an immune-suppressive TME in HMs relative to unaffected livers, characterized by increased abundances of M2-like SPP1+ macrophages, C1QC+ macrophages, Tregs, and Tex. We also identified potential interactions that may mediate this suppressive microenvironment.

## Effectiveness of anti–PD-1 combined with anti-LAG3 for PDAC HMs
Current treatment strategies have shown considerably limited efficacy in PDAC ( ). The above analysis revealed that pancreatic tumor cells are capable of remodeling the liver TME, leading to an immunosuppressive milieu that, in turn, promotes tumor cell growth. Targeting this immunosuppressive TME may serve as an effective treatment strategy for PDAC. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of immunosuppressive and immune-activating genes in T cells derived from PTs and HMs ( ). Notably, Tex (T3) in HMs exhibited elevated expression of immune checkpoint genes PDCD1 and LAG3 ( ). Elevated expression of PD-1 and LAG3 in HMs compared with PTs was also confirmed via IHC in another 22 patients with PDAC ( and ). These results suggest a more pronounced state of T-cell exhaustion in metastatic liver lesions than in PTs.
 Beyond the well-known suppressive receptor PDCD1, LAG3 attracted our attention as a novel immune checkpoint molecule expressed on T cells with ligands FGL1 and CLEC4G that can suppress T-cell responses ( ). Interestingly, FGL1 is produced by hepatocytes especially when injured, and CLEC4G is highly expressed by liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSEC), raising the possibility that LAG3 inhibition could be a relevant therapeutic strategy for patients with HM. Our scRNA-seq data confirmed this exclusive pattern of expression in hepatocytes and LSECs in HMs (Supplementary Fig. S3A).
Given the concurrent upregulation of both PDCD1 and LAG3, we hypothesized that dual blockade of these inhibitory receptors might enhance therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, we administered anti–PD-1 alone (aPD-1), anti-LAG3 alone (aLAG3), or a combination of anti–PD-1 and anti-LAG3 (aPD-1 + aLAG3) to metastatic mouse models to evaluate their therapeutic effects on liver metastases ( ). Compared with the untreated control, aPD-1 treatment delayed the growth of metastatic tumors, whereas aLAG3 mildly repressed the growth of metastatic tumors. Strikingly, the combination therapy significantly outperformed either monotherapy, demonstrating enhanced antitumor efficacy ( and ).
To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this improved therapeutic response, we performed bulk RNA-seq on tumor tissues harvested from metastatic mouse models following treatment. PCA revealed a clear transcriptional separation between the treated and control groups, underscoring the substantial impact of immunotherapy on tumor gene expression ( ). Compared with the control group, the aPD-1 + aLAG3 group exhibited upregulation of 460 genes and downregulation of 517 genes. Of these, only 94 upregulated and 181 downregulated genes overlapped with those affected in the aPD-1 monotherapy group, suggesting that the combination therapy induces broader and distinct transcriptional reprogramming ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3B). We further conducted GO enrichment analysis to explore the functional changes at the pathway level. The aPD-1 group was associated with immune-related pathways, such as leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, regulation of T-cell activation, and cell chemotaxis, consistent with the effect of aPD-1 therapy in reinvigorating antitumor immunity ( ; Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). Compared with aPD-1 alone, the combination treatment further enhanced pathways associated with chemokine responses and myeloid cell migration ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S3C). We speculate that aLAG3 therapy may relieve the immune suppression caused by the immune-privileged phenotype of the liver, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy when combined with aPD-1 therapy. Moreover, the dual blockade decreased the activity of pathways involved in epithelial cell morphogenesis and the negative regulation of lymphocyte differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S3D; Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). In summary, we observed T-cell state heterogeneity within and across organs and primarily explored the possibility of treating PDAC using a novel combination strategy of aPD-1 and aLAG3.

## Discussion
Metastasis is a highly dynamic, multistep biological process during which tumor cells disseminate from the primary site, colonize distant organs, and adapt to new microenvironments. In this study, we applied scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, IHC, and experimental validation to patients with PDAC and murine models, revealing a malignant cellular subpopulation and an immunosuppressive microenvironment closely associated with PDAC liver metastasis ( ). Based on the characteristics of the metastatic microenvironment, we proposed a novel combination strategy targeting both PD-1 and LAG3, which significantly inhibited tumor growth in mouse models of metastasis. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of malignant cells and the tumor microenvironment in metastatic PDAC and propose a new exploratory combination therapy approach that warrants further investigation.
 Tumor cells, as the “seeds” of metastasis, are undoubtedly the most critical and central cell population of interest in studies of metastatic progression. However, focusing on these cells is particularly challenging, as analyses based solely on PTs or unpaired samples cannot fully capture the postmetastatic state and are prone to substantial noise arising from interpatient heterogeneity. Previous studies by Lee and colleagues and Lin and colleagues applied scRNA-seq to unpaired PTs and HMs, offering insights into TME remodeling ( , ). However, the tumor cells in these studies are not directly comparable, as they originate from different patients, and the heterogeneity of tumor cells is known to be substantial. Zhang and colleagues ( ) addressed this limitation by performing scRNA-seq on paired PT and HM samples, identifying subpopulations associated with metastatic TME formation. Nevertheless, their study was constrained by only three pairs of samples, making the analysis highly sensitive—any difference in a single patient could substantially affect the conclusions. To overcome these limitations, we expanded our cohort of paired samples and incorporated these publicly available datasets to enable more robust intrapatient comparisons. Comparative analysis of paired PTs and HMs within individual patients allows better control of interpatient variability and facilitates the identification of more conserved metastatic signals. Although our dataset is still limited in size due to the difficulty of obtaining simultaneously resected paired samples, the results have been validated from multiple perspectives. Larger cohorts and more advanced technologies, such as single-cell resolution spatial omics, will be necessary to further elucidate the roles of tumor cells and uncover more subtle cues in PDAC liver metastasis.
Previous studies have demonstrated that the metastatic potential of different tumor cells differs, with some tumor cell lines exhibiting inherently higher metastatic potential ( , ). In this study, we identified a tumor subpopulation, C2, characterized by a less differentiated, highly proliferative phenotype and associated with enhanced metastatic potential. This phenotype seems to be largely driven by H2AFZ, a histone variant previously implicated in tumor growth and progression across multiple malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma and canine melanoma ( , ). In hepatocellular carcinoma, Dong and colleagues ( ) revealed that H2AFZ overexpression is regulated by TP53 mutation, which is one of the predominant mutations in PDAC. This prompted us to explore the relationship between H2AFZ and the most common genomic alterations in PDAC. Using genomic and transcriptomic data from the TCGA_PDAC cohort, we observed an association between H2AFZ expression and KRAS and TP53 expression, whereas SMAD4 and CDKN2A did not affect H2AFZ expression (Supplementary Fig. S4A). In addition, H2AFZ seemed to be associated with KRAS mutant dosage, which is a risk factor involved in PDAC progression ( ). Patients with gain/amplification and higher variant allele frequency at the KRAS locus exhibited higher H2AFZ expression (Supplementary Fig. S4B and S4C). However, the mechanisms by which these genomic alterations regulate H2AFZ expression remain to be elucidated and warrant further investigation.
The Tex in immunosuppressive HMs exhibited elevated expression of both PDCD1 and LAG3. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting these two genes led to enhanced effects in our study. Similar to our results, this combination strategy has also shown promising results in advanced melanoma ( ). The comparison of pre- and posttreatment transcriptomes showed that the upregulated expression of immune-related genes may contribute to the augmented effects. Further mechanistic investigations and clinical trials are necessary and valuable to determine whether this therapy is effective and safe for treating metastatic PDAC in humans.
Although our study provides new insights into metastatic PDAC, several points should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, although we incorporated publicly available datasets and performed multifaceted validation to strengthen the robustness of our findings, the number of patients included in our cohort remains relatively limited. Future studies involving larger, independent cohorts and more advanced spatial and multiomics technologies will be necessary to further substantiate and extend our conclusions. Second, our analyses were primarily based on patients with oligo-HMs. The biological features of these cases may differ from those of patients with multiple liver metastatic lesions or multiorgan metastases; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing our findings to broader metastatic settings. Third, in the final part of this study, we evaluated the efficacy of combined immunotherapy in mouse models and observed encouraging therapeutic responses. Nevertheless, given the intrinsic physiologic and immunologic differences between murine models and humans, the safety and efficacy of such combination strategies require more systematic and rigorous evaluation in future preclinical and clinical studies.
In summary, our work collectively advances the understanding of PDAC liver metastasis at the single-cell level and offers new insights that may improve treatment responses for patients with PDAC.
