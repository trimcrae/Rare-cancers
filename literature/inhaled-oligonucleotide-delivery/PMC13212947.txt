## Abstract
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive tumor. EIF3A has been identified as a reader of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, influencing the translation levels of various molecules and functioning as an oncogene. Our study presents novel findings demonstrating that the m6A of EIF3A, regulated by METTL3, influences the stability of EIF3A mRNA and its protein expression, ultimately impacting the progression of PDAC. Notably, through high-throughput drug screening targeting m6A of EIF3A, we identified filgotinib—a commercially rheumatism drug, Janus kinase (JAK) -STAT3 inhibitor—as an effective inhibitor of PDAC proliferation. Filgotinib increased DNA damage in PDAC cells via the ERG-TBP-METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis. In contrast, other JAK-STAT3 inhibitors such as ruxolitinib and knockdown of STAT3 does not replicate this effect, indicating that the anti-tumor effect of filgotinib is independent of JAK-STAT3 signaling. This study offers innovative insights and potential therapeutic strategies for the treatment of PDAC through EIF3A m6A.

## Introduction
PDAC is a highly aggressive solid tumor, currently ranked as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States . Surgical R0 resection remains the sole curative intervention for individuals diagnosed with PDAC . For patients with advanced PDAC, chemotherapy is the main treatment method. The AG regimen (gemcitabine combined with albumin-bound paclitaxel) and FOLFIRINOX (pentafluorouracil, oxaliplatin, folinic acid, and irinotecan) are the current first-line chemotherapy regimen for patients with advanced PDAC, primarily targeting the DNA damage mechanism . However, the current chemotherapy regimens still have the characteristic of being prone to drug resistance. This underscores the pressing necessity for the development and identification of novel therapeutic agents specifically targeting PDAC.
The occurrence and progression of tumors are intricately linked to the dysregulation of protein translation processes . A critical factor among the translation process is the RNA-binding protein and translation factor, EIF3, which facilitates the unwinding of structured 5’ UTRs to enhance the translation initiation of specific mRNAs in cancer . EIF3A, the largest subunit of the EIF3 complex, plays a crucial role in maintaining the structural integrity of the complex and regulating the process of protein translation . Its function is particularly significant in the pathogenesis and progression of tumors, presenting a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of pancreatic cancer . In the gastric cancer cells, a reduction in EIF3A levels activates ATM, elevates γ-H2AX expression, and leads to an increase in DNA damage . Gemcitabine and 5-Fluorouracil, both of which are first-line chemotherapeutic agents for pancreatic cancer, exert their therapeutic effects by inducing DNA damage to inhibit cancer progression. The involvement of EIF3A in DNA damage response indicates its potential as a pivotal target gene for the treatment of pancreatic cancer.
M6A modification is among the most widespread RNA modifications observed in both mRNA and non-coding RNA within eukaryotic organisms . This modification plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of pancreatic cancer. For instance, the enzyme METTL3 can influence the proliferation and stemness of pancreatic cancer cells by modifying the m6A status of ID2 mRNA . Additionally, FTO modulates the m6A levels of NEDD4, thereby improving resistance to gemcitabine in pancreatic cancer . This modification plays a pivotal role in regulating a variety of cellular processes, such as RNA splicing, translation, stability, and nuclear localization . The methylation status of m6A is distinguished by its dynamic and reversible characteristics, facilitated by the enzymatic activities of methyltransferases and demethylases, including METTL3, METTL14, FTO, and ALKBH5, etc . EIF3A is often regarded as a ‘reader’ protein of m6A methylation modification , . Research has demonstrated that YTHDF1 facilitates the recruitment of EIF3A to the m6A site on YAP mRNA, consequently influencing its translational efficiency and modulating the progression of lung cancer . However, it remains unreported whether EIF3A itself can undergo m6A modification.
Filgotinib is both safe and effective in the treatment of ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn’s disease, and psoriasis, as demonstrated by the findings of the TORTUGA trial . It reduces inflammation by targeting the JAK1-STAT3 pathway, slowing disease progression in inflammatory conditions . Beyond its applications in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, the potential role of filgotinib in oncology has been investigated. Research indicates that the downregulation of circPTTG1IP facilitates the progression of liver cancer via the miR-16-5p/RNF125/JAK1 axis . Filgotinib has shown promise in modulating this pathway, potentially reversing the oncogenic effects associated with circPTTG1IP downregulation. In breast cancer, NFIC1 has been demonstrated to inhibit the invasive migration of cancer cells. Conversely, filgotinib has been found to counteract the effects of NFIC1, thereby promoting the invasive migration of breast cancer cells . This suggests that filgotinib exhibits diverse functional roles across different cancer types, acting both to inhibit tumor development and, paradoxically, to exert a protective effect against tumors. Furthermore, filgotinib has been reported to inhibit the proliferation and viability of peripheral blood cells through a mechanism involving DNA damage . The role of filgotinib in PDAC, however, remains unclear. This study aims to investigate and confirm the inhibitory effects of filgotinib on PDAC cells and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.
In this study, we observed a significant reduction in the m6A modification level of EIF3A in PDAC. Conversely, EIF3A protein expression was markedly elevated, and this upregulation correlated with an unfavorable prognosis. Our findings indicate that METTL3 regulates the mRNA stability of EIF3A through m6A methylation, thereby influencing EIF3A expression and subsequently affecting PDAC proliferation via mechanisms involving DNA damage. Furthermore, we discovered that the JAK inhibitor filgotinib specifically targets the m6A modification of EIF3A to exert an anti-cancer effect. This effect was mediated via ERG, a transcription factor that targets TBP, the upstream molecule of METTL3, rather than through the JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway. Our results suggest that filgotinib induces DNA damage in PDAC through the ERG-TBP-METTL3-m6A-EIF3A pathway, thereby exerting its anti-cancer effects.

## Results

## Overexpression of EIF3A is correlated with an unfavorable prognosis in pancreatic cancer
Tumor development is complex and influenced by protein translation, affecting cell proliferation, cancer progression, and treatment resistance. Studies show that translation initiation factors are often upregulated or malfunctioning in cancer, causing abnormal mRNA translation that drives malignancy . Targeting these factors can decrease protein synthesis in cancer cells, inhibiting tumor growth and spread. EIF3, a critical regulator of translation, is integral to protein synthesis, with 13 of its subunits exhibiting elevated expression levels across numerous cancer types , . To identify subunits potentially involved in the pathogenesis of PDAC, we employed the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database to investigate the correlation between the expression of all subunits of EIF3 and the survival prognosis of pancreatic cancer (Figure ). We identified two subunits, EIF3A and EIF3J, as being significantly associated with prognosis, with hazard ratios of 1.25 and 1.28, respectively (Fig. ). EIF3A is the largest and central subunit of EIF3 , and serves as the focal target for our subsequent research endeavors. Consistent with these findings, data from the GEPIA2 database revealed that elevated EIF3A expression is associated with poorer survival outcomes in patients with PDAC (Fig. ). Further Immunohistochemical analysis of pathological specimens from our institution confirmed that EIF3A is significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. ). Analysis of the TCGA and GTEx database showed significantly increased EIF3A mRNA expression in PDAC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. ). EIF3A protein expression was observed to be upregulated in several PDAC cell lines compared to normal pancreatic epithelial cells, HPDE6C7 and HPNE (Fig. ). EIF3A knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation of PDAC cells (Fig. , Figure ). Collectively, these findings suggest that EIF3A may act as an oncogenic driver in PDAC.
 

## METTL3-dependent RNA m6A modification regulates the expression level of EIF3A
EIF3A, recognized as an m6A reader, is typically recruited by m6A modifications to regulate mRNA translation; however, research specifically focusing on m6A modifications of EIF3A remains limited. We conducted m6A sequencing on pancreatic tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from nine randomly selected patients. An unanticipated m6A modification of EIF3A was identified in these patients. Notably, these modifications are significantly reduced in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. ), despite the elevated EIF3A protein expression observed in PDAC. We propose that the host may attempt to suppress EIF3A expression through m6A modifications as a compensatory mechanism, but this is insufficient to offset its upregulation in cancer. To further explore the impact of m6A modification on EIF3A, we initially verified the presence of m6A modification at the cellular level, revealing a significant abundance of this modification on the mRNA of EIF3A in MiaPaCa-2 cells (Fig. ). METTL3 is a m6A methyltransferase that plays a pivotal role in m6A modification. The expression of METTL3 demonstrates a positive correlation with EIF3A in pancreatic cancer (Fig. ), and elevated levels of METTL3 expression are linked to a poor prognosis in this disease (Figure ). Upon knockdown of METTL3, there was a significant reduction in the m6A modification levels of EIF3A, which was accompanied by a decrease in EIF3A mRNA levels (Fig. , Figure ). Correspondingly, the level of EIF3A protein also decreased following the knockdown of METTL3 (Fig. ). Considering that m6A modifications commonly influence RNA stability, we assessed the impact of METTL3 on the stability of EIF3A mRNA through actinomycin D chase assays. After METTL3 knockdown, the stability of EIF3A mRNA was found to be reduced. (Fig. ). Meyer et al.'s research found that EIF3A’s involvement in m6A-dependent translation in the 5’ UTR . In order to know whether METTL3 modulation affects EIF3A binding to its own mRNA. We performed the RIP experiment, and the results showed that EIF3A does indeed bind to its own mRNA, but after METTL3 knockdown, the efficiency of EIF3A binding to mRNA did not change significantly (Figure ). This further indicates that it is METTL3 that affects the stability of EIF3A mRNA through m6A modification, rather than the binding of EIF3A to its own mRNA, which in turn leads to a decrease in EIF3A expression. To further elucidate the role of m6A modifications in the regulation of EIF3A, we utilized the SRAMP online tool to predict potential m6A sites within the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of EIF3A. We subsequently selected the two sites with the highest predictive scores to construct dual luciferase reporter plasmids, incorporating either the wild-type EIF3A 3’UTR (EIF3A-3’UTR-WT) or a mutant version (EIF3A-3’UTR-Mut) (Fig. ). A dual luciferase reporter assay revealed that the luciferase activity of EIF3A-3’UTR-WT decreased by nearly 45% following METTL3 knockdown, whereas the activity of EIF3A-3’UTR-Mut decreased by approximately 24% (Fig. ). Due to the presence of additional m6A sites on EIF3A mRNA, knockdown of METTL3 can still lead to changes in the luciferase activity of the EIF3A-3’UTR-Mut. However, since we specifically mutated the two primary m6A sites, the observed change in luciferase activity for EIF3A-3’UTR-Mut was significantly less pronounced compared to that of EIF3A-3’UTR-WT (Fig. ). These results indicate that m6A modification exists in EIF3A in PDAC, and that METTL3 regulates the stability of EIF3A mRNA through m6A modification, thereby influencing the expression of EIF3A protein.
 

## EIF3A and METTL3 KO enhances DNA damage and reduces proliferation in PDAC cell lines
Similar to EIF3A, METTL3 is correlated with a poor prognosis in PDAC (Figure ). Knockdown of METTL3 can inhibit the proliferation of PDAC cells (Fig. ). To further explore the function of EIF3A in PDAC, we conducted RNA-Seq after EIF3A knockdown in MiaPaCa-2 cells and performed KEGG enrichment analysis on the results. The downstream pathways enriched included TGF-β, cGMP-PKG, and other downstream molecular pathways, all of which can affect the proliferation of PDAC cells. Literature reports indicate that these pathways can also affect DNA damage – (Figure ). GSEA enrichment analysis focused on DNA damage-related signaling pathways, including DNA damage stress-induced senescence and the P53 signaling pathway (Fig. , Figure ). Intriguingly, after EIF3A knockdown, we observed a significant increase in the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX, indicating enhanced DNA damage in PDAC cells (Fig. ). Similarly, after METTL3 knockdown, we noted an enhancement of DNA damage effects in PDAC cells (Fig. ). Taken together with our previous findings, these results indicate that inhibiting METTL3 can reduce EIF3A expression by m6A modification, thereby enhancing the DNA damage effects and inhibiting PDAC proliferation.
 

## Filgotinib inhibits PDAC cells in vitro and in vivo
Studies have indicated that mimosine, a natural herbal derivative, and NCE22, a small molecule inhibitor, are effective in suppressing EIF3A expression , . However, neither of these two drugs has been clinically verified for safety and efficacy, and their specific mechanisms have not been studied yet. To provide patients with more options, targeting EIF3A m6A modification represents a promising therapeutic strategy. We conducted a high-throughput drug screen and identified 7 compounds from the L1600-Kinase Inhibitor Library with cytotoxic effects against Bxpc-3 cells. Among them, filgotinib, a clinically approved anti-inflammatory drug, significantly reduced EIF3A m6A modification levels (Fig. ). It also regulated the mRNA and protein levels of EIF3A (Fig. ). We then determined the 24-hour IC50 of filgotinib in PDAC cells and the normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line HPDE6C7 using a CCK-8 assay. The IC50 values were 55.33 µM for MiaPaCa-2, 22.77 µM for Bxpc-3, and 183.5 µM for HPDE6C7 cells, respectively (Fig. , Figure ). These results indicate that filgotinib exhibits significantly stronger cytotoxicity toward tumor cells than normal cells. Subsequently, concentrations of 50 µM and 20 µM were selected in MiaPaCa-2 and Bxpc-3 cell lines, respectively, for 24 h treatments in all subsequent filgotinib-related drug experiments. To assess the efficacy of filgotinib on PDAC, we established a subcutaneous tumor transplantation model in mice using Luc-MiaPaCa-2 cells. The mice were regularly injected intraperitoneally with filgotinib, and the drug’s effectiveness was monitored (Fig. ). The real-time fluorescence imaging of subcutaneous tumors in mice, real-time curves of tumor volume in mice, and subsequent analysis of excised tumor specimens, demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in tumor size in the drug-administered group compared to the control group (Fig. ). Furthermore, after treatment with filgotinib, the tumor mass of the mice was significantly reduced, and the tumor/body weight ratio also decreased significantly. The results indicated that this drug could effectively inhibit tumor growth in the body, and its anti-tumor effect was not caused by the systemic weight loss induced by the drug, suggesting that this drug has good specific anti-tumor activity (Fig. , Figure ). And the Ki-67 immunohistochemical results of the mouse tumors indicated that the treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation ability of the tumors (Figure ). The above results indicate that filgotinib can significantly inhibit the proliferation of PDAC in vitro and in vivo.
 

## Filgotinib enhances DNA damage and inhibits METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis in PDAC
To investigate the molecular alterations induced by filgotinib in PDAC, RNA-seq was performed after filgotinib treatment of MiaPaCa-2 cells in vitro. The resulting volcano plot showed notable changes in the expression of many molecules in PDAC cells after filgotinib treatment. Notably, there was a substantial decrease in the levels of EIF3A and its upstream m6A methyltransferase, METTL3 (Fig. ). Similar to EIF3A, upon filgotinib treatment, we observed a marked decline in both the mRNA and protein of METTL3 (Fig. ). However, overexpression of METTL3 can reverse the down-regulation effect of filgotinib on EIF3A (Fig. ). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis of the sequencing data revealed that filgotinib treatment elicited anti-inflammatory effects and induced modifications in several immune and inflammatory pathways, including those associated with Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection, Hepatitis B, and Chronic myeloid leukemia. Additionally, similar to the sequencing results after EIF3A knockdown, filgotinib also caused alterations in the TNF and MAPK pathways (Fig. ). GO enrichment analysis showed that filgotinib treatment led to changes in pathways such as DNA-binding transcription factor activity and cellular response to DNA damage stimulus (Fig. ). Subsequent GSEA revealed that filgotinib treatment enhanced the enrichment of DNA damage-related pathways, including the P53 and mismatch repair pathways (Fig. ). This suggests that filgotinib may exert its anti-cancer effects through the DNA damage pathway. Immunofluorescence staining corroborated these findings by showing a significant increase in γ-H2AX levels in PDAC cells following filgotinib treatment, thereby supporting our hypothesis that filgotinib inhibits PDAC proliferation through the DNA damage pathway (Fig. ). We also performed immunohistochemistry, which detected significantly elevated γ-H2AX expression in drug-treated mouse tumor tissues compared with the control group (Figure ). These findings further substantiate that filgotinib exerts its effects via the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis.
 

## The METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis affected filgotinib-induced DNA damage
To validate our hypothesis, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing EIF3A (Fig. ) and METTL3 and then treating the cells with filgotinib. We found that overexpressing EIF3A and METTL3 partially rescued the DNA damage effects induced by filgotinib (Fig. ). Furthermore, we employed camptothecin (CPT) to induce DNA damage to assess whether filgotinib influences the DNA damage repair process. Following 4 h of CPT treatment, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant increase in DNA damage levels compared to normal cells (Figure ). This effect was further enhanced when CPT was combined with filgotinib, resulting in greater DNA damage than with CPT treatment alone. Concurrently, we observed that the DNA repair marker protein RAD51 was significantly upregulated after CPT exposure (Fig. ). Following combined treatment with filgotinib and CPT, RAD51 levels decreased significantly compared to CPT treatment alone, indicating that filgotinib inhibits DNA damage repair and enhances DNA damage levels. This conclusion was further supported by immunofluorescence experiments (Fig. ). These findings suggest that filgotinib induces DNA damage and impairs DNA damage repair mechanisms through the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A pathway, thereby inhibiting PDAC proliferation.
 

## Filgotinib regulates the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis through the non-JAK-STAT3 pathway molecule TBP
Filgotinib is a JAK-STAT3 inhibitor approved for treating inflammatory diseases. In PDAC cells, we also observed that filgotinib could effectively inhibit p-STAT3 without a significant downregulation of STAT3 (Figure ). To determine if filgotinib regulates the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A pathway in PDAC relies on the JAK-STAT3 pathway, we used ruxolitinib, a distinct JAK inhibitor with comparable target affinity, as a control. We found that while ruxolitinib also effectively inhibited the p-STAT3 without a significant downregulation of STAT3 (Fig. , Figure ). Meanwhile, it did not significantly impact the protein expression of the METTL3 and EIF3A (Fig. ). Additionally, STAT3 knockdown did not result in significant changes in METTL3 and EIF3A protein levels (Fig. ). This suggests that filgotinib does not exert its effects via the classical pathway and likely targets another molecule. Since filgotinib was found to inhibit METTL3 mRNA levels, we subsequently initiated an investigation into the upstream regulators of METTL3 that modulate its mRNA expression. There are reports in the literature that TBP, ATF2, ETS1, and RELA can regulate the expression of METTL3 in various cancers – . To further screen out the upstream of METTL3, we analyzed their expression correlation with METTL3 in the pancreas through GEPIA2. Among them, TBP had the highest score, with an R value of 0.33, significantly higher than that of the other molecules (Figure ). Moreover, we observed a significant decrease in TBP levels in transcriptome sequencing after filgotinib treatment, mirroring the trend in METTL3. Post-treatment, both TBP mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced (Fig. , Figure ). Furthermore, TBP knockdown resulted in a marked reduction of both METTL3 and EIF3A mRNA and protein levels (Fig. , Figure ). After TBP knockdown, we also observed suppressed proliferation and increased DNA damage in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. ). These findings indicate that filgotinib can regulate the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis through TBP independently of the JAK-STAT3 pathway, thereby affecting PDAC proliferation.
 

## Filgotinib molecular docking simulation screened the targeted molecule ERG
To further elucidate the interaction between filgotinib and TBP, we conducted molecular docking simulations. The results obtained from the Glide SP analysis yielded an average binding score of −9.124 for the filgotinib-TBP complex. This score suggests that TBP is not directly recognized as a binding target for filgotinib (Table ). Consequently, we sought to identify a transcription factor that could potentially bind to filgotinib and exert an effect on TBP. Comparative analysis between known human transcription factors and filgotinib’s predicted targets identified ERG as a candidate upstream regulator, with the highest binding affinity (Fig. ). Filgotinib binds to ERG through two directed hydrogen bonds that anchor the ligand at both ends of the pocket: LYS355 hydrogen-bonds to the ligand carbonyl at the entrance, while ALA398 engages the distal nitrogen-bearing group in a clamp-like “retainer”. The ligand scaffold nestles into a narrow hydrophobic groove formed by MET305, PRO306, TRP351, TRP314, TYR371, TYR372, ALA368, LEU313, LEU399, ILE377, and ILE395, providing extensive hydrophobic and aromatic (π–π/π–alkyl) contacts that dominate affinity. Pocket-edge residues GLN312 and LYS375 contribute mild polar compensation and partial solvent shielding. No persistent salt bridges or metal coordination were observed, consistent with the 2D interaction map, which clearly shows the two terminal H-bonds and a dense intrapocket hydrophobic/aromatic network (Fig. , Figure ). The complex exhibits stable binding dynamics: protein Cα RMSD rises rapidly and then plateaus at ~2.97 ± 0.35 Å; the ligand RMSD relative to the protein stabilizes at ~6.00 ± 0.76 Å; and the ligand’s internal RMSD remains low and essentially invariant (~0.303 ± 0.056 Å), indicating a rigid internal conformation with only limited post-binding wobble (Fig. ).MM/GBSA binding free energies are stable over 0–100 ns, averaging −60.12 ± 6.18 kcal/mol with fluctuations of approximately −50.12 to −77.58 kcal/mol and a slight drift toward more favorable values (Fig. ). Decomposition highlights van der Waals interactions as the principal stabilizing factor (−48.23 ± 3.75 kcal/mol); electrostatics are stabilizing but more variable (−32.76 ± 16.00 kcal/mol); hydrogen bonding contributes modestly (−0.85 ± 0.50 kcal/mol); and polar solvation (GB) is the dominant destabilizer (+33.72 ± 12.57 kcal/mol). A multiple-regression model accounts for ~70% of the variance (R² ≈ 0.703), supporting a “van der Waals–driven, polar solvation–offset” mechanism. Trajectory-based residue–ligand contact analysis singles out TYR371 and LYS375 as the primary binding hotspots, showing the highest and most persistent contact frequencies across the trajectory (Fig. ). The ligand’s central aromatic ring forms a dominant cation–π interaction with LYS375 (~43% occupancy) and sustained π–π/π–alkyl contacts with TYR371 (~24%) and HIS402 (~18%), which together constitute the main interaction nodes that organize the interface. Supporting contacts from MET305, ILE377, and GLN397 reinforce this core; ALA398 maintains mid-trajectory lock-like contacts, while GLN400, PRO401, and HIS402 strengthen later along the pocket wall. Intermittent contacts involving TYR372, ASP374, GLN312, and TRP351 likely reflect conformational breathing or water-mediated events. Finally, alanine substitutions at the two high-frequency hotspots (TYR371→Ala and LYS375→Ala) were evaluated. Under identical simulation protocols, both mutant complexes underwent more pronounced conformational excursions, producing markedly larger fluctuations in the ligand-fit-on-protein RMSD relative to the wild type; moreover, the ligand’s own RMSD no longer remained stable. These results further corroborate TYR371 and LYS375 as primary binding determinants (Figure ). Further analysis using the JASPAR database predicted high-confidence ERG binding sites within the TBP promoter region (Fig. ). Additionally, ERG expression exhibited a positive correlation with TBP(R = 0.62), METTL3(R = 0.11) and EIF3A(R = 0.5) expression in PDAC and normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. ).
 

## ERG affected filgotinib-induced PDAC cells proliferation inhibition and DNA damage
To investigate the functional role of ERG in pancreatic cancer, we performed ERG knockdown in PDAC cells (Fig. ). ERG knockdown significantly suppressed PDAC cell proliferation (Fig. ). Consistent with expectations, ERG knockdown also enhanced DNA damage levels in BxPC-3 cells (Fig. ). To further validate the direct interaction between ERG and filgotinib, we generated a Mut ERG plasmid in which the predicted key binding residues TYR371 and LYS375 in WT ERG were mutated to alanine (Fig. ). Upon co-overexpression of WT ERG and Mut ERG (Fig. ), cells were treated with filgotinib to assess its effects on cell proliferation and DNA damage. The results showed that overexpression of WT ERG effectively attenuated filgotinib-induced cell proliferation inhibition and associated DNA damage. Compared with WT ERG, overexpression of Mut ERG could more effectively alleviate the inhibition of cell proliferation and the corresponding DNA damage caused by filgotinib (Fig. ). These findings further support that ERG is a downstream target of filgotinib and mediates its biological functions through direct binding.
 

## Discussion
PDAC is a highly malignant tumor characterized by late detection, which often precludes surgical intervention. Although existing therapies can delay disease progression to some extent, drug resistance remains a significant challenge, necessitating the development of new and effective therapeutic agents. In this study, we identified a novel regulatory pathway in PDAC, termed the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis. Based on the regulatory mechanism of m6A modification on EIF3A, we screened and identified filgotinib as a potential therapeutic agent. Filgotinib, a clinically approved JAK inhibitor commonly used as an anti-inflammatory drug for autoimmune diseases, exhibited unexpected non-JAK-STAT3 inhibitory effects in our study. Through bioinformatics analyses and experimental validation, we demonstrated that filgotinib exerts its antitumor effects by targeting the transcription factor ERG, which regulates TBP expression, thereby influencing the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis and ultimately enhancing the DNA damage effects and inhibiting PDAC proliferation. (Fig. ).
Translation regulation is critically involved in tumorigenesis, frequently linked to the aberrant activation of oncogenes and the evasion of immune surveillance by tumors , . This process is particularly significant in PDAC. According to the literature, in PDAC characterized by KRAS mutations, these mutations can activate EIF4A, thereby increasing the translational efficiency of certain oncogenes and facilitating tumor proliferation and metastasis . The EIF3 complex, a crucial regulatory protein assembly in the process of translation, is associated with the progression of various tumors and facilitates the development of breast cancer , prostate cancer , among others. EIF3A, a central component of the EIF3 complex, has been implicated in the advancement of various malignancies, including lung and colon cancers. While existing studies indicate that EIF3A inhibition can suppress PDAC cell proliferation , these findings are predominantly confined to proliferation phenotypes following isolated knockdown experiments. Building upon this foundation, our research further elucidates the association between EIF3A expression and the survival prognosis of patients with PDAC. Additionally, we demonstrate that EIF3A inhibition impedes PDAC proliferation by enhancing DNA damage effects.
M6A modification is a widespread RNA modification in mammals, known to regulate gene expression by modulating RNA stability or mRNA translation . In PDAC, m6A modifications play essential roles, as demonstrated by Yanfen Zheng et al., who found a global reduction of m6A levels in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues, with a negative association with patient prognosis . EIF3A, recognized as a reader of m6A modification, has been documented to enhance the translation efficiency of m6A-modified mRNA mediated by METTL16, thus facilitating the progression of liver cancer . However, recent studies suggesting that m6A modifications in the 5′UTR do not affect transcriptome-wide translation efficiency should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations . First, their conclusions are based solely on experiments involving single-base m6A modifications in the 5′UTR. In reality, multiple m6A sites may exist within this region, and cluster effects among these sites could cooperatively influence translation. Moreover, in the real-time initiation single-molecule assays conducted, although the translation initiation environment was partially reconstituted by including the EIF4 initiation complex, essential m6A reader proteins—such as EIF3A, members of the YTHDF family, or IGF2BP family—were not incorporated. It is well established that the functional outcomes of m6A modification depend critically on the involvement of such reader proteins. In our pioneering study, we have identified and validated, through m6A sequencing and comparative analyses of cancerous and adjacent tissues in PDAC, that EIF3A has m6A modification. This modification level is modulated by METTL3. Furthermore, METTL3 influences the mRNA stability of EIF3A via m6A modification, consequently impacting the protein expression of EIF3A.
Filgotinib, a small-molecule JAK inhibitor, was developed in 2014 and subsequently obtained marketing authorization in Japan and the European Union in 2020 . This approval primarily pertains to its use in the management of rheumatic inflammatory diseases and inflammatory bowel diseases. Clinical evaluations have demonstrated filgotinib’s efficacy in alleviating symptoms associated with rheumatic inflammation and inflammatory bowel disease. Furthermore, its safety profile has been corroborated through multiple phases of clinical trials – . A recent report indicates that filgotinib can inhibit both the onset and progression of liver cancer , primarily through the inhibition of the JAK pathway. Notably, filgotinib has also been documented to exert effects beyond JAK inhibition. Specifically, it has been shown to effectively suppress the transcription of HIV-1 splicing and inhibit the expression of associated oncogenic genes, thereby demonstrating its antiviral properties . These findings suggest that filgotinib may possess additional molecular targets beyond its role as a JAK inhibitor. In our study, we identified for the first time that filgotinib inhibits the proliferation of PDAC cells by modulating the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis, thereby inducing DNA damage. This finding highlights a novel mechanism through which filgotinib exerts its anti-proliferative effects on PDAC. Furthermore, upon investigating the downstream regulatory mechanisms of filgotinib and employing ruxolitinib as a control, we observed that the previously characterized pathways were not mediated by the JAK pathway. This suggests that filgotinib may act on alternative molecular targets.
Through a comprehensive literature review, it was identified that METTL3 is associated with several transcription factors—namely, RELA, ATF2, ETS1, and TBP—which facilitate the transcription of METTL3 in liver , gastric , and cervical cancers , , respectively. Consequently, we conducted a correlation analysis using GEPIA2, revealing that TBP exhibited the strongest correlation with METTL3 expression in PDAC. Intriguingly, we observed that TBP expression correlated positively with the METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis following filgotinib treatment. TBP knockdown reduced both METTL3 and EIF3A expression, and inhibits the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells and causes DNA damage, indicating that filgotinib indirectly modulates this pathway through TBP. However, molecular docking analysis did not predict TBP as a direct target of filgotinib. Instead, ERG, a transcription factor with the highest binding score, was identified. Subsequent bioinformatics analyses demonstrated that ERG positively correlated with TBP, METTL3, and EIF3A expression, and the JASPAR database predicted a stable binding site between ERG and TBP. Knockdown of ERG was found to suppress the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells and induce DNA damage. Furthermore, overexpression of either WT ERG or Mut ERG could partially reverse the filgotinib-induced suppression of cell proliferation and DNA damage, with Mut ERG exhibiting a more pronounced rescue effect. These findings establish ERG as a downstream molecular target of filgotinib.
In conclusion, we identified a novel regulatory pathway in PDAC, the ERG-TBP-METTL3-m6A-EIF3A axis, and demonstrated that filgotinib, a clinically available anti-inflammatory drug, could specifically inhibit this pathway, leading to DNA damage and suppression of PDAC proliferation. Our findings not only advance the mechanistic understanding of PDAC but also suggest filgotinib as a promising therapeutic agent. Future studies will further evaluate the clinical efficacy of filgotinib in PDAC patients to facilitate the development of innovative treatment strategies.

## Methods

## Samples collected from clinical sources
All the person-source samples and patient medical records involved in this article were obtained from Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital Affiliated to Zhejiang University. The research protocol received review and approval from the Research Ethics Committee at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, affiliated with the School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, No.2025-2329-01, 2023-496-01. This part of the experimental manipulation follows the guidelines of the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki and the associated ethical standards.

## Reagents and antibodies
The reagents utilized in this study are detailed as follows, presented in the format of name (supplier, country, catalogue number, working concentration): Filgotinib (MCE, USA, HY-18300, 50/20 μM), Ruxolitinib (MCE, USA, INCB18424, 100/50 μM), Actinomycin D (Sigma, USA, A9415, 5 μg/ml), and Doxycycline (DOX, Sangon Biotech, China, A603456, 1 μg/ml). The antibodies employed in this study are enumerated in Supplementary Table .

## Cell culture
Human PDAC cell lines were procured from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, located in Shanghai, China. The ASPC-1 and BxPC-3 cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (MeilunBio, Dalian, China) with the addition of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, New York, USA). Meanwhile, the PANC-1, HPNE, MiaPACA-2, and HPDE6-C7 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, MeilunBio, Dalian, China), also supplemented with 10% FBS.

## Plasmids and transfection
The plasmids pGL-SV40-EIF3A-3’UTR, encompassing both wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) constructs, were developed and characterized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The plasmid pcDNA3.1-METTL3, WT pcDNA3.1-ERG-3XFLAG and Mut pcDNA3.1-ERG-3XFLAG were synthesized in-house, while the plasmid pcDNA3.1-EIF3A was also constructed by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Transfection of the plasmids into cells was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA, L3000015), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Lentiviral production and creation of stable overexpression cell lines and RNA silencing
The construction and packaging of lentiviral vectors for METTL3SH and EIF3ASH were conducted internally. Lentiviruses were generated, and lentivirus infection was performed as described previously . siRNAs targeting EIF3A, METTL3, TBP, ERG, and STAT3, along with a negative control, were procured from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Transfection of siRNA was carried out utilizing the riboFECT CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio, China, C10511- 05), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription (RT-qPCR) assay
Total RNA was extracted from the cells utilizing the Ultrapure RNA Kit (Cwbio, China, CW0581) and subsequently quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with genomic DNA removal, employing the Hifair® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeason, China, 11121ES60). Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was conducted using the Hieff UNICON® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeason, China, 11200ES03) on an ABI Q6 real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The primer sequences utilized in this study are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from cells post-treatment using RIPA buffer (Fdbio, China, FD009) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China, P1050). The extracted proteins were subsequently resolved via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h and then incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following this, the membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

## RNA-seq, m6A-RNA sequencing, and Bioinformatic analysis
RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), m6A-RNA sequencing, and the associated bioinformatics analyses were conducted by LC-Bio, located in Hangzhou, China. The clinical survival analysis of pertinent genes was performed utilizing the GEPIA 2.0 platform (gepia2.cancer-pku.cn) and the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (kmplot.com). Genetic association analyses were executed using both GEPIA 2.0 and the Home for Researchers platform (www.home-for-researchers.com). Transcription factor binding sites were predicted by JASPAR (jaspar.elixir.no). The GSEA software (www.gsea-msigdb.org) was employed to identify gene signatures derived from RNA-seq data.

## RIP-qPCR assay
The Magna RIP kit (Millipore, USA, 17–701) was utilized according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cellular lysis was performed using RIP lysis buffer, and the lysate underwent overnight co-immunoprecipitation with specific antibodies. Subsequently, RNA was extracted and analyzed via RT-PCR, with normalization conducted against the input.

## Cell viability assay
PDAC cells were cultured in a 96-well plate and exposed to varying concentrations of filgotinib (0–200 μM) for a duration of 24 h. Post-treatment, the cells were incubated with a 10% CCK-8 solution in fresh culture medium at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, optical density values at 450 nm (OD450) were determined using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madrid, Spain).

## RNA stability assay
PDAC cells were exposed to actinomycin D at a concentration of 5 μg/ml for varying durations (0, 2, and 4 h). Following treatment, the cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted for subsequent reverse transcription and RT-qPCR analysis. The expression levels of the target gene were normalized to those of beta-actin.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
HEK 293 T cells, initially transfected with either control siRNA or siRNA targeting METTL3, were subsequently co-transfected with the pGL-SV40-EIF3A-3’UTR plasmid, containing either the WT or Mut EIF3A sequence. Following transfection, cell lysates were collected and subjected to a dual-luciferase assay, conducted by the manufacturer’s protocol (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Following the specified treatment, cells immobilized on slides underwent washing with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. Post-fixation, the cells were blocked with a solution containing 3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA, 9002-93-1). The cells were then incubated with the primary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBST, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (Abcam, UK, ab104139). Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan) equipped with a 60x oil immersion objective.

## Immunohistochemistry staining
Immunohistochemical staining was conducted following established protocols. Briefly, paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent dewaxing and rehydration processes. Post-blocking, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by a one-hour incubation with a secondary antibody (Zsbio, China, PV6000) at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with Gill’s hematoxylin. Images of the stained sections were acquired using an automated digital pathology system (KFBIO, China).

## Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations
In the initial phase, MD simulations were performed using the Desmond module within the Schrödinger suite in Maestro. Docked complexes were solvated in a cubic water box with a 10 Å buffer, employing the SPC water model and 0.15 M NaCl to ensure physiological relevance. Electrostatic interactions were treated with the particle-mesh Ewald method, while van der Waals interactions were handled with a 9.0 Å cutoff. Following solvation, energy minimization and equilibration were carried out according to Desmond’s default protocols in both NVT and NPT ensembles. A 100 ns MD simulation was conducted under periodic boundary conditions using the OPLS4 force field in the NPT ensemble. Temperature and pressure were maintained at 300 K and 1 atm, respectively, via the Nosè-Hoover chain thermostat and the Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat. This initial phase established a multi-step protocol that began with Brownian dynamics at 10 K and progressed through restrained and unrestrained NVT and NPT simulations, culminating in a 500 ns NPT simulation under the same temperature and pressure conditions.

## Ligand Docking
Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the interaction mechanisms between filgotinib and ERG targets. Potential binding pockets on the filgotinib were first identified using the compute_binding_pocket.py script. Filgotinib (as the receptor) and the ERG molecule (as the ligand) were subsequently prepared in pdbqt format for docking, utilizing the prepare ligand and prepare receptor modules of AutoDock Tools (Morris et al. 2009), respectively. Docking was carried out using the run.sh script with AutoDock Vina version 1.2.5 (Eberhardt et al. 2021), targeting the specified binding pockets. To conduct a detailed analysis of the docking results, Discovery Studio was employed. This software provided insights into the interactions, including key contacts and binding orientations.

## In vivo experiments
Female mice supplied by Hangzhou Ziyuan Company were randomly distributed into experimental or control cohorts for the development of a xenograft tumor model. Each study group consisted of four animals (n = 4). MiaPaCa-2-Luc cells were then implanted subcutaneously in the right axillary region. When the tumor volume of the mice reached 50 - 100 mm3, the drug treatment experiment began. Tumor dimensions were recorded twice every week, and volumes were calculated as V = (length × width²)/2. Following a 28-day administration of filgotinib, the tumors were excised and both their volumes and weights were documented. In the animal experiment, the solution composition injected into the abdominal cavity of the experimental group was 10% filgotinib mother solution dissolved in 40 mg/ml DMSO + 40% PEG300 + 5% Tween 80 + 45% ddH₂O. The control group was a mixture of 10% pure DMSO + 40% PEG300 + 5% Tween 80 + 45% ddH₂O. To minimize potential bias, all tumor evaluations were performed by researchers unaware of the treatment assignments. Throughout the investigation, mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and randomized appropriately. All experimental protocols followed the animal welfare guidelines approved and supervised by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (No. ZJU20250254). When anesthesia was required, 3% isoflurane was utilized for induction and maintenance. At the conclusion of the in vivo study, euthanasia was humanely performed via carbon dioxide inhalation, consistent with institutional animal care standards.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± SEM, unless specified otherwise. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using ANOVA, while differences between two groups were assessed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001, with “ns” indicating non-significance (p > 0.05). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Each experiment was conducted at least in triplicate, unless stated otherwise.
