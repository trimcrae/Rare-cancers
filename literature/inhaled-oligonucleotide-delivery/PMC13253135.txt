## Abstract
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and ultimately fatal interstitial lung disease with severely limited therapeutic options. MicroRNAs, especially miR-26a-5p, have exhibited potent anti-fibrotic effects in preclinical studies. However, their clinical translation has been impeded by intrinsic susceptibility to enzymatic degradation in physiological environments and poor cellular internalization efficiency. In this study, we developed a tetrahedral framework nucleic acid-based bioswitchable miRNA delivery system, termed BiRDS26, for the targeted delivery of miR-26a-5p. BiRDS26 demonstrated robust anti-fibrotic activity in both in vitro and in vivo models, mechanistically mediated by the coordinated suppression of the Hippo and Wnt signaling pathways. Collectively, these results position BiRDS26 as a rationally engineered nanotherapeutic platform with significant translational potential for IPF treatment.
Keywords: Bioswitchable miRNA delivery system, Gene delivery, miRNA, Lung fibrosis, Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids, EMT

## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a chronic and progressive interstitial lung disease characterized by sustained pulmonary inflammation and pathological deposition of extracellular matrix components within the pulmonary interstitium and alveolar walls [ ]. It is characterized primarily by profound structural remodeling of the pulmonary parenchyma, aberrant alterations in extracellular matrix composition, and increased tissue stiffness. These pathological changes that collectively underlie the progressive decline in respiratory function. Pathogenesis typically begins with injury to alveolar epithelial type II cells, triggering sequential recruitment and activation of innate and adaptive immune effectors, ultimately resulting in dysregulated repair responses and pathological fibrotic remodeling [ ]. PF is broadly classified into IPF and secondary forms. IPF is a chronic, progressive interstitial lung disease characterized by dysregulated wound healing responses and pathological fibroblast activation, driven by intricate interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers. It predominantly affects individuals in mid-to late adulthood. The disease follows a relentlessly progressive clinical course and is associated with a poor prognosis [ ]. Global epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of IPF ranges from 13 to 20 per 100,000 individuals, with a marked age-related increase; among adults aged ≥70 years, the incidence rises to approximately 35 per 100,000 [ , , , ]. In China, the reported incidence ranges from 1 to 5 per 100,000 population, and this rate has been steadily increasing which primarily attributes to demographic aging and deteriorating environmental quality [ , ]. In 2014, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted approval to two antifibrotic agents: pirfenidone (PFD) and nintedanib for the treatment of IPF. Clinical evidence demonstrates that these agents slow the decline in pulmonary function; however, they do not reverse preexisting fibrotic pathology nor offer a curative intervention. Moreover, both therapeutics are associated with clinically significant adverse effects, notably gastrointestinal disturbances such as nausea and diarrhea [ , ]. Consequently, there remains a pressing unmet need for novel, noninvasive, safe, and efficacious therapeutic approaches capable of improving clinical outcomes particularly in patients diagnosed with early-stage IPF.
In recent years, non-coding RNA (ncRNA)-based therapeutics have emerged as a highly promising frontier in biomedical research and precision medicine. Among various non-coding RNAs, including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs) stand out particularly due to their well-defined mechanism of action: they can directly and sequence-specifically bind to target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), thereby leading to post-transcriptional gene silencing or translational inhibition. [ ]. A growing body of evidence implicates dysregulated miRNAs in the pathogenesis of numerous human diseases, including malignancies, cardiovascular disorders, respiratory conditions, and neurological disorders. Through modulation of key signaling pathways and regulation of fundamental cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, development, and pathological remodeling. MiRNAs exert broad and context-dependent biological influence [ , , , , ]. Notably, the capacity of a single miRNA to coordinately regulate multiple target genes underpins its functional pleiotropy and positions it as a compelling candidate for therapeutic intervention. In IPF, accumulating preclinical data indicate that specific miRNAs critically modulate fibrotic progression, particularly by regulating fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix deposition, and aberrant tissue repair [ , ]. Nevertheless, clinical translation of miRNA therapeutics remains hindered by persistent challenges, including inherent nuclease susceptibility, suboptimal pharmacokinetics, inefficient cellular uptake, and limited ability to traverse biological barriers such as the alveolar–epithelial interface [ ]. Thus, achieving rapid and efficient delivery of miRNAs to lung target cells remains a critical challenge.
To address these limitations, advanced nucleic acid delivery platforms have become a major focus of nanotherapeutic development. Nanomaterials offer distinct advantages as carriers, including protection of encapsulated oligonucleotides from enzymatic degradation, prolonged systemic circulation, enhanced permeability and retention (EPR)-mediated accumulation, and tunable surface functionalization for cell-specific targeting [ ]. Tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDN) are self-assembled, monodisperse nanoscale architectures formed via precise hybridization of four complementary single-stranded DNA oligonucleotides into a rigid, three-dimensional tetrahedral geometry. Recent studies have established TDN as exceptionally promising nanocarriers, owing to their outstanding biocompatibility, high-yield and scalable synthesis, remarkable thermodynamic and kinetic stability, mechanical robustness, and facile site-specific bioconjugation [ , , ]. As programmable gene delivery vehicles, TDN demonstrate strong affinity for hydrophobic molecules and nucleic acids rendering them especially suitable for RNA-based therapeutics. Early-generation TDN–miRNA conjugates relied on vertex- or edge-based “sticky-end” hybridization for miRNA attachment; however, this strategy left miRNA sequences partially exposed, rendering them vulnerable to nucleolytic cleavage, while structural enlargement compromised diffusion across tissue barriers and reduced cellular internalization efficiency. Subsequent refinements in design and assembly protocols have progressively enhanced TDN stability, biocompatibility, and intracellular delivery fidelity.
In this study, we developed a TDN-based biologically switchable system capable of co-encapsulating three different miRNA molecules - thereby generating a structure named “BiRDS” (biologically switchable miRNA delivery system), which is fundamentally different from traditional TDN. Critically, this design fully encapsulates miRNA within the nanostructure, conferring robust protection against nucleases and enabling RNase H–triggered, spatiotemporally controlled release [ , , ]. Structural and biophysical characterization confirms that BiRDS maintains high conformational integrity, thermal stability, and low cytotoxicity in vitro and in vivo [ , ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that intravenous administration of TDN leads primarily to accumulation in the liver and kidneys, resulting in suboptimal pulmonary targeting and potential systemic toxicity [ ]. To achieve efficient pulmonary delivery of BiRDS, airway instillation was selected as the route of administration. This approach enables direct delivery of therapeutics to the lungs, thereby achieving high local drug concentrations, maximizing therapeutic efficacy, and minimizing systemic exposure and off-target effects [ ]. For the first time, we applied airway-instilled BiRDS loaded with miR-26a-5p (BiRDS26) in the context of IPF, offering a strategy for miRNA-based interventions in this disease.
In this study, we report the application of a bioswitchable miRNA delivery system loaded with miR-26a-5p in the context of IPF, thereby introducing a therapeutic strategy for miRNA-based intervention in IPF. The primary objective of this work was to design and comprehensively evaluate a TDN-based biological switch system tailored specifically for efficient and controlled miRNA delivery. This engineered platform exhibits superior stability, high delivery efficiency, and an excellent safety profile, enabling the precise, airway-mediated delivery of miR-26a-5p whose antifibrotic activity is well established to lung cells. Our goal was to ameliorate early-stage IPF by suppressing pathogenic fibroblast activation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby preserving pulmonary function, improving clinical prognosis, and enhancing patients’ quality of life. Collectively, this study offers mechanistic insights and lays the foundation for potentially transformative therapeutic approaches in IPF management.

## Results
2.1.

## Design, preparation, and characterization of BiRDS26
To identify key miRNAs implicated in IPF progression, we performed a comparative analysis of differentially expressed miRNAs in lung tissues from IPF patients and identified overlapping candidates across independent datasets (( , Supporting Information)). Bioinformatics analysis of dataset GSE45789 revealed 62 significantly dysregulated miRNAs (p < 0.05), comprising 18 upregulated and 44 downregulated species. In dataset GSE32538, 176 miRNAs exhibited significant differential expression (p < 0.05), including 57 upregulated and 119 downregulated miRNAs. A comprehensive analysis identified a total of 18 overlapping miRNAs, including 1 upregulated and 17 downregulated types. Among these, miR-26a-5p emerged as a particularly promising candidate due to its established involvement in fibrotic pathobiology. Prior studies have demonstrated that miR-26a-5p attenuates lens fibrosis through negative regulation of the Jagged-1/Notch signaling pathway [ ]. Additionally, another study indicated that it may alleviate tubulointerstitial fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy through targeting PAR4 [ ]. Inspired by these findings and guided by the Watson-Crick base-pairing principle along with the BiRDS design scheme previously reported by Li et al. [ ], we successfully developed BiRDS26 containing miR-26a-5p to investigate its therapeutic effects on IPF. The structure of BiRDS26 consists of three single-stranded DNA molecules, with its unique nucleic acid sequence representing the “sun core”, while the three miR-26a-5p molecules symbolize the “immortal bird” ( A). Following the methods established by Zhou and Li et al. [ , ], we self-assembled the TDN from four single-stranded DNA sequences( ). Using an annlogous approach, we synthesized BiRDS26 in this study. To confirm successful synthesis of BiRDS26, we modified the 5’ end of siRNA with a sulfocyanine 5 (Cy5) residue to enable red fluorescence detection. As illustrated in the left panel of the gel electrophoresis image, lanes 1-7 correspond respectively to: (i) free miR-26 single strand; (ii) S1 (one single-stranded DNA sequence); (iii) S1-S2 (two single-stranded DNA sequences); (iv) S1-S3 (three single-stranded DNA sequences); (v) +1R (TDN conjugated with one miR-26 strand); (vi) +2R (TDN conjugated with two miR26 strands); and (vii) +3R (TDN conjugated with three miR26 strands; BiRDS26). Notably, each BiRDS26 molecule accommodates up to three miR-26a-5p molecules, thereby conferring enhanced delivery efficiency relative to conventional miRNA carriers [ ]. Moreover, BiRDS26 exhibited significantly higher Cy5 fluorescence intensity compared with the free (unstructured) miR-26a-5p strand ( B). Importantly, under identical electrophoresis conditions, the Cy5 signal of the free strand was markedly weaker than that of the same sequence when assembled into the stacked-bridge architecture which indicated that the nanostructure enhances the conformational stability of the oligonucleotide relative to its unbound state. Successful loading of miR-26a-5p onto BiRDS26 was visually confirmed via co-localization of Cy5 fluorescence (red) and GelRed-stained nucleic acid fluorescence (green), together with a progressive increase in the miR-26a-5p–to–Cy5 fluorescence intensity ratio ( C). Morphological characterization of TDN and BiRDS26 by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; D) and atomic force microscopy (AFM; E) revealed that they were both equilateral triangular nanoparticles with an edge length of approximately 10 nm and clear morphology. These dimensions align closely with the hydrodynamic size distribution obtained from dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements ( F–G). The compact, angular geometry is structurally favorable for enhanced tissue and cellular penetration, potentially mediated by a corner-driven interaction mechanism. DLS analysis further showed that BiRDS26 exhibits a hydrodynamic diameter of 8.66 ± 3.04 nm and a zeta potential of −7.36 ± 8.19 mV. Both parameters were marginally reduced relative to those of TDN. Such physicochemical properties collectively contribute to improved resistance against reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced degradation and facilitate selective binding to functional proteins; notably, these same attributes govern electrophoretic mobility during gel separation.
 Subsequently, BiRDS26 was incubated with 1–10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for 2 h to mimic physiological serum exposure under conditions supportive of cellular viability. Native agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) analysis revealed minimal structural degradation of BiRDS26 across the entire FBS concentration range ( H–I). Notably, BiRDS26 demonstrates exceptional thermal stability at ambient temperature (25 °C), retaining its structural integrity for a minimum of 7 days ( J–K). This property enhances its practical utility in resource-constrained settings or complex biological workflows. Structurally, BiRDS26 incorporates three DNA-RNA hybrid duplex domains on its lateral arms, each stabilized by canonical Watson-Crick hydrogen bonding and functioning as specific recognition and cleavage sites for endogenous RNase H29. When incubated with RNase H at concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 200 U mL−1, BiRDS26 exhibited pronounced resistance to enzymatic degradation ( L–M). In the in vivo animal model, BiRDS26 was administered to mice via airway instillation. To evaluate its stability under physiologically representative alveolar conditions, BiRDS26 was incubated in freshly collected mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF); it retained structural integrity for up to 96 h, as confirmed by native AGE and fluorescence quantification( O–P).
Collectively, these data establish BiRDS26 as a well-characterized DNA-RNA hybrid nanostructure with a size of less than 10 nm. It exhibits facile synthesis, high miR-26a-5p loading capacity, exceptional biostability in serum, enzymatic, and pulmonary microenvironments, as well as favorable biophysical properties. These features collectively support the strong translational potential of BiRDS26 for RNA-based therapeutics.

2.2.

## Cellular uptake of BiRDS26
Cellular internalization efficiency represents a primary determinant of BiRDS26's biological activity. In vitro evaluations were conducted in two human pulmonary cell lines: MRC-5 (human embryonic lung fibroblasts) and A549 (human alveolar epithelial cells). Previous studies have demonstrated that TDN is predominantly internalized via caveolae-mediated endocytosis. Given the considerable structural similarity between BiRDS26 and TDN, including their nearly identical size (approximately 10 nm), equilateral triangular geometry, and negatively charged surface, it is hypothesized that their cellular uptake occurs via a mechanistically analogous pathway [ ]. To quantify cellular uptake, free miR-26a-5p and BiRDS26-loaded miR-26a-5p were independently conjugated with the Cy5 fluorophore. Three rigorously defined experimental groups were implemented: (i) an untreated control; (ii) cells transfected with 750 nM Cy5-labeled free miR-26a-5p; and (iii) cells transfected with 250 nM Cy5-labeled BiRDS26. Cellular internalization of BiRDS26 under physiological culture conditions was further evaluated by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry (FCM). CLSM imaging after 6 h and 12 h incubation demonstrated significantly enhanced intracellular accumulation of Cy5 fluorescence in A549 cells treated with BiRDS26, relative to both untreated controls and cells exposed to free miR-26a-5p ( A–B). In contrast, free miR-26a-5p exhibited negligible internalization under identical experimental conditions. Comparable uptake enhancement was observed in MRC-5 fibroblasts ( C–D). Quantitative FCM analysis revealed that at 6 h, the proportion of Cy5-positive cells in the BiRDS26 treatment group exceeded 60% in both A549 and MRC-5 cell lines, and at 12 h, this proportion exceeded 90% – while the corresponding value of free miR-26a-5p was consistently lower at all time points ( E–G). These quantitative results were in complete agreement with the quality-controlled CLSM observations and the statistical assessment results of fluorescence intensity distribution [ , , , , ]. These findings indicate that BiRDS26 can achieve efficient intracellular uptake of miR-26a-5p. To comprehensively assess biocompatibility, live/dead viability staining ( H–J) and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays ( K–L) were conducted to evaluate cytotoxicity and proliferative activity, respectively [ ]. Both MRC-5 fibroblasts and A549 epithelial cells exhibited significantly enhanced proliferative activity and reduced apoptotic incidence following incubation with miR-26a-5p, TDN or BiRDS26, confirming the favorable biocompatibility and negligible cytotoxicity of both nanocarriers under the tested conditions.
 
2.3.

## BiRDS26 suppresses fibroblast activation and EMT in epithelial cells
IPF is pathologically characterized by aberrant activation of lung fibroblasts and EMT, leading to pathological expansion of the myofibroblast population. This expansion enhances fibroblast-mediated contraction of the extracellular matrix (ECM) through upregulation of contractile stress fibers, including α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), thereby promoting excessive ECM deposition and tissue stiffening [ ]. Bleomycin (BLM) is a well-established pharmacological inducer of EMT in alveolar epithelial cells; accordingly, an in vitro EMT model was established by treating human alveolar adenocarcinoma A549 cells with BLM. As shown by Western blot analysis and quantitative densitometric assessment ( A–C), BiRDS26 significantly attenuated BLM-induced EMT, as evidenced by upregulated expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and concomitant downregulation of the mesenchymal marker vimentin. These results were independently validated by dual immunofluorescence staining for E-cadherin and vimentin ( D–F). To further recapitulate hallmark features of IPF in vitro human fetal lung fibroblast MRC-5 cells were exposed to BLM [ ]. Western blot analysis and quantitative densitometric assessment ( G–I) revealed that BiRDS26 treatment significantly reduced protein expression of the fibrotic markers fibronectin and α-SMA in BLM-stimulated MRC-5 fibroblasts. These findings were corroborated by immunofluorescence staining, which showed markedly diminished cellular signal intensity for both markers ( J–M and , Supporting Information). Collectively, these data demonstrate that BiRDS26 effectively inhibits BLM-induced fibroblast activation. Beyond fibroblast activation, EMT is a well-established pathogenic mechanism in IPF, contributing to myofibroblast accumulation and ECM deposition. Consequently, therapeutic targeting of EMT represents a mechanistically rational strategy for IPF intervention. In parallel experiments, TDN exhibited substantially lower efficacy than BiRDS26 in attenuating both fibroblast activation and EMT progression. Taken together, these results establish that BiRDS26 mediates robust anti-fibrotic activity via two complementary mechanisms: direct suppression of fibroblast activation and coordinated inhibition of EMT in alveolar epithelial cells.
 
2.4.

## BiRDS26 suppresses fibroblast activation and EMT in epithelial cells through modulation of the Hippo and Wnt signaling pathways
To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying BiRDS26's dual inhibition of fibroblast activation and EMT, we integrated experimentally validated and computationally predicted target databases for miR-26a-5p including miRDB, TargetScan, miRWalk, and miRTarBase. Intersecting predictions across all four resources yielded a high-confidence set of 57 evolutionarily conserved miR-26a-5p target genes ( A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these targets identified four significantly enriched signaling pathways (adjusted p < 0.05); notably, the Wnt and Hippo signaling pathways emerged as top candidates ( B). Connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), a well-established downstream effector of the Hippo pathway and a potent pro-fibrotic mediator, has been consistently implicated in IPF pathogenesis across preclinical and clinical studies [ , , , ]. Similarly, Wnt5a has been functionally linked to fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation, and ECM remodeling in pulmonary fibrosis. In contrast, canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling is a well-documented driver of EMT in alveolar epithelial cells and a key promoter of pathological ECM accumulation [ ]. To contextualize these findings within human disease, we analyzed the publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE135893. Following stringent quality control, batch correction, and cell-type annotation using canonical marker genes, we resolved four major cellular compartments: Epithelial, Fibroblast, Endothelial, and Immune cells ( C). Pathway activity scoring performed separately within the epithelial and fibroblast clusters using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed significant upregulation of Wnt signaling in epithelial cells and Hippo signaling in fibroblasts in IPF samples relative to controls ( D). These data collectively support the pathological co-activation of both pathways in human IPF. Given that miR-26a-5p targets multiple regulatory nodes across both pathways including CTGF and Wnt5a its modulation likely exerts coordinated, functionally convergent effects. Such multi-target engagement may underlie the observed synergistic (rather than merely additive) anti-fibrotic activity of BiRDS26. To determine whether BiRDS26 recapitulates and amplifies this regulatory effect, we assessed its impact on CTGF and Wnt5a expression in BLM-stimulated MRC-5 fibroblasts and A549 epithelial cells. Immunoblotting analyses ( E–H) demonstrated that BiRDS26 treatment significantly reduced CTGF and Wnt5a protein levels in a dose-dependent manner. Consistent with these biochemical findings, immunofluorescence staining confirmed marked attenuation of CTGF and Wnt5a signal intensity at the single-cell level ( I–L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that BiRDS26 may attenuate fibroblast activation and suppress EMT through concurrent, pathway-integrated inhibition of the Hippo and Wnt signaling axes.
 
2.5.

## Safety and efficacy of BiRDS26 in vivo
The results presented above demonstrate that BiRDS26 effectively alleviates IPF in vitro. To determine whether this therapeutic effect is recapitulated in vivo, we subsequently performed animal experiments. First, we evaluated the biodistribution of the therapeutic agent in living organisms. In the context of IPF, Cy5-labeled miR-26 (Cy5-miR26) and Cy5-labeled BiRDS26 (Cy5-BiRDS26) were administered to mice via airway instillation, followed by quantitative assessment of fluorescence signal intensity across major isolated organs. Compared with Cy5-miR26, Cy5-BiRDS26 exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity specifically in the lungs indicating enhanced local accumulation of BiRDS26 within the fibrotic lung microenvironment ( A–B). Overall, pulmonary fluorescence intensity markedly exceeded that observed in other major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys ( , Supporting Information). While the liver showed the second-highest signal and the kidneys displayed comparatively low fluorescence, consistent with limited renal clearance of the conjugate. Critically, the distribution pattern aligns with canonical pharmacokinetic principles: hepatic accumulation reflects metabolic processing, whereas low renal signal suggests minimal glomerular filtration. These features support favorable pharmacokinetic properties for an inhaled therapeutic [ ].
 Collectively, these biodistribution data support BiRDS26 as a promising candidate for airway-targeted therapy in IPF. To rigorously evaluate its therapeutic impact on disease progression, we assessed survival outcomes and longitudinal body weight trajectories in a chronic, multi-dose BLM murine model of pulmonary fibrosis.
The single-dose BLM model is widely employed in mechanistic studies owing to its advantages of experimental simplicity and rapid onset of fibrotic pathology [ ]. However, this model fails to recapitulate the hallmarks of human IPF, including insidious onset, sustained progression, and irreversible architectural remodeling [ ]. In contrast, repeated low-dose bleomycin administration more faithfully recapitulates chronic lung injury, persistent alveolar epithelial cell hyperplasia, and progressive interstitial fibrosis, while inducing a milder, non-resolving inflammatory response that favors sustained rather than transient fibrotic remodeling [ ]. This paradigm is particularly suited for investigating EMT, aberrant tissue repair, and long-term structural deterioration. To establish a model that robustly recapitulates the chronicity and irreversibility of human IPF while minimizing the inherent spontaneous resolution observed in acute single-dose models, we administered BLM via intratracheal instillation every four days. To mitigate risks associated with repeated prolonged anesthesia which include pulmonary edema secondary to cumulative fluid load we implemented a staggered dosing protocol: BLM instillation and therapeutic compound administration were alternated on consecutive days, with a consistent two-day interval maintained between each intervention ( C).
Tissues and biological samples were collected on day 21 post-initial BLM challenge. During the induction phase, BLM-treated mice exhibited hallmark signs of systemic illness, including reduced food intake, hypoactivity, piloerection, and loss of fur luster. At the end of the experiment, the survival rates of the mice were statistically analyzed: the survival rate of the control group was 100%, while that of the BLM group was 40%. In contrast, the survival rates of the BiRDS26 and TDN treatment groups were 80% and 60%, respectively ( D). Concomitant with survival gains, all treatment groups showed progressive clinical recovery which includes restored locomotor activity, normalized feeding behavior, and improved coat condition with BiRDS26 producing the most pronounced and sustained amelioration ( E). Notably, starting from the 4th day post-modeling, the body weight of BLM-treated mice was significantly reduced compared to the control group, indicating the successful establishment of the fibrosis model. Furthermore, in comparison to both the TDN group and the control group, mice in the BiRDS26 treatment group exhibited earlier onset of body weight recovery with a more pronounced magnitude of restoration. These findings indicate that BiRDS26 not only attenuates mortality but also meaningfully preserves physiological function and quality of life in chronic pulmonary fibrosis. To comprehensively assess potential off-target toxicity, we conducted a systematic safety evaluation comprising histopathological examination of major organs and serum biochemical profiling of organ-specific functional biomarkers ( F–L). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of heart, liver, spleen, and kidney tissues revealed no evidence of drug-induced structural damage, cellular infiltration, necrosis, or architectural disruption across all treatment groups ( F). Serum analyses included cardiac markers (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), hepatic enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), total bilirubin, and renal parameters (uric acid, total protein) ( G–L). All measured parameters remained within normal physiological ranges and showed no statistically significant deviation from vehicle-treated controls (p > 0.05). Collectively, these data demonstrate that BiRDS26 is well tolerated at therapeutically effective doses, with no evidence of acute cardiotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, or nephrotoxicity.

2.6.

## Therapeutic benefits of BiRDS26 in BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis in vivo
In vitro studies demonstrated that BiRDS26 effectively suppresses fibroblast activation and EMT in alveolar epithelial cells. To evaluate its therapeutic efficacy especially its capacity for targeted pulmonary delivery, in a physiologically relevant murine model of IPF, we employed a chronic, multi-dose BLM regimen. To circumvent the self-limiting nature of acute BLM-induced fibrosis, BLM was administered via oropharyngeal aspiration every four days; BiRDS26 (or PBS/TDN) was co-administered on the same schedule. To minimize cumulative anesthetic exposure and mitigate risks associated with repeated airway fluid loading including potential pulmonary edema, we implemented an alternating intervention protocol: BLM instillation and therapeutic compound administration were performed on alternate days, ensuring a 48-h interval between consecutive anesthesia procedures ( C). On day 21 post-initial BLM challenge, mice underwent terminal micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging under anesthesia, followed immediately by euthanasia for tissue and biological sample collection. Micro-CT analysis revealed characteristic radiological hallmarks of advanced pulmonary fibrosis in BLM-treated mice, including reticular opacities, honeycombing, and traction bronchiectasis. These features is consistent with human IPF. 3D reconstruction further demonstrated a marked reduction in ventilated lung volume ( A). Both TDN and BiRDS26 treatment attenuated these structural abnormalities, with BiRDS26 producing significantly greater improvement in parenchymal architecture and airspace preservation ( A). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis confirmed that miR-26a-5p expression was significantly downregulated in lung tissue from BLM-challenged mice which is consistent with prior bioinformatic predictions and clinical observations in IPF patients ( , Supporting Information). As previously documented, pathological collagen deposition in IPF increases two-to threefold relative to healthy controls, resulting in interstitial matrix expansion, increased lung weight, and impaired gas exchange efficiency [ ]. Masson's trichrome staining quantitatively corroborated this, revealing extensive blue-stained collagen deposition in BLM-treated lungs which is consistent with established histopathological criteria for fibrosis severity ( B) [ ]. Concurrently, H&E staining coupled with semi-quantitative histopathological scoring demonstrated that BLM induced severe alveolar damage, inflammatory cell infiltration (predominantly lymphocytes and macrophages), and early architectural distortion which is fully aligned with the pathogenic cascade of human IPF ( B–C). Therapeutic intervention mitigated these changes in a dose- and compound-dependent manner, with BiRDS26 exhibiting superior efficacy over TDN and vehicle controls. Fibrosis severity was independently quantified using the validated Ashcroft scoring system, which showed strong concordance with qualitative H&E assessment ( D). Prior to tissue harvest, we evaluated molecular markers of EMT and fibroblast activation in whole-lung lysates. Western blot analysis ( E–I) revealed that BLM challenge significantly downregulated the epithelial marker E-cadherin and upregulated the mesenchymal marker vimentin which is indicative of active EMT [ ]. Concomitantly, expression of canonical fibrotic effectors including fibronectin and α-SMA was markedly elevated, confirming robust myofibroblast activation within fibrotic lesions. These protein-level changes were robustly recapitulated by immunofluorescence imaging and quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis ( J–K and S2A-D, Supporting Information), demonstrating spatially resolved suppression of vimentin and restoration of E-cadherin membrane localization in BiRDS26-treated lungs. Collectively, these multimodal data demonstrate that BiRDS26 exerts potent anti-fibrotic activity in vivo through dual inhibition of EMT and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation, thereby preserving lung structure, function, and cellular homeostasis.
 
2.7.

## BiRDS26 suppresses fibroblast activation and EMT in epithelial cells in vivo through modulation of the Hippo and Wnt signaling pathways
The in vivo findings described above demonstrate that BiRDS26 ameliorates established pulmonary fibrosis in a murine IPF model through attenuation of histopathological fibrosis and suppression of EMT. To determine whether the mechanistic insights derived from in vitro studies are recapitulated in vivo, we evaluated pathway modulation in lung tissue harvested on day 21 post-treatment. Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining confirmed robust target engagement: BiRDS26 significantly reduced protein expression of the Hippo effector CTGF and the non-canonical WNT ligand Wnt5a in fibrotic lung parenchyma ( A–D). These biochemical changes were spatially validated by immunofluorescence, which revealed markedly diminished CTGF and Wnt5a signal intensity specifically within alveolar and interstitial compartments of BiRDS26-treated lungs ( E–H). Collectively, these data confirm that BiRDS26 exerts synergistic inhibition on both the Hippo and Wnt signaling axes in vivo, thereby suppressing two convergent drivers of fibroblast activation and EMT (see ).
 Given that immune cell infiltration constitutes a hallmark feature of the fibrotic niche in IPF and contributes to epithelial injury, TGF-β1 release, and subsequent collagen deposition, we further investigated whether BiRDS26 modulates the canonical TGF-β1/SMAD pathway. As TGF-β1-driven SMAD2/3 phosphorylation is a well-established molecular trigger for EMT initiation and fibroblast recruitment, its inhibition represents a therapeutically relevant node [ ]. In vivo ELISA and Western blot analyses demonstrated that both TDN and BiRDS26 significantly suppressed phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 (p-SMAD2/3) relative to BLM-only controls; however, BiRDS26 exhibited substantially greater inhibitory potency than TDN ( , Supporting Information). These results indicate that BiRDS26's anti-fibrotic activity extends beyond Wnt/Hippo modulation to include potent, selective suppression of the TGF-β1/SMAD axis suggesting a multi-pathway mechanism that simultaneously targets epithelial plasticity, mesenchymal activation, and immune–epithelial crosstalk.

## Conclusion
Multiple microRNAs (miRNAs) are established regulators of fibrotic pathogenesis, critically modulating fibroblast activation, ECM homeostasis, and pathological tissue remodeling. Consequently, miRNA-based therapeutics represent a mechanistically rational strategy for IPF. However, clinical development has been impeded by the inherent nuclease sensitivity of unmodified miRNAs and by the immunogenicity, insertional mutagenesis risk, and suboptimal biodistribution associated with viral delivery systems. To address these challenges, we engineered BiRDS26—a TDN-based, bioswitchable delivery platform specifically designed for the targeted pulmonary delivery of miR-26a-5p. Relative to TDN carriers, BiRDS26 exhibits several key advantages: simplified one-pot synthesis, uniform nanoscale architecture (∼10 nm), exceptional resistance to serum nucleases, high-efficiency cellular internalization, enhanced penetration across biological barriers including the alveolar–epithelial interface and a favorable in vivo safety profile with no evidence of systemic toxicity. In preclinical models, BiRDS26 demonstrated potent anti-fibrotic activity both in vitro and in vivo, significantly attenuating BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Critically, BiRDS26 achieves rapid, selective, and sustained accumulation within fibrotic lung tissue, thereby maximizing target engagement while minimizing off-target exposure. Mechanistically, its efficacy stems from coordinated suppression of two convergent pro-fibrotic signaling axes: the Hippo effector CTGF and the non-canonical WNT ligand Wnt5a. Future work will focus on structure–activity optimization to further enhance lung-specific targeting and intracellular release kinetics, as well as on evaluating rational combination regimens. A particularly promising candidate is co-administration with PFD, which may yield additive or synergistic anti-fibrotic effects through complementary mechanisms, potentially improving therapeutic index and delaying disease progression.
In summary, BiRDS26 constitutes a clinically translatable, airway-deliverable miRNA therapeutic platform that selectively inhibits CTGF- and Wnt5a-driven fibrotic signaling. Its favorable pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and safety properties position it as a versatile candidate not only for IPF but also for other miRNA-dysregulation-associated disorders.

## Experimental section
4.1.

## Analysis of publicly available gene expression and high-throughput sequencing data
The datasets GSE135893, GSE45789, and GSE32538 were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; URL: http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, accessed on August 25, 2024). The GSE135893 dataset comprises 20 samples of IPF with sequencing conducted on lung tissues obtained from patients. The GSE32538 dataset includes miRNA profiles from 106 IPF patients alongside 50 non-disease controls. Additionally, the GSE45789 dataset features miRNA profiles derived from the lung tissues of three mice diagnosed with IPF and three healthy control mice.
The RNA analysis for the GSE135893 dataset was performed utilizing platforms GPL18573, GPL20301, and GPL24676. The R package “Seurat” facilitated this analysis. Following data acquisition and quality control procedures, the SCTransform function was employed to standardize and scale the data appropriately. In instances where multiple datasets were present, integration was achieved using the “harmony” package. Dimensionality reduction was executed through the RunUMAP() function. To identify markers and differentially expressed genes, Seurat's FindMarkers() and FindClusters() functions were utilized. Cell types were annotated via CellMarker (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/search.jsp; accessed on October 14, 2024) in conjunction with the “SingleR” package.
The miRNA analysis of GSE45789 and GSE32538 was performed using the GPL7723 and GPL8786 platforms, respectively. GEO2R was employed for online analysis to identify differentially expressed miRNAs (p < 0.05). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) version 4.3.3 was utilized for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, with results deemed significant when the p-value was less than 0.05. The online databases miRDB (https://mirdb.org/, June 18, 2024), TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/mmu_80/, June 18, 2024), miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/, June 18, 2024), and miRTarbase (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/databasecommons/database/id/167, June 18, 2024) were utilized to identify the target genes of miR-26a-5p.
This study leveraged three publicly available GEO datasets, all of which had received prior ethical approval in their original research contexts. Informed consent forms for the patients can be accessed through these three public databases.

4.2.

## Design and synthesis of TDN and BiRDS26
BiRDS26 comprises three single-stranded DNA molecules (S1–S3) and Cy5-labeled miR-26a-5p ( ). All oligonucleotides and miRNAs were commercially synthesized by Chengdu Yun Hai Tetrahedron Biotech Co. Ltd.(Chengdu, China). S1, S2, S3, and Cy5-labeled miR-26a-5p were mixed in TM buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5; 50 mM MgCl2) at a molar ratio of 1:1:1:3. The mixture was denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by rapid cooling to 4 °C for 20 min using a thermal cycler. BiRDS26 was assembled in a single-step, one-pot reaction driven by Watson-Crick base pairing and spontaneous self-assembly. Likewise, TDN was prepared under identical conditions using equimolar quantities of its constituent single-stranded DNA strands.

4.3.

## Authentication and characteristics of TDN and BiRDS26
The successful synthesis of BiRDS26 was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis (100 V, 20 min). Particle size and ζ-potential measurements for both BiRDS26 and TDN were performed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 system (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Furthermore, the morphology and dimensional characteristics of BiRDS26 were investigated using atomic force microscopy (AFM; Shimadzu, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi Ltd., Japan). The stability and degradation profile of BiRDS26 were assessed under identical electrophoretic conditions (100 V, 20 min), and the resulting gels were visualized and analyzed with a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad).

4.4.

## Cell culture and grouping
MRC-5 and A549 cells (Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), supplemented with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat. No. 10099141) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution, at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Cells were assigned to four experimental groups: control, miR-26a-5p, TDN, and BiRDS26. The final concentrations of TDN and BiRDS26 were set at 250 nM, whereas the miRNA component was administered at 750 nM.

4.5.

## Cellular uptake
MRC-5 and A549 cells (Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat. No. 10099141) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator equilibrated with 5% CO2. Cells were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: control, miRNA-only, TDN, and BiRDS26. The final concentrations of TDN and BiRDS26 were 250 nM, while the miRNA component was administered at 750 nM. FCM was performed as follows: cells were collected by digestion, samples were washed and centrifuged, and miR-26a-5p and BiRDS26 entry levels were quantified using a flow cytometer.

4.6.

## Cell viability measurement
MRC-5 and A549 cells (Fuheng, Shanghai, China) were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat. No. 10099141) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution, under humidified conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2. To minimize protein corona formation prior to treatment with nucleic acid–based agents, the FBS concentration in the culture medium was gradually reduced to 1% (v/v) over a 3-h period. For the cell viability assay, MRC-5 or A549 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subsequently incubated with culture medium containing either TDN (250 nM) or BiRDS26 (250 nM). Cell proliferation was assessed on days 1 and 2 using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, the culture medium was aspirated, and 100 μL of freshly prepared CCK-8 working solution was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). For the live/dead cell staining assay, MRC-5 or A549 cells were seeded into 48-well plates and incubated for 24 h with culture medium containing TDN (250 nM) or BiRDS26 (250 nM). Following this incubation period, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, 100 μL of staining reagent and 200 μL of reaction buffer, prepared as per the manufacturer's instructions to contain calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI), were added to each well. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 45 min, followed by three washes with the reaction buffer. Live and dead cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) using excitation/emission wavelengths of approximately 490 ± 10 nm (calcein-AM, live cells) and 545 nm (PI, dead cells), respectively. Except for the control group, all other groups were treated with BLM (5 ng mL−1) for 24 h to establish an IPF model.

4.7.

## Western blotting analysis
Following treatment, proteins were extracted using a lysis buffer and subsequently mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Samples were denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min. Protein separation was carried out by SDS-PAGE, followed by electrotransfer of resolved proteins onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with blocking buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies ( ). On the following day, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000 dilution; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an iBright CL1500 Imaging System (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified using appropriate software.

4.8.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Immunofluorescence staining was performed on both paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections and cultured cells. For lung tissue sections, antigen retrieval was carried out after deparaffinization and rehydration. Sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 90 min at room temperature. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies corresponding to those used in immunoblotting (see ). After thorough washing with PBS, sections were incubated with appropriate species-matched fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 900) to evaluate the expression levels and subcellular localization of target proteins. For cell-based IF staining, cultured cells were fixed with a cold methanol–acetone (1:1, v/v) mixture and permeabilized as required. All subsequent steps, including blocking, primary and secondary antibody incubations, and washes, were conducted identically to those described for tissue sections. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence signal intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

4.9.

## Establishment of animal models and grouping
Adult male C57BL/6J mice aged 8 weeks were used in this study. Anesthesia was induced with Delivector™ Avertin (tribromoethanol). To establish the IPF model, BLM was administered via intratracheal instillation every four days at the following cumulative doses: 0.4, 0.4, 0.3, and 0.3 USP/kg—totaling 1.4 USP/kg per mouse. Therapeutic agents were delivered via the same route (intratracheal instillation) on the same schedule. To minimize the risk of anesthetic overdose and excessive pulmonary fluid accumulation, BLM administration and therapeutic interventions were alternated, with a one-day interval between consecutive procedures. Mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 10 per group): Control, BLM, TDN, and BiRDS26. Treatments consisted of intratracheal instillation of: (i) PBS (50 μL) for the Control group; (ii) BLM alone (0.4 and 0.3 USP/kg, 50 μL) for the BLM group; (iii) BLM co-administered with TDN (250 nM, 50 μL) for the TDN group; or (iv) BLM co-administered with BiRDS26 (250 nM, 50 μL) for the BiRDS26 group. All treatments were repeated every four days over a total duration of 21 days. Body weight was recorded daily throughout the experimental period. At the end of the study, major organs including heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys were harvested for histopathological examination using H&E staining. Lung tissues underwent comprehensive evaluation, including ELISA assays, H&E and Masson's trichrome staining, as well as immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with institutional guidelines and received prior approval from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee.

4.10.

## Biodistribution of miR-26a-5p and BiRDS26 in IPF mice
Following the modeling protocol described previously, C57BL/6J mice were selected on the 14th day post BLM administration for assessment of the in vivo biodistribution of the test agent. This pathological state better recapitulated the retention profiles of TDN and BiRDS26 in the lung tissue as observed in IPF pathogenesis. Mice were anesthetized with Delivector™ Avertin (tribromoethanol), and Cy5-miR-26a-5p and Cy5-BiRDS26 were directly delivered to the lungs via airway instillation at a single dose identical to that used in the IPF mouse treatment experiment. At five distinct time points post-administration (0.5, 1, 3, 6, and 12 h), tissue samples from the lung, heart, liver, spleen, and kidney were harvested. Each organ was imaged using an IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer).

4.11.

## Serum biochemical tests
Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital puncture and allowed to clot at 4 °C prior to centrifugation for serum isolation. Serum biochemical parameters including high-density lipoprotein (HDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), total protein (TP), and uric acid (UA) were quantified using a Cobas c311 clinical chemistry analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland), in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

4.12.

## Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
According to previous studies, RNA was extracted from BMSCs using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts). RNA samples were purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (R0027, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to ensure quality and purity. The purified RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the miRNA cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (stem-loop method) (AG11742, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) for subsequent gene expression analysis. RT-PCR was performed using the ABI QuantStudio 6 instrument in combination with the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix II qPCR Kit (AG11702, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) to detect the expression level of miR-26a-5p in the samples. The sequences of the primers used are shown in , with U6 serving as the internal control gene to correct for sample differences.

4.13.

## Micro-CT imaging and three-dimensional reconstruction
On day 21, longitudinal in vivo micro-CT imaging was performed using a Quantum GX system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA). Mice were anesthetized with Delivector™ Avertin (tribromoethanol), which induces stable respiratory depression reducing respiratory rate and tidal volume while maintaining regular breathing rhythm to minimize motion artifacts during acquisition. Animals were positioned supine on the scanning bed with the thoracic cavity centered within the field of view. A respiratory gating region of interest (ROI) was placed over the diaphragm to monitor real-time respiratory motion. Respiratory waveform, cycle duration, and frequency were continuously recorded throughout image acquisition. Scanning parameters were standardized as follows: X-ray tube voltage = 90 kV, tube current = 88 μA, total acquisition time = 4 min. Retrospective respiratory gating was performed in high-speed mode; projection data were acquired in list-mode format during a single continuous gantry rotation. Images were reconstructed using filtered back-projection with a Ram-Lak kernel, yielding two isotropic 3D volumetric datasets, one corresponding to peak inspiration and the other to end expiration, with a voxel size of 50 μm. Reconstructed datasets were systematically evaluated in transverse, coronal, and sagittal planes to assess image sharpness and confirm absence of motion-induced blurring. Scans exhibiting residual motion artifact due to inadequate anesthesia depth were repeated immediately.

4.14.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). For group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's t-test was employed. Differences with P-values <0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001 were deemed statistically significant.
