## ABSTRACT
In recent years, the potential neurotoxicity of inhaled anesthetics on the developing brain has increasingly garnered attention, yet its mechanism remains unclear. Parthanatos is a newly discovered form of programmed cell death dependent on PARP‐1, and it is believed to be closely associated with cellular oxidative stress response. However, it is still to be proven whether isoflurane, a commonly used clinical anesthetic, can induce parthanatos in developing brain neurons and whether it activates the oxidative stress signaling pathway in neuronal cells. In this study, we treated SH‐SY5Y cells and rat hippocampus neuron cells (RN‐h) with isoflurane, measured cell viability using the MTT assay, examined the activation of the parthanatos‐related PARP‐1/AIF/PAR signaling pathway using western blot analysis, detected the accumulation of ROS using DCFH‐DA, detected mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) by a JC‐1 assay, and assessed the activation of the oxidative stress‐related JNK signaling pathway using western blot. In vivo, we examined the damaging effects of inhaled isoflurane on neonatal rat hippocampal neurons using HE staining. The results showed that 2% and 4% concentrations of isoflurane significantly inhibited cell survival and upregulated the expression levels of PARP‐1, AIF, and PAR in both types of neuronal cells. Moreover, isoflurane significantly enhanced ROS levels and decreased Δψm, and activated the JNK signaling pathway in both cell types. Importantly, we found that pretreatment with N‐Acetylcysteine (NAC) could inhibit isoflurane‐induced parthanatos and the accumulation of ROS in cells, as well as the activation of the JNK pathway. The experimental results in neonatal rats also demonstrated that isoflurane led to significant neuronal death in the hippocampal CA1 region. However, pretreatment with NAC significantly increased the survival rate of pyramidal neurons in this region. In summary, through our experiments, we confirmed that isoflurane can induce parthanatos in neuronal cells, and NAC can decrease ROS accumulation in neuronal cells and thus mitigate the damage isoflurane causes to neuronal cells.
Keywords: c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase, isoflurane, N‐Acetylcysteine, neurotoxicity, parthanatos, poly ADP‐ribose polymerase 1, reactive oxygen species

This study demonstrates that isoflurane induces parthanatos in developing neuronal cells through oxidative stress mechanisms, as evidenced by increased PARP‐1, AIF, and PAR levels, along with elevated ROS and activated JNK signaling pathways. Importantly, N‐Acetylcysteine (NAC) pretreatment mitigates these effects, reducing ROS accumulation and neuronal damage.

## Introduction
Studies indicate that the inhaled anesthetic isoflurane possesses potential neurotoxicity to the developing brain, raising increasing concerns about the safety of using isoflurane anesthesia in infants and young children [ ]. Animal and clinical experimental studies have confirmed that exposure to isoflurane in the developing brain can lead to neurodegenerative changes and cognitive dysfunctions [ , ]. Neuronal cell death has been recognized as a major contributor to neurodegeneration and cognitive impairments, which are associated with isoflurane‐induced neurotoxicity in the developing brain. Although recent studies have discovered that isoflurane induces neuronal death in the developing brain through mechanisms like apoptosis and ferroptosis, the exact mechanism of neuronal death following exposure to isoflurane in the developing brain remains unclear [ , ].
Parthanatos is a newly discovered form of programmed cell death, characterized by excessive activation of poly ADP‐ribose polymerase 1 (PARP‐1) leading to the accumulation of PAR in the cytoplasm and the translocation of apoptosis inducing factor (AIF) to the nucleus [ ]. Studies have confirmed that parthanatos is associated with brain damage and the onset of neurodegenerative diseases, such as stroke, Alzheimer's, and Parkinson's [ , ]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced oxidative stress plays a crucial role in various types of cell death, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, and parthanatos. Research reports that accumulation of ROS in cells can induce parthanatos death mediated by PARP‐1 in mouse fibroblasts [ ]. Since the developing brain is more susceptible to oxidative stress‐induced neuronal death, excessive accumulation of ROS leading to neuronal death is considered one of the mechanisms of isoflurane‐induced neurotoxicity in the developing brain.
c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK), a member of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPKs) family, plays a critical role in physiological processes such as inflammatory responses and cell death [ , ]. As a protein kinase, JNK can be activated through phosphorylation under conditions like oxidative stress. Abundant evidence suggests that the activation of the JNK pathway can lead to apoptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy [ ]. Recent studies have shown that JNK activation under oxidative stress conditions is associated with parthanatos [ , ]. Research has also confirmed that the activation of the JNK pathway is related to isoflurane‐induced neurotoxicity in the developing brain [ , ].
Research has found that the antioxidant N‐Acety‐L‐cysteine (NAC), as a frequently used antioxidant, can inhibit the accumulation of ROS in cells [ , ]. NAC also has the capability to suppress the phosphorylation of JNK and reduce isoflurane‐induced neuronal cell death [ , ]. Our previous study also confirmed that NAC can inhibit ROS and alleviate parthanatos in glioma cells induced by hydrogen peroxide [ ]. However, whether isoflurane activates the JNK pathway during the parthanatos process remains unclear.
In this study, we used human SH‐SY5Y cells and rat RN‐h cells to investigate the correlation between isoflurane exposure and cell parthanatos; we also examined the effects of isoflurane exposure on the accumulation of ROS and the activation of the JNK pathway in cells. Additionally, through both cell experiments and animal studies, we confirmed that NAC provides protection against neuronal cell damage caused by isoflurane. Our study raises a noteworthy concern regarding the use of clinical anesthetics, but it also provides a potential solutive strategy.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Cell Culture
Human SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were purchased from Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (authenticated by STR profiling and confirmed negative for bacterial and mycoplasma contamination, Cat No. SCSP‐5014, Shanghai, China) and maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, HyClone, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin‑streptomycin) (HyClone). Rat primary hippocampal neuron cells RN‐h were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (CA, USA) and maintained in neurobasal medium (Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco), 0.5 mM L‐Glutamine (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), and 1% antibiotics (Gibco). All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with a constant airflow of 5% CO2.

2.2.

## Animals
The experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines set by the National Institutes of Health for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received the approval of the Ethics Committee at the First Hospital of Jilin University (No. 20200149). Rat pups of the Sprague‐Dawley breed, exactly 7 days postnatal (P7 rat pups), were obtained from the Yisi Institute of Laboratory Animal Technology (Jilin, China). Pups were maintained under standard laboratory conditions (12/12‐h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2°C, 60% humidity) with free access to food and water and allowed to stay in cages with maternal rats until sacrifice. To prevent unnecessary suffering of animals during the experiment, inhalation of isoflurane supplemented with cervical dislocation is established as humane endpoints. Specifically, during/after blood gas analysis and brain tissue extraction, and at the conclusion of the entire experiment (for spare experimental animals, detailed below), animals will be anesthetized with 5% isoflurane (more than 5 min) plus cervical dislocation. Criteria for determining animal death (including cessation of respiratory and cardiac activity, dilated pupils, and loss of toe pinch reflex) and all animal handling procedures adhere to standards described in the AVMA Guideline 2020 edition.

2.3.

## Isoflurane Exposure and Treatment
In the conducted in vitro experiments, cells were treated with or without isoflurane (Abbott Pharmaceutical Co., Shanghai, China) in a gas mixture of 5% CO2, 21% O2 and balanced N2 at 37°C in a tightly sealed plastic chamber (Billups‐Rothenberg, CA, USA). The humidified gas mixture was passed through a vaporizer calibrated for the specific agent at a flow rate initially set to 2 L/min for the first 5 min, and subsequently reduced to 0.5 L/min for the duration of the exposure period. Isoflurane treatments were administered to the cells at varying concentrations: 0% (control), 1%, 2%, and 4%, over different durations of 6, 12, and 24 h. An infrared monitor (Ohmeda 5330, Datex‐Ohmeda, Louisville, Co., KY, USA) was used to continuously track and maintain the levels of isoflurane, O2, and CO2 within the chamber, ensuring they stayed at the predetermined concentrations for the entire duration of the experiment. Furthermore, to investigate the impact of PARP‐1 and ROS in the process of neuronal cell death induced by isoflurane, a set of cells were pretreated with either the PARP‐1 inhibitor 3AB (500 μM/L, Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, US), the JNK inhibitor SP600125 (20 μM/L, Sigma‐Aldrich), or the antioxidant NAC (5 mM, Sigma‐Aldrich) [ , , ]. This pretreatment occurred 1 h before the exposure to 4% isoflurane for a period of 24 h.
In the conducted in vitro experiments, P7 rat pups, both male and female, were assigned randomly into four distinct groups (n = 15 per group): a control group, an isoflurane‐only group, a NAC‐only group, and a combination group receiving isoflurane or NAC (90 mg/kg) [ ]. The NAC was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 0.2 mL to the relevant groups 1 h before the commencement of isoflurane exposure. For the induction of anesthesia, the pups were placed in a hermetically sealed, transparent chamber. The induction began with 3% isoflurane mixed with 60% O2, continued until the pups lost their righting reflex. The anesthesia was then maintained at a lower concentration of 1.5% isoflurane combined with 60% O2, delivered at a flow rate of 2 L/min for a duration of 6 h. Pups in the control group were exposed only to a similar flow of O2 without isoflurane in an identical chamber setup. The concentrations of gases within the chamber were consistently monitored, as in the in vitro experiments. To absorb CO2, sodium lime was placed at the bottom of the chamber. Throughout the experiment, the rectal temperature of the pups was carefully monitored and maintained at a normothermic range of 37 ± 0.5°C, using a heating pad beneath the chamber. To mitigate respiratory distress during anesthesia with isoflurane, the pups were periodically removed from the chamber every 2 h for a brief period of stimulation through massage [ ]. At the end of anesthesia, six pups from each group were randomly selected and euthanized using 5% isoflurane in 60% O2 [ ]. Immediately after, thoracotomy was performed to collect arterial blood samples directly from the left ventricle for blood gas analysis, which was conducted using a GEM Premier 3000 blood gas analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory Co., MA, USA). The remaining pups were revived with 60% O2 at a flow rate of 3 L/min until they regained their righting reflex, after which they were returned to their maternal rats.

2.4.

## Brain Tissue Preparation and HE Staining
Seven days post‐isoflurane exposure, six pups were randomly selected from each group were euthanized with 5% isoflurane and subjected to a transcardial perfusion with ice‐cold PBS, followed by fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, rat brains were carefully extracted for further post‐fixation processing [ ]. This perfusion procedure was consistently performed by a skilled researcher experienced in such techniques. The remaining pups (n ≤ 3) in each group serve as spare experimental animals. At the conclusion of the entire experiment, these remaining pups will be euthanized (as described in the Animal section above).

2.5.

## Cell Viability Assay
SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h (1 × 104 cells/well) cells were cultivated in a 96‐well plate for 24 h and were treated with isoflurane at concentrations of either 1%, 2% or 4% for 24 h. The assessment of cell viability was conducted using the MTT assay (Beyotime Biotech, Jiangsu, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured with a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA) at 490 nm.

2.6.

## LDH Release Assay
SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h (1 × 104 cells/well) cells were cultivated in a 96‐well plate for 24 h and were treated with isoflurane at concentrations of either 1%, 2% or 4% for 24 h. The concentration of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) present in the culture medium was quantified utilizing a detection kit (Beyotime Biotech) and the absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

2.7.

## Determination of Intracellular ROS Levels
SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h (1 × 104 cells/well) cells were cultivated in a 96‐well plate for 24 h and were treated with 4% isoflurane for 24 h. Cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS, then incubated in 300 μL cell culture medium containing 20 μM redoxsensitive dye dichloro‐dihydro‐fluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) for 30 min at 37°C (ROS Assay Kit, Beyotime Biotech). ROS fluorescence was measured at an excitation and emission of 490 and 525 nm using microplate reader.

2.8.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining
Brain sections, 4 μm in thickness, underwent a deparaffinization process and were then immersed in distilled water before being stained with hematoxylin. Following this, they were rinsed in distilled water, subjected to a progressive dehydration through an alcohol gradient, and then stained with eosin. After a second dehydration and vitrification process, the sections were finally sealed with a resin‐based medium. An observer, who was not aware of the experimental details, examined the pyramidal neurons in the hippocampal CA1 area of each slice using a light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Neuronal death and injury were identified based on several criteria: sparse and disordered neuronal arrangement, cellular shrinkage, a pink hue in the cytoplasm, and pyknotic nuclei.

2.9.

## Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (JC‐1) Assay
The SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h (5 × 105 cells/well) cells were seeded in a 6‐well plate for 24 h and were treated with 4% isoflurane for another 24 h. Mitochondrial membrane potential Δψm was measured by a JC‐1 assay kit (Beyotime Biotech). Briefly, after incubation with the JC‐1 staining solution at 37°C in the cell incubator for 20 min, cells were washed with JC‐1 staining buffer two times and then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa and BD FACSymphony A5, BD Bioscience, NJ, USA).

2.10.

## Transfection of Small Interfering Rna (SiRNA)
SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h (1 × 106 cells/well) were cultivated in a 6‐well culture plate for 24 h. SiRNA was transfected to knock down PARP‐1 by using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The siRNA sequences targeting PARP‐1 were: sense 5′‐GCAAAGGCCAGGAUGGAAUUU‐3′ and antisense 5′‐AUUCCAUCCUGGCCUUUGCUU‐3′ (for SH‐SY5Y cells), and sense 5′‐GGACAAGGAGAGUAGUAAAUU‐3′ and antisense 5′‐UUUACUACUCUCCUUGUCCUU‐3′ (for RN‐h cells) (GenePharma, China). The siRNA sequences, sense 5′‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUUU‐3′ and 5′‐ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAAUU‐3′ were used as the siRNA negative control (GenePharma). At 48 h posttransfection, cells were harvested and the efficacy of target gene knockdown was evaluated by western blot analysis. All further experiments were conducted at 48 h posttransfection.

2.11.

## Western Blot
Equal amounts of 25 μg protein from each sample were loaded to 6%–12% SDS polyacrylamide gel and separated by electrophoresis. After that proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were incubated in 5% BSA (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China)/TBST solution at 37°C for 1 h and then washed in TBST (Beyotime Biotech) for 3x5min. Membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The primary antibodies were: anti‐PARP‐1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (Ab191217, 1:1000) (Abcam, MA, USA); anti‐PAR polymer mouse monoclonal antibody (AM80, 1:1000) (Sigma, MO, USA); anti‐AIF rabbit monoclonal antibody (ab32516, 1:1000) (Abcam); anti‐JNK rabbit monoclonal antibody (ab179461, 1:1000) (Abcam); anti‐phosphor‐JNK (ab76572, 1:1000) (Abcam); anti‐β‐actin (AA128, 1:3000) (Beyotime Biotech); anti histone‐3(17168‐1‐AP, 1:2000) (Proteintech, Hubei, China). After washing in TBST for 3x5min, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐coupled goat anti‐rabbit or goat anti‐mouse secondary antibody (#A0208 and #A0216, 1:1000, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) at room temperature for 45 min. The chemiluminescence signals of the target proteins were visualized using ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). Densitometric values of the blots were analyzed using Quantity One software (version 4.6, Bio‐Rad).

2.12.

## Statistical Analysis
SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Data were collected from at least three independent experiments and expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were analyzed with Student's t‐test (two groups) or a one‐way ANOVA followed by a post hoc LSD or Tukey's test (more than two groups). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Isoflurane Inhibited Cell Viabilities
To assess the impact of isoflurane on neuronal cell health, we exposed SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells to concentrations of 1%, 2%, and 4% isoflurane over periods of 6, 12, and 24 h. We then measured cell viability using the MTT assay. Results depicted in Figure  indicate a notable decline in the viability of both SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells at 2% and 4% isoflurane exposure, across all time points, in comparison to the control group with 0% isoflurane. Subsequently, the LDH method was employed to determine if isoflurane triggered cell death in these neurons. As illustrated in Figure  , there was a significant increase in the cell death ratio for both cell types when exposed to 2% and 4% isoflurane for more than 6 h, relative to the control. These findings imply that isoflurane exposure reduces cell viability and prompts cell death in neonatal neurons in a manner that is both concentration and duration dependent.
 
3.2.

## Isoflurane Induced Neuronal Cell Death Though PARP‐1‐Dependant Parthanatos
To elucidate the underlying mechanism leading to neuronal cell death induced by isoflurane, our investigation focused on the potential involvement of parthanatos. A key indicator of parthanatos is the overactivation of PARP‐1, as noted by Fatokun et al. [ ]. In this context, we measured the levels of PARP‐1, which is expressed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, as well as the levels of PAR polymer in the cytoplasm, a direct indicator of PARP‐1 activation. The data presented in Figure  show a significant increase in PARP‐1 and PAR polymer expression levels in SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells after 24 h exposure to 4% isoflurane, as determined by Western blot analysis.
 To confirm the involvement of PARP‐1 in the isoflurane‐triggered death of neuronal cells, we employed two approaches: pretreatment with the specific PARP‐1 inhibitor 3AB and silencing PARP‐1 expression using siRNA in both SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells. The efficacy of these interventions was evident through MTT and LDH assays. Results showed that either inhibiting PARP‐1 with 3AB or reducing its expression via siRNA notably enhanced cell viability (Figure  ) and reduced cell death (Figure  ) following isoflurane exposure. Furthermore, Western blot analysis demonstrated that both 3AB treatment and PARP‐1 siRNA effectively hindered the isoflurane‐induced upregulation of PARP‐1 and the subsequent accumulation of cytoplasmic PAR polymer (Figure  ).
During parthanatos, the PAR polymer targets AIF. Under normal circumstances, AIF resides in the mitochondria. However, a decline in Δψm, brought on by an excessive build‐up of PAR polymer, leads to the release of AIF from the mitochondria and its subsequent translocation into the nucleus, resulting in cell death. Consequently, we explored whether isoflurane‐induced accumulation of PAR polymer could cause mitochondrial damage. The results of JC‐1 staining, which was measured by flowcytometry, depicted in Figure  , demonstrate a notable decrease in red fluorescence in neuronal cells exposed to 4% isoflurane for 24 h, which signals the occurrence of mitochondrial damage. Consistent with the JC‐1 stainings, western blot analysis also revealed that after treatment with isoflurane, there was a significant increase in the expression of AIF in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of neuronal cells (Figure  ). Furthermore, both the 3AB treatment and silencing of PARP‐1 through siRNA were able to significantly reverse the isoflurane‐induced upregulation of AIF (Figure  ).
Summarizing these data, it appears that, through the activation of the PARP‐1/PAR pathway‐dependent parthanatos, isoflurane induces mitochondrial damage and mediates the accumulation of AIF in the nucleus, thereby leading to neuronal cell death.

3.3.

## Isoflurane Induced Accumulation of ROS and Activation of JNK Pathway in Neuronal Cells
Given that ROS accumulation can lead to changes in Δψm [ ], we hypothesize that abnormal ROS build‐up might be involved in isoflurane‐induced parthanatos and mitochondrial damage. To test this, we first measured the level of intracellular ROS using DCFH‐DA following exposure to isoflurane. The results showed a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels in neuronal cells after a 24 h exposure to 4% isoflurane (Figure  ).
 Our team's previous research indicated that the JNK signaling pathway plays a role in ROS‐induced parthanatos in H2O2‐treated glioma cells [ ]. In this context, we also investigated whether isoflurane could activate the JNK pathway in neuronal cells. As shown in Figure  , western blot results indicated a significant increase in p‐JNK expression levels in SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells after 24 h of treatment with 4% isoflurane. Moreover, the JNK‐specific inhibitor SP600125 notably enhanced cell survival rates and reduced cell mortality post‐isoflurane treatment (Figure  ). Additionally, pretreatment with SP600125 significantly prevented and reversed the activation of the isoflurane‐induced the JNK and PARP‐1/PAR/AIF signaling pathway, as shown in Figure  . These experimental findings suggest that isoflurane activates JNK through ROS accumulation, and the activated JNK is involved in parthanatos.

3.4.

## NAC Mitigates Isoflurane‐Induced Neuronal Cell Parthanatos
Numerous studies have demonstrated that NAC can effectively scavenge intercellular ROS [ , , ]. In our experiment, we investigated whether NAC could attenuate isoflurane‐induced neuronal cell parthanatos. We pretreated SH‐SY5Y and RN‐h cells with NAC 1 h before isoflurane exposure. MTT and LDH assays revealed that pretreatment with NAC significantly mitigated the toxic effects of isoflurane on neuronal cells, increasing cell survival and reducing cell mortality (Figure  ). Western blot results also showed that NAC significantly reversed the isoflurane‐induced activation of the PARP‐1/PAR/AIF signaling pathway (Figure  ). Additionally, NAC inhibited the accumulation of intracellular ROS (Figure  ) and the isoflurane‐induced activation of JNK (Figure  ). The results of flow cytometry also demonstrated that pretreatment with NAC was able to elevate Δψm (Figure  ). These experimental data suggest that NAC can inhibit neuronal cell parthanatos by suppressing the ROS accumulation and the ROS induced JNK signaling pathway.
 To determine whether the mitigation of isoflurane‐induced neurotoxicity by NAC is dependent on ROS scavenging, we selected nerve growth factor (NGF), which promotes neuronal growth and damage repair but is not reliant on ROS scavenging [ ], and another reagent, mitoquinone (MitoQ), which is known to have ROS scavenging capabilities and functions similarly to NAC [ ]. Upon comparing the effects of these two substances with those of NAC, we observed that NGF was unable to reverse the isoflurane‐induced neuronal cell damage (Figure  ), whereas MitoQ exhibited a protective effect similar to NAC: it enhanced cell viability and attenuated isoflurane‐induced parthanatos (Figure  ). This clearly demonstrates that isoflurane‐induced parthanatos in neuronal cells is ROS‐dependent, and that scavenging ROS can effectively inhibit this effect.

3.5.

## NAC Counteracts Isoflurane‐Induced Neuronal Damage in Hippocampus of Neonatal Rats
To further assess the protective effects of NAC on neuronal cells treated with isoflurane, animal experiments were conducted. The findings, as depicted in Figure  , showed that histopathological changes, such as sparse and disordered neuronal arrangement, cell shrinkage, pink cytoplasm, and pyknotic nuclei, were evident in the hippocampal CA1 region following isoflurane anesthesia in neonatal rats. These changes indicated significant neuronal death, with only about 78.9% survival of pyramidal neurons (Figure  ). In contrast, NAC pretreatment not only significantly reduced these morphological alterations induced by isoflurane but also markedly increased the survival rate of pyramidal neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region, as shown in Figure  . These results suggest that NAC effectively prevents isoflurane‐induced hippocampal neuronal damage (Figure  ).
 

4.

## Discussion
Compared to other inhaled anesthetics (e.g., sevoflurane), isoflurane is extensively utilized in both clinical surgical procedures and laboratory animal research owing to its cost‐effectiveness, superior anesthetic efficacy, and suitability for prolonged anesthetic maintenance [ ]. Like all anesthetics, isoflurane exerts broad‐ranging effects on cerebral physiology [ , ]. In recent years, the impact of isoflurane on brain nerves has attracted widespread attention from clinical researchers, especially those who apply isoflurane in surgeries involving infants and young children. Brambrink et al. found that subjecting 6‐day‐old rhesus macaques to 5 h of isoflurane exposure elicits a significant neuroapoptosis response, affecting both glial cells and neurons, across both the white and gray matter regions of the brain [ ]. Wang et al. reported that exposure to 3% isoflurane for 1 h increased neurodegeneration in the hippocampal CA1 area and the retrosplenial cortex in the developing brain of fetal rats. However, Dallasen et al. [ ] and Stratmann et al. [ ] demonstrated that exposure to isoflurane did not induce neuroapoptosis in the brains of young adult mice, regardless of the duration of exposure. They argue that the process of neural development in rodents is not directly comparable to that in humans. Therefore, caution is advised when extrapolating findings from rodent studies to pediatric anesthesia. Regardless, the dosage, exposure time, and recipient type (fetus/newborn/infant/adult) of inhalational anesthetics all play significant roles in the side effects of anesthesia. However, there are currently no definitive conclusions regarding these differences in research.
Recent studies have revealed that inhalational anesthetics induce neurotoxicity and trigger neuronal cell death via the parthanatos pathway. Wang et al. found that sevoflurane significantly hindered neurite growth and increased cell death in fetal rat brains by activating parthanatos [ ]. Similarly, Piao et al. observed an upregulation of parthanatos‐related proteins and neuronal cell death following sevoflurane exposure [ ]. However, whether parthanatos contributes to neuronal cell death triggered by isoflurane exposure and whether inhibiting parthanatos could serve as a potential strategy to mitigate isoflurane‐induced developmental neurotoxicity remain largely unknown. In this study, we found that exposure to 2% and 4% isoflurane for more than 6 h similarly inhibited neuronal cell viability and induced cell death. Further analysis revealed that isoflurane activated the PARP‐1/PAR signaling pathway in neuronal cells. Additionally, we observed a decrease in neuronal cell Δψm, release of AIF from the mitochondria, and its translocation into the nucleus following isoflurane treatment. These findings collectively indicate that isoflurane induces cell death through the parthanatos pathway.
The phenomenon of inhalational anesthetics inducing cells to produce large amounts of ROS has been widely reported [ , , ]. Our team has also discovered that both isoflurane and sevoflurane can induce excessive ROS in neuronal cells [ ]. Growing evidence suggests that ROS can trigger cell death in various ways, including pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and parthanatos [ ]. In our study, we also found that isoflurane can induce the production of ROS in neuronal cells and activate the JNK signaling pathway. The JNK signaling pathway demonstrates a notable responsiveness to stress triggers, readily activating in response to various external stimuli such as growth factors, cytokines, and environmental stressors [ ]. Evidence suggests that activated JNK can translocate into mitochondria to directly initiate apoptotic signaling [ ]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of the JNK signaling pathway plays a critical role in parthanatos in both glioma cells and neuronal cells [ , ]. Another research team has also reported crosstalk between JNK and PARP‐1 during the process of parthanatos [ ]. Zhang et al. revealed that in an H₂O₂‐induced non‐apoptotic cell death model, JNK1 contributes to the sustained activation of PARP‐1 and identified a direct protein‐protein interaction between JNK1 and PARP‐1 [ ]. Similarly, Zhu et al. reported the co‐localization of PARP1 and JNK in the nucleus during PARP1‐dependent cell death [ ]. These findings collectively highlight the significant role of the JNK‐PARP1 interaction in the regulation of cell death processes.
Various substances have been shown to counteract the neurotoxicity induced by isoflurane [ , , ]. NAC possesses antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and glutamate modulation properties, making it widely utilized in the treatment of psychiatric disorders [ ]. In this study, we confirmed that pretreatment of neural cells with NAC significantly alleviates isoflurane‐induced neuronal cell parthanatos in a ROS‐scavenging‐dependent manner. Animal experiments also demonstrated that NAC pretreatment significantly protects against damage to hippocampal CA1 region pyramidal neurons caused by isoflurane.
Our study confirms the neurotoxic effects of isoflurane on neural cells and proposes a protective strategy based on NAC. However, it should be noted that our study has certain limitations. The potential neurotoxicity of inhaled anesthetics may affect individuals’ behavior in adulthood if used during fetal or neonatal periods. Additionally, the impact of inhaled anesthetics on the epigenetics of neural cells and whether this effect has a memory effect remain unclear and beyond the scope of our study [ ]. These questions require further observation through more in‐depth animal experiments. However, as mentioned earlier, conclusions from animal experiments cannot be directly extrapolated to humans, presenting a significant challenge for future research in this area.

5.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our findings suggest that isoflurane can induce parthanatos in neuronal cells, and NAC can reduce ROS accumulation in neuronal cells, thereby mitigating the damage caused by isoflurane. Our work further highlights the potential neurotoxic effects of anesthetics, providing valuable insights for the clinical guidelines on the safe use of anesthetics.
