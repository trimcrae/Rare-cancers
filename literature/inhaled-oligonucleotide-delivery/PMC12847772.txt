Little is known regarding the endotype of BAO. Here we recruit patients with a primary diagnosis of bronchiectasis and co-existing asthma. The levels of interleukin (IL)-17C are positively correlated with the levels of IL-17A or group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) in peripheral blood samples from patients with BAO. An in vivo mouse model of Pseudomonas aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection followed by ovalbumin-induced asthma shows that IL-17C potentiates IL-17A expression via interacting with IL-17 receptor E in ILC3s. Additionally, ablation of Il17re in mice attenuates ILC3 responses and IL-17A-mediated asthma endotype switching towards neutrophilic asthma driven by P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection. Lastly, impaired epithelial barrier integrity by P. aeruginosa exposure is associated with IL-17C production in vitro. Collectively, our study implicates evidence for IL-17C governing IL-17A pathogenicity and promoting asthma endotype switching in bronchiectasis, implicating IL-17C as a potential therapeutic target for individuals with BAO.

Bronchiectasis and asthma can co-exist in the same patient, and the characteristics may be different from bronchiectasis alone. Here the authors characterise the function of ILC3 cells and how IL-17C potentiates IL-17A expression promoting a neutrophil dominated asthma endotype in mouse bronchiectasis-asthma models.

## Introduction
Non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis and asthma exhibit the same degree of heterogeneity in terms of clinical manifestations and outcomes – . Based on the related immune-inflammatory pathways, asthma endotypes broadly cover three subgroups: type 2-high, type 2-low, and mixed endotypes. Type 2-high asthma is characterized by a predominance of eosinophils, while neutrophilic asthma is typical of type 2-low asthma , where with release of cytokines such as interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-17 from T helper (Th)1, Th17 cells, group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) to recruit neutrophils and dampen the acquisition of eosinophils – , type 1 and type 3 immune responses predominate, leading to the suppression of type 2 immune responses . In clinical practice, bronchiectasis and asthma frequently coexist, however, it is uncertain whether there is a cause-effect relationship between these two disorders. The overlap between bronchiectasis and asthma can be viewed as asthma-bronchiectasis overlap (ABO), and bronchiectasis-asthma overlap (BAO) with asthma accounting for 3-8% of bronchiectasis cases – , which are both clinical issues. The endotype of BAO has yet to be deciphered, and identifying the underlying mechanisms is therefore of key importance in precision medicine. Moreover, managing patients with BAO poses challenges, particularly when it comes to the use of corticosteroids. Inhaled corticosteroids as conventional asthma therapy are not regularly recommended for treating bronchiectasis, as the potential inhibition of host immunity and further increased risk of infections , . The conflicting treatment approach poses a significant challenge for health care providers.
Literature on BAO is still scarce , , and the endotype of BAO remains to be fully defined, which has become a barrier to treatment decision-making. Furthermore, the lack of a mature animal model mimicking BAO has limited the availability of basic research data. Notably, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most common pathogen in the lower respiratory tract among bronchiectasis patients , , and it is also an independent risk factor for acute exacerbation in bronchiectasis . And P. aeruginosa chronic infection model has been widely used in a repertoire of research on bronchiectasis – . Recent studies have indicated that colonization of nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae promotes the development of Th17-related neutrophilic asthma with glucocorticoid resistance , . Both P. aeruginosa and nontypeable H. influenzae belong to Gram-negative bacteria, but the precise asthma endotype which P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection drives remains unclear.
Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) comprise group 1 ILCs (ILC1s), group 2 ILCs (ILC2s), and ILC3s, share the same features as three major effector CD4+ Th subsets, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 in the expression of transcription factors and cytokines, and act as pre-primed effector cells without de novo antigen-triggered effector cells differentiation – . The IL-17 family consists of six structurally related members (IL-17A, -17B, -17C, -17D, -17E, -17F). IL-17A and IL-17F are released by immune cells, mostly including ILC3, γδT17, Th17 cells, and signal through IL-17 receptor A (RA)/IL-17RC and IL-17RA/IL-17RD complexes, and thus participate in host defense, inflammatory diseases, and autoimmune disorders partly by mediating neutrophil accumulation , . Other IL-17 members -IL-17B, IL-17C, IL-17D, IL-17E- contribute to regulating tissue inflammation by interacting with receptor complexes in epithelial cells or specific lymphocyte subsets – . However, the involvement of IL-17A, IL-17C, and ILC3s in the induction and development of endotypes within the context of BAO has not been deciphered.
In this work, we report a model of P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection followed by ovalbumin-induced experimental asthma mimicking BAO to investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in the endotype of BAO. This study indicates evidence for IL-17C governing IL-17A pathogenicity and promoting asthma endotype switching in bronchiectasis, implicating IL-17C as a potential intervention target for individuals with BAO.

## Results

## Exposure to P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection drives asthma endotype switching towards neutrophilic asthma associated with Th17 cells and ILC3s
In the present study, 20 healthy controls (HC), 31 bronchiectasis patients, 30 asthmatics, and 31 patients with BAO were enrolled. And as shown in Supplementary Table  , there was no obvious significance concerning the sex distribution and the percentages of participants with smoking history across the groups (P = 0.1643, P = 0.4304). Patients with BAO had worse lung function, as indicated by lower FEV1, FVC, FVC% pred values, lower scores on Asthma Control Test (ACT), lower levels of eosinophils and higher percentages of IgE to be below 100 compared to asthmatic patients, but there was no significance of FEV1% pred, FEV1/FVC, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) values in patients with BAO (Supplementary Table  ). These characteristics suggested that BAO may tend to be type 2-low asthma with poor control.
As described in Fig.  , consistent with patients with a primary diagnosis of bronchiectasis and coexisting asthma within the context of BAO, we established a model of P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection through intratracheal (i.t.) instillation with P. aeruginosa-laden agar beads, followed by ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma to explore the specific endotype of BAO. Considering P. aeruginosa clinical strains are more efficient in building chronic infection compared with the PAO1 laboratory strain , we confirmed a slower growth rate of 52D5 compared to another three existing clinical strains isolated from bronchiectasis patients in our lab (Supplementary Fig.  ), and therefore adopting the 52D5 clinical strain in the subsequent experiments. To improve the efficiency of airway colonization with P. aeruginosa, we constructed the agar beads of diameter ranging between 100 and 200 μm . Additionally, compared to sterile agar beads, P. aeruginosa-laden beads seemed denser as shown by visible single bacterial cells interiorly (Supplementary Fig.  ). The establishment of chronic infection was successful as determined by recovery of more than 1000 colony forming units (CFUs) of P. aeruginosa from lung cultures at different time points post infection (Supplementary Fig.  ). There was no significance of survival rates post infection between P. aeruginosa and P. aeruginosa/OVA (PA/OVA) group (Supplementary Fig.  ). 
Hematoxylin & eosin (HE) staining showed more marked peribronchial infiltration of inflammatory cells in treatment with OVA, PA, and PA/OVA compared to controls (Fig.  ). Moreover, airway resistance was pronouncedly augmented in mice exposed to PA/OVA compared to mice exposed to phosphate-buffered saline/OVA (PBS/OVA) (Fig.  ). And the levels of serum IgE and Th2-related cytokines IL-5, IL-13 in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were reduced in mice exposed to PA/OVA compared to PBS/OVA, whereas the concentration of IL-17A was increased (Fig.  ). Consistent with pathological presentations, the total cell numbers in BALF were elevated in mice exposed to OVA, PA, and PA/OVA (Fig.  ). Furthermore, as depicted in Fig.  , the cell percentages and numbers of eosinophils whether in BALF or in lung tissues from PA/OVA group were consistently reduced compared to PBS/OVA group, whereas the cell percentages and numbers of neutrophils were elevated and the cell counts of alveolar macrophages (AM) were comparable (Fig.  ). These findings suggested that exposure to P. aeruginosa chronic infection drove the asthma endotype switching towards neutrophilic asthma.
It is known that Th2 cells, together with ILC2s, contribute to eosinophilic asthma by producing IL-5 and IL-13 , , while IL-17A-producing cells such as Th17, γδT17, and ILC3 cells contribute to airway neutrophilia via IL-17A , , , . Consistently, compared to PBS/OVA exposure, PA/OVA exposure resulted in mitigated Th2 cell responses and upregulated Th17 cell responses (Fig.  ). Additionally, through the assessment of ILCs based on the expression of transcription factors or cytokines, we found that the cell percentages and counts of ILC2s were consistently reduced in mice administered with PA/OVA in comparison with PBS/OVA-exposed mice, whereas ILC3s elevated (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, with the determination of ILC2s based on cellular surface receptors, exposure to PBS/OVA increased the levels of ILC2s, whereas exposure to PA/OVA decreased (Supplementary Fig.  ). The percentages of IL-17A-producing cells among CD3+CD4- lymphocytes which might be regarded as γδT17 cells were increased in PA/OVA-treated mice compared to PBS/OVA-treated mice, but the cell numbers weren’t affected (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, mRNA level of Il17a was also significantly upregulated in lungs of mice administered with PA/OVA compared to PBS/OVA (Fig.  ). Notably, we found no pairwise significance of the levels of Th1 and ILC1 cells among four groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, human data also indicated that patients with BAO presented higher levels of ILC3s compared to asthmatic patients or healthy participants (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, in line with the reported link of P. aeruginosa infection to Th2 responses , , our data showed raised TSLP in P. aeruginosa-infected patients with bronchiectasis or BAO, whereas no significance of CCL-17 levels was observed (Supplementary Fig.  ). Thus, exposure to P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection led to a shift in asthma endotype towards neutrophilic asthma associated with IL-17A-producing Th17 cells and ILC3s. 

## IL-17A mediates asthma endotype switching towards neutrophilic asthma driven by P. aeruginosa chronic exposure
To understand the role of IL-17A in asthma endotype switching, an IL-17A neutralizing antibody (anti-IL17A) was administered to determine whether P. aeruginosa exposure-driven asthma endotype could be rescued as depicted in Fig.  . The administration of anti-IL17A attenuated peribronchial infiltrates of inflammatory cells and epithelium thickening induced by PA/OVA exposure, however, the effect was not observed in OVA group (Fig.  ). Furthermore, IL-17A neutralization led to a mild increase in the levels of IgE, IL-5, IL-13, eosinophils, Th2 and ILC2 cells, whereas a significant decrease in the levels of IL-17A, total BALF cells, neutrophils, Th17 and ILC3 cells in the PA/OVA group (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, although the administration of anti-IL17A augmented the ratio of AM from mice treated with PA/OVA, the total numbers of AM, Th1, and ILC1 cells weren’t affected (Supplementary Fig.  ). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that IL-17A mediated a shift in asthma endotype towards neutrophilic asthma driven by P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection. 

## Epithelial barrier integrity is impaired in a P. aeruginosa-exposed asthma model
Previous studies have reported the defective epithelial barrier integrity among patients with asthma , . Consistent with the mouse model, following pre-stimulation with P. aeruginosa, A549 cells were treated with OVA or IL-13 to resemble the airway epithelium within the context of BAO. We found that PA/OVA or P. aeruginosa/IL-13 (PA/IL13) exposure resulted in marked morphological changes in A549 cells from a typical multilateral paving stone-like appearance to a round or fusiform appearance accompanied by protrusions (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, exposure to PA/OVA or PA significantly increased the intercellular permeability compared to OVA treatment (Fig.  ). Moreover, the mRNA and protein levels of tight junction (TJ) markers, ZO-2, ZO-3, Claudin-18 and adhesion junction (AJ) marker, E-cadherin were remarkably decreased after PA/OVA or PA/IL13 exposure compared to OVA or IL-13 exposure (Fig.  ). 
Airway epithelium, as the first line of the physical barrier in the host, produces several alarmins such as TSLP . Here we observed that exposure to PA/OVA or PA/IL13 augmented Tslp expression compared to the administration of OVA or IL-13 (Fig.  ), which corresponds to the upregulated TSLP expression in human corneal epithelial cells exposed to P. aeruginosa infection . In addition, the expression of ZO-3 and Claudin-18 located at the most apical part of neighboring epithelial cells were both decreased in the lungs of mice exposed to PA/OVA compared to OVA or their control (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Altogether, P. aeruginosa exposure resulted in a loss in epithelial barrier integrity characterized by the increased intercellular permeability and the decreased cell-cell adhesions.

## The level of IL-17C positively correlates with IL-17A and is associated with the defective epithelial barrier integrity
Given the critical role of IL-17A in the model, we further explored the potential upstream regulator of IL-17A expression. Previous researches have demonstrated that IL-17C or IL-17B can regulate the activation of Th17 cells via a direct or indirect manner , , , . Moreover, IL-17B can inhibit eosinophilic inflammation by antagonizing IL-17E (IL-25) expression associated with type 2-high asthma , . We then examined the expression of IL-17 family members in P. aeruginosa chronic infection-driven asthma model. We found that Il17c expression was significantly upregulated in lung tissues of PA/OVA group compared with OVA group, whereas no significance of Il17b, Il17d, and Il25 mRNA expression was detected (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, we observed significantly elevated expression of Il17c followed by remarkable upregulation in Il17a expression at the indicated time points in lung tissues of mice initially exposed to P. aeruginosa infection compared to their control (Fig.  ). Furthermore, compared with OVA exposure, PA/OVA exposure elevated the concentration of IL-17C in BALF (Fig.  ). Consistently, we found more productions of IL-17A and IL-17C in serum from patients with BAO than asthmatics (Fig.  ). Notably, there was a consistently positive correlation between IL-17C and IL-17A levels (Fig.  ). More importantly, IL-17C levels were also positively correlated with ILC3 levels in patients with BAO (Fig.  ). 
In line with animal and human data, IL-17C expression was upregulated in both BEAS-2B and A549 cells exposed to medium with a certain concentration of OVA following P. aeruginosa pretreatment (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, exposure to PA/OVA or PA/IL13 induced significantly elevated the expression of IL-17C compared to the treatment with OVA or IL-13 (Fig.  ). MMP-9, secreted partly from neutrophils, is reported to modulate epithelial junction integrity . Interestingly, exposure to PA/OVA or PA/IL13 expressed more Mmp9 than OVA or IL-13 administration (Fig.  ). Notably, the pretreatment of MMP-9 inhibitor HY attenuated the impaired epithelial integrity and the upregulation of IL-17C expression induced by PA/OVA or PA/IL13 exposure (Supplementary Fig.  and Fig.  ). Together, MMP-9 could modulate epithelial barrier integrity and thus regulating IL-17C expression.
Collectively, these data indicated that the levels of IL-17C positively correlated with IL-17A or ILC3 levels. Defective epithelial barrier integrity was associated with IL-17C production via MMP-9-dependent manner.

## Ablation of Il17re inhibits IL-17A production in neutrophilic asthma driven by chronic infection with P. aeruginosa
It is known that IL-17C signals through IL-17RA/IL-17RE complex . To investigate the potential role of IL-17C in asthma endotype switching, airway inflammation was evaluated in Il17re-/- mice. Exposure to PA/OVA exhibited milder peribronchial inflammatory infiltrates in Il17re-/- mice than wild-type (WT) littermates, but ablation of Il17re didn’t affect inflammatory infiltrates in the OVA group (Fig.  ). Compared to WT littermates, CFU counts in the whole lung were markedly decreased in Il17re-/- mice exposed to PA/OVA or PA (Fig.  ). Moreover, ablation of Il17re reduced the levels of IL-17A, total cell numbers, the cell counts of neutrophils, Th17 and ILC3 cells in mice exposed to PA/OVA, but failed to impact the levels of IL-5, IL-13, eosinophils, Th2 and ILC2 cells other than a mild increase in IgE levels (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, total numbers of AM were not affected by ablation of Il17re (Supplementary Fig.  ). Lastly, we found that interfering Il17c expression amplified the upregulated expression of Tslp in exposure to PA/OVA (Fig.  ). Collectively, these data indicated that ablation of Il17re attenuated neutrophilic airway inflammation driven by P. aeruginosa chronic infection by preventing IL-17A production derived from Th17 cells and ILC3s. 

## IL-17C binds directly to ILC3s through interacting with IL-17RA-IL-17RE complex in lung tissues and potentiates IL-17A expression
To investigate the mechanisms by which IL-17C regulates IL-17A expression in ILC3s, we initially searched for the potential signaling targets in ILC3s. Interestingly, according to published RNA-seq data , a significant increase in the expression of Il17re and Il17ra was supported in ILC3s of small intestine from WT mice, with Cd93 and Vipr2 expression being positive controls, but small amounts of transcripts expression of other IL-17R family members Il17rb, Il17rc, and Il17rd were observed (Fig.  ). We observed that IL-17RE was situated on the surface of ILC3s and 98.9% among sorted ILC3s were RORγt+ (Fig.  ). Next, we validated the binding capacity of IL-17A or IL-17C to ILC3s and identified more IL-17C or His labeling IL-17C binding to ILC3s, whereas ablation of Il17re attenuated the binding capacity (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we found that following IL-17C treatment, ILC3s from C57BL/6 mice, compared to those from PBS-injected control mice, showed the elevated expression of IL-17A, whereas the comparable expression of the other transcripts associated with ILC3 function (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, we discovered that IL-17RE expression was comparable among ILC3 cell populations, whereas IL-17RE+ ILC3s expressed more IL-17A than IL-17RE- ILC3s in lung tissues (Supplementary Fig.  and Fig.  ). Lastly, we found that IL-17RE was mostly expressed in T cells, macrophages/dendritic cells (MΦ/DC), and ILC3s (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these findings indicated that IL-17C can interact with IL-17RE in ILC3s and potentiate ILC3 responses. 

## Discussion
In this study, we recruit patients with a primary diagnosis of bronchiectasis and coexisting asthma and reveal the positive correlation between the high levels of IL-17C and IL-17A or ILC3 among these patients. Correspondingly, we establish a model of P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection followed by experimental asthma mimicking BAO to investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in the endotype of BAO. By directly interacting with IL-17RE in ILC3s, IL-17C potentiates IL-17A expression, which mediates asthma endotype switching from eosinophilic asthma towards neutrophilic asthma driven by P. aeruginosa chronic infection. Our study not only identifies the relation of the epithelial barrier integrity to IL-17C production via the involvement of MMP-9 secreted partly from neutrophils, but also uncovers the role of IL-17C in negatively regulating epithelium-derived Tslp expression (Supplementary Fig.  ).
The management of BAO has become an important clinical issue to be addressed in the present study. Mao et al. showed that concomitant asthma is an independent risk factor for acute exacerbations in bronchiectasis. Chen et al. identified FeNO values as a predictor for distinguishing between bronchiectasis with or without asthma. Recent study indicated that BAO is associated with the increased risk of acute exacerbations and improved outcomes with inhaled corticosteroid use . However, little is known regarding the endotype within the context of BAO. In our study, the endotype in patients with BAO may tend to be type 2-low asthma characterized by lower eosinophil counts, higher levels of IL-17A, ILC3s and difficultly controlling asthma symptoms with the lack of an effective regimen for treatment, and therefore suggesting the importance of investigating mechanisms and exploring potential treatment for BAO. Notably, another potential factor that could influence asthma endotype might be smoking history being associated with airway inflammation. Nevertheless, the percentages of participants with smoking history were comparable across the groups involved in the study. With a limited sample size, subgroup analysis based on smoking history couldn’t be performed.
Our findings linked P. aeruginosa infection to Th2 responses. P. aeruginosa infection is correlated with significantly higher levels of Th2 cells and CCL-17 in part of cystic fibrosis patients , . Th2-endotype of bronchiectasis characterized by raised blood eosinophils or FeNO has been increasingly recognized. Evidence of elevated TSLP in P. aeruginosa-infected patients with bronchiectasis or BAO might facilitate us to provide precision medicine approach based on Th2 responses for these patients. Lessons from cystic fibrosis management could offer valuable insights into the treatment for P. aeruginosa infection in these patients. This could be an important avenue for further research.
The roles of ILC3s and IL-17A in asthma have been widely reported. An increase in the cell number of IL-17A+ ILC3s has been reported in BALF from patients with severe asthma . ILC3 gene signatures are enriched in the nasal epithelium of asthmatics . And IL-17-high group with asthma has been identified . Consistent with human data, we found by adopting an animal model that chronic infection with P. aeruginosa drove asthma endotype switching towards neutrophilic asthma associated with IL-17A-producing Th17 cells and ILC3s, as well as significant airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), whereas Th1 and ILC1 cells associated with type 1 immune responses have not been affected. Importantly, by IL-17A neutralization, we reported that IL-17A mediated asthma endotype switching in the model, which is in line with the role of IL-17A in mediating neutrophil accumulation and inhibiting eosinophilic inflammation – , . Notably, Busse et al. indicated that a clinical trial aimed at blocking the IL-17 pathway doesn’t demonstrate improvement of asthma symptoms in asthmatics. One caveat in the latter study is that IL-17-high group (in which promising anti-IL-17 antibody efficacy is expected) was not defined among these patients, and it is likely that IL-17-targeted therapy may be more beneficial for specific IL-17-high asthmatic patients. Currently, the potential mechanisms regulating IL-17A expression in P. aeruginosa chronic infection-driven neutrophilic asthma require further investigation.
Like IL-25 and IL-17D, IL-17C is also produced by epithelial cells. However, the role of IL-17C in regulating tissue inflammation has been controversial. IL-17C can promote P. aeruginosa acute infection-induced neutrophilic airway inflammation . Vella et al. showed that ablation of IL-17RE prevents polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (pIC)-induced neutrophil recruitment and lung inflammation in experimental allergic asthma. Additionally, IL-17C promotes Th17 cell responses and Th17 cell-dependent experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, glomerulonephritis as well as kidney ischemia/reperfusion injury , , . On the other hand, IL-17C mitigates murine acute graft-vs.-host disease by promoting Treg differentiation . Moreover, IL-17C deficiency exacerbates intestinal inflammation associated with γδT17 and Th17 cell responses in acute experimental colitis . Although IL-17C has been implicated in the development of various conditions, its role in airway inflammation of BAO remains further investigation.
The airway epithelium is the first line of defense against external stimulus and then releases inflammatory mediators . Neisseria cultivated from bronchiectasis patients promotes the loss of epithelial integrity . Exoproteins from P. aeruginosa clinical isolates can induce barrier disruption in human nasal epithelial cells . Consistent with these researches, our finding uncovered the relation of epithelial barrier integrity to IL-17C production via the involvement of MMP-9, which is secreted partly from neutrophils. TSLP contributes to type 2-high asthma by regulating the function of Th2 cells and ILC2 – . Our study identified the negative modulation of Tslp expression by Il17c, which may support the inhibition of eosinophilic inflammation in mice exposed to PA/OVA with higher Il17c expression. Notably, we identified that elevated IL-17C levels positively correlated with the augmented levels of IL-17A and ILC3 in patients with BAO. Ablation of Il17re attenuated the bacterial loads, Th17 cell responses, ILC3 response, and IL-17A-mediated neutrophilic lung inflammation, which coincided with that IL-17C can promote the differentiation of CD4+ T cells towards Th17 cells by interacting with IL-17RE . To the best of our current knowledge, there are few studies addressing the relation of IL-17C to IL-17A expression in ILC3s. Our data exhibited that Il17ra and Il17re were relatively highly expressed in ILC3s, and IL-17RE was situated on the surface of ILC3s. We showed that IL-17C directly bound to ILC3s by interacting with IL-17RE and subsequently potentiated IL-17A expression. Furthermore, we also confirmed that IL-17RE was mostly localized on the surface of not only T cells but also ILC3s, and more importantly, IL-17RE+ ILC3s expressed more IL-17A.
The present study does have some limitations. Firstly, sputum cytology reflecting human airway inflammation is still lacking and need further investigation. Secondly, healthy controls should take a systematic and thorough physical examination, such as spirometry and the ACT questionnaire, although these have been identified by chest imaging. Thirdly, it should be noted that the conditional deletion of Il17re in ILC3s has yet to be adopted to determine whether IL-17RE is essential for ILC3 responses. Fourthly, RNA-seq of IL-17RE+ and IL-17RE- ILC3s could be supplemented to further understand how IL-17RE-mediated signaling affects ILC3 responses. Fifthly, the underlying mechanisms involving in the reduced bacterial loads in Il17re-/- mice require further investigation. Sixthly, and the last, we didn’t rule out the potential involvement of principle cells associated with type 1 and type 3 immune responses, such as Tc1, Tc17 cells, in asthma endotype switching driven by P. aeruginosa chronic infection, although the roles of other cells, such as Th1, ILC1, Th17, ILC3, have been investigated. Therefore, the conclusion that IL-17C governs IL-17A pathogenicity by interacting with ILC3s should be interpreted cautiously due to this limitation.
In conclusion, we identify that impaired epithelial barrier integrity is associated with IL-17C production via the involvement of MMP-9, which originates in part from neutrophils. By interacting with IL-17RE, IL-17C potentiates ILC3 responses. Furthermore, ablation of Il17re attenuates ILC3 responses and IL-17A-mediated asthma endotype switching from eosinophilic asthma towards neutrophilic asthma driven by P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection. Here, these findings implicate evidence for IL-17C governing IL-17A pathogenicity and promoting asthma endotype switching in bronchiectasis and supply important clues for a potential strategy against airway inflammation in patients with BAO.

## Methods

## Patients’ samples collection
Human samples were collected from healthy participants, patients with asthma, bronchiectasis, or BAO in Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. The diagnosis of asthma was based on the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines . Asthma control was assessed using the ACT score. The diagnosis of idiopathic bronchiectasis was referred to the non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis guideline published by the British Thoracic Society in 2019 . Patients with BAO met one of the following diagnostic criteria: (1) Bronchiectasis was clinically reported prior to asthma; (2) Asthma was newly diagnosed according to the bronchodilator reversibility test on the basis of a primary diagnosis of bronchiectasis. Patients with cystic fibrosis or allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) were excluded from the study. Participants without respiratory symptoms and an indication of acute, active, as well as chronic pulmonary lesions were recruited as healthy controls. The clinical characteristics of the patients and healthy controls are shown in Supplementary Table  . Study protocols for research related to human samples, including informed consent, publication of demographic data, and associated corresponding source data, were approved by the ethics committees of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (approval number K21-318). This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles, and signed informed consent was obtained from all participants.

## Mice strains and animal experiments
Il17re-/- mice (Il17retm1Lex/Mmucd) on B6N.129S5 background were provided by Prof. Youcun Qian, Chinese Academy of Sciences. C57BL/6 mice were bought from Shanghai SLRC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free animal facilities with a 12-h light and dark circle, temperature of 20–25 °C, and relative humidity of 50% at the Laboratory Animal Center of Tongji University. All animals were age-matched and sex-matched, and then randomized into different groups. The age for all strains of mice was 6–8 weeks. OVA-induced asthma is known to result in eosinophilic inflammation and induction of Th2 cytokines in BALF. To construct a model of P. aeruginosa chronic lower respiratory tract infection followed by experimental asthma mimicking BAO, following i.t. instillation with P. aeruginosa-laden agar beads (2 × 106 CFUs in 35 μl PBS) to establish chronic infection with P. aeruginosa , 5 days later Il17re-/- mice and wild type (WT) littermates or C57BL/6 mice entered into the establishment of an experimental asthma model , in which intraperitoneal injection of 20 μg of OVA (Sigma-Aldrich) mixed with 2 mg of alum (Thermo Scientific) was given, followed 6 days later by continuous three airway challenges with 1% OVA. For IL-17A neutralization, 30 μg of Anti-IL17A (BE0173, BioXcell) or IgG1 isotype control (BE0083, BioXcell) was intratracheally given into the four groups, that is, PBS, OVA, PA, and PA/OVA at 2 h before airway challenge with 1% OVA. For IL-17C administration, 1 μg of IL-17C (2306-ML, R&D) or PBS was intratracheally instilled into C57BL/6 mice every 2 days for a total of five times. All mice were sacrificed 24 h post the last airway challenge or the last instillation via isoflurane inhalation, and samples, including serum, BALF, and lung tissues, were collected for further analysis. Apart from the quantification of IgE in serum and the assessment of indicators, including bacterial load, IL-17 expression, immune and inflammatory cell infiltrates in lung tissues, the levels of IL−5, IL-13, IL-17C, IL-17A, and cell typing were determined in BALF. Experiments were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki conventions for the use and care of animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji University (approval number TJBE00120101).

## Measurement of AHR
In PA/OVA-induced airway inflammation model, changes in AHR after allergen exposure were determined by invasive measurement using R&C system (EMMS). At 9 days post OVA sensitization, the trachea was intubated, and then the lungs were mechanically ventilated. Airway resistance was regarded as an indicator of AHR and measured after increasing doses (0, 16, 32, 64, 256 mg/ml) of aerosolized acetylcholine chloride (Ach, A6625, Sigma-Aldrich).

## Cell culture and treatment
The A549 lung epithelial cell line (SCSP-503, Chinese Academy of Sciences) and BEAS-2B bronchial epithelial cells (SCSP-5067, Chinese Academy of Sciences) obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, were both cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). Unless otherwise indicated, P. aeruginosa clinical strain 52D5 was employed in the subsequent experiments. Following pretreatment with live 52D5 (multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 1) for 5 h, the previous medium was replaced by medium containing OVA at various concentrations (50, 100, 250 μg/ml) or added with exogenous IL-13 (PeproTech, 100 ng/ml). Finally, cells and supernatants were both collected for further analysis after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Il17c small interfering RNA (siRNA) used in the experiment was synthesized by GenePharma, and the sequence (5′–3′) was as follows: GCGAGACAGCUGCGCUCAACU; UUGAGCGCAGCUGUCUCGCGG. For Il17c siRNA transfection in A549 cells, GP-transfection-Mate (G04008, GenePharma) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the inhibition of MMP-9 in A549 cells, cells were primed with the inhibitor (HY-135232, MCE, 50 μM) for 2 h before P. aeruginosa stimulation.

## Bacterial growth curve
Growth rates of P. aeruginosa strains were assessed as described by Montanari . Briefly, P. aeruginosa strains were grown overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (12780052, Invitrogen), and each culture was diluted at 1:200 and standardized to an OD600 in the range of 0.01 to 0.02. Samples were taken every 0.5 or 1 h and the OD600 was determined. Growth curves were plotted according to the elapsed time and the corresponding OD600 value.

## Bacterial culture and preparation
The P. aeruginosa clinical strain 52D5 was thawed from −80 °C, cultured in LB broth overnight, and then re-grown for 8 h at a diluted factor of 50 to reach the mid-log phase. Finally, Bacteria were washed and resuspended in PBS for cell pre-stimulation. P. aeruginosa-laden agar-beads were made as described previously . Briefly, a 1:9 (v/v) mixture of bacterial suspension and melting broth was added to heavy mineral oil (330760, Sigma-Aldrich) that was equilibrated at 55 °C, rapidly stirred for 6 min at room temperature, then cooled 35 min at 4 °C, followed by washing six times in PBS. CFUs were enumerated on an aliquot of homogenized bead slurry via a 10-fold serial dilution. Sterile beads were prepared using PBS.

## Quantification of bacterial loads in lung tissues
The whole lung tissue was removed and then homogenized in 1 ml of sterile PBS. And 0.1 ml of the homogenate was transferred for a 10-fold serial dilution, followed by inoculation on the LB agar plates. Finally, bacterial colonies were counted after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h.

## Lung histology
Lung lobes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned, followed by HE staining. An individual blinded to experimental conditions assessed airway inflammation. The average of histological scoring on airway inflammation by randomly selecting five visual fields of each lung section from at least three mice per group under a light microscope (Olympus) was used for further analysis.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells or lung tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the instructions, followed by reverse transcription using a cDNA synthesis kit (Yeasen). qPCR analysis was performed with a SYBR RT-PCR kit (Yeasen) on Light Cycler 480 (Roche). Gapdh was used as an internal reference gene for data normalization. The primer sequences were shown in the Supplementary Table  .

## Western blotting
The samples were separated by electrophoresis through a 4–12% Precast Bis-Tris gel (Tanon) and electrically transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% fat-free milk, followed by incubation with the primary antibodies at 4 °C and secondary antibodies at room temperature, as well as repeated washes with TBST solution (0.1% Tris, 0.1% Tween-20). And finally, proteins were visualized using Tanon imager.

## ELISA
IL-17A, IL-17C, TSLP, CCL17 in human samples and IgE, IL−5, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-17C in mouse samples were measured using an ELISA kit as shown in the Supplementary Table  . Assays were referred to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## BALF collection
The lavage fluid was collected with 0.4 ml sterile PBS imjected into the whole lung each time for a total of three times, immediately stored in ice, and then centrifuged at 350 × g for 7 min. The supernatants were maintained at −80 °C for cytokine assays. The cell pellets were removed of red blood cells, re-suspended in PBS, and then taken out an aliquot for cell number counting. Finally, the remaining cell suspension was prepared for flow cytometry analysis.

## Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolation
PBMCs were isolated from the peripheral blood samples collected from the recruited participants with Ficoll Paque PLUS (GE healthcare) using density gradient centrifugation layering according to manufacturer’s protocols. Cells were suspended with a centrifuge at 350 × g and then stored in −80 °C for further flow cytometry analysis. Human sera were available by centrifuging at 3000 rpm and were also stored in −80 °C for cytokine assays.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Lung tissues of the indicated mice were prepared into a single cell suspension with the digestion buffer (sterile PBS supplemented with 2.5 mg/mL type 2 collagenase, 1% DNase Ⅰ). After red blood cell lysis and filtering, cells were centrifuged, and subsequently pellets were suspended with 30% (v/v) Percoll solution (GE healthcare). After centrifuging, cell pellets were washed and re-suspended in sterile PBS containing 3% (v/v) FBS, 0.05 mM EDTA, 100 U/ml penicillin, and streptomycin. An aliquot of suspension was used for cell number counts, and the remaining cells were stimulated with a cell activation cocktail with Brefeldin A (Biolegend) for 4 h prior to staining for flow cytometry analysis by CytoFLEX LX (BECKMAN COULTER). Additionally, fluorescein-conjugated antibodies against CD45 (30-F11, 103116), Lineage (17A2; RB6-8C5; RA3-6B2; Ter-119; M1/70, 133310), CD90.2 (30-H12, 105305), RORγt (AFKJS-9, 12-6988-82), GATA3 (16E10A23, 653806), T-bet (4B10, 644806), EOMES (W17001A, 157703), CD127 (A7R34, 135022), CD3 (17A2, 100218), CD4 (GK1.5, 100443), IL-5 (TRFK5, 504306), IL-13 (W17010B, 159403), IL-17A (TC11-18H10.1, 506930), IFN-γ (XMG1.2, 505806), IL-17RE (944904, FAB7997R), His (J095G46, 362605), IL-17C (311522, IC23061R) were purchased from Biolegend, eBioscience or R&D. Cell surface staining was carried out by incubating cells with the antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C after blocking with Fc antibody. GATA3, RORγt, T-bet, EOMES, IL-17A, IL-5, IL-13, and IFN-γ staining was performed using the intracellular transcription factor kit (Invitrogen) or the cytokine staining kit (BD Biosciences). For flow cytometry sorting of ILC3s, FACSAria III (BD Biosciences) was employed in the experiment. The detailed reagents were shown in Supplementary Table  . Flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.7.2)

## Immunohistochemistry Staining
Lung sections were completely immersed into Tris-EDTA (pH = 8.0, Servicebio) or citric acid (pH = 6.0, Servicebio) antigen retrieval solution and then heated with a microwave for 15–20 min, followed by blocked by 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15–20 min. After 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking, the sections were incubated with antibodies against ZO-3 (Invitrogen) or Claudin-18 (Abcam) at 4 °C overnight, followed by colorimetric detection using DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. The sections were examined using a light microscope and quantified by the Image-Pro Plus 6.0. An individual blinded to experimental conditions performed a semiquantified analysis.

## Immunofluorescence microscope
Sorted ILC3s from lymphoid cells were cultured on poly-L-lysine-coated plates and were then fixed and blocked with 5% (v/v) goat serum (Yeasen) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with an antibody against IL-17RE (bioss) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the cells were stained with DAPI (Beyotime) for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were rinsed with PBS for three times, 5 min each time. Images were captured under a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Zeiss).

## Binding assay
For the binding assay, the sorted ILC3s ex vivo were treated with exogenous IL-17A (Biolegend), IL-17C (R&D), IL-17C-His (ACRO biosystems), or His (Beyotime) individually for 30 min, followed by staining with the indicated antibodies conjugated with proper fluorescein for flow cytometry analysis.

## Epithelial permeability measurement
A549 cells were grown in 24-well Transwell (Corning Costar) inserts until 90% confluence, and then paracellular flux across the epithelium was measured using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled 4-kDa dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, 0.5 mg/ml) added to the upper chamber followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, intercellular permeability was indicated by fluorescence intensity of FITC-dextran.

## Statistical analysis
The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed for analyzing the distribution of quantitative variables. Normally distributed data were presented as mean (SD), or mean ± SEM, and non-normally distributed data were presented as median (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and the Chi-square test was adopted for the comparisons. Spearman correlation was used to investigate the relationship between the level of IL-17C and IL-17A or ILC3s in serum from patients. Log-rank test was performed to evaluate the difference in the comparison of survival curves. The statistical differences in both groups were compared by using Mann-Whitney test, paired or unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas for the comparison of more than two groups, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Tukey’s, Dunn’s, or LSD multiple comparisons test was applied. All statistical analysis was performed by the GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Each value in the statistical graph represents an individual biological sample, where technical replicates have been averaged. Two-sided significance level of 0.05 was chosen for all tests due to uncertainty in the preset results of experiments relevant with the study (in which identifying a > b or a < b was absolutely correct).
