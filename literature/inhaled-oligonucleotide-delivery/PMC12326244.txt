## Abstract
Intestinal ischemia-reperfusion (II/R) injury is a significant clinical concern with high mortality rates. Mitochondria play a crucial role in this process, and maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis is a potential treatment target. SENP1 is a de-SUMOylated hydrolase that may regulate SIRT3, a major mitochondrial deacetylase. However, the role of SENP1 and SIRT3 in II/R remains unclear. Employing a combination of in vitro cell culture experiments utilizing Caco-2 cells and in vivo II/R models with SD rats, along with an array of molecular biology techniques such as gene silencing, protein detection methods, immunoprecipitation, histological analysis, and functional assays, this study delved into the role of SENP1 and SIRT3 in intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury. Statistical analysis was meticulously conducted to evaluate the significance of the obtained results. SENP1 and SIRT3 are co-expressed and interact in Caco-2 cells. In models of II/R, the expression of SENP1 increased while that of SIRT3 decreased. Reducing SENP1 expression by siRNA or enteral nutrition intervention with bupropion alleviated intestinal II/R injury, reduced mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress, and improved the number and function of mitochondria. Our study demonstrates the importance of SENP1 and SIRT3 in intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury. Reducing SENP1 expression through siRNA or enteral nutrition intervention shows promise as a potential therapeutic approach. This research provides new insights into the mechanism of II/R injury and paves the way for further investigations.
Keywords: intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury, SENP1, SIRT3, mitochondrial homeostasis, enteral nutrition

## Introduction
Intestinal ischemia-reperfusion (II/R) injury is a common clinicopathological process that primarily occurs in situations such as shock, trauma, intestinal obstruction, and organ transplantation and has a high mortality rate.( – ) II/R not only directly impairs the intestinal structure and function but also may cause secondary damage to distant organs and even lead to multiple organ failure, seriously endangering the life and health of patients.( ) In healthy individuals, the intestinal epithelium possesses a series of defense barrier mechanisms that can effectively prevent harmful substances from passing through the intestinal barrier. However, due to the lack of intestinal motility and the destruction of the intestinal mucosal epithelial barrier after II/R injury, bacteria and endotoxins in the intestinal lumen migrate into the bloodstream and lymphatic circulation and thereby reach systemic tissues and organs, resulting in localized and even systemic inflammation.( )
Mitochondria, known as the intracellular “energy factories”, play a crucial role in the II/R process. The imbalance of mitochondrial homeostasis, including the disruption of mitochondrial morphology and structure, the disturbance of energy metabolism, and the exacerbation of oxidative stress, is one of the important mechanisms for the development of II/R.( ) Therefore, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis is expected to be a potential target for the prevention and treatment of II/R. The activity of metabolic enzymes in mitochondria is controlled by acetylation modifications, which affect mitochondrial metabolism by regulating the activity of these enzymes.( , ) Acetylation modifications in mitochondria are regulated by members of the Sirtuins family of deacetylases. Three members of the Sirtuins family, SIRT3–5, are located in mitochondria. Among them, sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) is the major deacetylase in mitochondria and controls mitochondrial metabolism under both physiological and pathological conditions.( ) SIRT3 influences mitochondrial metabolism by regulating the acetylation modifications of a number of metabolic enzymes, especially those related to energy metabolism such as fatty acid β-oxidation, ketone body synthesis, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle.
SUMO-specific protease 1 (SENP1) is a type of de-SUMOylated hydrolase that influences substrate protein function through de-SUMOylation and is involved in the regulation of numerous biological processes such as the cell cycle, cell proliferation, and apoptosis.( ) SENP1 is a specific protease for de-SUMOylation modification of SIRT3. SENP1 is localized in mitochondria and accumulates there to rapidly de-SUMOylate and activate SIRT3.( , ) One study found high expression of SENP1 in intestinal injury in Crohn’s disease.( ) Additionally, SENP1 was highly expressed in an in vitro cellular model of hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury.( ) However, it remains unclear whether SENP1 affects the II/R process by regulating SIRT3 in II/R. In this study, we initially demonstrated the expression of SENP1 and SIRT3 and the presence of an interaction in intestinal mucosal epithelial cells. Additionally, we observed the dysregulation of SENP1-SIRT3 expression in a model of II/R. After enteral nutrition intervention or direct down-regulation of SENP1 by si-SENP1, II/R can be ameliorated, and intestinal mucosal barrier and mitochondrial dysfunction can be restored via the SENP1-SIRT3 pathway.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell culture
Caco-2 cells were obtained from the cell bank of the Typical Culture Preservation Committee of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were maintained in MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) under conditions of 37°C, 95% relative humidity, and 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator. When the cell density reached 80% or higher, they were treated with 1 ‍ml of 0.25% trypsin for 1 ‍min. Once the cells became rounded and detached, 2 ‍ml of complete medium was added to stop the digestion. The cells were then passaged and seeded.

## Animals
Six- to eight-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. The rats were housed in individual cages. The bedding was changed regularly to ensure unrestricted access to food and water. Six rats were housed per cage. The temperature was maintained at 23 ± 1°C, and the room was illuminated in a 12-h daytime and 12-h nighttime cycle. The rats were allowed to acclimatize to the living environment for five days prior to the experiment. II/R injury was induced by occluding the superior mesenteric artery in rats. The rats were anesthetized with isoflurane through inhalation. Once the mice were anesthetized, they were fixed in a supine position on the operating table. The abdominal hair was shaved, and the abdominal cavity was incised along the midline of the abdomen. Subsequently, the intestines were unfolded by blunt separation using cotton swabs until the retroperitoneal tissues were exposed. The superior mesenteric artery, which is located contralateral to the right renal hilum, was found and vertically divided from the abdominal aorta and separated. A double thread was then threaded for later use. Next, a rubber hose of approximately 1 centimeter was taken and placed on the superior mesenteric artery along the direction of arterial flow. The superior mesenteric artery was ligated with cotton threads to completely block the blood flow. After one hour, the ligated cotton thread was cut, the rubber hose was removed, and the color change of the intestinal tract was observed to determine whether the blood flow has been restored. All drug administrations were performed via the tail vein, the concentration of bupropion was 100 ‍mg/ml.

## Cell transfection
The CDS sequence of SENP1 gene, NM_001432472.1, was obtained from NCBI to construct SENP1 siRNA, and the following siRNA sequence was designed based on the SENP1 gene sequence: SENP1 siRNA-1 (5'-ACUAGUUAAUUCUUUGAUCCA-3'; 3'-GAUCAAAGAAUUAACUAGUGU-5'), SENP1 siRNA-2 (5'-AUUCAUGUAGAAAUUGAUGAU-3'; 3'-CAUCAAUUUCUACAUGAAUAU-5'), si-NC (5'-CACCGTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTTCAAGAGAACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATTTTTTG-3'; 5'-GATCCAAAAAATTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTCTCTTGAAACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAAC-3'). In the transfection process, 2 ‍μl of 20 ‍μM siRNA duplex was added to 200 ‍μl of serum-free medium and gently mixed by pipetting five times. Then, 10 ‍μl of RNAFit (HB-RF-1000; Hanheng Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to the 200 ‍μl mixture, and immediately vortexed for 10 ‍s. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 ‍min to allow the siRNA duplex and RNAFit to form a transfection complex (incubation time should not exceed 30 ‍min). Meanwhile, the original culture medium was removed and replaced with 1.8 ‍ml of preheated fresh complete medium containing serum. The incubated transfection complex was added to the transfected wells after medium replacement. The cell culture plate was gently shaken in a straight line to mix. The final volume of medium in each well was 2 ‍ml, and the final concentration of siRNA was 20 nM. After transfection, the cells were cultured in an incubator for 48 ‍h before detection.

## Western blotting
For cell protein extraction: Take log phase growth cells of different groups and inoculate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in a 6-well plate. Treat cells according to groupings and culture in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. Remove the plate, discard supernatant, wash samples twice with pre-cooled PBS. Add 0.5 ‍ml of RIPA with PMSF to each dish, fully lyse, centrifuge at 12,000 g for 5 ‍min at 4°C. Aspirate supernatant into a pre-cooled EP tube for extracted cellular proteins and freeze at −80°C.
For tissue total protein extraction: Cut tissue into small pieces and place in a 1.5 ‍ml centrifuge tube. Add 150 ‍μl of RIPA lysate (with PMSF) to a homogenizer for homogenization and place on ice. After a few minutes, pulverize lysate and place on ice again. Repeat several times for maximal tissue pulverization. Adjust lysate volume to 500 ‍μl per 100 ‍mg of tissue. Shake at 4°C for 20 ‍min. After full lysis, centrifuge at 12,000 ‍g for 15 ‍min at 4°C. Dispense supernatant into 1.5 ‍ml centrifuge tubes and store at −80°C.
Prepare 12% separation gel and 5% concentration gel. Pour SDS-PAGE gel. Add pre-cooled 1 × electrophoresis buffer. Add protein extract and Marker to lane. Conduct 80 ‍V electrophoresis for about 30 ‍min. When samples enter separation gel, adjust to 120 ‍V. Transfer at constant pressure. Wash membrane with TBST for 2 ‍min. Incubate with primary antibody overnight at 4°C after 30 ‍min shaking closure at room temperature. Wash 3 times with TBST for 10 ‍min each. Add secondary antibody and incubate at room temperature for 2 ‍h. Wash 3 times with TBST for 10 ‍min each.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) detection
Cell lysates were prepared by seeding cells at a certain density, culturing until appropriate confluence, lysing with RIPA containing PMSF, and centrifuging to obtain the supernatant. The cell lysates were then pre-cleared by incubating with Protein A and Protein G agarose beads. Subsequently, pre-cleared lysates were incubated with SENP1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or SIRT3 (BIOSS, China) and non-specific control antibody overnight at 4°C. After adding Protein A and Protein G agarose beads again, immunocomplexes were precipitated by gentle mixing at 4°C. The precipitates were washed multiple times and resuspended in SDS-PAGE electrophoresis loading buffer. Samples were boiled and centrifuged. Finally, Co-IP samples were analyzed by Western blot.

## Histological examination
Sample fixation for at least 12 ‍h followed by storage in 70–80% ethanol. Tissue dehydration through graded ethanol solutions. Clearing in xylene. Paraffin infiltration and embedding. Sectioning on a microtome and placing sections in a warm water bath for spreading. Baking slices. Gradient dewaxing and HE staining with hematoxylin and eosin, followed by rinsing and drying for photography.

## Tetramethylrhodamine ethylester (TMRE) staining
Prepare TMRE staining working solution by diluting TMRE (1,000×) in detection buffer at a specific ratio. Incubate sections with 1 ‍ml of working solution at 37°C for 30 ‍min. Aspirate supernatant and wash twice with preheated cell culture medium. Add Dapi staining solution. Incubate the sections with Dapi staining solution for 5 ‍min at room temperature in the dark. Aspirate the Dapi staining solution and wash twice with PBS. Add 2 ‍ml of preheated medium. Observe under fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, German) with maximum excitation at 550 ‍nm and emission at 575 ‍nm.

## Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection
DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) staining was conducted as follows: The DCFH-DA solution was diluted to a final concentration of 10 ‍μM in serum-free medium at a 1:1,000 ratio. A suitable amount of the diluted probe was added to the samples to ensure thorough mixing. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 ‍min with intermittent mixing every 3–5 ‍min to promote complete interaction between the probe and the cells. After incubation, the samples were washed twice with PBS to remove any unentered DCFH-DA. Finally, fluorescence microscopy (ZEISS) was used for observation and imaging.

## RNA extraction and RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using (Detroit R&D, Detroit, MI). Specific primers for target genes and a housekeeping gene were designed and validated. Quantitative PCR was carried out using (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ) on CFX96 touch (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) with conditions including initial denaturation at 95°C, followed by cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension. Melting curve analysis was done to ensure specificity. Ct values were obtained and relative expression levels calculated by ΔΔCt method with the housekeeping gene as control. Statistical analysis was performed to determine significance.

## Statistical analysis
Data were statistically analyzed and graphed using Graphpad Prism 9 (ver. 9.5.1) and ImageJ software. Organized composite plots were performed with Adobe Illustrator (2023). All data were expressed as Mean ± SEM, two-by-two comparisons were performed by t test, multiple comparisons were performed by One-way test, and p value less than 0.05 was considered as significant difference.

## Results

## SENP1 and SIRT3 are co-expressed and interact with each other in Caco-2 cells
To investigate whether II/R injury affects the expression of SENP1-SIRT3 and simulate the physiological state of II/R, a hypoxia/reoxygenation cell model was constructed by subjecting cells to 12 ‍h of hypoxia followed by 2 ‍h of reoxygenation. In Western blotting experiments ( A–C), compared with the control group, the expression level of SENP1 protein in the model group was significantly increased, while the expression level of SIRT3 protein was decreased. At the same time, we examined whether SENP1 and SIRT3 interact with each other. As shown in the ( D), both SENP1 and SIRT3 were expressed in Caco-2 cells and interacted with each other.
 

## II/R injury influences the expression of SENP1-SIRT3
SD rats were used as the animal model to mimic ischemia-reperfusion injury. HE staining ( A) showed severe tissue damage in the small intestine of experimental mice compared with the control group. We also quantified the expression of SENP1 and SIRT3 ( B–D) under the disease condition and found that the content of SENP1 increased in the experimental group of mice, whereas that of SIRT3 decreased. The results clearly indicate that II/R injury changes the contents of SENP1 and SIRT3 in the intestines of SD rats.
 

## Reducing the expression of SENP1 alleviates II/R injury in SD rats
With the intention of probing into whether the content of SENP1-SIRT3 can modulate II/R injury, we constructed siRNA to downregulate the expression of SENP1. Intestinal luminal nutrient provision can alleviate II/R injury.( ) When compared to the control group, the addition of 100 ‍mg/ml bupropion was found to ameliorate intestinal injury in SD rats ( A). The II/R + si-SENP1 group also demonstrated remarkable improvement compared to the control group. Interestingly, the improvement in si-SENP1 was a substantial enhancement relative to the bupropion-supplemented group ( B–D). Moreover, there was a synergistic effect when the two were combined. Subsequently, we examined the levels of SENP1 and SIRT3 in the small intestine. As anticipated, si-SENP1 reduced the protein content of SENP1 and increased that of SIRT3. Meanwhile, the level of E-cadherin increased after si-SENP1 intervention ( E), suggesting that SENP1-SIRT3 alleviated small intestinal mucosal injury.
 

## Suppression of SENP1 mitigates mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress in II/R rats
To thoroughly explore whether SENP1-SIRT3 can effectively mitigate the mitochondrial homeostatic imbalance arising in II/R. We labelled the mitochondrial cell membranes with TMRE ( A and B). By means of this labeling method, we are able to closely observe and analyze the changes that occur in mitochondrial membranes. It is found that both bupropion and si-SENP1 make significant contributions to restoring the membrane potential of II/R. When compared with the control group, these two elements display a remarkable synergistic effect. Additionally, si-SENP1 simultaneously undergoes a remodeling process in an effort to restore mitochondrial ATP levels ( C), which is essential for maintaining the normal function of mitochondria. In addition, we determined the level of ROS in the tissues. It was found that the level of ROS was increased in the II/R group ( A and B). However, the supplementation of bupropion and si-SENP reduced the accumulation of ROS in the intestines of II/R rats. These results clearly illustrate that maintaining the levels of SENP1-SIRT3 is beneficial for improving the mitochondrial homeostasis of the small intestine.
 We further examined the level of mitochondrial DNA in the intestinal cells of SD rats. We found that the copy number of mitochondrial DNA decreased in the control group, which indicates impaired mitochondrial function and a decreased mitochondrial number in the disease state. A significant increase in the copy number of mitochondria was observed after injection of si-SENP1, similar to the bupropion-supplemented group. In addition, we examined COX1, FIS1, and DRP1 proteins ( ), which are related to mitochondrial function and replication. We found that the expression of COX1 decreased in the disease state and increased after si-SENP1 treatment. The protein expression of FIS1 and DRP1 also reversed its increase after treatment. These results clearly illustrate that supplementation with si-SENP1 can improve the quantity and function of mitochondria in intestine.
 

## Discussion
This study aimed to explore the role of SENP1 and SIRT3 in II/R injury and its underlying mechanism. Our results demonstrated several important findings that contribute to a better understanding of this complex pathological process. We initially found that SENP1 and SIRT3 are co-expressed and interact with each other in Caco-2 cells. In a hypoxia/reoxygenation cell model mimicking II/R, the expression of SENP1 increased while that of SIRT3 decreased. In SD rats with II/R injury, we observed severe tissue damage in the small intestine along with changes in SENP1 and SIRT3 expression levels. Reducing SENP1 expression by siRNA or through enteral nutrition intervention with bupropion alleviated II/R injury in SD mice. Additionally, decreased SENP1 expression mitigated mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress in the small intestine and improved the number and function of mitochondria.
Theoretically, our study provides new insights into the role of SENP1 and SIRT3 in II/R injury. The interaction between SENP1 and SIRT3 and their impact on mitochondrial homeostasis suggest a novel mechanism for the development of II/R. Mitochondria play a vital role in energy metabolism and cellular homeostasis, with mitochondrial dysfunction being linked to various diseases, such as cardiovascular disease( ) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).( ) Metabolic enzyme activities in mitochondria are adjusted in response to metabolic stress. Evidence has increasingly shown that mitochondrial acetylation is both extensive and a key regulatory process for mitochondrial proteins.( , ) Mitochondrial acetylation mainly depends on the action of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)+-dependent deacetylase SIRT3 and thus mitochondrial homeostasis, gives a certain mechanistic explanation that can help our understanding and treatment of the disease. In our study, it was discovered that both bupropion treatment and si-SENP can ameliorate II/R. Intriguingly, when both are administered to II/R rats simultaneously, the therapeutic effect is superior to that achieved by using either one alone. This also implies that there might be other mechanisms through which nutrient treatment can improve II/R. However, in this paper, we focused on the modulation of SENP1-SIRT3 and did not explore this interesting phenomenon in depth.
Recent studies have shown that SENP1 and SIRT3 also play crucial roles in various other diseases. For instance, in cancer, such as breast cancer and primary hepatocellular carcinoma, alterations in SENP1 expression have been associated with tumor progression and metastasis.( ) Additionally, in neurodegenerative diseases including Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and other central nervous system degenerative disorders,( – ) SIRT3 has been implicated in the regulation of metabolic pathways and oxidative stress. The fact that these two factors are involved in such diverse pathological processes suggests that they may have broad regulatory functions in cellular homeostasis.
In summary, this study investigated the role of SENP1 and SIRT3 in II/R injury. It was found that SENP1 and SIRT3 are co-expressed and interact in Caco-2 cells. In models of II/R, the expression of SENP1 increased while SIRT3 decreased. Reducing SENP1 expression by siRNA or enteral nutrition intervention with bupropion alleviated intestinal II/R injury, mitigated mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress, and improved the number and function of mitochondria. This research provides new insights into the mechanism of II/R injury and suggests potential therapeutic approaches.
