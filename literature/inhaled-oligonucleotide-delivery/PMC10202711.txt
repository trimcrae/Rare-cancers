## Introduction
The Matsumoto Eosinophilia Shinshu (MES) rat strain is a model for eosinophilia [ , , , , ]. MES rats spontaneously show a marked increase in peripheral blood eosinophils (>500
/µl) at about nine weeks of age. Eosinophilia deteriorates with age until the number of eosinophils eventually exceeds the level that is characteristic of human idiopathic
hypereosinophilia (>1,500 /µl) [ ]. The incidence of eosinophilia is 100% in both female and male MES. A part of the eosinophils
infiltrate the mesenteric lymph nodes, where they degenerate, and release eosinophilic factors which cause granulomatous inflammation. Eosinophil-related gastroenteritis or aortitis is also
observed.
Eosinophilia in MES is a genetically recessive trait [ , ]. In a previous study, we uncovered that the primary
cause of eosinophilia in MES is a loss-of-function mutation in the gene for cytochrome b-245, alpha polypeptide (Cybames mutant allele) [ ]. CYBA protein is an essential component of the superoxide-generating NADPH oxidase complex [ ]. The catalytic subunit of the NADPH
oxidase is NOX proteins. Rats have four NOX isoforms of NOX1, NOX2, NOX3, and NOX4, which are encoded by independent genes (Nox1, Nox2, Nox3,
and Nox4). While these NADPH oxidases show distinct or overlapping expression patterns in organs, tissues, and cell types, all these NOX proteins require CYBA for superoxide
production. Therefore, MES rats are assumed to lack all four NADPH oxidase activities. However, the molecular mechanisms for the loss of CYBA to cause eosinophilia have been unknown.
Eosinophilia in MES rats is likely due to the loss of one of the four NADPH oxidases. Specification of the NOX isoform involved in the development of eosinophilia should give insight into the
mechanisms of development of the disease. Hence, we attempted to resolve this issue by generating knockout rats for the Nox1, Nox2, and Nox4
genes with the genetic background of F344/N rats. The Nox3 gene was excluded from the study because it is expressed predominantly in the inner ear and thus is supposed
irrelevant to eosinophilia. Eosinophils proliferate and differentiate from bone marrow-derived hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. Human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells express the
catalytic subunits of the NADPH oxidase, NOX1, NOX2, and NOX4 isoforms along with the complete set of their regulatory subunits [ ]. In addition to
Nox knockout rats, we bred F344/N-Cybames congenic rats that have a similar genetic background to the Nox knockout rats. We bred
the congenic strain because we were concerned that blood eosinophilia caused by loss of NADPH oxidase function might be deterred by the genetic background of F344/N. This concern was based on
our previous observation that BN.MES-Cybames congenic rats do not develop the disorder [ ]. Herein, we demonstrate that
blood eosinophilia in rats is caused by the loss of NOX2. We also show that the incidence of the disorder under loss of NOX2 or CYBA is diminished under the genetic background of F344/N and in
male rats.

## Materials and Methods

## Ethics statement and rearing condition of rats
All experimental procedures involving rats were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for Animal Experimentation of Shinshu University and were consistent with the National Institute
of Health guide for the care and use of laboratory animals. The animal protocol was reviewed by the Committee for Animal Experiments of Shinshu University and approved by the president of
Shinshu University (approval numbers 260051, 280117, and 019126). Rats were maintained under clean conventional conditions and had free access to a commercial diet (MF; Oriental Yeast Co.,
Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and tap water.

## Generation of Nox knockout rats by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing
Exon 1 of the rat Nox1, Nox2, and Nox4 genes was targeted for simultaneous editing of the three genes. Preparation and microinjection of
Cas9 nuclease and guide RNA (gRNA) to fertilized eggs of F344 were performed as described [ ]. Expression plasmid vectors for Cas9 (pMLM3613:
ID#42251) and gRNA (pDR274: ID#42250) were obtained from the Addgene repository (www.addgene.org/CRISPR). The gRNAs were designed by using a software tool (crispr.genome-engineering.org),
and oligonucleotides designed for target sites were cloned into pDR274. Cas9 mRNA and gRNA were prepared by transcription in vitro from the expression plasmids using a
MessageMAXT7 ARCA-Capped Message Transcription Kit (CELLSCRIPT, Madison, WI, USA) and a MEGAshortscript T7 Kit (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NE, USA), respectively. Using a
micromanipulator (Narishige, Tokyo, Japan), 100 ng/µl Cas9 mRNA and 50  ng/µl gRNA were microinjected into the pronuclei of the embryos obtained from
superovulated rats. Embryos were then cultured in modified Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate medium overnight and the two-cell embryos were transferred into the oviducts of pseudopregnant Wistar
rats. Genomic DNA was isolated from the tail tips of gene-edited F0 founder rats. We genotyped the rats by PCR amplification and nucleotide sequencing of the target exons. The
sequences of oligonucleotide primers used in this study are summarized in . F0 rats with
appropriate mutations were mated with F344/N rats purchased from Japan SLC (Hamamatsu, Japan), and then rats heterozygous for the mutation were mated to breed homozygous mutant rats.

## Breeding of F344.MES-Cybames congenic rats
The MES rats were maintained at the Institute of Experimental Animals, Shinshu University. A congenic strain F344.MES-Cybames which had the mutant
Cybames gene in the F344/N background was developed by standard procedures with consecutive backcrossings to F344/N. At each generation, rats heterozygous for
the mutant Cybames allele were discriminated from homozygotes for the wild allele by PCR-genotyping [ ] and used for
breeding of the next generation. After seven generations of backcrossing, F344.MES-Cybames congenic rats homozygous for the mutant
Cybames gene were obtained by intercross between heterozygous rats and subsequently maintained by brother-sister mating between homozygous rats.

## Reverse transcription-PCR analysis of Nox genes
Total RNA was extracted from the organs of rats using the TRI Reagent (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA
using ReverTra Ace -α- reverse transcriptase (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). cDNA fragments for the Nox genes were then amplified by PCR with specific primers ( ). PCR amplification was performed using TaKaRa LA Taq DNA polymerase (TAKARA BIO INC., Kusatsu, Japan) in 25
µl reactions according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR reactions were carried out using the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 1 min, followed
by 40 cycles at 94°C for 20 s and 65°C for 30 s. A 5 µl aliquot of each PCR product was then electrophoresed on 1.5% agarose gels, stained with ethidium bromide, and
photographed with UV illumination using a digital camera. The resulting PCR products were purified using an UltraClean PCR Clean-Up Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and sequenced
using a BigDye Cycle Sequencing FS Ready Reaction Kit (Life Technologies) and an ABI 310 automated sequencer.

## Immunoblot analysis of NOX proteins
Immunoblot detection of NOX proteins was performed as described [ ]. Tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing 1% Triton
X-100. The protein concentration in the soluble fraction was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). An aliquot equivalent to 50
µg protein was incubated at 37°C for 15 min and separated by SDS-PAGE using Tris-Tricine-SDS buffer and 12% polyacrylamide gels. After electrophoresis, the separated
proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Immobilon, 0.2 µm pore, Merck Millipore Burlington, MA, USA) and incubated overnight at 4°C with the
primary rabbit anti-NOX1 polyclonal antibodies (1:2,000, PA5-103220, Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific and 1:2,000, GTX103888, Gene Tex, Los Angeles, CA, USA), anti-NOX2 monoclonal
antibody (1:2,000, GTX133715, Gene Tex), or anti-NOX4 polyclonal antibody (1:1,500, PA5-88106, Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific). An anti-β-actin antibody (1:1,500, sc-47778, Santa Cruz
Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) was used as the loading control. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:3,000, Cell Signaling Technology,
Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, and the target proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence.

## Hematologic examination
Male and female rats used for hematologic examination were kept separate from each other after weaning. Blood samples were collected into a syringe containing 10% EDTA-2K solution as an
anticoagulant from the jugular vein of rats under anesthesia with isoflurane inhalation. The number of eosinophils in the blood was measured with an automated cell counter (XT-2000iV, Sysmex
Co., Ltd., Kobe, Japan).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical differences in the incidence of eosinophilia between rat strains were evaluated by the Fisher’s exact probability test using js-STAR free wear (http://www.kisnet.or.jp/nappa/software/star/). A probability value of P<0.05 was considered
statistically significant.

## Results

## Generation of Nox knockout rats
To specify the NOX isoform involved in eosinophilia, we created knockout alleles for Nox1, Nox2, and Nox4 genes in F344 rats by
CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. Fifteen founder (F0) rats were acquired. Examination of target exon 1 of the three Nox genes revealed that the F0 rats
carried deletion or insertion mutations of various sizes ( ). Also, this screening indicated that most of
these rats were compound heterozygotes for different mutant alleles. Then, one mutant allele was selected for the individual Nox gene ( ). The Nox1em1 mutant allele contains a deletion of a 29 nucleotide sequence including the starting methionine codon. The
Nox2em1 mutant allele contains a deletion of a seven nucleotide sequence that should lead to a frameshift and the appearance of a termination codon immediately
downstream of the starting codon. The Nox4em1 mutant allele has an insertion of one nucleotide that should result in a frameshift and the appearance of a
premature termination codon. Rats homozygous for these mutant alleles were then bred. Mutant rats were examined for expression by RT-PCR analysis of the representative organs for the
Nox genes. The analysis revealed the expression of mutant transcripts of Nox1, Nox2, and Nox4 ( ). Immunoblotting revealed an absence of NOX2 (65 kDa) and NOX4 (67 kDa) proteins in the mutant rats ( ). For NOX1 (65 kDa), we used two anti-NOX1 antibodies developed by different manufacturers (Invitrogen and Gene Tex). When the antibody from Invitrogen was used, no band was observed
for the large intestine of all rats including the Nox1 knockout rat, whereas a band of 40 kDa was observed in the spleen of these rats. It was possible that this antibody
did not react with rat NOX1. Meanwhile, a band of 65 kDa equivalent to the molecular weight of NOX1 was detected in the large intestine and spleen of all rats including the
Nox1 knockout rat with the antibody from Gene Tex. It was possible that this antibody cross-reacted with other proteins. Thus, we could not verify the absence of NOX1 in
the Nox1 knockout rats at the protein level.

## A portion of female F344/N-Nox2em1 knockout rats develop blood eosinophilia
Nox knockout rats were then measured for blood eosinophil levels at 12 weeks of age and older. This time point was considered adequate because the incidence of the disease
in susceptible MES rats is 100% [ ], whereas that in resistant BN.MES-Cybames congenic rats is 0% [ ]. Two of the 12 female Nox2 knockout rats (17%) had blood eosinophilia (>500 /µ1) ( and ). In contrast, all male Nox2 knockout rats had normal blood eosinophil levels.
Also, all knockout rats for Nox1 and Nox4 genes had blood eosinophil levels of the normal range (30–200 /µl) ( and ).

## The incidence of blood eosinophilia is not elevated in triple Nox knockout rats
As described above, the incidence of blood eosinophilia in F344/N-Nox2em1 knockout rats was considerably lower compared to that of MES rats (100% in both sexes).
The low incidence could be because of compensation by the remaining wild-type Nox1 and/or Nox4 genes. To test this hypothesis, we created triple
Nox knockout rats by compound crossbreeding of single knockout rats and measured the blood eosinophil level. Immunoblotting revealed an absence of NOX2 and NOX4 proteins
in the triple Nox knockout rats. Similar to the result in the single Nox1 knockout rat, a band of 65 kDa equivalent to the molecular weight of NOX1 was
detected in the triple Nox knockout rat. Contrary to the hypothesis, blood eosinophil levels of male triple knockout rats were within the normal range ( and ). Four of 16 triple knockout female rats (25%) had blood eosinophilia.
Thus, the incidence of the disorder in triple knockout female rats was not different from that in single Nox2 knockout female rats (P=0.48 for the one-sided
test). This result suggested that compensation by NOX1 and/or NOX4 was not the cause of the low incidence of blood eosinophilia of Nox2 single knockout rats.

## All females but a portion of male F344.MES-Cybames congenic rats develop blood eosinophilia
The low incidence of blood eosinophilia in the Nox2 knockout rats could be due to the differences in the genetic background of F344/N from MES. MES has been derived from
the Sprague-Dawley strain [ ]. To examine the effects of the genetic background, we bred F344.MES-Cybames congenic rats.
Oddly, F344.MES-Cybames congenic rats had a peculiar incidence of blood eosinophilia. Similar to MES, all 10 female F344.MES-Cybames
rats (100%) had blood eosinophilia. In contrast, only seven of 13 (54%) male congenic rats had high blood eosinophil levels ( and ).

## Discussion
The principal objective of this study was to specify the NOX isoform, loss of which causes eosinophilia in rats. The data obtained in this study indicate that loss of NOX2 is the cause of the
disorder. NOX2 NADPH oxidase is expressed in the process of differentiation of granulocytes including neutrophils, basophils, and eosinophils from hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells [ ]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by NADPH oxidase serve as intracellular second messengers by activating transcription factors, protein tyrosine
kinases, or phosphatases to regulate cell fate [ , , , ]. For hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells as well, ROS is supposed to fine-tune signaling to control the balance between cell growth, proliferation, and
differentiation [ ]. Loss of NOX2 NADPH oxidase activity might deregulate the signaling and shift the fate of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells
towards eosinophilopoiesis. Further study should lead to the elucidation of the molecular mechanisms by which NOX2 NADPH oxidase deficiency ends in eosinophilia in rats.
Curiously, evidence in other mammals including humans and mice to validate the etiological link between a deficiency in NOX2 NADPH oxidase or CYBA and eosinophilia is poor. A prototypical
role of NOX2 NADPH oxidase is that of phagocytes to kill invading bacteria and fungi. Inactivating mutations in NOX2 or CYBA genes in humans give rise to
impaired function of the phagocytic NADPH oxidase. People with such gene mutations are highly susceptible to certain bacteria and fungi. As a result of recurrent bacterial and fungal
infections, these people may develop the chronic granulomatous disease, the symptom of which includes lymphadenopathy, inflammatory bowel disease, granulomatous colitis, fever, skin
infections, osteomyelitis, and/or abscesses [ ]. However, eosinophilia has been reported in only a few cases of these patients [ , ]. Similarly, a Cyba mutant mouse strain exhibited the spontaneous formation of granulomas composed of
macrophages in the lung and more intriguingly eosinophilic crystals in the organ [ ]. Other Cyba mutant mouse strains showed a
significant increase in CD11b+ myeloid cells in peripheral blood and spleen [ , ]. However, blood
eosinophilia was not observed in these Cyba mutant mice. These observations in humans and mice suggest a possibility that eosinophilia due to a deficiency in NOX2 NADPH
oxidase or CYBA is unique to rats. Alternatively, humans and mice with a deficiency in NOX2 NADPH oxidase under a particular genetic background may develop eosinophilia.
Although eosinophilia was observed in F344/N-Nox2em1 and F344.MES-Cybames, its incidence was distinct from that in MES and
BN.MES-Cybames ( ). These eosinophilic and non-affected rats of each strain were reared in the same cage from birth. Therefore, it is unlikely that differences in the microbial environment or care
conditions of pups by mothers were the determinant of whether they developed eosinophilia or not. Rather, these results suggested that in addition to NOX2 NADPH oxidase deficiency, gender and
genetic factors of the F344/N strain influence the development of blood eosinophilia. Previously, we established another congenic rat strain BN.MES-Cybames, and
found that blood eosinophilia was completely suppressed in the congenic rats [ ]. Subsequently, we proved by breeding (MES x
BN.MES-Cybames)F2 rat progenies, the presence of quantitative trait loci that differentiate the blood eosinophil level of the two rat strains [ ]. It is likely that F344/N rats also possess gene(s) that suppresses the development of blood eosinophilia under loss of NOX2 NADPH oxidase or CYBA
function. Yet, the observation that only a part of F344/N-Nox2em1 rats was free from eosinophilia suggested that the effect of the suppressor gene is
incomplete.
Another intriguing observation in F344/N-Nox2em1 and F344.MES-Cybames was the predominant incidence of eosinophilia in females ( ). Such sex bias in the incidence of eosinophilia is not observed in MES and BN.MES-Cybames. Namely, the incidence of
eosinophilia in MES is 100% in both females and males whereas that in BN.MES-Cybames is 0% in both sexes. For MES, blood eosinophil levels of both sexes increase
with age and are almost equal by 12 weeks of age [ ]. However, after 16 weeks of age, blood eosinophil levels become higher in females than in males. For
example, the mean eosinophil count in the peripheral blood of males at 16 weeks of age is approximately 3,700/µl, while that in females is 9,400/µl [ , ]. These observations suggest the presence of an endogenous factor in rats to promote (in females) or suppress (in
males) eosinophilia. Sex hormones are a strong candidate for the endogenous factor to directly or indirectly regulate eosinophilopoiesis. Estrogen receptors are expressed in eosinophils
isolated from the peritoneal cavity of ovariectomized rats [ ]. Furthermore, estrogen acts to amplify the eosinophilic component of pre-existing
Th2-high immunophenotype, possibly acting at the level of the common eosinophil/neutrophil precursor in the bone marrow to preferentially drive eosinophil differentiation in rats [ ]. Although both F344/N-Nox2em1 and F344.MES-Cybames showed a predominance of females for the
incidence of blood eosinophilia, the incidence was higher in F344.MES-Cybames for both sexes. There are two possibilities as a cause for this difference. One
possibility is the influence of gene(s) on a congenic chromosome 19 segment that was introduced with Cybames in F344.MES-Cybames.
F344.MES-Cybames was established by seven times of backcrossing. Theoretically, after seven times of backcrossing, a 28 cM of chromosomal segment encompassing
the differential locus (Cyba) and hundreds of other genes on chromosome 19 derived from the donor MES strain remains in the recipient F344/N strain. Another possibility is
that the loss of the CYBA function has a greater impact than the loss of the NOX2 function. This possibility is more likely because F344/N-Nox2em1 and
F344.MES-Cybames have an identical genetic background of F344/N.
Overall, we established a series of mutant rats for essential components of NADPH oxidases, NOX, and CYBA. We demonstrated through the study of these rats that the development of eosinophilia
could be influenced by the gender and genetic factors of the F344/N strain. These Nox and Cyba mutant rats together with MES and
BN.MES-Cybames with different eosinophilic phenotypes should be useful to elucidate molecular mechanisms and factors involved in the development of
eosinophilia.
