## Background
Colon cancer, a prevalent malignancy occurs in the gastrointestinal tract with annually increasing incidence and mortality, and Clinacanthus nutans (Burm.f.) Lindau (CN) possesses anticancer activity in a wide spectrum of tumors. This investigation aims at illuminating potential anti-cancer properties and related mechanisms of CN against colon cancer.

## Methods
A BALB/c mice model of colon cancer administered CN to the experimental group was constructed, followed by transcriptome sequencing on tumor tissues and screening out cuproptosis-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) through bioinformatics. The anti-tumor efficacy of CN was validated in HT29 and HCT116 cells co-treatment of CN and drug serum by functional assays and therapeutic effects on tumorigenicity were also evaluated in vivo. In addition, the impact of PDE3B silencing on cuproptosis was elucidated and the Apelin pathway activator, CMF-019, was applied to further verify the role of PDE3B on the Apelin pathway in CN-treated cells.

## Results
CN restricted tumorigenesis of colon cancer in vivo. A total of 6 cuproptosis-related DEGs were discovered containing decreased 4 genes and elevated 2 genes in tissues from tumor mice with or without high dose CN treatment. PDE3B deficiency exerted intensified inhibitory effects of CN treatment on proliferative, migratory, and invasive capability, as well as further facilitated cuproptosis. Moreover, PDE3B deletion enhanced the suppression of CN in delaying tumorigenesis. Additionally, CN retarded the malignant phenotypes and contributed to the initiation of cuproptosis in cells via the PDE3B-mediated Apelin pathway.

## Conclusion
Our study revealed CN delayed colon cancer by regulating PDE3B via suppression of the Apelin pathway, indicating the potential clinical relevance of CN in treating colon cancer.

## Introduction
Colon cancer accounting for almost 10% of all cancer cases ranks among the top three malignancies in terms of both annually increasing morbidity and mortality that represents a major health problem worldwide [ , ]. Surgical resection and postoperative chemotherapy are deemed as current standard treatment options, while the intrinsic vulnerability of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic bioactive or high incidence of complications limit effectiveness of these methods [ , ]. Moreover, conventional chemotherapeutic medications are unsatisfactory in clinical application due to their poor targeting and severe toxic adverse effects [ ]. Therefore, it is still in dire need to comprehend the complex etiopathogenesis against colon cancer progression, which may contribute to developing more highly efficient therapeutics for patients with colon cancer.
Clinacanthus nutans (Burm.f.) Lindau (CN) is a medicinal herb belonging to a constituent of the Acanthaceae family with extensive pharmacological properties such as immunoregulatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral [ , ]. Moreover, CN possessing anticancer activity in a wide spectrum of tumors makes it recognized as a promising alternative regimen in the tenets of treating tumors [ ]. The complex extracts from leaves of CN exhibit the highest inhibition ratio on gastric cancer cells [ ]. CN plays a suppressive role in triple-negative breast cancer, which is possibly achieved by reducing the inflammation state [ ]. CN in combination with gemcitabine shows synergistic anti-tumor interactions in squamous pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma treatment [ ]. However, the therapeutic effects of CN on colon cancer and the precise mechanism by which CN in colon cancer progression remain largely vague.
Cuproptosis refers to a recently discovered unique kind of cell death mediated by copper that is linked to lipoylated protein oligomerization and disintegration of iron-sulfur protein clusters [ , ]. Currently, cuproptosis has garnered enormous interest in the realm of colon cancer therapeutics. For instance, increased copper levels can to induce oxidative stress in colon cancer cells, which further results in cell death [ ]. Cuproptosis-related molecular subgroups and prognostic signatures may help predict survival for colon cancer [ ]. In addition, copper-based synergistic strategies open the door for combating colon cancer [ ]. Therefore, targeting cuproptosis may hold great promise to ameliorate colon cancer development.
Cuproptosis-associated gene phosphodiesterase 3B (PDE3B) is a subtypes of PDE3 family displaying high affinity for both cAMP and cGMP, which plays a crucial role in gastrointestinal stromal tumors [ – ]. Apelin and apelin receptor (APJ) are implicated in regulating motility of colon cancer cells [ ]. However, the role of PDE3B and the correlation with the Apelin pathway in colon cancer, and whether this mechanism is related to CN in modulating colon cancer progression remain poorly elaborated. BALB/c mice are commonly used in the construction of colon cancer models [ , ]. Herein, we attempted to delineate the potential anti-cancer properties of CN and further unveil whether CN could affect colon cancer by regulating the PDE3B-mediated Apelin pathway through constructing a colon cancer BALB/c mice model administered CN and using CN-treated HT29 cells, which may endow the potential of CN application for treating colon cancer.

## Materials and methods

## Non-targeted metabolomics analysis
Metabolite extraction: After the samples completely unfreeze on the ice, they were subjected to vortex shaking and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (Heraeus Fresco17; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 15 min, then dissolved in 1000 μL mixture solution (methanol: water = 4:1) including 10 μg/mL of internal standard. Subsequently, the mixture was vortexed, sonicated for 5 min in an ice bath, and cooled at − 40 °C for 1 h. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was harvested and carefully filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, then preserved at −80°C until subsequent analysis.
Method conditions: Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) 1290 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Waters UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm ID, particle size 1.7 μm, Waters, USA) was applied to profile LC–MS/MS analysis. The injection volume of each sample was 5 μL with flow rate adjusted to 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phase comprised of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid with detailed conditions displayed in Table S1. Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer under the control of the control software (Xcalibur, Thermo) was employed for acquiring the primary and secondary mass spectrometry data (MS/MS) based on the IDA acquisition mode. The top three of every cycle were screened and the corresponding MS/MS data were further acquired during each acquisition cycle with the mass range set from 100 to 1500. In addition, the detailed specifications were as follows: sheath gas flow rate: 30 Arb, auxiliary gas flow rate: 10 Arb, capillary temperature: 350℃, full ms resolution: 70,000, MS/MS resolution: 17,500, collision energy: 15/30/45 in NCE mode, spray voltage: 5.5 kV (positive) or −4.0 kV (negative).

## Animal models and treatment
All procedures of animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Hainan Hospital (ethics number: IACUC-HPHCM-2304012). Thirty-six healthy mice (BALB/c, female, 5–6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were procured from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under pathogen-free conditions at a constant temperature of 22 °C in plastic cages with a 12-h light–dark cycle. They had free access to sterile food and water. Mice were randomly allocated into the following three different groups (n = 6 per group following the 3R principle) according to a random number table: colon cancer, low-CN (200 μL), and high-CN group (300 μL). Approximately 2 × 106 of HT29 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right armpit of the mice. The tumor volume in each mouse had reached approximately 80 mm3, so all mice were included in this study. Each group exhibited comparable mean tumor sizes and standard errors. The investigators remained blinded to the details of the treatment being investigated, as well as data processing and exclusion decisions. The different doses of CN were administered intragastrically every day, and the mice in the Colon cancer group were daily given the volume of saline (300 μL). During the intervention, the body weight of mice was recorded every seven days, and the mice were euthanized after four weeks through inhalation of isoflurane (Rayward, Shenzhen, China) for 2–3 min. The complete tumors were dissected and weighted, the tumor size was computed using the formula: V = (length × width2)/2.
For the knockdown PDE3B models experiment, the mice were randomly divided into LV-NC, CN + LV-NC, and CN + LV-PDE3B group (n = 6 per group) according to a random number table. The mice were administered orally with high-CN every day for 28 days. Silencing of PDE3B (LV-PDE3B) and negative controls (LV-NC) were constructed through the lentivirus-based vectors by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Approximately 2 × 106 transfected HT29 cells were injected subcutaneously into nude mice. The body weight of mice was also assessed weekly, the mice were sacrificed through inhalation of isoflurane (Rayward, Shenzhen, China) for 2–3 min, and tumor weight and volume were recorded four weeks later.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining
The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and cut into a thickness of 4 μm section. Subsequently, the slices were dewaxed, impregnated with hematoxylin, and dyed with eosin. The pathological sections following neutral gum sealing were imaged under a microscope (IX71, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Briefly, the 4 μm thick paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated in graded ethanol solutions, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer at high temperature. After soaking in 3% hydrogen peroxide, the slices were incubated overnight at 4°C with Ki67 antibody (ab15580, 1:1000, Abcam), and treated with corresponding secondary antibody the following day. Then the sections were subjected to 3, 3′-diaminobenzidine solution (DAB, zli-9018; zsbio, Beijing, China) working solution and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Finally, the tissue slides were sealed with neutral gum, and visualized using a microscope (Olympus).

## Western blotting
Total protein from cultured cells was extracted and determined concentration using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Then the protein samples were separated on the sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel and electroblotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, which were then blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h. These membranes were immunoblotted using primary antibodies against VEGF (ab32152, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD147 (13,287, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), MMP9 (ab283575, 1:1000, Abcam), PDE3B (ab99290, 1:1000, Abcam), LIAS (8641, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), DLST (5556, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), FDX1 (ab108257, 1:1000, Abcam), and GAPDH (5174, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology) at 4 °C overnight. Following incubation with specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, the chemiluminescence was recorded utilizing an imager system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with served as an internal control, and band intensities were calculated by ImageJ software (V1.8.0.112, NIH, Madison, WI, USA).

## Transcriptome sequencing
The transcriptome sequencing and raw data analysis were supplied by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Briefly, the total RNA was isolated from the tissues of each group (3 each for the Colon cancer and High-CN groups) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed with NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which then detected the integrity using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Afterwards, the libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The clean reads were aligned with the reference genome using hisat2.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Following the transcriptome sequencing, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were filtered with p < 0.05 and |log2fold change | > 2 being required. The distribution of DEGs was visualized through the “ggplot2” and “Pheatmap” packages in the R software. The Venn diagram was generated via the EVenn tool (http://www.ehbio.com/test/venn/#/) to analyze intersection of the DEGs with cuproptosis-related genes.
Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) analysis were carried out using the “clusterProfiler” R package, which were depicted in the bubble plots with threshold standard set at p < 0.05 considered significant enrichment.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
The Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) was applied to extract total RNA from cells, then reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent kit (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan). Afterward, qRT-PCR analysis was carried out to elucidate the mRNA levels on the ABI7500 quantitative PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The relative expression of different samples was quantified using formula 2−ΔΔCt with GAPDH as the internal standard. The primers of target genes were designed by Shenggong Bioengineering Company (Shanghai, China) and presented in Table S2.

## Preparation of medicated serum
Male Sprague Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 180–200 g, n = 2) purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. were received CN by gavage administration at the dose of 3 mL once a day for 3 days. After intragastric treatment for 1 h on the 3rd day, the mice were euthanized with inhalation of isoflurane (Rayward) for 2–3 min, and abdominal aortic blood samples were collected from the rats. The serum was extracted from blood by centrifugation and inactivated in a 56 °C water bath for 30 min. After filtrating, the resulting serum was preserved at −80 °C.

## Cell culture, transfection and treatment
The human colon cancer cells (HT29 and HCT116 cells) were procured from iCell (Shanghai, China), then cultivated in RPMI 1640 culture medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) with supplements of 10% prepared drug serum (DS), 100 UI/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Hyclone) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.
To achieve knockdown PDE3B in HT29 and HCT116 cells, the cells were transfected with PDE3B siRNAs. Briefly, HT29 and HCT116 cells were inoculated in 6-well plates until reaching approximately 70%−80% confluency per well, PDE3B suppressor siRNA or negative control (si-NC) were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The siRNA sequences targeting PDE3B were designed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) with presented in Table S2. In addition, HT29 cells were treated with the Apelin pathway activator CMF-019 (1 μM, Aobious, Gloucester, MA, USA) for 5 h.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
Briefly, the cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were planted into 96-well plates for culture of 0, 24, 48, and 72 h in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% DS at 37° C. Afterward, 10 μL of CCK8 reagent was added and incubated for another 2 h. Cell viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at the wavelength of 450 nm with a microplate reader (VL0000D0, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

## 5-ethynyl-2'deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay
The cells at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per well were seeded in 24-well plates and cultivated for 24 h. After incubating with EdU working solution (Beyotime) at 37 °C for 2 h, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by permeabilization with 0.4% Triton X-100 and the staining of nuclei 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution. Finally, the cells incorporated EdU were evaluated using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). ImageJ software was employed to assess the percentage of EdU-positive cells.

## Wound healing assay
The colon cancer cells were seeded (5 × 105/well) in 6-well plates until fusion rate was more than 90%. A linear gap was created across the monolayer cells using a sterile pipette tip, then cultivated in fresh serum-free RPMI 1640 medium for 24 h at 37 °C. Finally, the area of the scratched region was monitored using microscopy (Olympus) at 0 and 24 h, and the distances of the scratch were calculated using ImageJ software. The wound healing rate was calculated following formula: migration rate = (0 h scratch width − 24 h scratch width) / (0 h scratch width) × 100%.

## Transwell assay
The colon cancer cells at a concentration of 2 × 104 cells resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium were seeded into the upper chamber pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and lower chambers were full of medium containing 20% FBS. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the filter were wiped off using a cotton swab. The cells on the reverse side underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and staining with 0.2% crystal violet, followed by observation under the microscopy (Olympus). In six random fields per insert, cells on the lower surface were quantified using ImageJ software.

## Measurement of pyruvic acid and α-ketoglutaric acid level
The content of pyruvic acid and α-ketoglutaric acid in colon cancer cell extracts were determined with corresponding assay kits. Briefly, the HT29 and HCT116 cells were centrifuged to obtain supernatant, and then the resulting supernatant was loaded into 96-well plates, followed by incubation with reaction reagents using pyruvate acid (Catl. No. A081, BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) and α-ketoglutaric acid assay kit (BC5420, Solarbio, Beijing, China).

## Statistical analysis
All data from three independent replicates were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with experiment's outcomes presented as mean ± standard deviation. To evaluate statistical significance, student's t-test was adopted for two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test was used for multiple groups. Statistical significance was achieved with the p < 0.05.

## Results

## CN attenuates tumorigenicity of colon cancer in vivo
The chemical profile of CN was investigated through UHPLC-QE-MS assay with the top 10 chemical ingredients labeled in the positive electrospray ionization mode (Fig.  A, Table S3). To evaluate the impact of CN on colon cancer growth, we established a tumorigenesis model that was administered CN. The body weight of mice that received CN was apparently increased (p < 0.001), and the mice in the CN treatment group exhibited lighter tumor weight and smaller tumor volume, especially mice given high dose CN (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig.  B-E). Tight cell arrangement was observed in the control group, and the mice following CN treatment exhibited a decrease in cell density and loosed cell arrangement (Fig.  F). Moreover, the percentage of Ki-67 positive cells significantly reduced following intervention with CN (p < 0.01, p < 0.001), and the trend was more obvious in High-CN group (p < 0.05; Fig.  G). In addition, the expression of VEGF, CD147, and MMP-9 was decreased after CN treatment (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig.  H). Taken together, CN effectively attenuated tumorigenicity of colon cancer in vivo, in which high dose CN exhibited a preferable effect. 

## Identification of DEGs and functional enrichment analysis
Through transcriptome sequencing, the heatmap exhibited the gene expression levels of significant DEGs with obvious classification and well parallelism in cancer and high-dose CN treatment samples (Fig.  A). A total of 1258 DEGs were detected between cancer and high dose CN treatment group, containing 832 upregulated and 426 downregulated DEGs (Fig.  B), and the top 10 upregulated and downregulated DEGs were presented in Table S4. To further figure out the potential functional role of DEGs, GO and KEGG analyses were conducted. The top enriched 7 GO terms with the lowest p value in each category of biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were investigated (Fig.  C). As for KEGG analysis, the DEGs primarily participated in TNF, IL-17, and Apelin signaling pathways (Fig.  D). 
Subsequently, a Venn diagram was generated between DEGs and cuproptosis-associated genes, and 6 DEGs related to cuproptosis were screened out (Figure S1). We next validated the expression of 6 intersected genes in tumor tissues or tissues from mice that received CN. The expression levels of ETFDH and DLD were increased, while PPAT, PDE3B, ULK1, and VEGFA were decreased in tissues from mice administered CN (p < 0.001; Fig.  ). PDE3B as the focal gene and the Apelin signaling pathway were selected for subsequent analysis. 

## Impact of PDE3B silencing in CN-treated colon cancer cells
To further unravel whether CN treatment affected the malignant phenotypes of colon cancer cells by regulating PDE3B, PDE3B was silenced in HT29 and HCT116 cells. The results showed that PDE3B expression was significantly reduced at both mRNA and protein levels in the si-PDE3B-1, si-PDE3B-2, and si-PDE3B-3 groups compared to the si-NC group (p < 0.001; Fig.  A-B). Then, si-PDE3B-1 was selected for subsequent experiments.PDE3B deletion showed a better inhibitory effect on cell viability and proliferation capacity in CN-treated HT29 cells (p < 0.05, p < 0.01; Fig.  C-D). Furthermore, the migratory and invading capability were further suppressed in CN-treated HT29 cells after PDE3B deficiency (p < 0.05, p < 0.001; Fig.  E-F). Additionally, the pyruvic acid and α-ketoglutaric acid content as well as cuproptosis-related hallmarks expression levels were elevated in the CN-DS + si-NC group, which this trend was further enhanced after PDE3B silencing (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig.  A-B). Collectively, CN could restrict the malignant phenotypes and enhance cuproptosis in colon cancer cells by targeting PDE3B. 

## CN treatment delayed tumorigenesis by suppressing PDE3B in vivo
We next checked the effects of PDE3B knockdown on tumorigenesis in colon cancer tumor mice with CN treatment. Western blot analysis showed that the protein expression level of PDE3B was notably reduced in the LV-PDE3B group compared to the LV-NC group (p < 0.001; Fig.  A). Increased body weight, and decrease of both tumor weight and volume were found in the CN + LV-PDE3B group (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig.  B-E). Besides, downregulation of PDE3B remarkably reduced tumor malignancy (Fig.  F). In addition, the protein level of proliferation marker Ki67 was decreased in PDE3B-silenced tissues from mice given CN treatment (p < 0.01; Fig.  G). The above data indicated that PDE3B deficiency enhanced the inhibition of CN treatment in delaying tumorigenesis in vivo. 

## CN treatment represses the malignant phenotypes and facilitates cuproptosis in colon cancer cells through regulating PDE3B via the Apelin pathway
To further determine the correlation between PDE3B depletion and the Apelin pathway in CN-treated colon cancer cells, the Apelin pathway activator, CMF-019, was employed. CMF-019 treatment attenuated the inhibitory effect of PDE3B silencing on cell viability and proliferative capability in CN-treated cells (p < 0.05, p < 0.01; Fig.  A-B). Furthermore, suppression of PDE3B in migration and invasion could be blocked by CMF-019 treatment in cells dealt with CN (p < 0.01, p < 0.001; Fig.  C-D). In addition, CMF-019 treatment reduced the pyruvic acid and α-ketoglutaric acid levels in CN-treated cells after PDE3B depletion (p < 0.05, p < 0.01; Fig.  E). Thus, these results revealed that CN retarded the malignant phenotypes and facilitated cuproptosis by regulating PDE3B via inhibition of the Apelin pathway in colon cancer cells. 

## Discussion
Colon cancer as one of the most prevalent malignancies occurs in the gastrointestinal tract, which can metastasize to other organs [ , ]. Further investigating of more personalized medicine with less harmful pharmacological effects can be leveraged to improve the existing methods in the management of colon cancer. Herein, a total of 6 cuproptosis-associated DEGs were extracted from tissues between cancer and high-dose CN treatment group combined to transcriptome sequencing technology and comprehensive bioinformatics methods. Additionally, our results illustrated that CN enhanced cuproptosis and delayed colon cancer progression by modulating PDE3B via inhibition of the Apelin pathway.
CN potentially serves as an alternative or complementary therapy in treating a range of malignancies. CN extracts can retard the proliferative capability in both cervical cancer and erythroleukemia cells [ ]. Besides, the antiproliferative activities on human leukemia MOLT-4 cells and lymphoma SUP-T1 cells are the most obvious following treatment with acetone extract of CN [ ]. In addition, CN exhibits antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects against human breast cancer [ ]. In the present study, we demonstrated that CN could restrict tumorigenesis of colon cancer in vivo with high dose CN exhibited preferable effect, and intensified cuproptosis in HT29 cells.
Among these 6 cuproptosis-related DEGs, ETFDH is implicated in the prediction of prognosis in colorectal cancer [ ]. Combination of C10orf2-PPAT- ZMYND19 with good sensitivity and specificity are recognized as possible diagnostic markers for colorectal cancer [ ]. In addition, by targeting VEGFA, downregulation of miR-503-5p modulates angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, which at least partially contributes to the carcinogenesis of colon cancer [ ]. Cafestol can ameliorate colon cancer development, which is mediated by the mechanism involved in the activation of LKB1/AMPK/ULK1-dependent autophagy [ ]. In this study, elevated expression levels of ULK1 and VEGFA, and decreased PPAT, ETFDH, and DLD were observed in tissues from mice that received CN treatment, whereas whether CN exerts its anti-tumor effects in colon cancer through the regulation of these genes is essential to further illuminate.
Of importance, PDE3B gene may act as the key regulator to participate in the regulation of tumorigenicity. KOLR from leaves of Cinnamomum pauciflorum Nees can facilitate apoptosis and repress metastasis in pancreatic cancer cells by enhancing RAPGEF3/PDE3B protein complex formation [ ]. Curcumin and oligomeric proanthocyanidins can cooperatively exhibit superior anti-tumorigenic activities in colorectal cancer, which may be linked to modulating PDE3B [ ]. In addition, PDE3B can resist the migration and invasion of bladder cancer cells and enhance bladder cancer cell sensitivity to cuproptosis [ ]. In this study, the expression level of PDE3B was downregulated in tumor tissues from mice administered CN. Furthermore, PDE3B depletion enhanced the inhibitory effect on proliferative, migratory, and invasive capabilities in CN-treated HT29 cells. Meanwhile, downregulation of PDE3B further facilitated cuproptosis in cells dealt with CN, as reflected by hallmarks of elevated levels of pyruvic acid, α-ketoglutaric acid, and cuproptosis-related hallmarks. In addition, PDE3B deficiency intensified the inhibition of CN treatment in delaying tumorigenesis.
Vast evidence has emerged indicating that there is a direct correlation between APJ system and numerous malignant tumor illnesses [ ]. High expression of APJ can lead to a significant increase on migratory and invading capability in ovarian cancer cells [ ]. Similarly, increase of prometastatic phenotypes including proliferation and metastatic properties in high-grade serous ovarian cancer cells are efficiently suppressed by the APJ inhibitor [ ]. Blockade of the Apelin pathway decreases proliferative pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells accompanied with a reduction of pancreatic tumor burden [ ]. Long non-coding RNA BACE1-AS deficiency retards the malignant behaviors of hepatocellular carcinoma, which is mediated by miR-214-3p/Apelin axis [ ]. Herein, our data elucidated that the Apelin pathway activator, CMF-019 treatment attenuated the inhibitory effect of PDE3B silencing on malignant phenotypes of CN-treated cells as well as restrained cuproptosis. CN is frequently used in traditional Southeast Asian medicine, particularly in Thailand and Malaysia, to treat conditions such as snake, scorpion, and insect bites, skin rashes, uric acid and gout, and tumors [ ]. However, the molecular mechanisms and clinically relevant scientific data are still not well established. Our study demonstrates that CN facilitates cuproptosis and ameliorates colon cancer progression by inhibiting the PDE3B-mediated Apelin pathway, which suggests the antitumor effects of CN on colon cancer and highlights a potential molecular mechanism for its use in tumor treatment.
However, there are some limitations to this study. First, although we identified six cuproptosis-related genes potentially regulated by CN, we only explored the function and mechanism of PDE3B, and the other genes require further investigation. Second, all findings are based on animal and cell models, which necessitate clinical validation.

## Conclusion
To summarize, 6 cuproptosis-related DEGs were screened out combined to transcriptome sequencing technology and bioinformatics with 2 genes upregulated and 4 genes downregulated. PDE3B deficiency enhanced the effects of CN treatment on repressing the colon cancer cells malignant phenotypes and delaying tumorigenesis in vivo. Mechanistically, CN in regulation of PDE3B via suppressing the Apelin pathway, ultimately ameliorated the likelihood of colon cancer development and facilitated cuproptosis. Taken together, findings of this study may offer an essential conceptual theoretical basis positioning CN as a hopeful contender in the field of colon cancer therapy.

## Tumor size statement
Animals exhibiting significant distress (severe anxiety, persistent restlessness, tumor burden exceeding 10% of body weight) were euthanized. The maximum allowable subcutaneous tumor diameter was 20 mm in mice. This study complied with the requirements of the Ethics Committee and was approved under protocol number IACUC-HPHCM-2304012.
