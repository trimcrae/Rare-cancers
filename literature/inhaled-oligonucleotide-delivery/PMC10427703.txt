## Abstract

## Introduction
Bepirovirsen (GSK3228836) is an antisense oligonucleotide that induced rapid and prolonged hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) reduction with a favorable safety profile following 4 weeks of treatment in participants with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. The objective of the phase 2b study B-Clear is to access the efficacy and safety of bepirovirsen in participants with chronic HBV infection.

## Methods
B-Clear is a phase 2b, multicenter, randomized, partial-blind (sponsor/participant-blinded, investigator-unblinded) study in participants with chronic HBV infection receiving stable nucleos(t)ide analogue (On-NA) or not currently receiving NA therapy (Not-on-NA). Eligibility criteria included HBsAg > 100 IU/mL, HBV DNA < 90 IU/mL (On-NA) or > 2000 IU/mL (Not-on-NA), and alanine aminotransferase ≤ 2 × upper limit of normal (ULN; On-NA) or < 3 × ULN (Not-on-NA). Participants were randomized 3:3:3:1 to one of four treatment arms, with treatment administered weekly as subcutaneous injections with or without loading doses (LD) on days 4 and 11: bepirovirsen 300 mg (with 300 mg LD) for 24 weeks; bepirovirsen 300 mg (with 300 mg LD) for 12 weeks then bepirovirsen 150 mg for 12 weeks; bepirovirsen 300 mg (with 300 mg LD) for 12 weeks then placebo for 12 weeks; placebo for 12 weeks (with placebo LD) then bepirovirsen 300 mg without LD for 12 weeks.

## Planned Outcomes
The primary endpoint of the study was HBsAg < lower limit of detection and HBV DNA < lower limit of quantification for 24 weeks after the end of bepirovirsen treatment in the absence of rescue medication. The study enrolled 457 participants (On-NA, n = 227; Not-on-NA, n = 230) and the last patient visit occurred in March 2022. The novel design of the B-Clear study will allow assessment of HBsAg and HBV DNA seroclearance post bepirovirsen treatment discontinuation in the presence and absence of background NA therapy.

## Trial Registration Number
ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04449029; GSK study 209668).

## Key Summary Points

 

## Introduction
Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major global health issue, affecting approximately 296 million people worldwide in 2019, and can lead to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with fatal outcomes [ – ]. Nucleos(t)ide analogues (NA), the recommended first-line therapy for chronic HBV infection, effectively suppress HBV DNA levels and, while in part, reduce the likelihood of liver-related complications [ – ], therapies providing further reduction in such complications are required. Recently, functional cure, defined as durable hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss with or without HBsAg seroconversion and undetectable HBV DNA, has been accepted as the optimal endpoint for new HBV therapies [ – ]. Functional cure is rare (≤ 5%) with NA therapy [ , , – ].
Bepirovirsen (GSK3228836) is an antisense oligonucleotide that targets all HBV RNAs, including HBV mRNA and pregenomic RNA [ ]. In a phase 2a trial, 4-week treatment with bepirovirsen demonstrated a rapid reduction in HBsAg, with some participants achieving undetectable levels, and HBV DNA in people with chronic HBV infection with a favorable safety profile [ ].
B-Clear was a phase 2b trial (NCT04449029) investigating different bepirovirsen dosing regimens in a broad population of participants with chronic HBV infection. While the optimal goal for new HBV therapies is both HBsAg and HBV DNA loss, most previous HBV studies have focused on assessment of HBV DNA levels only [ ]. The B-Clear study facilitated the evaluation up to 24 weeks of treatment with bepirovirsen, taking into account both HBV DNA and HBsAg levels.
The primary objective of the B-Clear study was to assess the efficacy of three dosing regimens of bepirovirsen: 24 weeks of treatment, 12 weeks of treatment with 12 weeks step-down dosing, and 12 weeks of treatment (with or without loading dose [LD]) in participants with chronic HBV infection on stable NA therapy or not currently receiving NA therapy. Data from the B-clear trial have been published previously [ ]. Here we describe the novel and innovative aspects of the trial design that allowed the study team to address multiple questions.
The eligibility criteria allowed for two independent cohorts to be assessed within the same study protocol.

## Methods

## Study Design
B-Clear was a phase 2b, multicenter, randomized, partial blind (sponsor/participant-blinded, investigator-unblinded) parallel-cohort trial that enrolled people with chronic HBV infection receiving stable NA therapy (On-NA) and not currently receiving NA therapy (Not-on-NA). Participants in both populations were randomized to one of four parallel treatment arms. The study was conducted across 123 sites globally [ ]. Participants were followed for up to 55 weeks, including a screening period of up to 45 days, a 24-week treatment period, and a 24-week follow-up period (Fig.  ).
 

## Randomization and Blinding
Patients were randomized using an Interactive Web Response System in a 3:3:3:1 ratio. Randomization was stratified on the basis of baseline hepatitis B e-antigen (HBeAg) status (positive or negative) and baseline HBsAg level (≤ 3 or > 3 log10 IU/mL). The study used a partial-blind study design, whereby site staff are unblinded to treatment assignment. Rationale for the study blinding is provided in the Supplementary Methods. As the primary endpoint was based on objective laboratory measurements (HBsAg and HBV DNA), unblinding of site staff would not compromise the assessment of this endpoint, although initiation of rescue medication based on the investigator’s judgment would result in failure to meet the endpoint. The sponsor’s Clinical Safety Department staff could unblind the intervention for any participant experiencing a serious adverse event (SAE), to meet regulatory reporting requirements.

## Treatment Arms
Participants received one of four treatment regimens (Fig.  ), administered weekly as subcutaneous injections, with or without an additional LD on days 4 and 11. In the previous phase 2a trial, a LD schedule was included to accelerate achievement of steady state concentration, resulting in anti-HBV activity with a short treatment duration. Inclusion of a treatment arm without a LD (treatment arm 4) in this study would allow investigation of its contribution to response. End of treatment was defined as the end of the planned bepirovirsen treatment period (12 weeks or 24 weeks).
Participants in arm 1 received bepirovirsen 300 mg (administered as two 150 mg injections) for 24 weeks, with a bepirovirsen 300 mg LD. In arm 2, participants received bepirovirsen 300 mg for 12 weeks, with a bepirovirsen 300 mg LD, then a step-down in dose to a single injection of 150 mg and a placebo injection for 12 weeks. In arm 3, participants received 12 weeks of bepirovirsen 300 mg, with a bepirovirsen 300 mg LD, then placebo (administered as two injections) for 12 weeks to maintain blinded treatment. Arm 3 allowed an assessment of HBsAg seroclearance for up to 36 weeks following the end of bepirovirsen treatment. In arm 4, participants received placebo (administered as two injections) for 12 weeks (with a placebo LD) then bepirovirsen 300 mg without LD for 12 weeks. Although no full placebo arm was included, arm 4 was intended to serve as a comparator of safety in the first 12 weeks of treatment, including exploratory analyses such as inflammatory and immune activation biomarkers. In addition, arm 4 enables assessment of 12-week active treatment without LD.
Participants On-NA therapy at baseline were expected to continue their NA therapy without changes for the study duration. Participants not receiving NA therapy at baseline were expected to remain off NA throughout the study. For these participants, NA therapy initiation was permitted if they had alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ≥ 3 × upper limit of normal (ULN; 40 IU/L [male participants] or 33 IU/L [female participants]) and bilirubin > 1.5 × ULN (18.81 µmol/L), and until other tests (e.g., HBV DNA, HBsAg) ruled out hepatitis B flare. NA therapy initiation was also permitted in arms 1–3 in case of virological breakthrough (defined as ≥ 1 log increase from nadir of HBV DNA or HBV DNA becoming quantifiable after being below the lower limit of quantification [LLOQ] at two sequential visits), virological non-response (< 1 log reduction in HBsAg by week 12) or partial response (> 1 log decline in HBV DNA, but subsequent plateau of HBV DNA levels such that achievement of HBV DNA below LLOQ was considered unlikely) to prevent development of resistance. In arm 4, NA therapy initiation for virological breakthrough would apply during the second 12-week treatment period.

## Sample Selection
Key clinical inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table  and full eligibility criteria are listed in Supplementary Methods. Eligible participants were male and female adults with a documented chronic HBV infection for ≥ 6 months before screening.
 Participants were included in the On-NA population if they had no changes in their NA regimen for ≥ 6 months before screening, with no planned changes to the regimen for the duration of the study, and had a HBsAg concentration > 100 IU/mL, HBV DNA concentration < 90 IU/mL, and ALT ≤ 2 × ULN.
Participants were included in the Not-on-NA population if they had either never received HBV treatment or ended NA therapy ≥ 6 months before screening or interferon-containing therapy ≥ 12 months before screening, and had a HBsAg concentration > 100 IU/mL, HBV DNA concentration > 2000 IU/mL, and ALT < 3 × ULN.
All participants were required to be capable of giving informed consent including compliance with the requirements and restrictions listed in the informed consent form and the study protocol.

## Measurements and Planned Outcomes
The primary efficacy outcome was achievement of HBsAg and HBV DNA seroclearance, defined as HBsAg < lower limit of detection (LLOD; 0.05 IU/mL) and HBV DNA < LLOQ (20 IU/mL) for 24 weeks after the end of treatment in the absence of rescue medication (arms 1–3). Rescue medication was defined as any medication initiated for the purpose of suppressing HBV replication, including administration of NA in the Not-on-NA population. The response should be maintained at every visit (at least every 8 weeks) until 24 weeks after the end of bepirovirsen treatment. Any HBsAg ≥ LLOD or HBV DNA > LLOQ after achieving HBsAg seroclearance and HBV DNA suppression was confirmed by re-test within 1 week of receiving the test result.
Secondary efficacy outcomes for all treatment arms included achievement of HBsAg < LLOD and HBV DNA < LLOQ at the end of bepirovirsen treatment (week 24 for arms 1, 2, and 4, and week 12 for arm 3); categorical changes from baseline in HBsAg (i.e., < 0.5, ≥ 0.5, ≥ 1, ≥ 1.5, ≥ 3 log10 IU/mL) and in HBV DNA (i.e., < 1, ≥ 1, ≥ 2, ≥ 3 log10 IU/mL); ALT normalization (ALT ≤ ULN) over time in the absence of rescue medication in participants with baseline ALT > ULN; absolute HBe antibody (anti-HBeAg) levels and change from baseline over time of HBsAg, HBV DNA, HBeAg, and HBs antibody (anti-HBsAg) levels; and difference between treatment arms 1, 2, and 3 in the proportion of patients achieving HBsAg < LLOD and HBV DNA < LLOQ for 24 weeks after end of treatment in the absence of rescue medication.
Safety outcomes included clinical assessments of vital signs, laboratory measurements, and adverse events (AEs). AEs will be graded for severity according to Division of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome criteria. On the basis of a phase 2a randomized controlled trial assessing safety, tolerability, and antiviral activity of bepirovirsen, in which ALT elevations associated with HBsAg reductions were reported for NA-naïve patients [ ], ALT increases were considered AEs of special interest. Class effects of antisense oligonucleotides, including vascular inflammation and complement activation, thrombocytopenia, renal injury, and injection-site reactions, were also considered AEs of special interest [ ].
Secondary pharmacokinetic (PK) outcomes and exploratory outcomes are described in Table S1.

## Data Collection
Scheduled activities for screening are listed in Table S2. During the off-treatment period, weekly laboratory assessments included urinalysis, hematology, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, HBsAg, HBV DNA, and HBeAg. The schedules of other activities for the on-treatment and off-treatment periods are shown in Tables S3–S5.

## Efficacy Assessments
Blood samples for measurement of HBsAg and HBV DNA levels were collected weekly until the end of treatment. Off-treatment samples were collected at off-treatment weeks 1, 2, and 4, then every 4 weeks until off-treatment week 24 (Table S5). To avoid risk of bias and match the number and timing of visit windows for the measurement of off-treatment virological response in arms 1–3, post-treatment visits were made at weeks 12, 13, 14, 16, 20, and 24 and off-treatment weeks 4, 8, and 12.

## Safety Assessments
AEs were monitored throughout the study, from time of informed consent until final follow-up. Vital signs (temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure), concomitant medication review, and electrocardiogram data were assessed at each visit. Time points for safety assessments could be added during the study to ensure appropriate safety monitoring.

## Data and Safety Monitoring
All AEs and SAEs were collected from the signing of the informed consent until the final follow-up visit at the time points specified in the Schedule of Activities. For SAEs, investigators were required to proactively follow each patient at subsequent visits/contacts, with all SAEs to be followed up until the event was resolved, stabilized, otherwise explained, or the patient was lost to follow-up.
An Independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC) met regularly to review ongoing unblinded safety data and the planned interim analyses. A separate internal safety review team regularly reviewed blinded safety data. Three interim analyses were planned in addition to quarterly IDMC safety reviews.

## Data Analyses

## Data Analysis and Presentation
An estimation approach with no hypothesis testing was used to assess the efficacy of three dosing regimens (arms 1–3). The primary assessments of interest were the point estimates of HBsAg < LLOD and HBV DNA < LLOQ for 24 weeks after the cessation of bepirovirsen without rescue medication, calculated using a Bayesian probability approach, and 95% credible intervals. Comparisons between treatment arms will be assessed using probability inference approaches. All analyses were conducted separately for the two populations (On-NA and Not-on-NA). No adjustments were made for multiplicity. Further details are reported in the Supplementary Methods.
A description of estimands for the primary and secondary efficacy endpoints can be found in Table S1.

## Sample Size
Approximately 440 participants were planned to be randomly assigned to study intervention, with 66 participants to be assigned to each of the first three treatment arms and 22 participants assigned to the fourth treatment arm, in both populations (On-NA and Not-on-NA). This sample size was based on a Bayesian model considering the operating characteristics of at least a 75% posterior confidence of the true response rate under various assumptions. For example, with a true response rate of 20%, the proposed sample size of n = 66 for arms 1–3 has approximately 70% probability of confirming a true response of at least 15%, and if the true rate is 30%, there is an 88% chance of confirming a true response of at least 20%. The lower bounds of 95% credible intervals excluded the historical placebo response of 3% if the observed response is ≥ 14% in arms 1–3 or 18% in arm 4.

## Strengths and Limitations
The B-Clear study was a prospective, randomized trial that, in contrast to earlier trials of therapies for chronic HBV infection, enabled assessment of maintenance of HBsAg and HBV DNA loss after cessation of bepirovirsen.
This trial administratively combined two studies in different HBV populations into one protocol, providing insight into the potential benefits of bepirovirsen for those on NA therapy and those not currently receiving NA therapy. This novel approach of analyzing two independent cohorts in one protocol is of great benefit, improving operational efficiencies in terms of time and resources. Investigators were able to recruit both On-NA and Not-on-NA participants to the same study; 68% (47/69) of sites had participants in both cohorts.
Treatment outcomes for the On-NA population evaluated the potential for bepirovirsen to induce HBsAg seroclearance 24 weeks after bepirovirsen treatment discontinuation. While this response cannot be considered equivalent to functional cure, as a result of the continuation of background NA therapy, it allowed determination of whether administration of bepirovirsen as an adjunct to NA therapy can increase the likelihood of functional cure. Treatment outcomes for the Not-on-NA participants, who receive bepirovirsen monotherapy, established the potential for bepirovirsen to achieve functional cure and to become an additional option for standard of care treatment of people living with hepatitis B. The durability of the response will be further evaluated in the long-term follow-up B-Sure study (NCT04954859).
This trial was strengthened by the inclusion of two different bepirovirsen treatment periods of 12 and 24 weeks, to identify the optimal treatment duration to achieve clinically relevant response for 24 weeks post treatment. In addition, the step down in dosing explored the impact of decreasing overall exposure to bepirovirsen on efficacy and safety. Omission of the bepirovirsen LD from the fourth treatment regimen allowed assessment of the benefits of rapidly attaining steady state concentration of bepirovirsen on anti-HBV activity, as achieved with the LD. Finally, as all participants in the first three arms received bepirovirsen 300 mg for 12 weeks, data can be pooled to assess the effect of genotype on treatment response at week 12 in an adequate number of participants.
Both intensive and sparse PK data were collected, allowing development of PK/pharmacodynamics (PD) models to explore various relationships between treatment exposure and efficacy/safety. These models will characterize the exposure–response relationship and define the factors (covariates) that may be predictors of efficacy/safety. Samples were collected for biomarker research, to evaluate the pathogenesis of chronic HBV infection, the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of bepirovirsen, and the participant’s response to treatment.
A full placebo arm was not included in the study on the basis of feedback from external experts and a focus group of patients with chronic HBV infection, who expressed concerns owing to the study burden. Therefore, all participants received active treatment with bepirovirsen. The absence of a full placebo arm limits interpretation of the results, preventing direct comparison with a group that received no treatment; however, arm 4, in which participants receive placebo for the first 12 weeks with a placebo LD, allowed direct comparison of efficacy and safety with 12 weeks of treatment in the other treatment arms and assessment of the need for a bepirovirsen LD. The unique design of arm 4 also presented an opportunity to analyze changes in key biomarkers in the first 12 weeks of treatment without a LD, allowing for identification of bepirovirsen-dependent changes. The size of arm 4 does limit interpretation of the LD requirement. This arm was originally planned to be a placebo-only arm and hence had a lower sample size; following the change in treatment regimen that occurred on the basis of protocol feedback, the sample size was not increased. Hence further investigations may be required to assess the need for a LD.
Although conclusions from the B-Clear study were limited to the 24-week off-treatment period for most treatment arms, except for arm 3 which includes a 36-week off-treatment follow-up period, longer-term open-label data will be collected in the B-Sure extension study mentioned above, to evaluate the durability of response. Generalizability of the data to the wider population of people living with chronic HBV infection is limited to the requirements for study inclusion.

## Ethics and Dissemination
The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, the applicable International Conference for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, and all applicable laws and regulations. Before study initiation, the protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board/Independent Ethics Committee of each participating center. Protocol amendments also required approval before implementation. The nature of the study was explained to each patient or their legal authorized representative by the investigator or their representative before being asked to provide written informed consent. For data protection purposes, each patient was assigned a unique identifier by the sponsor, which was used in patients’ records or data sets.

## Conclusions
The study design of the B-Clear study allowed evaluation of different bepirovirsen dosing regimens on HBsAg and HBV DNA seroclearance after 12 and 24 weeks of treatment in a broad population of patients with chronic HBV infection in the presence and absence of background NA therapy. The study enrolled 457 participants, 227 On-NA and 230 Not-on-NA at study entry, and the last patient visit occurred in March 2022.
