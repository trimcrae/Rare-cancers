## Background
Ankyrin repeat domain 49 (ANKRD49) is highly expressed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and promotes tumor cell invasion and metastasis by activating the p38/ATF-2/MMP or JNK/ATF-2/c-Jun/MMP pathways. Although epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is critical for tumor dissemination, the role of ANKRD49 in EMT remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effects of ANKRD49 on EMT in NSCLC and to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms.

## Methods
The expression levels of ANKRD49 and EMT markers were analyzed in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissues using tissue microarrays (TMAs) combined with immunohistochemistry (IHC). The role of ANKRD49 in EMT was assessed in vitro and in vivo through real-time qPCR, Western blot, and IHC. Key signaling molecules were quantified by real-time qPCR, Western blot, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and IHC. Transcriptional regulation of TGF-β1 by PKNOX1 was verified using luciferase reporter assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). Protein-protein interactions between ANKRD49 and PKNOX1 were examined by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunofluorescence microscopy.

## Results
TMA analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between ANKRD49 and E-cadherin expression, whereas positive correlations were observed with α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and TGF-β1. ANKRD49 overexpression promoted EMT, whereas its knockdown suppressed EMT in both cellular and animal models. Furthermore, ANKRD49 upregulation increased TGF-β1 levels, which are transcriptionally regulated by PKNOX1, while ANKRD49 silencing had the opposite effect. Mechanistically, ANKRD49 physically interacts with PKNOX1, and PKNOX1 binds to the TGF-β1 promoter, elevating TGF-β1 expression and subsequently activating SMAD signaling. Consistently, ANKRD49 and PKNOX1 levels were positively correlated in LUAD tissues.

## Conclusion
ANKRD49 facilitates EMT in NSCLC by interacting with PKNOX1 to activate the TGF-β1/SMAD pathway. PKNOX1 mediates this process through transcriptional regulation of TGF-β1.

## Background
Tumorigenesis and tumor progression are fundamentally driven by somatic and/or germline alterations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [ ]. These genetic and epigenetic changes disrupt normal cellular processes, such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, leading to uncontrolled growth and neoplastic formation. The accumulation of multiple oncogenic alterations, coupled with significant intra- and inter-tumor heterogeneity, underpins the diversity of tumor types and their varying clinical behaviors. This complex molecular landscape enables tumors to evolve, adapt, and develop resistance to therapies over time [ ].
Current cancer treatment strategies target diverse oncogenic mechanisms and include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, pharmacological hormone therapy, photodynamic therapy, antibody-drug conjugates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, bispecific T-cell engagers, oncolytic virus therapy, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, and treatments directed against genes with oncogenic alterations and their related signaling pathways [ , – ]. Therefore, elucidating the biological functions of oncogenic alterations in tumorigenesis and progression, as well as their associated molecular signaling pathways, is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.
Lung cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide, accounting for approximately 12.4% of newly diagnosed cancer cases and nearly 18.7% of cancer-related deaths [ ]. Histologically, lung cancer is classified into two main subtypes: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with NSCLC comprising 85–90% of cases. NSCLC is further categorized into three histological subtypes: lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large-cell carcinoma [ ].
Chronic inflammation, including prolonged pathogen-induced inflammatory responses, is a crucial driver of neoplastic progression and can induce dysplastic precursor lesions in inflamed tissues through field cancerization [ , ]. Moreover, pathogen-induced inflammation promotes cancer cell migration, a key mechanism in metastasis [ ]. Metastasis is the primary cause of lung cancer mortality, and aberrant reactivation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is closely associated with malignant progression, including enhanced migratory and invasive capacities [ ]. Thus, identifying novel regulators of EMT in NSCLC may reveal new therapeutic targets.
EMT is a cellular reprogramming process characterized by the transformation of epithelial cells into a mesenchymal phenotype and plays a pivotal role in tumor metastasis [ ]. Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling is a potent inducer of EMT [ ]. During this process, epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) are downregulated, while mesenchymal markers including Vimentin, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and N-cadherin are upregulated [ ]. These molecular changes drive phenotypic alterations, such as a morphological shift from cuboidal to spindle-shaped cells and loss of cell adhesion, ultimately conferring metastatic potential to tumor cells [ ]. Consequently, targeting the TGF-β-induced EMT signaling pathway has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer therapy [ ].
Ankyrin repeat domain 49 (ANKRD49) contains four ankyrin repeat motifs, each consisting of 30 ~ 34 amino acid residues that mediate protein-protein interactions. It has been implicated in several cancers, including gastric cancer, NSCLC, and glioma [ – ]. Previous studies report that ANKRD49 promotes NSCLC invasion and migration via the p38/ATF-2/MMP or JNK/ATF-2/MMP pathways and is associated with poor prognosis [ , ]. Given the critical role of EMT in NSCLC metastasis, we hypothesized that ANKRD49 may regulate EMT in NSCLC. In this study, we investigated the functional and mechanistic role of ANKRD49 in EMT using both in vitro and in vivo models. Our results demonstrate that ANKRD49 drives EMT through the PKNOX1/TGF-β1/SMAD signaling axis, providing new insights into NSCLC progression and potential therapeutic targeting.

## Methods

## Human tissue samples and ethics statement
Commercially acquired lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissue microarrays (TMAs) were provided by YiBeiRuibiotek Inc. (Shanghai, China). The clinicopathological characteristics of the patient cohort are summarized in Table S1, including sex, age, tumor size, lymph node metastasis status, and tumor grade. All patients underwent surgical resection as the primary treatment with no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Intraoperatively collected tissue samples were fixed in formalin and paraffin-embedded for subsequent analysis. Histopathological diagnosis and tumor staging were independently confirmed by two pathologists under blinded conditions. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital, Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. 2019pK042). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives/attending clinicians prior to tissue collection.

## Cell lines and construction of stable cell lines
NSCLC cell lines, A549 (a LUAD cell line) and H1299 (a large cell carcinoma cell line), human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells were obtained from the Cell Culture Center, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Cat# PYG0025), RPMI 1640 medium (Cat# PYG0006) and DMEM medium (Cat# PYG0073, Boster Biological Technology, China), respectively, with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Cat# 11011–8611, TIANHANG Biological Technology, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.
The stable ANKRD49 overexpression or knockdown cells were constructed according to published protocols and named vector-A549 or H1299, ANKRD49 OE-A549 or H1299, shcontrol-A549 or H1299, ANKRD49 KD702-A549 or H1299, and ANKRD49 KD829-A549 or H1299, respectively [ , ].

## RNA extraction and real-time PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Fast Total RNA Extraction Kit (Cat# SM130-01, Seven Biotech, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and concentration were verified by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop) prior to reverse transcription. For gene expression analysis, cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using the Fast Two Step RT&qPCR Kit (Cat# SRQ-01, Seven Biotech, China), quantitative PCR was performed in triplicate using the same kit on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system, β-actin served as the endogenous control for normalization. The thermocycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was subsequently performed by gradually increasing the temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.5 °C/s to verify amplification specificity. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2. The levels of gene expression were calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔ Ct).

## Western blot
Cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 10% glycerol, 2% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Cat# AR0197, Boster Biological Technology, China). Equal amounts of protein (25 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm pore size) using standard wet transfer protocols. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk or 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated with indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then incubated with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit HRP-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibodies used in this study are as following: ANKRD49 (Cat# 25034-1-AP, 1:1000), PKNOX1 (Cat# 10614-1-AP, 1:750), Twist (Cat# 25465-1-AP, 1:1000, Protein Tech, USA), E-cadherin (Cat# 3195 S, 1:1000), EpCAM (Cat# 93790, 1:1000), N-cadherin (Cat# 13116, 1:1000), ZEB1(Cat# 70512, 1:1000), Vimentin (Cat# 5741 S, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Slug (Cat# sc-166476, 1:1000), Snail(Cat# sc-2719777, 1:1000), α-SMA (Cat# sc-53142, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), TGF-β1 (Cat# BA0290, 1:1000, Boster Biological Technology, China), SMAD2 (Cat# D155233, 1:1000), SMAD3 (Cat# D161451, 1:1000, Sangon Biotech, China), P-SMAD2 (Cat# AP0269, 1:1000, ABclonal, China) and P-SMAD3 (Cat# AF1759, 1:1000, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), Tubulin monoclonal antibody (Cat# CPA9126, 1:5000, Cohesion Biosciences, UK) or GAPDH monoclonal antibody (Cat# bsm-33033 M, 1:5000, Bioss Biotechnology, China). Finally, the membranes were incubated with enhanced ECL detection reagent (Cat# MA0186, Meilunbio, China) and imaged using AlphaImager MINI system (ProteinSimple, USA).

## Animal experiments
All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. SYDL-2020016 and SYDL2025037) and conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines for animal research. Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 5 per group) were obtained from BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE CO., LTD. Subsequently, 1 × 10⁶ vector-control or ANKRD49-overexpressing (ANKRD49-OE) A549/H1299 cells, resuspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium, were subcutaneously injected into the inguinal region of the hind limb of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored regularly by caliper measurements, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width². Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation when tumor volumes reached approximately 1,500 mm³, and tumor tissues were harvested for analysis.
For the lung metastasis assay, ANKRD49-OE A549, ANKRD49-OE H1299, or corresponding vector-control cells were injected via the caudal vein. Lung tissues were collected from all mice (n = 5 per group) at the experimental endpoint. All harvested tissues were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at 4 μm thickness for subsequent histological examination [ , ].

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Immunohistochemical staining was performed on deparaffinized sections of human LUAD tissue microarrays, mouse lung tissues, and subcutaneous tumor tissues as described above. The following primary antibodies were used: ANKRD49 (1:100), E-cadherin (1:50), α-SMA (1:50), N-cadherin (1:50), EpCAM (1:100), Slug (1:100), Snail (1:100), ZEB1 (1:100), TGF-β1 (1:50), P-SMAD2 (1:50), P-SMAD3 (1:50), and PKNOX1 (1:100). Detection was carried out using the appropriate Mouse/Rabbit IgG-SABC Ready-to-use Immunohistochemical Kit (Cat# SA1020, Boster Biological Technology, China). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Protein expression levels were quantified by measuring the average optical density of stained tissues using ImageJ software. All antibodies were obtained from the same suppliers as those used in Western blot analysis.

## Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extractions
Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein of A549 or H1299 cells were extracted using a Subcellular Proteome Extraction Kit (Cat# AR0106, Boster Biological Technology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Quantification of TGF-β1 secretion levels in the supernatant of A549 or H1299 cells was detected using an ELISA kit (Cat# EHC107b, NeoBioscience, China).

## Luciferase reporter assay
The pSBE4-TA-Luc reporter plasmids were purchased from the Beyotime Biotechnology Company (Cat# D4320). The promoter sequence of TGF-β1 was obtained using PCR and the truncated promoter sequences of TGF-β1 which was − 1455 ~ −1441 or −1511 ~ −1500 deletion were acquired using overlap PCR and then cloned into pGL3-basic vector (Cat# BioVector1058 17 − 1, kindly provided by Prof. Wei Song, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Peking Union Medical College) and named as pGL3-basic-TGF-β1-promoter (wild-type, −2000 ~ + 50), pGL3-basic-TGF-β1-promoter-Mut1 (Δ−1455 ~ −1441) or pGL3-basic-TGF-β1-promoter-Mut2 (Δ−1511 ~ −1500). The coding sequence (CDS) of human PKNOX1 was cloned into p3×Flag-CMV-14 vector and named as p3×Flag-PKNOX1. To perform the luciferase reporter assay, 0.5 µg reporter plasmid (wild-type/mutant TGF-β1 promoter or empty pGL3-basic), 0.25 µg p3×Flag-PKNOX1 or empty vector and 0.05 µg pRL-TK plasmids were transiently co-transfected into HEK 293 T cells using LipoFiter 3.0 transfection reagent (Cat# RLF3-1000, Hanbio, China). After 48 h, cells were lysed and analyzed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Cat# RG027, Beyotime, China). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to renilla luminescence. Data represent three independent experiments (mean ± SEM). All cloning primers are listed in Table S2, including TGF-β1 promoter amplification primers, deletion mutant construction primers (overlap PCR) and PKNOX1 CDS cloning primers.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using a commercial kit (Cat# P2078, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Vector-control A549 or ANKRD49-OE A549 cells, transfected with p3×Flag-PKNOX1 for 48 h, were fixed with 1% formaldehyde in PBS to crosslink chromatin. Cell lysates were prepared and digested with micrococcal nuclease to obtain chromatin fragments averaging ∼150–900 bp. After a pre-clearing step, immunoprecipitation was carried out overnight at 4 °C using anti-Flag antibody (1:100; positive IP) or normal mouse IgG (negative control). Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted, and protein–DNA crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65 °C for 2 h. DNA was purified via proteinase K digestion and collected using a DNA clean-up column. PKNOX1 binding to the TGF-β1 promoter region was detected by quantitative real-time PCR using primers listed in Table S2. Enrichment was calculated by the fold-enrichment method (2-ΔΔCt). The following formulas were applied: ΔCt [normalized ChIP] = (Ct [ChIP] - (Ct [Input] -Log2 (Input Dilution Factor)), ΔΔCt [ChIP/NIS] = ΔCt [normalized ChIP] - ΔCt [IgG], Fold Enrichment = 2^ (-ΔΔCt [ChIP/NIS]) [ ].

## Immunofluorescence
The pDsRed2-N1-ANKRD49 (ANKRD49-Red2) plasmid was constructed by cloning the human ANKRD49 coding sequence (CDS) into the pDsRed2-N1 vector (Cat# 632406, Clontech, USA). Similarly, the pEGFP-N1-PKNOX1 (PKNOX1-GFP) plasmid was generated by inserting the human PKNOX1 CDS into the pEGFP-N1 vector (Cat# 6085-1, Clontech, USA). For colocalization analysis, HEK 293 T cells were co-transfected with 1.5 µg of ANKRD49-Red2 plasmid and 1.5 µg of PKNOX1-GFP plasmid using LipoFiter 3.0 transfection reagent. Live cells were imaged 48 h post-transfection without fixation or staining using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ts2). GFP fluorescence was excited at 488 nm and collected through a 505–530 nm band-pass filter; DsRed2 fluorescence was excited at 563 nm and detected using a 585–620 nm band-pass filter. The red and green channel signals were subsequently overlaid to assess protein colocalization.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
HEK 293 T cells were co-transfected with p3×Flag-ANKRD49 and pEGFP-N1-PKNOX1 for 48 h. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Cat# P0040, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C). Protein concentration was quantified by BCA assay. For each immunoprecipitation reaction, 500 µg of total protein was incubated with 2 µL of mouse anti-Flag antibody or control mouse IgG at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. Subsequently, 20 µL of Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads was added to the mixture and incubated for an additional 4 h at 4 °C with rotation. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with ice‑cold IP wash buffer. Bound proteins were eluted and resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting with the indicated primary antibodies.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. Graphical representations, including bar graphs and scatter plots, were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0. A two‑tailed unpaired Student’s t‑test was applied for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one‑way or two‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test where appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as 𝑝 < 0.05. Correlations between ANKRD49 and E‑cadherin, α‑SMA, TGF‑β1, or PKNOX1 were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

## Results

## ANKRD49 expression correlates with EMT markers in NSCLC
To investigate the potential role of ANKRD49 in EMT within NSCLC, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of ANKRD49, E-cadherin, α-SMA, and TGF-β1 protein expression in 89 human lung adenocarcinoma samples (Figure S1). As demonstrated in Fig.  A, tissues derived from NSCLC patients exhibiting high ANKRD49 expression displayed increased levels of α-SMA, TGF-β1, and PKNOX1, coupled with decreased E-cadherin expression. Furthermore, correlation analysis confirmed a significant negative association between ANKRD49 and E-cadherin, while ANKRD49 expression was positively correlated with α-SMA, TGF-β1 and PKNOX1 (Fig.  B).

 

## ANKRD49 redounds epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in NSCLC cells
Using our previously established cell models [ , ], ANKRD49-overexpressing (ANKRD49-OE) and ANKRD49-knockdown (ANKRD49-KD) A549 and H1299 cell lines were generated (Figure S2). Morphological analysis revealed that ANKRD49-OE cells displayed characteristic EMT features, including diminished cell-cell contacts and an elongated, spindle-like shape (Fig. A).

 

At the molecular level, ANKRD49 overexpression significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of the epithelial markers E-cadherin and EpCAM, while upregulating the mesenchymal markers α-SMA, N-cadherin, Snail, Slug and ZEB1 in both cell lines; Twist1 and Vimentin mRNA levels remained unchanged (Figure S3A-D). Furthermore, ANKRD49 overexpression decreased E-cadherin and EpCAM protein levels and concomitantly increased those of α-SMA, N-cadherin, Snail, Slug, and ZEB1, whereas Twist and Vimentin levels were unaltered (Fig.  B, S4A). Conversely, ANKRD49 knockdown had the opposite effect, increasing E-cadherin and EpCAM expression but decreasing that of α-SMA, N-cadherin, Snail, Slug, and ZEB1, with no effect on Twist or Vimentin levels. (Fig.  C, S4B). Taken together, these results indicate that ANKRD49 drives EMT in NSCLC cells by suppressing epithelial markers and promoting mesenchymal markers, with the exception of Vimentin and Twist.

## ANKRD49 cooperates with TGF‑β1 signaling to induce full EMT
In unstimulated conditions, ANKRD49 overexpression alone did not alter the expression of specific EMT markers (e.g., Vimentin) in A549 or H1299 cells, indicating its insufficiency to drive complete EMT. To examine whether ANKRD49 synergizes with TGF‑β1 to promote a full EMT program, cells were treated with recombinant human TGF‑β1 (10 ng/mL, Cat# 10804‑HNAC, SinoBiological, China) for 24 h. Upon TGF‑β1 stimulation, ANKRD49 overexpression markedly upregulated mesenchymal markers (Vimentin, α‑SMA, N‑cadherin) at both mRNA (Figure S5A, B) and protein levels (Figure S5C), while downregulating the epithelial marker E‑cadherin. Conversely, ANKRD49 knockdown attenuated TGF‑β1‑induced expression of Vimentin, α‑SMA, and N‑cadherin, while elevating E‑cadherin levels (Figure S5D). Together, these results demonstrate that ANKRD49 alone initiates partial EMT but requires TGF‑β1 signaling to orchestrate a complete EMT transition in NSCLC cells.

## ANKRD49 promotes EMT in vivo
To determine whether ANKRD49 enhances tumor metastasis through EMT in vivo, we intravenously injected ANKRD49‑OE or vector‑control A549 and H1299 cells into BALB/c nude mice via the tail vein [ , ]. After four weeks, immunohistochemical analysis of lung tissues showed that mice injected with ANKRD49‑OE cells exhibited significantly lower levels of E‑cadherin and EpCAM, along with markedly higher expression of α‑SMA, N‑cadherin, Slug, Snail and ZEB1, compared with vector‑control groups (Fig. A, B and Figure S6A, B). Successful ANKRD49 overexpression in the experimental group was confirmed by IHC. These results indicate that ANKRD49 drives EMT during lung colonization of NSCLC cells.

 

To further assess the role of ANKRD49 in EMT within a localized tumor context, we performed a subcutaneous xenograft assay. ANKRD49 overexpression did not significantly affect subcutaneous tumor volume or weight (Figure S6C, D), suggesting it does not regulate tumor cell proliferation – consistent with prior in vitro findings [ , ]. However, immunohistochemical analysis of xenograft tissues revealed that ANKRD49 significantly suppressed E‑cadherin and EpCAM expression while markedly upregulating α‑SMA, N‑cadherin, Slug, Snail and ZEB1, compared with controls (Fig. A, B and Figure S6E, F).

 

Together, these in vivo findings demonstrate that EMT represents a key mechanism through which ANKRD49 promotes tumor progression.

## ANKRD49 activates TGF-β/SMAD signaling via TGF-β1 upregulation
Since TGF-β1 is a well-established inducer of EMT in cancer progression [ – ], we examined whether ANKRD49 modulates TGF-β1 expression. Western blot analysis showed significantly higher TGF-β1 protein levels in ANKRD49-OE A549 and H1299 cells than in vector controls (Fig. A and S7A), whereas ANKRD49 knockdown reduced TGF-β1 expression (Fig. B, and S7B). Moreover, ELISA revealed increased TGF-β1 in conditioned media from ANKRD49-OE cells (Fig. C) and decreased secretion upon ANKRD49 knockdown (Fig. D). Consistent with these in vitro findings, immunohistochemistry of lung xenografts (Fig. E, F) and subcutaneous tumor tissues (Fig. G, H) showed elevated TGF-β1 levels in mice injected with ANKRD49-OE cells compared with controls. Together, these results establish ANKRD49 as a positive regulator of TGF-β1 expression and secretion across multiple experimental systems.

 

The TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in diverse cellular processes, including apoptosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and adipocyte differentiation [ – ]. Notably, TGF-β-induced EMT primarily depends on canonical SMAD signaling activation [ , ]. To determine whether ANKRD49-mediated EMT requires TGF-β/SMAD pathway activation, we performed Western blot analyses and found that ANKRD49 overexpression in A549 and H1299 cells significantly increased phospho-SMAD2/3 levels (Fig. A and S7C), while ANKRD49 knockdown reduced them (Fig. C and S7D). Luciferase reporter assays further confirmed that ANKRD49 enhanced SMAD-binding element (SBE)4(BV) activity (Fig. B), indicating upregulation of SMAD-dependent transcription. In vivo, lung tissues (Figs. A, B and Figure S6A, B) and subcutaneous tumor tissues (Fig. A, B and Figure S6E, F) from mice injected with ANKRD49-OE cells exhibited stronger p-SMAD2/3 immunostaining than controls. Pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β/SMAD signaling with Galunisertib (5 µM) reversed ANKRD49-driven EMT, as shown by decreased α-SMA and N-cadherin and increased E-cadherin expression (Fig. D, E and Figure S7E, F).

 

Collectively, these data demonstrate that ANKRD49 promotes EMT in NSCLC cells by activating the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway.

## ANKRD49 activates PKNOX1-mediated transcriptional regulation of TGF-β1
To elucidate how ANKRD49 activates TGF-β/SMAD signaling, we analyzed the human TGF-β1 promoter region using the UCSC genome database and identified two predicted PKNOX1-binding sites (–1455 to − 1441 and − 1511 to − 1500) (Fig.  A). We constructed luciferase reporter plasmids carrying the TGF-β1 promoter (pGL3-Basic-TGF-β1-promoter) along with expression vectors for Flag-tagged ANKRD49 and PKNOX1 (p3×Flag-ANKRD49, p3×Flag-PKNOX1).

 

First, Western blot revealed that endogenous PKNOX1 was either absent or expressed at very low levels in HEK 293 T cells (undetectable with the PKNOX1 antibody), whereas PKNOX1 expression was markedly increased upon transfection with p3×Flag-PKNOX1 (Figure S5E). This observation explains the lack of change in TGF-β1 promoter activity in HEK 293 T cells co-transfected with only the TGF-β1 reporter and empty vector. Notably, PKNOX1 overexpression alone significantly enhanced TGF-β1 promoter activity, and co-expression of ANKRD49 further amplified this effect, suggesting that ANKRD49 potentiates PKNOX1 binding to the TGF-β1 promoter and enhances its transcriptional output (Fig. B).
To identify the functional PKNOX1-binding site(s), luciferase assays were performed in 293 T cells co-transfected with p3×Flag-PKNOX1 and either the wild-type TGF-β1 promoter (WT) or mutants lacking the − 1455 to − 1441 (Mut1) or − 1511 to − 1500 (Mut2) regions. Activity was significantly attenuated only when the − 1455 to − 1441 site was deleted (Mut1), identifying this region as critical for PKNOX1-dependent TGF-β1 transcriptional activation (Fig.  C).
To validate this interaction in NSCLC cells, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed in ANKRD49-OE A549 cells transfected with p3×Flag-PKNOX1 using an anti-Flag antibody. qPCR primers targeting the functional region (–1458 to − 1347, containing the − 1455 to − 1441 site) or the region (–1605 to − 1470, containing the − 1511 to − 1500 site) were used. ChIP-qPCR analysis showed specific enrichment of the − 1455 to − 1441 region in Flag-PKNOX1 pulldowns from ANKRD49-OE cells compared with controls (Fig.  D), confirming that PKNOX1 directly binds this site and that ANKRD49 strengthens the interaction.
We next asked whether ANKRD49 regulates TGF-β1 signaling via PKNOX1. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that both mRNA and protein levels of PKNOX1 were significantly elevated in ANKRD49-OE A549 or H1299 cells relative to controls (Fig.  A, B and Figure S8A), whereas ANKRD49 knockdown reduced PKNOX1 expression (Fig. C, D and Figure S8B). Additionally, our in vivo assays revealed that PKNOX1 protein levels were elevated in both lung xenograft (Figure S8C, D) and subcutaneous tumor tissues (Figure S8E, F) derived from mice implanted with ANKRD49-OE A549 or H1299 cells, compared with tissues from vector control mice.

 

Subcellular fractionation assays further demonstrated increased nuclear accumulation of PKNOX1 in ANKRD49-OE cells (Fig.  E and Figure S8G). Importantly, siRNA-mediated PKNOX1 knockdown abrogated the ANKRD49-induced upregulation of TGF-β1 in both cell lines (Fig.  F and Figure S8H).
Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed co-localization of ANKRD49 and PKNOX1 in the cytoplasm and nucleus of NSCLC cells (Fig.  G), suggesting a potential physical interaction. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in 293 T cells co-transfected with Flag-ANKRD49 and PKNOX1-GFP confirmed a direct interaction between the two proteins (Fig.  H).
Together, these data demonstrate that ANKRD49 binds PKNOX1 and promotes its nuclear translocation. The ANKRD49-PKNOX1 complex specifically targets the − 1455 to − 1441 region of the TGF-β1 promoter, thereby amplifying its transcriptional activity and driving TGF-β/SMAD-dependent EMT in NSCLC.

## Discussion
Distant metastasis remains the leading cause of mortality in patients with lung cancer [ ]. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) serves as a crucial biological mechanism driving metastatic progression and is strongly associated with poor clinical prognosis [ ]. Ankyrin repeat domain 49 (ANKRD49) has been identified as a metastasis-associated gene that is frequently overexpressed in lung cancer [ ]. Our previous work demonstrated that ANKRD49 promotes the invasion and migration of NSCLC cells in vitro and in vivo through activation of the p38 MAPK/ATF-2/MMPs and JNK/ATF-2/MMPs signaling pathways [ , ]. While these studies established a role for ANKRD49 in tumor cell motility, its potential involvement in EMT – a fundamental process underlying cancer metastasis – had not been investigated in NSCLC.
Emerging evidence indicates that EMT in tumors exists along a spectrum of intermediate states rather than as a simple binary switch [ , ]. Most tumor cells undergoing EMT retain both epithelial and mesenchymal characteristics, a phenotypic state commonly termed “partial EMT” (p‑EMT) or “hybrid EMT” [ ]. Notably, accumulating data suggest that carcinoma cells in p‑EMT states exhibit greater metastatic capacity than those at either fully epithelial or fully mesenchymal poles, and p‑EMT is clinically correlated with higher tumor grade and worse patient outcomes [ , ]. These observations highlight p‑EMT‑related molecules as promising therapeutic targets for intervening in metastatic progression.
In the present study, we provide direct evidence that ANKRD49 induces EMT in NSCLC, as demonstrated by consistent shifts in established EMT markers both in vitro (Fig. B, C) and in vivo (Figs. and ). Intriguingly, ANKRD49 overexpression did not alter the expression of the canonical mesenchymal marker Vimentin, suggesting that the transition driven by ANKRD49 may represent a p‑EMT state rather than a complete EMT program. This finding aligns with the established concept that terminal mesenchymal markers such as Vimentin are often absent in p‑EMT [ ]. Classical EMT and p‑EMT are now understood to play distinct roles in tumor progression. While complete EMT typically promotes a solitary, invasive phenotype conducive to distant colonization [ ], p‑EMT represents a dynamic, plastic state that equips tumor cells with enhanced collective motility, survival, and stem‑like properties – characteristics critical for successful metastasis [ ]. This conceptual framework positions p‑EMT as a pivotal target for anti‑metastatic therapy.
Multiple signaling pathways, including those mediated by TGF‑β, integrins, and IL‑6, have been implicated in EMT induction [ – ]. Among these, TGF‑β is recognized as one of the most potent and well‑characterized EMT inducers. In mammals, the TGF‑β family comprises three isoforms (TGF‑β1, ‑β2, and ‑β3), with TGF‑β1 being the most abundantly expressed and functionally predominant subtype [ ]. In NSCLC, TGF‑β1 has been specifically shown to drive EMT progression [ , ]. Our current findings demonstrate that ANKRD49 significantly upregulates both intracellular TGF‑β1 expression and its extracellular secretion (Fig. ), thereby identifying ANKRD49 as a novel upstream regulator of this key EMT‑inducing pathway.
The canonical TGF‑β/SMAD signaling cascade is a well‑established mediator of EMT in NSCLC [ ]. Ligand binding initiates receptor‑mediated phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, which then forms a complex with SMAD4 and translocates to the nucleus to regulate transcription of EMT‑related genes [ ]. Here, we provide compelling evidence that ANKRD49 activates SMAD signaling through TGF‑β1 upregulation in NSCLC cells (Fig. A‑C). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of SMAD2/3 phosphorylation using the selective TβRI inhibitor Galunisertib effectively reversed ANKRD49‑induced EMT (Fig. D‑E). This observation holds clinical relevance, as Galunisertib has demonstrated antitumor activity in several malignancies – including lung, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [ – ] – and is currently under clinical evaluation. Our results thus support the therapeutic potential of targeting the TGF‑β/SMAD axis in advanced NSCLC, particularly in tumors exhibiting ANKRD49 overexpression.
While we established that ANKRD49 promotes EMT via TGF‑β1‑mediated SMAD activation, the upstream mechanism regulating TGF‑β1 expression remained unclear. Bioinformatic analysis of the TGF‑β1 promoter revealed two putative binding sites for the homeodomain transcription factor PKNOX1 (Fig. A). PKNOX1 plays essential roles in embryonic development and has been reported to exhibit tumor‑suppressive functions in adults [ ]. Interestingly, however, Risolino et al.. demonstrated that PKNOX1 can paradoxically induce EMT in NSCLC, and its knockdown attenuates TGF‑β1‑mediated EMT [ ]. Our investigation showed that ANKRD49 overexpression upregulates PKNOX1 expression, and that PKNOX1 knockdown abolishes ANKRD49‑induced TGF‑β1 upregulation (Fig. A‑F). Luciferase reporter and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed that PKNOX1 directly binds to the TGF‑β1 promoter and enhances its transcriptional activity (Fig. B‑D). These results establish a direct transcriptional mechanism through which ANKRD49 modulates TGF‑β1 expression via PKNOX1.
ANKRD49 belongs to the ankyrin repeat domain protein family, whose members typically mediate protein‑protein interactions through conserved 30ཞ34 amino acid repeats. Given that the family member ANKRD1 functions as a transcriptional co‑regulator in cardiac gene expression [ ], we hypothesized that ANKRD49 might similarly act as a transcriptional co‑factor within the PKNOX1‑TGF‑β1/SMAD axis. This hypothesis was supported by our experimental findings: ANKRD49 and PKNOX1 show clear subcellular co‑localization in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, and co‑immunoprecipitation assays confirm a direct physical interaction between the two proteins (Fig. G‑H). Notably, ANKRD49 contains four distinct ankyrin repeat domains. Future studies should aim to identify which specific domain(s) mediate its interaction with PKNOX1, as this could offer important structural insights for targeted therapeutic development.
Beyond transcriptional regulation, ion channels have emerged as critical players in tumorigenesis by modulating multiple cancer hallmarks [ ]. Voltage‑gated sodium channels (VGSCs), for example, are upregulated in several cancer types, including breast and lung cancer [ ]. In human breast cancer, activity of the VGSC subtype Nav1.5 enhances metastatic cascades in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Moreover, Nav1.5‑mediated currents can promote cellular aggressiveness by stimulating cysteine cathepsin activity in both breast and NSCLC cells [ ]. These findings position VGSCs as promising therapeutic targets. Local anesthetics – which suppress action potentials by blocking VGSCs – have shown potential anticancer effects; for instance, lidocaine reduces proliferation and invasion in gastric cancer cells via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [ , ]. Additionally, traditional medicines are increasingly used in adjunctive cancer therapy [ ]. Certain traditional toxin‑based agents exert direct effects on cancer cells, partly through modulation of ion channels [ ]. These observations raise an intriguing question: could the antimetastatic effects of local anesthetics or traditional medicines be mediated, at least in part, through the ANKRD49‑PKNOX1‑TGF‑β1/SMAD signaling axis? To systematically identify compounds targeting this pathway, integrated strategies combining bioinformatics with high‑throughput experimental approaches – such as CRISPR screening coupled with mining of the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) and Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) datasets – could be effectively employed [ , ].

## Conclusion
Our study elucidates a novel molecular mechanism through which ANKRD49 drives EMT in NSCLC by activating the TGF-β1/SMAD signaling axis. We demonstrate that ANKRD49 physically interacts with the transcription factor PKNOX1, thereby enhancing PKNOX1-mediated transcriptional activation of TGF-β1. Subsequent TGF-β1 secretion establishes an autocrine/paracrine signaling loop that sustains SMAD activation and promotes EMT progression (Fig.  ). These findings establish ANKRD49 as a critical regulator of the metastatic cascade in NSCLC and highlight its potential as a promising therapeutic target for clinical intervention.
