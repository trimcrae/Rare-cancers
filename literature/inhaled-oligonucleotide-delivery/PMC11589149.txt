However, the precise role of endothelially expressed eNOS and how its deficiency advances DN are still unclear. Here, we targeted endothelial eNOS expression (E-eNOSKO) after the onset of diabetes using the floxed eNOS and endSCL-CreERT alleles. Diabetes was induced by low-dose streptozotocin injections. To evaluate the role of nuclear factor of activated T cells-2 (NFAT2) in podocyte injury in this condition, podocyte-specific NFAT2KO mice were also generated on eNOSGKO mice. The mechanisms of podocyte injury were investigated using cultured podocytes. Compared with diabetic wild-type mice, diabetic E-eNOSKO mice showed more advanced DN accompanied by NFAT2 expression in podocytes. NO donor suppressed NFAT2 expression and activation in high-glucose cultured podocytes as well as in diabetic E-eNOSKO mice. Furthermore, podocyte-specific deletion of NFAT2 attenuated DN in diabetic eNOSGKO mice accompanied by decreased heparanase (HPSE) expression in podocytes. Consistent with this finding, HPSE was upregulated by NFAT2 transfection and suppressed by NFAT2 siRNA or NO donor treatment in cultured podocytes. HPSE transfection reduced podocyte attachment to extracellular matrix concurrent with syndecan-4 (SDC4) shedding, and this effect was attenuated by co-transfection of SDC4. Finally, HPSE inhibitor treatment attenuated podocyte injury in diabetic E-eNOSKO mice with increased SDC4 expression in podocytes. Collectively, our data suggest that endothelial eNOS deficiency causes podocyte HPSE expression in diabetic mice through NFAT2, and HPSE promotes podocyte detachment in part through SDC4 shedding, advancing DN.

## Introduction
Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a major microvascular complication that determines morbidity and mortality of diabetic patients. A body of literature has shown that eNOS expression and activity is upregulated in diabetic animals and the early phase of human DN and decreases on the disease’s progression. The dysfunction of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which decreases endothelial nitric oxide (NO) production in glomerulus, has been implicated as a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of DN – . The eNOS deficient diabetic mice showed advanced nephropathic changes that resemble human DN , . Furthermore, we have shown that diabetic global eNOS knockout (eNOSGKO) mice show advanced podocytopathy , suggesting the mechanism of glomerular endothelial cell—podocyte crosstalk. However, how endothelial eNOS deficiency advances podocyte injury has not been clarified.
The eNOSGKO mice exhibit renal abnormalities, including crowded atubular glomeruli, abnormal structure of renal vasculature and focal renal scars, and show progressive renal disease even in the absence of diabetes – . The eNOSGKO mice also develop congenital vascular anomalies including septal defects that lead to heart failure , abnormal aortic valve development , and pulmonary vascular defects . Furthermore, eNOS was shown to be expressed in several cell types other than endothelial cells, including cardiomyocytes, erythrocytes, platelets, adipocytes, and renal tubules , . Thus, there are limitations to study the NO-mediated crosstalk mechanism between glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes using eNOSGKO mice. For this reason, we generated a floxed eNOS mice that enabled endothelial cell specific eNOS deletion in diabetic mice .
In this study, we conditionally deleted endothelial eNOS expression in mice after the onset of diabetes to better mimic the clinical setting, and investigated the glomerular endothelial cell-podocyte cross talk mechanisms in DN. Our data demonstrated a critical role for endothelial cell-derived NO in the negative regulation of nuclear factor of activated T cells-2 (NFAT2) and heparanase (HPSE) pathways in diabetic podocytes, which leads to the detachment of podocytes from glomerular basement membrane (GBM).

## Results

## Generation of endothelial cell-specific eNOSKO mice
In order to investigate the role of endothelially expressed eNOS in DN, we created the endSCL-CreERT eNOSflox/flox (E-eNOSKO) mice that enabled deletion of eNOS in endothelium after the onset of diabetes (Fig.  A). The E-eNOSKO mice showed normal renal histology without TAM treatment (Supplementary Fig. ). We then induced diabetes in 8 week-old E-eNOSKO male mice by low-dose STZ injections, and TAM was injected at 14 weeks of age to delete endothelial eNOS expression (E-eNOSKO + STZ) (Fig.  B). Endothelial eNOS deletion was confirmed by 4,5-diaminofluorescein (DAF-2DA) stain that visualizes NO production as well as by eNOS immunostaining. Shown in Fig.  C,D, TAM treatment reduced eNOS expression and NO production in glomerulus by over 80% in both diabetic and non-diabetic E-eNOSKO mice. 

## Diabetic E-eNOSKO mice develop progressive DN
Phenotypic analysis showed no significant difference in blood glucose (BG), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and a ratio of left kidney weight to body weight (LKW/BW) between WT + STZ and E-eNOSKO + STZ mice (Fig.  A–C). However, compared with WT + STZ mice, E-eNOSKO + STZ mice showed significantly increased albuminuria and serum creatinine at 22 weeks after STZ injections (Fig.  D,E). Furthermore, E-eNOSKO + STZ mice exhibited more advanced glomerular pathology including advanced mesangial expansion and significantly decreased WT1-positive cells (podocytes) in glomeruli than WT + STZ mice, indicating advanced podocyte damage in these glomeruli (Fig.  F–H, Supplemental Fig. A,B). Nephrin-positive area in glomerulus was also significantly reduced (F g.  I). Electron micrograph of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice (22 weeks after STZ injections) showed endothelial structural changes that indicate endothelial injury, including subendothelial insudation and inflammatory cell infiltration, as well as mesangial matrix expansion, irregular GBM thickening, and local foot process effacement (Fig.  J). Tubulointerstitial lesions were not observed in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice. Plasma lipids were not measured in the current study as significant difference was not observed between eNOSGKO + STZ and WT + STZ mice in our previous study, yet eNOSGKO + STZ mice showed higher plasma cholesterol levels . 

## Podocytes of diabetic E-eNOSKO mice express NFAT2
Since the inhibitory role of NO for the TRPC6-Ca2 + /calcineurin (CN)-NFAT pathway has been reported in non-renal cells , , we hypothesized that eNOS deficiency activates Ca2 + /CN-NFAT signaling in diabetic glomeruli and causes pathological gene expression. Based on this hypothesis, we evaluated the expression of NFAT isoform (NFAT 1–4) in our models. While NFAT1, 3 and 4 did not change along with endothelial eNOS deletion (Supplementary Fig. A), NFAT2 is remarkably upregulated and localized to the nuclei of over 70% of podocytes in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice (Fig.  A). The number of NFAT2-positive cells in E-eNOSKO + STZ mouse glomeruli was significantly higher than that in WT + STZ mouse glomeruli at 22 weeks after STZ injections (Fig.  B). Highly phosphorylated NFAT2 resides in the cytoplasm; however, once NFAT is dephosphorylated by CN and activated, it translocates to nucleus and induces target gene transcription . Therefore, nuclear localization of NFAT2 indicates activation of NFAT2 in these podocytes. Cytoplasmic NFAT2 was limited in our immunofluorescence stain. Since the specificity of our NFAT2 immunofluorescence stain was confirmed by the conditional knockout study (Fig.  B), this may be due to the limited immunoreactivity of this antibody to cytoplasmic NFAT2. Of interest, NFAT2 expression was progressively increased in podocytes of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice, from 10 to 22 weeks after STZ injections (Fig.  A,B). Since Cre recombinase is inactive after TAM treatment, this would be the secondary effects. It is of note that similar NFAT2 immunostain is observed in podocytes of human DN (Supplementary Fig. B, C). 
The C57BL6/J strain was reported to be resistant to DN . Therefore, we also examined glomerular NFAT2 expression in nephropathy-prone mice such as DBA2 + STZ , Lperdb/db, and eNOS deficient Lperdb/db mice . In DBA2 + STZ and Lperdb/db mice glomeruli, obvious NFAT2 immunostain was not observed in podocytes whereas evident NFAT2 immunostain was observed in podocytes in eNOS deficient Lperdb/db mice (Supplementary Fig. C,D). Podocyte NFAT2 immunostaining was also observed in eNOSGKO + STZ mice (Supplementary Fig. E). For STZ or Lperdb/db diabetic mice, it is thought that eNOS expression and activity do not largely decrease. It is thought that this is a reason why their DN does not progress as it does in humans , . This could be the reason why NFAT2 immunostain was not observed in these mice. These results suggest that eNOS deficiency together with diabetes facilitates NFAT2 expression and activation in podocytes.
Next, we conducted cell culture experiments to verify the role of NO in negative regulation of NFAT2 expression and activation in diabetic podocytes. Immortalized mouse podocytes were cultured with different concentrations of NO donor (Sodium Nitroprusside, SNP) in high glucose (HG) conditions for 24 h. As shown in Fig.  C–E, HG treatment up-regulated NFAT2 protein and mRNA expression and facilitated its nuclear translocation in podocytes. These effects were dose-dependently suppressed by the NO-donor.
To evaluate if NO negatively regulates podocyte NFAT2 activity in vivo, NO donor (NaNO3) was added to the drinking water of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice for 4 weeks, starting at 14 weeks of age (6 weeks after STZ injections) (Fig.  F). As shown in Fig.  G,H, NO donor treatment significantly decreased NFAT2 immunostain in podocytes and albuminuria, although significant effects were not observed in BG, SBP, and LKW/BW ratio (Supplementary Fig. ). Collectively, these results suggest an inhibitory role for NO in NFAT2 expression and activation in diabetic podocytes.

## Podocyte-specific NFAT2 deletion attenuates DN in diabetic eNOSGKO mice
To evaluate the role of NFAT2 in diabetic podocyte injury, next we conditionally deleted podocyte NFAT2 expression in diabetic eNOSGKO mice using Podocin-Cre (Pd-Cre) and floxed NFAT2 mice (Fig.  A). Since endothelial NFAT2 is deleted by endSCL-CreERT transgene in E-eNOSKO mice, eNOSGKO mice were used for this study. As shown in Fig.  B, podocyte NFAT2 immunoreactivity was largely diminished in Pd-Cre NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice compared to NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice, and albuminuria was significantly reduced in Pd-Cre NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice 22 weeks after STZ injections (Fig.  C). There was no difference in BG, SBP, and LKW/BW ratio between these two groups (Fig.  D–F). Histological investigation showed milder glomerular pathology and mesangial expansion, higher number of podocytes (WT1-positive cells), preserved nephrin immunoreactivity, and preserved podocyte morphology in Pd-Cre NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice (Fig.  G–J).

## NFAT2 induces HPSE expression in diabetic podocytes
Recent studies have shown that Heparanase (HPSE) is upregulated in diabetic glomeruli in humans and mice, and plays a critical role in DN – . However, the detailed mechanisms of this, including responsible transcriptional factors, are incompletely understood. Since a recent study has shown that eNOS deficiency up-regulates glomerular HPSE expression in adriamycin nephrosis model , we evaluated HPSE expression in NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ and Pd-Cre NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice. In NFAT2flox/flox eNOSGKO + STZ mice, HPSE immunoreactivity was observed mainly in podocytes that were identified by anti-podoplanin (PDPN) immunostaining (Fig.  A). This podocyte HPSE expression was largely diminished in Pd-Cre NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO + STZ mice, suggesting a causal relationship between NFAT2 and HPSE expression in these podocytes (Fig.  A). The HPSE activation in diabetic eNOSKO glomeruli was further confirmed by immunostaining of a substrate of HPSE, heparan sulfate (HS) as well as cathepsin L/D, which is critical for the enzymatic activation and processing of inactive pro-heparanase – . As expected, HS immunoreactivity in glomerular wall was reduced and Cathepsin L and D expression was observed in NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO + STZ mouse glomeruli, while Pd-Cre NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO + STZ mouse glomeruli exhibited stronger HS immunoreactivity and reduced Cathepsin L and D expression (Fig.  B). HPSE was shown to facilitate the shedding of heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) such as syndecans (SDCs) by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) ; therefore, we also evaluated the expression of MMPs in these glomeruli, and found that MMP2 and MMP9 immunoreactivity were observed in NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO + STZ mouse glomeruli, while they were reduced in Pd-Cre NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO + STZ mouse glomeruli (Fig.  C). These findings suggested that NFAT2 induces HPSE expression and activation in diabetic podocytes, which causes HSPGs shedding as well as cleavage of their HS chains. 
To evaluate if NFAT2 upregulates HPSE expression in podocytes, transient transfection of a constitutively active form of NFAT2 (CA-NFAT2) or NFAT2 siRNA to cultured podocytes was carried out. As shown in Fig.  D, overexpression of CA-NFAT2 significantly up-regulated HPSE mRNA expression at 18 h and 48 h after transfection. In support of above described in vivo findings, CA-NFAT2 also upregulated CathL/D and MMP2/9 mRNA expression (Fig.  D). On the other hand, NFAT2 siRNA down-regulated HPSE, CathL/D, and MMP2/9 mRNA expression in podocytes in HG culture conditions (Fig.  E). These findings indicated that NFAT2 serves as a key transcriptional factor in the induction of HPSE, CathL/D, and MMP2/9 in diabetic podocytes.

## Endothelial eNOS deficiency upregulates HPSE expression in diabetic podocytes
Next we asked if endothelial eNOS deficiency upregulates HPSE expression in podocytes in diabetic mice. As shown in Fig.  A,B, -eNOSKO + STZ mouse glomeruli showed significantly higher HPSE immunoreactivity than WT + STZ mouse glomeruli, and this was co-localized with podoplanin (PDPN) immunostain. In support of this finding, HS immunoreactivity was decreased in E-eNOSKO + STZ glomeruli, while Cathepsin L and D were increased in these glomeruli (Supplementary Fig. ). It is noteworthy that HPSE as well as NFAT2 expression progressively increased in podocytes of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice, from 10 to 22 weeks after STZ injections (Fig.  A,B). In nephropathy-prone DBA2 + STZ and Lperdb/db mice, weak HPSE immunoreactivity was observed in their glomeruli; however, podocyte HPSE expression was highly limited in these glomeruli, while eNOS deficient Lperdb/db or eNOSGKO + STZ mice showed strong HPSE immunoreactivity in podocytes (Supplementary Fig. A–C), further confirming an important role of eNOS in inhibiting HPSE expression in diabetic podocytes. HPSE immunoreactivity was also observed in podocytes of human DN (Supplementary Fig. D) as described in previous reports , . 
In order to further validate NO regulation of HPSE expression in diabetic glomeruli, especially in podocytes, we also examined HPSE expression in NO donor (NaNO3)-treated E-eNOSKO + STZ mice shown in Fig.  F–H. As shown in Fig.  C, -week NaNO3 treatment largely decreased HPSE expression in podocytes in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice. In addition, NO donor (SNP) also dose-dependently suppressed HPSE activities (in culture medium), mRNA and protein expression in podocytes cultured in HG (Fig.  D–F). It is of interest that NO donor showed significant effects on glomerulus without altering SBP, including NFAT2 and HPSE expression and albuminuria (Fig.  G,H, Supplemental Fig. B). This finding suggests that glomerular microvasculature is more sensitive to NO than arteries. In this context, it is noteworthy that E-eNOS + STZ mice showed significant glomerular phenotype although their SBP was comparable to those in WT + STZ mice (Fig.  ).

## HPSE reduces podocyte attachment in part through syndecan-4 shedding
Given the fact that HPSEs regulate cell attachment to matrix protein , , podocyte attachment assays were carried out to assess the role of HPSE in diabetic podocytes. As shown in Fig.  A, the podocytes that were transfected with wild-type HPSE showed decreased adhesion to various extracellular matrix proteins (ECMs) in both control and HG conditions, including vitronectin, laminin, collagen IV, and fibronectin. Conversely, HPSE siRNA transfection restored the reduction of podocyte attachment, which was induced by HG culture (Fig.  B). To determine whether this HPSE activity requires its enzymatic activity, we also transfected catalytically inactive forms of HPSE to podocytes, in which the enzyme active sites (Glu-225 or Glu-343) are mutated . As shown in Fig.  C, transfection of catalytically inactive HPSE to podocytes did not decrease their attachment to ECMs. These results demonstrate that HPSE reduces podocyte attachment to ECMs through its enzymatic activity. Interestingly, transfection of CA-NFAT2 also decreased podocyte attachment to ECM, and this effect was largely attenuated by a selective HPSE inhibitor (SST-0001) , treatment (Fig.  D), indicating the relationship between NFAT2 and HPSE. 
Heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) serve as a substrate for HPSE . Among HSPGs, syndecan 4 (SDC4) is localized to focal contacts and regulates cell-ECM adhesion with integrin , . Importantly, HPSE was shown to accelerate the shedding of SDC4 by MMPs, decreasing the cell surface level of extracellular domain of SDC4 (Ext-SDC4, Fig.  E), and this impairs SDC4 function to promote cell adhesion , . Therefore, we further examined the effects of HPSE on SDC4 activity in diabetic podocytes. As shown in Fig.  F, HPSE transfection to podocytes significantly increased soluble SDC4 in cell culture medium. In support of this finding, immunoblot analysis showed that HPSE transfection to podocytes increases SDC4 in the cell culture medium, which is detected by anti-SDC4 antibody that recognizes the Ext-SDC4, while it decreased the amount of Ext-SDC4 in cell extracts (Fig.  G). Furthermore, immunoblotting using anti-SDC4 antibody that reacts to cytoplasmic domain of SDC4 (Cyt-SDC4) showed that HPSE transfection decreases full-length SDC4, while it increases a small fragment of SDC4, perhaps produced by SDC4 shedding (Fig.  G). The expression levels of integrin α3, α5, αV, β1, and β3 did not change with HPSE transfection, HG, or HG + SNP treatment (Supplementary Fig. A). These results suggested that HPSE impairs podocyte attachment to ECMs through SDC4 shedding as well as cleavage of its HS chains. Therefore, next we asked if co-transfection of SDC4 with HPSE attenuates the effect of HPSE to decrease podocyte attachment. As shown in Fig.  H, co-transfection of SDC4 with HPSE significantly increases podocyte attachment in both control and HG conditions, yet it did not fully restore the podocyte attachment.

## HPSE inhibitor treatment attenuates DN in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice
We conducted the HPSE inhibitor treatment study to further assess the role of HPSE in diabetic podocyte injury in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice. Subcutaneous injections of a selective HPSE inhibitor (SST0001) or saline were given to E-eNOSKO + STZ mice from 14 to 18 weeks of age (Fig.  A). Renal phenotypes were assessed at 10 weeks after STZ injection. As shown in Fig.  B–E, HPSE inhibitor treatment significantly attenuated albuminuria, while it showed no effects on BG, SBP, and LKW/BW ratio. Histological analysis revealed that HPSE inhibitor-treated mice show milder mesangial expansion and increases in WT1-positive cells and Nephrin-positive area in glomerulus (Fig.  F to 8I). Furthermore, we found that immunoreactivity to Ext-SDC4, but not Cyt-SDC4, is decreased in the podocytes of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice and that HPSE inhibitor treatment restores Ext-SDC4 levels in these podocytes (Fig.  J), indicating that HPSE promotes SDC4 shedding in podocytes in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice. HPSE inhibitor did not change the integrin expression in these glomeruli (Supplementary Fig. B). Collectively, our data suggest that endothelial eNOS deficiency in diabetes causes podocyte HPSE expression through NFAT2, and HPSE reduces podocyte attachment in part through SDC4 shedding, leading to advanced podocyte injury such as detachment (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
The present study demonstrated that: (1) endothelial eNOS deficiency worsens DN in mice, in part by inducing NFAT2 and HPSE in podocytes and enhancing podocyte injury, (2) NFAT2 induces HPSE in podocytes, and (3) HPSE reduces podocyte attachment in part through SDC4 shedding.
The E-eNOSKO + STZ mice showed milder phenotypes than eNOSGKO + STZ mice , . No difference was observed in SBP and LKW/BW ratio between E-eNOSKO + STZ and WT + STZ mice. Furthermore, significant difference was not observed in albuminuria between these two groups at 10 weeks after STZ injections. Thus, DN of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice is milder and more slowly progresses than that of eNOSGKO + STZ mice. It was recently shown that red blood cells (RBCs) as well as endothelial cells express eNOS in mice and humans , and that eNOS in both endothelial cells and RBCs contributes to regulation of blood pressure in mice , . Furthermore, the deletion of eNOS in glomerular endothelium is not complete in E-eNOSKO + STZ mice; ~ 15% of glomeruli showed eNOS expression or activity. These would be the reason why E-eNOSKO + STZ mice show milder phenotypes.
In this study, we examined the GEnCs and podocytes crosstalk using E-eNOS + STZ mice. Our data demonstrated that endothelial eNOS deficiency activates podocyte NFAT2 pathway in the condition of diabetes and advances podocyte injury. Previous studies described the role of NFAT2 in podocyte injury. The transgenic mice in which a constitutively active form of NFAT2 is expressed in podocytes developed massive proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis . A cell-permeable NFAT inhibitor 11R-VIVIT was shown to attenuate podocyte injury and nephropathy in diabetic mice , yet the responsible NFAT isoform remains unclear as 11R-VIVIT blocks all NFAT (1–4) isoforms . Last, it was shown that HG stimulates NFAT2 activation in podocytes and 11R-VIVIT or NFAT2 knockdown suppresses HG-induced podocyte apoptosis or dysfunction in culture , . However, the role of NFAT2 in diabetic podocyte injury was still unclear in vivo. In this context, our study is significant, showing that deletion of podocyte NFAT2 protects mice from diabetic podocyte injury in the condition of eNOS deficiency. Although the detailed mechanisms by which NO negatively regulates podocyte NFAT2 activity remain to be elucidated, given the facts that NO inhibits TRPC6-CN signal, which activates NFAT2 in podocytes, by activating cGMP-protein kinase G (PKG) , , , , and that NO negatively regulates the production and action of angiotensin-II (Ang-II), ET-1 and TNFα that activate TRPC6-CN cascade , , the observed NFAT2 activation may be caused by inhibition of PKG or by increased activities of ET-1, Ang-II and TNFα or both.
Recent studies have shown a pivotal role of HPSE in DN . HPSE is upregulated in diabetic glomeruli in humans and mice, including GEnCs and podocytes – , . Importantly, HPSE deficiency or inhibitor largely protected mice from DN . Our data demonstrated that endothelial eNOS deficiency upregulates HPSE expression in diabetic podocytes. This model was also supported by NO donor treatment studies. Furthermore, eNOS deficiency was also shown to upregulate glomerular HPSE expression in adriamycin nephropathy model . Collectively, these findings demonstrate an important role of eNOS-derived NO in negative regulation of glomerular HPSE expression. Although the detailed mechanisms of this are currently unknown, our data demonstrated that NFAT2 upregulates HPSE expression in podocytes. To date, several transcriptional factors have been implicated in HPSE induction in renal and non-renal cells. These include Egr1 , GABP , NFkB , , and Sp1 . However, NFAT regulation of HPSE expression has not been reported yet. Through the computational analysis using JASPAR , we noted that NFAT binding sequence (GGAAAA/CCTTTT) is present in both mouse and human HPSE promoter region. Further investigation would be required to verify the NFAT2 regulation of HPSE gene expression, including the chromatin IP assay to assess the binding of NFAT2 to HPSE promoter. In this context, it is noteworthy that ET-1 and Ang-II that activate CN-NFAT2 cascade were also shown to upregulate HPSE in diabetic animals – .
HPSE degrades HS chains of HSPGs and regulates a variety of biological events, including inflammation, angiogenesis, growth factor or cytokine activity, cell migration, and cell attachment , . In glomerulus, negatively-charged HSPGs serve as a key component of endothelial glycocalyx that constitutes glomerular filtration barrier , and HPSE was shown to disrupt glomerular endothelial glycocalyx and cause albuminuria , . Furthermore, HPSE produced by glomerular cells stimulates macrophages and contributes to chronic inflammation in diabetic kidney . However, the role of HPSE in podocytes is still poorly described, although podocytes express several HSPGs such as syndecan-1, syndecan-4, glypican-1, and glypican-5 . Among HPSGs, SDC4 distributes to focal adhesions, interacts with integrins (β1, β3), and positively regulate cell adhesion , . HPSE was shown to impair cell attachment to ECMs by accelerating SDC4 shedding that is induced by matrix metalloproteinases (such as MMP2, MMP7, MMP9 and ADAM17) as well as by cleaving HS chains of SDC4 . Our data demonstrated that HPSE reduces the podocyte attachment to ECMs in part through SDC4 shedding. Since SDC4 transfection did not completely block HPSE activity to reduce podocyte attachment, other mechanisms would also be involved in this HPSE effect. Given the fact that glypican-1 interacts with ECMs , HPSE may also impair glypican-1 and ECM interactions in podocytes.
We have shown that HPSE inhibitor SST001 attenuates DN in the setting of endothelial eNOS deficiency, accompanied by reduction of podocyte injury. Nevertheless, HPSE is also expressed in GEnCs and inflammatory cells such as macrophages; therefore, the role of podocyte HPSE in DN in E-eNOSKO or eNOSGKO diabetic mice is still not clearly defined. The conditional knockout study to target podocyte HPSE would be required to address this issue. However, both eNOS and HPSE genes are in chromosome 5; this made it difficult for us to conduct this study. The efforts to create the floxed HPSE allele on eNOSGKO mice is currently underway. Also, further investigation would be required to examine whether eNOS-derived NO similarly regulate HPSE pathway in GEnCs as GEnCs cells express NFAT2 as well as HPSE.
Collectively, the present study demonstrates a novel mechanism connecting endothelial eNOS deficiency and diabetic podocyte injury, which is mediated by NFAT2 and HPSE. The development of pharmacological agents that effectively target this pathway (e.g. a highly selective NFAT2 inhibitor) may provide a new treatment for DN.

## Methods

## Antibodies

## Plasmids and siRNAs
Wild-type HPSE and catalytically inactive HPSE (mutated at amino acid 225 or 343) plasmids were provided by Dr. Sanderson (University of Alabama, Birmingham AL). Constitutively active NFAT2 and mouse SDC4 plasmids were purchased from Addgene (#11102, Watertown, MA) and Origene (#MR201975, Rockville, MD) respectively. HPSE siRNA (#s67768, #s67770, Thermo Fisher Scientific,) were used to knockdown HPSE. Control siRNA (#4390843, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a negative control and GAPDH siRNA (#4390849, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a positive control.

## Animal models
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with AAALAC guidelines and under the approval of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Vanderbilt University. The floxed eNOS mice were generated as described previously . Since the endSCL-Cre ERT mice is C57BL/6J strain, the floxed eNOS mice that was backcrossed onto C57BL/6J strain (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) for over 10 generations were used. To determine the role of endothelially expressed eNOS in DN, the floxed eNOS mice were crossed with the tamoxifen-inducible endSCL-Cre-ERT mice (C57BL/6J strain) , , in which a Cre-ERT recombinase is driven by the 5’ endothelial enhancer of the stem cell leukemia (SCL) locus, to generate the endSCL-CreER eNOSflox/flox (E-eNOSKO) mice. To investigate the role of NFAT2 in podocytes in diabetic eNOSKO mice, podocyte-specific NFAT2 KO mice were generated on eNOSGKO mice. The podocin-Cre NFAT2floxflox eNOSGKO mice were created using eNOSGKO mice (C57BL/6J strain, Jackson Laboratory) , floxed NFAT2 mice (C57BL/6J strain, Jackson Laboratory) , and Podocin-Cre mice (C57BL/6J strain, provided by Dr. Ray Harris) . DBA2/J, C57BLKS/J and Leprdb/db (C57BLKS/J background) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Lperdb/db eNOS global knockout (eNOS-/-/Lperdb/db) mice were prepared as described previously . Mice were genotyped by genomic PCR using the following primers. endSCL-CreERT: Fw 5ʹ-TCGATGCAACGAGTGATGAG-3ʹ, Re 5ʹ-TCCGGCTA TACGTAACAGGG-3ʹ; floxed eNOS: Fw 5ʹ-GGAGCTTGTGAAGGATAG-3ʹ, Re 5ʹ-CTGGGTCAAGTTGAAGAG-3ʹ; global eNOSKO: Fw 5ʹ-AATTCGCCAATGACAAGACG-3ʹ, Wild type; Fw 5ʹ-AGGGGAACAAGCCCAGTAGT-3ʹ, and Common Reverse 5ʹ-CTTGTCCCCTAGGC ACCTCT-3ʹ; floxed NFAT2: Fw 5ʹ-GGACAGTCTAAGGCCTGCTG-3ʹ, Re 5ʹ-ACCCCA CATCCCAGAGTGA-3ʹ: Podocin-Cre; Fw 5ʹ-GCATAACCAGTGAAACAGCATTGCTG-3ʹ, Re 5ʹ-GGACATGTTCAGGGATCGCCAGGCG-3ʹ.

## Animal experiments
Diabetes was induced in the endSCL-CreERT eNOSflox/flox or eNOSflox/flox mice at 8 weeks of age by low-dose STZ injections (E-eNOSKO + STZ and WT + STZ) as described previously , . In detail, Diabetes was induced in the mice at 8 weeks of age by administering STZ (50 mg/kg) intraperitoneally for 5 consecutive days. STZ (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5) immediately before injection. Non-diabetic control mice were prepared by citrate buffer injections (E-eNOSKO and WT) , . Diabetes was confirmed at 2 weeks after STZ injections by the measurement of blood glucose (> 300 mg/dL). Blood glucose was measured after a 6-h fast using Accu-check test strips (Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, IN). Tamoxifen (TAM; Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma) and injected (125 mg/kg) intraperitoneally five consecutive days at 14 weeks of age to delete endothelial eNOS expression in adulthood. Corn oil (Sigma) was used as vehicle. Their phenotypes including albuminuria and blood pressure was assessed at 10 and 22 weeks after STZ injections. Twenty-four hours urine was collected using a metabolic cage (Techiplast, Varese, Italy), and urine albumin and creatinine was determined using the kits (Exocell, Newtown Square, PA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions , . Systolic blood pressure was measured at least five times in trained conscious mice at room temperature using a tail-cuff monitor (IITC Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA) as described previously , . Serum creatinine was determined by underivatized stable isotope dilution LC–MS/MS at UAB O’Brien Center (Birmingham, AL). For NO donor treatment, NaNO3 (Sigma) was added to the drinking water at a concentration of 85 mg/L (1 mM) for 4 weeks, starting at 14 weeks of age of E-eNOSKO + STZ mice for the treatment group. Regular water was given to E-eNOSKO + STZ mice for the control group. Renal phenotype was analyzed at 10 weeks after STZ injections. For HPSE inhibitor treatment, SST0001 (Roneparstat) , , a selective HPSE inhibitor, was given to E-eNOSKO + STZ mice via subcutaneous injections (300 μg in 100 μL saline, twice per day) for 4 weeks, starting at 14 weeks of age. The same volume of saline was given to E-eNOSKO + STZ mice for control group. Renal phenotype was analyzed at 10 weeks after STZ injections.

## Light and electron microscopic assessment
The mice were anesthetized, and kidneys were perfused via left ventricle with PBS, removed, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for overnight. Three micrometer-thick paraffin sections were stained with a PAS stain kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The results were observed and photographed using a light microscopy (Olympus BX51, Tokyo, Japan). The degree of glomerular mesangial expansion was assessed as described previously , . In brief, mesangial matrix expansion occupying < 25, 25–50, 50–75, or > 75% of glomerular tuft was scored 1, 2, 3, and 4 respectively, and no mesangial expansion was scored as 0. For electron microscopy, kidneys were cut into small tissue blocks (1 mm3), fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA), then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in Epon 812 (Okenshoji, Tokyo, Japan). Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were observed by transmission electron microscopy (H-7500; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV.

## Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining
Kidney tissues were placed in OCT compound (Sakura Finetec, Torrance, CA) and snap frozen in a dry ice-acetone bath. Cryostat sections were fixed in 100% acetone for 10 min at − 20 °C, washed three times in cold PBS, and subjected to immunostaining. For eNOS immunofluorescence, cryostat sections were blocked using 5% skim milk, followed by incubation with rabbit polyclonal anti-eNOS antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for overnight at 4 °C. The immunoreaction was visualized using the fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody. The results were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51, Tokyo, Japan). The eNOS-positive glomeruli were counted in 10 randomly selected, non-overlapping fields at × 400 magnification in the renal cortex of kidney section of each mouse. For HPSE, Cath D, Cath L, WT1, Podocin, Podoplanin, and CD31 immunostaining, cryostat sections were blocked using 5% skim milk, then incubated with above described primary antibodies and fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies. At least randomly selected ten cortical glomeruli were examined per mouse. For the quantification of nephrin positive area or HPSE/PDPN co-stained area in glomerulus, at least randomly selected ten cortical glomeruli were examined per mouse and the percentage of immunostained area was evaluated using Image J package (NIH, Bethesda, MD) , . To examine how endothelial deletion of eNOS alters glomerular heparan sulfate (HS) status, HS immunofluorescence was conducted. After incubation with anti-HS single chain antibodies (HS4C3) for 1 h, sections were incubated with anti-VSV-G antibody for 1 h. Fluorescent-labeled anti-Rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to visualize. The HS4C3 (N-, 2-O, 3-O, and 6-O sulfation) antibody primarily stains the glomerulus and peritubular capillaries .
For HPSE and NFAT2 immunohistochemistry of human kidney samples, paraffin sections of 10% formalin-fixed renal biopsy specimens (provided by Dr. Akira Shimizu, Nippon Medical University) were used. Nonspecific reaction for horseradish peroxidase was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide in methyl alcohol. Antigen retrieval was carried out in 10 mM citrate buffer/pH 6.0 (HPSE; microwave 95 °C for 10 min, NFAT2; autoclave 120 °C for 20 min). Antibodies against HPSE and NFAT2 were applied to sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The subsequent procedures were performed using ABC system (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the substrate–chromogen reaction, 3, 3′- diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (Vector Laboratories) was used. These sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Vector Laboratories). The results were observed and photographed using a light microscopy (Olympus BX51).

## In situ detection of NO
NO production in renal vasculature was evaluated using 4,5-diaminofluorescein (DAF-2DA, Alexis biochemicals, San Diego, CA) as previously reported , . In brief, mice were anesthetized, whole body was perfused through left ventricle with PBS using infusion pump. After blood had been removed, the mice were perfused with PBS containing 0.01 mmol/L DAF-2DA, 0.1 mmol/L l-arginine and 2 mmol/L CaCl2 for 10 min. Unreacted DAF-2 was removed with additional PBS perfusion. Finally, the kidneys were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde, and removed. Cryostat sections were processed, and fluorescent images of NO were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51). NO-producing glomeruli were counted in 10 randomly selected and non-overlapping fields at × 400 magnification in the renal cortex of kidney section of each mouse.

## Cells and culture conditions
Cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4)-transformed mouse podocytes, an immortalized podocyte that expresses podocyte proteins including nephrin and WT1 , was provided by Dr. Jeffrey Kopp (NIH/NIDDK, Bethesda, MD). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For high-glucose culture study, cells were grown to 80% confluence, incubated with or without 25 mM D-glucose (HG or Cont) or with D-mannitol (25 mM; HM) for 24 h. For NO-donor treatment, different concentrations of NO-donor (Sodium Nitroprusside, SNP) were added to high-glucose medium and cultured for 24 h prior to harvesting for Western blotting or real-time qPCR.

## Transfection of cultured mouse podocytes
Transient transfection of cultured mouse podocytes was carried out using FuGENE HD transfection reagent (Promega, Madison, WI), according to the manufacturer’s protocol . In brief, cells were cultured in 100 mm dishes in growth medium to achieve a confluence of 70–80%. Transfection was performed using 2 μg of the DNA and 4 μL of Fugene HD transfection agent (added to 200 μL of serum-free medium). To knockdown HPSE, HPSE siRNA or control siRNA were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAi Max (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacture’s protocol. GAPDH siRNA was used as a positive control. The transfection efficiency assessed by the GFP plasmid transfection was ~ 60% and the knockdown efficiency assessed by GAPDH siRNA transfection was over 70%.

## Real-time quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cultured podocytes at 18 or 48 h after transfection using TRIzol (Invitrogen Corp., Carlsbad, CA). For NFAT2 knockdown study, the transfected cells were cultured in high- or normal glucose conditions for 48 h after transfection, then total RNA was extracted. A High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) was used to synthesize cDNA from total RNA. Transcripts encoding HPSE and 18S were amplified using SYBR Green-based quantitative PCR with SYBR Green super mix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) and a sequence detection system (CFX96; Bio-Rad), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Assays were run at least three times with triplicate measurements for each condition. The sequences of primers are as follows: murine NFAT2 (forward: 5′– GGAAGAAGATGGTGCTGTCT–3′; reverse: 5′– GCACAGGTCCCGGTC AGTCT–3′), murine HPSE (forward: 5′–GGAGCAAACTCCGA GTGTATC–3′; reverse: 5′–CAGAATTTGACCGTTCAGTTGG–3′) , murine Cath L (forward: 5′–ATCAAACCTTTAGT GCAGAGTGG–3′; reverse: 5′–CTGTATTCCCCGTTGTGTAGC–3′), murine Cath D (forward: 5′–GCTTCCGGTCTTTGACAACCT–3′; reverse: 5′–CACCAAGCATTAG TTCTCC TCC–3′), murine MMP-2 (forward: 5′–TGCAGGAGACAAGTTCTGGAG–3′; reverse: 5′–GCTAT GACCACCACCCTG–3′), murine MMP-9 (forward: 5′– GCGTGTCTGGAGATTCGACTTG–3′; reverse: 5′– GGAAACTCAC　ACGCCAGAAG–3′), and murine 18 s (forward: 5′–CGGAC AGGATTGACAGATTG–3′; reverse: 5′–CAAATCGCTCCACCAACTAA–3′). Standard curves were prepared for each gene and 18S rRNA in each experiment to normalize the relative expression of the genes of interest to 18S rRNA.

## Measurements of SDC4 and HPSE activity
The amount of SDC4 in cell culture medium was measured using the mouse SDC4 ELISA kit (LSBio, Seattle, WA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. HPSE activity in culture medium was measured using heparanase assay kit (amsbio, MA, USA. Cat.#. Ra001-BE-K). according to the manufacture’s instruction. The data was expressed as ng HS removed per minute. Assays were run at three times with triplicate measurements for each condition.

## Podocyte attachment assay
Podocyte attachment assays were carried out as described previously , . The transfected cells were cultured in high- or normal glucose medium for 48 h after transfection, then cells were subjected to cell attachment assay. Before seeding, 96-well plates were coated with indicated concentrations of laminin (2, 10 μg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), vitronectin (1, 5 μg/mL; Sigma), fibronectin (2, 10 μg/mL; Sigma) or collagen IV (2, 10 μg/mL; Corning, Corning, NY) for overnight at 4 °C. Plates were then incubated with PBS containing 0.5% BSA for 1 h to block nonspecific adhesion. The cells were trypsinized and seeded into the wells (96-well plate) at a density of 35,000 cells per well. Adhesion assays were run for 1 h. At the end of the assay, the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times to remove unattached cells, then fixed in EM-grade 4% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Science) for overnight at 4 °C. Cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma) for 1 h, solubilized in 20% acetic acid, and read out at 580 nm. Cells bound to 0.5% BSA-coated wells were used as a negative control. Podocyte attachment assays were run at three times with triplicate measurements for each substrate condition.

## Immunoblot analysis
The expression of HPSE, NFAT2, and SDC4 in cultured podocytes was assessed by Western blot analysis. Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 1% NP-40) containing protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For NFAT2 detection, nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction was carried out using NE-PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #78833) as instructed. For the detection of SDC4 in cell culture medium, the proteins were precipitated using 20% Trichloroacetic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein lysates were separated on a sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore), and the membranes were incubated with HPSE, NFAT2, and Ext- or Cyt- SDC4 antibodies for overnight at 4 °C. The immunoreaction was visualized using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The membranes were then incubated in a stripping buffer (0.2 M Glycine, PH 2.3) to remove the bound antibodies, and reblotted using anti-α tubulin or anti-HDAC1 antibodies.

## Ethics statement
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and under the approval of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Vanderbilt University. Mice were anesthetized with 1–5% isoflurane in oxygen for induction and maintained with 1–3% isoflurane. For the euthanasia method, mice were euthanized by inhalation of carbon dioxide overdose and subsequent cervical dislocation.

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as mean values ± SEM. Differences among experimental groups were determined using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism4 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). For two-group comparisons, an unpaired Student’s t test was used to calculate the P value. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
