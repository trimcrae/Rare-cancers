## Significance
The β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR), the prototypic G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR), controls critical cardiopulmonary physiology, with an exemplary role in regulating airway tone. Basal expression of the β2AR is regulated by the ubiquitin ligase pVHL, but the role played by pVHL in pulmonary function is not known. Here, we show that the bronchodilator nitric oxide (NO) targets pVHL for degradation to improve β2AR stability and function. pVHL mutant animals resistant to S-nitrosylation (NO regulation) exhibit decreased airway function. Thus, pVHL serves as an important regulatory locus controlling airway tone and a potential target for therapeutic intervention in asthma and other obstructive airway diseases.
Keywords: nitric oxide, S-nitrosothiol, redox signaling, posttranslational modification, pVHL ubiquitin ligase

## Abstract
The β2-adrenergic receptor (β2AR), a prototype G protein–coupled receptor, controls cardiopulmonary function underpinning O2 delivery. Abundance of the β2AR is canonically regulated by G protein–coupled receptor kinases and β-arrestins, but neither controls constitutive receptor levels, which are dependent on ambient O2. Basal β2AR expression is instead regulated by the prolyl hydroxylase/pVHL-E3 ubiquitin ligase system, explaining O2 responsivity. Interplay between O2 and nitric oxide (NO, a potent bronchodilator) is central to cardiopulmonary function. Here, we demonstrate that pVHL-mediated β2AR degradation is counteracted by NO, revealing pVHL control of pulmonary function. NO S-nitrosylates Cys77 in human pVHL (cognate to mouse Cys43), which induces binding of the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl to degrade pVHL, thereby increasing β2AR expression. pVHL–C43S mutant mice refractory to S-nitrosylation exhibit decreases in β2AR signaling and increases in airway tone. Thus, pVHL controls adrenergic pulmonary function and contributes to bronchodilation by NO. Our findings suggest therapeutic approaches to asthma and obstructive airway disease.

The human body responds to stress, danger, or physical activity through activation of the sympathetic nervous system, in which catecholamines such as epinephrine activate adrenergic signal transduction through α and β receptors to prime organs for immediate activity ( ). There are three subtypes of β adrenergic receptors (β1, β2, and β3), with the β1 adrenergic receptor predominant in cardiac tissue, the β3 adrenergic receptor predominant in brown adipose tissue, and the β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR), the prototypic G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR), predominant in vascular and airway smooth muscle ( , ). In response to ligand activation, the β2AR signals through heterotrimeric G protein- and β-arrestin-mediated pathways ( ). G protein–coupled receptor kinases (GRKs) then phosphorylate the β2AR, creating binding sites for β-arrestins that internalize activated, phosphorylated β2AR to dynamically regulate its cell surface expression and activity. In the absence of ligand stimulation, basal β2AR abundance is controlled instead by the E3 ubiquitin ligase pVHL, which ubiquitinates β2AR leading to receptor degradation ( ). Ultimately, β2AR abundance determines the sensitivity of the cardiopulmonary system to adrenergic signals, as well as the magnitude of acute response ( , ), with dysregulation contributing to diseases such as heart failure and asthma ( , ).
Signal transduction through the β2AR controls O2 uptake and delivery in the lungs (bronchodilation), skeletal muscle (vasodilation), and heart (increased contractility and ischemic protection) ( ). These β2AR-mediated responses are regulated by NO derived from constitutive NO synthases (eNOS or nNOS) in response to receptor activation ( ). Specifically, NO modifies myriad GPCR regulatory proteins through S-nitrosylation [including GRK2, β-arrestins, dynamin, and the β2AR itself ( – )] ( ) to regulate receptor signaling, desensitization, and recycling. Coordinate regulation of β2AR function by S-nitrosylation thus facilitates O2 delivery to tissues. It has been suggested that NO has evolved a general function in control of O2 sensing and utilization, which operates at both the integrated system and molecular levels, as exemplified by β2AR biology ( ).
The von Hippel-Lindau E3 ligase (pVHL) is a key component of the hypoxia-sensing system in cells that degrades specific proteins during normoxia but increases their stability and expression level during hypoxia ( ). pVHL accomplishes this task through a unique mechanism of targeting proteins marked by hydroxyproline for ubiquitinylation and proteasomal degradation ( ). Proline hydroxylases (PHDs) catalyze hydroxylation of specific proline residues on target proteins in normoxia to promote their pVHL-dependent ubiquitinylation and degradation. PHDs are inactive in hypoxia, resulting in increased substrate protein expression ( ). While the best characterized targets of the PHD-pVHL system are the hypoxia-inducible transcription factors (HIFs) ( ), β2AR levels are similarly responsive to O2/hypoxia through PHD/pVHL degradation ( ). Thus, β2AR expression and signaling are regulated by both NO and O2. At a molecular level, this implies crosstalk between O2-dependent hydroxylation and ubiquitinylation on the one hand, and S-nitrosylation on the other hand, although this is neither widely appreciated nor well understood. Here, we explore NO–O2 crosstalk impacting β2AR signaling through pVHL S-nitrosylation, identify a previously unreported site of S-nitrosylation, and demonstrate a physiological role of pVHL in control of airway tone.

## Results

## Endogenous S-Nitrosothiol Induces pVHL Degradation and β2AR Stabilization through Ubiquitinylation.
S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO), the main endogenous bronchodilator in the airway ( ), enhanced β2AR abundance in HEK293 cells stably expressing β2AR (W9 cells) in a dose-dependent manner ( ). GSNO Reductase (GSNOR) eliminates endogenous GSNO, and inhibition of GSNOR protects from airway hyperresponsivity in asthma, indicating physiologic relevance of GSNO ( ). The stabilizing effect of GSNO on β2AR expression was not seen for β1AR (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B) and was accompanied by decreased β2AR ubiquitinylation, suggesting an effect of GSNO on the PHD/pVHL system ( ). GSNO treatment did not affect binding of pVHL to its ubiquitin ligase complex partners Elongin C or Cullin 2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) nor was expression of the endogenous PHD protein EGLN3 affected by GSNO (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). GSNO treatment did, however, robustly reduce pVHL expression ( ; endogenous pVHL expression shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1F) and concurrently increased pVHL ubiquitinylation ( ). pVHL ubiquitinylation by GSNO inhibited β2AR ubiquitinylation and proteasomal degradation ( ).
 

## pVHL Stability Is Regulated by S-Nitrosylation.
pVHL has been reported to be S-nitrosylated at Cys162 in 786-O cells ( ) with unclear biochemical or functional consequences. Using S-nitrosothiol resin-assisted capture (SNORAC), we confirmed that GSNO treatment (30 min) led to robust S-nitrosylation of pVHL, forming SNO–pVHL ( ). However, of the two cysteine residues in pVHL (Cys77 and Cys162), Cys77 was identified (by site-directed mutagenesis) as the SNO site in our system ( ). We therefore hypothesized that whereas native pVHL (in complex with Elongin B/C, Cullin 2, and Rbx2) degrades the β2AR, when S-nitrosylated at Cys77, pVHL is prevented from doing so ( ). The experimentally determined structure of pVHL in complex with a substrate [hydroxylated HIF-1α ( )] demonstrates that Cys77 is located in the substrate recognition domain, supporting the idea that S-nitrosylation of Cys77 might interfere with substrate binding ( ).
 Next, we assessed whether GSNO-mediated degradation of pVHL was dependent on pVHL S-nitrosylation. In W9 cells transfected with EV control, WT pVHL, or mutant pVHL lacking the SNO site (pVHL–C77S), treatment with GSNO for 18 h induced degradation of WT pVHL but not pVHL–C77S ( ). S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 is thus required for pVHL degradation.

## pVHL S-Nitrosylation Is Regulated by the Denitrosylase GSNOR.
SNO–pVHL was increased ( ) in the lungs of mice lacking GSNOR, suggesting regulation of pVHL by GSNO in vivo. Furthermore, pVHL expression was significantly decreased in the lungs and hearts of GSNOR−/− mice ( ), without meaningful change in mRNA levels of pVHL or β2AR (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–D). Thus, low amounts of SNO–pVHL in wild-type (WT) mice reflect constitutive denitrosylation by GSNOR (stabilizing pVHL), whereas SNO–pVHL accumulates in GSNOR−/− mice ( ), leading to pVHL degradation. Taken together, these data are consistent with S-nitrosylation of pVHL inducing its degradation in situ ( ).

## β2AR Stability Is Mediated by S-Nitrosylation of pVHL.
We assessed whether stabilization of the β2AR by GSNO could be attributed to pVHL S-nitrosylation. In cells expressing WT pVHL vs. pVHL–C77S, mutation of the pVHL SNO site prevented β2AR stabilization by GSNO ( ). S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 was thus required for SNO-induced β2AR stabilization. One mechanism by which S-nitrosylation might inhibit pVHL would entail inhibition of β2AR binding; however, β2AR–pVHL interaction was not affected by GSNO (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E and F). Such findings support a model in which pVHL S-nitrosylation may either promote its interaction with a ubiquitin ligase or reduce interaction with a deubiquitinase, promoting degradation of pVHL thereby increasing β2AR stability ( ).

## Endogenous NO S-Nitrosylates pVHL to Stabilize β2AR.
Endogenous NO is derived primarily from the three NOS isoforms: nNOS, eNOS, and iNOS ( ). pVHL coimmunoprecipitated with each NOS isoform ( ). Domain analysis—by expressing the amino terminal residues (1 to 155; majority of β subdomain) or carboxyl-terminal residues (156 to 213; including α subdomain)—revealed that pVHL interactions with NOS were dependent on N-terminal residues 1 to 155, which includes the Cys77 SNO site ( ). Endogenous pVHL also bound to iNOS in immunoprecipitates from RAW264.7 cells treated with LPS/IFNγ to induce iNOS expression ( ), confirming physiological relevance ( ). Further, we observed that pVHL was S-nitrosylated at Cys77 by endogenously generated NO from both nNOS ( ) and iNOS ( ).
 The β2AR was stabilized by endogenously generated NO in W9 cells expressing iNOS ( ). Further, overexpression of nNOS, iNOS, or eNOS resulted in degradation of pVHL ( ), confirming findings with GSNO ( ). Cumulatively, our data suggest that endogenously generated NO, likely acting via S-nitrosothiol, leads to S-nitrosylation of pVHL, inhibiting β2AR ubiquitinylation and degradation ( ).

## Effect of S-Nitrosylation on pVHL Binding Interactions.
More than a dozen proteins are known to regulate pVHL stability, either positively ( – ) or negatively ( – ), including HSP90 ( ), UBE2S/E2-EPF ( ), WSB1 ( ), SRC ( ), and c-Cbl ( ) ( ). We therefore assessed whether S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 altered binding of pVHL to these protein partners as well as the pVHL-associated deubiquitinase VDU1/USP33. We observed that S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 decreased binding with the deubiquitinase VDU1/USP33 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B), while increasing binding with the prodegradation proteins HSP90, UBE2S, SRC, and c-Cbl (without effect on prodegradation protein WSB1) ( ).
 We next assessed the functional role of specific protein–partner interactions on SNO-driven pVHL degradation. Neither the small interfering RNA (siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSP90 ( ) or VDU1 ( ) nor treatment with the HSP90 inhibitor 17-AAG (tanespimycin) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) had any effect on SNO-mediated pVHL degradation. Further, knockdown of VDU1 (simulating decreases in SNO–pVHL binding) actually increased pVHL expression ( ), suggesting that VDU1 could not account for decreased stability of SNO–pVHL. UBE2S/Ubiquitin Carrier Protein is reported to target pVHL for ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis ( ), but we observed that knockdown of UBE2S down-regulated pVHL ( ); UBE2S was therefore unlikely to mediate SNO-induced ubiquitinylation and degradation of pVHL. Notably, knockdown of the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl successfully rescued SNO-induced pVHL degradation ( ). Further, c-Cbl demonstrated a binding preference for SNO–pVHL relative to non-nitrosylated pVHL, indicating recognition of SNO–pVHL ( ). c-Cbl thus appears responsible for mediating SNO-induced pVHL degradation and subsequent β2AR stabilization.

## SNO-Mediated β2AR Stabilization Impacts Receptor Function.
We tested whether β2AR abundance regulated by SNO–pVHL translated to changes in β2AR function ( ). In HEK293 cells [with low levels of native eNOS ( )] expressing WT pVHL, we observed a >30-fold increase in cAMP produced in cells treated with the β-agonist isoproterenol (ISO) relative to vehicle ( ). ISO-induced cAMP synthesis in the presence of WT pVHL was diminished significantly by removal of the Cys77 SNO site (pVHL–C77S; ), in accordance with reduced receptor abundance ( ). In other words, S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 increased ISO-induced cAMP production due to stabilization of β2AR.
 HEK293 cells stably overexpressing β2AR (W9 cells) behaved similarly, with ISO inducing a greater rise in cAMP in W9 cells ( ) than in WT HEK293 cells ( ). ISO-induced cAMP synthesis was again reduced in the presence of pVHL–C77S relative to WT pVHL, indicative of constitutively active pVHL insensitive to negative regulation by S-nitrosylation. The magnitude of ISO-induced cAMP production was reduced in the presence of pVHL–C77S relative to WT pVHL, whereas the sensitivity of cells to ISO (apparent EC50) was not significantly altered ( ), further suggesting that S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 specifically regulates basal β2AR abundance, not the intrinsic capability of the receptor to respond to stimulation.
We used the NOS inhibitor L-NAME to block the effect of endogenous S-nitrosylation. In W9 cells transfected with EV, WT pVHL, or pVHL–C77S, L-NAME rescued the reduction in cAMP synthesis observed in cells expressing pVHL–C77S (vs. WT pVHL) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). These findings demonstrate that the effect of endogenous NO on cAMP production (vis-à-vis reduced β2AR abundance) is mediated by S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77.
We sought to confirm that the effect of pVHL–C77S mutation was upstream of adenylyl cyclase, i.e., at the level of the β2AR, to rule out effects of dysregulated pVHL–C77S on substrates other than the β2AR. Forskolin, a natural product of the Coleus forskohlii plant, increases intracellular cAMP through direct activation of adenylyl cyclases ( ), acting downstream—and therefore independent of—β2AR activation. We assessed cAMP production in the presence of forskolin and observed that cAMP synthesis was not reduced by pVHL–C77S mutation compared to WT pVHL, signifying that the effect of the SNO site mutation in this system occurred upstream of adenylyl cyclase (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). In fact, cAMP synthesis was increased by pVHL C77S mutation, likely due to pVHL activity on other targets with distinct regulatory roles in this system.

## Airway Tone Is Regulated by SNO–pVHL.
Mutation of murine pVHL Cys43 (cognate to human pVHL Cys77) to alanine or serine abolished pVHL S-nitrosylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), indicating that Cys43 is the murine pVHL SNO site. We constructed a VHL–C43S knock-in mouse expressing SNO-site mutant pVHL via germline CRISPR-Cas9-mediated TGC → AGC editing to generate the Cys → Ser point mutation in residue 43 of exon 1 in murine Vhl (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). pVHL–C43S knock-in was validated by DNA sequencing, and heterozygous pVHL–C43S knock-in mice were interbred to generate homozygous knock-in mice and littermate WT controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C) used for assessment of airway mechanics. We found reduced β2AR abundance in cell membranes isolated from lungs of pVHLC43S/C43S vs. pVHL+/+ mice by [3H]-alprenolol binding ( ), as expected. We hypothesized that reduced pulmonary β2AR expression would have deleterious consequences for airway function ( ). In response to the bronchoconstrictor methacholine (that opposes endogenous bronchodilatory tone), mice expressing SNO site-mutant pVHL–C43S demonstrated greater airway resistance relative to mice expressing WT pVHL ( ). pVHLC43S/C43S mice also demonstrated decreased airway compliance and increased elasticity relative to pVHL+/+ ( ), in accordance with an airway more resistant to β2AR-induced bronchodilation, secondary to reduced receptor abundance.
 Our data thus suggests that S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 results in recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl, leading to ubiquitinylation and degradation of pVHL. This culminates in increased basal β2AR abundance that improves bronchodilatory signaling in the airways ( ).

## Discussion
GPCRs are the most abundant class of membrane-bound receptors and the largest family of targets for pharmaceutical therapies ( ), with the β2AR the most studied prototype. Basal abundance of the β2AR is regulated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase pVHL, which ubiquitinates β2AR that has been hydroxylated in proportion to tissue O2 level by PHD/EGLN3, thus targeting the receptor for proteasomal degradation ( ). Here, we demonstrate that pVHL itself is constitutively S-nitrosylated at a newly discovered site (Cys77 in humans, Cys43 in mice; SNO–pVHL). SNO–pVHL promotes interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl to ubiquitinate and degrade pVHL. This leads to an increase in β2AR abundance that enhances receptor signaling and airway function. Collectively, these findings expand the paradigm of ubiquitinylation–S-nitrosylation crosstalk, further our understanding of SNO-mediated regulation of GPCRs, and establish a role for pVHL in regulating airway tone.
At the system level, basal β2AR expression and thus responsiveness to sympathetic tone is regulated by both NO and O2. That is, constitutive degradation of β2AR by PHD/pVHL under normoxia is prevented by NO, thereby maintaining basal β-adrenergic bronchodilator tone [this regulation is distinct from agonist-driven receptor downregulation by GRK/arrestins ( , )]. These findings resolve a paradox: Why is β2AR expression not suppressed by PHD/pVHL in normoxia (as the PHD/pVHL system does for its most well-known target, HIF1α) and why would nature exploit this mechanism to regulate β2AR in the first place (as β2AR is needed in normoxia)? That β2AR is well-expressed in normoxia is self-evident, demonstrating that PHD/pVHL must not be fully active vs. β2AR. Because β2AR is coupled to NO production ( ), tonic β2AR activity likely promotes constitutive S-nitrosylation and degradation of pVHL in normoxia, limiting pVHL activity to preserve β2AR responsiveness in lung, heart, and vasculature. Abundant GSNO in lungs ( ) may act similarly to provide a receptor-independent pool of NO bioactivity to maintain SNO–pVHL. Under hypoxia, PHD hydroxylation of β2AR is inhibited, preventing remaining pVHL from acting upon β2AR ( ). Such sequelae of hypoxia may result in a further increase in β2AR stability, heightening responsiveness in lung and vasculature to β2AR-mediated bronchodilation and vasodilation that together maximize delivery of available O2. Thus, by integrating NO and O2 signaling to limit pVHL activity, the β2-adrenergic system appears to retain sensitivity in both normoxia and hypoxia to ensure uptake of O2.
NO and O2 thus demonstrate significant interplay at multiple levels—biochemical, cellular, and physiological—that serves to coordinate O2 uptake (in lungs) and delivery (in tissues). This is classically illustrated in hemoglobin’s vasodilatory activity ( – ) and, newly appreciated here, in the bronchodilatory activity of pVHL. In the lung, oxygenation of hemoglobin catalyzes S-nitrosylation, while in peripheral tissues, deoxygenation promotes NO signaling to increase blood flow ( ). Thus, NO–O2 crosstalk in hemoglobin (O2-dependent S-nitrosylation) can be understood in terms of vasodilatory activity regulating O2 availability ( ). Here, we report similar NO–O2 interplay in the case of pVHL, which acts to degrade the β2AR following O2-dependent hydroxylation. S-nitrosylation of pVHL augments bronchodilatory activity by preventing pVHL-mediated receptor degradation. Thus, NO–O2 crosstalk in pVHL expands the paradigm regulating O2 availability.
NO is commonly thought to act as a bronchodilator through cyclic GMP-mediated signaling. In fact, much of bronchodilation attributed to NO is mediated instead by S-nitrosothiol (i.e., GSNO), which predominates in airway lining fluid ( , ). Airway S-nitrosothiols engender bronchodilation through GPCRs (including the β2AR), acting via S-nitrosylation ( , ). Here, we show that S-nitrosylation of pVHL by GSNO stabilizes the β2AR in vitro and in vivo, enhancing bronchodilatory activity. Mutant mice lacking the pVHL SNO-site (i.e., refractory to S-nitrosylation) demonstrate reduced β2AR abundance, accompanied by increased airway resistance. S-nitrosylation of pVHL thus provides a molecular basis for NO-mediated bronchodilation and establishes a role for pVHL in regulating airway tone ( , ). pVHL S-nitrosylation may represent a therapeutic objective in obstructive airway disease.
Agonist stimulation of the β2AR is coupled to S-nitrosylation of myriad regulators of receptor signaling and trafficking, including GRK2, β-arrestins, dynamin, and even the β2AR itself ( – , , – ). Moreover, at least 65 GPCRs have been reported to be S-nitrosylated ( ), suggesting a class effect. S-nitrosylation is regulated by specific writer and eraser enzymes ( , ), which include known examples of receptor nitrosylases ( , ) and denitrosylases ( , , ). In particular, multiple studies suggest β2AR regulation by the protein denitrosylase GSNOR acting on SNO-β2AR, SNO-GRK2, and SNO-β-arrestins ( – , ). Here, we show that GSNOR also regulates adrenergic responsiveness via SNO–pVHL, consistent with benefits of GSNOR inhibition in asthma ( , ). Pharmaceutical interventions designed to elevate SNO–pVHL levels may thus represent a new class of bronchodilatory agents potentially synergistic with β2-agonist therapies by preventing β2AR downregulation. Taken together, this and published work suggest that S-nitrosylation is a bona fide regulatory process for GPCR signaling under both basal conditions and in response to agonist, with direct implications for asthma ( , , ) and heart failure ( , , , ).
While numerous reports infer crosstalk between ubiquitinylation and S-nitrosylation ( – ), this is not widely appreciated nor understood mechanistically. Our work provides among the first molecular details. Specifically, S-nitrosylation of pVHL at Cys77 induced robust changes in binding partners known to regulate pVHL stability, including c-Cbl, which preferentially binds SNO–pVHL to induce pVHL ubiquitinylation and degradation. c-Cbl can thus be categorized as a SNO-responsive E3 ubiquitin ligase. It will be interesting to determine whether S-nitrosylation of other c-Cbl targets [including PD-1 ( ), EGFR ( ), and β-catenin ( )] similarly augment ubiquitin ligase activity and to explore the basis of molecular recognition. Preferential recognition of an S-nitrosylated protein substrate by ubiquitin ligases may represent a general mechanism to regulate protein stability.
Ubiquitinylation and degradation of pVHL induced by S-nitrosylation at Cys77/Cys43 may have implications for other pVHL substrates, notably HIF-1α. The interaction between pVHL and HIF-1α is inhibited by NO, which has been attributed to S-nitrosylation of HIF-1α at Cys520 ( ). S-nitrosylation of pVHL provides a possible alternative explanation. HIF-1α is also S-nitrosylated at Cys533 leading to stabilization independent of prolyl hydroxylation/pVHL recruitment ( ) and highlighting the coordinate role of S-nitrosylation in this system. The hypoxia response is canonically regulated by O2-dependent PHD hydroxylation of HIF-1α, wherein pVHL ubiquitinates hydroxylated HIF-1α in normoxia leading to its degradation ( ). However, S-nitrosylation of pVHL represents a distinct mechanism by which HIF-1α may plausibly be stabilized, independent of hypoxia. Notwithstanding further study necessary to support such a mechanism, pVHL and HIF-1α may thus conform to a systemwide model of O2-sensing and NO-responsive signal transduction that is foundational for redox signaling ( ).

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Lines.
HEK293 (ATCC CRL-1573) and RAW264.7 (ATCC TIB-71) cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with added 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotic (#15240096; Gibco) (complete DMEM). Cells were grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2. W9 cells are HEK293 origin, stably expressing human Flag-β2AR and maintained in the presence of neomycin, courtesy of the Lefkowitz laboratory (Duke University) ( ). HEK293-nNOS and HEK293-eNOS HEK293 origin lines with stable expression of rat WT nNOS in pcDNA3 vector or human WT eNOS in pcDNA3 vector (made in-house) were maintained in the presence of neomycin; HEK293-iNOS cells were maintained in the presence of blasticidin and zeocin, and iNOS expression was induced with doxycycline. RAW264.7 cells were treated with LPS (0.1 μg/mL; #L2654; Sigma) and IFN-γ (100 U/mL; #300-02; Peprotech) overnight to induce iNOS.

## Plasmid Construction and Mutagenesis.
Human pVHL with a C-terminal HA tag was cloned into pcDNA3 mammalian expression vector by Liang Xie, as previously published ( ). pcDNA3-iNOS (human, WT), pcDNA3-eNOS (human, WT), and pcDNA3-nNOS (rat, WT) were employed in a prior study ( ). Myc-DDK-tagged mouse pVHL was obtained from Origene (ID: SR185390, MR201630, NM_009507). These vectors were transformed into One-Shot Omnima cells (#C8540-03; Invitrogen), streaked onto LB/ampicillin plates, and a single colony was selected, expanded in LB media containing ampicillin. Plasmids were purified using Plasmid Maxi Kit (#12362; Qiagen) and subsequently sequenced for validation. Human pVHL mutants (C77S, C162S, C77F, C162F, 1-155, 156-213) and mouse pVHL mutants (C43A, C43S) were generated using QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (#200523; Agilent). Empty vector (pcDNA3.1(+); Invitrogen) was purified and sequenced as above.

## Chemicals.
S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) was prepared using equimolar concentrations of L-glutathione reduced (#G4251; Sigma) in 0.5 M hydrochloric acid (Millipore Sigma) and sodium nitrite (#71759; Fluka) in ddH20, which were combined and then immediately diluted in assay buffer (100 mM NaH2PO4, 100 µM ethylene diamine tetraacetate (EDTA), 100 µM diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid, pH 7.4) to form a working solution to be added to cell culture (final = 0.2 to 1.5 mM). 17-AAG (17-N-allylamino-17-Demethoxygeldanamycin or Tanespimycin; #A8476; Sigma) was used at concentrations of 0.2 to 2 mM as an HSP90 inhibitor. Forskolin (#F6886; Millipore Sigma) was used at 100 µM as an activator of adenylyl cyclase. Isoproterenol (#I2760; Millipore Sigma) was used at 10 µM as a β2AR agonist. L-NAME (N(G)-Nitro-L-arginine methyl ester; #80210; Cayman) was used at 2 mM as a NOS inhibitor. MG-132 (#474790; Millipore Sigma) was used at 10 µM as a proteasome inhibitor. [3H]-alprenolol (PerkinElmer; NET720250UC; 100.3 Ci/mmol) was used at 5 nM as a β-adrenergic antagonist for radioligand-based analysis of β2AR expression.

## Transfection.
For pVHL transfections, HEK293 cells were plated onto dishes coated with 5 mg/cm2 poly-D-lysine (Corning) and cultured for 24 h in growth media. Cells were transfected with indicated empty vector, WT, or mutant pVHL plasmids using PolyJet transfection reagent (Signagen) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, cells were treated with vehicle or 0.2 to 1.5 mM GSNO, freshly prepared (glutathione was omitted from the vehicle controls). At the completion of the experiment, cells were harvested, pelleted, washed with cold 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Gibco), and subsequently resuspended in NP-40 lysis buffer for biochemical analysis.

## siRNA-Mediated In Vitro Gene Knockdown.
Gene knockdown was accomplished by siRNA transfection using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (#13778; Life Technologies) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, RNAiMAX and Silencer Select Validated siRNA (Life Technologies) targeting genes HSP90 (ID: s6994), VDU1 (ID: 105180), UBE2S (ID: s26182), c-Cbl (ID: s2477, s2478), or nontargeting control (#4390843) were diluted in Opti-MEM medium (#31985-070; Gibco), combined in a 1:1 ratio, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, and siRNA–lipid complex was added (final RNAi duplex concentration of 10 nM) to cultured HEK293 or W9 cells at 60% confluency in normal cell culture media. Cells were assessed for gene knockdown by Western blot 48 h posttransfection.

## SNORAC.
SNORAC ( ) was performed as described previously ( ). Mouse heart samples were mechanically homogenized in lysis buffer [100 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 100 μM neocuproine (HEN), 50 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) NP-40, 0.2% S-methylmethanethiosulfonate (MMTS), 1 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor (Roche)]. Samples were centrifuged (10,000× g, 4 °C, 20 min, ×2), 2.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 0.2% MMTS were added to the supernatant, and samples were incubated for 25 min at 55 °C. Proteins were precipitated with −20 °C acetone, dissolved in HEN/1% SDS, repeated 1×, and final pellets were resuspended in HEN/1% SDS. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method. Total lysate (1 to 4 mg) was incubated with ascorbate (50 mM, freshly prepared) and 100 μL thiopropyl-Sepharose [50% slurry; made as described previously ( )] and incubated in the dark for 3 h with rotation. Separate lysate samples were incubated with resin in the absence of ascorbate (-Asc) as a control for nonspecific free thiol background. Proteins bound to beads were washed sequentially with HEN/1% SDS and 10% HEN/0.1% SDS, eluted with 10% HEN/1% SDS/10% β-mercaptoethanol, and analyzed by Western blot involving SDS/polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and immunoblotting with primary and secondary antibodies quantified by chemiluminescence.

## Immunoprecipitation.
Immunoprecipitation was performed as described previously ( ). Briefly, HEK293 or W9 cells (transfected with EV or HA-tagged pVHL) were collected from culture, washed with 1× cold PBS (Gibco), and pellets were sonicated in nondenaturing lysis buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, protease inhibitor (Roche)], clarified, precleared with 20 μL protein G agarose, and incubated with 20 μL anti-HA agarose (#26181; Pierce) or 50 μL of anti-Flag M2 agarose (#A2220, Sigma) for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Beads were washed 5× with lysis buffer, and proteins were eluted with 2× SDS loading buffer for 5 min at 95 °C. Samples were centrifuged briefly, the supernatant was collected, and analyzed by gel electrophoresis using TrueBlot secondary antibodies [#18-8816-33 (mouse), #18-8817-33 (rabbit); Rockland].
For analysis of protein ubiquitinylation, the same protocol was performed with the following changes: The proteasome inhibitor MG-132 was added to culture (final = 10 μM) at least 4 h before collection, and anti-ubiquitin antibody (#43124; Cell Signaling) was used after immunoprecipitation to visualize ubiquitinated proteins by chemiluminescence.

## Western Blot Analysis.
Proteins were extracted from cells or tissues and subjected to 4 to 20% Criterion Precast Midi Protein Gel electrophoresis. Blotted membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies, washed with PBST (PBS+0.1% Tween-20), and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies [anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (Promega)] for 1 h, followed by chemiluminescent detection [ECL (GE Healthcare)]. Primary antibodies used included mouse anti-Flag (β2AR or β1AR; #F1804, Sigma), rabbit anti-HA (pVHL; #CST3724; Cell Signaling), mouse anti-β-actin (#A1987; Sigma), mouse anti-ubiquitin (#sc-8017; Santa Cruz) mouse anti-nNOS (#610309; BD), mouse anti-iNOS (#610329; BD), mouse anti-eNOS (#610297; BD), rabbit anti-VDU1/USP33 (#A300-925A; Bethyl Laboratories Inc.), mouse anti-HSP90 (#610419; BD), rabbit anti-UBE2S (#9630; Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-Src (#2109; Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-c-Cbl (#2747; Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-WSB1 (#11666-1-AP; Proteintech), mouse anti-α-tubulin (#T6199; Sigma), rabbit anti-GAPDH (#10494-1-AP; Proteintech), rabbit anti-EGLN3/PHD3 (#NB100-303; Novus), mouse anti-Elongin C (#3610760; BD), and rabbit anti-Cullin2 (#sc-10781; Santa Cruz). Quantification of Western blots was performed with ImageJ (NIH).

## cAMP Measurement.
cAMP was measured using the GloSensor cAMP assay (#E2301; Promega) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HEK293 cells cultured in 10 cm2 dishes were transfected with the luciferase-based pGloSensor-22F cAMP plasmid in addition to empty vector, WT pVHL or pVHL–C77S using PolyJet as previously described. After 24 h incubation, cells were split to 96-well plate, incubated for 8 h, then treated with 1.5 mM GSNO or vehicle (as previously described) in fresh cell culture media for 18 h. Culture media was then exchanged to equilibration medium containing GloSensor cAMP Reagent for 1 h. The plate was transferred to a GloMax Discover luminometer (Promega) set at 37 °C and a preread measurement was taken. 10× isoproterenol (or vehicle) was injected to a final concentration of 10 µM, and cAMP measured by luminescence in 1 min cycles for 30 min (integration: 0.5 s). Results are quantified in relative light units and expressed relative to baseline.

## β2AR Expression Analysis by Radioligand ([3H]-Alprenolol) Binding.
β2AR expression was measured by [3H]-alprenolol binding as described previously ( ). Briefly, mouse lung tissue was mechanically homogenized in lysis buffer without detergent [10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4 with addition of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)] using a Bead Beater homogenizer. Lysate was centrifuged at 500× g for 2 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 20,000× g for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet containing the cell membranes was resuspended in 1 mL lysis buffer using an insulin syringe and centrifuged at 20,000× g for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 0.1 mL lysis buffer, protein concentration assessed using the BCA method, and membranes aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C prior to analysis. β2AR expression was assessed by competition binding assay, in which 3 to 5 μg membrane was incubated with 5 nM [3H]-alprenolol and 1.0 μM propranolol (P0884; Millipore Sigma) or vehicle in assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4). Nonspecific binding was quantified as bound [3H]-alprenolol in the presence of the β-antagonist propranolol. Reactions were incubated for 10 min in a 37 °C water bath with agitation. Binding was terminated by GF-C filtration (#1822-025; Whatman) under vacuum. Filters were washed with ice-cold assay buffer (2.0 mL twice), and bound [3H]-alprenolol was assessed by liquid scintillation counting.

## β2AR Cell Surface Labeling Assay.
Cell surface β2AR was assessed using a cell surface labeling assay, in which W9 cells in 10 cm2 plates were serum-starved for 3 h, washed twice with ice-cold Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (#14025-076; Gibco), 10 mL of 0.25 mg/mL Sulfo-NHS-SS-biotin in ice-cold HBSS (pH 7.4) was added, and samples were incubated 30 min on ice with agitation. Labeling buffer was discarded, cells were washed 3× with 10 mL of 100 mM glycine/HBSS, then harvested with 1.5 mL 100 mM glycine/HBSS and centrifuged at 300× g for 2 min at 4 °C. Cells were lysed in 500 μL of lysis buffer (1% NP-40, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol) containing protease inhibitor (Roche), centrifuged at 20,000× g for 12 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected, protein quantified by BCA assay, and 30 μL of streptavidin agarose (#20357; Pierce) was added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. Agarose was washed 3× with lysis buffer, sample eluted with 2× SDS sample buffer (5% 2-mercaptoethanol for 10 min at 42 °C), subjected to gel electrophoresis, antibody staining, and chemiluminescent detection.

## Realtime qPCR for pVHL and β2AR mRNA Expression.
Total RNA was extracted from mouse heart and lung tissue by homogenization in TRIzol reagent (#15596026, Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were synthesized with High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4368814, Applied Biosciences). The relative abundance of pVHL and β2AR mRNAs was quantified with the TaqMan Gene Expression Assay. β-actin expression was used for normalization.

## Mice.
C57BL/6 mice were used as WT, and congenic GSNOR−/− mice were made as described previously ( ), having been backcrossed 10 consecutive times to C57BL/6 mice. Heterozygous pVHL–C43S knock-in mice were made by Ingenious Targeting Laboratory. Targeted iTL BF1 (C57BL/6 FLP) embryonic stem cells were microinjected into Balb/c blastocysts; resulting chimeras were mated to C57BL/6 N WT mice to generate Germline Neo Deleted mice. These mice were screened for deletion of the Neo cassette and subsequently for the presence of FLP transgene by PCR. PCR was performed to confirm retention of the introduced point mutation (TGC → AGC), and heterozygous mice confirmed for Germline Neo Deletion were bred together to generate homozygous pVHL–C43S knock-in offspring used for experiments, with homozygous pVHL+/+ animals as litter-mate controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Adult mice were fed with standard chow and housed in a pathogen-free facility before use in experiments. Only male mice were utilized in this study.

## Airway Mechanics In Vivo.
Airway responsiveness to inhaled methacholine (cholinergic bronchoconstriction agent) was measured as respiratory system resistance (Rrs), respiratory system compliance (Crs), and respiratory system elasticity (Ers) using a commercially available small animal ventilator (flexiVent-FX2, SCIREQ Inc., Montreal, Canada), as previously described ( , ). Briefly, mice were anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine cocktail (Phizer, St. Joseph, MO; Lloyd Laboratories, Shenandoah, IA) delivered I.P. and laid supine on a heated surgical bed. Mice were then tracheostomized via a midline incision using an 18 gauge blunt-tip cannula. Mice were paralyzed using pancuronium bromide (Sigma-Aldrich) delivered I.P. to block spontaneous breathing, then ventilated using default settings of 10 mL/kg tidal volume, a positive end expiratory pressure of 3.0 cm H2O, a respiratory rate of 150 breaths/min, and FiO2 of 50%. Following two recruitment breaths (deep inspiration up to 30 cm H2O for 3 s), increasing doses of methacholine (0 to 200 mg/mL dissolved in saline; Sigma-Aldrich) were aerosolized over 10 s using an ultrasonic nebulizer (Aeroneb, SCIREQ) diverted into the ventilator’s inspiratory flow. Serial Rrs, Crs, and Ers measurements were calculated using a 2.5 Hz single-frequency forced oscillation maneuver (Snapshot 150) using flexiWare 5.1 software (v7.2, SCIREQ). Results were reported as the average of six sequential measurements per mouse after an inhaled methacholine dose. Mice were continuously monitored by electrocardiogram, such that mice that could not complete the full bronchoconstrictor dose–response due to bradycardia (<60 bpm), asystole, or sustained cannula blockage (due to excessive respiratory secretions) were excluded from the analysis.

## Statistical Analysis and Bioinformatics.
All studies were done in at least triplicate, unless otherwise noted. Studies using mice utilized at least three mice per condition/treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD, except where noted. Student’s t test and ANOVA were used to test for strain and treatment effects as appropriate and are indicated within each figure legend. Analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v9). P values < 0.05 were reported to indicate a statistically significant difference between groups of interest.
