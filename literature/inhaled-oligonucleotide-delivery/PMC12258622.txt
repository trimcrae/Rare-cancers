## Abstract

## Objective
Dravet syndrome (DS) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the SCN1A gene, which encodes the voltage‐gated sodium channel Nav1.1 α subunit. Experiments in animal models of DS—including the haploinsufficient Scn1a
+/− mouse—have identified impaired excitability of interneurons in the hippocampus and neocortex; this is thought to underlie the treatment‐resistant epilepsy that is a prominent feature of the DS phenotype. However, additional brain structures, such as the medial septum (MS), also express SCN1A. The MS is known to play an important role in cognitive function and thus may contribute to the intellectual impairment that also characterizes DS.

## Methods
In this study, we employed whole cell patch clamp recordings in acute brain slices to characterize the electrophysiological properties of MS neurons in Scn1a
+/− mice versus age‐matched wild‐type littermate controls.

## Results
We found no discernible genotype‐related differences in MS cholinergic neurons, but significant dysfunction within MS parvalbumin‐expressing (PV) inhibitory neurons in Scn1a
+/− mice. We further identified heterogeneity of firing patterns among MS PV neurons, and additional genotype differences in the proportion of subtype representation.

## Significance
These results confirm that the MS is an additional locus of pathology in DS and may contribute to comorbidities such as cognitive impairment.

Keywords: acetylcholine, epilepsy, inhibition

## Key points
Medial septum cholinergic neurons have preserved excitability in Scn1a
+/− mice.
Medial septum parvalbumin‐expressing inhibitory neurons can be classified into several subtypes with distinct firing patterns.
All subtypes of medial septum parvalbumin‐expressing inhibitory neurons are impaired in Scn1a
+/− mice.
The proportionate representation of medial septum parvalbumin‐expressing inhibitory neuronal subtypes differs between Scn1a
+/− and wild‐type mice.

1.

## INTRODUCTION
Dravet syndrome (DS) is an infantile onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy characterized by treatment‐resistant epilepsy, temperature‐sensitive seizures, developmental delay, intellectual disability, features of autism spectrum disorder, and an increased risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy. Most patients with DS harbor pathogenic loss‐of‐function variants in the SCN1A gene, leading to effective haploinsufficiency of the voltage‐gated sodium channel Nav1.1 α subunit. 

The hemizygous Scn1a
+/− mouse is a well‐established preclinical model that accurately replicates key phenotypic features of the human condition. When maintained on a 50:50 hybrid 129SvJ:C57BL/6 genetic background, these mice exhibit spontaneous seizures and sporadic deaths beginning prior to postnatal day (P)21. 
Scn1a

+/−
 mice exhibit a range of behavioral and cognitive abnormalities, including hyperactivity, social interaction deficits, and impaired spatial memory. , Studies using the haploinsufficient model (Scn1a

+/−
) and other DS models have provided crucial insights into cellular pathology of the disorder. Notably, all major classes of γ‐aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) neurons have been found to exhibit impaired action potential (AP) generation in mouse models of DS. , , , 

Most preclinical data characterizing DS pathology have focused on seizure‐prone regions, such as the hippocampus and neocortex. However, in addition to seizures, patients with DS also have prominent cognitive, motor, and sleep dysfunction. , Even as seizure burden tends to decrease over time, these other symptoms persist and significantly impact quality of life. As the expression of SCN1A is brain‐wide, there is therefore a need to better understand the pathology of DS in other brain regions that may contribute to these diverse symptoms.
Hippocampal theta oscillations provide temporal organization for neuronal assemblies, with relevance for learning and memory. , Theta oscillations are controlled by the medial septum (MS), a cholinergic nucleus in the basal forebrain that sends dense projections to the hippocampus. , A significant majority of cholinergic neurons and nearly all GABAergic neurons in the MS are reported to express Nav1.1. Furthermore, abnormalities of the hippocampal theta rhythm have been identified in animal models of DS, , , which may contribute to cognitive impairment.
We hypothesized that MS neurons have impaired excitability in DS. To test this, we employed acute slice whole cell patch clamp recordings to compare the electrophysiological characteristics of cholinergic (ChAT) and inhibitory, parvalbumin‐expressing (PV) neurons in Scn1a
+/− mice versus age‐matched wild‐type (WT) littermate controls. Our findings revealed no discernible genotype‐related differences in MS ChAT neurons; however, we observed a pronounced hypoexcitability across all subtypes of PV neurons in Scn1a
+/− mice. These findings support the concept that the MS is a locus of pathology in DS and may contribute to intellectual disability and other comorbidities.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
The studies were conducted and reported in accordance with ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines 2.0.

2.2.

## Acute slice preparation
Mice (P21–P30) were deeply anesthetized using inhaled isoflurane, confirmed by a toe pinch test. Subsequently, their brains were swiftly removed and immersed in an ice‐cold sucrose solution composed of the following concentrations (in mmol·L−1): NaCl 87, sucrose 75, KCl 2.5, CaCl2 1.0, MgSO4 2.0, NaHCO3 26, NaH2PO4 1.25, and glucose 10, supplemented with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Coronal brain slices of 300 μm were prepared utilizing a VT‐1200S vibratome (Leica Microsystems). These slices were transferred to a holding chamber filled with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing the following concentrations (in mmol·L−1): NaCl 125, KCl 2.5, CaCl2 2.0, MgSO4 1.0, NaHCO3 26, NaH2PO4 1.25, and glucose 10. The ACSF was oxygenated by continuous bubbling with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Following transfer, the slices were allowed to recover at 32°C for 30 min, followed by an additional 30 min at room temperature before initiating recording procedures.

2.3.

## Slice electrophysiology
Whole‐cell patch clamp recordings were conducted using a SliceScope Pro 6000 electrophysiology system (Scientifica). Slices were transferred to a recording chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF at a rate of approximately 1 mL/min. Slice physiology experiments were conducted at 31°C, with the exception of Hm1a wash‐on experiments (see below). For whole‐cell recordings, borosilicate glass electrodes were employed, pulled to achieve a tip resistance of 3–4 MΩ using a P‐97 puller (Sutter Instruments). These electrodes were filled with a K‐gluconate internal solution (K‐gluconate 130, KCl 6.3, ethyleneglycoltetraacetic acid .5, MgCl2 1.0, hydroxyethylpiperazine ethane sulfonic acid 10, Mg–adenosine triphosphate 4.0, and Na–guanosine triphosphate .3, in mmol·L−1). The pH of the internal solution was adjusted to 7.30 using KOH, and the osmolarity was set to 285 mOsm with 30% sucrose. Electrode positioning was facilitated using a PatchStar manipulator (Scientifica).
Current was injected as needed to maintain cells at −60 mV during the current step protocol. Throughout current clamp experiments, series resistance compensation (bridge balance) was consistently applied, with periodic readjustments as deemed necessary. No liquid junction potential correction was performed. Data were included for final analysis only from cells with a resting potential below −50 mV for ChAT cells and below −60 mV for PV cells.
Hm1a (Alomone Labs STH‐601) was used at a 250 nmol·L−1 final concentration in ACSF. Hm1a was perfused at a rate of 1 mL/min after a baseline recording was obtained. To improve the stability of our recordings across longer time periods, we performed these experiments at room temperature and with a more limited range of current steps.

2.4.

## Analysis of whole‐cell electrophysiology data
The analysis was conducted blind to genotype using custom Python software (https://github.com/mattis‐laboratory/Slice‐Physiology‐Automated‐Analysis) and the pyABF package (available at https://pypi.org/project/pyabf). An AP was defined as crossing 0 mV and having a threshold characterized by a derivative of the voltage (dV/dt) greater than 10 mV/ms. Single AP properties were derived from the initial AP elicited during the rheobase sweep, with rheobase defined as the minimal current injection necessary to evoke an AP using 1‐s sweeps with 20‐pA intervals. The current/frequency plot was generated based on the average steady‐state firing frequency computed at each current step. Similarly, the fold‐rheobase plot was derived from the same dataset, normalizing the current to each cell's rheobase and considering only integer values of fold‐rheobase. The maximal steady‐state firing frequency was computed as the highest average firing frequency across all 1‐s sweeps. Additionally, the maximal instantaneous firing frequency was calculated as the inverse of the shortest interspike interval observed across all sweeps. AP duration 50 (APD50) and 90 (APD90) represented the durations from threshold to the points at which the AP achieved 50% or 90% of repolarization, respectively.
The interspike interval (ISI) covariation was calculated as the SD divided by the mean of all ISIs in the maximum frequency sweep. To determine the length of the first burst in each maximum frequency sweep, we first determined the mean and SD of all ISIs. We defined a burst as having terminated when the gap between APs was greater than the mean + 3 SD. The burst length was then the time difference from the first and last AP within the burst. 

All other parameters were computed according to previously established methodologies. 

2.5.

## Immunohistochemistry
Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Brains were removed and postfixed at 4°C overnight in 4% PFA, then equilibrated overnight at 4°C in 30% sucrose in PBS. Tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 40 μm using a frozen microtome (Leica Biosystems), and sections were placed in cryoprotectant (25% glycerol, 30% ethylene glycol in PBS, pH adjusted to 6.7 with HCl) for long‐term storage at −20°C. Sections were washed in PBS prior to immunostaining to remove residual cryoprotectant.
For both ChAT and PV immunostaining, sections were blocked and permeabilized in PBS containing .3% Triton X‐100 and either 3% normal donkey serum or 10% normal goat serum as appropriate for the choice of secondary antibody. Sections were incubated for 24 or 48 h at room temperature with guinea pig anti‐PV (Synaptic Systems, cat. no. 195308) or rabbit anti‐ChAT (Invitrogen, cat. no. PIPA529653), respectively. Primary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in the appropriate blocking solution. Following primary antibody incubation, sections were washed with PBS. Fresh 1:500 dilutions of secondary antibodies in the appropriate blocking solution were prepared prior to incubation. Sections were incubated for 2 h in goat anti‐guinea pig Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A‐11073) or Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. A‐11076) or for 3 h in donkey antirabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 711‐545‐152) for PV or ChAT immunostaining, respectively. All sections were then washed in PBS, incubated in 1:50 000 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. D1306) in PBS at room temperature for 15 min, and washed a final time in PBS. Sections were mounted and cover‐slipped with PVA‐DABCO mounting medium (MilliporeSigma, cat. no. 10981). Images were acquired using a Nikon fluorescent microscope.
To label PNNs, free‐floating sections were washed briefly in PBS. Sections were then incubated for 24 h at room temperature with biotinylated Wisteria floribunda lectin (Vector Laboratories, cat. no. B‐1355‐2) diluted 1:1000 in PBS containing .3% Triton X‐100 (PBST). Sections were then washed again with PBS and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with streptavidin conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. S11223) diluted 1:1000 in PBST. Sections were washed a final time with PBS, mounted on slides, and cover‐slipped with PVA‐DABCO.

2.6.

## Image analysis
Intensity of fluorescence signal of labeled PNNs in MS was quantified using Nikon NIS‐Elements software. Images of MS‐containing sections stained for PNNs were acquired, and a region of interest (ROI) was manually drawn around the MS. For each section, a second ROI was also drawn in an adjacent region containing no apparent fluorescent labeling to obtain a background signal for normalization. The Nikon software was used to measure the mean fluorescence intensity for each ROI; normalized mean fluorescence was then calculated by dividing the mean intensity of each MS‐containing ROI by that of the background ROI within the same section. These data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

2.7.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis of electrophysiology data was performed using both Python and Prism GraphPad, as follows. For analysis of electrophysiologic properties for which there was one single value per cell (e.g., rheobase, AP peak) the mean and SE were calculated for each genotype. For simple two‐way comparisons between genotypes (Tables  and ) we performed unpaired t‐tests for each parameter. Each parameter was labeled as one of three measurement types (“intrinsic properties,” “repetitive AP properties,”, and “individual AP properties”), and we performed Benjamini–Hochberg corrections to control the false‐discovery rate within each measurement type (using Python). For analysis of baseline versus post‐Hm1a wash‐on steady‐state firing frequency (Figure  ), we performed a paired t‐test (using Python). For multiway comparisons across MS PV cell subtypes (Figure  ,) we performed ordinary one‐way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) followed by Tukey multiple comparisons tests (using Prism GraphPad). For multiway comparisons across both genotype and cell subtype (Table  ), we performed a two‐way ANOVA for each parameter (using Prism GraphPad) and reported p‐values for source of variation from both genotype and cell subtype. For analysis of the distribution of different MS PV cell subtypes within each genotype (Figure  ), we performed a chi‐squared test (using Prism GraphPad). For analysis of current versus frequency, AP number versus AP peak, and fold‐rheobase versus frequency (Figures  , , , , ), we performed two‐way ANOVAs (using Python) and reported the p‐values for source of variation from genotype.
 Statistical analysis of PNN immunofluorescence intensity (Figure  ) was performed using a nested t‐test (in Prism GraphPad), to account for both technical replicates (multiple slices per animal) and experimental replicants (multiple animals) per genotype.

## RESULTS
As Nav1.1 is reportedly expressed in approximately 90% of cholinergic neurons in the MS, we began by evaluating the excitability of choline acetyltransferase‐expressing (ChAT) MS neurons from Scn1a
+/− mice compared to age‐matched, WT littermate controls. To enable targeted patching of ChAT neurons in slice, we crossed Chat‐Cre and tdT reporter mice (Chat‐Cre.TdT), which we bred to double‐homozygosity on a C57BL/6 background. We then further crossed these mice with Scn1a
+/− mice, which were maintained on a 129S6.SvEvTac background. All experimental F1 mice (Scn1a

+/−
.Chat‐Cre.TdT and Scn1a

+/+
.Chat‐Cre.TdT) were therefore on a 50:50 129S6:C57BL/6J genetic background, as is standard in the field (Figure  ). We next confirmed that tdT is selectively expressed in MS ChAT neurons by immunostaining for ChAT and quantifying its colocalization with tdT. We found a substantial overlap of tdT expression and ChAT immunopositivity; 99% of tdT‐positive neurons stained for ChAT, and 73% of ChAT neurons were well labeled by tdT (Figure  ).
We prepared acute brain slices from these triple‐transgenic mice and performed targeted whole‐cell patch clamp recordings from tdT+ (i.e., ChAT) neurons in the MS. We performed this and all subsequent patching at P21–P30, by which time Nav1.1 channel protein expression is well established (in mouse cerebral cortex ) and mice exhibit spontaneous seizures. We found that MS ChAT neurons were slow‐firing, consistent with previous reports (Figure  ). , , We identified no significant differences between the genotypes, as shown by similar input–output curves (Figure  ) and quantification across multiple measurements of electrophysiological properties (Figure  ; Table  ).
We hypothesized that deficits may be evident in noncholinergic MS cell types, so we next compared the properties of tdT‐negative (i.e., ChAT‐negative) MS neurons in Scn1a

+/−
.Chat‐Cre.TdT versus control mice. We found that MS ChAT‐negative neurons in Scn1a
+/− mice were hypoexcitable, with reduced firing frequency (Figure  ). However, MS ChAT‐negative neurons are known to be a heterogeneous mix of glutamatergic neurons and at least two types of GABAergic neurons—PV and somatostatin‐expressing (SST) cells—which have distinct firing properties. We therefore opted to selectively record from MS PV neurons, because nearly all GABAergic MS neurons reportedly express Nav1.1, and because Scn1a
+/− PV neurons show pronounced deficits in hippocampus and neocortex. , , 

To selectively assay MS PV neurons, we employed the above‐described breeding strategy to generate triple‐transgenic mice in which tdT expression was under Cre‐dependent control and expressed in PV neurons (Figure  ). Quantification of tdT expression and PV immunostaining revealed 86% of tdT‐expressing neurons were PV‐positive, and 77% of PV neurons were successfully labeled by tdT (Figure  ). We prepared acute brain slices at P21–P30 and performed whole‐cell current clamp recordings from 101 fluorescently labeled MS PV neurons across Scn1a
+/− mice and age‐matched WT littermate controls. We found that, in aggregate, MS PV neurons from Scn1a
+/− mice exhibited multiple abnormalities relative to age‐matched WT littermate controls (Table  ), including a marked decrease in repetitive AP firing (Figure  ) and significantly reduced AP peak (Figure  ).
As illustrated in Figure  , we observed that MS PV neurons in both WT and Scn1a
+/− mice exhibited several distinct firing patterns in response to depolarizing step currents. To determine whether the observed genotype difference was present across all subtypes of MS PV neurons, we reanalyzed our aggregate PV neuron dataset (Figure  ), by first parsing the neurons into subtypes and then comparing across genotypes within each subtype.
We used two parameters to separate these subtypes—burst length (i.e., the length of the maximal burst of continuous firing) and the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the ISI (as in Goff and Goldberg )—both of which were calculated from the current step trace in which a cell fired the maximal number of APs (Figure  ). We defined “fast‐firing” neurons as those with sustained firing throughout the pulse train (burst length > 800 ms for a 1000‐ms current step); these all had a low ISI CoV. We defined “burst‐firing” neurons as those with a shorter burst length (<800 ms) and low ISI CoV (<1); these invariably fired their burst at the onset of the pulse. Finally, we defined “cluster‐firing” neurons—which fired in clusters of spikes throughout the pulse—as having a high ISI CoV (>1).
We compared these subtypes of MS PV neurons across a wide range of additional parameters (Table  ). As a direct reflection of our clustering strategy, there were highly significant differences across subtypes in burst length (as well as maximal steady‐state firing frequency) and ISI CoV. We additionally found significant subtype variability in rheobase, with the fast‐firing group having the lowest (Figure  ), suggesting these were more readily excitable. However, all subtypes displayed characteristic properties of PV cells, with high maximal instantaneous firing rate (Figure  ) and narrow spikes (Figure  ).
We evaluated whether the impairment observed in the combined pool of Scn1a
+/− MS PV cells (Figure  ) was seen across each of these different subtypes. We noted reduced firing of Scn1a
+/− MS fast‐firing, burst‐firing, and cluster‐firing PV cells (Figure  ). Quantitatively, all Scn1a
+/− MS PV cells subtypes had significantly impaired repetitive firing that emerged in response to larger depolarizing current injections (Figure  ). We again found significantly reduced AP peak (Table  ) and pronounced spike run‐down (Figure  ) in Scn1a
+/− cells relative to WT controls. There were no significant genotype differences in intrinsic neuron properties, including resting potential, input resistance, membrane time constant, and rheobase (Table  ).
We next asked whether there was a genotype difference not only in the properties of neurons within each MS PV subtype, but also in the relative proportion of the subtypes (Figure  ). The burst‐firing subtype constituted only ~18% of MS PV cells in WT mice but was more than double that (~42%) in Scn1a
+/− mice. Conversely, fast‐firing PV cells were the large majority (~72%) encountered in WT mice but less than half (~48%) in Scn1a
+/− mice. Cluster‐firing cells constituted ~10% of the population in both genotypes.
To explore why MS PV cells were hypoexcitable in Scn1a

+/−
 mice, we examined whether there were abnormalities in MS PNNs, which form a lattice of extracellular matrix components around neurons, particularly PV neurons, including in the MS, and may be disrupted in epilepsy. However, we detected no genotype difference in the intensity of fluorescence signal of labeled PNNs within the MS (Figure  ).
Finally, we tested whether the hypoexcitability of MS fast‐firing PV neurons in Scn1a

+/−
 mice was due to insufficient Nav1.1 expression. We examined the impact of Hm1a, a spider venom toxin that selectively potentiates Nav1.1 by preventing its inactivation. (Note that this patching was performed at room temperature to increase stability across long recordings, but for consistency the same criteria—i.e., burst length and ISI CoV—were applied to classify the PV cells.) We found that bath application of Hm1a significantly increased the firing of Scn1a

+/−
 fast‐firing MS PV neurons (Figure  ). Hm1a also increased the firing of WT fast‐firing MS PV neurons (Figure  ). However, the marginal gain was greater in the Scn1a

+/−
 condition, especially in response to large current injections; subtracting the baseline from the Hm1a curves revealed that Hm1a increased the firing frequency by 50–97 Hz in response to 400–600‐pA current injections in Scn1a

+/−
 PV neurons, versus an increase of only 12–56 Hz across the same range in WT PV neurons. A statistical comparison of these subtracted curves across the entire frequency range (20–600 pA) revealed a highly significant genotype effect (p < .0001, two‐way ANOVA).

## DISCUSSION
4.1.

## 
MS ChAT neurons are functionally preserved in Scn1a

+/−
 mice
Although approximately 90% of MS ChAT neurons were reported to express Nav1.1, we identified no differences in this neuronal population—including no changes in cell intrinsic properties, measurements of repetitive firing, or features of individual APs—in Scn1a
+/− mice relative to WT littermate controls (Figure  ).
There are multiple potential explanations for this negative result. First, Nav1.1 may not be prominently expressed within MS ChAT neurons in our study; Nav1.1 and MS ChAT antibody staining were previously observed to colocalize, but that was in P60–P180 rats, whereas our study focused upon P21–P30 mice. Nav1.1 protein is prominently expressed by P21 within the cerebral cortex of mouse brain, but the developmental trajectory may differ in this subcortical neuromodulatory neuron population. Second, compensatory upregulation of other sodium channel subtypes (as in Yu et al. ) may have counteracted any functional reduction in sodium current in the Scn1a
+/− MS ChAT cells. Finally, MS ChAT cells, which have inherently slow‐firing characteristics, may be less sensitive to changes in Nav1.1 expression levels, such that normal AP generation can be maintained in these cells even with a reduction in sodium current.
Future studies could confirm Nav1.1 expression within the mouse MS ChAT neurons across different developmental timepoints, for instance using the recently generated Scn1a‐GFP transgenic mouse line. It may also be the case that Nav1.1 has greater relevance for axonal propagation of APs than for AP generation in MS ChAT cells (as in Kaneko et al. ); this could be explored by quantifying downstream cholinergic transmission within hippocampus. However, it is notable that hippocampal theta power was preserved in Scn1a
+/− mice ; as hippocampal theta power is mainly established by septal cholinergic inputs to hippocampus, this suggests that MS ChAT neurons are likely to be functionally normal in this DS model. However, as there are multiple DS models, future studies could also explore the impact of background strain and type of mutation (missense vs. nonsense) on the functionality of MS ChAT neurons.

4.2.

## Diversity of firing patterns identified within MS PV neurons
Multiple prior studies have identified subtypes of MS neurons based upon their firing patterns. , , Our results further identify the diversity of firing patterns that can be seen even within the PV neuronal population.
Fast‐firing neurons have been previously demonstrated to be PV‐positive. , , This is consistent with our data, especially noting that most WT MS PV neurons are in this category.
Burst‐firing neurons, which fire only at the onset of a depolarizing pulse, have also been previously described in the MS. , , Although we did not test the voltage dependence of the burst response—which has been shown to be more pronounced when cells are hyperpolarized , , —our data demonstrate that at least a subset of burst‐firing MS cells are PV‐positive. A recent characterization of PV versus SST neurons in MS did not identify this subtype—perhaps because it is less prominent in WT mice (Figure  )—but this burst‐firing pattern has been identified for PV neurons within other brain regions. 

Finally, cluster‐firing cells have also been previously described in the MS, , , and in one study several of these were found to be glutamatergic. Our results suggest that MS PV cells also exhibit cluster firing properties, and these cluster‐firing cells had high maximal instantaneous firing frequency and narrow spike width (Table  ) characteristic of PV cells. However, we cannot exclude the alternative possibility that these cells were among those that were tdT‐positive but PV‐negative (Figure  ). This could be clarified by biocytin‐filling and poststaining these neurons, although the yield would be low as they constitute only ~10% of those encountered with tdT‐guided patching.

4.3.

## 
MS PV neurons are impaired in Scn1a

+/−
 mice
We found that MS PV cells were in aggregate highly impaired in Scn1a
+/− mice (Figure  ; Table  ) and that this overall pattern was seen across subtypes of PV cells (Figure  ; Table  ). This is consistent with PV neuron impairment identified in other brain regions, including hippocampus and neocortex, in DS models. , , The Hm1a‐mediated rescue of Scn1a

+/−
 MS fast‐firing PV cells (Figure  ) suggests that their impaired firing is due to reduced Nav1.1 expression. Although Hm1a had a more dramatic impact in Scn1a

+/−
 MS fast‐firing PV cells, we did also observe increased excitability of WT MS fast‐firing PV cells, in contrast to previous data from hippocampal interneurons. Future studies will explore whether levels of Nav1.1 expression and impacts of Hm1a differ across subtypes of MS PV cells (i.e., fast vs. burst vs. cluster), which may help elucidate why their firing patterns are disparate at baseline.
In addition to hypoexcitability within MS PV neuron subtypes in Scn1a
+/− mice, we also observed a genotype difference in the proportion of different PV subtypes present in the MS (Figure  ). The most straightforward explanation is differences in ion channel expression converting Scn1a
+/− PV cells from a fast‐firing to burst‐firing pattern. Additionally, neuromodulation can dynamically impact the firing pattern within individual inhibitory cells. , Regardless of underlying mechanism, our results highlight that neuronal dysfunction in Scn1a
+/− mice may manifest not only as impaired firing within a given subtype of neurons but also as a shift in representation of different subtypes across the population.

4.4.

## Implications for septohippocampal circuitry and associated behavior
What might be the circuit and behavioral implications of MS PV cell dysfunction in DS? Septal inhibitory neurons synapse selectively onto inhibitory neurons in hippocampus, thereby disinhibiting hippocampal principal cells and pacing hippocampal theta oscillations. Our data suggest that this inhibitory neurotransmission is likely to be reduced in Scn1a
+/− mice. This could be assayed by activating septal efferents within hippocampal slices (although this would directly depolarize septal terminals, bypassing AP initiation within the MS). Ultimately, in vivo experiments will be needed to test the impact of MS dysfunction in hippocampal activity, parsing the relative contributions of septal and hippocampal circuit elements to impairment of theta oscillations.
Reduced hippocampal theta frequency was observed in a haploinsufficient mouse model of DS, which may contribute to the cognitive deficits seen in related mice. , WT rats with focal siRNA‐induced knockdown of Nav1.1 exclusively within the MS also had reduced theta frequency, , directly implicating the MS in this finding. These rats also exhibited impaired working memory, as assayed by Morris water maze and a rewarded alternation T‐maze task. Future studies to selectively reduce—or rescue—Nav1.1 within MS PV cells could be used to elucidate their specific contribution to hippocampal‐dependent cognitive tasks in Scn1a
+/− mice.
Therapeutic interventions to restore Nav1.1 expression levels have demonstrated tremendous preclinical promise in DS. , , , , Our results underscore the “whole‐brain” nature of DS; optimizing these treatments to achieve a brain‐wide rescue will likely maximize their therapeutic effect across both seizure and nonseizure symptoms of DS.
