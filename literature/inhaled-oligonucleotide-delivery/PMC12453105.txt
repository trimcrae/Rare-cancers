In patients with T2DM, chronic hyperglycemia and inflammation can disrupt immune responses, affecting collateral circulation development. However, the specific changes in immune cell activity and gene expression patterns in patients with T2DM with coronary chronic total occlusion (CTO), and how these changes impair collateral formation, remain unclear.
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology provides a systematic approach to studying immune cell expression patterns, secreted factors, and interactions, which aids in understanding the role of immune cells in collateral formation under diabetic conditions. Therefore, we utilized scRNA-seq to analyze peripheral blood samples from patients with T2DM and CTO to identify immune cell subsets and genes associated with impaired collateralization. The analysis revealed that mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are a critical immune cell subset closely linked to poor collateralization. Furthermore, we conducted in vitro experiments, hindlimb and myocardial ischemia mice models, and flow cytometry to investigate the functional changes in MAIT cells. Our findings demonstrated that MAIT cells impair collateral formation through the CCL3L1 (C-C motif chemokine ligand 3-like 1)-CCR5 (C-C chemokine receptor type 5) signaling axis by inducing macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory phenotype, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying impaired collateralization.

## Study Population
This study encompassed 2 distinct study cohorts. The first was primarily subjected to in-depth investigation using scRNA-seq technology, whereas the second was utilized for data validation and confirmation purposes. A total of 12 patients (6 with good and 6 with poor collateralization) with stable angina and at least 1-vessel CTO in a major epicardial coronary artery for a duration of over 3 months were enrolled in the first study cohort from September 2022 to November 2022. Detailed information on the first study population is attached in Table S1. This present study was registered on clinicaltrial.gov (URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT06054126), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. ## Isolation of Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells Using Density Gradient Centrifugation
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from fresh whole blood samples. Blood samples were collected in EDTA-treated tubes to prevent clotting. The PBMCs isolation procedure was performed using a density gradient centrifugation method with Ficoll-Paque PLUS (17144002; Cytiva). In brief, blood samples were diluted with an equal volume of PBS, gently mixed, and layered onto Ficoll-Paque PLUS in centrifuge tubes. Centrifugation was performed at 400g for 15 minutes at room temperature without brakes. After centrifugation, PBMCs formed a distinct layer at the interface between plasma and Ficoll-Paque PLUS. The PBMCs layer was carefully collected, washed twice with PBS, and centrifuged at 300g for 10 minutes to remove any residual contaminants. The isolated PBMCs were resuspended in RPMI 1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS (SH30084.03HI; Cytiva).

## THP-1 Culture and Differentiation
THP-1 monocytes were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The THP-1 cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator, in RPMI 1640 medium (10-040-CV; Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. To induce macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were seeded in culture dishes and treated with 100 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, P8139; Sigma) for 72 hours. After PMA treatment, cells were thoroughly washed with PBS to remove residual PMA and subsequently incubated for an additional 24 hours in fresh RPMI 1640 medium without PMA.

## MAIT Cells Isolation and Coculture Experiments
In this study, we isolated and cultured MAIT cells from PBMCs. We enriched MAIT cells from PBMCs using fluorescence-activated cell sorting methodology. In brief, PBMCs were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting cell surface markers, including CD3 (300430, BioLegend), CD161 (339904, BioLegend), and the MAIT cell–specific TCR (T-cell receptor) marker Vα7.2 (351716; BioLegend). MAIT cells were identified as CD3+CD161+TCR Vα7.2+ cells, and cell sorting was performed using the FACS Aria II Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). After MAIT cells isolation, the purified MAIT cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator, in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Before coculture experiments, MAIT cells were activated with 2.5 μg/mL anti-CD3 (317353; Biolegend) and 1 μg/mL anti-CD28 (377803; Biolegend) under standard culture conditions. For coculture experiments, MAIT cells and THP-1–derived macrophages were cocultured at a 1:1 ratio in sterile culture dishes. The coculture was maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for hours. After the entire coculture period, we utilized flow cytometry and relevant functional assays to observe changes in macrophage functionality.

## Immunofluorescence Assay
After culturing or stimulating the cells for a specified period, fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were applied to the samples and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with the primary antibodies, cells were washed with PBS to remove excess antibodies. Subsequently, appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dyes were applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. After thorough washing, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Finally, cover slips were placed on glass slides using an antifluorescence quenching sealing agent. Immunofluorescent signals were observed and captured using a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ analysis software.

## Flow Cytometry Analysis
Human PBMCs or macrophages were resuspended in 200 μL of stain buffer (420201; BioLegend) and incubated with antibodies against human CD45 (561863; BD), CD14 (982508; BioLegend), CD16 (980104; BioLegend), CD3 (300430; BioLegend), CD4 (357406; BioLegend), CD8 (344722; BioLegend), CD161 (339904; BioLegend), TCR Vα7.2 (351716; BioLegend), CD11b (746004; BD Biosciences), CD86 (374205; BioLegend) at 4 °C for 20 minutes. After adding 1 mL of PBS and washing once, flow cytometric analysis was conducted using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences).

## RNA Extraction and Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction
Based on the previous description, RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction were performed to assess changes in gene expression levels. Total RNA was extracted from macrophages using the TRIzol reagent (15596018, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified RNA was then used to synthesize cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (R323, Vazyme). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction was conducted on cDNA samples using quantitative polymerase chain reaction SYBR Green Master Mix (11202ES03, Yeasen) and a QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primers used to determine the expression of specific genes are listed in Table S2. Gene expression levels were normalized to housekeeping genes such as β-actin. The ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative gene expression. Statistical analysis was performed to determine significant differences between experimental groups.

## RNA Extraction and mRNA Sequencing
We used the TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions to extract total RNA and assessed RNA integrity using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed by Hangzhou Cosmos Wisdom Biotechnology Co, Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Paired-end libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and raw reads were saved as fastq files. After the removal of low-quality reads through a 4-step filtering pipeline, including (1) removal of adapter-contaminated and insert-free reads, (2) trimming of 3′-end bases with Phred quality scores <20 (with complete discard if any posttrimming bases scored <10), (3) elimination of reads containing >10% N bases, and (4) exclusion of reads <20 bp postprocessing—clean reads were obtained for subsequent data analysis. Alignment of clean reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) was performed using HISAT2 software. Reads count for each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count. Gene expression levels were calculated in terms of fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R Bioconductor package (v1.44.0), with genes meeting a P<0.05 threshold being defined as differentially expressed genes. Furthermore, the functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was specifically performed using the clusterProfiler R Bioconductor package (v4.12.6). 

## Construction and Transfection of Small Interfering RNAs
Three small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences were designed against the sequences of the human CCR3 and CCR5 coding regions and synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China; Table S3). THP-1 cells, induced with PMA, were transfected with the siRNA (siRNA-CCR3 or siRNA-CCR5). For groups without siRNA-CCR3 or siRNA-CCR5, siRNA-NC was added as the negative control. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAi MAX reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Measurement of CCL3L1 in Plasma by ELISA
Plasma levels of CCL3L1 were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (EH2774; FineTest). The detection range of the kit was 78.125 to 5000 pg/mL, with a sensitivity of 46.875 pg/mL. The intra-assay coefficient of variation was <8%, and the interassay coefficient of variation was <10%. Plasma samples were diluted 2-fold with sample dilution buffer before the assay. Each assay included negative and positive controls, and samples were run in duplicate. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. CCL3L1 levels were calculated based on the standard-curve generated from the assay.

## Single-Cell Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Analyzing
In patients with T2DM and coronary CTO, we conducted single-cell sequencing on PBMCs samples from 2 distinct groups: 6 patients with good collateralization and 6 patients with poor collateralization. There were no statistically significant differences in age and sex between the good and poor collateralization groups. In addition, to minimize the potential impact of heterogeneity in PBMCs among different patients on the experimental results, we combined PBMCs of 3 patients into 1 sample for single-cell sequencing.
The library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis were all conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech Co, Ltd (Shanghai, China). Raw data generated from high-throughput sequencing were saved in fastq format.
The detailed content on single-cell data analysis regarding quantification, quality control, and cluster analysis of single-cell expression, cell type recognition and visualization, identification of differentially expressed genes, single-cell metabolic pathway analysis, transcription factor regulon analysis, cell-cell communication analysis, and characterization of the impact of different T-cell subsets can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

## Mouse Model of Hindlimb Ischemia by Femoral Artery Ligation and of Myocardial Infarction by Coronary Artery Ligation
All animal experiments were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The animal study was reviewed and approved by the Rui Jin Hospital Animal Care Committee, and in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used for experimental purposes.
Ten-week-old male mice were made diabetic 2 months before the surgical procedure by administering a 60% fat high-fat diet for 4 weeks, accompanied by daily intraperitoneal injections of 55 mg/kg streptozotocin (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer at pH 4.5, administered over 5 days. The diabetic condition was verified by measuring a plasma glucose level exceeding 250 mg/dL, with subsequent weekly checkups up to the time of sacrifice. The hindlimb ischemia model was established as described previously. , Briefly, the mice were initially anesthetized with 3.0% isoflurane for 3 to 5 minutes, followed by intubation and maintained under anesthesia with 1.0 to 1.5% isoflurane. The hindlimbs of the mice underwent surgical procedures, where the left femoral artery and vein were tied off and removed. A noninjurious procedure was conducted on the right hindlimb as a control. The restoration of blood flow to the hindlimbs postsurgery was assessed using a Laser Doppler imaging technique.
Induction of myocardial ischemia was achieved through the ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, as described previously. Under anesthesia, the mice underwent left thoracotomy to access the heart, which was then visualized under a microscope for precise LAD ligation using an 8-0 silk suture at its origin from the left atrium. The effectiveness of the ligation was verified by the observation of ischemic pallor in the myocardial region supplied by the LAD artery. Sham-operated animals underwent the same procedure without LAD ligation. Postoperatively, the intercostal and dermal incisions were meticulously closed, and the mice were provided with warmth before being returned to their housing. Mice that failed to recover from anesthesia were not included in the study’s data.
Mice were euthanized after completion of the experiments. The gastrocnemius, soleus muscles, and heart samples were harvested at different time points for subsequent experiments.

## Treatments by MR1 Ligand
From 1 day before the induction of femoral and LAD artery ligation in diabetic rodents until the conclusion of the experiments, a daily intraperitoneal administration of Ac-6-FP (0.25 mg/kg body weight, no. SMB01333; Sigma-Aldrich) or an equivalent volume of PBS was carried out to assess the effects of MAIT cell suppression on the processes of arteriogenesis and angiogenesis. Because dimethyl sulfoxide (no. D12345; Thermo Fisher Scientific) served as a solvent for preparing the Ac-6-FP solution, an equivalent volume of PBS containing diluted dimethyl sulfoxide was also administered intraperitoneally to serve as a control group.

## Laser Doppler Imaging
Sequential blood flow assessments of the hindlimbs were conducted before surgery, right after the operation, and again on the 21st day after the procedure in both the affected ischemic limbs and the unaffected counterparts, with the use of inhalation anesthesia to standardize hemodynamic conditions. Hindlimb flow was measured using a real-time microcirculation imager (MoorLDI2; Moor Instruments), which operates on the Laser Speckle Contrast Analysis Technology and measures blood perfusion in arbitrary Perfusion Units. To ensure accuracy, 3 separate images were captured at each observation time, and the average of these images was calculated. The data were then presented as a comparative ratio between the blood flow of the ischemic and nonischemic hindlimbs.

## Echocardiography
Echocardiography was performed with a Vevo 2100 system (manufactured by Fujifilm, Japan), which was outfitted with an MS-400 ultrasound transducer. The mice underwent anesthesia induction with 5% isoflurane and were kept sedated with a concentration of 1% to 2% isoflurane throughout the procedure. To standardize the heart rate at 400 to 500 bpm before taking recordings, the concentration of isoflurane was adjusted accordingly. Both M-mode and B-mode images were captured along the longitudinal section of the heart. Various echocardiographic measurements, such as left ventricular ejection fraction and left ventricular fractional shortening, were obtained by LV TRACE mode.

## Histology and Immunofluorescence Staining for Mice Samples
After euthanasia, the gastrocnemius, soleus muscles, and heart tissues from individual mice were promptly collected and rinsed in chilled PBS solution. These tissues were subsequently immersed in a 4% solution of paraformaldehyde and preserved in a refrigerated environment for a day. After fixation, the tissues were encased in paraffin and sectioned. Histological examination involved the application of hematoxylin and eosin and Masson’s trichrome stains. For immunofluorescence analysis, the paraffin-embedded sections of the gastrocnemius, soleus muscles, and cardiac tissues were treated with antibodies that target CD31 (abcam, United Kingdom) and α-SMA (α smooth muscle actin; Thermo Fisher). For IHC analysis, paraffin-embedded sections of skeletal and cardiac muscles were incubated with antibodies against TNF (tumor necrosis factor)-α (Thermo Fisher), IFN-γ (Thermo Fisher), and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor; Thermo Fisher). The cellular nuclei were highlighted using DAPI stain. The samples were then visualized under a Leica DMI6000B microscope (Leica Microsystems). For further analysis, the images were processed and quantified using Image J software (National Institutes of Health in Bethesda).

## Statistics Analysis
In our study, continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α=0.05) supplemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD for normally distributed data and as medians (25th–75th percentiles) for non-normally distributed data, while categorical data were summarized as frequencies (percentages). Differences between groups for continuous variables were analyzed using parametric tests after confirming:(1) normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test (P>0.05) supplemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots, (2) homogeneity of variances via the Levene test (P>0.05). Standard Student t test was applied when both assumptions held, whereas the Welch t test was substituted for unequal variances. Differences between groups for categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test, respectively. Cardiac function data (left ventricular ejection fraction and left ventricular fractional shortening) were analyzed using 2-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests for group comparisons. Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed variables with linear relationships, while the Spearman method was applied to non-normal data or monotonic nonlinear associations. We constructed multivariable logistic regression models to assess the independent determinants of poor collateralization formation. In model 1, age, male, body mass index, history of hypertension, glycated hemoglobin A1c, glomerular filtration rate, non-HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol, and log-transferred hsCRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) were included. In model 2, we further adjusted for the proportion of MAIT cells. The restricted cubic splines were also used to explore the nonlinear relationships between the proportion of MAIT cells and poor collateralization. All analyses were performed using a 2-sided test with an overall significance level (α) of 0.05 and executed using R software (version 4.3.0) and SPSS 25.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL).

## Results

## Mapping Single-Cell Transcriptome of Patients With T2DM With CTO
To delineate the peripheral immune cell landscape and unravel cellular heterogeneity and molecular signatures in patients with T2DM with CTO, we performed scRNA-seq on PBMCs from 6 patients with good collateralization and 6 patients with poor collateralization, using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform (Figure A). Patient information is provided in Table S1. Based on known gene markers (Figure B through E) and functional enrichment analysis (Figure F), 36 315 high-quality cells (Figure S1) were classified into 7 immune cell populations, including T cells (CD3D and CD3E), B cells (CD19 and MS4A1), natural killer cells (GNLY and GZMB), monocytes (LYZ and CD68), dendritic cells (FCER1A and CELEC10A), platelets (PF4 and PPBP), and progenitor cells (CD34 and PROM1; Figure S2). While the overall cell lineage distribution was similar between patients with good and poor collateralization, significant differences were identified in cell proportions (Figure G) and the number of differentially expressed genes (Figure H). Notably, monocytes and T cells demonstrated the highest level of heterogeneity.
 

## Proinflammatory Characteristics of Myeloid Cell Subsets in Patients With T2DM With Poor Collateralization
Using classic monocyte gene markers (CD14 and FCGR3A) and gene set variation analysis, we classified monocytes into 3 subsets: CD14_monocytes, CD14_FCGR3A_monocytes, and FCGR3A_monocytes (Figure A through D). Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that monocyte subsets in patients with poor collateralization demonstrated impaired antigen presentation and activation of TNF receptor signaling pathways (Figure E). Compared with patients with good collateralization, CD14_monocytes were significantly increased, whereas FCGR3A_monocytes were markedly reduced in patients with poor collateralization (Figure F). Flow cytometry analysis (Figure S3) further validated these findings, showing that the proportion of CD14_monocytes within CD45+ cells was significantly higher in patients with poor collateralization compared with those with good collateralization (61.76±16.81% versus 55.43±14.39%, P=0.041; Figure G and H). Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering analysis identified significant activation of FOS in CD14_monocytes from patients with poor collateralization (Figure S4A), with a markedly elevated expression level in these patients (P<0.001; Figure S4B). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis showed that FOS-regulated target genes, upregulated in patients with poor collateralization, were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with proinflammatory responses and abnormal collateralization, including apoptosis, cellular senescence, advanced glycation end product signaling, and TNF signaling pathways (Figure S4C and S4D). These results highlight that monocytes in patients with poor collateralization exhibit pronounced proinflammatory characteristics, suggesting a critical role in the pathogenesis of impaired collateral formation.
 

## Heterogeneity of T-Cell Subsets in Patients With T2DM With Good or Poor Collateralization
Given the significantly higher proportion of T cells in peripheral blood and the pronounced differences observed between patients with good and poor collateralization (Figure G), we further investigated the heterogeneity of T-cell subsets in patients with T2DM with CTO. Using classical marker genes and gene set variation analysis, we classified T cells into 10 distinct subsets: naive_CD4 T cells (CD4, LEF1, SELL), APQ3_CD4 T cells (CD4, APQ3), CCR6_CD4 T cells (CD4, CCR6), Naive_CD8 T cells (CD8A, CD8B, LEF1), GZMK_CD8 T cells (CD8A, CD8B, GZMK), GZMA_CD8 T cells (GZMA, GZMB, GNLY), FOXP3_Tregs (FOXP3), KLRF1_NKT cells (KLRF1, NCAM1), SLC4A10_MAIT cells (SLC4A10), and TRGV9_γδ_T cells (TRGV9, TRDV2; Figure A through C; Figure S5A through S5C). In patients with poor collateralization, the proportions of Naive_CD4_T cells, CCR6_CD4_T cells, FOXP3_Tregs, SLC4A10_MAIT cells, and TRGV9_γδT cells were significantly increased (Figure D). These cells showed activation of pathways associated with fatty acid elongation, lysine degradation, D-glutamine and d-glutamate metabolism, glycosaminoglycan degradation, and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis. Conversely, in patients with good collateralization, the activated pathways were primarily involved in glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, pyruvate metabolism, and the citric acid cycle (Figure E). Furthermore, T-cell subsets in patients with good and poor collateralization exhibited distinct transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering analysis identified significant activation of STAT1 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 1), NR3C1 (nuclear receptor subfamily 3 group C member 1), and ELF1 (E74-Like factor 1) in T-cell subsets from patients with poor collateralization (Figure F).
 

## SLC4A10_MAIT Cells Have the Potential to Impair Collateralization
To investigate the role of different T-cell subsets in regulating collateral formation, we introduced a contribution score that accounts for alterations in both cell numbers and gene expression to evaluate the impact of various T-cell types. Among these, SLC4A10_MAIT cells exhibited the highest score, indicating a greater potential to impair collateralization (Figure G). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated genes in patients with poor collateralization were primarily associated with macrophage activation, cell killing, leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, innate immune response activation, endothelial cell apoptosis, and cytokine production (Figure H). Given the dual functional roles of SLC4A10_MAIT cells in cytokine secretion and cytotoxic activity, we further compared these properties between patients with good and poor collateralization. Patients with poor collateralization showed significantly higher scores in both cytokine production (P=0.0020) and cytotoxicity (P<0.001) compared with those with good collateralization (Figure I). These findings suggest that in poor collateralization, SLC4A10_MAIT cells exhibit pronounced proinflammatory characteristics, including enhanced cytokine secretion and cytotoxic activity.

## Inhibition of MAIT Cells Activation Promotes Collateralization in Diabetic Mice of Hindlimb or Myocardial Ischemia
The appropriate and timely transition of proinflammatory macrophages to reparative macrophages plays an essential role in the recovery after infarction. Through analysis of scRNA-seq data, we have demonstrated that MAIT cells from patients with poor collateralization exhibit pronounced proinflammatory characteristics. Next, we investigated whether suppressing MAIT cells in diabetic mice promotes angiogenesis. As previous study described, , diabetic mice received intraperitoneal injections of Ac-6-FP starting 1 week before surgery until the end of the experiment. We first tested this in an animal model of diabetic mice with hindlimb ischemia produced through femoral artery ligation. Laser Doppler imaging examination revealed that Ac-6-FP administration significantly improved blood flow in ischemic hindlimbs (Figure A). Compared with the vehicle group, the Ac-6-FP group exhibited a higher number of neonatal skeletal muscle cells (Figure B) and less fibrosis (Figure C) in the infarcted area. Meanwhile, the capillary density and the number of arterioles in the Ac-6-FP group significantly increased (Figure D). We also tested the effects of MAIT cell inactivation in ischemic myocardium of diabetic mice with coronary artery ligation. The results consistently showed that compared with Vehicle, Ac-6-FP administration improved cardiac function (Figure E), promoted angiogenesis and arteriogenesis in the ischemic myocardium (Figure F), and reduced fibrosis (Figure G). All these results indicated that inhibition of MAIT cell activity can promote collateralization in diabetic mice.
 

## MAIT Cells From Patients With Poor Collateralization Induce proinflammatory Phenotype in Macrophages
Consistent with previous reports, , our CellChat analysis indicated a significant cell communication link between MAIT cells and monocytes. In patients with poor collateralization, the frequency of T-cell–monocyte interactions was higher than in those with good collateralization (Figure A through C), particularly involving SLC4A10_MAIT cells (Figure B, circle). To investigate the heterogeneity in how MAIT cells regulate monocyte/macrophage function, we isolated MAIT cells from the peripheral blood of T2DM and CTO patients (good and poor collateralization) using flow cytometry and cocultured them with THP-1–derived macrophages. MAIT cells from patients with poor collateralization induced a proinflammatory differentiation of THP-1–derived macrophages compared with those from patients with good collateralization (Figure D through F). Pathways related to pattern recognition receptors, IFN signaling, Nik/NF-κB (nuclear factor-κB), and TNF were significantly enriched (Figure F). Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence further demonstrated an increased proportion of proinflammatory macrophages in cocultures with MAIT cells from poor collateral patients (Figure G through J), with upregulated expression of proinflammatory markers, such as CD86, NOS2 (NO synthase 2), IFNG, and TNFA (Figure K; Table S2). Notably, inhibition of MAIT cell activation using Ac-6-FP mitigated the impact of MAIT cells on macrophage phenotypic conversion (Figure L through O). Consistently, Ac-6-FP treatment in mice significantly reduced the number of proinflammatory macrophages in both ischemic heart and skeletal muscle (Figure P through R). In addition, we found that MAIT cell inactivation significantly reduced TNF-α and IFN-γ expression while enhancing VEGF expression in ischemic regions of both models (Figures S6 and S7). These findings suggest that MAIT cells from patients with poor collateralization promote a proinflammatory macrophage phenotype, potentially disrupting the angiogenic microenvironment.
 

## MAIT Cell–Derived CCL3L1 Increases Inflammatory Macrophages via CCR5 Dependence
By comparing differentially expressed genes in MAIT cell populations relative to other cell populations, we found that the expression levels of secretory genes, such as CCL3L1, GZMK, and TGFB, were specifically elevated in MAIT cells (Figure A; Figure S8A through S8C). Notably, CCL3L1 exhibited the most pronounced upregulation in the MAIT cell populations and was significantly elevated in the plasma of patients with poor collateralization (Figure B; Figure S8D). As a proinflammatory factor, CCL3L1 is closely associated with macrophage inflammatory polarization. Treatment of THP-1–derived macrophages with recombinant CCL3L1 significantly upregulated inflammatory markers (Figure C through E). Neutralization of CCL3L1 in the MAIT cell conditioned medium markedly attenuated the proinflammatory phenotype of cocultured macrophages (Figure F through I). Furthermore, knockdown of CCR5, but not CCR3, in macrophages mitigated CCL3L1-induced inflammatory changes (Figure J). These findings suggest that MAIT cells promote macrophage inflammatory polarization through CCL3L1 secretion and CCR5 activation, potentially contributing to the inflammatory microenvironment in patients with poor collateralization.
 

## Elevated MAIT Cells as an Indicator of Poor Collateralization: Validation Study
We evaluated the proportion of MAIT cells in PBMCs from 104 patients with T2DM with CTO (59 with good collateralization and 45 with poor collateralization). The study flowchart is presented in Figure S9, and the flow cytometry gating strategy is detailed in Figure S10. Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table . While no significant differences were observed in the proportions of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells, the proportion of CD3+ MAIT cells was significantly higher in patients with poor collateralization compared with those with good collateralization (4.08±2.80% versus 2.41±1.82%, P<0.001, Figure A through F). The incidence of poor collateralization progressively increased across quartiles of MAIT cell proportions (P for trend=0.0030, Figure G). Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a positive, linear association between MAIT cell proportion and the risk of poor collateralization (nonlinear P=0.22; Figure H).
 After adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, glycated hemoglobin A1c, non-HDL cholesterol, glomerular filtration rate, and log-transformed hsCRP, MAIT cell proportion remained an independent risk factor for poor collateralization (odds ratio, 1.44 [95% CI, 1.01–1.88]; P=0.0070, Table ). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed that the MAIT cell proportion had an area under the curve of 0.71 ([95% CI, 0.61–0.81]; P<0.001) for predicting poor collateralization, with an optimal cutoff of 2.78% (sensitivity=66.7%, specificity=67.8%; Figure I). Subgroup analyses further confirmed the diagnostic value of MAIT cell proportion across all patient subgroups (Pinteraction≥0.14; Figure J). These findings suggest that the MAIT cell proportion is a potential diagnostic marker for poor collateralization in patients with diabetes.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we used single-cell sequencing technology to comprehensively analyze PBMC samples from patients with T2DM with CTO, aiming to elucidate the peripheral immunocyte landscape and uncover cellular heterogeneity and molecular characteristics. We identified specific immune cell subsets, including CD14+ monocytes and MAIT cells, which are closely associated with impaired collateralization. In diabetic mice, inhibition of MAIT cell activity promoted collateralization in models of hindlimb ischemia and myocardial infarction. We further revealed a novel mechanism whereby MAIT cells contribute to impaired collateral formation through secretion of CCL3L1, which acts on macrophages via CCR5 to induce a proinflammatory phenotype. In addition, we confirmed that the increased presence of MAIT cells is an independent risk factor for poor collateral formation.
MAIT cells represent a unique subset of T cells with a distinctive phenotype. They are predominantly functional within tissues such as mucosal surfaces, the liver, and the lungs, where they exhibit a rapid response to pathogenic challenges. – MAIT cells are defined by their invariant TCR α-chain and their restriction to the MR1 (major histocompatibility complex–related protein 1). , On antigen recognition through MR1, MAIT cells become activated and secrete a variety of cytokines, thereby directly or indirectly participating in the host immune response. , In the context of chronic diseases, the activation of MAIT cells may be associated with various factors, including but not limited to the persistent presence of pathogens, metabolic disorders, and chronic inflammatory states. , – While our study did not directly investigate mechanistic triggers, we propose the following pathways—supported by prior literature—as plausible drivers of MAIT cell enrichment in patients with impaired collateral formation: (1) Gut microbiome dysbiosis: MAIT cells recognize microbial riboflavin metabolites (eg, 5-OP-RU) via the MR1-dependent pathway, and T2DM-associated gut dysbiosis—characterized by enrichment of riboflavin-synthesizing bacteria (eg, Bacteroides)—elevates circulating MAIT-activating ligands. This process is amplified by gut barrier disruption and bacterial translocation, which redistribute MAIT cells from mucosal sites to peripheral blood. (2) Proinflammatory cytokine: In T2DM, prolonged hyperglycemia and the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products result in a chronic inflammatory state, which often leads to poor collateralization. Meanwhile, the chronic inflammation increases TNF-α and IL-6 release, creating an environment that activates MAIT cells, potentially promoting their activation directly or indirectly. , , (3) Metabolic reprogramming: studies have revealed functional heterogeneity in MAIT cells. The MAIT1 subset mediates type 1 immunity through IFN-γ secretion. In contrast, the MAIT17 subset drives type 17 immune responses via IL-17 production. , MAIT1 cells maintain a quiescent, low-metabolic state at baseline but shift toward glycolytic dependence on activation. Conversely, MAIT17 cells remain constitutively active and require sustained energy production through mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. Genetic enhancement of glycolytic pathways resulted in MAIT1 cell expansion, demonstrating that metabolic reprogramming directly modulates MAIT cell phenotypic differentiation. Previous studies have found that inhibiting the activation of MAIT cells can reduce infarct size and local inflammatory responses after acute ischemia caused by transient middle cerebral artery occlusion. Consistent with these findings, we observed that inhibiting the activation of MAIT cells improved collateralization formation in a diabetic mouse model. Therefore, we suggest that inhibiting MAIT cell activation in the context of diabetes might be beneficial for the establishment of collateralization. This may offer a novel therapeutic avenue and strategy for ameliorating impaired collateralization in diabetic conditions.
MAIT cells can be activated through either MR1-dependent or MR1-independent pathways. On activation, they upregulate various inflammation-related molecules, including transcription factors (eg, T-bet), chemokine/cytokine receptors (eg, CCR6, IL-18R), cytokines (eg, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-17), and cytotoxic molecules (eg, granzymes, perforin, granulysin). , – As such, MAIT cells are considered to play roles in both innate and adaptive immune processes. Previous studies have demonstrated that MAIT cells can influence disease onset and progression by regulating macrophage polarization. , , In hepatic fibrotic tissues, MAIT cells accumulate within fibrotic septa, exhibiting an activated phenotype associated with profibrotic characteristics. These cells may promote fibrosis progression by directly acting on hepatic myofibroblasts and enhancing local inflammatory responses. However, in mouse models, pharmacological or antibody-mediated inhibition of MAIT cell activity can limit fibrosis progression and even reverse liver damage caused by chronic toxicity or nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. Mechanistic studies suggest that inhibiting MAIT cell activity induces a shift in macrophages from a proinflammatory to a reparative phenotype, thereby attenuating fibrosis progression. Our study further revealed that MAIT cells derived from patients with impaired collateralization can induce macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory phenotype, while inhibition of MAIT cell activity diminishes this effect. Moreover, we identified a specific CCL3L1-CCR5 signaling pathway, which appears to play a critical role in MAIT cell–induced collateralization impairment. CCL3L1, a well-known proinflammatory chemokine, has been shown to regulate macrophage polarization in various contexts. , However, its specific role in angiogenesis, particularly in the context of diabetes, represents a novel finding. This study demonstrates that CCL3L1, through interaction with its receptor CCR5, orchestrates a proinflammatory macrophage phenotype that inhibits angiogenesis, thereby exacerbating cardiovascular complications in diabetic patients.
In patients with T2DM with CTO, inadequate collateral vessel formation is often associated with an increased incidence of adverse cardiovascular events. While pharmacological treatments, interventional procedures, and biologic therapies have been used, significant physiological variability among patients has hindered the development of universally effective therapeutic strategies. From the perspective of immune cell dynamics, our study identifies a novel immune cell subset closely associated with poor collateralization, providing crucial insights into the immune mechanisms underlying diabetic cardiovascular complications. This finding offers an important basis for the development of immune-based therapeutic strategies to ameliorate impaired collateralization in patients with T2DM.

## Limitations of the Study
Despite exploration of the alterations in gene expression patterns, transcription factors, and metabolic pathways in monocytes and T cells in patients with T2DM with CTO, we acknowledge that there are still certain limitations in our study. First, due to the limited number of patients with T2DM with CTO who could be matched for age and sex within the same time frame, the scarcity of patients in the scRNA-seq data might introduce statistical bias. Therefore, we have undertaken supplementary experiments to validate our findings. Second, this study focuses on peripheral immune cells; further research into immune cells within the myocardial tissue of patients with T2DM with CTO is needed to deepen our understanding of the relationship between immune cells and collateral formation. Third, although this study provides compelling evidence for the involvement of the CCL3L1-CCR5 axis in MAIT cell–mediated inhibition of collateral formation, further research is needed to explore the potential contribution of additional mechanisms.
