## INTRODUCTION
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a chronic, progressive, and fatal lung interstitial disease with limited therapeutic options ( ). Recent advancements in pulmonary gene therapies have shown potential to halt or even reverse PF, particularly by focusing on correcting gene mutations in alveolar epithelial cells ( , ). Despite these advancements, a clinical trial using nebulized gene-liposome complex for inhaled cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator mRNA failed to show notable efficacy in patients with cystic fibrosis ( ), underscoring the need for more effective pulmonary delivery systems. Most studies have focused on the chemical surface modifications or physical structures of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to address nebulization challenges ( , ); however, these factors may not fully address the complexities of pulmonary RNA delivery, particularly within a diseased microenvironment. In PF, dense and viscous mucus barriers in the lungs present a substantial challenge for inhaled LNPs to effectively deliver local gene therapy ( ). In addition, macrophages in the lungs may alter the delivery pathway due to their high phagocytic uptake and processing of NPs ( – ). This issue may be exacerbated in patients with PF, who experience a remarkable accumulation of macrophages in the lungs, further restricting the access of transgene therapeutics to target epithelial cells ( ).
Driven by the need for efficient RNA delivery to the lungs and the potential for long-lasting treatment of PF, we present a highly efficient aerosol inhalation system in this study. The nose-only aerosol inhalation device consists of the following key components ( ): (i) a vibrating mesh nebulizer to aerosolize LNPs, (ii) a medical-grade hydrogen supplement system to facilitate the delivery of nebulized LNPs across the mucus barrier, and (iii) a central chamber connected to six rodent restraints to enable the controlled administration of LNPs at a low dose with the desired concentration of hydrogen gas. We used the unique properties of hydrogen gas, including its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects ( , ), and its use as an effective inhalation therapy for patients with injuries and inflammation ( , ). Unlike other gases, such as oxygen or nitrogen, hydrogen gas has lower viscosity and smaller Reynolds numbers ( , ). Despite its promising potential, the use of therapeutic hydrogen gas to facilitate LNP delivery, potentially reducing aerodynamic barriers, remains largely unexplored. We hypothesize that using hydrogen as the nebulization gas for pulmonary RNA delivery could enhance deposition in the deep alveolar regions, promoting efficient in vivo transfection.
 Given the limited transduction efficiency of lung epithelial cells using conventional viral and nonviral methods ( ), we propose an alternative strategy targeting phagocytic macrophages. Recent evidence highlights the intricate interplay between aberrantly activated profibrotic alveolar macrophages (AMs) and fibroblasts as key drivers of PF pathogenesis ( ). Profibrotic macrophages notably secrete transforming growth factor–β1 (TGFβ1), which activates fibrogenic signaling pathways ( ). Thus, we hypothesize that inhibiting TGFβ1 production by macrophages could be an effective strategy to disrupt the fibrotic cascade at its source. To enhance siRNA transfection efficiency, we hybridized T cell–derived apoptotic body membranes (TABMs) with a pH-dependent charge-inverting lipid, dioleoylglycerophosphate-diethylenediamine (DOP-DEDA), to construct hybrid NPs (HNPs) that precisely target macrophages and improve endosomal escape ( ). Previous studies have shown the high phagocytic capacity of macrophages in clearing apoptotic cells ( ). The instillation of apoptotic T cells into bleomycin-stimulated lungs also has been shown to increase hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) production in macrophages, mitigating pulmonary injury and fibrosis ( , ). We demonstrated that HNPs remain stable after nebulization (aHNPs) and can be exposed to the desired concentration of hydrogen gas using our custom-designed device. The laminar flow of hydrogen gas generates positive pressure, facilitating lung inflation and promoting the movement of aerosolized HNPs/TGFβ1 siRNA through the mucus layer. This enables precise targeting of macrophages for blocking fibrogenic signaling pathways and induced efficient HGF production in macrophages, promoting alveolar epithelium repair. These favorable outcomes not only reshape the macrophage profile but also modulate the lung immune microenvironment, resulting in long-lasting therapeutic effects for PF.

## RESULTS

## Fabrication of inhalable biomimetic NPs with efficient siRNA loading
The fabrication process of HNPs/siRNA involves three steps ( ): (i) isolation of TABM from Jurkat T cells, (ii) preparation of LNPs loaded with siRNA (LNPs/siRNA), and (iii) fusion of LNPs/siRNA with TABMs. Initially, we successfully isolated T cell apoptotic bodies (TABs) from apoptotic T cells ( and fig. S1, A and B). We again found the abundance of two crucial protein markers, CD3 and CD44, in both T cells and TABs ( ). In addition, TABs exhibited high expression of cleaved caspase-3, indicating successful induction of apoptosis in T cells ( ). We then analyzed the size distribution and morphology of TABM vesicles using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging (fig. S1C). We found that the HNPs could be successfully fabricated by optimizing the hybridization of TABM and lipids at a weight ratio of 1:1 (TABM to lipid film, w/w) (fig. S1D). In addition, the successful fusion was confirmed by observing the marked yellow fluorescence resulting from the overlap of red (LNPs) and green (TABMs) fluorescent signals using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; ). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images further revealed the spherical and uniform dispersion of HNPs ( ), consistent with the 102.1 ± 1.2 nm hydrodynamic diameters ( ). We again found a significantly lower zeta potential of the HNPs compared to the LNPs ( and fig. S1E). This difference might be attributed to the successful fusion of negatively charged TABM (−17.1 ± 0.4 mV) with the LNPs. Furthermore, we observed a significant increase in the zeta potential of both LNPs and HNPs when exposed to pH 5.0 conditions, as opposed to pH 7.4 conditions for NPs ( ). This observation is consistent with the previously reported charge-reversible property of DOP-DEDA LNPs ( ), indicating their potential to facilitate siRNA lysosome escape. In addition, the HNPs demonstrated a protective effect on siRNA against ribonuclease (RNase; ). In addition, the HNPs maintained stability for 72 hours when stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under two pH conditions (pH 7.4 and 6.8; ), indicating the potential for our HNPs to retain the encapsulated siRNA within the mildly acidic environment of PF lung tissues ( ). Next, we further evaluated the release profiles of siRNA from HNPs in PBS at pH 5.0, which simulate the acidic endosomal environments after macrophage uptake ( ). The HNPs demonstrated a faster release observed at pH 5.0 compared to pH 7.4 ( ). Together, these data verify the potential applicability of HNPs for nebulized delivery.
 To enhance the lung delivery efficiency of nano-formulations, we developed a home-built aerosol inhalation device that connected a mouse-holding chamber to a mesh nebulizer via an inhalation chamber (fig. S1F and ). This setup ensured that the mice were exposed to the desired concentration of hydrogen gas while inhaling the HNPs/TGFβ1 siRNA (hydro HNPs). We initially conducted a comparison of HNP morphology before and after nebulization using TEM. The results were consistent with our DLS measurements ( ). In addition, we observed no significant differences in terms of siRNA encapsulation efficiency (EE) (fig. S1E) and protein profiles ( ) within the HNPs before and after nebulization. This finding further strengthened the evidence for the HNPs’ capability to withstand the shear stress experienced during the nebulization process. To assess the feasibility of the aerosol inhalation device for pulmonary delivery of HNPs, we tested the distribution homogeneity of nebulized NPs ( ), nebulization time, and output efficiency ( ). These data indicated the reliability of our home-built device for the efficient inhalation of nano-formulations, even at low dosages. Collectively, these results emphasized the potential of using hydrogen gas–based aerosol inhalation devices and biomimetic NPs to effectively deliver siRNA into lung tissues.
 

## Enhanced cellular internalization and in vitro gene silencing efficacy
Efficient cellular internalization of NP cargoes is crucial for enhancing siRNA transfection efficiency ( ). Initially, we evaluated the cellular uptake selectivity of NPs in RAW 264.7, A549, and HFL-1 cells (fig. S2, A and B). CLSM images demonstrated a significant increase in red fluorescence of HNPs in RAW 264.7 cells compared to LNPs before fusion ( ). However, negligible uptake of HNPs was observed in both A549 and HFL-1 cells. These observations were further supported by flow cytometry analysis ( and fig. S2C). Collectively, these results provide strong evidence supporting the specific targeting property of HNPs toward RAW 264.7 cells, which was endowed by the introduction of the TABM on the LNPs ( ). Furthermore, we did not observe substantial differences in terms of HNPs uptake when they were nebulized and exposed to a hydrogen flow. To further validate the relevance of our results to human macrophages, we assessed NP uptake selectivity in the THP-1 cell line. Initially, we induced differentiation in THP-1 cells by stimulating them with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) to establish an in vitro model of monocyte differentiation. Following PMA treatment, the THP-1 cells exhibited adherence and displayed a macrophage-like morphology (fig. S2D). Confirmed by CLSM images and flow cytometry analysis (fig. S2E), our findings reaffirmed the targeting specificity of HNPs toward macrophages. Subsequently, we proceeded to investigate their distribution among various cell types within PF lung tissues. Mice with PF were administered Cy5.5-labeled NPs via inhalation, and lung tissues were collected 2 hours after the treatment. We then conducted flow cytometry analysis on the lung cells obtained from each experimental group. Consistent with the in vitro uptake findings, both HNPs and hydro HNPs exhibited preferential internalization by macrophages, with 77.8% of macrophages internalized NPs as compared to 11.6% internalization by epithelial cells and 10.6% by endothelial cells ( and fig. S2F). Together, our aerosol inhalation device effectively facilitated the pulmonary delivery of HNPs/siRNA and demonstrated a promising ability to selectively target macrophages in PF lung tissues.
 We then assessed cellular uptake mechanism of HNPs by pretreating RAW 264.7 cells with various inhibitors ( ), such as chlorpromazine (CPZ), filipin, and amiloride, followed by flow cytometry analysis. We found a significant reduction in the uptake of LNPs in the presence of filipin, while much fewer HNPs were delivered into cells when exposed to CPZ or amiloride. These findings indicated that the cellular internalization of HNPs is primarily mediated by clathrin-dependent endocytosis and micropinocytosis ( ) (fig. S3A). The cellular uptake mechanism of HNPs was not altered after nebulization and exposure to a hydrogen flow. In addition, we evaluated the ability of the hydro HNPs to escape from endosomes after uptake. We observed a gradual decrease in the fluorescence signal of HNPs within lysosomes over a time period of 1 to 6 hours ( ). Furthermore, the calculation of Pearson’s correlation coefficient from CLSM images provided additional evidence for the successful escape of HNPs from endosomes and subsequent release of siRNA into the cytosol (fig. S3B). To provide a comparison, we also prepared conventional NPs lacking DOP-DEDA using a dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) film, followed by hybridization with TABM (fig. S3C). This observation was consistent with prior studies showing that siRNA delivery via DOP-DEDA LNPs leads to siRNA escape from endosomes ( ), a process facilitated by the charge reversal effect of the DOP-DEDA lipid. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the escape of our NPs from endosomes remain unclear. In future studies, we intend to conduct a more detailed assessment of endosome escape, including galectin-8 tracking ( ), to effectively screen and improve intracellular drug delivery systems.
Next, we evaluated the silencing efficiency of HNPs by measuring the expression of TGFβ1 in profibrotic macrophages. The transition of murine bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) to profibrotic macrophages (referred to as M2) was induced using interleukin-4 (IL-4) ( ) (fig. S3D). Treatment with hydro HNPs resulted in the lowest TGFβ1 mRNA expression in M2 macrophages, representing an approximate 80.1% reduction compared to PBS treatment ( ). Furthermore, we evaluated TGFβ1 protein expression in the M2 macrophages by collecting the culture medium using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). We observed a notable and sustained reduction in TGFβ1 protein levels after hydro HNP treatment (a 2.5-fold decrease compared to PBS treatment), aligning with the results obtained from the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis ( ).

## Enhanced mucus penetration and biodistribution of hydro HNPs
While the introduction of the TABM on LNPs enabled specific targeting properties toward AMs, the presence of abnormal mucus in conditions like PF still posed a challenge for effective pulmonary delivery of NP to target cells ( ). To gain insights into the interaction between NPs and the mucus layer, we have successfully synthesized Muc/poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogels with a water content ranging from 97.6 to 98.1% over three freezing-thawing cycles. This method was chosen to prevent any chemical reactions that could potentially alter the mucin (Muc) structure, as illustrated in ( , ). The gelation process relies on the development of microcrystalline structures of PVA, resulting in hydrogels with robust mechanical properties without the need for chemical cross-linkers. SEM images further confirm the highly porous nature of the Muc/PVA hydrogels, showcasing a combination of large and small pores with narrow gel walls across the matrix, typically with pore sizes below 5 μm ( ). Next, we delved into the penetration of NPs into Muc/PVA hydrogels designed to simulate the PF mucus. The findings, illustrated in , revealed that both LNPs and HNPs displayed intensified red fluorescence signals in the supernatant above the hydrogel, signifying limited transmucosal penetration capability of the NPs. It is not unexpected that exposure to gas flow (air or hydrogen) enhanced the NP permeation through Muc-PVA hydrogels. However, only the application of hydro HNPs resulted in a significant increase, showing a 1.4-fold rise compared to air HNPs. This raises the question of why the flow of hydrogen gas substantially improved NP permeation ability through mucus. To gain further insights, we replaced hydrogen with conventional compressed air and investigated the permeation of HNPs through the Muc-PVA hydrogel under the same flow conditions. The exposure of HNPs to compressed air did enhance NP permeation through the mucus, but it was not as effective as hydrogen supplementation. The supplement of hydrogen gas not only exerted an external force to propel NPs through the mucus layer but also potentially altered the interaction between the NPs and mucus. To gain a mechanistic understanding of the impact of hydrogen on Muc-particle interactions at the molecular level, we conducted a comparison of the association constant (Ka) between NPs and mucus using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). For this comparison, mucus samples were pretreated separately with hydrogen and compressed air. Subsequently, the NPs were used to titrate Muc solutions. Irrespective of additional flow supplementation, we observed that the interaction between Muc and NPs was exothermic and entailed an entropy loss process (fig. S4A). This suggested that the interaction was driven by enthalpy and involved the formation of noncovalent bonds, such as hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces ( , ). Consistent with the enhanced permeability observed under hydrogen conditions, the Ka value of hydro HNPs was approximately 2.3 times lower than that of conventional HNPs without hydrogen supplementation. In contrast, when exposed to compressed air, although the NP-Muc interaction was decreased compared to NPs alone, it was not as effective as hydrogen supplementation. These findings consolidated that hydrogen supplementation could disrupt the formation of noncovalent bonds between mucus and NPs, rather than simply exerting positive pressure to push NPs through the mucus layer.
 To validate these findings in vivo, we conducted further investigations into the real-time diffusion of nebulized NPs in mouse tracheas using multiple particle tracking (MPT) ( ). Initially, we assessed mucus expression in airway tissue sections from mice with PF. Alcian Blue (AB)–periodic acid Schiff staining revealed goblet/mucous cell hyperplasia in the experimental murine models ( , ) (fig. S4B), demonstrating the utility of our approach in directly examining the impact of thickened mucus on inhaled NPs. The mean square displacement (MSD) of nebulized particles was quantified to assess their diffusion (fig. S4C). Hydro HNPs exhibited significantly more diffusive trajectories in the tracheal mucus of mice with PF compared to conventional nebulized NPs. This resulted in a 4.4-fold increase in median MSD values ( , and movies S1 to S4). Subsequently, the tracheas of mice were cryo-sectioned and stained to visualize the distribution of NPs. The results confirmed that hydro HNPs traveled evenly through the lung airways, with a substantial increase in airway coverage (92.1% versus 46.3%) compared to conventional nebulized NPs without hydrogen supplementation ( ). Having established the enhanced mucus penetration capabilities of hydro HNPs, we proceeded to evaluate their distribution within lung tissues. Initially, we assessed the nebulization efficiency of the hydro HNPs by quantifying the fluorescence signal of the retained NPs in the lungs after intratracheal administration for 0.5 hours. Notably, our hydro HNPs exhibited significantly higher retention in the lung compared to conventional nebulized NPs, exhibiting approximately a 2.5-fold increase (fig. S4D). Subsequently, we evaluated the distribution of NPs among different lung tissues at various time intervals. Imaging using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS) demonstrated that hydro HNPs significantly improved siRNA delivery to deeper lung tissues, exhibiting a uniform fluorescence signal within each lung lobe ( and fig. S4E). In contrast, nebulized NPs without hydrogen supplementation were sparsely distributed in lung lobes following intratracheal administration for 2 hours. Furthermore, fluorescence images of the lung parenchyma clearly indicated that hydro HNPs were predominantly located in the fibrotic areas of lung tissues ( and fig. S5, A and B), whereas PF mice treated with conventional nebulized NPs only showed weak fluorescence signals in the lung parenchyma, with most of the NPs being trapped in small airways. On the basis of these findings, we further investigated the cell types that internalized the particles following inhalation. Consistent with the previous flow cytometry analysis, immunofluorescence data further confirmed that approximately 80% of hydro HNPs colocalized with F4/80+ macrophages rather than prosurfactant protein C (pro-SPC)+ epithelial cells, indicating a high delivery efficiency of siRNA to macrophages ( and fig. S5C). A strong fluorescence signal could still be detected in the lung tissues 72 hours postinhalation of hydro HNPs, indicating their superior lung retention capacity after nebulization using the aerosol inhalation device (fig. S5D). The findings presented in this study offer compelling evidence that nebulized HNPs, administered via our specialized aerosol inhalation device, significantly decreased their adherence to mucus barriers by disrupting hydrophobic interaction through hydrogen flow–induced shear stresses. As a result, the HNPs could readily penetrate the deeper regions of lung tissues and selectively target macrophages, thereby demonstrating a high efficacy in gene silencing.

## Hydro HNPs improved gene silencing efficiency in bleomycin-induced mice
We next assessed whether the enhanced distribution in the deeper alveoli and precise uptake of hydro HNPs by macrophages improved the efficiency of gene silencing in lung fibrosis. Severe PF was induced in C57BL/6 mice by intratracheal bleomycin injection for a duration of 21 days. Nebulization of NPs loaded with TGFβ1 siRNA was conducted at 3-day intervals from day 9 to day 18 ( ). On day 21, the mice were euthanized, and their lungs were isolated. We initially analyzed the alterations in TGFβ1 expression in macrophages using immunofluorescence staining. Consistent with the observed in vitro gene silencing efficacy, mice treated with HNPs demonstrated a significant reduction in TGFβ1 expression levels in F4/80+ macrophages compared to the groups treated with PBS or LNPs ( and fig. S6A). Treatment with hydro HNPs led to a significant suppression of TGFβ1 expression levels in macrophages, exhibiting a notable 1.2-fold decrease compared to the air HNPs treatment. This effect could potentially be ascribed to better mucus penetration of NPs the anti-inflammatory properties of hydrogen ( ). Consistently, treatment with hydro HNPs significantly reduced the levels of TGF-β and other profibrogenic markers ( ), such as IL-1β, in both mRNA and protein expression ( ).
 These data prompted us to consider the contribution of silencing the TGFβ gene in macrophages to the attenuation of lung injury and fibrosis. We therefore investigated the polarization of macrophages following NP treatment for lung fibrosis, considering the notable contribution of M2 macrophages in producing elevated levels of TGFβ1, which in turn promote profibrotic processes ( ). Notably, inhalation of hydro HNPs led to a significant decrease in the expression levels of the M2 marker CD206 compared to the PBS or other NPs groups ( ). This finding was further supported by a marked reduction in CD206 and Arg mRNA expression ( ). Furthermore, the dysregulated activation of profibrotic monocyte–derived AMs (Mo-AMs) has been implicated in the pathogenesis of lung fibrosis ( ), and it is noteworthy that Mo-AMs exhibit the highest expression of the cytokine TGFβ1 during fibrosis ( ). In line with the observed shift in macrophage phenotype, treatment with hydro HNPs significantly reduced the population of TGFβ1-producing Mo-AMs in the lungs ( and fig. S6, B and C). In addition, we investigated the expression of HGF, which plays a key role in alveolar epithelial regeneration and reconstruction after lung injury ( ). Previous studies have shown that direct instillation of apoptotic cells promotes the release of HGF by macrophages in PF lungs ( ), leading to the attenuation of lung injury and fibrosis. The anti-inflammatory effects of HGF are thought to be mediated through phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt/glycogen synthase kinase-3β signaling and inhibition of nuclear factor κB signaling ( ). Furthermore, HGF diminishes fibrogenesis not only by blocking TGF-β signaling but also by reducing TGFβ levels in multiple experimental fibrosis models ( – ). In addition, HGF has been shown to prevent apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells in a bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis model ( ) and small airway epithelial cells in vitro ( ). In line with these observations, mice treated with hydro HNPs showed elevated expression of HGF mRNA and protein ( ). This sustained increase in HGF levels has the potential to bolster anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects, thereby facilitating improved alveolar wound repair. Together, these outcomes emphasize the notable enhancement in pulmonary delivery of HNPs/siRNA to macrophages within the lungs facilitated by our self-designed aerosol inhalation device. This approach resulted in a reduction in the number of TGFβ1-producing macrophages, a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a less profibrotic profile, and the promotion of HGF for lung repair.

## Hydro HNPs alleviated bleomycin–induced PF
Last, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of hydro HNPs treatment in a PF mouse model. The mice received treatment every 3 days, beginning from day 9 to day 18 postbleomycin insult, and were euthanized on day 21 for outcome assessment. As illustrated in , the lungs of bleomycin-challenged mice treated with PBS exhibited severe injury. In contrast, lung injury was significantly alleviated in mice treated with NPs loaded with siRNA. We also examined the effects of inhaling blank HNPs or hydrogen alone on lung fibrosis, but unfortunately, neither the blank nanocarrier nor hydrogen exhibited any impact on lung fibrosis (fig. S7, A to D). In contrast, the hydro HNPs treatment group demonstrated a significant reduction in the total protein content in BALF, which was substantially increased after bleomycin challenge (fig. S7E). Compared to mice treated with conventional nebulized NPs or air HNPs, the survival rate of mice receiving hydro HNPs treatment was extended by 100% at 21 days ( ). These findings underscored the potential of hydro HNPs in the treatment of lung fibrosis. Furthermore, histological analyses using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining collectively showed a significant decrease in collagen deposition, parenchymal disruption, while preserving alveolar epithelial structures after hydro HNP treatment ( ). Consistently, the hydro HNPs treatment group exhibited significantly lower levels of hydroxyproline in the lung ( ), accompanied by a substantial reduction in collagen deposition as demonstrated by immunostaining results ( , and fig. S7F). In addition, compared to the PBS or other NP groups, mice receiving hydro HNPs treatment displayed the lowest expression levels of proliferation markers ( ), including fibronectin, collagen I, and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) ( , and fig. S7G). Nonetheless, administering hydro HNPs notably prevented lung damage.
 Last, we sought to determine whether hydro HNPs could effectively reverse lung fibrosis. To address this, we induced a mouse model of PF using a single intratracheal injection of bleomycin for a 15-day period, as outlined in . Following the bleomycin challenge, mice were treated with hydro HNPs on days 15 and 18, respectively, while another group of mice received air HNPs for comparison. On day 21, the mice were euthanized to assess the therapeutic outcomes. Inhalation of hydro HNPs not only alleviated bleomycin–induced lung injury ( ) and improved mouse survival ( ) but also reversed fibrosis, as demonstrated by histopathological analysis ( ). In addition, mice treated with hydro HNPs exhibited significantly lower levels of hydroxyproline in the lungs ( ). Micro–computed tomography (CT) images also confirmed that hydro HNPs had significant effects in reversing lung fibrosis (fig. S8, A and B). Collectively, these findings provided evidence for the potential therapeutic effects of hydro HNPs in reversing PF by efficiently delivering TGFβ1-siRNA into macrophages ( ).
 

## DISCUSSION
Nebulized RNA delivery offers remarkable potential for treating a range of lung diseases that currently lack effective therapies. However, the success of nebulized RNA therapies depends on the development of clinically relevant delivery methods, as shown by a recent clinical trial in which nebulized gene therapy failed to achieve sufficient efficacy ( ). The authors specifically emphasized the need for improved delivery techniques. Despite notable advancements in lung biology, LNP design has predominantly focused on RNA delivery via intravenous or intramuscular routes, rather than inhalation-based administration ( ). Inhalation-based delivery, unlike systemic delivery, poses unique challenges due to the need for compatibility with inhalation devices and the distinct characteristics of the respiratory system, such as its anatomical structure and the presence of thick, adhesive mucus commonly found in many lung diseases, in particular PF ( ).
Our study demonstrated efficient siRNA delivery to the lungs and the potential for durable PF treatment using HNP formulations delivered through a custom-built aerosol inhalation device. AMs exhibit a robust phagocytic capacity and are considered the primary cells in the lung responsible for the uptake and processing of NPs. Prior studies have used antibody-based methodologies to target miR delivery to various cell types while minimizing particle uptake by AMs ( ). Recognizing the pivotal role of AMs as profibrotic cells in initiating the fibrotic cascade ( ), we aimed to directly deliver TGFβ1 siRNA to AMs by fabricating inhalable LNPs. To maximize the therapeutic potential of macrophage targeting, we developed HNPs coated with TABMs. Our research revealed that these HNPs selectively colocalize with AMs in fibrotic lung lesions, effectively disrupting the fibrotic cascade at its source. A notable aspect of our study involved using the T cell membrane coating to induce HGF production in macrophages, thereby promoting alveolar epithelium repair. These findings align with previous research indicating that the recognition and phagocytosis of apoptotic cells by macrophages stimulate the production of HGF, a critical factor in the regeneration and restoration of alveolar epithelial cells following lung injury ( , ). Specifically, we prioritized the use of apoptotic body membranes from Jurkat T cells over other cell types. While macrophages respond similarly to different apoptotic cells, the use of apoptotic Jurkat T cells enables us to differentiate them from the predominant inflammatory cells (neutrophils and macrophages/monocytes). For instance, the secondary necrosis of neutrophils could introduce additional pro-inflammatory and harmful substances ( ). Besides, the method of using TABMs potentially provides sufficient host ABM vehicles to be used in drug delivery in vivo ( ). Compared to conventional LNPs, the mechanisms of our HNPs to reverse lung fibrosis are likely multifaceted. These particles not only reduce the number of TGFβ1-producing macrophages but also shift macrophage phenotypes toward a less profibrotic profile while promoting alveolar epithelium repair. These effects have lasting implications for lung fibrosis treatment. In addition, unlike surface-modified NPs, our findings show that nonmodified HNPs remain stable during nebulization while maintaining precise targeting of AMs. This is important, as LNP stability has been a concern for both storage and nebulization, with instability potentially leading to the dissociation of surface-modified components and compromising the targeting ability of nanodelivery systems.
We critically optimized the delivery process to ensure efficient deposition of siRNA-loaded HNPs in the lungs. Previous studies and our observations revealed that nebulization causes undesired drug loss in the upper airways of mice due to inadequate breathing patterns and insufficient breath-holding ( ). As a result, only a small fraction of the drug dose (approximately 10 to 15%) is deposited in the lungs ( ). This limitation is further exacerbated in diseases characterized by thick, viscous mucus. To address these challenges, we incorporated a hydrogen supplement system into a vibrating mesh nebulizer. We also used individual mouse restrainers connected to a nose-only exposure system, where their noses were exposed to a central chamber that delivered aerosolized particles with the aid of hydrogen gas. Compared to conventional nebulized LNPs, our hydro HNPs offered several advantages, including uniform distribution across all five lung lobes and efficient deposition in the lung fibrotic areas. Our findings also offer compelling evidence supporting the pivotal role of hydrogen flow–induced shear stresses in disrupting NP-mucus interactions. Previous studies have confirmed that high-speed airflow leads to momentum transfer to mucus accumulated on airway surfaces, propelling it toward the larynx ( ). The high airflow–induced stresses in larger airways can remove more substantial mucus accumulations with a length of approximately 1 cm, as evidenced by the average mass of sputum expectorated by subjects with mucoobstructive lung disease, which is around 1 g (equivalent to a volume of approximately 1 cm3) ( ). However, in smaller airways (<2 mm, generations 7 and beyond), mucus does not accumulate to a mass or height that would allow shear stress forces to act. Our studies were further designed to explore how accumulated mucus in both large and small airways responds to hydrogen flow. Specifically, our findings, based on a study involving NP permeation through native Muc–based hydrogel and ITC analysis, revealed that the introduction of hydrogen gas not only exerts an external force to propel NPs through the mucus layer but also disrupts the formation of noncovalent bonds between mucus and NPs. Similarly, the in vivo transmucosal study confirmed the exceptional mucus permeation ability of our hydro HNPs, aligning with our in vitro findings. These results are in line with recent data indicating that hydrogen gas, with its lower viscosity and smaller Reynolds numbers compared to oxygen or nitrogen, exhibits high shear force, enabling it to reduce aerodynamic barriers and access deeper areas ( , ). We hypothesized that the laminar flow of hydrogen may also contribute to tearing apart the Muc-Muc and/or other protein-protein bonds ( ), offering remarkable benefits to improve the lung delivery of nebulized NPs. This property of our inhalation system may also provide benefits for other mucoobstructive lung diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and cystic fibrosis. In present, international guidelines recommend mucolytic agents as add-on therapy in selected patients with COPD due to their potential to reduce exacerbations and enhance health status. Our data indeed demonstrate that hydrogen inhalation may also disrupt or break interactions that contribute to the viscous dissipation in mucus, thereby directly improving the lung delivery of nebulized therapy, as opposed to traditional combination therapies. In addition, inhaled hydrogen has been demonstrated as a therapeutic gas for treating neurological disorders and inflammatory diseases such as asthma ( – ). While mechanical ventilation remains a crucial supportive intervention for patients with respiratory disease, it is crucial to acknowledge its potential adverse effects, such as inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the lungs ( ). In contrast, inhaled hydrogen has been recommended as an auxiliary therapy to alleviate symptoms in patients with COPD ( , ). Furthermore, hydrogen inhalators are more portable, enabling patients to use them conveniently at home. Given its clinical relevance, we believe that it may be possible for patients with respiratory diseases to inhale hydrogen to relieve symptoms while concurrently receiving inhaled drug treatments. Further research and clinical studies are needed to fully explore the potential benefits and safety of combining inhaled hydrogen with drug delivery systems such as HNPs. Individual patient factors and specific disease conditions should also be considered when designing and implementing such therapies.
In summary, the synergistic combination of HNPs with a hydrogen gas–based aerosol inhalation system significantly enhanced lung siRNA delivery by overcoming both extracellular and cellular barriers, surpassing the efficacy of currently used nebulized LNPs. Our hydro HNPs demonstrated excellent mobility through the dense mucus barrier, efficient deposition in fibrotic lung lesions, precise macrophage targeting, and successful endosomal escape for gene silencing. These favorable outcomes not only suppressed profibrotic cytokine production but also modulated the lung immune microenvironment, contributing to the reversal of PF. Overall, our inhalable platform represents a notable breakthrough in nebulized gene therapy, offering potential opportunities for the treatment of chronic lung diseases.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Preparation of T cell–apoptotic body membrane vesicles
Jurkat T cells were exposed to ultraviolet irradiation at 254 nm for 30 min to induce apoptosis ( ) and then incubated at 37°C for 2.5 hours. TABs were then isolated from the culture media by subjecting them to a centrifugation step at 4°C and 50g for 5 min, followed by another centrifugation step at 4°C and 1000g for 15 min. The protein concentration of the TABs was determined using the BCA method. Last, the TABs were resuspended in PBS and stored at −80°C for future use. The TABs were further characterized using annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) staining (annexin V–FITC/PI apoptosis assay kit; Beyotime, China) and C1q staining. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (CLSM; Leica SP8, Germany), and flow cytometry analysis was also performed.
To obtain the TABM vesicles, the collected apoptotic bodies were subjected to hypotonic treatment and sonication ( ). Initially, the ABs were suspended in a membrane protein extraction buffer solution containing 10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and then cooled in an ice bath for 1 hour. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to a gentle sonication for 5 s and centrifuged at 4°C and 100g for 10 min. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 4°C and 10,000g for 10 min, followed by three washes with water to obtain the TABM. Last, the TABM was redispersed in water, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. The TABM was stored at 4°C for subsequent experiments.

## Preparation of HNPs/siRNA
The LNPs, consisting of DOP-DEDA, DPPC, and cholesterol in a ratio of 45:10:45 (w/w/w), were prepared as previously reported ( ). Initially, the lipids were dissolved in chloroform to achieve a final lipid concentration of 10 mM. Separately, siRNA was dissolved in 1 mM sodium citrate (pH 4.0) at a concentration of 285.7 nM. The lipid and siRNA solutions were then heated to 40°C and mixed in a 1:5 (v/v) ratio. After vortexing for 30 s, dialysis was used, resulting in the formation of siRNA-loaded LNPs.
Hybrid LNPs loaded with siRNA (HNPs/siRNA) were constructed by mixing equal volumes of LNP/siRNA and TABM vesicles at a 1:1 weight ratio ( ). The mixture was then sequentially extruded through polycarbonate membranes with pore sizes of 400 and 200 nm, respectively. To optimize the hybrid ratio of lipids and TABMs in HNPs, the lipids and TABMs were individually labeled with DiI and DiO. The resulting dual fluorescence-labeled HNPs were detected using a microplate reader (excitation at 440 nm, emission from 470 to 650 nm; Perkin-Elmer, USA). Subsequently, the HNPs were applied to a slide for colocalization analysis using a confocal microscope (CLSM; Leica SP8, Germany). Furthermore, a gel retardation assay was performed to evaluate the siRNA-loading capacity.

## Nebulization of HNPs/siRNA
The aerosol inhalation device was self-designed and consisted of an oxyhydrogen supply system (AMS-H-03; Shanghai Asclepius Meditec Co. Ltd. China), a mesh nebulizer (Yuyue Investment Co. Ltd. China), and an inhalation chamber equipped with six mouse-holding chambers. During delivery, the siRNA-loaded NPs were transformed into aerosol droplets by a vibrating mesh nebulizer. These droplets could then be inhaled by the mice confined within the mouse-holding chambers, aided by the flow of oxy-hydrogen gas at a rate of 0.5 liter/min (hydrogen-to-oxygen volume ratio of 2:1). In this setup, the cotton within the chamber was presoaked, and a hole was drilled on the opposite side of the chamber to connect with the atmosphere. With the oxyhydrogen supply system providing a 2:1 hydrogen-to-oxygen volume ratio, the molar concentration of hydrogen in our system, maintained at 1 atmosphere pressure, was 66.7%, while oxygen was at 33.3%. The nebulization time and output efficiency were calculated according to the following equationThe output efficiency (%)=Mass of NP actually nebulized Mass of NP before nebulization(1)
In addition, the uniformity of NP distribution within the nebulization device was assessed. To achieve this, filter papers were tightly secured to the mouse-holding chambers, and NPs encapsulating Cy5.5 siRNA were introduced into the nebulizer. Subsequently, fluorescent images of each filter paper were captured using an IVIS system (PerkinElmer, USA).

## Characterization of HNPs/siRNA before and after nebulization
The diameters and zeta potentials of LNPs and HNPs/siRNA before and after nebulization were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK). TEM (JEM-2100F, Tokyo, Japan) and SEM (S-4700, Hitachi, Japan) were used to observe the morphology of the NPs and microsized vesicles, including TABs and TABMs, respectively. Western blotting analysis and Coomassie blue staining were conducted to identify the protein profiles of TABs, TABMs, and HNPs/siRNA. The EE of siRNA was determined using a Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the instructions. A gel retardation assay was also used to investigate siRNA EE and the protective effect of siRNA-loaded NPs. In brief, free siRNA, LNPs/siRNA, HNPs/siRNA were mixed with RNase (10 μg/ml) or 1% Triton X-100 at 37°C for 15 min. The resulting samples were subjected to electrophoresis on GelRed-infused 3% agarose gels and photographed under ultraviolet illumination. To assess the stability of siRNA-loaded NPs, freshly prepared NPs were stored in PBS containing 10% FBS (pH 7.4 or 6.8) ( ) at 37°C for 24, 48, or 72 hours, respectively. Changes in particle size were measured using DLS (Malvern, UK).
The release profile of siRNA from NP formulations was evaluated in simulated physical (pH 7.4 PBS) and endosomal (pH 5.0 PBS) environments at 37°C ( ). NPs encapsulating Cy5.5 siRNA were placed inside dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 100 kDa (Millipore, Germany) for release. At predetermined time intervals, samples were collected, and fresh buffer solution was replenished. The released siRNA was quantified using a microplate reader (excitation at 683 nm, emission at 703 nm; Perkin-Elmer, USA).

## Cellular uptake study
To evaluate the selective targeting effect of HNPs, RAW 264.7, THP-1, A549, and HFL-1 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well, respectively. Subsequently, the cells were treated with LNPs or HNPs loaded with Cy5.5-siRNA at a final concentration of 100 nM in a serum-free medium. After a 2-hour incubation, the cells were washed with cold PBS, collected, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). In addition, the cells were seed in confocal dishes (2 × 105 cells per well) and treated as described above. Following a 2-hour incubation with NPs loaded with Cy5.5-siRNA, the cells were fixed, stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and imaged using a confocal microscope (CLSM; Leica SP8, Germany).
To explore the nebulization process on NP uptake behavior, HNPs were nebulized onto the seeded cells using a small nebulizer unit, while being exposed to a hydrogen flow. This was followed by a 2-hour incubation period. Another procedure, including the investigation of cellular uptake mechanisms, intracellular distribution, and in vitro silencing efficiency, was conducted following the aforementioned method.

## Cellular uptake mechanism and intracellular distribution
Initially, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (1 × 104 cells per well) and incubated overnight, then treated with LNPs or HNPs loaded with siRNA. After a 24-hour incubation, a CCK8 assay was performed to assess cell viability. In addition, RAW 264.7 cells were plated in six-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well) and treated with various inhibitors including amiloride (2.5 mM), CPZ (30 μM) and filipin (5 μg/ml). Following a 1-hour incubation, the yielded Cy5.5-loaded NPs were exposed to the cells for an additional 2 hours. The cells were then washed three times with cold PBS solution and collected for flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA).
Confocal microscopy (CLSM; Leica SP8, Germany) was used to explore the intracellular distribution of the NPs. RAW 264.7 cells were seed in confocal dishes (2 × 105 cells per well) and incubated with NPs loaded with Cy5.5-siRNA for 1, 2, and 6 hours, respectively. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with cold PBS, stained with LysoTracker (Beyotime, China) for 30 min, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Before fluorescence imaging, the nucleus was stained with DAPI. Pearson’s coefficient was determined using ImageJ software.

## Silencing efficiency of TGFβ-siRNA
Murine BMDMs were seeded into 6-well tissue culture plates (2 × 105 cells per well) and treated with IL-4 (20 ng/ml) for 24 hours to induce differentiation into M2 macrophages ( ). Next, the NPs loaded with siRNA at a final concentration of 100 nM were exposed to the cells for an additional 6 hours. Total RNA was extracted from cell lysates, and quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to measure the TGFβ mRNA level. Moreover, the culture medium was collected to assess TGFβ1 protein expression in the M2 macrophages using ELISA kits from BioLegend, US.

## In vitro transport behavior through native Muc–based cross-linked hydrogel
An in vitro mucus model was established using native Muc–based cross-linked hydrogels ( ). To mimic the chemical environment of native mucus, a PVA hydrogel containing 5 wt % Muc (Muc/PVA) was synthesized using freezing-thawing method ( , ). In brief, a 10 wt % Muc dispersion was prepared in ultrapure water by magnetic stirring for 2 hours at room temperature. In addition, a 20 wt % PVA solution was prepared by adding PVA to water and stirring constantly at 80°C for 12 hours. To prepare the Muc/PVA hydrogels, the 20 wt % PVA solution was combined with the Muc dispersion in a 1:1 weight ratio and stirred for an additional 4 hours. Subsequently, 1 ml of the mixture was dispensed into the wells of 24-well plates, covered with a glass plate, and subjected to freezing at −20°C for 18 hours followed by thawing at room temperature (25°C) for 6 hours in three successive cycles. A gravimetric swelling test was conducted on Muc/PVA hydrogels to assess their water content. The hydrogels were immersed in water and allowed to reach equilibrium swelling at 37°C for 24 hours. Subsequently, excess water was removed, and their wet weight (Ms) was noted. After overnight freeze-drying, their weight (Md) was recorded, and the equilibrium mass-swelling ratio (QM) was calculated as the ratio of the mass of the swollen hydrogel to the dry samples. To visualize the porous structure of the Muc hydrogels, freeze-dried samples were sectioned with a razor blade, placed on double-sided carbon tape, and observed using SEM (S-4700, Hitachi, Japan).
For particle penetration studies in vitro, 300 μl of LNPs or HNPs loaded with Cy5.5 siRNA were introduced into the Muc/PVA hydrogels. After a 2-hour incubation, the supernatant above the hydrogel was carefully removed, and the hydrogel surface was washed to ensure complete retrieval of particles that did not penetrate the hydrogel. The Cy5.5 fluorescence in the water aliquots was measured using a microplate reader (Perkin-Elmer, USA) with excitation at 683 nm and emission at 703 nm. The permeated NPs through artificial mucus were calculated based on the difference between the initial amount of Cy5.5-loaded NPs and the amount detected in the supernatant. Particle-free hydrogels exposed to the water column were used as controls and processed following the same protocol. To investigate the impact of hydrogen supplementation on NP interaction with the mucus barrier, HNPs were nebulized onto the Muc/PVA hydrogel using a nebulizer unit, while being exposed to a hydrogen flow. This was followed by a 2-hour incubation period. For comparison, the permeation of aerosolized HNPs through the mucus was further investigated under compressed air flow (AP-100; Yuyanbio, China) instead of hydrogen.

## Isothermal titration calorimeter characterization
The interaction between Muc and NPs was analyzed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical, USA). The experimental procedure involved injecting 50 μl of artificial mucus solution (0.004 mM) into 300 μl of NP solution over a series of injections ( ). The interval between injections was 200 s and stirring at 500 rpm was maintained to ensure proper mixing after each injection. The temperature was set at 25°C for the experiment. All the data obtained from the ITC analysis were processed using the NanoAnalyze Data Analysis software, with the first injection being disregarded for analysis.
Artificial CF mucus was prepared using a previously reported method ( ). In brief, 25 μl of sterile egg yolk emulsion, 25 mg of Muc (type II, from porcine stomach), 20 mg of DNA, 30 μl of aqueous DTPA (1 mg/ml), 25 mg NaCl, and 11 mg KCl were added to 5 ml of water, and the dispersion was stirred until achieving a homogenous mixture. To evaluate the impact of hydrogen supplementation on the interaction between NPs and mucus, a rubber hose linked to an oxyhydrogen supply system (AMS-H-03; Shanghai Asclepius Meditec Co. Ltd. China) was inserted into the CF suspension (purging time 10 min) with gentle vortexing before the titration experiment. The pressure was maintained at 1 atmosphere pressure, and after the 10-min purging period, the natural gas in the CF suspension was replaced with electrolysis oxyhydrogen gas. For comparison, the mucus solution underwent treatment with compressed air, provided by the AP-100 Rodent Anesthesia Machine (Yuyanbio, China), for the same purging duration. The flow rates of oxy-hydrogen gas and compressed air were both set at 0.5 liter/min.

## Mouse models of PF
Male C57BL/6 mice, 6 to 8 weeks old (Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Macau) were used in all experiments. All experiments were performed under approved by The Animal Ethics Committee of University of Macau (ethical request no. UMARE-010-2021). A mouse model of PF was established by administering a single intratracheal installation of bleomycin sulfate (1.5 U/kg) to the mice ( ). The in vivo study was conducted with two objectives: first, to confirm the fate of inhaled nano-formulations in vivo, and second, to assess their therapeutic effect. To evaluate the in vivo fate of inhaled nano-formulations, NPs loaded with Cy5.5 siRNA were prepared as described previously. To investigate the therapeutic effect on PF, mice were subjected to treatment every 3 days, starting from day 9 to day 18 after the bleomycin insult. The treatments composed of PBS, LNPs/siRNA, HNPs/siRNA, hydro HNPs, and air HNPs. To further investigate the effect of formulations on reversing PF, mice were subjected to treatment every 3 days, starting from day 15 to day 18 after the bleomycin insult. The treatments composed of PBS, hydro HNPs, and air HNPs. A siRNA dose of 0.5 mg/kg was administered in all treatments. To assess the efficacy of our custom-designed aerosol inhalation device in enhancing the deposition efficiency of nebulized siRNA-loaded NPs in the lungs, 600 μl of HNPs was added to the nebulizer unit. The nebulizer unit was connected to the inhalation chamber equipped with six mouse-holding chambers and exposed to a flow of oxy-hydrogen gas at 0.5 liter/min (hydrogen-to-oxygen volume ratio of 2:1).

## In vivo transmucosal penetration assay
The mice induced with bleomycin were euthanized at 0.5 hours after inhaling Cy5.5-labeled NPs, and tracheal tissues and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were collected, respectively. Following collection, the BAL samples underwent centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min to separate the cells and supernatants. To evaluate the nebulization efficiency of NPs, the fluorescence of the labeled NPs in the supernatant was quantified using a microplate reader (excitation at 683 nm, emission at 703 nm; Perkin-Elmer, USA). The nebulization efficiency was then calculated by determining the difference between the fluorescence of the HNPs added to the nebulizer and the fluorescence detected in the supernatant. To perform MPT in mucus, the tracheas of mice were longitudinally cut and placed flat on a microscope coverslip to expose the mucosal surfaces ( ). NP trajectories were recorded for a duration of 20 s at a frame rate of 15 frames per second using a fluorescence confocal microscope (Nikon A1MP+, Japan) equipped with a Photometrics Evolve EMCCD 512 camera and ImageJ/MTrack2 software. The ensemble-averaged MSD values of individual particles at a timescale of 1 s were then determined using a previously developed formula ( ). Furthermore, to determine the distribution of NPs in the large airways, the tracheal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then frozen and sliced for fluorescent staining with cell nucleus (DAPI). The stained slices were observed using a fluorescent microscope (DMi8; Leica, Germany).

## Biodistribution study
The mice induced with bleomycin were euthanized at 2 or 72 hours postadministration to examine the distribution of inhaled Cy5.5-labeled NPs and NP retention, respectively. For biodistribution analysis, frozen lung slices were subjected to immunofluorescent staining using antibodies against F4/80 or prosurfactant protein C. Subsequently, the slices were stained with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit antibody and a nuclear stain (DAPI), respectively. The fluorescent microscope (DMi8; Leica, Germany) was employed to observe the localization of NPs within the lungs. To further validate the distribution of NPs among different cell subtypes, the lungs were perfused with cold PBS and processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis (BD Biosciences, USA). To assess lung retention, fluorescence images of lung tissues were captured using an IVIS system (PerkinElmer, USA).

## Flow cytometry analysis
At 21 days postbleomycin induction, lung tissues from groups treated with saline, bleomycin + PBS, and various therapeutic regimens were harvested. Subsequently, the collected lung tissues were enzymatically digested ( ) by incubating them with 2 ml of RPMI medium containing collagenase D (0.625 mg/ml), collagenase I (1.5 mg/ml), and deoxyribonuclease (30 U/ml) for 45 min at 37°C in a shaking incubator. The digested suspension was then filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, treated with red blood lysis buffer, and washed with fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer containing PBS with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Subsequently, the samples were filtered through a 40-μm strainer, counted, and stained with antibodies for flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA). All data were analyzed using FlowJo version 10 software.

## Cytokine assay
At 21 days postbleomycin induction, BAL samples were collected from each experimental group by washing the lungs three times with 0.8 ml of cold PBS. The total protein content, TGFβ1, IL-1β, and HGF in the recovered fluids were measured using ELISA kits (BioLegend, US) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Hydroxyproline assay
At 21 days postbleomycin induction, lung tissues were harvested from each experimental group. Lung tissue samples weighing between 30 and 100 mg were precisely weighed and hydrolyzed for the determination of hydroxyproline content using an alkaline hydrolysis kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Histological and immunohistochemical analysis
The body weight of mice was measured individually every 2 days, and the survival curve was recorded throughout the experimental period. For histological analysis of lungs from naive, fibrotic, and treated mice, half of the lungs were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned. Subsequently, these sections were stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome, respectively. Two researchers independently assessed interstitial fibrosis severity in each field using the Ashcroft scoring system ( ) in a blinded approach. Lung fibrosis scores ranging from 0 to 8 were defined as follows: 0 (normal), 1 (minimal fibrous thickening of alveolar or bronchiolar walls), 2-3 (moderate thickening without apparent lung architectural damage), 4-5 (increased fibrosis with evident structural harm and fibrous bands/small masses), 6-7 (severe structural distortion and extensive fibrous areas), and 8 (complete fibrous obliteration of the field). Each lung section was individually scored from 0 to 8, and these scores were summed to derive the total disease score.
For immunofluorescence staining, the remaining lungs were dehydrated using a sucrose gradient sedimentation method, embedded in OCT compound, and cryosectioned. The frozen lung slices were incubated with antibodies against F4/80, TGFβ1, or COL1A1, and then stained with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit antibody or Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated anti-mouse antibody, respectively. The cell nucleus was stained with DAPI. Last, the stained slices were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8; Leica, Germany), and the quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

## Quantitative RT-PCR analysis
Total RNA extraction was performed by homogenizing snap-frozen lung tissues or cultured cells in ice-cold TRIzol reagent. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (TaKaRa, Japan), and the relative expression of the target mRNA was normalized to GAPDH. Fold changes of target mRNAs were calculated using the Ct method (2−ΔΔCt). The quantitative PCR primers of each target gene were as follows: HGF (5′-GTCCTGAAGGCTC-AGACTTGGT-3′ and 5′-CCAGCCGTAAATACTGCAAGTGG-3′), TGFβ1 (5′-CTGCTGACCCCCACTGATAC-3′ and 5′-GGGGCTG-ATCCCGTTGATT-3′), CD206 (5′-TCTTTGCCTTTCCCAGTCT-CC-3′ and 5′-TGACACCCAGCGGAATT-TC-3′), IL-1β (5′-TGGA-CCTTCCAGGATGAGGACA-3′ and 5′-GTTCATCTCGGAGCCT-GTAGTG-3′), and Arg1 (5′-GAACACGGCAGTGGCTTTAAC-3′ and 5′-TGCTTAGCTCTGTCTGCTTTGC-3′).

## Western blotting analysis
Snap-frozen lung tissues or harvested TABs were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Total protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay protocol (Beyotime, China). The equal volumes of the lysates were loaded onto SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for separation and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Following blocking with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated with specific antibodies against cleaved Caspase-3, CD3, CD44, fibronectin, α-SMA, or COL1A1, and then subjected to antibody staining. Last, the target bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

## Micro-CT imaging
Each mouse was positioned in a prone position under anesthesia and subjected to scanning using Sedecal SuperArgus Micro-CT equipment. The micro-CT had a field of view measuring 24 mm by 24 mm, and the following parameters were used: 90 kV, 160 μA, and a spatial resolution of 0.11 mm. The acquired images were reconstructed and evaluated using a constant window width/window level of 5000/2000. Random selection of lung lobes was performed for each mouse.

## Statistics
Statistical tests were performed using Prism 9.0.2 software (GraphPad Software). The results were displayed as means ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed as indicated in figure legends, and survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test. Differences detection index between the treated groups and control groups were determined via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If a significant effect was determined by ANOVA, a Tukey post hoc test for individual group comparisons was performed. Significance was defined as a P < 0.05.
