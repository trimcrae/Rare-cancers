## Abstract

## Background
Acetaminophen (APAP) overdose is a leading cause of drug-induced liver injury, with limited treatment options. Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a central role in the pathogenesis of APAP-induced liver injury (AILI). Mitochondrial damage induces cell death, liver necrosis, and sever inflammation, leading to irreversible liver failure. Therefore, it is urgent to develop alternative treatments. Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based cell therapy recently has received increasing attention for its role in regulating mitochondrial function.

## Objective
This study aimed to investigate the regulation of mitochondrial function and the therapeutic potential of MSCs in treating AILI and underlying mechanisms.

## Methods
AILI mouse model was established by injection of overdosed APAP. Mice were treated with adipose-derived MSCs (AMSCs) via the tail vein. The blood and liver tissues were collected for assessment of liver necrosis and mitochondrial function. To further verify the mechanism, hepatocyte-specific DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4) knockout (Ddit4△Hep) mice were generated.

## Results
We showed that AMSCs treatment significantly reduced liver necrosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction in AILI. DDIT4 expression in hepatocytes was identified as playing an important part in AMSCs treating AILI by using RNA-Seq and histological analysis. DDIT4 upregulation in liver samples of AILI patients characterized by snRNA-Seq and spatial transcriptomics analysis, which indicates DDIT4 plays a role in AILI. Ddit4△Hep mice exhibited exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction and liver damage to APAP overdose, and these changes could not be reversed by AMSCs treatment. Mechanistically, AMSCs induced mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy through DDIT4-mediated PGC-1α upregulation, consequently leading to the restoration of mitochondrial mass and function in AILI. Finally, inhibition of PGC-1α abolished the protective effects of AMSCs against APAP-induced mitochondrial damage.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study indicates the potential role of stem cell therapy in modulating mitochondrial function and highlights the role of activation the DDIT4/PGC-1α pathway in protecting hepatic mitochondria to alleviate AILI. This study provides a new mechanistic perspective for stem cell therapy in the treatment of AILI and potential targets for clinical drug development.

## Introduction
Acetaminophen (APAP) overdose is the most frequent cause of drug-induced acute liver failure in developed countries. It is safe and effective at recommended doses, while an overdose can result in hepatotoxicity or even liver failure. APAP overdose promotes the formation of the reactive metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinoneimide (NAPQI) and depletes glutathione (GSH), leading to oxidative stress, DNA damage, and liver necrosis [ ]. N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a known antioxidant, is currently the sole available treatment option for APAP-induced liver damage (AILI); nevertheless, this medication has drawbacks, such as side effects and a limited therapeutic window [ ]. Liver transplantation will be the only choice for improving patient survival if the most treatable stage of AILI is missed. Hence, the development of effective novel treatments for AILI is critical.
Mitochondrial dysfunction is a major event in the pathophysiology of AILI [ ]. Many studies have shown that enhancing mitochondrial function can protect against AILI [ – ]. Mitophagy is a crucial part of mitochondrial quality control. It serves as an effective pathway for selectively eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria [ ]. Mitochondrial biogenesis is another mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis by producing new mitochondria [ ]. Mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis jointly increase mitochondrial turnover and renewal [ ]. Overdosed APAP causes zonated liver damage, with hepatocytes showing active autophagy around centrilobular necrosis and mitochondrial spheroids zones. Mitochondrial biogenesis was increased in the hepatocytes adjacent to this region [ ]. This phenomenon suggests that both mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis are crucial for the recovery from liver injury.
DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4) is an injury-induced protein that inhibits the cellular energy hub mTORC1 by blocking the interaction of 14-3-3 proteins and the TSC1/2 complex. This inactivates the small GTPase protein Rheb, which is responsible for activating mTORC1 [ – ]. Notably, DDIT4 is involved in various biological processes, including oxidative stress, autophagy, and metabolism [ ]. Increasing evidence suggested that DDIT4 has an impact on regulating mitochondrial function [ – ]. Studies also showed that induction of DDIT4 could prevent oxidative stress and lipotoxicity-mediated injury in hepatocytes [ , ].
Cell therapy has emerged as a promising way to treat AILI. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) play multiple roles in treating liver diseases such as promoting regeneration [ ], paracrine [ ], and immunomodulatory effects [ ]. In the previous study, we found that the use of adipose-derived MSCs (AMSCs) could effectively ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction in AILI [ ]. The potential of MSCs to modulate mitochondrial function is being investigated in the treatment of various diseases. Identifying the unique molecular mechanisms in target cells and organs by MSCs can provide clinical therapeutic targets and establish a basis for developing alternative treatments based on cell therapy.
This study demonstrated that activating DDIT4/PGC1α pathway protects against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity through enhancing mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis. Cell therapy by infusion MSCs can significantly alleviate AILI in mice by modulating mitochondria function through the DDIT4/PGC1α pathway and therefore propose a novel therapeutic target for treating AILI.

## Materials and methods

## Animal
Five- to seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were procured from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Mice had free access to food and drink and were kept in an environmentally controlled room with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The experiment was conducted at the institutional animal care and use committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Ddit4fl/fl mice were kindly gifted by Prof. Zhi Zeng. Alb-cre mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Ddit4fl/fl mice were crossed with Alb-cre mice to generate the hepatocyte-specific DDIT4 knockout (Ddit4△Hep) mice, which was verified by the expression of DDIT4 in the primary hepatocyte (Fig. S1A).

## Experimental design
This work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Commission of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2023 − 702). Mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups. For intraperitoneal injections (i.p.) of APAP, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (RWD, Shenzhen, China) via inhalation. Mice were given 300 mg/kg APAP (Cayman, Michigan, USA) to induce liver injury or saline for control following an overnight fast. AMSCs (1 × 106) were intravenously (i.v.) injected into the lateral tail veins immediately after APAP administration (5 mice/group, total 15 mice per experiment). To clarify the role of PGC-1α, SR-18292 (HY-101491) and ZLN005 (HY-17538) were acquired from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). SR-18292 (45 mg/kg) and ZLN005 (12 mg/kg) were dissolved in a vehicle (10% DMSO + 10% Tween-80 in saline) and injected i.p. once a day for three days before APAP administration, while the control group was injected with a vehicle (5 mice/group, total 25 mice per experiment). Mice were euthanized with isoflurane 6 and 24 h after APAP overdose. Liver and blood samples were collected at the indicated time points for further examination.

## Isolating of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells
As we previously reported, stem cells were extracted from the inguinal adipose tissues of mice [ ]. Following digestion with collagenase type I (Stemcell, Vancouver, BC, Canada) and washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA), cells were grown in the MesenCult™ Expansion Medium (Stemcell, Vancouver, Canada). AMSCs at passage 3 were used in all experiments.

## Cell culture and treatment
The AML12 cell line was cultured at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 40 ng/mL dexamethasone, 5.5 µg/mL transferrin, 10 µg/mL insulin, 5 ng/mL selenium and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Considering that DDIT4 is involved in the insulin pathway [ ] and dexamethasone can induce DDIT4 expression [ ], the culture medium of AML12 was removed with insulin and dexamethasone 16 h before the cell treatment [ ]. AML12 cells with Ddit4 deletion were generated by the CRISPR/Cas9 technology. SgRNA targeting Ddit4 (5′-GCAGGAGTTCCTTGCCCACC-3′) was cloned into the plasmid LentiCRISPRv2GFP (Plasmid #82416, Addgene, MA, USA). AML12 cells were transfected with the lentivirus and single clones were picked. For overexpression of DDIT4 in AML12 cells, Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) was used to transfect the m-Ddit4 plasmid (Hanbio Tech, Shanghai, China) into cells. For specific gene knockdown, small interfering RNA (siRNA) were transfected into cells by using RNAiMax (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). The sequences of siRNA are presented in Table S1. SR-18292 (HY-101491), Mdivi-1(HY-15886), SBI-0206965 (HY-16966), rapamycin (HY-10219) and chloroquine (HY-17589 A) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). To reduce PGC-1α or ULK1 activity, the cells were pretreated with SR-18292 (20 µM) or SBI-0206965 (10 µM) for 1 h. Then they were treated with 5 mM APAP for 24 h. To inhibit mitophagy, cells were pretreated with Mdivi-1(10 µM) for 1 h. To study autophagic flux, cells were exposed to rapamycin (Rapa, 100 nM) and chloroquine (CQ, 20 µM) for 4 h.

## Co-culture assay
AMSCs and hepatocytes (AML12 cells or primary mouse hepatocytes) were cocultured using an indirect Transwell system (Corning, NY, USA). Hepatocytes (8 × 105/well) were cultured in the bottom chamber of the plate, and AMSCs (4 × 105/well) were cultured in the upper chamber. Overnight, the upper chamber containing AMSCs was placed on the hepatocytes, and 5 mM APAP was added to the co-culture system. An upper chamber without AMSCs was placed on the hepatocytes as a control, and the two cells were co-cultured for 24 h. Then, hepatocytes in the bottom chamber were collected for western blot or OCR analyses as described subsequently.

## Isolation of primary hepatocytes
Primary mouse hepatocytes were obtained using a modified version of a two-step collagenase perfusion technique described by Seglen [ ]. Mouse liver was perfused with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) without Ca2+ & Mg2+ and containing 0.5 mM EGTA (Sigma, MO, USA) for 5 min through the portal vein. Then Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) containing 100 U/ml collagenase type Ⅳ (Sigma, MO, USA) and 15 mM HEPES (Shanghai Shenggong Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was utilized to perfuse the liver for an additional five minutes. Cut out the liver and gently tear the liver capsule in a culture dish containing DMEM. Then collected the cell suspension after filtering the digested tissue by a 70 µM strainer. After centrifuging the cell suspension at 50×g for 3 min, collect cell pellet and purify with 40% percoll (Cytiva, MA, USA) to increase the percentage of viable hepatocytes. Mouse primary hepatocytes were cultured in DMEM solution containing 10% FBS at 37℃ on collagen Ⅰ-coated plates. The next day, cells were used for the following experiments. Primary hepatocytes were harvested at the indicated time points, and whole cell proteins and RNA were isolated.

## Histological and biochemical analysis
Formalin-fixed mouse tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and cut at 5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) in order to assess the necrotic areas. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis carried out in the liver sections was detected using the primary antibodies: a 3-Nitrotyrosine antibody (1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), a DDIT4 antibody (1:100, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and a PGC-1α antibody (1:100). An automated biochemical analysis instrument (Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences, Shenzhen, China) was used to measure the serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) levels. The levels of GSH and malondialdehyde (MDA) in liver samples were detected using reagent kits following the manufacturer’s directions (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

## Isolation of mitochondria from liver samples
Isolated mitochondria were obtained from fresh liver tissues of mice by using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) as we previously described [ ]. The obtained mitochondrial pellet was extracted and used for WB detection. To preserve mitochondria activity for JC-1 and OCR detection, the remaining part of the mitochondria was stored in Mitochondrial Storage Solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

## Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) analysis
The mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to detect the altered liver ΔΨm. Briefly, 100 µl of fresh mitochondria extracted from liver tissues were mixed with 900 µl staining working solution and then the fluorescence intensity was assessed using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) assay
After cutting liver samples into 1 mm3 blocks, 2.5% glutaraldehyde/phosphate buffer was utilized for a 2 h fixing. Then using 1% osmium tetroxide for the secondary fixation. After the samples were dehydrated, embedded, and stained, images were acquired using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700, Tokyo, Japan). The autophagosomes and mitophagosomes of hepatocytes were quantified in at least 10 high-power fields in each sample.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Frozen liver tissue slides were thawed at room temperature and then stained with dihydroethidium (DHE) solution (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then slides were counterstained for 10 min using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Cells were grown on 12-mm coverslips, rinsed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, rinsed again with PBS, and incubated with 0.1% Triton for 10 min. Cells were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h and then incubated with anti-PGC-1α (1:100, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) antibody at 4 ℃ overnight. Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody was used at 1:500 dilution. DAPI was used to stain the cell nucleus. Images were generated with a confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Mitophagy assay with mito-Keima reporter
Keima with mitochondrial localization signaling (Mito-Keima) acts as a pH-sensitive fluorescent biosensor [ ]. The adenoviruses encoding Mito-Keima were obtained from Hanbio Tech (Shanghai, China). According to the instructions, AML12 cells were transfected with mito-Keima at the indicated MOI. Images were acquired using a confocal microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Measurement of mt-Keima was performed by a dual-excitation at 440 nm (in a neutral environment) and 550 nm (in an acidic environment) lasers and detection through a 620 nm filter. The fluorescence intensity ratio of Keima (550/440 nm) was assessed as an indicator of mitophagy activity.

## Western blot (WB) analysis
Proteins extracted from liver tissues and cell lysates were used for western blot analysis. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) was used to determine protein concentration. Using 4–20% Bis-Tris MOPS gels to separate denatured proteins by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Then PVDF membranes were treated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The protein expression levels were normalized using anti-GAPDH and anti-β-ACTIN antibodies. The antibodies used in this study are shown in Table S2.

## Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis
Basal OCR was evaluated by the Extracellular OCR Plate Assay Kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) to determine the mitochondrial respiration of liver tissues and cells in the absence of any inhibitors. Briefly, AML12 cells were pretreated according to the experimental purpose. Resuspend the cells in working solution containing oxygen probe (2.5 × 106 cells/ml) and seed 100 µL per well in a black clear-bottom 96-well plate. For the detection of OCR of purified mitochondria from fresh liver tissues, an appropriate amount of mitochondria was resuspended in the mitochondrial storage solution containing the oxygen probe and seeded into a 96-well plate at 100 µL per well. The protein concentration of the mitochondria was measured using the BCA method. After adding cells and mitochondria into the well plate, mineral oil was applied dropwise. The fluorescence intensity was recorded at 10-min intervals for 200 min (excitation and emission wavelengths of 500 and 650 nm, respectively) using a microplate reader. The OCR results were analyzed using the kinetic calculating profiles obtained from the manufacturer.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
Using Trizol to extract the total RNA from liver tissues and cells. The reverse transcription and qPCR reactions were performed by using PrimeScript RT reagent and SYBR Premix Ex Taq kits (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The amount of mRNA was determined based on the ΔΔCt method and normalized by Actb. The expression of Ddit4, Cyp2e1, and PGC-1α downstream targets (Nrf1 and Tfam) and genes involved in autophagy (Map1lc3b and Atg5) were measured by qPCR analysis. The primer sequences are shown in Table S3.

## mtDNA/nDNA measurement
Total DNA from liver samples and cells was isolated using the Cell/Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Bioteke, Beijing, China). The relative mtDNA content (mtDNA/nDNA) was determined as the ratio of the amplifications of the mtDNA gene (mMito) relative to a single copy nuclear gene (B2m) by real-time PCR using 10 ng of total DNA, as previously described [ ].

## RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and analysis
The Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer system was used to assess the RNA integrity after total RNA extraction. Libraries of amplified RNA for each sample were prepared following the Illumina protocol. Then constructing cDNA library and sequencing were carried out according to the procedures by Novogene Co., Ltd. DESeq2 R package (1.20.0) was used to identified differential expression between the two groups (three biological replicates per group). Genes with padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2 (foldchange)| ≥ 1 were classified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) is used to determine if predefined gene sets show notable variations between two biological states. This study employed hallmark gene sets and used GSEA analysis program 4.3.3. The volcano plot was generated using TBtools (version 2.083) [ ]. The RNA-Seq datasets used in this study were previously published [ ] and are available through NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) Series accession number GSE218879.

## Single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and Spatial transcriptomics analysis
The human single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNAseq) data were acquired from the GSE223581. This dataset consisted of liver samples from healthy (n = 9) controls and APAP-induced acute liver failure patients (n = 11). The snRNA-seq data was processed following standard Seurat methodology (version 4.3.0.1) [ ] and samples were integrated using Harmony (version 0.1.1) [ ]. Cell clusters were identified using a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization based clustering algorithm in Seurat. The FeaturePlot of DDIT4 relative expression was optimized by ggplot2 (version 3.4.3). The publicly available spatial transcriptomic was acquired from GSE223559 at Liver Regeneration Atlas (https://shiny.igc.ed.ac.uk/7efa5350ba94425388e47ea7cdd5aa64/) [ ].

## Statistical analysis
The data are expressed as means ± SD. The differences between each group were compared using Student’s t-tests and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. For statistical analysis, GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was used. The graphical abstract and schematic diagram were created with BioGDP.com [ ].

## Results

## AMSCs alleviate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity
As shown in the H&E stain assessments, the centrilobular hepatic necrosis induced by APAP administration was reduced after AMSCs treatment. Similarly, the serum ALT and AST levels were markedly reduced after AMSCs treatment, which are released after hepatocyte necrosis (Fig. A). Overdosed APAP depletes cellular GSH and is converted to the toxic metabolite NAPQI to induce oxidative stress [ ]. We found hepatic GSH content was elevated in AMSCs-treated mice (Fig. B). The MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation, was significantly reduced after AMSCs treatment (Fig. C). IHC results showed that 3-Nitrotyrosine, a marker of protein peroxidation, was reduced after AMSCs treatment (Fig. D). NAPQI targets hepatocyte mitochondrial proteins for adduct formation, causing mitochondrial dysfunction which plays a central role in AILI [ ]. In order to detect the effect of AMSCs therapy on mitochondrial function, we selected the time point (6 h) when mitochondrial damage is significant after APAP injection for the experiment [ ]. We isolated mitochondria from liver tissues and used the JC-1 dye to assess ΔΨm. It showed AMSCs infusion could significantly increase the impaired ΔΨm caused by APAP (Fig. E). DHE staining showed that reactive oxygen species (ROS) in APAP-intoxicated liver was alleviated by AMSCs treatment (Fig. F). Therefore, reduction of oxidative stress and restoration of mitochondrial function in AILI can be achieved by AMSCs treatment.
 

## AMSC-based cell therapy alleviate APAP-induced hepatotoxicity by enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy
In the previous study, we found that AMSCs treatment can improve mitochondrial function by reducing JNK activation, but the underlying mechanism is unclear [ ]. GSEA was performed to identify enriched gene sets in RNA-seq data after AMSCs treatment in AILI liver tissues. The oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway, a main function of mitochondria to generate cellular energy, was markedly upregulated in the AMSCs-treated group (Fig. A). The WB results showed that AMSCs treatment exhibited a restoration in liver OXPHOS subunits expression that was impaired by overdosed APAP (Fig. B). During OXPHOS, mitochondria consume oxygen to produce ATP [ ]. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of isolated mitochondria from AMSCs-treated mice was also improved (Fig. C). In order to explore how AMSCs improve mitochondrial function, we studied the effects of AMSCs treatment on mitochondrial biogenesis. The results showed that AMSCs not only increased mitochondrial DNA content and the mitochondrial mass illustrated by TOM20 expression but also upregulated the expression of mitochondrial biogenesis-related proteins (PGC-1α, TFAM, and NRF1) (Fig. D and E). Removal of damaged mitochondria by mitophagy protects against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity and coordinates mitochondrial homeostasis with mitochondrial biogenesis [ ]. The PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is triggered when PINK1 is stabilized on the outer membrane of damaged mitochondria and recruites Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, followed by ubiquitination of mitochondrial proteins, which are then recognized by autophagy receptors [ ]. Then, these complexes bind to LC3 to form the autophagosome and are degraded [ ]. AMSCs treatment significantly induced the conversion of LC3BI to LC3BII while reduced the expression of the autophagy adapter P62, indicating promotion of autophagy in the AILI liver. We further found that AMSCs treatment could promote Parkin and PINK1 mitochondrial translocation to trigger mitophagy. LC3B, a protein specifically localized in autophagic structures, was increased in the liver mitochondrial fraction after AMSCs treatment compared with the AILI group. This also indicated that mitophagy was enhanced in the AMSCs-treated group (Fig. F). The TEM analysis of liver tissues revealed that mitophagosomes were increased after AMSCs administration (Fig. G). Therefore, AMSCs treatment can promote mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy, thereby increasing OXPHOS and restoring mitochondrial function.
 

## DDIT4 is involved in the protective modulation of AMSCs treating APAP-induced hepatotoxicity
Next, we explored the potential mechanisms of how AMSCs treatment affects mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy. Bulk RNA-Seq was used to identify DEGs in mouse liver tissues after AMSCs treatment. We discovered that Ddit4 was markedly upregulated in AMSCs-treated mice (Fig. A). Recent studies have highlighted the role of DDIT4 in promoting autophagy and mitochondrial function [ , , ]. A publicly available single-nuclei RNA-Seq (snRNAseq) data set (GSE223581) containing healthy human liver samples (n = 9) and AILI liver samples (n = 11) was used to identify the expression of DDIT4 in AILI human liver samples [ ]. After integration and subclustering of the AILI and healthy human hepatocytes, we identified eight clusters (Fig. B, left panel). Then we analyzed the expression of DDIT4 in healthy and AILI liver samples and found that cluster 0 and cluster 6, which were enriched in AILI livers, showed higher DDIT4 expression (Fig. B, right panel). Publicly available spatial transcriptomics of human liver tissue (GSE223559) also confirmed that DDIT4 was upregulated in hepatocytes surrounding central veins and necrotic areas in AILI human liver (Fig. C). These results may indicate that DDIT4 plays an important role in AILI. Then we used IHC to detect the expression and distribution of DDIT4 in mouse liver tissues. Under normal conditions, DDIT4 is expressed at a low level. Consistent with human AILI, DDIT4 was upregulated in APAP-challenged mice, and AMSCs treatment could further upregulate the expression of DDIT4 in hepatocytes around the central vein (Fig. D). This result was also verified by WB and qPCR (Fig. E). Overdosed APAP produces a centrilobular hepatic necrosis and induces adaptive changes such as autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis at the peri-necrotic area [ ]. Therefore, we speculate that DDIT4 plays a role in AMSCs protecting against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity.
 Co-culture experiments showed that AMSCs can significantly reduce JNK activation (mitochondrial stress) and the expression of γH2AX (DNA damage) caused by APAP toxicity and increase the expression of DDIT4 in AML12 cells and primary hepatocytes (Fig.  F). This suggested that AMSCs could also increase DDIT4 expression and reduce APAP-induced liver injury in vitro. To explore the role of DDIT4 in vivo, we generated hepatocyte-specific Ddit4-knockout mice (Ddit4△Hep) by crossing floxed mice (Ddit4fl/fl) with Alb-cre mice. Ddit4 deficiency in hepatocytes did not affect CYP2E1 expression, which metabolizes APAP in the liver (Fig. S1B and C). Ddit4△Hep mice manifested enhanced JNK activation (Fig.  G), more severe liver necrosis, and increased serum ALT and AST levels after APAP challenge (Fig.  H). In addition, Ddit4△Hep mice showed a similar intolerance to APAP under non-fasting conditions (Fig. S2A and B). These data collectively suggest the involvement of DDIT4 in AMSCs treatment.

## DDIT4 deficiency abrogates mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in AMSCs-treated mice
Next, we focused on intrahepatic mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy to investigate the mechanisms underlying the effects of DDIT4 upregulation. APAP-induced mitochondrial damage in the liver of Ddit4△Hep mice was more severe, as shown by the lower content of OXPHOS subunits in the liver tissue of Ddit4△Hep mice after APAP injection than that of Ddit4fl/fl mice (Fig. A). The TEM analysis revealed that mitophagy structures (red triangles) could be observed in the Ddit4fl/fl mice but were rare in the Ddit4△Hep mice after APAP challenge. In Ddit4fl/fl mice, direct donation of mitochondrial membrane to form autophagosomes (Fig. B.ab) and mature autophagosome structures containing mitochondria were observed (Fig. B.c). This form of mitophagy is associated with the Parkin-PINK1 mechanism [ ]. Significant abnormal mitochondrial ultrastructure could be observed in Ddit4ΔHep mice, as evidenced by decreased matrix density (asterisks) and abnormal mitochondrial morphology, such as swelling, deformation, and shrinking (arrows) (Fig. B, d–f). In order to eliminate the effects of fasting on autophagy, we used 500 mg/kg APAP to induce liver injury in non-fasting mice. Ddit4 deletion increased the P62 expression and decreased the LC3BII/LC3BI ratio and autophagy-related genes (Map1lc3b and Atg5) within the liver, suggesting autophagy was also impaired in non-fasting mice (Fig. S3A and B). Whether AMSCs treatment could protect mitochondrial function through DDIT4 was further confirmed in APAP-challenged Ddit4ΔHep and Ddit4fl/fl mice. Ddit4ΔHep mice exhibit no improvement in massive necrosis, elevated ALT and AST levels, and JNK activation after AMSCs treatment (Fig. C and D). AMSCs treatment failed to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in Ddit4ΔHep mice (Fig. E and F). Consistently, there are no significant effects of AMSCs administration on restoring impaired mitochondrial function in APAP-challenged Ddit4ΔHep mice, as reflected by no improvement in mtDNA content or mitochondrial OCR (Fig. G and H). In summary, in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity, DDIT4 plays an important part in enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in hepatocytes by AMSCs treatment.
 

## DDIT4-mediated upregulation of PGC-1α modulate mitochondria in APAP-challenged hepatocytes treated by AMSCs
Next, we explore how DDIT4 induced by AMSCs exerts mitochondrial remodeling roles. Recent data have suggested the protective role of DDIT4 in regulating mitochondrial function related to PGC-1α [ , ]. First, DDIT4 overexpression upregulated PGC-1α and induced the expression of downstream TFAM in AML12 cells and primary hepatocytes regardless of APAP challenge (Fig. A and Fig. S4A). Immunofluorescence revealed DDIT4 overexpression led to an accumulation of PGC-1α in the nucleus, where it exerted transcriptional activity (Fig. B). Next, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to knock out DDIT4 in AML12 cells. AMSCs co-culture failed to induce PGC-1α expression in DDIT4-deficient AML12 cells during the APAP challenge (Fig. C). Meanwhile, in APAP-treated primary hepatocytes obtained from Ddit4ΔHep mice, DDIT4 deficiency diminished PGC-1α expression induced by AMSCs (Fig. S4B). These results indicated that PGC-1α functioned as a downstream effector of DDIT4. PGC-1α is a key player in maintaining metabolic homeostasis and is closely related to mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial antioxidant defense [ ]. To determine the role of PGC-1α in AMSCs treatment of APAP-challenged hepatocytes, SR-18292, a PGC-1α antagonist, was used in the co-culture system. PGC-1α inhibition reversed the beneficial effects of AMSCs in enhancing OXPHOS and mitochondria mass, as reflected by significantly diminished upregulation in OCR and mtDNA content induced by AMSCs in SR-18292-treated cells (Fig. D and E). Co-culturing AMSCs with APAP-overdosed AML12 cells increased the expression of OXPHOS subunits and mitochondrial biogenesis-related proteins, whereas SR-18292 inhibited these effects, indicating that PGC-1α was involved in AMSCs-induced mitochondrial biogenesis (Fig. F). Moreover, PGC-1α is involved in autophagy [ , ], and may regulate mitophagy by the PINK1-Parkin dependent [ , ] and independent pathways [ , ]. The mt-Kemia assay demonstrated that mitophagy activity was promoted by AMSCs treatment and was repressed by PGC-1α inhibition (Fig. H). In AML12 cells damaged by excessive APAP, the increase in mitophagy induced by AMSCs co-culture was abolished by DDIT4 knockout, as indicated by increased PINK1, Parkin, and LC3BII with decreased P62 (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, SR-18292 reversed the increase in the expression of mitophagy-related proteins (PINK1, Parkin, and LC3BII) and decrease in P62 induced by AMSCs (Fig. I). In addition, we used siRNA to knock down PGC-1α in AML12 cells. Similarly, AMSCs co-culture failed to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in PGC-1α knockdown cells treated with APAP (Fig. S5A). Finally, we examined whether mitophagy or mitochondrial biogenesis is necessary for the protective effects of AMSCs in treating APAP-induced injury. Mdivi-1 or PINK1 siRNA was added to the co-culture system to inhibit mitophagy, and PGC-1α siRNA was added to inhibit mitochondrial biogenesis. We found inhibition of either mitophagy or mitochondrial biogenesis can impair the beneficial effects of AMSCs in reducing JNK activation (mitochondrial stress) and the expression of γH2AX (DNA damage) (Fig. S5B). These data collectively confirm that AMSCs restore mitochondrial function through DDIT4/PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy and highlighting that mitochondrial protective mechanisms are essential for the protective effects of AMSCs in APAP-induced injury.
 

## AMSCs treatment promotes mitochondrial function in APAP-challenged mice via the DDIT4/PGC-1α pathway
To further determine the roles of AMSCs in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy through DDIT/PGC-1α in vivo, we detected the expression of PGC-1α in the livers of APAP-injured Ddit4fl/fl and Ddit4△Hep mice after AMSCs treatment. IHC results showed increased PGC-1α expression in the centrilobular hepatocytes after AMSCs treatment in Ddit4fl/fl mice, while DDIT4 knockout reversed the effect of AMSCs administration (Fig.  A). Consistently, Ddit4△Hep mice showed substantial resistance to the AMSCs-induced PGC-1α activation against APAP overdose, as Nrf1 and Tfam mRNA were not upregulated (Fig.  B). Therefore, AMSCs treatment also increased PGC-1α activity via DDIT4 in vivo. To obtain evidence of remodeling mitochondrial function induced by the AMSCs-mediated DDIT4/PGC-1α pathway, the PGC-1α antagonist SR-18292 was injected into wild-type mice as indicated (Fig.  C). H&E results showed that PGC-1α inhibition reversed the protective effect of AMSCs on APAP-induced liver necrosis. This result was also supported by insignificant improvements in serum AST and ALT levels (Fig.  D). In addition, WB analysis revealed a significantly higher level of mitochondrial stress (p-JNK/JNK) in the SR-18292 group (Fig.  E). SR-18292 administration diminished the enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy induced by AMSCs during the APAP challenge (Fig.  F and G). Next, we used ZLN005 (a PGC-1α agonist) to determine whether enhancing PGC-1α could protect against liver injury in APAP-challenged Ddit4△Hep mice. The results suggested that activation of PGC-1α can ameliorate severe APAP-induced liver injury caused by DDIT4 deficiency, as confirmed by histological and biochemical assessments (Fig.  H). In conclusion, AMSC-based cell therapy restores mitochondrial function in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity via the DDIT4/PGC-1α pathway in vivo.
 

## Discussion
Drug-induced liver injury is a common cause of acute liver failure, exhibiting a high mortality rate. Its main mechanism is that drug metabolites disturb and damage liver mitochondrial, but there is currently a lack of effective treatment methods and therapeutic molecular targets. MSC-based cell therapy as a potential strategy for mitochondria modulation to treat disease has gained increased interest. At present, the mechanisms of MSCs’ regulation of mitochondria are being explored, such as mitochondrial transfer [ ]. However, the ways that MSCs help improve mitochondrial function in target cells are still not fully understood, which could lead to develop novel drug targets and stem cell-derived alternative therapy.
In this study, we reported that MSC therapy not only reduced oxidative stress but also improved mitochondrial respiratory function and increased mitochondrial abundance in APAP-damaged hepatocytes. Based on bioinformatics analysis, we found AMSCs significantly upregulated DDIT4 expression in APAP-overdosed mice livers. Meanwhile, the expression of DDIT4 in hepatocytes exposed to excessive APAP was also increased compared with the healthy group, both in mice and human samples. IHC results showed that excess APAP and AMSCs treatment enhanced DDIT4 expression in hepatocytes closest to the central vein. APAP overdose triggers unique zonated alterations in the liver, including mitochondrial damage and adaptive responses such as autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis [ ]. From this perspective, increased expression of DDIT4, which is associated with autophagy and mitochondrial function, may play a protective feedback mechanism in mitochondrial regulation in AILI. Further, Ddit4△Hep mice were generated to demonstrate that the protective roles of DDIT4 in APAP overdose were attributed to enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in hepatocytes. PGC-1α is critical for mitochondrial biogenesis, and recent evidence highlights that PGC-1α also plays a role in mitophagy through regulating molecular expression such as PINK1 and FUNDC1 [ , ]. By utilizing co-culture assay, upstream-downstream relationships between DDIT4 and PGC-1α were confirmed in AML12 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes. Meanwhile, reactivation of PGC-1α in Ddit4△Hep mice attenuated AILI. Inhibiting PGC-1α using a pharmacological compound abolished mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in isolated hepatocytes and mice mediated by AMSCs therapy. Therefore, we concluded that DDIT4/PGC-1α is important for AMSCs regulating mitochondrial function in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity and that DDIT4/PGC-1α may serve as a molecular target for the treatment of AILI.
DDIT4 is known to be a negative regulator of mTOR signaling and thus acts as an autophagy regulatory factor [ ]. Knocking out DDIT4 in AML12 cells significantly activated mTORC1, as shown by the increase in p-ULK1 expression, and this effect could be significantly reduced by adding Rapa (an mTOR inhibitor). Then we used CQ (a lysosomal inhibitor) to block the autophagic flux and found that LC3BII increased in sgDDIT4 cells. Moreover, Rapa treatment further promoted the accumulation of LC3BII induced by CQ, indicating that knockout of DDIT4 affected the initiation of mTORC1-mediated autophagy but did not affect the degradation of autophagosomes (Fig. S6A). Co-culture with AMSCs enhanced DDIT4 in AML12 cells, which triggered ULK1 activity as manifested by increased phosphorylation of ATG14. This effect could not be observed in sgDDIT4 cells (Fig. S6B). In addition, ULK1 inhibitor (SBI-0206965) treatment significantly reduced mitophagy in AML12 cells induced by AMSC co-culture (Fig. S6C). Therefore, DDIT4 may also promote mitophagy through the mTORC1-ULK1 pathway.
Our study provides compelling evidence that the DDIT4/PGC-1α axis is a critical novel pathway through which AMSCs restore mitochondrial function and confer protection against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity. It is important to note, however, that the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs is undoubtedly multifaceted. For example, MSCs secrete hepatocyte growth factor and upregulate c-Met expression to reduce hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury [ ] or regulate other signaling pathways, such as increasing NRF2 to reduce acute liver injury [ ]. Therefore, we examined the expression of other mitochondrial regulators (AMPK, SIRT1, and NRF2) in AMSCs-treated mice and found that AMSC therapy increase the expression of NRF2 in AILI, but there is no significant increase in the activation of AMPK and the expression of SIRT1 (Fig. S7). These results undoubtedly indicate that stem cell therapy involves multiple mechanisms, and given that the pathogenesis of AILI is a dynamic process involving the transition from liver injury to liver regeneration, these molecular mechanisms may not be mutually exclusive but may act in a complementary or even sequential manner. Future work aimed at dissecting other mitochondrial regulation pathways or potential crosstalk with other mechanisms will be crucial for a comprehensive understanding of MSC-based liver failure treatment. In addition, we utilized SR-18292 and ZLN005 as widely accepted pharmacological modulators of PGC-1α activity to probe its functional role in this study. However, these compounds are not entirely selective and may have off-target effects. We used PGC-1α siRNA verify its role in AMSCs treating AILI in co-culture experiments (Fig S5). The congruent results obtained from both chemical and genetic interventions suggested that the observed protective effects of AMSCs are primarily mediated through PGC-1α. Future studies employing more specific inhibitors or genetic models in mice will be valuable to further refine these conclusions. Finally, although our in silico analysis of public transcriptome data suggests elevated DDIT4 expression in human liver tissue with AILI, these data indicate that the DDIT4 expression has spatial specificity as AILI-related moleculus. Therefore, further studies are needed to address this gap. For example, genetic manipulation of DDIT4 could be performed using humanized mouse models reconstituted from primary human hepatocytes or advanced 3D organoid cultures derived from patient samples to validate its role in AILI.
In conclusion, this study has shown therapeutic effects of MSC therapy in the mouse AILI model and identified DDIT4/PGC-1α as potential therapeutic targets. This opens avenues for developing small-molecule PGC-1α agonists or strategies to modulate DDIT4 as pharmacologic alternatives to cell therapy, which could circumvent the logistical and regulatory complexities associated with live cell administration. Our study also suggests that upregulating the expression of DDIT4 in target cells and organs could serve as a strategy to improve AMSC therapeutic potency, which establish a theoretical foundation for the further development of stem cell-based alternative therapies. To advance these findings toward the clinic, future efforts may focus on optimizing stem cell-based and cell-free alternatives strategies as well as conducting rigorous safety and efficacy studies in larger animal models.

## Conclusion
Our results show that AMSC therapy could be a promising treatment for AILI by activating the DDIT4/PGC-1α pathway, which contributes to mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis. These results advance the understanding of the mechanisms of MSC-based treatment for acute liver failure and propose new targets for treating AILI.
