## Abstract
Septic cardiomyopathy (SC) is a sepsis-mediated cardiovascular complication characterized by cardiac dysfunction. Emerging evidence indicated that exercise training confers protection against SC; however, the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. In this study, we identified a novel exercise-derived peptide, apolipoprotein A1 peptide (named ApoA1tide), through mouse plasma peptidomic screening. Subsequent experiments revealed that ApoA1tide mitigated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced SC in murine models, as evidenced by enhanced cardiac function, prolonged survival, and improved myocardial structure. Moreover, ApoA1tide was found to inhibit inflammatory responses, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and oxidative stress in mice subjected to LPS induction. Correspondingly, in vitro experiments demonstrated that ApoA1tide suppressed LPS-induced apoptosis and oxidative stress in neonatal rat cardiac myocytes (NRCMs). Additionally, ApoA1tide enhanced autophagic activity in both LPS-induced mice and NRCMs. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing identified CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ) as the primary target of ApoA1tide. The expression of C/EBPβ was upregulated following ApoA1tide treatment. Overexpression of C/EBPβ in NRCMs resulted in increased expression of autophagy-related genes, including LC3B, Atg3, Atg4b, and Atg7. Conversely, Knockdown of C/EBPβ eliminated the enhancement of autophagy induced by ApoA1tide, along with the protective effects of ApoA1tide on cardiac functionality, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. These findings indicate that ApoA1tide exerts its protective effects against SC by enhancing C/EBPβ-mediated autophagy. Our study provides a novel therapeutic strategy for SC.

Schematic representation of the effects of ApoA1tide on sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction. ApoA1tide, a peptide derived from exercise, attenuates LPS-induced SC. ## Introduction
Sepsis, a life-threatening systemic infection induced by a severely dysregulated host response, is associated with acute organ dysfunction and an increased risk of mortality [ ]. Without appropriate and effective treatment, the fatality rate for sepsis can exceed 30–50% [ ]. Within this critical landscape, septic cardiomyopathy (SC) emerges as a pivotal cardiovascular complication characterized by reversible myocardial depression and associated with particularly adverse clinical outcomes [ ]. The known related pathogenesis of SC, involving inflammatory cascade, oxidative stress, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and autonomic system dysregulation, remain unclear and contradictory [ ]. While contemporary management strategies focusing on antimicrobial therapy, hemodynamic optimization, and vasoactive support provide symptomatic relief, their therapeutic efficacy remains fundamentally limited by an insufficient mechanistic understanding. Elucidating the precise molecular interplay between inflammatory mediators, cellular stress responses, and myocardial dysfunction is imperative for developing mechanism-driven therapies to improve clinical outcomes.
Physical exercise is reportedly known as a multimodal therapeutic intervention capable of modulating inflammatory cascades and attenuating oxidative stress damage across diverse pathological states, including sepsis, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disorders [ – ]. Preclinical investigations demonstrate that voluntary aerobic exercise conditioning, which is associated with good cardiovascular fitness, confers protection against sepsis-induced inflammatory hyperactivation and prothrombotic responses in aged murine models [ , ]. Clinically, observational studies reveal improved functional recovery trajectories and reduced mortality risk in patients with sepsis participating in structured exercise rehabilitation programs, contrasting with adverse outcomes in sedentary cohorts [ , ]. While these findings establish exercise as a viable adjunct therapy in sepsis management, its mechanistic interplay with SC pathogenesis remains poorly defined. Current understanding of exercise-mediated benefits predominantly centers on skeletal muscle adaptations, particularly redox homeostasis stabilization and inflammation resolution [ ]. Nevertheless, in distant organs such as the heart, evidence has indicated that exercise-mediated cardioprotective functions are fulfilled by various signaling molecules named circulatory “exerkines” released from skeletal muscles and other tissues [ – ]. Exerkines are defined as signaling moieties released in response to acute and/or chronic exercise, which exert their effects through endocrine, paracrine and/or autocrine pathways [ ]. Exerkines demonstrate pleiotropic therapeutic potential across multiple physiological domains, modulating cardiovascular performance, metabolic homeostasis, immune regulation, and neuroprotective pathways. For instance, the prototypical exerkines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and irisin demonstrate therapeutic efficacy in heart failure models and diabetic cardiovascular complications [ ]. However, the exerkine signaling landscape in SC remains virtually unexplored, prompting the search for a potential active substance derived from exercise that can improve cardiac dysfunction during SC progression.
Herein, we identified a new exercise-derived peptide, Apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) peptide (ApoA1tide), a derivative of ApoA1, which is released into the circulatory system in response to physical exercise. ApoA1tide exerted significant cardioprotective effects against LPS-induced SC, such as reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and inflammatory injuries in mice with sepsis and in vitro studies. Furthermore, our findings indicate that ApoA1tide ameliorates SC by promoting C/EBPβ-dependent autophagy. This study provides new insights into potential treatments for SC.

## Materials and methods

## Reagents
ApoA1tide (purity ≥ 99%) and humanized ApoA1tide (purity ≥ 99%) were synthesized by Genscript (Nanjing, China). LPS (L2880, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, United States) was purchased from Sigma. 3-Methyladenine (3-MA) and bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) was purchased from MedChem Express (Brea, CA, United States).

## Human subjects
Human SC plasma samples were obtained from patients with infective endocarditis or degenerative valve regurgitation. Intravenous blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 1600 ×g at 4℃ for 10 min. The plasma was transferred to a new tube and stored at − 80℃ for future experiments.

## Animal study
Eight-week-old wild-type C57BL/6 male mice (20–25 g) were purchased from SPF Technology (SPF Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). Mice were kept in specific-pathogen-free rooms at a constant temperature (25 ± 2℃) in a light/dark cycle and 50%–60% humidity. The mice were fed with abundant food and tap water. They were grouped using the random number table method. Mice were randomly divided into four groups: control group, ApoA1tide treatment group (ApoA1tide), LPS group (LPS), and LPS + ApoA1tide group (LPS + ApoA1tide). In the control group, normal saline was injected intraperitoneally. Mice in the LPS group received 20 mg/kg of LPS, as previous reported [ ]. Mice in the LPS + ApoA1tide group received 15 mg/kg of ApoA1tide through the tail vein 2 h before intraperitoneal injection of 20 mg/kg of LPS, and mice in the ApoA1tide group received 15 mg/kg of ApoA1tide. Mice in the LPS group received 20 mg/kg of LPS. After 12 h, cardiac function was assessed via M-mode echocardiography, after which all mice were sacrificed. The plasma and hearts were collected from all mice and stored at − 80 °C for the subsequent experiments. The survival rate was measured using the remaining mice in each group (n = 8) treated with 20 mg/kg LPS or/and 15 mg/kg ApoA1tide. The mortality was recorded for 7 consecutive days.
For swimming training, we followed a protocol described previously [ ]. In brief, male C57BL/6 mice (8-week-old) were trained to swim 5 days weekly for 3 months. The water temperature of the swimming pool was maintained at 33℃–35℃. Mice in the control group (n = 15) were raised normally without swimming training. After swimming training was completed, the plasma was collected from the blood of the mice and then stored at − 80℃ until the subsequent experiments.

All procedures complied with ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. All animal-related experimental procedures were approved by the Institute’s Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital (KS(Y)21282), and were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Mouse blood samples were collected at preset time points, and plasma was obtained from the blood samples after centrifugation at 3,000 rpm and 4℃ for 15 min. The levels of mouse plasma cytokines, including cardiac troponin (cTnI), LPS, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) were measured using ELISA kits (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plasma levels of ApoA1 in humans and mice were measured using Human Apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) ELISA Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and Mouse Apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) ELISA Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Peptidomic analysis
Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was performed to screen for potential exercise-derived peptides. The detailed methods for LC–MS/MS have been reported previously [ ]. Briefly, plasma samples were collected and subjected to plasma precipitation and liquid–liquid extraction; the samples were then used for LC–MS/MS analysis. The LC–MS/MS system consisted of an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC System coupled to an Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer and an electrospray ionization source. The plasma peptides whose levels were significantly increased (up to 3-fold) from the baseline in exercise-trained mice were collected for further analysis.

## Cell culture and treatment
Neonatal rat cardiac myocytes (NRCMs) were isolated as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the hearts of 2-day-old neonatal rats were harvested and minced into 1-mm3 cubes using scissors and digested with a digestion solution containing 0.6% pancreatin and 0.4% collagenase type II (C6885, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37℃; the digestion process was repeated five times. The supernatant was collected and then incubated in a dish for 2 h at 37℃ to remove cardiac fibroblasts. NRCMs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% horse serum, 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.
The AC16 human ventricular cardiomyocyte cell line was purchased from Qingqi (Shanghai) Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. The AC16 cell line was cultured in DMEM/F-12 nutrient mixture medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. When the cells reached 70% confluency, they were pretreated with ApoA1tide (50 µM) for 2 h. Then, LPS (20 µg/mL) was added into the cell culture, and the mixture was incubated for 24 h at 37℃ to establish the septic model.

## Echocardiography
After 12 h of LPS injection, the cardiac function and structure were measured via echocardiography (Vevo 2100, Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada) using a high-resolution imaging transducer for small animals. Mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane inhalation. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fraction shortening (LVFS), left ventricular internal systolic dimensions (LVIDs), and left ventricular internal diastolic dimension (LVIDd) were calculated using Vevo 2100 software.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining
Samples of heart tissue were harvested after 12 h of LPS exposure and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed heart tissues were then dehydrated using various concentrations of alcohol, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into thicknesses of 5 μm. The tissue sections were then stained with HE to evaluate cardiomyocyte morphology and inflammatory cell infiltration. Six sections were selected for each group. For each section, heart injury was scored according to the percentage of myocardial fiber damage as follows: 0 for no damage, 1 for 1%–25% damage, 2 for 26%–50% damage, 3 for 51%–75% damage, and 4 for 76%–100% damage.

## Western blot analysis
NRCMs, AC16 cells (2 × 10⁶ LPS-treated cardiomyocytes per sample, cultured in 6-well plates), and heart tissue (25 mg precisely weighed per sample) were washed with PBS, then lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) on ice for 20 min, and finally centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Equal protein loads (20 µg/lane) were resolved on Bis-Tris gels using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) at 120 V for 90 min and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) at 300 mA for 120 min. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4℃. After the initial incubation, the membranes were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies (1:10000) for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software. The antibodies were as follows: Bax (1:1000; 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Bcl-2 (1:1000; 26593-1-AP, Proteintech), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000; 9664 T, Cell Signal Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), β-actin (1:1000; AF2811, Beyotime Biotechnology), gp91phox (1:1000; 19013-1-AP, Proteintech), LC3B (1:1000; AL221, Beyotime Biotechnology), p62 (1:1000; AF5312, Beyotime Biotechnology).

## Real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR)
100 mg of heart tissue was lysed to extract the total RNAs by TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). 1 µg total RNA input per 20 µL reaction (PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix, Takara). Real-time PCR was performed using AceQ® Universal SYBR® qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) in the 7300 Plus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers used in the study are as follows: mouse-IL-1β Forward: ATGCCACCTTTTGACAGTGATG; Reverse: TGATGTGCTGCTGCGAGATT; mouse-TNF-α Forward: CCCTCACACTCACAAACCAC; Reverse: ACAAGGTACAACCCATCGGC; mouse-C/EBPβ Forward: AAGCTGAGCGACGAGTACAAGA; Reverse: GTCAGCTCCAGCACCTTGTG; rat-C/EBPβ Forward: CAAGCTGAGCGACGAGTACAA; Reverse: ACAGCTGCTCCACCTTCTTCT; rat-Atg4B Forward: CAGTGTCCTCAACGCTTTCCT; Reverse: TAGACTTGCCTTCGCCAACTC; rat-LC3B Forward: AGTGGAAGATGTCCGGCTCAT; Reverse: GCTGCTTCTCACCCTTGTATCG; rat-Atg7 Forward : GTGTACGATCCCTGTAACCTAACCC; Reverse: CGAAAGCAGAGAACTTCAACAGACT; rat-Atg3 Forward: GCAGCACCATGCAGGTGAG; Reverse: TGGTCACTCGGTCCAGGATC; 18 S: Forward: CGCCGCTAGAGGTGAAATTCT; Reverse: CATTCTTGGCAAATGCTTTCG; GAPDH: Forward: GTCCCAGCTTAGGTTCATCAGG, Reverse: CAATACGGCCAAATCCGTTCA. The results from the 7300 Plus Real-Time PCR System were presented as Ct values, GAPDH as an internal reference, and shown as 2−∆∆Ct.

## Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining
TUNEL fluorescent staining was conducted using a TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The prepared samples were washed once with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After incubation with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min, 50 µL of TUNEL assay solution was added to the sample, and the mixture was incubated for 60 min at 37 °C under dark conditions. Nuclei were stained with diphenyl phenylindole (DAPI). The cells were then analyzed using fluorescence microscopy at 450–550 nm. The apoptosis index was calculated as the ratio of the number of TUNEL-labeled cells to the total number of cells labeled with DAPI.

## Flow cytometry
The apoptosis rate in LPS-induced NRCMs was tested via flow cytometry. NRCMs were seeded into 6-well plates and treated differently. Cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. After resuspending the cells in binding buffer, Annexin V/PI solution was added to each tube, and the tubes were incubated for approximately 20 min at room temperature under dark conditions, according to the instructions provided in the Annexin V-FITC Cell Apoptosis Kit (C1062, Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China). A BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer was used to determine the apoptotic rate of NRCMs.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was estimated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK 8) Assay Kit (C0038, Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China). Cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a concentration of 5 × 103 and incubated overnight. Once the cells reached 70% confluency, they were treated with LPS and ApoA1tide at various concentrations for 24 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microtiter plate reader (SkanIt Software 5.0, RE, ver. 5.0.0.42). The cell viability was calculated using the following formula: Cell viability = (Treatment Group optical density [OD] − Blank Group OD)/(Control Group OD − Blank Group OD).

## Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) determination
The ROS levels were evaluated using a ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China). Cells were cultured in 12-well plates. When the cells reached 70% confluency, they were pretreated with 50 µM ApoA1tide for 2 h before being treated with LPS for 24 h. Subsequently, 2–7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was added to the wells, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37℃ under dark conditions. Then, the cells were observed using fluorescence microscopy. The relative intensity of DCFH-DA fluorescence was calculated using ImageJ software.

## Measurement of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)
A JC-1 Mitochondria Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) was used to evaluate the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. All experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS and then incubated with JC-1 for 30 min at 37 °C. The solution was then removed, and the cells were washed twice. Finally, the fluorescence was measured at 540/570 nm (JC-1 aggregates: red fluorescence) and 485/535 nm (free JC-1 monomers: green fluorescence) using fluorescence microscopy. The ratio of aggregates to monomers of JC-1 was expressed as MMP. The relative intensity of fluorescence was calculated using ImageJ software.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Heart tissues were separated from the left ventricular myocardium to quantify autophagosomes. Samples were cut into 1-mm3 cubes and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the heart samples were processed through a series of steps, including fixation, dehydration, immersion, embedding, sectioning, and dyeing. The autophagosomes were observed under a JEOL JEM-2000EX transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Random slices were imaged and analyzed by two technicians who were blinded to the experiment.

## Monodansylcadaverine (MDC) assay
An MDC Staining Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) was used to detect autophagic staining in cells. After the cells were treated, 250 µL of the MDC staining reagent was added into each well, and cells were incubated for 30 min at 37℃ under dark conditions. Following incubation, the MDC staining solution was discarded, and the cells were washed thrice with serum-free cell culture medium. The cells were then observed using fluorescence microscopy with UV excitation. The relative intensity of MDC fluorescence was calculated using ImageJ software.

## Adeno-associated virus (AAV) application
Recombinant AAV serotype-9 (AAV9) harboring the full-length mouse C/EBPβ gene with the cardiac troponin T (cTNT) promoter (AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ) as well as control vectors (AAV9-cTNT-GFP) were prepared. The AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ and AAV9-cTNT-GFP vectors were constructed by Bioscien Biotechnology Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). C57BL/6J male mice were transduced with purified adeno viruses through a single tail-vein injection of AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ or AAV9-cTNT-GFP containing 1 × 1012 viral particles in 100-µL of sterile PBS.

## Transfection assay
Cells cultured in 6-well plates were transfected with siRNA targeting C/EBPβ or C/EBPβ- overexpressed plasmid using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA of C/EBPβ were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and targeted to the junction region of C/EBPβ. siRNA sequences were as follows: C/EBPβ-siRNA-1, 5′- CUUGAUGCAAUCCGGAUCATT-3′ (sense) and 5′-UUGAUCGGAUUGCAUCAAGTT-3′ (antisense); C/EBPβ-siRNA-2, 5′- GGUUUCGAGCAUUAAAGUGATT-3′ (sense) and 5′-UUCACUUUAAUGCUCGAAACTT-3′ (antisense); C/EBPβ-siRNA-3, 5′- CCCCUGAGUAAUCACUUAAATT-3′ (sense) and 5′-UUUUAAGUGAUUACUCAGGGTT-3′ (antisense).

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
Total RNA was extracted from NRCMs treated with LPS and LPS + ApoA1tide using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Haimen, Jiangsu, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Purification of mRNA was performed using Oligo (dT)-attached magnetic beads. RNA-seq was performed using single-end 50-bp 20 M clean reads via a DNBSEQ platform (BGI, Wuhan, China). The ‘pheatmap’ (v1.0.8) package in R software was used to construct heatmaps to illustrate the fold-change in gene expression levels across different samples. Pathway enrichment was evaluated using Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and all experiments were repeated at least three times. Student’s t-test was used to calculate statistical differences between two groups. Comparisons between multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. Normality testing was systematically performed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to ANOVA, with data transformation (logarithmic) applied to non-normally distributed variables. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve with a log-rank test was used for survival analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## ApoA1tide is a potential exercise-derived exerkine
Murine plasma peptidomics was conducted to determine potential exercise-derived exerkines. As shown in Fig. A, mice were forced to swim five times a week for 3 months, after which the plasma was collected. Differentially expressed peptides were screened via LC–MS. As shown in the heatmap, a total of 60 differentially expressed peptides were detected (Fig. B). Among these, based on the thresholds of an average cutoff change of 4-fold and a p-value of < 0.05, we selected 27 peptides. Of these 27 peptides, 16 were upregulated and 11 were downregulated in the swimming group (Fig. C-D). After combining our results with those of a previously published study, we focused on ApoA1-pep990 (referred to as ApoA1tide), whose precursor protein is ApoA1. ApoA1 is a major high-density lipoprotein (HDL) protein that regulates HDL structure and function [ ]. It has a significant therapeutic effect on mice with sepsis [ ]. A clinical study indicated that decreased levels of ApoA1 were correlated with sepsis and adverse outcomes in patients admitted to the intensive care unit [ ]. We revealed that the levels of ApoA1 in the plasma of humans and mice with sepsis were significantly reduced compared with those in the control group (Fig. A-B), and the level of ApoA1 in the plasma of mice following exercise was increased following exercise (Fig. C). As shown in Fig. E, ApoA1tide contains six amino acids located between the 68th and 73rd amino acids at the N-terminus of ApoA1. The amino acid sequences for mouse and human ApoA1tide are NLLENW and KLLDNW, respectively.
 

## ApoA1tide ameliorated LPS-induced cardiac dysfunction and improved survival rate in mice
To investigate the cardioprotective effect of ApoA1tide, mice were pretreated with ApoA1tide (15 mg/kg) via tail-vein injection 2 h before LPS (20 mg/kg) stimulation. The 7-day survival rate and plasma cTnI and LPS levels after 12 h of LPS stimulation were monitored. Our results showed that ApoA1tide significantly improved the survival rate of mice compared with the LPS group (Fig. A). In parallel, the plasma cTnI level was significantly reduced in the ApoA1tide group (Fig. B). Interestingly, there is no significant change in plasma LPS levels between the LPS group and the LPS + ApoA1tide group, indicating that ApoA1tide does not affect the production of LPS in vivo (Fig. C).
 Cardiac function and damage were evaluated via echocardiography and HE staining, respectively. Our results showed that the cardiac function values, including the LVEF and LVFS drastically decreased after 12 h of LPS stimulation; however, these values were increased by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. D). The LVIDs was significantly increased by LPS stimulation but was reduced by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. G). In contrast, the LVIDd did not change after LPS stimulation or ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. H). HE staining revealed that LPS-administered mice exhibited broken and disordered myocardial fibers, interstitial edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration, and these pathological changes were ameliorated by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. I). Taken together, these results revealed that ApoA1tide ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and improved the survival rate in LPS-stimulated mice.

## ApoA1tide attenuated LPS-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis
Cardiomyocyte apoptosis is a key driver of SC. Therefore, to explore the effect of ApoA1tide on cell apoptosis, a TUNEL assay was performed. As shown in Fig. A, LPS induced a greater number of TUNEL-positive cardiomyocytes in the heart tissues of mice compared with the control group, and this phenomenon was inhibited by ApoA1tide treatment. Consistently, western blot analysis revealed that the expression levels of cleaved caspase-3 and Bax were significantly increased, whereas the expression level of Bcl-2 was significantly decreased in the heart tissues of LPS-stimulated mice. Meanwhile, these protein expression changes were reversed by ApoA1tide (Fig. B).
 To determine the effect of ApoA1tide on cell apoptosis in vitro, NRCMs were primarily cultured and their cell viability was evaluated. The results of the CCK8 assay indicated that 20 µg/mL LPS reduced cell viability to the greatest extent (Fig. C). Based on this finding, we determined the effect of ApoA1tide on cell viability in NRCMs after the administration of 20 µg/mL LPS and found that 50 µM ApoA1tide optimally restored cell viability from 50% to 70% with LPS stimulation, representing the maximal efficacy plateau without inducing cytotoxicity (Fig. D). Therefore, 50 µM ApoA1tide was used in subsequent experiments. Next, the apoptosis of NRCMs was detected via flow cytometry, and the results showed that ApoA1tide significantly reduced LPS-induced cell apoptosis (Fig. E). In addition, TUNEL staining showed that ApoA1tide significantly decreased the number of TUNEL-positive cells compared to the LPS group (Fig. F). Accordingly, western blot analysis revealed that the LPS-induced increase in Bax expression and decrease in Bcl-2 expression were suppressed by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. G). Taken together, these data revealed that ApoA1tide inhibited LPS-induced cell apoptosis both in vivo and in vitro.
In addition, to further investigate whether human-derived ApoA1tide (hm-ApoA1tide) could also exert cardioprotective effects, we synthesized humanized ApoA1tide, which differs from mouse ApoA1tide by two amino acids (Fig. E). The results indicated that the cardiac function of mice after LPS stimulation was ameliorated by hm-ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. A). Consistently, western blot analysis revealed that the LPS-induced increase in Bax expression and decrease in Bcl-2 expression were reversed by hm-ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. B). In the in vitro experiments (Fig. D-E), hm-ApoA1tide significantly inhibited the LPS-induced increase in the number of TUNEL-positive AC16 cells, while increasing Bax expression and decreasing Bcl-2 expression, suggesting that hm-ApoA1tide exerts antiapoptotic effects in vitro. Taken together, these results indicate that hm-ApoA1tide exerts a cardioprotective effect.

## ApoA1tide attenuated LPS-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage
Next, we determined whether ApoA1tide attenuated LPS-induced inflammation. We revealed that the plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, in mice were significantly increased after LPS challenge and were decreased by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. A). Consistent with this finding, ApoA1tide significantly reduced the mRNA levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis (Fig. B). Oxidative damage is believed to be a consequence of sepsis and may lead to cardiac dysfunction. We also evaluated the effect of ApoA1tide on oxidative stress. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression of gp91phox (gp91), the major catalytic component of triphosphopyridine nucleotide oxidase (NOX) and a biomarker for NOX activation, was upregulated in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis and that this effect was reversed by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. C). A similar finding was observed in NRCMs (Fig. D). Moreover, the LPS-induced increase in the expression of gp91 in heart tissues and AC16 cells was also inhibited by hm-ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. C, F). DCFH-DA staining revealed that ApoA1tide treatment reduced LPS-induced ROS production (Fig. E), and a similar result was observed for hm-ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. G).
 Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key contributor to SC and is lethally detrimental to the heart. Therefore, we detected MMP in NRCMs via JC-1 staining. As expected, the LPS-induced decrease in MMP was stabilized by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. F). Taken together, these results suggest that ApoA1tide attenuates LPS-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage.

## ApoA1tide promoted autophagy in LPS-induced septic mice and NRCMs
It is conceivable that functional autophagy is critical for maintaining cardiac function. During the late stage of sepsis, autophagy becomes insufficient and maladaptive, leading to severe consequences [ ]. Thus, we investigated whether ApoA1tide affects autophagy. TEM was utilized to observe autophagosomes in the heart tissues of mice. We found that in LPS-induced mice, the number of autophagosomes was increased, and the myotome arrangement in the heart tissues was disordered compared with the control group. Meanwhile, ApoA1tide treatment increased the number of autophagosomes and restored the structure of the myocardium in mice with sepsis (Fig. A).
 Western blot analysis was utilized to assess the levels of expression of autophagy-associated proteins in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis. As shown in Fig. B, compared with control mice, the LC3 II/LC3 I rate was significantly increased and p62 expression was significantly decreased in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis, and these changes were further enhanced by ApoA1tide treatment. Similar results were observed in the NRCMs (Fig. C). Moreover, we performed the tandem mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter assay to comprehensively evaluate autophagic activity. The results showed that ApoA1tide intervention markedly increased both yellow puncta (GFP+/mCherry+, autophagosomes) and red puncta (GFP−/mCherry+, autolysosomes) compared with LPS group (Fig. A). These data were consistent with the elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and p62 degradation observed by western blot. MDC staining was used to determine the number of autophagic vesicles in the NRCMs. The number of autophagic vesicles in LPS-induced NRCMs was markedly increased, and ApoA1tide treatment further increased this number (Fig. D). To further investigate whether ApoA1tide confers protection against SC by enhancing autophagy, 3-MA, an early-stage autophagy inhibitor that suppresses autophagosome formation, was used in the next phase of this study (Fig. E). 3-MA was administered at 5 mM for 24 h to inhibit autophagy [ ]. As shown in Figs. and 4F-MA significantly reversed the decreasing LC3 II/LC3 I rate and increasing p62 expression induced by ApoA1tide in the heart tissues of mice. In NRCMs, western blot analysis and MDC staining showed that ApoA1tide-induced enhancement of autophagy was eliminated by 3-MA (Fig. G-H). Next, we conducted additional experiments using bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, an end-stage autophagy inhibitor) to dissect the stage-specific effects of ApoA1tide on autophagy. Our findings indicated that BafA1 significantly increased LC3-II levels, confirming its effective inhibition of lysosomal acidification. LPS + ApoA1tide treatment further elevated LC3-II levels compared to LPS alone and reduced p62 degradation, indicating ApoA1tide promotes autophagosome formation. LPS + ApoA1tide + BafA1 induced a higher LC3-II accumulation than LPS + ApoA1tide, demonstrating that ApoA1tide does not impair autophagosome-lysosome fusion but activates the early stage of autophagosome formation (Fig. B). Combined with 3-MA data, these results conclusively showed ApoA1tide specifically enhances autophagic flux by increasing the early stage of autophagosome formation in LPS-induced mice with sepsis and NRCMs.

## Inhibition of autophagy abolished the protective effect of ApoA1tide
Then we wondered whether 3-MA could abolish the protective effects of ApoA1tide both in vivo and in vitro. As shown in Figs. and 5A-MA reversed the protective effect of ApoA1tide on cardiac function in vivo. Accordingly, the decrease in the cTnI level caused by ApoA1tide treatment was reversed by 3-MA treatment (Fig. B). In addition, 3-MA treatment abolished the effect of ApoA1tide on cardiomyocyte apoptosis and oxidative stress in vivo (Fig. C). In Figs. and 5D-E-MA diminished the inhibitory effect of ApoA1tide on MMP decrease and ROS levels in vitro. Likewise, the effect of ApoA1tide on TUNEL-positive cells rate and apoptotic proteins expression was abolished by 3-MA in vitro (Fig. F-G). Taken together, these results validated that ApoA1tide alleviated cardiac dysfunction, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and oxidative stress by promoting autophagy.
 

## ApoA1tide promoted autophagy through upregulating C/EBPβ
To illustrate the molecular mechanism by which ApoA1tide confers protection against SC, we conducted RNA-seq of NRCMs after treatment with LPS or LPS + ApoA1tide. KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes between the LPS and LPS + ApoA1tide groups revealed that ApoA1tide regulates several pathways. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was the most enriched pathway (Fig. A). Next, to identify the specific target genes on which ApoA1tide exerts its protective effect, we selected 14 candidate genes based on padj (padj ≤ 0.001). C/EBPβ, which is highly associated with autophagy, was identified (Fig. B). Subsequently, we investigated the expression of C/EBPβ in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis and NRCMs. As shown in Fig. C, ApoA1tide significantly elevated the protein and mRNA levels of C/EBPβ in LPS induced NRCMs. Consistent with these in vitro findings, ApoA1tide increased the protein and mRNA levels of C/EBPβ in the heart tissues of mice with sepsis (Fig. E).
 To explore the effect of C/EBPβ on ApoA1tide-induced autophagy in cardiomyocytes, we silenced its expression in NRCMs using C/EBPβ-siRNA. First, the efficiency of C/EBPβ-siRNA was validated via western blot analysis, and the results indicated that C/EBPβ-siRNA-3 exerted the maximum effect; thus C/EBPβ-siRNA-3 was used in subsequent experiments (Fig. A). As expected, silencing C/EBPβ abolished the effect of ApoA1tide on autophagy, as evidenced by western blot analysis (Fig. G). In addition, MDC staining indicated that silencing C/EBPβ inhibited the increase in the number of autophagic vesicles caused by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. H).
C/EBPβ is involved in autophagy activation via the regulation of autophagy-related gene expression [ , ]. To investigate whether C/EBPβ directly regulates the transcription of autophagy-related genes in cardiomyocytes, we overexpressed C/EBPβ in NRCMs (Fig. B) and determined the mRNA levels of autophagy-related genes, including Atg3, Atg7, Atg4b, and LC3B. The results indicated that overexpression of C/EBPβ strongly increased the mRNA levels of these genes, (Fig. I). Likewise, RNA-seq analysis of ApoA1tide-treated NRCMs revealed significant upregulation (fold change >1.5, p < 0.05) of Atg3, Atg7, and Atg4b, mirroring the effects of C/EBPβ overexpression (Fig. ). These data suggested that ApoA1tide enhanced autophagy by upregulating C/EBPβ expression, solidifying the mechanistic link between ApoA1tide, C/EBPβ, and autophagy gene expression.

## Knockdown of C/EBPβ abolished the protective effect of ApoA1tide both in vitro and in vivo
To explore whether the protective effect of ApoA1tide was C/EBPβ-dependent, we knocked down C/EBPβ using AAV9 vector carrying C/EBPβ under the control of the cTNT promoter (AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ). The AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ or the negative control (AAV9-cTNT-GFP) was administered to mice via tail-vein injection. We treated mice with AAV9-cTNT-C/EBPβ or AAV9-cTNT-GFP for 4 weeks before LPS stimulation (Fig. A). The efficiency of C/EBPβ knockdown in the heart tissues of mice was confirmed via immunofluorescence staining and western blot analysis (Fig. C). The results showed that C/EBPβ knockdown significantly diminished the effect of ApoA1tide on autophagy, as evidenced by western blot analysis (Fig. ). ELISA revealed that the plasma levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in mice with sepsis were significantly reduced in ApoA1tide + AAV-NC–treated mice but not in the AAV-shC/EBPβ–infected mice (Fig. B). Moreover, the cTnI level and cardiac function were significantly restored in ApoA1tide + AAV-NC–treated mice but not in AAV-shC/EBPβ–infected mice (Fig. D). Furthermore, western blot analysis and TUNEL staining showed that C/EBPβ knockdown significantly compromised the inhibitory effect of ApoA1tide on apoptosis, and oxidative stress evidenced by gp91 expression was also abolished (Fig. F). Meanwhile, the silencing of C/EBPβ neutralized the antiapoptotic and antioxidative stress effect of ApoA1tide in vitro (Fig. H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ApoA1tide confers protection against LPS-induced SC by upregulating C/EBPβ expression.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we conducted plasma peptidomics of mice subjected to either swimming or a state of quiescence to identify underlying exerkines. Our analyses revealed the presence of an exerkine, which was named as ApoA1tide. ApoA1tide significantly improved impaired cardiac function, reduced apoptosis, and diminished the inflammatory response and oxidative stress in mice with SC. The protective effect of ApoA1tide was mediated via autophagy, as evidenced by the finding that autophagy inhibition diminished the protective influence of ApoA1tide. In addition, we elucidated that C/EBPβ acts as the primary downstream effector of ApoA1tide. The silencing of C/EBPβ eradicated the amplification of autophagy induced by ApoA1tide, thereby negating its protective influence on cardiac function, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation.
Exercise preconditioning has been shown to attenuate LPS-induced alterations in arterial pressure in rats, suggesting that exercise training mitigates cardiovascular abnormalities during sepsis [ – ]. In addition, exercise contributes to cardiovascular protection by releasing beneficial exerkines into the circulation system [ ]. Peptides, which are significantly smaller than other biomolecules such as large proteins or antibodies, can effectively serve as mid-size therapeutics with high specificity and low toxicity. Therefore, we aimed to discover a new exercise-derived peptide that confers protection against SC.
Through plasma polypeptide omics and a literature review, we identified ApoA1tide, which contains six amino acids (Fig. ). ApoA1 is the main protein component of HDL, which may counteract the development of sepsis [ ]. Its cardioprotective mechanisms, including regulation of lipid metabolic homeostasis and suppression of inflammatory cascades, are well-characterized [ , ]. Notably, reconstituted HDL (rHDL) or HDL mimetic particles have advanced to clinical trials [ , ]. However, whether ApoA1-derived peptide exerts protective effect on SC is unclear.
To investigate this issue, LPS was used to induce sepsis conditions in mice. ApoA1tide significantly improved the survival rate and cardiac function of mice with sepsis as well as reduced the level of cTnI, a specific marker of sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (Fig. A). HE staining revealed that ApoA1tide attenuated LPS-induced myocardial damage, including myocardial fiber rupture and disorder, interstitial edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. I). Taken together, these data indicated that ApoA1tide ameliorates LPS-induced SC in mice.
Excessive cell apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses are widely recognized as primary contributors to sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction. It is well-established that the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines and ROS facilitates the activation of caspase-3 and subsequent cardiomyocyte apoptosis during SC [ – ]. In our subsequent study, we observed that ApoA1tide significantly enhanced cell viability and mitigated LPS-induced apoptosis in mice with sepsis. Furthermore, the expression levels of certain apoptosis-related proteins, such as cleaved caspase-3, Bax, and Bcl-2, in the heart tissues of mice were restored following ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. A-B). Similar results were observed in vitro (Fig. C-G). Notably, hm-ApoA1tide also exerted an antiapoptotic effect on LPS-treated AC16 cells (Fig. D-E). Considering the two-amino acid difference between the sequences of mouse ApoA1tide and hm-ApoA1tide, this finding further suggests the beneficial effects of ApoA1tide in mice and humans.
IL-1β, and TNF-α, IL-6 are the key inflammatory mediators in sepsis [ ]. In subsequent experiments, we observed elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the plasma and heart tissues of mice following LPS challenge, which were mitigated by ApoA1tide treatment (Fig. A-B). Furthermore, our findings revealed that ApoA1tide reduced LPS-induced increased ROS level in NRCMs (Fig. D-E).
Mitochondria are the main source of energy supply in cells and play a vital role in maintaining the homeostasis of cardiomyocytes. Mitochondrial dysfunction, associated with a decline in ATP content, is correlated with cardiac dysfunction during the late stage of sepsis [ ]. A previous study illustrated that an excessive amount of ROS directly causes mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes [ ]. Furthermore, dysfunctional mitochondria lose the antioxidant defensive capacity to detoxify ROS, leading to ultrastructural damage or even cell death [ ]. In this study, we determined the effect of ApoA1tide on mitochondrial function and revealed that ApoA1tide significantly improved mitochondrial function by increasing MMP (Fig. F). These findings indicate that ApoA1tide exerts a cardioprotective effect that is manifested as improvement of cardiac function and reduction of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses.
Next, we investigated the mechanism by which ApoA1tide exerts its cardioprotective effect. Previous studies have demonstrated the crucial role of autophagy in SC [ , ]. Autophagy can influence multiple cellular responses including metabolism balance, inflammation, and apoptosis [ ]. Autophagy deficiency caused by a high concentration of LPS may result in a series of pathological clinical manifestations, such as decreased cardiac function, increased inflammation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ]. In our study, we administered 20 mg/kg LPS to induce a severe septic condition, consistent with previous research [ ]. The autophagic level was slightly elevated, which was recognized as a failure of the persistent adaptive response during sepsis. Recent studies have demonstrated that enhancing autophagic activity contributes to cardioprotection [ , , ]. In the present study, we observed that pretreatment with ApoA1tide enhanced autophagic activity, as evidenced by an increased in the number of autophagosomes, elevated expression of LC3 II/LC3 I, and decreased expression of p62 compared with the LPS group (Fig. ).
To determine whether the cardioprotective effect of ApoA1tide was dependent on autophagy, autophagy inhibitors, 3-MA and BafA1 were used in our next experiment. We demonstrated that ApoA1tide specifically enhanced autophagosome biogenesis in LPS-induced mice and NRCMs. Furthermore, 3-MA counteracted the protective effect of ApoA1tide on SC, which was manifested by preventing ApoA1tide from improving the recovery of cardiac function in mice with sepsis and resisting the antioxidative stress and antiapoptotic effects of ApoA1tide (Fig. ). The results suggested that ApoA1tide ameliorates cardiac function by enhancing autophagy. Nevertheless, the mechanism by which ApoA1tide regulates autophagy remains elusive. To further elucidate the molecular mechanism, we performed RNA-seq on NRCMs following treatment with LPS or a combination of LPS and ApoA1tide, the transcription factor C/EBPβ was identified. Previous studies have shown that C/EBPβ is a potent activator of autophagy, playing a crucial role in various stages of the autophagy pathway by regulating genes such as Ulk1, Gabarapl1, LC3B, and Bnip3 [ ]. To further verify the expression of C/EBPβ under ApoA1tide stimulation, we detected the expression of C/EBPβ both in vivo and in vitro. Our data showed that ApoA1tide elevated C/EBPβ expression in both LPS-induced NRCMs and mice with sepsis. In addition, we demonstrated that knockdown of C/EBPβ abolishes the protective effect of ApoA1tide both in vitro and in vivo (Figs. and ).
While our current study focused on elucidating the C/EBPβ-autophagy axis in SC, other mechanisms related to autophagy should also be considered, such as the PI3K-Akt pathway, which is also enriched in KEGG enrichment analysis. It is documented that PI3Kγ signaling through Akt and mTOR inhibits NFκB activation while stimulating C/EBPβ activation, enhancing its transcriptional activity [ ]. On the contrary, deacetylating C/EBPβ via SIRT2 activates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [ ]. Moreover, Previous studies reported that NRG1-ErbB4-C/EBPβ and IGF1-PI3K-Akt signal pathways mediate exercise-induced myocardial adaptation [ , ]. Building upon established mechanisms of exercise-induced myocardial adaptation, the potential role of transcriptional regulation of C/EBPβ and its post-translational on autophagy need to be further explored.
Crucially, exercise promotes the secretion of ApoA1 and its cleavage into ApoA1tide. Although the two have a common amino acid sequence, their functional divergence is unequivocal: full-length ApoA1 exerts systemic anti-inflammatory effects via HDL-mediated LPS scavenging [ ], whereas ApoA1tide, on the other hand, acts more on cardiomyocytes through the C/EBPβ -mediated autophagy pathway, rather than relying on the lipid-binding domain of ApoA1. Whether ApoA1 shares autophagy-regulating functions analogous to ApoA1tide remains to be further studied. Therefore, our study originated from a classical lipid transport protein and innovatively identified a small-molecule peptide with cardioprotective effects.
Overall, we discovered a small molecule peptide with cardiovascular protective effects. This endogenous short peptide exhibits three distinct pharmacological advantages over native ApoA1 and existing analogues, which include reduced immunogenicity, attenuated cytotoxicity and economically viable synthesis processes. Our findings reveal that this ApoA1 derived-peptide confers myocardial protection through autophagy pathway modulation, which broadens the understanding of the molecular mechanism of ApoA1 in cardiovascular diseases.
Although we confirmed that ApoA1tide protects against SC by enhancing C/EBPβ-mediated autophagy, our study had several limitations. First, the use of LPS stimulation does not adequately capture the diversity and combinations of the infectious agents involved in human sepsis. Therefore, alternative sepsis models, such as cecal ligation and puncture, are required for a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective effects of ApoA1tide. Second, in this study, we did not thoroughly explore how circulating ApoA1tide activates intracellular C/EBPβ. Future studies will delve deeper into these aspects using receptor pulldown coupled with proteomics and conditional C/EBPβ knockout models. Finally, the molecule mechanism underlying the regulation of C/EBPβ by ApoA1tideis remains unknown. Further comprehensive investigations are warranted to conclusively establish this.

## Conclusion
This study provides insights into the role of exercise-derived ApoA1tide as a novel cardioprotective agent that attenuates LPS-induced SC by enhancing C/EBPβ-mediated autophagy. Our findings offer a potential therapeutic strategy for SC.
