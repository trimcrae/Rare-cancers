Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) offers neuroprotection, though its delivery across the blood–brain barrier remains a challenge. This study investigates exosomes as a vehicle to enhance brain delivery of HSP70 for treating chronic sleep deprivation. We engineered HEK293T cells to stably express HSP70 mRNA and the brain-targeting RVG-Lamp2b fusion protein, generating HSP70@ExoRVG exosomes. These were characterized via transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and Western blotting. In vitro uptake and HSP70 expression were assessed in neural progenitor cells (NPCs). In vivo efficacy was evaluated in a sleep-deprived mouse model using behavioral tests and molecular analyses of inflammatory and neurotrophic markers. HSP70@ExoRVG exosomes efficiently delivered HSP70 mRNA to NPCs, increasing intracellular HSP70 protein. In vivo, systemic administration restored memory performance, reduced hippocampal TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels, and increased IL-10. Treatment also elevated brain-derived neurotrophic factor and phosphorylated CREB, indicating enhanced neurotrophic signaling. These effects surpassed those of non-targeted or empty exosomes. Engineered exosomes enabled effective brain-targeted HSP70 mRNA delivery, reversing cognitive and inflammatory consequences of sleep deprivation. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of RVG-modified exosomes successfully delivering mRNA (rather than siRNA or miRNA) for the treatment of sleep deprivation–induced deficits. HSP70@ExoRVG offers a promising, noninvasive therapeutic strategy for sleep-related neurodegenerative conditions and highlights the potential of exosome-based mRNA delivery systems.

## Introduction
Sleep plays a fundamental role in maintaining cognitive function, synaptic plasticity, and neural immune regulation [ ]. Chronic sleep deprivation, a growing concern in modern society, has been causally linked to memory deficits, impaired learning, mood disorders, and increased vulnerability to neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. At the cellular level, sleep deprivation induces oxidative stress, disrupts neurotrophic signaling, and activates pro-inflammatory pathways, particularly in the hippocampus [ , ]. Despite the profound consequences of sleep deprivation on brain health, there is currently no effective molecular therapy that directly mitigates its long-term neurological impact.
Among endogenous protective mechanisms, heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) is a highly conserved molecular chaperone known for its potent anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects [ ]. HSP70 has been shown to regulate protein homeostasis [ ], inhibit NF-κB–mediated inflammation [ ], and reduce cellular apoptosis under stress conditions [ ]. Several studies have suggested that upregulation of HSP70 in the brain may protect against ischemia, traumatic injury, and neuroinflammation [ , ]. However, due to the impermeability of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), systemic delivery of HSP70 protein or plasmid DNA has shown limited therapeutic benefit [ ]. Moreover, direct brain injections or viral gene delivery raise safety and practicality concerns for clinical application [ ]. Thus, efficient and noninvasive delivery of functional HSP70 into the brain remains a major challenge.
Exosomes, a class of extracellular vesicles 30–150 nm in diameter, have emerged as promising drug delivery vehicles [ ]. They are naturally secreted by many cell types and are capable of carrying proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, including mRNA. Due to their biocompatibility and ability to cross the BBB [ ], exosomes are increasingly being explored for targeted delivery of therapeutic molecules to the central nervous system. Engineering exosomes to load therapeutic mRNA while incorporating targeting ligands has become a cutting-edge strategy. Notably, the incorporation of rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptides into exosomal membranes enables targeted delivery to neuronal cells by binding nicotinic acetylcholine receptors [ ]. However, most prior studies have focused on protein or microRNA delivery [ , ]. The potential of exosome-based mRNA therapy-especially using RVG-modified exosomes to deliver neuroprotective transcripts like HSP70 mRNA-remains underexplored. Furthermore, while exosomal delivery has been used in models of traumatic brain injury, stroke, or Alzheimer’s disease, there is a lack of studies that focus on its application to sleep deprivation–induced cognitive dysfunction. There is a clear need to bridge this gap by developing and validating brain-targeted mRNA delivery systems specifically for sleep deprivation-related cognitive impairment. Recent studies have highlighted the expanding landscape of mRNA therapeutics, particularly the use of exosome-based systems for delivering complex transcripts to hard-to-reach tissues [ , ].
In this study, we address these unmet needs by designing a novel exosomal delivery platform, HSP70@ExoRVG, which combines mRNA loading of HSP70 with RVG-mediated brain targeting. HEK293T cells were engineered to stably co-express HSP70 and RVG-Lamp2b, generating exosomes that simultaneously carried therapeutic mRNA and targeting motifs. We characterized the physicochemical properties of the engineered exosomes and confirmed efficient mRNA delivery into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) in vitro. Using a well-established sleep deprivation mouse model, established by the modified multiple-platform water environment method, we demonstrated that systemic administration of HSP70@ExoRVG significantly improved recognition memory, suppressed hippocampal inflammation, and enhanced neurotrophic signaling compared to non-targeted or unmodified exosome controls. This study aims to demonstrate the potential of exosome-mediated delivery of HSP70 mRNA-when combined with brain-targeting capability-in mitigating sleep deprivation-induced cognitive and molecular deficits.

## Methods

## Construction of HSP70@ExoRVG
To generate brain-targeted exosomes carrying HSP70 mRNA (HSP70@ExoRVG), the coding sequence of HSP70 was amplified based on its full-length cDNA using specific primers (Forward: 5′-ATGGCTCGTGCGGTCGGGATC-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTGGCCTCCCGGCCGTCGTC-3′). The PCR product and pLVX-IRES-Puro plasmid backbone were double-digested with EcoRI and BamHI, followed by purification with a DNA recovery kit. The insert and vector fragments were ligated using T4 DNA ligase. The ligation mixture was transformed into Stbl3 competent cells, and positive clones were selected by plating on LB agar containing 100 mg/mL ampicillin. Following incubation at 37 °C for 15 h, plasmids were extracted from positive colonies and verified by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing.
The RVG-Lamp2b construct was generated using a similar cloning strategy. The PCR primers used were: Forward 5′-TCTACTAGAGGATCTATTTCCGGTGAATTCACTAGTGCCACCATGGTGTGCTTCCGC-3′ and Reverse 5′-GGGGGAGGGAGAGGGGCGGGATCCGCGGCCGCTTAAGCGTAATCTGGCACATC-3′. After cloning and validation, both recombinant plasmids were used for lentiviral packaging.

## Lentiviral packaging and stable cell line generation
HEK293T cells were seeded in poly-L-lysine–coated dishes and cultured to approximately 90% confluence. For lentivirus packaging, cells were transfected with psPAX2 and VSVG helper plasmids along with the respective expression plasmids (pLVX, pLVX-HSP70, or pLVX-RVG-Lamp2b) using Lipofectamine 2000. Transfection complexes were prepared in serum-free DMEM, incubated for 20 min at room temperature, and added dropwise to the cells. After 5 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and stored at –80 °C.
For stable cell line generation, HEK293T cells were infected with lentivirus and selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days. To create dual-expressing cells, HEK293T cells stably overexpressing HSP70 were further infected with RVG-Lamp2b lentivirus. These cells were labeled with anti-Lamp2b-FITC antibodies and sorted by flow cytometry to isolate RVG-Lamp2b–positive populations. All stable lines were validated by qRT-PCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence to confirm co-expression of HSP70 and RVG-Lamp2b.

## Exosome isolation and characterization
Exosomes were isolated from conditioned medium of the stably transfected HEK293T cells using a combination of ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation, following guidelines from the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV). Cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 48 h to minimize contamination from bovine exosomes. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1,000 ×g for 30 min to remove cell debris and larger vesicles. The clarified medium was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and concentrated using Vivaspin-20 ultrafiltration columns. The concentrated solution was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 ×g for 70 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed once with PBS and centrifuged again at 100,000 ×g for 70 min. The final exosome pellet was resuspended in 50–100 μL PBS and stored at –80 °C for further use.
Characterization of exosomes nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and western blotting. NTA was performed using a NanoSight system to measure particle size distribution and concentration. Exosome doses were normalized based on protein concentration using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). We further validated the particle-to-protein ratio, ensuring a coefficient of variation (CV) < 8.1% across batches. Batch-to-batch variability was strictly controlled, with CVs for size, marker expression, and HSP70 content all maintained below 15%. For protein marker analysis, exosome lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted with antibodies against Alix (#2171, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), CD63 (ab217345, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), and Tsg101 (ab83, Abcam) to confirm exosomal identity. Additional western blotting was performed to assess RVG-Lamp2b (ab18529, Abcam) protein loading.

## Sleep deprivation model and treatment protocol
To model chronic sleep deprivation, 6-8 week-old C57BL/6 J male mice (a total of 48 mice) were subjected to the modified multiple-platform water environment method. The deprivation chamber measured 41 × 34 × 17 cm and contained 12 small platforms (3 cm diameter) partially submerged so that mice could not enter REM sleep without falling into the water. Mice were placed in the chamber for 20 h daily and returned to dry cages for 4 h of rest for 7 consecutive days. Control mice were placed in similar chambers with larger platforms (6 cm diameter), allowing normal sleep.
A total of 48 male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly divided into experimental groups receiving either saline, non-targeted exosomes, RVG-targeted exosomes, or HSP70-loaded exosomes with or without RVG modification. Exosomes (50 μg in 100 μL saline) were administered via tail vein injection once daily for 7 days, starting on the first day of sleep deprivation.
At the end of the study, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. To avoid blood contamination, mice underwent transcardial perfusion with 50 mL of pre-cooled PBS until the liver was blanched. The tissues were collected for further biochemical assays.
The study was approved by the ethics committee of Quanzhou First Hospital, and was performed in strict accordance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23 Rev. 1985).

## Behavioral testing
Cognitive function was evaluated using the novel object recognition (NOR) and Y-maze tests (Figure ). For NOR, mice were habituated to the testing arena for 10 min at day 14. During the training phase (day 15), animals explored two identical objects for 3 min. 24 h later, one object was replaced with a novel one. Exploration time was recorded, and the discrimination index (DI) was calculated as (Time_novel - Time_familiar) / (Total_exploration_time). The Y-maze apparatus (30 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm, opaque resin) was used to assess spatial memory under fixed spatial cues. Novel arm exploration (5 min training, 5 min test after 30 min) and spontaneous alternation (5 min free exploration) were recorded. The alternation rate was calculated as [Number of alternations / (Total arm entries - 2)] × 100%.
Representative training and exploratory trajectories for both NOR and Y-maze tests are shown in Figure , illustrating the experimental protocols and animal behavior patterns during cognitive assessment.

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR
Total RNA from cells or exosomes was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry. Reverse transcription was performed using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit with random hexamers. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Reactions were run in triplicate, and thermal cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 sec and 60 °C for 1 min.
Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. GAPDH was used as the endogenous control. The following primers were used for amplification: HSP70: GCATCGAGACTATCGCTAATGAG (forward), TGCAAGGTTAGATTTTTCTGCCT (reverse); GAPDH: TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG (forward), ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT (reverse);
Lamp2b: GAAAATGCCACTTGCCTTTATGC (forward), AGGAAAAGCCAGGTCCGAAC (reverse).

## Western blot and ELISA
Western blot was conducted followed a routine procedure. The following antibodies were used including p-CREB (#9198, CST), t-CREB (#4820, CST), BDNF (ab108319, Abcam), Bax (50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), Bcl-2 (26593-1-AP, Proteintech), cleaved caspase-3 (#9661, CST), GFAP (16825-1-AP, Proteintech), Iba-1 (#17198, CST), β-actin (#4967, CST).
ELISA assays included the kits for TNF-α (EM0183), IL-6 (EM0121), IL-1β (EM0109), IL-10 (EM0100), which were from FineTest (Wuhan, China). The assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Statistical analysis
Sample size (n = 8 for in vivo, n = 3 for in vitro) was determined by power analysis to achieve ≥80% power at α = 0.05. Data normality was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test. All measurement data followed a normal distribution (mean ± SD). Variance homogeneity was assessed by the Brown-Forsythe test. For unequal variances, Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test was used. Exact p-values are reported in the figures. For behavioral studies and biochemical assays, sample size per group (e.g., n = 8 for animal experiments and n = 3 for cell-based assays) was based on prior publications and power estimation to detect meaningful differences. All p-values were two-tailed, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was used for all statistical analyses and figure generation.

## Results

## Stable overexpression of RVG-Lamp2b and HSP70 established in HEK293T cells
To establish a system for generating engineered exosomes, HEK293T cells were stably transfected with RVG-Lamp2b, HSP70, or both constructs. As shown in Fig. , qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated a significant increase in Lamp2b mRNA expression in the pLVX-RVG-Lamp2b and pLVX-RVG-Lamp2b + HSP70 groups compared to control (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, HSP70 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the pLVX-HSP70 and pLVX-RVG-Lamp2b + HSP70 groups (Fig. ; p < 0.001), confirming successful transcription of the transgenes. 
To validate protein-level expression, western blotting was performed (Fig. ). Lamp2b protein levels were markedly increased in RVG-Lamp2b-expressing cells, with or without co-expression of HSP70. Quantification (Fig. ) confirmed a significant elevation of Lamp2b protein (p < 0.01). Similarly, HSP70 protein was robustly expressed in cells transfected with HSP70 alone or in combination with RVG-Lamp2b (Fig. ; p < 0.05 to p < 0.01). These results confirm that HEK293T cells were successfully engineered to stably express RVG-Lamp2b and HSP70, both at the mRNA and protein levels.

## Engineered exosomes show high HSP70 mRNA content and RVG-Lamp2b protein enrichment
Following successful cell line generation, exosomes (HSP70@ExoRVG) were isolated and characterized. Nanoparticle tracking analysis showed a particle size distribution centered around 100 nm (Fig. ), confirming the expected physical profile. 
Exosomal markers Alix, CD63, and Tsg101 were detected by western blotting (Fig. ), verifying the identity of the isolated vesicles. Importantly, qRT-PCR demonstrated that HSP70 mRNA was highly enriched in HSP70@ExoRVG compared to control exosomes (Fig. ; p < 0.001), indicating successful loading of the transcript. Moreover, RVG-Lamp2b and HSP70 proteins were both elevated in the exosome lysates, as shown in Fig. . Quantitative analysis (Fig. ) confirmed significantly increased levels of both proteins in HSP70@ExoRVG compared to controls (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that HSP70@ExoRVG effectively encapsulates HSP70 mRNA and expresses the targeting ligand RVG-Lamp2b.

## HSP70@ExoRVG efficiently delivers HSP70 mRNA to NPCs
To evaluate the delivery efficiency of engineered exosomes, NPCs were treated with varying doses of HSP70@ExoRVG. As shown in Fig. , HSP70 mRNA levels in NPCs increased in a dose-dependent manner after 6 h of exposure, indicating efficient uptake and transcript delivery. ELISA analysis further confirmed increased intracellular HSP70 protein levels in NPCs treated with 100 μg of HSP70@ExoRVG, peaking at 6 h post-treatment (Fig. ). These results validate that HSP70@ExoRVG can deliver functional mRNA and translate it into protein within neural cells. 

## HSP70@ExoRVG improves cognitive performance in sleep-deprived mice
To investigate functional relevance in vivo, HSP70@ExoRVG was administered to sleep-deprived mice, and cognitive performance was assessed. In the novel object recognition test, mice treated with HSP70@ExoRVG spent significantly more time exploring the novel object (Fig. ) and showed a higher discrimination index (Fig. ) compared to vehicle, unmodified exosome, and RVG-only exosome groups (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). Representative behavioral trajectories and performance patterns during both tests are illustrated in Figure . 
In the Y-maze test, HSP70@ExoRVG-treated mice also spent more time in the novel arm (Fig. ) and exhibited improved spontaneous alternation behavior (Fig. ), suggesting enhanced spatial working memory. In contrast, exosomes lacking either brain-targeting capability or mRNA cargo showed little to no improvement. These results demonstrate that brain-targeted delivery of HSP70 mRNA via exosomes can restore memory function impaired by sleep deprivation.

## HSP70@ExoRVG attenuates hippocampal neuroinflammation in sleep-deprived mice
Next, we assessed the anti-inflammatory effects of HSP70@ExoRVG in the hippocampus. ELISA showed that treatment significantly reduced levels of TNF-α (Fig. ), IL-6 (Fig. ), and IL-1β (Fig. ), while increasing IL-10 expression (Fig. ). Compared to the non-targeted HSP70@Exo group, HSP70@ExoRVG elicited stronger suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and a more pronounced increase in IL-10 (p < 0.01 to p < 0.001). These results indicate that brain-targeted exosomal delivery of HSP70 mRNA effectively mitigates neuroinflammation associated with sleep deprivation, as further evidenced by the reduction in GFAP and Iba-1 protein expression (Figure ). While molecular markers indicate reduced inflammation, future studies using immunohistochemistry for microglia would provide additional spatial context. 

## HSP70@ExoRVG upregulates neurotrophic signaling in the hippocampus
Finally, we examined whether HSP70@ExoRVG enhances neurotrophic factor expression. Western blotting revealed that brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and p-CREB were significantly upregulated in the hippocampus of mice treated with HSP70@ExoRVG (Fig. ). Densitometric quantification showed a significant increase in both BDNF (Fig. ) and p-CREB/CREB ratio (Fig. ), as shown in the representative blots (Fig. ), when compared to sleep-deprived controls and the HSP70@Exo group (p < 0.05 to p < 0.01). Additionally, HSP70@ExoRVG treatment significantly reduced the expression of pro-apoptotic markers Bax and cleaved caspase-3 while increasing anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 levels in the hippocampus (Figure ), suggesting a protective effect against sleep deprivation-induced apoptosis. These results suggest that brain-targeted delivery of HSP70 mRNA can enhance neuroplasticity pathways and contribute to cognitive recovery. 

## Discussions
This study presents a novel strategy for addressing the cognitive and molecular consequences of chronic sleep deprivation by using engineered exosomes to deliver HSP70 mRNA specifically to the brain. Our data show that RVG-modified exosomes carrying HSP70 mRNA (HSP70@ExoRVG) significantly improve memory performance, suppress hippocampal neuroinflammation, and enhance neurotrophic signaling in sleep deprivation mice. These findings establish the therapeutic potential of mRNA-loaded, brain-targeted exosomes in modulating the deleterious effects of sleep deprivation, which remains a largely untreated condition in clinical practice.
Previous work has demonstrated that sleep deprivation leads to impairments in hippocampal-dependent memory and promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. For example, previous studies [ , ] described how sleep deprivation-induced inflammation alters synaptic homeostasis and neural plasticity. While anti-inflammatory agents such as NSAIDs or glucocorticoids have been tested in experimental settings, they are not suitable for long-term use due to systemic side effects and lack of brain specificity. Our results suggest that HSP70@ExoRVG achieves a similar or superior anti-inflammatory effect with localized action and a favorable safety profile.
HSP70 has been previously identified as a key endogenous modulator of stress responses in the brain. It was also [ , ] shown that HSP70 overexpression in transgenic mice or viral-mediated gene delivery can reduce infarct size and apoptosis in models of stroke and traumatic brain injury. However, the clinical translation of HSP70 therapy has been hindered by poor delivery efficiency and the inability to target specific cell types in the brain. Unlike those earlier approaches, our study employs a nonviral, exosome-based system that delivers mRNA rather than protein or plasmid DNA, offering improved biocompatibility, flexibility, and translational potential.
The transition from delivering small RNAs or proteins to delivering full-length mRNA via exosomes represents a significant advancement in therapeutic precision. mRNA delivery allows for transient, tunable protein expression without the risks associated with genomic integration. The use of exosomes as carriers of therapeutic RNA is supported by recent breakthroughs in regenerative medicine and oncology. For example, Kamerkar et al. [ ] used exosomes to deliver siRNA to KRAS-mutant pancreatic cancer cells, demonstrating tumor regression in vivo. Similarly, another study [ ] delivered mRNA via exosomes to restore myocardial function after cardiac injury. However, only a few studies have focused on exosomal mRNA delivery for central nervous system (CNS) applications. Alvarez-Erviti et al. (2011) were among the first to show that RVG-modified exosomes could deliver siRNA to neurons and glial cells. Our work extends this concept by demonstrating that RVG-targeted exosomes can efficiently deliver functional mRNA (not just siRNA) into neural progenitor cells and mitigate CNS damage caused by sleep deprivation.
Notably, previous exosome-based studies have focused largely on miRNA delivery (e.g., miR-124 [ ], miR-132 [ ]) in stroke or neurodegeneration models. Compared to miRNAs, mRNA delivery provides the advantage of transient and tunable protein expression, allowing for the therapeutic translation of stress-response proteins such as HSP70. Our data provide clear evidence that exosomal HSP70 mRNA was translated into protein in recipient NPCs, and that the observed benefits-including improved memory performance and increased hippocampal BDNF and p-CREB levels-were specific to mRNA loading and brain targeting. Neither unmodified exosomes nor RVG-modified exosomes lacking HSP70 cargo yielded comparable effects, reinforcing the critical importance of dual functionalization.
In addition to improved cognition, HSP70@ExoRVG significantly modulated hippocampal cytokine profiles, increasing IL-10 and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines. The low basal level of IL-10 in control mice reflects the physiological steady state in the absence of inflammatory stress, whereas the increase in sleep deprivation and treatment groups represents a compensatory and therapeutic response, respectively. This is consistent with the known immunomodulatory role of HSP70 in macrophages and microglia [ ]. Our findings further show that HSP70@ExoRVG upregulates BDNF and p-CREB, both of which are suppressed under sleep deprivation conditions and are key regulators of memory formation and synaptic plasticity. These results support the hypothesis that HSP70 exerts its neuroprotective function through both anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic mechanisms.
Despite these promising outcomes, several limitations should be addressed. First, while we demonstrated HSP70 mRNA delivery and function in NPCs and whole-brain extracts, future studies should use cell-type–specific markers to confirm the identity of exosome-recipient cells in vivo. Second, we did not conduct long-term behavioral assessments or monitor the biodistribution and clearance kinetics of HSP70@ExoRVG. These factors will be important for understanding safety and optimizing dosage for potential clinical use. Third, the effects of HSP70 on synaptic remodeling, mitochondrial function, and autophagy in the sleep deprivation brain remain to be elucidated.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate that exosomal delivery of HSP70 mRNA—when combined with RVG-mediated brain targeting—can reverse sleep deprivation-induced cognitive impairment and neuroinflammation. Our work expands the application of exosomal mRNA therapeutics to neurological disease and supports the feasibility of a modular delivery platform for stress-response genes. Given the prevalence of sleep deprivation and its association with chronic neurological and psychiatric disorders, HSP70@ExoRVG represents a promising intervention that merits further development toward clinical translation.
