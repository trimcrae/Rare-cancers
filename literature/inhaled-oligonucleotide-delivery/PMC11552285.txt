## Abstract
A series of triazole-tethered triazines bearing pharmacophoric features of DNA-targeting agents and non-hydroxamate MMPs inhibitors were synthesized and screened against HCT-116, Caco-2 cells, and normal colonocytes by MTT assay. 7a and 7g surpassed doxorubicin against HCT-116 cells regarding potency (IC50 = 0.87 and 1.41 nM) and safety (SI = 181.93 and 54.41). 7g was potent against liver cancer (HepG-2; IC50 = 65.08 nM), the main metastatic site of CRC with correlation to MMP-13 expression. Both derivatives induced DNA damage at 2.67 and 1.87 nM, disrupted HCT-116 cell cycle and triggered apoptosis by 33.17% compared to doxorubicin (DNA damage at 0.76 nM and 40.21% apoptosis induction). 7g surpassed NNGH against MMP-10 (IC50 = 0.205 μM) and MMP-13 (IC50 = 0.275 μM) and downregulated HCT-116 VEGF related to CRC progression by 38%. Docking and MDs simulated ligands-receptors binding modes and highlighted SAR. Their ADMET profiles, drug-likeness and possible off-targets were computationally predicted.
Keywords: Colorectal cancer; DNA targeting; MMP-10/13 inhibition; 1,2,3-triazole-tethered s-triazines; molecular dynamics

## Introduction
Cancer continues to be a barrier to increased human longevity and satisfactory life quality . Recent WHO cancer statistics addressed the burden posed by cancer globally, where almost 19.3 million cases and 10 million deaths were recognized in 2020 with expectations to project to ∼28.4 million cases by 2040 . Colorectal cancer (CRC) is ranked the fourth most common incident and the third most fatal globally according to GLOBOCAN 2018 . The main CRC therapeutic approach is surgical excision accompanied by chemotherapeutic treatment with a special focus on DNA-binding agents as exemplified by doxorubicin being cost-effective compared to other anticancer agents . However, due to distant metastasis, nearly 30% of advanced-stage cases reported recurrence and high death rates . The severe adverse effects of conventional therapy, CRC molecular complexity, and resistance mechanisms also indicate that it is nearly impossible to achieve the desirable treatment goals using single drug therapy , . Alternatively, the multitarget therapy approach grants not only enhanced anticancer efficacy and improved safety but also tackles resistance mechanisms .
Since the structural elucidation of DNA by Watson, Crick, and Wilkins in 1953 , DNA has been viewed as a structurally precise molecular target for cancer therapy. Drug discovery studies initiated during the 1960s employed molecular modeling for introducing DNA intercalators that were advanced to clinical trials and worldwide use . Antibiotic research prompted the identification of DNA-binding anthracycline doxorubicin that proved efficacy against a variety of malignancies . Continuous progress led to a plethora of small molecules targeting DNA . Along these lines, extensive research has focused on investigating the mode of action of DNA-targeting agents and the related cellular events in the tumor environment , . Most recently an elegant study investigated the binding modes and thermodynamic parameters of various DNA-damaging agents (doxorubicin, mitoxantrone, ethidium bromide, and capecitabine) with the DNA duplex in different cellular environments employing combined microcalorimetry and fluorescence spectroscopy . Results revealed that oxidative stressed conditions, as in the tumor microenvironment, hindered the drug’s DNA targeting efficacy . In this context, a growing body of evidence linked oxidative stress in tumor microenvironment to matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) , a family of nearly 26 zinc-dependent endopeptidases . MMPs are viewed as the central signaling nodes of colorectal epithelial-mesenchymal transition linked to distant metastasis , , promoters of angiogenesis , and key players in tumor progression . Further mechanistic studies also highlighted the correlation between DNA damage and MMPs, where accumulated DNA damage is linked to the genetic induction of MMPs . On the other hand, MMPs knockdown significantly promoted DNA fragmentation , and halted its repair pathway . Thus, MMPs inhibition seems to be a complementary therapeutic strategy to DNA-damaging approach. Reviewing literature revealed that designing MMP inhibitors has relied on understanding the biochemical nature and the crystal structures of MMPs assisted by computational studies .The MMPs family comprises collagenases, gelatinases, matrilysins, stromelysins, membrane-type MMPs, and others . Structurally, all members have nearly identical catalytic domains with the active site Zn2+ orchestrated by neighboring histidine residues. The catalytic domain is divided into C- and N-terminals subdomains by a shallow cleft with six binding pockets. S1′ subsite is regarded as the MMP selectivity pocket due to the variance of the pocket’s composition among the MMPs family members , , . Early inhibitors were designed as structural mimics of endogenous ligands capped with hydroxamic acid, a zinc-binding group (ZBG) . Despite their high anticancer potency , hydroxamates failed in clinic due to side effects, dose-limiting toxicity , and pharmacokinetic challenges associated with the hydroxamic acid moiety. The doubts about the suitability of hydroxamate MMPs inhibitors then directed research strategies to avoid hydroxamic acid for eliminating off-target side effects and pharmacokinetic problems , . These non-hydroxamate MMPs inhibitors share certain thematic structural features, such as being long with linked aromatic rings or otherwise planar. Despite being hydrophobic in general, amino and carbonyl groups are usually incorporated to allow additional hydrophilic interactions within the catalytic domain , , .
Taken together this study aims to structure optimization of novel DNA-binding agents endowed with intrinsic complementary MMPs inhibition, especially the druggable isoforms related to DNA damage (MMP-9 , MMP-10 , and MMP-13 ) as multitarget anticancer agents for halting CRC progression. Such a polypharmacology strategy is intended to augment the anticancer efficiency of DNA binding/damaging through concomitant MMPs inhibition. From another point of view, direct MMP-9 and -13 inhibition by rationally designed inhibitors can also inhibit angiogenesis which is crucial for metastasis majorly through lowering the expression of the major angiogenic factor VEGF .

## Rational design
The target dual-acting molecules were designed to imitate the thematic structural features of DNA-binding agents and MMPs inhibitors. The scaffold was thus rationalized to incorporate multiple aromatic rings for intercalation between the DNA strands together with incorporating functionalities conferring hydrogen bonding interactions with DNA bases . On the other hand, the MMPs inhibitory component of the designed hybrids was based on the non-hydroxamate model inhibitors being generally long hydrophobic molecules with amino and carbonyl groups offering some hydrophilic interactions with the catalytic site , , . With that, the hybrid scaffolds were tailored as triazole-tethered s-triazines bearing phenyl rings spaced by amide linkage as hydrogen bond donor/acceptor motifs. Firstly, the heterocyclic rings linked to the phenyl ring may generally assemble the lengthy aromatic system required for DNA intercalation together with resembling the features of hydrophobic MMPs inhibitors, whereas the amide linkages may pose hydrogen bonding and hydrophilic interactions favored for both mechanisms. Secondly, literature review revealed that the triazine ring holds potentiality as the heterocyclic core of numerous potent DNA-binding agents and inhibitors of MMPs, especially the targeted isoforms MMP-9 , -10, and -13 , . The triazole ligation was also rationalized as non-classical amide isostere offering opportunities for hydrogen bonding interactions with the MMP catalytic domain and DNA bases together with allowing DNA intercalation being a planar aromatic ring. This is reflected by its widespread representation as an integral motif of various DNA-binding agents and potent MMPs inhibitors . Moreover, the design strategy adopted broadening the chemical space of the designed compounds and influencing their electronic and steric environment to perform SAR study via diversification of the terminal phenyl substitution and isomeric switch of the amide linkage while conserving the heterocyclic rings. Accordingly, a library of fourteen new derivatives was successfully synthesized and screened for their cytotoxic activities against HCT-116 and Caco-2 CRC cells relative to normal human colonocytes by MTT assay in reference to the DNA intercalator doxorubicin. The most promising derivatives regarding potency and selectivity were subjected to further MTT experiments on HepG-2 liver cancer cells to probe their activity against tumorigenesis in the liver, the first metastasis site of CRC , which was found to be correlated to MMP-13 expression . The hit derivatives were then evaluated for their DNA binding affinity and damaging potential via employing luminescent terbium (III) chloride (Tb3+) biosensor in comparison to doxorubicin, followed by in vitro assessment of their MMP-9, -10, and -13 inhibitory activities in reference to the broad-spectrum MMPs inhibitor NNGH . The HCT-116 cellular responses secondary to DNA damage and MMPs inhibition were then studied. In this part of the study, HCT-116 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis induction were analyzed following treatment with the hit derivatives compared to doxorubicin. Herein, apoptosis may be triggered not only by DNA damage , but also by MMPs inhibition where several reports demonstrated the vital role of MMP-13 in tumor cell growth especially CRC growth and proliferation , . These studies were mirrored by experiments investigating the effects of MMP-13 inhibition or downregulation on apoptosis induction, where targeted inhibition of MMP-13 suppressed tumor growth, reduced the number of proliferating cells , and induced apoptosis . Furthermore, animal studies showed that selective MMP-13 inhibition delayed primary tumor growth and onset of tumor-associated lesions . Similarly, MMP-10 stimulated cell growth, exerted in vitro antiapoptotic properties in CRC , , and was considered as a negative prognostic marker in colon cancer patients . Besides, targeting MMP-10 with siRNA in vivo resulted in a diminution of xenograft tumor growth with a concomitant reduction of angiogenesis and stimulation of apoptosis . Downregulation of VEGF protein levels was also quantified in HCT-116 as another cellular response to MMPs inhibition , besides evaluating the antiangiogenic activity of the studied compounds relying on studies correlating VEGF to proliferation, vascularity, and metastasis of colon cancer . Finally, docking and molecular dynamics were simulated to aid in exploring the most stable binding mode of the hit derivatives within their molecular targets and concluding the structural determinants of activities. A brief computational ADMET study was then conducted to assess the drug-likeness of the studied lead compounds followed by in silico prediction of the most promising derivative’s probable off-targets ( ).
 

## Results and discussion

## Chemistry
The designed triazine derivatives 4 and 7a–h were synthesized according to . The starting material 2-chloro-4,6-dimethoxytriazine (1) was reacted with propargylamine in the presence of anhydrous K2CO3 in dioxane to produce the N-propynyl derivative 2 as previously reported . Copper-catalyzed [3 + 2] cycloaddition reaction , of the alkyne derivative 2 with 4-azidobenzamide (3) and 4-azidobenzoic acid (5) then afforded the corresponding triazoles 4 and 6, respectively. The IR spectrum of 4 proved the absence of an absorption band assigned to the alkyne group and showed the novel absorption bands at 3396, 3313, and 1703 cm−1 assignable to NH2 and carbonyl groups, respectively. Its 1H NMR spectrum revealed singlet signals at δ 3.79 ppm and δ 4.59 ppm assigned to the methoxy and methylene protons, respectively. Furthermore, it assured the existence of two multiplets at δ 8.10 and 7.50 ppm for NH2 protons and the distinctive singlet peak found at δ 8.70 ppm corresponded to the triazole C5–H. According to the 13C NMR spectrum, signals at δ 36.48, 54.66, and 54.73 ppm, respectively, corresponding to the methylene and methoxy carbons were verified. The triazine ring carbons resonated as anticipated at δ 168.23, 172.24, and 172.53 ppm . Also, the diagnostic carbonyl carbon was detected at δ 167.27 ppm, besides the aromatic carbons at δ 119.91–172.53 ppm. IR spectrum for compound 6 showed a broad absorption band at 3400–2400 cm−1 and a strong band at 1693 cm−1 characteristic for the carboxylic acid group. In the 1H NMR spectrum of 6, the characteristic singlet for the triazole C5–H was assigned at δ 8.72 ppm. Also, the broad singlet at δ 13.27 ppm indicated the presence of the carboxylic acid proton.
 The title compounds 7a–h were synthesized by the coupling of compound 6 with various aniline derivatives utilizing the DIC/Oxyma coupling cocktail in the presence of DIPEA , . The IR spectra of 7a–h showed absorption bands diagnostic to the amide groups at 3382–3325 cm−1 (NH) and 1690–1642 cm−1 (C = O). Furthermore, the IR spectra of 7b and 7g exhibited an absorption band at 1357 and 1358 cm−1, respectively assigned to the C–F bond. The 1H NMR spectra of compounds 7a, 7c, and 7d showed two singlet signals at δ 3.79 and 3.80 ppm corresponding to the methoxy protons, while 7b and 7e–h showed only one singlet signal at δ 3.79–3.81 ppm. The methylene protons appeared as doublets at δ 4.61–4.63 ppm for 7a and 7d–f and singlets at δ 4.61–4.63 ppm for 7b, 7c, 7g, and 7h. The aromatic protons of 7a–h resonated at δ range of 6.92–8.26 ppm. In the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 7a–h, diagnostic singlets were observed at δ 8.74–8.76 ppm range assigned to triazole C5–H. Furthermore, two D2O exchangeable singlet signals were detected at δ 8.42–8.44 and 10.25–10.91 ppm corresponding to the NH and CONH, respectively.13C NMR spectra of 7a–h showed signals at δ 36.46–36.53 ppm and 54.63–54.72 ppm ranges corresponding to the methylene and methoxy carbons. Moreover, signals at δ 164.46–167.78 ppm assigned to the amide carbonyl carbons were observed. Aromatic carbons appeared at their expected chemical shifts.
Referring to , the reaction of compound 2 with 4-nitrophenyl azide (8) using ‘click’ [3 + 2] cycloaddition reaction conditions , afforded the triazole derivative 9. The synthesis of compound 8 took place according to the previously reported method . The spectroscopic data of compound 9 were consistent with its structure. IR spectrum of 9 showed absorption bands at 1559 and 1343 cm−1 corresponding to symmetric and asymmetric stretching for the NO2 group. In addition, its 1H NMR spectrum showed a singlet signal at δ 8.82 ppm, confirming the cyclization of the triazole derivative. Reduction of the nitro group using iron, hydrochloric acid, and ammonium chloride at 65 °C afforded the corresponding amine 10. The IR spectrum of this compound showed two significant bands at 3411 and 3340 cm−1 corresponding to the NH2 group. The 1H NMR spectrum showed NH2 protons as a singlet at δ 5.45 ppm. Other protons resonated as expected.
 Benzoic acid derivatives were coupled with compound 10 using DIC/Oxyma coupling reagents in the presence of DIPEA to produce the targeted benzamides 11a–h as outlined in Scheme 2 , . IR spectra of 11a–h showed bands in the range of 3417–3294 cm−1 and 1674–1652 cm−1 diagnostic to (NH) and (C = O) groups, respectively. Moreover, IR spectra of compounds 11b and 11g showed a significant band at 1372 cm−1 corresponding to the C–F bond, whereas the IR spectrum of 11e showed additional symmetric and asymmetric absorption bands at 1555 and 1363 cm−1 assigned to the NO2 group. 1H NMR spectra of 11a–c and 11g showed only one singlet signal at δ 3.79 or 3.80 ppm assigned to the methoxy protons. However, compounds 11d–f showed two singlet signals at δ 3.79 and 3.80 ppm. Also, the methylene protons appeared as singlets at δ 4.58 or 4.59 ppm for compounds 11a–h except in 11f the same protons were observed as a doublet at δ 4.59 ppm (J = 5 Hz). An additional singlet peak at δ 2.35 ppm was observed in compound 11f assigned to the p-methyl group. Furthermore, compound 11h showed a singlet signal at δ 3.80 ppm integrated for the nine methoxy protons. In the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 11a–h, aromatic protons appeared at the range δ 7.00–8.36 ppm, in addition to characteristic singlets at δ 8.55–8.57 ppm range assigned to triazole C5–H. Furthermore, two D2O exchangeable singlets were observed at δ 8.40 or 8.41 ppm and δ 10.30–10.77 ppm corresponding to the NH and NHCO, respectively. 13C NMR spectra of 11a–h detected the presence of signals at δ 36.50–36.51 and 54.66–54.74 ppm corresponding to the methylene and methoxy carbons. Additional signals were observed at δ 21.56 and 55.98 ppm for compounds 11f and 11h, respectively corresponding to the p-methyl and p-methoxy carbons. Moreover, signals at δ 164.63–166.31 ppm were assigned to the amide carbonyls as well as the aromatic carbons, which resonated at their expected chemical shifts.

## Biology

## Cytotoxicity screening
All the newly synthesized triazine derivatives were preliminary tested for their cytotoxic potential on normal colonocytes, HCT-116, and Caco-2 cells in comparison to doxorubicin via MTT assay ( and ). The highest cytotoxicity against HCT-116 cells was exhibited by the triazine derivatives 7a (IC50 = 0.87 ± 0.22 nM) and 7g (IC50 = 1.41 ± 0.18 nM), followed by 11b (IC50 = 12.18 ± 0.23 nM) and 7h (IC50 =12.35 ± 0.35 nM). 7f (IC50 = 27.61 ± 1.01 nM) and 7d (IC50 = 27.91 ± 0.98 nM) exhibited relatively moderate potency, whereas the remainder derivatives exhibited IC50 values ranging from 32.92 to 79.74 nM. All compounds were more potent than doxorubicin.
 Besides considering cytotoxic potency against HCT-116 cells, assessment of their safety on normal colonocytes and selectivity to tumor cells is the key to real evaluation of the studied triazines. Interestingly, all compounds except 11a, 11d–f, and 11h as well as the precursor 4 were selective to HCT-116 cells. The highest selectivity index was recorded for 7a (SI = 181.93), followed by 7g (SI = 54.41). 7h, 7f, 7d, 7b, and 7c exhibited remarkably high safety profiles and reported selectivity index values exceeding 3. Further MTT experiments on Caco-2 cells revealed that the studied compounds exhibited lower cytotoxic activities against Caco-2 cells than HCT-116 cells regarding potency and selectivity. However, a nearly similar pattern was observed where the most active derivatives were 7g (IC50 = 171.28 ± 4.18 nM) and 7a (IC50 = 207.98 ± 23.41 nM) followed by 7h (IC50 =434.81 ± 3.69 nM), 7f (IC50 = 513.51 ± 13.30 nM), 7c (IC50 = 542.61 ± 15.67 nM) and 11b (IC50 = 550.15 ± 0.92 nM). Although all compounds were more active than doxorubicin (except 7b), their selectivity to Caco-2 over normal colonic cells was beyond the acceptable limit.
Morphological examination of HCT-116 cells, using a phase contrast microscope, after treatment with 7a and 7g demonstrated characteristic shrinkage reflecting the apoptotic potential of the tested compounds in comparison to the untreated cells (Supplementary Data, Figure S82). It is worth mentioning that the wells were examined after 24, 48, and 72 h treatment. However, the marked cellular collapse was noted only after 72 h in the treated wells.
Cytotoxicity studies were extended to explore the antiproliferative activities of the most promising derivatives 7a and 7g against liver cancer, the most common site of metastasis in CRC patients , . Results showed that 7g (IC50 = 65.08 ± 4.33 nM) was superior to 7a (IC50 = 1477.41 ± 227.99 nM) and even more potent to doxorubicin (IC50 = 355 ± 24 nM) against HepG-2 cells.

## DNA binding/damage and HCT-116 apoptosis studies
The most promising cytotoxic agents 7a and 7g were selected for studying their DNA binding/damage potential utilizing luminescent terbium (III) chloride biosensor compared to doxorubicin as a positive control, followed by analysis of HCT-116 cell cycle and apoptosis induction secondary to DNA binding and damage , following treatment with the hit derivatives in reference to doxorubicin.

## Tb3+ fluorescence analysis of DNA interaction/damage
Nucleic acids genosensors are the first choice for selective and sensitive determination of different forms of damage induced in nucleic acids , , . For the current work, terbium (III) chloride (Tb3+) was selected as a luminescent genosensor for investigating any possible drug-induced DNA damage. Terbium ion as a trivalent lanthanide, in aqueous solutions, has low native fluorescence due to low cross-sectional absorption and non-radiative decay of OH vibrations of water molecules. After Tb3+ genosensor is added to the mixture, it binds to the DNA in the medium. In double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), the Tb3+ binding site varies from that in single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). Tb3+ ions, in undamaged dsDNA, are attracted electrostatically to the DNA-phosphate backbone. On the other hand, in ssDNA, Tb3+ coordinates with electron lone pairs of the free nucleobases. Thus, after DNA excitation, energy is transferred to the Tb3+ biosensor causing an increase in its fluorescence . So, if DNA damage was induced by 7a and 7g, single-stranded regions will be formed opposite to the dsDNA damaged sites. The free unpaired nucleobases opposite to the damaged site will directly coordinate with the Tb3+. Energy transfer from excited DNA to Tb3+ after irradiation at 270 nm leads to an increase in its native fluorescence with production of a detectable signal that is proportional to the amount of the induced DNA damage.
To study the interaction of both compounds 7a and 7g on DNA, a synthetic dsDNA of sequence; 5′-GACTGGCATGCTAAGGCGTGC-3′,3’-CTGACCGTACGATTCCGCACG-5’ was incubated with each compound for 24 h and Tb3+ biosensor was added to each mixture. Similarly, a control experiment was performed where DNA was incubated with doxorubicin, a commonly used DNA-damaging agent, under the same experimental conditions to confirm the DNA-damaging effect of the studied compounds. Tb3+ fluorescence was measured at 545 nm after excitation at 270 nm and was compared with that of all possible experimental controls, including Tb3+ alone, Tb3+-DNA mixture, and Tb3+-compound mixture. As shown ( ), the presence of the two studied compounds together with doxorubicin resulted in pronounced Tb3+ fluorescence enhancement compared to the Tb3+-DNA mixture where no compounds are present. However, Tb3+ alone and Tb3+-compound mixture showed no increase in fluorescence. Such fluorescence enhancement, caused by the two studied compounds and doxorubicin, can be correlated to the chelation of free electron-donating groups to Tb3+ in damaged dsDNA due to DNA damage induced by the studied compounds. It is noted that no similar change in fluorescence intensity was observed with the Tb3+-dsDNA mixture.
 The potency of the studied compounds to induce DNA damage was assessed using calf thymus DNA (ctDNA) and compared to that of doxorubicin. Several concentrations ( ) of the studied compounds were mixed for 24 h with ctDNA. Tb3+ was added to the aliquots of the compound/ctDNA solutions and the fluorescence was recorded. presents changes in Tb3+ fluorescence with the concentration for various compounds incubated with ctDNA. Results of 7a and 7g ( ) showed a very similar trend to that of doxorubicin ( ). This further confirmed that the two studied compounds induced direct DNA damage. In , when no studied compounds were added (zero concentration), minimum fluorescence was recorded demonstrating that no damage has occurred in ctDNA. By increasing the concentration of the studied compound, the proportional increase in fluorescence was recorded indicating that DNA damage increases till reaching 6, 4, and 4 μM concentrations for 7a, 7g, and doxorubicin, respectively. After such concentrations, no further increase in fluorescence was recorded for the compounds demonstrating that maximum DNA damage occurred. shows the analytical parameters for quantifying the DNA damage induced by each compound from . The parameters of linearity were assessed from the calibration curve in -insets to assess the minimum concentration of the studied compounds that would initiate damage in DNA. Results showed that small concentrations of 2.67, 1.87, and 0.76 nM of 7a, 7g, and doxorubicin, respectively, were enough to cause detectable damage in ctDNA ( ). Doxorubicin was the most potent compound, however very small concentrations of both 7a and 7g were still enough to cause detected DNA damage. This is very beneficial to therapists to have a variety of DNA-damaging agents to select the most suitable for use in terms of potency and side effects.
 

## HCT-116 cells cycle arrest
Evaluation of anticancer agents usually involves manipulation of tumor cell cycle arrest and selective induction of apoptosis . Herein, HCT-116 cell cycle analysis was assessed utilizing flow cytometry after treatment with the hit triazine derivatives 7a and 7g at their IC50 concentrations for 24 h compared to doxorubicin-treated cells and the control untreated HCT-116 cells ( and ). Results showed that the investigated derivatives 7a and 7g caused interference with HCT-116 cell cycle distribution demonstrating increased DNA content during both G0/G1 phase by respective 1.21 and 1.14 folds with subsequent reduced content during S and G2/M phases compared to the cell cycle of untreated cells. Accumulation of HCT-116 cells in the G1 phase was notable by higher G1 peaks in the treated cells cycle profile. These observations may be due to DNA degradation and apoptosis induction. Doxorubicin displayed cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase with accumulated DNA content during both G2/M phase by approximately 2.5 folds with subsequent reduced content during the S phase.
 

## HCT-116 cells apoptosis induction analysis
DNA-binding agents induce apoptosis because of DNA damage . In other words, apoptosis is among the major expected cellular responses to DNA-damaging agents . The apoptotic induction potential of 7a and 7g in HCT-116 cells was evaluated employing Annexin V and propidium iodide double staining assay illustrating the biparametric cytofluorimetric analysis . After 24 h treatment of HCT-116 cells with 7a, 7g, and the reference chemotherapy doxorubicin at their IC50 concentrations, apoptosis was induced 38.14, 41.99, and 50.89 times, respectively, more than the untreated cells ( and ). 7a induced total apoptosis by 30.13% with pronounced early apoptosis (18.42%), whereas 7g induced slightly higher total apoptosis (33.17%) that occurred mainly in the late stage (19.51%) compared to doxorubicin (40.21%) and control HCT-116 cells recording total cell death of 0.79%. Furthermore, the study revealed a mild increase in the recorded necrotic cells relative to the control. While the main goal of anticancer agents is apoptotic induction, diminished or negligible necrosis is favored to avoid inflammation of the nearby tissues. It is worth mentioning that the apoptotic cell populations in HCT-116 cells treated with the studied compounds or doxorubicin were below 50% ( and ). Such observation may be attributed to the possibility of induction of other forms of tumor cell death (non-apoptosis pathways) following DNA damage as exemplified by cellular senescence induced by DNA damage , which may be sufficient to suppress tumorigenesis . Recent evidence also indicated that DNA stress induces lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis induction . Moreover, the necroptosis pathway is activated in response to treatment with DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic drugs such as doxorubicin . Similarly, autophagy is accelerated by a variety of cellular stressors such as DNA damage , . Interestingly, the results of HCT-116 treated with doxorubicin were consistent with studies showing that doxorubicin induces HCT-116 cellular senescence , and autophagy in various cancers . In other tissues, like the heart, other forms of cell death mediated by doxorubicin have been extensively studied. Doxorubicin induces autophagy, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and release of Il-1β and Il-18 resulting in death due to pyroptosis. Additionally, doxorubicin treatment leads to necroptosis via different pathways . On the other hand, some reports linked MMPs inhibition to autophagic tumor cell death . Collectively, these results reflected the possibility that our compounds, being DNA-damaging agents and MMPs inhibitors, may mediate non-apoptosis cell death together with their apoptotic induction potential.
 

## MMPs inhibition and VEGF downregulation
In light of the preceding studies, the MMPs inhibitory profiles of the hit triazine derivatives 7a and 7g were in vitro investigated in comparison to the reference inhibitor NNGH. Then, VEGF downregulation was determined in HCT-116 after treatment with the active compounds to assess the cellular response to MMPs inhibition , and to evaluate their antiangiogenic potential . This is in accordance with studies correlating MMP-13 expression to VEGF levels , which are associated with colon cancer proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis .

## In vitro MMPs inhibition assay
Results of in vitro MMPs inhibition assay ( ) revealed that 7g showed remarkable potency against MMP-10 and -13 relative to 7a. In detail, 7g recorded % inhibition comparable to NNGH against MMP-10 and -13 at 0.1 μM, whereas 7a did not show significant activity against both isoforms at similar concentrations. Further investigations revealed that 7g surpassed the reference inhibitor and exhibited submicromolar IC50 against MMP-10 (IC50 = 0.205 μM) and MMP-13 (IC50 = 0.275 μM). However, the studied compounds didn’t exhibit any detectable activities against MMP-9, reflecting their MMP-10/13 selectivity.
 

## VEGF downregulation
The HCT-116 cellular levels of VEGF were quantified after treatment with 7a and 7g relative to the untreated control using a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Results ( ) were expressed as relative decrements in VEGF levels. The MMP-10/13 inhibitor 7g downregulated VEGF to nearly 0.62 folds its levels in untreated HCT-116 cells compared to 0.78 relative decrement value for 7a.
 

## Molecular modeling
Molecular modeling studies were conducted including docking of the biologically active triazine derivatives 7a and 7g into their molecular targets to simulate their binding modes and possibly elucidate their structural determinates of activities. Molecular dynamic simulations were then performed to further evaluate their binding stability in the studied catalytic domains. Finally, ADMET profiles and drug-likeness were predicted.

## Docking simulations

## DNA binding modes analysis
Docking of the studied triazine derivatives 7a and 7g into the DNA dodecamer (PDB code: 2DND ) was simulated employing Molecular Operating Environment (MOE 2019.102) to predict their possible DNA binding modes with regard to the co-crystallized ligand, distamycin. The DNA dodecamer (PDB code: 2DND ) was retrieved from the protein data bank. Different docking methodologies including flexible and rigid protocols were investigated employing different MOE fitting options for docking validation. Rigid docking into the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site restored most of the experimental interactions of distamycin into the DNA dodecamer recording free binding energy (ΔG) equals −11.29 kcal/mol at acceptable RMSD (1.32 Å) (Supplementary Data, Figure S98) and was considered a valid protocol. Three repeated rigid docking experiments of 7a and 7g into the DNA dodecamer were then conducted. The three top-ranked docking mods representing the lowest energy conformers were studied and compared with the reference ligand considering orientation between the DNA double strands and predicted interactions (Supplementary data, Tables S4 and S5). The calculated free energy of binding (ΔG) for the three top-ranked docking mods ranged from −9.56 to −9.42 kcal/mol for 7a and from −9.81 to −9.59 kcal/mol for 7g. These records highlighted that the studied compounds may reside in the ligand binding site of DNA dodecamer despite showing lower binding affinities to the DNA compared to the redocked distamycin (ΔG = −11.29 kcal/mol).
As illustrated ( ), mod (I) of 7a-DNA complex showed that scaffold was predicted to bind to the DNA double strands via key π–H bond interactions linking the thymidine bases of DT:A9, DT:B20, and DT:B21 to the terminal phenyl ring, triazole ligation and the triazine core, respectively. The docking pose II of 7a (Supplementary Data, Table S5) displayed a relatively different orientation of the scaffold between the DNA strands. Herein, the terminal phenyl was positioned towards the thymidine base of DT:B21 displaying H-π bond allowing alignment of the triazine core towards the thymidine base of DT:A9 and possible H-π bond interaction. In mod (III) (Supplementary Data, Table S5), 7a was aligned between the DNA double strands in the same orientation as mod (II). However, relatively fewer interactions were predicted. Herein, the compound was stabilized by H-bond and H-π interactions posed by the triazole and phenyl rings linking the scaffold to the thymidine base of DT:A8.
 Binding mode analysis of 7g-DNA revealed that the top-ranked mod (I) ( ), displayed nearly similar DNA binding pattern and scaffold alignment as those observed in the corresponding 7a-DNA mod (I). Herein, the trifluoromethylphenyl group interacted with the thymidine bases of DT:A8 via π-H bond interactions. The compound’s backbone was oriented between the DNA strands so that both the triazole and the triazine rings were posed towards the thymidine base of DT:B21 displaying two π-H bond interactions. In mod (II) (Supplementary Data, Table S6), the triazine core interacted with the thymidine bases of DT:A8 via π–H bond interactions, whereas the aromatic tail was oriented between the DNA strands posing the amide ligation to interact with the thymidine base of DT:A5 via H-bond. Mod (III) (Supplementary Data, Table S6) showed that the triazole and linker phenyl rings were predicted to display π–H bond interactions with the thymidine bases of DT:B21 and DT:B20 adopting an orientation similar to mod (II).
Collectively, the studied complexes mods showed that 7a and 7g were predicted to fit in between the DNA double strands posing some interactions resembling distamycin (Supplementary Data, Figure S98) especially with DT:B20, DT:B21, and DT:A8. Additional interactions with DT:A9 (only 7a) or DT:A5 (only 7g) were also predicted.

## MMPs binding modes analysis
Docking simulations of 7g into the MMP-10 and -13 active domains were performed by MOE 2019.0102 to explore the hit derivative possible binding modes and structural determinants of activity. The coordinates of the catalytic domains of both isoforms MMP-10 and -13 were retrieved from their crystal structures PDB ID: 1Q3A and PDB ID: 1XUD , respectively. The optimized domains consist of homomers, two Zn2+, and three Ca2+ ions ( ). For docking validation, flexible and rigid docking protocols were repeatedly explored to restore the experimental interactions of the co-crystallized ligands using various MOE fitting settings including labeling the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site as the docking site or locating the most suitable binding site in reference to the key residues using the MOE ‘Site Finder’ feature. Results showed that rigid docking into the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site was considered valid for binding mode analysis of the studied compound 7g by reproducing most of the key experimental interactions of the reference ligands at favored RMSD values (1.55 Å for MMP-10 and 0.35 Å for MMP-13) (Supplementary Data, Table S2 and Figures S89 and S96). It is noteworthy that the co-crystallized ligand active site, encompassing the catalytic Zn2+ and S1′ pocket that determines the inhibitor’s activity and selectivity profiles , , , was favored to allow comparing the binding modes of the studied compound to those of the co-crystallized reference inhibitors and other lead inhibitors as routinely reported in similar studies. In the quest for better analysis, three repeats of the validated rigid docking protocol were run to predict the most possible binding modes of 7g into the reference inhibitors’ binding sites of MMP-10 and -13. At the completion of the trials, three complexes with the lowest binding free energies and almost reproducible interactions were selected and termed the top-ranked docking poses (I, II, and III). The selected mods were then analyzed and compared with each other as well as the respective reference inhibitor regarding ligand orientation in the active site, Zn2+ binding mode, and possible interaction with the active site key amino acids.
 Docking simulations revealed that the best presumed three docking mods of 7g within the MMP-10 active site recorded binding free energy (ΔG) ranging from −9.06 to −8.72 kcal/mol (Supplementary Data, Table S3). Binding mode analysis of mod (I) ( ) demonstrated that the triazole ring chelated the catalytic Zn2+ posing the s-triazine core in the vicinity of the three essential zinc-chelating histidine residues and allowing the core to display critical H-π interaction with the His221 through the installed 2-amino group. With this orientation, the aromatic tail could extend through the S1’ pocket adjacent to the MMP-10 active site where the linker phenyl and the amide oxygen could interact with the Leu180 and Tyr239 residues defining S1′ pocket through H-π and hydrogen-bonding interactions, respectively. Moreover, the amide NH donated hydrogen bond to His178 of the catalytic site loop where calcium ion is bound . In the docking mod (II) (ΔG = −8.91 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Data, Figure S85) 7g was predicted to adopt opposite orientation in the active site, where the triazine core and the triazole ligation accommodated in the S1’ subunit and were able to bind with Tyr239 via H-π interactions and hydrogen bond, as well as Leu238 via H-π interactions. Additional hydrogen bond involving the triazine amino group and His178 was also predicted. On the other side, the amide carbonyl oxygen was able to coordinate the catalytic Zn2+. The third docking mod (III) (ΔG = −8.72 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Data, Figure S87) showed that 7g displayed almost the same orientation observed in mod (I), the aromatic tail was positioned near the S1’ pocket via H-π interactions with Tyr239 and Leu180 posed by the terminal and linker phenyl rings, respectively, whereas the triazole ring was bound to the catalytic Zn2+ via coordinate bond. A holistic view of the best three binding mods of 7g into MMP-10 active site indicated that the triazole ring was predicted to offer key interactions with Zn2+, Tyr239, and Leu238. The nonterminal phenyl ring could possibly interact with Leu180, whereas the amide ligation may coordinate Zn2+ or bind to His178 and Tyr239. The heterocyclic and aromatic termini of the molecule were suitable for fitting to the active site in different orientations with possible interactions with Tyr239. These results highlighted the possible alignment of 7g along the active site and interaction with the catalytic Zn2+ and S1’ subunit resembling the co-crystallized zinc-binding MMP-10 inhibitor NNGH which binds the nearby key amino acids, especially, Ser179, Ala181, and Leu234 (Supplementary Data, Figure S89).
The top-ranked three docking mods of 7g into MMP-13 catalytic domain recorded free binding energy values ranging from −10.32 to −10.01 kcal/mol (Supplementary Data, Table S4) relative to the redocked co-crystallized inhibitor (ΔG = −9.89 kcal/mol). The predicted results of the docking mod (I) (ΔG = −10.32 kcal/mol) ( )) showed that the triazin-2-yl amino motif chelated the active site Zn2+, whereas the triazole ring interacted with the nearby essential zinc-chelating His222. The linker phenyl group was extended to interact with the specificity loop residue Thr245 at the zinc-distal part of the S1′ pocket , , through π–π stacking. In the selected mod (II) (ΔG = −10.20 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Data, Figure S92), 7g was predicted to exhibit a slightly bent conformation in which most of the scaffold is packed into the specificity loop and stabilized by hydrogen bonds with His251 and Thr245 as well as H-π interactions with Thr247 and Lys249. The terminal aromatic ring could reach the zinc-chelating His222 residue and display π–π stacking interactions. The docking mod (III) (ΔG = −10.01 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Data, Figure S94) showed dual interaction of the 2-amino group tethered to the heterocyclic core with the active site Zn2+ and the proximal Glu223 residue via coordinate and hydrogen bond, respectively. The aromatic tail was aligned along the active site posing similar interactions as those observed in the docking mod (I), where the linker triazole and phenyl rings were bound to His222 and Thr245 through π–π stacking and H–π bond, respectively, allowing the aromatic tail to occupy the specificity loop (residues 245–253) , , . Collectively, the top docking mods showed that 7g could possibly bind to the MMP-13 active site zinc-chelating residue His222 via π–π sacking, the specificity loop residue Thr245 via H-π interactions, and the catalytic Zn2+ in most cases. Thus, 7g was postulated to share some key amino acids interactions with the co-crystallized pyrimidine dicarboxamide-based MMP-13 inhibitor which binds His222 via π–π sacking, Thr245 and Thr247 via H-bonds, as well as Tyr244 and Phe252 via H–π bonds (Supplementary Data, Figure S96). Moreover, 7g showed the potential to bind the active site Zn2+ whereas the reference was devoid of Zn2+ binding.
These results might explain the inhibitory profile of 7g against MMP-10 and -13 relative to the reference inhibitors, where 7g could possibly chelate the active site Zn2+ besides interacting with the zinc-chelating histidine residues and other key active site amino acid residues.

## Molecular dynamics
To study the dynamic behavior of the hit 7g into the active sites of MMP-10 (PDB ID: 1Q3A ) and MMP-13 (PDB ID: 1XUD ), molecular dynamics simulations of the three top-ranked docking mods (I, II, and III) were run at comparable physiological conditions for 50 ns employing GROMACS program. Then, analysis was based on comparing the three MDs data for each respective complex (7g-1Q3A and 7g-1XUD) and reporting the averaged results. Accordingly, root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (rGyr), hydrogen bonding, average center-of-mass distance between ligand and protein (COM), contact frequency analysis (CF), ligand interactions network and binding free energy (MMPBSA) were computed.

## Root mean square deviation (RMSD)
The RMSD of the studied complexes, backbones, and ligands were calculated as a function of the simulation time to quantitatively estimate the degree of deviation of each complex relative to its initial behavior. For 7g-1Q3A complex, the RMSD plot of the docking mod (I) ( ) displayed RMSD average of approximately 2.5 Å for the simulated protein-ligand complex with low fluctuations all over the simulation time. Mod (II) simulation (Supplementary Data, Figure S86) recorded a relatively higher RMSD average (3.5 Å), whereas mod (III) (Supplementary Data, Figure S88) displayed an average RMSD value of 2.4 Å with some fluctuations detected between 30 and 40 ns. In all trials, the ligand and protein recorded RMSD values less than 1.5 Å. Collectively, the studied complex 7g-1Q3A three independent simulations recorded an average RMSD of approximately 2.7 Å. On the other hand, results of 7g-1XUD simulations showed relatively higher RMSD records for both complexes and protein backbones. This may be attributed to the fact that, unlike most MMPs, MMP-13 has a uniquely deep and large open hydrophobic S1′ pocket with a highly flexible specificity loop (Ω-loop) consisting of the residues 245–253118, , that were predicted to encompass most of the docked 7g scaffold in the top-ranked docking mods. Herein, further investigation of the RMSF plots, contact frequency analysis, ligand interactions network mapping, and visual inspection of the trajectories should be considered for better evaluation of the system’s conformational behavior and the simulation quality. Among the simulated 7g-1XUD mods, mod (III) recorded RMSD values around 4 Å through most of the simulation time with transient fluctuations at 40 ns ( ). The docking mod (II) (Supplementary Data, Figure S93) showed average RMSD value of approximately 4.5 Å with a steady pattern all over the simulation, whereas the mod (I) (Supplementary Data, Figure S91) displayed RMSD values ranging from 4.2 to 5 Å with some fluctuations. In practice, the top-ranked docking mod is not always the best one after MDs. It is noted that the trends of protein backbones RMSD plots (approximately 4.4 Å) were nearly consistent with those of the simulated complexes in all trials reflecting reproducibility of the MMP-13 reported intrinsic flexibility , , . Moreover, MDs simulation of the MMP-13 apoprotein (Supplementary Data, Figure S97) indicated inherent flexibility (RMSD = 4.3 Å) comparable to the holo states flexibility throughout simulations of all the investigated complexes (Supplementary Data). It is worth mentioning that generally proteins tend to have the same conformational flexibility within their apo and holo states based on previous studies .
 

## Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)
The RMSF was analyzed to study the side chain residues dynamics and local changes due to the inhibitor binding. Calculations were based on ‘C-alpha’ atoms using the GROMACS program. Overall, the fluctuation intensity remained stable in the studied mods of 7g-1Q3A and 7g-1XUD complexes, where most fluctuations were averaged below 1 Å and 1.5 Å, respectively (Supplementary Data). In 7g-1Q3A mod (I) ( ), fluctuations were below 0.75 Å. 7g-1XUD mod (III) ( ) recorded 1.4 Å as an average RMSF value. Particular attention was paid to the RMSF values of the key residue in the active sites of MMP-10 and -13 in the presence and absence of the ligand 7g. Indeed, exploring the implications of these fluctuations could provide valuable insights into the binding stability and dynamics of the inhibitor. The key interacting residues His178, Leu180, and His221 in the 7g-1Q3A mod (I) complex recorded higher RMSF values (0.73, 0.46, and 0.41 Å, respectively) ( ) compared to their corresponding values in the simulated apo-MMP-10 (0.31, 0.25, and 0.21 Å, respectively) (Supplementary Data, Figure S97). These results may indicate the potential of the active site to interact flexibly with the ligand which could be possibly related to the active site’s induced fit binding thus allowing the ligand to adapt well in the binding pocket . In other words, the ligand binding induces some conformational changes in the active site. Notably, these results were mirrored by some fluctuations in the ligand’s RMSD plot reflecting the ligand’s mobility ( ).
On the other hand, in 7g-1XUD mod (III), the specificity loop amino acid residues 245-253 of MMP-13 catalytic domain displayed RMSF values ranging from 0.77 to 2.51 Å reflecting the intrinsic flexibility of this region , , . Regarding the other key active site amino acids interacting with 7g, relatively lower RMSF values were recorded as exemplified by His222 (RMSF = 0.49 Å) ( ). It is noted that this critical residue showed lower fluctuations in the complex (RMSF = 0.49 Å) than in the apo-MMP-13 (RMSF = 0.63 Å) (Supplementary Data, Figure S97) implying stable ligand-protein binding with a steady pattern of interaction , . Herein, lower RMSF values in the bound form compared to the apo-MMP-13 may indicate rigidification of regions directly interacting with the ligand .

## Radius of gyration (rGyr)
Investigation of rGyr suggested that the systems were well-compacted. The calculated values were consistent with their respective RMSD indicating stability with low fluctuations (less than 1 Å) throughout simulations of the selected mods ( and ). The other studied mods of both complexes 7g-1Q3A and 7g-1XUD showed acceptable results (Supplementary Data).

The COM distance between the ligand and protein was computed during the simulation time for all the docking mods of both complexes (Supplementary Data). The average COM distances over the whole simulations of 7g-1Q3A mod (I) ( ) and 7g-1XUD mod III ( ) were 13.49 and 12.14 Å, respectively. ## Contact frequency (CF) and ligand interaction network analysis
To further explore the ligand-protein binding nature and stability in the studied 7g-1Q3A and 7g-1XUD complexes, contact frequency (CF) and ligand interactions network were analyzed over the whole simulation time for all the simulated mods. CF analysis was performed employing VMD software using a VMD script (contactFreq.tcl module), where contact is defined as the amino acid being within a distance cutoff of 4 Å. CF analysis of 7g-1Q3A complex (mod I) ( ) showed that 7g was in contact with many active site amino acids. The residues with the highest CF% were Tyr171 (91.01%), Ala183 (84.92%), Ser172 (80.02%), His221 (79.02%), and His227 (65.93%). In 7g-1XUD complex (mod III) ( ), many key residues were detected at high CF % such as His222 (99.90%), Pro242 (99.90%), Ala238 (99.30%), and Leu239 (98.80%). Notably specificity loop residues, especially Thr245 (95.30%) were detected among the residues with high occupancy. CF analyses of other mods showed comparable results for each respective complex (Supplementary Data). Ligand binding network was then mapped utilizing Prolif tool to evaluate all possible target/ligand interactions, especially those with high occupancy, throughout the simulation time of each mod of the studied complexes (Supplementary Data). Results revealed that in mod (I) of 7g-1Q3A complex (Supplementary Data, Figure S84), the active site Zn2+ was coordinated to the triazole nitrogen ligated to the phenyl group, whereas the nearby Ala183 and His221 were involved in hydrogen bond and H-π interactions with the amino and methoxy groups installed on the triazine core, respectively. Both Ser172 and His178 were able to bind the amide ligation by hydrogen bonds stabilizing the aromatic tail in the active site.
 On the other hand, mod (III) ligand binding network of 7g-1XUD complex (Supplementary Data, Figure S95) showed that the aromatic tail of the molecule was stabilized several interactions in the specificity loop exemplified by key hydrogen bond between the amide group NH and Thr245. The linker phenyl and triazole rings were able to bind to the proximal Leu239 and Ala186, respectively, via hydrophobic interactions, whereas the 2-amino group tethered to the triazine core was extended to pose hydrogen bond with Pro242 proximal to the zinc-chelating histidine residues.
Finally, visual analysis of the trajectories post simulation confirmed that 7g remained bound to the active sites of MMP-10 and -13 and did not detach from the proteins. These results supported the reliability of the starting poses and provided more information about MDs of the ligand/target contacts highlighting key interactions with the active site residues.

## Molecular Mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) and entropy calculations
The Molecular Mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method, being among the most employed force field-based methods, was selected for rescoring the complexes. Accordingly, gmx-mmpbsa module was employed to compute the ΔG binding energy, van der Waal, electrostatic, polar solvation, and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) energies of the three simulated mods of each complex (Supplementary Data, Table S7). Results showed that the ΔG binding free energies ranged from −175.57 to −58.51 kJ/mol for 7g-1Q3A mods and from -229.71 to −136.33 kJ/mol for 7g-1XUD mods. Collectively, the outcome demonstrated that 7g remained in stable bound forms within MMP-10 and -13 active sites during simulations and recommended its promising MMP-10/13 inhibitory activities.
The entropic contribution to binding free energy was analyzed for 7g-MMP-10 and 7g-MMP-13 complexes, employing the C2 method , , to provide more insights into their binding affinities and thermodynamic properties. Results showed that the computed entropic values for the simulated 7g-1Q3A mod (I) and 7g-1XUD mod (III) were 784.66 ± 37.40 and 17.82 ± 3.84 KJ/mole, respectively. It is noteworthy that the C2 entropies are accurate in most cases but may be large in magnitude . Results showed that a higher entropic component was computed in the 7g-MMP-10 complex, where the ligand most likely retains much of its flexibility and freedom, than when it is relatively less mobile upon binding to the active site in the 7g-MMP-13 complex. These results were consistent with the ligand’s RMSD plots during simulations of both complexes. As shown in , 7g showed fluctuations during the first 20ns of 7g-MMP-10 simulation till it equilibrated, whereas it remained steady almost throughout the whole simulation of 7g-MMP-13 ( ). Additionally, the key interacting residues in the 7g-MMP-10 complex recorded higher RMSF values ( ) compared to their corresponding values in the simulated apo-MMP-10 (Supplementary Data, Figure S97) indicating flexible interaction with 7g. On the other hand, the active site residues of MMP-13 showed lower RMSF in the bound form ( ) compared to the apo-MMP-13 (Supplementary Data, Figure S97) indicating stabilized relatively rigid binding of the MMP-13 active site with 7g.

## Physicochemical properties, ADMET parameters, and drug-likeness prediction
The physicochemical properties fulfilling Lipinski’s rule of five were computed for predicting the hit compounds’ bioavailability. Results ( ) demonstrated that 7a fulfilled Lipinski’s parameters, whereas 7g only violated Lipinski’s limit of molecular weight and hydrogen bond acceptors while fulfilling the other parameters. The TPSA and aqueous solubility, being useful descriptors of oral bioavailability , were also considered. Both 7a and 7g displayed drug-like TPSA values , and acceptable solubility requirements, especially 7a.
 Pre-ADMET and ProTox-II were additionally utilized to predict the compounds’ ADMET parameters. The investigated triazines 7a and 7g were predicted to display good intestinal absorption (>94.8%), moderate Caco2 (approximately 21.2 nm/sec), low MDCK permeabilities, medium BB (Cbrain/Cblood) and CNS absorption (approximately 0.1), as well as plasma proteins binding (approximately 90%). The compounds were expected as none CYP2D6 inhibitors . Finally, the compounds were classified as class IV according to GHS with a predicted LD50 of 500 mg/kg. Further toxicity assessment excluded their potential immunogenicity. Accordingly, both 7a and 7g were predicted to be safe leads.
The reported reference values for TPSA , HIA , PPB , , , BBB , Caco2 , , and MDCK , as well as the toxicity classes according to GHS are stated in the Supplementary Data.

## Probable targets prediction of 7g
The free target prediction tool Polypharmacology Browser 2.0 (PPB2) , was employed to predict the potential off-target effects of the most promising triazine derivative 7g exhibiting dual MMP-10/13 inhibition and DNA damage (Supplementary Data, Tables S8–S10). This data highlights the compound’s selectivity and potential side effects. PPB2 proposes possible targets for any drug-like molecule by similarity to compounds with known activities from the ChEMBL database based on substructures (ECfp4), molecular shape and pharmacophores (Xfp), and molecular fingerprints encoding composition (MQN). The tool also features combination of nearest neighbor (NN) searches and Naı¨ve Bayes (NB) machine learning. Accordingly, eight search options are available namely, nearest-neighbor (NN) searches with each of the three aforementioned fingerprints, NN searches combined with a Naïve Bayes machine learning model based on ECfp4 (NN+NB) and Naïve Bayes (NB) machine learning or deep neural network (DNN) based on ECfp4. In this study, we analyzed the predicted targets for the triazine derivative 7g using each of the eight search methods available in PPB2 and analyzed the top ranked targets as well as other possible metalloenzymes targets. Melanin-concentrating hormone receptor 1, tyrosine-protein kinase SRC, and thrombin were the top predicted targets using nearest neighbor (NN) search with extended connectivity fingerprint NN(ECfp4), shape and pharmacophore fingerprint NN(Xfp), and molecular quantum numbers NN(MQN), respectively (Supplementary Data, Table S8). On the other hand, carbonic anhydrase II, vanilloid receptor and serine/threonine-protein kinase Aurora-A were at the top of the list using combination of NN(ECfp4), NN(Xfp) and NN(MQN) searches, and the ECfp4 Naı¨ve Bayes machine learning NB(ECfp4), respectively (Supplementary Data, Table S9). Finally, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1 and carbonic anhydrase IX were predicted based on Naive Bayes machine learning model NB(ECfp4) and deep neural network model using extended connectivity fingerprint DNN(ECfp4), respectively (Supplementary Data, Table S10). Besides MMP-13, other zinc-dependent metalloenzymes were predicted as probable targets for 7g such as histone deacetylases-1, -2, -3, -6, and -8 as well as carbonic anhydrases I and XII. Interestingly, most of these enzymes are potential druggable anticancer targets .

## Structure-activity relationship
The observed cytotoxicity pattern of the studied compounds against HCT-116 cells revealed that the newly designed isomeric scaffolds ( ) were intrinsically active. However, the observed cytotoxicity profile regarding potency and selectivity to HCT-116 over normal colonocytes was found to be a function of the terminal N-phenyl substituents and the isomeric nature of the amide linkage ( ). Among the screened derivatives, the 4-(4-(((4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)amino)methyl)-1H-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)-N-phenylbenzamide (7a) exhibited the highest potency and selectivity. Within the same series, p-substitution of the terminal N-phenyl group by trifluoromethyl group 7g conserved the scaffold’s potency against HCT-116 but decreased the observed selectivity to HCT-116 over normal colonocytes by approximately 1 fold. Further diversification of the terminal N-phenyl substitution with halogens led to a substantial decrease in the cytotoxic potency against HCT-116 cells (28-34 folds) and selectivity (42–49 folds) compared to the unsubstituted parent compound 7a. The p-bromophenyl derivative 7d was slightly more active and selective than the fluorinated 7b and chlorinated 7c equipotent analogs. Replacement of halogen with a nitro group in 7e led to loss of selectivity, whereas a methyl group in 7f enhanced selectivity by approximately 1.5 folds. Increasing the electron donating potential of the terminal substituent by insertion of oxygen offered the more active and selective p-methoxy derivative 7h. Deletion of the N-phenyl group in 4 was detrimental to both potency and selectivity reflecting its significance and essential contribution to the scaffold’s anticancer profile as simulated in docking studies. On the other hand, the isomeric series 11a–h generally lacked considerable HCT-116 selectivity over normal cells hampering their anticancer suitability despite their observed cytotoxic potency. The p-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl derivative 11g was an exception where it recorded a selectivity index approaching 2 with relatively moderate anticancer potency. MTT experiments on Caco-2 cells revealed nearly similar potency pattern where the trifluoromethyl substituted derivative 7g and its unsubstituted analog 7a were the most potent derivatives followed by the p-methoxy 7h, the p-methyl 7f and the p-chloro 7c derivatives from the 4-(4-(((4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)amino)methyl)-1H-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)-N-(4-substitutedphenyl)benzamide series 7a–h. The 4-fluorobenzamide derivative 11b from the isomeric series 11a–h also showed considerable potency. However, their selectivity to Caco-2 over normal colonic cells was unacceptable. Further evaluation of the hits 7a and 7g against HepG-2 cells showed that the dual colorectal/hepatic anticancer activity was optimized for p-trifluoromethyl substitution. Mechanistic and apoptosis studies showed that the trifluoromethyl group in 7g was critical for MMP-10/13 inhibition, VEGF downregulation, enhanced DNA damaging, and apoptotic induction potential to the scaffold.
 

## Conclusion
Novel triazole-tethered s-triazine derivatives were designed as dual DNA-binding agents and MMP-10/13 inhibitors for complementary therapeutic management of CRC. The design strategy relied on a hybrid representation of the thematic structural requirements of lead DNA intercalators and MMPs inhibitors. All the synthesized compounds were preliminarily screened utilizing MTT assay for their cytotoxic activities against HCT-116, Caco-2 CRC cells, and normal human colonocytes in reference to doxorubicin. Results declared that, unlike Caco-2 cells, HCT-116 cells were sensitive to most of the evaluated derivatives. The 4-(4-((triazinylamino)methyl)triazolyl)-N-phenylbenzamide (7a) and its trifluoromethyl analog 7g were the most potent and selective derivatives against HCT-116 cells (IC50 = 0.87 and 1.41 nM, SI = 181.9 and 54.4, respectively) even superior to doxorubicin (IC50 = 830 nM and SI = 0.84). Further investigation of the cytotoxic potential of the hit derivatives 7a and 7g against liver HepG-2 cancer cells revealed the promising activity of 7g (IC50 = 65.08 nM) compared to doxorubicin (IC50 = 355 nM). Mechanistically, both compounds induced DNA damage at 2.67 and 1.87 nM, respectively, compared to doxorubicin (0.76 nM), disrupted HCT-116 cell cycle distribution in the G0/G1 phase and induced apoptosis to 33.17% in reference to 40.21% for doxorubicin and compared to control HCT-116 cells. Out of the two hits, 7g surpassed NNGH against MMP-10 (IC50 = 0.205 μM) and MMP-13 (IC50 = 0.275 μM) in vitro and downregulated the HCT-116 cellular levels of the correlated VEGF by a relative decrement value of 0.62. Docking simulations declared the possible receptor binding modes and concluded the significance of the heterocyclic core, the triazole ligation, and the terminal phenylbenzamide substitution pattern for optimum receptor fitting and binding affinity. Molecular dynamics and entropic analysis provided more insight into the stability and thermodynamics of the docked derivatives within MMP-10 and -13 active sites. In silico ADMET studies predicted the drug-likeness of the studied derivatives. A holistic view of the results pointed to 7g as a promising anti-colorectal cancer agent with dual DNA damaging and MMP-10/13 inhibition potential. The hit derivative surpassed the cytotoxic potency of doxorubicin, despite its lower DNA binding efficacy compared to that of doxorubicin. This may be attributed to the synergistic outcome of simultaneous MMP-10/13 inhibition. In silico ADMET studies highlighted the drug-likeness profile of 7g, and PPB2 tool predicted its possible interaction with other zinc-containing metalloenzymes including histone deacetylases and carbonic anhydrases which are druggable anticancer targets. Future studies will direct optimization of hybrid dual mechanism molecules incorporating versatile pharmacophoric motifs from each component to be evaluated against different types of cancer as well.

## General procedure for the synthesis of compounds 4, 6 and 9
Compound 2 (0.3 g, 1.54 mmol) was added together with CuSO4·5H2O (0.063 g, 0.25 mmol), L-ascorbic acid (0.126 g, 0.715 mmol), water (4 mL), and methanol (4 mL) to a solution of the adequately azidobenzene derivative (1.54 mmol) in THF (15 mL). The reaction mixture was then left to stir at RT for a further 24 h. ## Biology

## Cytotoxicity screening on normal colonocytes
Nine male albino rats weighing 30–35 g were kept in plastic cages under standard laboratory conditions (relative humidity of 55–65% and room temperature of 25 °C), maintained at a natural day-night cycle, and provided with standard laboratory food and tap water ad libitum for adaptation. The animals were then starved and anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation before isolation of the colon epithelial cells following the modified Follman’s and Birkner’s method , in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/) and as approved by the specified ethics committee (see ethics approval statement). Each colon was incubated with type I collagenase (400 U, Sigma, USA) in 5% incubator at 37 °C for 45 min and then crypts were collected by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min. The pellets (crypts) were washed twice with α-MEM (Lonza, USA). The freshly prepared crypt pellets were resuspended in α-MEM-containing 15% FBS. Within 7 days, cells become confluent and then sub-cultured using trypsin-EDTA. After two passages, cells were seeded in 96-well plate for further investigation of the cytotoxic activity of the tested compounds using MTT assay . Briefly, colonocytes were seeded in 96-well cell culture plates as 10,000 cells/well. After 24 h, serial concentrations of the tested compounds were added. Plates were incubated in a humidified air incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 72 h. MTT (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h, then replaced by 150 µL DMSO. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (BMG LabTech, Germany). The concentrations at 100% and 50% cell viability (EC100 and IC50) were calculated using GraphPad InStat software.

## Cytotoxicity screening against HCT-116, Caco-2 and HepG-2 cells
HCT-116 (CCL-247™), Caco-2 (HTB-37™) and HepG-2 (HB-8065™) cells from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Lonza, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA). Cells were seeded as 4 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 24 h for cell attachment, serial concentrations of tested compounds were incubated with cells for 72 h in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The cytotoxicity of tested compounds was evaluated by MTT assay as described above and reported . IC50 values were computed by the GraphPad Instat software.

## DNA binding/damage studies
DNA oligonucleotide sequence was purchased from Bio Basic Canada Inc., Ontario, Canada. The calf thymus DNA (ctDNA) and the terbium (III) chloride were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Canada Ltd. (Oakville, Ontario), sodium chloride from EMD Chemicals Inc. (Gibbstown, New Jersey), and Tris from ICN Biomedicals (Aurora, Ohio), then used as received.
Fluorescence measurements were performed with a spectrofluorimeter (Schimadzu, model RF-1501, Version 3, Kyoto, Japan) fitted with a Suprasil quartz fluorescence cuvette (1 cm path-length, 100 L volume) and a 150 W Xenon lamp. Fluorescence spectra, between 290 and 650 nm with excitation at 270 nm of 100 μL aliquots of such solutions in a 1 cm path length Suprasil quartz fluorescence cuvette, were measured as reported , . Herein, synthetic DNA sequence: 5′-GACTGGCATGCTAAGGCGTGC-3′,3’-CTGACCGTACGATTCCGCACG-5’ was mixed and incubated with each of 7a, 7g, and doxorubicin, separately for 24 h. After the incubation period, 100 μL aliquots of each mixture were mixed with TbCl3 in buffer solution (40 mM Tris, 10 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) to give final concentrations of 0.2 µM DNA, 10 µM TbCl3, and 2 μM of each compound. Fluorescence spectra of such solutions were obtained, between 290 and 650 nm after excitation at 270 nm. For the calf thymus DNA (ctDNA) experiment, ctDNA was mixed with different concentrations of each compound (0.0–6.0 μM for 7a and 0.0–4.0 μM for 7g and doxorubicin). After 24 h of incubation, TbCl3 was added to the ctDNA/compound mixtures in buffer solution (40 mM Tris, 10 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) to give final concentrations of 1 μg/mL ctDNA and 3 mM TbCl3. Fluorescence spectra of such solutions were obtained, between 290 and 650 nm after excitation at 270 nm.

## Apoptosis studies
HCT-116 (CCL-247™) cells from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) (3 × 105/well) were plated in 6-well plates, cultured overnight, treated with IC50 of either compound then harvested after 24 h incubation. For Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis analysis, the cells were suspended in 500 µL binding buffer and adjusted to 1 × 106/mL. Staining solutions containing 5 µL Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI) (BD Biosciences) were added, then cells were incubated at 2–8 °C for 15 min in the dark and analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The percent of viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells were evaluated using CellQuest software version 5.1 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) . Data was represented as the mean values ± SEM for three independent experiments. Statistical significance was assessed by a two-way ANOVA test.

## In vitro MMPs inhibition assay
The assay was performed utilizing MMP-9 Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit (Abcam, catalog # ab139448), MMP-10 Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit (Abcam, catalog # ab139457), and MMP-13 Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit (Abcam, catalog # ab139450), respectively, following the protocols described in the Supplementary Data.

## VEGF protein level analysis in HCT-116 cells
Cellular levels of VEGF were quantified in HCT-116 (CCL-247™) from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) cell supernatant of 7a- and 7g-treated wells (at IC50 doses), relative to the untreated control wells, by a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay employing the human VEGF ELISA kit (Invitrogen, US, catalog # KHG0111) following the assay protocol described in the Supplementary Data.

## Molecular modeling studies
Docking was conducted to possibly predict the most favorable bound conformations of the studied compounds within DNA and the active sites of MMP-10 and -13 employing Molecular Operating Environment (MOE), 2019.102 provided by Chemical Computing Group . Different docking methodologies including flexible and rigid protocols utilizing various fitting settings (labeling the co-crystalized ligand atoms as docking site and predicting the possible sites encompassing key amino acids) were investigated for validation. Rigid docking into the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site was considered valid thus adopted for docking the studied compounds into their respective targets. Docking results were evaluated based on analyzing the three top-ranked docking poses from three repeated runs, then molecular dynamics simulations were employed to evaluate the dynamic behavior of the generated top three docking poses in a bid to better understand the nature of the most possible receptor-ligand binding modes. GROMACS was utilized to run the post-docking 3-fold molecular dynamics simulations study. The VMD: visual molecular dynamics was used for trajectory visualization, detailed hydrogen bonding information, and contact frequency analysis. Prolif tool was utilized for mapping ligand-protein interactions achieved during simulations. The Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) force field-based method was used to calculate the ligand-receptor binding free energies , . Finally, in silico physicochemical properties, ADMET profiles, and drug-likeness of the studied compounds were estimated employing free available web servers , , .

## Docking
The coordinates of DNA dodecamer and MMPs (MMP-10 and -13) active domains crystalized with their respective ligands were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 2DND , 1Q3A , and 1XUD , respectively) and handled with MOE 2019.102 . Unwanted crystallographic artefacts (waters) and repeated chains were eliminated from the MMPs catalytic domains. The default “Structure preparation” MOE setting was employed for preparing and refining the DNA dodecamer and MMPs catalytic domains . It is worth mentioning that, for MMPs, the correct ionization state and binding mode of the active site Zn2+ were ensured after employing the structure preparation module as previously reported . This tool corrected any structural problems, assigned ionization and protonation states, added tethers to the receptor heavy atoms, and fixed distant atoms , . Then, energy minimization was conducted employing Amber10: EHT force field with reaction-field electrostatics (an interior dielectric constant of 1 and an exterior dielectric of 80) using an 8–10 Å cutoff distance. The docking site was located at the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site for each target. Rigid docking protocol was validated by restoring most of the experimental interactions following redocking the crystallized ligands into their respective binding sites with RMSD <2 Å. RMSD values were provided in the output docking database files (*.mdb) of the docked ligand poses with their respective free binding energies (Supplementary Data). The bioactive triazine derivatives were built in silico, energy minimized via MMFF94x force field at 0.01 RMSD gradient, then docked into the co-crystallized ligand atoms binding site of the studied target employing the validated rigid docking protocol utilizing triangular matcher algorithm as the ligand placement method , and London dG as the default scoring function for generating the top ten non-redundant poses for each ligand. The top three docking poses for each target from three repeated docking experiments were selected based on the lowest recorded free binding energy. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted for all the selected docking mods of 7g-1Q3A and 7g-1XUD complexes, then analyzed based on the averaged results.

## Molecular dynamics simulations
The CHARMM-GUI solution builder was employed to generate the input files required for MDs calculations utilizing the CHARMM force field parameters. The selected ligand topologies were generated employing CHARMM General Force Field via the CgenFF server . Five steps were included in the CHARMM-GUI solution builder. Firstly, the protein-ligand complex coordinates were read by the tool. Secondly, the protein-ligand complex was solvated, and the system’s shape and size were determined. In this step, Na+ and Cl- ions were added to neutralize the system. In the third step, Periodic Boundary Conditions (PBC) were set for approximating large system by using a unit cell that was then replicated in all directions. The MDs simulation took place only for atoms in a PBC box of 64, 64, and 64 Å dimensions. Short minimizations were run to remove bad contacts. The last steps involved system equilibration and production. Equilibration occurred in two phases; NPT ensemble and NVT ensemble to ensure the system had achieved the required pressure and temperature. The input files for both equilibration and production were downloaded and the desired changes were made including steps of MDs run, frequency of saving trajectories, and energy calculation. Nonbonded interactions were treated with a cutoff distance of 12 Å, and the neighbor searching list was then buffered with the Verlet cutoff scheme. The long-range electrostatic interactions of the complex were treated during the simulations with the particle mesh Ewald method. Energy minimization of the system was carried out, prior to production, by employing the steepest descent algorithm (5000 steps). Then, the complex was equilibrated to stabilize its temperature and pressure by subjecting the complex to NVT and NPT ensemble and simulating it for 125 ps at 300.15 K using 400 kJ mol−1 nm−2 and 40 kJ mol−1 nm−2 positional restraints on the complex backbone and side chains, respectively. The complex was finally subjected to the production simulation run in NPT ensemble at 1 bar and 300.15 K for 50 ns. To maintain the temperature and pressure, Nose-Hoover thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat were used. The LINCS algorithm was utilized for constraining H-bonds using the CHARMM-GUI inputs. The V-rescale thermostat was used at 300 K with 1 ps coupling constant. The trajectories during simulations were stored every 2 ps . GROMACS was employed for running the MDs simulations. RMSD was computed during the simulation by gmx_rms, RMSF by gmx_rmsf, number of H-bonds inside the protein-ligand interface by gmx_hbond, rGyr by gmx_gyrate, and COM by gmx_distance. The VMD molecular graphics program was used for contact frequency (CF) analysis and trajectory visualization . Prolif tool was employed using default parameters to evaluate possible protein-ligand interactions over the simulation time. MM/PBSA calculations were performed using the gmx_mmpbsa module. The complex binding free energy in solvent is expressed as: , where , and are the total free energies of the complex, the isolated protein, and the ligand in the solvent, respectively. The binary run input file (.tpr) was regenerated by GROMACS 5.1.4. for MMPBSA calculations through gmx_mmpbsa. The topology (.top), molecular structure (.gro), and MDs parameter (.mdp) files were necessary for generating the binary run input file (.tpr), and all came from the MDs simulation process . Entropies were calculated employing the C2 method found in MMPBSA parameter file .
