## Abstract
 After RNAi was first discovered over 20 years ago, siRNA-based
therapeutics are finally becoming reality. However, the delivery of
siRNA has remained a challenge. In our previous research, we found
that spermine-based poly(β-amino ester)s are very promising
for siRNA delivery. However, the role of hydrophobic modification
in siRNA delivery of spermine-based poly(β-amino ester)s is
not fully understood yet. In the current work, we synthesized spermine-based
poly(β-amino ester)s with different percentages of oleylamine
side chains, named P(SpOABAE). The chemical structures of the polymers
were characterized by 1H NMR. The polymers showed efficient
siRNA encapsulation determined by SYBR Gold assays. The hydrodynamic
diameters of the P(SpOABAE) polyplexes from charge ratio N/P 1 to
20 were 30–100 nm except for aggregation phenomena observed
at N/P 3. Morphology of the polyplexes was visualized by atomic force
microscopy, and cellular uptake was determined by flow cytometry in
H1299 cells, where all the polyplexes showed significantly higher
cellular uptake than hyperbranched polyethylenimine (25 kDa). The
most hydrophobic P(SpOABAE) polyplexes were able to achieve more than
90% GFP knockdown in H1299/eGFP cells. The fact that gene silencing
efficacy increased with hydrophobicity but cellular uptake was affected
by both charge and hydrophobic interactions highlights the importance
of endosomal escape. For pulmonary administration and improved storage
stability, the polyplexes were spray-dried. Results confirmed the
maintained siRNA activity after storage for 3 months at room temperature,
indicating potential for dry powder inhalation.

## Introduction
The approval of siRNA
therapeutics by the FDA in recent years brings
new hope for siRNA therapeutics. However, the therapeutic use of siRNA
is still limited by several extracellular and intracellular barriers.
siRNAs are negatively charged macromolecules (7–8 nm in length
and 2–3 nm in diameter), membrane-impermeable, and highly unstable
in the systemic circulation due to the degradation by endogenous enzymes
and the elimination through kidneys. The
half-life of most naked or unmodified siRNAs is around 5–10
min. Once delivered into cells, the siRNA
must escape from endosomes and lysosomes into the cytoplasm and the
antisense strand needs to be loaded into the RNA-induced silencing
complex (RISC).
In fact, endosomal entrapment is one of the
biggest barriers to
efficient siRNA delivery. According to previous reports, only 3.5%
or even less than 1% of the internalized siRNA can escape from the
endosome to be incorporated into RISC and to eventually mediate gene
silencing. , Thus, a carrier with an enhanced disruption
of the endosomal membrane is critical for successful endosomal escape
and gene silencing by siRNA.
Cationic polymers are one of the
main categories of nonviral vectors
for siRNA delivery. After the first successful mammalian gene delivery
experiments based on spermine in the early 1960s by Szybalska and
Szybalski, many polycations were developed
such as poly(ornithine), poly(arginine), poly(l-lysine), and poly(ethylene imine), etc. Poly(β-amino
ester)s, abbreviated as PBAEs or PAEs, refer to polymers synthesized
from acrylates and amines by Michael addition. PBAEs were first described
in the 1970s and first introduced for nucleic acid delivery by Lynn
and Langer in 2000. PBAEs are biocompatible,
biodegradable, and pH responsive. These
properties make them very promising for siRNA delivery. Spermine is
an endogenous linear tetramine and an important component of nucleic
acid delivery vehicles. It occurs naturally, is safe, and aids in
packaging cellular DNA into compact state, which is essential in cell
growth in eukaryotic cells. , However, the siRNA
delivery efficiency of spermine is limited due to its low molecular
weight (202.34 g/mol). In addition, spermine
has limited ability to escape the endosome despite of its good proton-buffering
capacity. Polymerization of spermine
to increase its molecular weight has been shown to be an effective
strategy to enhance its siRNA delivery efficiency.
We have synthesized
various spermine-based poly(β-amino ester)s
in previous work, but several materials did not efficiently mediate
gene silencing due to endosomal entrapment. Literature indicates the same problem, as outlined by Benns et al.,
who synthesized N-Ac-poly(l-histidine)-graft-poly(l-Lysine) to deliver plasmid DNA. Only the inclusion
of chloroquine enhanced the transfection efficiency of their materials. 
Based on our own work and a broad body
of literature, hydrophobic
modification can be beneficial for endosomal escape and gene silencing
efficiency of polyplexes. Some reports
also point out that increasing the hydrophobicity of polyplexes improves
the transfection efficiency and stability. For example, Alshamsan
et al. modified polyethylenimine with oleic acid and stearic acid,
and the hydrophobically modified polymers resulted in 3-fold increased
siRNA delivery compared with the parent PEI for a more pronounced
reduction of integrin α levels. Thanou et al. synthesized trimethylated chitosan for gene delivery.
The trimethylated derivatives appeared to enhance the stability of
the polyplexes so that transfection efficiency was not affected in
the presence of serum. In addition, the
unsaturation of the hydrophobic fragments was reported to enhance
the gene silencing efficiency of siRNA nanocarriers. For example,
MacLachalan and colleagues prepared lipoplexes with hydrophobic tails,
including different numbers of double bonds. They found that lipoplexes
containing unsaturated cationic lipids enhanced luciferase gene knockdown
compared to those containing only saturated cationic lipids. However, in contrast to the current literature
where often commercially available polymers are modified, we aimed
to rationally design amphiphilic polymers from hydrophobically modified
monomers to assess whether chemical properties of lipids in hydrophobic
repeating units can result in PBAEs with conducive properties for
RNA formulation and delivery.
RNA therapeutics have not yet
reached their fullest potential owing
to their poor stability and biodistribution, resulting in insufficient
delivery at the target site. Inhalation, however, efficiently delivers
RNA therapeutics to the lungs for the treatment of pulmonary disorders, − and spray drying nucleic acid nanoparticles improves storage stability. , Spray drying is a widely used technique in the chemical, materials,
cosmetic, food, and pharmaceutical industries, resulting in respirable dry powders.
In this work,
we polymerized spermine and 1,4-butanediol diacrylate
with different amounts of oleylamine, which is an unsaturated fatty
amine. By changing the ratio of the cationic fragment spermine to
the hydrophobic fragment oleylamine, we aimed to identify a balanced
hydrophobic modification for enhanced endosomal escape and gene knockdown
efficiency to establish rational design guidelines for PBAEs for siRNA
delivery. The polymer was able to mediate around 95% gene silencing
at very low polymer excess (N/P 5), compared to other PBAEs, which
require N/P ratios up to 100. The best
performing polyplexes were spray-dried and tested in vitro in an eGFP
expressing cell line to confirm the bioactivity of siRNA against GFP
after storage at room temperature for 3 months.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Materials
Spermine, oleylamine, and
trifluoroacetic acid were purchased from Acros Organics (Thermo Fisher
Scientific), and 1,4-butanediol diacrylate was purchased from Tokyo
chemical industry Co., Ltd. (TCI, Japan). Technically pure n-hexane, dichloromethane, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and
methanol were provided by the Ludwig-Maximilians-University of Munich.
Deuterium oxide, HEPES (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic
acid), thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT), RPMI-1640 medium,
fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin solution,
Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (PBS), trypsin–EDTA
solution (0.05%), and Geneticin (G418) disulfate solution were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich (Taufkirchen, Germany). SYBR Gold dye, Lipofectamine
2000, and Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488) were purchased from Life Technologies
(Darmstadt, Germany).
Amine-modified eGFP siRNA (5′-pACCCUGAAGUUCAUCUGCACCACcg,
3′-ACUGGGACUUCAAGUAGACGGGUGGC), scrambled siRNA (5′-pCGUUAAUCGCGUAUAAUACGCGUat,
3′-CAGCAAUUAGCGCAUAUUAUGCGCAUAp), and siRNA against GAPDH (5′-pGGUCGGAGUCAACGGAUUUGGUCgt,
3′-UUCCAGCCUCAGUUGCCUAAA-CCAGCA) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies
(Leuven, Belgium). “p” denotes a phosphate residue,
lower case letters are 2′-deoxyribonucleotides, capital letters
are ribonucleotides, and underlined capital letters are 2′-O-methylribonucleotides.

2.2.

## Polymer Synthesis
2.2.1.

## Synthesis of Tri-Boc-spermine
Tri-tert-butyl carbonyl spermine, abbreviated as tri-Boc-spermine,
was synthesized as described elsewhere. , In brief,
spermine (1 equiv) was dissolved in methanol and stirred at −78
°C, and ethyl trifluoroacetate (1 equiv) was added dropwise subsequently
and stirred at −78 °C for 1 h and then 0 °C for 1
h. Without isolation, di-tert-butyl decarbonate (4
equiv) was added dropwise to the solution and stirred at room temperature
for 2 days. Finally, the solution was adjusted to a pH above 11 by
25% ammonia and stirred overnight to cleave the trifluoroacetamide
protecting group. The mixture was then evaporated under vacuum, and
the residue was diluted with dichloromethane (DCM) and washed with
distilled water and saturated NaCl aqueous solution. The DCM phase
was finally dried by MgSO4 and concentrated to give the
crude product. The crude product was purified by column chromatography
(CH2Cl2\MeOH\NH3, aq. 7:1:0.1, SiO2, KMnO4; Rf = 0.413).
Tri-Boc-spermine was isolated and characterized by 1H NMR.
Yield: 37%.

2.2.2.

## Synthesis of P(BSpOABAE)
Tri-Boc-spermine
(0.9 equiv) and oleylamine (0.1 equiv) were first mixed, and 1,4-butanediol
diacrylate (1 equiv) was then added and stirred at 120 °C overnight.
The product was dissolved in 2 mL of DCM and precipitated in 50 mL n-hexane. The precipitation was dried under vacuum (50 °C,
20 mbar) for 2 days and finally characterized by 1H NMR.
The target product contains 10% oleylamine. To obtain the polymers
with 30 and 50% oleylamine units, tri-Boc-spermine (0.7 equiv), oleylamine
(0.3 equiv), and tri-Boc-spermine (0.5 equiv), oleylamine (0.5 equiv)
were reacted with 1,4-butanediol diacrylate (1 equiv) under the same
conditions. All of the products were named poly(tri-Boc-spermine and
oleylamine based β-amino ester)s abbreviated as P(BSpOABAE).

2.2.3.

## Synthesis of P(SpOABAE)
The N-Boc
groups of P(BSpOABAE) were removed to yield the final product P(SpOABAE).
To give an example, 100 mg of P(BSpOABAE) was dissolved in 20 mL of
DCM and reacted with 1 mL of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at room temperature
for 2 h. The polymers DCM and TFA always used the same ratios as described.
The reaction solution was then evaporated by rotary evaporation, and
the product was dissolved with 2 mL of methanol and precipitated in
50 mL of n-hexane. The precipitate was dried under
a vacuum (50 °C, 20 mbar) for 2 days to afford the final product.
The product was then characterized by 1H NMR. The product
with 10% oleylamine repeating units was named Sp0.9/OA0.1; the product
with 30% oleylamine units was named Sp0.7/OA0.3; and the product with
50% oleylamine units was named Sp0.5/OA0.5.
The reaction conditions,
yield, and protonable unit of the polymers are summarized in .
 

2.3.

## Preparation and Characterization of Polyplexes
To prepare polyplexes, the polymers were dissolved in high-purity
water at 1 mg/mL as a stock solution and mixed with siRNA stock solution
at 100 μM. The amounts of polymer required were calculated according
to the following equation:
M (polymer in pg) = n siRNA (pmol) × 52 × N/P × protonable unit (g/mol),
where 52 is the number of nucleotides of 25/27mer siRNA; N/P ratio
is the molar ratio of the polymer amino groups (N) and the siRNA phosphate
groups (P); and the protonable unit was calculated by dividing the
molar mass of the repeating unit by the number of protonable amines
in each repeating unit.
P(SpOABAE) and siRNA in an equal volume
of 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH
= 5.3) were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The
P(SpOABAE) polyplexes were then characterized regarding hydrodynamic
diameters and zeta potentials by using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern
Instruments, Malvern, UK). For instance, 100 μL of polyplexes
was added to a disposable microcuvette (Malvern Instruments, Malvern,
UK) and measured for 15 runs 3 times for each sample. The polyplex
suspension was then diluted with 10 mm of HEPES to 700 μL and
filled into a Zeta Cell (Zetasizer Nano series, Malvern, UK) to measure
the zeta potential.

2.4.

## siRNA Encapsulation Efficiency
The
SYBR gold assay was used to evaluate the capacity of the P(SpOABAE)s
to condense siRNA at various N/P ratios (0–20). In brief, P(SpOABAE)
polyplexes were prepared as described in . The polyplexes (100 μL per sample)
were then transferred to a black 96-well plate, and 30 μL of
SYBR Gold (4×) was subsequently added to each well and incubated
at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The fluorescence was then
measured by using a plate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). The fluorescence
intensity of free siRNA (N/P = 0) was set as 100%. Excitation: 485/20
nm and emission: 535/20 nm.

2.5.

## Atomic Force Microscopy
The morphology
of the polyplexes was observed via atomic force microscopy (AFM) (DPN
5000, Nanoink) as described elsewhere. In brief, 10–15 μL of polyplex suspension (N/P 10)
was dropped onto a glass coverslip and dried overnight. The samples
were then observed using AFM in the tapping mode. The data were processed
by using Gwyddion software.

2.6.

## Cellular Uptake
H1299 cells were
seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/well, and the
plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h.
Polyplexes were prepared as described above with 50 pmol Alexa Fluor
AF488-labeled siRNA (AF488-siRNA) at N/P ratios of 5, 7, and 10. Free
AF488-siRNA and hyperbranched PEI 25 kDa (HyPEI25K) were used as negative
control and positive controls, respectively. Cells were incubated
with the polyplexes in a complete RPMI1640 medium for 24 h. The medium
with polyplexes was then discarded, and the cells were rinsed with
PBS and treated with 0.05% trypsin–EDTA (100 μL/well)
for 4 min to detach the cells. Fresh medium (500 μL/well) was
added to deactivate the trypsin. Samples were then transferred to
microcentrifuge tubes and washed with PBS 2 times. Fluorescence was
quantified by flow cytometry (Attune NxT Acoustic Focusing Cytometer,
Thermo Fisher, USA) with or without extracellular quenching by trypan
blue.

2.7.

## GFP Knockdown of Fresh Polyplexes
H1299/eGFP cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 25,000
cells per well and cultured in a CO2 incubator (37 °C,
5% CO2) for 24 h before transfection. P(SpOABAE) polyplexes
at N/P 5, 7, and 10 were prepared as described above in using siRNA
against GFP (siGFP) and scrambled siRNA (siNC) for negative control.
Lipofectamine 2000 with siNC and siGFP was used as the controls. Polyplexes
and lipoplexes containing 50 pmol siNC or siGFP were added to each
well and incubated with the cells for 48 h. The medium was finally
discarded and the cells were washed PBS and treated with 0.05% trypsin–EDTA.
The cells were collected and washed with PBS 2 times and measured
by flow cytometry by using a 488 nm excitation laser and the emitted
light passing through a 530/30 nm band-pass emission filter set (BL-1H)
was detected (Attune NxT Acoustic Focusing Cytometer, Thermo Fisher,
America).

2.8.

## Cell Viability
The cell viability
of the polymers P(SpOABAE) and the polyplexes was determined by an
MTT assay. In brief, H1299 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at
a density of 5000 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. P(SpOABE) and hyPEI25K were diluted with complete
cell culture medium in the range of 1 to 100 μg/mL and incubated
with the cells for 24 h. After the incubation, the medium was discarded
and MTT solution prepared in serum-free RPMI1640 medium was added
to the plate (0.5 mg/mL, 100 μL/well) and incubated for 4 h
in the CO2 incubator. Subsequently, the cell culture medium
was removed, and DMSO (100 μL/well) was added and incubated
at room temperature for 30 min. The optical absorbance was measured
at 570 nm and corrected with background absorbance measured at 680
nm by using a plate reader (TECAN, Switzerland).
Polyplexes
at N/P 5, 7, and 10 were prepared as described in . To each well, 10 μL
of polyplexes containing 5 pmol of siRNA was added to 90 μL
of complete RPMI1640 cell culture medium and incubated in the CO2 incubator for 24 h. The plates were then treated and measured
the same way as described above. HyPEI25K polyplexes were used as
the control.

2.9.

## Endosomal Entrapment
Endosomal entrapment
was determined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). In brief,
H1299 cells were seeded on 13 mm microscope cover glasses (VWR, Allison
Park, PA, USA) in each well (24-well plate, 50,000 cells/well) 24
h before use. The Sp0.9/OA0.1, Sp0.7/OA0.3, and Sp0.5/OA0.5 AF488-siRNA
polyplexes were prepared (N/P 10) and incubated with the cells as
described in . Subsequently, the polyplex-containing medium was discarded
and incubated with 75 nm LysoTracker Red dnd 9 in RPMI1640 complete
medium at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were then washed with PBS
twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature
for 15 min. After washing with PBS twice, the cells were stained with
DAPI (1 μg/mL) at room temperature for 20 min. Finally, the
cells were washed with PBS another 5 times and mounted with FluoSave
reagent (EMD Millipore Corp, USA). The fluorescence images were acquired
using a confocal laser scanning microscope with excitation by a diode
laser (405 nm), an argon laser (488 and 552 nm), and detection in
the blue channel for DAPI (627 nm-750 nm); green channel for AF488
(750–755 nm); and red channel (755–760 nm) for LysoTracker,
respectively (Leica SP8 inverted, software: LAS X, Leica Microsystems
GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

2.10.

## Spray Drying
The best performing
P(SpOABAE) polyplexes Sp0.5/OA.5 were chosen for spray drying. For
production of spray-dried Sp0.5/OA0.5, a B-290 spray drying tower
(Büchi Labortechnik, Flawil, Schweiz) was used as described
before. Nitrogen was chosen as the atomizing
gas, whereas the drying gas was air. In order to avoid dust and other
airborne particles, both nitrogen gas and air were filtered through
a 0.2 μm pore PTFE membrane. Pressurized air was used to ensure
sufficient heating of the air supply and to avoid overheating of the
Büchi’s vacuum pump. The aspirator was set to 70% and
vacuum ranged from −40 to −35 mbar. The airflow was
set to 40 mm, corresponding to 473 NL/h. For particle collection,
a high-efficiency cyclone was attached. The inlet temperature (T-In)
was set to 145 °C, resulting in outlet temperatures (T-Out) of
84 ± 2 °C. The pump rate was set to 1.4 mL/min. Each individual
batch of the Sp0.5/OA0.5-siRNA formulations was diluted to a concentration
of 30 μg of siRNA in 5000 μL of a specified solvent [highly
purified water (HPW) with trehalose at 5% w/V, sterile filtered].
To obtain particularly dry powders, a secondary process was appended
as described before. Briefly, subsequent
drying was performed by switching off the liquid feed and keeping
the heat supply on for 20 min. Spray-dried and subsequently dried
powders resulted in an siRNA to sugar concentration of 0.12 μg
of siRNA/mg of trehalose. The spray-dried powder was stored at 4 and
25 °C for 3 months.

2.11.

## Residual Moisture Content (Karl Fischer Titration)
The residual water content for spray-dried Sp0.5/OA0.5 in 5% trehalose
(W/V) was determined by weighing 10 mg of samples into 2R vials as
described before with equally prepared 1% water standards and empty
vials for blank values using an Aqua 40.00 Karl Fischer Autosampler-Titrator
with corresponding software from Analytik Jena AG (Jena, Germany). For all samples, the three different batches
of spray-dried polyplexes (SDPs) were measured in triplicates. Results
are presented as mean residual moisture (%) ± SD.

2.12.

## Scanning Electron Microscopy
Scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) was used to determine geometric diameters
and morphologies of spray-dried microparticles. A small amount of
spray-dried Sp0.5/OA0.5 was placed on top of a stub covered with double-sided
carbon tape, which was then coated with carbon under a vacuum for
40 s. The powder was imaged using an FEI Helios G3 UC (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, Schwerte, Germany).

2.13.

## GFP Knockdown of SDPs
To evaluate
the in vitro gene silencing efficiency of spray-dried Sp0.5/OA0.5,
H1299-GFP cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2.5
× 104 cells per well in 500 μL of RPMI 1640
medium adding 0.4% of G418 at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For each sample,
7 mg of SDPs, encapsulating siNC or siGFP, were resuspended in 100
μL HPW. At 24 h after the seeding, 100 μL of each sample
was added to 400 μL of fresh culture medium and incubated for
24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The medium was renewed after 24 h.
After a total incubation time of 48 h, cells were washed with PBS,
trypsinized, and collected. After centrifugation at 400 rcf for 5
min, the supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was washed
twice in PBS before being resuspended in PBS with 2 mM EDTA. Samples
were analyzed by flow cytometry (Attune NxT, Thermo Fischer Scientific,
Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) as described under 2.7 with normalizing
the GFP expression by that of untreated (blank) cells, which was considered
100%.

## Results and Discussion
3.1.

## Synthesis and Characterization of P(SpOABAE)
As shown in , spermine was modified into tri-Boc-spermine using the same method
as described elsewhere. Tri-Boc-spermine
was then polymerized with oleylamine and 1,4-butanediol diacrylate
to yield polymer 6 ( ) P(BSpOABAE). As shown in B, the peaks at 0.83, 1.25, and 5.35 ppm
are the representative peaks belonging to oleylamine units, and the
peaks around 1.45 and 3.15 ppm can be assigned to tri-Boc-spermine.
The peaks around 2.43, 2.36, and 4.08 ppm belong to 1,4-butanediol
diacrylate. The appearance of these peaks indicates the existence
of the three monomers in the product. The three resulting P(BSpOABAE)
polymers were named BSp0.9/OA0.1, BSp0.7/OA0.3, and BSp0.5/OA0.5,
respectively.
 P(BSpOABAE) was finally treated with TFA and obtained
the target
product P(SpOABAE). As shown in C, the disappearance of the peak at 1.45 ppm indicates
that the N-Boc groups were removed successfully. Finally, the molar
ratio of the repeating units of spermine and oleylamine units was
calculated according to the integrations of the peak at 5.31 and 4.19
ppm. P(SpOABAE)s with 10, 30, and 50% of oleylamine were named Sp0.9/OA0.1,
Sp0.7/OA0.3, and Sp0.5/OA0.5, respectively. The normalized integrals
of the peaks and the composition of the P(SpOABAE) polymers calculated
accordingly are shown in .
 
3.2.

## siRNA Encapsulation Efficiency
The
siRNA encapsulation efficiency of the three P(SpOABAE)s was measured
by the SYBR Gold assay. In this assay, free siRNA is accessible to
the intercalating dye SYBR Gold and is quantified on the basis of
the fluorescence emitted. As shown in , Sp0.9/OA0.1 had the highest siRNA encapsulation
efficiency at N/P 1, SP0.5/OA0.5 had the lowest encapsulation efficiency,
and Sp0.7/OA0.3 was characterized by intermediate encapsulation efficiency,
correlating with cation content. P(SpOABAE)s with a high percentage
of spermine have a higher density of primary amines and secondary
amines, but all of the P(SpOABAE)s can encapsulate about 100% of the
added siRNA from N/P 5 on. These results indicate that the three polymers
can encapsulate siRNA much more efficiently than previously described
PBAEs, considering that literature reports 30- to 900-fold PBAE weight
excess over siRNA, corresponding to similarly high N/P ratios, for
efficient encapsulation. − 
 
3.3.

## Size and Zeta Potential of the Polyplexes
The hydrodynamic diameter (size) and polydispersity index (PDI)
of the polyplexes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS)
and the zeta potential of the polyplexes was measured by laser Doppler
anemometry (LDA). As shown in , the sizes of the polyplexes were below 100 nm except
for the Sp0.7/OA0.3 polyplexes at N/P 3 and the Sp0.5/OA0.5 polyplexes
at N/P 3. The large sizes of these two formulations may be caused
by the lack of stabilizing charge of the polyplexes thus leading to
aggregation. The zeta potential of the polyplexes corroborates the
SYBR Gold assay results. When siRNA is not fully encapsulated, the
zeta potential is negative because siRNA is present on the surface
of the polyplexes. For example, the Sp0.9/OA0.1 polyplexes at N/P
1, Sp0.7/OA0.3 polyplexes at N/Ps 1 and 3, and the Sp0.5/OA0.5 polyplexes
at N/Ps 1 and 3 present accessible siRNA on their surfaces as reflected
in measurable fluorescence and negative zeta potentials. When siRNA
is fully encapsulated, the zeta potential turned to positive. The
PDI of the polyplexes was lowest when the siRNA was quantitatively
encapsulated, while an excess free polymer or free siRNA led to higher
PDIs. Additionally, it needs to be noted that the polymers alone associate
at concentrations above 10 μg/mL (Figure S4), which is above the concentrations used for cell culture
experiments, however.
 
3.4.

## Morphology of the Polyplexes
As shown
in , the morphology
of the polyplexes was visualized via AFM. The polyplexes prepared
with Sp0.5/OA0.5 and Sp0.7/OA0.3 appeared mostly spherical (A and
B) and showed more uniform shape than the polyplexes made from Sp0.9/OA0.1
(C). The measured height can be used to estimate the size of the polyplexes.
Compared to the DLS data, the sizes of the polyplexes seems smaller,
although some bigger particles exist, which could be due to the shrinkage
of polyplexes after drying while the DLS measured the hydrodynamic
diameter.
 
3.5.

## Cellular Uptake
Cellular uptake was
quantified by flow cytometry and compared to hyPEI25K polyplexes as
a positive control and free AF488-siRNA as a negative control. P(SpOABAE)
polyplexes with 50 pmol AF488-siRNA at N/P 5, 7, and 10 were compared
as full siRNA encapsulation was observed from N/P 5 on according to
the SYBR Gold assays ( ). Polyplexes at higher N/P ratios were not used to avoid
possible cytotoxicity. In half of the cell samples, 0.4% trypan blue
was used to quench the extracellular fluorescence associated with
polyplexes, which bind to the surface of the cells but are not internalized.
As shown in , the fluorescence intensity of the quenched and unquenched samples
did not significantly differ, which means the polyplexes were efficiently
internalized into the cells. Among the P(SpOABAE) polyplexes with
different percentages of oleylamine, the formulations with 50% oleylamine
units (Sp0.5/OA0.5) achieved the highest cellular uptake, the formulations
with 30% oleylamine units (Sp0.7/OA0.3) showed the lowest cellular
uptake, and the cellular uptake of the group with 10% oleylamine units
(Sp0.9/OA0.1) resulted in intermediate values. This interesting observation
was explained by different factors that affect the cellular uptake
of polyplexes. While cationic charge is known to mediate strong interaction
with the negatively charged cell membrane and trigger adsorptive endocytosis, amphiphilic and hydrophobic materials, which
can induce a hexagonal phase, have been described to have fusogenic
character. Oleic acid has been reported
to form such hexagonal phases, and it
was therefore concluded that both, high contents of cationic side
chains (>70%) and high contents of hydrophobic, unsaturated side
chains
(>30%), can mediate highly efficient cellular uptake of RNA polyplexes.
Altogether, all the P(SpOABAE) polyplex formulations, even the (Sp0.7/OA0.3)
polyplexes, showed higher cellular uptake than hyPEI25K polyplexes.
Regardless of the size and PDI of the polyplexes, the cellular uptake
of P(SpOABAE) polyplexes increased from N/P 5 to N/P 10.
 
3.6.

## GFP Knockdown
To investigate whether
high cellular uptake also correlates with corresponding gene silencing,
an enhanced green fluorescence protein (eGFP) knockdown assay was
performed in stably expressing H1299/eGFP cells to assess the gene
silencing efficiency of the P(SpOABAE) polyplexes on the protein level.
P(SpOABAE) polyplexes with 10, 30, and 50% oleylamine units at N/P
5, 7, and 10 were compared in the knockdown experiment. As shown in , the P(SpOABAE)
polyplexes with 10% oleylamine units (Sp0.9/OA0.1), although they
had resulted in high cellular uptake, did not achieve an efficient
GFP knockdown. On the other hand, P(SpOABAE) polyplexes with 30% oleylamine
(Sp0.7/OA0.3), despite their less efficient cellular uptake in comparison
with the other two polyplex formulations, mediated higher GFP knockdown
efficiency than Sp0.9/OA0.1. Finally, the P(SpOABAE) polyplexes with
50% oleylamine (Sp0.5/OA0.5) not only were internalized most efficiently
but also achieved the highest GFP knockdown efficiency. These results
could be explained by the different interactions with the cell membrane,
as discussed above, but also by differing endosomal escape abilities
of the P(SpOABAE) polyplexes. The high cellular uptake of Sp0.9/OA0.1
could be due to its relatively high amount of primary and secondary
amines, which enhanced the permeability of the polyplexes. However, due to the problem of endosomal entrapment,
these polyplexes did not achieve an efficient GFP knockdown despite
efficient cellular uptake. Polymers Sp0.7/OA0.3 and Sp0.5/OA0.5 are
expected to have higher endosomal escape ability due to their higher
oleylamine content. As a result, the higher cellular uptake of Sp0.5/OA0.5
in comparison to Sp0.7/OA0.3 polyplexes led to very high GFP knockdown
of >95%.
 Based on the results above, the physicochemical
properties of PBAE
polyplexes, such as size, size distribution, and morphology, did not
have decisive effects on the gene silencing efficiency. Once the N/P
increased within the biocompatible range, the cellular uptake in vitro
also increased regardless of the polyplexes’ size. However,
high cellular uptake did not always lead to high gene silencing efficiency
due to the obstacle of endosomal entrapment, the release of siRNA
into the cytoplasm, and its incorporation into the RISC.
Compared
with the pristine polymer, although the complexation ability
of the hydrophobically modified PBAE was comparable, the gene silencing
efficiency was dramatically improved from 4 to over 90%. Many studies have shown that incorporation of hydrophobic
chains can enhance internalization and improve the nucleic acid delivery
efficiency. For example, Rata-Aguilar et al. found that inclusion
of small hydrophobic fragments into the polycation backbone improves
the stability and transfection efficiency of plasmid DNA. Alshamsan et al. modified branched PEI with
oleic acid and stearic acid and observed higher cellular uptake of
siRNA polyplexes and more pronounced reduction of integrin levels. In addition, incorporation of lipophilic or
hydrophobic groups into the polymers should also affect interactions
with endo/lysosomal membranes, resulting in a more efficient escape. Oleylamine contains unsaturated double bonds
in its structure, which is similar with the so-called fusogenic lipid
DOPE. The unsaturation of the hydrocarbon chains is one of the conditions
for inducing the transition from the lamellar to the hexagonal fusogenic
phase for rapid fusion with anionic membranes. Oleic acid has a particular tendency to form such hexagonal
phases at pH = 6.4, which can be found in the endosome. Heyes et al. compared the fusogenicity of lipid
nanoparticles with different numbers of double bonds in the hydrophobic
tails. They found that lipoplexes containing
unsaturated cationic lipids enhanced luciferase gene knockdown compared
with those containing only saturated cationic lipids. The improvement
in efficiency was correlated with the extent of unsaturation of the
cationic lipid hydrophobic domain and the fusogenicity of the delivery
system. Hence, the very high gene silencing efficacy of the most hydrophobic
polyspermines at comparably low polymer excess in the formulation
described here can be explained by a balance between cationic and
hydrophobic interactions between the polyplexes and (intra)cellular
membranes. It is therefore hypothesized that the oleic acid analogues
in the polymers promoted efficient endosomal escape, reflected by
a stronger impact of hydrophobic content on gene silencing than on
cellular uptake. To further assess the suitability of the most promising
polymer for pulmonary siRNA delivery, gene silencing in an air–liquid
interface model of mucus-producing 16HBE14o-cells on Transwells was
quantified as described before, where
significant endogenous gene silencing was observed (Figure S5).

3.7.

## Cell Viability
Polycations can protect
siRNA against replacement from the polyplex by other polyanions and
against degradation by enzymes. However, positive charges, needed
for interactions with cell membranes, can also inhibit crucial biological
processes and lead to cytotoxic effects. The cell viability of cells treated with the polymers P(SpOABAE)
and polyplexes was therefore measured by MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium
bromide) assays. Hyperbranched polyethylenimine 25 kDa (hyPEI25K)
was used as the control. As expected, with increasing polymer concentrations,
the cell viability decreased. As shown in A, the free P(SpOABAE) polymers were more
biocompatible than hyPEI25K at concentrations higher than 20 μg/mL.
At lower concentrations, P(SpOABAE)s caused comparable toxic events
as hyPEI25K, which could be attributed to the hydrophobic fusogenic
lipid that enhanced the cell permeability of the polymer. However,
all three P(SpOABAE)s did not show strong cytotoxicity up to 50 μg/mL.
The typical PBAE concentrations for transfection experiments described
above were between 2 and 7 μg/mL, avoiding cytotoxicity-related
measurement artifacts in the flow cytometry-based experiments.
 The cellular biocompatibility of the P(SpOABAE)
polyplexes at N/P
5, 7, and 10 was also determined, and hyPEI25K polyplexes were used
as the control. As shown in B, the cytotoxicity of P(SpOABAE) polyplexes was more
or less comparable with hyPEI25K. Sp0.9/OA0.1 polyplexes showed a
slightly higher toxicity than the other P(SpOABAE)s, which could be
caused by the higher content of primary and secondary amines in this
polymer.

3.8.

## Endosomal Entrapment
The endo- and
lysosome entrapment of the polyplexes was visualized by CLSM. Endosomal
entrapment of polyplexes and other nanoparticles is one of the main
barriers for RNA delivery with nonviral carriers. Typically, nanoparticles entrapped in endosomes follow
the endosomal-lysosomal route, which is a series of organelle merging
in the endocytic pathway and a dynamic, interconnected vesicular network. The endocytotic vesicles fuse with pre-existing
early endosomes within seconds, which mature into late endosomes and
finally fuse with lysosomes. 
As
shown in ,
polyplexes containing AF488-siRNA are displayed in green; lysosomes
were stained with LysoTracker (displayed in red), and the merged picture
indicated the colocalization of polyplexes with endosomes. Sp0.5/OA0.5
polyplexes showed the highest cellular uptake of particles widely
distributed in each cell, indicating escape from the endo/lysosomes.
This result confirms that increasing degree of modification with oleylamine
can improve endosomal escape of polyplexes and explains its high GFP
knockdown efficiency. As discussed above, oleic acid shares a structure
with so-called fusogenic lipids containing an unsaturated double bond
in its structure, which can enhance the interaction with endosomal
membranes to promote endosomal escape. Sp0.7/OA0.3 polyplexes did not show a different intracellular distribution
compared with Sp0.9/OA0.1 despite their different gene silencing behavior,
which is probably due to the overall lower cellular uptake of Sp0.7/OA0.3.
However, it can be concluded from the transfection results that the
small amount of Sp0.7/OA0.3 polyplexes taken up did indeed escape
the endosome more efficiently than Sp0.9/OA0.1 polyplexes.
 
3.9.

## Polyplex Spray Drying, Characterization, and
Evaluation
Since Sp0.5/OA0.5 showed the best performance
regarding the cellular uptake and gene silencing, we investigated
spray drying of Sp0.5/OA0.5 polyplexes for pulmonary delivery of siRNA
and for improving the storage stability of the polyplexes. For pulmonary
delivery, aerodynamic sizes between 1 and 5 μm are crucial for
successful delivery to the alveolar space. Trehalose was used as a typical matrix for obtaining amorphous microparticles,
which based on previous studies allow full siRNA recovery and colloidal
stability of the polyplexes. In order
to visualize the spray-dried microparticles, we employed SEM. As shown
in , the spray-dried
particles showed a spherical morphology, and the sizes of the particles
were overall below 5 μm.
 Pulmonary delivery via dry powder formulation requires
low residual
moisture to avoid possible aggregation processes. Residual moisture can also be a source of microbiological
instability and RNase contamination. Therefore, the water content
of the spray-dried Sp0.5/OA0.5 polyplexes (SDP) were determined by
Karl Fischer titration. As shown in , the residual moisture of freshly spray-dried
SDP was around 2.21%, and the residual moisture of SDP after storage
at 4 and 25 °C for 3 months increased to 2.64 and 2.86%, respectively.
The SDP powder, which was prepared with 20 min subsequent drying,
on the other hand, had a residual moisture of only 1.99%, which after
storage at 4 and 25 °C for 3 months increased to 2.00 and 2.27%,
respectively. It was reported recently that spray-dried lipid nanoparticle
formulations require residual moisture levels <4% to maintain storage
and colloidal stability. Since all SDP formulations achieved comparably
low residual moisture levels, it can be expected that the obtained
formulations can avoid nucleation and degradation processes over time
to maintain the amorphous state of the formulation which is favorable
for polyplex preservation. 
 It is fundamental to maintain siRNA bioactivity
during preservation
processes, such as spray drying for therapeutic siRNA delivery. Therefore,
the gene silencing efficiency of the SDPs was determined by measuring
GFP expression using flow cytometry and was normalized by the GFP
expression of untreated (blank) cells. As cytotoxicity could also
lead to decreased GFP expression, the H1299/eGFP cells were treated
with pure spray-dried 5% trehalose (SDP 5% T) for comparison. SDP
5% T did not have a significant influence on the fluorescence intensity
of cells in comparison with untreated cells (blank), which confirms
the biocompatibility of the matrix excipient. Additionally, freshly
prepared Sp0.5/OA0.5 polyplexes were used as a nonspray-dried positive
control. All spray-dried formulations mediated GFP silencing efficiency
of more than 90% even after storage at room temperature for 3 months,
which confirms siRNA bioactivity after spray drying and storage ( ). Storage stability
of RNA formulations is very important for clinical applications. For
example, the COVID-19 mRNA vaccine produced by BioNTech/Pfizer requires
ultracold (−80 °C) long-term (6 months) storage and can
be transported at −20 °C and stored for up to 2 weeks. These cold and ultracold delivery and storage
conditions are extremely energy-consuming, prone to faults and failure,
and a challenge for low- and middle-income countries. A formulation, which is stable at room temperature, would
improve not only convenience but also socio-economic factors and accessibility
to RNA therapeutics.
 

4.

## Conclusions
Amphiphilic spermine-based
poly(β-amino ester)s are reported
to be very promising for siRNA delivery. However, the hydrophobic
modification of poly(β-amino ester)s and its role in siRNA delivery
have not been investigated in detail. In this study, we synthesized
several poly(β-amino ester)s with different ratios of spermine
and oleylamine and formulated polyplexes that can achieve more than
90% GFP silencing. We found that increasing degrees of hydrophobic
modification with oleylamine can enhance the siRNA delivery efficiency
and gene silencing efficiency of spermine-based poly(β-amino
ester)s. The best performing polyplexes composed of Sp0.5/OA0.5 were
spray-dried with 5% trehalose. After storage at 25 and 4 °C for
3 months, the spray-dried powder still reached more than 90% gene
silencing efficiency, which confirms the RNA and polyplex stability
as well as their translational potential. Taken together, this study
provided guidelines for rational design of RNA nanocarrier materials
and established a stable spray-dried formulation for siRNA delivery.
