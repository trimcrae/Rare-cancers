## Background
Sepsis remains a major global health burden and is characterized by a dysregulated host immune response that evolves from early hyperinflammation to late-stage immunosuppression. Despite advances in supportive care, effective pharmacological therapies capable of modulating this dynamic immune landscape are still lacking. Messenger RNA (mRNA)–based therapeutics have emerged as a promising approach due to their ability to enable transient, controllable expression of immunomodulatory proteins; however, their clinical translation is hindered by intrinsic instability, rapid degradation, and inefficient tissue delivery.

## Main body
Nanotechnology offers innovative solutions to overcome these limitations by improving mRNA protection, cellular uptake, and targeted delivery. A variety of nanocarriers, including lipid nanoparticles, polymeric systems, inorganic nanomaterials, and biomimetic vectors, have been developed for mRNA delivery in sepsis. Emerging strategies incorporating macrophage-targeting ligands and stimuli-responsive release mechanisms further enhance delivery precision and therapeutic efficacy. Preclinical studies demonstrate that mRNA-based nanotherapeutics can suppress cytokine storms, restore immune function, and attenuate organ injury in experimental models of sepsis.

## Conclusions
This review summarizes recent advances in mRNA-based nanotherapeutics for sepsis, with a focus on delivery strategies, immunomodulatory mechanisms, and translational potential. Key challenges related to delivery efficiency, safety, and regulatory considerations are discussed. Finally, future directions are proposed for the development of individualized, stage-specific mRNA nanotherapies aimed at restoring immune homeostasis in critically ill patients.

## Introduction
Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host immune response to infection, characterized by systemic inflammation, immune dysfunction, and eventual progression to multiple organ failure [ , ]. Despite advancements in supportive care and antimicrobial therapies [ ], sepsis remains a major clinical challenge worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality rates and limited effective therapeutic options [ – ]. The central obstacle in sepsis management lies in its complex and dynamic immunopathology—typically manifesting as an initial hyperinflammatory phase (cytokine storm), followed by a state of immunosuppression that predisposes patients to secondary infections and delayed recovery [ – ].
mRNA therapeutics have recently emerged as a promising intervention strategy capable of modulating cellular functions through the transient expression of therapeutic proteins, such as cytokines, transcription factors, and antioxidant enzymes [ , ]. Unlike DNA-based approaches, mRNA does not integrate into the genome, allows rapid protein translation, and is highly programmable, offering a controllable platform for therapeutic design [ , ]. These features make mRNA an attractive candidate for stage-specific interventions in sepsis, enabling both suppression of excessive inflammation and restoration of immune competence. However, the clinical application of mRNA remains hindered by intrinsic challenges, including its structural instability, susceptibility to extracellular RNases, and difficulty in achieving efficient delivery to target cells within inflamed or damaged tissues [ , ]. To overcome these barriers, the development of safe and efficient delivery systems has become a research priority. Nanomedicine offers innovative solutions, with LNPs, polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic nanomaterials, and biomimetic nanocarriers showing promise in protecting mRNA, enhancing cellular uptake, and achieving precise delivery to specific immune cells or inflammatory sites [ – ]. Nevertheless, studies on mRNA nanodelivery systems for sepsis therapy are still in their infancy, and comprehensive evaluations remain scarce. While several preclinical studies have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic potential, major challenges persist regarding delivery efficiency, targeting specificity, long-term safety, and translational feasibility [ – ]. Furthermore, stage-adapted mRNA therapeutic strategies for sepsis are underexplored, and there is a lack of design frameworks for personalized and multi-targeted interventions.
In recent years, several reviews have summarized nucleic acid-based nanotherapeutics for sepsis [ – ]. However, most of these studies mainly focus on general nucleic acid drugs or nanomedicine platforms. In contrast, this review specifically focuses on mRNA-based nanotherapeutics, highlighting their immune modulation strategies in sepsis and the key challenges for clinical translation. By integrating recent representative studies, we aim to provide a comprehensive perspective on how mRNA nanotechnology may reshape therapeutic strategies for sepsis.

## Immunopathology of sepsis
Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory syndrome triggered by infection, with its hallmark being a profound dysregulation of host immune responses [ , ]. Current paradigms describe a biphasic immunological trajectory during sepsis: an initial hyperinflammatory phase (cytokine storm) followed by a subsequent immunosuppressive phase (immune paralysis) [ , ]. This dynamic immune shift not only increases the complexity of clinical management but also raises the bar for developing effective targeted therapies [ ].
In the early stage, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activate host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), initiating downstream signaling cascades—particularly the NF-κB pathway [ , ]. Activation of these pathways leads to the massive release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, creating a “cytokine storm” that results in systemic inflammation, coagulation dysfunction, and multi-organ injury [ , ]. The NLRP3 inflammasome also plays a pivotal role in amplifying the inflammatory response by promoting the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, exacerbating tissue damage [ , ]. However, many patients transition rapidly from this hyperinflammatory state into profound immunosuppression [ ]. This stage is characterized by immune cell dysfunction, T-cell exhaustion, lymphocyte apoptosis, and the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [ , , ]. In addition, the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules such as TIM3 [ ], BTLA-4 [ ], and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [ ] further suppresses T-cell activity and impairs host defense, increasing susceptibility to secondary infections [ , ]. (Figure ) Immunoparalysis is closely associated with poor clinical outcomes, highlighting the critical need for therapies that can restore immune competence during this phase [ , , ]. Thus, there is an urgent demand for precision therapies capable of stage-specific modulation: For example, during the early hyperinflammatory phase of sepsis, mRNA therapeutics encoding anti-inflammatory mediators or regulators of inflammatory signaling may help attenuate excessive cytokine production. In contrast, during the later immunosuppressive phase, mRNA-based strategies aimed at restoring immune cell function or enhancing antimicrobial responses may be beneficial. These concepts suggest that stage-specific modulation could guide the design of mRNA nanotherapeutics by enabling the selection of appropriate therapeutic targets and delivery strategies according to the immunological status of sepsis [ , ]. Traditional anti-inflammatory agents and immune stimulators often lack the selectivity required for such dynamic control, and many targeted therapies have failed to significantly improve outcomes in clinical trials [ – ]. Against this backdrop, mRNA therapeutics—with their high programmability, transient expression profiles, and robust protein production capabilities—have emerged as an ideal platform for individualized, stage-adapted immunomodulation [ , ]. The inherent customizability of mRNA offers a new paradigm for correcting immune dysregulation in sepsis, facilitating precise transitions between “immune suppression” and “immune activation” and paving the way for innovative translational strategies.

 

## mRNA Therapeutic Strategies
In recent years, mRNA therapeutics have demonstrated remarkable flexibility and programmability, showing immense promise in cancer immunotherapy, vaccine development, and tissue regeneration [ , – ]. Native mRNA consists of a 5′ cap, 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR), open reading frame (ORF), 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), and a poly(A) tail [ ]. To enhance its stability, translational efficiency, and immunocompatibility in vivo, extensive structural optimizations have been implemented. At the 5′ cap, anti-reverse cap analogs (ARCA) and Cap 1 structures are employed to improve stability and reduce innate immune recognition [ ]. Within the coding region, codon optimization, adjustment of GC content, and elimination of unfavorable secondary RNA structures further promote efficient protein expression [ ]. Chemical modifications, such as incorporating pseudouridine (ψ) and 5-methylcytidine (5mC), significantly reduce mRNA immunogenicity and prolong its in vivo half-life [ ]. Together, these modifications provide a molecular foundation for improved biocompatibility and therapeutic potential [ ]. Mechanistically, mRNA therapeutics exert their effects through cell reprogramming, signaling pathway modulation, organ protection, and immune homeostasis restoration [ , , , ]. For example, mRNA-driven expression of specific proteins can induce macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, modulate dendritic cell maturation, and activate or reinvigorate effector T-cell responses [ , ]. Additionally, mRNA therapies can balance pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine production, promoting the restoration of immune homeostasis [ ]. These properties underscore the substantial potential of mRNA therapeutics as a next-generation precision intervention for sepsis. ## Nanocarriers for mRNA delivery
Despite the immense therapeutic promise of mRNA-based interventions, their clinical application is hindered by inherent limitations such as susceptibility to nuclease degradation, poor stability in biological fluids, and inefficient cellular uptake [ , ]. To enable effective and safe delivery, the design of rational nanocarrier systems is critical. Ideal delivery vehicles should protect mRNA integrity, facilitate cellular internalization and endosomal escape, and provide organ- or cell-specific targeting capabilities [ ].

## LNPs
LNPs represent the most widely adopted mRNA delivery platform and have been pivotal in enabling cell-selective CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing [ ], as well as serving as the core technology for COVID-19 mRNA vaccines [ , , ]. Composed of ionizable lipids, structural lipids, cholesterol, and PEGylated lipids, LNPs encapsulate negatively charged mRNA through electrostatic interactions, offering high encapsulation efficiency and robust protection. Once internalized, their positive charge under endosomal acidic conditions promotes endosomal escape and facilitates cytoplasmic release of mRNA for efficient transfection [ – ]. Siegwart and colleagues recently reported the design and optimization of densely brush-shaped polymer lipids that reduce anti-PEG antibody binding, thereby improving protein expression consistency during repeated dosing of mRNA therapies [ ]. However, systemic administration of LNPs under inflammatory conditions has been associated with a phenomenon termed inflammation exacerbation (IE), characterized by heightened inflammatory responses [ ]. In murine models of acute inflammation, modRNA-LNP delivery significantly intensified local and systemic inflammatory markers, with the effect largely attributable to the LNP components rather than the mRNA cargo itself [ ]. These findings underscore the urgent need to refine LNP immunogenicity and organ-targeting properties for applications in sepsis, particularly during the acute inflammatory phase (Fig. top left). Recent studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of RNA-loaded nanoparticles in lung inflammatory diseases. For example, pulmonary delivery of TNF-α siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles has been shown to preferentially accumulate in macrophages and neutrophils and significantly reduce TNF-α and IL-6 levels in murine acute lung injury models [ ]. Similar strategies using nanoparticle-mediated delivery of TLR4 siRNA or anti-inflammatory microRNAs have also effectively suppressed inflammatory signaling pathways and attenuated lung injury [ , ]. These findings highlight the potential of optimized lipid nanoparticle platforms for RNA-based therapies in inflammatory diseases such as sepsis.

 

## Polymer-based nanoparticles
Polymeric nanoparticles offer a promising alternative for mRNA delivery due to their structural versatility, ease of synthesis and modification, high drug-loading capacity, and favorable biocompatibility. Common synthetic polymers include poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polylactic acid (PLA), polyethyleneimine (PEI), and polyethylene glycol (PEG), while natural polymers such as chitosan, dextran, and hyaluronic acid (HA) have also been widely employed [ , ]. Cationic polymers like PEI and poly-L-lysine enhance mRNA encapsulation efficiency and cellular uptake, although high-molecular-weight PEI is associated with notable cytotoxicity [ , ]. Chitosan, by virtue of its inherent cationic nature, facilitates efficient nucleic acid binding and delivery without additional modifications [ ]. Furthermore, polymeric systems can be engineered for controlled release by adjusting material composition, particle size, and surface functionalization, prolonging mRNA expression in vivo and supporting multi-stage therapeutic interventions in sepsis [ ]. ## Biomimetic nanocarriers
Biomimetic nanocarriers integrate the advantages of natural biological membranes with synthetic nanomaterials, forming innovative delivery platforms that include cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, exosomes, and systems derived from natural membranes of platelets, macrophages, and other immune cells. By cloaking nanoparticles with cellular membranes or exosome-derived membranes, these systems retain the physicochemical benefits of synthetic materials while inheriting immune stealth, biocompatibility, and targeting capabilities from natural membranes. Such features confer excellent immune evasion and extended circulation half-life, making biomimetic carriers particularly suitable for addressing immune suppression during the late phase of sepsis and for targeting inflamed tissues or specific immune cell subsets such as macrophages [ , ]. These biomimetic platforms have already demonstrated therapeutic potential in diseases such as SARS-CoV-2 infection, cancer, and atherosclerosis [ – ]. However, challenges remain in scalable manufacturing, drug loading capacity, and structural stability, which limit their translational prospects [ ]. Advances in membrane engineering using sources such as red blood cells, leukocytes, platelets, tumor cells, and exosomes have markedly improved the efficiency of mRNA nanodelivery systems for targeted drug delivery, diagnostics, and therapeutic interventions across diverse pathological states [ ]. ## Metal–Organic frameworks (MOFs) and inorganic nanoparticles
Emerging metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and other inorganic nanoparticles (e.g., silica-based systems) exhibit highly tunable structures and strong environmental responsiveness, enabling stimuli-triggered mRNA release in inflammatory microenvironments characterized by lowered pH and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS). Compared with linear cationic polymers, dendritic MOF-cationic polymers have demonstrated superior colloidal stability and enhanced protection of mRNA cargo from enzymatic degradation, leading to robust transfection in target cells [ ] Silica nanoparticles, with their high surface area, tunable size and porosity, and excellent biodegradability and biocompatibility, have also emerged as attractive mRNA carriers [ , ]. Modifications such as biomimetic silicification [ ], CaClOH coating [ ], and polyethyleneimine conjugation [ ] have markedly improved delivery efficiency across various cell types. Notably, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) coated with polysaccharides can suppress inflammatory responses, providing a promising avenue for sepsis mRNA therapeutics [ ].
Given the heterogeneity of nanocarriers in physicochemical properties, biodistribution, degradation pathways, and immunomodulatory effects, their selection or combination should be tailored to the distinct pathological stages of sepsis. Current efforts focus on developing active targeting strategies, such as decorating nanocarrier surfaces with ligands specific to macrophage receptors (e.g., mannose) to enable precise localization to diseased tissues or immune cell subsets [ , ]. Additionally, stimuli-responsive systems that leverage sepsis-associated microenvironmental cues (pH, ROS) can ensure site-specific mRNA release while minimizing off-target effects [ , ]. PEGylation and related strategies are also employed to modulate blood circulation time and enhance accumulation in critical organs such as the lungs, liver, and kidneys [ ]. Collectively, rational design of mRNA nanocarriers must balance delivery efficiency, safety, biocompatibility, and therapeutic targeting to address the complex immunopathology of sepsis and enable personalized, stage-specific interventions. ## Preclinical Studies of mRNA nanotherapeutics in sepsis
With the rapid evolution of mRNA nanodelivery technologies, an increasing number of preclinical studies have demonstrated promising results in sepsis therapy, offering innovative strategies to address persistent infection, immune suppression, and target organ injury (Fig. ). To combat immune paralysis induced by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infection in sepsis, Chunwei Tang et al. developed CRV peptide-modified lipid nanoparticles (CRV/LNP-RNA) capable of co-delivering mRNA encoding a chimeric antigen receptor (SasA-CAR) and siRNA targeting caspase-11 to macrophages. This strategy enabled in situ reprogramming of host macrophages into CAR macrophages (CAR-MΦ) with enhanced specificity and bactericidal activity. The engineered CAR-MΦ exhibited markedly improved phagocytosis and clearance of MRSA, effectively suppressing immune evasion mechanisms and restoring host immune homeostasis. This nanotherapeutic approach based on immune cell reprogramming offers a novel platform for tackling multidrug-resistant bacterial infections and holds strong translational potential [ ]. (Fig. ) In another study addressing immune suppression, researchers designed a lipid-based nanocarrier encapsulating mRNA encoding a fusion protein of antimicrobial peptide and cathepsin B (AMP-CatB), further modified with vitamin C to enhance targeting specificity. This system generated “macrophages with augmented antimicrobial capacity” (MACs), which accumulated bactericidal molecules within lysosomes, thereby increasing intracellular killing of pathogens. Animal experiments demonstrated that mRNA-transfected MACs achieved excellent antimicrobial effects in vivo, effectively clearing S. aureus and E. coli infections and significantly improving survival in immunocompromised mice. This work highlights a promising mRNA delivery-based cell therapy strategy for treating resistant sepsis [ ]. (Fig. ).

 

 

 

For sepsis-associated lung injury, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), researchers have developed a structurally simple delivery platform based on single-component ionizable amphiphilic Janus dendrimers (IAJD). These materials self-assemble into stable nanoparticles via simple mixing and exhibit high mRNA loading capacity with pulmonary targeting potential. Delivery of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) mRNA via this platform to the lungs of mice induced local anti-inflammatory cytokine expression, which rapidly alleviated inflammation and prevented long-term fibrosis. In acute lung injury (ALI) models, this system significantly reduced pulmonary pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and preserved lung tissue architecture, demonstrating strong preclinical potential for localized mRNA delivery in sepsis-related lung injury [ ].
Additionally, a separate study utilized cationic lipids to form mRNA complexes that were delivered to the lungs of rats via a vibrating mesh nebulizer for rapid local therapy. The mRNA encoded either the NF-κB inhibitory super-repressor IκBα (IκBα-SR) or the antioxidant enzyme SOD3. In vitro, target protein expression was detectable in pulmonary epithelial cells within 4 h of transfection, accompanied by significant suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine release. In an E. coli pneumonia model, IκBα-SR mRNA reduced pulmonary edema (assessed via wet/dry ratio) and arterial pCO₂, while SOD3 mRNA improved lung compliance and oxygenation (AaDO₂) and decreased bacterial loads. Both mRNA treatments effectively lowered inflammatory cytokine levels and leukocyte infiltration in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and serum, confirming the rapid, potent anti-inflammatory potential of nebulized mRNA delivery in ARDS [ ]. In another study, sepsis-induced MEK/ERK pathway activation was found to downregulate Skp2 expression, impairing SLC3A2 ubiquitination and membrane localization, which compromised cellular antioxidant capacity and triggered ferroptosis. Intravenous administration of Skp2 mRNA encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles restored Skp2 levels, inhibited ferroptosis in pulmonary cells, and ameliorated lung injury, revealing a novel target and intervention strategy for sepsis therapy [ ].(Fig. ).

 

Collectively, these animal model studies provide robust preclinical evidence supporting the application of mRNA nanomedicines in sepsis. Future research should prioritize further optimization of delivery systems to improve organ- and cell-level targeting, reduce toxicity, and enhance delivery efficiency. Equally critical are investigations into intervention timing, combinatorial target strategies, and long-term therapeutic outcomes to facilitate clinical translation. These advancements hold great promise for realizing precise and effective mRNA nanotherapies in the management of sepsis.

## Challenges and future perspectives in clinical translation
With the rapid advances of mRNA nanotherapeutics across various disease areas, their application in sepsis has garnered significant interest. However, the intricate pathophysiology of sepsis, involvement of multiple organs, and the highly heterogeneous immune states present formidable challenges for the design and clinical translation of mRNA delivery systems [ , ]. Importantly, the pathophysiological characteristics of sepsis may also influence the safety profile of mRNA nanotherapeutics. Sepsis is associated with increased vascular permeability, dysregulated immune responses, and altered immune cell distribution, which may affect nanoparticle biodistribution and increase the risk of off-target delivery. In addition, excessive immune activation induced by immunostimulatory strategies could potentially trigger secondary inflammatory responses or cytokine storms if not carefully controlled. Therefore, careful optimization of dosing, delivery systems, and immunomodulatory targets will be critical for improving the safety of mRNA nanotherapeutics in sepsis. Systematically identifying and overcoming these hurdles is critical for bringing mRNA therapeutics into clinical use for sepsis.
Firstly, mRNA’s intrinsic instability and limited expression efficiency in vivo remain major barriers [ ]. Unmodified mRNA is highly susceptible to nuclease degradation and can inadvertently trigger innate immune responses, resulting in excessive immunogenicity that compromises therapeutic safety and efficacy [ ]. Moreover, the multi-organ involvement in sepsis necessitates delivery systems capable of traversing biological barriers to achieve precise targeting of the lungs, liver, kidneys, heart, and other vital organs. Off-target expression in non-diseased tissues may provoke adverse effects and raise safety concerns [ , ]. Finally, the large-scale production of high-quality mRNA therapeutics and nanocarriers involves substantial technical and financial challenges, while quality control standards and regulatory frameworks for mRNA-based interventions remain underdeveloped—further constraining their translational progress [ ].
To address these bottlenecks, several innovative strategies have been proposed. At the molecular level, chemical modifications such as pseudouridine (ψ) incorporation significantly reduce innate immune activation while improving in vivo stability and translational efficiency. Structural optimization of 5′ cap analogs, untranslated regions (UTRs), and poly(A) tails further enhances the bioactivity of mRNA. Regarding delivery systems, biomimetic nanocarriers such as cell membrane-coated nanoparticles and exosomes demonstrate superior immune evasion and tissue-targeting properties, offering safer and more precise delivery. Additionally, the integration of stimuli-responsive materials—sensitive to the inflammatory microenvironment—enables spatiotemporal controlled release of mRNA. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning also provide powerful tools for rational design of mRNA sequences and carriers, facilitating prediction of immunogenicity hotspots and protein expression efficiency to accelerate system optimization.
Looking ahead, mRNA therapeutics for sepsis are anticipated to evolve toward multi-target synergistic delivery approaches, enabling the concurrent expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, immune-modulatory proteins, and tissue repair factors for comprehensive, multi-mechanistic intervention. The development of integrated theranostic platforms could enable real-time monitoring of therapeutic responses and dynamic adjustment of treatment regimens, advancing precision medicine in critical care. Furthermore, rapid mRNA design and production platforms tailored to individual immune phenotypes may fulfill the need for personalized interventions during the acute phase of sepsis, ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

## Conclusions
The convergence of nanodelivery platforms and mRNA therapeutics represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of sepsis, a complex and highly lethal condition. By enabling efficient and precise delivery of functional mRNAs, this strategy offers the potential for dual modulation of hyperinflammation and immune dysregulation, thereby restoring immune homeostasis, mitigating multi-organ dysfunction, and improving patient prognosis.
Nevertheless, given the complexity and heterogeneity of sepsis, as well as the historical challenges associated with translating experimental therapies into clinical practice, further rigorous preclinical and clinical investigations will be required. Future efforts should focus on deepening mechanistic insights, refining delivery system design, and conducting rigorous, systematic preclinical assessments to pave the way for widespread clinical application of mRNA nanotherapeutics in sepsis.
