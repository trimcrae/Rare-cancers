## Abstract

## Background
Major depressive disorder (MDD) remains a debilitating global health issue with limited treatment efficacy. Liver qi stagnation (or called liver depression) has been associated with MDD, but the underlying mechanisms are poorly understood. This study investigates whether impaired hepatic function contributes to depressive symptomatology.

## Methods
Using retrospective clinical data, chronic unpredictable stress (CUMS) models, orthotopic liver transplantation, and microglia-specific genetic tools (including TRPM2 and SYK conditional knockouts), we combined behavioral assays, multi-omics, electrophysiology, and optogenetics to explore the liver-brain axis in depression.

## Results
Compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance in depressive subjects drives depressive symptoms by enhancing microglial engulfment of dendritic spines in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Clinical evidence highly reveals a correlation between hyperbilirubinemia and MDD severity, mirrored in chronic stress mouse models. Liver transplantation from stressed to non-stressed mice impaired bilirubin clearance and induced depressive behaviors, accompanied by ACC glutamatergic neuronal hypoactivity (ACCGlu) and microglial overactivation. Conversely, transplanting healthy livers into stressed mice alleviated these symptoms. Mechanistically, hyperbilirubinemia activates the bilirubin-TRPM2-SYK axis in microglia, promoting excessive spine pruning and synaptic loss in ACCGlu by employing transcriptional pause-release mechanisms to prioritize protein synthesis of pro-phagocytosis machines. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of microglial TRPM2 rescues spine density and depressive behaviors. And the maladaptation of ACCGlu neurons in hyperbilirubinemic mice was reversible by TRPM2 blockade, either.

## Conclusion
Our results reveal a novel liver-brain pathway whereby impaired bilirubin clearance drives depression via microglial synaptic pruning. Targeting microglial TRPM2 offers a promising therapeutic strategy for MDD.

## Introduction
Major depressive disorder (MDD) is the most common and debilitating disease globally, with increased worldwide prevalence, disability, and economic burden on society. The treatments for MDD largely rely on medications targeting the neurotransmitter systems, psychotherapy, and somatic non-drug therapies; most treatments have not achieved satisfactory clinical results [ , ]. 
Liver qi stagnation (or called liver depression) has been implicated in the development of negative emotions such as depression, irritability, or anxiety, and intervention of Liver qi stagnation can mitigate depressive symptoms in traditional Chinese medicine [ , ]. The hypothesis of their theory lies that the liver is the ‘General’ for the human body, responsible for planning of neurological functions and decisions on the countermeasures upon external environment disturbance, such as cognition [ ], memory [ ], feeding behavior [ , ], social behavior [ ], sweet and alcohol preference [ ]. A significant proportion of patients undergoing allogeneic liver transplantation develop psychological and psychiatric disorders, such as sleep disturbances, depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [ ]. Patients with MDD are usually accompanied by a higher prevalence of chronic liver dysfunction, such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [ , ], especially with hyperlipidemia and hyperbilirubinemia [ , ]. Elevated bilirubin concentrations have also been shown to induce depressive-like manifestations in rodent models through hippocampal synaptic plasticity impairment and pro-inflammatory phenotypes [ ]. 
Microglia, which continuously surveil the extracellular milieu in both physiological and pathological contexts, are implicated in regulating micro-circuits known as activity-dependent synaptic remodeling to be involved in multiple neuropsychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety [ – ]. Peripheral liver damage can induce sickness behaviors [ ], and interestingly, liver dysfunction-induced brain effects are usually accompanied by microglia activation [ , ]. Although evidence suggests that abnormal liver function and depression are closely associated. However, the neurobiological mechanism underlying the dysfunctional liver in depressive patients initiates the occurrence and development of negative emotions largely unexplored. 
In the present study, we first included clinical patients with MDD and performed a retrospective study to explore the association between liver function and the severity of MDD. And we demonstrated that hyperbilirubinemia is a dependent risk related to the symptoms of human major depressive disorder and depression mouse model. The ability of hepatic bilirubin clearance was compromised in a mouse model of chronic unpredictable stress (CUMS). We next transplanted a liver from CUMS mice (defined as ‘stressed’ liver) to mice under non-stressed conditions. The ability of hepatic bilirubin clearance was damaged. Meanwhile, mice represented more serious depressive symptoms, mainly accompanied by neuronal activity decline of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and microglia overactivation. Liver from non-stressed mice was next transplanted to CUMS mice. And we founded that depressive symptoms were partially alleviated and the increased activity of ACCGlu was more apparent. PLXPharmaceutical inhibition or genetic depletion of microglia counters the symptom progression of depression in ‘stressed’ liver transplanted mice. Mechanism analysis found that hyperbilirubinemia promotes microglial engulfment of dendritic spines from ACCGlu neurons via microglial TRPM2 and drives the progression of negative emotions. Pharmaceutical inhibition of microglial TRPM2 or accelerating the clearance of bilirubin ameliorates depressive symptoms in hyperbilirubinemia. So, advances of our study in deciphering the molecular underpinnings of depressive disorders can establish liver-brain axis-based frameworks for broadening therapeutic strategy across clinical MDD, to maximize treatment precision and clinical response rates [ ].

## Methods and materials

## Clinical data collection and analysis
In this study, we used a case-control study design to collect electronic medical record data from patients diagnosed with depression in the outpatient and inpatient departments of our hospital in the past 5 years, and selected non-depressed patients in the same period as the control group. We established a standardized data collection form, and key indicators in the medical records were extracted uniformly by trained researchers. The data included the following: clinical diagnosis and severity grading of patients’ depression; patients’ and serum biochemical indicators, mainly including liver function test results (ALT, AST, AST/ALT, ALP, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, total protein, albumin, prealbumin), renal function indicators (creatinine and urea nitrogen levels), etc.; patients’ age, gender, body mass index (BMI), medication history, etc. The data were collected and then analyzed by logistic regression analysis to assess the correlation between serum biochemical indicators and depression by IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 software. This study only used the available electronic medical record data and did not involve any additional intervention or sampling of the subjects to ensure the scientific and ethical nature of the research process.

## Animal
All mice were maintained in a 12:12-hour light-dark cycle within a specific pathogen-free (SPF) mouse rearing facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Anhui Provincial Hospital. Mice were provided unrestricted access to water and food, and were kept at a stable temperature of 25 ± 1 °C and humidity of 50 ± 5%. All wild-type C57BL/6 mice were acquired from Slac Laboratory Animal Co. CX3CR1− CreERT mice (Cyagen, Stock No: C001247) were mated with TRPM2fl/fl mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., NM-CKO-2107711) to produce CX3CR1− CreERT; TRPM2fl/fl mice. Microglia-specific TRPM2 knockout mice (MgTRPM2 KO) were generated by administering tamoxifen (20 mg/kg, dissolved in corn oil) intraperitoneally for 5 consecutive days to 8–10-week-old male CX3CR1− CreERT; TRPM2fl/fl mice, while control male littermates received an equivalent volume of corn oil (MgTRPM2 WT). CX3CR1− CreERT mice (Cyagen, Stock No: C001247) were also mated with SYKfl/fl mice (Cyagen Biosciences Inc., S-CKO-05474) to produce CX3CR1− CreERT; SYKfl/fl mice. Microglia-specific SYK knockout mice (MgSYK KO) were generated by administering tamoxifen (20 mg/kg, dissolved in corn oil) intraperitoneally for 5 consecutive days to 8–10-week-old male CX3CR1− CreERT; SYKfl/fl mice, while control male littermates received an equivalent volume of corn oil (MgSYK WT). CX3CR1− CreERT mice were mated with ROSA26iDTR mice (Jackson laboratory, Stock No: 007900) to create a microglia-specific diphtheria toxin A(DTA)-expressing ROSA26iDTR; CX3CR1− CreERT mouse lineage. ROSA26iDTR; CX3CR1− CreERT male mice, aged 8–10 weeks, received a 20 mg/kg tamoxifen injection for 5 consecutive days to produce Cre-inducible DTR expression. A 100 ng solution of diphtheria toxin (DT) diluted in 1× PBS was administered intraperitoneally for three consecutive days to achieve microglia-specific ablation in mice (Mgdepletion). CaMKIIα-Cre mice were also acquired from the Jackson Laboratory (Stock No: 005359). To observe neuronal activity patterns in specific time intervals was conducted using B6. Cg-Gt (ROSA)26Sortm9(CAG−tdTomato)Hze/J mice (commonly referred to as Ai9 tdTomato mice, The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No: 007909) in conjunction with fos neuron-expressing Fos2 A − iCreERT2 mice (also known as TRAP2 mice, The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No: 030323) were crossed to produce Fos2 A − iCreERT2; Ai9 (RCL-tdT) mice. Fos2 A − iCreERT2; Ai9 (RCL-tdT) male mice, aged 8–10 weeks, underwent continuous intraperitoneal injections of bilirubin (100 mg/kg) for 7 days to create a hyperbilirubinemic model, while cage-mates received equivalent volumes of vehicle as a control. The mice were subsequently administered tamoxifen (20 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection for three consecutive days. Then they underwent the air puff stimulus 30 min post-TAM treatment on each of those days. Following three weeks of tdTomato fluorescent protein expression, slices were stained to evaluate the neoactivation of c-Fos in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of mice from each group.

## Drug administration
Minocycline (Mino) (Cat# M9511, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in water (10 mg/ml) and diluted in saline to prepare a solution at a concentration of 50 mg/kg, which was administered by intraperitoneal injection for 7 consecutive days at the 7th day after liver transplantation to achieve inhibition of microglia activation. 
Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) (Cat# C0832, Sigma-Aldrich) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Cat# HY-Y0320C, MedChemExpress) to formulate a 10 mg/ml master solution, which was subsequently diluted with 0.9% saline and injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 5 mg/kg, 30 min prior to the behavioral test. 
Tamoxifen (Cat# SC-45564, Struchem) was dissolved in corn oil (Cat# C7030 Solarbio) at 20 mg/kg intraperitoneally for 5 consecutive days to induce Cre-inducible gene expression.
Diphtheria toxin (DT) was diluted with 100 ng of diphtheria toxin (Cat# D684675, Aladdin) in 1× PBS, and tamoxifen-induced Cre expression was continued by intraperitoneal injections for three days in ROSA26iDTR; CX3CR1− CreERT male mice after tamoxifen induction in order to obtain microglial-specific ablated mice. 
Bilirubin (Bil) (Cat# HY-N0323, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in 50% PEG 300, diluted with 50% saline, and ultrasonically solubilized to obtain a suspension, which was injected intraperitoneally for 7 consecutive days (100 mg/kg) to establish a hyperbilirubinemia model.
TRPM2-specific inhibitor JNJ-28583113 (Cat# HY-149143, MedChemExpress) was diluted in 2% DMSO solution and 40% PEG300, and 5% Tween-80 (Cat# HY-Y1891, MedChemExpress), and then finally diluted with 53% saline to make a clarified solution of 2 mg/ml, which was injected intraperitoneally for 14 days (20 mg/kg). 
SYK-specific inhibitor Entospletinib (GS-9973) (Cat# HY-15968, MedChemExpress) was first dissolved in 10% DMSO solution and subsequently diluted with 90% corn oil to a liquid of 4 mg/ml and administered at a dose of 40 mg/kg by continuous intraperitoneal injection for 14 days.
TCPOBOP (Cat# HY-103243, MedChemExpress) was first dissolved in 10% DMSO solution and subsequently diluted in 90% corn oil to a clarified liquid of 3.3 mg/ml and administered at a dose of 3 mg/kg for 7 days by continuous intraperitoneal injection. 
PLX5622 was administered at a concentration of 1200 ppm in the chow diet (provided by Plexxikon via Research Diets) to deplete microglia in mice. Control chow mice received standard mouse chow (Research Diets).

## Chronic unpredictable mild stress model (CUMS)
Experiments were conducted using 8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, and the intervention was implemented after completing one week of environmental acclimatization in a standard SPF-grade animal laboratory. Mice in the CUMS groups were randomly exposed to different stressors: 24 h cage tilt, swimming in cold water at 4 °C for 5 min, 24 h water or food fasting, tail pinching at 1 cm from tail tip for 2 min × 2, 24 h reversal of circadian rhythms, high temperature stress at 45 °C for 5 min, restraining cage restraint stress for 2 h. During the stimulation process, the daily stimulus sources were randomized and the same stressor was not applied on two consecutive days so that the mice could not predict the type of stimulus, and the above stimuli were applied three times each during the experimental period, and the stimulation lasted for 21 days. No intervention was made in the Ctrl groups, and tissues were collected on day 21.

## Liver transplantation
By the recognized technique for in situ liver transplantation in mice and our surgical expertise [ , ], we selected recipient mice weighing 25–30 g and donor mice that were somewhat lighter than the recipients for the procedure. We conducted in situ mouse liver transplantation, and all mice were positioned supinely in an incubator post-surgery. Cefodizime sodium (100 mg/kg/d) was administered intraperitoneally for 7 days post-surgery to avert infection. Every mouse was kept in a 12:12-hour light-dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water, and was maintained at a constant temperature of 25 ± 1 °C and humidity of 50 ± 5%. Recipient mice were deemed successful from the fourth day postoperatively; behavioral experiments were conducted on the fourteenth day following transplantation. After the behavioral experiments, all groups of mice were uniformly euthanized, and samples of blood, brain tissue, and liver tissue were collected for subsequent analyses. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines established by the Committee for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) and received approval from the Laboratory Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of USTC.

## Hyperbilirubinemia model
Bilirubin (Bil) (Cat# HY-N0323, MedChemExpress) powder was solubilized in 50% PEG 300 (Polyethylene glycol 300, MedChemExpress) and subsequently diluted in 0.9% saline, resulting in a 100 mg/kg bilirubin suspension, and the suspension was subjected to oscillation at 40 kHz for 10 min prepared by an ultrasonic device (DL-180D, WuXiang, Shanghai, China), maintained in a light-protected environment during the procedure. Then administered intraperitoneally using an insulin syringe (BD Corporation) for 7 consecutive days. The mice were designated as a model for hyperbilirubinemia, while the control mice received an equivalent volume of solvent devoid of bilirubin powder.

## Virus and injection
In the course of brain stereotactic surgery, mice were anesthetized using a combination of isoflurane and oxygen (induction concentration of 3%, maintenance concentration of 1.5–2.5%), shaved afterward, and positioned on a stereotactic apparatus (acquired from RWD Life Sciences, Shenzhen, China). A heating pad was employed to regulate the mice’s body temperature, and sterile eye ointment was administered to each eye. The needle point on the cranial surface was identified and designated based on the coordinates of the injected brain region, and the cranial drill was employed to meticulously perforate the cranium. Virus injection was conducted using a microinjector pump, administering Cre-dependent viruses AAV-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry [rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-pA] bilaterally into the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of CaMKIIα-Cre mice. The rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-mCherry-WPRE-pA virus served as a control. 100 nl was administered into each brain region bilaterally (injection rate of 30 nl/min). Following the completion of the injection, the needle was retained for 10 min to prevent viral leakage, after which it was withdrawn gradually. Antibiotic ointment was administered post-suturing of the scalp and positioned on a 37 °C steady-state resuscitation table until the restoration of voluntary activity. The mCherry expression was observed after three weeks, and chemogenetic treatments were conducted via intraperitoneal administration of CNO (5 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, United States of America) 30 min before behavioral assessment. We used [rAAV-TBG-mCherry-5’miR-30a-shRNA(Ugt1a1)−3’-miR30a-WPREs] viruses diluted with PBS via tail vein injection into mice, and after 3 weeks of viral expression and thus knockdown of the Ugt1a1 gene in liver tissues. Control mice were injected with an equal amount of [rAAV-TBG-mCherry-5’miR-30a-shRNA(scramble)−3’-miR30a-WPREs virus]. All viruses were acquired from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China). Tissue fixation was achieved using sequential perfusion of pre-cooled PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA/PBS, 4 °C) via the left ventricle during end-arterial perfusion. Immunofluorescence labeling was conducted on 40-µm brain slices of ACC and 8-µm tissue sections of liver photographed with a confocal microscope (LSM800, ZEISS, Germany). The ACC coordinates about bregma were: Anterior/Posterior (AP), 0.98 mm; Medial/Lateral (ML), ± 0.34 mm; Dorsal/Ventral (DV), - 1.50 mm.

## Depression-like behavior test

## Marble burying test
Fifteen black glass beads were evenly allocated in transparent polycarbonate rearing cages (30 cm × 20 cm × 15 cm) containing 5 cm of new bedding at the base within a standardized behavioral laboratory. Each row comprised three beads, uniformly dispersed across five rows with consistent spacing. The mice were acclimated in an isolated cage for 24 h before the experiment to create territorial awareness. During the examination, they were carefully relocated to a sanitized enclosure containing prearranged glass beads. The primary observation metric was the quantity of beads that were buried, with beads deemed effectively buried when obscured by at least two-thirds of the sheeting.

## Open field test
The experiment was performed in a white polypropylene box of 40 cm in length and 40 cm in height, featuring 16 uniformly distributed virtual squares at the base, with the central region of the box (20 cm in length) designated as the “Central area.” The mice were habituated to the experimental area for 30 min before the test. Subsequently, the animals were carefully seized and positioned in the center of the corner squares, oriented towards the wall of the box. At the same time, an infrared camera system was promptly engaged above the box to incessantly document their motions for 6 min. The observation metrics encompass the overall movement distance, the duration in the central zone, and the frequency of entries. Following each series of testing, the box must be meticulously cleansed with 75% ethanol to eradicate any odor influence. Data analysis must include professional behavioral monitoring software, EthoVision XT 14, to extract parameters automatically.

## Elevated plus maze test
The experiment was conducted using a white polypropylene cross-shaped maze device (two open arms 30 cm × 6 cm without fences, two closed arms 30 cm × 6 cm × 15 cm with transparent baffles, a central platform 6 cm × 6 cm, and an overall shelf height of 80 cm). Mice were placed in the laboratory for 30 min to acclimatize to reduce environmental stress before testing, and then the animals were gently grasped and placed facing the open arms in the center of the central platform, and the top infrared tracking system was activated to record movement trajectories over 6 min. Following each series of testing, the box must be meticulously cleansed with 75% ethanol to eradicate any odor influence. The “Time in the open arm” was calculated using EthoVision XT 14 software.

## Tail suspension test
Before the experiment, mice in each group were housed in a soundproof, dimly lit, temperature-regulated behavioral room for 24 h of tail-stimulation acclimatization to mitigate their stress reaction. The mice were inverted, with the tips of their tails positioned 1 cm above the floor, affixed with medical tape at a height of 50 cm to guarantee that their heads remained suspended and unable to access proximate things. The suspension apparatus had a blackout backdrop to eradicate visual distractions while ensuring a tranquil atmosphere during suspension. Behavioral observations were continually documented for 5 min using a lateral video system, with mice deemed immobile when they exhibited no intentional movements, including instances of passive swaying. A person who was uninformed of the experimental group recorded the cumulative resting time.

## Western blot
Following the uniform collection of samples from all groups of mice, an equivalent mass of liver tissue from the same site was procured on ice and combined with pre-cooled RIPA buffer [containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl,1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (MedChemExpress), pH 7.6], homogenized, and subsequently lysed on ice for 30 min. This was followed by centrifugation at 12000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate supernatant proteins. Following the determination of protein concentration via the BCA method (Vazyme), 1 × SDS buffer was applied at 95 °C for 10 min. Approximately 20 mg of protein was utilized for SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, and the proteins were subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was incubated with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h at room temperature for blocking. The membrane was rinsed three times with TBST following the addition of the primary antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at ambient temperature, subsequently washed, and then uniformly covered with ECL chemiluminescent reagent. The protein bands on the membrane were detected via chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ software.

## RNA interference
Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) (Scrambled Control siRNA and Trpm2 siRNAs, Ribobio) were dissolved in 5× siRNA buffer (Dharmacon). Primary microglia were transiently transfected with these siRNAs at a final concentration of 40 nM using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Culture medium was replaced 24-48 h post-transfection for subsequent assays.

## Golgi staining
Solution A and Solution B in the kit (FD Rapid GolgiStain™ kit, FD Neuro Technologies, USA) were thoroughly combined in the specified proportions 24 h prior. The experimental animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation following inhalation anesthesia, and the entire brain tissues were swiftly excised and promptly rinsed three times with pre-cooled sterile PBS buffer (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to eliminate surface contaminants. The purified brain tissues were immersed in an AB mix fixative. They underwent primary fixation at room temperature in light-avoidant circumstances for 16–18 h, after which they were replaced with a new AB mix to continue the light-avoidant, room-temperature dip fixation for 14 days. The samples were subsequently transferred to Solution C for permeabilization, with the permeabilization solution being refreshed after 24 h and maintained at room temperature in a light-avoidant environment for 7 days. Tissues were sectioned into 200 μm-thick coronal slices utilizing a VT1200S vibratome (Leica, Germany), and the slices were positioned flat on Solution C pretreatment gelatin-coated slides and cured at ambient temperature for 72 h. The samples were successively cleaned twice with distilled water for 4 min each to eliminate surface contaminants. A pre-prepared working solution was formulated in the ratio of Solution D: Solution E: ultrapure water = 1:1:2 before application. The slides were immersed in the staining solution for 10 min and subsequently washed twice with distilled water for 4 min each time. The slides underwent dehydration using graded ethanol concentrations (50%, 75%, 95%) for 4 min each, followed by four thorough dehydrations (4 min each) with anhydrous ethanol. After dehydration, the samples underwent three transparent treatments with xylene (4 min each) and were ultimately sealed with Permount (Fisher Scientific, USA). Images were acquired using a 40X microscope objective, and dendritic spine density was quantitatively evaluated using ImageJ software.

## In vitro brain slice electrophysiology

## Acute brain slice preparation
Mice were profoundly anesthetized and subjected to transcardial perfusion with a solution of 95% O2 and 5% CO2 oxygenated ice-cold NMDG cutting solution, which contained (in mM): 93 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 93 HCl, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 25 D-glucose, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 2 thiourea, 0.5 CaCl2 and 10 MgSO4 adjusted to pH 7.35 with NMDG or HCl. The brain was promptly excised and submerged in an ice-cold NMDG cutting solution. ACC slices (250 μm) were sectioned using a VT1200S vibratome (Leica, Germany), incubated at 34 °C for 10–13 min in NMDG cutting solution, and subsequently preserved at 25 °C in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; in mM: 2.5 KCl, 126 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 2 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, and 10 glucose) for 1 h before electrophysiological recordings. Slices were placed in the recording chamber and superfused with oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid at a rate of 2.5 to 3 ml/min.

## Whole-cell patch-clamp recording
Neurons were observed utilizing an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus, Tokyo) fitted with a 40 × water-immersion objective lens. Pyramidal cells from the anterior cingulate cortex were distinguished by their anatomical position and morphological characteristics. For whole-cell recording of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) and action potentials (APs), whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed with patch pipettes (resistance 3.5-5 MΩ, Sutter Instruments, BF150-86-10) that were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (outer diameter 1.5 mm) on a P1000 micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments). Pipettes were filled with a solution including (in mM): 135 K-gluconate, 5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 10 HEPES, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.1 GTP, and 5 EGTA, 300 mOsm (pH adjusted to 7.3 with KOH). Bicuculline (10 µM), DL-APV (50 µM), and TTX (1 µM) were employed to inhibit the GABAA receptors, NMDA receptors, and sodium channels, respectively. Peak events were identified automatically with an amplitude threshold of 5 pA. The holding potential was set at − 70 mV to record miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs). Action potentials induced by current were generated using 500-ms current injections at eleven intensities (20, 60, 100, 140, 180, 220, 260, 300, 340, 380, and 420 pA), with a trial interval of 20 s. The frequency of action potentials was defined as the number per time interval. All signals were obtained using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), filtered at 1 kHz, and sampled at 5 kHz with a Digidata 1440 A interface utilizing Clampex 10.2 (Molecular Devices). Data were deemed acceptable when series resistance varied within 15% of the starting values (25–35 MΩ). Miniature events were examined with MiniAnalysis 6.07 software (Synaptosoft). A recording period of five minutes was utilized for frequency and amplitude analyses. Peak occurrences were identified automatically with an amplitude threshold of 5 pA.

## In vivo fiber photometry recording
Mice were sedated with isoflurane and positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Sciences, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a heating pad to regulate body temperature, while sterile eye ointment was applied to their eyes. Mice received bilateral injections of 200 nl of AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m (rAAV-EF1a-DIO-GCaMP6m-WPRE-hGH-pA, BrainVTA, Wuhan, China) into the ACC brain area at a rate of 30 nl/min. Maintaining the needle in position for 10 min before slow retraction to avert viral leakage. An optical fiber (200 mm OD, 0.37 NA, Inper) was subsequently inserted 0.2 mm above the virus injection site in the ACC brain area and secured with dental cement (Super Bond C&B). Three weeks later, the mice recuperated utilizing a multi-channel fiber optic photometer equipment (Inper Studio, Hangzhou, China) for fiber optic recording. Fiber optic sleeves were linked with fiber optic patch cords of corresponding aperture dimensions. A DA sensor was employed to emit a 470 nm laser for assessing the activity of glutamatergic neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) area of the brain. The light intensity at 470 nm was established between 20 and 40 µW, whilst the intensity at 410 nm was set between 10 and 20 µW. The analog voltage signal operated at 50 Hz. Mice were situated in a cylindrical observation chamber for 30 min for acclimation, with unrestricted access to water and food. The mice underwent an air puff experiment, during which the fluorescent calcium signal was recorded. The laser power at the optical fiber’s tip was calibrated to 20 µW to mitigate laser bleaching. The fluorescent signals were subjected to a low-pass filter. Calcium transient change values (ΔF/F) were computed from − 5 to 20 s, with 0 s being the onset of each air puff stimulus. The outcome (F-F0)/F0 was documented, where F represents the GCaMP6m signal in each image and F0 is defined as the mean value of the GCaMP6m signal before the initiation of the air puff stimuli, adjusted for the autofluorescence background. The captured signals were subsequently processed using the analysis software (Inper Datz Process) to derive the conclusion ΔF/F. Mice lacking fiber-optic recording targets or exhibiting unsuccessful viral expression were omitted from the experiment.

## Immunofluorescence and imaging
Following deep anesthesia, mice were perfused with pre-cooled PBS solution through the heart, followed by a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Mouse brains were excised and immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight. The subsequent day, they were immersed in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions for three days for stepwise dehydration. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) was sectioned into 40-µm coronal slices at low temperature utilizing a cryosectioner (CM1860 UV, Leica). Sections had three washes with PBS, each lasting 7 min, before staining. The sections were then incubated for 90 min at room temperature with a blocking solution comprising 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS and 0.2% Triton X-100. The slices were treated with the designated primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The sections underwent three washes with PBS, each for 10 min. The sections were incubated with the designated secondary antibody for 90 min at ambient temperature, covered from light. The parts were rinsed with PBS three times for ten minutes each. Each section was treated with an anti-fluorescence quenching blocker containing DAPI (BL739B, Biosharp), and the nuclei were labeled with 10 µl of DAPI per brain slice before being promptly covered with a coverslip. Fluorescence image data were acquired using a confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss) or a ZEISS Axio Imager 2 microscope to deliver quantitative immunofluorescence staining outcomes. An uninformed observer conducted cell counts and intensity colocalization analysis utilizing ImageJ software (Fiji, National Institutes of Health); statistical data were illustrated using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software.

## Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and quantitative assessments
Coronal slices of 40 μm from the ACC brain region of mice in each group were fluorescently stained with anti-Iba1 and anti-PSD95, following the aforementioned staining protocol, and subsequently treated with Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594-labeled secondary antibodies. Sections were blocked with an anti-fluorescence quenching agent containing DAPI. Brain areas were imaged utilizing a Zeiss LSM800 microscope. A depth range of 30 μm was traversed with 2 μm intervals along the Z-axis. A series of images obtained from microglial stratification was captured using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope equipped with a 40x lens. The acquired project files were recreated with IMARIS 9.6.2 software (Bitplane) in three dimensions. Upon opening the file, choose “Add new filaments” and “Create Calculate Diameter of Filaments” within the designated area, and configure the diameter to range from 0.25 to 15 μm. Adjust the diameter to 0.25–15 μm, identify and categorize the dendrites and dots based on the current conditions, and then designate the diameter of the “Sphere Region” as 30 μm. Establish the dendritic threshold at 1.70 or below to guarantee the precision of microglia reconstruction and to aid in the rebuilding of synaptic phagocytosis thereafter. The quantity of microglial total branch points, total process length, and normalized soma size was measured and recorded. The “Surface” module depicts the morphology of microglia. Subsequently, we employed the “Point” tool in IMARIS to recreate the PSD95+ puncta in three dimensions. We utilize the “Split into Surface Objects” feature to ascertain the quantity of PSD95+ that exhibit significant colocalization with Iba1+, subsequently counting and analyzing them.

## Total bilirubin measurement
The mice in each group were secured in a supine posture on the surgical table following deep anesthesia, and the skin of the thoracic area was prepped and sterilized. A 1 ml sterile syringe was utilized for a terminal intracardiac blood sample, and 0.5–0.8 ml of blood was extracted, thereafter transferred to a sterile 1.5 ml EP tube (MCT-001–150, LABSELECT). The mice were later euthanized. The collected blood was permitted to stand for 60 min at ambient temperature, shielded from light, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant serum had been aspirated and transferred to a new sterile EP tube, followed by a second centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to procure serum samples. Bilirubin is readily decomposed; therefore, all procedures are conducted at low temperatures and shielded from light exposure. Bilirubin in serum samples is quantified using nitrite oxidation in a fully automated biochemistry analyzer (BX-3010, Sysmex) with the Total Bilirubin Assay Kit (H115, MeiKang Biotech). Before the test, a multiple biochemical calibrator (JZ903T, MeiKang Biotech) was employed to establish a measurement standard, and a multiple biochemical quality control (ZK904T1, MeiKang Biotech) was utilized to assess system performance. Each group of samples was diluted in the same proportion by PBS and placed in a colorimetric cup on a sample holder. The serum bilirubin levels of each group were measured and compared to the normal reference values of clinical biochemical indices in mice, with the results documented for each group.

## Hematoxylin-eosin staining
Mice were thoroughly sedated and perfused with pre-cooled PBS via the heart, then followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The fixed-position livers were excised through dissection and subsequently preserved in PFA solution at 4 °C for 24 h. Gradient dehydration utilizing ethanol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% ethanol for 1 h each) was conducted, followed by xylene hyalinization for 2 h. Thereafter, the liver tissue was subjected to paraffin embedding to create paraffin blocks. A rotary paraffin microtome (S700A, RWD) was employed to produce 5-µm paraffin tissue sections. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene before staining, followed by elution of the xylene using a gradient of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, and 70% ethanol for 10 min each) to ensure adequate hydration. Hematoxylin staining was conducted for 4 min, followed by differentiation using a 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol solution for 3 s, rinsing under running water to achieve a blue hue for 10 min, and eosin staining for 40 s. Dehydrate with anhydrous ethanol three further times for five minutes each. Subject the specimen to xylene for three cycles of ten minutes each to achieve transparency. Ultimately, neutral balsam (C0173, Beyotime) was employed to seal the slices, which were allowed to cure naturally before being photographed under a microscope (Yuescope, China) to examine the necrotic area, with counts conducted by an unbiased observer.

## Primary microglial cultures
Brains from postnatal day 2–3 CX3CR1− CreERT; TRPM2fl/fl mice were dissected, meninges and cerebella removed, and the cortices mechanically dissociated in ice-cold HBSS buffer (Solarbio). The resulting mixed glial cells were plated onto poly-L-lysine-coated flasks and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM/FBS containing 50 ng/ml mouse GM-CSF (R&D Systems). Microglia were harvested 14 days post-plating by shaking (37℃, 200 rpm, 30 min). 
After the microglia were wall-adhered for 48 h, 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (final concentration of 500nM) was added, and rehydration was performed by changing the solution every 24 h for 3 days to achieve TRPM2-specific knockdown in primary microglia. The control group was treated with an equal volume of control solution DMSO. 
In the bilirubin group, microglia were stimulated with bilirubin (30 µM) for 24 h. 
In the GS-9973 treatment group, GS-9973 (2 µM) was pre-treated for 24 h before stimulation with the same concentration of bilirubin.

## Microglial phagocytosis assay
Microglia from each set of conditions were co-incubated with pHrodo red fluorescent dye-labeled synaptic vesicles for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by moving the suspension onto ice to terminate phagocytosis. Phagocytosis of microglia was examined by detecting the fluorescence intensity of intracellular pH-dependent particles, and phagocytosis was then analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACSFortessa, BD Biosciences). The data were analyzed using FlowJo 10 software.

## scRNA-seq library preparation, sequencing and analysis
Freshly dissociated single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform to generate Gel Bead-In-Emulsions (GEMs). Subsequent scRNA-seq library preparation followed the manufacturer’s protocol for the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit v3.1. Libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (PE150) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome using Cell Ranger (v8.0.0) to produce gene expression count matrices for each sample. These individual sample matrices were merged using Scanpy (v1.9.6) for combined quality control. Cells were retained if they met the following criteria: (1) 500–7000 detected genes, (2) mitochondrial RNA content < 20%, and (3) total UMI counts ≤ 50,000. Genes detected in fewer than 50 cells were excluded. Potential doublets were removed using DoubletDetection (10.5281/zenodo.2678041) with parameters boost_rate = 0.5 and voter_thresh = 0.9, yielding 63,283 high-quality cells for downstream analysis. Expression counts per cell were normalized to a total of 10,000 UMIs and log-transformed. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed, and the first 40 principal components informed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding. Initial cell clustering (Louvain, resolution = 0.3) based on canonical markers identified major types: Astrocytes, Oligodendrocytes, Microglia, Macrophages, Ependymal Cells, ChP Epithelial Cells, Endothelium, Pericytes/SMCs, VLCMs, Neurons, T cells, and Erythrocytes. Microglia subpopulations were further resolved using similar clustering parameters. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type were identified using Scanpy’s rank_genes_groups function. The top 300 DEGs per type underwent pathway enrichment analysis via Metascape.

## Isolation of microglia from adult murine brain
The following steps and reagent preparation are performed on ice to ensure cell activity. Isolation of microglia from adult murine brain was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat#130-107-677, Miltenyi, Adult Brain Dissociation Kit). After adult mice were anesthetized and perfused with pre-cooled, calcium-magnesium-containing D-PBS (Cat# C0221G, Beyotime), the ACC brain regions were rapidly stripped and sheared into tissue fragments. Enzyme mixes were pre-prepared at the indicated ratios: 50 µL of enzyme P was mixed with 1900 µL of buffer Z to form enzyme mix 1 (Enzyme Mix 1), and 20 µL of buffer Y was mixed with 10 µL of enzyme A to form enzyme mix 2 (Enzyme Mix 2). In addition, the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator was set to program 37C_ABDK_01 and the corresponding C-tubes were prepared. Tissue fractions with the configured enzyme mix were transferred separately from the C-tubes and enzymatically dissociated by the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator at 37 °C for 30 min. At the end of the procedure the cell suspension was transferred and centrifuged at 300 g at 4 °C for 10 min and the supernatant was completely aspirated and discarded. After resuspending the cellular precipitate with pre-cooled D-PBS, gently add debris removal solution and mix well. In the upper layer of its mixture, carefully add the sealing layer with pre-cooled D-PBS (be careful not to mix the liquids), and centrifuge the sample through 3,000 g at 4 ℃ for 10 min to visualize the cellular precipitate, the cellular suspension, the tissue debris, and the supernatant layer from the bottom to the top. After aspirating the supernatant and tissue debris layers, the remaining cells were resuspended in ice pre-cooled PBS and centrifuged again (1000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and 90 ul of PB Buffer (D-PBS + 0.5% FBS) was added to each tube for every 10⁷ cells after thoroughly aspirating the supernatant, as well as 10 µL of CD11b (Microglia) MicroBeads (Cat#130-093-636, Miltenyi), mix thoroughly and incubate at 4 °C for 10 min. After incubation, cells were washed with 1–2 mL of buffer (300 × g, 4 °C, 10 min) and finally resuspended in 500 µL of buffer per 10⁷ cells. The sorting column was placed in the magnetic field of the MACS sorter, the column was wetted with 3 ml of PB buffer, the cell suspension was added to the column, the flow-through solution was collected, and the column was washed with the appropriate amount of buffer and the flow-through solution was collected to sort the column again. After removing the sorting column from the magnetic field, CD11b positive cells were eluted and collected using 5 mL of buffer. Purified adult mouse microglia were obtained by resuspension with D-PBS followed by centrifugation and removal of most of the supernatant.

## CUT&Tag assay and analysis
CUT&Tag sequencing libraries were constructed according to the experimental protocol provided by Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit (Cat# TD903-01, Vazyme). 1 × 10⁵ microglia cells were collected, washed with washing buffer and then bound to ConA magnetic beads for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with 1 µl of the corresponding antibody overnight at 4 °C. The corresponding secondary antibody was added and gently mixed, and the cells were rotated and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with DIG-300 wash buffer, added 0.04 mM pA/G-Tnp and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with rotation, then washed three times with DIG-300 buffer, resuspended in tagmentation buffer and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The fragmentation was terminated by 0.5 M EDTA (Cat# AM9260G, Thermo Scientific), 10% SDS, proteinase K (Cat# 26160, Thermo Scientific), 50 °C for 1 h. The beads were then gently washed twice with 80% ethanol and the DNA was eluted with double-distilled water. The libraries were labeled using TruePrep Index Kit V2 (Cat# TD202, Vazyme) and amplified for 24 cycles. The final product was purified using VAHTS DNA Clean be (Cat# N411-01, Vazyme) to create a sequencing library. Library quality control and sequencing were performed by Genewiz (Suzhou, China). The raw data were subjected to quality control in double-ended sequencing mode using Trim_Galore software to trim the splice sequences as well as remove low quality bases. The high-quality sequencing data were then aligned to the mouse mm10 reference gene library by Bowtie2 software and PCR duplicate sequences were removed using Picard tools. Biological duplicates were combined to generate a genome browser track map in bigwig format using Samtools software, and peak detection was performed using MACS2 software. The final visualization of specific genes in bigwig files was achieved by IGV software.

## Statistical analysis
This study conducted all the statistical analyses via GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 or IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 software. The data are presented as the means ± SEMs. The unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. One-way or two-way ANOVA was utilized for multiple comparisons. These principles were adhered to by the statistical methods. p ≥ 0.05 was defined as no significance (ns); p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.005).

## Results

## Hepatic bilirubin clearance is compromised in MDD subjects and hyperbilirubinemia is associated with MDD severity
In our clinical study, we enrolled patients diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD) alongside healthy controls without MDD or apparent depressive symptoms (Fig. A). We analyzed liver function indices (including ALT, AST, and bilirubin) and discovered that serum bilirubin levels were significantly elevated in MDD patients (Fig. B) (Total bilirubin: 11.55 ± 4.49 vs. 20.38 ± 8.81 µmol/l, p < 0.001; Indirect bilirubin: 7.88 ± 3.38 vs. 11.08 ± 6.39 µmol/l, p < 0.005) and positively correlated with MDD risk (Fig. S1A). Bilirubin metabolism involves a well-characterized pathway: following erythrocyte hemoglobin breakdown, unconjugated bilirubin is transported into hepatocytes via OATP1A1, conjugated by UGT family enzymes (primarily UGT1A1 and UGT1A6), and excreted via the MRP2 transporter [ ]. Intriguingly, in our chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model, we observed progressive downregulation of OATP1A1, UGT1A1, and MRP2 (Fig. C), paralleled by a gradual increase in serum bilirubin (Fig. D). These findings suggest impaired hepatic bilirubin clearance capacity under chronic stress conditions. To determine whether impaired bilirubin clearance persists in donor livers after stress exposure, we performed orthotopic liver transplantation experiments (Fig. S1B). Transplantation of livers from stressed mice (defined as SLT group) into healthy recipients resulted in significantly elevated serum bilirubin levels in recipients at day 14 post-transplantation (3.71 ± 2.68 vs. 13.15 ± 5.84 µmol/l, p < 0.005; Fig. E, F, and S1B). Conversely, transplantation of healthy livers (defined as NSLT group) into stressed mice partially attenuated bilirubin accumulation (15.72 ± 5.04 vs. 9.22 ± 4.00 µmol/l, p < 0.01; Fig. G, H). Histopathological analysis revealed greater vulnerability to damage in stressed donor livers (Fig. S1C, D). And importantly, recipients of stressed livers showed significantly reduced survival (Fig. S1E). Collectively, our findings demonstrated that chronic stress impairs hepatic bilirubin metabolism through downregulation of key transporters and enzymes. And that impaired hepatic bilirubin metabolic dysfunction induced hyperbilirubinemia can persist several days during chronic stress environment and may contribute to pathogenesis in recipients.
 

## The impaired hepatic bilirubin clearance dictates depression-related ACCGlu hypoactivity
To investigate whether impaired hepatic bilirubin clearance contributes to stress-induced depressive phenotypes, we first performed orthotopic liver transplantation from stressed or healthy donor mice into recipient mice. Two weeks post-transplantation, recipient mice underwent a series of behavioral tests—including marble burying, open field, elevated plus maze, and tail suspension tests—conducted in a chronologically paired manner (Fig. A). Mice receiving stressed livers exhibited depressive-like behaviors, as evidenced by increased number of buried marbles (Fig. B, C), decreased total travel distance, and reduced time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. D-F). Additionally, these mice spent significantly less time in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. G, H). Similarly, these mice had significantly longer immobility time in the tail suspension test (Fig. I). To further assess neural activity in stress-responsive brain regions, we performed immunofluorescence staining for the immediate-early gene marker c-Fos (Fig. S2A). Mice transplanted with stressed livers showed increased neural activity in the lateral septum (LS), while regions such as the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and piriform cortex (Pir) exhibited reduced activity. Notably, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) displayed pronounced hypoactivity (Fig. S2B). Further analysis confirmed c-Fos expression specifically in glutamatergic neurons (Fig. S2C, D). We established a mouse model of hyperbilirubinemia caused by impaired bilirubin clearance by specifically knocking down the expression of Ugt1a1, a key enzyme for bilirubin clearance, in the liver by viral injection into the tail vein of mice (Fig. S2E). Compared with shRNA-scramble mice, immunofluorescence staining of liver tissues demonstrated that Ugt1a1 expression levels were specifically knocked down (Fig. S2F, G), while their serum bilirubin levels were significantly increased (Fig. S2H). The liver Ugt1a1-specific knockdown mice exhibited markedly depressive-like behavior: reduced time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. S2I) and spent less time in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. S2J). The functional activity of ACC brain region neurons in the shRNA-Ugt1a1 mice was significantly reduced compared with that of other groups. (Fig. S2K, L).
 The ACC region has previously been reported to participate in sensing and responding to extra-brain stressful stimuli [ , ]. Synaptic deficits of glutamatergic neurons are closely accompanied by microglial activation, modulating responses to stress and emotional outcomes [ ]. Compared to the NSLT group, the SLT group exhibited enhanced microglial activation in the ACC (Fig. J), characterized by reduced branch points (Fig. K), shorter process lengths (Fig. L), and larger soma sizes (Fig. M).
To determine whether restoring hepatic bilirubin clearance could mitigate depressive symptoms, we transplanted healthy livers into chronic unpredictable mild stress mice and repeated depression related behavioral testing (Fig. S3A). Remarkably, NSLT→S mice showed partial recovery in depressive-like behaviors, including decreased marble burying (Fig. S3B, C) and longer durations in the central zone of the open field (Fig. S3D, E). Microglia could modulate neural circuits to regulate behaviors via synaptic elimination [ , ]. And enhanced microglial engulfment of ACC glutamatergic neuronal spines can lead to dendritic spine loss and hypoactivity, exhibiting depressive like behaviors consequentially [ ]. Three-dimensional reconstruction was next performed between postsynaptic components maker (stained with PSD95) and microglia (labeled with Iba1) in ACC. Colocation analysis demonstrated that microglial engulfment of dendritic spines is enhanced in the SLT group. While transplanting healthy livers into stressed mice alleviated this phenotype (Fig. S3F, G). mEPSCs frequency but amplitude in ACCGlu neurons was apparently decreased in the SLT→S group, compared with the control group, and the action potential frequency of ACCGlu neurons also decreased. Replacing the impaired bilirubin clearance liver in CUMS mice can partially rescue this decline (Fig. S3H-M). These findings collectively support that stress-induced hepatic bilirubin clearance dysfunction contributes to depressive-like behaviors, apparently via suppressing ACCGlu activity and increasing microglial activation and synaptic engulfment.

## Compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance drives depressive symptoms were mitigated by microglial inactivation
To investigate whether microglial activation inhibition could alleviate depressive-like symptoms induced by transplantation of “stressed” livers, recipient mice were administered minocycline (a microglial activation inhibitor) intraperitoneally for one week beginning at day 7 post-transplantation [ ]. Following intervention completion, recipients underwent sequential behavioral assessments—including marble burying, open field, elevated plus maze, and tail suspension tests—conducted in a temporally paired design (Fig.  A). Minocycline-treated mice exhibited significantly attenuated depressive-like phenotypes, as demonstrated by decreased marble-burying counts (Fig. B, C), increased central zone exploration time in open field testing (Fig. D-F), prolonged open-arm duration in elevated plus maze trials (Fig. G, H), and decreased immobility during tail suspension tests (Fig. I). Due to the potential off-target effects of minocycline administered via intraperitoneal injection on peripheral myeloid cells (e.g., suppression of systemic inflammation), we selected the more specific CSF1R inhibitor PLX5562 to replicate the aforementioned experiments (Fig.  J). By depleting microglia and conducting a series of behavioral tests, results demonstrated a significant reduction in depressive-like phenotypes in PLX5562-treated mice. specifically manifested as: increased exploration time in the central area during the open field test (Fig.  K, M); prolonged stay time in the open arms during the elevated plus maze test (Fig. L, N). For targeted microglial depletion, we generated adult ROSA26iDTR; CX3CR1-CreERT mice through crossing microglia-specific Cre recombinase mice with ROSA26iDTR mice. This model enables selective microglial ablation via sequential tamoxifen induction and diphtheria toxin (DT) administration [ ]. First, we administered tamoxifen (20 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days to mice, followed by 3 days of intraperitoneal injections of diphtheria toxin (100 ng/day), thereby achieving glial cell-specific elimination. After achieving microglia-specific clearance, we transplanted livers from stressed donor mice into Mgdepletion mice or MgWT mice. Following intervention completion, recipients underwent sequential behavioral assessments—including open field, elevated plus maze, and tail suspension tests—conducted in a temporally paired design. (Fig. S4A). Tamoxifen pretreatment facilitated diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR/HBEGF) expression in microglia, permitting effective microglial depletion (Mgdepletion) upon subsequent DT treatment (Fig. S4B, C). The Mgdepletion mice exhibited a significantly improved depressive-like behavioral phenotype: longer exploration time in the central region of the open field experiment (Fig. S4D-F), increases in dwell time in the open arm of the elevated cross maze (Fig. S4G, H), and decreases in immobility time in the hanging tail experiment (Fig. S4I). To rule out the possibility that DT ablation of other CX3CR1-expressing cells (e.g., peripheral myeloid cells such as those in the spleen/liver) could confound the results, we repeated the previous modeling and behavioral tests after stereotaxic injection of DT into the ACC brain region following consecutive intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen. We transplanted liver tissue from stress-donor mice into mice with microglia depletion in the ACC region (MgACC depletion) or into wild-type microglia in the ACC region (MgWT). Following the intervention, recipient mice underwent a series of behavioral assessments (Fig. S4J). Immunofluorescence results demonstrated specific ablation of microglia in the ACC and adjacent brain regions (Fig. S4K). The depressive-like phenotype in MgACC depletion mice was significantly alleviated, manifested by increased exploration time in the central area during the open field test (Fig. S4L) and prolonged stay time in the open arms during the elevated plus maze test (Fig. S4M). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence staining of synaptic co-localization with PSD95 in the ACC brain region was significantly decreased in MgWT mice after stressful donor liver transplantation, which was alleviated in the Mgdepletion mice (Fig. S4N, O). In addition, the functional activity of ACC brain region neurons in the Mgdepletion mice was significantly restored compared with that of MgWT mice (Fig. S4P, Q).
 Given the observed association between ACCGlu hypoactivity and enhanced microglial activation, we next examined whether chemogenetic activation of ACCGlu neurons could rescue depressive-like behaviors in “stressed” liver recipients. Using Cre-dependent viral delivery of excitatory hM3Dq receptors into the ACC of CaMKIIα-Cre mice, we achieved targeted neuronal expression. Three weeks later, these animals received liver transplants from stressed donors, followed by intraperitoneal administration of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to activate ACCGlu neurons before behavioral tests (Fig. O). Notably, while ACCGlu activation alone produced modest behavioral improvements, combined minocycline treatment and chemogenetic activation substantially ameliorated depressive-like phenotypes compared to either single intervention (Fig. P-Q). These findings collectively demonstrate that ACCGlu neuronal hypoactivity drives depressive symptom progression through microglia-dependent mechanisms, and that impaired hepatic bilirubin clearance-induced depressive behaviors are rescued by microglial inactivation strategies.

## Targeting TRPM2 in microglia counters the progression of depressive symptoms in hyperbilirubinemia
In order to further explore whether and how hyperbilirubinemia is caused by compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance, we established a hyperbilirubinemia mouse model by intraperitoneal injection of bilirubin. Depression related behaviors were performed subsequently after bilirubin administration for 2 weeks. Mice with hyperbilirubinemia manifested reduced time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. S5A-C), decreased open-arm duration in elevated plus maze trials (Fig. S5D-E), and prolongation of immobility time of the tail suspension test (Fig. S5F). Immunofluorescence staining for microglia in ACC confirmed that bilirubin induces apparent microglia activation (Fig. S5G), manifesting decreased total process length (Fig. S5H), total branch points (Fig. S5I), and enlarged soma size (Fig. S5J). We used TRAP2 (FosCreERT2): Ai9 mice to trap bilirubin caused changes in brain nucleus activity, in which tdTomato is specifically expressed in highly active neurons after aversive air puff stimuli (Fig. S5K) [ ]. Results manifested that bilirubin treatment induced an obvious reduction in the number of c-Fos+Tdtmato+ cells in the ACC region (Fig. S5L, M). This evidence illustrated that hyperbilirubinemia induced ACC hypoactivity, phenotyping with transplanted “stressed” liver mice. 
TRPM2, a classical ROS receptor, is one of the several highly Ca2+ permeable non-selective cation channels [ ], widely expressed in immune cells, such as neutrophils, macrophages, et al. TRPM2 has been newly identified to sense extracellular bilirubin to participate in neuronal damage [ ]. We crossed the TRPM2fl/fl; CX3CR1CreERT mouse to a specific knockout microglial TRPM2 after tamoxifen administration (MgTRPM2 KO mice). Immunofluorescence staining was performed for TRPM2 and other cell types (such as microglia, astrocytes, and neurons). Statistical results show that TRPM2 is specifically depleted in microglia but astrocytes and neurons (Fig. S5N, O). MgTRPM2 KO mice or MgWT mice were intraperitoneally injected with bilirubin for 1 week. Following intervention completion, mice underwent sequential behavioral assessments—including marble burying, open field, elevated plus maze, and tail suspension tests (Fig. A). Results showed that hyperbilirubinemia-induced depressive behaviors were partially rescued by microglial TRPM2 blockade. Detailed results manifested that a decreased number of buried marbles (Fig. B, C), increased time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. D-F). Additionally, MgTRPM2 KO mice spent significantly more time in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. G, H) and less immobility time in the tail suspension test (Fig. I). To exclude the possibility that intraperitoneal tamoxifen injection might knock out TRPM2 receptors on CX3CR1-expressing peripheral cells, we injected rAAV-TMEM119-shRNA(TRPM2)-mCherry virus into the ACC region of mice to specifically knockdown microglial TRPM2 in ACC. Four weeks after viral expression, mice underwent consecutive intraperitoneal injections of bilirubin for one week to establish a hyperbilirubinemia model. Following the intervention, behavioral tests, including the open field test and elevated plus maze test, were conducted (Fig. S5P). Immunofluorescence results from the ACC region confirmed a significant knockdown of TRPM2 (Fig. S5Q, R). Behavioral results indicated that the depression-like behaviors were significantly alleviated in shRNA(TRPM2) mice. Specifically, in the open field test, mice spent more time in the central area (Fig. S5S); in the elevated plus maze test, shRNA(TRPM2) mice exhibited significantly prolonged residence time in the open arms (Fig. S5T). 
 In addition, the spine density of ACC was reduced in bilirubin-treated mice, and this synaptic decline was modest in MgTRPM2 KO mice (Fig. J, K), which was confirmed by Golgi-Cox staining of fixed brain tissues. In addition, hyperbilirubinemia-induced c-Fos expression in the ACC was greatly reduced, but the ACC hypoactivity was partially alleviated in MgTRPM2 KO mice (Fig. L, M). We revalidated the above experiments in a model of impaired natural clearance of bilirubin by subjecting MgTRPM2 KO mice to specific knockdown of liver Ugt1a1 at the same time as MgWT mice, and performing behavioral tests at 21 day (Fig.  N). The results showed that MgWT mice also showed markedly depressive-like behaviors: reduced time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. O) and spent less time in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. P), whereas MgTRPM2 KO mice showed marked remission. These results reveal that hyperbilirubinemia promotes maladaptation of ACCGlu neurons via microglial TRPM2 and the progression of negative emotions.

## Bilirubin activates the TRPM2 to facilitate microglial engulfment of ACCGluneuronal spines
To address how TRPM2 regulates microglia functions, we extracted adult mouse microglia and performed scRNA-seq after bilirubin burden on day 7 from the brains of Ctrl, MgWT mice, and MgTRPM2 KO mice. All expected cell types were identified in both sample sets, such as astrocytes (Gfap, Aldh1l1), microglia (P2ry12, Tmem119), antigen presenting cell (CD74, H2-Eb1 and H2-Aa), oligodendrocytes (Olig1, Olig2 and Mog), ependymal cell (Dynlrb2, Pifo and Tmem212), chip endothelial cells (Folr1, Prlr and Hemk1), endothelium (Acvrl1, Pecam1 and Adgrl4), pericyte (Rgs5, Cspg4 and Vtn) neurons (Syt1, Meg3, Stmn2 and Tubb3) and lymphocytes (Cd3e and CD3d) and neutrophil (Lcn2, S100a8 and S100a9) (Fig. A and Fig. S6A). We next confirmed the identity of seven distinct microglia populations according to the sc-RNA data (Fig. B and Fig. S6B). We next compared the percentages of various microglia subsets between the two groups. The results revealed that the percentage of microglia cluster 1 (c1) increased notably in the bilirubin treated group and decreased after microglial TRPM2 knockout (Fig. C). 
 We further compared the differential genes between microglia cluster 1 and other clusters (Fig. D). Pathway enrichment analysis manifested that the function of microglia cluster1 mainly involved with cell activation, endocytosis, lysosome, and actin-filament-based processes, et al. (Fig. E). While, microglia cluster 2 is mainly involved with oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain et al. (Fig. S6C); Microglia cluster 3 is mainly involved with synapse organization, synaptic signaling and dendrite development et al. (Fig. S6D); Microglia cluster 4 is mainly related with neuron projection development, and amino acid transport, brain development and et al. (Fig. S6E); Characteristics such as vasculature development, cell migration and endothelium development are belong to microglia cluster 5 (Fig. S6F); Microtubule-based movement, cell projection organization and regulation of neurofibrillary tangle assembly are the features of microglia cluster 6 (Fig. S6G); the functions of microglia cluster 7 defined as neutrophil degranulation, inflammation response and granulocyte chemotaxis et al. (Fig. S6H). However, no proportional change was observed in the microglia cluster 2 to microglia cluster 7. In addition, proinflammatory genes were increased in mice treated with bilirubin and decreased after microglial TRPM2 knockout (Fig. S6I). Integrated pathways of actin cytoskeleton signaling, phagocytosis and SYK pathways were all enriched in microglia after mice were treated with bilirubin and less enriched after microglial TRPM2 knockout (Fig.  F). Three-dimensional reconstruction was next performed between postsynaptic components maker (stained with PSD95) and microglia (labeled with Iba1) in ACC. Colocation analysis demonstrated that microglial engulfment of dendritic spines is enhanced in the bilirubin group. While microglial TRPM2 knockout alleviated this phenotype (Fig. G-I).

## The bilirubin-TRPM2-SYK axis facilitates microglia rapidly unleashing of phagocytosis capacity via transcriptional pause release
The spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) is regarded as instructing signaling and effector functions downstream of TREM2, CD33, and CD22 receptors, which are critical for the phagocytic activity of microglia [ ]. We then asked whether bilirubin could activate the TRPM2-SYK axis to facilitate microglial phagocytosis. Notably, residue Tyr525 is located in the activation loop of SYK, and its phosphorylation is important for inducing SYK activation [ ]. Phosphorylation of SYK at T519 in mouse is equivalent to T525 in human, which couples the activated immunoreceptors to downstream signaling events including phagocytosis, proliferation, and differentiation [ – ]. Our outcomes indicated that bilirubin increases the p-SYKT519 in microglia, and this effect is compromised in TRPM2 knockout microglia, exactly similar to the SYK inhibitor (GS-9973) treatment (Fig.  J). We crossed SYKfl/fl; CX3CR1CreERT mice to construct an animal model that specifically knocks out microglial SYK after tamoxifen induction (microglial SYK knockout mice, MgSYK KO). We administered bilirubin intraperitoneally for 1 week to MgSYK KO mice and wild-type controls (MgWT) as well. Behaviorally, MgSYK KO mice were shown to alleviate depressive behaviors in the hyperbilirubinemia model: increased residence time in the central area of the open field experiment (Fig.  J) and increased residence time in the open arm of the elevated plus maze (Fig.  K); and immunofluorescence showed that MgSYK KO mice also alleviated the increase in lysosomal content and enhanced reactivity of microglia in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) region of mice caused by bilirubin loading (Fig. L, M). Three-dimensional reconstruction of postsynaptic component markers (PSD95 staining) with microglia (Iba1 labeling) was then performed in the ACC. The co-localization results indicated that microglia SYK knockdown similarly attenuated bilirubin load-induced phagocytosis of dendritic spines by microglia (Fig.  N-P). Data from scRNA seq, we confirmed that mRNA transcripts of phagocytosis-related genes all boom in microglia during bilirubin stimulation. mRNA transcripts are transcribed by molecular machines such as RNA polymerase II [ , ]. Then, we performed RNAP II CUT-Tag assays on isolated microglia in the ACC region from various groups under the liver bilirubin clearance disorder model (Fig.  A). RNAP II ChIP-seq assays confirmed that increased RNAP II occupancy was obvious after bilirubin treatment. RNAP II usually undergoes a key rate-limiting step of pausing, whereby it stalls and accumulates 30-60 nt downstream of the transcription start site [ ]. This pausing is particularly crucial for the regulation of cellular reprogramming and stimulus-controlled gene expression [ ]. We further investigated the activity of RNAP II Ser2 phosphorylation (Ser2P) (a hallmark of transcription elongation) and Ser5P (a hallmark of transcription pause) by ChIP-seq experiments. Bilirubin treatment decreased Ser5P level along the gene body and increased Ser2P occupancy. This evidence indicated that the transition from paused state to productive elongation in microglia is affected after bilirubin stimulation. We further validated that RNAP II Ser2P enrichment at the promoter is decreased and Ser5P occupancy is increased after microglial TRPM2 or SYK conditional knockout (Fig. B, C). Using the IGV visualization tool, we observed that actin-filament-related genes (such as Rac1, Rock2, Rhob, and Cdc42) exhibited rapid escapes from the paused state in the bilirubin treatment group and increased transcriptional pausing state in the microglial TRPM2 or SYK conditional knockout group (Fig. D). Microglia in vitro assay demonstrated that bilirubin stimulation enlarges microglia cellular surface area with an additional formation of filopodia-like structures, which might act as phagocytic tentacles and facilitate phagocytosis [ ]. And this phenomenon can be inhibited after microglia TRPM2 knockout (Fig. E, F). At the same time, we also observed that the Ser2P enrichment at the promoter in phagocytosis-related genes (such as Hexb, Ctss, C1qb, and Lgmn) is decreased and Ser5P occupancy is increased after microglial TRPM2 or SYK conditional knockout (Fig. G), phenocopying the microglia phagocytosis assay in vitro (Fig. H, I). And bilirubin stimulation could directly induce microglial phagocytosis changes in an SYK-dependent manner (Fig.  K, L). Correspondingly, microglia in the ACC region from bilirubin-burdened mice exhibited increased lysosomal content and microglia reactivity; these phenotypes are all countered after SYK inhibitor treatment (Fig. S7A, B). Inhibition of microglial SYK signaling mitigates hyperbilirubinemia-induced depressive behaviors (Fig. S7C, D). In addition, the ACC hypoactivity is also reversed after SYK inhibitor treatment (Fig. S7E, F). To exclude the possibility that intraperitoneal tamoxifen injection might knock out SYK expression on peripheral CX3CR1-expressing cells, we performed targeted injections of rAAV-TMEM119-shRNA(SYK)-mCherry virus into the ACC region of mice to specifically knockdown microglial SYK in ACC. After four weeks, mice were subjected to consecutive daily intraperitoneal injections of bilirubin for one week to establish a hyperbilirubinemia model. Subsequently, mice underwent behavioral tests, including the open field test and elevated plus maze test (Fig. S7G). Immunofluorescence analysis of the ACC revealed significantly reduced SYK expression in shRNA(SYK) mice compared to controls (Fig. S7H, I). Behavioral results demonstrated marked alleviation of depressive-like behavior in shRNA(SYK) mice. Specifically, shRNA(SYK) mice exhibited increased time spent in the central area during the open field test (Fig. S7J) and significantly prolonged time spent in the open arms during the elevated plus maze test (Fig. S7K). Collectively, our data demonstrate that bilirubin activates the TRPM2-Syk axis to facilitate microglial engulfment of ACCGlu neuronal spines, which is closely related to the progression of depressive behaviors.
 

## Inhibition of TRPM2 ameliorates the progression of hyperbilirubinemia-induced depressive symptoms
To study whether systemic use of TRPM2 inhibitors can systematically treat depressive symptoms induced by hyperbilirubinemia, hyperbilirubinemia mice were intraperitoneally treated with JNJ-28583113 for 2 weeks. Marble burying, open field, elevated plus maze, and tail suspension tests were performed orderly (Fig. A). The JNJ-28583113 treatment obviously mitigates depressive behaviors, manifesting decreased marble-burying counts (Fig. B, C) increased time spent in the central area of the open field (Fig. D-F), prolonged open-arm duration in elevated plus maze trials (Fig. G, H) and decreased immobility time of the tail suspension test (Fig. I). Immunofluorescence staining for the JNJ-28583113 pre-treatment group microglia in ACC confirmed that microglia reactivity was decreased, with the characteristic of longer total process length, more total branch points, and reduced soma size (Fig. S8A-D). Colocation analysis demonstrated that microglial engulfment of dendritic spines is enhanced in the bilirubin group, while the JNJ-28583113 pre-treatment group alleviated this phenotype (Fig. J, K). In addition, the spine density of ACC was reduced in bilirubin-treated mice, and this synaptic decline was modest in the JNJ-28583113 pre-treatment group, which was confirmed by Golgi-Cox staining of fixed brain tissues (Fig. L, M).
 We then used fiber photometry recording of GCaMP6m-expressing ACCGlu neuronal activity in CaMKⅡα-Cre mice subjected to a bilirubin burden experimentally linking ACCGlu neuronal activity to the depressive-like behaviors. ACCGlu neuronal calcium activity is sensitive and responsive to acute air puff stress stimuli [ , , ]. JNJ-28583113 or non-JNJ-28583113 treated hyperbilirubinemia mice were next stimulated with an air puff (Fig.  N, O). We found that the intensity of the ACCGlu neuronal calcium signal in response to an air puff was significantly lower on day 14 in the bilirubin treatment group. The JNJ-28583113 pre-treatment group manifested higher ACCGlu neuronal calcium activity (Fig. P, Q). This phenotype is accompanied by mEPSCs frequency in ACCGlu neurons, and the action potential frequency of ACCGlu neurons also decreased. In contrast, the JNJ-28583113 pre-treatment group showed a phenotype that partially reversed the downward trend of the above electrophysiological indicators (Fig. S8E-J). We then pretreated JNJ-28583113 mice with liver-specific knockdown of Ugt1a1 for 2 weeks (Fig. S8K). Behavioral tests showed that JNJ-28583113 pretreatment was also effective in treating depressive behaviors caused by impaired natural clearance of bilirubin: prolonged residence time in the central area of the open field (Fig. S8L) and increased residence time in the open arm of the elevated plus maze (Fig. S8M). TCPOBOP, a bilirubin scavenging drug, partially ameliorates hyperbilirubinemia by activating the alternative bilirubin scavenging pathway in the absence of Ugt1a1 [ ]. TCPOBOP treatment is effective in alleviating depressive-like behaviors caused by hyperbilirubinemia caused by liver Ugt1a1 knockdown (Fig. S8N), including prolonged residence time in the central area of the open field (Fig. S8O) and increased residence time in the open arm of the elevated plus maze (Fig. S8P), after intraperitoneal injection of TCPOBOP for 7 consecutive days. These findings collectively support that inhibition of TRPM2 ameliorates the progression of hyperbilirubinemia-induced depressive symptoms, apparently via decreasing microglia reactivity, increasing spine density, and increasing neural activity in ACC.

## Discussion
Although the current standard strategy has achieved some effectiveness in the treatment of MDD, a considerable number of patients can’t get satisfactory treatment in the clinic. This suggests that the symptom progression and mechanism of depression are various and systematic [ , ]. So, the innovations of clinical treatment strategies are limited. This study elucidates a novel liver-brain axis mechanism by which compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance drives depressive symptoms through microglial engulfment of dendritic spines in the ACC. Our findings demonstrate that hyperbilirubinemia, frequently observed in MDD patients and chronic stress models, promotes microglial overactivation via the bilirubin-TRPM2-Syk pathway, leading to synaptic loss in ACCGlu neurons and depressive-like behaviors. These results provide a mechanistic link between liver dysfunction and neuropsychiatric disorders, offering potential therapeutic strategies targeting TRPM2 or hepatic bilirubin metabolism for MDD treatment. 
The liver can directly remodel brain functions from evidence of animal experiments and clinical liver transplantation recipients. Liver transplantation could restore liver function absolutely, while behavioral, psychological, or mental problems such as anxiety, cognitive dysfunction, depression, restlessness, et al. have not been completely recovered [ – ]. Liver-derived metabolites or secretory factors can remotely affect the cerebral circuits, regulating emotions and behaviors such as appetite [ ], metabolism regulation [ ], and psychiatric disorders [ , , ]. The current research not only discovered that compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance drives depressive symptoms via regulating microglial engulfment of dendritic spines, but this process can persist after liver from stressed donors transplanted to non-stressed recipients. Conversely, transplanting healthy livers into stressed mice alleviated these symptoms. In addition, a considerable number of patients have persistent neuropsychiatric disorders after liver transplantation [ ]. This greatly suggests that the liver has memory of external stress stimuli, which may be associated with inflammatory memory or tissue memory [ – ]. 
This research demonstrated that bilirubin is one of the candidate pathogenic factors in the progression of MDD. Bilirubin readily accumulates in brain tissue, causing damage to specific highly sensitive regions such as the cortex and hippocampus [ ], which has been reported to induce anxiety or depressive behaviors in patients or rodents [ ]. However, the underlying mechanism is largely uncovered. Bilirubin can induce oxidative stress in cortical neurons or glial cells and can bind directly to a specific cavity of TRPM2 channels, gate channel opening [ , ]. Previous studies also demonstrated that oxidative or nitrosative stress can promote mitochondrial NADase or nucleus PARP catalyze nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide to ADPR, which is an intracellular agonist for TRPM2 [ ]. So, bilirubin can also indirectly activate TRPM2 by producing intracellular ADPR [ ]. The activation of TRPM2 can cause neurological diseases via synaptic plasticity at glutamate synapses [ ], governing stress-induced depressive-like behaviors [ ]. In chronic unpredictable stress model of depression in mice or human MDD patients, TRPM2 is significantly higher expressed compared with healthy controls [ ]. This evidence indicates that TRPM2 activation may be at least partly participant or sensitive to the progression of depression. In this research, although we can’t completely rule out the contribution of TRPM2 in direct regulating neuroplasticity to depression, we have expanded the mechanism that bilirubin activate TRPM2-SYK axis to facilitates microglial engulfment of ACCGlu neuronal spines. 
Although this study reveals a novel metabolic symbiosis between compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance and behavioral regulation, several critical questions remain unresolved. First, mouse models replicate key features of MDD; species differences in bilirubin metabolism and neural circuitry necessitate validation in human cohorts. We acknowledge that animal models have limitations and cannot fully replicate the complexity of human disease. The value of this model lies in its focus on and confirmation that elevated bilirubin—a single, clinically common metabolic abnormality—suffices as an independent driver of liver-brain axis dysfunction. It elucidates the specific mechanism through which this occurs via the glial cell TRPM2-SYK axis-mediated synaptic pruning. This provides a distinct mechanistic target for understanding and intervening in depressive subtypes associated with hepatic metabolic disorders. Secondly, though TRPM2 was microglia-specific in our models, its roles in astrocytes, neurons, or peripheral immunity merit further exploration. Previous research have demonstrated that bilirubin could also enhance other voltage-gated calcium channels such as ASIC channels [ – ], which are reported to regulate emotions in multiple neuropsychiatric disorders. In addition, bilirubin may influence depression via inflammatory cytokines or gut-liver crosstalk avenues not examined here. These unresolved aspects warrant further investigation to fully dissect the complexity of the liver-brain interaction mechanism. From a therapeutic perspective, traditional Chinese medicine has long associated “liver qi stagnation” with emotional disorders, though the mechanism remained unclear. This study first links impaired hepatic bilirubin clearance with glial cell-mediated synaptic loss, providing a modern pathophysiological explanation for this theory. Targeting TRPM2 or SYK interventions could be considered a precise regulation of the pathological pathway underlying “liver qi stagnation”.

## Conclusion
In summary, we demonstrated that microglial phagocytic activity is functionally coupled to hyperbilirubinemia resulting from compromised hepatic bilirubin clearance in the progression of MDD. This hyperbilirubinemia activates the bilirubin-TRPM2-Syk axis in microglia, driving excessive spine pruning and synaptic loss in the ACCGlu by employing transcriptional pause-release mechanisms to prioritize protein synthesis of pro-phagocytosis machines. Targeting the liver-brain axis via TRPM2 blockade presents a novel therapeutic strategy for MDD.
