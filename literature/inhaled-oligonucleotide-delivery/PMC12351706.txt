Among the most studied and broadly used lipid-based systems in vivo for RNA delivery are lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). LNPs are typically manufactured using rapid mixing approaches, such as microfluidics, with a standard formulation consisting of an ionizable lipid, a phospholipid, cholesterol, and a PEG-lipid conjugate (Hou et al. ; Kon et al. ). The incorporation of a PEG-lipid on the surface of lipid-based nanocarriers is mainly intended to create a hydrophilic shield that reduces opsonization and nanoparticle aggregation in biological fluids, thereby prolonging circulation time (Suk et al. ; Krishnan et al. ).
However, in some cases, PEGylation may enhance LNP tissue targeting, as seen with the formulation of Onpattro®, a siRNA-based LNP therapy designed for gene silencing in hepatocytes (Akinc et al. ). Studies revealed that Onpattro’s liver tropism is primarily driven by the rapid desorption of PEG and the subsequent adsorption of apolipoprotein E, along with other proteins, onto LNPs surface, thereby promoting targeting via LDL receptors (Akinc et al. ; Francia et al. ). An additional strategy for targeted therapeutic delivery involves conjugating ligands to the LNP surface, including peptides, hyaluronan (also known as hyaluronic acid (HA)), aptamers and other small molecules (Priya et al. ). Other studies used an alternative approach to enhance targeted tissue accumulation of LNPs, known as selective organ targeting (SORT) molecules (Cheng et al. ; Wei et al. ). Increasing the molar percentage of permanently cationic lipids in LNP formulations has been shown to shift LNP tropism from the liver to the spleen and, subsequently, to the lungs (Cheng et al. ).
mRNA-based therapeutics present a promising approach for ocular applications that require transient protein expression, particularly in gene editing via mRNA-mediated delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 constructs (Mirjalili Mohanna et al. ; Pulman et al. ) or neuroprotection (Jiang et al. ). Currently, several challenges hinder the advancement of synthetic mRNA-based retinal therapies, including overcoming physical and biochemical barriers, such as the outer and inner limiting membranes (OLM and ILM), ensuring efficient mRNA delivery to the retina, and facilitating endosomal escape (Huang et al. ; Tawfik et al. ; Chatterjee et al. ). Furthermore, targeted delivery to specific retinal cell populations remains a critical consideration. In preclinical studies, subretinal injections of mRNA-LNPs showed limited success, with tropism primarily restricted to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Patel et al. ; Ryals et al. ; Gautam et al. ). However, certain formulations containing standard ionizable lipids (MC3 and KC2) or ionizable lipids with a thiophene moiety (Eygeris et al. ) also enabled transfection of Müller cells (Patel et al. ) and photoreceptors (Eygeris et al. ). In some cases, surface modifications of LNPs with PEG variants (Gautam et al. ) and peptides (Herrera-Barrera et al. ) have been shown to influence LNP tropism toward photoreceptors through mechanisms that remain unclear.
Various LNP formulations have been also evaluated for mRNA delivery via intravitreal injection in mouse models (Huang and Chau ; Ryals et al. ; Chambers et al. ; Jiang et al. ). The efficiency of mRNA delivery varied across studies, targeting different retinal and ocular structures, including Müller glia, the optic nerve head, and the trabecular meshwork (Ryals et al. ), as well as ganglion cells (Jiang et al. ) and the RPE (Chambers et al. ). Their partial success is mainly due to LNP entrapment in vitreous humor and poor diffusion through the ILM, established barriers to ocular drug delivery (Mains and Wilson ; Huang et al. ; Peynshaert et al. ).
Compared to LNPs, lipoplexes have a simpler composition, are easily assembled, and offer a more cost-effective alternative. Their formulations generally contain permanently cationic lipids, which complex with negatively charged nucleic acids, and incorporate neutral helper co-lipids to facilitate membrane fusion (Anderson et al. ; Kim et al. ). One commercially available lipoplex-based system, Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX (LP), is a widely used mRNA transfection reagent with a good profile of low cellular toxicity. LP-mRNA lipoplexes, primarily utilized for in vitro applications, have demonstrated high transfection efficiency (Devoldere et al. ; Graef et al. ; Moradian et al. ; Jarzebska et al. ), including in neuronal cells (Graef et al. ), as also reported in manufacturer data available on the product page. However, due to the instability of mRNA-lipoplexes, limited studies have tested the reagent in vivo (Sultana et al. ; Devoldere et al. ; van Asbeck et al. ), including for retinal application (Devoldere et al. ).
Interestingly, the outcomes of mRNA delivery to the mouse retina using LP-based lipoplexes were comparable to those achieved with LNPs. Subretinal delivery of LP-formulated enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) mRNA lipoplexes led to eGFP protein expression in the RPE and, to some extent, in photoreceptors for up to seven days after administration (Devoldere et al. ). Furthermore, eGFP mRNA was detected in the inner retinal layers at least seven days after intravitreal injection (Devoldere et al. ).
Although these findings underscore the potential of LP-based lipoplexes for retinal delivery, this study, like all previous in vivo murine studies, prepared mRNA lipoplexes in a reduced-serum Opti-MEM™ medium following an in vitro protocol, which may have contributed to the reported suboptimal mRNA encapsulation efficiency (Devoldere et al. ). Besides, it was optimized for producing mRNA lipoplexes at a low final concentration. This poses challenges for large-animal models, where injection buffers must meet stringent regulatory guidelines and higher mRNA doses are required compared to murine studies. Additionally, the instability of mRNA lipoplexes may underlie their clearance and limited diffusion across retinal layers.
Our research group has been conducting translational studies on recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene replacements for retinal degenerative diseases using clinically relevant canine models (Beltran et al. ; Guziewicz et al. ; Gardiner et al. ; Aguirre et al. ). In parallel, we have been investigating disease mechanisms (Beltran et al. ; Appelbaum et al. , ) and exploring novel therapeutic approaches for treatment of retinal diseases, including synthetic RNA-based strategies. For the latter, the development of an effective and retina-compatible RNA delivery system remains a critical prerequisite for preclinical evaluation of RNA-based therapeutics. In this study, we present an optimized method for preparing mRNA lipoplexes and a streamlined protocol for their stabilization via PEGylation. Using various PEG derivatives, we characterized the properties of PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes in vitro and evaluated the intraocular delivery of selected formulations to the canine retina in pilot feasibility studies.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Animals and study samples
The justification for animal experiments was reviewed and approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol # 803813; Title: Optimizing viral and non-viral retinal therapies for late-stage disease). This study is part of a larger project that has resulted or may result in multiple related publications. Dogs were selected for this study as they are a well-accepted large animal model with a human-sized eye, suitable for evaluating ocular drug delivery. Each individual RNA delivery system was tested in a single eye of one animal for a defined experimental duration (24 or 72 hours), as a pilot assessment of an RNA carrier formulation validated in vitro. A total of four dogs (clinically normal, one male and three females, aged 21–174 weeks) were included; animal details and procedures are provided in Supplementary Table S1. No control group was used, and neither randomization nor blinding was applied in the experimental design. Retinal distribution and efficiency of cellular uptake of administered RNA served as the primary outcome measures. The dogs were not obtained from a commercial source; they were bred and housed at the Retinal Disease Studies Facility (University of Pennsylvania) under standardized conditions, including consistent diet, medications, vaccinations, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle.
General anesthesia was induced by intravenous injection of 4–6 mg/kg of propofol (Virbac, Westlake, TX) which was maintained with 1–5% general inhalation anesthetic isoflurane (Virbac, Westlake, TX). For terminal procedures, the dogs were euthanatized via intravenous overdose of pentobarbital sodium and phenytoin sodium mixture (Euthasol; Virbac, Westlake, TX) at a dose 200 mg/kg, followed by immediate enucleation of the eyes. Fixation, cryoprotection, and embedding of eye tissues were performed as previously described (Beltran et al. ). Briefly, the posterior eye cups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA) for three hours, followed by 2% PFA for 24 hours. Next, tissues were incubated in a 15% sucrose solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 24 hours at 4 °C, then transferred to a 30% sucrose solution for an additional 24 hours at 4 °C and subsequently embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) media (Sakura Finetek USA, Inc., Torrance, CA). Preparation of all solutions was carried out with diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water, and all tissue handling used sterilized instruments and gloves. In addition to newly collected samples from the present study, we used archival PFA-fixed retinal sections from two canine retinas, originally obtained as a part of a previous project (Beltran et al. ). The ethical approval for those samples was obtained at that time, and details are included in the referenced publication. Where appropriate, these archival sections were used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and RNA in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH) experiments.
Consent to publish. Not applicable. This study did not involve human participants or identifiable individual data.

2.2.

## mRNA lipoplex materials
Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). 1,2-Dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (PEG-DMG) and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (PEG-DSPE) were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc. (Alabaster, AL). Hyaluronan (10 kDa) was purchased from HAworks LLC (Bedminster, NJ). HA (10 kDa) conjugated to 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[amino(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (HA-PEG-DPPE) as well as a double conjugated molecule (PEG-HA-DPPE), in which HA (10 kDa) is covalently linked to both group amino group (NH2)-PEG and NH2-DPPE groups, were custom synthesized by HAworks LLC (Bedminster, NJ). CleanCap eGFP mRNA fully substituted with 5-methoxyuridine (L-7201) was purchased from TriLink BioTechnologies (San Diego, CA).

2.3.

## Lipoplex preparation, PEGylation, and characterization
The complexation of eGFP mRNA (1 μg/mL in 1 mM sodium citrate pH 6.4) with transfection reagent Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX was done in pure water. The final lipoplex volumes ranged from 10 to 350 μL, maintaining a constant ratio of 1 μg mRNA per 10 μL of final volume. Each newly purchased lot of the transfection reagent was assessed to determine the optimal volume-to-weight (v/w) ratio for mRNA complexation. A v/w ratio of 2.5 was selected and applied in all subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments described in .
Briefly, to prepare non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes, predetermined volumes of the transfection reagent and water were mixed by brief pulse vortexing, followed by a 10-minute incubation at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, mRNA was added and gently mixed with the emulsified transfection reagent by pipetting, and the complexes were incubated for an additional five minutes at RT. To prepare PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes, a predetermined volume of the selected PEG derivative (solubilized in water) was added to preformed mRNA lipoplexes, gently mixed by pipetting, and incubated for 15 minutes at RT, followed by 30 minutes at 4 °C. The final molar concentrations of PEG derivatives in the lipoplex solutions were 0.04 pmol/mL. For all experiments, freshly prepared non-PEGylated and PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes were utilized within 30 minutes and two hours, respectively.
Size, distribution, and zeta potential of non-PEGylated and PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes were determined via dynamic light scattering using a PMX220 TWIN Laser ZetaView instrument (Particle Metrix Inc., Mebane, NC).

2.4.

## Cell culture and transfections
Aliquots of RAW264.7 (a macrophage cell line) and ARPE-19 (a spontaneously arising retinal pigment epithelial cell line), along with cell culture media and reagents were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). Cells were cultured in recommended growth conditions. For all in vitro transfection experiments, cells were transfected with 0.5 μg mRNA in lipoplex formulation per 1 × 105 cells. One day before transfection, cells were seeded into chambers on a Nunc Lab Tek II chamber slide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and cultured in growth media supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The following day, the growth media was removed, and cells were rinsed with 1× Dulbecco’s PBS. PEGylated or non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes, diluted to the appropriate concentration in Opti-MEM™ medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 1.5% FBS, were added directly to the cells and incubated for 20 hours at 37 °C. For the HA-functionalized lipoplex uptake competition assay, cells were pretreated with 2 mg/mL of free HA for 30 minutes at 37 °C prior to lipoplex administration.
For in vitro experiments aimed at assessing the effect of vitreous on lipoplex uptake, vitreous humor was mechanically disrupted as follows: 300 μL of fresh porcine vitreous, obtained from a local abattoir, was applied through a Gel Breaker Tube (IST Engineering Inc., Milpitas, CA) by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 30 minutes at 4 °C. ARPE19 cells were seeded onto 0.4 μm pore membrane inserts (Corning, Glendale, AZ) in a 24-well plate. Forty microliters of processed vitreous was added on top of the cells and overlaid with 70 μL of PEGylated or non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes diluted in Opti-MEM™ media containing 1.5% FBS, followed by incubation for 20 hours at 37 °C.
When applicable, fluorescent and brightfield live-cell images were taken using the EVOS™ M5000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Cell counts and transfection efficiency evaluation (calculated as the ratio of eGFP-positive cells relative to the total cell count) were performed using Cellpose software (version 3.0.7) (Stringer et al. ), employing the cyto2_cp3 model for cell segmentation and counting. Cells were imaged at ×10 magnification under brightfield and eGFP fluorescence conditions (green channel). At this magnification, RAW264.7 cells measured approximately 30 pixels in diameter, while ARPE19 cells measured around 45 pixels. In graphical form, data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and are representative for at least three independent experiments. Differences between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. A p value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

2.5.

## Subretinal and intravitreal injections
Subretinal and intravitreal injections of mRNA lipoplexes were performed under general anesthesia via a transvitreal approach without vitrectomy. Subretinal and intravitreal administrations of 120–150 µL and 210–300 µL eGFP mRNA lipoplex solutions, respectively, were performed using a commercially available subretinal injector (MicroDose™ Injection kit with 25g/38g PolyTip® cannula, MedOne, Sarasota, FL). All formulations contained 100 µg/mL of eGFP mRNA, corresponding to a total dose of 12–15 µg (subretinal) or 21–30 µg (intravitreal), depending on the injection volume. Injections were conducted under direct visualization with an operating microscope (Lumera700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Oberkochen, Germany). Fundus photography (RetCam Shuttle; ClarityMedical Systems, Pleasanton, CA) confirmed the formation of a subretinal bleb following injection. Ophthalmic examinations were performed before tissue collection at 24- or 72-hours post-injection, and included fundus photography. At 72 hours post-injection, dogs underwent confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy with the Blue Autofluorescence mode (cSLO; BAF mode) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging (Spectralis HRA/OCT2, Heidelberg Engineering Inc., Heidelberg, Germany). Pre- and post-operative medical management was performed as previously reported (Aguirre et al. ). Note: The injection solutions consisted of eGFP mRNA (at a concentration of 100 µg/mL) formulated in either PEGylated or non-PEGylated lipoplexes. Prior to the injection, 10× PBS was added to achieve a final concentration of 1× PBS.

2.6.

## RNA in situ hybridization
The levels and cellular distribution of target mRNAs were assessed by RNA-ISH on retinal cryosections or fixed cells using RNAscope™ assays (Advanced Cell Diagnostics Bio Techne, Hayward, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor optimizations outlined in our previous publication (Aguirre et al. ). Labeled RNAscope probes against eGFP (TriLink), peptidylprolyl isomerase B (PPIB) (ACDBio positive control) and canine rhodopsin (RHO) were designed by ACDBio. The signal was developed with the RNAscope 2.5 HD Assay-Red following the product manual guidelines. The staining was further examined using brightfield microscopy, and images were captured with a Zeiss Axioplan microscope (Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany). In certain cases, RNA-ISH was combined with IHC. For this, tissue slides with developed probe signals were incubated with appropriate primary antibodies as detailed in the corresponding section below.

2.7.

## ONL isolation using laser capture microdissection (LCM)
The LCM procedure was conducted to isolate outer nuclear layer (ONL) regions from PFA-fixed, OCT-embedded retinal sections, as previously reported (Takahashi et al. ). Briefly, freshly made 10 μm retinal cryosections were mounted onto glass polyethylene naphthalate membrane slides (Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL), following their staining with 5% cresyl violet acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). ONL regions were captured with an LCM microscope (Leica LMD7000, Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL) equipped with a 20× objective lens, collected in AdhesiveCap 500 clear tubes (Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany) and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

2.8.

## RNA isolation and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis
RNA isolation from LCM-captured tissue was carried out using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) treatment was performed to eliminate genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) contamination. The first round of cDNA synthesis, DNA template preparation, in vitro transcription, and second round of cDNA synthesis were done according to the protocol detailed in our recent publication (Appelbaum et al. ), using RNA isolated from PFA-fixed, OCT-embedded retinal sections. Briefly, the isolated RNA underwent polyadenylation using Poly(A) Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) following the first round of cDNA synthesis, which was performed using the Maxima H Minus cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with T7-Oligo(dT) primer (see Supplementary Table S2). For the preparation of a double-stranded (ds) DNA template, containing a ds promoter region and a single-stranded (ss) region downstream, the T7AS primer (listed in Supplementary Table S2) and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) from the first round of cDNA synthesis were added to GoTaq® Long PCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI) followed by incubation at 54 °C for 2.5 min. In vitro transcription reactions were performed using HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) from the previous step. Generated RNA was subsequently employed for second round of cDNA synthesis using the Maxima H Minus cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

2.9.

## Absolute quantification assay
The SYBR green platform was used to determine RHO and eGFP copies in LCM samples. Each reaction was performed in a total volume of 25 μL, containing GoTaq® Long PCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI), second round cDNA generated from 10 ng RNA, SYBR® Green I (Biotium Inc., Fremont, CA), 1.125 μL ROX reference dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 1 M betaine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and 0.4 μM primers (sequences listed in Supplementary Table S2). To construct an absolute standard curve for each amplicon, the corresponding polymerase chain reaction (PCR) product was used as a template, with dilutions ranging from 103 to 107 molecules. Template DNA copy numbers were determined following a previously described method (Kuznetsova et al. ). Briefly, the dsDNA mass was converted to moles, and dsDNA copy number was calculated using Avogadro’s number. All reactions were conducted in triplicate. The reaction data were analyzed using the 7500 Software version 2.0.1 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA).

2.10.

## In vitro synthesis of circular RNA (circRNA)
The small circRNA, 240 nucleotides (nt) in length and containing random sequence, was synthesized in vitro using a permuted intron–exon (PIE) construct as previously described (Rausch et al. ). This transcription template exploits the properties of self-splicing group I introns found in a subset of bacterial genes (Chu et al. ; Xu et al. ). Briefly, the PEI transcriptional template, containing T7 promoter and the circRNA sequence with complementary 5′- and 3′-terminal ‘homology arms’ to facilitate co-transcriptional RNA folding and backsplicing, was generated through artificial gene synthesis (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Coralville, IA). The sequences of the 5′- and 3′-terminal arms in the PEI construct were as reported (Rausch et al. ). Linear precursor RNA was generated by standard in vitro RNA synthesis from the transcription DNA template using the HiScribe™ T7 RNA synthesis kit (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA template was subsequently digested with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). CircRNA, formed co-transcriptionally through an autocatalytic reaction involving excision from a linear precursor RNA, was further purified from linear RNA byproducts by incubation with the 3′–5′ exoribonuclease RNase R (Lucigen, Middleton, WI) for two hours at 37 °C, followed by spin-column purification (Zymo Research Inc., Irvine, CA).

2.11.

## Fluorescent immunohistochemistry
The IHC was carried out as described in our previous publications (Appelbaum et al. , ). Briefly, 10 μm-thick retinal cryosections were washed in PBS and incubated overnight with primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table S3). Appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor Dyes; Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR) were used at 1:200 dilution. Counterstaining of nuclei was performed using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and the sections were mounted using Invitrogen ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Immunolabeling was examined by epifluorescence microscopy with a Zeiss Axioplan microscope (Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany) and captured images were analyzed with Zeiss microscopy software Zen 3.1. Confocal imaging was performed using the Stellaris 8 confocal microscope system (Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL). Confocal images are shown as a z-stack of 11 z-steps or of five z-steps each of 0.2 µm. Maximum projection of all images was equally adjusted for brightness and contrast with ImageJ software (Bethesda, MD) (Schneider et al. ).

2.12.

## Immunocytochemistry (ICC)
Cells were fixed with 3.7% PFA in PBS for 15 minutes at RT, followed by a two-minute PBS wash to remove residual fixative. All subsequent steps, including primary and secondary antibody incubation, were performed as described for IHC in tissue sections.

2.13.

## Western blot analysis
Protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Western blot analysis was performed as previously described (Appelbaum et al. ), utilizing primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S3. Briefly, proteins were separated by size using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with 10% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA) and subsequently transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE). The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies against CD44 and beta-actin (ACTB), followed by corresponding IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies (LI-COR). Protein normalization to ACTB, quantification and data analysis were conducted with the LI-COR Odyssey imaging system (LI-COR) using Image Studio Software.

## Results
3.1.

## Formulation and characterization of non-PEGylated and PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes
3.1.1.

## Non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes
We hypothesized that assembling lipoplexes in low-ionic-strength solutions, such as pure water, may enhance mRNA encapsulation efficiency by optimizing electrostatic interactions between cationic lipids and mRNA. The complexation of eGFP mRNA with the LP transfection reagent at varying v/w ratios in water was assessed by gel electrophoresis and quantified using ImageJ software (Bethesda, MD). As shown in (top), LP fully complexed eGFP mRNA at the v/w ratio of 2.5 in water, whereas only ∼70% of mRNA was complexed at the same v/w ratio in PBS buffer, supporting our hypothesis. The v/w ratio of 2.5 was chosen for all subsequent in vitro and in vivo, given its reported low cytotoxicity to cells (Devoldere et al. ). We also found that the same formulation conditions can be effectively applied to other structurally distinct RNA types, such as circRNAs, which are generally more challenging to complex. Under optimized conditions, approximately 90% of synthetic circRNA (240 nt) was successfully complexed at a 2.5 v/w ratio in water ( , bottom).
 We next evaluated the hydrodynamic diameter (size), zeta potential, and stability of mRNA lipoplexes prepared using different LP lots. Some variability between lots was anticipated, potentially arising from slight differences in the production process that could impact the final product. Although all formulated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes were relatively small, with peak sizes ranging from 98.7 nm to 121.5 nm ( , ), their zeta potential varied substantially across lots, ranging from −14.4 mV to −58.7 mV. Notably, the highly negatively charged lipoplexes, LPlot4 (−40.9 mV) and LPlot5 (−58.7 mV), exhibited greater stability than those with less negative zeta potential values ( ), likely due to reduced nanoparticle aggregation with increasing surface charge negativity. These findings are consistent with previously reported correlations between the magnitude of the zeta potential and the stability of nanoparticles in colloidal solutions (Kumar and Dixit ).
 
3.1.2.

## Effect of PEGylation on the stability of mRNA lipoplexes
PEGylation of RNA lipoplexes as an approach to enhance their stability in physiological buffers was previously reported (Decastro et al. ; Nosova et al. ; Hattori et al. ). In this study, the surface of preformed LPlo4- and LPlot5-based mRNA lipoplexes was modified with various PEG derivatives using the post-insertion method, which has been shown to achieve a high degree of PEGylation in cationic liposomes (Nosova et al. ). The selection of the LPlot4 and LPlot5 types of Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX was primarily based on their prevalence among the purchased Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX reagents. A total of four PEG derivatives were evaluated: PEG-DMG and PEG-DSPE, and two PEG-DPPE conjugates functionalized with hyaluronan, a ligand for CD44 receptor (Aruffo et al. ).
The exact concentration of cationic lipids in Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX is not disclosed by the manufacture, making it challenging to determine the optimal LP/PEG ratio required to modify lipoplex properties as intended. To address this, we initially estimated the required molar content of PEG as ‘one PEG molecule per lipoplex’, considering the experimentally determined concentration of nanoparticles listed in , alongside with previously published data on RNA packaging characteristics in LNPs, including payload distribution and capacity (Li et al. ). The required molar concentration of PEG derivatives was subsequently refined in vitro by assessing the stability of the resulting PEGylated lipoplexes and validating candidate PEG-lipid concentrations in cultured cells ( , ).
 As anticipated, coating preformed mRNA lipoplexes with PEG derivatives improved nanoparticle stability over time. Data in show that mRNA lipoplexes formulated with LPlot4(HA-PEG-DPPE) and LPlot5(PEG-DMG) retained their particle size, concentration, and zeta potential for up to 48 hours. Comparatively, the concentration of LPlot4(PEG-DSPE) and LPlot4(PEG-HA-DPPE) nanoparticles decreased by approximately 3.3-fold within 48 hours post-production, indicating their partial decomposition.
The formulation of PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes based on LPlot5 yielded particle sizes and concentrations comparable to those obtained with LPlot4 ( ). However, the values of zeta potential were more negative, reaching approximately −54.2 mV for LPlot5(HA-PEG-DPPE) and −51.6 mV for LPlot5(PEG-DMG), vs. −28.2 mV and −31.5 mV for the corresponding LPlot4-based formulations. These differences highlight the influence of lipid composition on the surface properties of mRNA lipoplexes.

3.2.

## In vitro transfection efficiency of non-PEGylated and PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes
Next, we assessed the transfection efficiency of formulated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes by measuring the percentage of eGFP-positive cells in cultured mouse macrophage RAW264.7 and human ARPE19 cells at 20 hours post-transfection. Both cell types have been previously shown to express the CD44 receptor (Hossaini Nasr et al. ; Laradji et al. ; Song et al. ), consistent with our findings ( ). However, CD44 expression in RAW264.7 cells is approximately three times higher than in ARPE19 cells ( ).
3.2.1.

## Effect of PEGylation on transfection efficiency in vitro
Five formulations of LPlot4-based mRNA lipoplexes were evaluated, including a non-PEGylated control and four PEGylated variants coated with PEG-DMG, PEG-DSPE, HA-PEG-DPPE, or PEG-HA-DPPE. Overall, ARPE19 cells exhibited higher transfection efficiency than RAW264.7 cells. PEGylation of LPlot4-based lipoplexes with PEG-DMG, PEG-DSPE, and PEG-HA-DPPE led to a reduction in transfection efficiency in RAW264.7 and ARPE19 cells, as indicated by the decreased percentage (%) of eGFP-positive cells ( ).
 Pretreatment of cultured RAW264.7 cells with free HA markedly reduced transfection efficiency of LPlot4(HA-PEG-DPPE) and LPlot4(PEG-HA-DPPE) lipoplexes, supporting the role of CD44-pathway in the predominant uptake of both HA-containing lipoplexes. In contrast, in ARPE19, pretreatment with free HA selectively impaired the transfection efficiency of LPlot4(HA-PEG-DPPE) lipoplexes, while having no significant effect on the efficiency of LPlot4(PEG-HA-DPPE) lipoplexes, suggesting that other mechanisms condition transfection.

3.2.2.

## Impact of LP type on transfection efficiency in vitro
Three formulations of LPlot5-based mRNA lipoplexes were evaluated in cultured cells: a non-PEGylated version and two PEGylated versions with PEG-DMG and HA-PEG-DPPE coatings ( ).
As illustrated in , the non-PEGylated LPlot5 lipoplexes resulted in approximately a 2.2-fold and 2-fold reduction in the percentage of eGFP-expressing RAW264.7 and ARPE19 cells compared to the LPlot4-based lipoplexes (presented in ). In RAW264.7 cells, the transfection efficiency of LPlot5-based PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes was comparable to both the non-PEGylated control and the corresponding LPlot4 lipoplexes. In contrast, ARPE19 cells treated with PEGylated LPlot5(PEG-DMG) lipoplexes exhibited a 1.5-fold increase in eGFP-positive cells compared to the non-PEGylated control. Furthermore, in these cells, transfection efficiency decreased approximately 1.5-fold for LPlot5(HA-PEG-DPPE). Pretreatment of cultured cells with free HA prior to transfection with LPlot5(HA-PEG-DPPE) significantly reduced the percentage of eGFP-positive cells in RAW264.7 cultures. Conversely, transfection efficiency in ARPE19 cells was mildly affected (p = .12), likely due to a shift from CD44-dependent to CD44-independent uptake mechanisms resulting from changes in the lipoplex formulation.
Our findings suggest that compared to RAW264.7 cells, ARPE19 cells exhibit greater sensitivity to variations in lipoplex formulation.

3.2.3.

## Vitreous barrier affects transfection efficiency in vitro
Vitreous humor is known to impede nanoparticle movement due to its gel-like structure and biochemical composition, posing a significant barrier to ocular drug delivery systems (Mains and Wilson ). Using ARPE19 cells, we evaluated the transfection efficiency of two lead PEGylated lipoplex formulations (LP(PEG-DMG) and LP(HA-PEG-DPPE)) in the presence of vitreous. As shown in , a notable reduction in the percentage of eGFP-positive cells was observed following eGFP mRNA delivery with both PEGylated lipoplex formulations (23% for LPlot4(PEG-DMG) and 32% for LPlot4(HA-PEG-DPPE)), as well as in non-PEGylated control (52% for LPlot4), compared to the values obtained under standard experimental conditions (58%, 65%, and 98%, respectively) presented in .

3.3.

## In vitro uptake of PEGylated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes
The substantial difference in transfection efficiency of PEGylated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes between the RAW264.7 and ARPE19 cell lines prompted us to investigate whether cellular uptake contributes to this variation. RNA-ISH was performed on RAW264.7 and ARPE19 cells treated with eGFP mRNA formulated in either LPlot5(PEG-DMG) or LPlot5(HA-PEG-DPPE). At 20 hours post-treatment, both cell types exhibited 100% eGFP mRNA uptake, as determined by the number of eGFP mRNA-positive cells ( ). These findings imply that cellular uptake of PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes is not a primary limiting factor for eGFP protein expression in these cell types.

3.4.

## Cellular distribution and protein expression following intravitreal and subretinal injections of mRNA lipoplexes in dogs
Intravitreal and subretinal injections were performed in normal adult canine eyes to evaluate the efficacy of eGFP mRNA delivery using LP(PEG-DMG) and LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) lipoplex formulations (based on LPlot5). Surface modification with HA was intended to facilitate CD44-mediated mRNA delivery. While CD44 is known to be abundantly expressed in the retina (Chaitin et al. ; Ayten et al. ), its distribution is limited to specific cell populations. In the adult normal canine retina, the CD44 receptor is localized to Müller cell membranes and the RPE apical microvilli, as demonstrated by IHC against excitatory amino acid transporter 1 (EAAT1), a glial-neuronal transmitter (Appelbaum et al. ), and the RPE apical marker, EZRIN (Guziewicz et al. ) ( ).
 3.4.1.

## Intravitreal delivery
eGFP mRNA in PEGylated or non-PEGylated lipoplex formulation was administered via intravitreal injection. The efficiency of eGFP mRNA delivery and cellular distribution at 24 hours post-injection was assessed in selected retinal cryosections using RNA-ISH followed by IHC against eGFP and glutamine synthetase (GS), a marker of Müller cells. Following the injection of LP(PEG-DMG) lipoplexes, a mild accumulation of eGFP mRNA was observed in the nerve fiber layer (NFL) within Müller glial foot processes ( , a1) with no eGFP protein expression ( , a2). In comparison, with LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) formulation, eGFP mRNA labeling in the NFL was localized to GS-negative retinal cells ( , b1, b2), with low levels of eGFP protein also detected ( , b2). In addition to the described pattern, punctate eGFP mRNA labeling was occasionally observed in the ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), and inner nuclear layer (INL) for both PEGylated lipoplex formulations.
This study also evaluated non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes, referring previous research on a similar formulation used in the mouse retina (Devoldere et al. ). Here, eGFP mRNA delivery and protein expression were assessed at 72 hours post-intravitreal injection of non-PEGylated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes. Ophthalmic examination of treated eyes with BAF cSLO/OCT imaging, conducted prior to tissue collection, revealed no evidence of retinal toxicity, degeneration, or eGFP-associated fluorescence (data not shown). Consistently, RNA-ISH/IHC analysis of retinal cryosections showed no detectable eGFP mRNA labeling or protein expression (Supplementary Figure S2, a1, a2), supporting clinical observations.

3.4.2.

## Subretinal delivery
eGFP mRNA lipoplexes stabilized with either PEG-DMG or HA-PEG-DPPE were delivered to the subretinal space. At 24 hours post-subretinal injection of LP(PEG-DMG) and LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) lipoplexes, prominent eGFP mRNA labeling was identified by RNA-ISH in all retinal cells within the injected/bleb region ( , a1, a2 and b1, b2). However, eGFP protein expression was confined to RPE ( , a3 and b3).
 In photoreceptor cells, where eGFP mRNA was predominantly localized to the soma, confocal imaging revealed its accumulation primarily in perinuclear regions with minimal to no nuclear localization ( , a1 and b2). This distribution pattern partially resembles that of endogenous RHO mRNA, which also exhibits perinuclear accumulation ( , c1 and c2). To quantitatively assess eGFP mRNA internalized by photoreceptors, regions of the ONL within the bleb were isolated using LCM procedure, followed by RNA extraction. Quantification of eGFP copy numbers in LCM samples of ONL showed 1.8-fold higher eGFP levels with LP(PEG-DMG) and 1.2-fold higher levels with LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) relative to RHO transcripts ( ).
 Next, eGFP mRNA delivery and protein expression were evaluated at 72 hours post-subretinal injection of PEGylated and non-PEGylated lipoplex formulations. Ophthalmic examination of treated eyes did not reveal any signs of ocular discomfort/toxicity, funduscopic appearance ( , a1, b1, c1) and retinal lamination assessed by OCT ( , a3, b3, c3) were normal within the treated area. Mild levels of eGFP fluorescence were detected in all three subretinally injected eyes by cSLO imaging (BAF mode), suggesting presence of some eGFP protein expression ( , a2, b2, c2).
 Analysis of retinal sections using RNA-ISH and IHC revealed the presence of eGFP mRNA and protein within the injected/bleb area, predominantly in the RPE. Patchy distribution was observed across retinal layers other than the RPE, with eGFP mRNA levels ranging from moderate to low, or absent (designated in as area-1, -2, and -3, respectively). Although both PEGylated lipoplex formulations exhibited moderate eGFP immunolabeling within the inner segments of photoreceptors ( , a3, d3, and e3), distinct differences in eGFP protein distribution were observed in the inner retina between the two mRNA lipoplex formulations. For the LP(PEG-DMG) formulation, eGFP staining exhibited a pattern suggestive of expression in Müller glia ( , a3 and b3). In contrast, the LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) formulation showed eGFP labeling in various cells within the INL ( , d3) as well as in the GCL ( , d3 and e3).
 Compared to the PEGylated formulations, the non-PEGylated mRNA lipoplex variant resulted in eGFP mRNA and protein being exclusively localized to the RPE within the injected/bleb area (Supplementary Figure S2, b1–b3).
In summary, the results demonstrate that subretinally injected PEG-stabilized lipoplexes efficiently diffuse across the OLM and retinal layers, achieving high cellular uptake, highlighting their potential as nanocarriers for retinal mRNA delivery. However, despite the presence of eGFP mRNA in retinal cells at 24 hours post-delivery, its rapid degradation in non-RPE cells restricts protein expression. It remains unclear whether insufficient endosomal escape or additional factors contribute to the observed outcomes. Intravitreal administration of PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes resulted in suboptimal mRNA delivery to the retina, likely due to diffusion barriers imposed by the vitreous and the vitreoretinal interface.

4.

## Discussion
This study assessed the potential of PEGylated mRNA lipoplex formulations for retinal applications. First, using a commercial transfection reagent Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX (LP), we optimized mRNA lipoplex production and demonstrated efficient complexation of LP with mRNA in pure water, achieving a more favorable v/w ratio than in PBS buffer. Our findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating the influence of buffer salts on the efficiency of cationic lipid–nucleic acid complexation (Moghaddam et al. ). PEGylation of mRNA lipoplexes resulted in their enhanced stability in aqueous solutions. Notably, mRNA lipoplexes modified with PEG-DMG and HA-PEG-DPPE exhibited greater stability than those modified with PEG-DSPE and PEG-HA-DPPE. Furthermore, the cellular uptake and transfection efficiency of selected lipoplex formulations were evaluated through both in vitro experiments and pilot in vivo feasibility studies.
As part of the in vitro evaluation, RAW264.7 and ARPE-19 cells – both CD44-positive – were used to model distinct retinal-relevant cell types: phagocytic and epithelial, respectively. This was particularly relevant for testing PEG-lipid conjugates functionalized with hyaluronan, a natural CD44 ligand (Aruffo et al. ). Although these cell lines do not fully replicate native retinal cells, they provided a controlled setting for comparing formulation uptake and transfection efficiency, offering complementary insights into delivery behavior. Interestingly, compared to RAW264.7 cells, ARPE19 cells exhibited higher transfection efficiency when treated with either PEGylated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes or non-PEGylated control. Though the exact mechanism underlying this pattern remains unclear, variations in transfection efficiency between different cell types have been reported, potentially influenced by a cell type specific-intracellular trafficking of internalized lipid-based nanoparticles and varied efficiency of endosomal escape (Paramasivam et al. ). We showed that RAW264.7 and ARPE19 cells internalize HA-functionalized PEGylated lipoplexes primarily via CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis. Pretreatment with free HA reduced transfection efficiency in both cell types, confirming the role of CD44. However, pretreatment with HA did not fully inhibit the uptake of HA-coated lipoplexes, suggesting the involvement of additional CD44-independent mechanism. The results align with previous observations that RAW264.7 and ARPE19 can utilize both CD44-dependent and -independent pathways for the uptake of HA-conjugated nanoparticles (Martens et al. ; Chittasupho et al. ; Song et al. ).
In subsequent pilot in vivo studies, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of PEGylated lipoplex formulations for mRNA delivery to the retina. To this end, eGFP mRNA lipoplexes modified with PEG-DMG and HA-PEG-DPPE were administered to the canine retina via subretinal and intravitreal injections. Examination of retinal sections at 24 hours post-subretinal injection, revealed prominent eGFP mRNA labeling across all retinal cell types within the boundaries of the injection site. In terms of protein expression, eGFP immunostaining was limited to the RPE, suggesting that cell-specific expression is governed by post-delivery mechanisms rather than the tropism of the PEGylated lipoplexes. Our results imply that both PEGylated mRNA lipoplex formulations successfully crossed the OLM and efficiently delivered eGFP mRNA to retinal cells, including photoreceptors, where eGFP accumulation was comparable to RHO transcript levels. Confocal imaging determined that the majority of delivered eGFP mRNA in photoreceptor cells was concentrated in the perinuclear region. Whether the detected eGFP mRNA remains within late endosomes is unclear, as the commercial antibodies used for this analysis were ineffective in canine tissue (see the list in Supplementary Table S3).
HA-functionalized mRNA lipoplexes exhibited no evidence of CD44-dependent distribution patterns, indicating that the targeting ligand was inactive in vivo, contrary to in vitro data. Potentially, the lack of HA-mediated targeting is attributed to the spontaneous adsorption of proteins onto the surface of administered mRNA lipoplexes upon exposure to biological fluids, a phenomenon previously documented for LNPs and other nanoparticles (Salvati et al. ; Francia et al. ; Aliakbarinodehi et al. ). This process leads to the formation of a so-called protein corona (or biomolecular corona), an occurrence that takes place in a complex and not yet fully understood manner. While the protein corona influences cellular uptake, it can also obscure targeting ligands, preventing their interaction with corresponding receptors (Mirshafiee et al. ; Salvati et al. ).
Compared to the findings at 24 hours, analysis of retinal sections at 72 hours post-subretinal injections with LP(PEG-DMG) and LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) mRNA lipoplexes showed marked reduction in eGFP mRNA labeling across the retina, except in the RPE. The remaining eGFP signal exhibited a patchy distribution pattern, likely influenced by the initial uneven diffusion of PEGylated lipoplexes through the retinal layers. Variations in eGFP protein expression patterns between the LP(PEG-DMG) and LP(HA-PEG-DPPE) lipoplex formulations (shown in ) cannot be attributed to their tropism, as evidenced by the widespread distribution of both lipoplex formulations at 24 hours post-subretinal injection ( ). One possibility is that lipoplexes with PEG-DMG vs. HA-PEG-DPPE coatings adsorb different proteins onto their surfaces, influencing the intracellular trafficking pathways or endosomal escape efficiency of internalized mRNA lipoplexes, thereby affecting transfection outcomes. This suggestion is supported by reports on how different LNP formulations affect the composition of the protein corona (Aliakbarinodehi et al. ; Simonsen ), which subsequently alters nanoparticle interactions, including those with endosomal membranes (Aliakbarinodehi et al. ).
Settling on an explanation for the limited retinal accumulation of mRNA following intravitreal administration of PEGylated lipoplexes is challenging. Despite both types of PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes being negatively charged and relatively small (<125 nm), their diffusion through the vitreous and ILM was lower than expected. Nanoparticle entrapment within the vitreous and diffusion throughout the vitreous body may limit their concentration near the ILM. Therefore, further evaluation may require testing higher doses or optimizing timing before assessment. However, it is worth noting that, to date, testing of intravitreally injected mRNA in lipid-based formulations has been limited to mouse models (Devoldere et al. ; Huang and Chau ; Ryals et al. ; Chambers et al. ; Jiang et al. ). Our findings, which fall within the range of outcomes reported in mouse studies, represent the first in vivo data obtained from a large translational animal model.
Non-PEGylated eGFP mRNA lipoplexes were also tested in this study, referencing a previous report on MessengerMAX-based mRNA complexes administered via subretinal and intravitreal injections in mice (Devoldere et al. ). In the canine retina, 72 hours post-administration, eGFP mRNA and protein were detected exclusively in the RPE after subretinal injection, with no detection after intravitreal delivery. This contrasts with eGFP protein detection in mouse photoreceptors and eGFP mRNA in inner retinal layers up to seven days post-subretinal and post-intravitreal injections (Devoldere et al. ). A plausible explanation may lie in compositional and structural differences between canine and mouse retinas and vitreous matrices, as supported by documented interspecies variations (Noulas et al. ; Mains et al. ; Soukup et al. ).
An important question remains regarding the mechanisms underlying the relatively rapid degradation of exogenous mRNA in non-RPE retinal cells, particularly neurons. Several factors, potentially specific to neuronal cells, may contribute. It is generally assumed that internalized lipid-based nanoparticles frequently traffic from early endosomes to late endosomes and, if endosomal escape fails, ultimately to lysosomes for degradation (Chatterjee et al. ). While this pathway is well established in non-neuronal cells, neurons exhibit highly polarized and compartmentalized endosomal trafficking due to their complex morphology (Sann et al. ; Lasiecka and Winckler ). In these cells, a significant fraction of internalized mRNA-carried nanoparticles could be redirected into recycling pathways rather than progressing toward lysosomal degradation. Furthermore, even if endosomal escape occurs efficiently, subsequent steps may influence the fate of delivered mRNA in neurons. For instance, exogenous mRNA could be sequestered into stress granules and processing bodies (P-bodies), affecting its translation, stability, and degradation rate (Lloyd ; Ivanov et al. ; Wang et al. ).
Speculatively, the precise localization of eGFP mRNA in photoreceptors to the region where endogenous RHO mRNA accumulates might be linked to neuronal mechanisms regulating protein expression. One of such mechanisms is through the active local storage of translationally silent mRNAs, which remain sequestered until receiving cellular signals for translation (Xing and Bassell ; Martínez et al. ; Glock et al. ). The localization of mRNAs to the neuronal soma, axons or dendrites involves the assembly of ribonucleoprotein particles, formed through the interaction of trans-acting RNA-binding proteins and cis-regulatory elements within the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of target mRNAs (Meer et al. ; Xing and Bassell ; Glock et al. ). It is worth noting that cis-acting RNA elements also regulate other aspects of endogenous mRNA metabolism, including translation efficiency and degradation rate (Kapur et al. ; Glock et al. ). Therefore, the same principles likely govern the fate of delivered synthetic mRNAs, raising the concern that their regulatory regions may not be optimized for efficient translation in retinal neurons.
If these mechanisms affect the transfection efficacy of synthetic mRNAs in retinal neurons, the outcome may not be solely determined by lipid-based carrier formulations but also by the design of the mRNA 5′ and 3′ UTRs, which are often undisclosed in published studies. Advances in the structural and sequence optimization of synthetic mRNAs have been driven by the development and success of mRNA-LNP vaccines. Modifications to UTRs, originally optimized for mRNA vaccines, are now widely applied in various in vitro and in vivo studies. Examples of 5’UTRs include a region derived from human α-globin mRNA with an optimized Kozak sequence (Kon et al. ) and a short, in vitro-assembled sequence containing a Kozak consensus around the start codon (GenBank accession MZ362875; Zhang et al. ), which also serves as the 5′ UTR in TriLink’s eGFP mRNA. For 3′UTRs common choices include the corresponding regions of β-globin (Kon et al. ) or α-globin mRNA (Verbeke et al. ), which also acts as the 3′UTR in TriLink’s eGFP mRNA. A relevant extension of the present work is therefore to investigate the optimal UTRs design for enhancing the translation efficiency of synthetic mRNA in retinal neurons.

5.

## Conclusions
Overall, this study presents a simple and cost-effective method for producing PEGylated RNA lipoplexes suitable for both in vitro and in vivo studies. Pilot experiments were conducted to assess the delivery of selected PEGylated mRNA lipoplex formulations to the canine retina. Our findings demonstrate that subretinally injected PEGylated mRNA lipoplexes efficiently diffuse through the OLM and retinal layers, with robust cellular uptake. Study data underscores the complexity of post-delivery mechanisms that regulate the fate of internalized mRNA in retinal neurons. Future studies are needed to determine whether endosomal escape of exogenous mRNA is the primary limitation for protein expression in retinal cells beyond the RPE, or if other factors, such as the structural features of synthetic mRNA, also contribute.
