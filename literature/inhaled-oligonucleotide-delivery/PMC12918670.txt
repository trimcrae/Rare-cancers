## Abstract

## Background
The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a critical external regulator along the gut-liver axis, influencing hepatocarcinogenesis and modulating responses to immunotherapy. However, the specific microbial determinants, underlying mechanisms, and potential clinical applications remain incompletely elucidated.

## Methods
Building on the observed association between gut microbiota and anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) immunotherapeutic efficacy in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we leveraged a suite of clinically relevant murine HCC models to comprehensively characterize tumor-associated microbial signatures using 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. By precisely manipulating microbial composition through a non-hepatotoxic antibiotic cocktail 3 (ABX-3), targeted microbial supplementation, human fecal microbiota transplant (FMT), and controlled Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (B.th) repopulation following gut sterilization with ABX-5, we demonstrated a direct causal relationship between microbiota modulation and intrahepatic immune activation. Single-cell RNA sequencing of hepatic non-parenchymal cells, together with functional validation experiments, was performed to elucidate the underlying immune mechanisms.

## Results
Bacteroides-enriched gut microbiota derived from anti-PD-1-responsive patients with HCC significantly suppressed tumor growth in murine HCC models. In parallel, within our murine HCC system, ABX-3 administration, implemented as both a preventive and therapeutic intervention, attenuated tumor initiation and progression by selectively enriching Bacteroides within the gut microbial community. Functionally, ABX-3 enhanced the capacity of tumor antigen-specific T-cell receptor-I T cells to mount robust immune responses, culminating in targeted tumor regression following antigen-specific immunization. Among the Bacteroides species, B.th emerged as a critical mediator that potentiated αPD-1 immunotherapy in HCC by relieving Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2)-dependent suppression in dendritic cells (DCs). Mechanistically, the KLF2–toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) signaling axis in DCs governed the activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, thereby amplifying antitumor immunity within the HCC microenvironment.

## Conclusions
B.th is identified as a key immunomodulatory species that enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by reprogramming DCs through the KLF2/TLR9 signaling pathway. These findings reveal a novel microbiota-informed strategy to improve immunotherapeutic outcomes in HCC.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Gut microbiota plays an important role in the gut-liver axis, influencing liver cancer development and shaping responses to immunotherapies, including anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) treatment.
However, the specific responsible microbial species, the underlying immune mechanisms, and actionable therapeutic strategies remain poorly defined.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study provides direct causal evidence that enriched Bacteroides, especially gut microbial species Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (B.th), enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment.
Using multiple immunocompetent and clinically relevant mouse HCC models, the study demonstrates that microbiota modulation through antibiotic cocktail-3, targeted B.th supplementation, and human fecal microbiota transplantation can prevent and slow tumor progression.
Mechanistically, B.th reprograms dendritic cells (DCs) by relieving Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2)-mediated suppression and activating the toll-like receptor (TLR9) pathway, thereby promoting tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell activation within the HCC tumor microenvironment.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Research: The work identifies B.th as the specific bacterial species that enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy in HCC by reprogramming DCs through the KLF2/TLR9 signaling pathway, highlighting actionable targets for microbiome-based immunotherapy enhancement and opening new avenues for preclinical and mechanistic studies.
Clinical practice: The findings support the development of microbial-based adjuvants, such as targeted bacterial supplementation or microbe-informed patient stratification, to improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in HCC.
Policy: This study reinforces the importance of integrating gut microbiome analysis into immunotherapy trials and may inform future guidelines on microbiome profiling, patient selection, and therapeutic co-interventions.

## Introduction
 Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), is revolutionizing cancer therapeutic modality by enabling unprecedented and durable responses in a significant proportion of patients with cancer. , Strong mid-stage clinical trial data accelerated the approval of anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) antibody (αPD-1 Ab) for second-line treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in 2017; however, a subsequent larger multicenter phase III study failed to demonstrate that αPD-1-Ab improved patient survival compared with controls. Consequently, αPD-1-Ab was withdrawn from the US market in 2021 by pharmaceutical company Bristol Myers Squibb as a single-agent therapy for patients with HCC previously treated with sorafenib. The combination of α-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) Ab and α-vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) Ab was approved for patients with unresectable HCC in May 2020; however, the median progression-free survival was only 6.8 months, compared with 4.3 months for sorafenib treatment. This critical challenge motivates our investigation of extrinsic factors that modulate antitumor immunity to improve therapeutic outcomes.
The microbiota has emerged as a key external regulator along the gut-liver axis, orchestrating both innate and adaptive responses, driving hepatocarcinogenesis, and modulating the efficacy of ICB immunotherapy. , For instance, oral administration of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (B.th) or Bacteroides fragilis to microbiota-depleted mice with established sarcomas restored the responsiveness to α-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 Ab. Sivan et al demonstrated that commensal Bifidobacterium promotes antitumor immunity and enhances αPD-L1 efficacy against melanoma. Growing evidence supports the prognostic and therapeutic potential of targeting gut microbiota to manage cancer. The gut-liver axis, , stemming from anatomical, physiological, and immunological connections between the gastrointestinal tract and the liver, plays a central role in maintaining metabolic and immune homeostasis. , Disruption of gut microbial balance significantly impacts HCC development, progression, and response to immunotherapy. , A pivotal 2016 study demonstrated that intestinal overgrowth of Escherichia coli promotes hepatocarcinogenesis by inducing liver inflammation and damage. In 2019, Zheng et al reported a significant correlation between gut microbiota profiles and improved responses to αPD-1 Ab therapy in human patients with HCC, providing initial evidence of causality. Clinically, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has shown beneficial effects in patients with melanoma by improving the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy, as demonstrated in recent phase I trials. However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms linking specific microbes to enhanced anti-HCC immunity remain incompletely understood.
The establishment of a suite of immunocompetent animal models, including a novel and clinically relevant murine HCC model expressing a trackable tumor-specific antigen, enables us to rigorously address this challenge. This model is established in male wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice using a two-step protocol: induction of liver fibrosis via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4), followed by intrasplenic (ISPL) inoculation of primary oncogenic hepatocytes derived from MTD2 transgenic mice. These MTD2 mice express SV40 large T antigen (TAg), a potent oncoprotein, under the control of the liver-specific, androgen-responsive major urinary protein promoter. Transplanting histologically normal, TAg-expressing hepatocytes from prepubertal male MTD2 mice into fibrotic livers of adult WT mice creates a model that recapitulates the fibrotic microenvironment of human HCC. Importantly, TAg is exclusively expressed in tumor cells and serves as a trackable tumor-specific antigen (TSA), allowing in situ investigation of antitumor-specific immune response. Using this platform, we have developed and tested several immune-based therapeutic strategies, including combinations of αPD-1 Ab or TSA CD8+ T cells with clinically approved agents such as sunitinib or a nanoliposome-loaded C6-ceramide. , This platform offers a powerful tool to study the mechanisms of tumor-specific immunity and to examine how the gut microbiota influences HCC progression and immune modulation, particularly in the context of TSA-driven immune response.
By selectively modulating the gut microbiota using antibiotic cocktails, we identified a non-hepatotoxic antibiotic cocktail 3 (ABX-3), consisting of three antibiotics, which induces a Bacteroides-enriched gut microbiota profile similar to that observed in patients with HCC who exhibit favorable clinical responses to αPD-1 immunotherapy. This antibiotic modulation approach demonstrated that ABX-3 treatment both preventively retards hepatocarcinogenesis and therapeutically decreases HCC progression by activating intrahepatic immune response. Targeted microbiota modulation further identified B.th, one species of Bacteroides, as a key microbial determinant, capable of enhancing αPD-1 therapeutic efficacy. Mechanistic investigations revealed that B.th-driven antitumor immunity is mediated through the restoration of TSA-specific CD8+ T-cell function by educating dendritic cells (DCs) via the Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2)/toll-like receptor (TLR)9 signaling pathway.
In summary, the gut microbiota serves as a critical extrinsic regulator of both hepatocarcinogenesis and anti-HCC immunity. Targeted modulation of the gut microbiota offers a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of ICB immunotherapy by priming intratumoral immune response. Together, these findings highlight the gut microbiota as a modifiable determinant of antitumor immunotherapy response, opening new avenues for precision oncology.

## Results

## Enriched Bacteroides in the gut microbiota of responder patients with HCC is associated with enhanced efficacy of αPD-1 immunotherapy
In 2019, Zheng et al employed metagenomic sequencing of serial fecal samples to track dynamic changes in gut microbial diversity and composition among patients with HCC undergoing αPD-1 immunotherapy. This study revealed a potential link between gut microbiota profiles and therapeutic response. Building on this dataset, we conducted a focused analysis and identified a significantly higher relative abundance of Bacteroides taxa in responders compared with non-responders ( ). To validate the potential role of responder-derived gut microbiota in increasing αPD-1 treatment efficacy, we sterilized the gut microbiota of mice bearing orthotopic HCC tumors induced with diethylnitrosamine (DEN) and CCl4 using a more potent antibiotic cocktail (ABX-5), followed by reconstruction via FMT from either αPD-1 responders or non-responders ( ). ABX-5 treatment successfully depleted commensal gut microbiota ( ). CCl4 treatment induced liver fibrosis and slightly altered gut microbial composition without statistical significance ( ). Mice that received responder-derived FMT followed by αPD-1 treatment exhibited significantly enhanced therapeutic responses, including smaller overall tumor burdens ( ), fewer tumor nodules ( ), and reduced tumor areas ( ), compared with all control groups. Importantly, quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis confirmed an increased abundance of B.th in the feces of the mice receiving responder-derived FMT ( ), along with a corresponding rise in its absolute quantity ( ). Collectively, these findings strengthen the evidence that B.th is associated with improved immunotherapeutic outcomes.
 

## ABX-3 treatment promotes Bacteroides enrichment in gut microbiota, suppresses HCC initiation and progression, and confers therapeutic benefits in established HCC tumors
Next, we evaluated various combinations of antibiotic cocktails (ABXs) to identify a formulation capable of significantly reshaping the gut microbiota toward a Bacteroides-enriched profile, similar to that observed in αPD-1 responder patients. With this objective, we treated our unique and clinically relevant murine HCC model with different ABXs via oral administration in drinking water. ABX3, one of the antibiotic cocktails that robustly suppresses commensal bacteria without causing hepatotoxicity, induced a significant reduction of colony-forming units (CFUs) per gram of fecal material compared with control mice ( ). Operational taxonomic unit relative abundance analysis of 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing depicts the relative abundance of gut microbiota family in ABX-3-treated and control groups ( ). Differences between groups were further characterized using the following analyses, including LEfSe (linear discriminant analysis effect size) analysis, shown as a taxonomic cladogram indicating enriched bacterial taxa ( ); a heatmap illustrating changes in bacterial families ( ); and a volcano plot highlighting significantly altered bacterial families, genera, or species ( ). Notably, these analyses of 16S RNA gene sequencing data revealed a significantly increased relative abundance of genus Bacteroides in fecal samples from HCC-bearing mice treated with ABX-3 compared with control mice ( , ). Consistently, culture-based analysis showed enrichment of gram-negative (G−) species, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and B.th in ABX-3-treated mice ( ). In addition, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) detected increased accumulation of B.th DNA in the tumors of ABX-3-treated mice ( ), suggesting possible bacterial translocation. Together, these findings indicate that ABX-3 significantly suppresses gut microbial populations, alters gut microbiota composition, and promotes the enrichment of specific bacterial taxa.
 Given the well-established role of gut microbiota in carcinogenesis and cancer therapy, we investigated whether ABX-3-modulated gut microbiota influences hepatocarcinogenesis. As illustrated in , administering ABX-3 to WT C57BL/6 mice prior to CCl4 and ISPL inoculation of oncogenic hepatocytes from MTD2 mice, a protocol that successfully induces orthotopic HCC development in fibrotic liver. Longitudinal MRI detected the first appearance of orthotopic HCC tumor nodules in control mice at week 17, whereas tumor development in ABX-3-treated mice was delayed until week 20 ( ). We quantified tumor size in each mouse using ImageJ based on MRI-derived tumor areas. Tumors in ABX-3-treated mice were significantly smaller than those in control mice at the matched time points ( ), suggesting that ABX-3 treatment delays tumor initiation.
We next evaluated whether ABX-3 treatment could therapeutically suppress established HCC tumors. To this end, we treated mice bearing size-matched tumors with ABX-3 and monitored tumor progression using longitudinal MRI imaging ( ). MRI scans revealed significantly larger tumor areas in control mice compared with ABX-3-treated mice ( ). Tumor volume quantification using ImageJ demonstrated markedly smaller tumors in ABX-3-treated mice relative to controls at week 13. The reduction of HCC progression was associated with decreased Ki67 expression ( ) and increased levels of cleaved poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) ( ), indicative of reduced cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis, respectively. These findings indicate that ABX-3 treatment also exerts a therapeutic effect by suppressing the progression of established HCC. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of microbiome-targeted strategies in the initiation, progression, and treatment of HCC.

## ABX-3 empowers tumor antigen-specific TCR-I T cells to trigger a potent immune attack and drive targeted tumor regression following tumor-specific antigen immunization
To investigate whether ABX-3-induced suppression of HCC initiation and progression is associated with enhanced antitumor immunity, we immunized WT C57BL/6 mice with B6/WT-19 cells, a transgenic mouse fibroblast line expressing full-length TAg, following ABX-3 treatment ( ). ABX-3 treatment significantly delayed tumor initiation and was accompanied by clear evidence of intrahepatic immune activation. Specifically, immunized mice receiving ABX-3 exhibited increased expression of CD69, an early activation marker, on liver-resident lymphocytes ( ), along with elevated production of interferon (IFN)-γ ( ) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α ( ) by liver-resident CD8+ T cells compared with vehicle-treated controls. Furthermore, ABX-3 enhanced the priming and activation of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in immunized mice ( ). This was evidenced by upregulated CD69 expression ( ), an increased frequency of TSA tetramer (I+IV)+ CD8+ T cells ( ), and elevated production of IFN-γ ( ) and TNF-α ( ). These data collectively demonstrate that ABX-3 promotes intrahepatic and tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T-cell activation in both naive and HCC-bearing mice, supporting its role in enhancing antitumor immunity during HCC development.
Building on these findings, we next evaluated whether combining ABX-3 with TSA CD8+ T cells could provide therapeutic benefit in HCC. HCC-bearing mice were treated with ABX-3 followed by adoptive cell transfer (ACT) of TSA CD8+ T cells (T-cell receptor (TCR)-I T cells), which are isolated from TCR-transgenic 416 mice and specifically target TAg epitope-I. To further boost tumor antigen-specific responses, mice were immunized with TAg-expressing B6/WT-19 cells ( ). Control mice received the same immunization regimen without ABX-3. Longitudinal MRI revealed the progressive tumor growth in control mice, whereas ABX-3-treated mice exhibited marked tumor regression ( ). Notably, the therapeutic effect of combined ABX-3 and ACT strategy was significantly greater than that observed with ABX-3 monotherapy ( ), suggesting synergistic interaction between ABX-3 and TSA CD8+ T-cell effector function.
 To further elucidate how ABX-3 modulates the anti-HCC immunity, we analyzed immune responses 7 days after TSA immunization. Splenocytes and tumor-infiltrating leukocytes (TILs) were isolated from euthanized mice. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant increase in the frequency of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in ABX-3-treated mice compared with controls ( ), which was further confirmed by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of tumor sections ( ). Strikingly, the frequency of tumor antigen-specific tetramer (I+IV)+ CD8+ T cells within the tumor increased from 2% to 16% following ABX-3 treatment, whereas no such increase was observed in the spleen ( ). Moreover, CD8+ TILs from ABX-3-treated mice exhibited significantly higher CD69 expression ( ), indicating enhanced activation, with only modest changes observed in splenic CD8+ T cells. Ex vivo stimulation of TILs with TAg epitope-I and epitope-IV peptides led to robust production of IFN-γ ( ) and TNF-α ( ) in CD8+ T cells from the ABX-3-treated mice, but not in those from control mice. Collectively, these data provide strong evidence that ABX-3 enhances antitumor immunity by promoting the activation and effector function of intrahepatic tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in HCC.

## B.th as a key Bacteroides species potentiates αPD-1 immunotherapy in a murine HCC model
Given the enrichment of Bacteroides in the gut microbiota from mice treated with ABX-3 and in human patients with HCC responding to αPD-1 therapy, both of which were associated with significant activation of TCR-I T cells and enhanced αPD-1 efficacy in HCC, we explored a microbial-based immunotherapeutic strategy for HCC treatment. To this end, we selected B.th, a representative species of Bacteroides genus in human gut microbiota, as a microbial mediator. We first evaluated its therapeutic potential as a monotherapy by gut supplementation. Longitudinal MRI imaging was used to monitor tumor progression ( ). B.th treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, which was evident by day 84 post-repopulation ( ). This suppression correlated with an increased abundance of B.th DNA in tumors and fecal samples, as well as an increased absolute amount of B.th in fecal samples, without a concurrent rise in hepatic endotoxin levels ( ). We also observed that B.th supplementation delayed HCC initiation ( ) and prolonged mice survival ( ). These findings demonstrate the potential of B.th to independently suppress HCC progression and hepatocarcinogenesis.
 We next assessed whether B.th could enhance the efficacy of αPD-1 immunotherapy in our clinically relevant murine model. Mice were preconditioned with ABX-5, a broad-spectrum antibiotic-antifungal cocktail. 2 weeks after inoculation with oncogenic hepatocytes, mice received treatment with αPD-1 Ab ( ). Longitudinal MRI imaging showed that B.th repopulation alone slowed tumor progression, which was associated with an increased abundance of B.th in HCC-bearing mice ( ). This effect was further amplified by the addition of αPD-1 therapy ( ). Quantitative analysis of MRI-derived areas using ImageJ confirmed the synergistic antitumor effect of combined B.th and αPD-1 treatment ( ). Quantification of B.th colonization in mice across cages did not show variability, similar to the drinking water ( ). However, individual water consumption was not quantitatively recorded for each mouse, which may have contributed to inter-animal differences in B.th engraftment. Together, these results highlight B.th as a promising microbial adjuvant capable of potentiating immune checkpoint blockade in HCC, supporting the development of microbiota-based immunotherapeutic strategies.

## KLF2 suppression in plasmacytoid dendritic cells mediates the synergistic antitumor activity of combined B.th and αPD-1 therapy
To investigate the mechanism by which B.th repopulation enhances αPD-1-mediated tumor suppression, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on tumor-infiltrating cells isolated from four groups of mice: untreated controls, B.th repopulation alone, αPD-1 monotherapy, and combined B.th plus αPD-1 treatment ( ). We analyzed a total of 20,350 cells: 4,031 from an untreated mouse, 4,975 from a B.th-treated mouse, 6,010 from an αPD-1-treated mouse, and 5,334 from the combination therapy group. These datasets were integrated for comparative analysis. Using canonical molecular markers, we identified distinct cell populations, including plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), B cells, natural killer cells, and hepatic stellate cells ( ). Differential gene expression (DEG) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that combination treatment with B.th and αPD-1 upregulated immune activation-associated gene sets and downregulated innate immune suppression-associated gene sets specifically in pDCs ( ). Among these DEGs, we observed reduced expression of GILZ (glucocorticoid-induced leucine zipper), a known tolerogenic marker, and increased expression of CD86, an immunogenic co-stimulatory molecule, in pDCs from the combination group ( ). These findings suggest a shift toward a more immunostimulatory phenotype in pDCs under combined treatment.
 Interestingly, KLF2, which encodes KLF2, a zinc finger transcription factor involved in vascular development, endothelial homeostasis, and immune quiescence, was significantly downregulated in tumor-infiltrating pDCs following combination therapy ( ). Although GSEA did not classify KLF2 within known immune activation pathways, its expression pattern in our scRNA-seq data implicates it in the immunomodulatory response to treatment. While KLF2 is known to regulate proinflammatory activation in myeloid cells and maintain quiescence in T cells and monocytes, its role in liver cancer immunity has not been previously defined. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that B.th repopulation synergizes with αPD-1 to suppress KLF2 expression in pDCs, thereby reversing tumor-induced immune tolerance and enhancing antitumor immunity. To test this hypothesis, tumor-size-matched HCC-bearing mice were divided into three groups: Group 1—combination treatment with B.th repopulation and αPD-1; Group 2—ACT of naive bone marrow-derived DCs transfected with a control plasmid followed by combination treatment; and Group 3—ACT of bone marrow-derived DCs expressing KLF2 followed by combination treatment. After 1 month of therapy, MRI imaging revealed that ACT with KLF2-overexpressing DCs (Group 3) abolished the tumor suppression observed in the combination therapy Group 2 ( ). These findings demonstrate that downregulation of KLF2 in DCs is essential for the antitumor effects mediated by B.th and αPD-1 therapy.

## Antibiotic-induced microbiota modulation and B.th recolonization promote tumor-resident DC activation via KLF2 suppression and TLR9 induction
Given that pDCs are a major population of liver-resident DCs and tumor-infiltrating DCs in our murine HCC model, as reported previously by others, we isolated total tumor-resident DCs to subsequently evaluate the effects of ABX-3 and B.th on DC activation. To determine whether DCs serve as cellular mediators of immune activation induced by ABX-3 treatment or B.th recolonization, tumor-resident DCs were isolated from HCC-bearing mice with or without ABX-3 treatment for transcriptional analysis ( ). qPCR analysis revealed a robust upregulation of immune-stimulatory genes, including those encoding TLR9, interferon regulatory factor (IRF)-7, and IFN-γ ( ). Concurrently, expression of KLF2, a negative regulator of DC activation, was significantly suppressed, alongside altered gene expression of pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive cytokines of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-10, TNF-α, and chemokines C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) ( ). These transcriptional changes indicate that ABX-3 treatment enriches microbial populations, such as B.th, that may promote a more immunogenic DC phenotype within the tumor microenvironment.
 To directly assess whether B.th drives this immunomodulatory effect, we used an antibiotic cocktail-treated mouse model. HCC-bearing mice were first treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics (ABX-5) to deplete endogenous microbiota, followed by targeted recolonization with either B.th or Enterococcus faecalis (E.fae), the latter serving as a control ( ). Strikingly, only B.th recolonization recapitulated the immunostimulatory gene expression pattern observed in ABX-3-treated mice. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed a pronounced increase in TLR9 expression in DCs specifically from the B.th-repopulated group ( ). Moreover, TLR9 deficiency impaired ABX-3-mediated HCC suppression ( ).
Collectively, these results identify B.th as a pivotal microbial regulator potentially mediating the DC-activating effects of ABX-3. Mechanistically, B.th appears to suppress KLF2 while inducing TLR9 expression, thereby reprogramming tumor-resident DCs toward a more activated, immunostimulatory phenotype. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how microbiota shapes antitumor immunity.

## KLF2 and TLR9 mediate DC-induced activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells
To explore the regulatory relationship between KLF2 and TLR9 signaling in DCs, we first performed KLF2 knockdown in bone marrow-derived DCs using KLF2-specific siRNA. After 48 hours, messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels of both KLF2 and TLR9 were assessed by quantitative real-time PCR. Knockdown of KLF2 resulted in a significant upregulation of TLR9 expression ( ), suggesting that KLF2 may act as a negative transcriptional regulator of TLR9. This observation is consistent with findings previously reported by Barton et al. To assess the functional consequences of KLF2 and TLR9 modulation on antigen-specific T-cell activation, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments. DCs were derived from WT C57BL/6 mice and transfected with KLF2 siRNA, TLR9 siRNA, or both, followed by pulsing with TAg epitope-I peptides. These antigen-loaded DCs were then co-cultured with CD8+ (TCR-I) T cells isolated from TCR-I transgenic 416 mice, in the presence or absence of CpG ODN (oligodeoxynucleotides containing unmethylated cytosine-guanine motifs) stimulation for 5 hours. Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular cytokine staining revealed that KLF2 knockdown in DCs enhanced CpG-induced TNF-α and IFN-γ production by TCR-I T cells ( ). Notably, this enhancement was attenuated when TLR9 was simultaneously knocked down, indicating that TLR9 is a key downstream effector in the KLF2 regulatory pathway. Similarly, modulation of KLF2 and TLR9 signaling pathways using the same strategy impacted tumor-resident DC-mediated activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell activation ( ). Taken together, these findings suggest that KLF2 acts as a negative regulator of tumor-resident DCs by suppressing TLR9 expression, thereby dampening antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell activation and potentially limiting antitumor immunity.
 

## Discussion
This study provides compelling evidence that targeted modulation of the gut microbiota significantly influences HCC initiation, progression, and responsiveness to immunotherapy by reshaping the hepatic immune microenvironment. Among the microbial constituents, B.th emerged as a critical species that enhances the therapeutic efficacy of αPD-1 immunotherapy, promoting robust tumor regression. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that B.th-activated antitumor immunity is orchestrated via DC activation through the KLF2/TLR9 signaling axis, unveiling a novel crosstalk between the microbiome and hepatic immunity. These findings highlight specific microbial species and associated molecular pathways as actionable targets for microbiome-informed immunotherapeutic strategies in HCC.
Our findings position microbial-targeted immunotherapy as a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC. In this study, administration of a non-hepatotoxic ABX-3 effectively reshaped the gut microbial ecosystem in HCC-bearing mice, significantly reducing overall microbial diversity and selectively enriching B.th ( ). This targeted modulation of the microbiota in WT mice subjected to tumor induction was associated with a marked delay in HCC initiation ( ) and robust inhibition of tumor progression in established HCC ( ), supporting the therapeutic relevance of gut-liver axis manipulation. It is important to note that microbiome profiling was performed only in HCC-bearing mice. We did not analyze the gut microbiota of normal (non-HCC-bearing) mice followed by ABX-3 administration; therefore, we cannot determine whether ABX-3 alone alters the baseline gut microbiota or preferentially expands Bacteroides spp in normal mice, as observed in the HCC context. Additionally, we did not assess longitudinal recovery kinetics of the microbiome in tumor-bearing mice following treatment.
Notably, monotherapy with B.th supplementation yielded only modest tumor-suppressive effects at late disease stages ( ), suggesting that microbial modulation alone may be insufficient for late-stage therapeutic intervention. However, combining ABX-3 preconditioning with ACT of TSA CD8+ T cells resulted in significant tumor regression ( ), highlighting the synergy between microbial restructuring and T cell-based immunotherapy. Moreover, a more extensive gut sterilization regimen ABX-5, followed by FMT from B.th repopulation in conjunction with αPD-1 treatment ( ), produced a potent antitumor effect. These results emphasize the importance of microbiota composition in modulating host response to checkpoint inhibitors and suggest that gut-targeted interventions could be leveraged to sensitize HCC tumors to immunotherapy. These findings have direct clinical implications. Recent clinical studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota composition correlates with immunotherapy responsiveness in patients with various cancers, , including HCC. Our data support this clinical association and expand on it by mechanistically demonstrating how selective microbiota modulation can enhance immune-mediated tumor control. This is consistent with preclinical findings by Ma et al, who showed that ABX-3 suppresses spontaneous HCC in MYC transgenic mice and inhibits hepatic metastasis from B16 melanoma, but not subcutaneous xenografted tumors. Similarly, Dapito et al demonstrated that a germ-free condition or late-stage gut sterilization reduces HCC incidence by up to 80%, further validating the gut microbiota’s pivotal role in HCC pathogenesis. Collectively, our studies contribute to a growing body of evidence identifying the gut microbiota as a central regulator of tumor progression and immune responsiveness. Importantly, we demonstrate that precise microbial interventions, whether through antibiotics, probiotics, or FMT, can reprogram the tumor microenvironment and significantly improve therapeutic efficacy in HCC. These findings warrant further investigation in clinical trials and support the development of microbiota-informed strategies to optimize immunotherapy outcomes in patients with liver cancer.
A critical mechanistic insight from our study is the role of intrahepatic DCs in orchestrating microbiota-driven anti-HCC immune responses via the KLF2/TLR9 signaling axis. Treatment with the non-hepatotoxic ABX-3 potentiated TSA immunization by promoting functional activation of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, as indicated by elevated expression of activation marker (CD69) and increased production of cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ ( ). These findings align with previous reports by Ma et al, Dapito et al, and Jia, which demonstrated that gut microbiota reprogramming can elicit liver-specific antitumor immunity. To dissect the cellular mediators underlying this response, we employed scRNA-seq and identified DCs as a pivotal immune regulator mediating the therapeutic synergy of combined αPD-1 checkpoint blockade and B.th supplementation ( ). Mechanistically, DC activation was closely associated with the downregulation of the transcription factor KLF2 ( ). The functional importance of this pathway was confirmed by DC-specific overexpression of KLF2, which abrogated the antitumor efficacy of αPD-1/B.th combined therapy ( ). Moreover, enrichment of B.th following ABX-5 treatment led to upregulation of TLR9 in DCs ( ), implicating the KLF2/TLR9 axis as a critical mediator of DC activation. This axis likely facilitates the subsequent priming and expansion of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, thereby enhancing anti-HCC immunity. Furthermore, ABX-3 treatment can increase IFN-γ expression in tumor-resident DCs in association with KLF2/TLR9 modulation ( ). We measured the secretion of type I and II IFNs in cultured tumor-resident DCs isolated from HCC-bearing mice, stimulated in the presence or absence of CpG ODN (a TLR9 agonist). Without stimulation, tumor-resident DCs ( ) secreted higher levels of IFN-β than IFN-α and IFN-γ. On CpG ODN stimulation, secretion of all IFNs increased significantly, with the highest level observed for IFN-γ ( ). IFN-γ production by DCs has been previously reported and may contribute to their role in linking innate and adaptive immunity. These findings are supported by data from WT DCs ( ) and tumor-resident DCs ( ). Collectively, our data reveal a novel mechanistic framework whereby microbiota modulation triggers DC-mediated immune reprogramming via KLF2 suppression and TLR9 induction, providing a promising therapeutic target for improving immunotherapeutic outcomes in HCC.
Given that B.th supplementation may induce the expansion of other components of the microbiome, we will investigate how shifts in additional bacterial taxa during B.th supplementation may synergize with B.th to enhance responsiveness to αPD-1 therapy in HCC. Beyond B.th, ABX-3 treatment induced broader microbial shifts in HCC-bearing mice, including enrichment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and depletion of Staphylococcus xylosus, E.fae, and Lactobacillus johnsonii. Given the significant role of B.th in modulating antitumor immunity, future studies will assess the individual contributions and therapeutic potential of these species in HCC. Recent studies have raised skepticism regarding the presence of microbes within tumors. In our experiment, B.th was not detected in the livers of mice with CCl4-induced fibrosis, nor in normal livers of healthy mice, regardless of ABX-3 treatment, indicating that B.th translocation is likely restricted to tumor-bearing livers. We recognize that FISH alone is insufficient to definitively demonstrate intratumoral bacterial translocation. Future studies will incorporate orthogonal approaches, including qPCR validation, culture-independent microbial profiling, and higher-resolution imaging, to substantiate the presence and spatial distribution of B.th within tumors. Indeed, qPCR detected increased B.th DNA abundance after B.th supplementation in HCC-bearing mice ( ). FISH analysis ( ) revealed that most Bacteroides-specific signals overlapped with small nuclei-associated regions, suggesting potential uptake by tumor-resident DCs and macrophages. This interpretation aligns with recent findings showing that tumor-associated DCs and macrophages can internalize bacterial components. , Nevertheless, additional validation is required to definitively identify the phagocytic cell types involved. Notably, a study using a bacterial strain Hominenteromicrobium YB328, isolated from fecal samples of patients who responded to αPD-1 therapy, demonstrated that YB328 activated tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell responses in murine melanoma (B16F10) and colon adenocarcinoma (MC38) models by accelerating the differentiation of CD103+CD11b− conventional DCs via stimulation of TLR signaling pathway. The same study also showed that bone marrow-derived DCs can engulf YB328 and activate IRF and NF-κB signaling pathways, while bacterial products were detected in tumor-resident macrophages. Our FISH observations ( ) are consistent with these findings, though further studies are needed to confirm the phagocytic uptake of B.th by DCs and macrophages in HCC. Additionally, the interaction between diet-derived microbial metabolites (eg, short-chain fatty acids, nucleic acids, and proteins) and host immunity represents an exciting avenue for further exploration, with potential implications for enhancing immunotherapy outcomes. Importantly, while our data revealed a regulatory link between KLF2 and TLR9 in DCs, the absence of a direct KLF2-binding motif on TLR9 promoter suggests the involvement of intermediary factors that remain to be characterized.
In summary, our studies establish a direct mechanistic link between gut microbiota modulation and hepatic immune activation in HCC via the gut-liver axis. We identified B.th as a beneficial microbial species capable of enhancing the efficacy of αPD-1 immunotherapy through DC-mediated immune reprogramming via the KLF2/TLR9 pathway. These findings provide a conceptual and mechanistic framework for the development of microbiome-informed immunotherapeutic strategies in HCC and lay the foundation for future translational applications. Further studies will be performed to evaluate how other recovering bacterial genera during B.th supplementation or repopulation can synergistically function together with B.th to enhance αPD-1 immunotherapy or other immune checkpoint inhibitors in HCC.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
Line MTD2 and Line 416  mice served as the source of tumorigenic hepatocytes and tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells (TCR-I T cells), respectively. Both lines were maintained by our laboratory. Line C57BL/6J-Tlr9M7Btlr mice, TLR9-mutant mice, were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) to study the role of TLR9 in the immune system. Male C57BL/6 mice from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) were used as recipient mice for the HCC tumor model. The number of mice per group ranged from 4 to 10 according to statistical power analysis and biological variation.

## Cell line and bacterial strain
B6/WT-19 cells were maintained in our laboratory. This is a transformed C57BL/6 mouse embryo fibroblast line expressing full-length SV40 large TAg.  Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cellgro, Manassas, Virginia, USA) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. B.th (No. 29148) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA) and cultured on tryptic soy agar plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 37°C under anaerobic conditions.

## A unique mouse model of HCC induced by CCl4-caused liver injury and subsequent inoculation of oncogenic hepatocytes
The detailed information on the preparation of our unique murine model of HCC was presented in our previous studies. Briefly, to model tumor growth in a fibrotic liver, 10% CCl4 solution (v/v) in corn oil (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was i.p. injected into 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice two times per week for 6 weeks at a dose of 8 mL/kg of body weight to induce liver fibrosis. 2 weeks after the final injection, mice received ISPL inoculation of histologically normal hepatocytes isolated from TAg-transformed young male MTD2 mice at a dose of 5×105 cells per mouse. At the onset of puberty, androgen production in the recipient mice initiated oncogenic TAg expression under the control of a liver-specific promoter. As a result, the transferred hepatocytes became cancer cells and formed tumors in the setting of liver fibrosis. Small tumors could be detected by MRI about 2 months after hepatocyte inoculation.

## A murine model of HCC induced by diethylnitrosamine and CCl4
14-day-old male C57BL/6 WT mouse received a single i.p. injection of DEN at a dose of 25 mg/kg body weight, followed by an i.p. injection of 10% CCl4 two times per week for 5 weeks at a dose of 8 mL/kg of body weight. DEN (N-nitrosodiethylamine, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) was prepared in sterile Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (DPBS, without calcium and magnesium, Gibco, Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA) at 950 mg/mL as a stock solution, then diluted to 9.5 mg/mL as a working solution. 10% CCl4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was prepared in corn oil (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) as described above.

## Human fecal microbiota transplantation
Fecal microbiota were isolated from stool samples collected from eight patients with HCC who were recruited at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University in China, following the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT02989922). All patients were histologically confirmed to have Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer Stage C disease and had experienced disease progression following first-line sorafenib treatment. Subsequently, patients received camrelizumab, a humanized monoclonal αPD-1 Abs (SHR-1210, HengRui Medicine, Jiangsu, China), administered intravenously at a dose of 3 mg/kg every 3 weeks until disease progression or treatment intolerance. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University. Written informed consent, including consent for the collection and analysis of microbiome samples, was obtained from each patient prior to study initiation.
To isolate fecal microbiota, fecal samples from each patient were collected in sterile containers, suspended in 10% glycerol, and stored in liquid nitrogen. The stocked samples were diluted in sterile DPBS at a ratio of 1:20 (w/vol), homogenized using a sterile commercial blender, and filtered through a 250 µm sieve to remove large particles. Bacterial concentration was measured with optical density at 600 nm (OD600), with an OD600 of 1.0 corresponding to approximately 5×108 CFU/mL.
To perform FMT, each mouse received a daily intragastric gavage of 1×109 of bacteria suspension in 200 µL of bacterial solution for three consecutive days.

## Ex vivo stimulation of liver/tumor-resident non-parenchymal cells with peptides
Freshly isolated splenocytes or liver/tumor-resident non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) were cultured in complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, in the presence or absence of the indicated epitope peptides at a dose of 2 µM for 5 hours. Brefeldin A (BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA) was added to inhibit cytokine secretion.

## Adoptive cell transfer of TCR-I T cells and immunization with TAg-expressing B6/WT-19 cells
For ACT, TCR-I cells were isolated from the spleens of line 416 mice and enriched with CD8α magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, San Diego, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 1×106 cells were suspended in 0.2 mL of Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and injected into mice via the tail vein.
For immunization, B6/WT-19 cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning, New York, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS (Atlanta Biologicals, Georgia, USA) to 80–90% confluence, harvested by trypsin-EDTA digestion, and suspended in DPBS. Cells were then i.p. injected into mice at a dose of 3×107 cells per mouse. 

## Co-culture of CD8+ T cells or DCs with CpG ODN stimulation
Isolation of liver or tumor-resident NPCs and splenocytes was performed as previously described. Purification of CD8+ T cells and DCs from mixed cells was performed with the indicated magnetic cell isolation kits for CD8a+ T cells and DCs (Miltenyi Biotec, San Diego, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
Purified CD8+ T cells and DCs were co-cultured at a ratio of 5:1 at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 hours, followed by stimulation with TLR9 agonist CpG ODNs (InvivoGen, San Diego, California, USA). αCD3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was added as a co-stimulator at a low dose of 0.1 µg/mL.

## ABX-3 and αPD-1 Ab treatment
ABX-3 is an antibiotic cocktail consisting of vancomycin (Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, Missouri, USA), neomycin (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), and imipenem (AK Scientific, Union City, California, USA), each at a concentration of 0.5 g/L in sterile water. For ABX-3 treatment, mice received administration of ABX-3 in their drinking water, which was replaced every other day until the endpoint of the experiment. For αPD-1 Ab treatment, anti-mouse PD-1 monoclonal antibody (Clone J43, Bio X Cell, Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA) was administered via i.p. injection at a dose of 200 µg per mouse every 3 days for five times, as described in our previous studies. 

## Isolation, identification, supplementation, and repopulation of gut microbiota or B.th following ABX-5 treatment
Fecal samples from each mouse were collected, weighed, and suspended in buffered peptone water (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL. 10 µL of fecal slurry was inoculated and cultured aerobically on MacConkey, tryptic soy blood agar plates (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), or anaerobically on blood agar plates at 37°C. After 24 or 72 hours, bacterial strains were identified using a matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry. For supplementation of gut microbiota or B.th, each mouse received oral gavage of 200 µL bacterial suspension two times per week at a concentration of 1.5×109 CFU/mL. For repopulation of gut microbiota or B.th following ABX-5 treatment, mice were first depleted of endogenous microbiota by receiving administration of an ABX-5 in drinking water for 2 weeks, followed by reconstitution of human gut microbiota or B.th. ABX-5 consists of ampicillin (100 mg/kg, Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, Missouri, USA), vancomycin (50 mg/kg, Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, Missouri, USA), metronidazole (100 mg/kg, Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, Missouri, USA), neomycin (100 mg/kg, Gold Biotechnology, St Louis, Missouri, USA), and the antifungal amphotericin B (1 mg/kg, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA).

## Isolation of liver-resident or tumor-resident NPCs
To isolate liver-resident or tumor-resident NPCs, liver perfusion was first performed in mice under anesthesia via the portal vein with 0.05% collagenase (Gibco, Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA) in Ca2+ and Mg2+-free DPBS at a pump speed of 4 mL/min. The harvested tumor or live tissues were cut into small pieces and incubated in 0.04% collagenase in Gey′s Balanced Salt Solution (GBSS, Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) at room temperature with continuous shaking at a speed of 240 rpm. After 20 min, the suspended samples were filtered through a 250 µm mesh and centrifuged at 350 g for 10 min at room temperature to collect cell pellets. The cell pellets were suspended and washed with GBSS (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). After centrifugation, the harvested cells were suspended in 15 mL of GBSS and mixed with 18.45 mL of 30% Nycodenz solution (Accurate Chemical & Scientific, Westbury, New York, USA). The cell suspension underwent gradient centrifugation at 1,400 g for 20 min at room temperature without a brake. The enriched NPCs in the top layer were harvested, washed with DPBS, and suspended in the culture medium or flow cytometry buffer for the following experiments.

## Flow cytometry
Ex vivo staining of lymphocytes with major histocompatibility complex tetramers and fluorochrome-labeled antibodies was performed on single-cell suspensions as described. The gating strategy is shown in . Stained cells were analyzed with a FACScan Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10.7.1, Tree Star, Ashland, Oregon, USA).

## 16S ribosomal RNA gene library preparation and sequencing
Stool samples from the distal rectum were aseptically collected from mice and were immediately frozen at −80°C. DNAs were isolated using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO Labs, Carlsbad, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 16S rRNA gene library preparation, sequencing, and informatics analysis were conducted at the DNA Core Facility and Metagenomics Center at the University of Missouri. 
 
Briefly, bacterial 16S rRNA amplicons were constructed via amplification of the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene with universal primers (U515F/806R) previously developed against the V4 region and flanked by Illumina standard adapter sequences. Oligonucleotide sequences are available at proBase. Dual-indexed forward and reverse primers were used in all reactions. PCR was performed in 50 µL reactions containing 100 ng metagenomic DNA, primers (0.2 µM each), dNTPs (200 µM each), and Phusion high-fidelity DNA polymerase (1U, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Amplification parameters were 98°C (3 min) + (98°C (15 s) + 50°C (30 s) + 72°C (30 s)) × 25 cycles + 72°C (7 min). Amplicon pools (5 µL/reaction) were combined, thoroughly mixed, and purified by the addition of Axygen Axyprep MagPCR clean-up beads to an equal volume of 50 µL of amplicons, followed by incubation for 15 min at room temperature. Products were then washed multiple times with 80% ethanol, and the dried pellet was resuspended in 32.5 µL EB buffer (Qiagen), incubated for 2 min at room temperature, and placed on the magnetic stand for 5 min. The final amplicon pool was evaluated using the Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer automated electrophoresis system, quantified using quant-iT HS dsDNA reagent kits, and diluted according to Illumina’s standard protocol for sequencing on the MiSeq instrument.
DNA sequences were assembled and annotated at the MU Informatics Research Core Facility. Primers were designed to match the 5’ ends of the forward and reverse reads. Cutadapt (V.2.6; https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt) was used to remove the primer from the 5’ end of the forward read. If found, the reverse complement of the primer to the reverse read was then removed from the forward read as were all bases downstream. Thus, a forward read could be trimmed at both ends if the insert was shorter than the amplicon length. The same approach was used on the reverse read, but with the primers in the opposite roles. Read pairs were rejected if either read did not match a 5’ primer, and an error rate of 0.1 was allowed. Two passes were made over each read to ensure the removal of the second primer, and a minimal overlap of 3 bp with the 3’ end of the primer sequence was required for removal.
The QIIME2 DADA2 plugin (V.1.10.0) was used to denoise, de-replicate, and count ASVs (amplicon sequence variants), incorporating the following parameters: (1) forward and reverse reads were truncated to 150 bases; (2) forward and reverse reads with number of expected errors higher than 2.0 were discarded; and (3) chimeras were detected using the “consensus” method and removed. R V.3.5.1 and Biom V.2.1.7 were used in QIIME2. Taxonomies were assigned to final sequences using the Silva.v132 database with the classify-sklearn procedure.

## Endotoxin measurement
Detection of endotoxin in liver/tumor samples and DNA solutions was performed with Pierce chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## MRI
MRI for tumor surveillance was conducted in a small animal imaging center at Harry S Truman Memorial Veteran’s Hospital. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation, and the vital signs were monitored throughout imaging. All MRI scans for tumor surveillance were obtained on a 7.0 Tesla system (Bruker Biospin, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) with an in-plane resolution of 0.1 mm and a slice thickness of 1 mm.

## Total RNA extraction and real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted with RNeasy@ Micro kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription of total RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) was conducted with High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster, California, USA). qPCR was performed with QuantStudio 3 Detection System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing SYBR Green I (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Reactions were run in triplicate. Expression of different genes was normalized to the geometric mean of housekeeping gene 18S RNA for controlling the variability of expression levels. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. All primers were synthesized by IDT (Skokie, Illinois) and their sequences were listed in .

## Immunohistochemical staining
Formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) blocks were used to make 4 µm tissue sections. For IHC, tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene for 15 min (two times), rehydrated with various grades of ethanol (100%, 95%, 80% and 70%) for 10 min each. Antigen retrieval was performed using antigen unmasking solution (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, California, USA). Sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in DPBS for 20 min, washed two times with DPBS for 10 min each, and blocked with 2.5% normal horse serum (ImmPRESS HRP Horse Anti-Rabbit IgG Polymer Detection Kit, Peroxidase, Vector Laboratories) for 1 hour. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using BLOXALL reagent (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, California, USA). Sections were then incubated sequentially with primary antibodies (anti-Ki-67 antibody, anti-cleaved PARP antibody, and anti-CD8 antibody, ( ) at optimized concentration at 4°C overnight, and secondary antibody (HRP horse anti-rabbit IgG reagent, Vector Laboratories) at room temperature for 1 hour. Color development was achieved using ImmPACT DAB substrate (Vector Laboratories) within 3 min. Tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Hematoxylin QS counterstain, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, California, USA) for less than 1 min, followed by dehydration with ethanol and xylene for mounting. The number of positive cells was counted in five randomly selected fields per slide with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization
A Bacteroides-specific identification probe BAC1080 (GCACTTAAGCCGACACCT), targeting B.th genomic DNA, was labeled with the fluorophore Cy5 (Creative Bioarray, Shirley, New York, USA). Detection of B.th genomic DNA in livers or tumors was performed using FISH according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol solutions (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%) followed by deionized water, FFPE tissue sections were immersed in pretreatment solution (FFPE FISH Pretreatment Kit, Creative Bioarray) at 95–100°C for 30 min, rinsed in deionized water, and washed in 2×saline-sodium citrate buffer for 5 min each. The sections then underwent protease digestion (pepsin solution) at 37°C for 10 min. After gradient immersion in deionized water and graded ethanol (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%) for 2 min each, approximately 30 µL of hybridization solution containing probe (0.25 µM) was applied to each slide, which was then covered with a coverslip. Then, the slides were co-denatured at 65–70°C for 5 min on a heat block, gradually cooled to 37°C, and hybridized at 37°C overnight in a humidity chamber in the absence of light. Finally, slides were washed, counterstained with DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole), and mounted. Fluorescence signals were detected using a Zeiss Axiovert 200M fluorescence microscope (White Plains, New York, USA).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis including Student’s t-test was performed with GraphPad Prism V.8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA). A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The survival curve was analyzed using the log-rank test. All assays except the 16S rRNA gene sequencing and scRNA-seq analyses were repeated at least once to confirm reproducibility.
