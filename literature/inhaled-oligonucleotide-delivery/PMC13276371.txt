## Abstract
In this study, we evaluated the in vivo activity of the E1 element within the insulin promoter region. While we successfully obtained mice carrying a deletion exclusively in the E1 element of Ins2 (2E mice), we were unable to obtain mice with a deletion limited to the E1 element of Ins1. To assess the role of the E1 element in Ins1, we compared the mice carrying deletions in the GG2-GG1/A2-C1 elements with or without an additional E1 element deletion (1GC/1GCE mice). 1GCE2E mice developed diabetes. In contrast, 1GCE23 mice (without deletion in the E1 element of Ins2) did not develop diabetes, suggesting that the E1 element of Ins2 is critical for transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, 1GC2E mice (presence of the E1 element of Ins1) developed diabetes. However, the severity of the disease was milder in 1GC2E mice than in 1GCE2E mice, indicating that the E1 element of Ins1 also contributes to transcriptional regulation. These data suggest that the E1 elements in both Ins1/2 promoters are regulated for the in vivo transcription of insulin genes in mice.
Subject terms: Gene regulation, CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing

The E1 elements in both Ins1/2 promoters are regulated for the in vivo transcription of insulin genes in mice.

## Introduction
The promoter of the insulin gene (Ins), which consists of DNA sequences located immediately upstream of the transcription start site – , contains three highly conserved enhancer elements; A3 (nt. –225 to –220 in the human insulin promoter) – , GG2–GG1/A2–C1 (nt. –149 to –116) , and E1 (nt. –111 to –102) , . In the insulin promoter, the E element (corresponding to the consensus sequence CANNTG) serves as a binding site for heterodimers that include the bHLH (basic helix-loop-helix) factor NeuroD – . The E1 element is found in the insulin promoters of mice, rats, and humans and is notably similar across species . The E1 elements of insulin (Ins1) genes are as follows , – : Mouse (nt. –106 to –97): 5’-GCCATCTGCC-3’, Rat (nt. –112 to –103): 5’-GCCATCTGCC-3’, and Human (nt. –111 to –102): 5’-GCCATCTGCC-3’. Experimental studies have demonstrated that a substantial protein complex is indeed bound to the E1 element in the insulin promoter , . Within this complex, NeuroD or ngn3 binds to the insulin E1 sequence in combination with the E2A gene products E47, E12, and E2/5, as well as with products of the HEB gene . Although these ubiquitously expressed cofactors are not essential for the interaction between NeuroD and the E1 element , , this protein machinery—which influences RNA polymerase II activity—appears to be conserved across several biochemical regulatory processes , . In vitro studies have shown that mutations or deletions in the E1 element lead to a marked reduction in insulin promoter activity, clearly indicating the critical role of this sequence in gene regulation. However, whether the E1 sequence within the promoters of insulin genes is important for the transcription of Ins1/2 in vivo remains unclear. This is because the insulin promoter contains multiple NeuroD binding sites, and NeuroD is also involved in pancreatic development.
In this study, we used CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat-Cas9) to generate mice carrying partial deletions in the Ins1/2 promoters and evaluated the in vivo activity of the E1 elements within the insulin promoter region.

## Results

## Mice with deletions in E1 elements
We previously analyzed mice with deletions in the C1 elements or GG2–GG1/A2 elements of both Ins1/2 genes using CRISPR-Cas9 technology , . In the present study, to analyze the E1 element, we used the pX330 vectors described in a previous study to comicroinject two guide RNAs (gRNAs) (Fig.  ) and Cas9 (pX330-1st/2nd gRNA vectors) into mouse zygotes – . Among the resulting neonates, multiple deletions were detected within the GG2–GG1/A2–C1–E1 element region of the Ins1/2 promoters (Fig.  ). With respect to Ins1, we were unable to obtain mice with deletions limited solely to the E1 element. We attempted to generate mice with deletion of the Ins1 E1 element alone by performing CRISPR-Cas9 experiments a total of three times; however, unfortunately, we were unable to obtain such mice. A PAM sequence (5′-NGG-3′) is present on the 5′ side of the E1 element, whereas on the 3′ side, a PAM sequence exists only at a considerably distant position (Supplementary Fig.  ). In the CRISPR-Cas9 system, DNA is cleaved immediately upstream of the PAM sequence, three base pairs toward the 5′ side. As a result, it was not possible to generate a deletion limited to the 3′ side of the Ins1 E1 element. Therefore, we compared two types of mice: those with deletions in both the E1 element and the GG2–GG1/A2–C1 elements (hereafter referred to as 1GCE mice) and those with deletions only in the GG2–GG1/A2–C1 elements (hereafter referred to as 1GC mice) (Fig.  ). With respect to Ins2, we successfully obtained mice with deletions limited to the E1 element (hereafter referred to as 2E mice). To rule out the influence of the CCTCTG sequence located between the C1 and E1 elements, we also analyzed mice with a deletion of the CTC sequence (hereafter referred to as 23 mice) for comparison (Fig.  ). Insulin promoter activity was significantly decreased for 1GCE and 1GC promoter plasmids and 2E promoter plasmid (Fig.  ).
 We performed siRNA knockdown experiments targeting MafA and NeuroD in the luciferase assay. When MafA siRNA and the 2E-Luc plasmid were introduced, a decrease in luciferase activity was observed (Fig.  ). In contrast, when NeuroD siRNA and the 2E-Luc plasmid were introduced, the luciferase activity was almost unchanged compared with the condition without NeuroD siRNA treatment (Fig.  ). These results suggest that MafA predominantly binds to the Ins2 promoter with the “CCTCTGGCC” deletion, whereas the sequence is less favorable for NeuroD binding.

## 1GCE2E and 1GC2E homozygous mice developed diabetes
Mice carrying deletions in the Ins1/2 promoters were crossed with wild-type C57BL/6 J mice (F1 mice). Four heterozygous mice with deletions in either the Ins1 or Ins2 promoter were obtained, and none of them developed diabetes. These F1 mice were intercrossed to generate homozygous F2 mice with deletions in either the Ins1 promoter alone or the Ins2 promoter alone (1GCE, 1GC, 2E, and 23 mice). The four homozygous F2 mice also did not develop diabetes. The homozygous F2 mice were subsequently intercrossed to obtain heterozygous F3 mice carrying deletions in both Ins1/2 promoters. These F3 mice likewise did not develop diabetes. Finally, by crossing F3 and F4 mice, we obtained four homozygous mice with deletions in both Ins1/2 promoters (1GCE2E, 1GC2E, 1GCE23, 1GC23 mice) (Fig.  , Supplementary Figs.  – ). Among these, 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice developed diabetes (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, 1GCE23 and 1GC23 mice did not develop diabetes but showed a marked decrease in Ins1 mRNA expression, which was accompanied by a compensatory increase in Ins2 mRNA expression (Supplementary Figs.  , ). Immunostaining of pancreatic tissue from 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice revealed reduced insulin expression in the islets (Fig.  B, , Supplementary Figs.  , , ). Regarding glucagon staining, no differences were observed compared with wild-type animals (Fig.  B, , Supplementary Figs.  , , )
 Fasting serum insulin and C-peptide levels in wild-type and 1GCE2E mice are shown in Supplementary Tables  – . Although many samples were below the limit of detection for insulin and C-peptide, SUIT index, HOMA-β, and HOMA-IR were calculated for the detectable samples. Compared with wild-type mice, 1GCE2E mice showed lower SUIT index and HOMA-β values, suggesting impaired insulin secretion. In contrast, HOMA-IR values were within the normal range in both mice, indicating the absence of insulin resistance. Islet number/mouse was not different between wild-type and 1GCE2E mice (Supplementary Table  ).
Islet insulin content and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) are shown in Supplementary Tables  , . Islet insulin content was significantly lower in 1GCE2E mice than in wild-type mice. In GSIS assays, insulin secretion under both low- and high-glucose conditions was significantly reduced in 1GCE2E mice, and the stimulation index was also significantly lower in 1GCE2E mice. These findings indicate impaired insulin secretory capacity in 1GCE2E mice compared with wild-type controls. The area under the curve (AUC) values of the glucose tolerance test (GTT) in wild-type male mice was 747.3 ± 61.3 mmol‧min/L whereas that in 1GCE2E male mice was 2628.8 ± 198.5 mmol‧min/L (Fig.  ). There were significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.01). The AUC values of the GTT in female mice was similar to that in male mice (wild-type female mice: 739.0 ± 52.6 mmol‧min/L, 1GCE2E female mice: 2660.6 ± 201.0 mmol‧min/L (p < 0.01), Fig.  ). For other biochemical examination of blood serum chemistry, there were no significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Tables  , )

## Comparison between 1GCE2E and 1GC23 mice
To evaluate whether the E1 element is important for Ins transcription in vivo, we compared 1GCE2E mice (with E1 element deletions in both Ins1/2) and 1GC23 mice (with no E1 element deletions in Ins1 or Ins2) (Fig.  ). Compared to those in 1GC23 mice, fasted blood glucose levels in 1GCE2E mice were elevated (the absolute value of difference: male; 0.8 mmoL/L, female 0.8 mmoL/L) at 6 weeks of age (Fig.  ). Similarly, fed blood glucose levels were higher in 1GCE2E mice (the absolute value of difference: male; 5.0 mmoL/L, female 4.8 mmoL/L) (Fig.  ). Furthermore, at 12 weeks of age, both fasted and fed blood glucose levels remained higher in 1GCE2E mice than in 1GC23 mice (the absolute value of difference: fasted: male; 1.0 mmoL/L, female 1.1 mmoL/L) (the absolute value of difference: fed: male; 5.2 mmoL/L, female 4.9 mmoL/L) (Fig.  ).
 Ins1/2 mRNA expression levels in fasted 1GCE2E mice were significantly lower than those in fasted 1GC23 mice (Fig.  ), suggesting that E1 elements in both Ins1/2 are important for the expression of each mRNA in vivo. In the GTT, blood glucose levels were significantly elevated in 1GCE2E mice compared with those in 1GC23 mice (Fig.  ). The AUC values of the GTT in 1GC23 male mice was 993.1 ± 68.3 mmol‧min/L whereas that in 1GCE2E male mice was 2667.1 ± 119.3 mmol‧min/L (Fig.  ). There were significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.01). The AUC values of the GTT in female mice was similar to that in male mice (1GC23 female mice: 903.9 ± 90.2 mmol‧min/L, 1GCE2E female mice: 2699.2 ± 82.3 mmol‧min/L (p < 0.01), Fig.  ). Additionally, the body weights of 1GCE2E mice were lower than those of 1GC23 mice at 6 weeks of age (female) and 12 weeks of age (male and female) (Fig.  F, ).
These results indicate that E1 elements are important for Ins transcription in vivo.

## Comparison between 1GCE2E and 1GCE23 mice
To evaluate the importance of the E1 element in Ins2, we compared 1GCE2E mice (with E1 element deletions in both Ins1/2) and 1GCE23 mice (with E1 element deletion only in Ins1) (Fig.  ). At 6 weeks of age, both fasted and fed blood glucose levels remained higher in 1GCE2E mice than in 1GCE23 mice (Fig.  ). Furthermore, at 12 weeks of age, both fasted and fed blood glucose levels remained higher in 1GCE2E mice than in 1GCE23 mice (Fig.  ).
 Ins2 mRNA expression in fasted 1GCE2E mice was significantly lower than that in fasted 1GCE23 mice (Fig.  ), suggesting that the E1 element in the Ins2 promoter is important for the expression of Ins2 mRNA in vivo. In the GTT, blood glucose levels were significantly elevated in 1GCE2E mice compared with those in 1GCE23 mice (Fig.  ). The AUC values of the GTT in 1GCE23 male mice was 960.5 ± 84.2 mmol‧min/L whereas that in 1GCE2E male mice was 2632.1 ± 208.3 mmol‧min/L (Fig.  ). There were significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.01). The AUC values of the GTT in female mice was similar to that in male mice (1GCE23 female mice: 911.3 ± 107.8 mmol‧min/L, 1GCE2E female mice: 2595.5 ± 92.6 mmol‧min/L (p < 0.01), Fig.  ). In addition, the body weight of 1GCE2E mice was lower than that of 1GCE23 mice at 6 weeks of age (female) and 12 weeks of age (both male and female) (Fig.  ).
These results indicate the critical importance of the E1 element in Ins2.

## Comparison between 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice
To evaluate the importance of the E1 element in Ins1, we compared 1GCE2E mice (with E1 element deletions in both Ins1/2) and 1GC2E mice (with E1 element deletion only in Ins2) (Fig.  ). Both 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice developed diabetes. The severity of diabetes, as assessed by fasting blood glucose levels, was significantly greater in 12-week-old 1GCE2E mice than in 1GC2E mice (Fig.  ). Moreover, fed blood glucose levels were significantly higher in 1GCE2E mice than in 1GC2E mice at both 6 and 12 weeks of age (Fig.  ).
 Ins1 mRNA expression in fasted 1GCE2E mice was significantly lower than that in fasted 1GC2E mice (Fig.  ), suggesting that the E1 element in the Ins1 promoter is important for the expression of Ins1 mRNA in vivo. In the GTT, both 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice presented elevated blood glucose levels after glucose loading, but compared with 1GC2E mice, 1GCE2E mice presented significantly higher glucose levels—in males at 120 min and in females at both 90 and 120 min postglucose loading (Fig.  ). The AUC values of the GTT in 1GC2E male mice was 2090.0 ± 146.9 mmol‧min/L whereas that in 1GCE2E male mice was 2665.4 ± 155.0 mmol‧min/L (Fig.  ) (p < 0.05). The AUC values of the GTT in female mice was similar to that in male mice (1GC2E female mice: 1929.6 ± 221.6 mmol‧min/L, 1GCE2E female mice: 2660.2 ± 172.8 mmol‧min/L (p < 0.05), Fig.  ). Additionally, the body weights of 1GCE2E mice were lower than that of 1GC2E mice at 6 weeks (male) and 12 weeks (both male and female) (Fig.  ).
These findings demonstrate the importance of the E1 element in Ins1.

## Discussion
In this study, we used CRISPR-Cas9 technology to analyze the in vivo function of the E1 element, which is considered important in the promoter regions of Ins1/2. Genome editing in mammals using the CRISPR-Cas9 system holds great potential for the treatment of human diseases – . The nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) mechanism induces site-specific repair at DNA breakpoints, causing insertions or deletions of various and unpredictable sizes. While NHEJ is disadvantageous for generating knockout mice by deleting a single protein-coding gene, it is advantageous for analyzing the functions of promoter regions in vivo, as it is possible to generate multiple mice with different promoter sequence deletions simultaneously.
We attempted to generate mice with a deletion of the E1 elements in the promoters of Ins1/2. With respect to the Ins2 promoter region, we were able to generate mice with a deletion of only the E1 element. However, in the Ins1 promoter region, deleting only the E1 element was not possible. Instead, we obtained mice with deletions of the GG2-GG1/A2-C1 elements and others with deletions of the GG2-GG1/A2-C1-E1 elements. Therefore, we analyzed the function of the E1 element in the insulin promoter in vivo by comparing these mouse models. Our study demonstrated that the E1 elements in both Ins1/2 promoters affect insulin expression, indicating that the E1 elements are functional regulatory components of the insulin promoters in vivo.
We previously reported that the GG2-GG1/A2 and C1 elements in insulin promoters are critical in vivo , . Moreover, we reported that mice with deletions in only one of the Ins1 or Ins2 promoters did not develop diabetes , . This is because the reduction in insulin mRNA caused by the deletion in one promoter is compensated by the other insulin gene. Another study also revealed that homozygous Ins1 or Ins2 knockout mice do not develop diabetes because of compensatory responses and, notably, that Ins1 transcription is dramatically upregulated in Ins2-deficient (Ins2−/−) mice . In the present study, mice with a deletion in only the Ins1 or Ins2 promoter did not develop diabetes.
In 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice with diabetes, the increase in blood glucose levels was mild. This finding is in stark contrast to the degree of insulin transcriptional reduction and glycemic excursions during glucose tolerance testing. This discrepancy is likely due to the reserve capacity of pancreatic endocrine function. Clinically, segmental pancreas transplantation from living donors has been performed, and the blood glucose levels of most donors were not increased, at least not immediately after donation . Despite the removal of half of the pancreas, these donors did not develop diabetes, suggesting that the pancreas has residual endocrine capacity. In the 1GCE2E and 1GC2E mice, despite the severe reduction in insulin transcription, the mild diabetic phenotype is likely due to this remaining functional capacity of the pancreas.
1GCE2E/1GC2E mice had significantly lower body weights at 6 and 12 weeks of age. On the other hand, no reduction in body weight was observed in neonatal 1GCE2E/1GC2E mice. The parental mice were maintained as heterozygotes and exhibited normal blood glucose levels. No developmental abnormalities were observed during the fetal period. We consider that the growth impairment is likely due to a chronic reduction in insulin secretion after birth.
NeuroD, a bHLH transcription factor, is involved in the protein complex that regulates the insulin gene – . The NeuroD complex functions by binding to E-box sequences within the insulin gene promoter , . In the human insulin gene promoter, three E elements, designated E1 through E3, are present, whereas in the promoter of the rodent insulin 1 gene, E1 and E2 are present. The core sequence CATCTG, which represents an E-box motif (CANNTG) , , is found not only within the insulin gene promoter but also in many other pancreatic gene promoters – . These E-boxes are recognized not only by NeuroD , but also by other bHLH proteins, such as ngn3 , . It has been reported that the endocrine pancreas of NeuroD-deficient mice undergoes extensive apoptosis, resulting in death from diabetes shortly after birth in mice . However, because NeuroD is involved in pancreatic β-cell development and multiple E elements are present in the insulin gene promoter, evaluating the function of the E1 element alone in NeuroD-deficient mice is not possible. This study is considered highly novel in that it specifically evaluates the function of the E1 element.
The deletion of “CCTCTGGCC” in the Ins2 promoter can also be interpreted as a deletion of “GCCCCTCTG” due to the upstream “GCC” sequence. A deletion of “CCTCTGGCC” corresponds to the loss of part of the E1 element, whereas a deletion of “GCCCCTCTG” corresponds to the loss of part of the C1 element. Therefore, this sequence deletion is expected to impair the binding of E1-binding proteins such as NeuroD, or C1-binding proteins such as MafA. Figure  show that the Ins2 promoter with the “CCTCTGGCC” deletion exhibited significantly lower activity compared with the wild-type promoter. This suggests that either C1-binding or E1-binding proteins were unable to bind, resulting in reduced promoter activity. We performed siRNA knockdown experiments targeting MafA and NeuroD in the luciferase assay. When MafA siRNA and the 2E-Luc plasmid were introduced, a decrease in luciferase activity was observed (Fig.  ). These data suggest that MafA binds to the Ins2 promoter with the “CCTCTGGCC” deletion. In contrast, when NeuroD siRNA and the 2E-Luc plasmid were introduced, the luciferase activity was almost unchanged compared with the condition without NeuroD siRNA treatment (Fig.  ). These results suggest that MafA predominantly binds to the Ins2 promoter with the “CCTCTGGCC” deletion, whereas the sequence is less favorable for NeuroD binding. We also performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays to provide evidence of E1 element dysfunction; however, we were unable to obtain convincing data. This may be due to the fact that MafA and NeuroD are unlikely to bind simultaneously to the “CCTCTGGCC” deleted sequence, although they may bind individually. Limitations in the sensitivity and specificity of the MafA and NeuroD antibodies may also be another reason.
In conclusion, the E1 element sequences in the Ins1 and Ins2 promoters play a critical role in insulin expression in vivo. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that the E1 element is essential for the activity of the insulin promoter in mice in vivo. Although there have been no reports demonstrating that mutations in the E1 element contribute to diabetes in humans, there is a report suggesting that naturally occurring insulin promoter variants may contribute to the diabetic phenotype in approximately 5–6% of Black patients with non–insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM) . CRISPR-Cas9 technology is not only a useful tool for analyzing protein function in vivo but also a valuable method for evaluating the function of promoter regions.

## Methods

## Generation of mice with insulin promoter deletions
We previously constructed two Cas9 single guide RNA (sgRNA) expression vectors . These two gRNAs were inserted into the pX330 vector (Addgene, Watertown, MA). Female C57BL/6 J mice were injected with pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) at 48-hour intervals and then mated with male C57BL/6 J mice. Fertilized one-cell embryos were collected from the oviducts and microinjected with 5 ng/μL pX330-1st/2nd gRNA vectors into the pronuclei. After injection, the embryos were transferred into pseudopregnant mice derived from the Institute of Cancer Research (ICR).
F0 mice were genotyped to determine whether deletions had been introduced into the Ins1/2 promoter regions. Additionally, the presence of the Cas9 transgene and potential off-target effects were examined in F0 mice . F0 mice were crossed with wild-type C57BL/6 J mice to generate F1 offspring. The F1 mice were genotyped, and the four types of mutant mice used in this study were selected (Fig.  ). F1 mice were intercrossed to generate homozygous F2 mice carrying deletions in either the Ins1 or the Ins2 promoter. Subsequently, homozygous F2 mice were crossed to obtain heterozygous F3 mice with deletions in both Ins1/2 promoters. Finally, through intercrossing F3 and F4 mice, four homozygous mice with deletions in both Ins1/2 promoters were obtained. F1‒F5 mice were used for analyses in this study.
The mutant and littermate wild-type mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background. All the mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment. Food and water were provided ad libitum . Diabetes was defined according to the following criteria: (1) fasting blood glucose ≥ 13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL), (2) fed blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L (300 mg/dL), or (3) a 120 min blood glucose level ≥ 13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) during the GTT.
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the University of the Ryukyus and the University of Tsukuba. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Anesthesia was induced using inhalation anesthesia with sevoflurane, and euthanasia was performed using CO₂ gas.

## Construction of insulin promoter-luciferase plasmids
Wild-type or mutated insulin promoter cDNAs containing approximately 1000 bp of the 5´-flanking sequences of the Ins1/2 promoter regions were amplified using PCR with appropriate linker-containing primers, which were then used to replace the promoter region of the Rat Ins2 promoter-reporter (luciferase) plasmid using a ligation kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan).

## Gene transfection and luciferase assay
Mouse Ins1/2 promoter-reporter (luciferase) plasmids (1.0 μg) containing approximately 1000 bp of the 5´-flanking sequences of the wild-type or mutated Ins1/2 promoter region were transfected into βTC6 cells (β cell line) (ATCC, Manassas, VA) using Lipofectamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) according to the conditions recommended by the manufacturer. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were harvested and assayed (Promega, Madison, WI). siRNA knockdown experiments targeting MafA and NeuroD (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were performed in the luciferase assay.

## Immunostaining
Pancreatic tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Paraffin sections were mounted on glass slides and then blocked at room temperature for 30 minutes with 20% AquaBlock (EastCoast Bio, North Berwick, ME, USA). The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a guinea pig anti-insulin antibody (1:100; Abcam, Tokyo, Japan), followed by incubation for 1 hour at room temperature with a FITC-conjugated anti-guinea pig IgG antibody (1:250; Abcam). The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse anti-glucagon antibody (1:200; Merck, Tokyo, Japan), followed by incubation for 1 h at room temperature with a TRITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:250; Merck). The sections were treated with mounting medium to detect the fluorescence emitted by DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Peterborough, UK).

## Isolation of pancreatic islets from mouse pancreas
Islets were isolated from the pancreases of normal and genome-edited mice , . For islet isolation, the common bile duct was cannulated and injected with cold Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 1.5 mg/ml collagenase (Roche Boehringer Mannheim, Indianapolis, IN). The pancreas was digested at 37 °C. Islets were separated using a Histopaque 1077 (Merck) density gradient and then hand-picked under a dissecting microscope to ensure pure islet preparation. The isolated islets were used immediately.

## Quantitative RT‒PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from isolated islets using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Tokyo, Japan). After the RNA was quantified by spectrophotometry, 2.5 µg of RNA was heated at 85 °C for 3 minutes and then reverse transcribed in a 25 µl reaction mixture containing 200 units of Superscript II RNase H-RT (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50 ng of random hexamers (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 160 µmol/l dNTPs, and 10 nmol/l dithiothreitol. The reaction mixture was incubated for 10 min at 25 °C, 60 min at 42 °C, and 10 min at 95 °C .
Quantitative PCR amplification of cDNA from mouse islets was performed using a TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix Core Reagent Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR was carried out for 40 cycles, with initial incubations at 50 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 10 min. Each cycle consisted of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. PCRs were performed in a 20 µl volume using cDNA synthesized from 1.11 ng of total RNA. The amount of mRNA was normalized by dividing the target mRNA level by that of Gapdh mRNA. Primers specific for mouse Ins1, Ins2, and Gapdh were purchased from Assays-on-Demand Gene Expression Products (Applied Biosystems) .

## Glucose tolerance test (GTT)
An intraperitoneal GTT was performed on 12-week-old mice. After fasting for 12 hours, the mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with glucose (2.0 g/kg body weight). A 100 mg/mL glucose solution was administered intraperitoneally at 0.4–0.6 mL per mouse (for example, for a 20 g mouse, 2.0 g/kg corresponds to 40 mg/20 g, so 0.4 mL of a 100 mg/mL solution is appropriate). Blood glucose levels were measured before injection and at 5, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after injection.

## Insulin secretory response
Islet function was assessed by monitoring the insulin secretory response of the purified islets during glucose stimulation according to a procedure described previously . Briefly, 1200 IE of the purified islets was pre-incubated with Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640; Sigma-Aldrich Japan) media containing 2.8 mM glucose for 1 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. 200 IE of the islets were then incubated with either 2.8 or 25 mM glucose in RPMI 1640 for 1 h. The supernatant was collected and the insulin levels were determined using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Levis Insulin ELISA kit; Levis Co., Ltd., Japan). The stimulation index was calculated from the ratio of the insulin released from the islets during stimulation with a high-glucose concentration to insulin released during stimulation with a low-glucose concentration. The islets were then sonicated in distilled water for the subsequent determination of intracellular insulin as described previously .

## Serum insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon levels
Serum insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon levels were measured using a LBISTM Mouse Insulin ELISA kit (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), LBISTM Mouse C-peptide ELISA kit (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and Mouse Glucagon ELISA Kit (Crystal Chem, Itasca, IL).

## Statistics and reproducibility
The error bars represent the standard error of at least three measurements. Student’s t tests were performed using Microsoft Excel to compare two independent groups. Repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to compare data among groups. Differences between groups were considered to be statistically significant when p < 0.05.
All the experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.
All methods were carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.
