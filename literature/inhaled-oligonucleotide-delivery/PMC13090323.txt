## Abstract
Fibrosis is a common pathological endpoint of numerous chronic diseases and a major driver of progressive organ dysfunction and failure. It is characterized by persistent fibroblast activation, excessive extracellular matrix deposition, and irreversible disruption of tissue architecture. Despite substantial clinical demand, current antifibrotic therapies largely rely on single-target interventions, often demonstrating limited efficacy. mRNA nanomedicine has emerged as a promising therapeutic paradigm due to its genomic safety, programmability, transient protein expression, and capacity for multipathway regulation. However, a unified framework integrating mRNA design, delivery technologies, and translational challenges in fibrosis remains lacking. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in mRNA-based nanotherapeutics for cardiac, renal, pulmonary, and hepatic fibrosis. We discuss the key principles of mRNA molecular engineering, including chemical modifications and sequence optimization, as well as innovations in lipid nanoparticles, polymeric carriers, and biomimetic nanoplatforms. These strategies enhance organ- and cell-specific targeting, improve pharmacokinetics, and mitigate immunogenicity. Importantly, mRNA nanomedicines enable coordinated modulation of profibrotic signaling networks, including the TGF-β/Smad, NF-κB, BMP, and PI3K/mTOR pathways, thereby promoting fibrosis resolution and functional recovery. Emerging delivery paradigms, such as inhalable formulations, hydrogel-based depots, and selective organ targeting (SORT) technologies, are also highlighted along with critical barriers, including endosomal escape, repeat-dosing immune responses, and manufacturing.
Keywords: MT: Delivery Strategies, mRNA therapy, fibrosis, lipid nanoparticles, TGF-β signaling, multipathway intervention, organ-specific delivery

## Introduction
Fibrosis is a common pathological process that represents the final stage of many chronic diseases. It is characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and subsequent remodeling of tissue structure, which disrupt the normal organization and function of organs. Nearly all organs can develop fibrosis; however, the liver, kidneys, lungs, and heart are among the most frequently affected and clinically important. , , , , As fibrosis leads to irreversible organ damage and may ultimately cause death, its clinical impact is profound and multifaceted. For example, fibrotic scarring after myocardial infarction can lead to heart failure, patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) have a median survival of less than 5 years, and both liver cirrhosis and end-stage kidney disease substantially reduce quality of life and are associated with high mortality.
There remains a strong clinical need for effective antifibrotic therapies; however, currently approved drugs offer limited benefits. , Fibrosis arises from complex interactions among multiple cell types and signaling pathways, making its pathogenesis highly multifactorial. Although several agents targeting well-established pathways—such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and fibroblast growth factor—have been developed, most have not advanced to clinical use due to insufficient efficacy or safety concerns ( ). , , , , The highly interconnected nature of fibrotic signaling networks further limits the effectiveness of single-target therapies, which address only specific components of the pathological process. Thus, therapeutic strategies capable of acting on multiple pathways and cellular processes are urgently needed to achieve better antifibrotic outcomes.
 In this context, messenger RNA (mRNA) nanomedicines have recently gained attention as a promising approach for antifibrotic therapy ( ). , , , , These therapeutics offer several advantages. Because they do not integrate into the host genome, they present a favorable safety profile while allowing transient and controllable protein expression. Additionally, surface modification of delivery carriers enables targeted delivery to specific tissues or cell types, thereby improving precision and minimizing off-target effects. , The programmable nature of mRNA also allows simultaneous regulation of multiple targets, and ongoing progress in sequence optimization and carrier design has markedly improved its stability and translation efficiency. Collectively, these developments position mRNA nanomedicines as emerging platforms for next-generation antifibrotic treatments.
 This review summarizes recent progress in the development of mRNA nanomedicines for fibrosis, with particular attention to advances in molecular design, delivery technologies, and multi-target strategies. The major opportunities and challenges associated with clinical translation are also discussed. By integrating insights from mechanistic and technological research, the aim of this study was to provide a clear conceptual and practical basis for the development of precise and effective antifibrotic therapies that can be translated into clinical practice.

## Pathogenesis of fibrosis and potential therapeutic targets

## Molecular pathways and regulatory networks driving fibrogenesis

## Canonical signaling pathways driving fibrogenesis
Fibrosis develops through a complex interplay of multiple signaling pathways, among which TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)/platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) pathways are central ( ). TGF-β binds to its receptors and activates Smad-dependent signaling, thereby inducing fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. , , , The Wnt/β-catenin pathway promotes fibrogenesis by stimulating the transcription of profibrotic genes, resulting in enhanced cell proliferation and ECM production. , Similarly, the PDGF/PDGFR pathway activates downstream signaling cascades that promote fibroblast migration, differentiation, and ECM secretion, driving progressive tissue remodeling. , 
 These pathways interact across multiple organs and cell types to form a core regulatory network that sustains fibrosis progression. Although pharmacological inhibition of these signaling routes reduces ECM accumulation in experimental models, broad suppression frequently causes adverse effects, such as immune dysfunction and impaired wound healing, restricting clinical translation. , , , , This observation underscores the need for more precise and integrative therapeutic strategies capable of modulating fibrosis without compromising normal physiological processes.
Because of their transient expression and programmable features, mRNA nanomedicines provide a promising platform for the simultaneous fine-tuning of multiple downstream pathways. Through rational molecular design, these therapeutics enable multidimensional regulation of the fibrotic network and may overcome the limitations of conventional single-pathway interventions.

## Inflammatory and immune-mediated amplification in fibrosis
Chronic inflammation and immune activation are central drivers of fibrosis development and progression of fibrosis. , , , Following tissue injury, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activate the Toll-like receptor (TLR)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway and the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, triggering a cascade involving IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and reactive oxygen species (ROS). These mediators promote macrophage polarization toward the profibrotic M2 phenotype, creating a self-sustaining feedback loop that perpetuates inflammation. Concurrently, the inflammatory milieu upregulates TGF-β1, PDGF-BB, and CTGF expression, resulting in sustained growth factor production within injured tissues. Persistent activation of these inflammatory and growth factor pathways ultimately drives the transition from transient inflammation to irreversible fibrosis.
Most existing antifibrotic therapies act on a single inflammatory or growth factor pathway, thereby limiting their ability to comprehensively address the complex pathogenesis of fibrosis. For example, the phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitor BI 1015550 significantly slowed the decline in lung function over 52 weeks in a phase 3 clinical trial in patients with IPF (NCT05321069). Preclinical and early clinical studies have shown that BI 1015550 exerts broad regulatory effects by suppressing IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β/Smad signaling, indicating that modulation of multiple pathways may enhance therapeutic efficacy. , , These findings highlight the potential advantage of dual-pathway intervention that targets both inflammatory and profibrotic processes. Building on this concept, mRNA nanomedicines—with their programmable, transient, and multi-target regulatory properties—offer a promising platform for developing customizable RNA-based combination therapies capable of precisely modulating the interconnected inflammatory and fibrotic signaling networks.

## Metabolic and stress imbalance as sustaining drivers of fibrosis
Disturbances in cellular metabolism and stress responses create a chronic environment that reinforces fibrotic signaling. Persistent energy imbalance leads to adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion and excessive generation of ROS, which in turn cause mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum dysfunction and maintain a state of prolonged cellular stress. Under these conditions, the TGF-β-induced protein TXNDC5 aggravates oxidative stress by stabilizing TGF-β receptors and amplifying downstream signaling. Consequently, fibroblasts acquire resistance to apoptosis and secrete excess collagen, forming a self-perpetuating cycle that accelerates fibrotic progression. 
Therapeutic targeting of metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress has demonstrated considerable promise. Recent studies have reported that a hyaluronic acid-bilirubin nanoplatform directed at the ROS-CD44 interaction effectively removes intracellular ROS in activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and alleviates oxidative injury. Based on this principle, mRNA nanocarriers can be engineered to encode antioxidant enzymes or metabolic regulators, enabling coordinated modulation of energy metabolism and oxidative stress to achieve enhanced antifibrotic efficacy.

## Multicellular origins and expansion of fibrogenic populations
One of the major obstacles in reversing fibrosis is the continuous replenishment of fibrogenic effector cells. In addition to the activation of resident fibroblasts, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), endothelial-mesenchymal transition, and recruitment of bone marrow-derived fibroblast-like cells contribute to the constant generation of additional ECM-producing cells. , This cellular heterogeneity markedly increases the complexity of antifibrotic therapy. For example, in renal fibrosis, epithelial cell apoptosis occurs alongside myofibroblast proliferation, accelerating interstitial remodeling and aggravating tissue scarring.
Given these intricate cellular dynamics, traditional pharmacological treatments have achieved limited success in halting fibrotic remodeling. Recently, mRNA-based interventions using lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have emerged as a promising alternative. By silencing profibrotic mediators such as heat shock protein 47 (HSP47) or modulating transcription factors involved in EMT, these strategies can effectively suppress myofibroblast formation and collagen deposition. Moreover, mRNA nanomedicines can be tailored to selectively target distinct fibroblast subsets, limiting their expansion at the source and offering a more precise and flexible approach to control fibrotic progression.

## Shared mechanisms and organ-specific pathways of fibrosis
Although organ fibrosis arises from diverse causes, striking molecular similarities exist across tissues. Whether in the heart, kidney, lung, or liver, the pathological process consistently centers on TGF-β-driven fibroblast activation, immune-stromal crosstalk, mechanotransduction signaling, and ECM remodeling. These conserved mechanisms constitute the core molecular framework underlying fibrotic progression and provide a theoretical basis for the development of antifibrotic therapies applicable across multiple organs.
Nevertheless, each organ exhibits distinct pathological features shaped by its structural and metabolic characteristics. In the heart, fibrosis develops through interactions between mechanical stress and metabolic remodeling. , , In the kidney, persistent inflammation and ROS signaling amplify fibrogenesis. , Pulmonary fibrosis is primarily triggered by epithelial injury and progressive tissue stiffening, whereas hepatic fibrosis is predominantly driven by the activation of HSCs. Collectively, these examples illustrate how organ-specific microenvironments shape shared molecular pathways to yield distinct fibrotic phenotypes. Understanding these common yet divergent mechanisms is crucial for identifying translatable therapeutic targets and refining mRNA nanomedicine-based interventions. In this section, the heart, kidney, lung, and liver are discussed as representative models to summarize key molecular events and therapeutic targets in fibrosis and to highlight how organ-specific regulatory networks inform precise antifibrotic strategies. 

## Cardiac fibrosis
Cardiac fibrosis is a pathological process marked by excessive accumulation of ECM within the myocardial interstitium. It frequently develops as a secondary outcome of myocardial infarction, cardiac hypertrophy, diabetic cardiomyopathy, or dilated cardiomyopathy. , , Persistent fibrosis significantly reduces myocardial compliance and contractility, thereby increasing the likelihood of heart failure. The fibrotic process usually begins with myocardial injury. Ischemia, mechanical overload, or metabolic disturbances trigger the release of DAMPs, which activate local immune responses. During the early inflammatory stage, cytokines such as IL-1β and CCL2 activate the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, inducing fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis. As fibrosis progresses, neurohumoral mediators, including angiotensin II and endothelin-1, together with persistent mechanical stress, further amplify these signaling cascades, establishing a self-reinforcing feedback loop that drives continuous ECM deposition and tissue remodeling. 
In diabetic cardiomyopathy, these mechanisms are further enhanced under hyperglycemic conditions. Elevated glucose levels upregulate thrombospondin-1, which activates latent TGF-β and promotes fibroblast differentiation and type I collagen synthesis through a Smad3-dependent pathway, forming a “high glucose-TGF-β-collagen” feedback loop. Concurrently, metabolic reprogramming in myofibroblasts provides continuous energy and biosynthetic support for fibrosis. Once activated, these cells shift their metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, resulting in increased ROS production. , , The upregulation and phosphorylation of the glycolytic enzyme pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) redirect metabolic intermediates toward the serine synthesis pathway, supplying precursors for collagen production while maintaining the highly synthetic and contractile phenotype of myofibroblasts.
Collectively, these molecular and metabolic alterations constitute the core regulatory axis underlying cardiac fibrosis. Despite growing mechanistic insights, no drug approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) is currently available that directly reverses myocardial fibrosis. The development of therapeutic strategies capable of achieving precise, multi-target modulation and efficient delivery across different stages of disease progression remains an important goal for improving antifibrotic therapy in the heart.

## Renal fibrosis
Renal fibrosis is a common terminal outcome of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and is characterized by excessive ECM accumulation within the tubulointerstitial compartment, ultimately resulting in irreversible renal dysfunction. Fibrosis typically begins with tubular epithelial cell injury. At this stage, DAMPs released from injured cells activate the TLR-NF-κB signaling pathway, triggering a cascade of inflammatory responses. , Evidence from the unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model demonstrates that inhibition of the TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB axis using TLR4 antagonists significantly decreases IL-1β and TGF-β expression, thereby alleviating early fibrotic changes.
As the disease advances, inflammation and cellular stress progressively intensify, with TGF-β emerging as a central regulatory factor. Elevated levels of TGF-β, in concert with ROS, sustain fibroblast activation, stimulate excessive ECM production, and disrupt tubular integrity. Prolonged activation of these signaling pathways eventually results in the loss and structural disorganization of renal parenchymal cells, culminating in the irreversible impairment of kidney function. 
Despite growing mechanistic insights, no specific or safe antifibrotic drug has been approved for clinical use. Thiazolidinediones can mitigate fibrosis by suppressing TGF-β expression and inhibiting fibroblast proliferation; however, their excessive activation of PPAR-γ often causes severe adverse effects. , Therefore, the development of multi-target, precisely regulated, and organ-selective therapeutic strategies has become a major priority in renal fibrosis research.

## Pulmonary fibrosis
Pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic progressive disorder characterized by interstitial remodeling and irreversible loss of lung function. Its development involves a multifactorial process in which epithelial injury, immune activation, and excessive ECM deposition interact to drive disease progression. Environmental exposure to air pollution, smoking, toxic particulates, and viral infections can damage epithelial cells and trigger the release of DAMPs and ROS. These factors activate NF-κB signaling and establish a chronic inflammatory milieu that fosters fibrotic remodeling.
As the disease progresses, integrin-mediated mechanical stress, oxidative stress, and proteolytic cleavage collectively activate latent TGF-β. The active TGF-β then induces EMT, recruits mesenchymal cells, and promotes ECM gene expression through Smad and MAPK/PI3K pathways, accelerating fibrogenesis. , , In bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, administration of an αvβ6 integrin-neutralizing antibody significantly reduces active TGF-β levels and interstitial thickening, highlighting the central importance of this pathway in disease development. 
Persistent TGF-β signaling also reshapes fibroblast metabolism, enhancing glycolysis and amino acid biosynthesis while promoting ECM gene transcription via lactate accumulation and histone lactylation. As matrix stiffness increases, YAP/TAZ-mediated mechanotransduction is further activated and cooperates with PI3K-Akt signaling to suppress Fas-dependent apoptosis, conferring apoptosis resistance and enabling long-term survival of myofibroblasts. 
Despite advances in understanding its pathogenesis, effective clinical management of pulmonary fibrosis remains limited. Although pirfenidone and nintedanib have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of IPF, their benefits are modest and adverse effects are frequent. Achieving efficient and targeted pulmonary delivery while ensuring long-term safety remains a major challenge for successful clinical translation.

## Hepatic fibrosis
Hepatic fibrosis is a common pathological consequence of chronic liver diseases, including viral, metabolic, and immune-mediated disorders. It is characterized by excessive and disorganized accumulation of ECM, which progressively disrupts hepatic architecture and impairs liver function. Recurrent injury to hepatocytes or cholangiocytes leads to the release of DAMPs, which activate Kupffer cells and infiltrating immune cells, thereby sustaining profibrotic signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad and PDGF. , In parallel, cytokines and growth factors such as IL-13, CTGF, and VEGF promote the transdifferentiation of HSCs from a quiescent state into activated myofibroblasts with high ECM-synthetic capacity, further driving matrix accumulation and fibrotic progression. , 
At the metabolic level, activated HSCs undergo profound metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glycolysis, lipid accumulation, and persistent production of ROS. These metabolic alterations establish a positive feedback loop that amplifies endoplasmic reticulum stress and reinforces the profibrotic phenotype. , Experimental evidence demonstrates that targeted deletion of GATA6 or PPAR-γ in HSCs markedly accelerates fibrosis, underscoring their essential roles in maintaining HSC metabolic homeostasis and preventing pathological activation. In addition to HSC-driven mechanisms, hepatic macrophages exhibit remarkable functional plasticity, transitioning between the profibrotic and fibrolytic states. By secreting matrix metalloproteinases and profibrotic mediators, these macrophages dynamically regulate ECM turnover and collectively maintain tissue equilibrium during fibrotic progression. , 
Despite considerable advances in elucidating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying hepatic fibrosis, effective targeted therapies for clinical use remain limited. Most available pharmacological agents fail to achieve both multi-target efficacy and long-term safety, constraining their translational potential. Given their programmable expression capacity and tissue-specific targeting properties, mRNA nanomedicines have emerged as promising next-generation platforms to overcome these limitations and provide innovative therapeutic avenues for hepatic fibrosis.

## Challenges of conventional antifibrotic therapies and emerging advances in mRNA nanomedicine
Current antifibrotic therapies are limited by their suboptimal efficacy, low target specificity, and cumulative systemic toxicity. Most conventional agents rely on single-target mechanisms, rendering them insufficient to modulate the complex and heterogeneous signaling networks that drive fibrosis. For example, pirfenidone and nintedanib, approved in 2014 for the treatment of IPF, , , can decelerate lung function decline but fail to reverse or repair established tissue injury. Moreover, frequent adverse effects, including photosensitive dermatitis, diarrhea, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, and dyspepsia, , often compromise patient compliance and diminish long-term therapeutic benefits.
In the cardiovascular field, no FDA-approved therapies currently exist that directly target cardiac fibrosis. Most candidate compounds remain at the preclinical stage, hindered by the lack of cardiac-specific biomarkers, limited myocardial penetration, and redundancy or compensatory nature of critical signaling pathways. , , , Likewise, antifibrotic research in the liver and kidney continues to face major challenges, including difficulties in achieving effective multi-target modulation, prominent adverse effects, and uncertain long-term outcomes. , , , Collectively, these limitations underscore a central issue: conventional “single-molecule, single-pathway” drug designs cannot adequately address the complex, multi-organ, and multi-layered pathophysiological networks underlying fibrosis.
In response to these limitations, mRNA nanomedicines have emerged as transformative therapeutic platforms capable of coordinated, multi-target regulation, with precise spatial and temporal control of gene expression. A key advantage of this approach is its ability to deliver multiple regulatory proteins or signaling molecules within a single administration, enabling simultaneous modulation of distinct stages in the fibrotic cascade. For instance, in pulmonary fibrosis models, targeted delivery of specific mRNA constructs has been shown to restore the regenerative capacity of type II alveolar epithelial cells while suppressing fibroblast activation, thereby achieving a bidirectional reparative effect at the epithelial-mesenchymal interface. , 
In a mouse model of heart failure, transient mRNA-driven expression of antifibrotic chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells allows the selective clearance of activated fibroblasts without impairing subsequent tissue repair, offering controllable, short-term therapeutic activity with reversible safety. Concurrently, advances in delivery technologies have laid the groundwork for achieving tissue specificity in mRNA-based interventions. , , , Multiple programmable targeting modules have now been engineered and incorporated into mRNA nanoparticle surfaces, enabling precise and efficient delivery to specific organs or cell populations. , 
Future research should aim to refine tissue-specific delivery systems and explore synergistic strategies that integrate mRNA therapeutics with small-molecule or cell-based interventions. Such combinatorial approaches could enable coordinated regulation of multiple signaling pathways, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and expediting clinical translation. With their intrinsic programmability, precise controllability, and strong safety profile, mRNA nanomedicines have substantial potential to overcome the limitations of conventional antifibrotic therapies and reshape the future landscape of fibrosis treatment.

## Structural features and delivery strategies of mRNA nanomedicines

## Molecular architecture and functional optimization of mRNA nanomedicines
mRNA nanomedicines have rapidly emerged as powerful platforms for gene therapy and vaccine development due to their programmability, transient protein expression, and favorable safety profiles. These therapeutics are primarily composed of chemically modified mRNA molecules synthesized via in vitro transcription (IVT) and encapsulated within specialized nanocarrier delivery systems. Structurally, an mRNA molecule comprises five cis-acting elements—a 5′ cap, a 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR), an open reading frame (ORF), a 3′ UTR, and a poly(A) tail. The sequence design and chemical modification of these elements critically determine mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and immunogenicity, thereby establishing a molecular foundation for optimizing mRNA-based therapeutics. , , 
After cellular entry, exogenous mRNA is rapidly recognized and degraded by innate immune sensors, which not only shortens its intracellular half-life but also may trigger nonspecific inflammatory responses. Consequently, minimizing immunogenicity while maintaining robust translational efficiency remains a central challenge in mRNA drug design. Among 5′-end modification strategies, CleanCap technology enables co-transcriptional synthesis of authentic Cap 1 structures with over 95% efficiency, while traditional enzymatic capping methods can produce Cap 1 or Cap 2 methylated variants that prevent decapping and reduce immune sensing. , , Anti-reverse cap analogs (ARCAs) and their thiophosphate derivatives can further enhance stability ; however, excessive thiolation may reduce IVT yield and introduce non-native immunogenic epitopes. Therefore, the degree of chemical modification must be carefully optimized according to the intended in vivo application to achieve the optimal balance between stability, translation, and immune tolerance.
Nucleotide substitution is another essential strategy for modulating immunogenicity and translation. Incorporating modified bases—such as 1-methylpseudouridine, 5-methylcytidine, or 5-formyluridine—within the ORF or across the entire mRNA strand effectively suppresses innate immune receptor activation, while enhancing protein synthesis. , , These chemical modifications have been successfully adopted in several mRNA vaccines and therapeutic candidates; however, excessive modification may introduce translation errors or interfere with long-term immune memory. Therefore, the extent of modification should be tailored to disease context and therapeutic purpose. Additionally, the 3′ UTR significantly contributes to mRNA stability, as reducing AU-rich elements within this region markedly slows degradation and extends transcript lifespan. 
The poly(A) tail is another structural determinant of mRNA stability. Moderate elongation of the tail or incorporation of branched poly(A) architectures can delay exonuclease-mediated degradation and sustain protein synthesis. Nevertheless, overly long tails can complicate IVT and purification processes. Thus, an optimal balance between molecular stability and manufacturing feasibility is essential for achieving scalable mRNA production.
Enhancing translational efficiency is the central focus of mRNA engineering. Shortening the 5′ UTR facilitates ribosome recruitment to the cap structure, while preserving the Kozak sequence promotes efficient translation initiation. Moreover, incorporating stabilizing elements—such as α- or β-globin sequences—into UTRs can prolong mRNA half-life and increase protein yield. Codon optimization within the ORF and reducing the use of rare codons further alleviate translational pausing and mispairing, thereby improving overall protein expression efficiency. , 
In summary, structural modification of mRNA constitutes a core strategy for optimizing therapeutic functionality. Through rational design of the cap structure, UTRs, nucleotide modifications, and poly(A) tail, a fine balance can be achieved between molecular stability, immunogenicity, and translational efficiency ( ). In antifibrotic applications, these design principles establish a solid molecular foundation for the development of mRNA nanomedicines with tissue-specific targeting, coordinated multi-target modulation, and controllable transient expression.
 

## Types and comparative advantages of nanocarriers for mRNA delivery
mRNA molecules are negatively charged and highly susceptible to degradation and cannot spontaneously cross cellular membranes, making the design of efficient and safe delivery systems crucial for therapeutic efficacy. Achieving an optimal balance among molecular stability, immunogenicity, and tissue specificity remains a major challenge in mRNA drug development. Conventional delivery platforms often exhibit preferential accumulation in the liver, thereby limiting their biodistribution and therapeutic potential in extrahepatic tissues. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed a diverse array of delivery systems, including LNPs, polymer-based nanomaterials, exosomes, bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), and DNA nanostructures. , , , Each carrier system offers distinct advantages in terms of delivery efficiency, immune compatibility, and targeting precision, collectively establishing a strong technological foundation for advancing mRNA-based antifibrotic therapies.

## Lipid nanoparticles
LNPs are currently the most established and widely used delivery system for mRNA therapeutics. They are composed of ionizable lipids, helper lipids, cholesterol, and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified lipids, which self-assemble into spherical nanostructures that protect mRNA from degradation and facilitate intracellular delivery. By adjusting the chemical backbone and surface charge of lipid components, biodistribution can be directed toward specific tissues. For example, in liver fibrosis models, introducing anethole amide groups into the ionizable lipid backbone enhances targeting of activated HSCs, thereby increasing mRNA accumulation and expression within fibrotic regions. In another study, partial replacement of the lipid composition with multivalent ionizable cationic dendrimers improved mRNA complexation and endosomal escape efficiency, enabling more effective cellular transfection. 
Despite these advances, LNPs provide substantial benefits in terms of drug stability and delivery efficiency; however, their immunogenicity and safety profiles remain critical considerations. Accumulating evidence suggests that specific LNP components, particularly ionizable lipids, may trigger innate immune responses in vivo, including activation of TLR4, thereby promoting the release of proinflammatory cytokines. , , , Beyond these acute effects, repeated administration presents an additional challenge, as PEG components can induce anti-PEG antibodies, leading to accelerated blood clearance and reduced therapeutic efficacy. This phenomenon is particularly relevant for fibrotic diseases that require long-term treatment. Consequently, mitigating immune activation while maintaining tissue-selective delivery remains a central objective in the optimization of mRNA nanomedicine platforms. Potential strategies include the development of PEG alternatives, such as stealth polymers, and the design of biodegradable PEG-lipid conjugates. 

## Polymer-based nanocarriers
Polymer-based nanocarriers have emerged as an important alternative to lipid-based systems for mRNA delivery due to their tunable chemical composition and favorable biocompatibility. Representative examples include polyethyleneimine, polyamidoamine, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), and poly (β-amino ester) (PBAE). The structural flexibility of these polymers allows precise control over molecular weight, charge density, and functional groups, thereby enhancing mRNA protection, cellular uptake, and tissue-targeting efficiency. For instance, incorporating ethanolamine functional groups into a glycidyl methacrylate backbone yields star-shaped cationic polymers that promote efficient mRNA transfection in cardiac tissues and alleviate myocardial fibrosis. Likewise, a dual-targeted mRNA delivery platform composed of PBAE and hyaluronic acid has demonstrated selective accumulation in both inflammatory macrophages and tumor cells following inhalation, significantly improving local transfection efficiency. 
Despite these promising advances, the in vivo application of polymeric materials remains constrained by factors such as their degradation behavior, potential immunostimulatory effects, and concerns regarding long-term toxicity. Consequently, achieving an optimal balance between delivery efficiency and minimal systemic toxicity represents a central challenge for their continued development. In addition to these biological considerations, polymer-based carriers may, relative to LNPs, encounter further limitations associated with formulation complexity and batch-to-batch reproducibility, particularly for polymers requiring precise structural and functional control.

## Exosome-based systems
Exosomes are nanoscale extracellular vesicles released by cells that typically measure 30–150 nm in diameter. They feature a natural lipid bilayer and possess the inherent ability to traverse biological barriers. Because of their high biocompatibility and minimal immunogenicity, exosomes are considered among the most physiologically relevant carriers for RNA delivery. These vesicles can evade immune surveillance and display natural tissue tropism, enabling efficient systemic distribution. , Their targeting capabilities can be further enhanced through surface modification. For instance, anchoring anti-HER2 single-chain antibody fragments to the exosomal membrane enables selective delivery of mRNA to HER2-positive breast cancer cells, while reducing off-target effects. 
In addition to these biological advantages, the lipid composition of exosomes protects encapsulated mRNA from degradation by plasma nucleases, thereby supporting prolonged circulation and enabling repeated administration. Despite these favorable properties, the clinical translation of exosome-based delivery systems remains constrained by challenges related to scalable production and purification. Manufacturing complexity, batch-to-batch variability, and fluctuations in cargo loading contribute to inconsistencies in product performance. These limitations are further compounded by stringent storage requirements (−80°C) and technical barriers associated with emerging lyophilization strategies, which collectively increase manufacturing costs and hinder clinical implementation. Compared with LNPs and polymeric carriers, the intrinsic biological characteristics of exosomes confer advantages in terms of reduced immunogenicity; however, persistent constraints related to production scalability and cost-effectiveness continue to limit broader therapeutic applications.

## Outer membrane vesicles
OMVs are nanoscale vesicles (20–250 nm) that are naturally secreted by Gram-negative bacteria. They contain lipopolysaccharides and various pathogen-associated molecular patterns that confer both delivery and adjuvant properties. Through genetic engineering, OMVs can be modified to present RNA-binding proteins such as L7Ae and endosomal escape factors such as listeriolysin O on their surface. These modifications facilitate targeted delivery and efficient translation of box C/D-structured mRNA, thereby eliciting robust antigen-specific immune responses. 
Despite these attractive functional attributes, several translational considerations require further investigation. This system is amenable to large-scale production via bacterial fermentation; however, its clinical translation requires detoxification strategies to mitigate inflammatory risk. Despite this manufacturing advantage, the mRNA delivery efficiency of OMVs remains inferior to that of established LNP platforms, underscoring the need for further optimization. OMVs possess distinct strengths, including inherent scalability and intrinsic immunoadjuvant properties. Therefore, realizing their translational potential depends on continued efforts to enhance both delivery performance and safety, enabling OMV-based systems to compete with more mature delivery technologies.

## DNA nanostructures
Owing to their precise base-pairing properties and programmable three-dimensional architectures, DNA molecules have been extensively employed to construct stable and tunable nanoframeworks. Representative structures include DNA tetrahedra, DNA polyhedra, DNA origami, DNA hydrogels, and spherical nucleic acids. These designs allow atomic-level control over size, geometry, and rigidity, thereby enabling precise encapsulation and controlled release of nucleic acids. An illustrative example is a DNA hydrogel system composed solely of DNA and mRNA in which pH-responsive i-motif domains are incorporated into the framework to trigger mRNA release within the acidic endolysosomal environment, thereby achieving targeted intracellular delivery. 
DNA-based carriers exhibit high structural programmability and favorable biocompatibility. , Despite these advantages, their fabrication remains highly dependent on mRNA length and sequence characteristics and significant challenges persist regarding large-scale production and quality consistency. DNA nanostructures exemplify the potential for programmable and precisely controllable delivery; however, their structural complexity and limited manufacturing scalability continue to impede clinical translation.
Collectively, the ongoing evolution of nanocarrier technologies has been instrumental in advancing mRNA therapeutics beyond merely improving expression efficiency toward practical therapeutic applications. Within this expanding landscape, various delivery platforms offer complementary advantages. Lipid-based systems are technologically mature and readily translatable, whereas polymer-based carriers offer extensive structural and functional flexibility. Exosomes and bacterial vesicles closely resemble physiological systems in terms of immune compatibility, whereas DNA nanostructures represent a promising direction for programmable and precision-guided delivery. Considering these platform-specific characteristics, future research should prioritize reducing the immunogenicity of LNPs, while improving targeting specificity, enhancing the biosafety profiles of polymer-based carriers, addressing batch-consistency challenges in scalable exosome production, and accelerating the clinical translation of emerging delivery systems. Collectively, these advances are expected to promote clinical development of mRNA nanomedicines for antifibrotic therapy.

## Recent advances in mRNA nanocarrier delivery strategies
The primary objective of mRNA therapeutics is to achieve precise and controlled delivery of genetic information to specific tissues, thereby enabling localized expression of therapeutic proteins. However, this goal is hindered by several technical barriers, including limited in vivo mRNA stability, excessive immunogenicity, and inefficient organ-specific delivery. Recent advances in nanomaterials science and molecular pharmaceuticals have led to innovative strategies designed to overcome these challenges. Improvements in carrier-surface engineering, fine-tuning of physicochemical properties, and optimization of administration routes have broadened the therapeutic potential of mRNA nanomedicines, offering new opportunities for precision-based antifibrotic interventions ( ).

## Surface ligand modification and targeted design
At the carrier level, surface ligand modification represents a well-established and readily engineered targeting strategy. By conjugating small molecules, peptides, glycans, or monoclonal antibodies to nanoparticle surfaces, selective cellular uptake and tissue-directed biodistribution can be achieved through high-affinity interactions with target cell receptors. For instance, mannitol-modified LNPs significantly enhance mRNA accumulation in hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells. Similarly, LNPs functionalized with anti-CD5 antibodies enable efficient cardiac targeting of CAR-encoding mRNA following intravenous administration. In contrast, renal targeting can be facilitated by surface modifications incorporating megalin ligands, folate, or hyaluronic acid, which exploit the elevated receptor expression of proximal tubular epithelial cells to promote selective uptake. 
In addition to surface ligand engineering, fine-tuning the physicochemical properties of nanocarriers offers an additional strategy for optimizing in vivo biodistribution. Increasing the proportion of PEG lipids in LNP formulations extends circulation time and improves hepatocyte uptake, whereas adjusting particle size alters cellular distribution patterns. Building on these physicochemical optimizations, the selective organ targeting (SORT) strategy provides a systematic framework for organ-specific delivery. By incorporating controlled ratios of cationic or anionic helper lipids into conventional LNPs, formulations can be tailored to preferentially accumulate in organs, such as the lungs, spleen, heart, or kidneys, thereby advancing personalized delivery systems for diverse disease contexts. 

## Optimization of administration routes and localized delivery
The route of administration is a critical determinant of both biodistribution and therapeutic performance of mRNA therapeutics ( ). Intravenous injection remains the most common method for systemic delivery but is often constrained by plasma protein binding, mechanical stress, and immune recognition. In circulation, conventional LNPs tend to associate with apolipoprotein E (ApoE), leading to a pronounced liver-targeting effect due to the abundance of ApoE receptors on hepatocytes. To overcome this limitation, researchers have explored surface modifications and incorporated alternative lipid components to redirect nanoparticle distribution, achieving efficient delivery to extrahepatic organs such as the lungs, spleen, and heart. , 
Local delivery strategies offer complementary advantages by increasing mRNA concentration at the target site while minimizing systemic toxicity. Intramyocardial injection provides a direct and effective route that bypasses hepatic metabolism and enhances target protein expression. For instance, administration of VEGF-A mRNA dissolved in citrate buffer directly into the myocardium has been shown to promote angiogenesis and significantly reduce myocardial fibrosis. However, this approach requires technically demanding procedures, and repeated injections may cause localized tissue injury, necessitating further evaluation of its clinical feasibility.

## Exploration of novel delivery routes
Expanding beyond traditional systemic and local approaches, recent research has focused on non-invasive and sustained-release delivery strategies to improve the efficacy and safety of mRNA therapeutics. Intranasal administration exploits the dense capillary network and immune cell populations within the nasal mucosa, enabling direct uptake of LNP-mRNA formulations by local antigen-presenting cells and facilitating targeted delivery to the lungs. Aerosol inhalation represents another promising modality. The LNP-mRNA therapeutic MRT5005 (NCT03375047), developed for cystic fibrosis, has progressed to phase 1/2 clinical trials, with preliminary results demonstrating favorable safety, preserved lung function, and reduced inflammation. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of inhalable mRNA formulations for fibrotic lung diseases.
Hydrogel-based sustained-release systems have also emerged as compelling platforms for achieving localized and prolonged mRNA delivery. Incorporation of mRNA-loaded nanoparticles into biodegradable hydrogels permits controlled, site-specific release, thereby extending therapeutic duration and maintaining stable local concentrations, while limiting systemic exposure. This approach is particularly advantageous for chronic fibrotic lesions; a tunable release kinetic supports sustained tissue repair and remodeling. Collectively, these inhalable hydrogel-based platforms reflect a growing trend toward spatiotemporally controlled, organ-specific mRNA delivery.
Ongoing advancements in mRNA delivery technologies are gradually overcoming the long-standing challenge of balancing efficiency with precision targeting. Progress in ligand engineering, material design, administration route optimization, and sustained-release systems has significantly expanded the applicability of mRNA therapeutics to multiple organs and disease types ( ). As our understanding of organ microenvironments deepens and delivery systems continue to evolve, achieving precise spatiotemporal regulation of mRNA expression is expected to mark a major breakthrough in antifibrotic therapy, facilitating clinical translation.
 

## Recent advances in mRNA nanomedicines for antifibrotic therapy

## mRNA therapeutics targeting core fibrotic signaling pathways
Fibrosis is a multifactorial pathological process driven by the complex interplay among inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired tissue repair. The core mechanism involves persistent activation and reciprocal amplification of intercellular signaling networks. Extensive evidence demonstrates that signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad, NF-κB, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), and PI3K/Akt/mTOR act as central regulators of fibrosis across multiple organs, orchestrating fibroblast activation, chronic inflammation, epithelial repair, and metabolic homeostasis. , , , , , , Traditional small-molecule or antibody-based therapies typically target single targets, limiting their ability to modulate the intricate and multi-layered signaling cascades underlying fibrotic progression. , 
In contrast, mRNA nanomedicines are characterized by programmable sequences, transient and reversible expression, and the potential for simultaneous multi-target regulation. These features provide a flexible platform for restoring signaling homeostasis and preventing pathological remodeling. Such properties make mRNA therapeutics particularly well-suited for diseases involving interconnected signaling pathways, such as fibrosis, where multifactorial regulation is required to rebalance tissue homeostasis. 
At the molecular level, these signaling pathways are tightly interconnected through positive and negative feedback loops, forming a dynamic and self-regulatory network. Excessive ECM production driven by the TGF-β/Smad axis amplifies inflammatory signaling via NF-κB activation, whereas BMP signaling acts as a compensatory mechanism to counterbalance TGF-β activity and facilitate epithelial regeneration. Meanwhile, the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway contributes to fibrotic progression by promoting metabolic reprogramming and sustaining cellular energy production. Therefore, precise modulation of these interconnected pathways is essential not only to suppress pathological remodeling but also to restore cellular homeostasis and regenerative potential.
Consequently, mRNA nanomedicines that enable pathway-specific modulation have emerged as a rapidly advancing frontier in antifibrotic therapies. The following sections provide a systematic overview of the molecular mechanisms and recent mRNA-based therapeutic advancements targeting four major signaling axes: TGF-β/Smad, NF-κB, BMP, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR.

## TGF-β/Smad axis: The principal driver of fibroblast activation
The TGF-β/Smad pathway functions as a central regulator of both the initiation and persistence of fibrosis. Upon binding to its receptor complex (TGF-βRI/II), TGF-β triggers phosphorylation of Smad2/3, which subsequently forms a complex with Smad4. This complex translocates into the nucleus and activates the transcription of profibrotic genes, such as collagen type I alpha 1 chain (Col1a1), fibronectin 1 (Fn1), and TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 1 (Timp1), thereby driving fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation and promoting excessive ECM synthesis.
In a pulmonary fibrosis model, researchers employed low-inflammatory phosphatidylcholine-ursolic acid (P-UA) LNPs to deliver nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1 (NR1D1) mRNA. NR1D1, a circadian nuclear receptor, concurrently inhibits TGF-β/Smad-dependent transcription and suppresses NF-κB signaling, thereby enabling coordinated regulation of fibrotic and inflammatory pathways. This therapeutic approach markedly reduced Col1a1 and LOX expression, decreased the proportion of α-SMA-positive cells, and restored lung architecture to an almost normal state.
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that transient, controllable mRNA expression can reestablish signaling homeostasis without impairing physiological tissue repair. Such precise modulation provides a balanced therapeutic paradigm that integrates pathological inhibition with functional preservation, offering a promising strategy for the long-term management of chronic fibrosis.

## NF-κB axis: The key regulatory hub linking inflammation and fibrosis
The NF-κB signaling pathway serves as a central hub connecting chronic inflammation and fibrotic progression. Following tissue injury, DAMPs released from necrotic or stressed cells activate the TLR/MyD88 complex, which subsequently phosphorylates and activates the IκB kinase (IKK) complex. Activated IKK promotes the degradation of IκB, allowing NF-κB to translocate into the nucleus and initiate the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2. Persistent activation of this pathway establishes a self-amplifying feedback loop in which chronic inflammation continuously drives fibroblast activation and ECM deposition, thereby sustaining fibrotic remodeling.
To break this pathological feedback cycle, researchers have developed an LA5-LNP-based delivery system for A20 mRNA. 
A20, a deubiquitinating enzyme, suppresses NF-κB activation by removing K63-linked ubiquitin chains from the IKK complex, thereby attenuating downstream inflammatory signaling. Moreover, A20 downregulates lysophosphatidic acid receptor 1 (LPA1) signaling, which limits fibroblast migration and ECM synthesis. Treatment with A20 mRNA significantly decreased α-SMA expression and collagen deposition, effectively mitigating fibroblast activation and halting fibrosis.
Collectively, these findings demonstrate the potential of mRNA therapeutics to restore endogenous negative feedback regulation within inflammatory signaling networks. By selectively disrupting the inflammation-fibrosis amplification loop, while avoiding broad immunosuppression, this approach provides a physiologically balanced and targeted therapeutic strategy for managing chronic inflammation-induced fibrosis.

## BMP signaling: A balancing mechanism in regeneration and tissue repair
As a major branch of the TGF-β superfamily, the BMP signaling pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining epithelial differentiation and facilitating tissue regeneration. , Its downstream mediators, Smad1/5/8, form transcription-activating complexes with Smad4, thereby counteracting TGF-β-induced profibrotic transcription and limiting excessive ECM deposition. In bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, delivery of BMP4 mRNA restores suppressed BMP signaling, significantly reduces matrix accumulation, promotes differentiation of alveolar type II (AT2) epithelial cells, and enhances the regenerative capacity. Unlike inhibitory strategies that primarily block fibrotic pathways, BMP4 mRNA functions as a replacement therapy by reactivating endogenous regenerative signaling to reestablish tissue homeostasis. This regenerative approach may offer a potential strategy for reversing advanced organ fibrosis.

## PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis: A metabolic amplifier of fibrotic progression
The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is a major regulator of cellular energy metabolism and protein synthesis, and its persistent activation provides a crucial metabolic basis for the progression of fibrosis. Inflammatory mediators such as IL-13 and PDGF enhance Akt phosphorylation and activate the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), which subsequently promotes collagen production, inhibits autophagy, and contributes to tissue stiffening. Experimental studies have shown that delivery of DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein (DEPTOR) mRNA suppresses mTORC1 activity, reduces phosphorylated ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p-S6K) levels, and decreases α-SMA expression. Similarly, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) mRNA restores the inhibitory control of PI3K, thereby preventing excessive Akt activation. These findings suggest that mRNA therapeutics can influence both intracellular signaling and metabolic pathways, providing a multi-layered strategy to rebalance cellular energy metabolism and slow fibrotic remodeling.
Building on these mechanistic findings, mRNA nanomedicine-targeted major signaling pathways—including TGF-β/Smad, NF-κB, BMP, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR—have expanded the current understanding of antifibrotic therapy. These strategies establish an integrated framework linking signal modulation, metabolic regulation, and tissue repair. By reversibly and precisely modulating key signaling nodes, mRNA platforms facilitate coordinated inflammation resolution, metabolic adaptation, and regenerative responses, thereby establishing an antifibrotic paradigm that more closely reflects physiological repair processes.
Future investigations should aim to improve delivery systems to achieve tissue-specific targeting, accurate dose control, and long-term safety, which are the key requirements for translating mRNA therapeutics from experimental models to clinical practice. With continued refinement, mRNA-based therapies may emerge as a central strategy for reprogramming the fibrotic microenvironment and restoring tissue homeostasis.

## Organ-targeted mRNA nanomedicines in fibrosis modulation and repair
Although initiated by diverse insults and influenced by organ-specific microenvironments, fibrosis progresses through a convergent sequence of cellular and molecular events, including fibroblast activation, immune amplification, metabolic dysregulation, and excessive ECM deposition. The advent of mRNA nanomedicines has made it possible to couple intracellular signaling modulation with tissue-selective delivery, offering precise temporal and spatial control of therapeutic actions. Because of their transient and programmable nature, mRNA-based systems are well suited to complex fibrotic settings that demand synchronized regulation of multiple pathways. Evidence from studies on cardiac, renal, pulmonary, and hepatic fibrosis indicates their dual potential in promoting early intervention and restoring tissue structures ( ).
 

## Cardiac fibrosis
Cardiac fibrosis represents a common pathological endpoint of diverse cardiomyopathies and is characterized by excessive ECM accumulation and reduced myocardial compliance. Conventional approaches—such as chronic inhibition of TGF-β, Ang II, or NF-κB signaling—can reduce collagen deposition but may also impair essential reparative processes following acute myocardial injury, thereby increasing the risk of ventricular rupture.
With their short-lived expression, mRNA therapeutics offer a strategy to balance between repair and remodeling. In a myocardial infarction model, injection of cellular communication network factor 5 (CCN5) mRNA (100 μg per mouse) into the endocardium suppressed TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling within 24 h, resulting in approximately 50% reductions in α-SMA and Col1a1 expression, reduction in fibrosis, and improvement in ejection fraction. Mechanistically, CCN5 blocked CTGF transcription and modulated integrin signaling to prevent myofibroblast formation. Notably, no ventricular wall thinning or rupture occurred, suggesting that transient mRNA expression supported healing, while preventing chronic fibrosis. , 
In addition to direct signal modulation, mRNA delivery has been explored as a strategy for immune regulation. Using macrophage-targeted LNPs, researchers co-delivered mRNAs encoding FAP-specific CARs (FAP-CARs) and legumain, generating CAR macrophages directly within infarcted myocardium. These macrophages selectively cleared FAP-positive myofibroblasts, reduced collagen content by approximately 40%, and improved cardiac function ( A). These studies illustrate how mRNA therapeutics can integrate signaling control with immune reprogramming.
By complementing these molecular and immune strategies, recent studies have extended advanced mRNA technologies to tissue-engineered cardiac patches, highlighting an additional avenue for myocardial repair. These bioengineered constructs typically comprise a poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone)/gelatin composite nanofibrous membrane integrated with human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiac progenitor cells. Within this framework, transfection of patch-resident cells with chemically modified mRNA encoding insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) prior to transplantation enables localized and transient IGF1 expression at the injury site. This approach enhances transplanted cell survival and proliferation, promotes angiogenesis, attenuates scar formation, and improves cardiac function. Collectively, these findings support the emerging therapeutic paradigm in which mRNA-based interventions are strategically combined with biomaterial platforms to facilitate tissue regeneration and functional recovery.
Collectively, these advances underscore the versatility of mRNA therapeutics in modulating cardiac remodeling through complementary molecular, immunological, and bioengineering strategies. Future efforts should focus on optimizing LNP composition, such as incorporating myocardium-targeting ligands or refining ionizable lipid ratios, as well as systematically evaluating immune tolerance during repeated dosing.

## Renal fibrosis
Renal mRNA delivery presents distinctive spatial targeting challenges arising from the unique anatomical architecture of the kidneys. Compared with the heart and lungs, where coronary circulation and alveolar capillary networks permit the transport of LNPs within the 80–100 nm range to target tissues, the kidneys impose substantially stricter barriers. In particular, the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) restricts the passage of larger nanoparticles, resulting in the retention of conventional LNPs within glomerular capillaries and limiting their access to the tubular interstitium. Nanoparticles that are sufficiently small to traverse the GFB and enter the tubular lumen face additional constraints, including rapid urinary clearance and the requirement to cross the tubular epithelial barrier to reach the interstitial compartment. Consequently, efficient accumulation within the tubular interstitium remains a fundamental challenge in renal mRNA therapeutics.
Renal fibrosis is a central pathological driver of CKD progression and is characterized by tubular epithelial injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, and ECM deposition. Current therapeutic strategies, including renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors and corticosteroids, primarily slow disease progression but rarely reverse established fibrotic remodeling and are frequently associated with systemic adverse effects. In this context, mRNA-based therapeutics have emerged as promising alternatives due to their capacity for transient, programmable, and multi-target protein expression. These properties enable simultaneous modulation of profibrotic and inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly those regulated by TGF-β.
Building on this rationale, a cubosome-based delivery platform was developed to transport pseudouridine-modified relaxin-2 mRNA into renal tubular epithelial cells, aiming to counteract TGF-β-driven fibrogenic responses. Following translation, relaxin-2 activates RXFP1-dependent ERK/NO/cGMP signaling, thereby inhibiting TGF-β1-induced Smad2/3 phosphorylation and downstream profibrotic programs. In NRK-52E cells, relaxin-2 expression remained elevated for 48–96 h and was accompanied by a marked reduction in α-SMA expression, consistent with attenuated myofibroblast activation. Intravenous administration on days 1, 3, and 5 following UUO surgery significantly reduced renal interstitial collagen I deposition, F4/80+ macrophage infiltration, and proinflammatory cytokine expression. Histological analyses confirmed a pronounced attenuation of fibrotic lesions, supporting the therapeutic potential of relaxin-2 mRNA delivery ( B).
From a drug delivery perspective, the cubosome exhibited a mean particle size of 93.9 ± 1.3 nm following mRNA loading. Although this diameter exceeds the effective pore size of the GFB, renal accumulation via glomerular filtration is unlikely. Instead, nanoparticle deposition may occur by pathological alterations in microvascular permeability. In parallel, the pronounced positive surface charge (zeta potential +43.0 ± 2.5 mV) may enhance electrostatic interactions with tubular epithelial cells. Despite these favorable physicochemical properties, renal targeting efficiency remains suboptimal and is associated with off-target distribution to multi organs, underscoring the need for continued platform optimization. Although repeated dosing may sustain therapeutic concentrations, the long-term safety associated with potential tissue accumulation warrants careful evaluation. Future strategies should, therefore, focus on incorporating stimuli-responsive linkers or biodegradable polyester-based materials to enhance renal selectivity, while minimizing systemic toxicity. , When integrated with localized delivery approaches, such as renal artery microcatheter administration or kidney-specific ligand modification (e.g., megalin-targeting peptides), these refinements may enable more precise and durable therapeutic interventions. 

## Pulmonary fibrosis
Physiological barriers in the lungs differ significantly from those in the heart and kidneys. Although inhalation-based drug delivery can bypass the vascular endothelium, it must overcome airway mucus sequestration. Negatively charged LNPs are particularly susceptible to entrapment within mucus, limiting delivery, with only approximately 20% of the administered dose reaching the alveolar region. This limitation contrasts sharply with the size-dependent filtration barriers encountered in renal delivery.
Pulmonary fibrosis is characterized by persistent epithelial damage and oxidative stress, which drives fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition. Approved drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib can slow disease progression but rarely reverse tissue scarring. Furthermore, the dense mucus barrier and immune surveillance in the lungs hinder drug penetration.
In this context, mRNA nanomedicines, delivered via inhalation or localized administration, can achieve high local concentrations at the target site, while minimizing systemic toxicity. , For instance, in a bleomycin-induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis, a γ-aminobutyric acid-based ionizable lipid LNP, incorporating the lung surfactant dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, was used to co-deliver cytochrome b5 reductase 3 (CYB5R3) and BMP4 dual mRNAs. This treatment significantly alleviated AT2 cell senescence, inhibited fibroblast activation, and notably reduced collagen deposition and α-SMA expression. Additionally, lung function parameters, including lung compliance, tidal volume, and survival rate, improved. The formulation also demonstrated excellent biocompatibility in a repeated inhalation dosing regimen, with no significant liver toxicity or inflammatory response ( C). 
In a related study, P-UA LNPs incorporated ursolic acid into the lipid backbone to enhance vacuolar-type H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) activity. Delivery of mNR1D1 mRNA resulted in reduced IL-6 and TGF-β1 expression and a significant reduction in collagen deposition (approximately 70%). The low immunogenicity of these lipids enabled safe repeated inhalation. These findings highlight the potential of optimizing lipid chemistry and aerosol behavior to enhance pulmonary delivery, while maintaining a low-inflammatory environment. Future advancements should focus on improving mucus penetration, refining particle uniformity, and confirming long-term safety.

## Hepatic fibrosis
Although the liver is a natural target of LNPs, it presents a unique challenge. APOE-mediated uptake primarily directs mRNA to hepatocytes rather than to HSCs, which are principal drivers of fibrosis. This discrepancy between the preferred delivery target and the intended cellular target creates a significant barrier to the effective targeting of HSCs.
Hepatic fibrosis, which results from chronic viral, metabolic, or inflammatory injuries, is primarily driven by prolonged activation of HSCs. Although current antiviral and metabolic agents can slow disease progression, they rarely reverse fibrotic architecture. However, the inherent ability of the liver to accumulate nanoparticles has facilitated the rapid development of mRNA-based strategies for hepatic repair.
In this context, mRNA therapeutics have gained significant momentum. For example, in a carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine-induced model, LNP-delivered hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4A) mRNA (2 mg/kg) targeting hepatocytes restored hepatic transcriptional activity, as evidenced by the upregulation of cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), albumin (ALB), and paraoxonase 1 (PON1). This treatment resulted in a 50% reduction in alanine aminotransferase levels and a 30% decrease in hydroxyproline content. Additionally, HNF4A restores lipid and glucose homeostasis by re-establishing the hepatocyte metabolic-antioxidant network. It also induced macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype via the liver-derived PON1-CCL2 axis, indirectly inhibiting HSC activation and alleviating fibrosis.
In another study, AA3G LNPs were employed to deliver IL-11 scFv mRNA, achieving liver-specific IL-11 neutralization in a high-fat, high-cholesterol diet-induced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis fibrosis model. This approach significantly reduced hepatic steatosis, collagen deposition, and inflammation, restoring pathological liver conditions to near-normal levels ( D). 
Despite these advances, conventional LNPs predominantly accumulate in hepatocytes with limited uptake by HSCs. Enhancing tropism by targeting peptides directed against PDGFRβ or integrins, together with careful evaluation of long-term immune tolerance, will be critical for improving both the efficacy and safety of repeated dosing in therapeutic applications. , , 
Across organ systems, mRNA nanomedicines have significantly advanced antifibrotic therapy, shifting from signaling-level intervention to structural-level repair. Cardiac studies emphasize transient modulation and immune reprogramming, , renal studies focus on site-specific delivery, pulmonary models validate inhalable formulations for chronic inflammation, , and hepatic studies highlight coordinated metabolic and immune control. , 
However, the cross-organ comparisons presented above reveal several important limitations in the current research. Drug delivery strategies vary considerably across studies. For instance, the heart is primarily targeted through local injection or cardiac patch delivery, the lungs are targeted via inhalation, and the kidneys and liver are targeted via intravenous injection. These differences arise from the unique physiological barriers of each organ, the natural accumulation properties of LNPs, and variations in the efficiency of targeting delivery routes. Therefore, comparing effective doses across studies remains challenging.
Furthermore, the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, characterized by acute inflammatory responses, differs from the CCl4-induced liver fibrosis model, which represents a more gradual and chronic injury process. These models are likely to exhibit significant differences in inflammatory intensity, immune cell infiltration, and delivery-targeting efficiency. However, direct comparative studies are lacking. Additionally, the causal relationship between transient mRNA expression and sustained antifibrotic effects remains unclear. Although short-term repeated dosing in the lungs and kidneys has been reported to be safe, long-term dosing protocols (i.e., >3 months) for chronic disease management still require validation. These differences in experimental design, together with uncertainties regarding the underlying mechanisms, underscore the need for careful evaluation before translating preclinical findings into clinical applications.
Despite proof-of-concept studies in mouse models demonstrating the potential of mRNA nanomedicines for multi-organ fibrosis, evidence supporting clinical translation remains limited. To date, no mRNA therapies specifically targeting fibrosis in the heart, liver, kidneys, or lungs have received regulatory approval or advanced into clinical trials. Current clinical investigations have primarily focused on the respiratory system. For example, Arcturus Therapeutics’ ARCT-032 (CFTR mRNA-LNP), which is currently undergoing phase 2 trials for cystic fibrosis, has shown improvements in mucus clearance; however, its benefits on lung function have been modest and have not consistently met predefined clinical endpoints. Similarly, Translate Bio’s MRT5005 failed to demonstrate consistent clinical efficacy. In the liver, clinical safety data from metabolic diseases, such as propionic acidemia, provide proof of concept; however, no mRNA-based therapies targeting liver fibrosis have entered clinical trials. In addition, no mRNA therapies specifically targeting cardiac or renal fibrosis have progressed to human clinical trials. This translational gap in clinical translation underscores the differences between animal models and human pathology and highlights the need to develop long-term intervention evaluation systems that better reflect clinical realities.
Developing quantitative metrics for delivery efficiency and immune tolerance and integrating mRNA with small interfering RNA (siRNA), small molecules, or cell-based therapies may enable the creation of multidimensional antifibrotic strategies.

## Challenges and future directions
The emergence of mRNA nanomedicines has introduced advanced therapeutic strategies for the treatment of fibrosis. However, their clinical application requires further comparison with established therapies. Compared with siRNAs, antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9), mRNA therapeutics differ significantly in the duration of action, depth of intervention, and applicability across different disease stages. Although these differences highlight the unique advantages of mRNA-based treatments, they present significant challenges for clinical translation.
siRNAs and ASOs achieve sustained gene silencing by sequence-specific degradation of target mRNA, enabling long-term suppression of pathogenic signals in advanced fibrosis. For example, siRNA targeting HSP47, co-delivered with nintedanib in LNPs, led to a 70% reduction in hydroxyproline levels in a liver fibrosis model. Similarly, cryopreserved monocytes engineered to deliver CTGF-targeted ASO, combined with nintedanib, reduced hydroxyproline levels to near-normal values in a pulmonary fibrosis model. These treatments exert prolonged effects lasting weeks to months, consistent with the chronic activation of fibrotic pathways such as TGF-β and CTGF. CRISPR-based gene editing offers permanent genetic correction. For instance, the inhalable delivery of a CRISPR-Cas9 system targeting secreted phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1) in pulmonary fibrosis results in a gene mutation rate exceeding 30%. However, off-target effects and irreversibility limit the broader application of this approach.
In contrast, mRNA therapies offer the advantages of transient and reversible protein expression, making them ideal for early intervention and acute tissue repair. However, mRNA expression is typically limited to several days, necessitating repeated dosing to maintain therapeutic efficacy. This requirement raises concerns regarding immune tolerance and patient compliance, particularly in advanced fibrosis. Accordingly, therapies should be tailored according to the disease stage. mRNA-based approaches may be most effective as early interventions aimed at blocking pathological initiation, whereas siRNA and ASO therapies are better suited for chronic suppression during later stages. By contrast, CRISPR-Cas-9-based gene editing holds promise for the treatment of inherited genetic diseases. Looking ahead, sequential combination strategies—such as early mRNA intervention to interrupt the inflammation-fibrosis cascade, followed by long-term siRNA/ASO silencing, and possibly gene editing—may represent an optimized framework for long-term disease management. However, the successful implementation of this strategy remains challenging because of issues related to delivery efficiency, immune safety, and large-scale production.

## Precision targeting and biodistribution constraints
The primary routes of administration for mRNA therapeutics include intravenous and local injections. Although these methods have shown antifibrotic effects in animal models and early clinical trials, they face two main challenges. First, mRNA formulations often accumulate in the liver, limiting their exposure to other organs such as the heart, lungs, and kidneys. Second, even minor variations in the manufacturing process can amplify immune and functional risks. Following intravenous injection, LNPs often bind to ApoE, leading to its accumulation in the liver. In liver fibrosis models, most mRNA is directed to hepatocytes rather than fibrotic HSCs, creating a targeting mismatch. This issue extends beyond the liver and is common in various types of organ fibrosis. For example, in pulmonary fibrosis, LNPs are often trapped by airway mucus. In renal fibrosis, GFB limits drug access to the renal tubular interstitial space, thereby preventing effective delivery to myofibroblasts. During cardiac fibrosis, dense collagen matrices impede fibroblast uptake of therapeutic nanoparticles. Additionally, the fibrotic microenvironment varies substantially across organs, creating distinct physical and chemical barriers to drug delivery systems. To address these limitations, researchers have optimized delivery systems by adjusting the ratio of ionizable lipids to PEG lipids, incorporating organ-selective lipids (SORT lipids), and modifying surface ligands with peptides to improve targeting. For instance, altering lipid charge and chain length can control organ-specific delivery, while incorporating integrin or glycopeptide ligands enhances targeting to myocardial and renal tubular tissues.
Despite advancements in organ-targeting strategies, fibrosis treatment requires long-term intervention. Invasive injections not only decrease patient compliance but also increase the risk of infection and tissue damage. As a result, non-invasive delivery methods such as transdermal, oral, inhalation, and mucosal routes have become key research areas. Among these, aerosolized inhalation is regarded as the most promising non-invasive approach. Inhaled mRNA-LNPs have entered phase 1/2 clinical trials for cystic fibrosis, demonstrating favorable safety and feasibility profiles. However, negatively charged LNPs tend to be trapped by airway mucus, with approximately 20% of the administered dose reaching the alveolar region. Furthermore, the short half-life of mRNA necessitates frequent dosing, and approximately one-third of the participants reported mild reactions, such as fever or urticaria-like symptoms, likely due to innate immune recognition of unmodified mRNA or direct stimulation by lipid components. 
Recent research on LNPs indicates that ionizable lipids can activate TLR4, triggering NF-κB activation through MyD88-dependent pathways, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. , Additionally, LNP endosomal rupture or mRNA itself may activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) or retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)/mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) pathways, exacerbating inflammation. Repeated dosing may also result in the generation of anti-PEG antibodies, which accelerate blood clearance and reduce drug efficacy. However, the immunogenicity of mRNA can be significantly reduced through nucleoside modifications and UTR optimization. Therefore, future mRNA nanomedicines must balance therapeutic efficacy and safety through a dual strategy of mRNA chemical modification and LNP formulation optimization.

## Endosomal escape as a dominant intracellular bottleneck
Although mRNA therapeutics can evade systemic immune recognition and reach target cells, efficient intracellular delivery remains a major determinant of therapeutic efficacy. Without effective endosomal escape and cytoplasmic release, mRNA translation is inherently constrained. Current evidence indicates that only 1%–2% of mRNA delivered by conventional LNPs reaches the cytoplasm. Even with optimized formulations, endosomal escape efficiency rarely exceeds 15%. Consequently, most internalized mRNA is either degraded through lysosomal pathways or removed via endosomal recycling mechanisms.
At the mechanistic level, endosomal escape is critically governed by the physicochemical properties of LNPs, particularly the compatibility between the pKa of ionizable lipids and endosomal acidification dynamics. An optimal pKa range of approximately 6.0–6.6 promotes lipid protonation during early endosomal acidification, thereby enhancing electrostatic interactions with endosomal membranes and facilitating membrane destabilization and fusion. Deviations from this range compromise delivery efficiency. Lipids with excessively low pKa values may trigger premature dissociation in circulation, whereas those with higher pKa values can limit protonation, resulting in endosomal retention and mRNA degradation.
Beyond lipid ionization behavior, cellular context further shapes the delivery outcomes. Endosomal acidification dynamics and V-ATPase activity vary across cell types, influencing both the kinetics of endosomal maturation and the effective window for cargo escape. In parallel, incorporation of cholesterol analogs such as β-sitosterol can modulate LNP architecture, thereby enhancing membrane fusion and promoting cytosolic mRNA release. , Together, these findings underscore the need for next-generation delivery systems that combine controlled escape mechanisms with biodegradable structural frameworks, enabling improved therapeutic efficacy, while preserving safety.

## Network-level regulation and therapeutic integration
To overcome the delivery and endosomal escape bottlenecks outlined above, research efforts are increasingly moving beyond single-parameter optimization toward multidimensional and dynamic regulatory strategies. This shift is largely motivated by the intrinsic redundancy of fibrotic signaling networks, which limits the efficacy of single-target interventions. For instance, inhibition of TGF-β signaling may provoke compensatory activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, whereas NF-κB blockade may inadvertently impair inflammation-associated repair processes.
Advances in systems biology and multiomics have facilitated the rational design of combinatorial mRNA therapeutic strategies. Such approaches encompass the simultaneous modulation of TGF-β, NF-κB, and PI3K pathways, as well as the coordinated expression of antifibrotic and pro-repair factors, thereby enabling cross-pathway complementarity and functional rebalancing. Moreover, mRNA therapeutics can be strategically integrated with siRNAs, protein-based agents, or cell-based therapies, allowing for layered interventions that operate through mechanistically distinct modes of action. Collectively, these synergistic precision strategies have the potential to reshape antifibrotic therapy by shifting the prevailing paradigm from isolated pathway inhibition to dynamic regulatory control and tissue regeneration.

## Manufacturing robustness and translational scalability
The clinical translation of mRNA nanomedicines depends not only on biological performance but also on stringent control of physicochemical attributes and manufacturing robustness. Within this framework, microfluidic mixing represents a pivotal step in LNP assembly. Parameters such as flow rate ratio (FRR) and total flow rate (TFR) regulate the interplay between interfacial mixing and molecular diffusion. Through these mechanisms, FRR and TFR critically determine key formulation characteristics, including particle size distribution index and mRNA encapsulation efficiency. , 
Given this strong parameter dependence, deviations in TFR can markedly impair formulation quality. Under low-TFR settings, extended diffusion distances delay electrostatic complexation between mRNA and ionizable lipids relative to phase separation, thereby favoring the precipitation of unencapsulated mRNA within ethanol-rich domains. Conversely, an excessively high TFR may generate local shear stresses that exceed the curvature elasticity tolerance of lipid bilayers, promoting non-lamellar phase defects and compromising structural integrity. Taken together, these pronounced process sensitivities underscore the inherent difficulty of maintaining formulation consistency and explain the reproducibility challenges encountered when translating laboratory-optimized systems to good manufacturing practice-compliant manufacturing.
Beyond manufacturing considerations, long-term storage stability constitutes an additional barrier to clinical translation, particularly for chronic conditions, such as fibrosis, that require sustained treatment. Most aqueous formulations rely on ultra-low-temperature cold-chain storage, which inherently limits scalability and broad accessibility. , Although lyophilization offers a viable strategy to reduce cold-chain dependence, current stabilization mechanisms remain suboptimal. Lyoprotectants preserve LNP structural integrity through glass transition effects; however, nonlinear interactions between protectant concentration and zeta potential may increase the risk of particle aggregation. Moreover, freeze-drying and subsequent reconstitution cycles can induce lipid phase transitions from lamellar to hexagonal or cubic structures, thereby compromising membrane fusion efficiency with endosomal membranes. Importantly, these physicochemical vulnerabilities may be further amplified during long-term antifibrotic therapy, in which elevated levels of ROS and reactive aldehydes promote the oxidation of unsaturated lipids and accelerate chemical degradation. 
In summary, the future development of mRNA nanomedicines will rely not only on incremental refinements in carrier design but also on the convergence of materials science, immunology, and translational medicine. Rather than representing isolated technical hurdles, challenges in manufacturing precision and formulation stability collectively influence translational feasibility. Within this evolving therapeutic landscape, mRNA-based strategies are unlikely to replace established antifibrotic therapies. Instead, they are poised to complement existing approaches by enabling controllable protein expression, precise tissue targeting, multipathway regulation, reduced immunogenicity, and compatibility with repeated long-term administration. As delivery technologies continue to advance and safety frameworks become increasingly robust, mRNA nanomedicines are expected to progress beyond experimental validation toward clinical application, thereby supporting a meaningful transition from fibrosis suppression to tissue repair and regeneration ( ).
 

## Conclusion
mRNA nanomedicines are reshaping the therapeutic landscape of antifibrotic interventions. Their distinctive properties, including nonintegrative activity, programmability, and transient yet precisely controllable protein expression, enable functional protein replacement, modulation of the immune microenvironment, cellular reprogramming, and facilitation of tissue repair. Consistent with these capabilities, preclinical studies across multiple organ fibrosis models, particularly in the liver, heart, and lungs, have demonstrated attenuation of pathological remodeling accompanied by improvements in functional outcomes. In contrast to conventional small-molecule therapies that primarily rely on pathway inhibition, mRNA-based strategies operate through the targeted, transient expression of functional proteins or regulatory factors, thereby providing a mechanistically distinct approach to restoring and maintaining tissue homeostasis.
Future progress in this field is likely to advance in three interconnected dimensions: delivery technologies, therapeutic strategies, and clinical translation. Organ- and cell-type-specific targeting is essential for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity. Although passive, active, and physicochemical targeting approaches have expanded the possibilities for precise delivery, balancing specificity with safety remains a major challenge. Overlapping receptor profiles between pathological and healthy cells, together with the complexity of fibrotic microenvironments, underscores the need for more refined targeting paradigms. Therefore, emerging strategies may emphasize multimodal targeting systems that integrate ligand recognition with responsiveness to microenvironmental cues, such as pH and redox conditions. In parallel, high-throughput screening and single-cell analytical technologies may facilitate the identification of optimal delivery parameters for heterogeneous fibrotic lesions.
Another critical consideration is the immune compatibility and therapeutic durability. The transient nature of mRNA expression, typically lasting 1–2 days, necessitates repeated administration to sustain therapeutic effects; however, long-term safety profiles remain to be fully established. Furthermore, lipid components and other excipients may contribute to mild inflammatory responses. Addressing these limitations requires continued optimization of carrier materials with improved biocompatibility, together with the development of RNA engineering strategies. Novel RNA formats, including self-amplifying RNA and circular RNA, offer promising opportunities to extend protein expression, reduce dosing frequency, and enhance therapeutic stability.
At the therapeutic level, mRNA programmability enables coordinated regulation of multiple molecular pathways and cellular processes. Integration with small-molecule agents, RNA interference technologies, or gene-editing systems may support the development of combinatorial and sequential intervention strategies. Such approaches have the potential to achieve more comprehensive modulation of fibrotic progression by simultaneously targeting inflammation, ECM remodeling, and tissue repair mechanisms.
Clinical translation remains a decisive determinant of the ultimate impact of mRNA nanomedicine. Progress toward routine clinical implementation requires systematic evaluation of formulation stability, manufacturing reproducibility, patient adherence, and long-term safety. Non-invasive and patient-compatible delivery strategies, such as inhalation and transdermal administration, have gained increasing attention but impose stringent physicochemical requirements on formulation design. In addition to technological considerations, the balance between production costs and clinical accessibility plays a pivotal role in shaping the scalability and real-world applicability of mRNA-based therapies.
Collectively, mRNA nanomedicines offer a versatile and controllable platform for therapeutic protein expression, whereas engineered carrier systems enable organ-selective targeting, as demonstrated in multiple preclinical fibrosis models. Nevertheless, substantial challenges remain, including limited organ-specific delivery efficiency, insufficient expression durability, and a lack of robust long-term safety data. Continued advances in delivery system design, RNA engineering, and translational validation are, therefore, essential to fully define the therapeutic potential of mRNA-based interventions for fibrotic diseases.
