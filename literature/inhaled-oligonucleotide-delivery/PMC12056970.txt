## Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the urgency of developing effective vaccines to combat infectious diseases, especially in vulnerable populations such as the elderly and immunocompromised. While recombinant protein vaccines offer safety, their poor immunogenicity highlights the need for advanced vaccination platforms. New genetic/nucleic acid vaccine formulations like plasmid DNA and mRNA showed efficiency and safety in preclinical and clinical studies; however, they demand innovative adjuvants because their mechanism of action differs from traditional protein vaccines. Genetic adjuvants—encoded by nucleic acids within DNA, RNA, or viral vectors—emerge as a promising solution by targeting and modulating specific immune pathways, including antigen presentation, T cell activation, and memory formation. These innovative adjuvants enhance vaccine efficacy by fine-tuning innate and adaptive immune responses, overcoming immune senescence, and addressing the challenges of CD8+ T cell activation in immunocompromised populations. This review explores the potential of genetically encoded adjuvants, including cytokines, chemokines, and other immune modulators. By comparing these adjuvants to traditional formulations, we highlight their capacity to address the limitations of modern vaccines while discussing their integration with emerging technologies like RNA-based vaccines. As genetic adjuvants advance toward clinical application, understanding their mechanisms and optimizing their delivery is pivotal to unlocking next-generation immunization strategies.

## Introduction
The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the extreme importance of preventing, controlling, and treating infectious diseases. Vaccination remains one of the most potent prevention and treatment approaches. Besides the quality of the provided antigen, vaccination efficiency strongly depends on the individual characteristics of the immune system. Considering the general life span prolongation and aging of the population, the efficiency and safety of vaccines in elderly and immunocompromised people must be improved. The use of recombinant purified protein/peptide subunit vaccines provides a modern and safe option but has poor immunogenicity due to the removal of danger signals important for innate immune system activation. This hampers the use of subunit vaccines in people with impaired immune functions. Similarly, innovative nucleic-acid-based vaccines, such as plasmid DNA and mRNA, suffer from low and unpredictable efficacy. Thus, new vaccine formulations require principally new adjuvants.
Achievements in understanding innate immune system function and adaptive immune response initiation provide a background for developing new vaccine formulations. New vaccines incorporating novel, tailored adjuvants must be developed to modulate type II T helper (Th2) and type I T helper (Th1) immune responses effectively, address immune senescence in the elderly, enhance robust T cell activation, and prolong immune memory for sustained protection. These vaccines should promote the formation of long-lived plasma cells, broaden the antibody repertoire, and strengthen the overall immune response. Inducing a high-quality B cell antibody response and generating follicular T helper cell (Tfh) populations are also crucial. Achieving an effective CD8+ T cell response (Th1) is essential for long-lasting immunity and the efficacy of therapeutic or anti-parasitic vaccines, yet remains a significant challenge in current vaccine formulations. Future research should clarify and optimize the parameters of antigen and adjuvant distribution and persistence, which are critical for relevant memory B and T cell formation.
The potent immune-stimulating proteins, such as cytokines, chemokines, and other proteins or peptides, represent the natural class of molecules able to enhance DNA and RNA vaccines. The incorporation of these protein genes into the DNA/RNA vectors could significantly improve genetic vaccines by targeting the regulation of the immune response. In this review, we have analyzed the nucleic-acid-based or genetically encoded adjuvants applied within DNA/RNA and virus-based vaccines. We have discussed their advantages compared to the traditional adjuvant types and considered their clinical prospects as components of advanced vaccine formulations.

## Current adjuvant types and their classification
This section provides a short overview of the adjuvant types in current vaccinology. Adjuvants are vaccine preparation additives that help to enhance and direct the immune response against the introduced antigen. These are very diverse materials of different sources, and physicochemical structures, utilizing broad mechanisms of action, reviewed elsewhere, e.g., in Fan et al. , , 
By mechanism of action, the adjuvants can be divided into (1) immune stimulants, which enhance the immune response to antigens through direct modulation of immune cells and (2) agents stimulating antigen delivery, processing, and recognition ( ). Importantly, the mechanism of action for both existing and newly developed adjuvants is not yet fully understood and requires further investigation. Consequently, there is no standardized classification of adjuvants based on their impact on the immune system. Moreover, adjuvants exert complex effects that are not limited to a single mechanism. Nonetheless, a schematic subdivision of vaccine adjuvants based on their mode of action can be proposed as follows:
a.Immune cell stimulation1.Immune cell communication molecules: cytokines, chemokines, co-stimulatory molecules, and immune checkpoint regulators. Due to their systemic toxicity and short half-life, cytokines are mostly used as the genes introduced within DNA vaccines or therapeutic virus vector vaccines.
2.Danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs): metabolic stress signals produced in result of tissue damage and cell death, such as ATP, extracellular DNA, and mitochondrial RNA. These signals likely contribute to the activation of immune responses when released in response to compounds such as alum (mineral salts), lipids, mineral oils, saponins, and other cytotoxic substances, used as adjuvants. 
3.Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or pattern recognition receptor (PRR) ligands are represented mostly by pathogenic microorganism derivatives such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists and non-TLR (C-type lectin receptors, NOD-like receptors, RIG-I-like receptors, stimulator of interferon genes) activators, e.g., flagellin, lipolplysaccharide (LPS), and CpG DNA motifs. 

bAntigen delivery and recognition1.Mucosal barrier and connective tissue permeability enhancers: hormones such as relaxin, enzymes such as papain, and bacterial enterotoxins, e.g., cholera toxin. In particular, bacterial enterotoxins bind to specific mucosal cell receptors—gangliosides and fucosylated glycoconjugates—assisting membrane barrier transport. It must be mentioned that the effect of these adjuvants is complex and is not restricted to mechanistic mucosal barrier/tissue permeability changes only but also involves immune cell stimulation.
2.Antigen presentation to the immune system: (1) protein stabilization/dispersion, protein aggregation prevention, which is important for antigen distribution, slow release, and uptake by immune cells, e.g., oil emulsions, mineral particles, and saponins; (2) specific support of antigen epitope presentation by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) through proteasomal degradation or stimulation of immune protein complex development, e.g., heat shock proteins (HSPs) and ubiquitin. 
3.Particulate antigen delivery vehicles: liposomes, virus-like particles (VLPs), and polymer nanoparticles. Again, the effect, e.g., of VLPs, is not only restricted to antigen transport but also includes the stimulation of innate immune response as a PAMP.

 Despite the clear need for new, efficient, and safe immune stimulants, the repertoire of clinically approved adjuvants remains very limited, with most being based on aluminum (aluminum salts and aluminum hydroxide), emulsion-based compounds, saponins, and liposomal formulations such as MF59 (Novartis), AS01, AS02, and AS04 (GSK), AF03 (Sanofi Pasteur), SE and derivatives (Infectious Disease Research Institute), ISA720 and ISA51 (Seppic), and Matrix-M (Novavax). , These adjuvants cannot be delivered genetically and are not optimal for the new class of nucleic-acid-based vaccine because of different antigen delivery and presentation principles. The only clinically approved nucleic-acid-based adjuvant is the CpG oligonucleotide used in the HBV vaccine (Dynavax) that can be delivered as nucleic acid integrated into a genetic vaccine, despite not encoding any immune-stimulating peptides/proteins. Thus, we also included non-coding nucleic-acid-based adjuvants recognized by the innate immune system in this review. Here, we define the key features and prospects of genetically encoded adjuvants for genetic vaccines.
Genetic vaccines are encoded by nucleic acids, either RNA or DNA, or by a viral vector. The main challenges of the DNA vaccines are inadequate targeting and poor immunogenicity due to the absence of PAMPs or DAMPs signals from the produced antigen. On the other hand, in contrast to traditional protein-based vaccines, DNA has the advantage of inducing prolonged antigen expression and immune stimulation, which can compensate the relatively low levels of protein expression from the plasmid. Therefore, the sustained antigen presentation may contribute to the durability and effectiveness of DNA vaccines, which can co-express immune-stimulating proteins as adjuvants.
RNA vaccines have the advantage of direct protein production in the cell cytoplasm, omitting the step of transcription in the cell nucleus in contrast to DNA, as well as there is no risk of random integration into the cell genome. However, to be efficient, a high number of RNA copies must be introduced into the cell, which is technically challenging. RNA-virus-based vectors delivering self-replicating recombinant RNAs represent a promising alternative to increase the efficiency of delivered RNA. The virus vector itself can work as an adjuvant elaborating the innate immune response through PAMP recognition. However, the design and production of viral particles are much more complex, time-consuming, and expensive than plasmid DNA or mRNA vaccines.
New generation of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines was the first successful use of this approach for worldwide vaccination. mRNA technology allows for rapid vaccine development and adaptation to new variants, which is crucial in responding to evolving infectious diseases like COVID-19. Two lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)–mRNA vaccines for COVID-19, mRNA-1273 (Moderna) and BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech), showed surprising efficiency in respect of antibody response in comparison to traditional inactivated virus or viral subunit vaccines. COVID-19 mRNA vaccines were formulated with LNPs, which induce the activation of the innate immune system and are employed not only as delivery vehicles but also as adjuvants. This activation leads to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), which are key mediators of inflammation. In turn, in the development of adenovirus-vector-based COVID-19 vaccines, additional adjuvants were deemed unnecessary due to the inherent immunogenicity of the adenovirus (Ad) vectors themselves. Despite modifications aimed at reducing high immunogenicity of adenovirus capsid proteins, these vectors retain significant immune-stimulating properties. These properties have been exploited in approved Ad-vector-based COVID-19 vaccines such as AstraZeneca’s ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, Johnson & Johnson’s Ad26.COV2.S, Sputnik V (Ad26/Ad5), and CanSinoBIO’s Ad5-nCoV.
Genetic vaccine adjuvants offer a versatile approach by employing DNA, RNA, or viral vectors for delivery. The main targets of genetically encoded adjuvants cover broad immune processes, including (I) immune cell chemotaxis, involving the attraction of immune cells to the site of antigen encounter; (II) dendritic cell (DC) maturation, facilitating the optimal presentation of antigens; (III) antigen presentation, where the processed antigens are displayed to immune cells for recognition; (IV) T cell activation, leading to the stimulation of T cells for a robust immune response; and (V) B cell maturation and immunoglobulin (Ig) isotype switching, essential for the generation of specific antibodies ( ). Encoded by gene vectors, the adjuvants can effectively target various steps of the immune response. By strategically influencing these key immune processes, genetic vaccine adjuvants have the potential to enhance the overall efficacy and specificity of vaccines, paving the way for advanced and tailored immunization strategies.
 The genes of cytokines, chemokines, and other immune signaling proteins/peptides can be easily introduced into nucleic acid vaccines to enhance the host response toward the desired balance of Th1-cell-mediated and Th2 humoral immune responses.
Numerous genes have been expressed as experimental genetic adjuvants, most of which are involved in innate immune signaling and immune cell communication. Below, we have summarized the preclinical studies of the promising proteins applied as genetic adjuvants for vaccines against human and animal pathogens. These proteins include cytokines, chemokines, TLR signaling proteins/peptides, and other molecules genetically expressed to manage and improve immune response to vaccination.

## Immune cell communication molecules

## Cytokines
Cytokines, as signaling molecules, play a vital role in immune responses by regulating immune cell functions and facilitating communication between different cell types. They contribute to the coordination of immune responses, including cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Cytokines also regulate inflammation by promoting immune cell recruitment and modulating cytokine production, ultimately maintaining immune homeostasis and supporting effective immune defences against infections and immune-related diseases. ## Granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor
Due to its capacity to directly influence DC formation, recruit antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to the antigen synthesis site, and promote DC maturation, granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) is a compelling adjuvant for DNA vaccines. Furthermore, the DNA-expressing GM-CSF was successfully employed as an adjuvant for cancer vaccines. 
The effectiveness of using GM-CSF cDNA as an adjuvant was demonstrated for a DNA vaccine targeting the glycoprotein B (gB) of pseudorabies virus (PRV) in a murine model. Co-injection of GM-CSF DNA resulted in a notable enhancement of PRV-specific IgG levels, and amplified T-cell-mediated immunity with a Th1-type bias, as evidenced by significantly higher levels of cytokines interleukin (IL)-2 and interferon γ (IFN-γ), but not IL-4. Importantly, when mice were challenged with a lethal dose of PRV, co-inoculation with a GM-CSF-expressing vector enhanced protective immunity.
GM-CSF plasmid (pGM-CSF) was employed in vaccination against HIV-1. Mahdavi et al. immunized mice with an HIV-1 tat/pol/gag/env DNA vaccine along with GM-CSF as an adjuvant and then boosted with a recombinant vaccine. The results showed that GM-CSF significantly increased lymphocyte proliferation, the production of IFN-γ and IL-17, as well as antibody levels. Moreover, Santana et al. developed a DNA vaccine containing multiple conserved HIV-1 subtype B peptides that bind to human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR. The results indicate that co-immunization with pGM-CSF led to an increase in proliferating CD4+ T cells that produced a combination of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 cytokines.
Furthermore, to enhance GM-CSF adjuvant activity, a new codon-optimized murine GM-CSF gene was developed. Although the injection of wild-type GM-CSF plasmids as an adjuvant for the HIV-1 gag DNA vaccine resulted in detectable immune responses, the co-administration of plasmids carrying the codon-optimized GM-CSF sequence with the DNA vaccine led to much stronger antibody and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses. This combination also resulted in a protective immune response against infection with recombinant vaccinia virus expressing HIV-1 gag.
In other studies, GM-CSF was delivered with Ad vectors. Thus, the potential immunostimulatory adjuvant activity of recombinant chicken (ch) GM-CSF expressed by adenovirus (rchGM-CSF) in an inactivated Newcastle disease virus (NDV) vaccine was investigated. The inclusion of rchGM-CSF adjuvant significantly enhanced the NDV-specific hemagglutinin-inhibition titers and upregulated the production of IFN-α/-β/-γ, IL-4, as well as MHC II. The use of the adjuvant significantly reduced clinical signs, tissue viral loads, tissue lesions, mortality, and viral shedding compared to the group that received the vaccine alone. In another study, adenovirus encoding murine GM-CSF was also used as an adjuvant with the recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) carrying the human papillomavirus (HPV) 16 L1 protein gene, allowing it to form VLPs in transduced cells. Co-vaccination of rAAV-16L1 and rAd-mGM-CSF resulted in comparable higher levels of neutralizing antibodies than those induced by the VLP vaccine.
Recombinant rabies virus (RABV) strain LBNSE expressing “GM-CSF” significantly enhanced the recruitment and activation of DCs (upregulation of CD86) and B cells at the site of infection, leading to elevated levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies. A single immunization with this recombinant virus provided superior protection against intracerebral challenge with virulent RABV compared to the parental virus, highlighting GM-CSF’s potential as an immunostimulatory adjuvant in vaccine development. For oral vaccination in a dog model, GM-CSF was incorporated into an LBNSE (LBNSE-dGM-CSF). Dogs vaccinated orally with LBNSE-dGM-CSF exhibited significantly greater activation of DCs (CD80 upregulation) and B cells in peripheral blood, along with higher levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies, compared to those vaccinated with LBNSE.
To enhance the immunogenicity of self-amplifying mRNAs (SAMs) derived from a modified alphavirus single-stranded (ss) RNA genome against the influenza virus, murine GM-CSF was incorporated. The administration of SAM-GM-CSF to mice effectively increased the recruitment of APCs at the injection site. As expected, co-administration of SAM-GM-CSF with SAM-NP (influenza A virus nucleoprotein) led to a significant improvement in the magnitude of the NP-specific CTL response and increased protection of mice against a lethal challenge with the influenza virus.

## G-CSF
Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) plays a key role in the development, proliferation, differentiation, survival, and functional activation of neutrophils and their precursors, contributing to innate immune responses and hematopoiesis. A recombinant virus expressing feline G-CSF was constructed as a vaccine against feline herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1). The inclusion of the adjuvant enhanced the production of neutralizing antibodies and neutrophils, leading to a significant reduction in clinical symptoms following FHV-1 infection.

## Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand
Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt-3L) is a growth factor stimulating hematopoietic progenitors in the bone marrow and is also important for DC and natural killer (NK) cell differentiation and survival. The potential of plasmid DNA encoding GM-CSF, Flt-3L, and IL-12 alone, or in combination, to act as adjuvants and enhance immune responses elicited by prototype DNA vaccines were tested. The co-administration of plasmid-based murine GM-CSF, Flt-3L, and IL-12 significantly boosted the cell-mediated immune response triggered by an HIV-1 env DNA vaccine. Additionally, the plasmid mGM-CSF enhanced immune responses induced by DNA vaccines encoding HIV-1 gag and nef-tat-vif. Furthermore, the use of pmGM-CSF as a vaccine adjuvant substantially increased antigen-specific proliferative responses and improved the quality of the T cell response by increasing the percentage of polyfunctional memory CTLs. Interestingly, co-delivering pmFlt-3L with pmGM-CSF did not result in a further increase in adjuvant activity. However, the co-administration of pmGM-CSF with pmIL-12 significantly enhanced env-specific proliferative responses and vaccine efficacy in the murine vaccinia virus challenge model compared to mice immunized with the env DNA vaccine adjuvanted with either pmGM-CSF or pmIL-12 alone.
In another study, a tumor model of human hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection was established using syngeneic mouse myeloma cells transfected with the HCV non-structural protein NS5, which was then employed for DNA vaccination targeting the NS5 protein. The authors demonstrated an increase in antiviral antibody titers after co-vaccination of DNA Flt-3L together with a plasmid encoding HCV NS5, which was further enhanced by co-immunization with GM-CSF plasmid DNA. This study demonstrated a local increase in DC numbers with an enhancement of the immune response against HCV. Furthermore, the vaccination promoted protection against tumor formation and/or reduction in immunized mice after challenge with NS5-expressing tumor cells.
Rabies virus vaccine LBNSE-Flt3L was developed to enhance DC maturation. Mice immunized with LBNSE-Flt3L had more mature DCs (CD86 upregulation), generated more Tfh cells, germinal center (GC) B cells, and higher levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies. In the case of the recombinant PRV vaccine, Flt3L enhanced DC maturation (CD80/86 upregulation), increased neutralizing and gB-specific IgG antibodies, and elevated IFN-γ levels in immunized mice. It also reduced mortality and histopathological damage upon PRV challenge. 

## IL-12
IL-12 plays a critical role in promoting Th1 immune responses and activating NK cells, which is crucially important for anti-cancer, anti-parasitic, and therapeutic vaccine efficiency. In a study of a nasal vaccine against Yersinia pestis conducted by Yamanaka et al. in 2008, it was found that bicistronic plasmids expressing IL-12 along with Yersinia pestis epitopes (capsular antigen [F1-Ag], virulence antigen [V-Ag], or a fused [F1-V] protein) had several beneficial effects. These plasmids increased mucosal IgA and serum IgG antibodies and protected mice against the Yersinia pestis challenge, which can lead to pneumonic plague. To adjust the amount of IL-12 produced, a stronger cytomegalovirus promoter was added to one of the constructs, resulting in an increased IL-12 expression level. Despite the different levels of IL-12 production induced by the two F1-V DNA vaccines, the antibody responses and Th cell responses to F1-Ag and V-Ag were similar.
In another study, plasmid encoding IL-12 (pIL-12) improved the ability of a DNA vaccine encoding parasite cell membrane component kinetoplastid membrane protein-11 (KMP-11) to induce a Th1 response against parasites Leishmania major and Leishmania donovani. Boyer et al. showed that pIL-12 is effective in stimulating antigen-specific IFN-γ-positive effector cells as well as granzyme B production when co-administered with simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)-gag-expressing plasmid. Similarly, macaques vaccinated with SIVgag pDNA combined with plasmid IL-12 enhanced cell-mediated and humoral immune responses, leading to better clinical outcomes after virus challenge compared to SIVgag pDNA alone. Also, the impact of pIL-12 on cellular and humoral immune responses against SIVmac239 gag and env antigens in a DNA prime followed by recombinant adenovirus serotype 5 (rAd5) boost regimen was investigated. Priming with electroporated DNA + pIL-12 resulted in an increased frequency of gag-specific CD4+ T cells regardless of the pIL-12 dose. However, memory env-specific CD4+ T cells were maintained only at the low pIL-12 dose. Interestingly, high-dose pIL-12 had a negative effect on the humoral response to env, significantly reducing SIV-env-specific IgG. Furthermore, both pIL-12 doses decreased the frequency of CTLs after DNA prime, but a rAd5 boost was able to restore CTL responses regardless of the pIL-12 dose.
In order to elicit a robust immune response against HCV proteins, a DNA vaccine that expresses the non-structural 3 (NS3) gene was developed. The immune response in female C57BL/6 mice was evaluated after co-administering the DNA vaccine with IL-12 encoding DNA as an adjuvant. The data indicate that the co-administration of the HCV NS3 DNA vaccine with IL-12 leads to the production of significant levels of IFN-γ.

## IL-2 and IFN-γ
IL-2, mainly produced by activated T cells, promotes the proliferation and differentiation of various immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, and NK cells. IL-2 is essential for the maintenance and expansion of immune cell populations, contributing to the development of an effective immune response against pathogens and tumors. 
Multiple DNA vaccination experiments evaluated the protective potential of a plasmid encoding the VP2 capsid protein gene of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) in chickens, both with and without the co-administration of chIL-2 plasmid DNA. Co-delivery of the chIL-2 plasmid DNA significantly enhanced protection against a challenge with the virulent IBDV strain.
IFN-γ is a cytokine that plays a crucial role in the immune response by activating immune cells like macrophages and stimulating the production of other cytokines. It is essential for host defense against intracellular pathogens and is associated with inflammation and the adaptive immune response. 
Co-immunization of plasmids encoding chIL-2 and chIFN-γ alongside the DNA vaccine against IBDV has been shown to exhibit potent adjuvant properties, enhancing the effectiveness of the DNA vaccine. On embryonation day 18, a plasmid DNA vaccine (pcDNA-VP243) encoding the VP2, VP4, and VP3 proteins of the very virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) SH/92 strain was injected into the amniotic sac, either alone or with a plasmid encoding chicken chIL-2 or chIFN-γ. At 1 week, chickens received a commercial IBD vaccine and were challenged with the vvIBDV SH/92 strain at 3 weeks. The study revealed that in ovo DNA immunization followed by a killed vaccine boost provided superior immunity, with no mortality observed post-challenge. Although in-ovo DNA vaccination alone was insufficient for protection, the addition of chIL-2 or chIFN-γ significantly enhanced immunity.

## IL-7 and IL-15
IL-7 and IL-15 are involved in regulating T cell immune responses. IL-7 is essential for T cell development and homeostasis, whereas IL-15 supports the survival and proliferation of memory T cells and NKs. 
Co-immunization with the chIL-2/chIL-7 bicistronic gene vector significantly enhanced the immune response, leading to increased levels of IBDV-specific antibodies, T cell proliferation, and IFN-γ production. Consequently, this synergistic effect of the bicistronic plasmid enhances the overall efficacy of the DNA vaccine in providing 93% protection against IBDV infection in chickens, surpassing the individual effects of chIL-2 or chIL-7 gene vectors.
RABV that expressed murine IL-7 (rLBNSE-IL-7) significantly enhanced the induction of Tfh cells and GC B cells in draining lymph nodes compared to the parent virus. This improvement translated into stronger and more durable primary and secondary antibody responses following RABV immunization.
In another study, the IL-7 or IL-15 genes were inserted into the lentiDNA vaccine CAL-SHIV-IN against the chimeric simian/human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV) model to increase vaccine-specific memory T cells. The resulting DNA vaccines were co-injected in BALB/cJ mice and rhesus macaques. The co-administration of vaccines successfully triggered robust CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses specific to the vaccine in both mice and rhesus macaques. The presence of antibodies capable of antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) was enhanced. Moreover, ADCC was observed in both plasma and mucosal regions of rhesus macaques for up to 40 weeks after immunization.
Macaques vaccinated with SIVgag pDNA combined with a combination of pIL-12 and pIL-15 showed significantly enhanced cell-mediated and humoral immune responses, leading to better clinical outcomes after virus challenge compared to SIVgag pDNA alone. In contrast, those receiving SIVgag pDNA with pIL-15 alone exhibited only slight immune response improvements without notable clinical benefit post-challenge.

## IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, and IL-23
IL-6 is an important pro-inflammatory cytokine expressed by macrophages in response to PRR stimulus; it induces fever; supports the production of neutrophils, Tfh cells, and B cell growth; and suppresses the Tregs. 
IL-6 was employed in the vaccine against vvIBDV. Two plasmid DNA constructs were engineered: one containing the VP2-4-3 genes of the vvIBDV SH/95 strain (pALTER-MAX-VP2-4-3) and the other incorporating the chIL-6 gene (pALTER-MAX-ChIL-6). The researchers conducted several DNA vaccination experiments on 1-week-old chickens. At 4 weeks old age, the chickens were orally inoculated with vvIBDV. The results demonstrated that immunizations with the mixture of pALTER-MAX-VP2-4-3 and pALTER-MAX-ChIL-6 protected 90% of the chickens. The chickens immunized with pALTER-MAX-ChIL-6 along with pALTER-MAX-VP2-4-3 showed higher antibody titers compared to those immunized solely with plasmid pcDNA3-VP2 or pALTER-MAX-VP2-4-3.
The recombinant pseudorabies virus expressing porcine parvovirus (PPV) Ag and IL-6 (rPRV-VP2-IL6) was developed and demonstrated the ability to induce PPV-specific antibodies and robust lymphocyte proliferation responses in immunized mice. It provided partial protection against a virulent PPV challenge and elicited high levels of neutralizing antibodies against PRV. Additionally, rPRV-VP2-IL6 significantly reduced mortality rates following the virulent PRV challenge.
IL-4 is a cytokine that promotes the differentiation of naive T cells into Th2 cells, leading to the activation of B cells and the production of antibodies. It is involved in allergic responses and plays a role in immune regulation. IL-5 is a cytokine primarily produced by Th2 cells and is involved in the stimulation and activation of eosinophils. It plays a critical role in the immune response against parasitic infections. IL-23 is primarily produced by activated APCs like macrophages and DCs. It promotes the proliferation of memory T cells and plays a significant role in the differentiation and maintenance of Th17 cells, which are involved in immune responses against bacterial and fungal pathogens. 
To enhance antibody production in adenovirus-based immunization, interleukins IL-4, IL-5, and IL-6 were selected as genetic adjuvants due to their role in B cell proliferation and maturation. Mice were vaccinated with an Ad vector encoding the Friend virus (FV) surface envelope protein gp70 in combination with Ad vectors encoding IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, or IL-23. Co-application of the gp70 vector with IL-5, IL-6, or IL-23 resulted in improved protection against FV-induced splenomegaly and reduced viral loads. Furthermore, co-immunization with IL-5 or IL-23 enhanced neutralizing antibody responses, whereas IL-6 or IL-23 improved FV-specific CD4+ T cell responses.

## IL-28b
IL-28b, also known as interferon lambda 3 (IFN-λ3), is a member of the type III interferon family. It plays a role in the immune response, particularly in antiviral defense. 
An intranasal gamma-irradiated influenza A virus strain H1N1 vaccine co-administered with a plasmid encoding mouse IL-28b demonstrated protective efficacy. The vaccination induced robust humoral, cellular, and mucosal immune responses, effectively reducing lung virus titers. These responses were strongly correlated with protection against viral challenge.
IL-28b was incorporated into a DNA vaccine targeting NDV to enhance its immunogenicity. The modified vaccine, pTwist-F-HN-VII-IL28b, demonstrated superior efficacy, achieving 80% protection against the highly virulent NDV strain. This performance exceeded the 60% protection provided by both non-adjuvanted vaccine and LaSota, a widely used attenuated NDV vaccine strain.

## IL-1 superfamily (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-18, IL-33, and IL-36α, β, and γ)
IL-1 superfamily includes various pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-18, IL-33, IL-36α, IL-36β, and IL-36γ. These cytokines are essential in regulating the inflammatory response and activation of the immune system. They are involved in various processes, including immune cell activation, inflammation, tissue repair, and host defense against infections. Dysregulation of the IL-1 superfamily cytokines is associated with various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. 
IL-1β is a pro-inflammatory cytokine produced primarily by immune cells, such as macrophages, monocytes, and DCs, in response to various stimuli, including infection, injury, or inflammation. IL-1β plays a central role in the regulation of the immune response and is involved in mediating inflammation and host defense against infections. IL-18 acts as an inflammatory cytokine and stimulates the production of IFN-γ, promoting Th1 immune responses. 
The efficacy of intranasal immunisations using recombinant Ad vectors (rAd) carrying hemagglutinin (HA) and nucleoprotein (NP) genes of influenza A virus was evaluated in mice, with the addition of rAd-IL-1β or rAd-IL-18. Mucosal delivery of rAd-IL-1β resulted in enhanced antibody responses specifically targeting HA, including the production of strain-specific neutralizing antibodies, whereas IL-18 had no significant effect. The increased immunogenicity enhanced protection against infections with various influenza strains (H1N1, pH1N1, H3N2, and H7N7). Co-expression of IL-1β and the antigen triggered processes involved in the formation of tissue-resident memory cells (TRM), such as activation of epithelial cells, expression of chemokines and adhesion molecules, recruitment of DCs from the lungs, and subsequent imprinting of local TRM cells. In another study, researchers co-administered plasmids containing IL-1β or IL-18 with plasmids containing HA and NP derived from influenza A virus H1N1/Puerto Rico/8/1934 by electroporation in murine model. Only when IL-1β was co-administered, a notable increase of antigen-specific IgG1 levels was observed.
Furthermore, IL-18 was also evaluated as a genetic adjuvant in the study of a therapeutic anti-HPV-16 vaccine in a mouse tumor model. Although the E7 DNA vaccine individually improved immune responses against cervical cancer in mouse model systems, combining IL-18 as a genetic adjuvant with the E7 DNA vaccine did not demonstrate any notable synergistic effect on the immune responses in vivo. In contrast, IL-18 was found to enhance protective immunity in the case of the DNA IBDV vaccine. Chickens were given two immunizations at 2-week intervals, with different plasmid DNA vaccines encoding the VP243 gene of IBDV: pCAGVP243, pCAGVP243-IL-18, and the blank vector pCAGGS. After the vaccinations, the chickens were challenged with vvIBDV (HLJ0504 strain). Chickens immunized with the plasmid carrying both VP243 and IL-18 genes showed significantly higher levels of antibodies, lymphocyte proliferation responses, and cytokines IL-4 and IFN-γ compared to those injected with pCAGVP243 containing the VP243 gene alone. Moreover, pCAGVP243-IL-18 provided superior protection (93%) against vvIBDV challenge in chickens compared to pCAGVP243 (60%).
IL-33 is a multifunctional cytokine involved in modulating both innate and adaptive immune responses. rRABV overexpressing IL-33 (rLBNSE-IL33) enhanced rapid antibody production via DC activation (CD80/86 upregulation) and sustained high virus-neutralizing antibody levels for 8 weeks through T-cell-dependent GC reactions, providing improved rabies protection. 
IL-36γ cytokine is an IL-1 family pro-inflammatory cytokine expressed in barrier tissues (skin, lung, and intestines) mainly by keratinocytes and epithelium cells, in response to PAMP and DAMP stimuli. Additionally, IL-36γ is expressed in DCs. Multiple studies suggest that IL-36γ is involved in the pathogenesis of psoriasis through activation of DCs with subsequent Th17 response activation. The addition of the molecular adjuvant pVAX-IL-36γ further improved the protective immunity elicited by the two-gene cocktail DNA vaccine (pVAX-IST + pVAX-NSM) against Toxoplasma gondii. Moreover, the truncated form of IL-36γ acted as a potent adjuvant for various DNA-encoded vaccines, including HIV, influenza, and Zika viruses. The adjuvant demonstrated dose-sparing effects by enhancing the immune response to a suboptimal dose of a Zika DNA vaccine, resulting in robust protection against a lethal Zika virus challenge.

## Chemokines
Chemokines are small signaling proteins that play a crucial role in immune responses by guiding the migration and positioning of immune cells. Additionally, chemokines contribute to immune cell activation, polarization, and the organization of lymphoid tissues, ensuring effective immune responses against pathogens. ## CCR4, CCR5, and CCR7 agonists
CCR7 agonists CCL19 and CCL21 are involved in terminal activation and migration of DCs and T cells to secondary lymphoid organs, enabling the initiation of adaptive immune responses. The impact of CCR7 ligand DNA on the expansion of memory CD4+ T cells was assessed using transgenic mice. The mice were injected with CCL19 or CCL21 expressing DNA and subsequently immunized with ovalbumin (OVA) and Freund’s adjuvant. The immunization led to a significant increase in the number of expanded memory CD4+ T cells in mice treated with CCR7 ligand DNAs compared to the control group. Moreover, the CCR7 ligand DNA treatment resulted in elevated recruitment of memory CD4+ T cells to inflammatory sites induced by an OVA-expressing herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). Importantly, after immunization with gB of HSV-1, this expansion of memory CD4+ T cells conferred enhanced protective immunity against virulent HSV-1 viral infection. In another study, co-delivery of a pCCL19 with HIV-1 gp140 antigen (in plasmid DNA or recombinant protein form) resulted in systemic and mucosal antibody responses, as well as T cell responses specific to the HIV-1 envelope. 
Furthermore, in the study of HSV-2 DNA vaccination, CCL19 was fused to the C- or N-terminal end of the extracellular gB of HSV-2. Immunization of mice with two fusion constructs (pgBIZCCL19 and pCCL19IZgB) resulted in enhanced antibody response, exhibiting increased neutralizing activity against HSV-2 in vitro. Moreover, mice vaccinated with the fusion constructs were effectively protected against intravaginal lethal challenges with HSV-2.
The CCR5 agonist CCL3 (macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1α) attracts NK and CTLs and regulates the functions of monocytes, B cells, and T cells, whereas CCL4 (MIP-1β) has a chemoattractant effect on DCs, Th1 cells, NK cells, and monocytes. The adjuvant properties of CCL3, CCL4, CCL19, and CCL21 were compared in the flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) model by Xu et al. Bicistronic plasmids containing the Vibrio anguillarum antigen A (VAA) gene of aquaculture pathogenic bacteria Vibrio anguillarum along with genes encoding flounder-derived chemokines were prepared. Immunization with pCCL3-VAA, pCCL4-VAA, pCCL19-VAA, and pCCL21-VAA led to higher relative survivals (62%, 84%, 78%, and 73%, respectively) compared to the control pVAA group (41%). The use of adjuvant resulted in enhanced levels of specific antibodies, with CCL4 and CCL19 demonstrating superior adjuvant effects compared to CCL3 and CCL21.
Recombinant RABV expressing CCL3 induced the recruitment and activation of DCs and B cells, enhancing the overall peripheral immune response. This led to higher levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies compared to the parental virus, underscoring the contribution of CCL3 to immune activation.
CCR4 agonists CCL17 and CCL22 are primarily produced by skin vessel endothelium and DCs and are important for the recruitment of T cells. Enhanced antibody response against the anti-caries DNA vaccine pCIA-P and reduced Streptococcus mutans infection in rodents were observed when a nasal adjuvant comprising a combination of plasmids encoding CCL17 and CCL19 (pCCL17/VAX and pCCL19/VAX correspondingly) was used. Results indicated that the intranasal co-administration of CCL17 and CCL19 genes in mice resulted in a greater increase in the number of mature DCs in the spleen and draining lymph nodes compared to the administration of CCL17 or CCL19 genes alone. The combined adjuvant of CCL17 and CCL19, along with pCIA-P, induced significantly higher levels of anti-S. mutans surface protein antigen (PAc) salivary IgA and anti-PAc serum IgG antibodies in mice and enhanced the ability of pCIA-P to inhibit S. mutans colonization on rat tooth surfaces.
Moreover, the recombinant RABV expressing macrophage-derived chemokine (MDC, CCL22) increased the peripheral activation and recruitment of DCs and B cells, resulting in improved production of virus-neutralizing antibodies. Immunization with this virus conferred significantly enhanced protection against intracerebral challenge with virulent RABV, demonstrating MDC’s role in boosting adaptive immune responses.

## CCR5 agonists
CCL5 (RANTES) chemokine recognizes monocytes, memory T cells, and eosinophils, inducing both mucosal and systemic immune responses. A superagonist of CCR5, known as 1P7, has been engineered through in-vitro evolution using natural CCL5. The molecule is employed as an adjuvant in DNA vaccines, where it is expressed fused to Ig (1P7-Ig). It was demonstrated that co-administration of a model antigen OVA with 1P7-Ig DNA resulted in higher levels of OVA-specific CTLs compared to co-administration with CCL5-Ig.

## CCR6 agonists
CCL20 (MIP3-α) attracts immature DCs, T cells (Th17, Tregs), and B cells and is produced in a variety of organs and tissues, in particular liver, intestine, airway epithelium, and lymphoid tissues. CCL20 was tested as a genetic vaccine adjuvant against viral infections.
In the case of a vaccine against HIV, mice were intramuscularly immunized, with plasmid DNA encoding the model antigen HIV-1 env gp140 along with CCL20, followed by an intravaginal boost with recombinant gp140 protein. The genetic chemokine adjuvant CCL20 enhanced humoral and cellular immune responses specific to the vaccine antigen and promoted the homing of immune cells to the intestinal and female genital mucosae.
Furthermore, co-administration of a plasmid expressing CCL20 (pCCL20) improved the efficacy of vaccination against HCV. The study aimed to investigate the adjuvant activity of a pCCL20 when co-administered with an HCV core DNA vaccine. Mice co-immunized with pCCL20 exhibited heightened levels of HCV core-specific IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio, IL-2 release, IFN-γ-producing cells, lymphocyte proliferation, and cytotoxic Granzyme B release. Moreover, pCCL20 significantly upregulated core-specific serum IgG levels and altered the IgG2a/IgG1 ratio.
CCL20 was also employed as an adjuvant to the foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) vaccine. Intramuscular injections of murine CCL20 DNA were compared to injections of CCL20 protein 2 days before immunization with inactivated FMD vaccine. Vaccination with pCCL20 plus FMD vaccine exhibited earlier and significantly higher levels of neutralizing antibodies compared to mice vaccinated with CCL20 protein plus FMD vaccine. Interestingly, the use of CCL20 as a protein did not exhibit any adjuvant effect, and the immune responses in this group were similar to those of mice vaccinated with the FMD vaccine alone. All vaccination groups exhibited serum IgG1 and IgG2 antibody responses; however, mice vaccinated with pCCL20 plus FMD vaccine displayed significantly higher IgG1 and IgG2 responses, which remained elevated throughout the post-vaccination period. Upon restimulation of splenocytes with the inactivated viral antigen, animals vaccinated with pCCL20 plus FMD vaccine exhibited a Th1 type of cellular immune response characterized by IFN-γ and IL-2 production. Following challenge with the homologous FMD virus on the 28th day post-immunization, complete protection (100%) was observed in mice vaccinated with pCCL20 plus FMD vaccine, whereas animals immunized with CCL20 protein plus FMD vaccine or FMD vaccine without an adjuvant showed 66% protection.
Moreover, CCL20 was used in anti-parasitic vaccines. For example, the CCL20 gene was fused with circumsporozoite protein (CSP) from either mouse or human malaria strain, Plasmodium yoelii or Plasmodium falciparum, respectively. The construct was used with or without the addition of lipid-based adjuvant Vaxfectin, known to effectively enhance the response to DNA vaccines. However, the fusion with CCL20 did not significantly alter the cellular, cytokine, or chemokine profile at the immunization site compared to Vaxfectin and CSP DNA alone. Although the CCL20 lacked traditional adjuvant functions in this vaccine construct, its presence, in combination with Vaxfectin, profoundly enhanced the antibody response and protected against malaria sporozoite challenge in mice.
β-Defensins are antimicrobial peptides that stimulate both innate and adaptive immunity by recruiting naive T cells and immature DCs through interactions with corresponding immune receptors (e.g., CCR6) and can be used as potential adjuvant. , Zhang et al. (2010) has investigated the potential adjuvant effects of avian-defensin-1 (AvBD1) when genetically fused with the viral protein 2 (VP2) gene of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) in a DNA vaccine. The researchers compared the immune responses and protection levels between chickens immunized with the DNA vaccine encoding the VP2 gene alone and chickens immunized with the DNA vaccine containing both the VP2 and AvBD1 genes. After the second vaccination, the group of chickens immunized with the pcDNA3.1(+)-AvBD1-VP2 DNA vaccine exhibited significantly higher antibody levels and percentages of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells compared to the group immunized with pcDNA3.1(+)-VP2 alone. Additionally, when the adjuvant was used, the vaccine provided better protection against IBD.

## CCR9 and CCR10 agonists
CCR9 agonist CCL25 is involved in thymocyte development and expressed in the thymus as well as the small intestine; subsequently, CCR9 primarily is expressed by thymocytes and intestinal T lymphocytes. Simultaneous administration of a plasmid expressing CCL25, along with antigenic constructs for HIV-1 gag or influenza A/PR/8/34 HA, resulted in elevated levels of antigen-specific inflammatory cytokines in both peripheral and mucosal sites. Moreover, in an influenza challenge model, co-immunization with CCL25 induced the production of long-lasting IgA antibodies, neutralizing antibodies, and providing protection.

## CCR10 agonists CCL27 and CCL28
CCR10 is expressed on T and B cells. Its agonist CCL27 stimulates the trafficking of T lymphocytes to the skin, whereas CCL28 attracts immunoglobulin-A-antibody-secreting plasma cells (B lymphocytes) to mucosal tissues. Mice were intramuscularly immunized with plasmid DNA encoding the model antigen HIV-1 env gp140 along with CCR9 and CCR10 agonists (CCL25, CCL27, and CCL28), followed by an intravaginal boost with recombinant gp140 protein. CCL25 enhanced antigen-specific T cell responses, whereas CCL28 specifically increased the levels of T cells in the vaginal mucosa. The data from this study demonstrate that genetic chemokine adjuvants CCL25 and CCL28, but not CCL27, enhance humoral and cellular immune responses specific to the vaccine antigen and promote homing of immune cells to the intestinal and female genital mucosae.
Similar to the previous study, the SIV DNA vaccine was adjuvanted with plasmid-expressed chemokines CCL25, CCL27, and CCL28 used in combination with an intravaginal SIVsmE660 challenge in a rhesus macaque. As a result, 68% of all vaccinated animals were found to either remain uninfected or have aborted infection, in contrast to only 14% in the vaccine-naïve group. The highest level of protection (89%), SIV-specific antibodies and neutralization titers were observed in the group treated with CCL27 and CCL28 chemokines.
In another study, CCL27 was used as an adjuvant to enhance immune responses to an HIV-1 or SIV antigen in mice and rhesus macaques. Interestingly, the co-administration of HIV-1gag/CCL27 resulted in enhanced immune responses both at peripheral and mucosal sites. It was observed that CCL27 with the antigenic HIV-1 or SIV gag plasmid slightly enhanced immune responses at unique immune sites in both mice and macaques. In mice, an increase in antigen-specific IFN-γ-secreting cells and IgA was observed in both peripheral and mucosal compartments. In a pilot macaque study, immunization with pCCL27 and SIV antigens modulated IgA levels at mucosal sites and enhanced T cell responses in the lung.
Co-immunization with plasmid DNA encoding HIV antigens along with plasmid-encoded CCL28 was performed to enhance anti-HIV IgA responses at mucosal surfaces. Co-administration augmented both IgA and IgG immune responses. Co-delivery of a pCCL28 with HIV-1 gp140 antigen enhanced systemic and mucosal antibody responses as well as T cell responses specific to the HIV-1 envelope. 
Yan et al. demonstrated that fusing CCL19 and CCL28 chemokines to the HSV-2 glycoprotein D ectodomain (gD-306aa) significantly enhanced immune responses against a lethal HSV-2 challenge in the genital tract. Enhanced immune responses were observed after intramuscular immunization of mice with the respective DNA constructs. Both CCL19 and CCL28 were effective in inducing long-lasting systemic immunity against HSV-2 gD. However, CCL28 required less dosage than CCL19 to elicit HSV-2 gD-specific serum IgA responses, Th1- and Th2-like immune responses characterized by Ig subclasses, cytokines, and enrichment of CCR3+ T cells in the spleen. These findings indicate that CCL28 tends to assist an immunogen more potently in inducing protective immunity compared to CCL19.
Kutzler et al. conducted a study to examine the role of CCR10 chemokine ligands in the immune response elicited by DNA vaccination using an influenza A HA plasmid vaccine. They found that co-administration of plasmids expressing CCL27 or CCL28, along with a plasmid encoding influenza A/PR8/34 HA, resulted in increased levels of antigen-specific T cell inflammatory cytokines and the production of IgA antibodies. After a lethal intranasal influenza virus challenge, mice co-immunized with CCL27 and CCL28 showed improved protection compared to those vaccinated without adjuvants.
Gary et al. investigated the immunogenicity and protective effectiveness of optimized synthetic DNA plasmids encoding the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 when co-administered with a plasmid encoding CCL27. The researchers observed that animals co-immunized with pCCL27 showed elevated levels of spike-specific antibodies in the mucosal surface and increased frequencies of IFN-γ-producing CTLs in the respiratory mucosa. Moreover, co-immunization with pCCL27 provided complete protection (100%) against the heterologous SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant challenge.

## CXCR5 agonists
CXCL13, also known as B lymphocyte chemoattractant (BLC) or B-cell-attracting chemokine 1 (BCA-1), plays a pivotal role in the organization and function of B cells. CXCL13 was incorporated as an adjuvant into the antigen-encoding circular RNA (circRNA) within a vaccine lipid nanoparticle (LNP). Co-delivering CXCL13 with the antigen enhanced cross-reactive antibody responses against both influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2, providing protection against homologous and heterologous influenza virus challenges in a mouse model.

## Co-stimulatory immune molecules
Co-stimulatory molecules play a critical role in enhancing immune responses by promoting T cell activation. This section focuses on the use of key co-stimulatory molecules, such as CD40 ligand and 4-1BBL, as genetically encoded adjuvants and explores how these molecules can be harnessed to boost the overall efficacy of vaccines. Genetically encoded co-stimulatory molecule adjuvants are summarized in .
 

## CD40L
CD40 ligand (CD40L) is a cell surface protein expressed on activated immune cells. Interaction with the APC receptor CD40 triggers signaling pathways involved in immune activation and maturation. CD40-CD40L interaction plays a critical role in promoting B cell and T cell responses, antibody production, and immune system functioning. , 
Sin et al. explored the effect of co-immunization of HSV-2 gD plasmid vaccine with plasmid DNA expressing CD40L. The use of plasmid adjuvant enhanced T cell responses and production of IgG2a, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and chemokines. Moreover, the adjuvant significantly improved the survival rate after the HSV-2 lethal dose challenge.
Furthermore, vaccination with bovine herpesvirus-1 (BoHV-1) glycoprotein D DNA vaccine (pCIgD) with plasmid-encoded murine CD40L (pCD40L), Montanide 1113101PR (101) adjuvant, or their combination resulted in significantly higher specific antibody titers in mice. The pCIgD + pCD40L + 101 vaccine induced the highest levels of IgG2a and IgG2b antibodies, stimulated significantly higher CTL activity against BoHV-1-infected cells, and showed increased CD4+ populations and higher levels of IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-17A.
Several attempts have been made to employ CD40L as the adjuvant against SIV. Boyer et al. reported that co-administration of plasmid expressing SIVgag in combination with pCD40L did not enhance the cellular immune response to antigen. Kwa et al. co-expressed CD40L with the SIV DNA vaccine, resulting in CD40L-anchored SIV VLPs. The DNA/SIV-CD40L vaccine was used for a prime immunization before two boosts with DNA/modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) vaccine in rhesus macaques. Incorporating CD40L adjuvant into the vaccine significantly enhanced the antiviral humoral and cellular immune responses, leading to improved protection against pathogenic SIV. De Castro et al. showed the immunization with the canarypox virus (CPV)-based vector vaccine ALVAC-SIV/CD40L, which co-expressed CD40L and SIVmac251 genes. The approach demonstrated superior immunogenicity, resulting in enhanced binding and neutralizing antibody responses against gp120. However, the ALVAC-SIV/CD40L vaccine blunted Th1 responses, promoted immune-suppressive IL-10 cytokine production, and induced myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tryptophan catabolism, ultimately failing to protect macaques from SIV susceptibility.
Some researchers have reported fusion constructs of CD40L with antigens. For example, pVAX1-EBV-LMP2-CD40L DNA vaccine encoding the fusion gene of the murine CD40L extracellular domain and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) latent membrane protein 2 (EBV-LMP2) demonstrated enhanced specific immunity compared to the group without adjuvant. The vaccination induced higher levels of anti-EBV-LMP2 antibody and stimulated a stronger EBV-LMP2-specific T cell response.
In the Li et al. study, porcine CD40L and GM-CSF were co-expressed to improve the immunogenicity of the porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) capsid protein (Cap) adenovirus vaccine. The results demonstrated that the Ad-CD40L-Cap-GMCSF vaccine induced higher specific antibody and neutralized antibody responses compared to Ad-Cap. Moreover, it elicited stronger cellular immune responses as observed in the lymphocyte proliferation assay and cytokine release assay. In the PCV2 challenge experiment, the viral loads in the Ad-CD40L-Cap-GMCSF-vaccinated group were significantly lower than the Ad-Cap-vaccinated group after the oronasal PCV2 challenge.

## CD80/CD86 (B7-1/B7-2)
CD80/86, also known as B7-1/B7-2, are cell-surface proteins expressed on APCs. They interact with the receptors, CD28 and CTLA-4 on T cells, providing co-stimulatory signals necessary for T cell activation and regulation. Tsuji et al. reported using B7-1 and B7-2 expression plasmids co-administered with the DNA vaccine encoding HIV-1 env and rev. The administration of B7-2, but not B7-1, gene enhanced the immune response. However, when incorporated in a replication-deficient HSV-2 strain used as a vaccine against HSV-2 genital infection, both B7-1 and B7-2 were effective in inducing protective immune responses, such as T cell activity, specific antibody production, and significantly increased survival. 
To study the ability of B7-1 and B7-2 to induce immune response during vaccination with DNA encoding the HIV-1MN envelope protein, Agadjanyan et al. used plasmids encoding chimeric constructs and deletion mutants. Results showed that CD80 Ig constant-like domain (C-domain) deletion enhances antigen-specific cellular response. According to the data, the use of engineered B7-1/B7-2 molecules could enhance the effectiveness of vaccine adjuvants.
The constructed DNA vaccine co-expressing CD80/86 and the outer membrane protein of V. anguillarum (p-OmpK-CD80/86) demonstrated enhanced immune responses in flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus), including increased proportions of IgM+ and CD4+ cells and upregulated expression of CD28 genes. Importantly, the p-OmpK-CD80/86 vaccine conferred significantly improved protection, with higher relative survival, compared to the groups receiving p-OmpK or p-CD80/86 alone.

## TNF superfamily ligands
The tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily ligands (TNFSFLs) consist of 19 ligands crucial for regulating the immune system and facilitating cellular communication. These ligands bind to a range of receptors, triggering diverse biological responses, such as apoptosis, immune activation, and inflammation. 
Plasmid constructs encoding TNFSFL fusion proteins with surfactant protein D (SP-D) were tested as adjuvants. The use of SP-D as a scaffold facilitates the formation of multi-trimeric soluble fusion proteins of these TNFSFLs. Adjuvants such as 4-1BBL (TNFSF9 or CD137L), OX40L (TNFSF4 or CD252), RANKL (TNFSF11 or CD254), LIGHT (TNFSF14 or CD258), CD70, and B cell activating factor (BAFF, TNFSF13B, or CD257) were evaluated for their ability to enhance immune responses when combined with an HIV-1 gag DNA vaccine. Mice were intramuscularly vaccinated with plasmids expressing secreted gag and SP-D-TNFSFL fusion constructs. Results showed that all SP-D-TNFSFL constructs enhanced at least one gag-specific immune response compared to the parent vaccine. Adjuvant SP-D-RANKL was not particularly effective; however, it enhanced IL-2-secreting lymphocytes. Notably, the fusion constructs SP-D-4-1BBL, SP-D-OX40L, and SP-D-LIGHT exhibited increased CTL avidity and CD8+/CD4+ T cell proliferation. Additionally, the constructs SP-D-OX40L, SP-D-LIGHT, and SP-D-BAFF enhanced gag-specific IL-2 secretion in memory T cells. Furthermore, adjuvants SP-D-OX40L and SP-D-CD70 enhanced Th1 (IgG2a), but not Th2 (IgG1), antibody responses in the vaccinated animals. Surprisingly, SP-D-BAFF did not enhance anti-gag antibody responses, but it did enhance CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses.
Moreover, Gupta et al. developed an HIV vaccine incorporating the molecular adjuvants pSP-D-BAFF, pSP-D-APRIL (a proliferation-inducing ligand, CD256), and pIL-12, aiming to promote the maturation of B cells and enhance the affinity or avidity of env-specific antibodies. The immunization results showed that the DNA vaccine encoding BAFF or APRIL multi-trimers, along with IL-12 and membrane-bound HIV-1 env gp140, induced neutralizing antibodies against both tier 1 and tier 2 (vaccine strain) viruses. The APRIL-containing vaccine notably generated tier 2 neutralizing antibodies following a protein boost. These molecular adjuvants also enhanced the germinal center response, increased anti-gp120 antibody-secreting cells, and improved anti-gp120 functional avidity without causing indiscriminate B cell expansion or raising total IgG levels. However, in Hu et al. study, co-delivery of a plasmid encoding APRIL with HIV-1 gp140 antigen did not enhance systemic and mucosal antibody responses as well as T cell responses specific to the HIV-1 envelope. 
Interestingly, although prior research demonstrated that TNF-α could boost the HIV-1 env gp120 serum IgG response when delivered via a gp120–TNF-α fusion expression plasmid, Aldon et al. did not observe a similar enhancement in the systemic gp140-specific IgG response. 

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors
Immune checkpoint inhibitors are pivotal in enhancing immune responses by blocking inhibitory signals that dampen T cell activity. This section highlights the use of genetically encoded immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockers, as adjuvants in vaccine strategies. Genetically encoded checkpoint inhibitor adjuvants are summarized in .

## sPD-1/sPD-L1
PD-1 (programmed cell death protein 1) is a receptor present on activated T cells, whereas PD-L1, its ligand, is found on various immune cells, including DCs and macrophages. When PD-1 interacts with PD-L1, it delivers inhibitory signals that dampen T cell activity and prevent excessive immune responses, contributing to immune tolerance and preventing autoimmune reactions. An immune response can be induced by blocking PD-1 and its ligands using soluble ectodomains of these proteins (sPD-1 and sPD-L1).
Tannig et al. co-electroporated DNA vaccines with plasmids encoding the sPD-1 or sPD-L1. Co-electroporation of influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/1934/H1N1 virus hemagglutinin (HA) DNA vaccine with a plasmid encoding sPD-1, but not sPD-L1, improved HA-specific immune responses, namely, a decrease of Treg and enhancement in neutralization titers. On the other hand, in the case of HIV-1, env-specific immune responses were mainly influenced by sPD-L1. Co-electroporation of HIV-1 DNA vaccine and a plasmid encoding sPD-L1 decreased the proportion of Treg and enhanced the production of IgG2a.
For the vaccination against HPV16 E7, sPD-1 encoding DNA was used. , The employment of adjuvant significantly enhanced antigen-specific CTL responses when co-administered with both DNA- and adenovirus-based vaccines. Furthermore, the co-delivery of sPD-1 DNA resulted in an improved maturation of DCs in vivo, as evidenced by an increase in MHC II expression.
Zhou et al. reported the generation of a DNA vaccine consisting of the HIV-1 gag p24 gene in combination with the sPD-1 gene. The vaccination with fusion DNA enhanced CTL response and anti-gag antibody production. The mucosal challenge with vaccinia gag viruses resulted in a significant reduction in virus titers after the administration of a fusion DNA vaccine.

## CTLA-4 and CD28
CTLA-4 is a protein receptor found on T cells that regulates the immune response by inhibiting T cell activity. It competes with CD28 for binding to B7 molecules on APCs, delivering inhibitory signals that suppress T cell activation. Blocking CTLA-4 with antibodies can enhance the immune response. D’Alise et al. used an adenovirus vector encoding a murine anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody as an adjuvant during immunization with adenovirus serotype 26 expressing the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein (Ad26-Spike). The co-delivery resulted in a stronger enhancement of T and B cell responses. On the other hand, when the vaccine was combined with the protein form of the anti-CTLA-4 molecule, only a small adjuvant effect was observed.
Fusing antigens to the extracellular domain of CTLA-4 has been demonstrated as an effective strategy to enhance the immunogenicity of DNA vaccines in case of influenza HA, woodchuck hepatitis virus nucleocapsid protein, and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). The vaccination with the CTLA4-fused DNA resulted in positive outcomes. It effectively stimulated immune responses, including antibody production and CTL response, leading to the control of viral replication and the breakdown of immune tolerance. Moreover, T cell co-stimulatory receptor CD28 was also used for fusion with woodchuck hepatitis virus nucleocapsid protein but it was not as effective as CTLA-4. A recent study introduced a fascinating strategy involving the application of recombinant Lactobacillus reuteri as a live vector vaccine. This approach involved the expression of two distinct forms of CTLA4, namely CTLA4-6aa and CTLA4-87aa, which were fused with the porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) protective antigen core neutralizing epitope. Piglets that received immunization with Lactobacillus expressing CTLA4-6aa exhibited elevated levels of anti-PEDV-specific IgG and IgA antibody responses.

## Cell stress/death molecules as genetic adjuvants
Cell-death-associated molecules specifically target DC maturation ( II). These molecules activate innate immune receptors upon extracellular release or intracellular mislocalization, engaging characterized receptors such as TLR2 and TLR4 (for HMGB1) and TLR9 (for DNA), alongside additional receptors that remain uncharacterized. Notably, Hsp70’s receptor interactions and downstream signaling pathways—unlike those of HMGB1 and DNA—remain incompletely defined. This section focuses on the use of two standard DAMPs—heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein—as genetically encoded adjuvants ( A (2)), and how these molecules can be utilized to promote the maturation of DCs, which is essential for enhancing the immune response and improving the efficacy of vaccines. Genetically encoded DAMP adjuvants are summarized in .
 

## Hsp70
Hsp70 is a highly conserved molecular chaperone protein found in cells. Hsp70 assists in protein folding, assembly, and transport, as well as in refolding of misfolded proteins. It is also involved in the presentation of antigens to immune cells and can act as an immunomodulatory molecule, influencing immune responses and promoting the activation of immune cells.
The Hsp70 gene was fused with various antigens to improve vaccination efficiency. Hsp70 is commonly used as an adjuvant in vaccines against human papillomavirus (HPV). For example, the DNA vaccine (pNGVL4a-SigE7(detox)Hsp70) encoded a fusion protein comprising the Mus musculus lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP-1) signal peptide connected to Mycobacterium tuberculosis Hsp70 and a modified form of the HPV16 E7 protein with an oncogenic potential-eliminating point mutation (E7(detox)). Clinical trials demonstrated that the vaccination was well tolerated, , including its use as a priming immunization before boosting with tissue-antigen human papillomavirus vaccine (TA-HPV). Peng et al. incorporated HPV18 E7 and HPV16/18 E6 genes into the pNGVL4a-SigE7(detox)Hsp70. When administered intramuscularly in mice, the modified vaccine elicited superior HPV-antigen-specific CTL immune responses.
To enhance the effectiveness of virus-replicon-based suicidal DNA vaccines against HPV, Hsu et al. investigated the impact of connecting Hsp70 to HPV16 E7. They utilized a DNA vector based on Semliki Forest virus (SFV) RNA replicon called pSCA1. The findings revealed that the suicidal DNA vaccine containing the fusion genes of E7 and Hsp70 induced significantly stronger T-cell-mediated immune responses specific to E7 than the vaccine without Hsp70. The E7-Hsp70 fusion protein was further modified with mutations introduced in two zinc-binding motifs of E7. This resulted in a stronger E7-specific cellular immune response compared to the unmodified E7-Hsp70 gene.
Besides its application in anti-HPV vaccines, Hsp70 has also demonstrated efficacy as an adjuvant for HIV and HBV antigens. Garrod et al. showed the fusion of DNA encoding HIV gag antigens with Hsp70 as an adjuvant. Three forms of adjuvant were examined: cytoplasmic, secreted, or membrane-bound Hsp70 (cHsp70, secrHsp70, and memHsp70). The incorporation of memHsp70 and secrHsp70 significantly induced DC maturation (CD86 upregulation)-gag-specific T cell response and, as a result, showed protection after challenge with a chimeric virus EcoHIV that replicates in mouse leukocytes in vivo.
The N-terminal domain and the C-terminal domain derived from mycobacterial Hsp70 have been tested to induce the effectiveness of HBV DNA vaccination. Specifically, it was found that only the HBV DNA vaccine fused with the C-terminal domain led to a significant increase in the production of antibodies specific to the HBsAg. Notably, this C-terminal-domain-fused DNA vaccine did not trigger the production of antibodies against Hsp70. It demonstrated the ability to elicit a robust humoral response, enhance CTL activity, overcome epitope suppression, and promote Th1 immunity. Moreover, it showed potential in reducing circulating HBsAg levels and suppressing HBV replication in an animal model.
Hsp70 has also been explored as an adjuvant in anti-parasitic vaccines. A less conserved fragment of Plasmodium falciparum Hsp70 (Pf70C) was found to exhibit adjuvant effects comparable to those of the complete Hsp70 molecule from Trypanosoma cruzi (TcHsp70). The potential of Pf70C as an adjuvant was evaluated using a chimeric DNA construct containing the malarial antigen EB200. Although DNA immunization alone did not elicit detectable antigen-specific antibody levels, it successfully primed the immune system. This priming effect was evident after a subsequent boost with recombinant fusion proteins, which induced a strong Th1 antibody response.

## HMGB1
HMGB1 can be released by cells in response to various stimuli, such as infection, injury, or stress. HMGB1 acts as a signaling molecule, interacting with receptors such as TLR4 and the receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE). The plasmid-encoded HMGB1 was used as an adjuvant for vaccines against influenza A virus and HIV. Fagone et al. investigated the potential of using plasmid-encoded HMGB1 to enhance the immunogenicity of DNA vaccines. They focused on DNA vaccination targeting the NP of influenza A/PR/8/34 and the HA of H1N1/09. The inclusion of HMGB1 as an adjuvant led to improved adaptive effector and memory immune responses and resulted in a stronger neutralizing antibody response. Additionally, the co-administration of HMGB1 enhanced the CTL responses, ultimately protecting against a lethal mucosal challenge with influenza A/PR/8/34. For the anti-HIV vaccine, Muthumani et al. conducted experiments to investigate the effectiveness of combining a plasmid encoding the HIV-1 gag and env antigens with an HMGB1 plasmid as a DNA adjuvant. The mice were intramuscularly immunized using electroporation, and the researchers measured both humoral and cellular immune responses. The co-administration of the HMGB1 plasmid as an immunostimulatory adjuvant resulted in a significant enhancement of both cellular IFN-γ production and humoral immune responses such as antigen-specific IgG when compared to mice immunized with the vaccine alone.
To enhance the potent adjuvant activity of HMGB1 through secretion, Wang et al. introduced specific modifications. They mutated all the serine residues within the nuclear localization signals (NLSs) of HMGB1 to alanine residues. Additionally, they inserted an IgE leader sequence. The resulting gene was inserted into the recombinant rabies virus (RABV) strain LBNSE genome. The results revealed that LBNSE-HMGB1mut exhibited a significant ability to activate DCs (CD80/86 upregulation) compared to LBNSE-HMGB1wt or the LBNSE itself. Furthermore, the use of modified adjuvant increased the recruitment of Tfh cells, GC B cells, and plasma cells. Mice vaccinated with LBNSE-HMGB1mut produced higher levels of RABV-neutralizing antibodies and offered superior protection compared to those vaccinated with LBNSE or LBNSE-HMGB1wt. Following the virus challenge, 85% of mice immunized with LBNSE-HMGB1mut were protected, which was significantly higher than the protection rates observed with LBNSE (45%) and LBNSE-HMGB1wt (65%).

## Pattern recognition receptor ligands (TLR ligands, PAMPs)
Immune adjuvants that activate the innate immune system through TLRs have shown promise in enhancing immune responses to vaccines. TLRs are transmembrane receptors involved in recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns, including extracellular and endosomal nucleic acids. For example, TLR3 binds double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), a hallmark of viral replication; TLR7/8 detects single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) from RNA viruses; and TLR9 recognizes unmethylated CpG DNA motifs in bacterial/viral DNA. There are also cytoplasmic sensors that recognize dsRNA (as RIG-I like receptors) and DNA (cGAS-STING pathway). PAMPs similar to DAMPs are employed to target the DC maturation pathway ( II). PAMPs, particularly TLR agonists, enhance DC activation by upregulating CD80 and CD86, thereby providing the essential co-stimulatory signal for effective T cell activation. This mechanism underlies the efficacy of PAMPs as vaccine adjuvants, as they improve antigen presentation, cytokine production, and adaptive immune responses. This section explores the use of PAMPs ( A (3)), such as CpG and flagellin, as genetically encoded adjuvants, which are recognized by TLRs to trigger a robust immune response. Genetically encoded PAMP adjuvants are summarized in .

## CpG
Unmethylated cytosine-guanine motifs (CpG) are recognized by TLR9 and serve as a potent immunostimulatory signal, triggering the activation of DCs and B cells. Recently, the synthetic CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN-39M) has been developed as an adjuvant, demonstrating efficacy across various vaccines. It has shown promise in dengue virus vaccines (protein-based and tetravalent capsid-based) and hepatitis C virus protein vaccines. , , For SARS-CoV-2, co-administration of ODN-39M with protein or VLP-based vaccines enhanced both local and systemic immune responses against the antigen. , , It also showed efficacy in recombinant Zika virus vaccine. 
When incorporating three types of human CpG motifs (A-type, B-type, and C-type) into nonviral DNA and viral DNA replicon vectors as adjuvants for DNA vaccines, significant enhancement of antigen-specific humoral and cell-mediated immune responses in mice were observed. The CpG modifications also had differential effects on immune responses and protective potency against botulinum neurotoxin serotype A and Bacillus anthracis A16R challenge. Kojima et al. showed that adding additional CpG motifs into a plasmid enhances MHC II, CD40, and CD86 levels expressed by bone-marrow-derived DCs in vitro. Co-administration of CpG-enriched plasmids with a DNA vaccine encoding the HIV envelope glycoprotein notably boosted both HIV-specific cell-mediated and humoral immune responses. The most effective immune enhancers both in vitro and in vivo were plasmids containing 20 copies of CpG motifs.
Similar effects were obtained when incorporating 5 or 20 copies of the C-Class CpG ODN (CpG-C) motif into a plasmid DNA vaccine encoding the human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) fusion glycoprotein. The inclusion of CpG-C motifs enhanced serum binding and virus-neutralizing antibody responses in mice. Moreover, mice vaccinated with CpG-modified vaccines, particularly those containing 20 copies of the CpG-C motif, exhibited an enhanced shift toward a Th1-biased antibody and T cell response. This modification resulted in decreased pulmonary pathology and virus replication, as well as reduced weight loss following the RSV challenge.
CpG motifs can be easily combined with other adjuvants. For example, the combination of CpG motifs with CTLA-4 led to notable antigen-specific lymphocyte-proliferative responses. The CpG-optimized plasmid encoding the pro cathepsin B antigen from Fasciola hepatica fused with ovine CTLA-4 has been constructed. Remarkably, only animals primed with the CpG-augmented/CTLA-4-mediated targeting construct exhibited the capacity to induce robust lymphocyte proliferative response.

## Flagellin
Flagellin is a highly conserved protein across bacterial species and plays a crucial role in host immune responses. Flagellin can activate innate immune receptors, such as TLR5, triggering signaling cascades that contribute to inflammation and host defense against bacterial infections. 
To explore the adjuvant activity of the phase-1 flagellin from Salmonella (FliC), the plasmid expressing FliC was co-administered with the plasmid expressing DNA-encoded soluble model Ag (OVA). The combination enhanced adaptive immune responses by increasing Ag-specific IgG and IgA levels, and MHC-I restricted T cell responses. Additionally, the combination of a vector encoding the influenza A virus NP and the FliC expression vector successfully triggered protective immunity against lethal influenza A virus infection. Moreover, the incorporation of the HIV gp41 membrane-proximal external region (MPER, gp41607–683) into DNA encoding FliC enhanced the immunogenicity of gp41607–683 in a TLR5-dependent manner, leading to modest but specific humoral immune responses against the MPER region in a mouse model. Therefore, the use of genetically encoded FliC showed efficacy in antiviral vaccines.
To enhance the immune response against bacterial infections, DNA vaccines and adenovirus vectors encoding the mycobacterial protein Ag85B with the inclusion of FliC were presented. Intramuscular administration of DNA-encoded flagellin enhanced the immune responses of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen to the co-expressed vaccine antigen, including the generation of memory responses. Subsequent boosting with either intramuscular or intranasal administration of Ad vectors expressing Ag85B without flagellin resulted in enhancement of Ag85B-specific antibody and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses. This correlated with significantly improved protection against the challenge of aerosolized pathogenic Mycobacterium tuberculosis. However, the inclusion of flagellin induced localized inflammation in lungs and transient weight loss. Therefore, caution is needed regarding its side effects when used in an Ad boosting vector, particularly when delivered via the pulmonary route.
In another study, secreted flagellin (pFliC(-gly)) encoding plasmid was used. pFliC(-gly) was capable of activating TLR5 and Nod-like receptor family CARD domain-containing protein 4 (NRLC4), two innate immune receptors known to detect flagellin. To assess its adjuvant potential, mice were immunized with pFliC(-gly) along with a plasmid encoding a model antigen (OVA). In the case of immunization through dermal, systemic, and mucosal tissues, the addition of pFliC(-gly) adjuvant led to increased antigen-specific antibody levels in the serum and MHC-I-dependent cellular immune responses. Mucosal vaccination with pFliC(-gly) also induced mucosal antibody responses and MHC-II-dependent cellular immune responses. Furthermore, when used in a heterologous prime-boost immunization strategy with a plasmid encoding HIV-1 gp160 followed by protein boosting, pFliC(-gly)-enhanced humoral immune responses, including cross-clade reactive IgA and broadened B cell epitope reactivity.
The adjuvant capacity of genetically encoded FliC was tested in combination with IL-18. For this purpose, the immunogenicity of an HIV-1 gag plasmid (pVAX/gag) combined with a chimeric plasmid encoding IL-18 fused to flagellin (pcDNA3/IL-18_fliC) or individual plasmids encoding IL-18 (pcDNA3/IL-18) and flagellin (pcDNA3/fliC) was evaluated in mice. , The fusion protein of IL-18 and flagellin effectively induced IFN-γ production. Co-immunization with pVAX/gag and pcDNA3/IL-18_fliC resulted in higher levels of gag-specific IgG antibodies, Th1 cytokine production, and proportion of gag-specific IFN-γ-secreting CTLs compared to pVAX/gag plasmid combined with pcDNA3/fliC, pcDNA3/IL-18, or both.

## Type I IFN response inducers
Type I IFN is a powerful adjuvant, important for Th1-type immune response produced naturally during viral infections. The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) is a type I IFN induction mediator protein. It was shown that the expression of a mutated constitutively active STINGV155M variant as an adjuvant for the anti-HPV mRNA vaccine significantly improved the efficacy of vaccine response in mouse model. The STINGV155M variant enhances the development of antigen-specific T cells by activating type I IFN responses through the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE) pathways. mRNA-encoded STINGV155M increased the efficacy of mRNA vaccines encoding the E6 and E7 oncoproteins of HPV, leading to reduced HPV+ TC-1 tumor growth and prolonged survival in vaccinated mice.
The adjuvant activity for Ebola virus DNA immunization of the plasmids encoding IL-12, GM-CSF, and IFN-αβ-stimulating genes was evaluated after intramuscular injection of mice. The results showed that co-immunization with any of the DNA encoding genetic adjuvants significantly enhanced vaccine immunogenicity, leading to increased anti-Ebola virus (EBOV) IgG and T cell responses. Furthermore, the co-delivery of genetic adjuvants improved the EBOV neutralizing capability compared to the vaccine alone. Importantly, vaccination with plasmid EBOV and genetic adjuvants provided complete protection against the mouse-adapted EBOV Mayinga ′76 (ma-EBOV) challenge via the intraperitoneal route.
Ad vectors co-expressing FMDV VP1 proteins and porcine IFN-α significantly enhanced the generation of Tfh cells, increased the number of GC B cells, promoted the formation of GCs, and had a positive impact on the antibody response. 

## RIG-I agonists
Retinoic-acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) is an important cell cytoplasm sensor that detects the presence of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA). When RIG-I recognizes dsRNA, it triggers the production of type I IFNs and inflammatory cytokines. Interferon-beta promoter stimulator 1 (IPS-1), also known as mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS), is a crucial adaptor protein involved in the RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) pathway.
RIG-I agonists have been employed as adjuvants for vaccines against the influenza A virus. RIG-I agonist RNA can be administered in a naked form or encoded within a DNA vaccine. Kulkarni et al. activated the RIG-I pathway using 5′ppp-double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) delivered with transfection reagent to enhance immune responses using influenza A H1N1 inactivated monovalent split virus vaccine. The use of adjuvant enhanced B and T cell responses, elevated germinal center reaction, and protective immunity against virus challenge. Importantly, the use of the RIG-I agonist reduced the antigen requirement by 10- to 100-fold to achieve optimal immune responses. When the dsRNA-based TLR3 agonist NexaVant (NVT) was combined with a commercially available influenza vaccine, it elicited a robust immune response. In ferrets, the vaccine demonstrated cross-protective immunity, generated strong mucosal and systemic immunity, and provided broader protection against diverse influenza virus subtypes through the induction of lung CD4+ TRM cells.
Similarly, Sendai-virus-derived 546 nucleotide-long defective interfering RNA agonist of RIG-I effectively enhanced the production of IgG antibodies specific to the influenza virus HA antigen. This enhancement was observed when the RNA agonist was co-administered with an inactivated H1N1 pandemic vaccine. Furthermore, the study showed that their adjuvant was comparably effective to both poly(I·C) and squalene-based adjuvants in improving the efficiency of an inactivated vaccine against the influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/34 virus.
In another study, Sendai-virus-derived RNA (IVT DI) was used in combination with oil-in-water nanoemulsion (NE) and influenza viral defective interfering RNA (3php). Mice immunized with inactivated A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) adjuvanted with NE/IVT DI or NE/3php showed enhanced systemic-virus-specific IgG levels, with greater avidity and virus neutralization activity compared to the individual adjuvants. Notably, NE/IVT DI induced protective neutralizing titers after a single immunization. NE/IVT DI was then successfully employed in the anti-SARS-CoV-2 protein vaccine. , , 
Synthetic RNA derived from the 546-nt Sendai virus (SeV) copy-back viral genome, namely, oligonucleotide DDO268, was evaluated as an adjuvant for an influenza A virus NP mRNA vaccine. DDO268 induced local type I IFN production, activated DC1, promoted their migration to draining lymph nodes, and enhanced IgG2c antibody production and Th1 CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses. When co-packaged with NP mRNA, DDO268 significantly improved protection against lethal IAV challenge and reduced the antigen dose required for effective immunity.
Luke et al. produced a DNA vaccine co-expressing the H5N1 influenza virus HA antigen and an expressed RNA (eRNA) RIG-I agonist. RIG-I agonist combines two parts: an immunostimulatory dsRNA expressed by convergent transcription (derived from two opposing promoters of partially overlapping genes producing transcripts with potential sense–antisense interactions in the overlapping region) and adenovirus viral-associated (VA) RNAI. After administering this vaccine to mice through intramuscular injection, the mice showed increased HA-specific serum antibody binding avidity.
In contrast, the co-administration of plasmids encoding the HA and NP proteins derived from the H1N1/Puerto Rico/8/1934 strain, along with plasmids encoding molecules from the RIG-I pathway, did not result in improved efficiency of the vaccine. The inclusion of plasmid-encoded cRIG-I and IPS1 did not enhance the immune response or the protective effectiveness of a DNA vaccine against the influenza A virus. However, it is worth noting that the presence of plasmids encoding the HA and NP proteins may already provide an adequate level of inflammation, suggesting that additional adjuvants may not be necessary in this context.

## TLR adaptor
As TLR ligands can cause unwanted side effects, such as hyperinflammation and organ damage, Takeshita et al. investigated the potential of genetic TLR adaptor molecules as adjuvants by constructing dual-promoter plasmids containing the molecule and a model antigen (LacZ). Two TLR adaptor molecules, Toll-IL-1 receptor domain-containing adaptor-inducing beta interferon (TRIF) and myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), were used in the study. The DNA vaccine incorporating the MyD88 gene resulted in improved antigen-specific humoral immune responses in mice, whereas the TRIF genetic adjuvant enhanced cellular immune responses. Furthermore, when the TRIF genetic adjuvant was included in a DNA vaccine targeting the influenza HA antigen or the HPV E7 antigen, it provided improved protection. Vaccination with CMV9-HA provided partial protection, with approximately 50% of mice surviving a low-dose influenza virus challenge and 20% surviving a high-dose challenge. In comparison, the CMV9-HA-TRIF vaccine offered superior protection, protecting 100% of mice against the low-dose challenge and 70% against the high-dose challenge.

## Genetic adjuvants for antigen delivery and recognition
In contrast to the broad group of genetic adjuvants that directly activate immune cells and target specific immune cell receptors, this group of adjuvants focuses on enhancing antigen stabilization, transport/delivery, and processing ( B). Rather than directly influencing immune cells, these adjuvants work to improve how antigens are handled within the body. By enhancing antigen stability and facilitating their efficient transport and processing, these adjuvants aim to optimize the immune system’s response to the vaccine, ultimately leading to a more effective and targeted immune defense. ## Mucosal and connective tissue permeability enhancers
To overcome the mucosal barrier, various types of vaccines are employed, each designed to induce immune responses at mucosal surfaces. These vaccines are administered orally, nasally, sublingually, or vaginally to target specific mucosal tissues such as the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts. By stimulating immune responses at these crucial sites, mucosal vaccines provide a first line of defense against pathogens entering the body through mucosal surfaces. Additionally, mucosal vaccines can elicit both local mucosal immunity and systemic immune responses, offering broader protection against infections. This approach is essential for preventing the spread of diseases and controlling respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary infections.

## Bacterial enterotoxins
Bacterial enterotoxins are toxins produced by certain bacteria that target and affect the lining of the mucosal surfaces, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract. These toxins compromise the mucosal barrier, thereby stimulating immune responses within the mucosal tissues. As a result, some enterotoxins have been explored as potential mucosal adjuvants in vaccines to enhance their efficacy in inducing mucosal immune responses. 
Introduction of A2/B subunit of cholera toxin into plasmid encoding enhanced Toxoplasma gondii major surface antigens SAG1 and SAG3 resulted in a more robust Th1-cell-mediated immune response, characterized by increased levels of IFN-γ, greater lymphocyte proliferation activity, and a higher percentage of CTLs. The use of adjuvant increased the survival rate twice (from 20% to 40%) when challenged with lethal doses of T. gondii.
A novel mucosal adjuvant expressing the receptor-binding domains of Clostridiodes difficile toxins A and B (TcdA/B) was investigated. To test its effectiveness, mice were immunized intranasally with adenovirus SC-Ad6 expressing clade C gp140 from SHIV-1157ipd3N4, along with SC-Ads encoding 4-1BBL, GM-CSF, or C. difficile toxin fragment TcdA/B. Six weeks after a single immunization, significant increases in serum and vaginal antibodies were observed in GM-CSF and TcdA/B groups.

## Antigen processing and presentation stimulants
Antigen presentation stimulants are essential for enhancing immune responses by promoting the activation and maturation of APCs. This section highlights the use of genetically encoded adjuvants, such as ubiquitin and protein aggregation domains, which improve the antigen processing and presentation mechanisms.

## LAMP-1
Lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) is a protein predominantly located on the membranes of lysosomes. Attaching a lysosomal target sequence from LAMP-1 to an immunogen within a DNA plasmid allows the antigen to be redirected from the proteasome-mediated MHC-I to MHC-II. LAMP1 was employed in a DNA vaccine encoding bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV) protective antigen E2. Immunization of mice with pVax-mLAMP1-E2 generated neutralizing antibodies and E2-specific IFN-γ-producing splenocytes. However, its immunogenicity was comparable to the control plasmid, pVax-E2, which lacked the LAMP1 molecule.
In another study, an HIV-1 p55gag DNA vaccine incorporating the complete LAMP1 cDNA sequence was studied. Constructs lacking the LAMP1 luminal domain showed poor expression, limited lysosomal trafficking, and weak immune responses in mice. Adding the LAMP1 luminal domain significantly improved expression, lysosomal targeting, and immune response, with 10-fold higher CD4+, 4- to 5-fold higher CD8+ T cell responses, and antibody titers exceeding 100,000. These findings highlight the LAMP luminal domain’s role in enhancing gag expression, trafficking, and immunogenicity.
An Ebola virus GP DNA vaccine fused with LAMP1 enhanced antigen presentation and immune responses. Three immunizations with pVAX-LAMP/GPEBO significantly increased serum antibody titers and neutralizing activity compared to controls. T cells responded robustly to three MHC-II-restricted peptides, supporting the vaccine’s potential for further evaluation against Ebola.

## Ubiquitin
Ubiquitin (Ub) is a critical regulator of protein degradation and antigen presentation. Lentivirus-mediated delivery of Ub-fused HBcAg (LV-Ub-HBcAg) was employed to examine its impact on immune responses and therapeutic effects in HBV transgenic mice. , The use of Ub induced potent CTL responses and therapeutic immunity. Notably, the vaccine elicited strong antibody responses and resulted in a high percentage of IFN-γ-producing HBV-specific T cells in mice. Additionally, the vaccination of HBV transgenic mice with LV-Ub-HBcAg had significant effects on reducing the levels of serum HBsAg, as well as the expression of liver tissue HBsAg and HBcAg.
Furthermore, the incorporation of LIGHT, a potent T cell co-stimulatory protein, into the lentivirus vector (LVDC-UbHBcAg-LIGHT) designed to target DCs resulted in robust T cell immune responses. Immunization with LVDC-UbHBcAg-LIGHT promoted the production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and Granzyme B by CTLs, as well as IFN-γ by CD4+ T cells, leading to improved HBcAg-specific CTL activities and antibody responses.

## Protein aggregation domains
The von Willebrand I/A domain is the protein domain involved in cell adhesion, extracellular matrix proteins, and integrin receptors. The adjuvant action of a plasmid vector (pATRex) encoding the von Willebrand I/A domain of the tumor endothelial marker-8 (TEM8) was investigated in the context of a DNA vaccine against malaria. Co-administration of pATRex with a plasmid encoding Plasmodium yoelii merozoite surface protein 4/5 (PyMSP4/5) enhanced antibody production and B cell response, promoting Th2 polarization. Mechanistically, pATRex induced the formation of insoluble intracellular aggregates and apoptotic cell death, mediated by a self-assembling peptide sequence (α3-β4) within ATRex. The aggregation-promoting plasmid acts as a genetic adjuvant by triggering caspase activation and apoptotic cell death, thus activating the innate immune system. Nonetheless, a reported finding indicates that adjuvants relying on plasmids encoding protein aggregation domains can potentially disturb the physiological characteristics of bone marrow elements. 

## Cyclophilin A
Cyclophilin A (CyPA), a peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase, facilitates protein folding in the cytoplasm. When used in bicistronic pDNA vaccine co-expressing HIV-1 gag, it elicited a more robust gag-specific cellular immune response compared to gag immunization alone. Additionally, CyPA promoted a broad gag-specific T cell response and sustained cellular immunity for up to 5 months. These findings suggest that CyPA could play a pivotal role in strengthening and prolonging immune responses in DNA vaccine formulations, warranting further exploration of its application as an adjuvant.

## Discussion and concluding remarks
The data presented in this review highlight the significant advancements in genetically encoded adjuvants and their application in vaccine development. Genetic vaccines, predominantly delivered as plasmid DNA, have emerged as a powerful platform due to their ability to sustain antigen expression and stimulate immune responses. Although viral vectors offer additional innate immune activation, their use remains limited. Among the plethora of viral vectors, adenoviruses are the most commonly applied due to their well-characterized biology, high transduction efficiency, and ability to stimulate robust immune responses. However, these vectors suffer from high anti-vector immunity and potential gene toxicity limitations. Other vectors based on RNA viruses could broaden the arsenal of the genetic adjuvants in future, providing efficient transient expression of immune cell activation molecules.
In contrast to traditional vaccines, genetic vaccines rely on intracellular antigen production, which lacks precise control over production longevity and intensity. Additionally, antigens may be secreted into the bloodstream, with potential consequences depending on their properties, including unintended antigen binding and unexpected systemic effects. Therefore, careful monitoring and further research are essential to fully understand and mitigate potential risks while maximizing the benefits of genetic vaccine technology. Given the strong immune stimulation induced by next-generation genetic vaccines, adverse reactions may occur, particularly in younger recipients. To enhance both safety and efficacy, we propose that precise modulation using advanced genetic adjuvants should be further investigated as a means of optimizing genetic vaccination strategies.
A comprehensive evaluation of various genetic adjuvants reveals a wide spectrum of mechanisms, from enhancing antigen delivery and presentation to modulating specific immune pathways. However, comparing the efficacy of these adjuvants remains challenging due to the lack of standardized methodologies and comparative studies. Most research focuses on isolated evaluations, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about the relative advantages of different adjuvant types or their combinations. For example, the activation of DCs and the expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/86) are crucial for the efficacy of adjuvants. However, many studies do not evaluate this aspect, making it difficult to compare results across different investigations. From the analyzed studies, DC-related cytokines (GM-CSF, Flt3L, and IL-33), DAMPs (Hsp70 and HMGB1), and PAMPs (CpG, FliC, dsRNA (NexaVant), synthetic RNA, and TRIF) were shown to upregulate CD80 and/or CD86. However, compiling a comprehensive list of genetically encoded adjuvants able to activate DCs is challenging due to variations in experimental designs. The development of comparative frameworks and cross-platform studies is essential to address this gap.
Traditional adjuvants, such as aluminum salts and oil-in-water emulsions, have been extensively used in vaccines to enhance immune responses. Despite their success, these adjuvants have limitations, including a lack of specificity, adverse side effects, and limited ability to stimulate cellular immunity. Genetic adjuvants, on the other hand, represent an innovative approach that involves encoding immunostimulatory molecules directly into nucleic-acid-based vaccines or therapeutic vectors. These adjuvants can target specific pathways to enhance innate and adaptive immune responses. Genetic adjuvants show great promise in enhancing vaccine efficacy and the longevity of immune responses, although they are not yet approved for clinical use. They offer cost-effective integration into genetic vaccines and could improve immunogenicity in vulnerable populations.
On the other hand, the review also identifies key challenges in the field, such as the complexity of cytokine networks and the safety considerations associated with pathogen-derived adjuvants. Although cytokine-based adjuvants like GM-CSF, IL-12, and IL-6 have shown promising immunostimulatory effects, their application requires careful optimization to avoid unintended immune modulation. Similarly, pathogen-derived molecular adjuvants are highly promising due to their potent immunogenicity and capacity to enhance immune responses, yet they present notable safety challenges. These include potential risks such as triggering unintended immune activation or excessive inflammation. Among the most studied genetic adjuvants in this category are Hsp70 and FliC, which exhibit significant potential for immune modulation. Alternatively, host-derived proteins as vaccine adjuvants offer a solution to some of these safety concerns. Key candidates in this category include GM-CSF, IL-12, CCL19, CCL28, CD40, and sPD-1, which have demonstrated notable immunostimulatory effects. Although cytokine-based adjuvants hold considerable promise, the complexity and incomplete understanding of cytokine networks remain a challenge for their optimal application. The effects of cytokines can be diverse, including enhancement, inhibition, or no effect, and these outcomes are influenced by factors such as the dosage, timing of administration, and the specific animal species. Predicting which effect will predominate is not always straightforward due to the complex interactions involved. , 
For future considerations, other proteins investigated for cancer vaccine development hold significant potential due to their ability to modulate the immune response. Examples include thymosin, which enhances T cell function, and relaxin, which may impact tissue remodeling and immune regulation. Additionally, Ig Fc fragments that can enhance antibody-dependent immune responses, and BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guérin), traditionally used as a vaccine against tuberculosis, , can be repurposed for their immunostimulatory effects as novel vaccine adjuvants.
Furthermore, a combination of different strategies can be promising. Adjuvants from different groups, e.g., antigen delivery and innate immunity stimulants, or checkpoint molecules in combination with antigen presentation, would be beneficial due to the personalized approach based on individual cytokine profiles and immune cell composition. For instance, combining TNF-α and IL-2 could be advantageous due to their complementary roles in activating immune cells and promoting robust immune responses.
Moreover, the therapeutic vector of the adjuvant is equally important, as it can significantly impact the effectiveness and safety of the vaccine. pDNA vaccines face issues with targeted delivery and immunogenicity but offer sustained antigen expression, potentially enhancing immune response over time. Furthermore, after injection of plasmid DNA, produced in bacteria, the cell expressing the foreign gene also activates the TLR ligand pathway, induced by CpG sequences from plasmid DNA, hiding additional unpredictable effects.
RNA vaccines, while avoiding genome integration and producing direct protein in the cytoplasm, are unstable and require a high copy number for efficiency. The problem of RNA instability was partially solved by the introduction of modified nucleotides and sequence optimization contributing to higher translational potency and stability. , mRNA modification by nucleoside substitutions [as N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ)] revolutionized the field of mRNA therapy, reducing recognition of modified RNA by TLR receptors and ribonucleases and subsequent translational silencing, thus prolonging its half-life and enhancing translation efficiency. The use of modified mRNAs leads to high antigen expression and a more robust immune response. However, the impaired binding to corresponding TLR receptors and recognition by cellular ribonucleases possess the risks of chronic mRNA expression and inflammation. The long-term effects and metabolic pathways of modified mRNA bases remain poorly understood and require further evaluation. 
Modified mRNA may lead to the production of unintended proteins due to ribosomal shifting during translation, potentially increasing the risk of autoimmunity. Intracellular production of antigens induces antigen presentation on MHC-I and stimulates the activation of CD8+ T cells. In turn, secreted antigens can be captured and presented by APCs through the classical MHC-II pathway. As a consequence, the transfected cell presenting the introduced antigen in the context of the MHC-I molecule may be recognized by the immune system as altered or foreign, leading to its destruction. This process could potentially trigger autoimmune reactions. Genetic adjuvants can be directly integrated into the mRNA vaccine itself, ensuring that the adjuvant and antigen are delivered together to the same cell. By encoding specific immune-stimulatory proteins or sequences, genetic adjuvants can tailor the immune response to the needs of the vaccine, such as inducing balanced Th1 and Th2 responses and reducing the side effects like excessive inflammation and autoimmunity. The right stimulation of both innate and adaptive immunity maintaining a favorable Th1/Th2 balance is the key task, which must be achieved. Therefore, it is important that chemically modified RNA genetic vaccines have additional genetic adjuvant sequences, which would help to activate the pathogen recognition pathways and stimulate the corresponding immune reaction.
The use of viral vectors offers the superior advantage of efficient transgene delivery, along with the activation of additional PAMPs and DAMPs. However, the innate and adaptive immune response to the virus vector itself can lead to the inefficiency of vaccination due to vector neutralization by the immune system, as well as life-threatening immune reactions. Virus delivery and targeting also present challenges that can result in low efficiency or unwanted side effects.
Genetic adjuvants possess advantages and limitations summarized in . To advance the field, future research should prioritize the following directions: (1) developing more effective and standardized evaluation models to compare adjuvant performance; (2) exploring combination strategies that synergize the benefits of multiple adjuvants, e.g., combining antigen delivery systems with immune checkpoint regulators; (3) improving the delivery mechanisms for RNA-/DNA-based adjuvants to ensure targeted and controlled immune activation; and (4) broadening the application of viral vectors, including RNA viruses, potentially possessing advantages over DNA viruses. Additionally, understanding the long-term effects and safety profiles of these adjuvants is critical, especially as they are integrated into vaccines targeting diverse pathogens and populations.
 In conclusion, genetically encoded adjuvants hold immense potential to revolutionize vaccine efficacy and immunogenicity. Their tailored immune modulation capabilities, combined with the adaptability of DNA and RNA vaccine platforms, position them as a cornerstone of next-generation immunization strategies. However, achieving their full potential will require addressing existing challenges.
