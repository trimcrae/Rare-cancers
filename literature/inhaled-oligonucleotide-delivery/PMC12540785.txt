## Abstract
The lymphatic system maintains tissue fluid balance, and its dysfunction can result in lymphedema. Although cholesterol is essential for cellular function, its role in lymphatic development has remained unknown. Here, we identify APOA1 binding protein (AIBP) as a key regulator that promotes lymphatic endothelial cell fate specification and lymphangiogenesis. Mechanistically, AIBP reduces plasma membrane cholesterol content, thereby enhancing VEGFR3 signaling by disrupting caveolae—small plasma membrane invaginations formed by the scaffolding protein caveolin-1 (CAV-1)—and relieving CAV-1–mediated inhibition. In zebrafish and mice, AIBP loss impairs VEGFR3 signaling and lymphatic development, defects that can be rescued by CAV-1 deletion or by a VEGFR3 mutant (VEGFR3AAA) lacking CAV-1 binding. Administration of recombinant AIBP augments VEGFC-induced lymphangiogenesis and accelerates the resolution of secondary lymphedema in adult mice. These findings define the AIBP–CAV-1 axis as a regulator of VEGFR3 signaling and lymphatic growth, offering potential therapeutic opportunities for treating lymphatic dysfunction.

## Introduction
The lymphatic vasculature is essential for maintaining interstitial fluid homeostasis, dietary lipid transport and immune surveillance – . A lymphatic contribution is implicated in the pathogenesis of a variety of diseases, including lymphedema, tumor metastasis, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, obesity, and diabetic mellitus – . Therapeutic augmentation of lymphangiogenesis has been documented to improve lymphatic structure and function – , , . Earlier studies show that the lymphatic system is derived from the embryonic cardinal vein, where lymphatic endothelial cell (LEC) progenitors are specified and subsequently migrate and establish the lymphatic network – , , . Recent studies indicate that a subset of lymphatics can be developed through non-canonical mechanisms – . During murine development, the assembly of the lymphatic vascular network is initiated at approximately embryonic day 9.5 (E9.5) under the control of VEGFC signaling through its cognate receptor VEGFR3 , . LEC fate commitment occurs through SOX18-induced expression of PROX-1, which, in concert with the orphan nuclear factor NR2F2 (also known as COUP-TFII), dictates LEC differentiation from the cardinal vein , . In addition, GATA2, HHEX, and PROX1 itself have recently been shown to up-regulate PROX1 expression – . The newly specified LECs subsequently bud from the cardinal vein and migrate (E10.0-E11.5) in a dorsolateral fashion to form lymph sacs and, thereby establish the functional lymphatic vessels. VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 signaling is the major driver of lymphangiogenesis in vertebrates. Interestingly, in addition to the regulation of LEC sprouting, VEGFR3 signaling also controls LEC progenitor homeostasis by maintaining PROX1 expression levels in a positive feedback loop . In zebrafish, Vegfc/Vegfr3 signaling increases Prox1+ LEC progenitors in the axis vasculature , . Conversely, zebrafish or mice deficient in either CCBE1 (a critical matrix protein regulating VEGFC bioavailability), VEGFC, or VEGFR3 reduce PROX1-positive LECs in the cardinal vein , – .
The developmental venous origin of the lymphatic endothelium and the requirement for VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling in lymphangiogenesis are highly conserved from zebrafish to mice to humans , . In zebrafish, from 30 to 32 hours post fertilization (hpf), bi-potential precursor cells in the posterior cardinal veins (PCV) express Prox1 and undergo a Vegfc/Vegfr3-dependent cell division to generate lymphatic and venous daughter cells. In response to the Vegfc cue, the daughter cells on the PCV progressively acquire the LEC fate at 36 hpf; among these LECs, approximately half form parachordal LECs at the horizontal myoseptum by 48 hpf , . These parachordal LECs subsequently migrate ventrally and dorsally along the arterial intersegmental vessels (ISVs) at ~60 hpf , to form the thoracic duct (TD), intersegmental lymphatic vessels, and dorsal longitudinal lymphatic vessels . Wnt5b has been reported to function upstream of Prox1 to regulate proper lymphatic specification . Nevertheless, the role of cholesterol in LEC fate determination and lymphangiogenesis remains to be understood.
Cholesterol is enriched in the endothelial caveolae, the 50–100 nm invaginations of the plasma membrane. CAV-1, the structural protein organizing the formation of caveolae, binds cholesterol directly and form these flask-shaped microdomains, which provide a platform to cluster and facilitate membrane-anchored receptor signaling , . CAV-1 ablation in mice eliminates caveolae , . Both the expression of CAV-1 and the formation of caveolae are dependent on cellular cholesterol levels – . In many cases, CAV-1 deficiency alters plasma membrane receptor signaling competence through disruption of caveolae – . How caveolae regulates lymphangiogenesis is unexplored.
We have demonstrated that the secreted protein AIBP limits angiogenesis in a non-cell autonomous fashion. Mechanistically, extracellular AIBP binds to plasma membrane of endothelial cells, accelerates cholesterol efflux from endothelial cells to high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and reduces lipid raft/caveolae abundance, which in turn disrupts VEGFR2 signaling, thereby restricting angiogenesis , . Lymphatic vessels are structurally and functionally related to blood vessels. Many genes that are required for angiogenesis, such as VEGFR3, FGF, DLL4-NOTCH, angiopoietin-TIE2, Ephrin B2, and TGFβ family member ALK1, EPSIN and others, also function in lymphangiogenesis , – . Thus, we sought to identify the role AIBP-regulated cholesterol metabolism in lymphangiogenesis. In contrast to its suppressive role in angiogenesis, our studies reveal an unexpected role of AIBP in promoting lymphangiogenesis. Mechanistically, AIBP augments VEGFC-engaged VEFGR3 signaling in a CAV-1-dependent fashion. Our findings provide potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of lymphatic disease.

## Results

## AIBP controls LEC lineage specification in zebrafish and from mESC
To examine the role of Aibp2 in lymphangiogenesis, apoa1bp2
−/− zebrafish were crossbred with fli1a:egfp zebrafish that express EGFP in the both the lymphatic and blood ECs , , generating apoa1bp2
−/−; fli1a:egfp. We performed immunostaining of Prox1 at 4 days post-fertilization (dpf) because Prox1 is the master transcription factor that dictates LEC fate specification and is used to demarcate LECs , , . In control siblings, ~4.3 of Prox1+ LECs per seven segments were found in the TD. By contrast, Aibp2 knockout reduced the number of LECs to ~2.8 (Fig.  ). The TD is the first functional lymphatic vessel formed in the zebrafish trunk, situated between the dorsal aorta (DA) and PCV. Consistent with this, TD conduit formation is impaired in Aibp2 deficient zebrafish at 4 dpf (Supplementary Fig.  ). The LEC-associated genes were also downregulated in the ECs isolated from apoa1bp2
−/− zebrafish compared with control animals (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data suggest that Aibp2 regulates lymphangiogenesis. In contrast, Aibp2 knockout accelerated intersegmental vessel (ISV) growth and sprouting (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, AIBP knockout had no effect on the formation of central artery (CtA) and sub-intestinal vein (SIV) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistent with our prior reports, Vegfr2 activation was increased but Notch signaling was reduced in the Aibp2 deficient zebrafish (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 We next determined whether AIBP regulates lymphatic cell fate using the mESC to LEC differentiation model. Mouse embryoid bodies prepared from mESCs can be differentiated into derivatives of ectodermal, mesodermal, and endodermal tissues and recapitulate certain developmental processes , . We prepared mouse embryoid bodies and assessed LEC generation in a differentiation medium containing BMP4 and bFGF, followed by additional supplements on day 3 through day 7 with recombinant VEGFA and VEGFC in combination or with AIBP alone (Fig.  ). From day 0 onwards during differentiation, LECs were identified by the expression of PECAM1, LYVE1, and PROX1, as previously reported , . Compared with control cells, VEGFA and VEGFC co-treatment increased the mRNA levels of the LEC-associated genes Prox1, Pecam1, and Lyve1, at day 5 and day 7 of differentiation (Fig.  ). AIBP also substantially increased LEC specification as evidenced by robust expression of the LEC markers (Fig.  ), comparable to the effect of VEGFA/C co-administration. A greater percentage of PECAM+/LYVE1+ LEC population was detected by FACS analysis at day 7 of LEC differentiation in the presence of AIBP or VEGFA/C (Supplementary Fig.  ). During LEC differentiation, our protocol also generated mesoderm and endoderm , resulting in significant upregulation of genes encoding Vegfc and Adamts3, the latter proteolytically processing VEGFC to promote its maturation (Fig.  ). These findings are consistent with the endogenous source of VEGFC observed in zebrafish , . These results suggest that AIBP regulation of LEC fate is conserved across evolution.
We then investigated the pathways transmitting AIBP effect on LEC specification. VEGFR3 and NOTCH1 are the critical regulators of lymphangiogenesis , , , , . Because AIBP also regulates NOTCH signaling , we queried whether NOTCH or VEGFR3 mediated AIBP effect on LEC specification. VEGFR3 inhibition using its specific inhibitor SAR13165 or knockdown using siRNA negated the effect of AIBP on promoting LEC specification, but NOTCH inhibitor DAPT had no effect (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). The inhibitor effects and siRNA-mediated knockdown of VEGFR3 were validated (Supplementary Fig.  ). The data suggest that AIBP promotes LEC fate through VEGFR3 signaling.
We further isolated caveolae fractions from human lymphatic endothelial cells (hLECs) following AIBP treatment using sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation. As expected, AIBP reduced the protein abundance of VEGFR3 and CAV1 in caveolae (Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Table  ). Among the significantly changed plasma membrane receptors, VEGFR3 is the key regulator of LEC fate and lymphangiogenesis (Supplementary Table  ). Supporting the role of AIBP in caveolae regulation, incubation of hLECs with recombinant AIBP increased fluorescent BODIPY-cholesterol efflux to HDL (Supplementary Fig.  ), measured using a surrogate non-radioactive assay for [³H]-cholesterol efflux , . Corroborating our cholesterol efflux analysis, AIBP treatment significantly reduced cholesterol levels in the caveolar fraction (~25% reduction, Supplementary Fig.  ). Thus, our unbiased proteomic studies also validate VEGFR3 as the plasma membrane receptor mediating the AIBP function in LECs.

## Aibp2-mediated cholesterol regulation is essential for proper lymphangiogenesis
Given that AIBP lowers plasma membrane cholesterol in hLECs (Supplementary Fig.  ), we postulated that this reduces the levels of caveolae, a microdomain that is enriched with cholesterol. The presence of caveolae can be analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In line with the reduction in plasma membrane cholesterol, AIBP treatment reduced the number of caveolae on hLEC plasma membrane (4.0 vs. 1.5/µm of cell edge, Fig.  ) . Conversely, loss of Aibp2 in zebrafish elevated caveolae abundance in the cardinal vein ECs compared with control animals (1.6 vs. 2.4/µm of cell edge, Fig.  ). Our data demonstrate that AIBP controls caveolae abundance.
 To further validate the role of cholesterol in LEC commitment, we overexpressed ubi:Gal4-ERT2; UAS:mEos2-APOA1 in apoa1bp2 −/− embryos. The resulting animals ubiquitously expressed 4hydroxy-tamoxifen-inducible and photoconvertible fluorescent protein mEos2-tagged human APOA1, the major protein constituent of HDL (Fig.  ). Human APOA1 was reported to function analogously to zebrafish APOA1 , and this mEos2-APOA1 was designed such that fluorescent protein tagged APOA1 retains its function as a cholesterol acceptor and is secreted . As anticipated, 4hydroxy-tamoxifen addition induced robust mEos2-APOA1 overexpression (Fig.  ). To investigate this APOA1 secretion, we photoconverted mEos2-APOA1 from green to red fluorescence in the anterior portion of the animals (blue region in Fig.  ). We found that the red fluorescence can be readily detectable in unconverted region 3 min post-conversion (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Enforced human APOA1 expression corrected LEC specification in apoa1bp2 knockout animals (3.4 vs 5.9, Fig.  ). To verify the cholesterol efflux function of APOA1 in this rescue, we overexpressed APOA1 mutant (originally designated apoA-IΔ[185–243] , here referred to as APOA1ΔC58) APOA1ΔC58 (ubi:Gal4-ERT2; UAS:mEos2-APOA1ΔC58) in apoa1bp2 −/− embryos. The cholesterol efflux function of HDL derived from this APOA1 mutant was reduced by ~89% . Compared with mEos2-APOA1, overexpression of mEos2-APOA1ΔC58 cannot rescue LEC numbers (Supplementary Fig.  ). As anticipated, the accessible free cholesterol levels of lyve1+ ECs in apoa1bp2 −/− embryos were reduced by overexpression of wild-type APOA1 but not APOA1ΔC58 (Supplementary Fig.  ), as illustrated by FACS-based analysis of D4-GFP staining, a GFP fusion protein of perfringolysin O that binds the pool of plasma membrane-accessible cholesterol not complexed with sphingomyelin . These data suggest that the cholesterol efflux function of APOA1 restores lymphatic development in apoa1bp2 −/− embryos. Collectively, our results indicate that Aibp2 regulates lymphatic vessel formation through cholesterol-mediated mechanism.

## AIBP-mediated reduction of plasma membrane cholesterol disrupts caveolae and enhances VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling
Following LEC specification from the venous ECs, VEGFR3 signaling is required for the maintenance of the LEC progenitor fate, as well as LEC proliferation and migration . Since recombinant AIBP treatment reduced caveolae abundance (Fig.  ), we postulated that this reduction was linked to enhanced VEGFR3 signaling. In line with this hypothesis, CAV-1 in caveolae was reported to repress VEGFR3 activity in human endothelial cells . However, the precise regulatory mechanism, biological impact, and relevance to lymphatics remain unexplored. We hypothesized that AIBP-mediated cholesterol efflux regulates lymphangiogenesis by orchestrating VEGFR3 signaling via CAV-1. We first evaluated the effect of methyl beta cyclodextrin (MβCD), a cholesterol-solubilizing agent, on CAV-1 levels. Our data reveal that treatment with MβCD reduced CAV-1 levels (Fig.  ). This result is consistent with previous findings that cholesterol is required to sustain CAV-1 protein content , , , . As a result, MβCD treatment increased VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 activation (Fig.  ), suggesting that caveolae abundance and associated CAV-1 bioavailability inhibit VEGFR3 signaling.
 Given that AIBP treatment disrupts caveolae (Fig.  ), we tested whether AIBP can enhance VEGFR3 signaling through lowering caveolae and the associated CAV-1. We first analyzed the effect of AIBP on VEGFR3 localization in caveolae. We performed sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation analysis of hLECs that were incubated with recombinant AIBP in medium containing 10% FBS, which inherently provides HDL levels comparable to those used in prior studies , , . As anticipated, VEGFR3 was present in the caveolar fractions that were positive for CAV-1 (Fig.  ). AIBP treatment disrupted caveolae, as shown by decreased CAV-1 levels (caveolae marker) in the caveolar fraction (Fig.   ). Furthermore, AIBP and HDL treatment reduced VEGFR3 levels in the caveolar/raft (CAV-1+&FLOT1+) fraction compared with control medium (Fig.   ). To investigate the effect of AIBP-mediated caveolae disruption on VEGFR3 signaling, we treated hLECs with AIBP, HDL, or their combination, followed by VEGFC stimulation. Our data unveil that AIBP and HDL in combination, but neither alone, facilitated VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 activation, as shown by markedly increased phosphorylation of AKT and ERK1/2, the two downstream effector kinases (Fig.  ). Consistent with these results, AIBP enhanced VEGFR3 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, Erk1/2 activation was attenuated in Aibp2 null zebrafish LECs (Fig.  ). Confocal microscopy analysis validated that AIBP treatment reduced VEGFC-elicited VEGFR3 co-localization with CAV-1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). AIBP treatment similarly decreased VEGFR2 colocalization with CAV-1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that AIBP treatment disrupts plasma membrane caveolae, thereby facilitating VEGFR3 activation.
VEGFR3 homodimerization and VEGFR2/3 heterodimerization contribute to VEGFR3 activation. To investigate their dimerization, EGFP-tagged VEGFR3 or EGFP control was overexpressed in hLECs. The resulting cells were treated with AIBP/HDL and VEGFC, and immunoprecipitation was performed using EGFP antibody. Western blot analysis revealed no change of VEGFR3 homodimerization or VEGFR2/VEGFR3 heterodimerization (Supplementary Fig.  ). While AIBP and HDL treatment further augmented VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 phosphorylation, this treatment had no effect on VEGFC-elicited VEGFR2 activation (Supplementary Fig.  ). VEGFC-induced endocytosis is required to sustain VEGFR2/3 signaling , . As expected, VEGFC stimulation induced endocytosis of both VEGFR2 and VEGFR3, as evidenced by their co-distribution with EEA1, an early endosome marker (Supplementary Fig.  ). VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 endocytosis can be further enhanced by AIBP (1.4-fold increase, Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, VEGFC-induced VEGFR2 endocytosis was mildly reduced by AIBP treatment (12% decrease, Supplementary Fig.  ). Interestingly, AIBP and HDL, in the absence of VEGFC, can lead to 1.3-fold and 1.8-fold potentiation of VEGFR2 and VEGFR3 endocytosis, respectively (Supplementary Fig.  ). This AIBP enhancement of endocytosis was corroborated by a recent study in macrophages . Taken together, our data suggest that AIBP regulates lymphangiogenesis primarily via potentiating VEGFR3 signaling.

## Disrupted CAV-1 and VEGFR3 interaction augments VEGFR3 signaling in hLECs
Cav-1 can function as a scaffolding protein that provides a spatially restricted platform for proper signaling of cell surface receptors . Cav-1 contains a signaling motif that interacts with a variety of membrane receptors and modulates their activities . This conserved motif is present in VEGFR3 (Fig.  ). Indeed, co-immunoprecipitation analysis shows that endogenous CAV-1 binds VEGFR3 in hLECs (Fig.  ). Next, we examined whether this motif played a role in VEGFR3 signaling. We thus generated a VEGFR3 mutant VEGFR3AAA that lacks the three conserved amino acids in the hypothetical CAV-1 binding domain. We overexpressed EGFP, VEGFR3-EGFP, or VEGFR3AAA-EGFP in hLECs using lentivirus-mediated gene transduction and tested CAV-1 and VEGFR3 interaction. Our results show that wild-type VEGFR3 but not VEGFR3AAA bound CAV-1 (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we generated APEX2 (a modified soybean ascorbate peroxidase)-tagged VEGFR3 or VEGFR3AAA and transfected them into hLECs. TEM analysis of APEX2 substrate deposition uncovered that VEGFR3AAA localization in caveolae was reduced compared to wild-type VEGFR3 (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, the loss of VEGFR3AAA and CAV-1 interaction was also validated using proximity ligation assay (PLA), which detects interactions within 40 nm distance (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 Point mutations in the CAV-1 binding domain did not alter binding of VEGFR3AAA to its adaptor protein GRB2, compared to wild-type VEGFR3 (Supplementary Fig.  ). To test the signaling potency of the VEGFR3AAA, we ectopically expressed EGFP, VEGFR3-EGFP, and VEGFR3AAA-EGFP in hLECs, followed by stimulation with VEGFC. The immunoblotting results showed that the AAA mutations resulted in more potent VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 phosphorylation (2.1-fold increase, Fig.  ) as well as greater AKT and ERK1/2 activation when compared to wild-type VEGFR3 (~2-fold elevation, Fig.  ). Taken together, our data suggest that CVA-1 restricts VEGFR3 signaling, and abolishing CAV-1 interaction increases VEGFR3 signaling in hLECs.

## Cav-1 regulates LEC progenitor development, and loss of Cav-1 rescues defective lymphangiogenesis in Aibp2-deficient zebrafish
To explore the role of Cav-1 in lymphatic vessel formation, we generated cav-1−/− mutant zebrafish (Supplementary Fig.  ). Western blot analysis revealed the absence of Cav-1 protein (Supplementary Fig.  ). Morphologically, Cav-1 null zebrafish are comparable to the control animals from 24 hpf to 4 dpf (Supplementary Fig.  ). TEM analysis of cardinal vein endothelium of 2 dpf cav-1−/− embryos revealed a marked reduction of caveolae number (from 1.7 to 0.3/µm of cell edge, Fig.  ). Because CAV-1 knockout murine endothelium manifests no caveolae , , we speculate that this residual caveola is due to maternal effects or certain genetic compensation. Stable Cav-1 knockout embryos and control siblings showed comparable numbers of Prox1 labeled LEC progenitors following evaluation of RFP+ cells in Cav-1−/−; Prox1aBAC:KalTA4-4xUAS-E1b:uncTagRFP (shortened as Prox1a:RFP hereafter) at 36 hpf (Supplementary Fig.  ) and similar blood vessel formation (Supplementary Fig.  ). Systemic Cav-1 knockout often triggers genetic compensation. To exclude potential genetic compensation, we employed efficient Cas12a/crRNA-mediated acute Cav-1 deletion (Supplementary Fig.  ). This method resulted in ~81% indel within Cav-1 exon 2, as evidenced using PCR amplification of the predicted editing region coupled with MslI digestion (Supplementary Fig.  ). Cav-1 protein deficiency was verified using Western blot analysis (89% reduction, Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast to stable Cav-1 knockout, acute Cav-1 deficiency in zebrafish increased lymphangiogenesis, as shown by the presence of increased number of Prox1+ LECs in cardinal vein (Supplementary Fig.  ). Molecularly, Cav-1 deficiency enhanced Erk activation (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistent with this finding, CAV-1 knockout neonatal mice at postnatal day 3 (P3) exhibited more lymphatic vessel formation in the tail and ear (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that CAV-1 is involved in lymphangiogenesis.
 Given that Aibp2 knockout increased caveolae levels and that CAV-1- negatively regulates VEGFR3 signaling, we next determined whether Cav-1 deficiency can correct lymphatic vessel development of Aibp2 null embryos. Cas12a/cav-1 crRNA co-administration, but not cav-1 crRNA alone, rescued LEC development in Aibp2 null embryos (Fig.  ). These data suggest that immediate Cav-1 deletion restores lymphangiogenesis in Aibp2 knockout zebrafish.
To determine whether Vegfr3 mediates the Cav-1 effect on lymphatic development in zebrafish, we generated a Vegfr3AAA knock-in zebrafish line (flt4+/AAA) by replacing the three amino acids (W1106/F1108/W1113) within the Cav-1 binding site of Vegfr3 with alanine (Supplementary Fig.  ). Vegfr3AAA knock-in zebrafish exhibited 2-fold increase in Prox1+ cells compared to control animals when crossed with Prox1a:RFP zebrafish (4.4 vs. 9.1; Fig.  ). Consistent with enhanced Vegfr3 signaling, flt4+/AAA mutant rescued LEC specification in the apoa1bp2 −/− embryos (Fig.  ). On the other hand, we found similar extent of angiogenesis in the flt4+/AAA vs. wild-type embryos (Supplementary Fig.  ). In sum, our data suggest that Cav-1 represses Vegfr3-dependent lymphatic progenitor development in zebrafish.

## Recombinant AIBP administration promotes adult lymphangiogenesis in mice
We further investigated the role of AIBP-CAV1 axis in adult lymphatic growth using the murine model. The corneas of adult mice lack lymphatic vasculature , , and is a widely used model to study injury or growth factor-induced lymphangiogenesis (Fig.  ). We implanted VEGFC-containing pellets into the corneas of B6 mice, either alone or in combination with recombinant AIBP, APOA1 (the core protein component of HDL), or the CAV-1-modulating peptide (CAV-1 scaffolding domain, CSD). We used APOA1 in this assay due to technical difficulties in preparing HDL pellets. As expected, VEGFC pellet implantation elicited robust lymphangiogenesis (Fig.  ). AIBP and APOA1 in combination or CSD further increased VEGFC-induced lymphangiogenesis (Fig.  ). AIBP in combination with VEGFC exhibited a similar extent of pro-lymphangiogenic efficacy as AIBP and APOA1 combination (Fig.  ). This is likely because mice generally exhibit high HDL levels resulting from APOA1 lipidation. In contrast, APOA1 and VEGFC combination did not show additive effects on lymphangiogenesis compared with VEGFC alone (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, no pro-lymphangiogenic effect was detected with AIBP/APOA1 or CSD treatment in the absence of VEGFC (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data suggest that the enhancement effect of AIBP or CAV-1 on corneal lymphangiogenesis is dependent on VEGFC.
 Inflammation, mirrored by greater inflammatory cell infiltration, has been reported to influence corneal lymphangiogenesis in injury-induced lymphangiogenesis , . We thus investigated the presence of leukocytes and macrophages in VEGFC-induced corneal lymphangiogenesis model. Immunostaining with CD45 and F4/80 antibodies followed by confocal microscopy reveals comparable numbers of corneal leukocytes and macrophages following by control PBS or VEGFC pellet implantation (Supplementary Fig.  ). Therefore, the AIBP-CAV-1 axis regulation of lymphatics is unlikely to be dependent on inflammation. In addition, AIBP is reported to be anti-inflammatory in both the peripheral and central nerve system , , , which argues against the possibility that AIBP facilitates lymphangiogenesis via an inflammation-dependent mechanism. Collectively, these data highlight the conserved role of AIBP-CAV1 axis in increasing lymphangiogenesis.

## AIBP gain-of-function improves VEGFC-elicited tail lymphedema recovery in mice
To determine the potential therapeutic significance of the AIBP-CAV-1-VEGFR3 signaling axis, we utilized the murine tail lymphedema model. The circumferential skin was surgically removed to disrupt the lymphatic flow and elicit lymphedema as previously described , . We intraperitoneally delivered recombinant VEGFC protein, alone or in combination with recombinant human AIBP, and assessed the recovery from lymphedema. Consistent with corneal lymphangiogenesis, AIBP/VEGFC co-administration enhanced recovery from lymphedema compared to VEGFC treatment alone (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
In this study, we uncovered a cholesterol-regulated mechanism underlying lymphatic development. Our data suggest that CAV-1 in caveolae represses VEGFR3 signaling. AIBP-mediated reduction of plasma membrane cholesterol in LECs—an effect to which accelerated cholesterol efflux to HDL contributes—leads to a decrease in caveolae abundance. Consistent with this model, caveolae is reported to facilitate cholesterol efflux to HDL . Consequently, less CAV-1 binds and represses VEGFR3, thereby facilitating VEGFR3 activation. As a result, AIBP increases VEGFC-induced VEGFR3 endocytosis and enhances VEGFR3 signaling (Supplementary Fig.  ).
In addition to our proposed model, AIBP is reported to promote endocytosis and reduces caveolae/lipid raft levels via modulating cytoskeletal rearrangement . Interestingly, endocytosis also plays a critical role in cholesterol efflux . Therefore, in addition to mitigating CAV-1 inhibition of VEGFR3, AIBP could enhance VEGFR3 signaling via increasing endocytosis that is coupled to cholesterol efflux.

## Opposing roles of AIBP in angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis
Our previous studies found that AIBP regulates both VEGFR2 and NOTCH1 signaling in angiogenesis and hematopoiesis , . Here, we show that AIBP increases lymphangiogenesis and instructs LEC progenitor specification, an event that is dependent on VEGFR3 signaling but not on NOTCH1 activity. Although LECs are derived from blood ECs, their functions and molecular identifications differ. The fundamental differences between blood ECs and LECs may contribute to the distinct effects of AIBP on angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis. While both VEGFR2/VEGFR3 and VEGFR3/VEGFR3 dimers function in lymphangiogenesis , AIBP preferentially regulates VEGFR3 homodimers. Importantly, Vegfr3 per se, but not Vegfr2/Vegfr3 signaling is essential for TD lymphatic development in zebrafish , .
It is not unprecedent that one protein may have the opposing effects on angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis. For instance, RhoB ablation augments lymphangiogenesis but impairs angiogenesis in the cutaneous wound healing model . Although NOTCH signaling limits angiogenesis – , NOTCH can inhibit or augment lymphangiogenesis in a context-dependent fashion , , , . In addition, a recent study also documents the contrary role of two angiostatic components, DLL4 and S1PR1, in lymphangiogenesis .

## AIBP-mediated CAV-1-dependent regulation of VEGFR3 signaling and lymphangiogenesis
Lymphatic vessel formation requires LEC fate commitment and subsequent sprouting. LEC fate acquisition, LEC sprouting from the embryonic cardinal vein, and dorsolateral migration, governed by VEGFC/VEGFR3/PROX1 signaling axis, is highly conserved . A positive feedback loop between VEGFR3 and PROX1 is necessary to maintain the identity of LEC progenitors in mice and zebrafish , . Restricted expression of PROX1 protein dictates LEC identity, which is critical for future LECs to migrate out of the PCV . Our studies reveal that AIBP enhances LEC fate commitment through enhancing VEGFR3 activation. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that AIBP also affects LEC motility post LEC fate specification.
Contrary to zebrafish, loss of CAV-1 in neonatal mice promotes lymphangiogenesis. The lymphatic formation in adult mice, however, is similar between wild-type and CAV-1 knockout mice . One possible explanation is that the CAV-1 effect on lymphatics is stage-specific, and the early effect is compensated for by other mechanisms in adults. However, the AIBP-CAV-1 axis promotes VEGFC-induced lymphangiogenesis in the cornea and rescues tail lymphedema in adult mice, suggesting a potential therapeutic role of enhancing this axis. This treatment regimen has theoretical superiority to isolated VEGFC augmentation, because VEGFC enhances neovascularization and vascular leakage . In contrast, AIBP supplement or CAV-1 inhibition confers an angiostatic effect , , .

## Conserved role of AIBP in the regulation of LEC fate and lymphangiogenesis
In the mESC-to-LEC differentiation model, we found that recombinant AIBP incubation displays a comparable effect as VEGFA/C combination on the LEC specification arising from mesodermal origin. Given that AIBP alone has no observable effect on VEGFR3 phosphorylation, this AIBP effect on LEC fate commitment is likely due to a synergistic effect of AIBP and increased VEGFC levels in the induced mesoderm/endoderm , . Furthermore, our siRNA-mediated VEGFR3 knockdown and inhibitor-based VEGFR3 inhibition compellingly demonstrate that AIBP controls LEC fate via VEGFR3.
Earlier studies support the fact that LECs emerge from venous origin , , . However, other mechanisms of lymphatic vessel formation have been reported, such as in the zebrafish facial lymphatics , and in murine dermal and cardiac lymphatic vessel formation , , and in zebrafish brain , . The Aibp2 effect on lymphatic vessel formation is blunted at later developmental stages in zebrafish, and we also did not detect a significant difference in tail lymphangiogenesis between AIBP knockout neonatal mice and control littermates, which could be due to the rescue effect of non-venous origin-derived lymphangiogenesis.

## Generation of human VEGFR3AAA mutant
Lentiviral vector expressing wild-type human VEGFR3 (FUW-hR3-EGFP) was purchased from Addgene, deposited by the Alitalo lab (#119231). Two µl of genomic DNA extract was used for PCR amplification. Five ml of the PCR product were digested using MslI restriction enzyme, and the resulting fragment size were determined using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. ## Caveolar fraction isolation and protein extraction
The hLECs of ~90%–100% density in two 15 cm dish were either incubated with vascular cell basal medium (Cat # PCS-100-030, ATCC) containing 10% FBS (Cat # 30-2021, ATCC) in the presence or absence of 100 ng/ml recombinant AIBP (Cat #13421-H08H, Sino Biological) for 2 h at humidified 37 °C incubator. The resulting cells were washed using ice-cold PBS. Following complete PBS removal, cells were scraped into 1 ml of 0.5 M sodium carbonate per 15 cm dish. The protein concentrations of hLECs treated with recombinant AIBP or control were measured using Coomassie Plus™ Protein Assay Reagent (Product # 1856210, ThermoFisher Scientific). Equal amount of protein extracts was used to prepare 45%, 35%, and 4% sucrose density, as we previously described , and centrifuged at 39,000 rpms for 16–20 h at 4 °C in an SW41 rotor (Beckman Instruments). Ten to eleven 1 ml fraction of the resulting spins were sequentially collected from the top to the bottom of the tube.
The caveolar fractions were harvested, and a final concentration of 20% Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was slowly added to extract proteins overnight at 4 °C. The resulting proteins of caveolar fractions were precipitated by centrifugation at 12,600 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Following three times of wash with 1 ml ice cold ethanol and air dry, the protein pellets were dissolved in 8 M freshly prepared urea in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH8.0), with the aid of sonication. Samples were loaded into the 50 µl well of Mini-PROTEAN TGX stain-free Gels (Cat #4568084, BIO-RAD) and run for 10–15 min at 20 mA. The portion of the lane containing the samples was cut out and sent for LS/MS/MS analysis.

## In-gel digestion of proteins
Caveolar proteins from control or recombinant AIBP-treated hLECs, collected from four independent experiments, were isolated using sucrose-based ultracentrifugation. The proteins were solubilized in freshly prepared 8 M urea in 100 mM Tris (pH 8.0) and 1×Laemmli loading buffer, separated by running approximately 1 cm into an SDS-PAGE gel, and the corresponding gel region was excised. Proteins within the dissected gel band were subjected to in-gel digestion as previously described .

## LC-MS/MS analysis
An aliquot of the tryptic digest (in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid in water) was analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap FusionTM TribridTM mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific), interfaced with a Dionex UltiMate 3000 Binary RSLCnano System. Peptides were separated onto an analytical C18 column (100 μm ID x 25 cm, 5 μm, 18 Å) at a flow rate of 350 nl/min. Gradient conditions were: 3%–22% solvent B for 40 min; 22%–35% solvent B for 10 min; 35%–90% solvent B for 10 min; 90% B held for 10 min (solvent B, 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The peptides were analyzed using the data-dependent acquisition method, and the Orbitrap Fusion was operated to measure FTMS1 at a resolution of 120,000 FWHM, with a scan range of 350–1500 m/z, an AGC target of 2E5, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. During a maximum cycle time of 3 s, the ITMS2 spectra were collected in rapid scan rate mode, featuring HCD NCE 34, a 1.6 m/z isolation window, an AGC target of 1E4, a maximum injection time of 35 ms, and dynamic exclusion set for 20 s.

## MS data acquisition
The raw data files were processed using ThermoFisher Scientific’s Proteome Discoverer™ software, version 1.4. The spectra were searched against the Uniprot-Homo sapiens database using Sequest. The search results were refined to a 1% FDR for strict conditions and 5% for relaxed conditions using Percolator. For the trypsin analysis, up to two missed cleavages were allowed. The MS tolerance was set at 10 ppm, and the MS/MS tolerance was set at 0.8 Da.

The Tg(fli1a:egfp) zebrafish embryos were utilized for the experiments. At 24 hpf, the embryos were treated with 1× PTU (0.003% of 1-phenyl 2-thiourea, Cat# P7629, Sigma) to inhibit pigment formation. Post chorion removal and treatment with 1× tricaine (3-amino benzoic acid ethyl ester, Cat# A5040, Sigma), 15-20 embryos were collected at either 36 hpf or 4 dpf. The embryos were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight, followed by a wash in 100% methanol. They were then stored at −20 °C for at least one day before use. Prior to staining, the embryos were incubated in 3% H2O2 diluted in methanol at −20 °C for 1 h, followed by a wash in 100% methanol. For permeabilization, the embryos were washed with ddH2O and stored in chilled acetone for 20 min at −20 °C. Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the embryos in 150 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.5) for 5 min, followed by incubation at 70 °C for 20 min, and then cooled to room temperature.
For 2nd step permeabilization, the embryos were washed twice with PBS and transferred to 1X Proteinase K solution (Stock 18.9 mg/mL, Cat# 03115828001, Roche, USA). Incubation times were 5 min for 36 hpf embryos and 30 min for 4 dpf embryos at room temperature. The embryos were then washed with 1X PBS followed by 1X PBS/0.5% Triton X-100.
The embryos were incubated overnight at 4 °C with shaking in blocking solution (1X PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 with 10% goat serum and 1% BSA). They were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 250–500 µl of blocking solution for 1–2 days at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were: Prox1a (rabbit IgG, 1:500, Cat# GTX128354, GeneTex, USA), Prox1 (rabbit IgG, 1:500, Cat# 11-002 P, Angiobio), pErk1/2 (rabbit IgG, 1:500, Cat# 9101, Cell Signaling Technology), and GFP (chicken IgY, 1:1000, Cat# A10262, ThermoFisher Scientific). After incubation, the embryos were washed six times in 1X PBS/0.1% Tween for 30 min at room temperature. Secondary antibodies, diluted in 250–500 µL of blocking solution, were applied for 1 day at 4 °C: Anti-chicken-488 (1:2000, Cat# A11039, ThermoFisher Scientific), anti-mouse-HRP (1X, Cat# B40962, ThermoFisher Scientific), and anti-rabbit-HRP (1X, Cat# B40961, ThermoFisher Scientific).
Post-secondary antibody incubation, the embryos were washed six times with 1X PBS/0.1% Tween for 30 min at room temperature. They were then transferred to 1× TSA working solution (20X stock, Cat# B40957, ThermoFisher Scientific) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. This was followed by overnight incubation with TSA working solution (1:500, Alexa Fluor™ 594 Tyramide SuperBoost™ Kit, Cat# B40925 or B40915, ThermoFisher Scientific) at room temperature with shaking. After 12 washes in 1X PBS/0.1% Tween, each lasting 30 min, the embryos were embedded in 1% low melting agarose on a 6-well glass bottom plate and imaged using either a Nikon A1 confocal microscope or Olympus FV 3000 confocal microscope.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
AIBP antibodies were generated in rabbits using tag-free recombinant human AIBP proteins. AIBP antibody was subsequently purified from the rabbit anti-serum using affinity purification, where AIBP antigen column was prepared by covalently conjugating recombinant AIBP to the amine-reactive, beaded-agarose resin.
The 5 µm human tissue sections were deparaffinized and serially rehydrated through xylene, 100%, 80%, and 70% ethanol, and then rinsed in Milli-Q water. The sections underwent antigen recovery by immersion in 100 °C citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 min. Subsequently, a 3% hydrogen peroxide incubation was applied at room temperature for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Following rinsing in TBST, the slides were further washed and blocked in TBST with 1% BSA. The slides were incubated with the AIBP antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After thorough washing in TBST, the slides were sequentially incubated with biotinylated anti-rabbit (1:200) and avidin-conjugated alkaline phosphatase for 30 min (1:1000) at room temperature. An AP blue substrate mixture was applied for final color development (15–30 min). Finally, the slides were mounted in an aqueous mounting medium.

## Mouse Husbandry
Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12-h light/12-hour dark cycle, with controlled ambient temperature (22 ± 2°C) and relative humidity (50 ± 10%). All mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Houston Methodist Research Institute and Boston Children’s Hospital, Harvard Medical School.

## Mouse cornea lymphangiogenesis
VEGFC pellet preparation and implantation were carried out as previously described with minor modifications , , . Briefly, pellets were prepared containing recombinant VEGFC (Cat # P49767, PEPROTECH; stock concentration: 5 mg/mL), AIBP (stock concentration: 10 mg/mL) combined with APOA1 (stock concentration: 15 mg/mL), CAV-1 scaffolding domain (CSD; Cat # ALX-153-064-M005, Enzo Life Sciences; stock concentration: 5 mg/mL), as well as combinations of VEGFC with AIBP and APOA1, or with CSD. The pellet components were mixed with a 10% sucralfate solution (Cat # S0652, Sigma; w/v in PBS) and 12% poly-HEMA (Cat # P3932, Sigma; w/v in ethanol). The final mixture was assembled as follows: 5 µL of poly-HEMA, 1 µL of sucralfate, 2 µL VEGFC, 2 µL AIBP, 2 µL APOA1, 2 µL CSD, and PBS to a final volume of 10 µL.
Droplets of 0.6–0.8 µL of the mixture were spotted individually onto PARAFILM, UV-irradiated for 15 min, and then air-dried in a cell culture hood. Each pellet contained ~800 ng of VEGFC, 1600 ng of AIBP, 2400 ng of APOA1, and 800 ng of CSD. After drying, pellets were carefully recovered from the PARAFILM, visually inspected for uniformity, and selected to ensure consistent sizes. Dried pellets were either used immediately or stored at 4 °C for up to one week.
For implantation, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and 0.5% proparacaine HCl was applied topically to the cornea. After 5 min, the mouse eye was positioned under a dissection microscope, and a gentle incision was made with a von Graefe cataract knife from the center of the cornea toward the corneal limbus, creating a pocket approximately 1.5–2 mm² by carefully maneuvering the knife. Pellets were inserted into the corneal pocket using forceps and the von Graefe cataract knife, flattening the pellet to secure its position. An ophthalmic antibiotic ointment was applied topically to the treated eye. Ten days post-implantation, the cornea was dissected, washed in PBS, and prepared for further analysis. For each experimental condition, age-matched C57BL/6 mice (6–12 months old) of both sexes were used without sex-based separation.

## Dissection of the ear and tail skin of neonatal mice
After euthanization, the tails of Cav-1−/− (#007083; JAX) or littermate control at P3 were dissected, with the tail tips removed. The tail bones and extra connective tissues were then manually excised. Tails were incubated in 20 mM EDTA/PBS overnight at 4 °C to facilitate dermis isolation. The dissected dermis was washed with PBS for subsequent immunostaining as described . Additionally, ear buds from mice at P3 were collected and similarly washed with PBS for subsequent immunostaining procedures.

## Immuno-fluorescence (IF) staining of mouse cornea, ear, and tail skin
For IF staining, the cornea, tail skin, and ear buds were dissected and fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, samples were washed 5 times with 0.05% tween/PBS (0.05% PBS-TW) and blocked with the blocking buffer (1% goat or donkey serum, 1% BSA, and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 3–4 h at room temperature. Samples were incubated for one day with anti-mouse LYVE-1 antibody (1:500, rabbit IgG, Cat # 11-034, AngioBio) and CD31 antibody (1:500, rat IgG, Cat # 553369, BD Biosciences), both diluted in the blocking solution.
Following primary antibody incubation, the samples were washed six times with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS at room temperature. The tissues were then incubated overnight with secondary antibodies solution containing anti-Rabbit 488 (1:1000, Cat# A11070, ThermoFisher SCientific) and anti-Rat 568 (1:1000, Cat# A11077, ThermoFisher SCientific), diluted in blocking buffer. This was followed by six washes with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS. The immunostained tissues were flat-mounted using mounting medium and stored at 4 °C until imaging.

## Mouse tail lymphedema model
To induce tail edema, a full-thickness circumferential dermal and subcutaneous excision, approximately 3 mm in width, was performed in the tail dermis 10 mm from the base of the mouse tail, effectively removing the superficial lymphatic network, as previously described , , . Briefly, 8- to 10-week-old mice were anesthetized using inhaled isoflurane (2.5%). The mice were then secured on the operating table, and their tails were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol and povidone-iodine (Medline, 10%). A 3 mm incision was made 10 mm from the tail base using a sterile surgical blade (Feather, MFR: 2976#21) to disrupt the lymphatic vessels. All procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions to ensure sterility. Care was taken to preserve the integrity of major underlying blood vessels and tendons to prevent necrosis of the tail distal to the incision. Each mouse was administered recombinant human VEGFC (0.7 µg) or a combination of VEGFC (0.7 µg) and AIBP (5 µg) intraperitoneally, twice a week. Age-matched C57BL/6 mice (4–6 months old) of both sexes were used.
Postoperative swelling progression was assessed by marking approximately seven sites, 5 mm apart, from the wound edge to the tip of the tail. ## Confocal imaging and image quantification
Flat-mounted tissues were imaged using Nikon A1 confocal microscope or Olympus FV 3000 confocal microscope. An auto-stitching function was applied to generate composite images of the entire cornea, tail, and ear images. Z-stacked images were maxi-projected and processed using Image J. Color balance and thresholds were standardized across samples to ensure consistent image quantification.
For the quantification of tail lymphatic vessels, images were converted to binary image using “Erode” or “Dilate” functions available in ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/docs/menus/process.html). Images were then skeletonized, and the length of the skeleton was quantified. Pixel values were converted to micrometer (µm) to provide measurements in actual units.
For quantification of corneal lymphatic vessel and blood vessel, regions of interest (ROI) were selected based on the location of the VEGFC-containing pellets. ROI images were processed using ImageJ to quantify the percentage coverage of lymphatic vessel or blood vessels, as previously reported .

## Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests with Welch’s correction. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, while two-way ANOVA was applied to assess multiple groups under different treatment conditions, followed by appropriate post hoc corrections. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
The substantial personnel turnover during the extended course of this study enhanced the reproducibility of the findings, as most experiments, if not all, were independently repeated by different researchers. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
