## Abstract

## Background
Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis. Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA) represents a novel class of small non-coding RNAs. However, the role and potential mechanism of piRNA in GIOP remain unclear.

## Methods
Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during the chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) and in osteoblasts treated with dexamethasone were screened. Osteogenic differentiation and adipogenic differentiation of hBMSCs were induced, and a rat GOIP model was constructed. qRT-PCR was used to screen the differentially expressed piRNAs (piR-27222). MiRDB and TargetRank databases were used to screen the potential target genes of piR-27222, and the key target gene was further screened by topological analysis based on protein-protein interaction network. The potential binding sites of piR-27222 and WW domain-containing protein 1 (WWP1) were predicted using miRanda database, which were verified by Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay. The piR-27222 was then selectively modulated in hBMSCs via transfection, and the piR-rno-27222 antagonist was injected into GIOP rats through the tail vein. The viability of hBMSCs was detected using CCK-8 and colony formation assay. Alizarin red staining and oil red O staining were respectively used to evaluate the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. The bone formation area of GIOP rats was detected by H&E staining. The contents of glutathione, malonaldehyde and Fe2+ were detected using the corresponding kits. The expression of piRNAs was detected by qRT-PCR, and the expression levels of osteogenic differentiation markers runt-related transcription factor 2 and osteopontine, WWP1, as well as ferroptosis-related proteins were detected by Western blot.

## Results
piR-27222 was significantly down-regulated in the GIOP rat model and significantly up-regulated during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. Overexpression of piR-27222 could significantly promote the proliferation and viability of hBMSCs, and piR-27222 inhibitors could significantly inhibit the proliferation and viability of hBMSCs. Overexpression of piR-27222 could also significantly promote osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and inhibit adipogenic differentiation. In addition, piR-27222 could significantly inhibit ferroptosis of hBMSCs and GIOP rat models. WWP1 was identified as a target gene of piR-27222, and negatively regulated by it.

## Conclusion
piR-27222 inhibits ferroptosis by repressing WWP1, promotes osteogenic differentiation and suppresses adipogenic differentiation, and thereby alleviates dexamethasone-induced osteoporosis.

## Introduction
Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disease characterized by an imbalance between bone formation and bone resorption, as well as destruction of bone microstructure. It can be classified into two major categories: primary osteoporosis and secondary osteoporosis. Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is a common adverse reaction in patients treated with glucocorticoids (GCs), and it is also one of the most common types of secondary osteoporosis [ , ]. Among patients who have undergone long-term GC treatment, more than 30% will develop osteoporotic fractures [ ]. The pathogenesis of GIOP is related to GC-induced osteoblast and osteocyte apoptosis. At present, although many drugs such as bisphosphonates, anti-receptor activator of nuclear factor-κ B ligand (RANKL) antibodies and teriparatide, which are selective estrogen receptor modulators, have been recommended for the prevention and treatment of GIOP, these drugs may cause toxic or adverse reactions [ ]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify new therapeutic targets of GIOP in order to develop new therapeutic drugs.
Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNA, circular RNA and long non-coding RNA, play significant regulatory roles in the pathogenesis of musculoskeletal diseases (including but not limited to tendon injury, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and osteoporosis), bone remodeling and metabolism [ – ]. Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA) is a newly discovered subtype of small non-coding RNA that exerts its regulatory functions by specifically binding to PIWI proteins [ , ]. In recent years, increasing evidence supports that piRNAs can participate in the pathogenesis of diseases by degrading target mRNAs at the post-transcriptional level [ ]. The 3’ terminal of piRNA is modified with 2’-O-methylation, and this structural characteristic enables it to bind to PIWI proteins, and then PIWI mediates piRNA recognition and silencing of target mRNA [ ]. Interestingly, piRNA exists in exosomes derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), a crucial cell type modulating the pathogenesis and progression of osteoporosis, and piRNA is dynamically expressed during the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [ ]. Some piRNAs have been reported to modulate bone formation, including piRNA DQ690018, piRNA-63049 and piRNA-36741 [ – ]. However, the role of piRNA in modulating the phenotypes of BMSCs and pathogenesis of GIOP is far from being clarified.
Ferroptosis plays a key role in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis. Its core significance lies in the bidirectional disruption of bone metabolic balance through an iron-dependent lipid peroxidation accumulation mechanism, becoming an important pathological link driving bone loss. Specifically, ferroptosis directly damages osteoblasts responsible for bone formation, leading to their death and a decline in bone formation capacity; at the same time, it can also indirectly promote the differentiation and activity of osteoclasts and intensify bone resorption [ , ]. This process links together multiple osteoporosis risk factors such as aging, estrogen deficiency, and chronic inflammation [ , ]. Therefore, targeting the ferroptosis pathway (such as using iron chelators or specific inhibitors) not only offers a novel perspective for understanding osteoporosis but also brings significant hope for the development of innovative treatment strategies aimed at restoring bone balance.
WW domain-containing protein 1 (WWP1) is a Nedd4 family E3 ubiquitin ligase. WWP1 can ubiquitinate and promote the degradation of the Smad transcription factor family (such as Smad2, Smad4), thereby inhibiting the activity of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway [ ]. WWP1 can also regulate the Hippo signaling pathway. WWP1 can ubiquitinate and inhibit the core inhibitory factor large tumor suppressor kinase 1/2 in the pathway, thereby activating the downstream growth-promoting transcription factors Yes-associated protein/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif and promoting cell proliferation [ ]. In addition, WWP1 can ubiquitinate p53, affecting its stability and activity, which is crucial for DNA damage response and apoptosis [ ]. WWP1 has been reported to be a regulator in bone disease pathogenesis. Specifically, WWP1 targets JunB for ubiquitination and degradation in mesenchymal stem cells to block the differentiation into osteoblasts [ ]. Additionally, WWP1 cooperates with Schnurri-3 to regulate the expression of runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), which is crucial in modulating osteoblast activity [ ]. Interestingly, some recent studies have indicated WWP1 is a regulator of ferroptosis [ , ]. However, the dysregulation mechanism of WWP1 in osteoporosis is unclear.
In the present work, to explore the role of piRNAs in osteoporosis, we screened the differentially expressed piRNAs and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during the osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) based on open access datasets. In vitro, osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of hBMSCs were induced, and in vivo, a GIOP rat model was constructed, to validate the dysregulation of the piRNAs and DEGs. piR-27222 was identified as a piRNA, which was related to GIOP and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. Furthermore, the role of piR-27222 in regulating differentiation and ferroptosis of hBMSCs was investigated, and its target gene WWP1 was identified. 

## Materials and methods

## Acquisition of datasets and identification of differentially expressed genes
The datasets GSE135587 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE135587), GSE216891 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE216891) and GSE276391 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE276391) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.The GSE135587 contains the expression profile data of piRNA during the osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation differentiation of hBMSCs. The GSE216891 dataset includes the gene expression profile of the control group and osteoblasts treated with dexamethasone. The GSE276391 dataset includes the gene expression profile of cortical bone cells of mice in the control group and the dexamethasone treatment group. The screening conditions of DEGs were P < 0.05 and |log2fold change|>1 are identified as DEGs. The online Venn diagram tool was used to screen the genes that were significantly upregulated and downregulated in osteogenic differentiation and chondrogenic differentiation respectively.

## Screening of the potential targets of piR-hsa-27222
The miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/) database and TargetRank (http://hollywood.mit.edu/targetrank/) database were applied to predict the candidate target genes of piRNA [ ], and all predicted target genes were included in this study. Subsequently, gene ontology (GO) analysis [including biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF)] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were conducted on the target genes. Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA 3.0 software. The miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/) database and TargetRank (http://hollywood.mit.edu/targetrank/) database were applied to predict the candidate target genes of piRNA [ ], and all predicted target genes were included in this study. Subsequently, gene ontology (GO) analysis [including biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF)] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were conducted on the target genes. Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA 3.0 software.
The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of the target genes was generated using the STRING online dataset (https://cn.string-db.org/) and imported into Cytoscape software. A minimum required interaction score of 0.4 was set. The CytoHubba plugin was used to conduct topological analysis of the target genes, and node importance was ranked according to four algorithms: maximal clique centrality (MCC), maximum neighborhood component (MNC), density of maximum neighborhood component (DMNC), and edge percolated component (EPC). For each algorithm, the top 15 genes were collected, and the genes in the intersection were selected as the key target.
MiRanda database (current version v3.3a) was then applied to predict the binding site between piR-27222 and WWP1, with the following parameter settings: Gap Open Penalty: −9.0; Gap Extend Penalty: −4.0; Score Threshold: 140; Energy Threshold: −1.0 kcal/mol; and Scaling Parameter: 4.0.

## Cell culture
hBMSCs (CP-H166), purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China), cultured in the BMSC culture medium (Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in an incubator at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. The hBMSCs after 3 passages (P3-P5) were divided into the osteogenic induction group (1 µM dexamethasone, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-Glycerophosphate, for 14 days) and the adipogenic induction group (induction: 0.5 mM 1-methyl-3-isobutylxanthine, 1 µM dexamethasone, 10 µg/mL insulin, for 3 days; maintenance: 10 µg/mL insulin, for 11 days) with the corresponding induction medium (Procell, Wuhan, China). piR-hsa-27222 mimics (piR-hsa-27222 MIM), piR-hsa-27222 inhibitor (piR-hsa-27222 INH), and their negative controls (NC) (MIM NC and INH NC), WWP1 overexpression plasmid (OE-WWP1) and the empty vector were designed and synthesized by Ruibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Transfected hBMSCs were treated with Ferrostatin-1 (2 µM; HY-100579, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 24 h.

## Establishment of GIOP rat models induced by dexamethasone
Dexamethasone is a recognized osteoporosis inducer [ ]. Nine 8-week-old female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (Tianqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China) were raised at 22 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, with a 12-hour / 12-hour light-dark cycle. All animals could freely obtain food and drinking water. Before starting the modeling, the animals had undergone a week of environmental adaptation. All animal experiments and operations involved in this research strictly adhered to the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and were approved by the Animal Care Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Pingyang. The rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 in each group; with random number table method): the control group, the GIOP model group, and the GIOP + piR-rno-27222 antagonist group. Among them, rats in the GIOP model group were intraperitoneally injected with dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a dose of 5 mg/kg/week for 4 weeks [ ]. The rats in the control group were injected with the same amount of normal saline. The piR-rno-27222 antagonist was synthesized by GenePharma Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for in vivo suppression of piR-27222. piR-rno-27222 antagonist was modified by phosphonothioate and cholesterol to increase the stability, and then it was injected into rats in the GIOP + piR-rno-27222 antagonist group at a dose of 10 mg/kg via the tail vein of rats once a week, and the scramble oligonucleotides were injected into the control group and the GIOP model group. The euthanasia of animals was carried out using carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Before placing the animals in the CO2 chamber (CO2 flow rate: 50% volume of the chamber per minute), an inhalation anesthetic (isoflurane concentration: 3%; air flow: 1 L/min; kept for 3 min) was first used to make them lose consciousness. After the rats were sacrificed, the muscles and connective tissues around the bone area near the knee joint at the distal end of the femur were stripped, and the bone tissues were collected. After fixation, decalcification, dehydration, paraffin embedding and slicing, hematoxylin-eosin staining was performed. The outcome assessments were conducted by researchers who were blinded to the group allocations.

## Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
The separated bone tissue was thoroughly crushed using a tissue homogenizer. After that, total RNA was extracted from bone tissue or hBMSCs using 1 mL of Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The concentration and purity of total RNA were determined by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). With the cDNA as the template, qRT-PCR was carried out with the SYBR Green PCR premix Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the corresponding primers. A series of concentrations of the cDNA template were used to perform the amplification, and the cycle threshold (Ct) values were collected to plot the standard curve, and the slope was calculated to evaluate the amplification efficiency. U6 was used as the endogenous control. The primer sequences used in this study, designed with Vazyme software (https://bio.vazyme.com/companyfile/653.html), are shown in Table  . Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to confirm the specificity of the amplification products.
 

## CCK-8 experiment
The hBMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured. At 0, 3, 7, and 14 days after transfection, 10 µL of CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc., Kumamoto, Kyushu, Japan) was added into each well, and the culture was continued for 2 h. Subsequently, the absorbance (optical density, OD) values of each well were detected at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

## Colony formation experiment
0.5 × 103 hBMSCs were inoculated in 6-well plates and cultured. When the cell mass was clearly visible at the bottom of the plate, the culture was terminated. The plates were gently washed twice with phosphate buffer saline (PBS), and the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After discarding the fixative, the colonies were stained with 1% crystal violet solution for 30 min, and then the plates were washed again, and the number of colonies (visible colony, generally containing ≥ 50 cells) were counted.

## Alizarin red staining
hBMSCs were inoculated in 24-well plates. When the cells adhered and grew to 70–80% confluence, osteogenic induction medium was used for further culture for 14 days. The cells were then fixed at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and then rinsed three times with PBS. Next, 0.2% alizarin red S staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added for staining for 15 min, and then washed with PBS three times. To quantitatively analyze the mineralized nodules, the mineralized nodules were dissolved in the 10% hexadecylpyridine chloride solution, incubated at room temperature for 1 h with slight shaking. After gently pipetting to mix well, the solution was drawn and transferred into a 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 562 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA).

## Oil red O staining
hBMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates, and when the cells have fully adhered and grown to approximately 70%-80% confluence, the medium was replaced with adipogenic induction differentiation medium. The induction lasted for 3 weeks. Subsequently, 4% paraformaldehyde solution was added to each well and the cells were fixed at room temperature for 30 min, and stained with oil red O staining working solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Next, the cells were washed thoroughly with PBS, and observed under a microscope. For quantitative analysis, isopropanol was added to dissolve the oil red O dye bound to the cells, and then the absorbance value at 510 nm wavelength was determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA).

## Western blotting
Bone tissue homogenates and hBMSCs were lysed respectively using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor mixture (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein quantification kit [Thermo Fisher Scientific (Pierce™), Waltham, MA, USA]. With 10 µg of protein sample in each lane, the protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Subsequently, the membrane was blocked at room temperature with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h, and then incubated overnight with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h: anti-RUNX2 [EPR14334] (ab192256, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), anti-osteopontin [EPR21139-316] (ab214050, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), anti-WWP1 (ab104440, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), anti-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [EPNCIR144] (ab125066, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), anti-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [EP1808Y] (ab62352, Abcam, United Kingdom) and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) [EPR16891] (ab181602, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom). On the next day, the membrane was incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab6721, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) at room temperature for 2 h. Band images were collected using the chemiluminescence Imaging System (ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Gray-scale value analysis was conducted through Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). GAPDH was used as the endogenous control.

## Detection of glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA) and Fe2+
The determination of GSH, MDA content and total iron level adopted the corresponding commercial colorimetric reagent kits. The bone tissue was prepared into a homogenate. The GSH content was detected by the kit of Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. This method was based on the reaction of GSH with DTNB to form a yellow product, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 412 nm. MDA, as the end product of lipid peroxidation, its content was determined by the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method, and the absorbance value was read at 532 nm. The total iron content was quantified using the iron colorimetric detection kit from Elabscience. All tests were set with standard curves, and the absorbance values of the samples were used to calculate the final concentration based on the standard curves.

## Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay
The binding relationship between piR-27222 and WWP1 was verified using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Briefly, the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of WWP1 was cloned into the pmirGLO vector, and a corresponding mutant construct was also generated. Both the wild-type and mutant reporter plasmids (WWP1-WT and WWP1-MUT) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Forty-eight hours after co-transfection with the piR-27222 mimic and either the WWP1-WT or WWP1-MUT plasmid, luciferase activity was measured using a Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China).

## Statistical analysis
All data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation” (mean ± SD), and each group of experiments included at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3), and repeated for at least 3 times for the in vitro assays. Statistical analysis and charting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). ROUT method with a Q value of 1% to identify potential outliers. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used when data conformed to a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, otherwise the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was utilized when data met the assumptions of normality and equal variance; if not, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was applied. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was evaluated via Levene’s test. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used for false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control for multiple comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

## Results

## Identification of differentially expressed piRNAs in osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs
We first used the GSE135587 dataset to screen for differentially expressed piRNAs during the chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. The results showed that There were a total of 4 significantly down-regulated piRNAs (piR-hsa-32187, piR-hsa-32161, piR-hsa-32160 and piR-hsa-28487) during the chondrogenic differentiation and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. In addition, There were two significantly upregulated piRNAs (piR-hsa-23533 and piR-hsa-27222) during the chondrogenic differentiation and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs (Fig.  A, B). Subsequently, the induction medium was applied to induce the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. Compared with 0 days, the expression levels of RUNX2 and OPN in hBMSCs were significantly higher after 7 days [RUNX2: fold change (FC) = 1.17, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.02–1.31; P = 0.026; OPN: fold change = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.14–1.35; P < 0.001] and 14 days (RUNX2: FC = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09–1.38; P = 0.004; OPN: FC = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.19–1.40; P < 0.001) of culture (Fig.  C), suggesting the successful induction of osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. In addition, the expression levels of RUNX2 (FC = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.08–0.76; P = 0.004) and OPN (FC = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.16–0.81; P = 0.009) in the GIOP rat model were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig.  D). The H&E staining results indicated that the area of bone tissue formation in the GIOP rat model was significantly lower than that in the control group (FC = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.37–0.81; P = 0.006) (Fig.  E). Next, we used qRT-PCR to detect the expression levels of piR-32187, piR-32161, piR-32160, piR-28487, piR-23533 and piR-27222 in hBMSCs after osteogenic differentiation and bone tissues of GIOP rat models. As shown, during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs, piR-27222 (FC = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.15–2.05; P = 0.031) was significantly upregulated, while piR-32187 (FC = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.37–0.99; P = 0.036) and piR-28487 (FC = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.30–0.95; P = 0.033) were significantly downregulated (Fig.  F). Furthermore, qRT-PCR confirmed that piR-27222 (FC = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25–0.81; P = 0.018) was significantly downregulated in the bone tissue of GIOP rats, while piR-32161 (FC = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.64–0.92; P = 0.030) was significantly downregulated (Fig.  G). Given that piR-27222 is significantly upregulated during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and significantly downregulated in GIOP model, we finally chose piR-27222 for the subsequent experiments. On the 0 day, 7 day and 14 day, in osteogenic induction of hBMSCs, the expression level of piR-27222 (7d: FC = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.32–2.36, P = 0.007; 14d: FC = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.55–2.78, P = 0.002) was gradually up-regulated, and the expression levels of ferroptosis suppressors NRF2 (7d: FC = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.51–2.50, P < 0.001; 14d: FC = 2.44, 95% CI: 1.90–3.14, P < 0.001) and GPX4 (7d: FC = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.82–3.16, P < 0.001; 14d: FC = 3.19, 95% CI: 2.42–4.20, P < 0.001) were gradually promoted; in adipogenic induction, the expression level of piR-27222 (7d: FC = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.47–0.74, P < 0.001; 14d: FC = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.37–0.60, P < 0.001) was gradually reduced, and the expression levels of NRF2 (7d: FC = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.69–0.92, P = 0.004; 14d: FC = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.39–0.66, P < 0.001) and GPX4 (7d: FC = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.44–0.66, P < 0.001; 14d: FC = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.18–0.37, P < 0.001) were gradually repressed (Fig.  H–K). This temporal dynamics analysis implied piR-27222 could probably modulate the differentiation of hBMSCs via modulating ferroptosis. 
 

## piR-27222 induces the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and inhibits adipogenic differentiation
To identify the role of piR-27222 in modulating the phenotypes of hBMSCs, piR-27222 MIM (FC = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.99–8.36; P = 0.005) and piR-27222 INH (FC = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.28–0.39; P < 0.001) were transfected into hBMSCs respectively (Fig.  A). Subsequently, osteogenic differentiation was induced. The CCK-8 assay indicated that at 3, 7, and 14 days, compared with the MIM NC group, piR-27222 MIM could significantly promote the proliferation of hBMSCs (3d: FC = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.21–1.51; P = 0.004; 7d: FC = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.19–1.66; P = 0.008; 14d: FC = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.27–1.64; P = 0.001), while compared with the INH NC group, piR-27222 INH could significantly inhibit the proliferation of hBMSCs (3d: FC = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.65–0.83; P = 0.010; 7d: FC = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.60–0.71; P < 0.001; 14d: FC = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.63–0.81; P = 0.005) (Fig.  B). At 14 days of osteogenic differentiation induction, piR-27222 MIM promoted the expression of RUNX2 (FC = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.11–1.83; P = 0.030) and OPN (FC = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.16–1.74; P = 0.013) proteins in hBMSCs, while piR-27222 INH could significantly inhibit the expression of RUNX2 (FC = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.33–0.64; P = 0.004) and OPN (FC = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.24–0.53; P = 0.003) proteins in hBMSCs (Fig.  C). The colony formation assay indicated that piR-27222 MIM could promote the colony number of hBMSCs cells (FC = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.15–1.63; P = 0.006), while piR-27222 INH inhibited the formation of colonies (FC = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.30–0.61; P = 0.003) (Fig.  D). Furthermore, the results of alizarin red staining and oil red O staining indicated that piR-27222 MIM could significantly promote the mineralization ability of hBMSCs cells (FC = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.76–4.68; P = 0.009) and inhibit adipogenic differentiation ability (FC = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.11–0.41; P = 0.004), while piR-27222 INH exhibited the opposite effects(alizarin red staining: FC = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.26–0.48; P < 0.001; oil red O staining: FC = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.94–3.73; P = 0.001) (Fig.  E, F).
 

## piR-27222 induces osteogenic differentiation and bone formation in GIOP rats
Next, piR-rno-27222 antagonist was injected into GIOP rats, and confirmed by qRT-PCR (FC = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.25–0.50; P = 0.001) (Fig.  A). Western blot indicated that piR-27222 antagonist significantly inhibited the expression of osteogenic differentiation marker proteins RUNX2 (FC = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.24–0.47; P < 0.001) and OPN (FC = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.40–0.59; P < 0.001) in the bone tissue of GIOP rats (Fig.  B). The H&E staining showed that the piR-27222 antagonist significantly reduced the bone area in the bone tissue of GIOP rats (FC = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.65–0.86; P = 0.006) (Fig.  C). Therefore, the above results supported that piR-27222 could induce osteogenic differentiation and bone formation in GIOP rats.
 

## Screening of the target genes of piR-27222
Subsequently, we identified the target genes of piR-hsa-27222 using the miRDB and TargetRank databases, and screened the differentially expressed DEGs in GIOP based on the GSE16891 and GSE276391 datasets. Finally, a total of 81 target genes were screened out (Fig. A). Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were applied to identify the potential biological functions and related signaling pathways of piR-27222. The results revealed that these target genes were significantly enriched in cell migration, cellular response to growth factor stimulus, positive regulation of skeletal muscle tissue development, positive regulation of osteoblast differentiation, BMP signaling pathway, positive regulation of cell population proliferation, etc.; in terms of KEGG pathway analysis, the target genes were significantly enriched in Hippo pathway, TGF-β pathway and PI3K-Akt pathway (Fig. B). Subsequently, a PPI network was constructed based on these target genes, and MCC, EPC, DMNC and MNC algorithms in the CytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape software were applied to screen the top 15 genes respectively, and a Venn diagram was used to screen the genes in the intersection, and 11 genes were obtained (Fig. C, D). Given that WWP1 has been proven to inhibit osteogenic differentiation by regulating RUNX2 [ , ], WWP1 was chosen for subsequent analysis.
 

## piR-27222 negatively regulates the expression of WWP1
Next, the miRanda database was applied to predict the complementary binding sites of piR-hsa-27222 and piR-rno-27222 with WWP1 (Fig.  A). Subsequently, the potential biological functions of WWP1 were identified using GSEA, and the results showed that WWP1 was associated with the regulation of osteogenic differentiation and bone mineralization (Fig.  B). In dual-luciferase reporter gene assay, piR-27222 MIN markedly repressed the signal intensity of the wide type reporter (FC = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.31–0.51; P < 0.001), while it showed no obvious effects on that the mutant type reporter (Fig.  C), which further supported that WWP1 mRNA was a target of piR-27222. As expected, WWP1 was significantly down-regulated during osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs (FC = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.40–0.66; P = 0.004) and significantly up-regulated in bone tissue of GIOP rats (FC = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.25–2.33; P = 0.011) (Fig.  D, E). Moreover, during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs, piR-27222 MIM could significantly inhibit the expression of WWP1 protein (FC = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.52–0.75; P = 0.002), while piR-27222 INH could significantly promote the expression of WWP1 protein (FC = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.11–1.64; P = 0.011). In the GIOP rat model, the piR-27222 antagonist could significantly promote the expression of WWP1 protein (FC = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.48–2.24; P = 0.003) (Fig.  F, G).
 

## piR-27222 regulates ferroptosis in hBMSCs and GIOP rat models via modulating WWP1
As shown, overexpression of WWP1 (FC = 4.48, 95% CI: 2.30–8.73; P = 0.008) could increase the levels of MDA (FC = 3.71, 95% CI: 2.45–5.62; P = 0.003) and Fe2+ (FC = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.63–3.12; P = 0.005) in hBMSCs, reduce the level of GSH (FC = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.44–0.76; P = 0.005), and repressed the expression of GPX4 (FC = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.32–0.56; P = 0.001) and NRF2 (FC = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.47–0.70; P = 0.002) (Fig.  A–E). Consistently, single-gene GSEA showed that WWP1 was associated with cellular response to oxidative stress and response to iron (Fig.  A). Subsequently, in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to verify whether piR-27222 could regulate osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and ferroptosis in the GIOP rat model. As shown, overexpression of piR-27222 could significantly inhibit the levels of MDA (FC = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.30–0.59; P = 0.002) and Fe2+ (FC = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.18–0.53; P = 0.002) in hBMSCs, increase the level of GSH (FC = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.48–3.58; P = 0.010), and promote the expression of GPX4 (FC = 2.84, 95% CI: 1.95–4.14; P = 0.003) and NRF2 (FC = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.46–2.40; P = 0.004), while piR-27222 INH showed the opposite effect (MDA: FC = 2.81, 95% CI: 2.00–3.94, P < 0.001; Fe2+: FC = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.27–2.17, P = 0.010; GSH: FC = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.42–0.65, P = 0.001; GPX4: FC = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.44–0.66, P = 0.002; NRF2: FC = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.44–0.68, P = 0.004) (Fig.  B–E). Notably, combination of ferrostatin-1 and piR-27222 showed more potent inductive effects on the expression levels of GPX4 and NRF2, compared with ferrostatin-1 treatment alone (GPX4: FC = 1.79, 95% CI: 0.93–3.45, P = 0.019; NRF2: FC = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.30–3.09, P < 0.001) or transfection of piR-27222 alone (GPX4: FC = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.35–5.01, P = 0.002; NRF2: FC = 3.27, 95% CI: 2.12–5.04, P < 0.001) (Fig.  F). Compared with the control group, the levels of MDA (FC = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.27–2.56, P = 0.013) and Fe2+ (FC = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.55–3.73, P = 0.009) in GIOP rats were significantly higher, while the levels of GSH (FC = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.46–0.78, P = 0.003), GPX4 (FC = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.36–0.73, P = 0.008) and NRF2 (FC = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.37–0.74, P = 0.006) were significantly lower; in addition, compared with GIOP rats, the levels of MDA (FC = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.33–2.16, P = 0.003), Fe2+ (FC = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.22–2.27, P = 0.017) and GSH (FC = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.53–0.80, P = 0.007) in GIOP rats injected with piR-27222 antagonist were significantly higher, while the expressions of GPX4 (FC = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.30–0.68, P = 0.010) and NRF2 (FC = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.26–0.71, P = 0.015) were significantly lower (Fig.  G–J). To investigate whether piR-27222 modulate ferroptosis of hBMSCs via WWP1, WWP1 and piR-27222 were co-transfected into hBMSCs, and as shown, WWP1 overexpression partly counteracted the effects of piR-27222 in regulating MDA (FC = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.44–2.32, P = 0.004), Fe2+ (FC = 2.68, 95% CI: 2.17–3.32, P < 0.001), GSH (FC = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.47–0.69, P = 0.002), GPX4 (FC = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.39–0.64, P = 0.001) and NRF2 (FC = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.47–0.76, P = 0.004) (Fig.  A–D).
 

## Discussion
The application of small nucleic acid drugs has opened up broad prospects for new treatment strategies. Such drugs, including small interfering RNA and antisense oligonucleotides, have demonstrated promising therapeutic effects in clinical applications. piRNA was discovered in 2006. It is a type of small non-coding RNA. It plays an important role in the pathogenesis of various diseases. However, its potential in the field of GIOP treatment has yet to be explored. This study discovered for the first time that the expression of piR-27222 was significantly upregulated during osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs, while it was significantly downregulated in the rat model of GIOP, suggesting its involvement in the disease process. In vitro experiments showed that piR-27222 inhibited ferroptosis of hBMSCs, promoted osteogenic differentiation and repressed adipogenic differentiation. In vivo experiments further confirmed that piR-27222 enhanced the osteogenic differentiation ability and bone formation process of GIOP rats. Additionally, piR-27222 may exert its biological functions by regulating WWP1.
piRNA was previously believed to be mainly expressed in germline cells and involved in maintaining genomic stability. However, recent studies have discovered the crucial role of piRNA in somatic cells [ ]. The levels of macrophage-derived piENOX2 in the synovial tissues of rheumatoid arthritis patients and collagen-induced arthritis mice are significantly elevated, and piENOX2 inhibitors can significantly alleviate the disease progression, and reduce systemic inflammatory responses, and protect the integrity of articular cartilage, and mechanistically, piENOX2 exerts its effect by targeting and degrading Alkbh5 mRNA [ ]. In addition, mmu_piR_037459 inhibits the expression of type II collagen in chondrocytes, suppresses their proliferation, and promotes their apoptosis; it can affect chondrocyte function by regulating the expression of ubiquitin-specific peptidase 7 [ ]. It is reported that, piR-27222 inhibits cell death in an m6A-dependent manner, and piR-27222 directly binds to eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4B (eIF4B) and promotes the deubiquitination and stabilization of eIF4B by reducing its interaction with parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase; the increase in eIF4B expression further promotes the expression of WTAP, leading to an increase in the m6A modification level on Casp8 transcripts and thereby reducing the stability of Casp8 mRNA [ ]. In this study, it was demonstrated that piR-27222 was significantly upregulated in the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and significantly downregulated in the GIOP rat model. In vitro experiments indicated that piR-27222 could significantly promote the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs and inhibit adipogenic differentiation. In vivo assay validated that piR-27222 could promote bone formation. To our best knowledge, this is the first study reporting the role of piR-27222 in osteoporosis.
Ferroptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death, closely related to oxidative stress disorders. Its typical pathological features include decompensation of the GSH-GPX4 system, accumulation of lipid peroxides, and Fenton reaction triggered by iron overload, etc [ , ]. In GIOP model mice, GPX4-deficient osteocytes presented with severe ferroptosis, accompanied by a decrease in bone mass and further destruction of bone microstructure, indicating that ferroptosis of osteocytes plays a key role in the progression of GIOP. In addition, the classic ferroptosis inhibitors ferrostatin-1 and deferoxamine can reverse the lipid peroxide accumulation, osteocyte ferroptosis and bone structure destruction caused by dexamethasone [ ]. Furthermore, piRNAs are involved in modulating ferroptosis. For instance, piR_016975 promotes the progression of liver cancer and sorafenib resistance by modulating Maspin/GPX4-mediated ferroptosis [ ]. Furthermore, piR-16404 drives the occurrence of iron overload induced liver injury by inducing dysregulation of the cytosolic arginine sensor for mTORC1/mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1/GPX4 axis [ ]. In this study, ferroptosis was observed in GIOP model, and piR-27222 could inhibit ferroptosis in hBMSCs and GIOP rat models.
The WWP1 gene is located on the long arm of chromosome 8 (8q21.3), adjacent to the human proto-oncogene MYC. It contains a total of 26 exons, with a full-length mRNA of approximately 4.2 kb and an open reading frame of 2766 bp. WWP1 is a multifunctional protein that is expressed in almost all human tissues and is distributed in various subcellular structures, including myofilament, sarcoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, endosomes and nuclei, etc [ , ]. During normal physiological processes, WWP1 participates in various biological activities by regulating protein ubiquitination and interacting with multiple proteins. Specifically, WWP1 regulate the function of osteoblasts and plays a significant role in maintaining bone mass in adults. From a mechanistic perspective, Shn3 interacts physically with RUNX2 by recruiting WWP1 to mediate the ubiquitination and proteasome degradation of RUNX2, thereby reducing its protein stability and transcriptional activity [ ]. In addition, WWP1 also inhibits the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts by promoting the ubiquitination degradation of JUNB and the lysosomal degradation of CXCR4 induced by tumor necrosis factor, thereby participating in the inflammation-mediated osteoporosis process [ ]. Interestingly, a previous study has shown that in cisplatin resistance of lung cancer, WWP1 can mediate ferritin autophagy and ferroptosis [ ]. In this study, we observed that WWP1 modulated the ferroptosis of hBMSCs, and we also confirmed that piR-27222 negatively regulated WWP1, and WWP1 could reverse the inhibitory effect of piR-27222 on ferroptosis during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs, partly explaining the downstream mechanism by which piR-27222 modulates ferroptosis during the pathogenesis of osteoporosis.
In addition to WWP1, our bioinformatics analysis also identified several other potential downstream targets of piR-27222, including cyclin D2 (CCND2), SMAD family member 1 (SMAD1), core-binding factor subunit beta (CBFB), collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2), insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1), protein phosphatase 2 catalytic subunit alpha (PPP2CA), DEAD-box helicase 5 (DDX5), bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 (BMPR2), nuclear receptor subfamily 3 group C member 1 (NR3C1), and cytoplasmic polyadenylation element-binding protein 2 (CPEB2). It is notable that these genes synergistically form the core network that regulates the life activities of hBMSCs and other bone formation-related cells. CCND2 drives cell cycle progression and regulates cell proliferation [ ]; IRS1 mediates insulin signaling and regulates cell metabolism and growth [ ]; SMAD1 and BMPR2 are key members of the BMP signaling pathway, acting as downstream transcription factors and upstream receptors, jointly regulating bone formation [ , ]; CBFB, as the core subunit of the transcriptional complex, collaborates with RUNX proteins to regulate osteogenic differentiation [ ]; COL1A2 encodes type I collagen, providing the core structural support for connective tissues [ ]. At the same time, PPP2CA negatively regulates multiple signaling pathways as a key phosphatase to maintain homeostasis [ ], and NR3C1 acts as a nuclear receptor to widely regulate the expression of genes related to stress and metabolism [ ]. At the level of gene expression regulation, DDX5, as an RNA helicase, participates in multiple RNA metabolic processes such as transcription and splicing [ ]; and CPEB2, by binding to specific mRNA sequences, performs precise temporal control of gene expression at the translation level [ ]. Our data suggests that, piR-27222 may exerts its function against osteoporosis via multiple downstream targets and biological processes, and direct interaction between piR-27222 and these potential targets remains to be further verified in the following studies.
The current study has several limitations. First, the in vivo experiments were only conducted in small sample size, and without WWP1 restoration group, and the use of a single animal model (GIOP rat model) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should consider expanding the sample size, setting the rescue group, and incorporating multiple animal models (such as ovariectomized rats), to better simulate different pathological conditions of osteoporosis and validate the role of piR-27222/WWP1 axis across various etiologies, and applying micro-CT analysis, more biomechanical tests, serum bone turnover markers analyses is helpful to evaluate the therapeutic effect of piR-27222 on rats more comprehensively. Second, the detection of mitochondrial morphology with scanning electron microscope to ultrastructural changes and evaluation of ferroptosis markers will furthe validate the role of piR-27222 and WWP1 in modulating ferroptosis of hBMSCs. Third, the study focused primarily on the effects of piR-27222 on ferroptosis during osteogenic differentiation; however, osteoporosis is a complex disease involving multiple signaling pathways and cell types, including osteoclasts and osteocytes. The potential regulatory role of piR-27222 in osteoclastogenesis or osteocyte function was not explored here, which warrants future research to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its role in bone homeostasis. Lastly, the clinical therapeutic potential of piR-27222 was not evaluated in this study. Although in vivo experiments showed that piR-27222 promotes bone formation in GIOP rats, the dysregulation of piR-27222 in human samples should be further validated, and the development of effective delivery systems for piRNAs, such as lipid nanoparticles or viral vectors, and the assessment of their safety and efficacy in long-term animal studies, and comparison with current available drugs, may provide preclinical mechanistic insights.

## Conclusion
This study confirms for the first time the role and molecular mechanism of piR-27222 in GIOP. piR-27222 is dysregulated in the GIOP rat model and during the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. Overexpression of piR-27222 significantly promotes osteogenic differentiation, inhibits adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and promotes bone formation and osteogenic differentiation in animal model. piR-27222 directly targets and inhibits the expression of WWP1, thereby activating NRF2/GPX4 signaling pathways and suppressing ferroptosis. These findings indicate the crucial role of the piR-27222/WWP1 axis in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis and also provide an important basis for the development of novel osteoporosis treatment strategies based on modulating piRNA.
