## Background:
Drug-eluting stents impair post-angioplasty re-endothelialization thus compromising restenosis prevention while heightening thrombotic risks. We recently found that inhibition of protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) effectively mitigated both restenosis and thrombosis in rodent models. This motivated us to determine how PERK inhibition impacts re-endothelialization.

## Methods:
Re-endothelialization was evaluated in endothelial-denuded rat carotid arteries after balloon angioplasty and periadventitial administration of PERK inhibitor in a hydrogel. To study whether PERK in smooth muscle cells (SMCs) regulates re-endothelialization by paracrinally influencing endothelial cells (ECs), denuded arteries exposing SMCs were lentiviral-infected to silence PERK; in vitro, the extracellular vesicles isolated from the medium of PDGF-activated, PERK-upregulating human primary SMCs were transferred to human primary ECs.

## Results:
Treatment with PERK inhibitor versus vehicle control accelerated re-endothelialization in denuded arteries. PERK-specific silencing in the denuded arterial wall (mainly SMCs) also enhanced re-endothelialization compared to scrambled shRNA control. In vitro, while medium transfer from PDGF-activated SMCs impaired EC viability and increased the mRNA levels of dysfunctional EC markers, either PERK inhibition or silencing in donor SMCs mitigated these EC changes. Furthermore, CXCL10, a paracrine cytokine detrimental to ECs, was increased by PDGF activation and decreased after PERK inhibition or silencing in SMCs.

## Conclusions:
Attenuating PERK activity pharmacologically or genetically provides an approach to accelerating post-angioplasty re-endothelialization in rats. The mechanism may involve paracrine factors regulated by PERK in SMCs that impact neighboring ECs. This study rationalizes future development of PERK-targeted endothelium-friendly vascular interventions.

## Introduction
Endovascular interventions for cardiovascular diseases often fail, mostly because of restenosis, the re-narrowing of recon-structed vessels. The rapidly gained popularity of drug-eluting stents (DESs) and drug-coated balloons (DCBs), which have substantially reduced restenosis rates, ushered in a new era of endovascular interventions. , However, evidence from multiple centers has accrued over the past decade strongly challenging their safety profiles with alarming concerns such as increased mortality, persistent restenosis, and increased thrombosis risk. – To deal with the risk of late and very late thrombosis, patients are often recommended to receive anti-platelet therapies of extended duration which, however, incur significant economic burden and risk of major bleeding. 
The cause of these life-threatening complications is traced down to insufficient or delayed re-endothelialization, the healing process of the endothelium damaged or denuded due to endovascular interventions such as angioplasty. The standard therapeutics coated on DES and DCB, such as sirolimus, paclitaxel, and their derivatives, are known to cause dysfunction of endothelial cells (ECs) and delay re-endothelialization. Preserving the integrity of the endothelium via re-endothelialization holds significant clinical implications, mainly in the following aspects: (1) an intact and healthy endothelial inner lining can stake off circulating platelets and inflammatory cells, thus preventing the coagulation and thrombosis cascades ; (2) the endothelium also serves as the gatekeeper to block blood-borne mitogenic factors (e.g., platelet-derived growth factors, or PDGF) from reaching medial smooth muscle cells (SMCs), whose invasion into intima and hyperplasia lead to restenosis ; (3) healthy, fully functional endothelial cells have been shown to temper medial SMC hyperplasia through a paracrine mechanism, for example, by secreting nitric oxide and microRNAs. Thus, undisrupted re-endothelialization could have a profound impact on the overall outcomes of endovascular interventions.
Despite the growing awareness of the deadly consequences of impaired re-endothelialization, by far, there is only a paucity of anti-restenotic agents (or targets) reported to preserve, if not enhance, the regeneration of the denuded endothelium layer. In an effort to address this unmet clinical need, previously our group initiated a high-throughput (HTS) drug screening campaign to identify small molecules that could exert inhibition on SMC hyperplasia while sparing EC, which has led to the discovery of multiple lead compounds. Of particular interest is a class of selective inhibitors specifically targeting protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), which represents one of the three endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response pathways. Although ER stress has long been proven to be implicated in cardiovascular diseases, the exact role of PERK in modulating SMC and EC pathophysiology is largely unknown. In our previous studies, we reported the striking outcomes of dual amelioration of restenosis and thrombosis after PERK inhibition in rodent models. We also unveiled a pivotal role of PERK in promoting SMC phenotype changes, including de-differentiation and hyperproliferation, and ultimately, the development of intimal hyperplasia. In addition, our data further suggested a previously unknown implication of PERK pathway in the thrombogenecity in vascular cells. Given the established contribution of phenotypically converted SMC to endothelial dysfunction, we were therefore encouraged to launch the current study to explore the potential of facilitating re-endothelialization via the blockade of PERK activity. We further investigated the underlying molecular basis that involves a novel PERK-mediated SMC-EC paracrine communication.

## Methods

## Animals
All animal procedures conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health No. 85e23, 1996 rev.). Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Ohio State University.

## Balloon angioplasty for endothelium denudation
Carotid artery balloon angioplasty was performed to induce mechanical denudation of endothelium as described in our previous reports. Male SpragueeDawley (SD) rats (300–350 g) were acquired from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA) and anesthetized with isoflurane through inhalation. Briefly, left carotid arteries were exposed, and a 2 Fogarty balloon catheter was introduced into common carotid arteries through an arteriotomy on external carotid arteries. Balloon catheters were then advanced proximally and inflated to 1.5 atm pressure. To induce homogenous denudation of endothelium, the inflated balloon was withdrawn to bifurcation, deflated, and re-introduced. This protocol was repeated 3 times, followed by a fourth cycle with concomitant rotation of balloon during withdrawing. The external carotid artery was then permanently ligated, and blood flow was resumed. The sternocleidomastoid muscle was excised to ensure longer segment of injured common carotid artery to be exposed for perivascular drug application. Analgesics were provided to ensure proper pain management, per instructions from University Laboratory Animal Resources (ULAR) and IACUC.

## Evans blue staining for evaluating re-endothelialization
Re-endothelialization of angioplasty-denuded arteries was evaluated on day 7 after injury, using an established Evans blue staining assay as previously described. Briefly, 1 mL of 0.5% Evans blue solution was injected through tail vein. After 30 min, rats were sequentially perfused with saline and RNAlater solution to transiently preserve RNA in endothelium. The common carotid arteries were dissected and connective tissues were trimmed to avoid contamination in downstream RNA extraction. Common carotid arteries were harvested and longitudinally opened for image acquisition. Re-endothelialization was evaluated based on blue staining (no endothelium coverage) and no-staining (with endothelium coverage) area using ImageJ. Basal level endothelium coverage was determined from acutely injured carotid arteries immediately after angioplasty and was subtracted from day 7 values.
Immediately after images were taken, the carotid segments were rinsed in saline to wash off residual RNAlater solution and subsequently snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage, until pulverized (BioPulverizer, BioSpec; Bartlesville, OK) and processed for tissue homogenate RNA extraction.

## Perivascular local delivery of PERK inhibitor
For perivascular delivery of PERK inhibitor GSK2606414, we adopted a recently developed Tri-block thermo-sensitive gel platform as described in our previous reports. Briefly, PLGAePEGePLGA tri-block polymer was prepared using established methodology, and lyophilized polymer was dissolved in saline (23% by weight) to form a thermosensitive hydrogel at 4°C. GSK2606414 was dissolved in DMSO solution and dispersed in 500 μL tri-block gel solution to achieve a final dose of 25 mg/kg. The drug-containing gel was kept on a rotator at 4°C before being applied in vivo. The same regimen has demonstrated outstanding anti-restenotic efficacy in rat angioplasty model in our most recent report. Immediately after balloon angioplasty, the gel containing GSK2606414 was applied to the outside of the injured segment of the carotid artery and solidified right away. In the control group, equal volume of vehicle mixed in tri-block gel was applied.

## Intraluminal PERK knockdown in angioplastied arteries
In vivo knockdown of PERK in endothelium-denuded arteries was performed as we have previously described with modifications. Briefly, a 24G IV catheter (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was introduced through the arteriotomy immediately after angioplasty. The catheter and external carotid artery were then tightly ligated to prevent leaking. Then lentivirus solution (150 μL, >1 × 109 IFU/mL) was then gradually injected into the temporarily looped common carotid artery through the IV catheter, and infused for 25 min. The catheter and virus solution were then withdrawn, and vessel lumen was flushed repeatedly with heparinized saline. In addition, perioperative heparin was administered to prevent thrombosis during the procedure, especially in the temporarily ligated internal carotid artery. Back bleeding from common and internal carotid arteries was confirmed.

## Materials and reagents
Human recombinant PDGF-BB was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN). TRIzol, High Capacity cDNA synthesis kit, SYBR Green PCR Mastermix, exosome isolation reagent, exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum, and siRNAs (scrambled versus PERK-specific siRNA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA). GSK2606414 was purchased from Apexbio (Houston, TX). CellTiter-Glo reagent was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI). RNAlater solution and Evans blue dye were purchased from Sigmae–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

## Primary cell culture and in vitro experimental procedures
Human aortic SMCs and ECs and their respective medium were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD). Human aortic SMCs and ECs were cultured in complete SmGM-2 and EGM-2 medium, respectively. Cells between passages 5 and 7 were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 and used for all experiments. Accutase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was used for detachment instead of Trypsin to ensure fast cell adhesion and optimal cell status.
For siRNA-mediated PERK knockdown, small interfering ribonucleic acids (siRNAs) for PERK were ordered and tested for efficiency. Validated siRNAs (4390824, Silencer Select; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) were transfected into SMCs using RNAiMax Reagent (13778–075; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s protocol at a concentration of 50 nM.
For SMC-derived conditioned medium transfer to EC, human aortic SMCs were plated in 10-cm collagen-coated dish to reach confluency, starved in medium containing 1% exosome-depleted FBS for 24 h before experimental treatment. SMCs were then stimulated with PDGF-BB and PERK inhibition or silencing using a 1:1 mixture of basal SmBm-2 and EBM-2 medium supplemented with 1% exosome-depleted FBS. Conditioned medium was collected every 24 h and pooled over the course of 92 h. To exclude the potential interference from residual PDGF-BB, inhibitors, and siRNAs, the conditioned medium was then subject to exosome isolation before transfer to starved, confluent ECs cultured in 24-well plates. Isolation of extracellular vesicles (EVs) was performed using the Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hour after transfer, the recipient ECs were then subject to either cell viability assay or mRNA isolation.

## Cell viability assay
EC viability was determined by using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 24 h after SMC-derived conditioned medium transfer, ECs seeded in 24-well plates were decanted, rinsed with PBS, and then refilled with 1 mL CellTiter-Glo reagent diluted with PBS at a 1:1 ratio. After 10 min incubation at room temperature, the liquid from each well was then distributed into 96-well plates and analyzed in a FlexStation 3 Benchtop Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California) (250-ms integration).

## Real-time quantitative PCR
mRNA was isolated from primary culture of SMCs and ECs or snap-frozen and pulverized rat carotid artery homogenates using TRIzol following the manufacturer’s instructions. Puri-fied mRNA (1 mg) was used for the first-strand cDNA synthesis and quantitative RT-PCR was performed using the Quant-Studio3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Each cDNA template was amplified using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. Primer sequences are provided in .

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism. Data sets were plotted and tested for normality using Shapiroe–Wilk test, followed by Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Sidak test, as specifically stated in figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significances were noted as *P < 0.05.

## Results

## Perivascular application of a PERK-selective inhibitor promotes post-angioplasty re-endothelialization in rat carotid arteries
Recently we developed local delivery platforms for lesion-targeted application of the first-in-class PERK inhibitor, GSK2606414, which demonstrated both anti-restenotic and anti-thrombotic efficacies in rodent models. Despite the endothelium friendliness and benefits we observed in our previous studies, the exact impact of PERK inhibition on the endothelium regeneration process in vivo, or re-endothelialization, remains unknown. Using the thermo-sensitive gel technology for perivascular local delivery, here we further determined the impact of PERK inhibition on re-endothelialization after endothelium denudation in the rat balloon angioplasty model ( ). At day 7 after denudation, rat carotid arteries were examined based on Evans blue staining that distinguished denuded (blue) and (re)endothelialized (white) areas. As shown in and , in comparison to vehicle control, perivascular application of PERK inhibitor led to significantly greater nonstained area, indicating enhanced re-endothelialization.
Our previous studies established the role of PERK in driving the phenotype changes of SMC toward de-differentiation and hyperplasia. In the present study, we determined if PERK inhibition could also affect other aspects of SMC phenotype changes, for example, toward an inflammatory phenotype upon injury. To this end, we performed qPCR using artery homogenates to analyze the mRNA levels of marker genes for SMC phenotype conversion. Consistent with our previous report, local PERK inhibition restored the expression of SMC maturation and contractile genes such as aSMA and calponin ( and ). Moreover, PERK inhibitor treatment effectively mitigated the inflammatory gene responses, evidenced by the reduced mRNA levels of IL1β, IL6, VCAM1, and tissue factor ( – ). Of note, we also observed a decrease in tissue factor, the initiator of extrinsic coagulation pathway, which is in accordance with our recent discovery of PERK’s implication in thrombosis. In addition, our data showed diminished mRNA level of eNOS, an authentic marker of endothelium physiology due to angioplasty, although treatment with PERK inhibitor failed to rescue the eNOS expression in angioplastied arteries in comparison to vehicle control group (DMSO). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first in vivo evidence for enhanced re-endothelialization effected by PERK inhibition.

## PERK silencing in endothelium-denudated arteries phenocopied the re-endothelialization–promoting effect of PERK inhibitor
We inferred that peri-adventitial application should generate a transmural gradient of PERK inhibitor concentration. Thus, SMC would be more accessible for the inhibitor than EC as in the peri-adventitial local PERK inhibition strategy used in our studies. Together with the previously reported role of SMC on modulating adjacent EC’s physiology and regeneration, we therefore postulated that the observed re-endothelialization enhancing effect of PERK inhibition might be primarily mediated through its impact on medial SMC. To test this hypothesis, we adopted an established methodology of intraluminal lentivirus infusion to achieve shRNA-mediated PERK knockdown specifically in the medial layer of endothelium-denuded artery segments ( ).
Similar to our pharmacological data, knocking down PERK in denuded artery segments augmented the regeneration of endothelium, as evidenced by increased nonstained area ( and ) as well as mRNA level of endothelium marker gene eNOS ( ) in comparison to scrambled shRNA lentivirus control. In addition, silencing PERK in the injured arterial segment phenocopied the effect of local PERK inhibition on SMC phenotype changes, including rescued SMC contractile phenotype and amelioration of inflammatory responses ( ). Taken together, these data suggest that the observed pro-re-endothelialization effect of PERK inhibition may be, at least in part, mediated through primarily affecting the phenotype changes of medial SMC, which subsequently modulate the regeneration of adjacent EC layer in an indirect, possibly paracrine manner.

## Phenotypically converted SMC exacerbates EC dysfunction in vitro in a PERK-mediated paracrine fashion
Past literatures indicate a role of SMC in paracrinally modulating the physiology and behavior of endothelium under both healthy and diseased conditions. Inspired by our in vivo observations on facilitated re-endothelialization upon PERK inhibition or silencing, we sought to investigate whether PERK activity could alter SMC-EC paracrine interaction in vitro and hence indirectly modulate EC regeneration. To this end, we established an in vitro model featuring SMC-derived conditioned medium transfer to EC ( ). Primary culture of human aortic SMC was treated with PDGF-BB to activate the disease-prone, de-differentiated phenotype, a key feature that is pivotal to the pathogenesis of restenosis in vivo. Conditioned medium from activated SMC treated with or without PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 was collected for concentration and EVs isolation, which were subsequently transferred to the primary culture of human aortic EC for evaluation of EC viability and marker gene expression analysis. As shown in and , concentrated conditioned medium or EVs derived from PDGF-BB-stimulated SMC both significantly reduced the viability of EC 24 h after transfer. In the meanwhile, we also observed increased mRNA levels of genes indicative of endothelium dysfunction (αSMA, suggesting early signs of endothelium-to-mesenchymal transition; and ICAM1, indicating a pro-inflammatory phenotype). By contrast, PERK inhibitor treatment of the activated SMC effectively attenuated the EC-damaging potency of the SMC-derived EVs, as evidenced by rescued EC viability ( ) and reduced mRNA levels of αSMA and ICAM1 ( and ). In addition, PERK knockdown with siRNA in SMC could partially recapitulate the effect observed with PERK inhibitor ( – ). These data, in accordance with enhanced re-endothelialization by PERK inhibition and knockdown in vivo, implicate that the SMC-EC paracrine interaction is a possible underlying cellular mechanism.

## PERK mediates the expression of re-endothelialization inhibitory chemokine by SMC in vitro and in vivo
To probe the molecular basis underlying the SMC-EC paracrine regulation, we focused on CXCL10, a recently identified SMC-derived chemokine that potently inhibits re-endothelialization. In the primary culture of SMC, PDGF-BB stimulation led to increased CXCL10 mRNA level. Conversely, PERK inhibition or knockdown both effectively mitigated PDGF-BB-induced CXCL10 upregulation ( and ). To further corroborate our findings, we also determined the mRNA level of CXCL10 in angioplasty-denuded arteries treated by either perivascular PERK inhibition or medial layer PERK knockdown. Consistent with the in vitro data, in vivo PERK antagonism also led to significant attenuation of CXCL10 expression ( and ). In light of the recent reports on the potent antire-endothelialization effect of SMC-derived CXCL10 using a transgenic model, our data suggest that PERK-promoted SMC expression of CXCL10 may have a paracrine effect on EC dysfunction.

## Discussion
Endovascular interventions, especially with the recent advancement of drug-eluting devices, have revolutionized the clinical management of cardiovascular diseases. , However, these procedures inevitably damage the endothelial inner lining. The disruption of re-endothelialization not only compromises the prevention of restenosis but also leads to heightened risk of thrombosis. In fact, the currently used anti-restenotic agents just do that. This inconvenient truth has been largely ignored in the status quo design of drug-eluting devices that focuses only on SMC hyperplasia. Fortunately, the safety concerns over DES and DCB have been highlighted in growing basic and clinical research reports and further alarmed by FDA this year. , 
However, there is a lack of an endothelium-friendly, anti-restenotic option in the clinic, posing significant threat to the overall well-being of cardiovascular patients. By far, there has been very limited basic and translational research to address this unmet clinical need. Recent reports, albeit sparse, pointed to potential molecular targets, including ADAMTS7, PI3K/p110α, and CTP synthase 1, for intervention to differentially inhibit SMC (versus EC) proliferation. , , However, these studies were conducted either in global knockout animals or through systemic drug administration, and hence the SMC- and EC-specific roles of these targets in vivo remain unclear. Moreover, these proteins lack potent and highly selective small molecule inhibitors, thus limiting their potential for translation, at least in the foreseeable future.
In our most recent report, we used an unbiased high-throughput drug screening approach designed to identify a common target for SMC inhibition yet preservation of EC viability. We found that three of the top-scored hits were inhibitors of PERK, which represents an ER stress response pathway. In a follow-up study, we found that PERK could be targeted to block both restenosis and thrombosis in rodent models. Furthermore, herein our new results indicated that either pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing of PERK enhanced re-endothelialization. While another ER stress response pathway, IRE1/XBP1, has been reported to promote SMC hyperplasia and restenosis, a role for this pathway in EC dysfunction is not established. A few reports identified compounds that inhibit SMC hyperplasia without inhibiting EC growth, but there is no evidence of acceleration of re-endothelialization. In this regard, to the best of our knowledge, our present study is the first to show re-endothelialization enhancement by PERK blockade, an effect validated by specifically silencing PERK in vivo. As evidenced by our previous and present studies in preclinical models, PERK activation is critically involved in the phenotype changes of SMC as well as endothelium dysfunction; and PERK inhibition appears to alleviate all three major postintervention complications that are interconnected, namely restenosis, impaired re-endothelialization, as summarized in .
Dysregulated ER stress response has been widely implicated in the pathogenesis of various cardiovascular diseases. Recently, ER stress pathways and novel downstream effectors have been discovered to play significant roles in SMC and EC pathophysiologies. , Pan inhibition of ER stress responses has also been experimentally evaluated for their therapeutic potential in treating atherosclerosis, restenosis, aneurysm, and so on. , However, these pan inhibitors, such as tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), usually bind promiscuous or unknown targets, which significantly limit their translational potential. PERK inhibitors, on the other hand, may offer a viable approach to effective mitigation of restenosis while promoting re-endothelialization, as implicated by our findings. An encouraging new development is a class of highly selective PERK inhibitors, which led to positive preclinical outcomes in treating a wide spectrum of illnesses, ranging from cancer to central neural system (CNS) diseases. , , Their systemic safety has been well characterized. One early study using prion infection models with long-term, extremely high-dose administration (50 mg/kg daily) identified pancreatic beta cell damage and other diabetic indications. Recent studies revisited this issue. However, the experiments with systemically administered PERK inhibitors of relatively low dosages did not show obvious toxicity, but rather improvement in insulin sensitivity and even enhanced pancreatic functions. , , Given these premises, further translation based on PERK inhibition is warranted, with more effort toward better, nonthrombogenic anti-restenotic therapy.
In the quest for mechanistic interpretation of our data, we identified a PERK-mediated SMC-to-EC signal transduction. The interaction between EC and SMC has long been the subject of research interests using co-culture or secretion transfer models. , Most of the studies focused on EC-to-SMC communication. Recently, new evidences have emerged demonstrating that SMC could also exert profound impacts on EC’s physiology and homeostasis through various routes. Healthy SMC has been shown to help maintain EC normal function through secreting soluble factors such as NOTCH and miR-143. , However, upon disease challenges, phenotypically converted SMC could conversely disrupt EC physiology. For instance, SMC of a synthetic phenotype could alter the rigidity and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, which subsequently compromises EC’s physiology. Similarly, when switching to an inflammatory phenotype, SMC could secrete a range of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., BMPs, CXCLs, ready-to-translate mRNAs) to damage EC homing, regeneration, and barrier function. , , – In line with the most recent report regarding the negative impact of SMC-derived CXCL10 on re-endothelialization, our in vitro and in vivo data implicated that CXCL10 was possibly one of the paracrine signaling factors that transmitted the impact of PERK in SMC onto EC. 
EV-mediated paracrine regulation represents a novel SMC-EC interaction mechanism. It is now known that phenotype changes in SMC lead to enhanced secretion of EVs. However, the disease and therapeutic implications of such phenomenon on EC physiology are poorly understood. Of note, Jia et al. described that microparticles (a submicron-size form of EV) derived from mechanically challenged SMC could induce an inflammatory and degenerative phenotype in EC and ultimately thoracic aortic aneurysm formation in an ER stress CHOP-dependent manner. Recent study from the research group led by Baker et al. further showed clear evidence of SMC-to-EC EV transfer in pulmonary system, which could lead to endothelium-to-mesenchymal transition, similar to our observation in the present study. Our data show an SMC-EC paracrine communication driven by PERK that at least partially involve EV-mediated transfer, which help unveils a novel layer of mechanism that may govern the re-endothelialization process.
Given the pivotal role of SMC and its humoral function in vascular biology and pathophysiology, it is of significant interest/value to understand its secretomic profiles that could dictate the vascular milieu and drive the disease progression. Earlier efforts are mainly limited to proteomic studies on SMC secretome, which revealed significant enrichment in extracellular matrix proteins and growth factors. – With the advances of -omics and sequencing technologies, recent research efforts have been characterizing the content of EVs secreted from SMC, which, as earlier studies suggest, are closely related with SMC phenotype change and vascular diseases. As of now, the EV secretory profiles of SMC are best characterized in pulmonary system and vascular calcification. Baker AH’s group initiated a series of studies on characterizing the coding and noncoding RNA cargos in EVs derived from pulmonary SMC, which led to the identification of several key candidates (e.g., miR-143, SMILR, TGFB3 mRNA) with profound influence to adjacent vascular cells. , , Kapustin et al. , provided the first proteomic analysis of EVs from phenotypically converted SMC and their implications in vascular calcification. In a recent report, Langley et al. examined the secretomic profiles from lipid-loaded human SMC as well as the ECM proteomic signatures in symptomatic carotid plaques. In addition, microparticle derived from disease-challenged SMC has long been shown to contain coagulation factors that may affect platelet aggregation and contribute to increased thrombosis risk, such as tissue factor. , Our previous study on PERK’s role in regulating tissue factor expression in vascular cells suggests a possible link to this prior finding, which would warrant future investigations into the direct and paracrine effect of PERK inhibition in platelet. 
Our present study has the following limitations. First, we only resorted to conditioned medium transfer and derived EV transfer to study the SMC-to-EC paracrine effect. A secondary experimental approach, such as SMC-EC co-culture system, would lend further strength to our conclusion. Second, our present study is limited to the description of SMC paracrine effect on EC, while the exact secretomic profiles and cargo contents are not determined. CXCL10, a recently identified anti-endothelium cytokine, was studied here as an example, although other candidates are possibly implicated here, including tissue factor which we have previously shown to be PERK-driven. Third, our present study did not fully differentiate the different EVs (e.g., exosome, microparticle), although a PERK-driven, EV-mediated effect from SMC is clearly demonstrated. Finally, our paracrine studies of EC physiology were limited to EC viability and inflammatory responses, while other significant aspects of endothelium functions were not included, such as angiogenesis and permeability. Future efforts are clearly warranted on these fronts to help further establish the role of such paracrine mechanisms in modulating re-endothelialization.

## Conclusion
In this study, we made the exciting findings that PERK inhibition or knockdown could promote re-endothelialization of denuded rat carotid arteries after balloon angioplasty. The in vitro and in vivo data implicate that excessive PERK activation in SMC drives its phenotype changes and consequently may paracrinally exert a negative regulation of EC homeostasis. Given the benefits of PERK inhibition displayed by our and others’ studies, the rationale is compelling for further evaluation of a PERK-targeting strategy to achieve endothelium-friendly anti-restenotic intervention.
