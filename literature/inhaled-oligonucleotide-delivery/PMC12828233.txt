Recent advancements in this field include the development and approval of several oligonucleotide therapeutics in the US and European markets. Among them, tricyclo-DNA antisense oligonucleotides (tcDNA-ASOs) represent a precise and potent approach to modulating gene expression across diverse genetic diseases. Antisense oligonucleotides are short, synthetic nucleotide sequences that bind to complementary RNA to regulate the production of specific proteins [ , ]. Chemical modifications, such as the incorporation of phosphorothioate linkages, where a nonbridging oxygen atom is replaced with sulfur, enhance resistance to nuclease degradation and improve cellular uptake. These modifications also increase the binding affinity of ASOs to proteins, including serum albumin and cell membrane proteins, as well as to their target RNA, thereby enabling the suppression, modification, or restoration of specific protein expression [ ]. However, tcDNA-ASO therapy faces significant challenges, including rapid systemic clearance, limited cellular uptake, inefficient endosomal escape, and potential cytotoxicity [ , ]. Therefore, optimizing delivery, enhancing stability, and improving tissue-specific targeting remain essential for advancing personalized oligonucleotide therapeutics [ ]. Notably, chemical conjugation of a palmitoyl group to tcDNA-ASOs has been shown to enhance potency and facilitate distribution to key tissues, such as skeletal and cardiac muscles, in mouse models [ , , ].
The lungs represent a promising gateway for systemic drug delivery, as nanoparticles can cross the thin alveolar epithelium into the richly vascularized bloodstream, bypassing the first-pass metabolism of the liver. This noninvasive route not only enhances patient comfort but also improves treatment adherence. Advancements in nanotechnology have demonstrated the potential of the respiratory tract as an effective route for administering diverse therapeutics, including small molecules, nucleic acids, proteins, and peptides. Pulmonary delivery has been investigated for the treatment of chronic respiratory conditions such as lung inflammation and cancer [ , ], as well as for systemic applications, such as insulin administration in diabetes, aerosolized vaccines against COVID-19 and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and intranasal delivery of hormones such as oxytocin [ – , ].
ASO-based nanotherapeutics have also progressed to late-stage clinical development. In phase 3 trials, intrathecal administration of Tofersen, an antisense oligonucleotide designed to suppress superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) protein synthesis, demonstrated therapeutic potential in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) associated with SOD1 gene mutations [ ].
Another therapeutic candidate currently under investigation is SQY51, a palmitoyl-conjugated tcDNA-ASO [ ]. SQY51 is a tricyclo-DNA antisense oligonucleotide bearing both phosphorothioate and phosphodiester linkages and a 5′ palmitoyl moiety, which increases its resistance to nuclease degradation, RNA-binding affinity, and protein binding, thereby enhancing systemic exposure and tissue delivery [ , ]. SQY51 is designed to target exon 51 of the human dystrophin pre-mRNA to modulate splicing and restore expression of a partially functional dystrophin protein in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). DMD is an X-linked monogenic disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the dystrophin gene. Dystrophin plays a critical role in linking the cytoskeleton to the muscle sarcolemma, and its absence results in inflammation and degeneration, ultimately leading to the progressive replacement of muscle tissue with fibrotic tissue and fat [ ]. The current standard of care relies on repeated intravenous (IV) injections. However, this invasive route poses significant limitations for a lifelong treatment regimen, given low patient adherence and the vascular dysfunction associated with DMD. These challenges underscore the need to explore alternative, less invasive delivery strategies to improve both therapeutic efficacy and patient quality of life.
Intratracheal (IT) instillation, a minimally invasive pulmonary delivery method, enables direct and controlled deposition of the therapeutics into the lower respiratory tract of mouse models, providing rapid systemic adsorption while minimizing systemic toxicity [ ]. However, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and biodistribution data are still needed to evaluate the feasibility, systemic distribution, and long-term fate of oligonucleotides delivered through this route.
In this study, we used SQY51 as a model compound and administered a single IT dose of 25 mg/kg to mice to evaluate whether the IT delivery could achieve systemic distribution and effective uptake in target organs. We characterized the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, cellular localization, and inflammatory response following IT instillation, and compared these outcomes to those obtained via IV administration. Our goal was to determine whether pulmonary delivery could serve as a viable and safe alternative for systemic administration to oligonucleotide-based therapeutics.

## Materials and methods

## Reagents and antisense oligonucleotides
DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium), RPMI (Roswell Park Memorial Institute) 1640 culture medium, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum were obtained from Eurobio Scientific. Maxima First Strand cDNA synthesis kit, Fluoromount-G™ (EMS), glycerol, LysoTracker Red DND-99, ELISA Ready-SET-Go kit (for the detection of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), and Trizol were purchased from ThermoFisher scientific. Deferasirox, carbon black nanopowder < 100 nm, Tween-20, anti-CD11b antibody, and Orange G loading buffer were purchased from Merck. Tetramethylethylenediamine, ammonium persulfate, 40% acrylamide/bis-acrylamide solution, Tris-acetate, Criterion system, and iTaq SYBR green supermix were purchased from Bio-Rad. Anti-Lamp1 antibody anti-CD68 antibody was purchased from Abcam.
The antisense oligonucleotide (TcSQY51) was synthesized by SQY Therapeutics. A palmitoyl group was conjugated to the 5′ end of the antisense tc-DNA.

## Cell culture
RAW 264.7 macrophages (ATCC® TIB-71™) were maintained in DMEM complete medium, consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate, in a humidified atmosphere at 37°C and 5% CO2.

## Animals, intratracheal instillation, and intravenous injection
All animal experiments were performed on adult C57BL/6J mice, chosen as one of the most widely used inbred strains to facilitate interpretation of pharmacokinetic and safety data, at the UFR des Sciences de la Santé Simone Veil-Université de Versailles Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and the Ministère de lʼEnseignement supérieur de la Recherche et de lʼInnovation (Apafis #34354). Mice were maintained in a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with access to food and water ad libitum. Both male and female mice aged 8–18 weeks were used in this study.
For the IT instillation group [ ], mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and received a single IT dose of 25 mg/kg SQY51 suspended in 50 µl of sterile PBS. Control mice were instilled with PBS alone.
For the IV group, mice were anesthetized with 5 % isoflurane and injected intravenously with 25 mg/kg SQY51 suspended in 50 µl of sterile PBS [ ].
For the biodistribution analysis of SQY51 in plasma, mice were sampled at one or two discrete time points per animal. For IV administration, 24 mice were allocated as follows: 0.25 h and 12 h (n = 5), 1 h and 6 h (n = 5), 2 h and 8 h (n = 5), 4 h and 24 h (n = 5), 2 h and 24 h (n = 1), 6 h and 24 h (n = 1), and 24 h (n = 2). For IT administration, 47 mice were allocated as follows: 0.25 h and 12 h (n = 4), 1 h and 6 h (n = 5), 2 h and 8 h (n = 4), and 0.25 h and 24 h (n = 3). An additional 31 mice from the IT cohorts used for bronchoalveolar lavage and for the persistence studies described below were sampled at the indicated time points to reinforce time points around the expected Tmax window: 1 h (n = 2), 2 h (n = 6), 4 h (n = 15), as well as later time points at 6 h (n = 1), 8 h (n = 2), and 24 h (n = 5).
For the biodistribution analysis of ΔSQY51 in organs, mice were divided into IV (n = 6) and IT (n = 7) groups and sacrificed 24 h after administration. For the assessment of persistence in organs, biodistribution analysis was performed in mice sacrificed 7 (n = 6), 14 (n = 6), and 21 (n = 5) days after IT administration.

## Macrophages isolation from bronchoalveolar lavage
Twenty mice were used for macrophage isolation. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed by inserting a cannula into the trachea and flushing the lungs four times with 1 ml of sterile PBS. The collected lavage fluid was pooled, kept on ice, and centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cell pellets from the BAL were resuspended in complete DMEM medium supplemented with 1× penicillin/streptomycin and cultured in a humidified atmosphere at 37°C and 5% CO2.

## Immunofluorescence microscopy
Macrophages isolated from BAL were seeded in 8-well LabTek chamber slides (Nunc, Thermo Scientific) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After 24-h incubation, macrophages were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. Slides and a SQY51 probe conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594 or 680 were preheated at 70°C for 5 min, then co-incubated at 55°C for 10 min in a humidified chamber. After PBS washes, slides were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with a primary antibody targeting Lamp1 (Abcam), diluted in 1% BSA in PBS. After additional PBS washes, cells were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Fisher Scientific) diluted in 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted using Fluoromount-G. Confocal image stacks were acquired using the SP8 confocal microscope (Leica) with a ×40 oil-immersion objective. Images were processed using Image J v1.53k (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Lung insufflation, H&E staining, FISH, and IHC analysis of lung sections
Animals were euthanized at 24 h, 1 week, 2 weeks, and 3 weeks after instillation. Lungs were insufflated with 4% PFA diluted in PBS, collected, and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. After washing with H2O, the fixed lungs were processed using the Tissue-Tek VIP 5Jr (SAKURA) and embedded in paraffin using the HistoCore Arcadia H (Leica Biosystems). Embedded lungs were sectioned at a thickness of 5-µm using the RM2265 microtome (Leica Biosystems). Lung sections were deparaffinized before labeling and, when required, stained with H&E using the ST5020 Stainer (Leica Biosystems). Images were acquired using the Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems).
For the fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) assay, deparaffinated lung sections were subjected to antigen retrieval (Tris-EDTA, pH 9.0) for 20 min at 85°C, washed in H2O, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and washed in PBS. Slides and SQY51 probe were preheated at 70°C for 5 min, then incubated together at 55°C for 10 min in a humidified chamber. Lung sections were washed in 1× SSC and PBS. After DAPI staining, sections were coverslipped and imaged using the ScanR high-content screening station (Olympus) and the SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).
For immunohistochemistry analysis, following FISH labeling, lung sections were incubated in blocking buffer (5% normal goat serum diluted in PBS) for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C in a humidified chamber with primary antibodies against Lamp1, CD68, or CD11b. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h, followed by additional PBS washes and DAPI staining. To reduce autofluorescence, lung tissues were treated with TrueVIEW quenching solution (Vector Laboratories) for less than one min, rinsed with PBS, and mounted using Vectashield vibrance antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired using the SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).

## Blood and tissue collection
For pharmacokinetic studies, blood samples were collected from awake mice and droplets from each mouse were collected at two different time points, and at the time of euthanasia. Briefly, blood drops were centrifuged and the sera collected and frozen at −80°C until analysis. Thawed sera were incubated in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, and 0.2% SDS) supplemented with 2 mg/ml proteinase K (Invitrogen) overnight at 55°C. SQY51 was quantified in mouse sera by polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assay.
For biodistribution analysis, whole organs were harvested, weighed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for further analysis. Organs were homogenized in lysis buffer supplemented with 2 mg/ml proteinase K, at a ratio of 50 mg of tissue per ml of lysis buffer using the Precellys Evolution Touch Homogenizer (Bertin Instruments, France). After overnight incubation at 55°C, lysates were centrifuged at 8000 × g and the supernatants were collected. The depalmitoylated ΔSQY51 was quantified in the supernatants by polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assay.

## Polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assay
SQY51 and ΔSQY51 from serum and tissue lysates, respectively, were quantified by polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assays as previously described [ ].
 Briefly, standard curves were generated using 2-fold serial dilutions of known concentrations of SQY51 (for serum) or ΔSQY51 (for tissue lysate supernatants). Samples and controls were diluted in lysis buffer containing 1 mg/ml deferasirox, followed by the addition of the SQY51 complementary probe conjugated to the Alexa Fluor 680 and incubation at 50°C for 5 min. Loading buffer was then added, and samples were loaded onto 15% polyacrylamide gels cast in Criterion cassettes (Bio-Rad). Electropheresis was performed at 110 V for 2 h in Tris-acetate running buffer (pH 7.4) using a Criterion cell (BioRad). Fluorescent signals were acquired using Odyssey CLx imaging system (LI-COR) and densitometric analysis was achieved using Image Studio Lite v4.0.

## Pharmacokinetic analysis
Pharmacokinetic analyses of SQY51 concentrations were performed to characterize absorption, distribution, and elimination profiles. Because each mouse contributed only 1–2 blood samples due to animal welfare considerations, individual pharmacokinetic profiles could not be reconstructed, so composite concentration–time profiles were generated by pooling data from multiple animals at each time point and analyzed by noncompartmental analysis using PKanalix2024R1 (Simulations Plus).
Population pharmacokinetic modeling and goodness-of-fit diagnostics were performed on the original unpooled data in Monolix2024R1 (Simulations Plus) using a two-compartment model with first-order absorption and linear elimination, providing a complementary characterization of SQY51 pharmacokinetics.

## RNA analysis
Total RNA was isolated from macrophages using the Trizol reagent and chloroform extraction technique following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentrations were determined using the NanoPhotometer® N120 (Implen; München, Germany). One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using Maxima First strand cDNA synthesis kit. Quantitative analysis was achieved using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), with each cDNA sample done in triplicate. qPCR was realized using the Bio-Rad CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection system (Bio-Rad) and iTaq SYBRgreen qPCR mix. Table lists the specific primers used for qPCR (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and 18S rRNA) and synthesized by Eurogentec. The cycle threshold (Ct) values of each target genes were first normalized to that of the reference gene 18S rRNA (ΔCt) then the final values (ΔΔCt values) were expressed as folds of control. Data were analyzed on the Bio-Rad CFX manager v3.1 using the ΔΔCt method [ ].
 

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in a 96-well plate. Cytotoxicity to indicated concentrations of SQY51 was assessed 24 h after treatment using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) cell viability assay kit to measure cellular metabolic activity following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance changes were measured at 550 nm and the background absorbance at 600 nm using the FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech). Negative control corresponded to untreated macrophages while positive control corresponded to addition of 30% ethanol in the culture medium for 10 min.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded at 1.5 × 105 cells/ml in six-well plates and incubated for 24 h prior to treatment with PBS, 5 nM SQY51, or 10 µg/ml carbon black. Following 1- or 4-day incubation, culture supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C until analysis. Levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were quantified using the Invitrogen Ready-SET-Go ELISA kits (eBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with capture antibody overnight at 4°C, blocked with assay buffer, and incubated with standards and samples overnight at 4°C. After incubation with the detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP, the substrate solution was added. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 570 nm as a reference wavelength using the FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech). Cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves.

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as mean ± standard errors of the mean (SEM). All experiments were performed independently and derived from at least three independent replicates. Statistical comparisons and graph design were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v8). Student’s unpaired t-test was used for comparison between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Pharmacokinetics of SQY51 in mice following intratracheal instillation
To evaluate the potential for systemic distribution of SQY51 following IT instillation compared to IV injection, we administered equivalent doses of SQY51 to mice via both routes and quantified its concentration in serum over a 1 to 48 h period using polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assays.
SQY51 was significantly and reproducibly detected in the bloodstream after IT administration, indicating that the compound entered systemic circulation following pulmonary delivery (Fig.  ). Compared to IV injection, IT administration resulted in lower plasma concentrations, with a peak at 1 h post-instillation. SQY51 levels remained relatively stable between 1 and 4 h, then gradually declined starting at 6 h. Notably, SQY51 remained detectable in the bloodstream up to 48 h post-IT, whereas it was no longer measurable beyond 24 h after IV injection. For IT administration, SQY51 levels at 4 h were significantly higher than at 0.25 h (P < 0.0001), whereas time points from 6 to 24 h did not differ significantly from 0.25 h. For IV administration, SQY51 levels were significantly lower from 2 h onward compared with 0.25 h (P < 0.0001).
 Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) was 4.5-fold higher following IV administration compared to IT (Fig.  ). The time to reach half of the plasma concentration (T1/2) was shorter for IV injection than for IT administration. While the area under the curve (AUC) was significantly higher after IV injection, the volume of distribution (Vz) for IV was significantly lower than the apparent volume of biodistribution corrected for bioavailability (Vz/F) observed following IT administration, suggesting broader tissue distribution over time with IT delivery. Clearance values (CL) after IV injection were comparable to the apparent clearance corrected for bioavailability (CL/F) after IT administration. Despite reduced plasma exposure, the absolute bioavailability (F%) of SQY51 after IT administration was 34.4 %, indicating that systemic distribution via the pulmonary route is feasible. The goodness-of-fit plot showed no apparent bias in the model predictions ( ).

## Biodistribution of SQY51 in mouse organs
We next quantified the distribution of SQY51 in various organs 24 h after administration via either IT or IV. Organs including lungs, liver, kidney, spleen, the heart, the diaphragm, and tibialis anterior muscles were harvested and analyzed using polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization assays (Fig.  ). Since internalized SQY51 undergoes enzymatic cleavage of its palmitoyl residue, the depalmitoylated form (ΔSQY51) was measured.
 Significantly lower levels of ΔSQY51 were detected in the heart, diaphragm, and kidney of IT-treated mice compared to IV-treated mice. In contrast, ΔSQY51 levels in the lungs were significantly higher following IT administration. No significant differences were observed in the liver, spleen, or tibialis anterior between the routes.
We also assessed the persistence of ΔSQY51 in organs following IT administration by quantifying levels at 1, 7, 14, and 21 days post-instillation (Fig.  ). ΔSQY51 levels gradually decreased over time in the heart, lungs, diaphragm, liver, spleen, and tibialis anterior, though small amounts remained detectable through day 21. In the kidney, ΔSQY51levels remained elevated at days 1 and 7, and decreased by approximately 65% by day 14. These results indicate that SQY51 is distributed systemically and accumulates in several organs. It is worth noting that the lower amount of SQY51 detected in the kidney may be associated with reduced renal toxicity.

## SQY51 localization in instilled lungs
Given that SQY51 was delivered via IT instillation, we examined whether this method affected lung morphology. Lungs from PBS- or SQY51-instilled mice were insufflated 24 h post-instillation, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Fig.  ). Lung architecture was preserved in both groups. Alveolar structures remained intact, and no inflammatory cell infiltration was observed.
 To assess the localization and persistence of SQY51, lungs from SQY51-instilled mice were collected at 1, 7, 14, and 21 days post-instillation. Prior to tissue collection, bronchoalveolar lavage was performed to isolated alveolar cells, and the lungs were insufflated and PFA-fixed. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) was then performed on fixed lung sections using the fluorescently labeled SQY51 probe, with DAPI as a nuclear counterstain (Fig.  ). Confocal microscopy revealed SQY51 signals in individual cells distributed throughout the lung tissue. Quantification of SQY51-positive cells showed a significant decrease from day 7 to 21 compared to day 1 (Fig.  ).
The polyacrylamide fluorescent hybridization analyses of alveolar macrophages recovered from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid done prior to lung collections validated those results (Fig.  ). A significant decrease in the depalmitoylated ΔSQY51 was observed at day 7 and 14 compared to day 1. No ΔSQY51 was detected at day 21.
These results suggest that SQY51 persists in individual cells within lung tissue for at least 21 days and remains detectable in alveolar macrophages up to 14 days after instillation.

## SQY51-positive vesicles in alveolar macrophages
As SQY51-positive signals were detected in individual cells within alveolar spaces, we hypothesized that these were immune cells. Immunofluorescence staining of paraformaldehyde-fixed lung sections revealed colocalization of SQY51 with CD68, a macrophage-associated marker (Fig.  ). To further explore the identity of SQY51-positive cells, separate lung sections were stained with the myeloid cell marker CD11b. Confocal microscopy showed that SQY51 signals were present in CD11b+ cells located within alveolar regions (Fig.  ).
 To investigate whether SQY51 was internalized via phagocytosis, alveolar macrophages isolated from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were cultured on LabTek slides. Cells were either labeled live with LysoTracker Red or fixed and stained for LAMP1, a lysosomal marker, followed by in situ hybridized with SQY51 fluorescent probe. Confocal imaging showed that SQY51 signals colocalized with both LysoTracker (Fig.  ) and LAMP1 (Fig.  ), indicating localization within acidic vesicles and lysosomes. Inhibition of actin polymerization with cytochalasin D did not reduce SQY51 uptake in RAW 264.7 macrophages, suggesting that internalization occurs through actin dependent and actin-independent endocytic pathways ( ).
These findings suggest that following IT administration, SQY51 is phagocytosed by macrophage-lineage cells, trafficked through endosomal pathway, and retained in lysosomes and cytoplasmic compartments. Moreover, within lung tissue, SQY51 was detected in both CD68+ and CD11b+ macrophages, suggesting uptake by alveolar, interstitial or monocyte-derived macrophages.

## Absence of inflammatory response following instillation of SQY51
Since the pulmonary route was employed as a means for systemic delivery of SQY51, it was necessary to evaluate whether IT administration of the tcDNA-ASO could induce local inflammatory responses in the lung. In order to investigate this, we compared its effects to those of carbon black, a well-characterized pro-inflammatory particulate used as a positive control for inflammation. Mice instilled with carbon black exhibited visible accumulation in alveoli 24 h post-instillation (Fig.  ).
 RT-qPCR analysis of lung tissue revealed no significant changes in the expression levels of genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α following SQY51 treatment (Fig.  ). In contrast, mice treated with carbon black displayed a significantly upregulation of IL-1β and TNF-α mRNAs. These results suggest that SQY51 does not elicit a detectable inflammatory response in the lungs when administered via the pulmonary route.

## Effects of SQY51 on RAW 264.7 macrophages
To further confirm the absence of inflammatory effects on macrophages, we used the RAW 264.7 cell line. Cytotoxicity was assessed via MTT assay after treatment with increasing concentrations of SQY51 ranging from 1 nM to 100 µM (Fig. ). A decrease in cell viability was observed at 100 nM, whereas concentrations below 10 nM had no significant effect. For subsequent experiments, 5 nM of SQY51 was used.
 Following a 6 h exposure of RAW 264.7 macrophages to 1 nM or 5 nM SQY51, RT-qPCR analysis revealed no significant upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, or TNF-α expression compared to untreated controls (Fig. ). ELISA quantification of cytokines in the culture supernatant showed no significant increase in the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in RAW 264.7 cells exposed to 5 nM SQY51 or 10 µg/ml carbon black at 1 day post-treatment compared to controls. Interestingly, after 4 days of exposure to carbon black, RAW 264.7 cells exhibited a significant increase in IL-1β and IL-6 levels compared to both the control and SQY51-treated groups (Fig.  ). No changes in TNF-α levels were detected in either the SQY51- or carbon black-treated cells. These results suggest that SQY51 does not induce a pro-inflammatory response in RAW 264.7 macrophages.

## Discussion
This study demonstrates that IT instillation of the palmitoyl-conjugated tricyclo-DNA antisense oligonucleotide SQY51 is a feasible alternative to IV administration for systemic delivery in mice. Our findings show that IT delivery enables SQY51 to reach systemic circulation, accumulate in peripheral tissues, and persist over time, while maintaining a favorable safety profile and avoiding the inflammatory responses typically associated with pulmonary delivery of particulate compounds. Moreover, pulmonary delivery circumvents hepatic first-pass metabolism, which may contribute to enhanced bioavailability and stability of tcDNA-ASOs.
We selected a single dose of 25 mg/kg for both IT and IV administration to align with the highest safe dose used in the AVANCE1 clinical trial of SQY51 in DMD patients, thereby maximizing the translational relevance of our preclinical evaluation [ ]. AVANCE1 uses an ascending IV dosing scheme of 2–25 mg/kg, followed by repeated administrations in the 10–25 mg/kg range, which defined the clinically meaningful exposure window that informed our dose selection. Consequently, the present work was not designed as a dose–finding study. Future studies will be designed to evaluate multiple IT doses to define the exposure–response relationship and identify conditions that maximize systemic exposure while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.
In comparison to IV injection, IT instillation resulted in lower peak plasma concentrations and slower systemic clearance, but extended the detectability of SQY51 in the bloodstream. This kinetic profile suggests that pulmonary absorption leads to a more gradual systemic release, likely mediated by tissue retention in lung tissue and uptake by alveolar, interstitial, and/or monocyte-derived macrophages. Although IV injection produced higher overall systemic exposure, the prolonged circulation time and potentially reduced renal load following IT instillation may be beneficial in therapeutic contexts requiring sustained oligonucleotide availability and minimal renal burden.
A pulmonary bioavailability of 34.4% after IT instillation is in line with previous reports using the lung as a delivery route. In rats, IT administration of a fully phosphorothioate ASO has been shown to produce dose-dependent systemic bioavailability reaching nearly 40% at a dose of 6 mg/kg, indicating that such levels are achievable when sufficient lung dose is delivered [ ]. More recent work with gapmer ASOs has also described lung to systemic exposure patterns and bioavailability estimates close to 40% at higher IT doses, further supporting efficient systemic entry from the lung compartment [ ]. Taken together, these studies place the 34.4% IT bioavailability observed for SQY51 in mice within a credible and pharmacologically meaningful range and reinforce the concept that pulmonary administration can provide robust systemic exposure for antisense oligonucleotides.
The absorption of palmitoyl-conjugated tcDNA-ASOs across the alveolar epithelium may occur via several plausible pathways. The increased lipid solubility conferred by palmitoyl conjugation enhances membrane partitioning and may allow passive diffusion through the lipid bilayer. Palmitoyl-ASOs also bind with high affinity to plasma proteins such as albumin and lipoproteins, which could promote transcytosis via caveolin-1-dependent pathway and support entry into systemic circulation following pulmonary delivery [ , ]. Endocytosis and vesicular transport contribute, as evidence with the SQY51 internalization by alveolar and interstitial macrophages and its trafficking to lysosomal compartments. The lymphatic system may further contribute to slow systemic release and lipid transport proteins expressed in alveolar epithelial cells may facilitate uptake of lipid-conjugated oligonucleotides. Together, these pathways likely account for the sustained systemic appearance and gradual clearance of SQY51 after IT instillation.
The biodistribution profile in mice supports the systemic potential of the IT delivery, although tissue levels were lower than with IV injection. Low concentrations of SQY51 were detected in the diaphragm and tibialis anterior, indicating limited access to peripheral skeletal muscles. By contrast, significantly higher levels were observed in the lung, suggesting that IT instillation may primarily favor local pulmonary exposure while still enabling systemic distribution. Such a profile may be particularly relevant in disorders with both respiratory and cardiac involvement, such as DMD, where respiratory muscle weakness and cardiomyopathy are key to disease progression.
The pharmacokinetic profile of SQY51 is consistent with prior reports on antisense oligonucleotides, showing higher concentrations in the lung, followed by the kidney and liver [ ]. Our data extend these observations by demonstrating that tcDNA-ASOs persist in lung tissue for more than one week, suggesting that the lung may act as a reservoir and a site of systemic entry. Direct comparison with the findings of Bäckström et al. remains limited due to differences in oligonucleotide chemistry and administered dose, but their findings provide supportive context for the potential systemic distribution of tcDNA-ASOs via the pulmonary route. Further PK/PD modeling and dose–response studies will be required to establish quantitative links between tissue exposure and exon-skipping activity.
tcDNA-ASOs share several key features with established antisense chemistries such as 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE) ASOs and phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) but also present specific properties that may influence their suitability for pulmonary administration [ , ]. Like 2′-MOE ASOs, tcDNA-ASOs exhibit increased strong exon-skipping activity and high metabolic stability while maintaining a phosphorothioate backbone that supports plasma protein binding and systemic absorption. Compared with PMOs, which are neutrally charged and often show limited systemic exposure following pulmonary delivery, palmitoyl-conjugated tcDNA-ASOs retain intrinsic amphipathic properties that may facilitate membrane interactions, endocytic trafficking across the alveolar epithelium, and subsequent entry into the systemic circulation. In addition, the tricyclic sugar constraint enhances RNA affinity. These combined characteristics suggest that tcDNA-ASOs are particularly well suited for strategies that leverage the lungs as a portal for systemic delivery to distal organs, while remaining conceptually aligned with other advanced antisense platforms.
Immunohistochemical analyses revealed SQY51 localization within CD68⁺and CD11b⁺ macrophages and accumulation in lysosomal compartments, suggesting internalization through phagocytosis and processing via the endosomal pathway. While CD68 is a pan-macrophage marker, CD11b+ cells may include interstitial or monocyte-derived macrophages [ ]. Future colocalization studies using markers such as CD11c, F4/80, Ly6C, and CCR2 will be required to distinguish between alveolar, interstitial, and recruited macrophage populations and clarify the identity of SQY51-containing macrophages [ , , ]. These investigations will be needed to decipher the pharmacokinetics and immune interactions of pulmonary-delivered tcDNA-ASOs.
Despite significant uptake by macrophages, SQY51 did not result in a pulmonary inflammatory response. In contrast to instillation of carbon black, a known inflammatory particulate, SQY51 administration did not lead to inflammatory cell infiltration, increased pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in vivo or in vitro [ ]. Histological analyses revealed preserved lung architecture. These results were supported by in vitro experiments using RAW 264.7 macrophages, where SQY51 exposure did not alter cell viability or induce pro-inflammatory cytokine release.
Evidence from both preclinical and clinical research supports the concept of effective systemic drug delivery via the lungs. Inhaled insulin formulations, such as spray-dried excipient-free insulin powder and Afrezza, have demonstrated significant reductions in blood glucose levels in Wistar rats compared with subcutaneous insulin injection. The inhaled powders were absorbed more rapidly, although the duration of glycemic control was shorter than with subcutaneous injection [ , ]. In humans, systemic absorption through the pulmonary route has been demonstrated with Afrezza, an FDA-approved inhaled insulin evaluated in phase IV clinical trials in patients with Type 1 diabetes [ ]. These trials confirmed effective systemic absorption and a rapid onset of action, while also noting mild, reversible reductions in lung function, highlighting the need for pulmonary monitoring. Despite those considerations, inhaled insulin is regarded as a noninvasive alternative to injections, supporting the broader translational potential of pulmonary administration for achieving systemic therapeutic effects, including emerging innovative drugs such as nucleic acid-based therapies. In addition, other non-IV routes such as subcutaneous, intramuscular, and inhaled administration have been used in clinical trials to achieve systemic delivery of oligonucleotide therapeutics and to cross biological barriers, typically following single or multiple ascending dose designs [ , , ].
Our next steps will focus on the chronic treatment of preclinical models, such as the mdx mouse model of DMD, with tcDNA-ASO administered intratracheally. These studies will enable the evaluation of cumulative tissue exposure, immune tolerance, therapeutic efficacy, and durability. We will further assess exon-skipping efficiency, restoration of dystrophin expression, and functional outcomes, including muscle strength, cardiac performance, and respiratory capacity. Nonetheless, several limitations with IT delivery in preclinical models must be carefully considered. The long-term stability of the tcDNA-ASO formulations under conditions of repeated IT administration remains to be established. Although no acute pulmonary toxicity has been observed thus far, chronic dosing carries the risk of inducing immune responses, such as neutrophil infiltration, cytokine release, or interactions with pulmonary surfactant that could impair drug distribution and cellular uptake. Furthermore, phagocytic clearance by alveolar macrophages, mucillary clearance, and nonuniform deposition across lung may restrict both systemic absorption and reduce the bioavailability of the active compound. These challenges highlight the need to optimize dosing regimens and careful monitoring for potential long-term pulmonary adverse effects. These chronic IT studies are currently ongoing in the mdx model using the corresponding tcDNA-ASO and will continue for several months. At the end of the treatment period, mice will be evaluated for pulmonary and renal toxicities, dystrophin restoration, muscle strength, and cardiac function. Renal safety is a key consideration for phosphorothioate-modified antisense oligonucleotides, which tend to accumulate in proximal tubular cells and can induce dose–dependent tubular alterations in preclinical models. Clinical and nonclinical analyses of marketed ASO drugs further indicate that nephrotoxicity, typically subclinical changes in renal function or proteinuria, is among the more frequent class-related adverse effects and are influenced by dose level, treatment duration, and the specific oligonucleotide chemistry [ , ]. These observations underscore the need to carefully balance target tissue exposure with renal safety margins.
In conclusion, our data support IT instillation as a promising, minimally invasive route for systemic delivery of tcDNA-ASOs. This approach enables effective pulmonary uptake, distribution to distal tissues, and a favorable safety profile, providing a viable alternative to IV administration, particularly for diseases requiring both local and systemic therapeutic effects.
