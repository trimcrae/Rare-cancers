## Summary

## Background
Antibiotic abuse has become a top public health problem worldwide. Ciprofloxacin (CIP) has the highest detection rate in human but its association with unexplained miscarriage (UM) is completely unknown. Senescence is a ubiquitous cellular process but its association with UM is still unclear.

## Methods
We combined case–control study using a UM case–control group and in vitro functional assays using mouse model and human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells. The associations between environmental exposure to CIP and cell senescence were explored. Trophoblast cell senescence, the mitochondrial dysfunctions, and the TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF were investigated.

## Findings
Higher urinary CIP levels are associated with villous tissue senescence and woman miscarriage. CIP-exposed mouse model further confirms that CIP exposure causes placental senescence to induce mouse miscarriage. Mechanistically, CIP up-regulates tripartite motif containing 21 (TRIM21) levels, promotes TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), and thus reduces MFF levels. Subsequently, the reduced MFF causes mitochondrial dysfunctions and cell senescence, further inducing miscarriage.

## Interpretation
Collectively, this study discovers new risk of CIP exposure on miscarriage, explores pathogenesis and biological mechanisms of CIP-induced miscarriage, provides targets for treatment against miscarriage, and describes the crosstalk among environmental antibiotics, biological mechanisms, and reproductive health.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
Antibiotic abuse has become a leading public health problem worldwide. Ciprofloxacin (CIP) is the most frequently detected antibiotic in humans, yet its association with unexplained miscarriage (UM) remains entirely unknown. Senescence is a ubiquitous cellular process, but its relationship with UM is still unclear. Therefore, whether CIP exposure might cause senescence to induce UM, as well as their underlying mechanisms, should be urgently explored.

## Added value of this study
We conduct a UM case–control group and CIP-exposed mouse model and cell-based assays and find that CIP exposure increase UM risk. CIP-exposed mouse model further confirms that CIP exposure causes placental senescence to induce mouse miscarriage. Mechanistically, CIP up-regulates TRIM21 levels, promotes TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF, and thus reduces MFF levels.

## Implications of all the available evidence
This study provides insights into CIP exposure as a miscarriage risk by characterising its pathogenesis, identifying therapeutic targets, and mapping the crosstalk between environmental antibiotics and reproductive health through biological mechanisms.

## Introduction

## Unexplained miscarriage
Approximately 15%–25% pregnant women experience miscarriage and 1%–5% suffer from recurrent miscarriage worldwide. Among patients with recurrent miscarriage, 41.2% have long-term anxiety, 8.6% have severe depression, and 1.4% even threaten life safety. The causes of miscarriage are very complex, including genetic factors, chromosomal abnormalities, infections, prothrombotic states, abnormal uterine anatomy, endocrine, metabolic and reproductive immune diseases. However, there are still 50% miscarriage with unknown causes, which are generally called as unexplained miscarriage (UM). , The unclear pathogenesis and biological mechanisms greatly restrict its effective clinical treatment. Therefore, it is particularly urgent to explore the potential pathogenesis and biological mechanisms of UM.

## CIP exposure and miscarriage
Increasing evidence have shown that various environmental pollutants might induce unexplained miscarriage. Recently, in this group, we have identified that multiple environmental toxicants, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon BaP/BPDE, , , , , , , , nanoplastics, , copper exposure, lithium exposure, , , Bisphenol B or Bisphenol F, , hypoxia, or disinfection by-products of tap water could induce UM. In addition to these, more importantly, antibiotic abuse has become one of the top public health problems worldwide. Antibiotic consumption is increased by 65% from 2000 to 2015, and is estimated to increase by 200% in 2030 relative to 2015. China has the highest consumption and production of antibiotics in the world. Firstly, antibiotics have been widely used in medicine, agricultural production, animal husbandry and aquaculture, and have high detection rates. For example, it has been reported that antibiotics detection rates are 100% (4/4, the number of positively detected samples/the number of total samples) in cabbage, corn, rice, spinach, and radish, and leafy vegetable in China, 22.9% (32/140) in fresh milk in Nepal, 13.6% (3/22) in commercial egg in China, 33.3% (25/75) in farmed fish in Turkey, and 35.3% (6/17) in pork in China. The contents of antibiotics are detected as 3.26 μg/kg in pak choi in low-dose contaminated soil and 0.400–203 μg/kg (dry weight) in corn in contaminated farmlands. Subsequently, these antibiotics inevitably enter and accumulate in human body through these food chains. Secondly, a large amount of antibiotics are also spread into environments due to various animal faeces, medical treatment, chemical sewage, and domestic wastewater. For example, the average contents of antibiotics are 786.2 ng/L, 311.2 ng/L, and 186.8 μg/kg in influent, effluent, and sludge from urban wastewater treatment plants in China, respectively. Subsequently, these antibiotics re-enter human body through inhalation, drinking water, and food chain enrichment.
Among these antibiotics, ciprofloxacin (CIP) has the highest detection rates in various foods, environments, and human bodies. CIP has been detected in ng/L ∼ μg/L in surface waters across multiple countries, including China, Spain, Brazil, Malaysia, and Kenya. Additionally, CIP was as high as 104.4 μg/kg in agricultural soils in one of the largest vegetable cultivation regions in Northeast China. The detection rates of CIP are 25% (17/68, the number of positively detected samples/the number of total samples) in vegetables in China, 8.75% (14/160) in fish in China, 13.95% (6/43) in pork and 9.09% (3/33) in chicken in Thailand. Moreover, CIP has been detected at median 73.5 μg/mg creatinine in urine samples of pregnant women in China. It has been reported that CIP exposure shows reproductive toxicity. Epidemiological study displayed that CIP exposure during pregnancy is associated with premature foetal delivery (Shanghai, China, n = 429, Adjusted OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.09). Animal model assays also discovered that CIP exposure during pregnancy in rats can cause oxidative damage in foetal liver tissues. However, till now, whether CIP exposure might induce miscarriage is still completely unknown.

## Cellular senescence
Cellular senescence is a ubiquitous process in which cell cycle is permanently arrested. Cellular senescence is associated with many human diseases, such as brain diseases (e.g., Alzheimer 's disease, cerebral haemorrhage, Parkinson' s disease), tumours, and cardiovascular diseases. , , Senescence is characterised as higher SA-β-gal (senescence-associated β-galactosidase) activity, higher protein levels of cell cycle inhibitors p16, p21, and p38, and higher levels of SASPs (pro-inflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotypes). , , It has been reported that the levels of senescence are significantly higher in placenta or decidual tissues of patients with miscarriage relative to those in healthy controls. , However, the association and causation between senescence and unexplained miscarriage are still largely unknown. Moreover, whether CIP exposure might cause senescence to induce unexplained miscarriage is completely unknown and should be fully explored.
Till now, as far as we known, the association, causation, and underlying mechanisms among CIP exposure, senescence, and unexplained miscarriage are still completely uncovered. For this aim, we collected urine and villous tissue samples from patients with unexplained miscarriage and their matched healthy control group (an UM case–control study), constructed various CIP-exposed mouse models, and used CIP-exposed human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cell models. We find that exposure to high levels of CIP causes senescence to induce miscarriage by promoting TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF. Collectively, this study not only reveals pathogenesis and biological mechanism of CIP exposure-induced unexplained miscarriage but also identify biotargets for treatment against miscarriage, providing the crosstalk among environmental antibiotics, biological mechanisms, and clinical reproductive health.

## Methods

## Study design and tissue collection
Villous tissue samples and urine samples were collected from 50 patients with unexplained miscarriage (UM group) and 50 women who had personally elective miscarriage to terminate their unwanted pregnancies (healthy control (HC) group) between 25 and 30 years old from the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, as the methods described previously. , , This belongs to Lu-Yue Environmental and Reproductive Health Cohort (LYEPHC). All women were 6–10 weeks of gestation. Any woman with one of the following features was excluded, including uterine abnormalities, abnormal karyotype, autoimmune abnormality, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, endocrine or metabolic diseases, polycystic ovarian syndrome, a documented history of pre-eclampsia or eclampsia in previous pregnancies, as described previously. , , The criteria of pregnancy was considered by the professional doctors according to factors such as the missed menstruation, morning sickness, β-hCG levels, early pregnancy factors, and so on. Miscarriage is defined as the loss of an embryo or foetus weight <500 g or before 20 weeks of gestation, including spontaneous loss or personally elective miscarriage. HC group had previous successful pregnancies. HC and UM women did not receive any treatment. The characteristics of these HC and UM women are listed in , including age, gestational day, BMI, education, household income, smoking, and drinking in the period of three month before miscarriage operation. All the information was obtained from medical records. A piece of villous tissue with dimensions of approximate 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm3 was manually dissected from the foetal side of placenta and was cleared of maternal decidua. After washing with sterile saline, villous tissue samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C before RNA or protein extraction. A single-spot of midstream urine sample was collected from these women using polypropylene containers on the same day before miscarriage operation (each n = 50).

## Determination of CIP levels in urine samples by HPLC-MS/MS
CIP levels in urine samples were detected by HPLC-MS/MS. , In brief, human urine samples were mixed with internal standard (ciprofloxacin-d8) and extracted through Oasis Prime HLB extraction cartridge (1 cc/30 mg, Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA). Then, the extracts were separated using HPLC-MS/MS using Agilent 1200 series rapid resolution LC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) containing a reversed-phase ZORBAX SB-Aq C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 um, Agilent Technologies), with 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile as mobile phases. Then, the samples were analysed by Agilent 6410 B triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray ionisation source (Agilent Technologies). The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: ESI-positive ionisation mode, nitrogen drying gas at 300 °C with 10 L/min, nebuliser 30 psi, capillary voltage at 4000 V. Mass spectrometric data were collected and processed using MassHunter Workstation software B.01.03 and B.01.04 (Agilent Technologies). The limit of detection (LOD) of CIP in urine samples was 0.047 ng/mL. CIP in reagent blanks were all below the LODs. CIP was detected in 40% (n = 50) of HC and 50% (n = 50) of patients with UM. The calibration standard was linear below 100 ng/mL (n = 3 per concentration) with determination coefficient of 0.9950 and accuracy of 95%. The spiked recoveries ranged from 85.3% to 97.6%, and the relative standard deviations were below 10.0%.

## Mice and experimental design
CIP-exposed mouse models were constructed as the similar methods described previously. , , C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks (Charles River Company, Beijing, China) were randomly divided into different groups (n = 6 per group) and the age distribution was consistent in all experimental and control groups. The experiments were blinded to data analysts. Mice were free access to excessive food and water. Females and males were caged together at a ratio of 4:1. The appearance of a vaginal copulation plug was considered as day 1 (D1) of pregnancy, which was further confirmed by weighing mouse body every day. The pregnant mice were treated with CIP everyday. To explore the effects of real environmental exposure dose (REED) of CIP on miscarriage, we calculated the actual daily intake dose of CIP in human body. One study has shown that CIP intake dose was approximately 37.5 μg/kg/d in the residents in Anhui, China. This dose could be considered as a representative of human REED under the inadvertent environment. According to body surface area coefficient (mouse/human = 12), the REED of CIP in mice corresponded to 0.45 mg/kg/day (37.5 μg/kg/d × 12) CIP. Clinically, patients with advanced chronic kidney disease might take 500–1000 mg/day CIP, corresponding to 85–170 mg/kg/day (500 mg/day/70 kg × 12) CIP in mouse. In another mouse model assays using C57BL/6 mice with aortic aneurysm and dissection, exposure to 100 mg/kg/d CIP for four weeks increased susceptibility to aortic dissection and rupture. Considering the doses used in literature, short lifespan of mice, and the feasibility of mouse model assays, we chose 0, 0.45, 12.5, 160, or 320 mg/kg/day CIP in this mouse model, corresponding to 0 (control), 1-, 28-, 356-, or 712-fold REED of CIP.
Model 1, CIP-exposed pregnant mice model was constructed: ① control group, treated with the same volume of saline solution, ② 1-fold REED of CIP group, treated with 0.45 mg/kg/d CIP, ③ 28-fold REED of CIP group, treated with 12.5 mg/kg/d CIP, ④ 356-fold REED of CIP group, treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP, ⑤ 712-fold REED of CIP group, treated with 320 mg/kg/d CIP. Mice were daily given CIP or equal volume of saline solution by oral gavage from D1 to D13. Model 2, we also constructed a miscarriage intervention model by treating CIP-exposed pregnant mice with D + Q (Dasatinib and Quercetin, cell senescence inhibitors): ① control group, treated with the same volume of saline solution, ② treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP in saline, ③ treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and the same volume of 10% PEG-400 as solvent, ④ treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 5 mg/kg/3 d D and 50 mg/kg/3 d Q in 10% PEG-400. Mice were daily given CIP or equal volume of saline solution by oral gavage from D1 to D13. Meanwhile, 5 mg/kg/3 d D and 50 mg/kg/3 d Q in 10% PEG-400 or equal volume 10% PEG-400 was orally given once per three days from D1 to D13. Model 3, we also constructed another miscarriage intervention models by treating CIP-exposed pregnant mice with pcDNA3.1-Mff (overexpression of murine Mff): ① treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 10 mg/kg/3 d empty vector pcDNA3.1, and ② treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 10 mg/kg/3 d pcDNA3.1-Mff. Pregnant mice were daily given CIP or equal volume of saline solution by oral gavage from D1 to D13. Meanwhile, pcDNA3.1 (empty vector as control) or pcDNA3.1-Mff (plasmid overexpressing murine Mff) were also intraperitoneally injected into mice once per three days from D1 to D13. Model 4, in another miscarriage intervention model, CIP-exposed pregnant mice were treated with AS-Trim 21 (knockdown of murine Trim 21): ① treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 10 mg/kg/3 d AS–NC, and ② treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 10 mg/kg/3 d AS-Trim 21. Pregnant mice were daily given CIP or equal volume of saline solution by oral gavage from D1 to D13. Meanwhile, 10 mg/kg/3 d AS–NC (control of siRNA) or AS-Trim21 (siRNA for knockdown of murine Trim21) were also intraperitoneally injected into mice once per three days from D1 to D13. Model 5, we also constructed a miscarriage treatment model by treating CIP-exposed pregnant mice with F + Q (Fimepinostat and Quisinostat, Trim 21 inhibitor, drug approved by FDA) : ① treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP, and ② treated with 160 mg/kg/d CIP and 50 mg/kg/2 d F and 10 mg/kg/2 d Q. Mice were daily given with CIP or equal volume of saline solution by oral gavage from D1 to D13. Meanwhile, mice were also given with 50 mg/kg/2 d Fimepinostat (F, APExBIO, A4097) in saline via oral gavage and 10 mg/kg/2 d Quisinostat (Q, APExBIO, A4090) through intraperitoneal injection on alternative days relative to Fimepinostat.
For Models 1–5, mice were euthanised by injection with Nembutal (100 mg/kg) for collection of uterus on D14. The operation and humane endpoints complied with The ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The embryo resorption was identified as smaller or darker appearance relative to the viable and pink healthy embryos. The miscarriage rate in each mouse and the average miscarriage rate in each group were calculated by (the number of adsorbed embryos)/(the total number of normal embryos and the adsorbed embryos). One random placenta was taken from each of six mice in one group. Then, RNAs and proteins were extracted from these placental tissues for RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis (n = 6 in each group), respectively. The animal project has been authorised by Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

## Cell culture
Human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells (immortalised by SVneo virus, purchased from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., CL0164; RRID: CVCL_7162) were grown in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 ng/mL streptomycin in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cell line was confirmed to be contamination-free through mycoplasma testing and STR authentication. To explore the effects of real environmental exposure dose (REED) of CIP on trophoblast cell functions, we calculated the actual exposure dose of CIP in human body and compared the doses used in other cell models. CIP levels were detected as mean 73.5 μg/mg creatinine in urine samples of pregnant women under real environmental exposure. , The mean minimal inhibitory concentration of CIP for Salmonella Typhi and Salmonella Paratyphi A were 1.20 and 1.97 μg/mL, respectively. The normal doses used for treatment of anti-bacterial infections were 50–400 μg/mL CIP. Exposure of human primary vascular smooth muscle cells to 110 μg/mL CIP accelerated angiotensin-II-induced senescence in vascular smooth muscle cells. Exposure of HT1197 and HT1376 cells to 50–100 μg/mL CIP exhibited profound cytotoxic effects. Based on the doses used in literature and pre-experiments, in this study, we constructed CIP-exposed human trophoblast cell models by treating HTR-8/SVneo cells with 0, 1, 10, 20, or 40 μg/mL CIP for 24 h at 37 °C.

## The comparability of human trophoblast cells, human villous tissues, and mouse placental tissues
In our recent studies, we always used human trophoblast cells, woman villous tissues, and mouse placental tissues for various omics analysis and experimental validation. , , , Trophoblast cells are primarily functional cells in villous tissues and play essential roles in healthy pregnancy. They invade the uterine spiral artery for vascular remodelling, invade the uterine decidual to promote embryo implant, and secrete hormones to regulate embryonic development. Dysfunctions of trophoblast cells are always related with various female reproductive diseases, including miscarriage. Therefore, we focused on trophoblast cells and studied the effects of CIP exposure on trophoblast cell functions. As for woman tissues, we selected villous tissues, which contained a large number of trophoblast cells and were easy to be separated from placental tissues. As for mouse placental tissues, mouse placenta is very small in size. Although labyrinth is analogous to human villous tissues, it is intimately intertwined with other tissues, which is difficult to cleanly and reproducibly dissect from mouse placenta without cross-contamination. Therefore, we used the whole placental tissues for subsequent massive experimental validation.
In mouse model, although exposure to ≥12.5 mg/kg/d CIP displayed phenotypes, we still selected a higher dose (160 mg/kg/d) for omics analysis and various mouse models with intervention or therapy. There were three reasons. This dose (160 mg/kg/d) stably induced a clear miscarriage phenotype in mice. This stable phenotype allowed us to comprehensively and deeply scan and select a robust and unambiguous molecular signal for subsequently mechanistic study, reducing the possibility of missing some molecules that might be ignored at lower exposure doses. Moreover, the 160 mg/kg/d dose was equivalent to the clinical therapeutic dose in humans, which had relatively higher clinical relevance. Therefore, we selected 160 mg/kg/d dose for omics analysis and various mouse models with intervention or therapy.
Similarly, although exposure to 20 μg/mL CIP showed the measurable toxic effects, we stilled used 40 μg/mL CIP-exposed trophoblast cells for 24 h for omics analysis. Under this condition, trophoblast cell viability was reduced by approximately 60% ( ), which allows us to comprehensively and deeply scan and select a robust and unambiguous molecular signal for subsequently mechanistic study, reducing the possibility of missing some molecules that might be ignored at lower exposure doses. Moreover, the exposure dose was also close to those reported in literature, such as exposure of HT1197 and HT1376 cells to 50–100 μg/mL CIP exhibited profound cytotoxic effects. The omics analysis data at this exposure dose might provide insights in toxicity for other types of cells. Therefore, we selected 40 μg/mL dose for omics analysis.

## High-throughput mRNA sequencing and data processing
Two pairs of random UM vs HC human villous tissues (0.1 g), 160 vs 0 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues (0.1 g), and 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP-treated human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells (5 × 106 cells) were used for mRNA sequencing. High-throughput mRNA sequencing was performed on HiSeq 2000 sequencing platform (BGI-Shenzhen) according to BGI commercial standard process (https://www.bgi.com/). , , Briefly, total RNAs were extracted by Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The process included the removal of rRNA, synthesis of double-stranded cDNA, end repair, degradation of one strand, and enrichment of the other strand by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). The library quality was confirmed by sequencing. The differentially expressed mRNAs (DEGs)with differences >1.3-fold and p < 0.05 were generated from read counts using the online bioinformatic platform Dr. Tom provided by BGI (biosys.bgi.com). The human and mouse DEGs were searched in the Ensembl database (Homo sapiens or Mus musculus, NCBI_GCF_000001635.27_GRCm39) to determine their genome loci. These DEGs were used for gene ontology (GO) and KEGG analysis to generate GO and KEGG plots, respectively. , 

## Proteomic analysis
Two pairs of random UM vs HC human villous tissues (0.1 g), 160 vs 0 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues (0.1 g), and 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP-treated human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells (5 × 106 cells) were used for proteomic analysis (Novogene, China). Proteomic analysis was performed on Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, proteins were extracted, and their concentrations were determined by Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad, USA). After proteins were digested with Trypsin Gold (Promega), shot-gun proteomics analysis was performed using an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the data-dependent acquisition mode. The differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) with differences >1.3-fold and p < 0.05 were generated from read counts using the online bioinformatic platform provided by Novogene (https://magic.novogene.com/customer/main#/loginNew). The DEPs were searched in the UniProt Mus musculus (Mus_musculus_uniprot_2022_1_27.fasta, 86492 sequences) database and Homo sapiens (homo_sapiens_uniprot_2022_1_27.fasta, 203711 sequences) database to determine their genome loci. These DEPs were used for gene ontology (GO) and KEGG analysis to generate GO and KEGG plots, respectively. , 

## IP-MS analysis of MFF-bound proteins
MFF-bound proteins were analysed by EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by Novogene Genetics company (Beijing, China), as the methods previously described. In brief, the proteins bound to MFF were separated and digested with trypsin (10 ng/μL) overnight at 37 °C. The samples were desalted, lyophilised, and re-dissolved in loading buffer A (0.1% formic acid, wt/vol). Subsequently, the samples were eluted and separated on a reverse-phase column with loading buffer B (0.1% FA in 80% acetonitrile, wt/vol), and then injected into the mass spectrometer at a constant flow rate of 600 nL/min. The eluted peptides were analysed by mass spectrometer by scanning m/z from 300 to 1500 in a positive polarity mode with spray voltage of 2.3 kV. Primary mass spectrometry resolution was set to 60,000 (at 200 m/z). The 40 most abundant precursor ions from full MS scan were selected for analysis at a resolution of 15,000 (at 200 m/z). Raw files of mass spectrometry were identified by MaxQuant (www.maxquant.org). Proteins were identified by searching based on UniProt homo_sapiens database (www.uniprot.org). 

## Cell transfection
Empty vector pcDNA3.1 (Catalogue No. V790-20) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Company. cDNAs that were used for construction of overexpression plasmid of MFF (pcDNA3.1-MFF) and TRIM21 (pcDNA3.1-TRIM21), which were synthesised and constructed by Addgene. The corresponding RNA sequences were obtained from National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (Gene Bank, Homo sapiens, GRCh38.p14; sequences in ). Empty vector pcDNA3.1 was used as negative control. Si-MFF, si-TRIM21, and si-NC (negative control) were customised by Thermo Fisher (sequences in ). HTR-8/SVneo cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to 80% confluence. Trophoblast cells were transfected with 1 μg plasmids or 50 nM siRNAs in turbofect transfection reagent (R0531, Thermo Scientific) for 24 h according to the manufacturer's protocols. The transfection efficiencies were validated by RT-qPCR and WB.

## Cell viability
Cell viability was evaluated using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8, ab228554, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Cells (5 × 103 cells per well, three replicates for each group) were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with 0, 10, 20, or 40 μg/mL CIP for 0, 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. In another assays, HTR-8/SVneo (5 × 103 cells per well, three replicates for each group) were seeded in 96-well plates, were treated with 1 μg plasmids or 50 nM siRNAs and then co-treated with 0 or 40 μg/mL CIP for 0, 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. Afterwards, 10 μL incubation reagent and 90 μL DMEM/F12 medium were added according to the manufacturer's protocols. The 96-well plate was fully covered with tin foil to avoid light. Subsequently, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with cell culture medium as background. The proliferation of cells was expressed as the change in absorbance at 450 nm. All experiments were replicated thrice.

## β-galactosidase activity assay
β-galactosidase activity was determined using senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Human villous tissues, various mouse placental tissues, and differently treated HTR-8/SVneo cells were used for β-galactosidase activity assays. The frozen sections of human villous tissues or mouse placental tissues and the cells were fixed in 1.0 mL fixation buffer at room temperature for 15 min. The staining mixture was added to the frozen sections or cells, which were further incubated overnight at 37 °C. On the next day, the cells were washed with PBS, and the staining cells were observed under a microscope. SA-β-Gal-positive cells (bluish-green colour) were counted in 15 random fields and expressed as % of total positive intensity for tissues or % of total cells.

## ROS (reactive oxygen species) measurement
The levels of intracellular ROS were measured using Cellular ROS Assays (ROS, ab113851, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The levels of ROS were determined in 0 or 40 μg/mL CIP-treated HTR-8/SVneo cells with co-overexpression of Mff or co-knockdown of Trim 21. Cells (5 × 103 cells per well) were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA (final concentration) at 37 °C for 30 min in dark. The cells were thoroughly washed thrice with PBS to remove excessive DCFH-DA. Subsequently, the resulting cells were visualised under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). DCFDA fluorescence was excited at 480 nm and detected at 525 nm for all the samples, and quantified using Image J 1.43 U (NIH Image J System, Bethesda, MD). All experiments were replicated thrice. The levels of ROS were expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity and were normalised against the levels in control group.

## Mitochondrial ROS measurement
The levels of mitochondrial ROS in cells were detected using the fluorescent probes MitoSOX™ Red (AbMole). Briefly, cells were seeded in confocal dishes, treated with 1 μg plasmids or 50 nM siRNAs and co-treated with 0 or 40 μg/mL CIP, and then incubated with 1–2 mL 5 μM MitoSOX Red at 37 °C for 10 min in dark. Subsequently, the cells were gently washed thrice with prewarmed appropriate buffer. Finally, cells were counter-stained with DAPI staining solution; and subsequently cellular mitochondrial reactive oxygen species levels were observed under confocal laser scanning microscopy. The levels of mitochondrial ROS were expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity and were normalised against the levels in control group.

## Determination of the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)
The MMP levels were determined using JC-1 fluorescent probe. HTR-8/SVneo cells were treated with 1 μg plasmids or 50 nM siRNAs and co-treated with 0 or 40 μg/mL CIP. After washing twice with PBS, cells were incubated with 1 mL JC-1 staining working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing twice with JC-1 buffer, the changes in MMP in cells were imaged on a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). JC-1 was a positively charged dye that aggregates in the inner membrane of mitochondria. The red fluorescence (excitation at 525 nm and emission at 590 nm) indicated healthy cells; and green fluorescence (excitation at 490 nm and emission at 530 nm) indicated the depolarisation of mitochondria due to a drop in membrane potential. The levels of MMP were expressed as the ratios of red/green fluorescence intensity and were normalised against those in control group.
MMP levels were also determined in human villous tissues and mouse placental tissues. Firstly, tissue mitochondria were isolated by Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Briefly, human villous tissues and mouse placental tissues were mixed with mitochondrial isolation medium A for homogenisation. After centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded; and mitochondria were collected from the precipitate after another centrifugation at 11,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. MMP levels were determined using enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Their levels were expressed as the ratios of red/green fluorescence intensity and normalised against those in control group.

## NAD+ and NADH measurements
Total intracellular NAD+ and NADH levels were measured using coenzyme Ⅰ (NAD+/NADH) content assay kit according to its instruction (AIDISHENG, Cat. No. ADS-W-FM001). Cellular mitochondria were isolated using Cellular Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3601, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Cells (20–50 million) were re-suspended in 1–2.5 mL mitochondrial isolation reagent and incubated on ice for 10–15 min. The suspension was then homogenised in a glass homogeniser with 10–30 strokes. After centrifugation at 600g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and further centrifuged at 11,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The final mitochondrial pellet was re-suspended in 150–200 μL mitochondrial storage buffer. Tissue mitochondria were isolated by Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3606, Beyotime Biotechnology). Briefly, women villous tissues and mouse placental tissues were mixed with mitochondrial isolation medium A for homogenisation. After centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded; and mitochondria were collected from the precipitate after another centrifugation at 11,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Mitochondrial NAD+ and NADH levels were measured using coenzyme I (NAD+/NADH) content assay kit according to its instruction (AIDISHENG, Cat. No. ADS-W-FM001). Briefly, HTR-8/SVneo cells treated with 0 or 40 μg/mL CIP and co-overexpression of Mff or co-knockdown of Trim 21, HC and UM human villous tissues, CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues, and placental tissues of CIP-exposed mice with treatment with Mboat1 overexpression plasmid, AS-Trim 21, or F + Q. NAD+ was extracted as following. Cells (5 × 107) or tissues (0.1 g) were incubated with 1 mL extract A solution in ice bath at 60 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was mixed with extract B solution to neutral pH. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to collect NAD+. NADH was extracted as following. Cells (5 × 107) or tissues (0.1 g) were incubated with 1 mL extract B at 60 °C for 30 min in ice bath and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with extract A to neutral pH. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to collect NADH. Subsequently, NAD+ or NADH was mixed with reagent one to four in the kit for absorbance detection at 450 nm and another detection after 30 min using a microplate reader. NAD+ and NADH contents were calculated based on their standard curve. The contents of NAD+ and NADH were expressed as nmol/104 cells or nmol/g tissues and were normalised against the levels in control group. 

## Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNAs were extracted from various HTR-8/SVneo cells, human villous tissues, and mouse placental tissues using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA); and RNA integrity number (RIN) were assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The concentration of RNA was in 500–1500 ng/μL and the RIN ≥9. The RNA purity was high as the A260/280 values were in 1.8–2.2. The isolated RNAs (800 ng) were converted into cDNAs using the first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen). Then, cDNAs were amplified using a 20-μL SYBR Green Supermix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The RT-qPCR programme was described as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, cycling reaction at 95 °C for 10 s for 40 cycles, dissolution at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and then 95 °C for 15 s. The sequences of the specific primers were shown in . The mRNA levels of GAPDH were not changed in our experiments and were used as normalisation internal standard for all mRNA detection. All experiments were replicated thrice. The levels of mRNAs were expressed as 2−ΔΔCt, where Ct was cycle threshold, ΔCt = testing gene (Ct)–average GAPDH (Ct), and ΔΔCt = sample group Δ(Ct)–average control group Δ(Ct).

## Western blot analysis
Total proteins were extracted from human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells, human villous tissues, and mouse placental tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce). Proteins (10–30 μg/well, equal amounts within group but different amounts among groups for better comparison) were separated on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred on an equilibrated polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, Amersham Biosciences, Buckinghamshire, UK). After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma–Aldrich) in TBST (10 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies contained anti-MFF (17090-1-AP, proteintech, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_2142463), anti-TRIM21 (67136-1-Ig, proteintech, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_2882435), anti-GLB1 (66586-1-Ig, proteintech, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_2881946), anti-p38 (80821-2-RR, proteintech, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_3670489), anti-p21 (ab109199, abcam, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_10861551), anti-p16 (ab189034, abcam, dilution 1:1000, RRID: RRID: AB_2737282), and anti-GAPDH (#5174, Cell Signalling Technology, dilution 1:1000, RRID: AB_10622025). The PVDF membrane was washed thrice with TBST and incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies included goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab205718, Abcam, dilution 1:10000, RRID: AB_2819160) and goat anti-mouse IgG (ab6789, Abcam, dilution 1:10000, RRID: AB_955439). Afterwards, PVDF membrane was washed thrice, and proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham Corporation, Arlington Heights, IL, USA). The intensity of each band was quantified by Image J. All experiments were replicated thrice. The value of each band density in experimental and control groups was normalised to that of its corresponding GAPDH band (loading control, ratio to GAPDH%).

## Protein stability assays
MFF protein stability was measured in cells treated with cycloheximide (CHX, Abcam, ab120093), an inhibitor of protein synthesis, to reflect the degradation of this protein. For CIP-exposed cells with co-treatment with CHX, cells were firstly treated with 40 μg/mL CIP for 48 h and then incubated with 10 μM CHX for 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 h. Afterwards, cells were harvested and the proteins in cell lysate supernatant were analysed by Western blotting. The protein band intensity was quantified by ImageJ. These experiments were independently performed thrice.

## Effect of CIP on protein degradation approach
Protein degradation assays were performed as the methods described previously. , Cells were treated with 10 μM MG132 (proteasome inhibitors) or 50 μM CQ (lysosomal blockers) for 6 h to suppress protein degradation. For CIP-exposed trophoblast cells with co-treatment with MG132 or CQ and CHX, cells were firstly treated with 40 μg/mL CIP for 48 h and then incubated in a fresh medium. Then, 10 μM CHX was added and incubated for 4 h, and subsequently 10 μM MG132 or 50 μM CQ was added and further incubated for another 6 h. Afterwards, the total proteins were extracted and the remained proteins were analysed by Western blotting. These experiments were independently performed thrice.

## Co-Immunoprecipitation assay
Trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells (1 × 107) was lysed with IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice. The villous or placental tissues (approximately 0.2 g) were mixed with IP lysis buffer and milled in a mill. Proteins in cell lysate were quantified using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit. The lysates were incubated with MFF antibody (17090-1-AP, proteintech, dilution 1:200, RRID: AB_2142463) or TRIM21 antibody (67136-1-Ig, proteintech, dilution 1:200, RRID: AB_2882435) at 4 °C overnight, with equal weight of IgG antibody (ab172730, Abcam, dilution 1:200, RRID: AB_2687931) as negative control, and then incubated with Protein A/G magnetic beads (HY–K0202, MedChemExpress) for another 6 h to form bead-immunoprecipitate complex. Subsequently, beads were washed five times with IP washing buffer and the immunoprecipitated proteins were harvested by boiling in 1 × SDS loading buffer at 98 °C for 10 min for subsequent Western blot analysis.

## Ubiquitination analysis
Cells and tissues lysates (1 × 107 cells in 0.1 mL IP lysis buffer or 0.2 g tissues in 1 mL IP lysis buffer) was incubated with anti-MFF (17090-1-AP, proteintech, dilution 1:200, RRID: AB_2142463) at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation, with equal weight of IgG antibody (ab172730, Abcam, dilution 1:200, RRID: AB_2687931) as negative control, and then incubated with 40 μL Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (B23201, MCE, USA) at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. Afterwards, the beads were separated and thorough washed with IP lysis buffer, and then mixed with SDS loading buffer and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and the ubiquitin-modified MFF was detected by Western blotting using anti-Ubiquitin (dilution 1:5000, ab179434, Abcam).

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were replicated thrice independently with similar results. The measured or calculated data (including control group) were presented as mean ± SD. SPSS26.0 or R software was used to analyse the data, and GraphPad Prism 8.0 as well as R software was used for figure generation. In cellular assays, the number n = 3 indicated that all experiments were replicated thrice independently. Data in other groups were normalised against that in the control group. In animal model, the number n = 6 indicated 6 mice in each group. In villous tissue assays, n = 50 indicated 50 pair of HC and UM women tissue or urine samples. Levene's test was used to evaluate the homogeneity of variance of the data. Student's t-test was used to compare differences between two groups. ANOVA and Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) test were used to compare differences among multiple groups. The qualitative data were compared using the Chi-square test. The correlation analysis of the relative expression levels was performed using Pearson analysis. The odds ratio (OR) of each variable and the risk of unexplained miscarriage and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) before adjusting potential confounders were analysed by univariate logistic regression analysis. The OR and 95% CI after adjusting potential confounders were analysed by multivariate logistic regression analysis. The data about the association among key molecules, cell phenotypes, and human diseases were obtained from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org). The alluvial plot was prepared using the online OmicShare tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools). When p < 0.05, the difference was considered to be statistically significant.

## Results

## Section I CIP exposure induced miscarriage

## An UM case–control study showed that CIP exposure was associated with women unexplained miscarriage
To explore the potential correlation between CIP exposure and unexplained miscarriage (UM), we collected urine samples from patients with UM (n = 50) and healthy control (HC) group who had personally elective miscarriage to terminate their unwanted pregnancies (n = 50) ( A). The known causes of miscarriage have been excluded, as the similar methods described previously. , To consider the effects of potential confounders, some variables were collected from medical records based on prior studies and their potential associations with miscarriage, , such as baseline characteristic, clinical information, and lifestyle ( ). These variables did not show statistically significant differences between two groups ( ). 
It has been reported that urinary CIP levels could well reflect the whole internal exposure levels of CIP in human body. , Relative to blood samples, collection of urine samples is relatively simple, non-invasive, and easy to obtain a large number of samples. Therefore, we detected CIP levels in urine samples by HPLC-MS/MS. The average level of CIP in urine samples was 0.277 ng/mL in UM group and 0.093 ng/mL in HC group ( B, ). CIP levels were significantly higher in UM vs HC groups ( B). In unadjusted model, univariate logistic regression analysis showed that higher CIP levels in urine samples (OR = 27.235, 95% CI of 2.942–252.158) were positively associated with miscarriage ( C, ). Based on directed acyclic graph analysis and previous literature, , the variables such as age, BMI, education, household income, smoking, and drinking were considered as potential confounders for miscarriage ( ). To consider their effects, multivariate logistic regression analysis by adjusting for all these confounders showed that CIP levels were still associated with miscarriage (the adjusted OR = 134.41, 95% CI of 4.78–3773.07, C, ). To further examine whether the estimated association differed among subpopulations, stratification analysis showed that the stratifying factors did not significantly alter the association between CIP levels and miscarriage (all P for interaction >0.05, D, ), confirming the robustness of these results. Collectively, all the statistical analysis confirmed that CIP exposure was positively associated with unexplained miscarriage.

## Mouse model assays confirmed that CIP exposure induced mouse miscarriage
Subsequently, we explored the causation whether CIP exposure might induce miscarriage. To this end, we constructed a CIP-exposed pregnant mouse model by orally giving pregnant mice with 0, 0.45, 12.5, 160, or 320 mg/kg/d CIP for continuous 13 days ( E). As validation, exposure to ≥12.5 mg/kg/d CIP reduced the increase in mouse body weight gain ( F–G, ), especially the uterus part containing placenta and embryos ( H, ), increased embryo resorption ( I), and elevated mouse miscarriage rates ( J) in CIP-exposed mice, indicating that exposure to higher levels of CIP during pregnancy could induce mouse miscarriage.

## Section II CIP exposure induced miscarriage by promoting senescence

## Transcriptomic and proteomic analysis indicated that senescence might be involved in CIP exposure-induced miscarriage
In order to explore which kind of phenotype might be involved in CIP exposure-induced miscarriage, we performed high-throughput transcriptomic sequencing to select the top altered cell phenotype using human trophoblast cells, human villous tissues, and mouse placental tissues. Their comparability was described in the Method section. Firstly, two pairs of random HC and UM villous tissues were used for transcriptomic sequencing ( ). GO and KEGG analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that cellular senescence-related GO items (cell ageing, ageing, cellular senescence, and inflammatory response) and KEGG pathways (cellular senescence) were significantly enriched ( ). Secondly, 160 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues, with unexposed tissues as control, were also used for transcriptomic sequencing ( ). The selection of this dose was explained in the Method section. GO and KEGG analysis of these DEGs revealed that cellular senescence-related GO items (cell ageing) and KEGG pathways (cellular senescence) were also significantly enriched ( ). Thirdly, human trophoblast cells in placental villous tissues play crucial roles in woman healthy pregnancy; and HTR-8/SVneo cells have been widely used as trophoblast cell model in various miscarriage studies. , , HTR-8/SVneo cells treated with 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP were used for transcriptomic sequencing ( ). The selection of this dose was also explained in the Method section. GO and KEGG analysis of these DEGs revealed that CIP exposure altered cellular senescence-related GO items (cell ageing and cellular senescence) and KEGG pathways (cellular senescence) ( ). Therefore, these transcriptomic sequencing analysis indicated that senescence might be involved in CIP exposure-induced miscarriage.
In addition to transcriptomic sequencing, these villous tissues, mouse placental tissues, and HTR-8/SVneo cells were also used for proteomic analysis. GO and KEGG analysis of the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in UM vs HC villous tissues ( ) revealed that cellular senescence-related GO items (cell ageing) and KEGG pathways (MAPK signalling pathway) were significantly enriched ( ). GO and KEGG analysis of the DEPs in 160 vs 0 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( ) revealed that cellular senescence-related GO items (ageing and inflammatory response) and KEGG pathways (Wnt signalling pathway) were also significantly enriched ( ). Meanwhile, GO and KEGG analysis of the DEPs in 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP-exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells ( ) also revealed that cellular senescence-related GO items (ageing) and KEGG pathways (oxidative phosphorylation) were altered ( ). Therefore, these proteomic analysis also indicated that senescence might be involved in CIP exposure-induced miscarriage.
GSEA analysis showed that CIP exposure regulated cellular senescence in transcriptomic and proteomic analysis of UM vs HC villous tissues, 160 vs 0 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues, and 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP-exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells ( ). Heatmap profile further showed that p16, p21, and p38 were up-regulated in UM vs HC villous tissues, 160 vs 0 mg/kg/d CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues, and 40 vs 0 μg/mL CIP-exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells ( ). Collectively, both transcriptomic and proteomic analysis indicated that CIP exposure might alter senescence and induce miscarriage.

## Assays with trophoblast cells, human villous tissues, and mouse models confirmed that CIP exposure caused senescence to induce miscarriage
To experimentally confirm this, firstly, in CIP-exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells, CIP exposure suppressed cell viability ( ) and increased the levels of SA-β-Galactose staining positive cells ( A–B). Meanwhile, CIP exposure also up-regulated the protein levels of cellular senescence-associated proteins (p16, p21, p38, and β-gal) in CIP-exposed HTR-8/SVneo cells ( C, ). These data supported that CIP exposure caused trophoblast cell senescence. 
Secondly, in human villous tissues, the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( D–E) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal were all higher in UM vs HC villous tissues ( F, ), indicating higher levels of senescence in UM vs HC villous tissues. Logistic regression analysis showed that higher protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal were associated with miscarriage ( , ), indicating that higher levels of senescence in villous tissues were associated with unexplained miscarriage. Moreover, Pearson correlation analysis also showed that CIP levels in urine were positive correlated with the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells, p16, p21, p38, or β-gal proteins in UM villous tissues ( , ). The datapoints in UM and HC groups were relatively separated. Collectively, these results indicated that CIP exposure, villous tissue senescence, and unexplained miscarriage were positively associated.
Thirdly, in mouse placental tissues, the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( G and H) and the protein levels of murine p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( I and J) were all higher in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues. The amino acid sequences of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal were all conserved in human, mouse, monkey, pig, and bovine ( ). Therefore, these results confirmed that CIP exposure caused mouse placental senescence and also induced mouse miscarriage. To further validate the roles of senescence in CIP-induced mouse miscarriage, we constructed a mouse miscarriage intervention model in which CIP-exposed pregnant mice were orally given with 5 mg/kg/3 d D and 50 mg/kg/3 d Q (D + Q) ( A). D + Q was a typical senescence suppressor, , which has been widely used in various cell and animal assays. , D + Q treatment greatly reduced embryo resorption and miscarriage rates in CIP-exposed mice ( B and C). This treatment also efficiently recovered (i.e. reduced) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( D and E) and the protein level of murine p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( F and G) in mouse placental tissues. Taken together, these results confirmed that the suppression in mouse placental senescence could efficiently reduce mouse miscarriage in this CIP-exposed mouse model. In another word, CIP exposure caused mouse placental senescence to induce mouse miscarriage. 
Collectively, based on CIP-exposed trophoblast cell assays, women HC and UM villous tissue assays, and two CIP-exposed mouse models, we confirmed that CIP exposure caused senescence to induce miscarriage.

## Section III CIP exposure caused senescence by down-regulating MFF

## CIP down-regulated MFF to cause senescence in human trophoblast cells
Subsequently, we explored which protein might play essential roles in CIP exposure-caused trophoblast cell senescence. In the intersection of three proteomic datasets as shown in , we identified that MFF was the top significantly down-regulated protein ( A). MFF has been known as an important mitochondrial fission protein for mitochondrial division and functions. , However, whether it might be involved in CIP exposure-caused trophoblast cell senescence was completely unknown. To explore its functions, firstly, we detected its mRNA and protein levels in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells. CIP exposure did not alter the mRNA levels of MFF but reduced its protein levels ( B and C), agreed with the significant difference in proteomic datasets but not in transcriptomic datasets. We also studied other mitochondrial proteins, such as MFN2, FIS1, and DRP1. , We found that CIP exposure did not obviously alter their protein levels in human trophoblast cells ( ). Subsequently, overexpression of MFF ( ) promoted cell viability ( ); whereas knockdown of MFF ( ) suppressed cell viability ( ). Meanwhile, overexpression of MFF down-regulated, whereas knockdown of MFF up-regulated, the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( D and E) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal in trophoblast cells ( F and G). Therefore, these results showed that CIP down-regulated MFF expression levels and MFF itself could effectively suppress trophoblast cell senescence. Furthermore, co-overexpression of MFF in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells restored (i.e. increased) cell viability ( H) and (i.e. decreased) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( I and J) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( K and L), showing that co-overexpression of MFF could reduce CIP exposure-caused trophoblast cell senescence. Collectively, these results supported that MFF could suppress trophoblast cell senescence; and CIP exposure down-regulated MFF protein levels and thus caused trophoblast cell senescence. 
It has been known that MFF serves as a pivotal regulatory molecule to safeguard mitochondrial functions. , Meanwhile, mitochondrial dysfunction has been firmly established to be associated with senescence. , , However, whether CIP and MFF might jointly regulate trophoblast cell mitochondrial functions was still unknown. CIP exposure reduced the copy number of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) ( A), NAD+/NADH ratios in total trophoblast cells ( B) or in mitochondria ( ), and the levels of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) ( C and D), and increased the levels ROS in total cells ( E and F) or in mitochondria ( G and H), showing that CIP exposure resulted in mitochondrial dysfunctions. However, co-overexpression of MFF reversed all these changes and rescued mitochondrial functions in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells ( A–H, ). Therefore, these results supported that MFF could well maintain mitochondrial functions; and CIP exposure down-regulated MFF protein levels and thus caused mitochondrial dysfunctions, which might further induce trophoblast cell senescence. 

## CIP exposure, lower MFF protein levels, mitochondrial dysfunctions, and villous tissue senescence were associated with woman unexplained miscarriage
Secondly, in human villous tissues, the protein levels of MFF were significantly lower in UM vs HC villous tissues ( A and B). However, its mRNA levels were almost unchanged in both groups ( C). Meanwhile, the copy number of mtDNA ( D), NAD+/NADH ratios in total cell lysates or in mitochondria ( E and F), and MMP levels ( G) were lower in UM vs HC villous tissues. Therefore, MFF-maintained mitochondrial functions were disrupted in UM vs HC villous tissues. Logistic regression analysis showed that the lower levels of MFF protein, copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios in total lysates or in mitochondria, and MMP were also associated with miscarriage ( H, ). Pearson correlation analysis further showed that the levels of CIP in urine were negatively and linearly correlated with the levels of MFF protein levels, copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios, and MMP in UM villous tissues ( I–M, ). Meanwhile, the levels of MFF protein were also positively and linearly correlated with the levels of copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios, and MMP ( N–Q) in UM villous tissues. Furthermore, the levels of MFF protein were negatively and linearly correlated with the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( ) in UM villous tissues. All the datapoints in UM and HC groups were relatively separated. Taken together, these results confirmed that CIP exposure, lower protein levels of MFF, mitochondrial dysfunctions, senescence, and unexplained miscarriage were closely associated. 

## CIP exposure down-regulated Mff protein levels to cause placental mitochondrial dysfunctions and senescence and thus induce mouse miscarriage in CIP-exposed mouse model
Thirdly, in mouse model, the protein levels of murine Mff were lower in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( A and B). However, its mRNA levels were almost unchanged ( C). The amino acid sequence of MFF was conserved in human, mouse, monkey, pig, and bovine ( ). To further dig the roles of Mff in CIP-caused senescence and miscarriage, we constructed another mouse intervention model, in which CIP-exposed mice were intraperitoneally injected with Mff overexpression plasmid, with empty vector as control ( D). As validation, overexpression of Mff could increase Mff mRNA and protein levels in mouse trophoblast cells ( ). Overexpression of Mff restored the weight gain of CIP-exposed pregnant mice during pregnancy from D1 to D14 ( ) and reduced embryo resorption and alleviated mouse miscarriage in CIP-exposed mice ( E and F). Overexpression of Mff also restored (i.e. decreased) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( G and H), (i.e. increased) the protein levels of Mff, and (i.e. reduced) the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( I and J) in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues. Moreover, overexpression of Mff also restored (i.e. increased) the levels of copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios in tissue lysates or in mitochondria, and MMP in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( K–M, ). Taken together, these results indicated that overexpression of Mff could recover mitochondrial functions, reduce placental senescence, and alleviate mouse miscarriage in CIP-exposed mice. In other words, CIP exposure down-regulated Mff protein levels and thus caused mitochondrial dysfunctions and placental senescence, which further induced mouse miscarriage. 

## Section IV CIP promoted TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF

## CIP promoted TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF to cause senescence in human trophoblast cells
Since CIP exposure down-regulated the protein but not mRNA levels of MFF, subsequently, we explored its underlying mechanism. Protein stability assays revealed that CIP exposure promoted the degradation of MFF protein ( A and B). In general, proteins are degraded through ubiquitination-proteasome pathway or autophagy-lysosome pathway, which could be specifically blocked by proteasome inhibitor MG132 or lysosomal inhibitor CQ, respectively. , In CIP-exposed trophoblast cells, treatment with MG132, but not with CQ, significantly increased the accumulation of MFF protein ( C). Meanwhile, IP assays using identical and excessive MFF antibody showed that CIP exposure also increased the levels of ubiquitinated MFF (MFF-Ub), an intermediate for ubiquitination degradation of MFF ( D and E). The results indicated that CIP exposure promoted the ubiquitination degradation of MFF protein. 
Since the E3 ubiquitin ligase of MFF was still unknown, subsequently, we tried to identify its E3 ubiquitin ligases. MFF IP-MS assays showed that an E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 was top interacted with MFF ( F, , ). In the intersection of three proteomic datasets in , TRIM21 protein levels were also highly expressed in all three datasets ( A). Therefore, we selected TRIM21 and explored its ligase functions in the following studies. IP analysis confirmed that TRIM21 and MFF proteins bound with each other ( S8B and C). IP assays using identical and excessive MFF antibody showed that overexpression of TRIM21 ( ) up-regulated MFF-Ub protein levels and reduced MFF protein levels in cell lysates ( G–I); whereas knockdown of TRIM21 gave the opposite results. To further identify the potential ligase active site of TRIM21, Cys-16 might be the active site of TRIM21, as analysed by Uniprot. Therefore, we mutate Cys-16 to Ala and constructed mutant TRIM21 (TRIM21-mt). For easier detection, we added His tag to MFF to construct His-MFF and added Flag tag to wild-type or mutant TRIM21 to construct Flag-TRIM21-wt or Flag-TRIM21-mt. IP assays using identical but excessive His antibody (anti-His-MFF) showed that, compared with Flag-TRIM21-wt, overexpression of Flag-TRIM21-mt reduced the protein levels of MFF-Ub and increased that of MFF in cell lysates ( ). As control, overexpression of both Flag-TRIM21-mt and Flag-TRIM21-wt gave similar protein amounts in cell lysates ( ). However, His-MFF pulled down similar protein levels of Flag-TRIM21-mt and Flag-TRIM21-wt ( ). These results showed that the mutation of the active site (Cys-16) of TRIM21 reduced the ubiquitination of MFF but did not affect its binding with MFF. Therefore, these results confirmed that TRIM21 was an E3 ubiquitin ligase of MFF and promoted its ubiquitination degradation.
Next, we investigated the joint roles of CIP and TRIM21 in MFF ubiquitination degradation. Firstly, we explored whether CIP might regulate TRIM21 expression levels. We found that CIP exposure up-regulated the mRNA and protein levels of TRIM21 ( A and B) and promoted the formation of TRIM21 and MFF complexes ( C and D). Moreover, co-transfection in IP assays using identical and excessive MFF antibody showed that co-knockdown of TRIM21 restored (i.e. decreased) the protein levels of MFF-Ub and (i.e. increased) the protein levels of MFF in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells ( E–I). Collectively, these results suggested that CIP exposure up-regulated TRIM21 protein levels, promoted TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF, and thus down-regulated the protein levels of MFF in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells. 
Subsequently, we further explored whether TRIM21 might be involved in CIP-caused trophoblast cell mitochondrial dysfunctions and senescence. Overexpression of TRIM21 suppressed, whereas knockdown of TRIM21 promoted, trophoblast cell viability ( ). Overexpression of TRIM21 up-regulated, whereas knockdown of TRIM21 down-regulated, the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( ) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( ) in trophoblast cells. Furthermore, knockout of TRIM21 did not affect the protein levels of other mitochondrial proteins, such as MFN2, FIS1, and DRP1 ( ). Co-transfection assays also showed that co-knockdown of TRIM21 restored (i.e. increased) cell viability ( A) and (i.e. reduced) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( B and C) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( D and E) in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells. Moreover, co-knockdown of TRIM21 also restored (i.e. increased) the levels of copy number of mtDNA ( F), NAD+/NADH levels in whole cells or in mitochondria ( G and H), and MMP ( I, ), and (i.e. reduced) the levels of ROS in whole cells ( J and K) or in mitochondria ( L, ) in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells. These results showed that CIP exposure up-regulated TRIM21 protein levels and thus resulted in mitochondrial dysfunctions and senescence in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells. 

## Verification of TRIM21/MFF in human villous tissues
Then, we studied TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF in human villous tissues. The mRNA and protein levels of TRIM21 were higher in UM vs HC villous tissues ( ). IP assays using identical but excessive MFF antibody showed that the protein levels of TRIM21 pulled down by MFF and MFF-Ub were higher and the protein levels of MFF in cell lysates were lower in UM vs HC villous tissues ( C–G). Logistic regression analysis showed that higher protein levels of TRIM21 and MFF-Ub were associated with miscarriage ( H, ). Moreover, Pearson correlation analysis showed that the levels of CIP in urine were positively associated with the protein levels of TRIM21 or MFF-Ub in UM villous tissues ( , ); the protein levels of TRIM21 and MFF-Ub were positively associated in UM group ( , ); and the protein levels of TRIM21 were negatively associated with the levels of the copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios in whole tissue lysates or in mitochondria, and MMP ( , ), and positively associated with the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( , ) in UM group. The datapoints in UM and HC groups were obviously separated. Taken together, these results confirmed that CIP exposure, the protein levels of TRIM21 and MFF-Ub in villous tissues, mitochondrial dysfunctions, senescence, and unexplained miscarriage were closely associated. 

## Verification of murine Trim21/Mff in CIP-exposed mouse model
Subsequently, we also validated the roles of Trim21 in this CIP-exposed mouse model. The amino acid sequence of TRIM21 was conserved in human, mouse, rhesus, dog, and elephant ( ). The mRNA and protein levels of murine Trim21 were higher in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( A–C). IP assays using identical and excessive Mff antibody showed that the protein levels of Trim21 and Mff-Ub pulled down by Mff were higher and the protein levels of Mff in lysates were lower in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( D, ). The protein levels of Trim21 and Mff-Ub were positively correlated in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues ( , ). To validate the roles of Trim21 in CIP-induced miscarriage, we constructed another miscarriage intervention model in which CIP-exposed pregnant mice were intraperitoneally injected with AS-Trim21 (antisense oligonucleotides of Trim21, E). As validation, treatment with AS-Trim21 reduced Trim21 mRNA and protein levels in mouse trophoblast cells ( ). Knockdown of Trim21 recovered (i.e. increased) the body weight gain of CIP-exposed pregnant mice from D1 to D14 ( ) and (i.e. reduced) embryo resorption and alleviated mouse miscarriage in this CIP-exposed mouse model ( F and G). IP assays using identical but excessive Mff antibody showed that knockdown of Trim21 restored (i.e. decreased) the protein levels of Trim21 and Mff-Ub pulled down by Mff, the protein levels of Trim21 in tissue lysates, and (i.e. increased) the protein levels of Mff in tissue lysates ( H, ). Knowdown of Trim21 also restored (i.e. decreased) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( I, ) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal ( J, ) in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues. Moreover, knockdown of Trim21 also restored (i.e. increased) the levels of the copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios, and MMP ( K–M). Collectively, these results indicated that CIP exposure up-regulated Trim21 protein levels, promoted Trim21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of Mff, caused placental mitochondrial dysfunctions and senescence, and thus induced mouse miscarriage in this CIP-exposed mouse model. 

## Section V miscarriage treatment
Having known the regulatory mechanisms, subsequently, we explored how to treat against CIP exposure-induced miscarriage. Till now, no clinical agonist of MFF is available. Fimepinostat and Quisinostat (F + Q) has been reported as a clinical inhibitor of TRIM21 and have been widely used in various cellular and animal assays. To validate its functions, we found that treatment with F + Q down-regulated Trim21 protein levels in murine trophoblast cells ( ). Then, we constructed a mouse miscarriage treatment model in which CIP-exposed pregnant mice were orally given with F and intraperitoneally injected with Q ( A). In this model, treatment with F + Q down-regulated Trim21 protein levels and up-regulated Mff protein levels in mouse placental tissues ( B–D), restored the body weight gain of CIP-exposed pregnant mice from D1 to D14 ( ), decreased embryo absorption ( E), and reduced mouse miscarriage rates ( F). Moreover, IP assays using identical but limited Mff antibody showed that this treatments also restored (i.e. decreased) the protein levels of Trim21 and Mff-Ub pulled down by Mff, the protein levels of Trim21 in tissue lysates, but (i.e. increased) the protein levels of Mff in tissue lysates ( G, ). This treatment also restored (i.e. decreased) the levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells ( H, ) and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal (Fig. 13I, ) in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues. Moreover, this treatment also restored (i.e. increased) the levels of the copy number of mtDNA, NAD+/NADH ratios, and MMP in placental tissues ( J–L). Collectively, these results indicated that therapeutic down-regulation of Trim21 could efficiently recover mitochondrial functions and suppress placental senescence, and reduce mouse miscarriage, providing an important biotarget for treatment against unexplained miscarriage. 

## Discussion

## CIP exposure causes senescence to induce miscarriage
With increasing global consumption of CIP, environmental CIP pollution and human health problems are becoming a important concern worldwide in the environment and health field. Increasing epidemiological studies have discovered that CIP exposure during pregnancy might be associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as major foetal malformations. However, whether and how pregnant CIP exposure might induce unexplained miscarriage remain completely unknown. In this study, we find that urine CIP levels are significantly higher in UM vs HC women and higher urinary CIP levels are positively associated with higher levels of senescence in villous tissues and unexplained miscarriage. To study the dose–response relationship between CIP concentrations and miscarriage risk, CIP concentrations were divided into four regions Q1-Q4 (Q1 was ≤ P50, Q2 was P50–P75, Q3 was P75–P90, and Q4 was ≥ P90 of the reference group). Logistic regression analysis showed that odds ratio (OR) in Q4 was higher than that in Q3 ( ), implying that CIP concentrations might be dose-dependently associated with miscarriage risk. The similar calculation is also used in a study about Bisphenol A analogues and serum hormone levels among reproductive-aged Chinese men. Furthermore, in mouse model, we confirm that CIP exposure induces mouse miscarriage by promoting placental senescence; and reduction in placental senescence by treating with D + Q could efficiently reduce miscarriage rates of CIP-exposed mice. Moreover, cellular assays further discover that CIP exposure causes trophoblast cell senescence. Collectively, these data comprehensively indicate that CIP exposure causes senescence to induce miscarriage based on the UM case–control study, mouse model assays, and cellular mechanistic study, discovering a new risk factor for unexplained miscarriage and also revealing pathogenesis and biological mechanisms of unexplained miscarriage. In our recent studies, we also find that BaP/BPDE exposure inhibits trophoblast cell migration/invasion, suppresses homologous recombination repair, or induces pyroptosis, any of which might induce unexplained miscarriage. , , , , Nanoplastics inhibit trophoblast cell migration/invasion and migrasome formation or lead to trophoblast cell apoptosis, which further induce miscarriage. , Copper exposure leads to mouse placental cuproptosis and causes miscarriage. Lithium exposure causes placental tissue ferroptosis and cuproptosis to induce miscarriage. , , Bisphenol B and F suppress trophoblast cell migration/invasion and thus induce miscarriage. , Hypoxia causes trophoblast cell ferroptosis to induce miscarriage. All these studies enrich the database of the pathogenesis and biological mechanisms of unexplained miscarriage caused by various environment toxicants.

## A pathway for CIP-induced trophoblast cell senescence
MFF has been known as a major mitochondrial fission protein and plays an essential role in mitochondrial division and various functions. Subsequently, mitochondrial dysfunction might further cause cell senescence. , , However, whether MFF might directly cause cell senescence has never been explored. In this study, we find that CIP exposure causes trophoblast cell senescence by promoting TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination modification of MFF. The regulatory mechanisms are proposed ( M). CIP exposure up-regulates the expression levels of TRIM21, which is identified as a E3 ligase of MFF, promotes TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF, and thus down-regulates MFF protein levels. Subsequently, the down-regulated MFF causes various mitochondrial dysfunctions, such as the decreased levels of the copy number of mtDNA and NAD+/NADH ratio in cells or in mitochondria and the increased the levels of ROS in cells or in mitochondria, which further result in cellular senescence, including the elevated levels of SA-β-Galactose positive cells and the protein levels of p16, p21, p38, and β-gal. Subsequently, trophoblast cell senescence further induces miscarriage. Our bioinformatic results did not discover other E3 ubiquitin ligases. It has been reported that MDM2 and Parkin are typical E3 ubiquitin ligases in neural cells. , However, PCR assays showed that CIP exposure did not alter their mRNA levels in human trophoblast cells ( ). Therefore, our data confirm that CIP exposure up-regulated TRIM21 expression levels and thus down-regulated MFF. The cellular mechanisms are consistent to those in UM human villous tissues and in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues. Reduction in placental senescence, supplement with murine Mff, knockdown of murine Trim21 could effectively reduce mouse placental senescence and reduced miscarriage rates of CIP-exposed mice. Therefore, this study provides pathways and biological mechanisms by which CIP exposure causes senescence to induce miscarriage. In addition to TRIM21/MFF pathway, we also detected other pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, inflammation) that might also contribute to cell senescence, such as mTOR, CCL2, IL-18, and TNF-α. , , GO and KEGG analysis showed that oxidative phosphorylation and inflammation were significantly enriched ( , ). Western blotting confirmed that CIP exposure up-regulated the protein levels of mTOR, CCL2, IL-18, and TNF-α in human trophoblast cells ( ). Therefore, CIP exposure also caused senescence possibly through other signalling pathways, which should also be further explored.

## Miscarriage intervention and treatment
Recently, increasing studies have uncovered the molecular mechanisms of unexplained miscarriage. , , , , , , , Fructose-1,6-diphosphate has been reported to prevent miscarriage by inducing COX-2 macrophage differentiation in the decidua. Also, increasing succinate levels intravenously during the first trimester reduces the risk of recurrent spontaneous miscarriage. In our recent studies, we have found that supplement with GPX4 could suppress ferroptosis, recover angiogenesis, and alleviate miscarriage in BaP-induced mouse model. Knockdown of either murine lnc-hz06 or Ncoa4 could efficiently suppress ferroptosis and alleviate miscarriage in hypoxic mouse model ; knockdown of murine Ahr efficiently recovers homologous recombination repair in placental tissues and alleviates miscarriage in a BaP-exposed mouse miscarriage model ; knockdown of Nlrp3 could efficiently reduce placenta pyroptosis and alleviate BaP-induced mouse miscarriage ; supplementing with murine SOX2 or ROCK1 could efficiently rescue migration/invasion and migrasome formation and alleviate polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs)-induced miscarriage ; supplement with Bcl-2 could efficiently suppress apoptosis in PS-NPs-exposed trophoblast cells and reduce apoptosis and alleviate miscarriage in PS-NPs-exposed pregnant mouse model ; treatment with TTM (a cuproptosis inhibitor) suppresses placental cuproptosis and alleviates miscarriage in CuCl2-exposed mouse mode ; supplement with Mboat1 could efficiently suppress ferroptosis in LiCl-exposed trophoblast cells and suppresses placental ferroptosis and relieves miscarriage in LiCl-exposed mouse mode ; and supplement with Pkca or Tspan4 could efficiently rescue migration/invasion and migrasome formation and recover placental migration/invasion and migrasome formation to alleviate Bisphenol B-induced miscarriage In this study, we find that supplement with D + Q or Mff or knockdown of Trim21 could inhibit placental senescence and alleviate mouse miscarriage in CIP-exposed mouse model, providing new targets for treatment against unexplained miscarriage. Collectively, all these studies enrich the biotargets and approaches for treatment against specific environment toxicant-induced unexplained miscarriage.

## Limitations and prospects
To reduce environmental antibiotic contamination is an important topic in the environmental-health field and should be addressed jointly by global governments and enterprises. The transport pathway and organ enrichment of antibiotic exposure in human remain unclear and need to be explored urgently. CIP can be metabolised into desethylene ciprofloxacin, sulfociprofloxacin, and oxociprofloxacin in human body. The antimicrobial activity of all three CIP metabolites is less than that of CIP. Moreover, the serum concentrations of its metabolites are less than 10% of CIP levels. Therefore, we directly detected the urinary levels of CIP and studied the toxic effects of CIP instead of its other metabolites in this study. Nonetheless, CIP metabolites might also potentially produce some unknown toxic effects, which should also be further explored. It has been known that CIP can cross human placental barrier, as confirmed by human placental perfusion studies and clinical detection in cord blood. , , , CIP could cross placental barrier and might reduce embryo development, which deserves to be further investigated.
In this study, we focus on senescence phenotype. In addition to this, bioinformatic analysis also shows that other cell phenotypes might also be altered. Therefore, CIP exposure might lead to other dysfunctions of human trophoblast cells to induce miscarriage. Moreover, the alluvial plot shows that CIP, TRIM21, and MFF are associated with various cell phenotypes ( ), such as cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, pyroptosis, protein transport, and are also associated with many human diseases, such as necrosis, weight loss, kidney diseases, inflammation, and hepatomegaly ( ). , The association and causation among chemical-gene-phenotype-disease should be widely investigated. Furthermore, to detect CIP levels or other antibiotic levels in urine might be concerned in annual routine physical examination. It is the best way to directly detect CIP levels in placental villous tissues to obtain an actual CIP abundance. Further study should be performed to distinguish the functions of mouse various placental tissues (including decidua, spongiotrophoblast, and labyrinth) by single-cell sequencing together with physical dissection of every tissue from mouse placenta. Moreover, whether CIP-induced senescence might contribute to chromosomal abnormalities or immune dysregulations (which belong to clinical causes of miscarriage) was completely unknown, was a very exciting research direction, and should be further explored.
Although the doses of CIP used in mouse and cell assays are higher than real environmental exposure doses, frankly, it could not completely exclude the possibility that women might exposure to excessive CIP in areas with high CIP pollution, with CIP occupational exposure or occupational poisoning, or with possible long-term cumulative exposure. To some extent, the short-term and high-dose CIP-exposed animal and cell models might reflect or predict the long-term low-dose effects of CIP exposure on miscarriage in human cohort, which should be further investigated. Moreover, to discover new signalling biotargets and mechanisms is of great significance for human risk assessment and miscarriage therapy at environmentally relevant doses, especially in susceptible populations or under cumulative exposure conditions. In general, environmental toxicants might have their corresponding receptors. For example, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, bisphenol analogues, and dioxins , might activate their common receptor AhR. AhR is typical transcription factor and activates the transcription of a group of target genes to perform down-stream signalling and functions. To identify the common receptors in various toxicant-induced miscarriage is also an important topic. Finally, the intervention of unexplained miscarriage should be further explored for better clinical application. 
Although the case–control studies were well designed and key clinical covariates were adjusted, several limitations should still be addressed. First, the sample size is modest, which might limit the statistical power to identify more subtle associations. Second, certain lifestyle and environmental variables, such as detailed dietary habits and specific environmental exposures, should also be considered. Third, a larger and prospective cohorts with more comprehensive exposure assessment should be constructed to validate these results and to generalise the conclusions.
In summary, based on the case–control study, mouse models, and cellular assays, we conclude that CIP exposure causes senescence to induce miscarriage. Mechanistically, CIP exposure up-regulates TRIM21 expression levels, promotes TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination degradation of MFF, and thus down-regulates MFF protein levels. Subsequently, the down-regulated MFF causes mitochondrial dysfunctions and results in senescence, which further induces miscarriage. The mechanisms are consistent in CIP-exposed trophoblast cells, in CIP-exposed mouse placental tissues, and in UM villous tissues which contain higher levels of CIP than HC tissues. Supplement with murine Mff, knockdown of Trim21, or direct reduction of senescence effectively suppresses placental senescence and alleviates mouse miscarriage in CIP-exposed mouse model. This study not only discovers new healthy risk effects of CIP exposure on miscarriage, explores pathogenesis and biological mechanisms in CIP exposure-induced unexplained miscarriage, but also provides potential biological targets for treatment against unexplained miscarriage, all of which deserves critical attention by governments and enterprises.
