## Abstract

## Background
Gastric cancer (GC) is the main cause for cancer-related mortality in the world. Recent studies have confirmed that metabolic reprogramming plays a significant role in cancer progression. This study aims to further clarify the underlying mechanisms that the tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p exerts anti-gastric cancer effects via glucose metabolic reprogramming pathways.

## Methods
High-throughput sequencing combined with bioinformatics analysis predicted significantly differentially expressed miRNAs and target mRNAs after salidroside treatment. RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) was used to verify miRNA interaction with its downstream target protein, while co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was applied to confirm downstream target proteins interaction. The IC50 of salidroside was determined by in vitro CCK-8 assay and colony formation experiment. qRT-PCR, Western blot, ELISA, and ATP detection were used to evaluate cancer cell proliferation, miRNA-mRNA expression and metabolite changes. Cancer cells were transfection with miR-1343-3p mimics or inhibitors, OGDHL-targeted siRNA (si-OGDHL), to verify the effects of salidroside against cancers. In vivo tumor-bearing nude mouse models after salidroside or miR-1343-3p agomir treatments were applied to analyze the targeted miRNA-mRNA molecules expression and metabolite changes.

## Results
Bioinformatics confirmed that tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p down-regulated OGDHL expression, a key α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex subunit of TCA cycle. Our findings first verified that OGDHL interacted with PDHB, a key pyruvate dehydrogenase E1-β subunit in gastric cancer. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that salidroside inhibited GC growth in a time and dose-dependent way through up-regulating tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p, down-regulating OGDHL expression, destabilizing PDHB protein homeostasis, reducing pyruvate oxidative decarboxylation, and decreasing acetyl-CoA and ATP production.

## Conclusion
Tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p inhibited gastric cancer cell proliferation by regulating OGDHL/PDHB-pyruvate glucose metabolism axis, which lay a better basis for targeted therapeutic strategy in cancers.

## Introduction
Gastric Cancer (GC), as the common malignant tumor and the main cause for cancer-related mortality in the world, demonstrates an incidence of 25.41/100,000 and mortality of 18.44/100,000 in China [ , ]. Current therapeutic strategies showed limited efficacy for advanced GC, with a 35% 5-year survival rate due to frequent recurrence and metastasis [ ]. Recent studies revealed that cancer progression was accompanied by abnormal energy metabolism [ – ]. 
Glucose metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of malignant tumors, and abnormal glucose metabolism serves as a fundamental basis to maintain the malignant phenotypes of cancer cells. It has proved that the aerobic glycolysis pathway, Warburg effect, plays a vital role in tumor growth, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [ , ]. Recent studies have revealed that the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle can enhance aerobic glycolysis [ , ]. Tumor cells could coordinate TCA cycle with aerobic glycolysis to generate high levels of key metabolites, such as pyruvate and lactate, which subsequently recycle into TCA cycle and promote ATP production in cancer cells, fulfilling the biosynthetic demands for tumor growth, invasion, metastasis and progresstion [ ]. 
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous small non-coding RNAs which regulate the expression of mRNA molecules, either inhibiting mRNA translation or promoting its degradation, thereby functioning as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) [ , ]. Recent studies demonstrate that aberrant miRNA expression plays a pivotal role in GC development, proliferation, migration, and apoptosis [ ]. Given miRNAs’ central role in ceRNA network regulation, by high-throughput sequencing we found tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p as the most differentially expressed miRNA in GC after salidroside treatment [ ]. While miR-1343-3p has been documented as a tumor suppressor in GC, primarily through targeting oncogenic transcription factors like TEAD4 [ ], its potential role in directly regulating cellular energy metabolism remains entirely unexplored.
Our bioinformatic analysis of the miRNA-mRNA network specifically revealed that miR-1343-3p potentially targets key enzymes in mitochondrial energy metabolism: α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase-like (OGDHL) and pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit β (PDHB). This targeted prediction provides the direct rationale for focusing on these specific enzymes, as they represent crucial nodes connecting miRNA regulation to core energy metabolism. OGDHL, a key component of the OGDHc enzyme complex family, localizes in the mitochondrial matrix and participates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. It catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of α-ketoglutarate (αKG) to succinyl-CoA, maintaining TCA cycle homeostasis and cellular energy metabolism [ ]. PDHB, a key member of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHc), which participates in aerobic glycolysis and TCA cycle [ ]. It catalyzes pyruvate oxidative decarboxylation to generate acetyl-CoA for mitochondrial energy production, playing an indispensable role in glucose metabolism [ ]. Analysis of GC transcriptomic data reveals that both OGDHL and PDHB are up-regulated in GC tissues compared to normal controls. We therefore propose that targeting the miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB axis constitutes a novel therapeutic strategy, wherein salidroside employs this pathway to coordinately suppress both metabolic enzymes, thereby implementing a precise intervention in GC energy metabolism [ ]. 
This study aims to investigate the interactions among salidroside-induced miR-1343-3p, OGDHL and PDHB in GC progression through glucose metabolic reprogramming. It is the first report that salidroside up-regulates miR-1343-3p in GC cells, which correspondingly regulates OGDHL and PDHB expression. The study demonstrated that the over-expression of miR-1343-3p down-regulated OGDHL, destabilizing PDHB homeostasis and reducing its expression. This metabolic dysfunction led to substrate pyruvate accumulation, decreased acetyl-CoA and ATP synthesis, and disrupted energy production, ultimately inhibiting GC cell proliferation and progression (Fig.  ). By targeting the miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB enzyme complex-pyruvate-glucose metabolic axis, salidroside displayed a novel molecular mechanism suppressing GC proliferation. These findings provide deep insights into GC metabolic reprogramming and cancer progression, holding prospects for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies in cancer.
 

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
Human gastric cancer MGC-803 cells, obtained from Nanjing Bioryg Biotech Co., Ltd., were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with: 10% FBS (EVERY GREEN, China) and 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Hyclone, USA).
Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

## Salidroside solution preparation
Salidroside was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Salidroside was dissolved in RPMI-1640 medium. Fresh solutions were prepared at final concentrations of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 µmol/mL for subsequent experiments.

## CCK-8 assay for cell viability
Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 10³ cells/well and treated with varying final concentrations of salidroside for 24 and 48 h. Cell viability was subsequently assessed using a CCK-8 kit (BOSTER, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Colony formation assay
Cells were seeded at 300 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for several weeks until macroscopically visible colonies formed. Colonies were fixed, stained, and imaged using a digital camera. Quantitative analysis of stained tumor cell colonies was performed with ImageJ (v1.52a; W. Rasband, National Institutes of Health, USA; http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/).

## RNA isolation and quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from pre-treated GC cell groups using the AG RNAex Pro RNA Extraction Reagent (Accurate Biology, China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription of the total RNA following the manufacturer’s instructions for the reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green qPCR kit (SparkJade, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The PCR amplification procedure consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 10 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Data analysis was carried out employing the 2−ΔΔCt method [ ]. Primer sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech and are listed in Table .
 

## Western blot analysis
Cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates, treated with specified concentrations of salidroside, and incubated for 48 h in a 5% CO₂, 37 °C incubator. Proteins were extracted, blocked with 5% BSA blocking buffer (Solarbio, China) on a shaker for 2 h, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 18 h. Secondary antibodies (HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, Abcam, USA) were incubated at room temperature on a shaker for 2 h. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence gel imaging system (BIO-RAD, USA), and band intensities were analyzed via ImageJ software for gray value quantification. The antibodies to be used are as follows: Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, USA) (1:10,000 dilution); Anti-GAPDH antibody (Abcam, USA) (1:1,250 dilution); OGDHL polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, CHINA) (1:4,000 dilution); PDHB polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, CHINA) (1:3,500 dilution); Rabbit IgG (Beyotime, CHINA).

## Cell transfection
Log-phase cells were seeded at 10⁵ cells/well in 24-well plates and cultured in antibiotic-free RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) under 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. miR-1343-3p mimics, miR-1343-3p inhibitor, negative controls, OGDHL-targeting small interfering RNA (si-OGDHL), and negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesized by Biomics (Jiangsu, China), with sequences listed in Table  . Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## RNA binding protein Immunoprecipitation assay (RIP)
Cancer cell proteins were extracted according to the RIP kit protocol. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime, China) were fully conjugated with primary antibodies or rabbit IgG (Beyotime, China) and incubated at room temperature on a shaker for 4 h to isolate RNA bound to the target protein. The miR-1343-3p content in the RNA was detected by qRT-PCR.

## Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Cells were divided into IgG negative control, IP: OGDHL, and IP: PDHB groups. Cell proteins were extracted using a Co-IP kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After cultivated with the primary antibodies or rabbit IgG conjugated with Protein A/G magnetic beads at room temperature for 1 h, the cell proteins were co-immunoprecipitated. Western blot was performed to detect the interacting target proteins.

## In vivo tumorigenesis model
Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): control group, Salidroside-treated group, and miR-1343-3p agomir group. All mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10⁶ cells (150 µL cell suspension) into the axillary region. Based on preliminary findings, the Salidroside-treated group received intraperitoneal injections of Salidroside (100 mg/kg) every other day for 4 weeks, while the control group was administered PBS via the same route. For the miR-1343-3p agomir group, miR-1343-3p agomir (3 mg/kg, Biomics, China) was delivered into xenograft tumors through intratumoral multipoint injections twice weekly for 4 weeks. Tumor growth was monitored weekly, and tumor size was measured using calipers on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 post-treatment. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (length × width²)/2. On day 28, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation. The animals were placed in a euthanasia chamber prefilled with room air. Compressed CO₂ gas was then introduced at a flow rate displacing 30%–70% of the chamber volume per minute. After loss of consciousness, the gas flow was maintained for at least one minute, followed by an observation period of over 10 min within the chamber. Death was confirmed by the cessation of breathing and the loss of corneal reflexes, and tumor tissues were isolated and weighed for further analysis.

## Assessment of pyruvate, acetyl-CoA (A-CoA), and ATP levels
The levels of pyruvate and acetyl-CoA was measured according to the ELISA kit instructions (MEIMIAN, China), and the optical density (OD) values in samples were calculated under ELISA microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). To estimate the relative ATP concentration, cell lysates were extracted using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China), and the RLU values were determined with a microplate reader (Biotek, USA).

## miRNA-mRNA database construction and RNA sequencing analysis
Six samples of MGC-803 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were randomly assigned into two groups using a random number table: a control group and a drug-treated group, with three replicates per group. The dosage and duration of Salidroside treatment in the drug group were determined based on IC50 evaluation. Total RNA was extracted from the samples, and fragments of interest were excised via agarose gel electrophoresis. The RNA was then subjected to reverse transcription and PCR amplification. Following agarose gel electrophoresis, RNA bands were recovered and purified to complete library construction. The constructed libraries underwent quality control using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and qPCR. Subsequently, the samples were analyzed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform employing a combination of different transcriptome sequencing strategies to detect both miRNA and mRNA, yielding RNA expression levels and enabling the identification of differentially expressed RNAs [ ]. 

## Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis
The target mRNAs interacting with miR-1343-3p in gastric cancer were screened and analyzed using the TarBase v9.0 database (https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/tarbasev9), TargetScanHuman v8.0 database (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/), miRwalk v2.0 database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/), and GeneCards Version 5.23 database (https://www.genecards.org/). Protein-protein interactions were predicted using the STRING version 12.0 database (https://cn.string-db.org/), and the results were visualized with Cytoscape v3.10.3 (https://cytoscape.org/). The correlations among target gene proteins were evaluated via the GeneMANIA v3.6.0 database (https://genemania.org/).

## Statistical analysis
All experiments in this study were independently repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analyses and visualization were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9 software (https://www.graphpad.com/) and R v4.2.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Differential expression analysis of miRNAs was performed using the edgeR software, with screening criteria set at a fold change > 2 and a P < 0.05. For mRNA differential expression analysis between the two comparison groups, the DESeq2 software was employed (edgeR was used for two-sample comparisons). Based on the differential analysis results, mRNAs with an FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were defined as significantly differentially expressed mRNAs.

## Results

## Salidroside inhibiting GC cell poliferation via the miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB axis-mediated glucose metabolic reprogramming
High-throughput sequencing analysis of GC cells before and after Salidroside treatment revealed 44 differentially expressed miRNAs and their corresponding 1,384 target mRNAs (Fig.  A). Further integrated analysis combining bioinformatics approaches with miRNA-mRNA target prediction confirmed that the key TCA cycle enzyme OGDHL was downregulated and closely associated with the negative feedback regulation of miR-1343-3p (Fig.  B). Simultaneously, we analyzed the miRNA-OGDHL interaction network, which revealed a significant correlation between miR-1343-3p and OGDHL (Fig.  C). Further Pearson correlation analysis and gene sequence comparison analysis demonstrated a significant negative correlation between OGDHL and miR-1343-3p in GC cells (Fig.  D). RIP confirmed the interaction between miR-1343-3p and OGDHL (Fig.  E). By further analyzing the interactions among the target mRNAs, we screened for 398 target mRNAs related with miR-1343-3p in GC cells (Fig.  A). Based on the STRING database analysis of target protein interactions, we identified PDHB, a glycolysis enzyme protein closely associated with OGDHL (Fig.  A). Additionally, the Geneminia database also verified the association between OGDHL and PDHB (Fig.  A). Co-IP experiments further confirmed the interaction between OGDHL and PDHB (Fig.  B). Combining the TCGA database, we determined the differential gene expression of OGDHL and PDHB between GC and normal gastric tissues, indicating high expression of OGDHL and PDHB in GC tissues (Fig.  C). Furthermore, based on the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), we analyzed the correlation between the expressions of OGDHL and PDHB and the clinical outcomes of GC patients by Kaplan-Meier Plotter, and found that patients with high expression of OGDHL and PDHB had significantly shortened survival period (Fig.  D). These findings suggest that salidroside likely inhibits the proliferation of GC cells by up-regulating miR-1343-3p to down-regulate the OGDHL/PDHB enzyme complex, thereby interfering with glucose metabolism and energy production in GC cells.
 

## Salidroside inhibiting GC cell proliferation by regulating miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB enzyme complex glucose metabolic pathway
After 24 h of salidroside treatment, the viability rates of GC cells were showed as 100%, 120.592%, 108.075%, 106.595%, 86.676%, and 67.7% at concentrations of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 µmol/mL, respectively. At the 24-hour time point, treatment with a low concentration (2 µmol/mL) of salidroside resulted in a slight, non-significant increase in cell viability compared to the control. This transient and minor stimulatory effect at low doses is reminiscent of hormesis, an adaptive stress response phenomenon that has been documented for various plant-derived compounds [ ]. However, this effect was not sustained, and a clear, dose-dependent suppression of cell viability was observed at higher concentrations and across later time points. At 48 h, cell viability rates decreased to 100%, 93.79%, 71.9%, 60.343%, 55.916%, and 33.78% at the same concentrations. These results demonstrated a definite time and dose-dependent way. Consequently, the 48-hour salidroside treatment was selected for further experiments due to its more significant inhibitory effects. Based on the IC50 value (7.72 µmol/mL), concentrations of 4, 6, and 8 µmol/mL were designated as low, medium, and high-dose groups for subsequent experimental analysis (Fig. A). The colony formation assay confirmed that the proliferation of GC cells progressively decreased with increasing concentrations of salidroside (Fig. B).
 Results revealed that salidroside up-regulated miR-1343-3p in GC cells, accompanied by down-regulation OGDHL mRNA and protein expression. However, PDHB mRNA levels remained unchanged and PDHB protein expression markedly decreased, therefore leading to reduced pyruvate oxidative decarboxylation, the accumulation of pyruvate, and the decrease of acetyl-CoA and ATP generation. the findings suggested that salidroside up-regulated miR-1343-3p and suppressed OGDHL expression, thereby resulting in PDHB enzyme protein homeostasis imbalance, which led to substrate pyruvate accumulation, reduced production of acetyl-CoA and ATP, disruption of glucose metabolism, ultimately suppressing GC cell proliferation (Fig.  C, S1A, B).

## miR-1343-3p interference regulating the proliferation of GC cells
By liposome-mediated transfection miR-1343-3p mimic, miR-1343-3p inhibitor, NC mimic or NC inhibitor were transfected into GC cell lines. The transfection efficiency of miR-1343-3p mimic and miR-1343-3p inhibitor was measured by qRT-PCR. (Figure S2A). The results showed that in GC cells transfected with miR-1343-3p mimic, along with the up-regulation of miR-1343-3p expression, OGDHL mRNA and protein expressions were significantly down-regulated. Although PDHB mRNA showed no significant changes, its protein levels decreased. Then substrate pyruvate accumulated, and the productions of acetyl-CoA and ATP were reduced (Fig.  A-C). Conversely, knockdown of miR-1343-3p resulted in the up-regulation of OGDHL at mRNA and protein levels, then PDHB protein expression increased, while PDHB mRNA levels remained unchanged. Therefore pyruvate oxidation and decarboxylation was enhanced and the productions of acetyl-CoA and ATP were elevated (Fig.  A-C). CCK-8 and colony formation experiments demonstrated that miR-1343-3p over-expression inhibited GC cell proliferation, whereas miR-1343-3p knockdown reversed this inhibitory effect (Figure S2B-E). The findings indicated that miR-1343-3p regulated GC progression through the OGDHL-PDHB enzyme complex-mediated pyruvate-glycolysis pathway.
 

## Targeted knockdown of OGDHL inhibits the proliferation of GC cells
To investigate the regulatory role of the downstream OGDHL-PDHB enzyme complex in GC cells, we first transfected GC cells with si-OGDHL and confirmed the successful transfection efficiency (Figure S3A). The expression of the miR-1343-3p gene remained unchanged, while the expression of the OGDHL gene and protein was downregulated. PDHB protein expression decreased, with no statistically significant difference in PDHB mRNA expression. Substrate pyruvate accumulated, and the productions of acetyl-CoA and ATP was reduced. When GC cells were treated with salidroside combined with si-OGDHL, the miR-1343-3p expression increased, accompanied by the down-regulation of OGDHL gene and protein expression. Although PDHB gene expression showed no significant change, PDHB protein levels decreased. Pyruvate accumulation increased, and the generation of acetyl-CoA and ATP was reduced (Fig.  A-C). Further experiments demonstrated that the knockdown of OGDHL inhibited GC cell proliferation and colony formation (Figure S3B, C). These findings suggested that salidroside suppresses GC cell proliferation through regulating miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB enzyme complex-pyruvate metabolic axis.
 

## In vivo Salidroside regulating the miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB glucose metabolism pathway to inhibit GC cell growth
Consistent with in vitro findings, both salidroside and miR-1343-3p agomir significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo, showed as Fig.  A-D.
 To elucidate molecular mechanisms, we analyzed expression of miR-1343-3p, OGDHL, PDHB, and the productions of pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, and ATP in tumor tissues. Compared to controls, salidroside and miR-1343-3p agomir treatments up-regulated miR-1343-3p expression, suppressed OGDHL mRNA and protein expressions, and reduced PDHB protein, though PDHB mRNA remained unchanged. These changes resulted in pyruvate accumulation and decreased acetyl-CoA and ATP production (Fig.  A-C).
 

## Discussion
Salidroside, the main component of Rhodiola rosea, is a natural compound with extensive pharmacological effects [ ]. In addition to its antioxidant, anti-aging, and anti-inflammatory bioactivities, salidroside has displayed a potential effect against cancers [ , ]. Studies demonstrated that it could suppress tumor development and progression in hepatocellular carcinoma, colonic cancer, and lung cancer [ – ]. Our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized function of miR-1343-3p that extends beyond its known role in inhibiting proliferation and invasion. Unlike previous reports focusing on targets like TEAD4, we identify the TCA cycle enzymes OGDHL and PDHB as critical downstream targets of miR-1343-3p in the context of salidroside treatment. This miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB axis represents a novel, pharmacologically-inducible metabolic vulnerability, providing a mechanistic basis for salidroside-induced metabolic collapse in GC cells. This distinguishes our work by positioning miR-1343-3p as a central regulator of cancer cell energetics, a function that is activated by a specific natural compound and operates through a distinct set of metabolic targets.
It is confirmed that tumor growth depends on metabolic reprogramming, accompanied by abnormal regulation of metabolic enzymes and pathways [ , ]. Glucose metabolism, as a main resource for cancer progression, enables cancer cells to mobilize glucose to generate energy efficiently [ ]. The Warburg effect, first described in 1956, highlights cancer cells’ reliance on aerobic glycolysis to produce extra ATP despite its low efficiency compared to oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. Recent studies revealed that GC glycolysis was regulated by non-coding RNAs. It is found that lncRNA H19 promotes glucose uptake, lactate accumulation, and GC proliferation by regulating the miR-519d-3p/lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) axis [ ]. Similarly, lncRNA SNHG7, up-regulated in cisplatin-resistant GC cells, enhances chemo-resistance by antagonizing miR-34a, which suppresses glycolysis via LDHA down-regulation [ ]. These findings indicated the complexity of metabolic regulation in GC. Studies proved that cancer cells could adapt tumor microenvironment stress to induce cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to produce lactate, a phenomenon termed the “reverse Warburg effect.” It is suggested that aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation synergistically promote tumor growth [ ]. Therefore, to maintain energy homeostasis, cancer cells demand substantial energy consumption, then aerobic glycolysis together with glucose oxidation phosphorylation produces sufficient ATP [ ]. Metabolic generated from glycolysis and TCA cycle between cancer cells and surrounding tissues, like lactate and pyruvate, enhance coordinated catabolism of cancers [ – ]. As well as glucose, lactate is emerged as the main substrate of glycolysis and TCA cycle in most tumors [ ]. Additionally, lactate and pyruvate exchange could balance NAD+/NADH ratios for tumor cells to sustain metabolic homeostasis [ ]. In this study, we identified for the first time an interaction between OGDHL, a key enzyme in the TCA cycle, and PDHB, a vital protein linking glycolysis to TCA cycle [ , ]. Intriguingly, OGDHc generates substantial reactive oxygen species (ROS) during TCA cycle, inducing oxidative stress that promotes aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells. This results in pyruvate accumulation, which is subsequently converted by PDHc into acetyl-CoA for mitochondrial oxidation, thereby enhancing energy production [ , ]. These findings demonstrate that the OGDHc and PDHc enzyme complexes cooperatively sustain tumor cell proliferation by integrating TCA cycle and aerobic glycolysis.
Furthermore, analysis of differentially expressed glycolysis-related genes in colonic cancer patient samples identified OGDHL as a risk factor. Western blot confirmed that OGDHL was over-expression in colonic cancers, which significantly correlated with poorer survival [ ]. Concurrently, studies verified that over-expression of OGDHL and PDHB in ovarian cancer could promote cancer cell growth through the coordinated of TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and aerobic glycolysis [ , ]. In this study, TCGA and GEPIA databases analysis further demonstrated that OGDHL and PDHB expressions were elevate in GC tissues. OGDHL knockdown significantly reduced PDHB protein levels without altering PDHB mRNA expression. Some researches verified that metabolic enzymes could be degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, a primary mechanism regulating protein stability [ , ]. It is found that TRIM65 over-expression in breast cancer enhances ANXA2 protein degradation through elevated ubiquitination, while aberrant FBXW7 expression mediates ZMYND8 ubiquitination to drive breast cancer development [ , ]. Based on the observed reduction in PDHB protein without a corresponding decrease in its mRNA levels, it is plausible that OGDHL down-regulation may lead to the destabilization of PDHB, potentially targeting it for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. This hypothesized cascade would explain the subsequent metabolic consequences: pyruvate accumulation, reduced acetyl-CoA production, decreased ATP synthesis, and the ultimate disruption of glucose metabolism and energy homeostasis in GC cells. However, direct validation of PDHB ubiquitination and proteasomal dependency awaits further investigation.
Among ceRNA network, miRNAs function as vital regulators by binding to the 3’UTR of target mRNAs to inhibit translation [ ]. In this study, with high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analysis, we identified tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p which cross-linked and down-regulated glycolytic enzyme OGDHL expression after salidroside treatment. RIP assays further confirmed the interaction between miR-1343-3p and OGDHL. miR-1343-3p over-expression directly suppressed OGDHL expression, accompanied by reduced PDHB protein levels, substrate pyruvate accumulation, and decreased acetyl-CoA and ATP production. Conversely, OGDHL expression was elevated after miR-1343-3p knockdown, completely reversing the metabolic effects. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that miR-1343-3p up-regulation or down-regulation influenced OGDHL mRNA expression, while PDHB mRNA remained unaffected. With OGDHL protein down-regulation, PDHB protein levels decreased accordingly, following by pyruvate accumulation, reduced acetyl-CoA and ATP production (Figure S4). The findings confirmed that salidroside elevated tumor suppressor miR-1343-3p to inhibit OGDHL-PDHB glucose metabolism pathway, which suppressed gastric cancer proliferation.
This study, for the first time in a GC cell model, reveals the molecular mechanism by which the tumor-suppressive factor miR-1343-3p cooperates with salidroside to regulate its downstream target mRNAs, collectively influencing tumor cell glycometabolic reprogramming and thereby suppressing tumor growth. This discovery provides crucial clues for further elucidating the miRNA-mRNA regulatory network in energy metabolism. However, the process by which non-coding RNAs and multi-target drugs co-regulate tumor energy metabolism is highly complex. As a biological modulator with pleiotropic effects, salidroside likely influences various tumor intervention pathways. Based on our findings, we propose the following limitations and future research directions: First, although our focused investigation on this particular pathway was based on its prominence in our initial screening data, we acknowledge that miR-1343-3p may regulate additional targets beyond those examined here. As a typical multi-target regulator, its complete functional network likely extends to other biological processes not explored in this study [ ]. Second, while our data are consistent with PDHB degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, this model would be strengthened by direct experimental validation. Additionally, while potential off-target effects of the miR-1343-3p agomir in vivo cannot be entirely ruled out, the consistent concordance between in vitro and in vivo phenotypes supports the conclusion that the agomir-induced activation of miR-1343-3p is the primary driver of the observed effects. Most critically, the absence of OGDHL overexpression rescue experiments represents a key limitation. Future work utilizing OGDHL expression constructs resistant to miR-1343-3p will be essential to conclusively establish OGDHL’s necessity within this metabolic pathway. To advance this field, integrated multi-omics analyses combined with bioinformatics and techniques such as Seahorse metabolic analysis will be crucial for systematically delineating energy metabolism dysregulation in gastric cancer [ ]. Finally, we note that the conclusions of this study are primarily derived from a single cell line model. Therefore, their generalizability requires systematic validation across expanded gastric cancer cell panels and in preclinical animal models.

## Conclusion
Based on previous research, this study demonstrated that salidroside inhibits GC cell proliferation through glucose metabolic reprogramming, specifically by destabilizing the homeostasis of the miR-1343-3p-OGDHL/PDHB enzyme complex-pyruvate metabolic axis, thereby disrupting cell energy production. The findings showned that salidroside up-regulates miR-1343-3p, which directly suppresses the expression of OGDHL, a key TCA cycle enzyme, leading to the homeostasis imbalance of PDHB, the vital enzyme participating in aerobic glycolysis and the TCA cycle. This disruption destabilizes the balance between substrate pyruvate and product acetyl-CoA, impairing GC cell glucose metabolism and ATP generation, ultimately inhibiting tumor proliferation and migration. These results provide a deep understanding of salidroside inhibiting gastric cancer and lay a better basis for precision targeted therapy for cancers.
