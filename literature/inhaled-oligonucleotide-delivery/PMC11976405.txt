## Highlights
•GTF3C6 is upregulated in LUAD tissues, LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− LUAD mouse models and LUAD organoid.
•Upregulation of GTF3C6 is associated with an unfavorable clinical prognosis in LUAD.
•GTF3C6 is driven by KRAS/PI3K axis in LUAD.
•GTF3C6 promotes anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells via FAK pathway.

## Abstract

## Introduction
Effective targeting drugs for KRAS mutation-mediated Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are currently are limited.

## Objectives
Investigating and intervening in the downstream key target genes of KRAS is crucial for clinically managing KRAS mutant-driven LUAD. GTF3C6, a newly identified member of the general transcription factor III (GTF3) family, plays a role in the transcription of RNA polymerase III (pol III)-dependent genes. However, its involvement in cancer remains unexplored.

## Methods
This study examined the expression, roles, and potential molecular mechanisms of GTF3C6 in LUAD tissues, LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− LUAD mouse models, and LUAD patients-derived organoid using Western blot, qRT-PCR, immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and gene manipulation assays.

## Results
We present the first evidence that GTF3C6 is highly expressed in LUAD tissues, LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− LUAD mouse models, and LUAD organoids, correlating with poor clinical prognosis. Furthermore, GTF3C6 was found to promote anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells. Mechanistically, KRAS mutation drives GTF3C6 expression through the PI3K pathway, and GTF3C6 knockdown reverses the malignant phenotype of KRAS mutation-driven LUAD cells. Additionally, the FAK pathway emerged as a crucial downstream signaling pathway through which GTF3C6 mediates the malignant phenotype of LUAD. Finally, GTF3C6 knockdown suppresses LUAD organoid formation and inhibits tumor growth in vivo.

## Conclusion
Our findings demonstrate that GTF3C6, driven by KRAS mutation, promotes LUAD development by regulating FAK phosphorylation, suggesting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target in KRAS mutant-driven LUAD.

## Introduction
Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Among lung cancer subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), characterized by its high aggressiveness, invasiveness, and resistance to therapy, is the most common type , , . Despite strides in diagnostic and therapeutic modalities, the prognosis for LUAD patients remains bleak, with a 5-year survival rate below 20 % . Thus, delving into the intricate molecular underpinnings of LUAD pathogenesis and pinpointing novel molecular indicators are imperative for advancing more precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
RNA polymerase III (RNAPIII), a vast assembly of enzymes, orchestrates the transcription of various RNA species . Reports indicate escalated levels of RNAPIII products in numerous cancer cells, influencing diverse facets of tumor proliferation and progression , . General transcription factor III (GTF3), a cluster of proteins, assumes a pivotal role in modulating gene transcription by RNAPIII . Dysregulated GTF3 has been implicated in carcinogenesis across various cancer types, including colorectal and breast cancer, as per recent investigations , . Notably, Anuraga et al. unearthed a substantial association between GTF3 family members' expressions and WNT/β-catenin signaling, oxidative stress, and the cell cycle, alongside a pronounced adverse correlation between patients' prognoses and heightened expressions of GTF3A and GTF3B in colorectal cancer . Additionally, Wang et al. demonstrated elevated GTF3A levels in metastatic lymph nodes and colorectal cancer tissues, elucidating its role in expediting colorectal cancer progression through epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) regulation by upregulating CSTA expression . Collectively, these revelations posit GTF3 as a prospective therapeutic target in cancer management. However, within the GTFA family, the functional role of GTF3C6 in tumorigenesis remains obscure. Our findings reveal heightened GTF3C6 expression in LUAD, markedly associated with adverse prognosis. Thus, elucidating the precise role of GTF3C6 in LUAD initiation and progression, along with its molecular regulatory mechanisms, holds pivotal clinical significance in delineating diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for LUAD.
One of the most often mutated oncogenes in human cancer is KRAS, which is also frequently mutated in LUAD. KRAS mutations in LUAD predominantly consist of subtypes such as KRAS (G12D), KRAS (G12V), KRAS (G12C), and KRAS (G12A), with KRAS (G12C) mutation accounting for approximately 41 %, KRAS (G12V) for about 18 %, and KRAS (G12D) for around 15 % . Upon binding with GTP, KRAS is activated, whereas GDP-bound KRAS remains in an inactive state . Mutations at key sites like G12C, G12D, and G12V result in KRAS being “locked” in the active GTP-binding state, leading to sustained activation of downstream signaling pathways including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and Ral guanine nucleotide exchange factor effector pathway, contributing to LUAD pathogenesis , . Despite extensive efforts, KRAS mutations have been considered “undruggable” due to their high affinity for GTP and unique nearly spherical structure lacking a deep hydrophobic pocket and obvious binding site , , . Presently, two covalent inhibitors targeting KRASG12C, sotorasib (AMG510) and adagrasib (MRTX849), have received accelerated approval for treating KRASG12C mutant lung cancer . Additionally, recent advancements include the development of a noncovalent KRASG12D inhibitor, MRTX1133, achieved through chemical space optimization based on G12C inhibitors, exhibiting promising selectivity and potency . Unfortunately, targeted drugs for KRASG12A and KRASG12V mutations are currently unavailable . However, the emergence of drug resistance against KRAS mutation-targeting therapies poses a significant challenge in clinical practice. Therefore, gaining deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of KRAS mutations is imperative for the development of efficacious targeted therapies. Additionally, identifying and modulating downstream KRAS target genes represents a viable strategy for addressing LUAD stemming from KRAS mutation.
This study demonstrates the upregulation of GTF3C6 in LUAD cells, LUAD tissues, LUAD patients-derived organoid, and KRAS-driven LUAD mouse models. Additionally, elevated GTF3C6 expression is associated with enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of LUAD cells both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, induction of KRAS mutation via the PI3K signaling pathway drives the upregulation of GTF3C6 expression, and knockdown of GTF3C6 significantly attenuates cell proliferation, invasion, and migration induced by KRAS mutation. Furthermore, GTF3C6 facilitates the malignant phenotype of LUAD cell lines through modulation of the FAK pathway. These findings position GTF3C6 as a novel oncogene implicated in LUAD onset and progression. Notably, as a downstream target gene of KRAS, GTF3C6 plays a pivotal role in promoting LUAD development and represents a promising target for intervention in KRAS-mutated LUAD.

## Results

## Elevated expression of GTF3C6 is observed in LUAD tissues, LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− LUAD mouse models, LUAD organoid, and cell lines
To confirm the expression of GTF3C6 protein in LUAD, we performed Immunohistochemistry (IHC) on 75 pairs of LUAD patient tissues in tissue microarrays. Elevated GTF3C6 expression was observed in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent tissues ( a). Subsequently, IHC analysis of GTF3C6 protein levels was performed on clinical LUAD specimens collected from Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University, revealing significantly higher expression in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent and normal lung tissues ( b and c). Further confirmation was sought by examining six randomly selected pairs of LUAD tissues and their adjacent normal tissues, demonstrating notable upregulation of both GTF3C6 mRNA and protein levels in LUAD tissues ( d). Evaluation of GTF3C6 expression in LUAD cell lines (BEAS-2B, A549, H23, H1975, HCC827, and 95D) using Western blotting (WB) and Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) revealed higher expression levels compared to normal lung bronchial epithelial cells, particularly evident in A549 and H23 cell lines ( e). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis highlighted nuclear expression of GTF3C6 in A549 cells ( a).
 In vivo investigation involved the LUAD mouse model generated by Kras mutation and P53 deletion (LSL-KrasG12D/+; LSL-p53−/−, KP). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of KP lung tissues depicted in f revealed the LUAD phenotype. IHC analysis confirmed elevated GTF3C6 protein levels in the KP model compared to the control group ( g).
LUAD Patient-Derived Organoids (PDOs) were established to mimic original tumor characteristics. Morphological evaluation and HE staining ( h) confirmed the accurate construction of PDOs. Immunofluorescence (IF) and IHC analyses demonstrated robust expression of the tumor marker cytokeratin 7 ( i and j), validating successful PDO generation. Consistency of GTF3C6 expression between organoids and original cancer tissues was assessed through IHC and IF assays, revealing high GTF3C6 expression in PDOs consistent with LUAD tissue IHC findings ( k and l). These results suggest that GTF3C6 is highly expressed in LUAD and may serve as an independent risk factor for LUAD mortality.

## GTF3C6 upregulation is associated with a poor clinical prognosis in LUAD
To determine the expression levels of GTF3C6 in various malignancies, we first used the Oncomine database to analyze GTF3C6 expression in different cancer types. Our findings revealed significant upregulation of GTF3C6 in multiple cancers, including LUAD ( a). Subsequently, gene expression analysis of GTF3C6 in the LUAD TCGA dataset demonstrated its significant elevation in LUAD tissues compared to matched adjacent tissues ( b). Additionally, utilizing the online bioinformatics tool GEPIA 2, we observed higher GTF3C6 levels in LUAD tissues compared to normal tissues (https://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) ( a). Further investigation of overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DSS) using TCGA databases revealed elevated GTF3C6 expression in deceased LUAD patients compared to living patients and normal lung tissues ( b). Employing the online bioinformatics tool UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html), we confirmed a significant upregulation of GTF3C6 protein levels in LUAD tissues ( c). Moreover, we explored the association between GTF3C6 expression and various clinicopathological characteristics of LUAD patients using the TCGA database. Our analysis unveiled a significant correlation between high GTF3C6 expression and advanced T stage, N stage, M stage ( d), and pathology stage ( e and c).
 Assessing the impact of GTF3C6 expression on LUAD patient outcomes, we scrutinized transcriptomic data from the TCGA, revealing a significant association between elevated GTF3C6 expression and poor prognosis ( f). Additionally, utilizing online survival analysis tools GEPIA2 and UALCAN, we observed worse prognoses in LUAD patients with higher GTF3C6 mRNA levels ( g and d). Moreover, employing the online bioinformatics tool, THE HUMAN PROTEIN ATLAS (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), we identified that LUAD patients with elevated GTF3C6 protein levels exhibited poorer prognoses ( e). Furthermore, analysis of lung cancer mutation data from the cBioportal database indicated infrequent GTF3C6 gene mutations in LUAD, with a relatively low frequency of less than 1.2 % ( h). These findings collectively suggest that elevated GTF3C6 expression serves as an independent predictor of poor survival in LUAD patients.

## GTF3C6 promotes anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells
To further investigate the function of GTF3C6 in LUAD, GTF3C6 was transiently knocked down or overexpressed in A549 and H23 cells, and the expression level of GTF3C6 was assessed by RT-PCR and WB ( a). Subsequent findings demonstrated that GTF3C6 knockdown significantly attenuated the migration and invasion of LUAD cells, whereas GTF3C6 overexpression promoted their invasion and migration ( a). To mitigate potential transient transfection artifacts, stable cell lines with GTF3C6 knockdown and overexpression were generated in A549 and H23 cells, with GTF3C6 expression levels confirmed by RT-PCR and WB ( a). Consistently, these stable cell lines exhibited significantly inhibited or promoted invasion and migration abilities of LUAD cells, respectively ( b). Additionally, we explored the impact of GTF3C6 on LUAD cell proliferation. In vitro assays including CCK8, EDU, and colony formation assays revealed no significant effects of GTF3C6 knockdown or overexpression on LUAD cell proliferation ( ). Notably, our investigations revealed that GTF3C6 overexpression enhanced, while GTF3C6 knockdown diminished, colony formation in soft agar, suggesting a potential role of GTF3C6 in anchorage-independent growth of LUAD cells.
 Hypoxia and nutrient starved conditions are common hallmark feature of the tumor microenvironment. So, we investigate the expression of GTF3C6 and its impact on LUAD cells under hypoxic(1 % O2) and low glucose (10 % serum, 5 mM glucose) conditions. Results indicated that neither hypoxic nor low-glucose conditions affected GTF3C6 expression ( c). Furthermore, CCK8 assays demonstrated that GTF3C6 exerted no influence on anchorage-dependent growth of LUAD cells under hypoxic or low-glucose conditions ( d). These findings collectively suggest the involvement of GTF3C6 in anchorage-independent growth, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells.

## KRAS positively regulates GTF3C6 through PI3K pathway to influence LUAD biological function
The aforementioned findings suggest that GTF3C6 is markedly upregulated in LUAD tissues and can influence anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells, thus indicating its potential as an oncogenic gene in LUAD. However, its specific upstream regulatory mechanism remains unclear. Utilizing RNA sequencing, we probed transcriptomic alterations following GTF3C6 knockdown in A549 cells. Intriguingly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis unveiled notable enrichment for signaling pathways pertinent to tumor cell migration and invasion, including the focal adhesion pathway, ECM-receptor interaction pathway, tight junction pathway, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton pathway ( a). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) of the differentially expressed genes identified enrichment of pathways associated with cellular movement, including the wound healing pathway ( b). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further unveiled enrichment of genes involved in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signaling pathway, which is implicated in cell movement, among the downregulated gene sets ( c). Additionally, IPA analysis of diseases and function highlighted the involvement of these differentially expressed genes in cellular movement ( d). Importantly, the results of GSEA showed that GTF3C6 regulated gene sets were significantly enriched in differentially expressed genes regulated by KRAS ( c).
Activating KRAS mutations is a major subtype of human LUAD . To ascertain whether GTF3C6 expression is regulated by KRAS, we either overexpressed KRASG12D or KRASG12C mutant plasmids or knocked down the KRAS gene in A549 and H23 cell lines, respectively. Subsequently, we analyzed GTF3C6 expression via WB and RT-PCR. Our observations revealed a significant increase in GTF3C6 expression upon overexpression of KRASG12D or KRASG12C mutant plasmids in A549 or H23 cells, while KRAS knockdown led to a notable inhibition of GTF3C6 expression ( a, b, and c). Notably, we found that GTF3C6 knockdown or overexpression had no discernible effect on KRAS expression (Data not shown), suggesting that KRAS acts as an upstream regulator of GTF3C6 expression. KRAS is involved in several downstream effector pathways, including the PI3K, ERK, STAT3, and Ral signaling pathways . To elucidate which KRAS effector pathway contributes to GTF3C6 expression, we utilized inhibitors targeting these crucial KRAS effectors. Our findings revealed a decrease in GTF3C6 protein expression in treated A549 and H23 cells, with PI3K inhibitors demonstrating the most significant efficacy ( d), while ERK, RAL, and STAT3 inhibitors had no effect on GTF3C6 expression ( e). Furthermore, we observed that PI3K inhibitors could significantly suppress GTF3C6 expression induced by KRASG12D or KRASG12C overexpression in both A549 and H23 cells ( f and S4e), thereby demonstrating that KRAS regulates GTF3C6 expression primarily via the PI3K signaling pathway. Overall, our results indicate that the PI3K signaling axis promotes GTF3C6 expression in KRAS-mutant LUAD cells.
 Moreover, to evaluate the impact of GTF3C6 on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion induced by activated KRAS, we depleted GTF3C6 in A549 and H23 cells overexpressing KRASG12D or KRASG12C. As depicted in g, h and f, KRASG12D or KRASG12C overexpression enhanced the migration and invasion capacities of A549 and H23 cells, whereas these cellular phenotypes were attenuated following GTF3C6 knockdown. Conversely, cell migration and invasion were diminished in KRAS-knockdown cells, and were rescued by GTF3C6 overexpression ( i and 4j). In line with these findings, GTF3C6 also influenced the anchorage-independent proliferation mediated by KRAS knockdown or KRASG12D overexpression ( g). Additionally, we observed that GTF3C6 overexpression in PI3K inhibitor-treated cells reinstated cell migration and invasion abilities ( h). Overall, these results suggest that KRAS modulates GTF3C6 expression through the PI3K pathway, thereby facilitating the anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells, consequently impacting LUAD progression.

## GTF3C6 affects malignant phenotype of LUAD cells by regulating the FAK pathway
The preceding findings unveiled that the KRAS/PI3K axis serves as the upstream regulators of GTF3C6. To further elucidate the potential downstream effector molecules through which GTF3C6 modulates the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells, we conducted an analysis of the GTF3C6 co-expression gene using the Linkedomics website (https://www.linkedomics.org/login.php). Combined with the KEGG pathway analysis from the aforementioned RNA sequencing results, we identified that GTF3C6 may be involved in regulating the focal adhesion signaling pathway ( a and a). The focal adhesion signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in cancer development and progression . Focal adhesions are dynamic multi-protein structures that govern various cellular processes, including cell migration, invasion, and survival . Activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK), a key component of the focal adhesions signaling pathway, is frequently observed in various cancer types and is correlated with poor clinical outcomes . FAK can be activated through phosphorylation in response to stimulation by integrins, growth factors, and chemokines . Therefore, we manipulated the expression of GTF3C6 in A549 and H23 cells to assess the levels of FAK and phosphorylated FAK. While knockdown or overexpression of GTF3C6 did not alter FAK protein levels, overexpression of GTF3C6 significantly enhanced the phosphorylation level of FAK, whereas GTF3C6 knockdown reduced the phosphorylation level of FAK(Y397) ( b). Previous studies have demonstrated that FAK is implicated in the proliferation, migration, and invasive ability of LUAD cells, thereby influencing LUAD progression , , . To further corroborate the role of FAK in GTF3C6-mediated cell invasion and migration, we knocked down FAK in GTF3C6-overexpressed cells and evaluated the aforementioned cell phenotypes. Transwell assays revealed that FAK knockdown attenuated GTF3C6 overexpression-induced migration and invasion of A549 and H23 cells ( c). These findings suggest that GTF3C6 may modulate the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells by regulating the phosphorylation level of FAK(Y397).
 

## GTF3C6 knockdown suppresses LUAD organoid formation and prevents lung tumorigenesis in vivo
To investigate GTF3C6′s role in LUAD progression, lentiviral-mediated knockdown of GTF3C6 was utilized to assess its impact on PDO proliferation. The efficacy of knockdown was validated via western blot analysis, as illustrated in a. Remarkably, GTF3C6 suppression using shRNA led to a notable decrease in organoid proliferation, evidenced by the smaller size observed four days post-treatment in PDO ( b).
 Subsequently, tumor formation assays were conducted by subcutaneously injecting GTF3C6 knockdown A549 cells or control cells into the axilla of nude mice. We observed that GTF3C6 knockdown significantly impeded tumor growth in the nude mouse model, consistent with our in vitro findings ( c, d). Moreover, the volume and weight of subcutaneous tumors in nude mice were markedly reduced following GTF3C6 knockdown in A549 cells ( e). Tumor IHC revealed decreased expression of Ki67, indicating reduced tumor cell proliferation ( f). Furthermore, to validate the impact of GTF3C6 on the focal adhesion pathway in vivo, tumor and PDO proteins were extracted, and changes in FAK and phosphorylated FAK were analyzed via Western blotting. While no alteration was observed in FAK protein expression, a decrease in phosphorylated FAK protein expression was noted in the GTF3C6 knockdown group compared to the control group ( a and 6g). These findings suggest that GTF3C6 promotes LUAD tumorigenesis.

## Discussion
Lung cancer, a malignancy with significant global incidence and mortality rates, poses a grave threat to human health and survival. Despite advancements, managing LUAD, the predominant histological subtype of lung cancer, remains a formidable challenge in clinical diagnosis and therapy , , . Presently, surgical resection coupled with adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation represents the primary treatment for LUAD. However, novel medications targeting molecular pathways show promise in alleviating the disease burden to some extent , , , . Therapeutic options for LUAD arising from genetic mutations, notably KRAS mutation, remain restricted . Despite some progress in targeted interventions against the KRAS gene, effective clinical drugs are still limited. Therefore, addressing the crucial clinical challenge of treating KRAS-mutated LUAD necessitates further research into drugs targeting KRAS mutation and the development of therapeutic strategies to modulate downstream target genes of KRAS.
GTF3C6, encoding transcription factors crucial for RNA polymerase III-dependent transcription, holds potential as a cancer therapy target due to its significance in tumor biology . Our study marks the initial report of significantly upregulated GTF3C6 expression in LUAD tissues, correlating with poor clinical prognosis ( , ). The LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− mouse model, widely employed in LUAD research, demonstrated a noteworthy elevation in GTF3C6 expression compared to adjacent normal tissue, as evident from IHC analysis ( g). Furthermore, tumor organoids derived from patient tumor cells offer a physiologically relevant context for studying tumor biology and drug response, replicating key features of the original tumor . In our study, elevated expression of GTF3C6 was also observed in LUAD organoids( j). These findings underscore the potential significance of GTF3C6 in LUAD development and progression.
Furthermore, our study shows that GTF3C6 promotes the anchorage-independent proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells( d). Nevertheless, we observed no impact of GTF3C6 on anchorage-dependent proliferation of LUAD cells. Thus, the modulation of cell invasion and migration resulting from GTF3C6 knockdown or overexpression appears to be independent of proliferation. Anchorage-independent growth, characteristic of tumor cells, signifies a more aggressive phenotype and metastatic potential . Tumor cells with the ability to grow in an anchorage-independent manner can invade surrounding tissues, spread to distant organs and form secondary tumors . Given GTF3C6′s role in promoting anchorage-independent proliferation, invasion, and migration of LUAD cell lines, it emerges as a pivotal player in the malignant progression of LUAD tumors.
The complex structure of KRAS presents challenges for targeted medications to effectively bind and inhibit mutant KRAS, posing difficulties for targeted treatment of KRAS-mutated cancers . Our investigation revealed that KRAS positively regulates GTF3C6 expression. Knockdown of GTF3C6 reversed cell proliferation, invasion, and migration induced by KRAS mutation activation, suggesting the potential therapeutic value of targeting GTF3C6 for treating KRAS-mutated tumors. Interestingly, high expression of GTF3C6 was observed not only in A549 and H23 KRAS-mutant cell lines but also in other non-KRAS mutant LUAD cell lines and clinical samples. These findings suggest that besides KRAS, other upstream genes may regulate GTF3C6 expression. Targeted intervention of GTF3C6 may thus have broader anticancer effects beyond KRAS-mutant LUAD, necessitating further research to identify additional upstream regulators and elucidate the mechanistic role of GTF3C6 in LUAD.
FAK is upregulated in various tumor cell lines and contributes to tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating associated proteins . Autophosphorylation of FAK at Y397 triggers its activation and the formation of a signaling complex, ultimately leading to cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis , . Our study demonstrates that GTF3C6 can enhance FAK phosphorylation at Y397, and knockdown of FAK can reverse the malignant phenotype induced by GTF3C6 overexpression. Overall, our results highlight the significant role of FAK phosphorylation at Y397 in mediating the effects of GTF3C6 on LUAD development.
In this study, we report for the first time that GTF3C6 is highly expressed in various forms of LUAD, including cell lines, tissues, KrasG12D/+; LSL-p53−/− LUAD models, and LUAD organoids. We demonstrate that GTF3C6 plays a critical role in anchorage-independent proliferation, invasion, and migration of LUAD cells. Moreover, we found a significant association between high GTF3C6 expression and poor prognosis. To investigate the upstream regulatory factors of GTF3C6, we identified KRAS as a potential driving factor for its upregulation. We showed that GTF3C6 knockdown can significantly reduce the anchorage-independent proliferation, invasion, and migration of KRAS-mutant LUAD cell lines. By combining transcriptome data, we further analyzed the downstream regulatory signaling pathways of GTF3C6 and found that it participates in regulating the local adhesion pathway. We validated the activation of this pathway by analyzing the phosphorylation level of FAK. Finally, we found that GTF3C6 knockdown suppresses the formation ability of LUAD organoids and weakens the tumorigenic ability of A549 cells in vivo. Collectively, our findings underscore GTF3C6 as a potential biomarker for LUAD occurrence and development, while also highlighting its promise as a therapeutic target for KRAS-mutant LUAD.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics statement
All experiments involving human tissues and animals were conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the ethics committee of the Shandong Provincial Hospital (Approval SWYX:NO.2020-301, Human patients) and (NSFC:NO.2019-021, animals).

## Mice
Transgenic LSL-KrasG12D/+ and LSL-p53+/+ mice were kindly provided by Pengju Zhang (Shandong university, China). Mice were crossed and genotyped to obtain animals with the following background: LSL-KrasG12D/+; LSL-p53−/−. Genotypes were determined by PCR. Only littermate mice were used in all experiments. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital.

## Patients and specimens
Human LUAD tissues and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection for LUAD at Shandong Provincial Hospital.
The tissue microarray used in this study was constructed by Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, employing specimens from 75 LUAD patients who underwent curative resection. All participants in the trial received no adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation. The Ethics Committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. authorized the tissue microarray creation.
The lung cancer cell lines (BEAS-2B, A549, H23, H1975, HCC827, PC-9, and 95-D) were purchased from the cell bank of Shanghai Institute of Biosciences (Shanghai, China). BEAS-2B, H23, H1975, HCC827, PC-9 and 95-D cell lines were cultured in RPMI1640 (Gibco®Grandisland, NY) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco®Grandisland, NY) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). A549 cells were cultured in F12K medium (HyClone) containing 10 % FBS and antibiotics. All cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C containing 5 % CO2.
The reagents employed in this experiment included LY294002 (15 μm, a PI3K inhibitor), Stattic (5, 10, 15, 20 μm, a STAT3 inhibitor), BQU57 (2, 4, 6, 8 μm, a GTPase Ral inhibitor), and PD0325901 (5, 10, 15, 20 μm, a MEK inhibitor). These reagents were procured from Selleck (Shanghai, China).

## Immunohistochemistry
The tissue slices were deparaffinized in dimethylbenzene and rehydrated using an alcohol gradient. Antigen retrieval was done in citric acid buffer (pH = 6.0). After that, the sections were rinsed with PBS and blocked for 30 min with 10 % goat serum. The tissue slides were treated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody, followed by 1 h at 37°C with the secondary antibody (Zhongshan Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China).

## Transfection assay
Genomeditech (Shanghai, China) constructed overexpression plasmids of GTF3C6 and control empty vectors. GenePharma (Shanghai, China) provided lentivirus to knock down GTF3C6 (sense: 5ʹ-GCTCAGCATGACAAGAACTCT-3ʹ). RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) synthesized siRNAs targeting KRAS (sense: 5ʹ-GAUGCCUUCUAUACAUUAGUUTT-3ʹ) and siRNAs targeting FAK (sense: 5ʹ-GAUGUUGGUUUAAAGCGAUUUTT-3ʹ). The cells in 6-well plates were transfected with plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China), or with siRNA using the riboFECTTM CP Transfection kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China), following the manufacturer's instructions.

## Western blotting
To begin, cells were collected in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The proteins were then electrophoresed on SDS gel and electroblotted on PVDF membrane (Merck, Nantong, Jiangsu, China). After that, the membranes were treated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. An HRP-conjugated goat-anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) was used to detect primary antibodies. Details of all antibodies are shown in .

## Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer's instructions using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China). The PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time) (TAKARA, Beijing, China) was used to reverse-transcribe 1 μg of mRNA to cDNA. ABGENE PCR STRIPS (Thermo, Shanghai, China) were used for quantitative real-time PCRs. The thermal cycling conditions were 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final step of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 1 min on a QuantStudioTM Real-Time PCR Instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). The expression of each targeted gene was normalized to β-actin. The primers were shown in .
 

## CCK-8 cell viability assays
In 96-well plate, cells were planted at a density of 3000 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated for 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 days. At the time of analysis, each well received 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). After 1 h of incubation, the optical density (OD) values were determined at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, USA).

## Ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine assay
Cells were grown in 96-well plates and treated for 5 h with 5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) per the manufacturer's instructions. Following three washes with PBS, the cells underwent treatment with the Apollo reaction cocktail for 30 min. Subsequently, the cellular DNA content was stained and assessed utilizing a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Ti-S, Tokyo, Japan).

## Colony formation assay
Cells were planted at a density of 1000 cells per well in 6-well plates. The cells were grown for 14 days, with the medium changed every 3–4 days, until noticeable colonies appeared. After washing with PBS, the colonies were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

## Soft agar assay
First, 1 ml of 1.2 % agarose in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 % FBS was poured into each well of a 6-well plate and allowed to solidify at room temperature. Then, 2000 cells were suspended in 1 ml of 0.7 % agarose in the same medium and plated on top of the base layer. The cells were cultured for 14 days, and the resulting colonies were observed and quantified under a microscope.

## Transwell assays
24-well plates were used for transwell experiments. In the upper chamber, about 4x104 cells/well were seeded and grown in serum-free media, whereas the lower chambers contained 30 % serum medium. The migration and invasion experiments differed in that the Transwell chambers for migration assays were not covered with Matrigel. The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin after migrating across the Transwell membrane.

## Immunofluorescence staining
For 48 h, cells were seeded onto chamber slides. Mitochondrial staining was performed by incubating the cells with 100 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at 37°C. The cells were then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China), washed 3 times for 5 min with PBS, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China), blocked with 1 % goat serum (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h, and finally incubated overnight at 4°C with the GTF3C6 primary antibody (1:300; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). The cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor®-488 secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Shanghai, China) at 37°C, and then nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Life Technologies, Shanghai, China). Fluorescent images were captured using a Nikon microscope imaging system (Nikon Ti-S, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed using Image J (version 1.52, NIH, USA).

## LUAD tumor-xenograft mouse model
BALB/c nude mice aged four to five weeks were used. A549 cells were produced at a density of 5 × 106 cells/100 µL. The cell suspensions were delivered subcutaneously to the naked mice. Tumor size was assessed weekly. All animals were euthanized four weeks later, and tumor volume and weight were compared. Tumors were fixed in 10 % formalin and embedded in paraffin for IHC analysis.

## Induction of LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-p53−/− LUAD model
At 8 weeks of age, KP mice were infected with intratracheal adenoviral-Cre (Ad-Cre; SyngenTech) at a dose of 5 × 107 PFU as previously described . At weeks 20 post infection, the lungs were obtained.

## LUAD organoid
Rinse fresh LUAD tissue twice with DPBS and cut it into small pieces. Digest the tissue fragments at 37℃ for 30 min using tumor tissue digestive solution (Biogenous, China). Collect the cells and perform a centrifugal filtration at 250 g for 3 min. Remove the supernatant and resuspend the particles in tumor organoid basal medium (Biogenous, China). Centrifuge the suspension at 4℃ and 250 g for 3 min, repeating the procedure once more. Remove the supernatant and resuspend the particles in BME (Corning). Grow approximately 10,000 cells in 40 μL BME. Invert a 24-well cell culture plate and drop 50 μL of the BME-containing organism around the center of each well. Place the culture plate upside down in a humidified incubator for 40 min to allow the BME to solidify. Add 500μL of organoid complete medium (Biogenous, China) to each well, and replace the medium every other day. Incubate the culture plates in a humidified incubator at 37℃ for 7–10 days.

## Statistical analysis
The data are presented in bar plots as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. A p-value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
