## Abstract
The organotypic retinal explant culture has been established for more than a decade and offers a range of unique advantages compared with in vivo experiments and cell cultures. However, the lack of systematic and continuous comparison between in vivo retinal development and the organotypic retinal explant culture makes this model controversial in postnatal retinal development studies. Thus, we aimed to verify the feasibility of using this model for postnatal retinal development studies by comparing it with the in vivo retina. In this study, we showed that postnatal retinal explants undergo normal development, and exhibit a consistent structure and timeline with retinas in vivo. Initially, we used SOX2 and PAX6 immunostaining to identify retinal progenitor cells. We then examined cell proliferation and migration by immunostaining with Ki-67 and doublecortin, respectively. Ki-67- and doublecortin-positive cells decreased in both in vivo and explants during postnatal retinogenesis, and exhibited a high degree of similarity in abundance and distribution between groups. Additionally, we used Ceh-10 homeodomain-containing homolog, glutamate-ammonia ligase (glutamine synthetase), neuronal nuclei, and ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 immunostaining to examine the emergence of bipolar cells, Müller glia, mature neurons, and microglia, respectively. The timing and spatial patterns of the emergence of these cell types were remarkably consistent between in vivo and explant retinas. Our study showed that the organotypic retinal explant culture model had a high degree of consistency with the progression of in vivo early postnatal retina development. The findings confirm the accuracy and credibility of this model and support its use for long-term, systematic, and continuous observation.

## Introduction
Retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) differentiate into seven main cell types, of which, bipolar cells (BCs), Müller glia (MG), non-GABAergic amacrine cells and most rod photoreceptors are specified at postnatal ages (Lyu et al., 2021; Petridou and Godinho, 2022; Ge et al., 2022). Abnormal postnatal retinal development can lead to a variety of diseases, such as juvenile macular degenerations (Tanna et al., 2017; Ku et al., 2023) and developmental anomalies (Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine et al., 2019; Thomas et al., 2022). The mammalian retina is a highly conserved organ, and thus the retinas of mice and humans are thought to undergo fairly similar developmental processes. Therefore, establishing an effective model for postnatal retinal development has significant potential for clinical applications and scientific studies (Hoon et al., 2014; Eastlake et al., 2021). In vivo models for postnatal retinal development are hampered by many factors, including low throughput, embryonic lethality, invasive intervention damage, and ethical concerns (Wisely et al., 2017). Cell culture in vitro also has intrinsic drawbacks, in particular, simulation of the complex retinal development microenvironment. Therefore, cultivation of organotypic retinal explants has started to garner attention.
The organotypic retinal explant model is one of the most appropriate models for translational research because it maintains intricate intercellular interactions within the retina and accurately replicates in situ reactions among cells, and it has been used in a wide variety of applications for retinal diseases and injury research over the last few decades (Jin and Xiang, 2012; Burnight et al., 2023). Busskamp et al. (2010) showed a potential halorhodopsin-based therapy for retinitis pigmentosa using human retinal explants. Previous studies have established an in vitro diabetic retinopathy model, optic nerve injury model and retinal hypoxia model based on organotypic rodent retinal explant cultures, which allow several therapies or agents to be tested at the same time (Stutzki et al., 2014; Valdes et al., 2016; Schnichels et al., 2017). Retinal explant culture models have also been used for retinal developmental studies (Zhang et al., 2002; Weir et al., 2021; Iwagawa et al., 2023). Previous studies investigated the regulation of apoptosis during retinal development by introducing exogenous agents into the retinal culture system (Donovan et al., 2011; Ghosh et al., 2012). Additionally, Wang et al. (2002) infected embryonic retinal explants with herpes simplex virus expression vectors to identify critical regulatory genes during retinal ganglion cell (RGC) differentiation.
It should be noted that continuous observation and comparison between the organotypic retinal explant model and in vivo development during early postnatal retinal development is lacking. These gaps severely affect the accuracy and credibility of the organotypic retinal explant model. To address this, we compared postnatal retinal development of the in vivo retina with organotypic retinal explant culture and performed long-term, systematic, and continuous observations that included retinal morphology, RPC distribution and proliferation, horizontal cell migration, and the timing and spatial pattern of the emergence of BCs, MG, mature neurons, and microglia. Through this study, we aimed to demonstrate that the organotypic retinal explant model exhibits consistent characteristics with in vivo retinas during developmental specification and maturation.

## Methods

## Animals and experimental design
For this study, 72 healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats (postnatal day 0 [P0]), specific-pathogen–free grade, were purchased from Laboratory Animal Center, Xi’an Jiaotong University Health Science Center, China (license No. SCXK (Shaan) 2021-002). The rats were bred and maintained in optimal conditions (25°C, 12/12-hour light/dark cycle) and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The experimental protocol of this study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval No. XJTUAE2021-605) on September 28, 2021. All animal experiments are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments; Percie du Sert et al., 2020).
The newborn rats (P0) were randomly assigned to the explant group and the in vivo group. Rats in the explant group underwent immediate preparation of the explants, whereas rats in the in vivo group were retained for further breeding. Retinas from both groups were collected at P1, P3, P7, and P10. In the explant group, retinas were promptly fixed in culture inserts upon sampling, whereas in the in vivo group, eyeballs were collected on the day of sampling and preserved after fixation. The harvested explant retinas and in vivo retinas were cryosectioned and subsequently subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, immunofluorescence staining, and TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay. Characteristics of the explant and in vivo retinas during postnatal development were compared, including retinal structure, RPC distribution, RPC proliferation, horizontal cell migration, BC and MG differentiation, neuron maturation, microglia distribution, and retinal viability ( ).
 

## Explant preparation
Animals were subjected to inhalation anesthesia with 4% isoflurane (Lunan Better Pharmaceutical, Linyi, China), then sacrificed at P0. Both eyeballs were taken immediately after cervical dislocation and collected in cold (4°C) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 U/mL; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat# 15070-063). For each isolated eyeball, the optic nerve was removed and a cut was made along the corneal limbus under a dissecting microscope. Next, the cornea, iris and lens were removed to expose the fundus vessels, which were then carefully removed with ophthalmic tweezers, as residual fundus vessels will interfere with retinal transfer and flattening. Next, the retina was separated from the sclera to isolate intact cup-shaped retinas. We removed fundus vessels before separating the sclera, as removing fundus vessels without sclera support can easily lead to disruption or damage in the retina. The cup-shaped retina was then gently collected using a cut 1000-μL pipette and placed on a tissue culture insert (Millicell Cell Culture Insert, 30 mm, hydrophilic PTFE, 0.4 µm, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, Cat# PICM03050) with the ganglion cell layer (GCL) facing up. Lastly, the retina was carefully flattened and excess medium was removed, which allows the retina to obtain both nutrients in the culture medium and oxygen in the air (Ahmed, 2023). Because postnatal retinas have better ductility than adult retinas, they can be flattened without been clipped, which avoids a local inflammatory response. Three explants were prepared at each time point, and three eyes were prepared concurrently for in vivo control.
Special care was taken during the isolation procedure to avoid disruption or damage to the retina. The culture inserts were placed into a six-well culture plate with 1 mL of culture media per well (media components for 100 mL: 50 mL MEM [Eagle] with Glutamax-1 [Gibco, Cat# 42360-024], 23 mL Earle’s Balanced salt solution [Gibco, Cat# 24010-043], 0.65 g D-glucose [Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat# G5767], 1 mL penicillin-streptomycin [Gibco, Cat# 15070-063], 25 mL horse serum [Gibco, Cat# 26050-070]). The explants were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 and the culture medium was exchanged every day. At P1, P3, P7, and P10, the explants were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, followed by staining ( ).
 

## HE staining
The fixed explants and eyes were dehydrated once in 20% sucrose and twice in 30% sucrose (both in phosphate buffer) for cryoprotection. Then, the tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) and then cryosectioned (12 μm) on a cryostat microtome (Leica, Nussloch, Germany).
For HE staining, cryosections were rinsed with PBS to remove OCT, immersed in hematoxylin solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 4 minutes, and then rinsed in water and counterstained in eosin (Solarbio) for 4 minutes. After being rinsed with water, samples were then dehydrated in an alcohol gradient and made transparent in xylene for 5 minutes. Then, the sections were mounted with neutral resin and analyzed with an Olympus BX51 microscope equipped with an Olympus DP70 digital camera and DP-Manager software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence staining analysis
The fixed explants and eyes were prepared for frozen sectioning using the method described above. For immunofluorescence staining, cryosections were rinsed in 1× PBS to remove OCT and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes. Then, the slides were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 hour and incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were: mouse anti-SOX2 (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA, Cat# sc-365823, RRID: AB_10842165), rabbit anti-PAX6 (1:400, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat# ab195045, RRID: AB_2750924), rabbit anti-Ki67 (1:400, Abcam, Cat# ab16667, RRID: AB_302459), rabbit anti-doublecortin (DCX; 1:400, Abcam, Cat# ab18723, RRID: AB_732011), mouse anti-Ceh-10 homeodomain-containing homolog (Chx10; 1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# sc-365519, RRID: AB_10842442), mouse anti-Gl Syn (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# sc-74430, RRID: AB_1127501), rabbit anti-neuronal nuclei (NeuN; 1:400, Abcam, Cat# ab177487, RRID: AB_2532109), rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1; 1:400, Abcam, Cat# ab178846, RRID: AB_2636859), and rabbit anti-Brn3a (1:400; Abcam, Cat# ab245230, RRID: AB_2916038). Samples were then washed three times with 1× PBS for 15 minutes each, and then the cryosections were incubated with the following secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab150077, RRID: AB_2630356), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab150113, RRID: AB_2576208), Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab150080, RRID: AB_2650602), and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab150116, RRID: AB_2650601) for 2 hours at room temperature. The sections were washed again, and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:10,000, Sigma-Aldrich) was added for 10 minutes for nuclei staining. Then, the sections were sealed with Mowiol (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA, USA). Visualization was performed with an Olympus BX51 fluorescent microscope equipped with an Olympus DP70 digital camera and DP-Manager software (Olympus). Fluorescence intensity analysis and distribution analysis were performed using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012).
The number of marker-positive cells per unit area (220 μm × 200 μm) was calculated and expressed as a percentage (marker-positive cell rate (%) = number of marker-positive cells/DAPI-positive cells × 100). The cells were counted manually using ImageJ software.

## TUNEL assay
TUNEL assays were performed using the CoraLite®488 TUNEL Fluorescence Assay Kit (Cat# PF00006, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cryosections were washed in 1× PBS and permeabilized with 20 μg/mL proteinase K (Proteintech) for 25 minutes at room temperature. The TdT-mediated labeling reaction was performed for 60 minutes at 37°C using fluorescent-labeled nucleotides (Proteintech). DAPI was used to stain the nuclei. Quantification of TUNEL-positive cells was performed with the ImageJ software.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed first on all measurements to test for normal distribution. Comparisons between groups were performed using an independent samples t-test. All values are presented as mean ± standard deviation. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Retinal structure development in vivo is consistent with explants
RPCs differentiate into seven main cell types, of which non-GABAergic amacrine cells, most rod photoreceptors, BCs, and MG are specified at postnatal ages in a stereotyped order and conserved between different vertebrate retinas ( A). To characterize the process of retinal structure formation during postnatal retinal development, we collected rat retinas and explants at different time points after birth: P1, P3, P7, and P10. Then, we examined the differences of in vivo and explant retinal structure using HE staining. Retinas in both groups (in vivo and explants) exhibited a bilayer structure (neuroblast layer [NbL] and differentiated cell layer [DCL]) (Li et al., 2022)) from P1 to P3, and the complete five-layer structure (GCL, inner plexiform layer [IPL], inner nuclear layer [INL], outer plexiform layer [OPL] and outer nuclear layer [ONL]) was formed from P7 to P10. There was a slight difference in the thickness of the layers between the two groups. In vivo, cells in the ONL were densely arranged, whereas cells in the inner GCL, IPL, and INL were loosely arranged. In explants, the ONL was thicker, whereas the inner GCL, IPL and INL were thinner compared with those in vivo ( B). These differences in thickness may have resulted from the lack of ocular structure support in the explant culture, leading to the occurrence of retinal infolding at the margins of the retinal cultures (Additional Figure 1). However, these variations had no discernible impact on the overall developmental process. These results suggest that the explants develop normal retinal structure.
 

## RPC distribution in vivo is consistent with explants
Next, we aimed to evaluate the distribution of RPCs using RPC-specific markers SOX2 and PAX6, and compare in vivo retinas and explants during postnatal development. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that approximately 80% of cells in the P1 retina were positive for SOX2, both in vivo and in explants. As RPCs differentiated, the number of SOX2-positive cells decreased significantly, and there was no significant difference between in vivo and explants ( A and C). A similar decrease was observed with immunofluorescence staining of PAX6 during postnatal retinogenesis, and there was no significant difference between in vivo and explants ( D and F). The distribution of RPCs in early (P1–P3) and late (P7–P10) postnatal retina was noticeably different. In early postnatal retina, SOX2- and PAX6-positive cells were mainly distributed in the NbL, and the inner NbL retained a higher percentage of SOX2- and PAX6-positive cells compared with the outer NbL. However, in late postnatal retina, only a few SOX2- and PAX6-positive cells were observed in the INL. Furthermore, fluorescence distribution analysis showed a high degree of coincidence in the distribution of SOX2- and PAX6-positive cells between explant and in vivo retinas during postnatal retinogenesis ( B and E).
 

## RPC proliferation in vivo is consistent with explants
Similarly, we measured Ki67 expression to investigate the proliferation levels of RPCs in vivo and in explants during postnatal retinal development. Immunofluorescence staining showed that approximately 65% of cells were Ki67-positive in the P1 retina both in vivo and in explants, representing the peak of RPC proliferation. As was observed with the SOX2 and PAX6 staining, the number of Ki67-positive cells dramatically decreased after P1 as RPCs transitioned from proliferation to differentiation. Only a few Ki67-positive cells were found at P3, and they were almost absent at P7 and P10 ( A). There was no significant difference between in vivo and explants ( C). Fluorescence distribution analysis of Ki67 showed that proliferative RPCs were mainly distributed in the NbL of P1 and P3 retina, which was consistent between in vivo and explants ( B).
 

## Horizontal cell migration in vivo is consistent with explants
After assessing RPC distribution and proliferation, we aimed to compare the migration of horizontal cells by using DCX labeling during postnatal development. In early postnatal retina, immunofluorescence staining identified horizontal cells with dispersed neurites in the NbL. In late postnatal retina, horizontal cells had gradually migrated inward to the outer INL both in vivo and in explants, and the neurites changed to their typical “horizontal” orientation. The migration progress of horizontal cells was observed in the fluorescence distribution analysis of DCX. Notably, inconsistent migration was observed at P7, mainly due to the difference in ONL thickness between in vivo and explants ( A and B). Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference between horizontal cell number of in vivo and explants during postnatal development ( C).
 

## BC and MG differentiation in vivo is consistent with explants
Next, we investigated the differentiation of BCs and MG by using BC-specific marker Chx10 and MG-specific marker Gl Syn. Immunofluorescence staining showed that approximately 30% of cells were Chx10-positive, with weak expression in the P1 retina both in vivo and in explants. During postnatal retinogenesis, the number of Chx10-positive cells decreased, whereas the Chx10 protein expression per cell continuously increased over time, and the cells ultimately amassed to form multilayered BCs in the outer INL at P10. The percentage of Chx10-positive cells in explants was slightly lower than that in vivo, but the difference was not statistically significant ( A and C). Gl Syn-positive cells did not emerge until P10 both in vivo and in explants, and immunofluorescence staining showed a single layer of Gl Syn-positive cells in the middle INL. The percentages of Gl Syn-positive cells were not significantly different between in vivo and explants ( A and C). The fluorescence distributions of Chx10 in vivo and in explants demonstrated a high degree of coincidence ( B), as did those of Gl Syn ( B).
 

## Neuronal maturation in vivo is consistent with explants
We also evaluated the presence of neurons using the generic marker NeuN. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that NeuN-positive mature neurons emerged exclusively during the P7–P10 period. It was predominantly distributed in the GCL, and was also observed within the INL ( A). The fluorescence distribution analysis demonstrated a consistent pattern of neuronal maturation between the explant and in vivo retinas throughout postnatal development ( B). Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference in the quantity of mature neurons between the explant and the in vivo retinas ( C).
 

## Microglial distribution in vivo is consistent with explants
Microglia are a critical group of glial cells in the retina. Previous research indicates that microglia play important functional roles in retinal development (Silverman and Wong, 2018). We performed immunofluorescence staining using the microglia-specific marker Iba1. There were almost no Iba1-positive cells in the interlayers of the retina during early postnatal development (P1–P3). However, Iba1-positive cells were extensively distributed in the INL and GCL in both in vivo and explants during later postnatal development (P7–P10) ( A). Throughout the entire developmental process, no microglial cells were observed in the ONL. The temporal and spatial patterns of microglial emergence were consistent between the in vivo and explant retinas. Statistical analysis of Iba1-positive microglia indicated no significant differences between the explants and the in vivo retina ( B).
 

## Retinal viability in vivo and in explants
Finally, we conducted a comparative analysis between the explant and in vivo retinas using the TUNEL assay to assess viability. TUNEL-positive cells were observed during retinal development in vivo. This form of programmed cell death initiates at approximately embryonic day 12.5 and persists for the first 2 weeks after birth (Braunger et al., 2014). During the early phase of explant culture (P1–P3), the number of TUNEL-positive cells was significantly higher than that in in vivo, and the cells were primarily concentrated in the GCL. This increase may be attributed to axotomy of RGCs and surgical injuries that occurred during explant preparation. As the culture progressed to the late stage (P7–P10), the number of TUNEL-positive cells gradually decreased to a level comparable to that observed in vivo, with a predominant presence in the INL ( ). Immunofluorescence staining of the RGC-specific marker Brn3a demonstrated similar results, indicating the rapid apoptosis of RGCs during the initial phase of explant culture (Additional Figure 2).
 

## Discussion
Establishing a highly efficient and suitable experimental model is crucial to the investigation of postnatal retinal development and related diseases. The retina’s multilayered structure can be easily flattened after dissection, making organotypic retinal explant culture a popular experimental model because of its inherent characteristics (Murali et al., 2019; Schnichels et al., 2021). However, the lack of systematic and continuous comparison between in vivo retinal development and organotypic retinal explant culture makes this model controversial. In this study, we compared retinal structure maturation, RPC distribution and proliferation, horizontal cell migration, and the specification and differentiation of BCs and MG during postnatal development of in vivo and explant retinas. The results showed that RPC distribution and proliferation in the postnatal retinal developmental model were almost identical to those of the in vivo retina. Further, the migration of horizontal cells and the emergence of BCs, MG, mature neurons, and microglia were in general highly congruent in time and space between our explant cultures and in vivo retinas. Our experimental evidence shows that this postnatal developmental model achieves specification and maturation consistent with in vivo retinas, enabling long-term, systematic, and continuous observation. These findings address the lacking continuous observation and comparison between the organotypic retinal explant model and in vivo development during early postnatal retinal development, and thus enhance the accuracy and credibility of this method.
Investigating the developmental mechanisms underlying early postnatal retinal development in vivo is challenging due to the limited intervention time window. In vivo studies of major molecular players and cellular processes during eye development are highly dependent on knockout animals or gene transfection (Fang et al., 2018; Iwagawa et al., 2020). However, the application of knockout animals in postnatal retinal development studies is limited in two respects. First, a significant proportion of systemic knockouts result in embryonic lethality or embryonic eye developmental disorders, making them unsuitable for postnatal studies (Hanke-Gogokhia et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2021). Second, systemic knockout lacks specificity within the limited postnatal development time window. Although conditional knockout can address cell type-specific problems, it is time-consuming, costly, and difficult to time accurately, thereby limiting its application in postnatal development studies (Kowalchuk et al., 2018). Similarly, viral transfection has limited use, as the effective intervention time window is very narrow, and viral transfection typically takes 3–5 days to achieve maximum expression efficiency. However, our study showed that RPCs mostly cease proliferation by P3 and major cell types appeared by P7. This leads to difficulties in altering gene expression during the early postnatal period. Further, strategies like transient transfection (e.g., plasmid and siRNA) or agent injection often require multiple rounds of operations to maintain sustained intervention effects, which can lead to invasive intervention damage and local inflammation, ultimately leading to developmental abnormalities. Pretransfection in late embryos is one possible solution, as demonstrated by Doh et al. (2010) and Vancamp et al. (2019) through their successful transfection of plasmid DNA and RNAi vector into chick embryos. However, gene transfection in mammalian embryonal retina is often challenging. Organotypic retinal explant culture therefore provides a valuable tool for investigating early postnatal retinal development in vitro, as it allows researchers to focus on specific factors in the postnatal period without systemic knockout or repeated invasive intervention. Postnatal retinal explant culture is time-saving, economical, and easier to use for intervention than in vivo gene transfection or agent delivery (Serjanov et al., 2018; Bassetto et al., 2021), and allows for electrophysiological experiments (Esteban-Linares et al., 2023; Katada et al., 2023). These features make organotypic retinal explant culture more suitable for postnatal retinal developmental studies compared with in vivo studies.
Cell culture, as the simplest model system, has been widely used for investigating postnatal retinal development alongside organ cultures (Lancaster and Knoblich, 2014). A variety of cell culture systems is available, including primary cells (e.g., primary RPCs, primary RGCs, and primary MG; Gan et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023; Luo and KC, 2024), cell lines (e.g., R28 rat retinal precursor and MIO-M1 human Müller cells), organ-on-a-chip models (Haderspeck et al., 2019; Manafi et al., 2021), and organoid cultures (Mao et al., 2019). The single cell type model systems (i.e., cell lines and primary cells) are advantageous due to their ease of manipulation, and fast and high throughput capabilities. However, they are limited by their lack of representativeness and intercellular interactions, which are critical for understanding the continuous and systemic developmental processes. Organ-on-a-chip models, which are based on the three-dimensional interaction of retinal cells, allow for analyses of intercellular interactions between different cell types. However, they lack the retinal structure and local environment compared with organotypic retinal explants. Retinal organoids are also three-dimensional culture systems, grown from stem cells, and generally possess the basic retinal structure (Cora et al., 2019; Temple, 2023; Yu et al., 2023). Although they provide a valuable tool for early retinal development-related research questions, they have limitations such as highly variable tissue quality and high generation costs (Astashkina et al., 2012). Furthermore, organoids are generally unsuitable for postnatal retinal development studies because long-term culture of retinal organoids leads to unsatisfactory structural interaction (Achberger et al., 2019), and there has been no report of retinal organoids achieving postnatal ages. Therefore, using organoids for postnatal retinal development studies may pose significant challenges.
Although organotypic retinal explant culture seems to be the most suitable model for postnatal retinal development studies (Murali et al., 2019), this model has some limitations. Producing organotypic retinal explant cultures requires adequate training and experience, which is the basis for the explant’s quality and reproducibility (Belhadj et al., 2020). Because of the absence of hemoperfusion and connectivity with the brain through the optic nerve, retinal explants cannot be cultured for an extended duration, which may result in the degradation and loss of major retinal neuron types (Muller et al., 2017; Schnichels et al., 2021). Specifically, our findings from Brn3a immunofluorescence and TUNEL staining demonstrated the rapid apoptosis of RGCs during the initial phase of explant culture. In our previous study, we demonstrated that preserving the optic nerve during retinal explant culture partially extends the viability of ganglion cells (Hu et al., 2023). However, this approach only delays RGC death to a certain extent, and the eventual fate of cell death remains unaffected. In addition, the absence of a vascular system in explant culture inevitably gives rise to issues such as retinal hypoxia, nutrient deficiency, and accumulation of metabolic waste. The current conventional approach to explant culture partially mitigates tissue hypoxia by exposing the explant surface to air. However, the long-term culture yields suboptimal results, and the exposed retina becomes more prone to surface cell death due to desiccation during procedures like medium replacement. A recently published highly complex approach that uses microfluidic systems to form self-assembling vascular-like structures holds potential as a feasible approach to address the lack of vascular perfusion in explant culture (Paek et al., 2019). Organotypic retinal explant cultures also lack ocular structural support and “passive stretching” of the retinal tissue by the growing eyeball (Kuhrt et al., 2012), which leads to thickness differences between retinal cultures and the in vivo retina. Overall, the inner GCL and IPL in explants collapsed because of the lack of structural support, whereas the ONL thickness showed little change. In vivo, the greatest amount of stretching occurred in the ONL, which led to thinning of the ONL during P7 to P10. This lack of passive stretching also disabled retinal expansion in explants. Instead, retinal infolding was observed at the margin of the retinal cultures. These limitations should be taken into consideration when using this model for experimental studies.
In conclusion, the organotypic retinal explant culture model provides a valuable experimental platform for studying postnatal retinal development. It closely mimics in vivo retinal development in terms of RPC proliferation, cell migration, and the specification and differentiation of various retinal cell types.
