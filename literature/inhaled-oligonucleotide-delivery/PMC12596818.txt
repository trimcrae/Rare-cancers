Endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress are central drivers of cardiometabolic diseases such as diabetes, where sustained ROS burden accelerates EC senescence and barrier failure. These findings suggest its potential role in combating vascular aging and atherosclerosis; however, the underlying mechanisms remain partially understood. In this study, we investigated whether TZ can prevent atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice fed a high-cholesterol diet and aimed to elucidate the mechanisms involved. Our results showed that TZ significantly reduced plaque size, EC senescence, vascular permeability, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, effectively inhibiting atherosclerosis independently of α1-AR signaling. In cultured primary human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs), TZ inhibited EC senescence via the Pgk1/Hsp90 pathway. It enhanced the interaction between Hsp90 and the antioxidant enzyme peroxiredoxin 1 (Prdx1), leading to lower ROS levels—a key driver of cellular senescence. These findings were confirmed in atherosclerotic ApoE−/− mice. Furthermore, senescent ECs exhibited increased levels of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and decreased levels of angiostatin, contributing to higher vascular permeability and exacerbating atherosclerosis. TZ effectively reversed these changes. Overall, our study demonstrates that TZ primarily alleviates EC senescence and atherosclerosis through the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 pathway, highlighting Pgk1 activation as a strategy that may also mitigate endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress in broader cardiometabolic contexts (e.g., diabetes), suggesting that TZ is a promising senomorphic agent for treating vascular aging and atherosclerosis in clinical settings and that Pgk1-targeted interventions could have implications beyond atherosclerosis.

## Introduction
Atherosclerosis, a common pathological basis for various cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, and acute myocardial infarction, is characterized by plaque accumulation within the arterial walls. Aging is a significant risk factor for atherosclerosis. Vascular aging, driven by cellular senescence is a key risk factor for atherosclerosis [ – ]. Senescence of ECs, in particular, is observed early in atherosclerosis and plays a significant role in its progression [ , – ]. Senescent ECs secrete ROS and exhibit a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which promotes atherosclerosis [ ]. Several SASP factors activate monocytes and facilitate their infiltration into the subendothelial space [ ]. Additionally, senescent ECs compromise endothelial integrity by downregulating key cell junction proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5. This disruption increases endothelial permeability, promoting the accumulation of oxidized LDL, which in turn accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis [ – ]. Therapeutic strategies targeting senescent cells, including senomorphic drugs that reduce senescence and senolytic drugs that eliminate these cells, have demonstrated protective effects against atherosclerosis in animal models [ , , ]. However, further validation is required before these approaches can be applied clinically [ , ]. Therefore, identifying novel, safe, and effective agents that target both cellular senescence and atherosclerosis is a key objective in medical research.
TZ, an α1-AR antagonist, is commonly used to treat hypertension and benign prostatic hyperplasia [ ]. Interestingly, a study has demonstrated that TZ confers protective effects on endothelial function, as evidenced by improvements in basal and flow-mediated dilation of the brachial artery measured by ultrasound [ ]. Notably, this vascular benefit has been observed with quinazoline-based α1-AR antagonists, such as alfuzosin (AZ) and TZ, but not with tamsulosin, a non-quinazoline antagonist. This suggests that quinazoline-based drugs may have distinct molecular targets compared to non-quinazoline drugs [ ]. Our earlier research demonstrated that quinazoline drugs, including TZ and AZ, can interact with Pgk1, a key glycolytic enzyme involved in ATP production. Specifically, TZ was found to activate Pgk1 and, through its interaction with Hsp90, enhance Hsp90’s ATPase activity. This activation enhances cellular stress resistance, leading to the inhibition of apoptosis, a reduction in ROS levels, mitigation of cellular senescence, and prevention of neurodegeneration [ – ]. Moreover, recent studies have reported that TZ can improve arterial stiffness, a key feature of aging arteries [ ]. These findings suggest that TZ may prevent EC senescence and reduce the progression of atherosclerosis. Building on this, we aim to establish a causal link between EC senescence and atherosclerosis development, potentially positioning TZ as a novel therapeutic agent in vascular medicine.

## Animals
All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility at the Animal Center of Capital Medical University. For the early-stage atherosclerosis mouse model, male WT mice (Charles River, 213) and ApoE−/− mice (Cyagen, C001067) on a C57BL/6 background were maintained on a standard, irradiated, non-atherogenic chow diet (BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE CO., LTD, 1032) until they reached 4 months of age. Subsequently, the ApoE−/− mice were subjected to a high-cholesterol diet (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd., XT108C) for 3 months [ ], while simultaneously receiving either TZ (0.22 μM/kg/day), Tam (0.028 μM/kg/day), TZ-md (0.22 μM/kg/day), or PBS through drinking water. For the late-stage atherosclerosis mouse model, 2-month-old ApoE−/− mice was started fed with high cholesterol diet until 12 months of age, while simultaneously receiving either TZ (0.152 μM/kg/day) or PBS via drinking water. Food and water were replaced every 3 days. High-cholesterol diet groups were randomly assigned. Body weight was monitored weekly. Mice were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital anesthesia (50 mg/kg), and samples of blood, hearts, and aortas were collected. The study adhered to the guidelines approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (AEEI-2021-129). All procedures were conducted in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. To euthanasia animals, the following protocol was applied. The mouse was placed in an induction chamber connected to a vaporizer delivering 3–5% isoflurane in oxygen at a flow rate of 0.5–1.0 L/min. The animal was monitored closely during this process, which typically took 2–3 min. Successful induction of anesthesia was confirmed by the loss of the righting reflex, indicating sufficient anesthesia. Once anesthetized, the mouse was carefully transferred to the experimental setup for blood pressure measurement. A nose cone connected to the vaporizer was used to maintain anesthesia, with the isoflurane concentration reduced to 1.5–2.5% in oxygen to ensure stable anesthesia. After completing the experimental procedures, the mouse was euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation. This dual approach was employed to ensure minimizing pain and distress.

## Cell cultures
Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Meisen CELL, CTCC-0804-PC) were cultured in endothelial basal medium (ScienCell, 1001) supplemented with endothelial cell growth supplement (ScienCell, 1052), penicillin/streptomycin solution (ScienCell, 0503), and fetal bovine serum (ScienCell, 0025). Human primary aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs; Meisen CELL, CTCC-186-HUM) were cultured in smooth muscle cell basal medium (ScienCell, 1101) supplemented with smooth muscle cell growth supplement (ScienCell, 1152), penicillin/streptomycin solution (ScienCell, 0503), and fetal bovine serum (ScienCell, 0010). Both cell types were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2.
To induce cellular senescence, cells were treated with 100 nM doxorubicin (Dox) or 100 μM H2O2 for 24 h. Concurrently, 10 μM TZ/TZ-md, 1.25 μM Tam, or PBS was co-administered at the senescence induction. After the 24-h induction phase, culture medium was replaced every 48 h until day 7 post-induction.
Separately, to model endothelial senescence under atherogenic conditions, HUVECs were exposed to 100 μg/ml oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL; Yiyuanbiotech, YB-002) for 48 h co-administration of the same experimental compounds (10 μM TZ/TZ-md, 1.25 μM Tam, or PBS) throughout the exposure period.

## SA-β-gal staining
Cellular senescence was detected using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0602) following manufacturer’s protocols with modifications based on established methodologies [ ]. Senescent cells were stained in blue and images were captured with light microscope (Nikon, Japan). The senescence index was calculated as: (Number of SA-β-Gal–positive cells/Total number of cells) × 100%. For quantitative analysis, the average senescence index of six random non-overlapping fields was used to represent the senescence index of the sample.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The concentrations of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in cellular supernatants were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits. The specific kits used were: IL-1β (E-EL-H0149), IL-6 (E-EL-H6156), and TNF-α (E-EL-H0109), all sourced from Elabscience. Supernatants were collected from HUVECs, and the cell numbers were determined. The ELISA procedure was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In summary, samples were added to wells coated with specific antibodies and incubated in a humidified box at 37 °C for 90 min. The solution was then discarded and the plate was washed 3 times. Then HRP-labeled secondary antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Afterward, the plate was washed 5 times and incubated with substrate solution for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

## Western blot
Cells or aortas were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, P1006) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, P1049). Protein concentrations were determined using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kits (Meilunbio, MA0082). The samples were then mixed with 5 × SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime, P0015L) and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated using 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, 03010040001). The membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBS-T (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies, were subsequently applied. The membranes were washed 5 times with TBS-T. Immunoreactive bands were detected using ECL western blot reagents (Meilunbio, MA0186) and visualized with Fusion FX SPECTRA system (VILBER BIO IMAGING, France). The semi-quantitative analysis of the Western blot results was conducted using ImageJ software to measure the intensities of the protein bands. The band densities were normalized to the background, and the relative optical density ratio was determined by comparing the intensities to GAPDH. Each experiment was replicated a minimum of 3 times.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Approximately 2000 cells were seeded in 96-well plates with 100 µl of medium per well. After 24 h of culture, different concentrations of TZ were applied. Following another 24 h, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution (Meilunbio, MA0225-2) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h in the dark. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## Immunoprecipitation
Cells or aortas were lysed in WB/IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) with added protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, P1006) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, P1049). The protein lysates were incubated with specific antibodies at 4℃ overnight, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, HY-K0202) for 4 h. The immunocomplexes were washed 6 times and then boiled in 2 × SDS loading buffer for 5 min. The co-precipitates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by western blot with specific antibodies.

## Silver staining
Silver staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo, 24,612). After staining, the gel was photographed using an imaging system (Biorad).

## Mass spectrometry analysis
The bands of interest were excised, subjected to in-gel trypsin digestion, and dried. The peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid, and 2 μl samples were loaded onto a C18 column. Samples were eluted over 60 min with gradients of 5–80% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.6 μl/min. Mass spectrometry data were acquired using a Triple-TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (Sciex, United States) equipped with a Nanospray III source (Sciex, United States) and analyzed with ProteinPilot software (V5.0, Sciex). The human UniProt Reference Proteome database was used for data analysis.

## DHE staining
Intracellular ROS was measured using Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining (Beyotime, S0063). Fresh brachiocephalic arteries were quickly frozen, sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a cryotome (Leica CM1950), and stained with 5 μM DHE and 1 × Hoechst for 30 min at 37℃. Representative images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8 X). Superoxide-specific oxidation product 2-hydroxyethidium (2-OH-E+) was detected at optimal wavelengths: excitation 490 nm, emission 560–570 nm [ ].

## Transduction siRNA and adenovirus
siRNAs targeting human Pgk1 (siPgk1), Prdx1 (siPrdx1), and non-targeting negative control (siControl) were synthesized by RIBOBIO. All siRNAs were transfected at 50 nM final concentration using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778-075). Additionally, human Pgk1 adenovirus (Ad-Pgk1), Prdx1 adenovirus (Ad-Prdx1) and negative control adenovirus (Ad-Control) were constructed and packaged by HanBio (Shanghai, China). All adenoviruses were applied at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50, with 6 μg/ml polybrene (HanBio, HB-PB-1000).

## Atherosclerotic lesion analysis
After being fed a high-cholesterol diet and given different drinking water for either 3 or 5 months, the mice were fasted for 14 h, anesthetized, and euthanized. Aortas and aortic valves were prepared as described previously [ ]. To assess aortic valve lesion areas, hearts were embedded in OCT compound and we systematically sampled the aortic root following anatomical landmarks to ensure full spatial representation. Sections began at the aortic root origin, specifically when all three aortic valve leaflets were simultaneously visible in cross-section, and ended when the coronary artery ostia disappeared distally. From this defined region, six serial sections were collected at 100 μm intervals (one section every 10 slices, covering a total depth of 600 μm). This method ensured that the sections were evenly spaced and representative of nearly entire aortic root region. Each section was stained with Oil Red O to visualize neutral lipids and counterstained with hematoxylin to highlight nuclei. Images of the plaques were captured using a microscope (Nikon, Japan). The area of each plaque was measured using ImageJ software, and the average plaque area across the six sections was calculated to represent the aortic valve lesion burden for each mouse. For the analysis of lesion areas in aortas, aortas were stained using the modified Oil Red O Staining Kit (Beyotime, C0158S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained aortas were placed in a dish with PBS, and images were captured using a high-resolution camera. These images were then analyzed with ImageJ software to determine the lesion areas as the percentage of the Oil Red O positive area relative to the total surface area of the aorta.

## EdU staining
EdU staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, C0071S). Briefly, cells subjected to different treatments were incubated with 1 × EdU working buffer for 3 h, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After washing 3 times with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated with Click reaction buffer for 30 min at room temperature, washed, stained with Hoechst to visualize nuclei, and observed using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8 X).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA. They were then incubated with the specified primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by a 1-h incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, P0131). Images were acquired using a Leica SP8 X confocal laser scanning microscopy. Representative images were selected based on their clarity and their capacity to accurately reflect the group mean across all available data. Quantitative analysis of immunofluorescence was performed using ImageJ software, as previously described [ ].

## Ultrasound analyses
The image of the LCCA utilized an ultra-high-resolution color doppler ultrasound system (Vevo 2100, FUJIFILM Visualsonics, USA) with the array transducer MS 550D at a center frequency of 40 MHz. Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane during the imaging session. The PWV was obtained using VEVO LAB software. The EKV two-dimensional dynamic image was analyzed by VEVO VASC software, and indicators representing vascular compliance (diameter/area percentage spread and global radial strain) were obtained. PWV was calculated by the following formula: Length of LCCA/Time of blood flowing through the LCCA. All the measurements were repeated 3 times. All the images were analyzed in a double-blind mode.

## Blood pressure measurements
Blood pressure was measured using an intelligent noninvasive sphygmomanometer (Softron Biotechnology Co., Ltd, BP-2010A) [ ]. To facilitate animal handling during the measurement, we refined the protocol by incorporating isoflurane anesthesia prior to blood pressure measurements. After anesthesia induction, the mice were placed in a preheated chamber to stabilize their physiological state. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was recorded, with six consecutive measurements taken per mouse. The average of these six measurements was calculated to determine the mean SBP for each individual mouse.

## Total hydrogen peroxide-scavenging enzyme activity
In this study, the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in cell samples was measured using the Beyotime S0051 assay kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The results represent the total H2O2-scavenging enzyme activity in cells, which reflects the cumulative effects of multiple enzymes, including Prdx, catalase (CAT), GPx, and other relevant enzymes acting in concert.

## Hydrogen peroxide analysis
Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) concentration was quantified utilizing the Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0038). A mixture of cell or tissue homogenates and diluted serum was mixed with the solution containing Fe2+ and xylenol orange and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Post incubation, absorbance at 560 nm was measured. The H2O2 concentrations were ascertained by reference to standard curves, adjusted for individual protein concentrations or equal volume and identical dilution. For ROS detection, HUVECs were exposed to 10 µM DCFH-DA (MedChemExpress, HY-D0940) for 30 min at 37℃ in endothelial basal medium (ScienCell, 1001). The resultant DCF fluorescence, indicative of ROS, was examined using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880), with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 488 nm and 517 nm, respectively.

## Blood vessel permeability assessment
Evans blue dye (MACKLIN, E6135) (30 mg/kg) was intravenously injected into the tail vein of mice. After 30 min, the mice were euthanized and perfused with PBS. The aortas were then excised, photographed, air-dried, and weighed. Each tissue sample was incubated with 500 μl formamide for 24 h at 55 °C to extract the Evans blue dye. The dye concentration was quantified by measuring absorbance at 620 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). Optical density readings were converted to milligrams of dye per milligram of tissue using a standard curve for Evans blue. Statistical analysis was performed to assess significant differences in vascular permeability [ ].
Aortas were cleaned and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. After washing with PBS, the descending aortas were frozen and sectioned into 10 μm cryosections, which were then counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red (Beyotime, C0151). Images were captured from at least 10 randomly selected sections. ImageJ software was used to quantify the portion of the intima stained by Evans blue. The ratio of dye-stained length to the total intimal circumference was used as an indicator of endothelial permeability [ , ].

## Glycolysis and mitochondrial stress measured by XFe-24 Seahorse assay
A Seahorse XFe analyzer (XFe-24, Seahorse Bioscience, Billerica, MA, USA) was used to measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). The HUVECs were seeded in XFe 24-well plates (Seahorse Bioscience), Assay medium was prepared by supplementing Seahorse XFe Base Medium (Seahorse Bioscience) with 2 mM L-glutamine for a Glycolysis Stress Test assay or 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM pyruvate and 10 mM glucose for a Mito Stress Test assay (Sigma). pH was adjusted to 7.4 at 37℃. Probes (Seahorse Bioscience) were hydrated in the calibrant (Seahorse Bioscience) in a non-CO2 incubator at 37℃ overnight. Cells were cultured with assay medium and kept in a non-CO2 incubator at 37℃ for 1 h before analysis. Glucose, oligomycin and 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG) were used for Glycolysis Stress Test while Oligomycin, FCCP and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A were used for Mito Stress Test.

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software) with data expressed as mean ± SEM. Prior to parametric testing, all datasets underwent validation of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Brown-Forsythe test). For normally distributed data with equal variance: unpaired Student’s t-test analyzed two-group comparisons; one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test analyzed single-factor multi-group comparisons. Multi-factorial designs employed: two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons for two independent factors; three-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons for three independent factors. Normally distributed data with unequal variance underwent Brown-Forsythe/Welch ANOVA with Tamhane’s T2 post hoc testing. Non-normally distributed data utilized nonparametric alternatives: Mann–Whitney test for two independent groups and Kruskal–Wallis test for ≥ 3 groups. Significance thresholds in figures denote: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001; “ns”, not significant (P ≥ 0.05).

## Results

## TZ alleviates atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice
To assess the efficacy of TZ in preventing atherosclerosis, in the early-stage atherosclerosis mouse model, four-month-old ApoE−/− mice were fed a high-cholesterol diet for three months to induce atherosclerosis. Concurrently, their drinking water was supplemented with either PBS or various pharmacological agents (Fig. A). The treatment groups included TZ (0.22 μM/kg/day), TZ-md (0.22 μM/kg/day, a modified compound of TZ that does not bind to α1-AR but retains Pgk1 targeting [ ], and Tamsulosin (Tam, 0.028 μM/kg/day), an α1-AR antagonist that does not target Pgk1. Using Oil Red O staining to assess neutral lipid content in aortas and aortic valve cross-sections, we found that both TZ and TZ-md reduced atherosclerotic lesion area, while Tam did not (Fig.  A–C). Ultrasound imaging of the left common carotid artery (LCCA) showed that treatment with TZ and TZ-md, but not Tam, improved arterial function, as evidenced by decreased pulse wave velocity (PWV) and improved vascular reactivity (measured as percent change in diameter, area, and global radial strain) (Fig.  D–G). Importantly, TZ treatment did not affect body weight or serum glucose and lipid profiles, suggesting that TZ may directly target the vasculature to alleviate atherosclerosis (Fig. B and D). The comparable anti-atherosclerotic efficacy of TZ and TZ-md, together with the ineffectiveness of Tam, indicates that TZ’s benefits are independent of α1-AR. Consistent with this interpretation, although ApoE−/− mice typically develop progressive systolic hypertension (SBP) over time [ ], TZ and Tam both lowered SBP in our study, whereas TZ-md did not (Fig.  H). These findings imply that while Tam’s α1-AR antagonism lowers SBP but fails to attenuate atherosclerosis in this mouse model. In a more advanced model of atherosclerosis, 2-month-old ApoE−/− mice were fed with high cholesterol diet until 12 months of age while simultaneously receiving either TZ (0.152 μM/kg/day) or PBS via drinking water. (Fig. E). Oil Red O staining of the aortic arch revealed that TZ effectively inhibited plaque growth (Fig. F and G). Ultrasound imaging of the LCCA also demonstrated that TZ treatment decreased PWV (Fig. H). Collectively, these results confirm that TZ exerts potent anti-atherosclerotic effects through α1-AR-independent mechanisms. 
In atherogenesis mouse models, senescent cells, identified by Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining, were primarily found within atherosclerotic plaques, with senescence first appearing in the EC layer [ , , , ]. Our results confirmed this pattern. Treatment with TZ and TZ-md significantly reduced SA-β-Gal staining in both the plaques and EC layer, whereas Tam showed no such effect (Fig. I and J). This reduction in senescence was supported by a decrease in P21 levels at both the protein and transcriptional levels (Figs.  K and I). Since EC senescence is linked to a pro-inflammatory state [ , ], treatment with TZ and TZ-md, but not Tam, led to decreased expression of inflammatory cytokines, adhesion molecules, and matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP3) (Fig. I). These findings suggest that TZ may prevent atherosclerosis primarily by reducing EC senescence, likely via a mechanism independent of α1-AR signaling.

## TZ alleviates senescence in vascular ECs
Cellular senescence is classified into stress-induced premature senescence (SIPS) and replicative senescence, with SIPS often triggered by DNA damage [ ]. To investigate SIPS in primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), we exposed the cells to a subtoxic dose of doxorubicin (Dox) for 24 h, then refreshed the medium every 48 h until day 7. Treatment with TZ significantly reduced the expression of the senescence marker P21, achieving optimal effects at a concentration of 10 µM (Figs.  A and A). Additionally, TZ decreased levels of the senescence marker P53 at both protein and mRNA levels, while P16 expression remained unchanged (Fig.  B and S2B). SA-β-Gal staining confirmed a significant reduction in SA-β-Gal–positive cells following TZ treatment (Fig.  C). Furthermore, TZ enhanced cell proliferation under senescent conditions, as evidenced by increased levels of the proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and a higher percentage of EdU-positive proliferating cells (Figs.  D and C). TZ also reduced mRNA and protein levels of several SASP genes, including IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM1 (Fig. D and E). To further validate these findings across diverse senescence models, HUVECs were subjected to senescence induction by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), or serial passaging to induce replicative senescence. In these senescence models, treatment with TZ consistently decreased the number of SA-β-Gal-positive cells and effectively reduced P21 levels (Fig.  F–L). However, in primary human aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) subjected to Dox-induced senescence, TZ did not exhibit significant protective effects, indicating that its anti-senescence activity may be cell type-specific (Fig. M). 
We also assessed the effects of TZ-md and Tam on EC senescence. TZ-md effectively replicated the anti-senescence properties of TZ, whereas Tam showed no significant effects (Figs. E and F, H–J and N). Senescent ECs compromise vascular integrity and increase permeability, contributing to oxidized LDL accumulation and exacerbating atherosclerosis [ ]. Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), a key regulator of vascular permeability, is upregulated in senescent ECs [ ]. VEGFA disrupts tight junctions by downregulating proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5, thereby increasing EC monolayer permeability [ ]. To investigate the functional impact of TZ and TZ-md on this process, we evaluated VEGFA protein levels in senescent HUVECs. Our results show that VEGFA levels were elevated in senescent HUVECs, and treatment with TZ and TZ-md significantly reduces this increase, while Tam has no significant effect (Fig.  G). Western blot analysis revealed that tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5) were downregulated in senescent HUVECs, but treatment with TZ and TZ-md restored their expression levels, in contrast to the lack of effect observed with Tam (Fig.  H).
Collectively, these findings suggest that the anti-senescence effects of TZ are more pronounced in ECs, likely through mechanisms independent of α1-AR.

## TZ activates Pgk1/Hsp90 axis to inhibit senescence in vascular ECs
Our previous research established that TZ activates phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (Pgk1), which co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) with heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90) and boosts Hsp90 ATPase activity [ – ]. This activation cascade significantly augments cellular stress resistance by reducing apoptosis, ROS levels, cellular senescence, and neurodegeneration processes. Crucially, these cytoprotective effects are abrogated upon pharmacological inhibition of Hsp90 ATPase activity [ – ]. To determine whether the anti-senescence effect of TZ specifically requires the Pgk1/Hsp90 pathway, we first assessed Pgk1 expression. Notably, neither Dox nor TZ treatment altered Pgk1 protein levels (Figs.  A and A). However, Pgk1 knockdown significantly abolished the anti-senescence effects of TZ. This was evidenced by a marked increase in elevated P21 levels and SA-β-Gal activity (Fig.  A–C). Conversely, Pgk1 overexpression via adenoviral infection effectively mitigated Dox-induced senescence in HUVECs (Fig.  D–G). Subsequently, we explored the role of Hsp90 using 17-AAG, a specific inhibitor of Hsp90 ATPase activity. Inhibition of Hsp90 significantly attenuated the anti-senescence effect of TZ, as demonstrated by increased SA-β-Gal staining and elevated P21 levels in HUVECs (Fig.  H–J). These results collectively suggest that the anti-senescence effects of TZ in vascular ECs depend on the functional integrity of both Pgk1 and Hsp90. 
Considering Pgk1’s established function in glucose metabolism, we used the Seahorse assay to investigate whether TZ influences glycolysis or mitochondrial respiration in senescent HUVECs. The results showed that TZ did not affect these metabolic pathways (Fig. B and C), indicating that its anti-senescence effect is likely independent of modulating glycolysis or mitochondrial respiration.

## TZ enhances the interaction between Hsp90 and Prdx1 to mitigate cellular senescence
Hsp90, as a chaperone protein, regulates hundreds of client proteins involved in various cellular processes [ ]. To identify Hsp90-associated client proteins involved in anti-senescence mechanisms, we performed a pull-down assay using an Hsp90 antibody. Silver staining revealed an enriched protein band following TZ treatment (Fig.  A), which was subsequently identified as peroxiredoxin 1 (Prdx1) via mass spectrometry (Fig.  B). Prdx1, a member of the peroxiredoxin family, is known for its role in mitigating senescence by neutralizing ROS-induced cellular damage [ – ]. Co-IP assays using antibodies against Hsp90 and Prdx1 demonstrated that TZ treatment increased the interaction between these two proteins (Fig. A). The anti-senescence effect of TZ was abolished when Prdx1 siRNA was expressed in HUVECs. This was evidenced by elevated levels of P21 protein and mRNA, along with increased SA-β-gal activity (Figs. C, D, and B). Conversely, overexpression of Prdx1 conferred an anti-senescence effect (Figs. E, F, and C). Interestingly, neither TZ nor Dox treatment altered the protein levels of Prdx1, suggesting that their effects may be mediated through modulation of Prdx1 enzymatic activity rather than its expression (Fig. A). To further investigate this, we measured the H2O2 decomposition capacity in cells, which reflects the total H2O2-scavenging enzyme activity, including contributions from Prdx, catalase (CAT), GPx, and other relevant enzymes. Under Dox treatment, the total H2O2-scavenging enzyme activity was significantly diminished compared to the control group (Fig.  G), indicating impaired antioxidant defense capacity in senescent cells. However, treatment with TZ markedly attenuated the Dox-induced reduction in total H2O2-scavenging activity (Fig.  G), further supporting that TZ enhances cellular antioxidant defenses. Consistent with these findings, DHE staining revealed that TZ/TZ-md—but not Tam—suppressed Dox-induced superoxide accumulation, confirming enhanced antioxidant functionality (Fig. D). To further assess the role of Prdx1 in this process, given the lack of a specific Prdx1 activity assay kit, we indirectly evaluated Prdx1 activity by measuring intracellular H2O2 levels. Dox treatment resulted in a significant increase in intracellular H2O2 levels compared to the control group, an effect that was substantially mitigated by TZ treatment (Fig.  H). However, this antioxidative effect of TZ was abolished by Prdx1 siRNA, indicating that TZ’s H2O2-reducing effect is predominantly mediated through Prdx1 (Fig.  H). Additionally, intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), a probe that produces fluorescent DCF when oxidized by ROS [ ]. TZ treatment reduced DCF fluorescence in senescent cells, indicating decreased ROS levels. However, this effect was abolished by Prdx1 siRNA or the Prdx1 inhibitor H7 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. I and J). These findings suggest that TZ maintains Prdx1’s enzymatic function through the Pgk1/Hsp90 pathway without altering Prdx1 protein levels. 
In conclusion, TZ primarily mitigates senescence in ECs by activating the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing Prdx1 enzymatic activity and reducing ROS levels. However, VSMCs express lower levels of Pgk1, Hsp90, and Prdx1 compared to HUVECs (Fig. E). This reduced expression likely accounts for the diminished sensitivity and efficacy of TZ in VSMCs.

## TZ activates Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 axis to prevent vascular leakage in ApoE−/− mice
To investigate the role of TZ in activating the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 signaling axis and its anti-atherosclerotic effects in vivo, we first evaluated baseline expression of pathway components. Comparative immunoblot analysis revealed no significant alterations in the protein levels of Pgk1, Hsp90, or Prdx1 in either ApoE−/− model mice or drug-treated cohorts relative to wild-type (WT) controls (Fig.  A and A). We next assessed functional activation of the axis through protein–protein interactions. Co-IP assays using anti-Hsp90 antibody for immunoprecipitation, followed by immunoblotting for Prdx1, demonstrated a significant augmentation of the Hsp90-Prdx1 interaction following treatment with TZ or its derivative TZ-md (Fig.  A). In contrast, Tam administration elicited no significant increase in this interaction (Fig.  A). Furthermore, we assessed ROS levels in the aortas using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. Elevated ROS levels were observed in ApoE−/− mice. Notably, treatment with TZ and TZ-md effectively reduced ROS levels, whereas Tam had no significant effect (Fig. B and C). Similar trends were observed for H2O2 levels in both aortic tissue and serum (Fig.  D). Previous studies have shown that VEGFA is upregulated in ECs in ApoE−/− mice [ ]. Upregulation of VEGFA and downregulation of angiostatin are known to promote vascular permeability [ , ]. Consistent with these findings, we observed elevated VEGFA levels in ECs within the vascular tissue of ApoE−/− mice (Fig. B). Treatment with TZ and TZ-md significantly reduced VEGFA levels, while Tam had no significant effect (Fig. B and C). By Western blot analysis, we confirmed that the upregulation of VEGFA and the downregulation of angiostatin in the vasculature of ApoE−/− mice were reversed by treatment with TZ and TZ-md, whereas Tam did not produce significant changes (Fig. E and F). We also examined the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5, which are regulated by VEGFA and are crucial for maintaining vascular integrity [ ]. Reduced expression of these proteins is associated with increased vascular leakage in ApoE−/− mice [ ]. Consistent with this, we observed downregulation of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5 in ApoE−/− mice (Fig.  G). However, treatments with TZ and TZ-md prevented this decline and improved tight junction integrity, whereas Tam treatment showed no impact (Fig.  G). These findings suggest that TZ may reduce vascular permeability by increasing angiostatin and decreasing VEGFA levels. To further assess vascular permeability, we conducted Evans blue staining. ApoE−/− mice exhibited a significant increase in Evans blue dye deposition within the aortic intima, indicative of enhanced vascular leakage (Fig.  H–J). Treatment with TZ and TZ-md significantly reduced Evans blue deposition, indicating decreased vascular permeability, while Tam treatment had no effect (Fig.  H–J). Collectively, these findings suggest that TZ exerts protective effects against atherosclerosis by activating the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 pathway. This aligns with previous studies and supports the therapeutic potential of TZ observed in humans [ , ]. 

## Discussion

## Therapeutic potential of TZ in atherosclerosis
The therapeutic potential of TZ for atherosclerosis has gained attention due to its vascular protective effects [ , ]. In ApoE−/− mice, administration of TZ was shown to primarily inhibit EC senescence, reduce vascular leakage, suppress plaque formation, and improve vascular function. Mechanistically, TZ activates Pgk1, which enhances the interaction between Hsp90 and Prdx1, thereby reducing ROS levels. Furthermore, activation of Hsp90 suppresses the expression of SASP genes and downregulates VEGFA in senescent ECs, potentially preventing the progression of atherosclerosis. Our data suggest that senescent ECs increase vascular leakage and play a crucial role in the development of atherosclerosis. Consistent with these findings, a prior study showed that targeted expression of a progeria-associated mutant protein in ECs accelerated vascular aging and promoted atherosclerosis in mice [ ]. This supports the link between EC senescence and the development of atherosclerosis.

## Mechanistic independence from α1-AR signaling
Although TZ is a known α1-adrenergic receptor (α1-AR) antagonist, its anti-senescence effects are mechanistically independent of α1-AR blockade. This is supported by the lack of effect with tamsulosin (another α1-AR antagonist) and the preserved protection with a modified, non–BP-lowering analog (TZ-md). Instead, TZ’s activity requires Pgk1: Pgk1 knockdown abrogated TZ’s anti-senescence effect, whereas Pgk1 overexpression alone mimicked it. Consistent with this scaffold-driven biology, alfuzosin—another quinazoline-based α1-AR antagonist—has been associated with endothelial benefits, suggesting quinazoline chemistry may contribute to vascular protection beyond α1-AR antagonism [ ]. In line with this mechanism, our data also implicate the Hsp90–Prdx1 complex as a critical mediator, consistent with prior reports of Hsp90’s anti-aging roles [ ]. Interpretation of studies using Hsp90 inhibitors should be cautious, as these agents frequently trigger compensatory responses (e.g., Hsp70 induction) [ – ].

## Blood pressure considerations
In ApoE−/− mice, systolic BP is modestly elevated relative to WT, and both TZ and tamsulosin lowered BP in our hands. However, only TZ reduced atherosclerosis and endothelial senescence, and the non–BP-lowering analog TZ-md reproduced these vascular benefits. Thus, the protective effects are unlikely to be explained by BP reduction alone and are better attributed to Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 pathway activation. ApoE deficiency itself has not been directly linked to sustained catecholamine excess; rather, comorbid metabolic and inflammatory stressors may secondarily activate the sympathetic nervous system, as suggested by elevated plasma catecholamines in coronary artery disease, which ultimately contributes to a rise in blood pressure [ ]. Nonetheless, the simplest explanation for TZ’s BP-lowering in ApoE−/− mice is its direct blockade of α1-adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle—reducing vasoconstriction—without requiring changes in overall (systemic) catecholamine levels [ ].

## Prdx1 and ROS in anti-senescence mechanisms
Prdx1 plays a critical role in scavenging ROS, which is a hallmark of aging and contributes to cellular senescence [ ]. Studies have shown that Prdx1 protects telomeres from ROS-induced damage, preventing senescence [ , ]. Mice deficient in Prdx1 exhibit higher ROS levels and shortened lifespans [ , ]. Our data show that overexpression of Prdx1 replicates TZ’s anti-senescence effects, while Prdx1 knockdown abolishes these effects. Previous research demonstrated that Prdx1 deletion exacerbates atherosclerosis progression by activating ECs, emphasizing the importance of Prdx1’s antioxidant capacity in protecting against vascular aging [ ].

## Regulation of VEGFA and angiostatin
VEGFA is a potent angiogenic and vascular permeability factor that promotes pathological vascular changes, including in ApoE−/− mice [ ]. For example, short-term gene transfer of human VEGFA into the vasculature accelerated atherosclerosis [ ]; and focal overexpression of VEGFA in advanced atherosclerotic plaques increased macrophage infiltration and lipid content, enhancing plaque expansion [ ]. In contrast, angiostatin, an endogenous angiogenesis inhibitor, stabilizes vascular integrity by inhibiting EC proliferation [ ] and may counteract VEGFA by inhibiting its transcription [ ]. Both TZ and TZ-md, as activators of Pgk1 [ , ], upregulate angiostatin and downregulate VEGFA in ApoE−/− mice, thereby inhibiting vascular leakage.

## Potential additional vascular benefits of TZ and study limitations
In addition to its anti-senescence effects, TZ may exert beneficial effects on atherosclerosis by enhancing nitric oxide (NO) production. This could occur through Hsp90-mediated stabilization of Akt, which promotes eNOS phosphorylation and activity [ , ]. TZ has also been reported to reduce platelet aggregation and slightly improve lipid and glucose metabolism in diabetic patients [ , , ], although we did not observe these metabolic changes in ApoE−/− mice. Such discrepancies may reflect cell-type-specific differences in metabolic reliance—for example, ECs depend more heavily on glycolysis than neurons or other cell types [ ].
Our study has several limitations. First, we did not perform vascular myography, so we cannot directly determine whether TZ improves endothelial function in vivo. Second, although we modeled endothelial senescence using four complementary triggers—doxorubicin, oxidative stress, lipid stress, and replicative exhaustion—we did not test other chemotherapeutics. This matters because recent work highlights chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity as a senescence-linked process [ , ]. Third, we used HUVECs rather than primary human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs). Given vascular-bed–specific transcriptional programs—including possible differences in Pgk1 abundance and stress-response pathways—future studies will validate the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 mechanism in HAECs and other arterial endothelial cells. Altogether, these gaps underscore the need for follow-up experiments to delineate additional mechanisms and to establish the functional impact of TZ on vascular health.

## Conclusion
Our findings suggest that TZ primarily mitigates EC senescence through the Pgk1/Hsp90/Prdx1 signaling pathway, thereby slowing the progression of atherosclerosis. Notably, endothelial senescence and oxidative stress are shared hallmarks of diabetes-related vascular complications, underscoring the relevance of Pgk1-targeted modulation beyond atherosclerosis [ , ]. Given TZ’s established safety profile and its reported benefits for vascular health, it warrants further clinical investigation as a potential therapeutic agent against vascular aging and as a strategy with translational potential in cardiometabolic settings such as diabetes.
