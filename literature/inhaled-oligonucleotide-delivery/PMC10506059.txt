## Highlights
•Hemin, hemin-hemopexin complexes and free iron upregulate hepatocellular XOR and trigger exocytosis.
•The hemin-mediated exocytosis of XOR is noncanonical and dependent on lysosomal trafficking.
•Hemin-mediated upregulation of XOR is TLR4 dependent and mediated downstream via p65 and SP1.
•Hepatocellular release of XOR may be an adaptive/protective response to hemin/Fe overload.

## Introduction
Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) is a molybdoflavin enzyme that catalyzes the last two steps in purine catabolism: oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid (UA). The term XOR is a collective name for two isoforms of the same enzyme, xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) and xanthine oxidase (XO). XOR is transcribed and translated as XDH and conversion to XO is dependent upon post-translational modification via oxidation of two critical cysteines (reversible) or limited proteolysis (irreversible). Both XDH and XO catalyze purine oxidation at their respective molybdenum cofactors; however, XDH utilizes NAD+ (NAD+ → NADH) as an electron acceptor whereas XO utilizes O2 (O2 → O2•- + H2O2). The bulk of intracellular XOR is found in the XDH configuration whereas extracellular XOR is mostly XO. For decades, the capacity to generate oxidants has placed XO as a critical contributor to numerous inflammatory disease processes including those encountered in the vasculature [ , ].
XOR has been studied for over 124 years with the enzymatic process first observed in 1899 and purification in the following decades [ , , ]. Particular interest has been given to XO released into circulation where it is predicted to bind to glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) on the luminal surface of the endothelium and disrupt vascular function [ , ]. This binding to GAGs greatly diminishes the efficacy of the FDA-approved XOR inhibitor allopurinol [ ]. The ability of bound circulating XO to evade inhibition greatly increases our need to understand mechanisms involved in XOR upregulation and release into circulation to identify new therapeutic approaches for maintaining vascular homeostasis. The source of circulating XO has long been suspected to be the liver [ ]. Many accounts show elevated plasma XO in patients and animal models with chronic liver disease or liver injury [ , , , ]. This led to a general consensus in the field that, in part, the death or breakdown of hepatic cells accounts for the elevated XO in the circulation. However, countervailing evidence does exist and demonstrates that circulating XO can increase without elevated indices of liver damage (e.g. plasma ALT and AST) [ ]. In addition, we recently developed a hepatocyte XOR knockout mouse and demonstrated, under normal healthy physiology, that roughly 60% of plasma XO activity was lost in these mice [ ]. When taken together, these studies reveal the potential for operative XOR export mechanisms in the liver in the absence of overt cellular membrane damage.
Increased circulating XO is also a common feature of hemolytic diseases; however, the signaling mechanisms are unknown [ , , , ]. Mechanistically, upregulation of XO in response to iron and iron conjugates has been demonstrated in lung epithelial cells which may serve to link hemolysis to the actions of iron or hemin [ ]. In addition, new epidemiological data shows a correlation between ferritin and urate which also links iron homeostasis with XOR activity, an association likely due to the physiologic role of H2O2 and UA in iron/heme-iron-associated reactions/signaling [ ]. For example, we recently demonstrated, in this journal, that XO can prevent heme-induced activation of platelets during intravascular heme crisis [ ]. While a previous report suggests that the liver releases XO into the circulation following hypoxia-reperfusion in sickle cell disease, the mechanisms driving this action is completely unclear [ ]. Based on our recent findings that XO is involved in heme degradation and that circulating XO can increase without overt liver damage, we hypothesized that stimulation of hepatocytes with hemin or iron results in transcriptional upregulation and active release of XO from hepatocytes to the circulation.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Animals
Eight-week-old C57BL/6J (23 wildtype; stock no. 000664) mice were ordered from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pittsburgh. The mice were supplied a normal chow diet (Research Diets no. 5234), had free access to drinking water, and were housed in pathogen-free conditions in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals from the Department of Laboratory Animal Research at the University of Pittsburgh.

2.2

## Hemin administration and hematologic phenotyping
Heme crisis was induced by injecting the C57BL/6J mice with two identical doses of hemin (Frontier Scientific, H651-9) 1 h apart via tail vein injection (200 μL). Hemin was made fresh in 0.25 N NaOH (Fisher Chemical, S318-500) and brought to a pH of 7.4 using HCl. The mice were observed for 24 h post injection at and then the mice were euthanized for blood collection. Blood (300–600 μL) was collected via the vena cava in 5% 0.5 M, pH 8.0 EDTA (Promega, V4231) and spun down at 2500×g for 10 min. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use. XO activity was measured with electrochemical detection (Dionex UltiMate 3000 RS Electrochemical Detector) of UA post reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) separation as described previously [ ].

2.3

## Cell culture
Murine hepatocytes (AML12) were obtained from ATCC and grown and treated as we previously reported [ ] in DMEM/F12 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) media supplemented with dexamethasone, (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) growth supplement (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and 10% FBS (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). For experiments cells were seeded in 6-well plates. Genetic knockdown was conducted at 70% confluency, cells were transfected with 10 nM scrambled or siRNA targeted to Sp1 or p65 (silencer select, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) [ , , ]. After reaching 90% confluency, the cells were washed with sterile PBS and then 1 mL of MEM alpha supplemented with 16 μM hypoxanthine (to match the concentration in DMEM/F12) was added along with indicated treatments: 10 μM hemin, 1 μM necrostatin-1, 10 μM FeSO4, 10 μM protoporphyrin IX, 10 μM Brefeldin A, 5 μM Vacuolin-1, 170 μM Triton-X, 500 nM Nocodazole, 300 μM Endosidin 2. Cells were cultured in MEM alpha for the indicated periods of time, after which the media was collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80 °C until XO activity analysis. After removing the media, the cells were collected in PBS with HALT phosphatase and protease inhibitors (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) or Qiagen RLT buffer for RNA extraction.

2.4

## Hepatocyte isolation
Primary hepatocytes were isolated from 12-week-old C57BL/6J mice as described in detail previously [ ]. The cells were plated at 80% confluency on collagen-coated 6-well plates. Collagen-coated plates were prepared at 6.25 μg/cm2 using collagen 1 (Cat#: C3867, Sigma), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Seeded plates were left in the incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 5 h to allow the cells to attach, then each well was gently washed with 3 mL of Waymouth medium (Cat#: W1625, Sigma) to remove unattached cells and cultured overnight. The medium was then replaced with MEM alpha as indicated above.

2.5

## XOR activity
XOR activity was measured as previously described [ , ]. In brief, samples were incubated at 37 °C diluted in PBS with xanthine, oxonic acid (to inhibit urate oxidase), ± allopurinol, for 1 h. Reactions were stopped with ice-cold acetonitrile to precipitate all proteins. Samples were dried and reconstituted in mobile phase and passed through a 0.2 μm filter. UA concentration was then measured by HPLC/electrochemical detection and XO activity was calculated as the allopurinol inhibitable signal for each sample (1 Unit of activity = 1 μM of urate/min).

2.6

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation
AML12 cells were grown in 150 cm plates and treated as specified above. After 6 h of hemin-treatment cells were fixed and processed for ChIP according to manufacturer's protocol (high-sensitivity ChIP kit, Active Motif). Sp1 and p65 antibodies from Active Motif were used in the protocol. Isolated DNA was analyzed by real-time PCR by 5 primer sets designed to cover the Xdh promoter. Delta Ct was calculated from input DNA and then fold-change from control treatment. The five primer sets used in the study are from 2 primer blasts of the Xdh promoter. Primer sets 2, 3, 6, & 7 were returned from the original blast (JOBID:CALXSteJ2iH9G0oeR35uLD1lfx4QdmQDEQ); however, there was a gap in coverage near the transcription start site. Therefore, a second blast was confined to a few hundred base pairs near the transcription start site to cover this gap and this generated primer set 2.1 (JOB ID:a2G0KbeKuiKdGCodJ30OL11mHx1wdQQAcQ). ( ).

2.7

## Western blotting
Samples were prepared with laemmli sample buffer and β-mercaptoethanol and run on a 4–20% gradient gel (Biorad, Hercules, California) at 70 V. Samples were then transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes, dried, reconstituted with ddiH2O. Membranes were blocked with Li-Cor (Lincoln, Nebraska) TBS blocking buffer, and incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies include β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas), phospho- & total Sp1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), phosphor- & total p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts) and xanthine oxidoreductase (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas). Membranes were then washed with TBS containing 1% tween (TBST), incubated in Li-Cor near-infrared secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, washed again with TBST, and finally imaged with the Li-Cor Odyssey Clx. Densitometry analysis was conducted in Image-J (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland) reported as target and normalized to β-actin and fold-change calculated from control.

2.8

## PCR
RNA was extracted from AML12 cells by Qiagen RNeasy Kit per manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was analyzed by nano-drop spectrophotometer and 1 μg of RNA used in High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Real-time PCR was then performed using iTaq SYBRgreen Supermix (Biorad, Hercules, California) on an Applied Biosystem Real-time PCR instrument. Primers: mouse XDH (IDT) and mouse GAPDH (Takara) as previous [ ]. Data are shown as fold-change from control.

2.9

## Hydrogen peroxide production
Hemin-induced hydrogen peroxide production was measured in AML 12 cells by coumarin boronic acid assay (CBA). CBA was conducted as previously published [ , , , ], in a protocol adapted from Zielonka et al. [ ]. In brief, cell culture media was removed and 100 μl of CBA buffer (PBS, l-Name (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), Taurine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and 500 μM CBA probe (Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI)) was added ± 1 KU catalase. Plates were then run in a 37 °C plate reader and fluorescence measured (ex: 350, em: 450) every minute over 2 h. Signal from the negative control catalase wells were subtracted out from the sample wells and only the catalase inhibitable signal was analyzed. The rate of the relative fluorescent units per minute was calculated for all samples and fold change from control treatment calculated.

2.10

## Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometry
Reactions were conducted in 200 μL total volume in Chelex 100 (Sigma) treated phosphate buffer. Reactions were mixed as indicated with 10 μM hemin and/or 20 μM UA and/or20 μM DTPA. After reactions were mixed, ascorbate was added to 1 mM final concentration and reactions incubated at 37 °C for 30 min then snap frozen in liquid N2. Ascorbate (EPR silent) is oxidized by reactive/Fe(III) species to form the ascorbyl radical (EPR active). EPR spectroscopy was used to measure the formation of ascorbyl radical [ ]. At the time of EPR measurements, frozen samples were rapidly thawed, loaded (50 μL) in a quartz capillary tube (Ref: 9600150; Hirschmann Laborgerate GmbH & Co. KG, D-74246 Eberstadt, Germany), sealed on one end, and placed inside the 4 mm (O.D) EPR quartz tube (Catalog: 707-SQ-250 M; Wilmad LabGlass, Vineland, NJ, USA). EPR spectra were recorded at room temperature using a Bruker EMXnano spectrometer (Bruker BioSciences, Billerica, MA, USA) operating at X-band with a 100 kHz modulation frequency as described previously [ , ]. The following EPR instrument parameters were used: microwave frequency, 9.617 GHz; center field, 3425 G; sweep width, 20 G; microwave power, 20 mW; modulation amplitude, 0.5 G; modulation frequency, 100 kHz; receiver gain, 60 dB; time constant, 5.12 ms; conversion time, 37.5 ms; sweep time, 30 s; number of scans, 10. Data acquisition was performed using Bruker Xenon-nano software. The signal intensity was measured using first peak (low field) height of the EPR spectrum.

2.11

## Statistics
Results are expressed as means ± SD. Data processing was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA). Statistical difference between 2 groups was calculated by a one-sided Student T-test, while ANOVA was used to analyze experiments with 3 or more groups with differences between groups tested by Holm-Sidak post-hoc analysis. Significance cutoff was placed a p < 0.05.

## Results
3.1

## Hepatocytes actively release XO following exposure to hemin
Similar to our previous report with sickle cell mice [ ], injection of 50 μmol/kg of hemin induced a 23-fold increase of XO activity in the plasma compared to control (1445 ± 97 vs 63 ± 7 μU/ml) ( A). This observation was suspected to be driven by liver-mediated export of XOR to the circulation as genetic ablation of XOR in hepatocytes abrogated this effect. To establish the role of hemin-induced XO release from hepatocytes and the role of cell death/loss of membrane integrity in the release, AML12 cells were exposed to a range of hemin concentrations with XO activity measured in the culture media and cells stained for cytotoxicity. Increasing concentrations of hemin induced increasing levels of XO activity measured in the culture media peaking at 10 μM of hemin ( B). Interestingly, this increase occurs in the absence of significant cytotoxicity. As hemin concentrations reached and exceeded 20 μM, the percentage of dead cells rose rapidly coinciding with a steady decrease in the detected XO activity in the culture media ( B). Using 10 μM of hemin we further demonstrate that XO (as assessed by enzymatic activity) is released into the media at an increased level compared to control ( C). To further interrogate the relationship between hemin-induced cell necrosis and XO release, AML12 cells were treated with necrostatin-1 prior to hemin exposure. Treatment of cells with necrostatin-1 had no impact on the appearance of XO in the culture media ( D). Next, the role of membrane integrity was tested by low dose Triton-X treatment, which failed to induce a significant increase of XO released into the media compared to control conditions ( E). To validate these observations in the immortal hepatocyte cell line, primary hepatocytes were isolated from mice and exposed to 10 μM hemin, which increased XO release into the media ∼25-fold ( F). 

3.2

## Hemin induces XDH transcription and XOR protein expression
To assess the mechanisms elevating XO activity in the media, we measured intracellular XOR protein abundance by Western blot and expression by PCR. At 24 h of hemin treatment, we found roughly a 2-fold increase in XOR expression (p < 0.01, A) while Xdh transcript abundance at 6 h was over 10-fold greater than control (p < 0.05 B). Combined, these data support hemin-induced upregulation of XOR expression and elevation of intracellular XOR protein. 

3.3

## XOR is exported through a non-canonical exocytosis pathway
Having established the hemin-mediated upregulation and release of XOR, we assessed potential exocytotic pathways. First, we assessed the canonical ER-golgi pathway by using the inhibitor brefeldin A. Interestingly, brefeldin A did not alter XO activity in the media post hemin exposure whereas the lysosomal trafficking inhibitor vacuolin-1 abrogated the hemin-induced increase in media XO activity ( A). Similarly, treatments with inhibitors that disrupt vesicle transport prevented XO release ( ). To confirm vacuolin-1 was blocking release of XO and not acting through an alternative mechanism, intracellular XOR activity was assessed post hemin treatment. Again, Brefeldin A had did not alter intracellular XOR activity whereas Vacuolin-1, by blocking XOR release, induced intracellular XOR accumulation approximately 3-fold greater than that seen with hemin alone ( B). Collectively, these data indicate XOR is exported via a non-canonical, lysosomal-dependent pathway. 

3.4

## Iron-mediated regulation and export of XO
Hemin is composed of a coordinated iron molecule in the center of a porphyrin ring. To examine the potential role of Fe in the upregulation and release of XOR from hepatocytes, we repeated hemin experiments with FeSO4 (equimolar to hemin) or with protoporphyrin IX (hemin without the Fe). As anticipated, exposure to Fe resulted in release of XOR to the media whereas protoporphyrin IX had no effect ( A). Surprisingly, when we treated cells with FeSO4 and protoporphyrin IX simultaneously, there was no detectable release of XOR activity ( A). This effect is likely due to protoporphyrin IX-mediated prevention of ferrous iron-induced XDH gene expression ( B). Experiments were repeated using ferric iron as the source of iron and similar results were obtained ( ). 

3.5

## Iron chelation diminishes whereas hemin-hemopexin amplifies XOR export
Pretreatment of hemin-containing culture medium with excess UA or DTPA (to chelate Fe loosely or tightly, respectively) prevented the hemin-induced release of XO into the media ( A). Similarly, UA or DTPA prevented the iron-induced release of XO into the media ( B). Physiologically, hemopexin binds hemin and delivers it to the liver. We therefore treated with hemin incubated with hemopexin and found that the XO release was significantly elevated compared to hemin alone ( C). Previous work has clearly established UA and DTPA as chelators of iron; however, their ability to chelate hemin is unknown. Interestingly, treatment with DTPA but not UA caused a significant reduction in the hemin-induced ascorbate radical formation ( D) under our conditions indicating a diminished capacity for UA to hold Fe in a state inactive for redox interaction with ascorbate. 

3.6

## Transcriptional regulation of XDH
Mapping of the XDH promoter identified multiple potential binding sites for both NF-κB and Sp1, which have been suggested to work in concert to regulate certain genes. Both of these transcription factors are known to be activated by TLR4 & H2O2 and indeed, we found elevated H2O2 production at 1 h post hemin treatment ( A). Here we show that 10 μM hemin activates both p65 (NF-κB subunit) and Sp1 by 2 h post treatment, which returns to or past baseline at 24 h ( B–E & ). To determine if either p65 or Sp1 regulate XDH, these factors were knocked down individually prior to stimulation with 10 μM hemin. Interestingly, neither p65 or Sp1 knockdown altered XOR expression in the control condition. However, both p65 and Sp1 knockdown prevented the hemin-induced upregulation of intracellular XOR ( F). This was in agreement with siRNA knockdown of TLR4 prevention of hemin-induced XOR expression ( F) and as we previously published [ ]. Likewise, knockdown of either Sp1 or NF-kB diminished XDH transcription ( G) and XO activity in the culture media ( H). ChIP experiments identified greater than a 4-fold enrichment of p65 in 3 of 5 primer sets and greater than a 5-fold enrichment of Sp1 in 3 of 5 primer sets on the XDH promoter following hemin treatment ( A–B). This further supports the concept that XO release is in-part mediated by transcriptional upregulation and suggests the direct involvement of NF-κB and Sp1 are important in regulating XDH gene transcription mediated by hemin.

## Discussion
XOR has been well studied for over a century with the detrimental impacts of both cellular and circulating XO-derived oxidants well documented for numerous pathologies. While the dogma for decades has been that elevated XO, especially in the circulation, is indicative of a poor outcome, more recent reports have clearly demonstrated salutary actions of XOR via reduction of nitrite to NO [ , , , , , ]. In this same context, we recently reported, in this journal, a novel beneficial action of XO in the vasculature whereby liver-derived XOR is exported to the circulation in response to intravascular heme crisis [ ]. In this study, we reported XO to be protective during heme crisis partially due to its capacity to degrade hemoglobin, bind and degrade hemin, and chelate the redox-active Fe with its product UA. The importance of the liver as a primary source of this protective XO was accomplished by bone marrow transplant of the sickle bone marrow into hepatocyte-specific XDH knockout mice before inducing heme crisis. In this case, the absence of XOR in the livers of these mice resulted in complete lethality from heme crisis. In the aggregate, these experiments strongly indicated that induction of heme crisis resulted in signaling to the liver and by extension, hepatocytes, to export XOR to the circulation where it could interact with hemoglobin, hemin and, Fe to afford protection to the vasculature.
While our previous report implicates the liver as a seminal source of circulating XO, the signaling mechanism and mode of cellular export was not revealed. The literature regarding XOR has suggested for decades that the liver exports the enzyme to the circulation in response to hepatic injury, sickle cell disease and, viral infection [ , , , , ]; yet, there is a void of reports that identify a signaling pathway or an exocytosis pathway that supports this assumption. Since our previous work clearly demonstrated a dependence on hepatocellular XOR for mediating protection against heme overload, we were then incentivized to pursue these signaling and export mechanisms as defined herein.
To recapitulate our previous work from the sickle cell mouse model, we exposed wild-type mice to intravascular heme crisis and observed a similar response whereby circulating XO levels were enhanced by greater than 20-fold, A. This observation generated confidence that our previous observations were valid outside the genetic models used and served as a stepping-stone to look closer at hepatocytes. It is important to note that hepatocellular export of XO has been reported previously [ ]. In this study, Franko and colleagues examined the secretome of hepatocytes using mass spectrometry on the culture media from untreated human hepatocytes and found XO present in every sample. However, it was regarded as “cellular contamination” [ ]. Herein, our study revealed hemin, Fe and, hemin-hemopexin to be operative in initiating XOR upregulation and export to the medium, , , , . This process was found to be independent of loss of membrane integrity and dependent on a noncanonical (ER-golgi) exocytosis pathway. Our initial supposition was that the newly synthesized XOR was processed through the ER-golgi pathway of exocytosis; however, an inhibitor of this pathway, Brefeldin A [ ] did not alter intracellular XOR in a basal state or hemin-induced XO activity/export to the medium, . This clearly indicated XO release was dependent on an alternative exocytic pathway. Recently, the lysosomal-exocytosis trafficking pathway has become recognized as a critical stress/stimuli response mechanism [ , ]. Fortunately, this pathway can be inhibited by vacuolin-1 [ , ] and a hemin-induced lysosomal stress response pathway is plausible as heme transporters have been located in lysosomes [ ] and shown to respond to loaded Hpx [ ]. Therefore, we speculate XOR is trafficked through this pathway to localize iron and chelator (UA) to maintain intracellular iron levels. Indeed, our findings show vacuolin-1 prevented accumulation of XO in the medium, retaining it inside the hepatocytes. While this pathway may or may not be ubiquitous for XOR export, further work to explore the mechanisms involved in shuttling XOR to this pathway may identify novel therapeutic targets.
It is clear the presence of Fe is required to initiate the signal associated with hemin-induced upregulation and export of XOR from hepatocytes. For example, hemin without Fe (protoporphyrin IX) was inactive and the Fe and hemin chelating agent, DTPA abolished the process. This observation is consistent with previous work demonstrating Fe-mediated upregulation of XOR in the lung [ ]. In this study, lung epithelial cells upregulated XOR in response to FeSO4 or silica-Fe conjugates; however, the cell signaling pathway driving this process was not determined. We found that both Fe2+ and Fe3+ as well as hemin and hemin-hemopexin initiated XOR upregulation and export. While it can be argued that exposing cells to ferrous sulfate does not equate to exposure to Fe2+ as Fe2+ is rapidly oxidized to Fe3+ (ferric nitrate) at neutral pH in our experimental conditions, we can speculate that some population of the ferrous state of iron did make it to the cells. However, we are limited in our conclusions by the proclivity of soluble free Fe to assume the ferric state rapidly in our model and thus admit further work is required to firmly state that both ferric and ferrous states of iron equally initiate our observed signaling. Furthermore, the mechanisms through which chelation of free iron or hemin blocked XOR upregulation need further exploration. DTPA chelates both iron and hemin (as in D), which likely prevents iron-uptake and interferes with hemin-TLR4 interactions [ ]. The case for the UA-mediated chelation is less straightforward as we demonstrate UA does not chelate hemin to an extent that alters outcome. However, some evidence suggests UA can inhibit TLR4-NF-κB signaling [ ]. In addition, the iron distribution/metabolism pathways following UA or DTPA chelation as it relates to XOR and lysosomal traffick warrant further investigation.
Based on the experiments herein and the time course of XOR release by AML12 cells from our previously published study [ ], it is clear that some transcriptional pathway is likely required for inducing XOR production to be capable of attaining such an abundant level of XO activity in the cell culture media as well as in the circulation in the in vivo studies (e.g. previous study [ ] and A). This supposition was supported by elevated XDH transcription 6 h after hemin stimulation. Important to the observations herein, hemin is known to activate receptors as well as increase oxidant production, both of which may contribute to activation of transcription factors. The most well-known hemin-receptor interaction is with TLR4 [ , , ] and LPS activation of TLR4 has been shown to induce XDH transcription [ ], making this a likely pathway for hemin regulation of XOR. Indeed, both siRNA against TLR4 and treatment with TAK242 block hemin-induced XOR expression ( F and as previously published) [ ]. As such, we thus focused our attentions on transcription factors that have been reported downstream of TLR4, hemin-induced, and have been reported to be redox regulated. Both Sp1 and NF-κB fit these parameters and reports suggest a cooperative action between these two factors in gene regulation [ , , , , ]. In addition, hemin has been shown to activate NF-κB via TLR4 in the liver of sickle cell mice [ ]. We demonstrate that both Sp1 and the p65 subunit of NF-κB are activated in hepatocytes stimulated with 10 μM hemin. When combined, these observations extend our previous work by linking TLR4 to Sp1 and p65 to further define the transmission of the signal initiated by hemin.
While there are some reports suggesting NF-κB-associated regulation of the XDH gene [ ], detailed mechanics of this process and specifically downstream of hemin or iron are lacking. Mapping of the mouse as well as human XDH promoter (TransFac®) revealed a number of potential NF-κB and Sp1 DNA-binding motifs which is not surprising as XDH upregulation is a common feature of inflammatory stimuli [ ]. Genetic knockdown of either p65 or Sp1 prevented the hemin-induced expression and export of XOR suggesting that transcriptional upregulation of XOR is, in part, dependent on both transcription factors. ChIP experiments revealed multiple regions enriched with p65 and Sp1 on the XDH promoter. Interestingly, only the region most proximal to the transcription start site demonstrated enrichment of both p65 and Sp1 following exposure to hemin. These experiments provide evidence that both p65 and Sp1 directly regulate hemin-induced XOR expression. Additionally, they provide circumstantial evidence that a cooperative NF-κB-Sp1 interaction near the transcription start site may be important. Genes regulated under the control of a cooperative NF-κB-Sp1 interaction have been reported [ , ] and this transcriptional activation appears to be resolved relatively quickly as indicated by assessment at 24 h. As shown in our previous report hemin-induced XDH message is only increased 2-fold at 24 h compared to over 10-fold when measured at 6 h ( ). Furthermore, NF-κB and Sp1 activation appears to assume baseline levels along with H2O2 production at the 24-h time point, suggesting a tightly controlled pathway. It is important to note while we show a detailed transcriptional pathway for hemin-induced XOR, Fe and Hpx-bound hemin may upregulate XOR through alternative pathways. In the case of free Fe, direct activation of TLR4 has not been shown. However, intracellular processing of excess Fe into hemin has been shown to activate TLR4, potentially making this pathway operable [ ]. Likewise, Fe-induced oxidants or lipid peroxidation may lead to NF-κB and Sp1 actiavtion. As for Hpx-induced XOR, this is unlikely to involve activation of TLR4-NF-κB. While not the focus of the current manuscript, these pathways warrant future investigation; however, the data presented herein serve to highlight the intricate involvement of XOR in hepatocyte response to numerous excess iron-based signals.
In toto, this set of experiments provides critical and novel insight into the regulation and export of XOR in hepatocytes challenged with hemin or iron as we demonstrate, for the first time, that XOR can be upregulated and exported via specific signaling. This process is dependent upon NF-κB and Sp1 activation of transcription as well as lysosomal-mediated exocytosis.
