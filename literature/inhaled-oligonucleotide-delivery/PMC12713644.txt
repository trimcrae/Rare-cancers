## Abstract

## Aims
Alternative mRNA splicing is a significant part of transcriptome reprogramming during the pathological manifestation of heart diseases. Earlier studies have identified a muscle-specific isoform of RBFox1 (RNA binding fox-1 homolog 1) to be a key RNA splicing regulator in pressure overload induced heart failure. However, the physiological impact of RBFox1 in myocardial infarction (MI), and the downstream mRNA alternative splicing events during MI induced cardiac remodelling remains unknown.

## Methods and results
Here we found RBFox1 expression was significantly decreased in Sprague-Dawley rat hearts post MI. Restoring the expression of RBFox1 prevented cardiac remodelling and dysfunction post MI characterized by improved cardiac function, reduced hypertrophy and fibrosis, associated with attenuated induction of cardiac stress marker genes. In cultured cardiomyocytes, expression of RBFox1 was sufficient to prevent hypoxia induced cell death measured by TUNEL staining and cleaved caspase 3, while inactivation of RBFox1 aggravated cardiac cell death. Mechanistically, we identified RBFox1 expression affected a broad spectrum of gene expression in post-MI hearts. In addition, a hypoxia-sensitive alternative splicing variant of Mbnl1 (Muscleblind-like 1) mRNA was identified to be regulated by RBFox1, resulting in the expression of a cell death related Mbnl1 isoform with 12 amino-acid deletion at the C-terminus (Mbnl1-ΔExon7). Strikingly, the selective inhibition of Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion using anti-sense oligo protected the heart from myocardial infarction induced injury in vivo.

## Conclusion
In summary, we have established a cardio-protective role of RBFox1 in myocardial infarction induced cardiac remodelling and dysfunction. Restoration of RBFox1 expression, and targeted modulation of its downstream alternative splicing target Mbnl1, is a potential therapeutic approach for cardiac dysfunction and remodelling in MI injured heart.

Keywords: Heart Failure, mRNA splicing, Myocardial Infarction, RBFox1, Mbnl1

## Introduction
Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in the United States and world-wide populations. Myocardial infarction (MI), also known as heart attack, is a life-threatening ischaemic insult to the heart caused by coronary pathologies. During the acute phase of myocardial infarction, metabolic and ionic perturbations in the affected myocardium led to rapid decrease of systolic function. When ischaemic injury is prolonged, permanent loss of cardiomyocyte due to cell death occurs, followed by formation of scar tissue resulted from extensive fibrosis. , Myocardial infarction and the ensuing ischaemic injury to myocardium are major contributors to sudden cardiac death and chronic heart failure. Despite years of intense research in this area, there are still no clinically approved therapies that can effectively ameliorate myocardial infarction related cardiac pathologies, highlighting the need for more translational research in this area. 
Post transcriptional processing of pre-mRNAs, including modification, splicing, and transportation is an important part of gene expression regulation in eukaryotes. , Among the different steps of RNA metabolism, alternative splicing, during which different mature RNA transcripts can be produced from a single gene substantially expands the overall complexity of the transcriptome. , Alternative mRNA splicing plays pivotal roles in both physiological and pathological conditions in heart, affecting cardiomyocyte development and maturation, as well as the onset and progression of cardiomyopathies. , Constitutive mRNA splicing is carried out by the spliceosome that recognizes the conserved RNA sequences located at 5′ and 3′ splice sites of each exon-intron junction. The alternative splicing, however, is mediated by trans-acting splicing regulators that recognize and bind to specific motifs located in the targeted exons or introns, resulting in altered RNA splicing events and different splicing derived transcripts. , , Mis-regulated splicing events, or mutations in splicing regulators have also been identified as potential pathogenic drivers in both acquired and congenital heart diseases. Myocardial infarction and hypoxia injury have been associated with abnormal RNA splicing, , , however, the underlying molecular players remain to be unveiled and RNA-splicing targeted therapies for myocardial infarction have not been fully explored.
RBFox1 is an RNA splicing regulator known to be involved in neurons as well as skeletal and cardiac muscle. , , In the heart, RBFox1 expression has a significant impact on cardiomyocytes maturation during postnatal development and is critical to the pathogenesis of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and heart failure induced by pressure overload. , However, whether RBFox1 could also play a role in myocardial infarction remains to be demonstrated. In this study, we have utilized preclinical animal models of myocardial infarction established by coronary artery ligation and hypoxia treatment in cultured cardiomyocytes as in vivo and in vitro model systems to simulate myocardial infarction related injury. , We have characterized the dynamic expression pattern of RBFox1 in cardiomyocytes post myocardial infarction or hypoxia injury in vivo and in vitro, respectively. We have further demonstrated that RBFox1 expression protected against myocardial infarction induced heart failure, through regulating alternative splicing events of cardiac genes, including Mbnl1.

## Methods
2.1.

## Rat model and adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection
Male Sprague-Dawley rats were purchased directly from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Company: http://www.slaccas.com, and housed at 22°C and 12-hour light,12-hour dark cycle with free access to water and standard chow. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with IACUC protocol. The Adeno-Associated Virus, including AAV9-cTnT-NC, AAV9-cTnT-RBFox1 were purchased from Genechem Company (https://www.genechem.com.cn). The AAV intramyocardial injection was performed under the guidance of ultrasound detection as previously described, each rat received a total of 4*10^12/kg BW virus particle. To perform the AAV9 injection, rats were first anaesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and six sections of rat left ventricle were chosen based on ultrasound vision, 1 mL syringe was used to penetrate intercostal muscles, and injection was performed at 1 point in each section of left ventricle. Terminal tissue collection was performed on rats under 3% isoflurane anaesthesia. Adult rats were euthanized using 3% isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. The neonatal rats were euthanized using 3% isoflurane inhalation followed by decapitation. No neonatal rat analgesics is available. All animal studies have been approved by the University of Cincinnati IACUC. All procedures are conformed to NIH guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

2.2.

## Jugular vein injection of antisense oligo (ASO) nucleotide
The Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) was synthesized by IDT according to the sequence and target domain as previously described. The ASO was dissolved in sterile saline (0.9% NaCl solution). To perform the ASO injection, mice were anaesthetized using 2% isoflurane inhalation and body temperature was monitored at 37°C prior to fur removal. After spotting jugular vein, a catheter needle was used to penetrate the jugular vein. The ASO was injected at a dose of 10 mg/kg bodyweight at the speed of 0.1 mL/5 s. For adult mouse analgesics, Buprenorphine ER was administrated at 3.25 mg/kg body weight through subcutaneous injection before surgery and then every 48 h post-surgery; Meloxicam was administrated at 5 mg/kg body weight through subcutaneous injection before the surgery and then every 72 h post-surgery. All the animal studies have been approved by the University of Cincinnati IACUC. All procedures are conformed to NIH guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

2.3.

## Myocardial infarction and echocardiogram analysis
For myocardial infarction, rats were anaesthetized using 2% isoflurane and body temperature was monitored and maintained at 37°C with a heating pad. The left coronary artery was permanently ligated; Sham operation is performed using the similar procedure without left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery ligation. For adult rat analgesics, Buprenorphine ER was administrated at 1.2 mg/kg body weight through subcutaneous injection before surgery and then every 48 h post-surgery; Meloxicam ER was administrated at 2 mg/kg body weight through subcutaneous injection before the surgery and then every 72 h post-surgery.
Echocardiography: Mice were anaesthetized and maintained with 2% isoflurane in 95% oxygen. A Vevo 3000 (Visual Sonics) echocardiography system with 46 mHz scan head was used to image the heart. Long axis and short axis views were recorded at different time points. The ejection fraction and fraction shortening, LVID; s, LVID;d; LVPW; s and LVPW; d were measured and calculated based on M-Mode images.
All the animal studies have been approved by the University of Cincinnati IACUC. All procedures are conformed to NIH guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

2.4.

## Cell culture and hypoxia chamber treatment
The H9C2 cell line was purchased from ATCC. The cells were recovered under 37°C water baths, then cultured in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 in high glucose DMEM (Gibco) medium contain 10% foetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher).
The neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVM) were prepared from neonatal rat at postnatal day 1, then plated on 6 well plates at the density of 1 million cells each well using high glucose DMEM with 10% foetal bovine serum. 24 h post plating; the medium was replaced by serum-free high glucose DMEM supplemented with 100 U/mL Pen/Strep (Corning) and 0.1% ITS (insulin-transferrin-selenium) (Thermo Fisher). The adenovirus, including adv-NC; adv-RBFox1, adv-sh-RBFox1 were purchased from Genechem Company (https://www.genechem.com.cn)
For the hypoxia chamber treatment, the H9C2 cells or NRVM were transferred into in VIVO 2500 hypoxia chamber (0.3% oxygen and 5% CO2) and incubated at 37°C for 48 h before harvest for analysis. For ASO transfection, antibiotics-free medium was used for 12 h post transfection with ASO (final concentration: 50 nM) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher).

2.5.

## RNA extraction and real-time RT- PCR
Total RNA was extracted from rat cardiac tissue or cultured NRVMs using TRIzol reagent (Life technologies) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Following RNA extraction, one µg RNA was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis using Random Primer (Thermo Fisher) and Maxima Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer's instruction. Real-time RT-PCR was performed using IQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) with CFX-96 Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). The Cq values of target genes were normalized to values for GAPDH or ACTB genes. The primer sequences are listed in Table .
 
2.6.

## Semi-quantitative RT-PCR
cDNAs from the hearts and cells were used for semi-quantitative PCR reaction using DreamTaq PCR Premix (Invitrogen, with 30 cycles of amplification, and annealing temperatures at 60°C (Mbnl1), 55°C (Camta1), 64.4°C (Kcnma1), respectively. The PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on 4% Low melting point agarose gel stained with GelRed Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Millipore). Images of the DNA bands were acquired with a high-resolution camera (BioRad GelDocGo) and quantification of the band intensity was performed by Image J.

2.7.

## RNA-Seq and data analysis
1 µg of total mRNA was extracted from tissues of AAV9-NC and AAV9-RBFox1 injected rats 28 days post MI from both infarct and remote areas (Four samples separately). The total mRNA was quantified, and the integrity was determined prior to cDNA library preparation. The cDNA library was sequenced at Novogene Co. using Illumina platform. The resulting raw sequencing data, provided as FASTQ files, were first assessed for quality using FastQC. Sequence reads were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus reference genome (Rnor_6.0) using STAR. For gene quantification, only reads uniquely mapped to exonic regions were considered, and gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts from the Subread package, focusing exclusively on exonic features. The gene-level count matrix was normalized for library size differences using the size factors method implemented in the DESeq2 package. Genes with low counts were filtered out prior to downstream analysis.
Additionally, alternative splicing events were profiled using rMATS (v4.3.0). Sorted sequencing data from STAR were analysed to detect and quantify splicing events, including exon skipping (SE), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), alternative 3′ splice sites (A3SS), alternative 5′ splice sites (A5SS), and retained introns (RI). The genomic positions of these events were recorded for further analysis. To visualize splicing patterns, sashimi plots were generated using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) v2.19.0, offering a clear depiction of splicing events across the genome.

2.8.

## Western blot
Proteins from heart tissue and cells were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher) containing 1× Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Single-Use Cocktail (Thermo Fisher). The concentration of protein lysates was measured using BCA protein assay reagent (Thermo Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. 20–40 µg of total protein was used for western blot analysis. Samples were separated by electrophoresis on 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore) prior to blocking with 5% milk/TBS for 1 h at room temperature. The blot was incubated with primary antibodies at 4 Degrees Celsius overnight. Antibodies used for Western Blot were listed in Table . Images of the gel were acquired with a high-resolution CCD camera (ChemiDoc XRS + Bio-Rad) and quantification of the bands was performed using Image Lab (Bio-Rad).
 
2.9.

## Histology
Rat hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and embedded in paraffin prior to sectioning into 6 µm-thickness slides. The tissue sections were then stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Sirius red following standard histochemical protocols.
Quantification of Sirius red staining areas was performed using the Image J software using twenty randomly selected fields from three to five rats in each experimental group.

2.10.

## WGA staining and TUNEL staining
WGA staining was conducted using Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated WGA reagent (1:200) (Life technologies) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were further counterstained with anti-troponin I (1:200) (Abcam) and DAPI (Fluor mount-GTM, Invitrogen). Secondary antibodies were obtained from Life Technologies. The stained images were recorded using a Leica Stellaris 8 Confocal microscope and the cross-sectional area were quantified using Image J.
TUNEL staining was performed using an in-situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained images were recorded using a Leica Stellaris 8 Confocal microscope and TUNEL positive cell nuclei were quantified using Image J. 20–40 cells were counted per field. In each biological repeat, 2–4 fields with appropriate cell confluency (50%) were randomly taken as technical repeats. The cells are imaged and quantified in a double-blind manner.

2.11.

## LDH assay
The cell cytotoxicity was assessed using CyQUANT™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Thermo Fisher) by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were harvested right after hypoxia and incubated with LDH reaction mix for 30 min at room temperature. The signal was read by a microplate reader at 490 nm excitation and 680 nm reading wavelengths. To calculate % cytotoxicity, LDH activity is calculated as the decrease of absorption over time.

2.12.

## RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation assay (RIP)
Magna RIP® RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore 17-700) was used according to manufacturer’s instruction. NRVM cells were infected with adv-NC or adv-RBFox1 for 48 h prior to cell harvesting for analysis.

2.13.

## Statistical analysis
Values are expressed as mean ± SEM. The data distribution normality was determined using Shapiro-Wilk test for any sample size n > 6. For normally distributed data, parametric tests were used. Comparisons of two variables were tested using the student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple variables were determined using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Fisher’s post hoc test. For the analysis involving two categories of variables (time/treatment groups), two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test or LSD post hoc test was used as indicated specifically in the figure legend. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1.

## RBFox1 expression is suppressed under pathological stress conditions
We first measured the level of RBFox1 in rat heart in response to myocardial infarction injury. Male adult rats were subjected to either permanent ligation of left anterior descending (LAD) artery to simulate myocardial infarction or Sham operation as control. At 28 days post-surgery, the rats showed a significant decrease in the systolic function characterized by reduced ejection fraction in the MI group vs. Sham controls measured by echocardiography (Figure A and B). Cardiac tissues from the infarct border zone and the remote area were collected, and the RBFox1 expression was measured at both mRNA and protein levels by Realtime-RT-PCR and western blot, respectively. As shown in Figure C–H, the expression of RBFox1 was markedly decreased at both mRNA and protein levels in the MI hearts compared with the Sham in both infarction border and remote zone (Figure C–H). The expression of RBFox1 was also reduced post hypoxia treatment in H9C2 cardiac cells (Figure I) compared to the cells under normoxia condition (Figure J–L). Therefore, consistent with the previously reported results in pressure-overloaded mouse failing heart and hypertrophy stimulated myocytes, loss of RBFox1 expression is a common and conserved molecular change in cardiomyocytes under different pathological stresses, including myocardial infarction and ischaemic injury.
 
3.2.

## Expression of RBFox1 attenuates heart failure progression post myocardial infarction
To determine the functional impact of RBFox1 expression in myocardial infarction, we performed RBFox1 gain of function analysis in rat hearts using Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV) mediated gene delivery (Figure A). Adult male rats were treated by intramyocardial injection of 4*10^12/kg particles of AAV9-NC or AAV9-RBFox1 (cardiomyocyte specific RBFox1 expression) vectors under the guidance of echocardiography as previously described. Two weeks post injection (when the target gene expression level is expected to reach stable level), the rats were subjected to myocardial infarction injury via LAD ligation and compared with sham operation followed by serial echocardiography analyses. We confirmed that RBFox1 was significantly elevated in the AAV9-RBFox1 injected hearts compared with AAV9-NC treated ones (Figure B and C). Remarkably, cardiac performance characterized by ejection fraction and fraction shortening was significantly improved in the AAV9-RBFox1 treated group 28 days after myocardial infarction (Figure D–F). In addition, the expression of RBFox1 prevented MI induced left ventricle end-systolic dilatation, consistent with better preserved systolic function (Figure G and H).
 
3.3.

## Expression of RBFox1 protects heart from MI induced deleterious pathological remodelling in vivo
Next, we explored whether RBFox1 expression could impact on pathological remodelling, including hypertrophy and fibrosis in the heart post myocardial infarction. As shown in Figure A,  Hematoxylin and Eosin staining of the whole cardiac chamber sections showed reduced cardiac scar area at 28 days after myocardial infarction. WGA staining showed the cardiomyocyte cross-sectional areas were smaller in the RBFox1 expressing hearts vs. the AAV9-NC treated controls post MI injury (Figure B and C). At molecular level, the expression levels of stress marker genes, including Nppa and Nppb, decreased in both border and remote areas from the AAV9-RBFox1 injected hearts compared with the AAV9-NC controls (Figure D and E). In addition, Sirius red staining demonstrated reduced fibrotic areas in the AAV9-RBFox1 injected myocardium compared to the AAV9-NC injected hearts (Figure F and G). As shown in Figure H and I, Col1a1 and Col3a1 expression levels were significantly repressed in the AAV9-RBFox1 injected rat hearts compared to the AAV9-NC injected groups.
 In summary, over-expression of RBFox1 in heart protected the rat hearts from myocardial infarction induced cardiac dysfunction and remodelling, including attenuated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and fibrosis.

3.4.

## RBFox1 expression has a protective role under hypoxia stress in vitro
To detect whether RBFox1 plays a direct protective role against ischaemic injury in cardiomyocytes, neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) were infected with Adv-NC (negative control) and Adv-RBFox1 for 48 h prior to hypoxia treatment as shown in Figure A. Real-time RT-PCR analysis confirmed the elevated expression level of RBFox1 in Adv-RBFox1 infected cells compared to Adv-NC infected cells (Figure B). Expression of RBFox1 significantly protected the NRVM from hypoxia induced cell death demonstrated by a reduction in cleaved-Caspase 3 level (Figure C and D) and per cent of TUNEL positive cells (Figure E and F).
 
3.5.

## Inactivation of RBFox1 exacerbated hypoxia stress induced cell death in vitro
RBFox1 expression was inhibited in NRVM and H9C2 cells using Adenovirus-delivered small hairpin RNA (shRNA) as previously described. 48 h after infection with either control (Adv-NC) or shRBFox1 (adv-shRBFox1), the NRVM and H9C2 cells were incubated in hypoxia chamber under 0.3% oxygen condition for 48 h and compared with cells cultured under normoxia condition (Figure G, , and ). Inactivation of RBFox1 in both NRVM and H9C2 cells exacerbated cell death post hypoxia stimulation characterized by a significantly elevated cleaved-Caspase 3 protein level (Figure H–J, , ) and LDH release (Figure K, , ). Lastly, significantly increased per cent of TUNEL positive cells was observed upon loss of RBFox1 expression post hypoxia challenge in NRVM, but not in H9C2 cells (Figure L–M, , and ). In summary, we have utilized two independent in vitro systems to demonstrate that inactivation of RBFox1 exacerbates cell death in response to hypoxia stress.

3.6.

## Expression of RBFox1 alters cardiomyocytes transcriptome at the level of alternative splicing
To elucidate the potential mechanism in RBFox1 mediated effect in post-MI cardiac dysfunction and remodelling, the transcriptome profiles were obtained by RNA-seq and compared between the RBFox1 expressing and the control rat hearts at day 28 post myocardial infarction with a focus on alternative splicing events (Figure A,   ). Among the differentially expressed genes, KEGG analysis revealed that top enriched pathways in the infarct areas included dilated cardiomyopathy as well as calcium signalling, while the enriched pathways in the remote areas included calcium signalling, cAMP and MAPK signalling (see , ). Notably, among the genes showed alternative splicing changes upon RBFox1 expression (Figure B), Mef2a and Mef2d were detected, consistent with previous report on downstream alternative splicing targets of RBFox1.
 To validate the RNA-seq results for RBFox1 mediated alternative splicing activities, we performed semi-quantitative RT-PCR on selected genes including Kcma1, Camta1 and Mbnl1 based on their known roles in calcium handling and cardiac function (Figure C–F). All three genes harbour conserved RBFox1 binding motif in their pre-mRNAs, suggesting they are potential direct downstream targets of RBFox1. Among these genes, Mbnl1 was further analysed for 1). Mbnl1 has a highly conserved RBFox1 binding motif in its pre-mRNA adjacent to Exon 7 (Figure G). Sanger sequencing confirmed the RT-PCR products as two splicing variants of Mbnl1, one with Exon 7 included and another excluded (Figure H). 2) Mbnl1 has a well-established role in hypoxia response in cancer cells , 3). Alternative splicing of Mbnl1 Exon 7 has been reported to have a major effect on its downstream gene function. We first validated that Mbnl1 Exon 7 alternative splicing, but not total mRNA or protein levels, was dynamically changed in the post MI hearts as comparing to the sham operated controls, and this alternative splicing was reversed upon RBFox1 expression (Figure I, , and ). Expression of wild-type RBFox1, but not mutant RBFox1 (without RNA binding domain) significantly promoted the exclusion of Mbnl1 Exon 7 in human AC16 cardiomyocytes (Figure J and K) and NRVM (see , and ). On the other hand, inactivation of RBFox1 using shRNA increased the Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion levels (see , and ). To further demonstrate that Mbnl1 exon7 alternative splicing is indeed a direct downstream target of RBFox1, we performed RNA-Immune-precipitation analysis followed by targeted real-time RT-PCR as previously described. A direct interaction between RBFox1 and Mbnl1 mRNA intron 6-7, but not intron 2-3, was detected, further confirming that Mbnl1 mRNA intron 6-7/exon7 is a direct downstream target of RBFox1 (Figure L). Lastly, hypoxia stimulation markedly altered the alternative splicing pattern of Mbnl1 exon 7 in NRVM and RBFox1 expression was sufficient to reverse this splicing change. (Figure M and N).

3.7.

## Mbnl1 Exon7 plays an important role in hypoxia induced cardiomyocytes cell death
To determine whether the protective effect of RBFox1 in myocardial infarction was mediated by Mbnl1 alternative splicing, we evaluated the functional impact of Mbnl1-Exon7 isoform in hypoxia induced stress in cultured cardiomyocytes. To specifically block Exon7 inclusion within Mbnl1, we took a previously described Antisense Oligonucleotide (ASO) mediated splicing silencing approach to inhibit the inclusion of Mbnl1 Exon7 and compared with either Scramble ASO, ASO for Mbnl1 Exon5 inclusion as another control (Figure A). Transfection with ASO-Exon7 efficiently reduced the inclusion of Exon7, without affecting the total Mbnl1 mRNA level (Figure B–D). To determine the impact of Mbnl1-Exon7 isoform in cardiomyocytes stress response, we challenged the cultured NRVM with hypoxia stress combined with different ASO treatment and compared with normoxia condition (Figure E). Interestingly, inhibition of Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion, but not scramble ASO or ASO targeting Mbnl1 Exon5 significantly protected hypoxia induced cell death based on released LDH activity and TUNEL staining (Figure F–H, , and ). Last, when ASO treatment was implemented in combination with RBFox1 inactivation (Figure I), inactivation of Mbnl1 Exon7, but not Exon 5, partially abolished cell death induction from RBFox1 inactivation (Figure J–L, , and ).
 
3.8.

## Inhibition of Mbnl1 exon 7 splicing via antisense oligo protected myocardial infarction induced cardiac injury
Finally, to determine the functional impact of Mbnl1 Exon 7 switch in vivo, mice were randomly divided into two groups and treated with ASO-E7 (ASO-Exon 7) or nc-ASO (negative control ASO) by intravenous injection at two time points, one at three days prior to the MI surgery and one during surgery. The injected mice were subjected to sham or myocardial infarction surgery (Figure A). Real-time RT-PCR and western blot analysis validated that the Mbnl1 Exon 7 was effectively blocked by ASO-E7 without impact on the total levels of Mbnl1 mRNA, or protein (Figure B and C, , and , ). Echocardiography analysis showed ASO-E7 treatment did not affect cardiac function at baseline, but significantly prevented the MI induced contractile dysfunction 3 days post-surgery, and this protective effect correlated significantly with ASO efficiency in individual mouse (Figure D–F; , ). Inhibition of Exon 7 inclusion also protected the hearts from myocardial infarction induced scar formation (Figure G, , ). Interestingly, the inhibition of Mbnl1 Exon7 markedly reduced cardiac inflammatory markers and improved fibrotic remodelling based on decreased collagen expression and reduced cardiac fibrosis (Figure H–J, , ). ASO-E7 injection significantly reduced cardiac hypertrophic response in post MI hearts based on marker gene expression including Nppa and Nppb, as well as a reduced cardiomyocytes cross section area without changing the heart, liver or lung weight (Figure K–N, , ). Lastly, to understand whether cell death contributes towards the cardioprotective effect in ASO-E7 injected mice, we analysed cleaved caspase 3 expression in cardiac tissue. Indeed, injection of ASO targeting Mbnl1 exon 7 reduced cleaved caspase 3 expression comparing NC-ASO injected mice post MI (Figure O).

## Discussion
Our study has identified a previously uncharacterized role of RBFox1 in myocardial infarction induced cardiac pathological remodelling. Results from both in vivo and in vitro analyses demonstrate that loss of RBFox1 in the ischaemic/hypoxia injured hearts contributes to cardiomyocyte cell death, cardiac dysfunction and remodelling. Restoring RBFox1 expression protects cardiomyocytes from myocardial infarction induced cell death and attenuates cardiac dysfunction and remodelling at least in part through regulating Mbnl1 Exon7 alternative splicing in the hearts.
RBFox1 has a well characterized role in neural and muscle physiology. , , , In the hearts, RBFox1 can regulate cardiomyocytes maturation, cardiac development and pressure overload induced pathological remodelling in zebrafish and mouse models. , , Mechanistically, RBFox1 regulates alternative splicing of numerous cardiac genes, including Mef2 transcription factor family members and focal adhesion molecules. , Our current study has further expanded the role of RBFox1 in the hearts, by establishing a potent protective role for RBFox1 against myocardial infarction induced cardiac dysfunction and pathological remodelling. Importantly, we have uncovered Mbnl1 as a previously unknown downstream target for RBFox1 mediated alternative splicing. Considering the broad impact of RBFox1 expression on cardiac hypertrophy, contractility, cardiomyocyte viability and fibrotic remodelling, it is expected that other downstream molecules of RBFox1 may also be involved. Future studies using unbiased RIP (RNA Immune-precipitation)-seq or other approaches will be needed to systematically characterize all functionally relevant RBFox1 downstream targets in the context of cardiac development and pathologies.
At translational level, we demonstrated that the inhibition of Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion is sufficient to protect the heart from myocardial infarction induced injury. This exciting observation represents proof-of-concept evidence that targeted manipulation of Mbnl1 alternative splicing, but not total expression, is a potential therapeutic strategy against MI injury. However, in the current study, the delivery of ASO was performed before MI injury and the observed effects should be viewed as preventative rather than therapeutic. This limitation should be addressed in future studies when targeted modulation of Mbnl1 Exon7 splicing is administered at later time points during MI injury. Stress induced alternative splicing changes have been extensively studied in various diseases including reports on hypoxia induced alternative splicing events in cancer. However, studies on cardiac alternative splicing regulation in the context of hypoxia response in heart remains sparse. The role of Mbnl1 in hypoxia induced cell death has been well described in cancer cells. , Like many other splicing regulators, Mbnl1 mRNA is also subjected to alternative splicing regulation. Among the different isoforms, the inclusion of 36 bp Exon7 has also been associated with cell death in cancer. In our study, we have identified the same pattern of Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion in the heart post myocardial infarction and established that this alternative splicing event is a direct downstream target of RBFox1. In contrast to the report in cancer cells, however, Mbnl1 Exon7 inclusion has an opposite effect in cell death regulation in cardiomyocytes, suggesting that Exon7 inclusion may fundamentally alter the function of Mbnl1 protein in a cell-type specific and context specific manner. It is also unclear whether non-cardiomyocytes targeted by ASO-E7, such as fibroblast, have also contributed to the observed outcome in post-MI heart as the antisense oligo we employed did not possess tissue or cell type specificity. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that Mbn1 splicing and its downstream effects in non-cardiomyocytes also play a role in post-MI dysfunction and remodelling. The molecular basis of Exon7 in Mbnl1 mediated cell death regulation remains entirely unclear. The 36 bp Exon7 is a microexon, encoding a 12-amino acids fragment located at the C-terminal region of Mbnl1. This is a highly flexible region within the protein structure and the inclusion of Exon7 microexon motif might affect how this Mbnl1 isoform interacts with other proteins to exert different downstream effects.  , Additional studies, including unbiased proteomics approach to dissect the Mbnl1 interactome for different isoforms might lead to better mechanistic insights on the Mbnl1 Exon7 function.
