## Abstract

## Background
Our recent systematic review highlighted key associations between ambient air pollution (AAP) exposure and COVID-19 severity. This systematic review aims to summarise toxicological studies on the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.

## Methods
On 17 July 2025, PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science were searched for in vitro, in vivo and in silico studies that examined the biological mechanisms of AAP exposure on COVID-19 health outcomes. Two independent reviewers engaged in the selection and data extraction process. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed with the Toxicological Data Reliability Assessment Tool. The Integrated Network and Dynamical Reasoning Assembler (INDRA) was used to provide visual biomechanistic summaries of the included studies by creating knowledge graphs of the described mechanisms.

## Results
A total of 18 studies were included in this review. Findings consistently indicated that AAP exposure can worsen COVID-19 severity through two key mechanisms 1) increased expression of viral entry factors (e.g. angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 and transmembrane serine protease 2), facilitating infection, and 2) immune dysregulation, resulting in increased inflammation and oxidative stress. These key mechanisms were also identified in the INDRA networks. While studies commonly focused on particulate matter (n=15), similar effects were seen with ultrafine particles and ozone.

## Conclusion
These findings highlight the impact of AAP exposure on COVID-19 health outcomes on the molecular level. The findings of this review illustrate the urgent need for air quality improvements to help shape public health strategies to reduce and prevent future health impacts caused by AAP exposure.

Shareable abstract

## Background
COVID-19 disease, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), was first identified in China in 2019 and declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) in March 2020 [ ]. As of 26 January 2025, the WHO reported over 777 million confirmed cases and more than 7 million deaths [ ]. SARS-CoV-2, a single-stranded RNA virus from the Coronaviridae family, infects humans primarily through respiratory transmission [ , ]. Containment of SARS-CoV-2 is further challenged by its high transmission rate and the potential for asymptomatic spread [ ].
SARS-CoV-2 uses angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as a receptor and transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) for spike protein priming to enter host cells [ ]. Upon infection with the SARS-CoV-2 virus, the host cells respond by initiating an immune response. This leads to a cascade of reactions, resulting in the activation of specific immune cells and consequently a release of (hallmark) inflammatory mediators [ ]. These inflammatory factors help to suppress the propagation of the virus and aid in clearing it from the body [ ]. While this response is crucial, a dysregulated immune reaction can damage the host's own cells. In severe cases, immune cell hyperresponsiveness can occur, leading to excessive production and release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [ , ]. This systemic and localised response, mediated in part by signalling lipids, may result in life-threatening organ and tissue damage [ – ]. In addition, 10–20% of individuals who recovered from acute COVID-19 develop long COVID, a condition that is characterised by symptoms persisting for at least 3 months after infection including fatigue, shortness of breath and cognitive difficulties [ , ].
Ambient air pollution (AAP) was identified by the WHO as a significant environmental health concern, with 99% of the global population residing in areas where AAP levels exceed WHO air quality guidelines [ ]. In 2019, exposure to AAP was linked to an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide [ ]. Our previous systematic review and meta-analysis found that exposure to AAP, specifically exposure to particulate matter (PM) with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5), may increase the risk of hospitalisation and mortality following SARS-CoV-2 infection [ ]. In addition to PM2.5, other pollutants such as nitrogen dioxide, ozone, sulfur dioxide and carbon monoxide were also found to be important [ , ]. Only three studies to date focused explicitly on the impact of AAP on long COVID and found associations of long-term PM2.5 exposure with several symptoms, including dyspnoea [ – ], fatigue, reduced functional status [ ] and changes in smell and/or taste [ , ]. PM2.5 can deposit in the lungs, triggering inflammatory responses and disrupting normal cell function [ ]. AAP exposure can further negatively affect the immune system by inducing inflammation and oxidative stress decreasing the effectivity of mucociliary clearing and weakening the barrier tissues protecting the airways from infection [ , ]. Chronic exposure to AAP has already been associated with higher lung epithelial and vascular endothelial expression of ACE2, the receptor that SARS-CoV-2 uses to enter host cells [ ]. Animal models have confirmed this effect, with increased ACE2 expression observed in lung tissue following PM2.5 exposure [ , ]. This may facilitate viral entry and replication, potentially increasing both susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 and severity of COVID-19 outcomes. These findings suggest that AAP exposure may not only exacerbate respiratory morbidity, but also enhance the biological mechanisms underlying viral infectivity [ ].
While our recent systematic review found important associations of AAP exposure with COVID-19 severity [ ], there is a gap in synthesising toxicological studies that examine the molecular and immunological pathways involved in these associations. Additionally, there is also a lack of literature summarising the biological mechanisms of AAP exposure on the development and severity of long COVID. Therefore, this review aims to address this gap by summarising the scientific literature on the molecular pathways between AAP exposure, SARS-CoV-2 infection and both acute and long-term COVID-19 health outcomes. In addition, the Integrated Network and Dynamical Reasoning Assembler (INDRA) was used to extract biomechanistic statements from the included papers. INDRA constitutes a platform that combines inputs from several logic-based reading systems to subsequently preprocess them towards denoised and harmonised relationships [ ]. Each relationship consists of the type of relationship (e.g. activation and phosphorylation) and the entities (e.g. proteins, metabolites and genes) involved, overall referred to as a “statement”. Combined, all statements can be collectively visualised in structured networks (i.e. knowledge graphs).

## Methods
On 17 July 2025, PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science were searched for studies that examined the biological mechanisms of AAP exposure on SARS-CoV-2 health outcomes and long COVID. The full search terms are provided in table S1. PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) (table S2) reporting requirements were met and a protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024576652).

## Eligibility criteria
The PECOS (population, exposure, comparison, outcome and study design) framework was used to define the scope of this systematic review. Without any language restrictions, this review included studies that investigated the biological mechanisms (molecular or cellular pathways) of exposure to AAP on the health outcomes of SARS-CoV-2 (also including long COVID). Populations included cell lines, organoids, animal and in silico studies. The air pollutants included in this systematic review were: PM2.5, PM10, ultrafine particles (UFPs), NO2, O3, CO and SO2, where exposed groups were compared to nonexposed groups. SARS-CoV-2 health outcomes that were investigated in this review were hospitalisation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, severity, mortality and development/existence of long-term symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection.
All published papers from the start of the pandemic (according to the WHO: 11 March 2020) up until the search date were considered for the initial screening. Papers included in this systematic review were toxicological studies (in vitro, in vivo and in silico studies). In vitro studies included papers on cell lines, human-derived biomaterials or organoids. In vivo studies included animal studies. In silico studies included studies that analysed previously obtained datasets (e.g. toxicogenomic datasets), often using bioinformatics, machine learning, or mathematical modelling techniques to search for interactions/associations. Pre-prints and letters to the editor were also considered. Review papers, conference summaries and nonpeer-reviewed research were excluded from the review. Furthermore, we excluded papers that exclusively focused on the incidence or transmission of SARS-CoV-2. The snowball method was employed to screen the reference lists of relevant reviews.

## Selection and data extraction
Two independent reviewers (L. Houweling1 and I. Rots) engaged in the selection and data extraction process. Both reviewers conducted the initial screening based on the title and abstract and subsequently extracted data from the included studies following a second full-text screening. If the full text was unavailable, the authors of the respective papers were contacted. Conflicts between the two independent reviewers emerged for five articles when applying the pre-established inclusion criteria. These disagreements were resolved through discussion, and in one case, the consulting of a third reviewer (G.S. Downward), leading to the inclusion of two of these papers. From the original articles, relevant information including title, author, year of publication, language, study design, sample size, experimental materials, exposure (including type, source, duration of exposure, dose, timing and duration), outcome measure(s) (e.g. cell entry factor expression, gene expression and immune response), source of viral sample, control group information, methodological approach, statistical analysis, main findings and the author's main conclusion were collected.
To further objectively extract biomechanistic statements from the included papers and present them in structured networks, INDRA was utilised. INDRA aims to extract biomechanistic statements using text-mining tools [ , ]. These statements consist of a relationship type (e.g. inhibition, activation, phosphorylation) that connects two entities (where, e.g., A activates B, which simultaneously shows directionality: e.g. A inhibits B). The extracted statements are pre-processed to, for example, bin synonyms together, de-duplicate identical statements within a text and filter out hypothetical statements. Moreover, by counting how often a statement is made over different papers, it quantifies the amount of evidence and translates this into a belief score ranging between 0.5 and 1 for uncertainty versus full certainty. The exact function is reading-system dependent. INDRA was used to provide additional biological interpretation and to generate visual summaries of the pathways identified by the included studies. In the current study, INDRA was run independently for in vitro, in vivo or in silico categorised studies.
INDRA statements were extracted using the INDRA database, an open and publicly accessible repository covering millions of pre-scanned papers (i.e. covering a multitude of text-reading systems) deposited on PubMed. Of the available text mining systems, REACH has been acknowledged for its relatively high accuracy and extraction quantities [ ]. Therefore, in the current work, if full-paper extractions papers were not present in the INDRA database, we scanned ourselves using the REACH-INDRA pipeline [ ]. The UpSet plot with an overview of the coverage of the selected papers by the diverse reading systems in the INDRA database and a flow chart for subsequent manual scanning of papers are presented in figures S1 and S2, respectively.
Knowledge graphs extracted using INDRA can contain errors, including incorrectly assigned entities for example. In view of the small number of papers the belief score alone was not suitable to filter out such errors. Instead, for visualisation we focused on nodes with either two incoming or outgoing relationships. Next, combined networks (i.e. based on the original knowledge graph assemblies) were created by visualising nodes in common between networks [ ]. The belief score was used to visualise the amount of evidence for statements.

## Risk of bias evaluation
The primary reviewer (L. Houweling) assessed the methodological quality of the included in vitro and animal studies using the Toxicological Data Reliability Assessment Tool (ToxRTool) [ ]. This tool evaluates the quality of toxicological data reported in publications, distinguishing between in vitro and in vivo studies [ ]. It consists of a set of evaluation criteria categorised into five groups: “test substance identification”, “test system characterization”, “study design description”, “study results documentation”, and “plausibility of study design and data”. A detailed description of these criteria and individual questions is provided in table S3. Each criterion is scored as either met (“1”) or not met (“0”), with a maximum of 21 points for in vivo studies and 18 points for in vitro studies, leading to a final reliability categorisation based on the total score.
The ToxRTool does not include criteria for evaluating in silico studies; therefore, a separate quality assessment was conducted. Since no validated tool for in silico studies is currently available, we applied the approach described by Myatt
et al. [ ] to assess their reliability. This method integrated multiple in silico toxicology (IST) protocols to support consistent, reproducible and well-documented toxicological evaluations. To determine the quality of the included in silico studies, we focused on four key principles from this framework, namely planning of the analysis, the execution of the analysis, validation and reliability assessment, and expert review and interpretation [ ].

## Data synthesis
According to our protocol, a random-effects meta-analysis was planned if at least two studies were sufficiently comparable in terms of outcomes and exposures. As this threshold was not met, no meta-analysis was performed.

## Results

## Selection of eligible papers
The literature search across the four selected databases yielded a total of 2551 documents. After the removal of duplicates (n=1174), 1377 unique records remained for the initial screening. Following this process, 28 studies were selected for full-text review, of which 10 were subsequently excluded. Ultimately, 18 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated in this review ( ). Of these, nine were in vitro studies [ – ], eight were in vivo studies [ – ] and two were in silico studies [ , ]. One study included both in vitro and in vivo experiments and is therefore listed in both categories [ ].
 

## Methodological quality of the included studies
The results of the methodological quality and risk of bias assessment for the included in vitro and in vivo studies are presented in tables S4 and S5, respectively. Among the nine in vitro studies, five were classified as “reliable without restrictions”, while four in vitro studies were assessed as “reliable with restrictions” due to the absence of positive controls (positive controls were not strictly necessary for the study objectives, which is why these studies were not classified as “not reliable”). Of the eight included in vivo studies, one was classified as “not reliable” due to missing information on sample size, while the remaining six animal studies were assessed as “reliable without restrictions”.
The quality assessment of the two included in silico studies is presented in table S6. As shown per component in the table, Manivannan and Sundaresan [ ] aligned more closely with the IST framework due to better data documentation, methodological transparency and result interpretation. In contrast, the study by Đukić-Ćosić
et al. [ ] lacked independent validation and full reproducibility, resulting in a lower methodological quality and higher risk of bias.

## Findings of the included in vitro studies
The characteristics of the nine included in vitro studies are summarised in . The studies utilised various human cell lines, such as human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and adenocarcinomic human alveolar basal epithelial (A549) cells, to assess the effects of AAP exposure using in vitro models. All studies focused on PM exposure across different fractions [ – ]. For the included in vitro studies, biological networks identified by INDRA are visualised in figure S3, with belief scores ranging from 0.65 to 0.98. As shown by this network, key pathways include cell-entry factor expression through ACE2 and inflammatory response (including cytokine production). Through these pathways, SARS-CoV-2 infection and PM exposure both contribute to endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammatory signalling, potentially worsening COVID-19 disease severity.
 Seven studies reported a significant upregulation of ACE2 transcript and/or protein level in response to PM exposure across various human cell lines, increasing the number and availability of viral entry points for SARS-CoV-2 and making lung tissue more susceptible to viral invasion [ – , – ]. The study by Jin
et al. [ ] showed different levels of ACE2 transcription increases after PM exposure for different cell types. Additionally, the study by Lin
et al. [ ] showed an increase of cellular ACE2 in several cell types. However, the study by Shahbaz
et al. [ ] found no impact of PM on viral entry or replication in primary human olfactory mucosa cells, including ACE2 or TMPRSS2 expression. Nonetheless, this study observed a mild induction of oxidative stress and change in cytokine levels when comparing exposure to PM alone with exposure to both PM and SARS-CoV-2, which could contribute to variations in COVID-19 outcomes. This was further supported by five studies that demonstrated that PM exposure upregulates the genetic or protein expression of key inflammatory cytokines (e.g. interleukin (IL)-8, IL-6, NF-κB and IL-1β) associated with COVID-19 severity in different cellular models (human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), alveolar epithelial cells, airway organoids, A549 cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells and T47D human breast cancer cells) [ , – , ]. Additionally, these studies linked PM-induced inflammation to oxidative stress, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which contributed to viral replication, cellular stress and an impaired immune response [ , , ]. The study by Lin
et al. [ ] found that production of ROS was increased after exposure to PM2.5, but that this increase was lowered in cells expressing humanised ACE2 (hACE2). Kim
et al. [ ] suggested that diesel-derived PM2.5 (dPM2.5) exposure not only promotes inflammation through upregulation of IL-6 and IL-1β, but also induces apoptosis in lung epithelial cells. Additionally, dPM2.5 downregulated genes associated with cell adhesion and epithelial integrity, making lung tissue more vulnerable to inflammation-induced damage [ ].

## Findings of the included in vivo studies
 presents the characteristics of the eight included in vivo studies [ – ]. These studies used murine models, including BALB/c, C57BL/6 and hACE2 mice, to replicate human-like responses to AAP exposure. Among these studies, five examined PM exposure [ – ], while one focused on UFPs [ ], another on diesel exhaust particles (DEPs) [ ], and one on O3 exposure [ ]. The biological networks detected by INDRA for the included in vivo studies are presented in figure S4, with belief scores ranging from 0.65 to 0.95. As also shown by this network, key pathways include ACE2 and TMPRSS2 regulation and inflammatory response. Both SARS-CoV-2 infection and PM exposure impact ACE2 and DEP, linking them to inflammatory signalling and oxidative stress. Additionally, the network highlights connections to vasodilation, vasoconstriction and O3 exposure, suggesting multiple mechanisms through which air pollution may worsen COVID-19 outcomes.
 All studies consistently reported that pollutant exposure led to the upregulation of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 in lung epithelial cells [ , , , ] or lung homogenates [ ] as well as in systemic organs [ , , ]. By enhancing ACE2 availability for viral attachment, pollutants simultaneously reduce the protective anti-inflammatory functions of ACE2, thereby exacerbating the severity of COVID-19 outcomes [ , , , ]. Some studies explicitly stated that the disruption of the ACE/ACE2 balance, which is essential for controlling inflammation and preventing lung injury, leaves the body less prepared to counteract the cytokine storm often seen in severe COVID-19 cases [ , , , ]. In addition to examining the pathogenic role of ACE2 in viral infection, Lin
et al. [ ] also found evidence of a protective effect. In mice exposed to PM2.5, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling was downregulated, suggesting that ACE2 may also help suppress inflammation-related pathways. While most studies examined the potential for increased viral entry based on ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression, the study by Zhu
et al. [ ] more directly examined this mechanism by measuring SARS-CoV-2 spike protein adsorption and viral replication in hACE2 mice exposed to PM. Their findings revealed that PM exposure not only increased viral replication in reverse transcriptase PCR, but also exacerbated lung tissue damage, hypoxaemia and severe respiratory injury as shown by their histology and blood gas analysis [ ]. However, these results must be interpreted with caution as the ToxRTool indicated these results were “not reliable” (table S5). Another study, by Nguyen-Alley
et al. [ ], investigated the interaction between a high-fat diet and DEP exposure, demonstrating that dietary factors can compound the negative effects of pollutant exposure. High-fat diet consumption exacerbated the DEP-induced upregulation of ACE2 and TMPRSS2, highlighting how metabolic health can influence pollutant-driven susceptibility to severe COVID-19 [ ].
In addition to increased ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression, some studies also reported pollutant-triggered inflammatory responses [ , – ]. Exposure to pollutants caused organ-dependent changes in expression of upregulated inflammatory-related proteins, such as cyclo-oxygenase-2, heme oxygenase 1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase in the lungs and systemic organs (e.g. heart and brain), which contribute to oxidative stress and further weaken tissue defences [ , ]. This heightened inflammation was linked to severe COVID-19 complications, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [ ]. Lin
et al. [ ] showed an increase in tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and IL-6 levels when mice were exposed to PM2.5 compared to the control, where the levels were highest in ACE2 knockout mice. Furthermore, Zhu
et al. [ ] also observed the upregulation of various pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-2) in the lungs when mice were exposed to both SARS-CoV-2 and PM compared to mice only exposed to SARS-CoV-2 and found that this increased cytokine expression was associated with elevated ACE2 and TMPRSS2 transcript expression in mice exposed to SARS-CoV-2 and PM compared to mice exposed to PM alone. This connection enhanced both viral entry and the inflammatory response, with these mechanisms collectively contributing to a higher risk of a cytokine storm, ultimately leading to a more severe disease course in SARS-CoV-2 infection [ ].

## Findings of the included in silico studies
The literature search identified two in silico studies that highlighted the molecular mechanisms linking air pollution and COVID-19 health outcomes ( ) [ , ]. Figure S5 depicts the networks identified by the in silico studies, with belief scores ranging from 0.65 to 0.95. Due to the large number of bio-mechanistic statements and the limited number of in silico studies (n=2), INDRA emphasised broader pathways. This network shows the role of inflammatory responses in both SARS-CoV-2 infection and PM exposure, without involving cell entry factors. Both influencing cytokine production, IL-6 expression and immune defence mechanisms, potentially exacerbating COVID-19 disease severity.
 Manivannan and Sundaresan [ ] used transcriptomic data from lung biopsies from COVID-19 patients, SARS-CoV-2-infected epithelial cells and tissues exposed to PM. Additionally, they used proteomic datasets to analyse molecular interactions. Their findings demonstrated significant overlap between gene expression changes modulated by PM exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly involving pathways associated with viral pathogenesis, but also inflammation-related pathways such as TNF, IL-17 and NF-κB signalling. They did not identify a notable change in ACE2 or TMPRSS2 gene expression [ ]. Đukić-Ćosić
et al. [ ] used a comparative toxicogenomic database (CTD) to identify genes associated with both air pollution exposure and COVID-19 complications. Their findings showed that air pollution exposure, including PM, SO2, NO2 and O3, upregulate cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF, contributing to cytokine storms and severe COVID-19 outcomes. Among the pollutants, PM2.5 exposure interacted with the largest number of genes, including those involved in ACE2 pathways [ ].

## Combined biological networks
The networks generated by INDRA from individual study designs visualised the biological pathways identified in the included studies, as previously shown in the results. displays the combined network derived from joined pathways identified in the in vitro and in vivo studies. In this merged network, the nodes are in common, while relationships vary. Hollow arrows highlight shared relationships across networks, with two belief scores indicating their presence in different sources. To provide a clearer and more detailed overview, the in silico studies were excluded from this combined figure, as they identified considerably fewer specific pathways.
 As shown in the figure, two similar mechanisms for PM exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection were identified, namely 1) the effect on viral entry factors ACE2 and TMPRSS2 and 2) the effect on the inflammatory response. The network illustrates the relatively high certainty (i.e. based on belief score) for relationships between PM exposure and ACE2/TMPRSS2, suggesting that exposure may enhance the expression of these viral entry factors, potentially increasing susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. In addition to facilitating viral entry, both PM exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection contribute to an amplified inflammatory response. The presence of feedback loops suggests that once the inflammatory response is activated, it can further amplify ACE2 expression, potentially sustaining a cycle of increased susceptibility and immune activation. The combined network also highlights joined bio-mechanistic relationships between ACE2, the renin–angiotensin system (RAS) and angiotensin (Ang) II, which are critical regulators of vascular function. PM exposure and SARS-CoV-2 both modulate these pathways, leading to effects on vasodilation and vasoconstriction. Importantly, the combined effects of altered ACE2 signalling converge on the development of lung injury, a key pathological outcome observed in COVID-19 disease. Overall, this combined network provides a visualisation of the joined pathways as identified by the included in vitro and in vivo studies influenced by both PM exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection.

## Discussion

## Summary of results
This systematic review investigated the molecular mechanisms linking AAP exposure to COVID-19 severity. Findings from the nine in vitro, eight in vivo and two in silico studies consistently indicated that exposure to AAP can exacerbate COVID-19 severity through multiple interconnected biological pathways. Using text mining and network analysis methods (i.e. INDRA), we found consistency between the strength of computationally inferred networks (moderate to high belief scores) and the reviewers’ evaluations. Both approaches identified two key mechanisms, as follows: 1) the upregulation (at the protein and/or gene level) of viral entry factors, such as ACE2 and TMPRSS2, in both pulmonary and systemic tissues, potentially enhancing viral entry and replication, and 2) pollutant-induced immune hypersensitivity and dysregulation, marked by heightened inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, which contribute to more severe disease outcomes. While most of the studies focused on PM exposure, similar effects were observed for exposure to UFP and O3, as well as certain pesticides and heavy metals.

## Exploration of findings
The findings of this systematic review indicate that AAP exposure increases ACE2 and/or TMPRSS2 transcript and protein levels in lung tissue and cell models, with interactions involving multiple gene regulators. Multiple epidemiological studies also found similar pathways involved in COVID-19 severity in humans [ – ]. An observational study of 88 COVID-19 patients (mean age 48 years), found significantly increased ACE2 and TMPRSS2 gene expression in severe cases (e.g. peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) <94%, respiratory rate>30 breaths·min−1) compared to those with mild cases (SpO2 >94%) [ ]. As a result, the ACE/ACE2 balance is disrupted, which is a key inflammation regulator and occurs as ACE promotes Ang II production, inducing vasoconstriction, inflammation, and fibrosis. ACE2 counteracts this by converting Ang II into the anti-inflammatory Ang-(1–7). An imbalance favouring ACE over ACE2 can drive excessive inflammation and tissue damage, worsening lung injury and increasing the risk of severe complications such as ARDS [ ]. A prospective study of 188 hospitalised COVID-19 patients showed that severe cases (primarily patients admitted to the ICU due to ARDS) had significantly elevated soluble ACE2 levels in the bloodstream [ ]. This increase may partly result from extensive lung tissue damage, leading to greater shedding of ACE2 from damaged cells into circulation. While this may indicate greater enzymatic activity, excessive ACE2 shedding could reduce its lung availability, disrupting the ACE2/Ang-(1–7) balance. This imbalance may also disrupt the RAS, worsening inflammation, tissue damage and COVID-19 severity [ ]. Furthermore, in a study performed in porcine intestine epithelial cells, ACE2 was shown to inhibit phosphorylation of MAPK pathway components extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) 1 and ERK2, thereby suppressing inflammatory effects [ ]. Together, these findings highlight the potential for AAP exposure to disrupt the ACE2 balance in similar ways. Most of our included studies examined the effects of acute or short-term air pollution exposure. However, chronic exposure may lead to even more prolonged upregulation of these proteins, potentially further increasing the risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes via the various described biological pathways [ ].
Beyond viral entry mechanisms, our findings reveal that AAP exposure significantly activates the immune system, leading to the release of key inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α and NF-κB, a transcription factor strongly linked to severe COVID-19 outcomes. These studies connected this to oxidative stress, increased ROS production and apoptotic signalling, collectively weakening the immune response and accelerating disease progression. These inflammatory responses can further disrupt the ACE2/Ang-(1–7) balance, increasing tissue damage and contributing to ARDS [ ]. In ICU-admitted COVID-19 patients, elevated levels of certain cytokines have been well-documented compared to non-ICU patients [ – ]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the excessive release of inflammatory cytokines, known as a cytokine storm, may be acutely fatal in critically ill COVID-19 patients [ , ]. Notably, the in vivo studies included in the current review demonstrated that pollutant exposure amplified systemic inflammation, affecting not only the lungs but also the heart and brain. A systematic review by Gupta
et al. [ ] highlights multiple pathways contributing to multi-organ complications in severe COVID-19 cases, including direct viral toxicity via ACE2, endothelial damage, immune dysregulation and RAS imbalance. These mechanisms drive cardiovascular, renal, neurological, gastrointestinal and endocrine complications [ ]. As our findings show that air pollution negatively impacts similar pathways, by promoting inflammation, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, AAP exposure may further amplify systemic damage and worsen outcomes in severe COVID-19 cases.
Currently, no mechanistic studies have directly examined the impact of AAP exposure on long COVID, limiting the exploration of molecular pathways in this review. However, research suggests that persistent inflammation is a key factor in experiencing long-term symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection, which may involve similar pathways affected by air pollution [ ]. Additionally, plasma ACE2 activity has been hypothesised to play a role in managing long COVID by affecting the ACE2/Ang-(1–7) balance [ ]. This disruption may promote persistent microclots and fibrosis, leading to impaired oxygenation, which is a key feature of long COVID impacting the respiratory, nervous and cardiovascular systems [ ]. Since our findings show that AAP exposure can disrupt the ACE2 balance in similar ways, it may worsen symptoms by further impairing protective mechanisms (e.g. reducing ACE2 availability, limiting its anti-inflammatory effects and further promoting inflammation and tissue damage). Future mechanistic studies using in vitro or in vivo models are needed to further investigate these hypotheses, ultimately providing treatment options for patients experiencing long-term symptoms.
The association between AAP exposure and respiratory infections has been studied not only in the context of SARS-CoV-2 but also for other viruses. The overexpression of ACE2 due to exposure to NO2 and PM2.5 was previously observed during the SARS-CoV-1 epidemic in 2003 and the Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS) outbreak in Saudi Arabia in 2012 [ ]. Other studies on respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) have shown a positive correlation between RSV cases and exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 [ ], with another study indicating that diesel engine emissions can enhance inflammation while impairing anti-infection mechanisms in RSV-infected mice [ ]. Furthermore, exposure to PM10 has been linked to weakened immune responses and increased viral replication in RNA virus infections such as SARS and MERS [ ]. In their in vitro models, this study showed that exposure to PM10 during an RNA virus infection, especially an influenza virus infection, significantly increases viral infectivity. This effect is linked to the suppression of innate immune responses and the activation of various metabolic processes, which may contribute to the ability of air pollutants to enhance viral replication within cells and lead to (severe) respiratory damage [ ]. Collectively, these findings suggest that AAP exposure may heighten susceptibility to various respiratory infections, thereby disrupting the immune responses to viral pathogens, which could increase infection severity.

## Strengths, limitations and recommendations
This systematic review offers a distinctive approach by synthesising evidence from a variety of study designs to explore the molecular pathways that connect AAP exposure to COVID-19 severity. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first review to make such a distinction, offering a comprehensive assessment of how air pollution influences the progression of COVID-19. A strength of this review was the application of a systems biology-driven text-mining approach (i.e. INDRA) to extract data from the included studies and structure it into biological networks. This allowed for the visualisation of reported molecular pathways related to SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19 and AAP exposure, and helps reduce subjective bias in the extraction of information. Additionally, INDRA incorporated belief scores, providing an objective measure to quantify the amount of evidence and assess the confidence of the identified networks. To further ensure the reliability and reproducibility of our findings, two independent reviewers conducted screenings and data extraction. We conducted an extensive search across four different databases and included a broad range of air pollutants in our study. However, most studies focused on PM exposure, likely due to its easier application in laboratory settings, while the biological effects of gaseous pollutants such as NO2 and SO2, which require specialised exposure systems, remain underexplored. Further research is needed to investigate the molecular pathways of other air pollutants. Furthermore, the studies reviewed primarily examined short-term and single exposure events, which may not fully represent the complexities of real-world urban pollution. Future research should focus on long-term and combined exposures to better assess their true impact. Additionally, most studies relied on cell or animal models, which may not fully mirror human biological responses. Importantly, there were no studies available that examine the molecular mechanisms underlying the effect of AAP exposure on the development or severity of symptoms after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, leaving this aspect of our research question unanswered. Further studies using similar methodologies to investigate these pathways are crucial to aid in the recovery of patients experiencing long-term symptoms. As a final consideration, while we did not explicitly exclude studies based on written language, our search was performed in English, raising potential language bias against non-English terms. In addition, publication bias may have affected our findings, as it is more likely for papers with positive findings to be published compared to papers with null results.

## Conclusion
Building on our previous systematic review and meta-analysis, which found significant associations between AAP exposure and COVID-19 severity, this systematic review explores the mechanisms behind these associations. Our findings, supported by in vitro, in vivo and in silico models, suggest that AAP exposure may worsen COVID-19 outcomes through multiple interconnected biological pathways, including increased viral entry, disrupted inflammatory responses and aggravated tissue damage. These factors contribute to ACE2 imbalance, a heightened cytokine storm and multi-organ complications, all of which are critical in COVID-19 severity. Public health strategies and interventions should therefore focus on mitigating the impact of air pollution exposure on COVID-19 and future respiratory pandemics.

## Points for clinical practice
Ambient air pollution can worsen COVID-19 severity by increasing viral entry factors and promoting immune dysregulation. These mechanisms may lead to heightened inflammation, oxidative stress and tissue damage, increasing the risk of severe respiratory outcomes.
Long-term exposure to ambient air pollution may have prolonged effects on lung health and immune function, highlighting the importance of public health strategies to reduce pollution-related respiratory health outcomes.

## Questions for future research
Through which mechanisms do ambient air pollution exposure impact long-term COVID-19 outcomes, including post-acute sequelae (long COVID)?
What are the molecular effects of combined exposure to multiple pollutants (e.g. PM, NO2 and O3) on COVID-19?
