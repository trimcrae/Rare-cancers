## Abstract

## Background
Liver fibrosis is a crucial pathological stage in the progression of chronic liver diseases. Yiguanjian (YGJ), a Chinese herbal formula, exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and hepatoprotective effects. Extracellular vesicles from bone-marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC-EVs) have shown potential in treating various disorders, including liver fibrosis. This study investigated the regulatory effects of EVs from YGJ-preconditioned BMSCs (YGJ-EVs) on TGF-β1-stimulated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and their therapeutic potential in a mouse model of liver fibrosis, with a focus on identifying the causative microRNA cargo.

## Methods
YGJ-EVs and control EVs were isolated from BMSC culture supernatants and characterized via western blotting, transmission electron microscopy, and nanoparticle tracking analysis. Their cellular uptake in vitro and in vivo was evaluated using DIR labeling. To identify candidate miRNAs mediating YGJ-EV bioactivity, miRNA microarray analysis was conducted. To assess the effect of YGJ-EVs on liver fibrosis, TGF-β1-activated HSC cells were treated with YGJ-EVs or control-EVs for 24 h, and then the expression of proteins related to fibrotic activation (COL1-A1 and α-SMA), lysosomal biogenesis (LAMP1, TPP1, CTSD, and CTSB) mitophagy (p62, LC3, PINK1, and Parkin), and the Akt/AMPK/TFEB pathway was assessed. To determine whether miR-7045-5p is the causative factor, HSC cells transfected with miR-7045-5p were similarly analyzed.

## Results
miRNA microarray analysis revealed miR-7045-5p upregulation in YGJ-EVs versus control EVs. In CCl4-treated mice, YGJ-EV-derived miR-7045-5p ameliorated the liver fibrosis, improved the hepatic function, and suppressed the HSC activation by inhibiting the Akt/AMPK/TFEB pathway. In vitro, miR-7045-5p overexpression attenuated TGF-β1-induced HSC activation.

## Conclusion
YGJ increases miR-7045-5p abundance in BMSC-EVs. YGJ-EVs alleviate liver fibrosis by delivering the anti-fibrotic miRNA miR-7045-5p, which inhibits the Akt/AMPK/TFEB pathway, thereby promoting lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in HSCs.

## Introduction
Chronic liver disease poses a major global health burden, accounting for approximately 2 million deaths annually worldwide [ ]. Hepatic fibrosis is a common pathological feature of chronic liver diseases. It is characterized by the progressive accumulation and deposition of excessive extracellular matrix (ECM). When ECM synthesis surpasses ECM degradation, hepatic fibrosis may progress to cirrhosis and ultimately develop into hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. The degree of fibrosis directly influences both the quality of life and survival outcomes of patients with liver disease [ ]. Despite extensive research, the underlying mechanisms of hepatic fibrosis remain incompletely understood, and effective diagnostic and therapeutic options are still lacking. Therefore, continued investigation into strategies for alleviating hepatic fibrosis remains of significant clinical importance.
Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based regenerative therapy is a clinical approach in which MSCs contribute to liver regeneration either directly, by migrating to hepatic tissue, or indirectly, via their paracrine secretome [ ]. This strategy has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic option for liver diseases, although potential risks such as immune rejection and tumorigenesis remain [ ]. Bone-marrow-MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (BMSC-EVs), nanoscale vesicles measuring 40–160 nm in diameter, are enriched with functional proteins, mRNAs, and microRNAs (miRNAs). As key mediators of intercellular communication, BMSC-EVs exert diverse regulatory effects on target cells [ ]. Studies have demonstrated that BMSC-EVs suppress hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and collagen deposition [ ]. Moreover, pretreatment of MSCs with cytokines or small-molecule compounds can enhance their therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. For instance, BMSC-EVs preconditioned with baicalin demonstrate improved inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and correction of Th17/Treg cell imbalance, thereby alleviating hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury [ ].
Yiguanjian (YGJ), a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formula composed of Rehmannia glutinosa (Shengdihuang), Glehnia littoralis (Beishashen), Ophiopogon japonicus (Maidong), Angelica sinensis (Danggui), Lycium barbarum (Gouqizi), and Melia toosendan (Chuanlianzi), has long been regarded as a principal remedy for hepatic fibrosis in both historical and modern medical practice. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that YGJ attenuates inflammatory responses and fibrotic progression in murine models of chronic hepatitis [ ]. Accordingly, this study hypothesized that EVs derived from YGJ-pretreated BMSCs (YGJ-EVs) have enhanced hepatic anti-fibrotic bioactivity. To test this hypothesis, EVs were isolated from YGJ-conditioned BMSCs and characterized experimentally. The results revealed that YGJ preconditioning modulates the abundance of miR-7045-5p within BMSC-EVs. Notably, YGJ-EVs were found to be enriched in miR-7045-5p and regulated the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling pathway, thereby promoting lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in mouse HSCs. This dual mechanism synergistically suppressed HSC activation and alleviated hepatic fibrosis in mice. These findings offer novel insights and mechanistic evidence supporting the clinical potential of YGJ-EVs in the treatment of hepatic fibrosis.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Ethics number: AEEI-2015-123). BALB/c mice aged 4–6 weeks were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at a controlled ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C. All the experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with institutional guidelines for animal welfare. Study findings were reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

## Cell culture
BALB/c mice (22–25 g) were anesthetized via isoflurane (RWD Life Science, China; Cat No.2023110301) gas inhalation in an anesthetic chamber (induction concentration, 2%; maintenance concentration, 1%), and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation in strict accordance with ethical guidelines to ensure a humane and painless death. Under aseptic conditions, bone marrow was extracted from the tibiae and femora of the mice. Primary cells were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, USA; Cat No. c12571500BT) containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning, USA, Cat No. 35-081-CV) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA; Cat No. 15140122) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Non-adherent cells were removed by medium replacement every 2 days. Adherent cells at passage 0 (P0) were designated as BMSCs. Cells from P3–P5 were used for subsequent experiments. Surface markers (CD11b, CD45, CD29, and CD90) were analyzed via flow cytometry to confirm BMSC identity.
Mouse hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) (iCell Bioscience Inc, China, Cat: iCell-0094a) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM complete medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected for subsequent experiments.

## Preparation of YGJ water decoction
The crude herbs of Yiguanjian (YGJ)—Shengdi, Maidong, Shashen, Gouqizi, Danggui, and Chuanlianzi (all sourced from Beijing Tongrentang, China)—were soaked in distilled water at a 6:1 (v/w) ratio for 2 h. The mixture was initially boiled vigorously over high heat and then simmered on low heat for 20 min. The decoction was collected, and the residues were re-boiled with 4× (v/w) distilled water for 30 min. The two batches of decoction were combined, concentrated to a final concentration of 6.67 g crude drug/kg, and stored in sterile bottles at 4 °C. Before use, the decoction was warmed to 37 °C in a water bath.

## Preparation of YGJ-containing serum
Twenty Sprague-Dawley rats were housed under SPF conditions with ad libitum access to food and water (Ethics number: AEEI-2015-123). They were randomly assigned to either the YGJ or control group (n = 10/group). The YGJ group received a YGJ suspension at 6.67 g/kg twice daily for 3 days, while the control group was administered an equivalent volume of 0.9% saline. All the rats were fasted for 12 h before the final administration and anesthetized using 200 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital 1 h post-administration and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation. Rats were confirmed dead when autonomous respiration, reflex activity, and heart activity were absent. Blood was collected via the abdominal aorta, allowed to clot at room temperature for 4 h, and then centrifuged at 4 °C (3000 rpm for 30 min). The resulting supernatant serum was heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min, filter-sterilized through 0.22 μm membranes, and stored at − 20 °C until use.

## Cell-viability assay
Cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (NCM Biotech, China; Cat No. C6005) according to the manufacturer’s protocol to assess the effects of YGJ-containing serum at various concentrations on BMSCs and the impact of YGJ-EVs at different doses on HSCs.
BMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated with YGJ-containing serum at concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, or 10% for 12–24 h. Their viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Based on the results, 10% YGJ-containing serum was selected for subsequent experiments.
HSCs were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Following pre-treatment with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, HSCs were exposed to YGJ-EVs at concentrations of 0, 12.5, 25, 50, or 100 µg/mL for 6, 12, or 24 h. Cell viability was then quantified using the CCK-8 assay.

## Isolation and characterization of EVs
To prepare the EV-free complete medium, FBS was cleared of intrinsic EVs via centrifugation at 100,000×g overnight. Then, α-MEM was supplemented with the resulting EV-free FBS according to standard preparation ratios. BMSCs at P3–P5, exhibiting optimal growth, were cultured until they reached 80% confluence. The cells were randomly assigned to control and YGJ-pretreated groups. The control group was cultured in α-MEM with 10% EV-free FBS, whereas the YGJ-pretreated group was incubated in α-MEM with 10% YGJ-containing serum for 48 h. Culture supernatants from both groups were collected, and EVs (control-EVs and YGJ-EVs) were isolated using an Exosome Extraction Kit (Umibio, China; Cat No. UR52121) and stored at − 80 °C.
The protein concentrations in EVs and YGJ-EVs were determined using a BCA assay kit (Lablead, China; Cat No. P00013B). Morphological evaluation was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The size distribution and concentration of the EVs were assessed via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Western blotting for the EV markers Alix, HSP70, TSG101, and CD81 was used to confirm EV identity. The endoplasmic reticulum protein Calnexin was used as a negative control.

## Tracking EVs
YGJ-EVs and control EVs were fluorescently labeled with DIR (Umibio, China; Cat No. UR21017). For in vivo tracking, 80 µg of DIR-labeled YGJ-EVs or control EVs were administered to mice via tail vein injection. After 6 h, the in vivo distribution of the EVs was visualized using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Perkin Elmer, USA).
For in vitro tracking, HSCs were incubated with 80 µg of DIR-labeled EVs for 12 h. Afterward, the cells were subjected to immunofluorescence staining, and EV uptake was observed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

## Animal experiments
Thirty-six male BALB/c mice aged 8–10 weeks were randomly assigned to a normal group (n = 6) and a model group (n = 30), and their body weights were recorded. After a 1-week acclimatization period, the mice in the model group received intraperitoneal injections of 4% CCl4 [in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] at a dose of 0.1 mL/10 g body weight twice weekly for 4 weeks to induce liver fibrosis. The normal group received equivalent volumes of olive oil following the same schedule. Upon completion of the modeling (4 weeks), the mice in the model group were randomly divided into the following five groups (n = 6/group): model, control-EV, and YGJ-EV groups (received 100 µL PBS, 80 µg EVs in 100 µL PBS, and 80 µg YGJ-EVs in 100 µL PBS, respectively, via tail-vein injection twice weekly for 2 weeks), as well as Akt-agonist and Akt-inhibitor groups (received 10 mg/kg SC79 [MCE, China; Cat No. HY-18749] and 50 mg/kg Akt inhibitor VIII [MCE, China; Cat No. HY-10355)], respectively, via intraperitoneal [i.p.] injection once weekly for 2 weeks).
At the experimental endpoint, the mice were anesthetized via isoflurane gas inhalation in an anesthetic chamber (induction concentration, 2%; maintenance concentration, 1%). Liver morphology was assessed using a high-resolution small-animal color Doppler ultrasound system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Inc.). The animals were subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation in strict accordance with ethical guidelines to ensure a humane and painless death. Blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis. The liver was harvested and then preserved in liquid nitrogen for western blotting and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses or in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological evaluation.

## Serum biochemical analysis
Liver function was evaluated by measuring serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in an automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

## Histological analysis
Liver samples preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis or with Masson’s trichrome to assess collagen deposition.

## Calcein-acetoxymethyl-ester (AM)/propidium iodide (PI) staining
Cells from each group were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL. After 12 h, they were rinsed with PBS, and then stained using a Calcein-AM/PI Cell Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime, China; Cat No. C2015M) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Live cells (green fluorescence) and dead cells (red fluorescence) were visualized using a Nikon Ti2-U fluorescence microscope.

## LysoTracker-green staining
Cells from each group were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL. After 12 h, they were rinsed with PBS, and then stained using a Lyso-Tracker Green Lysosomal Fluorescent Probe Kit (Beyotime, China; Cat No. C1047S) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Lysosomes (green fluorescence) and nuclei (blue fluorescence) were visualized under a Nikon Ti2-U fluorescence microscope.

## TEM
Cells from each group were collected via centrifugation. After removing the supernatant, they were fixed with electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, China; Cat No. G1102) for 4 h. Following PBS washing, the cells were embedded in a pre-warmed 1% agarose solution. After cooling, the agarose-embedded cells were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (Ted Pella Inc., USA; Cat No. 18456) in the dark for 2 h. Gradual dehydration was performed at 20–30 °C, followed by infiltration and polymerization with 812 epoxy resin (SPI, USA; Cat No. 90529-77-4). Ultrathin sections (60 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed via TEM to assess cellular ultrastructure.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The COL1-A1 and α-SMA protein levels in cell-culture supernatants were measured using commercial ELISA kits (JONLIBO, China; Cat No. JL14118 and JL20208) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Extraction of nuclear proteins
Nuclear proteins from liver samples and HSCs were isolated using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China; Cat No. P0027) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted nuclear proteins were stored at − 80 °C for subsequent western-blot analysis.

## Transfection of HSCs with the miR-7045-5p mimic
To evaluate the effect of miR-7045-5p on HSCs, a miR-7045-5p mimic (AGCGGGUGGGGGAGGGGGACU; KeyGEN, China) and negative control (miR-7045-5p-mimic-NC) were designed. HSCs were activated via incubation in the serum-free medium with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h. Following trypsinization, centrifugation, and cell counting, activated HSCs were randomly assigned to the model, mimic, or negative-control group. Non-activated HSCs served as the normal group. Transfection was conducted using a liposomal transfection reagent (Yeasen, China; Cat No. 40802ES) when HSCs reached 70% confluence in 6-well plates. Each well was transfected with 100 pmol of the corresponding construct. Cells and culture supernatants were collected 24 h post-transfection for subsequent analyses.

## MiRNA microarray analysis
YGJ-EVs and control EVs were subjected to miRNA microarray analysis (n = 3 per group). Total RNA was extracted from EVs by using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, USA). Biotinylated complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 250 ng total RNA by using the Ambion® WT Expression Kit, followed by fragmentation and labeling according to the Affymetrix standard protocol. Labeled cDNA was hybridized to GeneChip® miRNA 4.0 Arrays (Affymetrix, USA) at 45 °C for 16 h. After hybridization, the arrays were washed and stained using the Affymetrix Fluidics Station 450. Scanning was conducted in a GeneChip® Scanner 3000 7G, and data were processed using the Affymetrix® GeneChip Command Console. Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified, and significance thresholds were set at P < 0.01 for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and P < 0.05 for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis.

## Immunofluorescence staining
HSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking with 10% normal goat serum at 37 °C for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-TFEB (1:500, Proteintech, China; Cat No. 13372-1-AP) or anti-PINK1 (1:500, Proteintech, USA; Cat No. 23274-1-AP). Following PBS washes, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200, Servicebio, China; Cat No. GB21303). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) by using a DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence signals were observed under a Nikon Ti2-U fluorescence microscope (Nikon Instruments, Wetzlar, Japan).

## Western blotting
Liver and cell samples were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Lablead, China; Cat: P0013B) supplemented with 1% PMSF (Biosharp, China; Cat No. BL507A) and 1% Phosphatase Inhibitor (NCM Biotech, China; Cat No. MP003). Subsequently, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Lablead, China; Cat No. P00013B). Lysate samples with equal amounts of protein were subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The resolved protein bands were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA).
After incubating the membranes in a blocking solution of 5% milk in TBST (Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween-20) for 1 h at 25–30 °C, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti-Akt (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 4685 S), anti-AMPK (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 2532 S), anti-phospho-AMPK (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 2535 S), anti-TFEB (1:500, Proteintech, China; Cat No. 13372-1-AP), anti-P62 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 39749 S), anti-LC3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 43566 S), anti-LAMP1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 9091 S), anti-PINK1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China; Cat No. 23274-1-AP), anti-Parkin (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 4211 S), anti-α-SMA (1:1000, Sigma, USA; Cat No. A2547), anti-COL1-A1 (1:1000, Abcam, USA; Cat No. ab270993), anti-PCNA (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 13110T), and anti-GAPDH (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 60004-1-Ig-100UL). All the antibodies were diluted in 5% milk in TBST.
Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit (1:5000, Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA; Cat No. 312-005-003) or anti-mouse (1:5000, Proteintech, China; Cat No. SA00001-1) IgG-HRP for 2 h at room temperature (20–30 °C). Target protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (NCM, China; Cat No. P10200), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ.

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from liver and cell samples using an RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, China; Cat No. RC112-01). For mRNA analysis, 3000 ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by using a reverse-transcription kit (Servicebio, China; Cat No. G3337). The reaction mixture consisted of 4 µL 5× SweScript All-in-One SuperMix, 1 µL gDNA remover, total RNA, and nuclease-free water, adjusted to a final volume of 20 µL. For miRNA analysis, 5000 ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using a miRNA-specific reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, China; Cat No. G3330). The reaction system included 4 µL 5× reaction buffer, 1 µL Servicebio-RT enzyme mix, 1 µL miRNA-7045-5p-specific RT primer, total RNA, and nuclease-free water, adjusted to a final volume of 20 µL.
qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green Taq kit (Servicebio, China; Cat No. G3320) under the following conditions: (1) initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; (2) 40 cycles of incubation at 95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. All the primers were designed using Primer 5.0, and their sequences are provided in Table  .
 

## Statistical analysis
All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9, with P < 0.05 considered the threshold for statistical significance. The data are presented as mean ± SD, based on a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical analyses included one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test.

## Results

## Characterization of BMSCs and BMSC-EVs
BMSCs at P3 exhibited the classical spindle-shaped morphology (Fig.  A). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the presence of CD29 and CD90 and the absence of CD45 and CD11b (Fig.  B). The CCK-8 assay revealed no significant differences in cell viability between the control group and those treated with 2.5%, 5%, or 10% YGJ-containing serum, indicating that YGJ-containing serum (up to 10%) was not cytotoxic to BMSCs (Fig.  C). Therefore, subsequent experiments employed 10% YGJ-containing serum with a 24-h intervention (see Supplementary Data for the analysis of the serum components).
 TEM images showed that nanoparticles isolated from the culture supernatants of both the control and test groups exhibited characteristic circular or elliptical membrane-bound vesicle structures (Fig.  D). NTA results indicated that particle diameters were predominantly around 100 nm (Fig.  E). Western blotting demonstrated the abundant presence of the EV-specific markers Alix, HSP70, TSG101, and CD81, along with the absence of the endoplasmic-reticulum marker Calnexin (negative control) in the nanoparticles isolated from both the control and test groups, unlike in the EV-free supernatant (Fig.  F). These results confirm successful EV isolation.
To track EV biodistribution in mice, DiR-labeled control EVs and YGJ-EVs were intravenously injected via the tail vein. IVIS detected strong fluorescent signals in the liver 6 h post-injection, demonstrating hepatic tropism for both the control EVs and YGJ-EVs (Fig.  G). Additionally, fluorescence microscopy confirmed efficient uptake of DiR-labeled control EVs and YGJ-EVs by HSCs after 12 h of co-incubation (Fig.  H).

## Identification of candidate anti-fibrotic MiRNAs in YGJ-EVs
Given the critical role of EV-encapsulated miRNAs in intercellular communication, we performed miRNA microarray analysis to compare miRNA profiles between control EVs and YGJ-EVs, aiming to identify miRNAs with potential therapeutic effects against hepatic fibrosis. Microarray analysis revealed 49 enriched miRNAs and 7 depleted miRNAs in YGJ-EVs compared with control EVs (Fig.  A). Notably, miR-7045-5p showed the most significant enrichment in YGJ-EVs (Fig.  B), which was validated via RT-qPCR (Fig.  C).
 Bioinformatic prediction using the MiRanda and TargetScan databases identified 44 putative target genes of miR-7045-5p, including Akt1 (Fig.  F). A miR-7045-5p-target gene interaction network was constructed to visualize regulatory relationships. Pathway enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed genes via KEGG annotation demonstrated significant downregulation of key pathways, such as the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig.  J). These findings suggest that YGJ-EVs alleviate hepatic fibrosis by enhancing autophagic activity, with miR-7045-5p and its downstream target Akt1 playing pivotal roles in this pathophysiological mechanism.

## YGJ-EVs alleviate CCl4-induced hepatic fibrosis
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of YGJ-EVs against hepatic fibrosis, mice with CCl4-induced hepatic fibrosis were used. Macroscopic examination revealed that the livers of the mice in the normal control group had smooth surfaces and exhibited a deep red color. Color Doppler ultrasound results indicated that, compared with the renal texture (blue arrows), the liver parenchyma (red arrows) showed homogeneous echogenicity with a well-defined capsule. In contrast, the livers from the model group exhibited granular surfaces with pale discoloration. Compared with the renal texture in the model group, ultrasound-detectable heterogeneous echogenicity, hepatocyte disorganization in H&E-stained sections, and collagen deposition in sections stained with Masson’s trichrome stain, confirming the successful induction of fibrosis. These histopathological signs of hepatic fibrosis were less pronounced in the groups treated with control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII than in the model group, suggesting that these treatments ameliorated the fibrosis. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the SC79-treated group and the model group, indicating that the Akt agonist SC79 caused no significant improvement (Fig.  A). Serum biochemical analysis showed markedly higher ALT and AST levels in the model group than in the normal group. Treatments with control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII significantly reduced ALT and AST levels, with YGJ-EVs exhibiting the strongest inhibitory effects (Fig.  B and C).
 Western-blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of COL1-A1 and α-SMA protein levels in fibrotic liver tissues. These fibrotic markers were markedly downregulated following treatment with control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII, whereas SC79 had no significant effect (Fig.  D–F). RT-qPCR confirmed that YGJ-EVs, EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII reversed the COL1-A1 and α-SMA upregulation in fibrotic livers, with YGJ-EVs demonstrating the greatest efficacy (Fig.  G and H).
Collectively, these data indicate that YGJ-EVs significantly ameliorate hepatic fibrosis in mice, outperforming EVs derived from BMSCs not primed with YGJ.

## YGJ-EVs alleviate hepatic fibrosis by inhibiting Akt and activating the AMPK/TFEB pathway
To elucidate the mechanism underlying YGJ-EV-mediated alleviation of hepatic fibrosis, the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling axis was analyzed in the liver. Western blotting demonstrated that the model group exhibited upregulated Akt expression and downregulated phospho-AMPK (p-AMPK) and nuclear TFEB levels compared with the normal group. Treatments using control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII differentially suppressed Akt expression and enhanced AMPK activation and nuclear TFEB translocation, with YGJ-EVs producing the most pronounced effects. In contrast, the Akt agonist SC79 exerted no significant effect (Fig.  A–D). RT-qPCR analyses confirmed these trends at the transcriptional level (Fig.  E–G).
 Immunofluorescence staining revealed predominant cytoplasmic localization of TFEB in fibrotic livers, whereas treatments with control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII induced nuclear translocation of TFEB (Fig.  I). These findings suggest that YGJ-EVs deliver miR-7045-5p to suppress Akt expression, thereby activating AMPK and promoting TFEB nuclear translocation.

## YGJ-EVs enhance lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in hepatic fibrosis
We evaluated lysosomal biogenesis by analyzing LAMP1 expression (Fig.  A and B) and mRNA levels of lysosomal markers (LAMP1, TPP1, CTSD, and CTSB) (Fig.  G–J). The livers from the model group exhibited significantly lower LAMP1 protein levels and significantly lower mRNA levels of the lysosomal markers than the normal group. Treatments with YGJ-EVs and Akt inhibitor VIII restored the levels of these markers, with YGJ-EVs inducing the most pronounced upregulation. The Akt agonist SC79 produced no significant effects.
 Next, the protein and mRNA levels of the mitophagy-related markers PINK1, Parkin, p62 and LC3 were assessed (Fig.  C–F and K–N). Western blotting revealed decreased PINK1, Parkin, and LC3 protein levels, alongside increased p62 accumulation in fibrotic livers, indicating suppressed mitophagy. Treatments with YGJ-EVs and Akt inhibitor VIII reversed these trends, promoting PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, enhancing LC3 lipidation, and reducing the p62 level. YGJ-EVs exhibited the strongest pro-mitophagic effects, corroborated by RT-qPCR results.

## YGJ-EVs suppress TGF-β1-induced HSC activation
Based on CCK-8 assay results, 100 µg/mL YGJ-EVs with a 24-h intervention were identified as the optimal treatment conditions, demonstrating no cytotoxicity toward HSCs (Fig.  A and B). Subsequent experiments utilized these parameters. Calcein-AM/PI staining revealed significantly enhanced HSC viability following TGF-β1 treatment. Control-EV and YGJ-EV treatments significantly reduced viable-cell counts and increased dead-cell populations, with YGJ-EVs exhibiting the most potent effects (Fig.  C and D). Western blotting showed upregulation of HSC-activation markers (COL1-A1 and α-SMA) in the model group compared with the levels in the normal group. Both control-EV and YGJ-EV treatments downregulated these fibrotic markers (Fig.  E–G). Consistent trends were observed in RT-qPCR and ELISA results for COL1-A1 and α-SMA, and YGJ-EVs showed a superior effect (Fig.  H–K). These findings demonstrate that YGJ-EVs inhibit TGF-β1-induced HSC activation more effectively than control EVs.
 

## YGJ-EVs modulate the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling in activated HSCs
To investigate the mechanism underlying YGJ-EV-mediated suppression of HSC activation, we analyzed the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling axis in TGF-β1-treated HSCs. Western blotting revealed elevated Akt protein levels and reduced p-AMPK and nuclear TFEB levels in TGF-β1-treated HSCs compared with the levels in the normal group. Both control-EV and YGJ-EV treatments significantly downregulated Akt expression, enhanced AMPK activation, and promoted nuclear TFEB translocation, but YGJ-EVs demonstrated more pronounced effects than control EVs (Fig.  A–D). RT-qPCR results corroborated these findings at the transcriptional level (Fig.  E–G). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the cytoplasmic retention of TFEB in TGF-β1-treated HSCs, whereas control-EV and YGJ-EV treatments promoted TFEB nuclear translocation (Fig.  H), indicating restored TFEB transcriptional activity.
 

## YGJ-EVs enhance lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in activated HSCs
Given that nuclear translocation of TFEB promotes lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy, we first assessed the protein level of the lysosomal marker LAMP1 (Fig.  A and B) and the mRNA levels of the lysosomal markers LAMP1, TPP1, CTSD, and CTSB (Fig.  G–J) in HSCs. TGF-β1-activated HSCs exhibited lower mRNA levels of all the lysosomal markers, as well as lower LAMP1 protein levels, than the normal group. YGJ-EV treatment significantly restored the expression levels of all the markers, including LAMP1 protein levels. Additionally, LysoTracker Green staining indicated increased lysosomal biogenesis in TGF-β1-treated HSCs following YGJ-EV intervention, quantified using fluorescence intensity (Fig.  O).
 Likewise, mitophagy-related markers (PINK1, Parkin, p62, and LC3) were analyzed via western blotting (Fig.  C–F) and RT-qPCR (Fig.  K–N). TGF-β1-induced fibrotic activation suppressed mitophagy in HSCs, as evidenced by upregulated p62 and downregulated PINK1, Parkin, and LC3 protein levels. Control-EV and YGJ-EV treatments reversed these effects, reducing p62 accumulation and enhancing PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, with YGJ-EVs demonstrating superior efficacy. Immunofluorescence confirmed PINK1 upregulation in YGJ-EV-treated HSCs (Fig.  P).
TEM revealed homogeneous cytoplasm, mildly swollen mitochondria with intact membranes, and occasional mitophagy in the normal group, whereas pale cytoplasmic protrusions, disrupted mitochondrial cristae, and autophagolysosomes were observed in the model group. In the control-EV group, partial mitochondrial swelling, fragmented cristae, and coexisting autophagolysosomes and mitophagy were observed. In the YGJ-EV group, clear cytoplasm, reduced mitochondrial swelling, preserved membrane integrity, and prominent autophagolysosomes and mitophagy were observed (Fig.  Q).

## miR-7045-5p attenuates TGF-β1-induced HSC activation by modulating the Akt/AMPK/TFEB axis
EVs mediate intercellular communication by delivering functional miRNAs to recipient cells. To determine whether miR-7045-5p suppresses HSC activation through the Akt/AMPK/TFEB pathway by enhancing lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy, HSCs were transfected with either a miR-7045-5p mimic or a non-targeting oligonucleotide (negative control). Western-blot analysis for activation markers and components of the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling revealed increased Akt levels and decreased p-AMPK and nuclear TFEB levels in TGF-β1-treated HSCs (Fig.  A–F). Transfection of HSCs with the miR-7045-5p mimic significantly reversed these effects, downregulating Akt and upregulating p-AMPK and nuclear TFEB. Results from RT-qPCR analysis supported these findings (Fig.  G–K). Both western-blot and RT-qPCR results showed significantly lower protein and mRNA levels, respectively, of the activation markers COL1-A1 and α-SMA in the mimic group than in the model group. These results collectively indicate that miR-7045-5p inhibits TGF-β1-induced HSC activation by modulating the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling.
 

## miR-7045-5p enhances lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in activated HSCs via the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling
Since our results showed that miR-7045-5p downregulates Akt and activates the AMPK/TFEB signaling, we assessed key markers of lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in TGF-β1-activated HSCs. Western blotting (Fig.  A–F) revealed reduced LAMP1, PINK1, Parkin, and LC3 levels, along with elevated p62 accumulation, in the model group compared with normal HSCs, indicating suppressed lysosomal biogenesis and impaired mitophagy. miR-7045-5p mimic transfection significantly reversed these effects, restoring LAMP1, PINK1, Parkin, and LC3 expression and reducing p62 levels. RT-qPCR analysis of lysosomal markers (LAMP1, TPP1, CTSD, and CTSB) and mitophagy-related markers (PINK1, Parkin, LC3, and p62) confirmed these trends (Fig.  G–N). Immunofluorescence staining further validated enhanced lysosomal biogenesis (Fig.  O) and PINK1 upregulation (Fig.  P) in HSCs transfected with the miR-7045-5p mimic.
 These findings demonstrate that miR-7045-5p promotes lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy in activated HSCs by modulating the Akt/AMPK/TFEB axis, thereby attenuating HSC activation.

## Discussion
Hepatic fibrosis, a common pathological process in various liver diseases, is characterized by excessive collagen deposition driven by persistent activation of HSCs within inflammatory microenvironments. This pathological remodeling leads to aberrant ECM accumulation and progressive liver dysfunction [ ]. If left untreated, fibrosis may advance to cirrhosis, accompanied by life-threatening complications such as hepatic failure, encephalopathy, and coagulopathy, resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates [ ]. Thus, inhibiting HSC activation or restoring quiescence in activated HSCs represents a critical therapeutic strategy for hepatic fibrosis. Recent advances have highlighted the therapeutic potential of BMSC-EVs in fibrosis management, owing to the high stability and biocompatibility of these nanoparticles [ ]. BMSC-EVs exert anti-fibrotic effects by delivering bioactive cargo—including cytokines, growth factors, mRNAs, and miRNAs—that modulate metabolic and signaling pathways in recipient cells [ ]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that BMSC-EVs transport anti-fibrotic miRNAs to suppress aberrant proliferation, excessive collagen production, and fibrotic pathway activation in TGF-β-stimulated fibroblasts [ ].
Emerging studies have demonstrated that preconditioned BMSC-EVs exhibit enhanced therapeutic efficacy. For instance, quercetin pretreatment enriches the expression and delivery of anti-fibrotic miRNAs in BMSC-EVs, thereby attenuating M1 macrophage polarization, reducing hepatic inflammation, and improving liver function [ ]. Furthermore, pretreatment of BMSCs with TCM compounds can remodel EV miRNA profiles to potentiate therapeutic effects. Notably, BMSC-EVs preconditioned with Tongxinluo (a TCM compound) exhibited a tenfold increase in miR-146a-5p levels compared with the increase induced by naive BMSC-EVs, significantly enhancing anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects to promote cardiac repair [ ]. YGJ, a classical TCM formula widely used in clinical practice for hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis [ ], has a well-documented therapeutic legacy. YGJ not only reduces serum ALT/AST levels [ ] but also ameliorates metabolic dysregulation and prevents osteoporosis [ ]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that YGJ modulates macrophage activation states, inhibits hepatic stem cell differentiation into fibroblasts, and facilitates cirrhotic liver repair [ ]. Although both YGJ and EVs have demonstrated multi-mechanistic anti-fibrotic effects, research on YGJ-preconditioned EVs—particularly their miRNA-mediated regulation of the Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling axis—remains limited. This study addresses this gap by elucidating how YGJ preconditioning optimizes BMSC-EV cargo to amplify therapeutic outcomes in hepatic fibrosis. Based on references [ ] and the experimental objectives of this study, YGJ-containing serum was used rather than direct YGJ treatment on BMSCs in the cellular experiments. This approach was adopted because drug-containing sera of TCM compounds contain complex mixtures of the drugs themselves, their metabolites and active components, and serum with altered pH levels. This helps avoid interference from inactive ingredients on experimental results [ ], better simulates the in vivo environment of YGJ action, more accurately reflects the specific effects of YGJ on BMSCs, and facilitates elucidation of the action mechanism of YGJ at the molecular level. To further investigate the specific components of the YGJ-containing serum, the bioactive substances in the serum of YGJ-administered rats were analyzed using UPLC. The results, presented as mass-spectrometry data and chromatograms (supplementary data) showed 73 differential components in the YGJ-containing serum versus the control serum, including specific monomers such as isorhamnetin, quercetin, myricetin, luteolin, and betaine. Therefore, based on the findings of this study, we hypothesize that the efficacy of YGJ-EV in alleviating liver fibrosis and inhibiting HSC activation may be attributed to natural antioxidant components present in the YGJ-containing serum, such as isorhamnetin, quercetin, and luteolin. Studies have shown that EVs derived from MSCs treated with quercetin exhibit enriched antioxidant proteins, particularly superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), thereby mitigating oxidative damage and promoting tissue repair and regeneration [ ]. Furthermore, researchers have found that pretreating BMSC-EVs with luteolin enables loading of luteolin into the EVs, achieving efficient drug delivery and release, and demonstrating superior anti-liver-fibrosis activity [ ]. Thus, future studies should examine whether antioxidant pretreatment can enhance the therapeutic effects of BMSC-EVs on liver fibrosis.
As a novel member of the miRNA family [ ], miR-7045-5p was previously identified in our studies as a suppressor of HSC activation through the enhancement of autophagic flux in activated HSCs via BMSC-EV-mediated delivery [ ]. The miRNA microarray analysis in the present study revealed significant enrichment of miR-7045-5p in YGJ-EVs, a finding further corroborated via RT-qPCR. Bioinformatics predictions and pathway analyses identified Akt as a direct target of miR-7045-5p, suggesting the pivotal role of this protein in mitigating hepatic fibrosis. To test this hypothesis, we administered the Akt agonist SC79 and Akt inhibitor VIII to distinct groups of fibrotic mice. This experimental design enabled direct assessment of the therapeutic relevance of Akt, with YGJ-EVs acting as carriers of an anti-Akt miRNA.
Autophagy is a conserved biological process that maintains cellular homeostasis by facilitating the lysosome-dependent degradation of damaged cellular components under stress conditions. Mitophagy, a specialized subtype of autophagy, selectively targets dysfunctional mitochondria for degradation. Impaired mitophagy leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which releases mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns, activating inflammasomes [ ] and exacerbating fibrosis. In contrast, enhanced mitophagy suppresses HSC activation by maintaining mitochondrial quality control [ ] or inducing apoptosis in activated HSCs [ ], thereby alleviating hepatic fibrosis. During mitophagy, mitochondria first undergo fission, are subsequently enveloped by autophagosomes, and finally fuse with lysosomes [ ]. Therefore, mitophagy is an important lysosome-dependent process, and fluorescent probes targeting lysosomes have been developed to track lysosome formation and fusion during mitophagy [ ]. The PINK1-Parkin axis plays a central role in mitophagy: Studies have shown that PINK1 protein accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane and activates Parkin, which subsequently ubiquitinates mitochondrial proteins. As a classic autophagy receptor, p62 recognizes the ubiquitinated markers on mitochondria and binds to LC3Ⅱ to form autophagosomes, ultimately accelerating the mitophagy process through fusion with lysosomes [ ]. Therefore, p62 is also an important indicator for assessing the extent of mitophagy. Akt has emerged as a key regulator of mitophagy [ ]. Hyperactivation of cytoplasmic Akt causes increased phosphorylation of AMPK at the inhibitory site (Ser485), hindering the phosphorylation of Thr172 necessary for AMPK activation. This inhibition of AMPK ultimately prevents the nuclear translocation of TFEB—the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy—resulting in diminished lysosomal generation and reduced mitophagic flux [ ]. Thus, AKT downregulation promotes the phosphorylation of AMPK, thereby facilitating the nuclear translocation of TFEB. Since TFEB can induce lysosomal biogenesis by activating lysosomal and autophagy-related genes, thereby increasing lysosome quantity and promoting autophagic degradation, we evaluated the expression patterns of lysosomal biogenesis markers [ ]—lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), cathepsin B (CTSB), cathepsin D (CTSD), and tripeptidyl peptidase 1 (TPP1)—to further assess lysosome generation.
In this study, we employed a well-established mouse model of hepatic fibrosis, induced via CCl4 administration. We assessed fibrotic progression and inflammation via Doppler ultrasound and histopathological analysis, and liver function via biochemical assays. Compared with the normal group, CCl4-treated mice exhibited significantly elevated serum ALT and AST levels. Subsequent interventions with control EVs, YGJ-EVs, and Akt inhibitor VIII significantly alleviated hepatic fibrosis and improved liver function. Among these interventions, YGJ-EVs demonstrated superior efficacy by promoting lysosomal biogenesis, enhancing mitophagy, and upregulating hepatic miR-7045-5p expression, thereby facilitating tissue repair and ameliorating fibrosis.
To validate these findings, we utilized TGF-β1-induced HSCs as a cell-culture model of fibrosis. Both control EVs and YGJ-EVs effectively suppressed TGF-β1-induced HSC activation, with YGJ-EVs exhibiting the most pronounced inhibitory effects. Ultrastructural analysis revealed increased mitophagy and enhanced autophagolysosome formation in YGJ-EV-treated HSCs, supporting the hypothesis that YGJ-EVs deliver miR-7045-5p, thereby promoting lysosomal and mitochondrial quality control. KEGG pathway analysis further substantiated these observations.
Mechanistic validation via transfection of activated HSCs with a miR-7045-5p mimic confirmed that miR-7045-5p enhances lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy through Akt/AMPK/TFEB signaling, aligning the predictions made from the microarray analysis.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that YGJ-EVs effectively mitigate HSC activation by promoting lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy via the Akt/AMPK/TFEB axis. The superior therapeutic efficacy of YGJ-preconditioned BMSC-EVs, compared with naïve EVs, likely stems from YGJ-induced enrichment of miR-7045-5p. This miRNA suppresses Akt expression and activates the downstream AMPK/TFEB signaling, thereby enhancing autophagic and mitophagic processes. These findings highlight YGJ preconditioning as a promising strategy to engineer therapeutic EVs for hepatic fibrosis, providing valuable insights into anti-fibrotic therapy through miRNA-mediated modulation of autophagic pathways.
