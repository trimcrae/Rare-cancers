This study evaluated the effects of short-term administration of sunitinib, a clinically used CSF-1R inhibitor, in a collagenase-induced mouse ICH model and an in vitro hemoglobin (Hb)-treated BV2 microglial model. These findings suggest that sunitinib alleviates acute ICH injury by modulating microglial functions, likely through inhibition of the CSF-1R axis, supporting its potential repurposing for central nervous system disorders like ICH.

## Introduction
Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) represents as one of the most fatal and disabling subtypes of stroke and is a great challenge to global public health [ ]. The neurological damage in ICH was not only derived from the primary injury caused by the hematoma, but also from the secondary injury caused by the peri-hematomal microenvironments [ ]. Approximately 80 ​% of ICH survivors still experience severe neurological deficits even at six months after ICH onset, highlighting the major limitation of current treatment strategies which focus on mainly the primary injury [ ]. Secondary injury of ICH initiates rapidly after hematoma formation, and involves multiple pathological cascades, such as oxidative stress, hemoglobin (Hb) release, neuroinflammation and demyelination, which collectively drive the progressive loss of functional neurons. However, interventions targeting secondary injury of ICH, including minocycline and deferoxamine, have failed, suggesting the urgent need for the development of novel strategies to halt secondary injury and improve the outcomes of ICH [ ].
Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), have become a major research focus [ ]. In the acute phase of ICH, peri-hematomal microglia rapidly transit into a disease-associated phenotype, characterized by the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, which drive early neuroinflammation [ ]. These peri-hematomal microglia also undergo metabolic reprogramming, leading to the impairments of phagocytic and chemotactic functions [ ]. However, after the acute phase of ICH, microglia could switch to a reparative phenotype with the ability to clear cellular debris and to produce neurotrophic factors to facilitate functional recovery [ ]. Therefore, an ideal microglia-targeting approach for ICH should focus on precise modulation, suppressing the pathological activation of peri-hematomal microglia at the acute phase of ICH.
Colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF-1R), highly expressed in microglia, is a potential target for suppressing the pathological activation of microglia [ ]. The inhibition of CSF-1R could further inhibit the downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, leading to the inactivation of microglia [ ]. CSF-1R inhibitors have shown promises to reset microglial homeostasis and could halt pathological progress in Alzheimer's disease (AD) animals [ ]. However, the therapeutic effects of CSF-1R inhibitors against ICH remain controversial. PLX3397 is a potent CSF-1R inhibitor. Long-term use of PLX3397 could completely deplete microglia, leading to the reduction of neuroinflammation at the acute phase of ICH [ ]. However, such strategy might impede debris clearance, exacerbate long-term neurological deficits [ ], and produce side effects via inhibiting the activation of peripheral macrophages [ ]. The current study proposes a short-term CSF-1R inhibition strategy with the aim to inhibit the pathological activation of peri-hematomal microglia at the acute phase of ICH. Sunitinib is a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, with an IC50 of 0.01 ​μM for CSF-1R [ ]. Sunitinib was approved by the FDA for the treatment of gastrointestinal stromal tumors and renal cell carcinoma, including brain metastases [ ]. Importantly, sunitinib exhibits favorable blood-brain barrier penetration ability and a well-established clinical safety profile [ ]. In addition, it was previously reported that sunitinib improved cognitive function in AD animals, providing further support that sunitinib could retain in the brain and inhibit microglial CSF-1R [ ].
In the current study, we evaluated the effects of short-term use of sunitinib on motor functions of collagenase-induced ICH mice. In addition, RNA-seq, Western blotting analysis and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were performed to explore how sunitinib inhibited pathological activation of peri-hematomal microglia at the acute phase of ICH. Furthermore, the action and mechanisms of sunitinib on pathological-activated neuroinflammation was elucidated in Hb-treated BV2 cells.

## Materials and Methods

## Ethics statement
Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ningbo University (AEWC-NBU20230325) and conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

## Animals and drug administration
Male C57BL/6 mice (20–25 ​g) were supplied by SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Animals were housed under controlled temperature and humidity conditions on a 12 ​h light/dark cycle, with five mice per cage. A total of 124 mice were used in the experiment, with 10 mice excluded due to anesthesia or surgical procedure-related deaths. The remaining 114 mice were divided into three segments as follows:
Segment 1: Behavioral and ultrastructural analysis. 48 mice were randomly assigned into six groups with 8 animals each for behavioral and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) examination. The groups were Sham, ICH, ICH plus low (20 ​mg/kg), medium (40 ​mg/kg) and high (60 ​mg/kg) doses of sunitinib, ICH plus PLX3397 (40 ​mg/kg). Sunitinib (LC Laboratories, Woburn, USA) and PLX3397 (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) were administered by intraperitoneal injection. The doses of sunitinib were determined based on our previous study [ ]. Mice in the Sham and ICH groups were treated with saline as vehicle. Treatment was initiated 1 ​h post-ICH and continued once daily for three consecutive days.
Segment 2: Transcriptomic analysis. 6 mice were randomly assigned to either ICH or ICH plus sunitinib (60 ​mg/kg) group, with three animals in each group. Following the establishment of the ICH model, sunitinib or saline was administered intraperitoneally as a single dose. Peri-hematomal brain tissues were collected 24 ​h post-ICH for transcriptome sequencing.
Segment 3: Molecular and histological analysis. 60 mice were randomly assigned into six groups with 10 animals in each group. The groups were Sham, ICH, ICH plus low (20 ​mg/kg), medium (40 ​mg/kg) and high (60 ​mg/kg) doses of sunitinib, ICH plus PLX3397 (40 ​mg/kg). Treatment was initiated 1 ​h post-ICH and continued once daily for three consecutive days. Then, peri-hematomal brain tissues were collected for Western blotting, Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) and IHC.

## ICH surgery
Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (40 ​mg/kg). ICH was induced by stereotaxic injection of collagenase IV (0.1 U in 0.5 ​μL; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) into the right basal ganglia (stereotaxic coordinates: 0.4 ​mm posterior to bregma, 1.8 ​mm lateral to midline, and depth 4.0 ​mm) [ ]. Sham-operated mice received an equal volume of saline. Injection was performed over 10 ​min using a micro-infusion pump. Rectal temperature was maintained at 37.0 ​± ​0.5 ​°C with a heating pad throughout the procedure.

## Neurological assessment
The modified neurological severity score (mNSS) was used to evaluate motor, sensory, reflex and balance functions, with a maximum score of 18 indicating severe impairments [ ]. The tests were performed before surgery and on day 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 post-ICH. Corner turn test was performed according to a previous study [ ]. Mice were placed into a 30° corner and allowed to turn freely. The test was repeated 10 times with intervals of at least 30 ​s. The percentage of right turns was recorded. The tests were performed on days 7, 14, and 21 post-ICH. Cylinder test was performed according to a previous study [ ]. Mice were placed in a transparent cylinder (diameter 8 ​cm, height 25 ​cm), and forelimb use during vertical exploration was recorded over 20 movements. Asymmetry was calculated as (left forelimb contacts)/(left ​+ ​right contacts), with lower scores indicating greater left-side impairment. The tests were conducted on days 7, 14, and 21 post-ICH. Open field test (OFT) was performed according to a previous study [ ]. Locomotor and exploratory behaviors were assessed in a white opaque chamber (40 ​× ​40 ​× ​40 ​cm). Each mouse was placed in the center and allowed to explore for 2 ​min. Total travel distance was recorded. The test was conducted on day 21 post-ICH.

## TEM examination
TEM was used to evaluate the thickness of myelin in the striatum as previously described [ ]. Mice were perfused with saline and 2.5 ​% glutaraldehyde on day 3 post-ICH. Peri-hematomal tissue (1 ​mm3) was dissected, fixed in 2.5 ​% glutaraldehyde overnight, and post-fixed in 1 ​% osmium tetroxide. Samples were dehydrated, embedded in epoxy resin, and sectioned at 80 ​nm. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a HITACHI HT-7800 TEM. The thickness of myelin was quantified as the G-ratio (inner axonal diameter/total outer diameter) from images captured at 5000 ​× ​magnification using ImageJ.

## Cell culture
BV2 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 ​% FBS and 1 ​% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 ​°C under 5 ​% CO2. To mimic ICH conditions in vitro, BV2 cells were exposed to 100 ​μM Hb for 1 ​h, followed by the addition of sunitinib (1, 3, or 10 ​μM) or PLX3397 (3 ​μM) at 1 ​h post-Hb treatment.

## CSF-1R knockdown
CSF-1R knockdown was performed using siRNA oligonucleotides (Genepharma, Shanghai, China). BV2 cells were transfected with the following siRNA sequences:
Sense strand: 5′-GCACCGAAGAACAUAUACATT-3′
Antisense strand: 5′-UGUAUAUGUUCUUCGGUGCTT-3′

## ELISA
Levels of IL-1β and TNF-α were measured in tissue homogenates and cell culture supernatants. Samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and centrifuged at 5000 ​g for 15 ​min at 4 ​°C. Cytokine concentrations were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Excellbio, Shanghai, China), with absorbance measured on a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA).

## Western blotting analysis
Western blotting analysis was performed as previously described [ ]. Peri-hematoma tissues were lysed on ice for 30 ​min and sonicated briefly. Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 ​g for 30 ​min at 4 ​°C. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. Proteins were denatured at 100 ​°C for 10 ​min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5 ​% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 ​h and incubated overnight at 4 ​°C with the following primary antibodies: Phospho-Akt (1:1000, #9271, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), Akt (1:1000, #9272, Cell Signialing Technology), Phospho–NF–κB p65 (1:1000, AF2006, Affinity Biosciences, OH, USA), NF-κB p65 (1:1000, AF5006, Affinity Biosciences), and β-actin (1:1000, AF7018, Affinity Biosciences). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham Bioscience, UK). Band intensities were quantified as optical density ratios.

## IHC staining
Mice were perfused and brains were post-fixed in 4 ​% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 ​h, followed by dehydration in 30 ​% sucrose. Coronal sections were prepared using a freezing microtome (Leica, Germany). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Iba1 (1:1000, ab5076, Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK), CD86 (1:200, sc-70396, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), Arg1 (1:500, PA5-85267, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and Myelin Basic Protein (MBP, 1:1000, ab218011, Abcam) at 4 ​°C overnight, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 ​h. Nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8) and analyzed with ImageJ. For BV2 cells, after treatment, cells were fixed with 4 ​% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1 ​% Triton X-100 and 2 ​% BSA, and stained similarly.

## Skeleton analysis of microglia
Morphological analysis of Iba1+ microglia was performed on skeletonized images using the Analyze Skeleton plugin in ImageJ. The number of branches and branch lengths were quantified.

## BODIPY staining
Brain sections or BV2 cells were stained with BODIPY 493/503 (1:1000, Thermo Fisher) to visualize lipid droplets. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a confocal microscope or high-content imaging system (ImageXPress Micro XL) and analyzed with ImageJ.

## Phagocytosis analysis
BV2 cells were incubated with fluorescent latex beads (1 ​μL/mL) for 2 ​h at 37 ​°C. Non-internalized beads were removed by washing. Cells were imaged using the ImageXPress Micro XL system. Phagocytosis was quantified using the Neurite Outgrowth module in MetaXpress software.

## RNA-seq analysis
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® Reagent. RNA quantification and quality assessment were performed using an ND-2000 spectrophotometer and a 5300 Bioanalyzer, respectively. Only high-quality RNA samples (OD260/OD280 ​= ​1.8–2.2, OD260/OD230 ​≥ ​2.0, RQN ≥6.5, 28S/18S ​≥ ​1.0, total amount >1 ​μg) were used for library construction. Strand-specific transcriptome library construction and sequencing were conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To ensure the accuracy of subsequent analyses, stringent quality control was applied to the raw sequencing data. The fastp software was used to filter the data according to the following criteria: removal of adapter sequences, elimination of reads containing more than 10 ​% ambiguous bases, trimming of low-quality bases from the 3′ end of reads, and discarding of reads with a post-trimming quality score below 10 or a length shorter than 20 bp. The resulting clean data were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, followed by transcript assembly and quantification using StringTie. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the DESeq2 algorithm, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those satisfying |log2FC| ​> ​2 and p ​< ​0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the identified DEGs was carried out using the "clusterProfiler" R package to explore relevant signaling pathways.

## Data analysis
Data are presented as mean ​± ​standard derivation (SD). Normality was tested before analysis. One-way analysis of variation (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA was utilized to decide the statistical significance, and Tukey's test was applied for post hoc multiple comparison. p ​< ​0.05 was considered to have statistical significance.

## Results

## Sunitinib ameliorates neurological deficits after ICH surgery
The experimental timeline of the study was illustrated in A. For mNSS, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that there were significant changes for treatment effect [two-way ANOVA, F (5, 210) ​= ​651.3, p ​< ​0.01], for time effect [two-way ANOVA, F (4, 210) ​= ​1275, p ​< ​0.01] and for treatment ​× ​time interaction [two-way ANOVA, F (20, 210) ​= ​44.73, p ​< ​0.01 B]. ICH mice have significantly higher mNSS scores compared to the sham group from day 1 to day 21 post-ICH, indicating severe and sustained neurological impairments in these mice. 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group showed significant improvement in neurological deficits from day 1 to day 21 post-ICH, with significantly lower mNSS scores compared to the ICH group on days 1, 3, 7, and 21 (one-way ANOVA, Tukey's test, p ​< ​0.05), and a pronounced reduction on day 14 (one-way ANOVA, Tukey's test, p ​< ​0.01). At the same conditions, 40 ​mg/kg sunitinib and 40 ​mg/kg PLX3397 groups showed a reduction in mNSS only at day 14 post-ICH (one-way ANOVA, Tukey's test, p ​< ​0.05). Post-hoc comparisons revealed that 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group demonstrated significant improvement compared to the 40 ​mg/kg sunitinib and 40 ​mg/kg PLX3397 groups on day 7 (one-way ANOVA, Tukey's test, p ​< ​0.01). These results suggest that high-concentration sunitinib may exert potent neuroprotective effects against ICH ( B). 
Somatosensory function, forelimb preference, and long-term motor function were evaluated by corner turn test, cylinder test, and OFT, respectively. Mice treated with sunitinib or PLX3397 exhibited improved performance across multiple behavioral tests. For corner turn test, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that there were significant changes for treatment effect [two-way ANOVA, F (5, 126) ​= ​37.23, p ​< ​0.01], for time effect [two-way ANOVA, F (2, 126) ​= ​46.49, p ​< ​0.01] and for treatment ​× ​time interaction [two-way ANOVA, F (10, 126) ​= ​2.070, p ​< ​0.05 C]. Compared to the Sham group, ICH model mice exhibited significant behavioral impairments, with a significantly reduced right-turn percentage on post-operative days 7, 14, and 21 (9 ​%, 19 ​%, and 31 ​%, respectively; p ​< ​0.01). All treatment groups ameliorated this behavioral deficits. Post-hoc analysis revealed a clear dose-dependent trend: the restorative effect of 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group (with right-turn percentages of 25 ​%, 36 ​%, and 46 ​% on days 7, 14, and 21, respectively) was significantly superior to that of 20 ​mg/kg sunitinib group (p ​< ​0.05). Although the differences compared between 40 ​mg/kg sunitinib group and 40 ​mg/kg PLX3397 group did not reach statistical significance, 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group demonstrated the highest level of recovery at all time points ( C). For cylinder test, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that there were significant changes for treatment effect [two-way ANOVA, F (5, 126) ​= ​26.78, p ​< ​0.01], for time effect [two-way ANOVA, F (2, 126) ​= ​99.81, p ​< ​0.01] and for treatment ​× ​time interaction [two-way ANOVA, F (10, 126) ​= ​3.989, p ​< ​0.01, D]. In the cylinder test, ICH mice exhibited significantly impaired use of the left forelimb, with usage rates of 16 ​%, 33 ​%, and 35 ​% on post-operative days 7, 14, and 21, respectively, significantly lower than those in Sham group (p ​< ​0.01). All drug treatment ameliorated this motor impairment to some extent. Although post-hoc tests indicated that the recovery effects produced by sunitinib (20–60 ​mg/kg) and PLX3397 did not reach statistical significance, 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group showed the greatest recovery trend across all time points, with left forelimb usage rates reaching 24 ​%, 39 ​%, and 51 ​%, respectively ( D). On day 21 post-ICH, both sunitinib- and PLX3397-treated groups traveled greater total distance compared to ICH mice, indicating improved long-term locomotor function recovery by these drugs (one-way ANOVA, F (5, 42) ​= ​9.427, p ​< ​0.01, E–F). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the total distance travelled by 60 ​mg/kg Sunitinib group was significantly greater than that of other groups (p ​< ​0.05). These results demonstrated that sunitinib ameliorated neurological deficits in ICH mice, with 60 ​mg/kg being the most effective dose.

## Sunitinib produces anti-ICH neuroprotection possibly via inhibiting CSF-1R in peri-hematomal tissue
Peri-hematomal tissues were collected from ICH and ICH ​+ ​sunitinib groups, respectively, on 1 day after ICH surgery for RNA-seq analysis. Totally 88 sunitinib-related DEGs, including 18 upregulated and 70 downregulated genes, were discovered compared between ICH and ICH ​+ ​sunitinib groups ( A). Sunitinib-related DEGs were significantly enriched in Metabolism, Environmental Information Processing, Cellular Processes, and Organismal Systems, suggesting that sunitinib might produce anti-ICH neuroprotection via the action on these functions ( B). The enrichment of sunitinib-related DEGs was in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway ( C). In addition, phagosome and immune-related pathways significantly enriched in these DEGs ( D–E). PPI analysis identified Akt as a hub gene ( F). Western blotting analysis of peri-hematomal tissue confirmed that sunitinib suppressed the phosphorylation of Akt ( G–H). Given that Akt is a key downstream molecule of CSF-1R, and sunitinib was reported as an inhibitor of CSF-1R, our results also suggested that sunitinib might produce anti-ICH neuroprotection via inhibiting CSF-1R. 

## Sunitinib prevents ICH-induced microglial activation and G-ratio reduction in peri-hematomal tissue
On day 3 after ICH surgery, the morphology of Iba1+ microglia were quantified at peri-hematomal region near myelinated areas. ICH surgery induced a reduction in the number and the length of microglial branches. Sunitinib and PLX3397 significantly restored ICH-induced reduction of microglial branching, with the most significant improvement observed in 60 ​mg/kg sunitinib group, indicating that sunitinib prevented ICH-induced activation of microglia ( A–C). The ultrastructure of myelin in the peri-hematomal striatum was observed on day 21 post-ICH. The G-ratio was significantly higher in ICH group compared with the sham group. Sunitinib concentration-dependently decreased G-ratio at the same condition ( D–E). 

## Sunitinib reduces ICH-induced neuroinflammation in peri-hematomal tissue
Immunofluorescence staining was used to detect Iba1+ microglia expressing either CD86 or Arg-1 in the peri-hematomal region on day 3 post-ICH. After the treatment with sunitinib or PLX3397, the proportion of CD86+/Iba1+ microglia decreased significantly, while that of Arg-1+/Iba1+ microglia increased ( A–D). IL-1β and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated after ICH. Sunitinib significantly reduced the expression of these cytokines, indicating that sunitinib might reduce neuroinflammation in peri-hematomal brain tissue ( E–F). 

## Sunitinib modulates hb-induced alteration of microglial functions, including lipid accumulation, phagocytotic reduction, and neuroinflammation possibly via acting on CSF-1R
To determine whether sunitinib modulated microglial function, BV2 microglial cells were used in the study. BV2 cells were treated with 5 ​μM Hb, followed by the addition of sunitinib or the knockdown of CSF-1R by siRNA (siCSF-1R). Cells were collected for transcriptome sequencing. 1008 sunitinib-associated DEGs, including 434 upregulated and 574 downregulated genes, were discovered compared between Hb and Hb ​+ ​sunitinib groups. Functional annotation of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to lipid metabolism, phagocytosis, and inflammatory response ( A). Under signal transduction, the PI3K/Akt pathway was the most enriched pathway ( B). Totally, 571 siCSF-1R-associated DEGs, including 207 upregulated and 364 downregulated genes were discovered compared between Hb and Hb ​+ ​si–CSF–1R groups ( C). The enriched pathways of these DEGs were similar as those of sunitinib-associated DEGs ( D). PPI analysis of si–CSF–1R-associated DEGs further identified Akt as a central node ( E and F). Western blotting analysis confirmed that both sunitinib and PLX3397 inhibited Akt phosphorylation after Hb exposure ( G and H). These results indicated sunitinib might modulate Hb-induced alteration of lipid metabolism, phagocytosis, and inflammation via acting on CSF-1R and its downstream PI3–K/Akt signaling pathway. 
After sunitinib or siCSF-1R intervention, Hb-induced increase of Acsbg1, Hsd17b7 and Gpat3, lipid synthesis-related genes, were downregulated ( A). Hb-induced decrease of Ulk1, an autophagy-related gene, Arap3, Tubb3, and Tubb4a, phagosome formation-related genes, and Smad3, an anti-inflammatory phagocytosis-related gene were reversed by sunitinib or siCSF-1R ( B). In addition, sunitinib and siCSF-1R reversed Hb-induced alteration of Lat, a pro-inflammatory microglial activation-related gene, Ccl9, a chemokine encoding gene, and Ccr1, Ccr4, and Cxcr3, chemokine receptor encoding genes in BV2 cells ( C). Both sunitinib and PLX3397 could significantly reduce Hb-induced increase of lipid droplet area as evidenced by BODIPY staining in BV2 cells ( D–E). In addition, these treatments could prevent Hb-induced decrease in phagocytotic function in BV2 cells as detected by the latex bead uptake assay ( F–G). Western blotting analysis further demonstrated that these treatments inhibited Hb-induced phosphorylation of NF-κB ( H–I). In addition, Hb induced the production of IL-1β and TNF-α, which were reduced by sunitinib or PLX3397 ( J–K). These results suggested that sunitinib might modulate Hb-induced alteration of microglial functions, including lipid accumulation, phagocytotic reduction, and neuroinflammation possibly via acting on CSF-1R. 

## Discussion
In this study, we have found that short-term application of sunitinib, a clinically used drug, at the acute phase prevented ICH-induced microglial activation and behavioral abnormity possibly via the inhibition of microglial CSF-1R axis ( ). 
The pathological mechanisms underlying the secondary brain injury following ICH are complex, with microglia playing a critical role [ ]. In this study, we have established a collagenase-induced mouse model of ICH and an in vitro BV2 microglial cells treated with Hb. Collagenase-induced injury triggered a series of microglial dysfunctions, including abnormal lipid droplet accumulation, impaired phagocytic capacity, and increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines at peri-hematomal during the 1–3 days post-ICH, indicating that the aberrant activation of peri-hematomal microglia at the acute phase of ICH is considered as a key driver of the injury progression. CSF-1R was mainly expressed in the microglia in the brain [ ]. CSF-1R and its downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is considered as a primary regulator of microglial survival and activation [ ]. Therefore, we proposed an interventional strategy involving the inactivation of peri-hematomal microglial activation via the inhibition of CSF-1R in the acute phase of ICH.
CSF-1R inhibitors, such as PLX3397 and PLX5622, are still in the preclinical or clinical trial stages for treating brain disorders. Sunitinib has already received clinical approval. It is not only used for managing brain metastases from renal cell carcinoma but also serves as an important adjuvant treatment for glioblastoma [ ]. Sunitinib presents considerable potential for drug repurposing in ICH therapy, owing to its proven ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier and its established clinical safety profile [ ].
During the acute phase of ICH, the peri-hematomal microenvironment triggers metabolic reprogramming in microglia, and the excessive phagocytosis of hematoma components can lead to abnormal intracellular lipid droplet accumulation [ ]. In this process, Acsbg1 catalyzes the formation of long-chain fatty acyl-CoAs, which provides substrates for triglyceride and phospholipid synthesis [ ]. Gpat3 acts as a key enzyme in triglyceride synthesis, which contributes to the formation of lipid droplet components [ ]. In our study, sunitinib downregulated the expression of Acsbg1 and Gpat3, and inhibited Akt/SREBP-mediated lipid droplet generation. These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that restoring lipid homeostasis ameliorates neurodegenerative pathology, suggesting that sunitinib may promote the recovery of microglial immune function by alleviating lipotoxic stress in these cells located in the peri-hematomal region during the acute phase of ICH [ ].
Sustained phagocytosis of cellular debris by microglia prevents accumulation-induced secondary damage to neighboring neurons and inflammatory responses [ ]. However, the phagocytic capacity of microglia in the peri-hematomal region is impaired during the acute phase of ICH [ ]. These microglia fail to efficiently degrade phagocytosed material such as myelin debris via autophagy, leading to impaired phagosome clearance and intracellular waste accumulation, which further disrupts cerebral homeostasis. The autophagy-initiating kinase ULK1 is negatively regulated by Akt/mTORC1 signaling [ ]. Our results revealed that sunitinib could prevent the downregulation of Ulk1, which might contribute to the enhanced microglial phagocytic efficiency and phagosome clearance. The in vitro study further confirmed that sunitinib could prevent Hb-induced inhibition of phagocytic function of microglia.
In the acute stage of ICH, microglia in the peri-hematomal are rapidly activated, releasing large quantities of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which amplifies neuroinflammation [ ]. In our study, sunitinib inhibited the activation of the NF-κB pathway, a signaling pathway at the downstream of Akt, and reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that sunitinib downregulated the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory chemokines and receptors, which might contribute to interruption of the inflammatory cascade. Our study also provided a support that tyrosine kinase inhibitors could attenuate abnormal microglial activation, which was proved by previous studies [ ].
There are still some limitations of our study. Sunitinib is a multi-target inhibitor that affects not only CSF-1R. Transcriptomic analysis showed that sunitinib could induce the alteration of signaling pathways other than CSF-1R, suggesting that CSF-1R-independent anti-ICH neuroprotective mechanisms might exist. In addition, the efficacy of acute sunitinib treatment, administration at 1 ​h post-ICH, was evaluated. This timeframe of drug administration is hard to practice in the hospitals. Therefore, the therapeutic time window of sunitinib requires further investigation. Moreover, the potential adverse effects induced by sunitinib, such as cardiotoxicity, must be carefully considered in the context of ICH treatment. Furthermore, this study used exclusively male animals without validating the generalizability of the findings. Finally, pentobarbital anesthesia has been reported to possess neuroprotective properties, subsequent studies may consider using inhaled anesthetics to eliminate this confounding factor.
In conclusion, our study demonstrated that sunitinib modulated multiple microglial functions and reduced ICH-induced acute injury, possibly via inhibition of CSF-1R axis. Our results not only indicated that the short-term CSF-1R inhibition could be benefit for treating post-ICH injury at the acute phase, but also provided experimental evidence to support the repurposing of sunitinib for the treatment of CNS disorders, especially ICH.
