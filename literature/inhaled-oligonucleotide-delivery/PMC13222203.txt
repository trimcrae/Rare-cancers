## Abstract
von Willebrand disease (vWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder. Various subtypes of vWD exist as either quantitative deficiencies or qualitative defects of the von Willebrand factor (vWF) protein and lead to an array of bleeding manifestations. Individuals with vWD typically have increased mucocutaneous bleeding including oral mucosal bleeding, epistaxis, and heavy menstrual bleeding. Other common bleeding manifestations including petechiae, easy bruising, surgical-related bleeding, postpartum hemorrhage, and trauma-induced bleeding. In more severe subtypes gastrointestinal bleeding, hemarthrosis, and intramuscular bleeding can occur. Given the spectrum of bleeding phenotypes, management can differ greatly from one individual to the next with the majority of individuals receiving on-demand treatment while more severely affected individuals may receive long-term prophylaxis. Common on-demand therapeutics include oral, intravenous, topical, and/or intranasal antifibrinolytics, intravenous, subcutaneous and/or intranasal desmopressin, and intravenous plasma-derived and recombinant-vWF replacement therapy. Long-term prophylactic regimens include hormonal therapies and regularly scheduled infusions of plasma-derived and recombinant-vWF concentrates. Over the past 100 years the therapeutic landscape for individuals with vWD has changed significantly and continues to evolve. There are numerous studies currently underway to evaluate new treatments including several drugs administered via subcutaneous injection, and vagal nerve stimulation. Historically individuals with vWD have poorer health-related quality of life and higher healthcare resource utilization compared to the general population, emphasizing the ongoing need for improved therapeutics.

## Key Summary Points

 

## Introduction
Dr. Erik von Willebrand first described von Willebrand disease (vWD) in 1926, though the von Willebrand factor protein (vWF) was not recognized as distinct from factor VIII until the 1970s [ – ]. In the mid-1980s, the von Willebrand factor gene was cloned (vWF) [ , ]. vWF is a large multimeric glycoprotein synthesized in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes [ ]. In primary hemostasis, vWF binds to platelets and collagen to aid formation of the platelet plug via promotion of platelet adhesion and platelet aggregation at sites of vessel injury. Within secondary hemostasis, vWF also binds to and serves as a carrier for factor VIII (FVIII) [ , ].

vWD is a result of decreased production or increased clearance of vWF, or synthesis of dysfunctional vWF. In general, vWD is associated with an increase in mucocutaneous bleeding as well as bleeding in the setting of surgery or trauma. The severity of bleeding manifestations varies somewhat by the type of vWD, though even within a given subtype there can be a spectrum of clinical phenotypes. There are three main vWD types: type 1, type 2, and type 3. Type 1 vWD tends to have the mildest phenotype, while type 3 vWD is the most severe. Individuals with type 1 and type 2 can have a wide range of bleeding manifestations and severity [ ].
In 2021 the American Society of Hematology (ASH), International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH), National Hemophilia Foundation (NHF), and World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH) released updated guidelines on the diagnosis of vWD highlighting new and improved diagnostic assays as well as recommendations for when genetic testing should be utilized [ ]. Concurrently, these organizations released management guidelines which addressed the management of acute bleeding, prophylaxis for recurrent bleeding, prevention of surgical-related bleeding, treatment of heavy menstrual bleeding, and management during labor, delivery, and the postpartum period for individuals with vWD [ ].
This review will cover the basics in regard to vWD epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, current therapeutics, therapeutics under investigation, and deficiencies in each of these areas. Suggestions on how to address some of these shortcomings will also be addressed.

## Epidemiology
Despite vWD being the most common inherited bleeding disorder, true global prevalence is not well understood owing to differences in awareness and testing availability across geographical areas. Studies estimate that vWD affects roughly 0.6–1.3% of the population, though only 1 in 1000 are estimated to be symptomatic. Roughly 1 in 10,000 are cared for by providers with expertise in bleeding disorders [ , ].
vWD is inherited autosomally, equally affecting male and female individuals. However, female individuals make up a higher proportion of those diagnosed (56–64%) due to unique hemostatic challenges around menstruation, pregnancy, and childbirth [ , ]. Type 1 is the most prevalent while type 3 is the least prevalent. Registries from Italy, the UK, and Australia estimate the prevalence of type 1 to be 2.7–7.2 per 100,000, prevalence of type 2 to be 0.8–2.5 per 100,000, and prevalence of type 3 to be 0.1–0.3 per 100,000 [ ].
Limited data is available regarding time from onset of bleeding to diagnosis. In one US study, the mean time from bleeding to diagnosis was 669 days with a range of 2–3284 days [ ]. Similarly, a study in Europe found a median time of 3 years from bleeding to diagnosis [ ]. This striking time gap suggests underlying flaws in recognition of atypical bleeding by affected individuals and healthcare providers alike, deficiencies in diagnostic tools, and/or insufficient access to healthcare.

## Pathophysiology, Inheritance, and Diagnostic Criteria
vWF is located on the short arm of chromosome 12 [ , ]. It is translated to form a pre-pro-vWF molecule which undergoes further posttranslational modifications in the endoplasmic reticulum to form pro-vWF monomers which dimerize and are transported to the Golgi complex for multimerization and additional modification [ ]. vWF is synthesized in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. The glycoprotein that is secreted into circulation contains a D′-D3 factor VIII binding domain, an A1 platelet Gp1b receptor binding domain, an A2 domain where ADAMTS-13 acts to cleave vWF, an A3 collagen binding domain, and a C1 platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa binding domain [ ]. Additional domains that are part of the mature protein allow for binding and formation of the large-molecular-weight vWF multimers (LMWM) and ultra-large-molecular-weight multimers (ULMWM) which are stored in Weibel–Palade bodies within the endothelial cells and platelet alpha granules. While the smaller multimers are secreted continuously, the LMWM and ULMWM, which are the most hemostatically active, are secreted in response to endothelial or platelet activation [ , ].
vWF serves several important roles in hemostasis including binding via the A3 domain to exposed collagen on the endothelial surface. There it is poised to bind platelets via the Gp1b receptor in the A1 domain triggering platelet adherence and activation with further binding of platelet GPIIb/IIIa to vWF within the C1 domain to form a platelet plug [ ]. The other primary role of vWF is to indirectly aid in formation of the fibrin clot by binding to FVIII, thus preventing rapid clearance and proteolysis of this protein [ ].
Type I and type 3 vWD are a result of a quantitative deficiency of vWF, whereas type 2 vWD is due to qualitative defects. Type 2 is further subdivided into types 2A, 2B, 2M, and 2N. Inheritance patterns and genetic landscape vary by subtype (Table  ).

## Bleeding Assessment Tools
The first step in diagnosing vWD is an in-depth assessment of bleeding symptoms. Bleeding assessment tools (BATs) were developed and validated to accurately and objectively evaluate symptoms through standardized questions with assignment of a quantitative bleeding score [ , ]. Current guidelines advise use of a validated BAT specifically for individuals with low suspicion of vWD to identify those who would benefit from laboratory testing [ , ]. While BATs can be useful in referral settings, laboratory testing is typically recommended, regardless of the BAT score, for individuals with a family history of vWD, or who have been referred to a hematologist [ ]. Experts are hesitant to recommend BATs as the sole determining factor when deciding which individuals require laboratory testing as they do have several limitations [ ]. Use of BATs in children and young male patients can miss affected individuals as this population has often not encountered many hemostatic challenges resulting in lower scores [ , ]. Another limitation is score saturation. As scores are based on the worst single episode, an individual with one severe bleeding episode could have the same BAT score as someone with numerous recurrent severe bleeding episodes [ , ]. Lastly, BATs are not necessarily universally applicable to all societies as they have not been studied in resource poor areas or non-English speaking societies [ ]. It is likely that if utilized in populations with decreased access to healthcare, BAT scores will be falsely low [ ].

## Laboratory Testing
Laboratory testing is recommended for patients with family history of vWD or a personal bleeding history. Initial screening should include measurement of the vWF antigen, vWF platelet binding activity, and FVIII [ ]. Recent guidelines suggest use of the newer platelet-dependent vWF activity assays such a vWF:GP1bM or vWF:GP1bR rather than the historically utilized vWF ristocetin cofactor assay [ , ]. The ristocetin assay has high variability and is also fallible in the presence of two vWF polymorphisms, p. P1467S and p. D1472H, which result in false positive testing. The p.D1472H polymorphism is present in 63% of African Americans and 17% of white individuals [ , ]. Newer assays assess ristocetin-independent platelet binding. They have decreased variation, greater precision, higher sensitivity, and are not impacted by the polymorphisms [ – ].
If initial testing is unremarkable, but concern for vWD remains high, next steps could include a collagen binding assay and repeating initial studies. vWF is an acute phase reactant with increased levels seen with illness, underlying inflammatory conditions, pregnancy, heavy menstrual bleeding, anemia, exercise, exposure to cigarette smoke, oral contraceptives, and traumatic blood draws. Multiple studies have suggested that initial testing values of > 1.0 IU/ml carry a high negative predictive value for diagnosis of VWD [ – ]. Efforts should be made to obtain laboratory assessment when patients are in their baseline state of health [ , , – ]. One of the other most important aspects of testing is utilizing a laboratory with expertise in the diagnosis of vWD and with capability of on-site processing. False positive testing is prevalent in laboratories that utilize off-site processing due to pre-analytical errors [ , , ]. Laboratory findings vary by subtype (Table  ).

 

## Barriers to Diagnosis
Within the USA, a well-resourced country, average time from first symptoms to diagnosis is 16 years [ ]. Despite its prevalence, diagnosing vWD can be quite challenging and delays in diagnosis are very common owing to the complexity of the disease and diagnostic process. Incomplete penetrance and variable expression can make family history appear inconsistent with the disease, and BATs, as previously discussed, are not generalizable to a significant portion of the population [ , , ]. Future studies should focus on developing BATs for resource-limited communities. Family history of bleeding manifestations can at times hinder diagnosis as bleeding is normalized, particularly heavy menses, and medical care is not sought out [ , ]. Diagnostic assays with improved sensitivity and less variability are not universally available [ ]. Laboratory variability and lack of access to healthcare providers with knowledge of vWD and the many facets influencing vWF levels continue to be significant factors influencing delay in diagnosis of vWD as well as misdiagnosis [ , ]. Globally, healthcare systems vary significantly so improving diagnosis will not be a one size fits all. Increasing awareness of diagnostic challenges for primary care providers and hematologists, and increased access to telehealth, when feasible, could lead to improvements in diagnosis. The 2021 ASH ISTH NHF WFH guidelines were a huge step in the right direction, though it should be noted that even these guidelines were conditional recommendations and more stringent evaluation of diagnostic criteria is necessary with the goal of simplifying the diagnostic criteria [ ]. Online resources are available to provide education and offer self-BATs [ ]. While these resources are a positive advancement, they also place excessive responsibility on patients and are not universally accessible [ ].

## Management
Management of individuals with vWD is best provided by multidisciplinary care teams who specialize in the treatment of individuals with bleeding disorders [ , ]. Care teams should include hematologists, nurses, social workers, physical therapists, orthopedists, gynecologists, obstetricians, and dentists. The comprehensive care model is associated with improved outcomes for individuals with bleeding disorders in part due to exceptional education and anticipatory guidance provided by the comprehensive team [ , ]. It is important that individuals with vWD minimize their risk of bleeding through avoidance of medications such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, avoidance of participation in collision sports and activities that can predispose to head trauma, optimization of dental hygiene to reduce the necessity of dental procedures, and maintenance of a healthy weight and active lifestyle to reduce comorbidities that can impact their vWD care and increase the need for surgical interventions. Education is also important so that patients and their caregivers are empowered to seek care when needed.
Affected individuals experience an array of bleeding symptoms. The most common reported symptoms include heavy menstrual bleeding (75–100%), excessive bruising (62–81%), oropharyngeal bleeding (64%), epistaxis (56%), post-dental procedure bleeding (26%), postsurgical bleeding (24%), and excessive bleeding from wounds (24–58%) [ ]. Additionally, individuals with type 2A and type 3 vWD may experience gastrointestinal bleeding, often in the setting of angiodysplasia. Roughly 50% of individuals with type 3 may experience joint bleeding, while this is far less common in patients with type 1 or type 2 vWD occurring in an estimated 5–10% of individuals [ ].
When patients do experience bleeding, individualized treatment plans are required. Factors that play a role in management include vWD subtype, vWF and factor VIII activity levels, site of bleeding, frequency of bleeding, severity of bleeding, impact of bleeding on quality of life, lifestyle, access to resources, and comorbidities. For some patients, on-demand treatment is appropriate, while others may benefit from prophylactic therapy. Determining which patients warrant prophylaxis is still under investigation.

## Current Therapeutics

## Antifibrinolytics
Antifibrinolytics are mainstays in treatment of mucosal bleeding including heavy menstrual bleeding, postpartum hemorrhage, epistaxis, and oral mucosal bleeding. They are used frequently as monotherapy for minor procedures in individuals with mild disease, or as adjuvant therapy for major procedures or procedures in individuals with moderate to severe disease [ ]. They are particularly effective for mucosal bleeding due to high fibrinolytic activity in these tissues [ , ]. Antifibrinolytics should be avoided in individuals with urinary tract bleeding given the risk of ureteral obstruction [ ]. Antifibrinolytics can be administered intravenously or orally for systemic effect, or applied topically for local control. Advantages of these medications include low cost, minimal side effects, and accessibility [ , ].

## Desmopressin
Desmopressin is a synthetic analogue of vasopressin which stimulates release of vWF and FVIII from the endothelium and platelet granules [ ]. Guidelines suggest a desmopressin trial to assess response prior to clinical use [ ]. They should not be used in those with type 3 vWD and are typically avoided in type 2B vWD due to enhanced platelet binding with release of dysfunctional vWF and worsened thrombocytopenia [ ]. Desmopressin can be administered intravenously, subcutaneously, or intranasally [ ]. Tachyphylaxis is frequently observed and thus use should be limited to a maximum of 3 days in a row [ , ]. Desmopressin has potential for more serious side effects including headache, nausea, tachycardia, flushing, hypotension, hyponatremia, fluid retention, seizures, and coronary events [ , ]. Desmopressin use is not advised for patients at opposite ends of the age spectrum. In young children under 2 years of age there is a higher risk of hyponatremia and seizures, while in older patients there is high risk of thrombotic events [ , , ]. Comorbidities which prevent use of desmopressin include active cardiovascular disease, underlying seizure disorders, and preeclampsia [ ]. For all users of desmopressin, strict fluid intake is imperative to avoid hyponatremia [ ]. In responsive patients it can be used for management of epistaxis, heavy menstrual bleeding, and surgical management [ ]. Regarding perioperative use, desmopressin is not recommended for major surgery, including surgical procedures that require precise bleed control such as those in a critical organ where small amounts of bleeding could have life-altering implications [ ]. The definition of what constitutes a major surgery for individuals with vWD requires further standardization [ ].

## vWF Replacement Therapies
Plasma-derived and recombinant vWF concentrates are available with variable ratios of FVIII to vWF, including FVIII free options. Within the USA available plasma-derived vWF concentrates include Wilate, Alphanate, and Humate, while Vonvendi is the only recombinant product available. Vonvendi of note contains no FVIII [ ]. vWF concentrates are utilized to normalize vWF levels for patients with lackluster response to desmopressin, or who cannot otherwise tolerate desmopressin. They are also used for patients with significant hemorrhage, undergoing major surgeries, or surgeries in critical areas [ , ]. Advantages to using vWF concentrates include tolerability and ability to titrate dosing based on degree of bleeding, type of surgery, baseline vWF levels, and response to therapy. Monitoring of vWF levels is recommended with repeat dosing [ , ]. Disadvantages of vWF concentrates include higher relative costs and intravenous administration [ , ]. Plasma-derived concentrates have added potential risk of pathogen transmission, though risk is minimal [ ]. With intravenous administration there is risk of infusion site reactions including paresthesia, pruritus, flushing, dysgeusia, chest discomfort, and tachycardia.
More significant adverse reactions can be seen with use of vWF concentrates including development of alloantibodies in patients with type 3 vWD, and thrombotic complications. Alloantibody formation is estimated in 5% to 10% of patients with type 3 vWD resulting in loss of response to vWF concentrates [ ]. In this situation, bypassing agents and emicizumab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody mimicking FVIII, have been utilized [ , ]. Products with both vWF and FVIII pose a thrombotic risk with repeat dosing as FVIII can accumulate with repeat doses [ ]. For individuals with normal FVIII levels at baseline, replacement with recombinant concentrates containing only vWF may be safer, particularly if there are also underlying thrombotic risk factors [ ]. In addition to lower thrombotic rates, the recombinant vWF concentrates also have the advantage of increased ULMWM and LMWM compared to plasma-derived concentrates which are subject to proteolysis by ADAMTS-13 [ , ]. Patients with decreased FVIII levels at baseline require replacement therapy with FVIII and vWF if full hemostasis is needed immediately [ ]. Plasma-derived vWF concentrates differ not only in their ratios of FVIII to vWF but also in their concentrations of vWF monomers and dimers which in turn influences FVIII binding. This should also be taken into consideration when selecting vWF concentrates [ ].

## Treatment of Acute Bleeding

## Epistaxis
A single-center US study found epistaxis to be the third most common bleeding event in patients with vWD presenting to the emergency room. Roughly 50% required pharmacologic intervention with either desmopressin or vWF concentrate, and those referred by a hematologist were more likely to receive treatment. Nearly 50% underwent nasal packing, and 17.7% received cautery [ ]. For individuals responsive to desmopressin, intranasal delivery can be utilized at home in combination with direct pressure to potentially avoid emergency care. Other local measures include use of vasoconstricting nasal sprays, or inhaled antifibrinolytics. vWF concentrates should be reserved for patients with epistaxis not responsive to local measures and with contraindications or lack of response to desmopressin. Antifibrinolytics and topical moisturizers are recommended for prevention of recurrence following an acute episode.

## Oral Mucosal Bleeding
Multidisciplinary care involving hematologists, dentists, and oral surgeons is important in the management of patients with vWD and oral mucosal bleeding [ ]. Standard local measures including direct pressure and application of ice should be used initially for oral mucosal bleeding. Topical hemostatic agents such as fibrin glue and liquid antifibrinolytics can be applied locally [ ]. Likewise, systemic antifibrinolytics are very effective for control and prevention of recurrent oral mucosal bleeding. Desmopressin or vWF concentrates can be used for refractory bleeding. Other supportive care aimed at minimizing dislodgement of formed clots should be emphasized including avoiding vigorous swishing, soft food diet, avoidance of straws, and application of extraoral ice packs [ ].

## Menorrhagia
Heavy menstrual bleeding affects 80% to 90% of female individuals with vWD [ ]. Antifibrinolytics and hormonal therapy are the two mainstays of treatment. There are a multitude of hormonal therapies available including various combined oral contraceptive pills, progesterone only pills, injections, transdermal patches, vaginal rings, and intrauterine devices [ , ]. While desmopressin can be utilized, it is less effective than tranexamic acid at reducing menstrual blood loss [ ]. Intrauterine devices when compared with tranexamic acid and norethisterone have shown the greatest reduction in menstrual blood loss and increase in quality of life [ , ]. In women who require contraception, the ASH ISTH NHF WFH guidelines recommend combined hormonal contraception, levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems, or tranexamic acid over desmopressin for treatment of heavy menstrual bleeding [ ]. The same guidelines suggest tranexamic acid rather than desmopressin in women who wish to conceive [ ]. Women with vWD and heavy menstrual bleeding also require routine screening for iron deficiency with or without anemia [ , , ].

## Prophylaxis
Though primary prophylaxis has been standard of care for patients with severe hemophilia for decades, indications for primary and secondary prophylaxis for individuals with vWD are less well defined. Regular prophylaxis with vWF concentrates should be considered in patients with vWD with recurrent or severe bleeding [ , ]. Additionally it should be considered in patients with vWD with a bleeding phenotype who require treatment with antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy [ ]. Studies evaluating efficacy of routine prophylaxis are limited. A systematic review of one randomized control trial and five observational studies concluded with moderate to low certainty that prophylaxis reduces bleeding episode risk, hospitalizations, heavy menstrual bleeding, and epistaxis in addition to improving the time to first bleeding event [ – ]. Depending on the type of bleeding, routine prophylaxis led to a 40–100% reduction in annualized bleeding rates [ ]. Despite prophylaxis, gastrointestinal bleeding continues to be a challenge to treat. Bevacizumab has recently been shown to be a promising treatment for individuals with gastrointestinal bleeding due to vWD and angiodysplasia [ ]. Additional studies are needed to elucidate which patients will benefit most from prophylaxis, optimal timing of prophylaxis initiation, and optimal prophylaxis regimens.

## Future Therapeutics
Treatment of vWD has come a long way in the last 100 years and continues to evolve today. While the number of therapeutic options has increased in this time span, there remains an unmet need for less burdensome treatments for individuals with vWD, particularly those who require regular prophylaxis, and those who require regular on-demand treatment for bleeding such as heavy menstrual bleeding. Intravenous administration is challenging, particularly in children, and vWF concentrates are expensive and not accessible in areas with limited resources. Several promising therapeutics are currently under investigation that could positively alter the vWD treatment landscape, providing more convenient treatment options and more type-specific therapeutics (Table  ) [ , – ].

 In particular, many of the new therapeutics are focused on filling the gaps in prophylactic treatment options, treatment for type 1 vWD, and treatment of heavy menstrual bleeding seen in all subtypes of vWD. The subcutaneously administered emicizumab or VGA039 could both be a welcome alternative for prophylaxis for individuals with type 2N or type 3 vWD who currently rely on intravenous treatment options [ , ]. Similarly, BT200 could provide a much-needed option for individuals with type 2B requiring prophylactic therapy [ ]. HMB-002 is another potential prophylactic therapeutic which could be utilized for many subtypes of vWD [ ]. KB-V12A12 offers hope for individuals with type 1 vWD requiring treatment, including those with heavy menstrual bleeding [ , ]. Heavy menstrual bleeding may also be addressed in the future with the use of transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation [ – ]. All of these therapeutics if proven to be safe and efficacious would go a long way in reducing treatment burden and likewise could reduce utilization of hospital resources through reduction in breakthrough bleeding.

## Health-Related Quality of Life
There is increasing awareness and appreciation of how vWD negatively impacts health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [ ]. HRQoL not surprisingly varies depending on bleeding manifestations. Individuals with vWD who are experiencing joint bleeds were found to have a significant reduction in physical and mental aspects of HRQoL [ , ]. Likewise heavy menstrual bleeding is well established to decrease HRQoL in individuals with or without bleeding disorders [ ].
Long-term physical complications as the result of hemarthrosis are well documented and support prophylaxis in individuals with severe vWD who are experiencing these bleeds [ ]. Likewise, heavy menstrual bleeding places individuals at risk of other complications, specifically iron deficiency anemia which can further worsen HRQoL.

## Conclusion
vWD is a complex disease with significant genotypic and phenotypic variability which contributes to difficulty in diagnosis. Resources should be allocated to healthcare provider and patient education to lessen delays in diagnosis. Similarly, better understanding and access to newer laboratory tests is needed to ensure appropriate subtypes are diagnosed as this drives management. This will be particularly important in the coming decades as many future therapeutics promise to be more type-specific. These therapeutics will ideally fill current gaps including more feasible prophylaxis delivered via subcutaneous or noninvasive manners. Additionally, future studies will hopefully help to identify subpopulations who will benefit most from prophylactic therapy.
