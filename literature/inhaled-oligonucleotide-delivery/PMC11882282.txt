## Abstract
Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent programmed cell death process driven by reactive oxygen species-mediated lipid peroxidation, is regulated by several metabolic processes, including iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, and redox system. Macrophages are a group of innate immune cells that are widely distributed throughout the body, and play pivotal roles in maintaining metabolic balance by its phagocytic and efferocytotic effects. There is a profound association between the biological functions of macrophage and ferroptosis. Therefore, this review aims to elucidate three key aspects of the unique relationship between macrophages and ferroptosis, including macrophage metabolism and their regulation of cellular ferroptosis; ferroptotic stress that modulates functions of macrophage and promotion of inflammation; and the effects of macrophage ferroptosis and its role in diseases. Finally, we also summarize the possible mechanisms of macrophages in regulating the ferroptosis process at the global and local levels, as well as the role of ferroptosis in the macrophage-mediated inflammatory process, to provide new therapeutic insights for a variety of diseases.
Keywords: Ferroptosis, Macrophage, Polarization, Iron metabolism

## Introduction
The 2018 Cell Death Nomenclature Committee proposed categorizing cell death into two types: accidental cell death (ACD) and regulatory cell death (RCD). ACD results from uncontrolled factors like severe chemical, physical, or mechanical stress, while RCD is initiated through signal transduction and can be controlled either through pharmacological or genetic means. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death introduced in 2012, is characterized by reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced phospholipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Ferroptotic cells exhibit morphological features distinct from other programmed cell death, including loss of cytoplasmic membrane integrity, swelling of cells and organelles, and mitochondrial crinkling with cristae loss.[ ] Recently, ferroptosis has been found to play a role in a variety of diseases, such as tumors, neurodegenerative diseases, ischemia–reperfusion, and inflammatory diseases.[ ]
Macrophages, which originate from monocytes or the embryonic yolk sac, are long-lived immune cells categorized as circulating macrophages and tissue-resident macrophages.[ ] Macrophages play pivotal roles in maintaining metabolic balance, regulating inflammatory responses, eliminating pathogens and abnormal cells, and shaping the tumor microenvironment.[ ] They are involved in various processes, such as clearing senescent or damaged erythrocytes, contributing to erythropoiesis in erythroid hematopoietic islands,[ ] absorbing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in arterial walls,[ ] and releasing ROS.[ ] Consequently, macrophages help manage iron, lipid, and ROS levels both systemically and locally, suggesting their potential influence on ferroptosis in tissue cells. Moreover, macrophage functions are intricately linked to their iron, lipid, and redox metabolic profiles. For instance, higher intracellular iron accumulation and ROS levels are often associated with macrophage pro-inflammatory phenotypes,[ ] indicating that ferroptosis may significantly affect macrophage activation.
In this review, we extensively explore the roles of iron, lipid, and redox metabolism in ferroptosis, along with their regulatory mechanisms. We delve into how macrophages influence these metabolic processes, the effects of ferroptotic processes on macrophage functions, and the significance of macrophage ferroptosis in different diseases.

## Mechanism and Regulation of Ferroptosis
Ferroptosis is primarily driven by ROS, which targets PUFAs in cell and organelle membranes. This triggers the formation of a highly reactive lipid peroxide radical, known as LOO·, initiating an uncontrolled chain reaction with PUFA in the membrane, ultimately leading to extensive lipid peroxidation.[ ] LOO· can be mitigated by redox systems such as glutathione peroxidase 4–glutathione (glutathione peroxidase 4 [GPX4]–glutathione [GSH]), effectively terminating the chain reaction.[ ] Additionally, the presence of Fe2+, a divalent metal ion with electron transfer capability, plays a significant role in ferroptosis. It generates ROS either through the Fenton reaction or enzymatic pathways, thereby promoting ferroptosis.[ ] In the following sections, we delve into the intricate regulation of these three metabolic processes within cells and their consequential impacts on ferroptosis [Figure ].
 

## ROS, redox system, and ferroptosis
ROS are oxygen single-electron reduction products with chemical activity in vivo, including superoxide anion (·O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radical (·OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2).[ ] ROS are derived from various intracellular processes, including reactions catalyzed by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (NOXs), cyclooxygenases (COXs), lipoxygenases (LOXs), cytochrome P450 oxidases (CYP450), and peroxisomes, and mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC). Under normal circumstances, ROS generated during cellular metabolism are effectively neutralized by intracellular redox systems. Additionally, LOO· can also be reduced by these redox systems.
Three key redox systems are involved in these reactions: (1) The System Xc-/glutathione (GSH)/GPX4 system[ ]: System Xc- comprises the solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and solute carrier family 3 member 1 (SLC3A1) subunits and enables the exchange of extracellular cystine with intracellular glutamate. Intracellular cysteine then serves as a precursor for the synthesis of GSH, a vital antioxidant produced by enzymatic reactions involving glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL) and glutathione synthetase (GSS). GPX4, a GSH peroxidase, plays a crucial role in inhibiting ferroptosis by reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOHs) to phosphatidyl alcohols (PLOHs) in a GSH-dependent manner. (2) The ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)/dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)/Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) system: Ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) reduces CoQ10 (ubiquinone) to CoQ10H2 (ubiquinol). CoQ10H2 functions as a lipophilic radical-capturing antioxidant and directly inhibits lipid peroxidation.[ ] Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), another enzyme found in the inner mitochondrial membrane, also reduces ubiquinone to ubiquinol.[ ] (3) The guanosine triphosphate (GTP) cyclohydrolase-1 (GCH)/tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) system: GCH serves as the rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). BH4 is then involved in the de novo synthesis of CoQ10 and also acts as a direct antioxidant to inhibit lipid peroxidation.[ ] Collectively, ferroptosis can be promoted by various pathways that contribute to increased production of ROS or can occur when the reducing system is impaired [Table ].
 

## Iron metabolism and ferroptosis
The accumulation of Fe2+ within the cytoplasm, forming unstable iron pools, plays a pivotal role in the process of ferroptosis. Fe2+ is a chemically active and potentially toxic form of iron that catalyzes the Fenton reaction (Fe2+ + H2O2 → ·OH + Fe3+) or participates in the synthesis of various ROS-generating enzymes such as LOXs, NOXs, CYP450, and ETC complexes that promote ferroptosis occurrence.[ , ] Extracellular Fe2+ enters the cell through metal ion transport proteins such as divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), Zrt-and Irt-like protein 14 (ZIP14), and Zrt-and Irt-like protein 8 (ZIP8).[ ] Extracellular Fe3+ is bound to transferrin (TF), endocytosed after binding to transferrin binding receptor 1 (TFRC/TfR1) on the cell membrane,[ ] then reduced to Fe2+ by the metal reductase six transmembrane prostate protein 3 (STEAP3) in the phagosome, and then Fe2+ is released from the phagosome via DMT1 into the cytoplasm.[ ] Ferritin, an iron storage protein consisting of ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and ferritin light chain (FTL), exhibits oxidase activity that converts Fe2+ to inert Fe3+ and effectively inhibits ferroptosis.[ ] The levels of Fe2+ within cells are also influenced by the ferritin autophagy process, in which nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) mediates ferritin phagocytosis, releasing Fe2+ from ferritin into the cytoplasm.[ ] Intracellular iron is predominantly exported through the membrane iron transporter protein ferroportin (FPN), the sole known membrane channel for Fe2+ export, which can be down-regulated by hepatocyte-secreted iron-regulating protein (hepcidin).[ ] Moreover, in cases of intracellular iron overload, prominin2 facilitates iron transport out of the cell in the form of ferritin-containing multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and exosomes.[ ] Any dysregulation in iron homeostasis that leads to increased intracellular Fe2+ levels, such as elevated iron intake, reduced iron export, heightened ferritin autophagy, or diminished iron stores, can contribute to the onset of ferroptosis [Table ].

## Lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis
Oxidants, such as ROS, can attack PUFAs in cell or organelle membranes, resulting in the production of lipid peroxidation products called PL-PUFAs-OOH. This process stands as the central link in ferroptosis. The synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs) involves enzymes like acyl CoA synthase 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3). ACSL4 converts PUFAs into PUFA-CoA, and LPCAT3 facilitates the creation of membrane PUFA-PLs.[ ] However, converting monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) into MUFA-CoA, facilitated by acyl-CoA synthase 3 (ACSL3), can replace PUFAs in cell membrane phospholipids. This reduces the potential for lipid peroxidation, serving as a ferroptosis inhibitor.[ ] Furthermore, various enzymes, such as arachidonate lipoxygenases (ALOXs), COXs, NOXs, and cytosolic CYP450, can catalyze the formation of PUFA-PL-OOHs from PUFA-PL through enzymatic reactions.[ ] Accumulations of PUFA,[ ] PUFA-PLs, and upregulation of oxidative enzymes,[ ] such as lipoxygenase, all possess the potential to promote ferroptosis [Table ].

## Macrophage Metabolism and Ferroptosis
Macrophages thrive in a diverse metabolic environment, playing a vital role in maintaining tissue balance. They achieve this by taking in, converting, storing, and releasing metabolites, thereby exerting an influence on the occurrence of ferroptosis in local tissue cells. Simultaneously, these metabolites alter the ferroptotic stress levels in macrophages themselves. Here, we delve into how macrophages’ distinctive metabolism generates ferroptotic stress and their resilience against ferroptosis. Additionally, we explore how macrophages globally and locally regulate iron, lipid, and redox metabolism, to impact tissue cell ferroptosis.

## Macrophage iron metabolism and ferroptosis
Macrophages play an important role in iron homeostasis to meet the needs of surrounding cells and prevent iron overload in the surrounding environment.[ ] Around 70% of the body’s iron is stored as heme in red blood cell hemoglobin. Macrophages in the spleen and liver detect and remove damaged red blood cells via phagocytosis.[ ] When red blood cells rupture or are infected, iron in the form of hemoglobin or heme is released and phagocytosed by macrophages through receptors like CD163 and CD91.[ ] TF-bound iron is internalized via TFRC-mediated endocytosis.[ ] Phagocytosed red blood cells are broken down to produce Fe2+ or heme. Fe2+ is then released into the cytoplasm via natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 1 (NRAMP1) on the phagosome, while heme is released into the cytoplasm via heme transporter protein (HRG1).[ ] Subsequently, heme undergoes breakdown by heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) within the cytoplasm, resulting in the production of Fe2+, carbon monoxide (CO), and biliverdin.[ ]
In the spleen’s red marrow and liver, macrophages regularly phagocytose erythrocytes, with an iron intake nearly 1000 times higher than other cells. Interestingly, these macrophages are less prone to ferroptosis due to unique protective mechanisms [Figure ].[ ] A specific transcription factor called Spi-C, found in these macrophages, helps regulate iron levels by increasing the expression of iron-related proteins like FPN, reducing the risk of iron overload.[ ] Additionally, heme, obtained from erythrocytes, binds to the transcriptional regulator Bach1. This interaction activates the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), leading to increased expression of HO-1 and FTH while preventing iron overload.[ ] HO-1 breaks down heme into Fe2+, biliverdin, and CO, reducing heme accumulation. Biliverdin is converted to bilirubin, both having antioxidant properties.[ ] CO also boosts GSH synthesis by activating Nrf2[ ] and inhibits ROS production by competitively binding to and inhibiting NOXs activity.[ ] Lack of HO-1 can make macrophages susceptible to ferroptosis after erythrocyte phagocytosis.[ ] Upregulation of FTH expression results in greater iron storage capacity in macrophages that phagocytose erythrocytes, and high levels of intracellular ferritin can be released directly to the extracellular level as plasma ferritin in a non-classical manner, thus reducing intracellular iron load.[ ] Fe2+ produced in this process exits through FPN,[ ] while heme exits via feline leukemia virus subgroup C cellular receptor 1 (FLVCR1)[ ] on the plasma membrane. This mechanism enables rapid reduction of intracellular Fe2+ after erythrocyte phagocytosis, enhancing macrophage resistance to ferroptosis.
 Macrophages not only regulate systemic iron levels by engulfing erythrocytes but also exert a crucial role in controlling iron levels within tissue microenvironments, impacting neighboring cells’ susceptibility to ferroptosis.[ ] In cardiac tissue, macrophages actively remove heme proteins released from damaged cardiomyocytes during heart failure, reducing local iron accumulation.[ ] Circulating monocytes/macrophages transport heme-binding proteins (Hpx) to the heart, where they bind to heme iron, mitigating ferroptosis induced by drugs like doxorubicin and acting as cardioprotective agents.[ ] In the nervous system, iron metabolism disorders contribute to neurodegenerative diseases.[ ] Microglia help limit further iron-related damage by upregulating ferritin to store iron released from central neurons, preventing neurons from ferroptosis.[ ] In bone tissue, osteoclasts’ expression of FPN is vital for bone homeostasis. Inhibiting osteoclast FPN activity results in intracellular iron accumulation, accelerating osteoclast proliferation and bone resorption.[ ] In the liver, when ischemia-reperfusion occurs, macrophages alter hepatocyte metabolism (downregulation of FTH and GPX4 and upregulation of ACSL4) and induce ferroptosis by forming lactoferrin-rich extracellular traps and releasing ROS.[ , ] In mice with asbestos-induced mesothelial carcinoma, ferroptosis-prone macrophages release iron-rich vesicles taken up by surrounding mesothelial cells, promoting mesothelial cell ferroptosis.[ ] The multifaceted role of macrophages in regulating ferroptosis in various tissues underscores their significance as potential targets for the modulation of ferroptosis processes, providing valuable insights into the upstream mechanisms of ferroptosis in disease contexts.

## Macrophage lipid metabolism and ferroptosis
Macrophages are also involved in the clearance of lipids from the internal environment by recognizing oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), which fuels lipid peroxidation in macrophages. Additionally, receptors binding to oxLDL trigger downstream ROS production, thereby promoting macrophage ferroptosis.[ ] Moreover, macrophage lipid metabolic processes can influence the occurrence of ferroptosis in tissue cells. In cigarette smoke-exposed mice, lung macrophages overexpress ALOX5, along with its metabolite leukotriene B4 (LTB4). This triggers the expression of the ferroptosis-related gene ACSL4 in lung epithelial cells.[ ]

## Macrophage redox metabolism and ferroptosis
ROS and NO form the cornerstone of macrophage defense mechanisms against pathogens.[ ] Following the recognition of pathogenic microorganisms by Toll-like receptors (TLR1/2/4), downstream signaling pathways recruit mitochondria into macrophage phagosomes, inducing the production of mitochondrial ROS.[ ] ROS serves as a potent agent for eliminating bacteria through direct attacks on bacterial DNA, RNA, and proteins. They can also initiate ROS-dependent signaling pathways that induce cytokine production, autophagy, and granuloma formation.[ ] Pathogen binding to TLR or inflammatory factor stimulation can induce macrophages to express inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). iNOS catalyzes the production of nitric oxide (NO·) through an enzymatic reaction that involves L-arginine, NADPH, and O2 as substrates. NO· exerts its pathogen-killing effects by reacting with ·O2− to generate toxic reactive nitrogen species, including peroxynitrite (ONOO−).[ ] Reactive nitrogen species can directly target pathogenic microorganisms by attacking their DNA or by chelating iron within these microorganisms to form Fe–NO complexes, which inhibit the activity of iron-containing enzymes.[ ] In addition to their role in pathogen defense, macrophages release ROS extracellularly, which can result in tissue cell damage. Conversely, NO·, produced by macrophages, possesses ferroptosis-inhibiting properties. NO· has the capability to diffuse between cells and can prevent ferroptosis stimulated by pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa in epithelial cells even at a considerable distance.[ ]

## Ferroptosis and Macrophage Functions
Macrophages, as intrinsic immune cells, perform various functions, including clearing pathogens and abnormal cells, regulating inflammation, and shaping the tumor microenvironment. They recognize foreign substances and damaged cells through receptors like scavenger receptors and TLR. Macrophages engulf these targets via endocytosis, forming phagosomes that fuse with lysosomes to create phagolysosomes.[ ] This process activates macrophages, leading to the production of enzymes like NOXs, iNOS, and lysozyme to combat pathogens.[ ] In inflammation, macrophages can be recruited and activated at infection sites by chemokines and cytokines, releasing pro-inflammatory factors like chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 3 (CCL3), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), intensifying the inflammatory response. Macrophages exhibit two primary phenotypes: Type 1 macrophages (M1) and Type 2 macrophages (M2). M1 macrophages, induced by pathogens or interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), focus on pathogen-killing and pro-inflammatory actions. M2 macrophages, stimulated by Th2-type cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, produce anti-inflammatory molecules like IL-10, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), contributing to anti-inflammatory effects and tissue repair.[ ] In the tumor microenvironment, tumor cells release cytokines like IL-13, IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β, pushing macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, resulting in immune heterogeneous tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) that facilitate tumor progression.[ ]
The connection between ferroptosis and macrophage function is reflected in two dimensions. First, when metabolic changes favor ferroptosis, macrophages experience ferroptotic stress. These stresses, including iron overload, redox unbalance, and lipid peroxidation, are closely related to macrophage function, suggesting ferroptotic stress can impact the function of macrophages. Second, when tissue cells undergo ferroptosis, it can influence macrophage function through reciprocal interaction. In the subsequent discussion, we will delineate this connection in six distinct aspects, shedding light on the intricate relationship between ferroptosis and macrophages [Figure ].
 

## Ferroptosis and macrophage recruitment and activation
Ferroptosis, as a form of immunogenic cell death, releases damage-associated pattern molecules (DAMPs) such as high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP).[ ] HMGB1 interacts with the advanced glycosylation end-product specific receptor (AGER) on macrophages, facilitating macrophage activation and sterile inflammation.[ ] In acute kidney injury, renal tubular cell ferroptosis recruits macrophages via monocyte chemotactic proteins monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), CCL2, and chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 17 (CCL17).[ – ] In pulmonary hypertension rats, endothelial cell ferroptosis releases HMGB1, upregulating macrophage Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation.[ ] Ferroptotic cells promote macrophage recruitment and activation, and macrophage activation can in turn inhibit the occurrence of ferroptosis. Itaconate, produced during macrophage activation, inhibits ferroptosis by upregulating Nrf2 and reducing lipid peroxidation by increasing GPX4.[ ]

## Ferroptosis and macrophage polarization
Ferroptotic stress varies between different macrophage polarization states, with M1 macrophages displaying higher ferroptotic stress and increased resistance to ferroptosis. Factors that promote ferroptosis are often linked to the induction of macrophage polarization toward the M1 proinflammatory phenotype. ROS accumulation and p53 acetylation, which promote ferroptosis, can also induce macrophage polarization to the M1 phenotype.[ ] Regarding iron metabolism, M1 macrophages have high ferritin expression and low FPN expression, favoring iron buildup. Conversely, M2 macrophages express high levels of CD163 and FPN and low ferritin, indicating an iron-export phenotype.[ , ] Notably, GPX4-deficient macrophages can be prompted to polarize into M1 macrophages, but not M2 macrophages. This suggests that GPX4 is vital for M2 macrophage growth and maintenance, yet M1 macrophages are inherently resistant to ferroptosis induced by GPX4 depletion.[ ] Exogenous NO· donors inhibit ferroptosis induced by GPX4/GSH inhibitors in M2 macrophages and microglia, suggesting that iNOS/NO· in M1 macrophages may contribute to their ferroptosis resistance.[ ] As mentioned earlier, M1 macrophages are accompanied by a large accumulation of ROS during pathogen killing and should theoretically be more susceptible to ferroptosis, but in fact, M1 macrophages exhibit ferroptosis resistance, which may be related to the following reasons: (1) ROS are primarily contained within phagocytic lysosomes, limiting damage to the plasma membrane.[ ] (2) Upregulation of free radical scavenging genes, e.g., SOD2 (gene of superoxide dismutase) and AIFM1 (gene of FSP1) during the monocyte-to-macrophage conversion process enables faster ROS scavenging.[ ] (3) Timely Fe2+ export counters ferroptotic stress. Studies have shown that after Erastin treatment in M1 macrophages, Fe2+ levels were reduced, a phenomenon not observed in non-M1 polarized macrophages.[ ] (4) NO· contributes to the resistance against ferroptosis. Specifically, there are multiple mechanisms by which NO· contributes to the resistance against ferroptosis. Firstly, NO· inhibits the oxidative function of ALOX15B/phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein 1 (PEBP1) on PUFAs-PL by competing with O2 for the catalytic site. O2 and NO· share the same entry channel to connect to the catalytic site of 15LOX-2/PEBP1, however, this competition was not evident in 15LOX-2 unbound to PEBP1.[ ] Secondly, NO· regulates iron metabolism levels through activation of Nrf2 to inhibit ferroptosis.[ ] iNOS/NO· upregulation has been found to be closely related to the activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in several studies. Nrf2 activation can inhibit ferroptosis by upregulating FPN, while Nrf2 downregulation can lead to the phosphorylation of extracellular regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), thereby activating ferritin phagocytosis and releasing Fe2+ to initiate ferroptosis in macrophages. Thirdly, NO· reacts with lipid radical intermediates to form truncated nitrogen oxide lipid products, thereby interrupting the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation.[ ]
In addition, ferroptosis cells usually promote macrophage polarization toward M1. For example, ferroptotic cardiomyocyte-derived exosomes induce M1 polarization through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.[ ] This phenomenon of ferroptosis-induced macrophage polarization has been predominantly investigated in the context of TAMs, as elaborated in the following sections.

## Ferroptosis and macrophage phagocytosis and clearance
Phagocytosis efficiency in macrophages increases with higher membrane PUFA levels through improved membrane fluidity.[ ] In the early stage of macrophage-bacteria phagocytosis, the activation of HO-1 and ferritin/NCOA4 axes increases Fe2+ level, oxidative stress, and lipid ROS, thus generating ferroptotic stress; however, these alterations are transient and do not result in cell death. As the infection progresses, all cellular indicators gradually return to normal in the late stages of infection.[ ] During this process, Fe2+ can be transported to the intracellular bacterial compartment via intracellular FPN to induce ferroptosis-like death of bacteria.[ ] In addition, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) binding to TLR can inhibit FPN transcription, leading to iron accumulation in phagosomes.[ ] Increased iron is involved in the synthesis of various functional enzymes in macrophages, such as NADPH oxidase 2 and iNOS, which are essential for the production of ROS to attack pathogens.[ ] In summary, appropriate ferroptotic stress can enhance phagocytosis and killing of pathogens by macrophages. In contrast, excessive stress may lead to macrophage death, reduced phagocytosis,[ ] and pathogen release,[ ] leading to detrimental consequences.
In addition to pathogens, macrophages can phagocytose and remove the body’s own dead cells, which secrete “find-me” signals such as DAMPs to recruit macrophages, and “eat-me” signals on the cell surface binding to specific receptors on the macrophage surface to ensure their specific recognition and eventual engulfment by macrophages. As with other regulated cell death (e.g., apoptotic and necrotic cells), ferroptotic cells can be phagocytosed and cleared by macrophages. However, unlike the classical phosphatidylserine ectopic signals in apoptosis, oxidized PUFA-PLs (PUFA-PL-OOHs) (e.g., 1-steaoryl-2-15-HpETE-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylethanolamine [SAPE]-OOH) on the surface of ferroptosis cells act as “eat-me” signals and direct phagocytosis by targeting Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on macrophages.[ ]

## Ferroptosis and macrophage synthesis and secretion of inflammatory factors
Ferroptosis-related metabolic mechanisms can modulate the expression of inflammatory factors in macrophages. When ferroptotic cells were co-cultured with macrophages, they notably induced M1 polarization of macrophages, resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-6, iNOS, TNF-α, and a significant increase in major neutrophil chemokines such as chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 1 (CXCL1) and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2 (CXCL2).[ ] Knockdown of Acsl4, which is involved in the synthesis of PUFA-PLs, inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.[ ] In an acute kidney injury model, ACSL4 expression is upregulated, and silencing Acsl4 leads to the downregulation of the chemokine CCL2 and inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-6.[ ] Furthermore, proteoglycan core protein (DCN) released from ferroptotic cells binds to the receptor AGER on macrophages and triggers the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in an nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)-dependent manner.[ ]
In contrast, some studies reveal a negative link between ferroptotic stress and inflammation in macrophages. For example, Acsl4 knockdown in mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages increases inflammation by releasing PGs.[ ] Intriguingly, ferroptosis inducer RSL3 reduces LPS-induced inflammation in microglia and macrophages via increased Nrf2 expression.[ ] Erastin, a System Xc- inhibitor, lessens iNOS, COX-2, and TNF-α expression in mouse macrophages, dampening the NF-κB pathway and curbing inflammation and sepsis development.[ ] This negative correlation may arise from the non-ferroptotic effects of inducers and inhibitors, necessitating further investigation.
In brief, factors promoting ferroptosis often lead to macrophage inflammation, whereas inhibiting ferroptosis tends to reduce inflammation. However, the exact mechanism linking ferroptosis and macrophage inflammation remains unclear. Shared pathways including iron accumulation, ROS increase, ALOXs action on PUFA, or a potential upstream-downstream relationship between inflammation and ferroptosis may be involved.

## Ferroptosis and tumor microenvironment macrophages
TAMs are a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, influencing cancer progression and the immune response. They can be categorized as M1 or M2 TAMs, resembling M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. M1-TAMs are thought to inhibit tumors, while M2-TAMs promote them.[ ] The relationship between cancer cell ferroptosis and TAMs is complex. Ferroptosis tends to correlate positively with M1 polarization of TAMs and negatively with M2 polarization. This link may be because M2 macrophages produce substances to inhibit ferroptosis, whereas ferroptotic tumor cells are immunogenic and induce M1 polarization. M2-TAM-produced chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 8 (CXCL8) protects endothelial cells from ferroptosis by activating SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression.[ ] In cervical cancer, M2-TAM generates miRNA-660-5p exosomes, which translocate to cancer cells to attenuate ALOX15 expression and inhibit ferroptosis.[ ] The protein transmembrane protein 147 (TMEM147), upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma, mediates ferroptosis resistance and M2 polarization.[ ] Ferroptotic tumor cells reduce M2-TAMs in the tumor microenvironment, possibly by adaptive immune responses triggered by DAMPs, such as calreticulin and HMGB1.[ ] Dihydroartemisinin-mediated ferroptosis in lung cancer cells led to DNA damage, activating the NF-κB pathway and promoting TAM polarization toward an M1 phenotype.[ ]
The mechanistic link between TAM infiltration and ferroptosis in cancer remains not entirely clear. Nevertheless, based on the properties of tumor microenvironment macrophages, novel strategies for anticancer therapy may involve nanomedicines targeting ferroptosis in tumor cells while simultaneously promoting TAM conversion from M2 to M1.[ ]

## Macrophages regulate tissue cell ferroptosis
In addition to influencing tissue cell ferroptosis by regulating the local metabolic environment, as discussed earlier, macrophages can also exert regulatory effects in other specific ways. In lipopolysaccharide-treated mice, the alveolar macrophage-derived exosome tRF-22-8BWS7K092 activates the Hippo signaling pathway to induce alveolar epithelial cell ferroptosis, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of acute lung injury.[ ] Alveolar macrophages also promote ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells through the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes signaling (STING) pathway via exosome-transported Ficolin B to exacerbate bleomycin-induced lung injury.[ ] In chronic heart failure and hypertension, a significant influx of macrophages enters the heart tissue. These macrophages transfer mitochondria to cardiomyocytes, changing how these cells process fatty acids and GSH. This transfer raises Fe2+ levels and triggers lipid peroxidation in mitochondria, causing ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes.[ ] In obesity, macrophages in adipose tissue release exosomes that reach cardiomyocytes. These exosomes contain miR-140-5p, which triggers cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by blocking SLC7A11 and reducing GSH production.[ ]

## Effects of Macrophage Ferroptosis and its Role in Disease
Although some macrophages with specific functions are resistant to ferroptotic stress, macrophages can still undergo ferroptosis when confronted with external stresses that exceed their regulatory capacity.[ ] Macrophage ferroptosis mainly has three primary consequences. Firstly, it can generate pro-inflammatory effects and promote the ferroptosis of neighbouring cells. Macrophage ferroptosis typically generates a pro-inflammatory response like that seen in other cell types. Interestingly, it has been observed that ferroptotic cells can trigger ferroptosis in neighboring cells in a wave-like manner. This propagation relies on iron and lipid peroxidation but does not necessarily require cell rupture.[ ] Secondly, immune downregulation follows a massive death of macrophages due to ferroptosis. Thirdly, the release of macrophage contents. Macrophages, as specialized phagocytes, usually have intracellular iron, lipid,[ ] ROS accumulation or pathogen accumulation[ , ] before ferroptosis occurs, which may release to the surrounding environment through vesicles,[ ] exosomes[ ] or rupture to affect other cells after ferroptosis. These consequences underscore the significant role of macrophage ferroptosis in the development and progression of several diseases.
They also provide therapeutic strategies for targeting macrophage ferroptosis. Two main therapeutic strategies have been proposed: (1) simultaneous inhibition of ferroptosis and macrophage pro-inflammatory effects by targeting upstream links[ , ]; and (2) increasing macrophage resistance to ferroptosis.[ , ] We present the consequences and therapeutic strategies for macrophage ferroptosis in the following different diseases.

## Infection
Infections occur when harmful microorganisms such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, or parasites invade and multiply in the body. Macrophage ferroptosis plays a role in various pathogen infections and is involved in pathogen transmission and inflammation production. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) infection triggers ferroptosis in macrophages, characterized by reduced GPX4 and GSH levels, increased ROS, and iron overload.[ , ]M. tuberculosis produces protein tyrosine phosphatase A and epigenetically down-regulates host cell GPX4.[ ] Knockdown of heme oxygenase 1 (Hmox1) by small interfering RNA (siRNA) increased the release of intracellular bacteria in Bacillus Calmette-Guerin-infected macrophages by modulating ferroptosis.[ ] The release of M. tuberculosis engulfed by macrophages undergoing ferroptosis leads to its dissemination in lung tissue and exacerbates lung injury.[ , ] Increased dissemination and pathogenicity by inducing macrophages ferroptosis also occurs in Pseudomonas aeruginosa,[ ]Brucella,[ ] and Staphylococcus aureus[ ] infection. Staphylococcus aureus promotes ferroptosis by up-regulating macrophage interferon-induced protein35 (IFP35), which promotes ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2.[ ] In sepsis, extracellular cold-induced RNA binding protein (eCIRP), a type of DAMP, induces ferroptosis in macrophages in a TLR4-dependent manner.[ ] Plasmodium infection induces Kupffer cell ferroptosis by hemozoin-caused iron overload and consequent macrophage depletion and liver inflammation and susceptibility to secondary infection.[ ] Thus, inhibition of macrophage ferroptosis may be a new strategy to reduce infection damage.[ , ]

## Neurological diseases
Neurological disorders are a group of diseases and disorders involving the central nervous system (including the brain and spinal cord) or the peripheral nervous system (including nerves and ganglia). Macrophage ferroptosis plays a role in several neurological disorders including Parkinson’s disease (PD), spinal cord injury (SCI), traumatic brain injury (TBI), and multiple sclerosis (MS). In MS lesions, NCOA4 was found to be upregulated in macrophages, leading to an increase in active ferrous iron.[ ] Microglia are the resident macrophage of the central nervous system and play an important role in ferroptosis. Increased microglial iron accumulation was found in PD, a manifestation that initially protected neurons from ferroptosis; however, after exceeding the upper limit, microglia undergo ferroptosis, which in turn induces neuronal death.[ , ] Microglia M1 polarization also showed resistance to ferroptosis and M2 polarization was sensitive to ferroptosis.[ , ] Moreover, the ferroptosis environment is often accompanied by microglia M1 polarization. In SCI, Hmox1 and Cathepsin B (Ctsb) are important molecules in the regulation of ferroptosis; Hmox1 is also involved in microglia M1 polarization, whereas inhibition of Ctsb induces microglia M2 polarization.[ , ] Overexpression of protein arginine methyltransferase 8 (PRMT8) inhibits ferroptosis and M1 macrophage polarization, thereby alleviating SCI.[ ] In TBI, the inhibition of neuronal ferroptosis by GPX4 overexpression suppresses CCL2 production and thus inhibits peripheral macrophage recruitment and infiltration to mitigate disease progression.[ ]

## Atherosclerosis
Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease that usually occurs in the intima of large and medium-sized arteries. Its characteristic feature is the deposition of lipids and the formation of fibrous plaques in the vascular wall, which leads to vascular stenosis and hardening. In atherosclerosis, erythrocytes are destroyed and accumulate in plaques that are phagocytosed by macrophages, leading to iron enrichment in macrophages. Transcriptomics analysis revealed that ferroptosis-related genes ALOX5 and neutrophil cytosol factor 2 (NCF2) may be involved in the formation of the necrotic core of atherosclerosis by regulating macrophage ferroptosis.[ ] Macrophages phagocytose and internalize oxLDL, and turn into foam cells. Ox-LDL in plaques can be recognized and phagocytosed by macrophages through TLR4 receptors and activate NF-κB, thereby upregulating hepcidin expression to produce hepcidin-mediated degradation of FPN and exacerbate cellular iron overload, whereas upregulating activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) inhibits SLC7A11 production, thus downregulating the GPX4/GSH pathway to promote macrophage ferroptosis.[ ] The knockdown of lnc-MRGPRF-6:1 could alleviate ox-LDL-induced ferroptosis in macrophages.[ ] Ferroptotic macrophages release inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, induce the production of metalloproteinases matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP-2) and matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP-9),[ , ] and release cellular components and lipids to form necrotic nuclei to promote the progression of atherosclerosis.[ ] Cigarette tar promotes atherosclerosis progression by inducing macrophage ferroptosis via the NF-κB-activated hepcidin/FPN/SLC7A11 pathway.[ ] Sirtuin1 activation[ ] and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) inhibition[ ] enhance autophagy of macrophages and inhibit ferroptosis to attenuate foam cell formation and alleviate atherosclerosis.

## Osteoarticular diseases
Macrophage ferroptosis also plays a role in osteoarticular diseases. In osteoporosis, bones lose and become brittle, which results in fragility and an increased risk of fractures. Studies have identified HIF-1α as a key regulator of osteoporosis,[ ] and ferroptosis of osteoclasts (specialized macrophages that break down bone tissue) may play a role. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α promotes the survival of osteoclasts by inhibiting NCOA4-mediated ferritin phagocytosis, thereby protecting receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL)-induced osteoclasts from ferroptosis, then promoting osteoporosis.[ ] In apical periodontitis, the TNF-α autocrine–paracrine loop in ferroptotic macrophages can inhibit osteogenesis in bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) via the NRF2/FSP1/ROS signaling pathway, leading to bone loss.[ ] Macrophage ferroptosis also occurs in osteoarthritis, and mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) can rescue cartilage damage by inhibiting macrophage ferroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway.[ ]

## Immunosuppression following blood transfusion
Immunosuppression following blood transfusion is a temporary suppression of the immune system that can occur in certain patients after blood transfusion. This occurs because massive input of erythrocytes leads to increased erythrophagocytosis of splenic red marrow macrophages. Excessive uptake triggers ferroptosis in these macrophages, thereby causing local macrophage depletion.[ ] This process is accompanied by migration of bone marrow Ly6Chi monocytes to the spleen and differentiation into splenic red marrow macrophages for replenishment,[ ] suggesting that macrophage ferroptosis and depletion may be a consequence of transfusion and one reason for the reduced host immunity and susceptibility to infection after transfusion.

## Mesothelioma
Mesothelioma is a type of cancer that affects the mesothelium, a thin layer of tissue that covers most internal organs. It is usually caused by exposure to asbestos, an iron-containing substance that can cause respiratory diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis in addition to mesothelioma after long-term inhalation. Asbestos exposure induces an iron-storage phenotype in macrophages and leads to iron-overload-dependent ferroptosis in macrophages.[ ] Ferroptotic macrophages release iron-rich extracellular vesicles (enriched with iron storage proteins FTH and FTL) that are taken up by mesothelial cells, which leads to oxidative DNA damage in mesothelial cells such as 8-hydroxy-2 deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and double-strand breaks, thereby altering the cell cycle and promoting mitosis, which mediates the development of malignant mesothelioma.[ ]

## Other diseases
Macrophage ferroptosis also plays a role in multiple acute and chronic inflammatory diseases and fibrotic processes. Liensinine ameliorates acute liver injury by enhancing macrophage resistance to ferroptosis through inhibiting ferritin autophagy.[ ] Uridine ameliorates sepsis-induced acute lung injury by inhibiting macrophage ferroptosis through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway.[ ] Silicosis is a lung disease caused by inhaling silica dust. Silica induces ferroptosis in macrophages through activation of Wnt5a/Ca2+ signaling and endoplasmic reticulum stress,[ ] leading to the secretion of pro-fibrotic cytokines and fibrosis, which is involved in the pathological progression of silicosis.[ ] Systemic sclerosis is a rheumatic disease with multi-organ inflammation and eventual fibrosis driven by immune dysfunction. Inhibition of ACSL4 reduced macrophage ferroptosis and alleviated fibrosis in a bleomycin-induced systemic sclerosis model.[ ] In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, the accumulation of lipid peroxides leads to M2 alveolar macrophage ferroptosis, in which macrophages lose their anti-inflammatory and reparative function, but triggers an inflammatory response.[ ] Inflammatory bowel disease is a group of chronic inflammatory disorders that affect the digestive tract. In experimental colitis inflammation, inhibition of macrophage ferroptosis by beta-caryophyllene (BCP) ameliorates inflammation.[ ]

## Conclusions and Perspectives
The unique metabolic characteristics of macrophages are intricately linked to the ferroptosis pathway and can influence tissue cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by regulating systemic and local metabolic levels. Notably, macrophages can withstand ferroptotic stress under physiological conditions, indicating the presence of protective mechanisms in these cells. This resilience aligns with their crucial functions in the body. Furthermore, macrophage activities such as recruitment, activation, phagocytosis, and polarization and their role in the tumor microenvironment are closely intertwined with ferroptotic stress. This leads to a synchronous relationship between macrophage function and the occurrence of ferroptosis. Macrophages can also modulate the ferroptosis sensitivity of tissue cells and recognize and remove ferroptosis cells in multiple ways.
However, numerous questions remain unanswered regarding the relationship between macrophages and ferroptosis. 1. Is the correlation between macrophage function and ferroptosis from sharing the same metabolic pathways or an upstream-downstream relationship, and where is the threshold between the changes in normal macrophage function and the occurrence of ferroptosis in macrophages? Answering this question will help achieve precise regulation of the macrophage ferroptosis process to maximize therapeutic goals. 2. How can the differences in the correlation between macrophage and ferroptosis in different tissue-resident macrophage populations be explained? In studies related to macrophage ferroptosis, how can it be determined whether regulatory molecules associated with ferroptosis act through the ferroptosis pathway or whether there are other bypasses that have the opposite effect? Because of the diversity of both tissue-resident macrophages and ferroptosis regulatory molecules, this question has important implications for the generalizability and accuracy of the conclusions. 3. How can macrophage ferroptosis be targeted to treat disease? Currently there is very limited research in diseases other than tumors, and better understanding of the multiple aspects of macrophage–ferroptosis interactions may provide new ideas for disease treatment.
Clarifying these questions will contribute to a deeper understanding of ferroptosis and the pathological processes of multiple diseases in which macrophages are involved. It will provide new therapeutic ideas for various diseases, such as targeting the common link between ferroptosis and macrophage activation to inhibit ferroptosis in tissue cells while reducing inflammation levels to treat diseases involving acute and chronic inflammation. Targeting the common link between promoting ferroptosis and macrophage polarization may be a strategy to promote tumor cell death and lift immunosuppression to treat diseases such as cancer. Precise regulation of the ferroptosis process and macrophage function has great therapeutic potential in numerous clinical diseases.
