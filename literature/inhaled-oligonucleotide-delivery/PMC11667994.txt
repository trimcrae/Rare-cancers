## Background
Preterm birth, a leading cause of perinatal mortality and morbidity, is often associated with inflammation and aberrant myometrial contractions. This study investigates the role of Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, in myometrium contraction and inflammation-associated preterm birth.

## Methods
We employed Western blotting, Immunofluorescence, and Quantitative real-time PCR techniques to examine Piezo1 expression in uterine tissues. Functional assays, including myometrial contractility studies and cell contraction assays, were conducted to elucidate the effects of Piezo1 on myometrial contractions. Piezo1 inhibitors and gene knockdown techniques were used to investigate the impact of Piezo1 on inflammation-associated preterm birth, complemented by inflammatory cytokine profiling and calcium imaging to investigate the mechanism.

## Results
Our findings reveal that Piezo1 is the predominant mechanosensitive channel in mouse myometrium tissue and mouse primary uterine smooth muscle (pUSMCs), with increased expression during mouse and human pregnancy. Following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) intrauterine injection, Piezo1 mRNA and protein levels were elevated in the mouse uterine smooth muscle layer. Direct pharmacologic activation of Piezo1 by Yoda1 increased the contraction of pUSMCs and shortened the pregnancy duration. In contrast, inhibition with Gsmtx4 or siRNA knockdown of Piezo1 attenuated LPS-induced pUSMCs contraction and spontaneous uterine myometrium contraction. Additionally, blocking or knocking down Piezo1 prolonged the pregnancy in an LPS-induced preterm birth model. Yoda1 stimulation increased intracellular Ca2+ levels in pUSMCs, while Gsmtx4 reduced these levels. Gsmtx4 decreased cox-2 expression and inflammation factors in LPS-stimulated pUSMCs.

## Conclusions
These results suggest that Piezo1 acts as a critical regulator of uterine function, and its overexpression may predispose to preterm labor through heightened myometrial activity and inflammation. The study underscores the potential of targeting Piezo1 as a therapeutic strategy to mitigate preterm birth associated with uterine inflammation.

## Background
Preterm birth (PTB), defined as a birth occurring before 37 weeks of gestation, is the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality [ ]. Approximately 15 million babies are born preterm annually [ ]. Surveys indicate that the global rate of PTB is about 11%, varying between 4 and 18% across different countries [ ]. PTB and its associated complications are the primary cause of death in children under the age of five [ ]. Moreover, PTB significantly increases the risk of adverse neurological outcomes, diseases related to immature organ systems, and several chronic conditions, imposing a severe burden on families and society [ ]. Despite extensive research, there remains insufficient evidence on the cellular and molecular mechanisms contributing to the development of PTB, resulting in a lack of effective treatment options.
PTB is suggested to be associated with some maternal factors, including multiple pregnancies, maternal age, maternal infections, and maternal alcohol or substance abuse [ ]. These factors trigger a shift in the myometrium from a stage of pregnancy quiescence to active labor contraction. However, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms ﻿underlying myometrial contraction remain unknown. The influx of extracellular calcium ions through various ion channels is crucial for initiating action potential in the uterine smooth muscle, ultimately leading to uterine contractility [ , ]. L-type voltage-gated calcium channels are believed to be the predominant ion channels that allow Ca2+ to enter myometrial smooth muscle cells [ ]. However, clinically used L-type Ca2+ channel blockers for preventing preterm labor have limited therapeutic efficacy, failing to prolong pregnancy or prevent adverse perinatal outcomes significantly [ , ]. This suggests that Ca2+ may enter the myometrium through an alternative route during labor. Targeting these ion channels in the myometrium is expected to be a novel therapeutic approach for preventing and treating PTB.
﻿ Mechanical stretch is hypothesized to be an essential regulator of uterine growth and contractility during pregnancy [ , ]. However, whether mechanosensitive ion channels serve as the primary pathway for Ca2+ entering the myometrium and participating in myometrial contraction remains unclear. The Piezo family of mechanosensitive ion channels, first discovered in 2010 [ ], has garnered significant attention in mechanobiology due to its unique properties and functions [ ]. The Piezo2 channel is primarily found in neurons, whereas Piezo1 exhibits a broader expression pattern and is found in many non-excitable cells and tissues [ , ]. Piezo1 preferentially facilitates Ca2+ influx under physiological conditions and is activated by mechanical stimuli such as pressure or shear stress, leading to cell depolarization and Ca2+ entry [ ]. Given that the uterus experiences mechanical tension throughout pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester, it is plausible that Ca2+ influx occurs via ﻿mechanosensitive ion channels like Piezo1.
Previous studies have demonstrated that Piezo1 regulates smooth muscle remodeling and contraction. In airway smooth muscle cells, Piezo1 is activated by stromal interaction molecule 1, resulting in potential downstream changes in contractility and extracellular matrix remodeling [ ]. In vascular smooth muscle cells, Piezo1 regulates contractility through the PKC/aquaporin-1 pathway in response to extracellular matrix stiffness [ ]. Moreover, the upregulation of Piezo1 in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells contributes to vascular remodeling and is associated with shear stress-associated experimental pulmonary hypertension [ ]. ﻿Piezo1 is also expressed in human fetoplacental endothelial cells, uterine smooth muscle, and microvascular endothelium [ , ]. However, the expression profiles of Piezo1 in a mouse model of preterm birth and its role in mouse uterine myometrium contraction and PTB have not been investigated.
In the current study, we aim to test the hypothesis that the increased expression of Piezo1 channels in the myometrium contributes to uterine myometrium contraction in a mouse model of PTB. To investigate this, we established a mouse PTB model by administering intrauterine lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and subsequently examined the expression of Piezo1 in the myometrium. In vitro, we measured uterine myometrium and uterine smooth muscle cell contraction. To further explore the role of Piezo1, we utilized a Piezo1 inhibitor and si-RNA to determine whether blocking Piezo1 channels could inhibit spontaneous uterine contraction and reduce the preterm birth rate. Finally, inflammatory cytokine profiling and calcium imaging were conducted to elucidate the underlying mechanism by which Piezo1 regulates myometrium contraction.

## Methods

## Animal
Seven to eight-week-old CD-1® (ICR) mice were obtained from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (China) and housed in the Laboratory Animal Centre of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. The mice were kept under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to food and water following a 12-h light/dark cycle. This study received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital of Sichuan University (Approval NO. 2,023,015). All animal procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health guidelines for treating and using laboratory animals. To generate pregnant mice, we mated one male with two nulliparous female mice in the evening, and the presence of a vaginal plug the following morning was used to confirm successful mating. The day a vaginal plug was observed was designated as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5).

## Surgical procedures and treatment
We utilized a PTB model described in a previously established study [ ]. Intrauterine LPS was performed on E15.5, with preterm birth defined as a pregnancy loss occurring on or before E18, as the full term is E18.5 [ ]. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane inhalation, and the uterine horns were exposed via a miniature incision in the lower abdomen. Subsequently, we administered either LPS (100 µg/dam, Sigma-Aldrich) or an equal volume of saline (vehicle) into the uterus, targeting the space between the two gestational sacs closest to the cervix while avoiding injection into the gestational sacs. To examine the effect of Piezo1 inhibitor on preterm delivery, Gsmtx4 (270 µg/kg, Abmol), a selective inhibitor of cation-permeable mechanosensitive channels, was administered intraperitoneally 0.5 h after the intrauterine injection of LPS. For Piezo1 knockdown, Piezo1-specific si-RNA (30 nmol/dam) or scramble si-RNA (30 nmol/dam) was administered via the tail vein 24 h and 48 h before LPS injection. To assess whether Piezo1 activation induces preterm delivery, the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 (30 µg/dam, Glpbio) or an equal volume of DMSO (vehicle) was injected intrauterine on E15.5.

## Preparation of siRNA and siRNA injection
The sequences of the siRNAs used were as follows: for Piezo1 siRNA, the top strand was 5'-GCUAAGCUGGAAUCGCAGUCUTT-3', and the bottom was 5'-AGACUGCGAUUCCAGCUUAGCTT-3'. For scrambled siRNA, the top strand was 5'-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3', and the bottom was 5'-ACUGGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3'. These si-RNAs were synthesized using 2'-OMe-A-CE-RNA Phosphoramidites (Hanbio Tech, Shanghai, China) and dissolved in RNase-free PBS. Synthetic si-RNAs (30 nmol in 100 µL of PBS) or 100 µL of PBS alone were rapidly injected (within 10 s) into one of the lateral tail veins under isoflurane anesthesia. To facilitate vein dilation, the tail was immersed in warm water (50–55℃) for 5 ± 1 s before injection.

## Tissue collection

## Human myometrial tissue
After written informed consent was obtained, we enrolled five term-pregnant women not in labor, scheduled for elective cesarean section, and five non-pregnant women undergoing elective total hysterectomy. All participants were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status I-II. The pregnant participants were free of any obstetrical, medical, or surgical complications, including gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes. The enrollment procedures were approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital (No. 2024140). As detailed in our previous study, biopsies of the myometrium were excised from the middle portion of the upper edge of the incision line in the lower uterine segment at cesarean section, following the separation of the amnion and chorion laeve. For the non-pregnant participants, similar biopsies were taken from the lower uterine segment during hysterectomy. The collected samples were immediately transferred to the laboratory in liquid nitrogen for further analysis.

## Mouse tissue
Twelve hours after LPS administration, the mice were anesthetized and euthanized. The right side of the uterus was carefully excised and opened longitudinally. The adipose tissue, placentas, fetuses, and fetal membranes were gently removed. Using a surgical blade, the endometrium was delicately scraped off and removed with a cotton swab, leaving only the myometrial layer intact. The myometrium samples were then randomly divided into three sections: one was stored at − 80 °C for Western blotting analysis or RT-qPCR, another was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding, and the final section was placed in ice-cold PBS to record spontaneous uterine myometrial contractions.

## Spontaneous uterine myometrium contraction recording
Mouse uterine tissue samples were placed in ice-cold carbonated PBS (95% O₂−5% CO₂). Longitudinal myometrial strips, approximately 1 cm in length, were collected and suspended in an organ bath system containing carbonated Krebs solution (119 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO₃, 1.2 mM KH₂PO₄, 2.5 mM CaCl₂, 1.5 mM MgSO₄, 11 mM glucose) maintained at 37 °C. Following our previous methodology [ ], one end of the myometrial strip was fixed with a hook, while the other was attached to a force transducer via an adjustable metal hook. The myometrial strip was then adjusted to a basal tension of approximately 2 g and allowed to equilibrate for 30 min. Once regular spontaneous myometrial contractions were observed, we recorded contractile activity for 10 min and analyzed the data using BL-420S Biological Data Acquisition & Analysis System (Chengdu TME Technology Co., Ltd., China). Four parameters—frequency (per minute), contractile amplitude (g), duration (s), and time between contractions (s)—were used to assess spontaneous myometrial contractility. Frequency was defined as the total number of contractile waveforms during the assessment period. The contraction amplitude is measured as the difference between the peak value of the contraction and the lowest baseline value. The contraction interval is the time from the end of one contraction to the beginning of the next. The contraction duration refers to the time from the onset of the contraction to its complete conclusion.

## Culture of mouse uterine smooth muscle cells and Piezo1 si-RNA transfection
Mouse uterine smooth muscle cells (USMCs) were isolated through enzymatic dispersion under sterile conditions. The uterus was cleaned of fatty tissue, fetuses, placentas, and fetal membranes and then rinsed with PBS containing 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The remaining smooth muscle layers were cut into small pieces and incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 2 mg/mL collagenase type II (C2-22, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 60 min. After enzymatic digestion, the cells suspension was centrifuged to pellet the cells, which were then resuspended in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cells were cultured in 10 cm2 flasks and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂−95% air-humidified atmosphere until they reached confluency. All cells used for subsequent experiments were at passage two.
This study specifically utilized small interfering RNA (si-RNA) to interfere with Piezo1 expression. Both Piezo1-targeting si-RNA and a scrambled control si-RNA were commercially obtained from Hanbio Tech (Shanghai, China) and transfected into the cells according to the manufacturer's instructions. Knockdown efficiency was assessed 48 h post-transfection using quantitative PCR (Q-PCR) analysis to confirm the reduction of Piezo1 expression.

## Cell contraction assay
Cultured myometrial cells were used to evaluate contractility through a collagen gel contractility assay, per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, myometrial cells were harvested with trypsin and resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS to a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. Collagen solution (CBA-201, Cell Biolabs, CA, USA) was then mixed with the cell suspension to achieve a final collagen concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. The mixture was pipetted into a 24-well plate and allowed to polymerize by incubating for 1 h at 37 °C. Two days later, the gel was gently detached from the sides of the culture dishes after refreshing the medium with DMEM containing 10% FBS. The gels were then stimulated with different treatments: LPS (100 ng/mL), Yoda1 (10 µM), Gsmtx4 (2.5 µM), LPS (100 ng/mL) + Gsmtx4 (2.5 µM), Yoda1 (10 µM) + Gsmtx4 (2.5 µM), or vehicle. Twenty-four hours later, the areas of the gels were measured using ImageJ Data Acquisition Software (NIH, Maryland, USA) to quantify the contraction induced by the various treatments.

## Western blotting
Protein extraction was performed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime), and the total protein content was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were separated by 10% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Pall, NY, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS)–Tween for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Piezo1 (1:600, Proteintech) and GAPDH (1:5000, ZenBioScience, Chengdu, China). The next day, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP), 511,103; goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP), 511,203, Zen-BioScience, Chengdu, China) at a 1:5000 dilution for 2 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent detection (Millipore) was used for signal analysis, and specific proteins were quantified using ImageJ Data Acquisition Software. GAPDH was used as a loading control for all samples to ensure consistency in protein quantification.

## Immunofluorescence
Paraffin-embedded sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated using standard protocols. Antigen retrieval was then performed by boiling the sections for 30 min in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Following this, sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. To block non-specific binding, the sections were incubated in PBS containing 5% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit anti-Piezo1 antibody (1:200, 15,939–1-AP, Proteintech), mouse anti-α-SMA (1:100, MAB1420, R&D systems). The next day, they were incubated with a FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Cyanine3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody (1:500, F-2761, and 1:500, A10520, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′-DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min after washing with PBS. For controls, some sections were incubated with the anti-Piezo1 antibody and the blocking peptide (1:1 ratio) (Mouse IgG Isotype Control, 10400C, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Additionally, other sections were processed without the primary or secondary antibody as negative controls. Digital images of the sections were captured using a Panoramic MIDI digital microscope (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary), and analysis was performed using Case Viewer (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

## Quantitative real-time PCR
RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA synthesis was conducted using PrimeScript reagent (Takara) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was carried out in triplicate using a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial incubation at 95 °C for 5 min to activate the polymerase, followed by 40 amplification cycles with denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. A melting curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of the amplification products. GAPDH was used as an internal control for mRNA. The relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The direct comparison of Piezo1, Piezo2, Trek1, Trek2, Trpc3, and Trpv4 using Ct values alone without a standard curve could lead to potential misinterpretation, as each gene may have different amplification efficiencies. To reduce errors, we designed the primers and optimized the reaction conditions to ensure that the amplification efficiency of each gene remained consistent and close to 100%. The primer sequences were as follows: Piezo1 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′; Piezo2 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′; Trek1 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′; Trek2 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3’; TRPC3 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′; TRPV4 (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′; and GAPDH (F): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′, (R): 5′-GGCTCTACTGGCTGTTGCT-3′. Sangon Biotech synthesized the primers.

## ELISA
We prepare standard solutions with a concentration gradient according to the instructions provided by the ELISA kit. Diluted tissue and cell samples are then added to a 96-well plate coated with the corresponding antibody. Following this, a pre-prepared working solution of biotinylated antibody is added to each well. The plate is incubated at room temperature for 2 h, after which it is washed five times to remove unbound antibodies. Next, an enzyme conjugate working solution is added, and the plate is incubated at room temperature for an additional hour. After this incubation, the plate is washed five more times to eliminate any excess unbound enzyme conjugate. Subsequently, a substrate solution is added to each well, and the plate is allowed to incubate for a designated time to enable color development. Finally, a stop solution is applied to halt the enzymatic reaction. Absorbance is measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the concentrations of the samples are calculated based on the standard curve generated from the standard solutions. Statistical analysis is conducted, and the results are plotted for further interpretation.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using Prism version 10.1.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). ﻿There were no missing data points, and outliers were not explicitly tested. Each experiment was performed at least three times to ensure reliability. A Student's t-test was used to compare two groups, while one-way ANOVA was applied to compare three or more groups. Statistical significance was established at a threshold of P < 0.05.

## Results

## Piezo1 is the most predominant mechanosensitive channel in the mouse myometrium and increases during both mouse and human pregnancy
Myometrial cells possess various mechanosensitive channels and receptors that stimulate or suppress contractility [ , ]. To investigate the gene expression of these mechanosensitive channels in the mouse uterine smooth muscle during pregnancy, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was employed to examine the expression level of Piezo1, Piezo2, Trek1, Trek2, Trpc3, and Trpv4 in uterine tissue and primary uterine smooth muscle cells (pUSMCs) at day 15.5 of pregnancy. The results showed that the Piezo1 gene expression was significantly higher in both uterine tissue and pUSMCs compared to the other mechanosensitive channels (P < 0.001, Fig.  A, B). Subsequently, we examined Piezo1 protein expression in the uterine myometrium of non-pregnant and 18.5-day pregnant mice. The findings revealed that Piezo1 protein levels were markedly higher in pregnant mice compared to their non-pregnant counterparts (P < 0.001, Fig.  C). Furthermore, we investigated whether a similar up-regulation of Piezo1 occurs in the uterus of pregnant women. Indeed, Piezo1 protein expression was significantly greater in pregnant women than in nonpregnant women (P < 0.01, Fig.  D). However, it is essential to interpret these results while considering the substantial difference (P < 0.001) in the mean age between the two groups: pregnant women averaged 27.40 ± 3.65 years, while nonpregnant women averaged 48.20 ± 9.88 years (P < 0.01) (Table  ). This age discrepancy may influence the expression levels and must be acknowledged as a limitation in our analysis. 

## Piezo1 expression increased in mouse uterine myometrium after LPS intrauterine infection
Piezo1 expression was significantly increased at both the messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels in the myometrium after LPS intrauterine injection (Fig.  ). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction showed that the Piezo1 mRNA levels increased by 2.25 ± 0.89-fold after LPS intrauterine injection compared to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig.  A). Similarly, Piezo1 protein levels in the uterus were 2.26 ± 0.22-fold higher post-LPS treatment than after normal saline injection (P < 0.001, Fig.  B). To investigate the distribution of Piezo1 in uterine myometrial tissue from LPS-treated and control pregnant (E15.5) mice, immunofluorescent double labeling showed that Piezo1 co-labeled with α-SMA in both LPS-treated and control slices. Notably, the intensity of Piezo1 staining was greater in the LPS-treated group, indicating an increased expression of Piezo1 in smooth muscle cells after LPS injection (Fig.  C). To further substantiate that LPS injection promotes Piezo1 expression, we isolated and cultured pUSMCs from pregnant mice. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated increased expression of Piezo1. However, when the tissue was incubated with the anti-Piezo1 antibody in combination with the hapten-blocking peptide, no Piezo1 immunoreactivity was detected (Fig.  D). Western blotting confirmed a significant increase in Piezo1 expression (2.22 ± 0.65-fold) in pUSMCs after LPS treatment compared to the control group (Fig.  E). These data collectively suggest that intrauterine infection with LPS enhances Piezo1 expression in uterine smooth muscle, which may play a role in the mechanisms associated with PTB. 

## Piezo1 activation mediates mouse pUSMCs contraction and is involved in LPS-induced mouse pUSMCs contraction in collagen gels
We performed Piezo1 knockdown with small interfering RNA (siRNA) to confirm the effects of Piezo1 on LPS-induced contraction. Piezo1 mRNA was decreased by 51.22 ± 11.83% (P < 0.001) in pUSMCs after injection of Piezo1-siRNA (Fig.  A). To assess the role of Piezo1 in mediating pUSMCs contraction, collagen gel contraction assays were performed. pUSMCs were embedded in collagen gels and treated with LPS, LPS + Gsmtx4 (a selective inhibitor of cation-permeable mechanosensitive channels), Gsmtx4 alone, Yoda1 (a Piezo1 agonist), Yoda1 + Gsmtx4, or a control treatment. The results showed that both LPS (1 µg/ml) and Yoda1 (10 µM) significantly reduced the collagen area by 34.8 ± 8.5% (P < 0.001) and 23.0 ± 3.1% (P < 0.001), respectively, compared to the control group (Fig.  B). Gsmtx4 treatment counteracted the contractile effects of LPS and Yoda1, increasing the collagen gel area by 27.0 ± 4.3% (P < 0.01) and 23.7 ± 5.4% (P < 0.001) related to LPS and Yoda1, respectively (Fig.  B). Compared with the gels containing scramble and vehicle, collagen gels area was significantly decreased by 39.67 ± 7.23% after scramble + LPS treatment (P < 0.001), which can be markedly reversed by Piezo1 si-RNA administration (P < 0.001) (Fig.  C). Together, these findings suggest that Piezo1 activation induces contraction in pUSMCs, while inhibition or knockdown of Piezo1 significantly reduces contraction. Thus, Piezo1 plays an essential role in mediating pUSMCs contraction. 

## Pharmacological blockade or knockdown of Piezo1 inhibits the spontaneous mouse uterine myometrium contraction
We further examined whether Piezo1 regulates spontaneous uterine myometrium contraction by uterine muscle strips analysis. The uterine contractility records (Fig.  A) indicated that Gsmtx4 exerted an inhibitory effect on LPS-increased frequency (1.01 ± 0.26 vs. 1.65 ± 0.52, P = 0.025, Fig.  B), contractile amplitudes (3.47 ± 1.16 vs. 5.87 ± 1.86, P = 0.03, Fig.  C), and time between contractions (29.19 ± 5.46 vs. 17.02 ± 7.93, P = 0.02, (Fig.  D) compared to LPS + vehicle (DMSO). However, Gsmtx4 did not significantly affect contraction duration (P > 0.05, Fig.  E). Further, si-RNA was injected to observe the myometrium contraction (Fig.  F). Similarly, Piezo1 si-RNA significantly decreased the spontaneous contraction frequency (1.02 ± 0.12 vs. 1.41 ± 0.31, P = 0.029, Fig.  G), contractile amplitudes (2.77 ± 0.49 vs. 6.43 ± 0.85, P < 0.001, Fig.  H), and time between contractions (30.25 ± 8.25 vs. 19.05 ± 6.21, P = 0.041, Fig.  I), but had no effect on contraction duration (P > 0.05, Fig.  J). The results suggested that both pharmacological inhibition and knockdown of Piezo1 significantly decreased the spontaneous uterine contraction in LPS-treated mice, suggesting a critical role for Piezo1 in regulating uterine myometrial contractility. 

## Inhibition of Piezo1 prolongs mouse delivery time after LPS treatment
In this study, we administered intrauterine LPS injections on gestational E15.5 to induce preterm delivery. The results indicated that LPS significantly shortened the pregnancy duration (P < 0.001, Fig.  A), with preterm delivery occurring within 6–12 h post-LPS administration (P < 0.5, Table  ). We then compared the preterm delivery rate within 12 h, preterm delivery rate before E18.5, and the duration of pregnancy following LPS treatment. As shown in Table  , 71.43% of the pregnant mice experienced pregnancy failure within 12 h of LPS injection on E15.5, with an average pregnancy duration of 11.2 ± 1.9 h. However, when Gsmtx4 was administrated intraperitoneally after LPS treatment, the preterm delivery rate within 12 h dropped from 83.33% to 0% (P < 0.05, Table  ), extending the pregnancy duration to 17.2 ± 3.2 h (P < 0.001, Fig.  B). We further examined whether intrauterine injection of the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 could induce preterm delivery. Intrauterine Yoda1 injection on E15.5 significantly shortened the pregnancy duration compared to vehicle treatment (18.0 ± 5.0 h vs. 91.8 ± 16.1 h, P < 0.001, Fig.  C). Although only 16.67% of the pregnant mice delivered within 12 h, all mice delivered before day 18.5 of gestation (P < 0.01, Table  ). Finally, we administered Piezo1 si-RNA via tail vein injection to knock down Piezo1 expression in pregnant mice. Western blot analysis confirmed that Piezo1-siRNA significantly reduced Piezo1 protein expression (P < 0.05, Fig.  D and E). This treatment significantly prolonged the pregnancy duration by six hours post-LPS (P < 0.001, Fig.  F) and reduced preterm delivery rate within 12 h (P < 0.01, Table  ) compared to control mice receiving scrambled si-RNA. These findings suggest that Piezo1 inhibition, either through pharmacological means or gene knockdown, prolongs pregnancy duration and decreases the rate of PTB following LPS treatment, whereas Piezo1 activation promotes preterm delivery. Therefore, Piezo1 appears to play a critical role in the development of premature birth. 

## Piezo1 activation increases cytosolic calcium levels of pUSMCs and amplifies the inflammatory cascade
To investigate Piezo1's role in regulating intracellular Ca2+ levels, pUSMCs were treated with Yoda1, LPS, LPS + Gsmtx4, or LPS + DMSO. LPS and Yoda1 treatments both significantly increased intracellular Ca2⁺ levels compared to the control group (P < 0.0001 and P < 0.01, respectively). This elevation was notably reduced by Gsmtx4 treatment (P < 0.01), suggesting Piezo1’s involvement in LPS- and Yoda1-induced Ca2⁺ influx (Fig.  A, B). Next, we detected the COX-2 protein level in pUSMCs stimulated with LPS with or without Gsmtx4. COX-2 protein levels were significantly elevated (2.99 ± 0.28-fold) after LPS treatment compared to the control (P < 0.0001), while Gsmtx4 markedly inhibited this upregulation (2.11 ± 0.19 vs. LPS 2.99 ± 0.28, P = 0.028) (Fig.  C). Additionally, the ELISA results showed that Gsmtx4 significantly reduced the LPS-induced increases in inflammatory cytokines TNF-α (1.45 ± 1.52 pg/ml vs. LPS 18.14 ± 9.13, P < 0.01), IL-1β (2.39 ± 1.19 pg/ml vs. LPS 5.69 ± 1.51, P < 0.01), and IL-6 (351.49 ± 24.91 pg/ml vs. LPS 550.32 ± 101.58 pg/ml, P < 0.01), (Fig.  D–F). These results suggest that Piezo1 may mediate premature birth by regulating intracellular Ca2+ levels and amplifying the inflammatory cascade (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
This study identified Piezo1 as the predominant mechanosensitive ion channel in the mouse myometrium, with elevated levels observed during both mouse and human pregnancy. LPS injection resulted in a significant increase in Piezo1 expression. Pharmacological blockade or knockdown of Piezo1 notably inhibited LPS-induced pUSMC contraction, spontaneous uterine myometrial contraction, and prolonged the pregnancy duration. Conversely, Yoda1 enhanced pUSMC contraction, reduced pregnancy duration, and accelerated the onset of labor. Additionally, Piezo1 was found to be associated with LPS-induced Ca2+ influx and inflammatory responses.
﻿ Studies have demonstrated that mechanical stretch can influence cell membrane potential and elevate intracellular calcium ion concentrations, thereby enhancing uterine smooth muscle contraction and increasing labor initiation and PTB rates [ , ]. Pregnant women with multiple pregnancies, macrosomia, or polyamniotic fluid are at a higher risk for PTB, partially due to uterine overdistension [ ]. Stretch-activated ion channels, including the Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) channels, the stretch-sensitive K + channels of the two-pore K +  (K2P) channel family, and the Piezo family, are responsive to mechanical tension [ , ]. Our study reveals that Piezo1 exhibits a predominant expression profile compared to other mechanosensitive ion channels, with a marked increase in uterine expression in both humans and mice during pregnancy. This finding contrasts with a previous study, which reported no change in Piezo1 expression between nonpregnant and term not-in-labor human myometrium [ ]. We acknowledge that species differences between humans and mice may account for the observed variations in Piezo1 expression. We hypothesize that Piezo1 plays a crucial role in mediating the response to mechanical tension in the uterus during pregnancy. However, the relationship between Piezo1 and the onset of labor remains to be determined.
Up to 40% of PTB have been associated with intrauterine inflammation [ ]. In our study, we used intrauterine injection of LPS as an animal model of infectious PTB. The data showed that LPS administration, both in vitro and in vivo, markedly promotes the expression of Piezo1 in uterine smooth muscle cells compared to the mice treated with normal saline. This contrasts with another study [ ], which found a decrease in Piezo1 expression in human myometrium tissue from preterm labor cases. This discrepancy may arise from differences in the models used: our study utilized an infection induced PTB model, whereas previous studies analyzed human preterm birth specimens, which are inherently more complex. LPS induces the production of inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IL-6, and IL-22 [ ]. Pro-inflammatory stimuli like ﻿TNF-α have been shown to regulate Piezo1 transcript levels in the endothelium, cultured BV2 cell, and primary cultured anterior cingulate cortex neuron [ , ]. Furthermore, uterine stretching can regulate TRPC expression and calcium signaling ﻿in human myometrial smooth muscle cells [ ]. Together, both ﻿the uterine stretch and inflammation may be involved in regulating Piezo1 expression. Notably, ﻿uterine overdistention or stretch stimulus also recruits immune cells from the uterine blood vessels to the myometrium and correlates with preterm labor [ , ]. ﻿Consequently, inflammation may be a more critical factor in regulating Piezo1 expression, the mechanism of which needs to be elucidated. When inflammation occurs in the uterus, uterine tension may not reach the level seen at term. Yet, the infection-induced upregulation of Piezo1 might still lead to increased uterine smooth muscle contractions, leading to PTB.
Uterine myometrium contraction is considered ﻿the final pathway to induce PTB. Our previous study identified multiple variables in spontaneous uterine myometrium contraction in our LPS-induced PTB mouse model [ ]. The current study demonstrated that intraperitoneal injection of Gsmtx4 or Piezo1 knockdown via si-RNA significantly affected LPS-increased uterine myometrium contraction in terms of frequency, contractile amplitudes, and time between contractions. In contrast, another study reported that spontaneous contractions in human myometrium were decreased following Yoda1 treatment [ ]. This discrepancy may arise from the differing administration methods and the nature of the tissues involved; we administered inhibitors in vivo to mice, whereas previous studies applied agonists to human tissues in vitro. LPS-induced pUSMC contraction was substantially reversed by Gsmtx4 or Piezo1 knockdown. Additionally, both Gsmtx4 administration and Piezo1 knockdown significantly prolonged pregnancy duration and reduced the onset of labor within 12 h of LPS administration. These findings suggest that Piezo1 activation is related to enhanced myometrial contraction and is responsible for PTB. Intriguing, Yoda1 induced pUSMC contraction, and intrauterine injection of Yoda1 significantly shortened pregnancy duration and increased the onset of labor within 12 h, similar to the effects of LPS injection. Although Piezo1 expression increases at the gestion of day 15.5, the ﻿mechanical force alone is insufficient to induce myometrial contraction. Therefore, additional stimulation of the Piezo1 channel with Yoda1 during this period might induce myometrial contraction, leading to PTB.
The Piezo1 channel is nonselective but preferential to Ca2+. Evidence indicates that the activation of Piezo1 in response to mechanical force induces Ca2+ influx, resulting in arterial remodeling, pulmonary hypertension, and gastrointestinal disorders [ , , ]. Our results demonstrated that activation of Piezo1 with Yoda1 increased intracellular calcium in pUSMCs, suggesting Piezo1's involvement in regulating Ca2+ influx in the uterine myometrium. Furthermore, we found that LPS-induced Ca2+ influx and pUSMC contraction were inhibited by Gsmtx4 administration, indicating that these effects are Piezo1-dependent. The amplification of the inflammatory cascade is widely recognized as a key factor contributing to preterm. A current study indicated that NCF1-driven oxidative proinflammatory cascades, along with the resulting pathological alterations in the myometrium, are key contributors to premature birth [ ]. Beyond its role in regulating intracellular calcium, Piezo1 can also trigger a robust and selective pro-inflammatory response, even in the absence of classical pathogen-associated molecular patterns [ ]. Piezo1 activation increases intracellular calcium concentration, promoting IL-6 and TNF-α levels in intestinal inflammation through NLRP3 activation [ ]. This study found that Gsmtx4 reversed LPS-induced expression of inflammatory factors, including cox-2, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, suggesting ﻿that Piezo1 might modulate myometrial inflammation. However, further studies are needed to clarify this mechanism.﻿ Collectively, the upregulated Piezo1 participants in preterm birth might mediate Ca2+ influx and inflammatory factors expression.
Our current study has several limitations. First, there was a statistically significant age difference between the pregnant and non-pregnant women from whom we collected samples. These variations may be associated with differences in factors such as hormone levels, potentially affecting the results. In future research, we will explore the expression of Piezo1 across various age groups and stages of pregnancy to determine its relationship with the initiation of labor. Additionally, we aim to validate and refine our animal experiment results using human tissue samples. Second, GsMTx4 primarily targets mechanosensitive ion channels and lacks specificity for Piezo channels. Therefore, further studies employing more selective inhibitors or genetic approaches, as well as varying routes of administration, will be necessary to precisely delineate the roles of individual mechanosensitive channels in our experimental model. Third, we have not yet conducted an in-depth investigation into the specific mechanisms by which Piezo1 regulates uterine smooth muscle contraction and its increased expression under inflammatory conditions. In our future studies, we will investigate the mechanisms of Piezo1's role in regulating uterine smooth muscle contraction, utilizing techniques such as electrophysiology, mechanical stretch stimulation, and live dynamic imaging.﻿
In conclusion, increased expression of Piezo1 induced by LPS administration results in enhanced myometrial contraction, contributing to PTB in mouse model. Therefore, targeting Piezo1 may be a novel treatment strategy for PTB. However, the specific signaling pathways by which Piezo1 participates in myometrial contraction and the upstream regulating Piezo1 expression are unclear, and the mechanisms remain to be determined with certainty.
