Currently, no approved treatments are available for sepsis-associated muscle wasting. In this study, we investigated the effects of C188-9, a STAT3-specific signaling inhibitor, on sepsis-induced muscle wasting in vivo and myotube atrophy in vitro. In wild-type male C57BL/6mice, sepsis induced by cecal slurry injection elevated interleukin-6 levels in both plasma and tibialis anterior muscles, increased phosphorylated STAT3, and activated the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy pathways in a severity-dependent manner. Similar dose-dependent responses were observed in lipopolysaccharide-treated C2C12 myotubes. In septic mice, intraperitoneal injection of C188-9 (50 mg/kg) suppressed activation of the STAT3 pathway and the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation pathway—but not autophagy pathways—and alleviated sepsis-associated skeletal muscle wasting. Likewise, pretreatment of C2C12 myotubes with C188-9 (10 µM) mitigated activation of the same inflammatory and proteolytic pathways and ameliorated myotube atrophy induced by lipopolysaccharide administration. In human sepsis patients, plasma interleukin-6 levels at intensive care unit admission positively correlated with sepsis severity and the degree of skeletal muscle wasting. Collectively, our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting STAT3 signaling through pharmacological inhibition to combat sepsis-associated skeletal muscle wasting.

## Introduction
Sepsis, a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated immune response to infection , is a significant global public health concern. The recent estimates suggest that the global incidence of sepsis is 48.9 million cases per year, leading to approximately 11.0 million deaths per annum and accounting for roughly one in five deaths worldwide. In the United States, healthcare costs associated with sepsis exceeded $38 billion in 2017, making sepsis one of the most expensive causes of hospitalization . As such, sepsis is a critical health concern, placing a substantial economic burden on society and warranting increased public attention.
Profound loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength, termed sepsis-associated muscle wasting (SAMW), is common in patients with sepsis . SAMW leads to general prostration and debilitation, negatively impacting survival and functional recovery. For example, SAMW is a strong independent factor of prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays, short-term and long-term mortality, physical disability, and poor quality of life – . Thus, there is an urgent need to advance our understanding of SAMW and develop therapeutic interventions because no pharmacological treatments are available in clinical settings.
Overwhelming and persistent systemic inflammatory responses to infectious insults are key pathological features of sepsis. SAMW has been primarily attributed to hypermetabolism and increased protein breakdown driven by proinflammatory cytokines. Among these cytokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6) serves as a key marker of sepsis , . Elevated circulating IL-6 levels and skeletal muscle loss are also observed in other medical conditions, such as COVID-19 , end-stage kidney disease , and cancer . These findings collectively suggest a link between IL-6 signaling and skeletal muscle atrophy. The Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway serves as a key downstream mediator of IL-6 signaling . Previous research indicates that STAT3 activation contributes to skeletal muscle atrophy through upregulation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system, including the muscle-specific ubiquitin E3 ligases . Moreover, a recent study found that mice with knockout of the IL-6 family signal transducer gene il6st were resistant to SAMW induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) . Another recent study proposed that the antioxidative activity of anisodamine reduced JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation and ameliorated sepsis-induced diaphragm atrophy in mice . These findings collectively suggest that inhibiting STAT3 signaling could help mitigate SAMW. However, data are scarce regarding the effects of pharmacological STAT3 inhibition on skeletal muscle wasting in the context of infectious insults.
On the basis of these prior findings, we postulated that pharmacological STAT3 inhibition would ameliorate SAMW in mice and cultured cells. Additionally, we conducted a clinical observational examination of the correlation between plasma IL-6 levels at ICU admission and SAMW in patients with sepsis. Our in vivo and in vitro experiments and clinical observations provide evidence supporting our hypothesis and indicate that further exploration of therapeutic STAT3 inhibition for SAMW is warranted.

## Results

## Validation of the cecal slurry (CS)-induced sepsis model
Sepsis induces skeletal muscle wasting in animals and humans , , , but the impact of its severity on the extent of wasting has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, we created model mice with two different severities of sepsis. The validity of the sepsis model induced by CS injection was assessed using blood samples and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) extracts from tibialis anterior (TA) muscle, liver, and spleen. Samples from CS-injected mice exhibited dose-dependent bacterial colony growth on sheep blood agar plates, whereas no bacterial colonies were detected in any of the samples collected from vehicle-injected mice (Fig. a). These results indicate that the CS-derived bacteria can reach the TA muscle as well as circulating blood and the reticuloendothelial system. Additionally, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing of bacteria in the blood, TA muscle, liver, and spleen samples at 24 h post CS administration revealed Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria as the dominant microbial phyla, mirroring the primary bacterial components of the CS injection itself (Fig. b).

 

Data regarding the microbial α-diversity within samples and β-diversity across samples are shown in Supplementary Fig. . Microbial diversity in the TA muscle was comparable to that in the CS and blood, but the similarity of microbial composition among the samples was relatively low. Collectively, these results indicate that CS injection successfully induced polymicrobial sepsis, leading to bacterial penetration of the TA muscles and reticuloendothelial system, with roughly half of the isolates identified as Gram-negative.

## Sepsis induces lethargy and skeletal muscle wasting in a severity-dependent manner in mice
Survival analysis revealed no mortality within 72 h following injection of vehicle or a 0.5 mg/g body weight (BW) dose of CS, whereas 1.0 mg/g CS induced approximately 40% mortality (Fig. c), in line with the mortality rate observed in human patients with septic shock , . In surviving mice, sepsis caused severity-dependent loss of appetite (Fig. d) and TA muscle weight (Fig. e), as well as reduced grip strength (Fig. f). As shown in Fig. g, mice lost nearly 15% of their baseline BW 1 day after receiving a 0.5 mg/g or 1.0 mg/g CS injection. While the BW of mice that received a 0.5 mg/g CS injection gradually recovered to baseline within 3 days, BW loss persisted in mice that received a 1.0 mg/g CS injection. Next, we evaluated the severity of sepsis in CS-injected mice via hypothermia, a recognized indicator of sepsis severity in both rodents and humans . Body surface temperature significantly decreased 1 day after CS injection at 0.5 or 1.0 mg/g (Fig. h). In mice that received a 0.5 mg/g injection, body surface temperature gradually recovered, whereas in mice that received a 1.0 mg/g injection, it remained low until 3 days post-treatment (Fig. h). Similarly, CS injection dose-dependently increased the murine sepsis score (Fig. i). Histological assessment of TA muscle sections using immunofluorescence staining showed that dose-dependent shrinkage of skeletal muscle fibers also occurred in response to CS injection (Figs. j– l). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CS injection induced severe sepsis in a dose-dependent manner, with increased severity associated with greater lethargy and skeletal muscle wasting in mice.

## Activation of IL-6/STAT3 signaling and the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation and autophagy pathways in skeletal muscle during sepsis in mice
To clarify the molecular pathway responsible for skeletal muscle atrophy during sepsis, we performed next-generation RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) of TA muscles isolated from CS (1.0 mg/g) and vehicle-injected mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering of the sham and sepsis groups, indicating significant differences in their gene expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. ).
Volcano plot analysis identified 4,252 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the sepsis and sham groups (Fig. a and Supplementary Data ). Examples of highly expressed DEGs in the sepsis group included Mt2/Mt1, Lcn2, and Saa1/Saa3, all of which are involved in cellular stress, inflammation, and immunological responses. By contrast, highly expressed DEGs in the Sham group included Ky, 6430571L13Rik, Sec14l5, and Ped4a, which are involved in intracellular signal transduction and the regulation of cellular functions. At 24 h post CS injection, TA muscles showed significantly increased expression of Il6, Il6ra, and Stat3, minor changes in three other IL-6 family members (Lifr, Il12rb, and Cntf), and upregulation of Trim63, Foxo1, Fbox32, Socs3, and Cebpd, which are ubiquitin–proteasome pathway genes downstream of IL-6 signaling (Fig. b). These findings align with RNA-seq data from mice with CLP-induced sepsis .

 

Next, we focused on molecular pathways associated with skeletal muscle wasting. Heat map analysis revealed that genes related to IL-6/STAT3 signaling and the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems were upregulated, whereas genes associated with myogenic differentiation, the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, and apoptosis remained relatively unchanged in septic mice (Fig.  c). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis showed that pathways involved in rRNA processing, ribosome synthesis, inflammatory cytokine-related immune responses, cellular stress responses, signal transduction, and cell cycle regulation were activated in the sepsis group (Fig.  d and e, and Supplementary Data ). The Reactome enrichment map showed two distinct clusters: one involving inflammatory cytokine-related immune responses, cellular stress responses, signal transduction, and cell cycle regulation, and the other centered on ribosomal synthesis and rRNA processing (Fig.  e). Additionally, enrichment analysis based on biological processes in Gene Ontology (GO) terms revealed significant enrichment of genes associated with signal transduction, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, response to cytokines, and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways at 24 h post sepsis induction (Fig.  f and Supplementary Data ). Among cytokine stimuli, IL-6 signaling was the most pronounced (Supplementary Data ). In summary, these data indicate that IL-6 signaling is activated in the TA muscle of mice with sepsis, with the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems emerging as the most prominently activated pathways associated with skeletal muscle wasting.

## Sepsis activates the STAT3 pathway and the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems through a severity-dependent mechanism in mice
A sustained systemic inflammatory response is a distinct characteristic of both sepsis and SAMW. To validate the RNA-seq results, we measured cytokine levels in plasma and TA muscles from septic mice using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and quantitative real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), respectively. Sepsis severity-dependently elevated plasma concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. a) and mRNA expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in the TA muscles (Fig. b). Next, we assessed the activation of the STAT3 pathway, a crucial downstream mediator of IL-6 inflammatory signaling. As expected, sepsis severity-dependently increased the phosphorylated (P)-STAT3/STAT3 protein ratio in TA muscles (Fig. c). These results indicate that sepsis causes systematic inflammation and activates the IL-6/STAT3 axis in TA muscle in a severity-dependent fashion. We then examined the expression of two major ubiquitin ligases, MuRF1 and atrogin-1/MAFbx, which are predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle and play an essential role in the muscle proteolytic pathway , . Consistent with the CLP-induced sepsis model , , CS injection dose-dependently increased MuRF1 and atrogin-1/MAFbx expression at both the transcriptional (Fig. d) and post-transcriptional levels (Fig. e). However, CS injection did not elevate the overall protein ubiquitination levels (Supplementary Fig. ).

 

Disruptions in cell growth signals represent another key factor in skeletal muscle atrophy, with the mTOR signaling cascade playing a pivotal role in promoting protein synthesis through Akt-mediated p70 S6 kinase (p70 S6K) activation . However, conflicting data on the effect of sepsis on the mTOR pathway in skeletal muscle have been reported , , . To examine the impact of sepsis severity on the PI3K/Akt pathway in mouse skeletal muscle, we analyzed the phosphorylation levels of p70 S6K at Thr389 and Akt at Ser473. Our findings showed minimal alteration of this pathway in TA muscles at 72 h post sepsis induction (Fig. f).
The pro-apoptotic and apoptosis signaling pathways also play pivotal roles in skeletal muscle proteolysis and have been linked to multiple catabolic conditions associated with muscle atrophy, including sepsis , . Evaluation of pro-apoptotic and apoptosis-related signals in septic mice showed that the levels of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, pro-apoptotic protein Bax, and apoptotic protein caspase-3 were not altered in TA muscles at 72 h post sepsis induction. This immunoblot analysis did not detect cleaved caspase-3 protein, the active form of caspase-3 (Fig. g).
Next, we investigated the effect of sepsis on the autophagy pathway, which contributes to skeletal muscle protein degradation , . Previous studies showed that CLP-induced sepsis and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory responses trigger the activation of autophagy and skeletal muscle atrophy , . Thus, we examined the expression of LC3B-II, an indicator of autophagosome formation , , in our sepsis model. Consistent with previous observations , , sepsis resulted in a significant increase in the LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio in TA muscles (Fig. h). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that LC3B puncta formation increased following sepsis induction (Fig. i). In an ex vivo autophagic flux assay, lysosomal inhibition using leupeptin plus NH3Cl further increased LC3B-II protein levels in septic mice, indicating that autophagy flux in skeletal muscles is heightened upon sepsis induction (Fig. j). Collectively, these results suggest that CS injection dose-dependently increases autophagosome formation in TA muscles.
In summary, our observations thus far indicate that sepsis activates the STAT3 pathway, ubiquitin–proteasome system, and autophagy system in a severity-dependent manner in vivo, with cell growth and apoptosis signals remaining relatively unchanged, consistent with the RNA-seq findings.

## LPS directly activates the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems to induce C2C12 myotube atrophy in vitro
Given that nearly 50% of bacteria detected in the TA muscle following CS injection were Gram-negative (Fig. a and b), and that skeletal muscle expresses Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in various species such as Homo sapiens and Mus musculus , , we hypothesized that these bacteria directly activate inflammation and damage skeletal muscle independent of immune cell mediation. To test this, C2C12 myotubes were incubated for 24–48 h with or without LPS, a key cellular component of Gram-negative bacteria. In preliminary experiments, we confirmed the abundant expression of TLR4 protein in mouse TA muscles and C2C12 myotubes, regardless of CS injection or LPS administration (Supplementary Fig. ). Aligned with our findings in septic mice, immunofluorescence staining of myotubes using a myosin heavy chain (MyHC)-specific antibody and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) revealed that LPS exposure reduced myotube diameter (Figs. a –c) and lowered the fusion index (Fig. d) in a dose-dependent manner. Immunoblotting confirmed that LPS suppressed MyHC protein expression (Fig. e). Collectively, these results indicate that LPS directly induces atrophy of C2C12 myotubes. Aligned with in vivo responses, in vitro LPS administration increased mRNA expression of IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. f), as well as the active form of STAT3 protein (Fig. g) and key ubiquitin ligase proteins (Fig. h), in a dose-dependent fashion. As both IL-6 and TNF-α are myokines that are directly produced by skeletal muscle cells, we next measured their concentrations in the culture medium 4 h after LPS administration. Consistent with the qRT-PCR results, LPS increased IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the culture supernatant in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. ). Collectively, these findings suggest that LPS directly induces IL-6/STAT3 pathway activation and protein catabolism in myotubes in vitro independent of immune system involvement.

 

Consistent with in vivo findings, LPS did not alter the total protein ubiquitination levels (Supplementary Fig. ), Akt and S6K phosphorylation levels (Fig.  i), nor the expression of pro-apoptotic and apoptosis-related proteins (Fig.  j), compared with PBS-treated C2C12 myotubes. Similarly, western blot analysis showed that LPS increased the LC3B-II/I ratio (Fig.  k), and immunofluorescence staining revealed that it elevated LC3B puncta (Fig.  l). Culturing C2C12 myotubes with the lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 for 2 h further increased LC3B-II protein levels in LPS-treated cells (Fig.  m). Together, these results indicate that LPS dose-dependently increases autophagosome formation in C2C12 myotubes.
In summary, similar to results in septic mice, LPS appears to activate the IL-6/STAT3 pathway, ubiquitin–proteasome system, and autophagy in a dose-dependent manner in vitro, with cell growth and pro-apoptotic and apoptosis signals remaining unchanged.

## C188-9 ameliorates SAMW in mice
Inspired to explore the effect of STAT3 signaling suppression on SAMW, we subjected male C57BL/6 mice to intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of vehicle or C188-9 (50 mg/kg), a STAT3-specific signaling inhibitor, at 1 h post sepsis induction. Sham-sepsis mice were injected with i.p. C188-9 or vehicle as controls. Although C188-9 treatment did not affect acute-phase mortality (Fig.  a) or appetite loss (Fig.  b) following sepsis induction, it significantly reversed sepsis-induced TA muscle atrophy (Fig.  c) and grip strength reduction (Fig.  d) compared with vehicle-treated septic mice. Similarly, while C188-9 did not alleviate body weight loss (Fig.  e) or sepsis-induced hypothermia (Fig.  f), it significantly reduced the murine sepsis score at both 1- and 3-days post sepsis induction (Fig.  g). Immunofluorescence staining of TA muscle samples revealed that C188-9 rescued sepsis-induced muscle fiber shrinkage compared with vehicle (Fig.  h– j). Collectively, these data suggest that pharmacological suppression of the STAT3 pathway mitigates SAMW both histologically and functionally.

 

## C188-9 inhibits skeletal muscle STAT3 and proteolytic signal activation in septic mice
Next, we aimed to evaluate the effect of STAT3 signaling inhibition on the cytokine storm triggered by CS injection. Given that STAT3 acts as a downstream effector in IL-6 signaling, its C188-9–mediated suppression may not influence the secretion of IL-6 or other inflammatory cytokines in response to infectious challenges. As predicted, C188-9 administration did not alter circulating levels of IL-6 or TNF-α (Fig.  a), or the mRNA expression levels of these cytokines in TA muscles (Fig.  b) at 24 h post CS injection. Immunoblot analysis revealed that C188-9 effectively suppressed sepsis-induced activation of STAT3 signaling (Fig.  c) along with its downstream targets, MuRF1 and atrogin-1/MAFbx, at the protein level in TA muscles (Fig.  d). Total protein ubiquitination levels were not altered in sham or septic mice treated with C188-9 (Supplementary Fig. ). Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining of TA muscle sections showed that C188-9 treatment in septic mice did not affect the LC3B-II/I ratio (Fig.  e) or LC3B puncta (Fig.  f), respectively. These findings suggest a limited influence of C188-9 on the autophagy pathway in TA muscles of septic mice. Collectively, these observations indicate that the protective effect of C188-9 against SAMW is primarily linked to the modulation of proteolytic pathways rather than autophagy pathways.

 

## C188-9 mitigates LPS-induced C2C12 myotube atrophy by reducing STAT3 and ubiquitin–proteasome pathway activation
To further investigate the C188-9 findings in vitro, C2C12 myotubes were pretreated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or C188-9 (10 µM), followed by LPS (1 µg/mL) 1 h later, as previously described – . Immunofluorescence staining using a MyHC-specific antibody and DAPI demonstrated that LPS administration reduced both the diameter and abundance of mature myotubes, effects that were reversed by C188-9 pretreatment (Figs. a – d). Similarly, MyHC protein expression was also diminished in response to LPS, but was partially restored following C188-9 pretreatment (Fig. e). These findings suggest that pharmacologically inhibiting STAT3 mitigates muscle-specific protein loss and LPS-induced myotube atrophy. In agreement with in vivo observations, C188-9 pretreatment did not influence the elevations in TNF-α and IL-6 mRNA expression observed in LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes (Fig. f). Western blot analysis confirmed that C188-9 significantly inhibited STAT3 activation (Fig. g) and reduced the expression of two ubiquitin ligase proteins (Fig. h) triggered by LPS exposure. However, C188-9 treatment did not alter the total protein ubiquitination levels (Supplementary Fig. ), LC3B-II/I ratio (Fig. i) or increase in LC3B puncta (Fig. j) in LPS-treated cells, as observed through western blotting and immunofluorescence staining, respectively. Taken together, these results indicate that STAT3 inhibition prevents LPS-induced myotube atrophy primarily through suppression of the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, rather than the autophagy pathway, aligning with the findings in C188-9–treated septic mice.

 

## Correlations among plasma IL-6 levels and severity of sepsis and SAMW in patients at ICU admission
To assess the clinical relevance of our experimental findings, we conducted a prospective observational study in the ICU at Kobe University Hospital to investigate the associations among proinflammatory cytokine levels, sepsis severity, and SAMW in patients. A flowchart of the study participants is shown in Supplementary Fig. .
Among the 86 patients with sepsis who were admitted to the ICU within the study period, 67 patients ultimately underwent cytokine assessment. Their clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table ; the median age was 72 years, and 65.7% were male. Regarding sepsis severity, the median sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score at admission was 7, and about one-fourth of patients were classified as having septic shock. The in-hospital mortality rate was 17.9%, while the 2-year post-sepsis mortality rate was 49.2% (Supplementary Table ). Patients with septic shock at ICU admission had significantly higher plasma levels of IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. a and Supplementary Fig. a). While plasma IL-6 levels positively correlated with SOFA scores and levels of lactate, creatine kinase (CK), C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin at ICU admission, TNF-α levels did not show such correlations (Figs. b –f and Supplementary Figs. b–f). These results suggest that IL-6 levels are associated with sepsis severity, as well as various markers of inflammation and skeletal muscle injury, aligning with the findings of multiple clinical studies examining the association between IL-6 and sepsis severity in patients , .

 

Next, we evaluated the relationship between proinflammatory cytokine levels and SAMW in a subset of 45 patients who underwent computed tomography (CT) scans both at ICU admission and 1 to 3 weeks post-admission (Supplementary Fig. ). In this subset, IL-6 levels at ICU admission were significantly correlated with the percentage decrease in the psoas muscle index (PMI) measured at the third lumbar vertebral level, whereas TNF-α levels were not (Fig.  g – i and Supplementary Fig. g). These findings suggest that plasma IL-6 levels, but not TNF-α levels, are associated with the severity of SAMW in human patients. Overall, the clinical study findings have been consistent with experimental results in mice and C2C12 cells.
To further investigate how SAMW influenced the long-term outcomes in our cohort, we dichotomized patients according to the degree of PMI reduction. The high PMI reduction group was defined as patients in the upper quartile of PMI reduction (PMI reduction > 19.2%), while the low PMI reduction group included patients in the remaining quartiles (PMI reduction ≤ 19.2%). Of the 45 patients, 12 were classified into the high PMI reduction group and 33 into the low PMI reduction group. As shown in Fig. j, the cumulative 2-year survival rate was significantly lower in the high PMI reduction cohort than in the low PMI reduction cohort. These results suggest that SAMW is associated with increased long-term mortality, which is consistent with the results of previous studies – .

## Discussion
Despite its high morbidity and severe consequences, critical questions regarding optimal management of sepsis remain unanswered. One such challenge is the prevention and treatment of SAMW, which worsens survival rates, functional outcomes, and overall quality of life – . Consistent with these previous findings, we found that a high degree of PMI reduction was associated with a decreased 2-year survival rate in our patient cohort with sepsis. Addressing SAMW is therefore imperative to improving outcomes in affected patients. Our in vivo experimental results in mice demonstrated that sepsis induces both systemic and local inflammation, activating the STAT3 pathway in skeletal muscle in a severity-dependent manner. Once this pathway is stimulated, the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems are also upregulated, leading to skeletal muscle atrophy and weakness. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that administration of C188-9, a STAT3 signaling inhibitor, suppressed the activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation pathway, but not autophagy pathways, and alleviated SAMW in mice. Treatment of C2C12 myotubes with C188-9 similarly inhibited the activation of the same pathways, restoring LPS-induced atrophy. In patients with sepsis, plasma IL-6 levels positively correlated with both the severity of sepsis and the extent of SAMW. Collectively, our findings indicate that blocking the IL-6/STAT3 axis mitigates skeletal muscle atrophy, highlighting a promising therapeutic strategy for SAMW.
Increased STAT3 activity is linked to enhanced skeletal muscle catabolism and proteolysis, a key feature of SAMW. We found that sepsis severity-dependently activated the IL-6/STAT3, ubiquitin–proteasome degradation, and autophagy pathways, causing skeletal muscle atrophy and weakness in mice. These findings were consistent across various assays (e.g., RNA-seq, qRT-PCR, and western blotting) and in vitro experiments using LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes. Suppression of the IL-6/STAT3 axis by C188-9 diminished the activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system, but not the autophagy system, and ameliorated SAMW in mice. Similarly, pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 signaling by C188-9 in vitro mitigated activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system, but had minimal impact on the autophagy system, resulting in restoration of LPS-induced myotube atrophy. Thus, ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated proteolysis in skeletal muscle under septic conditions appears to be primarily regulated by STAT3 signaling. A previous study demonstrated that Foxo1 and Foxo3, well-known downstream effectors regulated by STAT3 signaling, drive transcription at the atrogin-1 gene promoter and cause skeletal muscle atrophy . Earlier studies suggested that P-STAT3 increases expression of the CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein δ, which binds to the MuRF-1 and atrogin-1/MAFbx promoter regions , . Consistently, this study found that STAT3 inhibition mitigated the increases in atrogin-1/MAFbx and MuRF-1 protein expression both in TA muscles of septic mice and in LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes.
Given that MuRF1 and atrogin-1/MAFbx are muscle-specific ubiquitin ligases, one might expect sepsis or C188-9 treatment to affect the total ubiquitination levels. However, total protein ubiquitination was not significantly affected in CS-injected mice or in LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes, regardless of whether C188-9 treatment was applied, within the timeframe examined. In addition to targeting proteins for degradation, ubiquitination regulates other cellular processes, such as signaling, endocytosis, and cell cycle control . Therefore, increased total protein ubiquitination does not necessarily indicate enhanced protein degradation. Sepsis or C188-9 treatment did not alter the total protein ubiquitination levels because of these multifaceted roles.
As a reservoir for amino acids, skeletal muscles may be more susceptible to sepsis-induced excessive inflammation than other tissues, such as adipose tissue. This is supported by the observation that C188-9-mediated STAT3 inhibition did not alleviate body weight or appetite loss, but significantly reversed TA muscle loss and shrinkage, as well as grip strength reduction. Therefore, the reduction in proteolytic pathway activity following C188-9 administration may exert a more pronounced effect on skeletal muscles than on other organs. Thus, STAT3 suppression may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for SAMW.
Consistent with our study, pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of STAT3 has been successful in treating skeletal muscle atrophy induced by chronic renal failure , cancers , and immobilization , primarily through reduced expression of atrogin-1/MAFbx and MuRF-1. These chronic conditions are all characterized by mild elevations in plasma proinflammatory cytokine levels. For example, in a Lewis lung carcinoma-induced cachexia model, the plasma IL-6 concentration was approximately 40 pg/mL . We observed IL-6 levels more than 350 times higher in septic mice injected with 1.0 mg/g of CS. Our data extend prior findings by demonstrating that pharmacological STAT3 inhibition can reverse skeletal muscle atrophy in acute conditions marked by cytokine storms with elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels.
In both our CS-induced sepsis and LPS-induced C2C12 myotube models, STAT3 inhibition minimally affected the autophagy pathways. There are several plausible explanations for these findings. First, in addition to the STAT3 signaling pathway, autophagy in skeletal muscle is also regulated by Foxo1/Foxo3 and the TLR4/NF-κB signaling cascade , which are less likely to be suppressed by C188-9 treatment. Therefore, autophagy may be activated by such inputs independent of STAT3 inhibition. Similarly, autophagy pathways in skeletal muscles are strongly activated by fasting or calorie restriction. We observed that C188-9 administration did not affect CS-induced appetite loss in mice, suggesting that autophagy triggered by underfeeding occurred independently of C188-9 treatment. Furthermore, STAT3 fine-tunes autophagy through the transcriptional regulation of various autophagy-related genes , including both negative (e.g., BCL2 family members, CTSB, CTSL, PIK3R1) and positive regulators (e.g., PIK3C3, HIF1A, BNIP3). Whether STAT3 signaling inhibits or stimulates autophagy depends on the cellular context, timing, and nature of the stimuli. We speculate that pharmacological STAT3 inhibition by C188-9 did not disrupt the overall balance in our sepsis and myotube models because of the interplay between these factors. In a recent study, muscle-specific ablation of the autophagy gene Atg7 significantly worsened sepsis-induced muscle dysfunction and reduced survival, possibly because of accumulated mitochondrial damage . Given the multiple roles of autophagy in microbial clearance, neutralization of microbial toxins, maintenance of mitochondrial integrity, and regulation of cytokine release, suppression of this process is likely to lead to poor outcomes in sepsis . Therefore, C188-9-mediated STAT3 suppression may represent a viable treatment for SAMW with a minimal impact on autophagy-related signaling.
Using colony-forming assays and 16 S rRNA sequencing, we observed bacterial infiltration into TA muscles 1 day following CS injection, with about half of the identified bacteria being Gram-negative. These findings align with a previous study demonstrating prompt infiltration of circulating LPS into peripheral tissues, including the extremities . We confirmed that TLR4 protein is abundantly expressed in TA muscles and C2C12 myotubes, regardless of CS injection or LPS administration. Furthermore, our in vitro observations indicate that LPS directly engages TLR4 on muscle cells, triggering the IL-6/STAT3 and catabolic pathways, including the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy systems in myotubes. Our in vitro experimental results are consistent with earlier reports , . Collectively, these results imply that circulating pathogen-associated molecules can induce skeletal muscle atrophy independently of immune system involvement. Under septic conditions, systemic inflammatory responses and TLR4-mediated recognition of pathogen-associated molecules by muscle cells may synergize to promote skeletal muscle wasting. Notably, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that pharmacological suppression of the STAT3 axis can also alleviate C2C12 myotube atrophy directly induced by LPS.
We found that the pro-apoptotic, apoptotic, and mTOR signaling pathways remained relatively unchanged in LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes and septic mice at 24- and 72-hours post CS injection. Given the close interaction between apoptosis and mTOR signaling , this result seems reasonable; however, previous research on apoptosis and growth signaling in skeletal muscles during sepsis has yielded conflicting findings , – , . For example, in murine models of endotoxic shock, caspase-3 activation and skeletal muscle atrophy have been observed in the diaphragm at 24 h post-LPS administration . These discrepancies are likely influenced by multiple factors, including variations in the sepsis models, animal species, skeletal muscle types, experimental time points, data measurement techniques, and combinations of these elements. We also recognize that C188-9 is commonly used to induce apoptosis in various experimental cancer models, including acute myeloid leukemia and breast cancer cell lines , . However, in this study, intraperitoneal injection of C188-9 (50 mg/kg) did not induce apoptosis in TA muscles in vivo. Similarly, administration of C188-9 (10 µM) did not induce apoptosis in C2C12 myotubes in vitro. The reasons for these discrepancies are likely multifactorial; for example, differences between cancer and normal cells, skeletal muscle and other organs, experimental design, data measurement techniques, and interactions among these factors. Further research is needed to investigate the interplay between pro-apoptotic, apoptotic, and growth signals in skeletal muscles during sepsis, as well as the detailed effects of C188-9 under both healthy and septic conditions.
One might question why sepsis induced by a 0.5 mg/g BW CS injection caused TA muscle loss and shrinkage, grip strength reduction, and loss of body weight and appetite, while P-STAT3 levels remained relatively unchanged. Although P-STAT3 levels were relatively unchanged following the 0.5 mg/g BW CS injection, Atrogin1/MAFbx and MuRF1 levels were approximately five-fold higher compared to the vehicle control groups. Upregulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system likely contributed to TA muscle atrophy, reduced grip strength, and body weight loss. In addition to the STAT3 signaling pathway, other mechanisms may have contributed to the increased activity of the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Notable examples include NFκB signaling pathways . Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that NFκB signaling pathways were significantly activated in TA muscle following CS injection. Alongside the lethargy induced by the CS injection, these factors may help explain the observed findings.
Since STAT3 is a ubiquitous molecule involved in numerous biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, it is also reasonable to infer that STAT3 inhibition by C188-9 could lead to unexpected adverse events. Certainly, off-target effects could not be ruled out entirely in this study. However, we did not observe any severe adverse events following C188-9 administration in either the in vivo mouse model or the C2C12 cells in vitro. Similarly, previous studies using C188-9 have reported no severe adverse events. Importantly, IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling inhibitors are generally well tolerated in clinical trials involving patients with cancer , . Furthermore, suppression of both membrane-bound and soluble IL-6 receptors using monoclonal antibodies (e.g., sarilumab and tocilizumab) has also been shown to diminish mortality in critically ill patients with COVID-19 , . Given their favorable tolerability and efficacy in patients with cancer and those with COVID-19, IL-6/STAT3 signaling inhibitors appear to be feasible candidates for clinical investigation in sepsis, although their effects on survival outcomes or SAMW remain largely unexplored in real-world settings. Taken together with our results, these findings provide a rationale for testing the impacts of STAT3 inhibitors on SAMW and survival in future clinical trials.
This study has four major limitations. First, our experiments did not assess the long-term functional or survival outcomes of septic mice following C188-9 administration. Therefore, we cannot determine whether mitigating skeletal muscle atrophy leads to improved overall outcomes. To replicate the mortality rate of patients with septic shock in modern ICUs , , we administered mice with CS at 1.0 mg/g BW. Consequently, most septic mice became lethargic and anorexic, losing ~ 15% of body weight within 3 days of CS injection. Given these conditions, long-term survival studies were deemed ethically inappropriate by us and our Animal Care and Use Committees. Previous studies utilizing severe sepsis models have also generally avoided long-term outcome evaluations , , , likely for similar ethical concerns. Prior research suggests that reversing skeletal muscle atrophy may improve survival in animal models of muscle wasting induced by chronic conditions such as cancer and end-stage kidney disease .
Second, although sepsis disproportionately affects older adults , this study used only relatively young male mice. Similarly, our animal experiments did not include female mice. Inter-individual variability generally increases with age, and employing aged mice would have necessitated a larger number of experimental animals. Similarly, sex differences can affect the quality of experimental comparisons. In adherence to the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), we limited experiments to young male mice. Previous sepsis research using animals has mostly employed young male mice , , likely for the same reasons. The effects of sex and aging on SAMW are important and relevant research topics, and future studies should address these variables.
Third, although muscle biopsy was desirable, it was not performed in patients with sepsis because of the risk of complications (e.g., hematoma and nerve injury), particularly in critically ill patients. In the Japanese clinical setting, performing invasive procedures on patients not directly linked to treatment presents significant challenges. However, previous studies that obtained muscle biopsies from patients with sepsis reported increased ubiquitin–proteasome activity , consistent with our experimental findings.
Fourth, although pharmacological STAT3 inhibition ameliorated SAMW in mice and LPS-induced C2C12 myotube atrophy, target specificity was not confirmed through STAT3 depletion. To strengthen the mechanistic insights obtained in this study, future studies should employ either a mouse model with conditional deletion of STAT3 specifically in skeletal muscle cells, or a C2C12 cell model with STAT3 knockdown using siRNA.
Despite these limitations, in addition to the in vivo mouse model of sepsis, we provide data from C2C12 myotubes in vitro and a clinical observational study. Our in vitro findings complement the in vivo mouse data by demonstrating that circulating pathogen-associated molecules can directly induce skeletal muscle atrophy, independent of the immune system. This real-world observational study further reinforces the clinical relevance of our in vivo and in vitro experimental results. We believe that this multifaceted approach is the key strength of our study.
In conclusion, using an in vivo mouse model of sepsis, we demonstrated that the STAT 3-specific inhibitor C188-9 suppresses the activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation system with minimal impact on autophagy pathways. This intervention successfully mitigated sepsis-induced skeletal muscle atrophy and weakness, consistent with in vitro results showing similar protective effects in LPS-treated C2C12 myotubes. Furthermore, in a cohort of sepsis patients receiving critical care in a Japanese ICU, plasma IL-6 levels positively correlated with both sepsis severity and the extent of SAMW. Taken together, these findings offer valuable insights that may guide the development of novel therapeutic strategies to counteract SAMW.

## Methods

## Animals
Wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (age: 8–10 weeks; BW 22–26 g) were acquired from The Jackson Laboratory Japan (Yokohama, Japan). The mice were kept under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light–dark cycle (6 AM to 6 PM) and ad libitum access to regular chow (Cat# 1314, Altromin, Lage, Germany) and tap water. Food consumption and BW were recorded daily throughout the duration of the experiment. To ensure accurate individual food intake measurements, mice were single housed. Body surface temperature (°C) of each mouse was measured daily between 9 and 11 AM using a FLIR E4 WiFi thermal camera (FLIR, Wilsonville, OR, USA). Mice were euthanized by decapitation under 3% isoflurane (Cat# 099-06571, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan) inhalation, and the TA muscles were collected at the indicated time points, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. The mouse TA muscle is predominantly composed of fast-twitch muscle fibers with few slow-twitch fibers . Because fast-twitch glycolytic muscle fibers are known to be more susceptible to the catabolic effects of sepsis than slow-twitch fibers , , we used the TA muscle in this study. All animal experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kobe University in July 2021 (approval no. P210704-R1). All animal experiments were carried out from September 2021 to April 2025, in compliance with relevant regulations and guidelines. Group allocation was randomized for each mouse using Research Randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/ Middletown, CT, USA). The required sample size was estimated on the basis of previous experiments that similarly assessed SAMW in CLP models , , . Sample sizes are denoted in the figure legends. All animal experimental procedures are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

## CS preparation
The CS was prepared in accordance with previously published methods , , with slight modifications. Briefly, male C57BL/6J mice (age: 8–12 weeks) were euthanized by decapitation under 3% isoflurane inhalation. The whole cecum was harvested, weighed, minced using Cooper scissors and tweezers, then mixed with PBS on a sterile petri dish. The mixture was filtered twice through a 70-µm nylon mesh cell strainer (Cat# 352350, Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the remaining residue was resuspended in 15% glycerol in PBS (Cat# 072-04945, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The CS stock was transferred to cryovials (Cat# 123263, Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) and stored at − 80 °C, which preserves bacterial viability for at least 6 months . For each experiment involving i.p. injection, a CS aliquot was thawed at room temperature and diluted with sterile PBS to attain a final concentration of 0.25 mg/mL CS and 7.5% glycerol in PBS.

## Sepsis induction and monitoring
To induce polymicrobial sepsis, mice received an i.p. injection of CS at two different doses (0.5 mg/g and 1.0 mg/g BW) using a 1-mL injector with a 27G needle (Cat# SS-10M2713, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). Sham control mice were injected with an equal volume of vehicle (7.5% glycerol in PBS). All animals received subcutaneous injections of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg; Nissin Pharma, Tendo, Japan) immediately after sepsis or sham-sepsis induction, followed by injections every 8 h until sacrifice. Mice were closely monitored by the investigator, who determined the murine sepsis score daily. The scoring system comprised a five-point assessment scale evaluating activity, appearance, eyes, level of consciousness, response to stimuli, respiration rate, and respiration quality . If the murine sepsis score exceeded 15, the mouse was determined to be moribund , euthanized by decapitation under general anesthesia, and counted as deceased. Investigator blinding was not possible because most septic mice exhibited lethargy.

## Grip strength measurement of mice
Forelimb grip strength was measured immediately before and 3 days after treatments using a grip strength meter (MK-380Si, Muromachi Kikai, Tokyo, Japan), following a previously published protocol . Briefly, each mouse was positioned above the metal grid attached to the grip strength meter, allowing it to grasp the grid with the forelimbs. The tail was then gradually pulled backward until the mouse released the grid. The peak force exerted by each mouse was recorded automatically by the built-in sensor. The grip strength assay was replicated three times, with a 3-min rest period between measurements to prevent fatigue. Data represent the average of the three measurements, which were taken by an investigator blinded to the treatment group.

## Drug treatment of mice
The STAT3-specific signaling inhibitor C188-9 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Cat# S8605, Houston, TX, USA) and was dissolved in PBS containing 5% DMSO (Cat# 049-07213, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) before use. C188-9 binds with high affinity to the phosphotyrosine peptide binding site within STAT3 Src homology domains, effectively disrupting its signaling , . Septic mice or sham control mice received an i.p. injection of C188-9 (50 mg/kg) or PBS containing 5% DMSO 1 h after receiving an i.p. injection of CS or 7.5% glycerol in PBS. The injection of C188-9 or vehicle was repeated every 24 h for 72 h. C188-9 doses and administration times were based on previous pertinent studies , .

## Cell culture
C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from the RIKEN Cell Bank (Cell no. RCB0987, RRID: CVCL_0188, Tsukuba, Japan) and cultured in growth medium comprising high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Cat# 044-29765, Fujifilm Wako) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat# FB-1285/500, Biosera, Cholet, France) and supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Cat# 15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), as previously described . Cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ until they reached 80% confluence. The growth medium was then switched to differentiation medium comprising high-glucose DMEM containing 2% heat-inactivated horse serum (Cat# 26050088, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, to induce differentiation into myotubes.

## Cell treatment
C2C12 myoblasts were allowed to differentiate into myotubes by culturing in differentiation medium for 2 days, as described above. The C2C12 myotubes were then exposed to LPS from Escherichia coli O26:B6 (Cat# L8274, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 0.1–1 µg/mL for 24–48 h, as indicated. Control cells received an equal amount of PBS. Inhibitor C188-9 was dissolved in DMSO and added to the medium at a final concentration of 10 µM 1 h before LPS treatment. Vehicle control cells received an equal volume of DMSO at a final concentration of 0.1%. The timing and concentrations of C188-9 were based on prior relevant studies , .

## Clinical study

## Study design, settings, and participants
This prospective observational study was carried out in an open ICU with 20 beds at Kobe University Hospital, which receives more than 3000 critically ill emergency, medical, and surgical patients annually. The annual sepsis volume of the study ICU is ~ 130 cases. The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was reviewed and approved by the research ethics committee at Kobe University Hospital (approval no. B210116). The study participants were all adult patients who met the clinical criteria for sepsis-3 and were treated in the study ICU between November 2019 and May 2020. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their close relatives. Exclusion criteria were ongoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation at ICU admission and refusal to participate in the study. Pediatric patients (< 18 years of age) and patients without collection of a blood sample at ICU admission were also excluded from the analysis.

## Clinical data collection
Clinical and demographic patient data were abstracted from electronic medical records by a trained intensivist (author S.I.), and prospectively entered into a database of the following characteristics: age, sex, septic shock based on clinical criteria for sepsis-3 , SOFA score, and laboratory examination on ICU admission; comorbidities; and routinely administered oral medications. During the follow-up period, the database captured other clinical variables, including the primary causes of sepsis, rate of positive blood cultures, microorganisms identified by blood culture, need for specialized treatments (e.g., mechanical ventilation, emergency operation, and renal replacement therapy), and outcomes (e.g., length of hospital stay and in-hospital mortality).
Plasma cytokines were measured as described below. Longitudinal changes in skeletal muscle mass were evaluated by abdominal CT. The cross-sectional area of the right and left psoas major muscles at the third lumbar vertebral level was divided by the square of the height to calculate PMI (cm2/m2), as previously described . The PMI was calculated both at ICU admission (baseline) and post ICU weeks 1 to 3 to determine the change over time. Similar longitudinal assessment of skeletal muscle mass was employed in a previous study conducted in the ICU setting . To assess the impact of SAMW on the cumulative 2-year survival rate, patients were classified into two groups based on the percentage change in PMI during this period. The high PMI reduction group comprised patients in the upper quartile of PMI reduction, whereas the low PMI reduction group included those in the remaining quartiles. To minimize the risk of bias, the investigator who constructed the database (author S.I.) did not participate in the cytokine measurement, PMI calculation, or statistical analyses. The minimally anonymized dataset for this study can be found in Supplementary Data .

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from TA muscle or C2C12 myotubes using Isogen (Cat# 311–02501, Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by reverse transcription to cDNA. Reverse transcription was conducted using 1 µg of total RNA, random primers (Cat# 3801, Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan), RNase inhibitors (Cat# 10777019, Thermo Fisher Scientific), a deoxynucleotide mix (Cat# 639125, Takara Bio), and Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Cat# 28025013, Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was carried out in duplicate using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Cat# RR820A, Takara Bio) and the Thermal Cycler Dice Real-Time System II (Takara Bio). Target mRNA levels were quantified using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to Gapdh in arbitrary units. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table . The thermal cycling profile was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 56 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s.

## Bacterial cultivation from tissues
Whole blood and TA muscle, liver, and spleen tissues were harvested from septic and control mice 1 day after administration of CS (1.0 mg/g BW) or vehicle, as described above. The tissues were weighed, minced with sterilized scissors, and ground using a 1 mL-syringe plunger and a 40-µm nylon mesh cell strainer (Cat #352340, Corning). Sterile PBS was added, and the mixture was filtered twice and adjusted to a final concentration of 100 mg/mL in PBS. The tissue lysates were centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and 100 µL of the supernatant was spread onto sheep blood agar plates (Cat# 51001, Shimadzu Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) using a bacterial spreader (Cat# DCS-1000, KENIS, Osaka, Japan). Similarly, 100 µL of whole blood from mice was also spread onto sheep blood agar plates. All plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to confirm bacterial colony growth.

## 16 S rRNA sequencing of CS and mouse tissues
Blood and TA muscle, liver, and spleen tissues from septic mice were collected 24 h post CS injection and stored at − 80 °C until use. Total cDNA from these samples and the CS stock was obtained as described above, and sent to the Bioengineering Lab (Kanagawa, Japan) for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing on the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).
A two-step tailed PCR method was employed, following the protocol for 16 S metagenomic sequencing library preparation (Illumina). Briefly, the first PCR was performed using a bacterial universal primer set of 1st-341f_MIX (5′- Seq A—TCT TCC GAT CT—NNNNN—CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG -3′) and 1st-805r_MIX (5′- Seq B—CTC TTC CGA TCT—NNNNN–GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA TCC − 3′) with KOD FX Neo polymerase (Cat# KFX-201, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). After purification of the PCR products with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Cat# N411-03, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), the second PCR was carried out using a primer set of 2ndF (5′- AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACAC-Index2-ACACTCTT TCCCTACACGACGC-3′) and 2ndR (5′- CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGAT-Index1-GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTG-3′) with KOD FX Neo. The prepared libraries were employed for paired-end sequencing using MiSeq v3 reagents and 2 × 300-bp reads on the MiSeq platform. Read sequences matching the primer sequences were extracted using the barcode splitter tool in the FASTX Toolkit.
Further processing of paired-end reads, including quality filtering (threshold > 20), removal of mismatched barcodes, and exclusion of sequences shorter than 130 bases, was conducted using fastx_trimmer and sickle in the FASTX Toolkit. The resulting paired-end reads of the 16 S rRNA amplicons were denoised using the Qiime2 (ver. 2024.2) DADA2 plugin.
Taxonomic assignment was performed using the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin, based on sequences with > 97% similarity in the Greengenes database. Diversity analyses were performed using the Qiime2 diversity plugin. The α-diversity was evaluated using the Chao1 index and Shannon index, while the β-diversity was estimated on the basis of unweighted UniFrac distance, Bray-Curtis distance, and Jaccard distance.
The 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data have been deposited at NCBI GEO and are publicly available (GEO accession no. GSE299753).

## RNA-seq of TA muscle
Three biological replicates of TA muscle samples were harvested from septic and control mice 24 h post injection of CS (1.0 mg/g BW) or vehicle. Total RNA was extracted as described above, and sent to Amelieff Lab (Tokyo, Japan) for RNA-seq. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Cat# E7770, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Index codes were incorporated to assign sequences to respective samples. The libraries were then purified using the AMPure XP system (Cat# A63882, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and their quality was evaluated using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The purified libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 150-bp paired-end read configuration. The quality of the sequencing reads was initially assessed with FastQC and MultiQC, using the --export option. Adapters were removed using Trimmomatic, and further processing with PRINSEQ-lite eliminated poly A/T tails, short reads, and low-quality bases at the extremities. The reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm39) using the STAR aligner. Gene expression levels were quantified using the featureCount function from the Subread package, while DEGs analysis was performed using the R package edgeR. For DEG analysis, expression levels were normalized to counts per million, and genes expressed in fewer than two samples were excluded. The resulting expression data were then normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method and used for subsequent analysis. Volcano plots were generated using the R package EnhancedVolcano. PCA was conducted based on Euclidean distances using the prcomp function in R, and the results were visualized on a two-dimensional plane. Hierarchical clustering was performed on genes exhibiting expression levels > 1 count per million in at least two samples. The analysis employed the R packages amap and gplots, with Euclidean distances and the complete linkage method used for clustering. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the R package topGO, employing the “classic” algorithm and Fisher’s exact test. Reactome pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with the R package ReactomePA, using org.Mm.eg.db as the database for both analyses.
RNA-seq data have been deposited at NCBI GEO and are publicly available (GEO accession no. GSE299752).

## Western blotting
TA muscles were harvested from mice euthanized by decapitation under 3% isoflurane inhalation. The muscles were weighed, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Frozen TA muscles were cut into small pieces with scissors and homogenized using a disposable BioMasher II homogenizer (Cat# 893062, Nippi, Tokyo, Japan) in 0.5 mL ice-cold RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [Cat# 35406-75, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan] pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40 [Cat# 56741, MilliporeSigma], 0.5% sodium deoxycholate [Cat# 190–08313, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical], and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate [Cat# 192-13981, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical]), complemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat#87786, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Similarly, C2C12 cells were washed twice in ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail. For phosphorylated proteins, 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat# 78440, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to RIPA buffer in lieu of protease inhibitor cocktail. TA muscle and cell homogenates were sonicated three times for 10 s each using a Handy Sonic (UR-21P, TOMY SEIKO, Tokyo, Japan) and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using the detergent-compatible DC colorimetric protein assay kit (Cat# 5000112, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Equal amounts of protein (10–20 µg) were separated on 10% or 15% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Cat# IPVH00010, MilliporeSigma) using an XCell SureLock Mini-Cell and XCell II Blot Module (Cat# EI0002, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transferred membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST; Cat# P9416, MilliporeSigma) for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibodies for 8 h at 4 °C. The membranes were washed in TBST three times (15 min each) and probed with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Details of primary and secondary antibodies are shown in Supplementary Table . Immunoreactivity was detected using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Cat#1705060, Bio-Rad) and Amersham Imager 600 image analysis software (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Protein expression was quantified by densitometric analysis, with target protein levels normalized to β-tubulin. To normalize total protein ubiquitination levels, Ponceau S staining (Cat#BCL-PSS-01, Beacle, Kyoto, Japan) was used instead.

## Autophagy flux assay
The autophagy flux assay was conducted as described previously, with some modifications , , . Briefly, C2C12 myotubes were cultured with LPS (1.0 µg/mL dissolved in PBS) or PBS alone for 48 h, as described above. The treated myotubes were incubated for 2 h with 100 nM PBS-dissolved bafilomycin A1 (Cat# SML1661, MilliporeSigma) or PBS alone at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. In the ex vivo flux assay, freshly collected TA explants were incubated for 3 h in high-glucose DMEM with or without 20 mM ammonium chloride (Cat# 017-02995, Fujifilm Wako) and 200 µM leupeptin (Cat# HY-18234 A, MedChemExpress, Monmouth, NJ, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells and TA explants were then lysed with RIPA buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and subjected to immunoblotting for LC3B, as described above. Autophagic flux was determined by calculating the difference in LC3B-II densitometry values between lysosomal inhibitor-treated and untreated samples .

## Cytokine measurements
The culture supernatant was collected 4 h following the treatment of C2C12 cells with PBS or LPS. In mice, whole blood was collected at 24 h post treatment from the inferior vena cava using a 1-mL syringe with a 27G needle, then transferred to EDTA-2Na tubes. In human sepsis patients, peripheral blood was collected into EDTA-2 K tubes (BD Vacutainer EDTA Tubes, Cat# 367863, Nippon Becton Dickinson Company, Tokyo, Japan) upon ICU admission. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The plasma layer was collected and stored at − 80 °C until measurement. IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations in cell culture supernatant or plasma were determined using mouse or human IL-6 and TNF-α ELISA kits (Cat#s KE10007, KE10002, KE00385, and KE00367, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an iMark Microplate Absorbance Reader (Bio-Rad).

## Immunofluorescence staining and histology
At 3 days post-treatment, mice were euthanized by decapitation under 3% isoflurane inhalation. TA muscle samples were harvested, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer solution (Cat# 163-20145, Fujifilm Wako) containing 0.2% (wt/vol) picric acid (Cat# 205–08672, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at the mid-belly region (5-µm thickness). C2C12 cells were disseminated onto poly-L-lysine-coated glass-bottom dishes (Cat# D11141H, Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan), differentiated into myotubes, and treated as described above. After 48 h, the C2C12 myotubes were similarly fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde containing 0.2% (wt/vol) picric acid. For immunofluorescence staining, deparaffinized TA sections or fixed C2C12 cells were rinsed three times with PBS (15 min each) and incubated in a blocking solution containing 2% bovine serum albumin (Cat# 013-27054, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) and 5% normal goat serum (Cat# 005-000-001, Jackson Immuno Research, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, TA sections or fixed cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies, diluted in blocking solution with 0.5% Triton X (Cat# 35501-02, Nacalai Tesque): mouse monoclonal anti-Myosin 4 (Cat# 14-6503-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:100 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-LC3B (Cat# ab51520, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:2000 dilution), or rabbit polyclonal anti-laminin (Cat# L9393, MilliporeSigma; 1:50 dilution). After three wash steps with PBS (15 min each), TA sections or fixed cells were incubated with the following secondary antibodies, diluted in blocking solution with 0.5% Triton X, for 1 h at room temperature under dark conditions: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Cat# A-11029, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:400 dilution), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Cat# A-11008, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:400 dilution), or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (A-11005, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:400 dilution). The fixed cells were then washed three more times (5 min each) and incubated with DAPI (Cat# D523, Dojindo Laboratories, Mashiki, Japan), diluted 1:1000 in PBS, for 1 h to visualize nuclei. A BZ-X700 confocal laser scanning microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) was used to acquire fluorescence images. The cross-sectional area of TA muscle sections was quantified using ImageJ software v1.39 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), as described previously , . A total of 220–262 myofibers were quantified from three randomly selected fields in four independent samples.
C2C12 myotube diameter was measured as previously described , . The diameters of 75–85 myotubes from at least three randomly selected fields in three independent samples were measured at three points using ImageJ v1.39 and averaged for each myotube. The fusion index, defined as the number of nuclei contained in MyHC-expressing myotubes divided by the total number of nuclei , , was also measured as a morphological indicator of mature myotubes. TA muscle cross-sectional areas and myotube diameters were quantified by an investigator who was blinded to the treatment groups. Data are expressed using histograms and violin plots.

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as means ± standard error (SEM) unless otherwise indicated. Differences in continuous variables across three groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), after verifying the normal distribution of the data using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When a significant difference was detected by one-way ANOVA, Tukey’s honest significant difference test was performed. If the data were not normally distributed, the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction, was used instead. Differences in continuous variables across two groups were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively, after applying the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons of survival curves were carried out using the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test. Spearman’s rank-order coefficient (rs) was used to test correlations between two parameters. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) unless otherwise indicated. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
The original, uncropped and minimally adjusted images of these immunoblots are presented in Raw images 5–8 in the supplemental information files. All data expressed as means ± SEM unless specified otherwise; ****p < 0.0001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05.
