## Abstract
Inflammatory diseases remain a major clinical challenge due to their complex pathologies and the limitations of current anti-inflammatory therapies. Conventional treatments, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and biologics, often provide incomplete relief and cause significant side effects, creating an urgent need for safer, more effective interventions. Plant-derived extracellular vesicles (PDEVs)—natural nanocarriers with inherent biocompatibility and cross-kingdom regulatory capacity—have emerged as a novel therapeutic approach that could address these shortcomings by safely delivering anti-inflammatory signals across species boundaries. This review examines PDEVs as both therapeutic tools and targets in inflammatory diseases, delineating their unique properties, anti-inflammatory mechanisms, and translational potential. Key topics include the biogenesis, composition, and isolation of PDEVs; their multifaceted roles in modulating immune responses; and evidence of efficacy in various models of inflammatory disease. Collectively, current findings indicate that PDEVs represent biocompatible, multi-target agents that effectively attenuate inflammation and promote tissue repair by overcoming the barriers to drug delivery and toxicity limitations of conventional therapies. Ongoing advances in omics technologies and bioengineering are expected to advance the characterization and engineering of PDEVs, fostering their integration into precision medicine approaches. Addressing challenges such as large-scale manufacturing, targeting specificity, and regulatory standardization will be crucial for translating PDEVs into safe, personalized anti-inflammatory therapies.

## Introduction
 Inflammatory diseases are a diverse group of disorders characterized by chronic inflammation, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and certain cancers. In the European region, the prevalence of IBD is around 322.0 per 100,000 person-years and has been increasing annually; in Asia, although the baseline prevalence is low, its annual growth rate is significantly higher due to lifestyle urbanization [ ]. These diseases have a great effect on patients’ quality of life and are a significant challenge to the health system [ ].
In response to the grim challenge that inflammatory diseases have been bringing forth, mankind has worked assiduously to find efficient therapies. Conventional therapy is mostly symptom alleviation and inflammatory control, and typical therapy is non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), glucocorticoids, and biologics [ – ]. However, these traditional approaches still face obstacles in clinical practice. A major issue is the adverse effects related to conventional medications. For example, NSAIDs induce digestive tract hemorrhage and nephrotoxicity [ – ], and glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (OP) is a frequent secondary risk factor for osteoporosis, leading to notable impairment due to bone fractures [ , ]. Also, the efficacy of conventional therapies, which focus on alleviating symptoms or inhibiting disease progression, is restricted due to variations in diseases and specific characteristics of patients. As an example, about 35% of patients suffering from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) show non-response to anti-TNF (Tumor necrosis factor) treatment [ ]. Additionally, inflammatory diseases are characterized by a network interaction involving many cytokines and signaling events. Just as in IBD, IL-23 and TGF-β contribute to a pro-fibrotic inflammatory microenvironment through different but related pathways [ ]. This makes it difficult to control disease progression using only anti-inflammatory therapy. Recent progress in understanding inflammatory molecules and cellular mechanisms prompted the evolution of immunotherapy, shifting treatment strategies toward precision medicine [ , ]. This has created new avenues for inflammatory disease treatment, greatly improving both efficacy and security. Nevertheless, with the advances in new techniques of treatment, such as immunotherapy, obstacles remain, such as in the delivery of medications [ ]. These challenges call for new therapeutic strategies. Plant-derived extracellular vesicles (PDEVs) offer a promising option. They use natural phospholipid-based delivery to reach target sites more effectively [ , ]. PDEVs also act on multiple inflammatory pathways at once. This combined action helps reduce toxicity and improve treatment precision compared to traditional therapies [ ].
PDEVs are tiny, membrane-bound particles released from plant cells [ ]. Based on their size and biochemical pathways, PDEVs can be grouped into three classes: exosomes (50–150 nm, come from multivesicular bodies), microvesicles (150–1000 nm, formed through budding of the plasma membrane), and apoptotic vesicles (1000–5000 nm, released during programmed cell death) [ , ]. Generally, PDEVs refer to plant nano- to micro-sized vesicles ranging from 50 to 1000 nm, which are the most widely studied subtypes [ , ]. PDEVs act as key messengers that enable communication between plant cells. They carry diverse active molecules, including lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and secondary metabolites. And some of these cargos are plant-specific (e.g., miR168a, curcumin) [ , ]. Through these active molecules, PDEVs support plant immunity, help plants cope with stress, and regulate interspecies interactions—a capability that also translates to clear benefits for anti-inflammatory therapy, especially when compared to mammalian extracellular vesicles (EVs) and synthetic nanocarriers [ , – ]. Three key advantages make PDEVs a preferred option for therapy. First, PDEVs are naturally derived, highly biocompatible, and less likely to trigger immune responses [ – ]. Second, some (e.g., turmeric-derived EVs, TDEVs) directly exert anti-inflammatory effects by delivering plant-specific metabolites like curcumin, known to regulate inflammatory and oxidative balance [ , ]. Third, their plant membranes are rich in phosphatidic acid, which helps escape endosomes, improve cellular uptake, and enhance therapeutic outcomes [ , ]. When it comes to mechanism, studies showed that PDEVs can fight inflammation in various ways, such as inhibiting inflammasome activation, regulating cytokine expression, and influencing macrophage polarization [ , , ]. Preclinical studies further confirm this, as PDEVs from ginger and grapes can effectively treat colitis in mice [ , ]. These findings suggest that PDEVs have strong potential for future medical use.
In recent years, PDEVs have gained attention in the biomedical field. Their unique anti-inflammatory properties and ability to act across species make them especially interesting. In medicine, researchers now view PDEVs as promising candidates for new anti-inflammatory therapies. They could lower the need for traditional medications, reduce side effects, and improve patient care. In drug development, studying PDEVs could lead to new nano-delivery systems and fresh ways to treat various diseases. To understand why PDEVs can overcome some shortcomings of traditional treatments, this review delves into the basics of PDEVs. It covers their definition, formation, composition, function, and isolation techniques. Furthermore, we explore how PDEVs work in treating inflammation-related diseases and summarize recent progress in this area. To date, little research has been conducted on the large-scale preparation and quality control technologies for PDEVs, and ensuring their stability and targeting in vivo is also one of the key directions of research. By discussing the challenges and future directions in this field, we hope to give researchers a broad view and encourage further investigation and clinical use of PDEVs.

## Overview of PDEVs
PDEVs and mammalian EVs share similarities in structure and formation. Both have lipid bilayer membranes and are released through similar processes, like multivesicular body (MVB)-mediated exocytosis or plasma membrane budding [ – ]. Despite these similarities, there is no globally standardized definition or terminology for PDEVs. Based on widely accepted descriptions, this review defines PDEVs as 50–1000 nm lipid bilayer vesicles actively secreted by plant cells, which play critical roles in cross-kingdom communication (such as plant–microbe interactions and environmental adaptation) and function as regulatory agents in processes such as immune modulation, targeted drug delivery, and gene transfer [ – ]. Moreover, the size and molecular composition of PDEVs vary widely depending on their cellular origin, physiological state, and environmental conditions [ ]. This inherent heterogeneity exerts a significant influence on their biological functions and potential applications. At the same time, it complicates the standardization of therapeutic protocols, the batch-to-batch reproducibility of products, and the development of GMP-compliant manufacturing processes and quality control frameworks, as discussed in later sections.

## The Biogenesis of PDEVs
PDEVs arise through several different but connected pathways, showing how different cellular processes work together. Biogenesis may occur through three principal routes: the MVB pathway, the vacuolar pathway, and the exocyst-positive organelle (EXPO) pathway (Fig. ) [ , ]. Similar to mammalian EVs, plants utilize the conserved MVB–intraluminal vesicle (ILV) pathway as a core mechanism for vesicle secretion [ ]. However, plants additionally employ distinct biogenetic routes—namely the vacuolar and EXPO pathways—which appear to be plant-specific and are not well documented in mammalian systems [ ]. Beyond differences in biogenesis, PDEVs also exhibit a unique membrane composition. Unlike mammalian extracellular vesicles, which are enriched in cholesterol-containing lipid rafts, PDEVs lack cholesterol-rich domains and instead incorporate plant-specific lipids and cell wall–derived polysaccharides, such as glycosylinositolphosphoceramides (GIPCs) [ , ]. These compositional features may influence vesicle stability, cargo protection, and resilience under extracellular conditions. Collectively, these features underscore the unique biogenesis characteristics of PDEVs, shaped by plant-specific cellular organization and physiological context.
 In plants, the MVB pathway initiates with plasma membrane invagination to form early sorting endosomes (ESEs), which mediate the cellular entry of extracellular proteins and small molecules. These nascent vesicles then interact with essential lipid components and transmembrane protein complexes—including PEN1 and TET8—synthesized through endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-driven biosynthesis, thereby establishing structural integrity and enabling precise incorporation of molecular cargo [ , , ]. Next, these components are transported to the Golgi apparatus, where the cargo undergoes post-translational modifications (e.g., glycosylation) before being directed to developing MVBs. The trans-Golgi network (TGN) further regulates MVB maturation: this process drives the inward budding of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) and the selective packaging of defense-related RNAs or antimicrobial proteins [ , ]. Subsequently, late sorting endosomes (LSEs)—also referred to as mature MVBs—are formed; these mature MVBs then fuse with the plasma membrane to release ILVs as exosome-like PDEVs [ , , ]. On the other hand, MVBs may fuse with lysosomes, leading to degradation of ubiquitinated cargo [ , ]. Evidence from Arabidopsis supports the role of the MVB pathway in plant defense: during fungal infection, TET8 (a tetraspanin protein) co-localizes with the Rab5-like GTPase ARA6 on MVBs, and these TET8-labeled vesicles are secreted into the extracellular space (e.g., at fungal infection sites)—clearly confirming their MVB-derived origin [ ]. These secreted vesicles often carry defense-related small RNAs and proteins. Notably, PEN1-positive vesicles represent another subclass of extracellular vesicles; their origin may involve a distinct trafficking route that remains unclear [ ].
Plants also employ unconventional EV biogenesis routes, such as the vacuolar and EXPO pathways. Notably, abiotic and biotic stressors directly shape the cargo of PDEVs, and this influence is particularly evident in the vacuolar pathway: first, small vacuoles (SVs) derived from MVBs can fuse with the central vacuole—a connection that links the vacuolar pathway to MVB dynamics [ , ]. Under stress conditions (e.g., during Pseudomonas syringae infection), these central vacuoles then fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing their intraluminal vesicles and cargo (including hydrolytic enzymes) into the extracellular space [ ]. Additional evidence supporting the vacuolar pathway comes from grapefruit: PDEVs have been detected in the central vacuoles of grapefruit epidermal cells [ ]. In comparison to the vacuolar pathway (which links to MVB dynamics), the EXPO pathway has no direct connection to MVBs. EXPOs are double-membrane organelles that, unlike MVBs or autophagosomes, do not rely on the endocytic route. Instead, they directly fuse with the plasma membrane to release single-membrane vesicles into the apoplast [ , ].

## Isolation techniques of PDEVs
Unlike mammalian EVs, which can be easily collected from body fluids, isolating PDEVs is more difficult [ ]. Plant cells have strong walls made of cellulose, lignin, and pectin [ ]. Researchers must first break this barrier before applying standard EV isolation methods. There are three main strategies for this step. The first is mechanical disruption, using high-pressure homogenization or bead milling [ , ]. The second is biological digestion, which relies on enzyme mixtures that target cell wall components [ ]. The third is chemical treatment with Tris-HCl or surfactants to weaken the wall structure [ , ]. Each method has limitations. Strong mechanical force may damage vesicle shape [ ], while long enzymatic digestion can lead to protein degradation [ ]. After the cell walls are disrupted, common isolation methods such as ultracentrifugation are used to purify the vesicles.

## Differential ultracentrifugation (DUC)
DUC is a widely adopted method for PDEVs isolation by leveraging differential sedimentation rates influenced by vesicle size and density [ , ]. Sequential centrifugation steps are performed at incrementally increased speeds, for example, 300-3,000 ×g for cellular debris, 10,000 ×g for organelles, and 100,000 ×g for PDEVs, followed by resuspension of the pelleted vesicles in a biocompatible buffer such as PBS to minimize contaminants [ – ]. This technique offers benefits such as cost-effectiveness, ease of operation, and the use of common laboratory equipment [ ]. However, some limitations remain: First, there is inadequate resolution to completely remove co-sedimented non-vesicular elements (for example, protein aggregates or extracellular nucleic acids); what’s more, prolonged ultracentrifugation can potentially cause mechanical damage to the vesicles [ , ].
To mitigate these issues, optimization strategies such as fine-tuning centrifugation parameters (speed-duration combinations aligned with target vesicle properties) and the inclusion of stabilizing agents have been implemented [ – ]. Furthermore, DUC is often coupled with supplementary techniques to enhance the purity and functional recovery of PDEVs.

## Density gradient ultracentrifugation (DGUC)
DGUC separates PDEVs based on buoyant density partitioning within stratified gradient media and is one of the most established techniques for PDEVs isolation, often used in combination with DUC for higher purity (Fig. ) [ , ]. Common DGUC protocols use either layered sucrose solutions (e.g. 8%, 15%, 30%, 45%, 60% w/v) or iodixanol (OptiPrep) gradients (typically 20–50% iodixanol) to float vesicles to their equilibrium densities [ , ]. Sucrose gradients are cost-effective and well-established, but at high concentrations (> 40% w/v), they become markedly hyperosmotic, which can dehydrate or compress vesicle structure. In contrast, iodixanol gradients can be formulated iso-osmotically at all densities, better preserving vesicle size and integrity [ , ]. Indeed, iodixanol-based DGUC tends to retain more exosomal marker proteins (e.g., higher counts of CD63-positive nanovesicles) compared to sucrose methods [ ].
 DGUC typically resolves PDEVs into multiple bands. For example, TDEVs form two distinct populations at 8–30% and 30–45% sucrose gradients, resembling mammalian exosomes in size and marker profiles [ ]. This method provides higher purity and specificity compared to DUC but requires time-consuming gradient preparation and lengthy centrifugation, restricting high-throughput applications [ , ].

## Size exclusion chromatography (SEC)
SEC (gel filtration) separates macromolecules by size using a porous stationary phase [ ]. It employs a porous stationary phase, typically composed of beads with a defined pore size distribution. Larger molecules are partially or completely excluded from the pores and elute faster, while smaller molecules diffuse into the pores, increasing their retention time. This results in separation based on the hydrodynamic radius of the molecules [ , ].
SEC preserves vesicle integrity through gentle separation without harsh chemicals, crucial for sensitive substances like PDEVs [ ]. It handles complex samples with minimal pretreatment, and has been successfully used to isolate EVs from diverse matrices (blood plasma [ ], fruit juices [ ], and milk [ ]). However, high equipment costs (especially advanced columns/detectors) and limited column capacity are barriers. By optimizing packing choices, reducing costs, improving collection efficiencies, and incorporating other separation tools, SEC can improve separation while maintaining the structural integrity of PDEVs, making it more economical and feasible [ – ].

## Ultrafiltration (UF)
This technique employs polymeric membrane filters with a defined pore size to retain EVs while allowing smaller molecules to pass under pressure [ ]. In a typical plant EV protocol, a tissue homogenate is first pre-filtered to remove large debris. Subsequently, pressurized ultrafiltration is applied for size-based separation [ ]. For instance, Lee et al. [ ] utilized a centrifugal ultrafiltration device (e.g., an Amicon Ultra-4 PL 100 K filter) to concentrate EVs from Dendropanax morbifera, demonstrating the method’s efficacy in isolating intact vesicles.
UF efficiently concentrates PDEVs and boosts detection sensitivity [ ]. However, UF can lead to vesicle aggregation and membrane fouling, reducing filtration efficiency and compromising vesicle integrity [ ]. Thus, regular membrane cleaning and proactive maintenance are essential to mitigate these issues.

## Co-precipitation
Co-precipitation makes use of polyethylene glycol (PEG) to induce EVs precipitation by altering solubility and dispersion [ ]. PEG forms a mesh-like network that entraps vesicles before they precipitate. In practice, plant tissue is first homogenized to release PDEVs, then mixed with PEG to initiate precipitation. The mixture is centrifuged to pellet the vesicles, and the pellet is subsequently washed and resuspended in an appropriate buffer. Researchers isolated PDEVs via the PEG 6000 method without ultracentrifugation: for example, Sreeram et al. [ ] isolated EVs from edible ginger via a PEG-based co-precipitation method, demonstrating its efficacy in concentrating and purifying PDEVs.
This cost-effective method, which requires minimal equipment, enables scalable production and has been used to develop EV extraction kits [ ]. However, it can risk co-isolating non-vesicular contaminants (e.g., protein aggregates) and inducing vesicle aggregation, compromising purity and structural integrity [ ].

## Other separation methods
Commercial pre-packaged kits (e.g., precipitation solutions by Qiagen, Invitrogen, etc.) provide standardized and user-friendly exosome isolation kits that enable reproducible and straightforward EV isolation from various biofluids, including plasma, without the need for specialized equipment. They enable rapid EV enrichment without ultracentrifugation or special equipment, and can be more reproducible between users than home-brew methods [ , ]. However, despite their convenience, commercial kits require caution and proper validation: EVs isolated via kits may need further cleanup for sensitive applications like proteomics [ ], and continuous large-scale separation of PDEVs can incur high costs [ ].
Asymmetric flow field-flow fractionation (AF4) is a gentle, high-resolution technique that separates particles by their hydrodynamic size in a laminar flow channel (using cross-flow fields instead of a solid matrix) [ ]. AF4 has been successfully applied to isolate EV subpopulations with high size resolution and without labels [ ]. Notably, an optimized AF4 protocol was developed to fractionate human plasma EVs away from abundant lipoproteins [ ]. The challenge with Field-Flow Fractionation (FFF) is the need for specialized instrumentation and expertise; the technique also has limited throughput for very large volumes [ ]. Nonetheless, AF4 provides a valuable analytical-scale method to obtain high-purity PDEV subsets or to characterize size distributions with precision [ ].
Immunoaffinity enrichment selectively isolates EVs via antibody-coated magnetic beads that bind to specific surface markers. Immunoaffinity is routinely used for mammalian exosomes (e.g., anti-CD63 or CD9 beads) [ ], but its application to PDEVs is currently limited. The main reason is the lack of well-established, universal surface markers for plant-derived EVs – reliable PDEV biomarkers and corresponding antibodies are still largely unavailable [ ].
In conclusion, each PDEVs separation method offers unique advantages and must be optimized for specific study goals, with the best results typically achieved by combining multiple techniques to maximize purity and yield [ ]. As the field advances, especially with better PDEV markers and microfluidic innovations [ ], more efficient isolation workflows are likely to emerge. For now, careful optimization and a multimodal approach are recommended to obtain high-quality PDEVs for downstream applications.

## Inflammatory modulation mechanisms of PDEVs
Building upon the preceding discussion on the biological features and therapeutic potential of PDEVs, this section delves into the molecular mechanisms by which PDEVs modulate inflammatory responses. Given their rich cargo—including proteins, lipids, metabolites, and non-coding RNAs—PDEVs influence inflammation through complex and interconnected pathways. These include the regulation of key inflammatory signaling cascades, modulation of immune cell functions, attenuation of oxidative stress, and reprogramming of gene expression (Fig. ) [ ]. Understanding these mechanisms provides critical insight into how PDEVs may serve as viable therapeutic agents against inflammation-related diseases. The subsections below present the major signaling pathways and processes influenced by PDEVs.
 

## Targeting inflammatory signaling pathways
A central means by which PDEVs exert their anti-inflammatory effects is through interference with canonical inflammatory signaling pathways. These include but are not limited to the Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and other immunoregulatory axes. Each pathway plays a distinct yet often overlapping role in inflammation onset and resolution.

## NF-κB signaling pathway
The NF-κB pathway is a master regulator of inflammation, orchestrating the transcription of numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators [ – ]. Multiple studies have shown that PDEVs derived from diverse botanical sources can attenuate this pathway at different regulatory nodes. For example, ginger-derived EVs (GrDEVs) significantly suppressed lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced NF-κB activation and downstream cytokine release in the murine macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7), an effect partly attributed to 6-gingerol and 6-gingerenol [ ]. Similarly, ginseng-derived EVs (GsDEVs) inhibited NF-κB signaling both in vitro and in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mice by reducing IKK/IκB phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB, thereby lowering TNF-α and Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression [ – ]. Other PDEVs also exhibit similar inhibitory effects on NF-κB-driven inflammation. For instance, Citrus limon-derived EVs reduce inflammation in macrophages by suppressing NF-κB activity [ , ], while TDEVs modulate NF-κB target genes to achieve anti-inflammatory outcomes [ , ]. The anti-inflammatory properties of apple-derived EVs (ADEVs) may involve a negative effect of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which downregulates the NF-κB pro-inflammatory pathway in NCTC L929 cells [ ]. Cabbage-derived vesicles downregulated IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2—key NF-κB targets—in LPS-stimulated macrophages [ ]. Although direct evidence is still emerging, vesicles from Momordica charantia and blueberries have also been shown to reduce cytokines typically downstream of NF-κB activation, indicating potential involvement in this signaling pathway [ , ]. Collectively, these findings underscore the broad applicability of PDEVs in dampening NF-κB-mediated inflammation, with potential variability in potency depending on plant origin and bioactive cargo.

## MAPK signaling pathway
Beyond NF-κB, the MAPK family —including p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)—represents another critical axis to suppress inflammatory signaling [ ]. GsDEVs have been reported to alleviate inflammation through TLR4-dependent MAPK signaling, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of p38, JNK, and ERK in both in vitro and ex vivo models [ ]. Specifically, these GsDEVs temper TLR4/MAPK signaling while activating the p62/Keap1/Nrf2 pathway, which collectively reduces oxidative and inflammatory burden. This dual regulatory effect has been shown to mitigate the severity of IBD in mice, further linking the control of these key pathways to measurable improvements in disease outcomes [ ]. Although most MAPK-related findings stem from whole plant extracts, lemon-derived EVs (LDEVs) have also been shown to inhibit ERK kinase activity in addition to NF-κB suppression [ ]. Notably, LDEVs exhibit encouraging anti-inflammatory properties in both in vitro and ex vivo models [ ]. While direct MAPK inhibition by GrDEVs is less studied, ginger constituents such as curcumin are known inhibitors of ERK and p38, suggesting potential for similar effects. These findings indicate that MAPK signaling could be a target for PDEV-based treatments. But this target hasn’t been studied much yet. Thus, further research into its mechanism is required.

## Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant stress pathway
PDEVs don’t just fight inflammation directly. They can also reduce it indirectly. They do this by activating cellular antioxidant responses, mainly through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. In vitro studies show that ginger-derived EVs (GrDEVs) promote the movement of Nrf2 into the nucleus. This activation increases the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and enhances IL-10 secretion in macrophages [ ]. It is worth noting that HO-1 may have the potential for preventing colitis by regulating macrophage polarization [ ]. Similarly, grape-derived EVs (GpDEVs) show protective effects in the liver. They boost Nrf2 nuclear translocation and raise HO-1 expression, which helps counteract toxin-induced oxidative stress and inflammation [ , ]. GsDEVs also appear to activate the p62/Keap1/Nrf2 axis, albeit their primary effects in recent studies focus on autophagy and NF-κB regulation [ ]. Blueberry-derived Evs (BlDEVs), rich in anthocyanins, increase intracellular glutathione levels, implying Nrf2 pathway activation despite indirect evidence [ ]. Overall, activation of Nrf2/HO-1 by PDEVs not only alleviates oxidative stress but also contributes to broader anti-inflammatory effects, positioning this pathway as a crucial therapeutic interface.

## Other anti-inflammatory signaling axes
Beyond classical inflammatory and oxidative pathways, PDEVs also modulate several additional signaling axes that contribute to their immunoregulatory potential. For instance, lipids contained in GrDEVs effectively suppress the assembly and activation of NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes by blocking Apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) oligomerization and speck formation, thus reducing IL-1β and IL-18 secretion [ ]. As a key mediator of innate immunity, the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes to immune regulation; its dysregulated activation is associated with the pathogenesis of multiple disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ ]. GsDEVs downregulate phosphorylated-AKT (p-AKT) and phosphorylated-mTOR (p-mTOR) in RAW264.7 cells, inducing autophagy through inhibition of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway [ , ]. Conversely, EVs from Panax notoginseng activate PI3K/Akt signaling, indicating that different PDEVs may exert divergent regulatory effects depending on their molecular composition [ ]. As for the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) axis, direct evidence of modulation by PDEVs is currently limited, with most insights deriving from plant-derived compounds such as loganin [ ]. However, studies of pure PDEVs affecting JAK/STAT lack clear examples.
While preliminary, these findings suggest that PDEVs possess the capacity to influence noncanonical pathways like NLRP3, PI3K/Akt, and possibly JAK/STAT. Future studies exploring these alternative axes may reveal novel mechanisms of immune modulation and identify context-specific effects based on plant origin and disease model.

## Regulation of immune cell differentiation and function
In addition to modulating intracellular inflammatory signaling cascades, PDEVs have been increasingly recognized for their capacity to shape the behavior and phenotypic polarization of immune cells. Through interactions with macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and T lymphocytes, PDEVs influence both innate and adaptive immune responses. The subsections below explore how PDEVs regulate immune cell differentiation and function, forming a crucial interface between exogenous plant-derived factors and host immunity.

## Macrophage polarization (M1/M2)
Among innate immune cells, macrophages serve as key effectors in orchestrating inflammation resolution or propagation, depending on their polarization state [ – ]. PDEVs have been shown to directly influence macrophage phenotype, shifting the M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) balance.
For example, GrDEVs not only suppress NF-κB signaling but also promote polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Yan et al. demonstrated that GrDEVs significantly increased expression of M2 markers (Ym1, Arg-1, CD206) while suppressing M1-associated cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β). This immunoregulatory shift was attributed to plant-derived miRNAs—osa-miR164d and zma-miR396g—which target the TGF-β-activated kinase 1 binding protein 1 (TAB1), interacting with the NF-κB axis to downregulate inflammatory signaling [ ]. However, contrasting findings have emerged from studies on GsDEVs. In one report, GsDEVs promoted M1 macrophage polarization through activation of the TLR4-myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) pathway, resulting in elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, IL-12, IL-1α, MCP-1) and increased recruitment of CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells into the inflammatory milieu [ ].
These divergent effects suggest that the immunomodulatory outcome of a given PDEV depends strongly on its plant origin and bioactive cargo. Accordingly, when experimental or therapeutic conditions are optimized, different macrophage polarization phenotypes can yield distinct therapeutic benefits. In fact, in the context of IBD, inducing such an M2 macrophage skew via PDEVs correlates with enhanced resolution of colitis: for instance, GsDEVs treatment in mice with colitis increased IL-10 release from intestinal macrophages, which in turn helped quell intestinal inflammation [ ]. In contrast, in the context of cancer-associated inflammation, shifting toward an M1 macrophage phenotype can exert anti-tumor effects. For example. PDEVs have been reported to induce M1 polarization and thereby reduce the metastasis of colon cancer [ ] and inhibit the growth of melanoma [ ]. This highlights the potential of tailoring PDEV formulations to favor disease-specific macrophage polarization: promoting anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes for chronic inflammatory diseases like IBD, while leveraging pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes for cancer-associated inflammation.

## Dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T cell activation
Beyond macrophage modulation, PDEVs also interact with professional antigen-presenting cells such as DCs, thereby influencing the downstream activation and differentiation of T lymphocytes. PDEVs derived from Petasites japonicus have been shown to promote DC maturation by activating both the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. This leads to upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86) and MHC (Major histocompatibility complex) class I and II molecules, thereby enhancing antigen presentation capabilities. Matured DCs subsequently induce naïve T cells to differentiate toward Th1 phenotypes and activated CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, characterized by increased secretion of IFN-γ and IL-2 and strengthened cytolytic responses [ ].
Interestingly, other plant-derived vesicles, such as those from broccoli, exhibit an opposite effect. Rather than activating DCs, broccoli-derived EVs (BrDEVs) appear to induce a tolerogenic DC phenotype via adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway activation. These tolerant DCs likely contribute to immune homeostasis and may mitigate overactive immune responses in inflammatory conditions. Mechanistically, sulforaphane functions as a metabolically active cargo that reshapes DC signaling. By engaging AMPK-associated pathways, this cargo suppresses the molecular machinery required for effective antigen presentation, including MHC-II and key co-stimulatory receptors [ ].
Similar tolerogenic effects have been reported for other PDEVs. Houttuynia cordata-derived EVs also exert tolerogenic effects on DCs in the context of intestinal inflammation. These vesicles preferentially accumulate in inflamed colonic tissue and modulate DC activation by regulating inflammasome-related pathways, including NLRP3-associated signaling nodes. This regulatory effect dampens downstream inflammatory cascades and curtails prolonged DC-mediated immune activation, thereby alleviating colitis-associated mucosal damage [ ]. Likewise, Aster yomena callus-derived EVs modulate DC function under inflammatory conditions. Their bioactive lipid components interfere with DC activation following LPS stimulation. This interference suppresses the expression of molecules required for antigen presentation. As a result, DC adopt a less immunogenic phenotype, which contributes to the attenuation of airway inflammation in allergic asthma models [ ].
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the immunomodulatory effects of PDEVs on DCs are highly plant species–dependent and closely linked to their distinct molecular cargo profiles, including phytochemicals, lipid species, and other bioactive metabolites. From a structure–activity relationship (SAR) perspective, immunostimulatory PDEVs are associated with cargo that activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways such as MAPK and NF-κB. In contrast, tolerogenic PDEVs preferentially engage metabolic or inflammasome-related pathways, including AMPK and inflammasome-associated signaling pathways. Differences in vesicle lipid composition and membrane-associated molecules may further influence DC uptake, intracellular trafficking, and signaling integration, thereby reinforcing source-dependent DC fate decisions. From a biomedical translation standpoint, this inherent diversity positions PDEVs as a vast, natural reservoir for the development of precision immunomodulators. Future research should pivot from descriptive phenotypic observations toward a deeper exploration of SAR mechanisms.
In sum, PDEVs exhibit multifaceted immunoregulatory capacities by modulating macrophage polarization, shaping dendritic cell activity, and influencing T cell responses. Their effects appear to be highly dependent on plant origin and the specific immunological context. The ability to either amplify or suppress immune activation presents a unique opportunity for leveraging PDEVs in diverse clinical scenarios—from immune enhancement in infections and cancer to immune suppression in chronic inflammation and autoimmunity.

## Synergistic antioxidant mechanisms
While immune regulation and inflammatory pathway suppression represent core anti-inflammatory actions of PDEVs, mounting evidence suggests that their antioxidant capacity plays an equally critical, synergistic role in mitigating inflammatory diseases. Oxidative stress—characterized by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—is both a cause and consequence of chronic inflammation, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of tissue damage. PDEVs interfere with this vicious cycle through two primary mechanisms: (1) direct neutralization of ROS by delivering antioxidant enzymes and molecules, (2) indirect modulation of host antioxidant defense systems via signaling pathways. By concurrently reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, plant vesicles can more effectively break the feed-forward loop that exacerbates conditions like IBD, RA, and pulmonary inflammation.

## Direct free radical scavenging by bioactive cargo
Some PDEVs possess intrinsic antioxidant activity owing to their rich cargo of enzymes and small molecules capable of neutralizing ROS directly. For instance, proteomic profiling of GrDEVs revealed the presence of copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/Zn-SOD), which was functionally confirmed by in vitro assays demonstrating superoxide scavenging activity [ ]. Similarly, EVs derived from Aloe vera enhanced the activity of both SOD and catalase (CAT) in cultured cells, highlighting their capacity to augment enzymatic antioxidant systems [ ]. In addition to enzymes, many fruit- and vegetable-derived EVs are enriched with low molecular weight antioxidants—including ascorbic acid (vitamin C), flavonoids such as anthocyanins, and glutathione. These molecules can directly react with ROS species like superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, thereby reducing cellular oxidative burden [ , ]. Such findings suggest that PDEVs serve not only as delivery vehicles but also as functional reservoirs of antioxidant agents. The direct scavenging of free radicals by PDEV cargo can have tangible protective effects in inflammatory disease models. For instance, EVs derived from antioxidant-rich tea leaves have shown strong protective effects in a mouse model of colitis. Their administration reduced colonic ROS levels and lipid peroxidation. As a result, the mice showed less mucosal damage and faster ulcer healing [ ]. These findings suggest that PDEVs can directly reduce oxidative stress. They protect lipids, proteins, and DNA from damage by delivering both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems.

## Indirect regulation via intracellular signaling pathways
Beyond directly scavenging free radicals, PDEVs help maintain redox balance in host cells. They do this by activating antioxidant signaling networks. These indirect effects mostly come from two things: activating transcriptional antioxidant programs and stopping pro-oxidant signaling.
Several plant vesicles can wake up the Nrf2/HO-1 defense line. This axis is the cell’s main shield against oxidative harm. In an oxidative stress model, BlDEVs significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels. They also promoted the movement of Nrf2 into the nucleus. This activation increased the expression of HO-1 [ ]—a cytoprotective enzyme key for mitigating inflammation-related oxidative damage. LDEVs have been shown to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)/Nrf2 pathway in human dermal fibroblasts, leading to reduced ROS production and enhanced cellular resistance to oxidative injury [ ]. Carrot-derived EVs similarly induced Nrf2, HO-1, and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) in cardiomyocytes and neuronal cells, thereby bolstering endogenous antioxidant defenses and suppressing ROS generation [ ]. In parallel, PDEVs can downregulate redox-sensitive pro-inflammatory pathways. For instance, GsDEVs have been shown to inhibit the activator protein-1 (AP-1) transcription factor complex—a key mediator of both oxidative stress responses and inflammatory gene expression—in a skin inflammation model. This inhibition results in reduced expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and IL-6, alongside decreased ROS levels and alleviated ROS-related tissue damage [ ]. By modulating redox-sensitive transcription factors such as Nrf2 and AP-1, PDEVs extend their antioxidant influence to the genomic level, promoting deeper antioxidant protection and supporting inflammation resolution.
Altogether, the antioxidant effects of PDEVs operate at two complementary levels: immediate neutralization of ROS and sustained regulation of antioxidant gene networks. This dual action not only alleviates oxidative stress but also dampens the downstream pro-inflammatory consequences, reinforcing the immunomodulatory effects discussed in previous sections.

## Epigenetic and post-transcriptional gene regulation
In addition to modulating classical signaling pathways and immune cell phenotypes, PDEVs exert anti-inflammatory effects by reprogramming host gene expression at both the post-transcriptional and epigenetic levels. Central to this function is the delivery of plant microRNAs (miRNAs), which mediate cross-kingdom RNA interference to silence pro-inflammatory genes in mammalian cells. For example, ginger-derived miRNAs such as osa-miR164d have been shown to target the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of TAB1, a key upstream activator of the NF-κB pathway, thereby attenuating downstream inflammatory transcriptional activity [ ]. A PeerJ analysis using bioinformatic prediction further revealed that numerous miRNAs encapsulated within edible PDEVs may target mammalian immune-related genes, including IL-1, IL-6, and IL-17, suggesting a broad potential for immunoregulatory interference. One experimental validation identified soybean-derived miR-5781 as a negative regulator of IL-17 A mRNA, providing mechanistic evidence for the suppression of Th17-associated inflammation [ ]. Expanding beyond canonical cytokine targets, a recent study characterized a novel miRNA (Rgl-exomiR-7972) from Rehmannia glutinosa root vesicles that mitigated LPS-induced lung inflammation in mice by silencing GPR161, a negative regulator of the Hedgehog signaling pathway. Downregulation of GPR161 resulted in Hedgehog pathway activation and resolution of inflammation, underscoring the potential for plant miRNAs to target upstream transcriptional regulators as well as terminal effector genes [ ]. Importantly, these gene-regulatory effects are not limited to miRNAs. PDEVs also carry a variety of epigenetically active molecules—such as polyphenols (e.g., resveratrol in grape vesicles [ ]) and anthocyanins (in blueberry vesicles [ ])—which are known to influence chromatin structure, histone acetylation, and transcription factor activity. These compounds may enhance antioxidant transcriptional programs via Nrf2 and SIRT1 activation while simultaneously suppressing inflammatory drivers such as NF-κB and AP-1.
Collectively, these findings support a growing recognition that PDEVs regulate inflammation not only by interfering with signaling cascades but also by reprogramming transcriptional and epigenetic landscapes. While the functional delivery of such gene regulators across species is an exciting frontier, key challenges remain. These include verifying in vivo bioavailability, tissue-specific targeting, and the durability of gene expression changes. Moreover, variability in cargo composition across plant species adds complexity to standardization and therapeutic translation.
Rather than operating in isolation, these mechanisms—ranging from transcriptional reprogramming to immune cell modulation and oxidative stress reduction—form a dynamic and integrated regulatory network. This complexity underscores the systemic nature of PDEV bioactivity and opens opportunities to design multi-functional therapeutic vesicles. As the field progresses, advancing delivery technologies, cross-species RNA biology, and high-resolution omics approaches will be critical to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of PDEVs in inflammation and beyond.

## Applications of PDEVs in inflammatory diseases
PDEVs have been investigated in a variety of inflammatory disease models, demonstrating their potential to restore immune homeostasis and alleviate pathological inflammation. In each disease context, PDEVs utilize the mechanisms described earlier (modulating inflammatory signaling, immune cell function, oxidative stress, and even epigenetic regulation) to counteract disease-specific inflammatory processes. Below, we discuss applications of PDEVs in several major inflammatory disorders—IBD, RA, lung inflammation, skin inflammation, neuroinflammation, cardiovascular Inflammation, hepatic Inflammation, and cancer-associated Inflammation—highlighting how PDEVs mediate therapeutic effects in these conditions (Fig.  ). To facilitate systematic comparison, the sources, key anti-inflammatory mechanisms, targeted signaling pathways, disease models, and representative preclinical evidence of major PDEVs are summarized (Table  ).
 

## IBD
IBD, encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is characterized by chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, breakdown of the intestinal epithelial barrier, and an aberrant immune response in the gut mucosa [ , ]. Disruption of the mucosal barrier and microbial dysbiosis fuel a cycle of inflammation that damages the intestinal wall [ ]. Current treatments for IBD, such as aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, often fall short [ , ]. They may not work for everyone, and their side effects can be serious. These challenges have pushed scientists to look for new and safer options.
PDEVs from many edible plants work in IBD models. GrDEVs are a good example of this. When given orally to mice with colitis, GrDEVs reduced inflammation and helped mucosal repair. Immune cells in the intestine, such as macrophages and monocytes, can take up these vesicles through caveolin-dependent endocytosis. This process allows the targeted delivery of ginger’s active molecules (e.g., 6-gingerol, 6-shogaol, and small RNAs) to the inflamed tissues [ , ]. Once inside, GrDEVs led to a shift in cytokine profiles: it lowered pro-inflammatory factors like TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, while raising anti-inflammatory IL-10 and tissue-repair mediator IL-22. In turn, this sped up the healing of intestinal ulcers. GrDEVs treatment also strengthens the intestinal barrier and promotes stem cell growth, which are both essential for mucosal regeneration [ – ]. Notably, in a chronic IL-10–knockout colitis model, long-term oral GrDEVs therapy significantly reduced colon inflammation and tissue damage. This result confirms that GrDEVs have immune-modulatory benefits in maintaining remission [ ]. Overall, GrDEVs highlight how plant-derived vesicles can work on multiple fronts—calming inflammation, protecting the barrier, and supporting healing.
Beyond GrDEVs, other plant-derived vesicles also show protective effects in IBD models. Grapefruit-derived EVs (GfDEVs) selectively home to intestinal macrophages, increase HO-1 and suppress IL-1β and TNF-α. In mouse models of colitis, GfDEVs help macrophages stay in a regulatory, inflammation-resolving state [ , ]. Their natural stability and compatibility also make them useful for oral drug delivery: methotrexate-loaded GfDEVs delivered the drug directly to colonic macrophages, reducing systemic toxicity and improving therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. Similarly, GpDEVs promoted intestinal stem cell growth and supported epithelial repair in DSS-induced colitis models [ ]. Another example comes from broccoli: BrDEVs activated AMPK in dendritic cells within gut-associated lymphoid tissue, leading to diminished inflammatory signaling and marked improvement of colitis in mice [ ]. In addition, ginseng- [ ], turmeric- [ ], tea- [ ], Portulaca oleracea L- [ ], notoginsenoside R1- [ ], and LDEVs [ ] have all been shown to exert protective effects in IBD models by reducing intestinal inflammatory cell infiltration and promoting barrier restoration.
Collectively, the efficacy of PDEVs in IBD has been demonstrated in both in vitro intestinal cell cultures and in vivo murine colitis models. Across these models, PDEVs exert their anti-IBD effects by modulating the same fundamental processes highlighted earlier but in an IBD-specific manner. These findings illustrate that in the context of IBD, PDEVs can create a more tolerogenic intestinal immune environment, strengthen the epithelial barrier, and foster tissue regeneration. PDEVs target key drivers of gut inflammation, including rogue cytokines and oxidative stress. The immune system then steps back from the fight. Patients feel fewer symptoms, and the disease itself may slow down.

## RA
RA is a systemic autoimmune disease that mainly affects the joints. It is marked by ongoing synovial inflammation, pannus formation, cartilage breakdown, and bone erosion [ – ]. In RA, immune cells such as macrophages and T cells enter the synovial tissue and release inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) that drive tissue destruction [ – ]. Among them, M1-type macrophages are key drivers of the inflammatory process. Treatments that shift macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype or reduce M1 activity have shown benefit [ , ]. Current RA treatments (NSAIDs, Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, biologics) can alleviate symptoms but often with high costs and side effects [ – ], motivating interest in cell-free nanotherapies like EVs. PDEVs offer an attractive therapeutic alternative in RA due to their biocompatibility and immunomodulatory cargo.
GrDEVs have been most extensively studied in this context. In a collagen-induced arthritis mouse model, orally given GrDEVs accumulated in inflamed joints and nearby lymph nodes. This selective targeting was linked to folate receptors, which are highly expressed on activated macrophages in RA joints. Han et al. (2025) used this folate receptor-mediated targeting feature to design folic acid–modified GrDEVs (FA-GrDEVs) for better M1 macrophage targeting [ ]. The FA-GrDEVs efficiently bound to folate receptors and delivered their cargo directly to synovial macrophages. Once inside the inflamed tissue, they showed strong immune-modulating effects. In vitro, FA-GrDEVs pushed human M1 macrophages to shift toward a reparative M2 state by activating the PI3K–AKT pathway [ , ]. This switch reduced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory factors. Translating this in vitro finding to in vivo settings, FA-GrDEVs treatment in arthritic mice clearly lowered joint inflammation and swelling compared to controls. Histological analysis also showed less pannus formation and bone erosion. Levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the joint tissue were significantly reduced, confirming that GrDEVs helped dampen the immune attack. Importantly, no adverse immune reactions occurred. The vesicles were well tolerated and did not cause the systemic immunosuppression often seen with conventional RA drugs [ ].
Beyond their effects on macrophages, PDEVs also bring their own natural antioxidants to the fight. GrDEVs are rich in 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol—two active compounds with strong anti-inflammatory activity. These molecules can block 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) and prostaglandin (PG) synthase, two key enzymes in inflammatory lipid metabolism. Their presence likely explains why GrDEVs lower oxidative stress and reduce matrix-degrading enzymes such as MMPs in RA joints [ – ].
Beyond ginger, other plant-derived vesicles rich in antioxidants and immunomodulators could be harnessed for RA. For instance, grape seed-derived EVs contain resveratrol and have antioxidant properties that might protect joints from oxidative stress [ , ], and curcuma-derived EVs carrying curcuminoids could locally suppress NF-κB in synovium [ , ]. While such applications are still under exploration, the RA case with GrDEVs illustrates the translational potential of PDEV-based nanomedicines: by delivering natural anti-inflammatories directly to immune effector cells in the joints, PDEVs can resolve inflammation and prevent tissue damage in RA. This cell-free approach could complement or substitute biologics, offering a cheaper and potentially safer alternative for long-term management of autoimmune arthritis.

## Acute lung injury and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS)
ALI and its severe form, acute ARDS, involve intense inflammation of the lung parenchyma, often triggered by infections (e.g., severe pneumonia, including COVID-19) or other insults (toxins, trauma). The pathology features a cytokine storm within the lungs, neutrophil and macrophage infiltration in alveoli, disrupted alveolar-capillary barriers, and often life-threatening impaired gas exchange [ , ]. In ALI/ARDS, overactivation of alveolar macrophages and the release of inflammatory mediators lead to pulmonary edema and acute respiratory failure. Treatments are mainly supportive, as few pharmacologic therapies effectively blunt the injurious inflammation without compromising host defenses.
PDEVs have been investigated as a novel strategy to mitigate lung inflammation. One intriguing avenue is the use of plant EV–borne microRNAs to modulate inflammatory gene expression in lung tissues. A striking example comes from the context of SARS-CoV-2-induced lung injury. GrDEVs were investigated for delivering anti-inflammatory microRNAs to the lungs in a COVID-19 model. GrDEVs naturally carry specific plant miRNAs (such as aly-miR396a-5p and rlcv-miR-rL1–28–3p) that can act on mammalian targets [ ]. In experiments by Teng et al. (2021), these GrDEV miRNAs were shown to bind and suppress viral gene products (e.g., the SARS-CoV-2 non-structural protein Nsp12) and concurrently modulate host inflammatory pathways [ ]. Intratracheal delivery of GrDEVs loaded with aly-miR396a-5p to mice inhibited coronavirus-induced lung inflammation (reducing IL-6, TNF-α, and immune cell infiltration), impeded viral replication via the miRNA’s cross-kingdom gene-silencing effect, and reduced lung pathology (edema, hemorrhage) while improving oxygenation compared to controls [ ]. Similarly, Rehmannia glutinosa–derived nanovesicles (from the root of a traditional medicinal herb) were recently shown to carry a specific microRNA, designated Rgl-exomiR-7972, that exerts protective effects in models of LPS-induced ALI [ ].
Beyond viral models, the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory payload of PDEVs is relevant to general ALI/ARDS etiologies. For instance, grape- or berry-derived EVs are rich in polyphenols that can quell oxidative stress in lung epithelial cells [ , ]. While direct studies in ALI models are still emerging, there is evidence that PDEVs can modulate macrophage activation states. One study noted that GsDEVs suppressed LPS-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages, suggesting potential to reduce inflammasome-driven lung injury (relevant in severe pneumonia and sepsis) [ ].
While research on non-viral ALI (such as LPS-induced lung injury) remains limited for PDEVs, promising analogies exist in probiotic-derived EVs (e.g., Lactobacillus plantarum-derived EVs alleviating LPS-induced ALI) and mammalian EV studies [ ]. PDEVs could similarly reduce neutrophil recruitment and oxidative injury in ALI. Thanks to their small size and lipid bilayer, many PDEVs can likely survive nebulization or inhalation delivery to reach the distal airways—a route that could maximize local concentrations in lung tissue while minimizing systemic exposure. Overall, early data suggest that leveraging the natural anti-inflammatory microRNAs and antioxidants within PDEVs can blunt excessive lung inflammation and improve outcomes, warranting further in vivo studies in this domain.

## Skin inflammation
Inflammatory skin disorders like psoriasis and atopic dermatitis (AtD) are driven by dysregulated immune responses in the skin. Both involve chronic inflammation, aberrant epidermal differentiation, and compromised barrier function [ – ]. Topical therapies are preferred in these conditions to minimize systemic exposure. PDEVs have shown exceptional potential as natural nanotherapeutics for skin inflammation, given their skin penetrability and payload of bioactive molecules.
A striking example is their application in psoriasis, an autoimmune skin disorder driven by IL-23/IL-17 axis inflammation and aberrant keratinocyte proliferation [ , ]. Kalarikkal et al. (2025) found that EVs from garlic, shallot, and apple carry components that simultaneously promote keratinocyte differentiation (evidenced by increased markers like involucrin and keratin-10 in vitro) and reduce inflammation (shown by decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines and NF-κB activation in keratinocytes and macrophages), and that topical application of a hydrogel loaded with shallot- and garlic-derived EVs (GlDEVs) to lesions in an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like mouse model resulted in complete normalization of skin appearance and histology, with treated mice regrowing a healthy epidermis and resolving erythema/scaling [ ]. Mechanistic analysis revealed that these PDEVs penetrate the suprabasal epidermal layers and deliver payloads that suppress IL-17/Th17 inflammatory signaling in keratinocytes by activating the upstream Nrf2 pathway, whose activation induces an antioxidant response and enhances the skin barrier (promoting keratinocyte differentiation and cornification) to counteract inflammatory hyperproliferation in psoriatic skin [ – ]. Thus, allium-derived EVs provided a dual benefit: anti-inflammatory effects (lowering TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β in local skin tissue) and pro-differentiation effects (restoring normal epidermal architecture). Furthermore, PDEVs demonstrated excellent safety and penetration Furthermore, PDEVs demonstrated excellent safety and penetration: ex vivo studies on porcine skin confirmed that these nanovesicles can penetrate the epidermis (up to the suprabasal layers) without evident toxicity, highlighting their potential as a new class of safe topical agents for psoriasis.
AtD (eczema) involves a Th2-skewed inflammation (IL-4, IL-13, etc.) along with skin barrier dysfunction, causing chronic eczematous lesions and severe itch [ , ]. PDEVs have been engineered for targeted skin inflammation therapy. Yin et al. (2023) developed hybrid vesicles (FV@CX5461) by fusing GfDEVs (rich in antioxidant and anti-inflammatory components such as ceramides and flavonoids) with nanovesicles from mesenchymal stem cells expressing the skin-homing chemokine receptor CCR6, and loading them with the anti-inflammatory drug CX5461. In both imiquimod-induced psoriasis and dinitrochlorobenzene-induced AtD models, FV@CX5461 significantly alleviated skin lesions. CCR6-mediated targeting enhanced drug and plant cargo delivery to lesional immune cells, reducing epidermal thickening, erythema/scale, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Th17 in psoriasis, Th2 in AtD), while restoring hair coat. The therapy rebalanced immune homeostasis by suppressing IL-17 (psoriasis) and IL-4/IL-13 (AtD) and increasing skin regulatory T cells, with GfDEV-derived flavonoids further reducing oxidative stress, which aggravates both conditions [ ]. Overall, PDEV interventions in psoriasis and AtD exemplify how natural nanovesicles can simultaneously repair barrier dysfunction and modulate pathogenic inflammation in skin diseases—a two-pronged approach difficult to achieve with conventional therapies.
Another emerging area of interest is hair follicle inflammation and alopecia. A recent study showed that GrDEVs promoted hair growth in an alopecia model by reducing scalp inflammation and activating dermal papilla cells [ , ]. PDEVs have also shown promise in wound healing. For example, vesicles from grapefruit [ ] and ginseng [ ] helped wounds close faster by regulating local inflammation and reducing oxidative stress [ ]. Taken together, these findings suggest that PDEVs act on several levels in skin health. They calm inflammation, boost antioxidant defenses, and strengthen the skin barrier by supporting keratinocyte maturation. These combined effects target both symptoms and causes, making PDEVs a promising new direction for dermatological therapy.

## Neuroinflammation: alzheimer’s disease (AD), parkinson’s Disease (PD), and stroke-related injury
Neuroinflammation plays a central role in many neurodegenerative diseases and acute injuries of the central nervous system (CNS) [ , ]. In AD and PD, chronic activation of microglia and astrocytes leads to a sustained release of inflammatory mediators that contributes to neuronal death and the progression of neurodegeneration [ – ]. Similarly, after a stroke or traumatic brain injury, acute inflammation in the brain can cause secondary damage to neural tissue [ ]. The CNS is protected by the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which complicates drug delivery for anti-inflammatory therapies [ , ]. An ideal therapeutic would modulate neuroinflammation while safely crossing the BBB.
PDEVs are being studied as smart nanotherapeutics for neuroinflammation, thanks to their unique ability to carry protective biomolecules and cross biological barriers. Notably, some PDEVs can even traverse the BBB to deliver their cargo directly into brain tissue [ ]. Their natural origin is a key advantage for CNS applications: they are free of mammalian pathogens and have minimal immunogenicity, reducing the risk of adverse reactions. A compelling example comes from studies on Momordica charantia (bitter melon)-derived EVs, tested in a mouse model of cerebral ischemia [ ]—a condition characterized by severe neuroinflammation and BBB disruption. These vesicles targeted matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), an enzyme that breaks down the BBB and extracellular matrix during inflammation, effectively inhibiting its activity. They also activated the AKT/GSK3β cell-survival pathway, a critical mechanism for protecting brain cells. As a result, mice treated with these EVs showed reduced BBB leakage, smaller infarct areas, and fewer infiltrating inflammatory cells. Complementary research supports this potential. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) visualization of GfDEVs revealed that with proper engineering, these vesicles can penetrate mouse brain tissue [ ]. Similarly, Panax ginseng-derived EVs were able to cross the BBB and influence the tumor microenvironment in glioma models [ ]. These findings suggest that similar strategies could be used to deliver anti-inflammatory molecules to the brain in diseases such as AD or other chronic encephalopathies.
PDEVs offer a promising vehicle for delivering antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents to the brain. A key advantage lies in their ability to encapsulate vitamins C and E—compounds prone to oxidation—and improve their stability and CNS penetration. This mitigates oxidative stress implicated in AD [ ]. Beyond classical antioxidants, PDEVs carry plant-derived miRNAs that exert multifaceted effects on neuroinflammatory pathways. For example, ginger-derived miRNAs, absorbed through the gut, can reshape the gut–brain axis by altering gut microbiota composition and regulating intestinal immune responses—an indirect regulatory route increasingly recognized as a critical link to neurodegenerative progression [ , ]. PDEVs also exert direct anti-inflammatory effects on brain immune cells, as demonstrated in vitro. EVs isolated from Allium tuberosum (garlic chives) attenuated LPS-induced microglial activation, reducing nitric oxide (NO) and pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Notably, this effect is thought to be mediated by naturally occurring mRNA within the vesicles [ ]—highlighting the unique bioactive cargo of plant-derived EVs. Overall, these findings indicate that PDEVs can both deliver traditional antioxidants into the brain and provide bioactive RNAs to calm overactive microglia. This dual action positions PDEVs as a versatile approach for combating neuroinflammation in diseases such as AD and PD.
Looking ahead, PDEVs show great promise for chronic use in neurodegenerative diseases like AD and PD, where sustained neuroinflammation suppression is key. Their low toxicity and high scalability overcome long-term therapy barriers, making them viable candidates. Administration can be tailored: oral delivery is convenient, leveraging GI tract stability for non-invasive dosing, while intranasal/intraventricular routes enable direct CNS targeting in advanced stages. A compelling direction is their miRNA-carrying ability—packaging native or engineered anti-inflammatory miRNAs allows simultaneous regulation of multiple brain inflammatory pathways. Combining PDEVs with phytochemicals like curcumin (a potent but poorly bioavailable polyphenol) also boosts bioavailability and brain penetration, enhancing therapeutic effects [ ].

## Cardiovascular inflammation
Cardiovascular inflammation plays a critical role in heart disease development [ – ]. Atherosclerosis and myocarditis are typical examples of such inflammation. In atherosclerosis, injury to the endothelium and buildup of lipids attract monocytes to the vessel wall. These cells then turn into macrophage foam cells, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines that sustain vascular inflammation and promote plaque growth [ – ]. Over time, chronic inflammation of the vessel wall leads to plaque instability and cardiovascular events. Targeting the inflammatory component is therefore an attractive therapeutic strategy in cardiovascular disease.
Emerging studies suggest that PDEVs can attenuate cardiovascular inflammation by modulating immune cell function. A plant miRNA (miR-156a) abundantly present in green leafy vegetables (like cabbage, spinach, lettuce) has been shown to exert a protective effect against atherosclerosis [ ]: Hou et al. found that this miRNA, delivered via PDEVs, can be absorbed and circulate to interact with macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques, where it downregulates genes involved in foam cell formation and inflammatory activation. The net result was a reduction in plaque size and stability of existing plaques in hyperlipidemic mouse models. In parallel, GlDEVs rich in microRNA have shown the ability to reprogram macrophage metabolism and polarization. In a high-fat diet model of metabolic inflammation, garlic-derived EVs carrying miR-396e were found to drive macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 state toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, dampening adipose tissue inflammation associated with cardiovascular risk [ ]. Likewise, EVs from ginger [ ] and carrots [ ] have a specific effect. They encourage a protein called Nrf2 to move into the nucleus of certain cells. These cells include macrophages—key drivers of atherosclerotic inflammation—and other immune cells. This process bolsters cellular antioxidant defenses.
In addition, BlDEVs can reduce oxidative and inflammatory damage in vascular endothelial cells, which is an early step in atherosclerosis [ ]. These vesicles carry anthocyanins and other antioxidants [ ]. In endothelial cell studies, BlDEVs lowered TNF-α–induced adhesion molecule expression and prevented monocyte attachment [ , ]. This effect may potentially reduce plaque initiation. In vivo, supplementation with BlDEVs in a high-fat diet model led to lower systemic inflammation and improved endothelial function, aligning with the observed epidemiological link between berry consumption and cardiovascular health [ , ]. Moreover, studies reported that EVs derived from curcumin [ ] can activate the AMPK-SIRT1-LXRα pathway in foam cells, which in turn upregulates ABCA1 and promotes cholesterol efflux [ ]. Similarly, EVs derived from Rhodiola, which are enriched with salidroside, can trigger the sirtuin 1-forkhead box O1 (SIRT1-FoxO1) pathway [ , ]. This process induces autophagy, lessens oxidative damage in endothelial cells, and simultaneously elevates ABCA1 levels while restraining ox-LDL receptor expression—thereby curbing foam cell formation. This mechanism further disrupts the foam cell-driven inflammatory cycle, a key driver of atherogenesis. Additionally, these vesicles can directly bind to and inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby reducing the downstream secretion of IL-1β and alleviating vascular inflammation involved in the progression of atherosclerosis [ ].
Collectively, these PDEVs deliver anti-inflammatory signals (via miRNAs, polyphenols, and other metabolites) to vascular immune cells, leading to decreased secretion of IL-1β, TNF-α, and other mediators of cardiac inflammation. Although in vivo cardiovascular models are still limited, the in vitro and related in vivo findings highlight the therapeutic potential of PDEVs to mitigate vascular inflammation, stabilize atherosclerotic lesions, and even reduce post-infarction inflammatory injury in the heart [ ].

## Hepatic inflammation
The liver, being a key organ for detoxification, is susceptible to inflammation from toxins, fat accumulation, or infections. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and its advanced form, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), are driven by metabolic inflammation, with Kupffer cells (liver macrophages) and hepatocytes producing inflammatory mediators in response to lipotoxicity [ , ]. Alcoholic liver disease similarly involves inflammation (often via the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway) and oxidative damage from ethanol metabolism [ ]. Moreover, oxidative stress and gut-derived inflammatory signals (via the gut–liver axis) further exacerbate hepatic inflammation [ , ]. Current therapies for conditions like NASH or alcoholic liver disease are limited, prompting exploration of PDEV-based interventions to protect the liver.
Studies have shown that PDEVs can exert hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory effects in models of liver injury. GrDEVs have been particularly effective in ameliorating liver inflammation. In a mouse model of alcohol-induced liver injury, Zhang et al. [ ] observed that mice administered GrDEVs during chronic ethanol feeding exhibited significantly lower serum liver enzyme levels and less histologic liver damage compared with control mice. The protection was mechanistically linked to GrDEVs delivering 6-shogaol to the liver, where it activated the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway in hepatocytes. This led to upregulation of cytoprotective genes (e.g., HO-1, glutathione S-transferases) and mitigation of oxidative stress and inflammation in the liver. In vitro, GrDEVs likewise inhibited LPS-induced ROS and cytokine production in primary hepatocytes. Similarly, recent research has found that in vitro data indicate GsDEVs reduce alcohol-induced reactive oxygen species levels by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [ ]. They also inhibit the NF-κB pathway, thereby decreasing levels of NO, TNF-α, and IL-1β to alleviate inflammation. In an in vivo model, GsDEVs improved liver parameters and pathology in mice with alcoholic liver injury.
On the other hand, BlDEVs have been tested in a metabolic syndrome model (high-fat diet-induced NAFLD). In HFD-fed mice, orally administered BlDEVs improved insulin resistance and decreased hepatic steatosis. Molecular analysis revealed that these EVs alleviate mitochondrial oxidative stress in the liver—by regulating Bcl-2 and related apoptotic pathways—and upregulate the expression of detoxifying and antioxidant genes through Nrf2 activation [ ]. As a result, treated mice had less liver inflammatory cell infiltration and lower levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in liver tissue. Other PDEVs also show promise in liver inflammation. GfDEVs naturally target the liver. In human hepatic cells, they reduce the production of inflammatory interleukins (ILs) and lower oxidative stress [ ].
There is also evidence that PDEVs can influence liver inflammation by shaping the gut microbiome. For instance, orally given goji berry-derived EVs eased alcohol-induced liver injury in mice. They did so by reshaping gut microbiota and their metabolites, which reduced the leakage of inflammatory molecules from the gut [ , ]. Together, these findings suggest that PDEVs act on two different levels. They deliver antioxidant and anti-inflammatory cargos directly to liver cells, and reduce pro-inflammatory signals along the gut–liver axis indirectly. As a result, they lessen liver injury, lower cytokine levels, and improve liver function in preclinical models.

## Cancer-associated inflammation
Chronic inflammation is a well-established driver of many cancers. At the same time, tumors can create an inflammatory microenvironment that helps them grow and spread [ ]. This cancer-linked inflammation involves many factors. Immune cells, cytokines, and reactive oxygen species interact in ways that damage DNA, promote new blood vessel growth, and support metastasis [ , ]. Examples include colitis-associated colon cancer (CAC), in which long-standing IBD elevates the risk of colon cancer through inflammatory damage [ ]. Targeting inflammation in the tumor microenvironment is emerging as a strategy to prevent or treat cancers [ ]. Plant-derived vesicles have shown remarkable versatility in modulating inflammatory pathways and can be applied to temper the cancer-promoting inflammation or deliver anti-cancer agents into tumors.
In CAC, as noted, GrDEVs by suppressing IBD can significantly reduce tumorigenesis in the colon [ ]. This was demonstrated by fewer and smaller neoplasms developing in mice with DSS + AOM (azoxymethane) treatment when they received ginger EV therapy, compared to untreated mice that developed numerous tumors. The likely mechanism was the suppression of NF-κB and pro-tumorigenic inflammatory cytokines in the colonic mucosa by the GrDEVs, breaking the cycle of inflammation-induced genetic damage that sparks cancer in chronic colitis.
Furthermore, PDEVs have been used as drug delivery vehicles in cancer immunotherapy with an anti-inflammatory twist. To start, they can activate anti-tumor immune responses by targeting key immune cells. For example, GfDEVs loaded with a heat shock protein (HSP70) were applied in a mouse model of colon cancer to stimulate anti-tumor immunity; intriguingly, the EVs preferentially targeted dendritic cells in the tumor, leading to an immune activation that overcame the otherwise immunosuppressive, inflammation-driven tumor milieu [ ]. Beyond immune activation, PDEVs can be engineered for precise delivery to inflamed tumor tissues. A notable example involves GfDEVs [ ]: researchers coated EVs with membranes from activated leukocytes, enabling them to target inflamed tumor vasculature by mimicking immune cell trafficking. When loaded with therapeutic agents (e.g., chemotherapeutic drugs or small RNAs), these leukocyte-hitchhiking vesicles accumulated at tumor sites and released their cargo into cancerous tissue. This approach enhanced drug concentration in the tumor microenvironment while sparing normal tissues, effectively suppressing tumor growth in preclinical models of inflammation-associated cancer. Additionally, PDEVs can carry anti-cancer microRNAs to reprogram the tumor microenvironment. For instance, GfDEVs delivering let-7 miRNA or miR-18a have been tested to reprogram tumor-associated macrophages and inhibit tumor proliferation [ ]. Mechanistically, PDEVs in the tumor context can re-educate macrophages from a pro-tumor (M2-like) phenotype to a tumor-fighting (M1-like) state, reduce the secretion of growth factors and angiogenic signals, and induce cancer cell apoptosis through delivering plant compounds.
The outcome of these studies is lower inflammatory cytokine levels in tumors, suppressed tumor growth, and increased cancer cell death. Overall, PDEVs appear to work on two levels. They can reduce chronic inflammation that triggers cancer, and they can also reshape the inflammatory tumor environment itself. Together, these effects make PDEVs a promising and versatile tool for treating cancer-related inflammation—not only in CAC but also in many other inflammation-driven cancers.

## Critical thinking and future perspective
Backed by growing preclinical evidence, PDEVs have rapidly emerged as a versatile platform for modulating inflammation. This capability arises from their diverse bioactive cargos (proteins, lipids, RNAs, metabolites) that regulate mammalian immune and inflammatory pathways via multiple mechanisms [ , ]. PDEVs exhibit intrinsic therapeutic potential in inflammatory disease models by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhancing anti-inflammatory signaling. Most importantly, their mechanistic diversity varies from direct immune cell modulation (e.g., macrophage M2 polarization or the induction of regulatory T-cells) to indirect effects (such as shifting the gut microbiome toward the production of anti-inflammatory mediators). As natural, biocompatible, and often orally available nanocarriers, they shuttle plant bioactive cargos across biological barriers to inflammatory sites. This broad and multi-level immunomodulatory effects (antioxidation, anti-inflammation, microbiome remodeling) make them promising for treating various inflammatory conditions. To further evaluate the clinical translational potential of PDEVs, Table compares them with conventional anti-inflammatory therapies. Although NSAIDs are effective, their clinical utility is limited by dose- and duration-dependent adverse effects (e.g., gastrointestinal injury and nephrotoxicity), restricting long-term use in chronic inflammatory disorders [ , ]. Biologics offer higher molecular specificity but commonly carry drawbacks, including immunogenicity, high production costs, and the requirement for parenteral administration [ , ]. In contrast, PDEVs display low intrinsic toxicity and minimal immunogenicity; their oral bioavailability and natural origin further support safer long-term administration [ , ]. Moreover, as shown in Table , PDEVs display a distinct translational profile when compared with mammalian EVs and synthetic liposomes. Unlike mammalian EVs, which are donor-dependent and can trigger donor-related immune responses [ , ], PDEVs exhibit low immunogenicity in preclinical studies, likely due to their plant origin and absence of mammalian surface antigens. In addition, being derived from abundant plant biomass, PDEVs offer a more sustainable and cost-effective production framework than cell culture–derived EVs. Although synthetic liposomes allow standardized large-scale manufacturing, their immunological properties and stability are highly formulation-dependent [ – ]. Despite remaining challenges in purification efficiency and batch standardization, these features collectively establish PDEVs as a promising, scalable, and low-immunogenic nanotherapeutic platform, rather than just an experimental delivery system.
 Despite this promise, several major challenges continue to limit the translation of PDEVs from bench to bedside. First, a lack of standardization in PDEV isolation, characterization, and potency assessment remains a critical hurdle. At present, there are no universally accepted protocols or guidelines for PDEV production [ , ]. Different studies employ widely varying isolation methods (ultracentrifugation, precipitation, size-exclusion, etc.) with inconsistent parameters, leading to high batch-to-batch variability in vesicle yield and composition. For example, different studies have reported that the protein yield of GsDEVs using ultracentrifugation combined with DGUC ranges between 168 mg and 500 mg per kilogram of ginseng [ , ]. This inconsistency hampers the comparability of results across laboratories and obscures our understanding of what constitutes an “active” PDEV preparation. Importantly, the absence of standardized quality control metrics (for purity, size distribution, cargo quantification, etc.) complicates regulatory evaluation of safety and efficacy [ , ]. Establishing robust, reproducible methods—including clear criteria for vesicle yield, sizing, and cargo loading—is thus essential. Recent reviews emphasize the urgent need for scalable standardization, calling for the development of large-scale extraction techniques, rigorous purification schemes, and agreed-upon potency assays to ensure consistent PDEV products [ , ]. Without such standardization and quality control, it will be difficult to advance PDEVs into clinical-grade therapeutics or even to compare outcomes between studies.
Second, there are technical bottlenecks in engineering PDEVs for therapeutic cargo delivery. While PDEVs naturally carry bioactive molecules, engineering them to deliver specific drugs or genetic materials is still nascent. A chief limitation is the low loading efficiency of exogenous nucleic acids and drugs into PDEVs. To provide a more practical perspective, commonly used PDEV cargo-loading strategies and their reported performance characteristics are summarized in Table . Conventional EV loading methods, such as passive incubation, are straightforward but often lead to limited and poorly controlled cargo loading, especially for hydrophilic molecules [ , ]. Electroporation can enhance nucleic acid encapsulation, yet it frequently causes cargo aggregation and membrane damage, thereby reducing delivery efficiency [ – ]. Physical approaches such as sonication [ ] or extrusion [ , ] may achieve higher loading efficiencies for some lipophilic drugs, but the associated mechanical stress can disrupt membrane structure and impair stability. Taken together, high apparent loading efficiency does not necessarily translate into better therapeutic efficacy. Inappropriate strategies may waste cargo, damage vesicle integrity, and limit translational potential, underscoring the need for more optimized, PDEV-specific engineering approaches. In addition, current PDEV formulations generally lack sophisticated stimuli-responsive release mechanisms. Ideally, a therapeutic nanocarrier would release its payload preferentially at the target site (for instance, an inflamed joint or acidic injured tissue) in response to local triggers (pH, enzymes, redox conditions, etc.) [ ]. PDEVs today do not inherently possess strongly tunable release properties, and engineering them for on-demand payload release remains difficult. However, there has been some preliminary progress: for instance, doxorubicin-loaded GrDEVs demonstrated significantly faster drug release under acidic, tumor-mimicking conditions (pH ~6.5) compared to neutral pH environments [ ], hinting that pH-responsive designs are feasible. However, truly smart PDEV delivery systems (such as vesicles that unload cargo only in the presence of specific inflammatory enzymes or low pH) have yet to be realized. Moreover, targeting specificity is another engineering hurdle: native PDEVs tend to accumulate in organs like the liver or gut [ ]; directing them to particular cell types or tissues (e.g., inflamed synovium in arthritis) likely requires surface modification or ligand attachment. Early studies have started exploring such engineering approaches—for example, conjugating targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides, folate) to plant vesicle membranes to guide them to specific target cells [ – ]—but these strategies still require further refinement. In summary, key engineering priorities to fully unlock the therapeutic potential of PDEVs include enhancing cargo-loading efficiency, incorporating stimuli-responsive properties, and improving targeting capabilities.
 Third, given the inherent heterogeneity of PDEVs, significant hurdles remain on the path to clinical translation of PDEV-based therapies. Of these challenges, standardized manufacturing and regulatory compliance are the most critical bottlenecks. At present, no Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant production pipeline has been formally established for PDEVs. This gap is largely attributed to the lack of scalable, reproducible isolation technologies that are suitable for plant-derived matrices [ ]. Conventional laboratory methods, such as ultracentrifugation (UC), have inherent technical limitations: low throughput, high and non-uniform shear stress, poor reproducibility, and challenges in ensuring sterility [ – ]. These characteristics make them incompatible with the core requirements of GMP manufacturing, which mandate process controllability, robustness, scalability, and sterility assurance. In contrast, closed and scalable workflows based on tangential flow filtration (TFF) combined with size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) have been proposed as clinically translatable alternatives for GMP-compliant PDEV production [ ]. In parallel, upstream standardization of plant source materials is essential to ensure product consistency. The implementation of Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP), together with controlled cultivation systems such as plant tissue culture or bioreactor-based biomass production, has been proposed to minimize variability arising from genetic background, environmental stress, seasonal factors, and agrochemical contamination [ , ]. Downstream GMP quality control must further include predefined critical quality attributes (CQAs), encompassing particle size distribution, particle-to-protein ratios, sterility, endotoxin levels, residual contaminants (e.g., pesticides and heavy metals), and stability during storage, consistent with Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) expectations for investigational new drug (IND) applications. Beyond manufacturing challenges, regulatory uncertainty remains a major barrier to PDEV translation [ ]. Owing to their complex composition and dual role as both bioactive entities and delivery vehicles, PDEVs reside in a regulatory gray zone between botanical drugs and biological products. Regulatory frameworks analogous to those established for exosome-based therapeutics will therefore be necessary to define acceptable standards for identity, purity, potency, safety, and efficacy [ ]. Early engagement with regulatory agencies to align on GMP-compliant manufacturing strategies, scalable isolation workflows, and CMC documentation will be essential for advancing PDEVs toward first-in-human clinical evaluation. A critical unmet requirement underlying these regulatory considerations is the lack of safety and efficacy data in large-animal models. To date, most PDEV studies have been confined to rodents, where favorable safety profiles—such as the absence of overt toxicity following oral administration—have been reported [ ]. However, the scarcity of data from large animals (e.g., pigs or non-human primates) limits the assessment of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, dose escalation, and immunogenicity in human-relevant systems. Evidence from mammalian EVs suggests that these parameters can differ substantially between rodents and higher-order species, indicating that large-animal studies will likely be required prior to human testing.
Looking ahead, the field of PDEVs is poised to greatly benefit from emerging technologies and interdisciplinary approaches, which could collectively help overcome current technical and translational limitations. Advanced “omics” technologies—including high-throughput genomics, proteomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics—are anticipated to enhance our understanding of the composition and functions of PDEVs, particularly by resolving vesicle heterogeneity and identifying bioactive cargo signatures. Comprehensive proteomic analyses have already identified hundreds of proteins in citrus-derived EVs [ ], including metabolic enzymes and signaling-related proteins, revealing that these vesicles carry a diverse array of enzymes and signaling proteins capable of modulating host physiology. Similarly, small RNA sequencing has confirmed that PDEVs contain many unique microRNAs that can regulate target genes in recipient cells [ ]. As analytical sensitivity continues to improve, integrative multi-omics approaches may enable the identification of key bioactive components within PDEVs, for example, miRNAs from tea-derived EVs exert anti-inflammatory effects by reprogramming macrophages [ ]. Such mechanism-oriented insights are expected to guide the rational design of PDEV-based therapeutics. Bioengineering tools are also expected to shape the next stage of PDEV research. Inspired by techniques used in mammalian exosome engineering, researchers have begun modifying PDEVs to enhance their therapeutic performance. Methods like chemical conjugation or membrane fusion have been used to attach PEG or targeting ligands to PDEVs without damaging their structure [ , ], thereby improving vesicle stability and circulation behavior. These hybrid nanotechnologies—combining natural vesicles with synthetic materials—open the possibility of constructing “smart” PDEVs capable of targeted delivery and controlled cargo release. Furthermore, advances in synthetic biology may enable the rational engineering of EV-producing plants. Genetic manipulation of donor plants to enrich specific RNAs, proteins, or metabolites within PDEVs could yield tailor-made vesicles optimized for defined therapeutic applications, offering a promising direction for next-generation PDEV-based therapies.
Another exciting frontier is the integration of PDEVs into precision medicine strategies for inflammatory diseases. Because of their favorable safety profile, low immunogenicity, and inherent biocompatibility, PDEVs could be used in a patient-specific manner with minimal risk of adverse immune reactions. In the future, clinicians might harness a “toolbox” of PDEVs from different plant sources, each with distinct anti-inflammatory or regenerative properties, and choose the optimal vesicle type (or mix) based on an individual patient’s molecular and clinical profile. For example, patients with IBD characterized by dysbiosis and barrier dysfunction might benefit most from oral PDEVs derived from ginger [ ] or grapes [ ], which not only reduce gut inflammation but also fortify the intestinal barrier and modulate the microbiome. In contrast, a patient with rheumatoid arthritis could receive targeted PDEVs (such as folate-tagged ginger vesicles) designed to accumulate in synovial macrophages and reprogram them from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-resolving phenotype [ ]. PDEVs can carry different cargos and be given in various ways, making them good for personalized therapy. Ultimately, the goal would be to incorporate PDEV-based nanotherapeutics into the precision medicine arsenal, either as standalone treatments or in combination with existing drugs, to provide safer and more targeted modulation of inflammation on a per-patient basis.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, PDEVs represent a novel and versatile class of therapeutic agents for inflammatory diseases. Their unique biological origin and multifaceted regulatory capacity position them at the intersection of natural medicine and nanotechnology. The field has made significant progress in proving its therapeutic potential—showing it can reduce inflammation, aid tissue repair, and even transmit genetic signals across biological kingdoms. While significant technical and translational barriers remain, ongoing interdisciplinary research—bridging plant biology, immunology, bioengineering, and clinical science—may pave the way for their future application in personalized anti-inflammatory therapies. With strategic investment in standardization, engineering, and regulatory alignment, PDEVs could evolve from experimental tools into clinically viable therapeutics that reshape the landscape of inflammation treatment.
