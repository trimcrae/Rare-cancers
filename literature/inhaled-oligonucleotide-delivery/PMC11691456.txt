## Abstract
Alzheimer’s disease is characterized by deposition of amyloid-β, which forms extracellular neuritic plaques, and accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau, which aggregates to form intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles, in the brain. The NLRP3 inflammasome may play a role in the transition from amyloid-β deposition to tau phosphorylation and aggregation. Because NLRP3 is primarily found in brain microglia, and tau is predominantly located in neurons, it has been suggested that NLRP3 expressed by microglia indirectly triggers tau phosphorylation by upregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Here, we found that neurons also express NLRP3 in vitro and in vivo, and that neuronal NLRP3 regulates tau phosphorylation. Using biochemical methods, we mapped the minimal NLRP3 promoter and identified FUBP3 as a transcription factor regulating NLRP3 expression in neurons. In primary neurons and the neuroblastoma cell line Neuro2A, FUBP3 is required for endogenous NLRP3 expression and tau phosphorylation only when amyloid-β is present. In the brains of aged wild-type mice and a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease, FUBP3 expression was markedly increased in cortical neurons. Transcriptome analysis suggested that FUBP3 plays a role in neuron-mediated immune responses. We also found that FUBP3 trimmed the 5′ end of DNA fragments that it bound, implying that FUBP3 functions in stress-induced responses. These findings suggest that neuronal NLRP3 may be more directly involved in the amyloid-β-to–phospho-tau transition than microglial NLRP3, and that amyloid-β fundamentally alters the regulatory mechanism of NLRP3 expression in neurons. Given that FUBP3 was only expressed at low levels in young wild-type mice and was strongly upregulated in the brains of aged mice and Alzheimer’s disease mice, FUBP3 could be a safe therapeutic target for preventing Alzheimer’s disease progression.

## Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease that mostly affects the elderly and is characterized by extracellular neuritic plaques (NPs) and intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) (Zhang et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2024). NPs consist of mainly amyloid-β (Aβ) protein derived from amyloid precursor protein (APP), and the NFTs are aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau protein, which is encoded by the MAPT gene (Zhang and Song, 2017). In addition to these two hallmarks, AD is also associated with neuroinflammation and neuronal death. Over the course of AD progression, NP is the earliest pathological manifestation, while NFTs appear much later and are also found in other diseases (Long and Holtzman, 2019; Wilson et al., 2023). Therefore, NP accumulation could contribute to NFT formation.
Inflammasomes have been shown to play an important role in neurodegenerative disorders (Yao et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024). One factor that might link NPs to NFTs is the NLRP3 (NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3) inflammasome (N3I), a protein complex that is involved in the innate immune defense. The N3I consists of NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1, with NLRP3 as a sensor protein to detect pathogenic insults (Rahman et al., 2020). During the innate immune response, NLRP3 is upregulated and activated, and ASC is subsequently recruited to oligomerized NLRP3 through interactions among PYD domains. ASC in turn recruits pro-caspase-1 to the complex, promoting its activation to caspase-1 (Lu et al., 2014). Active caspase-1 cleaves pro-interleukin-1β (IL1β) and pro-IL18 to facilitate their maturation and secretion (Kostura et al., 1989). Pro-caspase-1 or mature caspase-1 within N3I also cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to produce a pyroptotic N-terminal GSDMD fragment (He et al., 2015; Ding et al., 2024). Enhanced N3I activity has been observed in AD brains, and NLRP3 deletion ameliorates behavioral defects and promotes Aβ clearance in AD model mice with NP pathology (Heneka et al., 2013). In addition, N3I promotes tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation by activating several tau kinases such as calmodulin-dependent kinase IIα (CaMK2α) and glycogen synthase kinase-3β, as well as inactivating protein phosphatase 2A through upregulating protein phosphatase methylesterase 1 (PME1) expression in neurons. In vitro evidence suggests that these effects are indirectly mediated through N3I activation in microglia, allowing secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Ising et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). However, the unconditional NLRP3 deletion in these studies abolished NLRP3 expression in all cell types; thus, the consequences of NLRP3 ablation may also have involved cell types other than microglia.
To determine whether N3I directly mediates the transition from NPs to NFTs in AD, it is essential to first establish whether NLRP3 is expressed in neurons. While NLRP3 is expressed in immune cells such as microglia, tau expression and NFT formation primarily occur within neurons. For N3I to directly induce tau hyperphosphorylation, it would need to be expressed in neurons as well. Neuronal NLRP3 expression has been observed in some conditions such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and spreading depolarization (Panicker et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023), but whether neurons express NLRP3 in AD remains unknown.
In this study, we sought to examine NLRP3 expression and function in neurons, and to identify transcription factors driving the expression of neuronal NLRP3. In addition to primary neurons, Neuro2A cells, a neuroblastoma cell line resembling neurons in some ways, and the microglial cell line BV2 were used for in vitro studies.

## Methods

## Design
The experimental design is summarized in . NLRP3 expression in neurons was first examined in vitro and in vivo, to establish a basis for the possibility that neuronal NLRP3 might directly regulate tau phosphorylation. After confirming that neuronal NLRP3 was involved in tau phosphorylation, biochemical methods were used to screen for NLRP3 transcription factor(s) in the neuronal cell line Neuro2A. Far upstream binding protein 3 (FUBP3) was identified to bind to the minimal NLRP3 promoter; and we further assessed the role of FUBP3 in regulating NLRP3 expression, both in the presence and absence of Aβ, in Neuro2A cells, wild-type primary neurons, and primary neurons expressing mutant APP.
 FUBP3 expression in the brains of different mouse models was assessed, as well as whether FUBP3 regulates NLRP3 expression. Finally, given that the biological function of FUBP3 remains poorly characterized, transcriptome analysis of Neuro2A cells with suppressed FUBP3 was conducted to investigate the role of FUBP3 in neuronal function.

## Patients with viral encephalitis
Patients over 18 years of age diagnosed with viral encephalitis (VE) were recruited from Xuanwu Hospital. The VE diagnoses were determined by two physicians using the 2013 International Encephalitis Consortium (IEC) criteria established by Venkatesan et al. (2013) through metagenomic second-generation sequencing or polymerase chain reaction. Patients with a history of stroke, seizure, head injury, neurosurgery, other neurological disease, or mental illness, incomplete clinical data, or contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were excluded from the study.

## Mouse strains
Three-month-old wild-type C57BL/6J mice (Stock No. 000664, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) or APPswe/PS1ΔE9 transgenic mice (Stock No. 005864, RRID: MMRRC_034832-JAX) on a C57BL/6J background, aged 8 months, were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (ME, USA). APPswe transgenic mice (Stock No. C001076, RRID: IMSR_JAX:001076) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). All mice were used at the indicated ages, and the specific numbers of mice used in each experiment are detailed in the figure legends.
For hydrogen peroxide treatment, APPswe/PS1ΔE9 transgenic mice were anesthetized by haloflurane (3%) inhalation and fixed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD, Shenzhen, China), and 2 μL of hydrogen peroxide (0.3%, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was injected into the right side of the hippocampus at a rate of 0.5 μL/min. The microsyringe was left in place for 5 minutes to prevent backflow before withdrawing the needle. Then, the skin was sutured, and the mice were returned to their cages.

## Plasmid construction
To construct plasmids for luciferase assay, DNA fragments were polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified from HEK293 cell genomic DNA and inserted into the pGL3-basic vector. For short fragments, sense and anti-sense strands were synthesized and annealed, and the double-stranded fragment was inserted into pGL3-basic.
To construct an FUBP3 overexpression plasmid, the cDNA coding for FUBP3 was PCR-amplified from SH-SY5Y total cDNA, with the reverse primer containing a sequence coding for FLAG tag ( ). The PCR program included 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 seconds, annealing at 68°C for 15 seconds, and elongation at 72°C for 1 minute. For plasmid constructions and digestions, the restriction enzymes NheI and Hind III (Transgen, Beijing, China) were mixed with DNA. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes.
 

## Cell culture and transfection
Neuo2A (RRID: CVCL_0470) cells and BV2 (RRID: CVCL_0182) cells were purchased from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC, Beijing, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C (5% CO2). Cells were routinely examined for misidentification and contamination. For the luciferase assay, Neuro2A cells and BV2 cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding luciferase driven by different promoters and pCMV-RLuc as an internal standard, using Lipo8000 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The FUBP3-specific siRNA (5′-GAA CUA UUA UGU AUU GUA A-3′), which was reported previously (Mukherjee et al., 2019), was transfected into Neuro2A cells using Lipo8000.
Primary neurons were isolated from the cortex of day 18 mouse embryos (C57BL/6 background). Pregnant wild-type female mice were bred with either wild-type male mice (C57BL/6J strain, sourced from Viewsolid BioTech, Beijing, China) or male APPswe transgenic mice (stock No. 000664, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664, bred in-house). The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (RWD, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) and sacrificed by decapitation.
Anesthesia was induced by placing each mouse in an induction chamber, which was supplied with a mixture of 3.5% isoflurane and medical oxygen at a flow rate of 3 L/min. The isoflurane concentration was maintained at 1.5% throughout the anesthesia process. After undergoing several different surgical procedures, the mouse was placed in a recovery chamber equipped with a heating pad and provided with medical oxygen at a flow rate of 3 L/min to facilitate recovery.
To achieve shRNA-mediated FUBP3 knockdown and FUBP3 overexpression in primary neuron cultures, 1 × 1011 genomic copies of adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing FUBP3-FLAG or the shRNA against FUBP3 were added to each well of a 12-well plate on day in vitro (DIV) 4, and the media were replaced on DIV5. For Aβ treatment, freshly solubilized Aβ1–42 (NJpeptide, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) was diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and added to the cells at a final concentration of 1 μM. The cells were then incubated overnight. CY-09 (1 μM) or CORM3 (50 μM) was added to the primary neuron cultures for 4 hours to inhibit NLRP3 ATPase activity or prevent glycolysis-dependent N3I activation, respectively.

## Luciferase reporter assay
The luciferase assay was conducted using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 hours of transfection, 100 μL of passive lysis buffer was added to each well. Subsequently, firefly luciferase and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). To ensure accurate comparison, firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity and expressed as a relative value compared with the activity seen in cells transfected with the pGL3-basic empty vector. Fluorescence was detected using a luminometer (Promega).

## DNA pull-down
The pNLRP3-I and Del5 promoter sequences were biotin-labeled at both the 5′ and 3′ ends. An unrelated DNA sequence served as the control. The labeled probes were incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads (Beyotime) for 3 hours at 4°C, with constant rotation, in a binding buffer consisting of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid, 2 M NaCl, and 0.01%–0.1% Tween-20. The beads were then washed with binding buffer and added to Neuro2A cell nuclear extracts, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C overnight with continuous rotation. Following this, the DNA-bead complexes were washed with binding buffer 3. Next, the precipitates were eluted with 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-sample buffer and subjected to 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and western blot analysis.

## Mass spectrometry analysis
LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in conjunction with a Vanquish system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, the peptide mixtures were separated by a binary solvent system, which comprised 99.9% H2O with 0.1% formic acid as phase A and 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid as phase B.

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay and electrophoretic mobility shift assay–western blot
An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) for FUBP3 was conducted using a Lightshift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclear proteins were extracted using NE-PERTM Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents/NE-PER kit following the manufacturer’s instruction (78833, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Nuclear extracts were prepared from Neuro2A cells, and biotinylated single-/double-stranded probes were added to the extracts. The mixtures were incubated at 22°C for 20 minutes in EMSA binding buffer, and then loaded onto 6% native gels. For the competition assay, nuclear extracts were incubated with either 1× or 5× unlabeled competition oligonucleotides, along with the labeled probes. For the super-shift assay, 1 μg of an anti-FUBP3 antibody or normal IgG was added to the probe–nuclear extract mixture. To ensure sufficient binding of the antibody with FUBP3, the reactions were incubated at 4°C overnight. For regular EMSA, the probes on the native gel were directly transferred onto a nylon membrane. However, for EMSA–western blot, the gels were first immersed in SDS-PAGE running buffer for 30 minutes to denature the proteins. Following this, the proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, as for a regular western blot.

## Western blotting
Neuro2A and BV2 cells, as well as primary neurons, were harvested, suspended in sample buffer, and lysed using an Ultrasonic Cell Crusher (model JN96II, Ningbo Jiangnan Instrument Company, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China). The resulting cell lysates were then resolved on either 8% Tris-glycine or 16% Tris-tricine gels (Biosharp, Beijing, China). Following gel electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) that was then blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 hour at room temperature (20–25°C) to minimize nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking with primary antibodies specific to the proteins of interest, including: anti-FUBP3 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, PAG312Mu01, RRID: AB_3083686, Cloud-clone, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-NLRP3 (mouse monoclonal, 1:1000, AG-20B-0014-C100, RRID: AB_2885199, AdipoGen, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-caspase-1 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, 2225S, RRID: AB_2243894, Cell Signaling, USA), anti-GSDMD (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, NBP2-33422, RRID: AB_2687913, NovusBio, MN, USA), anti-tau (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, PAV538Ge01, RRID: AB_3083687, Cloud-clone), anti-phosphorylated tau at site 181 (p-tau181; rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, 28866-1-AP, RRID: AB_2881222, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-phosphorylated tau at sites 202/205 (p-tau202/205; mouse monoclonal, AT8, MN1020,1:1000, RRID: AB_223647, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), anti-PME1 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, 14435-1-AP, RRID: AB_2284364, Proteintech), anti-phosphorylated-calmodulin (CaM)-dependent protein kinase IIα (CamKIIα; rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, 12716, RRID: AB_2713889, Cell Signaling), anti-protein phosphatase 1 regulatory inhibitor subunit 1A (PPP1R1A; rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, K108186P, RRID: AB_3083689, Solarbio, Beijing China), GAPDH (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, PAB932Hu01, RRID: AB_3083694, Cloud-clone), anti-β-actin (mouse monoclonal, 1:2000, PAB340Mi01, Cloud-clone, RRID: AB_3095306) or anti-histone H3 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000, PAA285Mi01, Cloud-clone, RRID: AB_3083688). After three washes, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (HRP-conjugated and purified goat anti-rabbit IgG/horse anti-mouse IgG, 1:3000, Cell Signaling, Cat# 7074/7076, RRID: AB_2099233/AB_330924) for 2 hours at room temperature with gentle shaking. The membranes were developed using ECL (Biosharp, Beijing, China), and then imaged using a chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon 5800, Shanghai, China). Protein expression was normalized to internal standards. To confirm the specificity of the phospho-tau antibodies, brain lysates from C57BL/6J mice were treated with 400 units of λ-protein phosphatase (Cat#P0753S, New England Biolabd, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions, before being subjected to western blotting.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Mice were euthanized by inhalation of gradually increasing concentrations of CO2 over 30 minutes until respiratory arrest occurred. The CO2 replacement rate was maintained between 30% and 70% to ensure that loss of consciousness preceded any perception of pain. After respiratory arrest, the CO2 flow was maintained for an additional 5 minutes. After anesthesia, the mice were perfused with ice-cold PBS to eliminate blood from the circulatory system. The brains were harvested from perfused animals and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the brains were immersed in a series of sucrose solutions: 10%, 20%, and 30%. Using a Leica Rotary Microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), sections of the brain were cut to a thickness of 14 µm, and the slices were mounted onto glass slides. The slices were then pretreated with 10% normal donkey serum (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by incubation with the appropriate primary antibodies, including: anti-FUBP3 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:500, PAG312Mu01, RRID: AB_3083686, Cloud-clone), anti-NLRP3 (mouse monoclonal, 1:500, AG-20B-0014-C100, RRID: AB_2885199, AdipoGen, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-Iba1 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:500, 019-19741, RRID: AB_839504, Wako, Shanghai, China), and anti-NeuN (rabbit monoclonal, 1:500, SAB5700017, RRID: AB_3083690, Millipore or mouse polyclonal, 1:250, 94403, RRID: AB_2904530, Cell Signaling). After extensive washing, the slices were incubated for 3 hours at room temperature with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies: Alexa fluor 488 and 564 donkey anti-rabbit and anti-mouse (1:500, ab150073/ab175470/ab150105/ab175700, RRID: AB_2636877/AB_2783823/AB_2732856/AB_3083693, Abcam, USA). Cell nuclei were stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Absin, Shanghai, China). Mowiol reagent (Millipore) was used to cover the slides with coverslips. A Lecia microscope (Lecia, Germany) equipped with a digital camera was used to capture digital images. Representative images are shown in the figures.

## RNA-sequencing and analysis
RNA extraction from Neuro2A cells, library preparation, and RNA sequencing were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNAs were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA), and 150-bp paired-end reads were generated. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html; Love et al., 2014). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and WikiPathways enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using R software (v 3.2.0) (https://www.r-project.org), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) (Subramanian et al., 2005), with the Reactome database (https://reactome.org/). Differentially expressed genes were defined as |log2(fold change)| > 1 and P < 0.05.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
Conditioned media were collected from primary neuron cultures and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 minutes to remove floating cells and cell debris. The interleukin-1 beta (IL1β) content of the cleared condition media was quantified using an IL1β ELISA kit (ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Statistical analysis
Three or more independent replicates were performed for each experiment. All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or two-tailed Student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## NLRP3 is expressed in neurons and regulates tau phosphorylation
To determine whether NLRP3 is expressed in neurons, we first stained the brains of 3-month-old wild-type mice and AD model mice (APP/PS1ΔE9) using an NLRP3-specific antibody. No distinct staining for NLRP3 was detected in the brains of young wild-type mice ( A). However, in APP/PS1ΔE9 transgenic mice at 8 months of age, a time point where NPs are abundant, NLRP3 staining was intense and primarily localized to the NPs surrounded by microglia. Furthermore, microglial NLRP3 expression was also upregulated ( B). In these AD brains, there was little neuronal NLRP3 expression; only occasionally were a few neurons clearly stained by the anti-NLRP3 antibody in the neocortex ( C). Upon injection of hydrogen peroxide into the hippocampus, robust NLRP3 expression was observed in the majority of neurons surrounding the injection site, with no significant upregulation of NLRP3 expression in neurons located in distal areas ( D). This suggests that neuronal NLRP3 expression is induced by oxidative stress.
 Western blot analysis of wild-type primary cortical neurons (PNWT) cultured in vitro showed a clear ~110 kDa band representing NLRP3. The neurons were also found to express endogenous pro-caspase-1 and GSDMD (full-length, but not the N-terminal fragment due to cleavage by activated caspase-1). However, compared with the microglia cell line BV2, these proteins were expressed at much lower levels in neurons ( E). Owing to the relatively low expression levels, cleaved forms of caspase-1 and GSDMD were undetectable in neurons, whereas cleaved GSDMD was detected in naïve BV2 cells ( E). In PNWT cultures, NLRP3 displayed a diffuse cytoplasmic distribution, with comparable signal intensity in neurons and contaminating astrocytes ( F). However, in contrast, NLRP3 staining in primary neurons derived from APPswe mutant transgenic mice (PNAPP) was significantly enriched in neurons ( F). We further examined the mouse neuroblastoma cell line Neuro2A and found that it expressed both NLRP3 mRNA and NLRP3 protein, as well as PYCARD (encoding ASC) and CASP1 (encoding caspase-1) mRNAs ( G and H).
To test whether neuronal NLRP3 helps regulate tau phosphorylation, we treated PNAPP with the NLRP3 inhibitors, CY-09 and CORM-3. Both inhibitors significantly reduced p-tau202/205 (tau with both Ser202 and Thr205 phosphorylated, recognized by the AT8 antibody) ( I). The specificity of the antibodies for phosphorylated tau was determined by treating APP/PS1ΔE9 mouse brain lysates with λ-protein phosphatase ( J).
Taken together, these findings demonstrate that NLRP3 and other subunits of the N3I complex are expressed in neurons and regulate tau phosphorylation.

## Identification of the minimal NLRP3 promoter
To identify the minimal promoter necessary for NLRP3 expression, a luciferase assay was performed by cloning a range of genomic fragments located upstream of the human NLRP3 gene transcriptional start site (designated as position +1) into the pGL3-basic vector. The longest fragment extended from –4427 bp upstream of the transcription start site to +217 bp, immediately following the translational start codon ATG ( A). This fragment was designated as pNLRP3-A. Additional 5′ truncations of pNLRP3-A, designated as pNLRP3-B to pNLRP3-J, were subsequently generated. When transfected into Neuro2A cells and normalized to the co-transfected internal standard, only the pNLRP3-I fragment (–172 to +217) demonstrated significantly elevated promoter activity compared with the control empty vector (2.756 ± 0.7831-fold). Given that NLRP3 is expressed in innate immune cells, we further examined the promoter activity of these fragments in BV2 cells. Unlike in Neuro2A cells, all fragments except the shortest (pNLRP3-J (–22 to +217)) significantly increased luciferase expression compared with the vector control. The fold changes observed in BV2 cells were significantly higher than those seen in pNLRP3-I in Neuro2A cells ( B). These results imply that the regulatory mechanism governing NLRP3 expression differs between the two cell lines. There could be repressive cis-elements located upstream of the -172 site in Neuro2A cells that are absent in BV2 cells. Additionally, the NLRP3 promoter appears to be more active in BV2 cells than in neurons. To further identify the minimal promoter region in Neuro2A cells, we truncated pNLRP3-I to –144 to +217 (designated as pNLRP3-K), –105 to +217 (pNLRP3-L), and –72 to +217 (pNLRP3-M). None of these truncated mutants exhibited higher promoter activity than the vector control, indicating that the minimal promoter sequence was between residues –172 and –144, including residue –144 ( C). Therefore, we tested the promoter activity of three fragments: –172 to –144 (Frag1), –145 to –105 (Frag2), and –158 to –125 (Frag3). Only Frag3 demonstrated a statistically significant increase in luciferase expression compared with the control vector ( D). Five deletion and truncation mutants of Frag3 (designated as Del1–5) were subsequently generated. Among these, only Del5 (–156 to –133) exhibited similar activity to Frag3. Therefore, Del5 was identified as the minimal NLRP3 promoter in Neuro2A cells. Notably, all deletions that encompassed the Del5 region (i.e., Del1–4) abrogated Frag3 promoter activity ( E), suggesting that the entire Del5 sequence is necessary for promoter activity.
 

## FUBP3 binds the NLRP3 minimal promoter in neurons
To identify transcription factors that bind to the NLRP3 promoter, we conducted a pull-down assay using the –172 to –22 fragment (the sequence that was included in pNLRP3-I but not pNLRP3-J) as bait to isolate proteins from Neuro2A nuclear extracts. Mass spectrometry analysis identified several transcription factors that bound this region ( A). To our knowledge, the function of FUBP3 (also known as FBP3) remained unclear, making it a particularly intriguing candidate for further investigation. FUBP3 belongs to the far upstream element binding protein (FUBP) family, which is capable of binding single-stranded DNA and RNA. The most well-characterized member of this family, FUBP1, activates the expression of the oncogene c-Myc in tumor cells (Duncan et al., 1994). Unlike FUBP1 and FUBP2, which are primarily intranuclear, FUBP3 is primarily cytoplasmic, with only a small portion present in the nucleus. While the biological function of FUBP3 remains largely unknown, a recent study showed that it suppresses NLRP3 expression in macrophages by binding to a long noncoding RNA released by mycobacteria (Yao et al., 2022).
 To further validate FUBP3 binding to the NLRP3 minimal promoter (the Del5 fragment), we conducted another pull-down experiment using Del5 as the bait. Western blot analysis clearly demonstrated a specific interaction between FUBP3 and Del5, while no binding to an irrelevant control DNA was observed. Notably, labeling the bait at either the 5′ or 3′ end did not affect its binding affinity for FUBP3 ( B).
To further validate FUBP3 binding to the NLRP3 promoter, an EMSA was conducted using biotin-labeled probes corresponding to the minimal promoter (Del5). These probes were incubated with or without Neuro2A nuclear extract, and the protein–probe complexes were separated on native gels. The labeled probes were then detected either on a nylon membrane (regular EMSA) or on a nitrocellulose membrane by western blot to specifically detect FUBP3 (EMSA–western blot; Lu et al., 2023). Unexpectedly, when both 5′ ends of the double-stranded probe were initially labeled, no upshifted probe band was observed on the nylon membrane. However, the EMSA–western blot revealed that the probe was able to pull FUBP3 protein into the gel ( C). We hypothesized that FUBP3 may have trimmed the 5′ ends of the probe, removing the biotin labels from the binding sites, while in the pull-down assay, the biotin was protected from trimming by avidin binding to the beads, allowing for the detection of the interaction. Despite this putative trimming activity, FUBP3 binding to the probe was not affected, explaining the presence of the FUBP3 protein band on the nitrocellulose membrane. This speculation is supported by a previous study showing that FUBP1 can bind to a 5′ end–P32-labeled single-stranded DNA fragment from the c-Myc promoter in a regular EMSA (Duncan et al., 1994).
When the identical single-stranded DNA fragment was labeled with biotin at its 5′ end, the shift in fragment migration during standard EMSA remained undetectable. However, the FUBP1 band was clearly visible on the EMS–western blot ( D). This suggests that, if 5′ end trimming occurs, this activity may be conserved among FUBP family members. To further confirm FUBP3 binding to the promoter using regular EMSA, we labeled the probe at its 3’ ends with biotin. The results indicated a binding interaction between the probe and FUBP3 ( E). Introduction of an unlabeled probe into the reaction mixture abrogated the biotin-labeled upshift in the probe signal in a dose-dependent manner ( E). To unequivocally demonstrate that FUBP3 was responsible for the upshift, a super-shift assay was conducted by combining nuclear extract, the 3′-labeled probe, and an anti-FUBP3 antibody. The presence of the FUBP3 antibody, but not the control (normal IgG), depleted the upshifted probe in the standard EMSA ( E). Given that FUBP family proteins bind to single-stranded nucleic acids, we also used 3’-labeled sense and antisense strands from the minimal promoter as probes in regular EMSA. Neither of these single-stranded probes interacted with FUBP3 ( E).

## FUBP3 is required for amyloid-β-induced NLRP3 expression in neurons
To determine whether FUBP3 is required for NLRP3 promoter activity, we suppressed FUBP3 expression in Neuro2A cells using an siRNA, then transfected the cells with a plasmid containing Del5. Luciferase assay revealed that Del5 promoter activity was significantly decreased when FUBP3 expression was knocked down compared with in control siRNA–expressing Neuro2A cells ( A).
 Neuro2A cells express endogenous NLRP3 mRNA and protein, and therefore we first suppressed FUBP3 and examined NLRP3 expression in this cell line. Although FUBP3 protein expression was markedly decreased, there was no reduction in NLRP3 mRNA levels, and NLRP3 protein expression was unaffected by FUBP3 inhibition ( B). We further treated Neuro2A cells overnight with freshly prepared Aβ1–42 diluted in DMSO to a concentration of 1 μM to induce oligomerization. Although we did not observe any changes in NLRP3 mRNA or NLRP3 protein expression in cells expressing the control siRNA ( B), in the presence of Aβ, FUBP3 knockdown strongly reduced NLRP3 expression at both the mRNA and protein level ( B).
To inhibit FUBP3 expression, primary neurons were infected with AAV9 encoding an FUBP3-targeting shRNA whose expression was driven by the neuron-specific synapsin-1 promoter (syn1). As seen in Neuro2A cells, Aβ did not appear to upregulate NLRP3 expression, and the FUBP3 shRNA inhibited NLRP3 expression only when Aβ was added to the culture ( C). In addition to Aβ, we tested whether pro-inflammatory cytokines would induce FUBP3-dependent NLRP3 expression in neurons. Because the conditioned medium derived from activated microglia enhances tau phosphorylation in neurons via pro-inflammatory cytokines, we asked whether these cytokines would stimulate neuronal NLRP3 to promote tau phosphorylation. To this end, we added mixed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from five patients with viral encephalitis to the PNWT cultures (neurobasal medium/B27: CSF = 4:1 (v/v), phosphate buffered saline (PBS) as control). However, FUBP3 knockdown still failed to affect NLRP3 expression in these neurons, even in the presence of CSF ( D). Hence, Aβ appears to be required for FUBP3-dependent induction of NLRP3 expression.
Aβ is produced and secreted at high levels in PNAPP. FUBP3 knockdown in these neurons directly reduced NLRP3 protein levels in the absence of Aβ ( E). As primary neuron cultures may be contaminated by other cell types, mostly astrocytes, which can confound the results, we stained PNWT and PNAPP with anti-NeuN (a neuronal cell marker) and anti-FUBP3 antibodies. In both types of primary neuron cultures, FUBP3 was preferentially expressed in neurons, and only very low levels of FUBP3 staining were observed in nonneuronal cells ( F). Therefore, the preferential expression of FUBP3 in neurons and the neuron-specific expression of the FUBP3 shRNA ensured that the effects of FUBP3 knockdown were derived from neurons, and not from contaminating cells.
In young (3 months old) wild-type mice, FUBP3 was very weakly expressed in the majority of neurons in the cortex ( G). In other brain regions such as the hippocampus, only a few FUBP3-positive neurons were observed (data not shown). In the brains of 8-month-old APP/PS1ΔE9 mice, FUBP3 was markedly upregulated in almost all cortical neurons ( G). Because aging is the most important risk factor for developing AD (Sita et al., 2023; Gharpure et al., 2024), we also stained the brains of 20-month-old wild-type mice for FUBP3. Robust FUBP3 expression was detected in essentially all neurons in the cortex and hippocampus ( G). However, even in aged mice, the neuronal NLRP3 signal was undetectable above the background (Additional Figure 1). Therefore, even if increased FUBP3 expression upregulates neuronal NLRP3 expression in the absence of Aβ in vivo, the upregulation is too weak to be detected by conventional immunohistochemistry. We also did not observe FUBP3 in nonneuronal cells in the brain tissue, probably also because of the low sensitivity of immunohistochemistry. However, when examined in vitro, FUBP3 expression in BV2 cells was comparable to or stronger than that observed in PNWT cells ( H).

## FUBP3 nuclear translocation is unlikely not required for NLRP3 expression in the presence of amyloid-β
In addition to serving as transcription factors, members of the FUBP family bind to single-stranded RNA, regulating RNA processing and translation. Thus, FUBP3 could regulateNLRP3 expression at multiple levels. Given that FUBP3 primarily localizes to the cytoplasm, if it functions as a transcription factor for NLRP3 in the presence of Aβ, it might relocate to the nucleus upon exposure to Aβ. However, Aβ does not upregulate FUBP3 expression in neurons, indicating that FUBP3 upregulation is not sufficient to increase its nuclear translocation and subsequent transcriptional activity. To assess the role of FUBP3 upregulation on NLRP3 expression in vitro, FLAG-tagged FUBP3 was overexpressed in Neuro2A cells. When overexpressed to levels comparable to endogenous FUBP3, a mild increase in NLRP3 expression was observed ( A). However, even when endogenous FUBP3 was expressed and exogenous FUBP3 was overexpressed, the majority of the FUBP3 remained cytoplasmic, and there was no increase in nuclear FUBP3 ( B). At higher overexpression levels where endogenous FUBP3 became comparatively negligible, the overexpressed FUBP3 accumulated in the nucleus ( B). This accumulation, however, appeared to be an artifact of high-level overexpression, as NLRP3 expression remained unchanged under these conditions ( A). Therefore, while an increase in FUBP3 at physiologically relevant levels contributes to NLRP3 expression, nuclear accumulation of FUBP3 does not seem to be essential for it to promote NLRP3 expression. Furthermore, treatment with Aβ did not result in FUBP3 upregulation in Neuro2A cells or PNWT ( C).
 To investigate whether Aβ can induce FUBP3 translocation into the nucleus without increasing its overall expression, we treated Neuro2A cells with Aβ and performed a nuclear fractionation assay. Upon Aβ treatment, we observed a mild but significant increase in the ratio of nuclear FUBP3 to cytoplasmic FUBP3 ( D). However, in PNWT, FUBP3 appeared to be diffusely distributed throughout most neurons, lacking a specific localization pattern ( E). Notably, a subset of neurons with larger soma showed clear enrichment of FUBP3 in the nucleus ( E). Treatment with Aβ resulted in an increase in the percentage of neurons exhibiting nuclear FUBP3 enrichment, ranging from ~20% to 70%. This response to Aβ was variable even among neurons from littermate embryos. Interestingly, in PNAPP, where Aβ production is elevated and FUBP3 directly regulates NLRP3 expression, no nuclear accumulation of FUBP3 was observed ( F). These findings suggest that Aβ likely induces FUBP3-dependent NLRP3 expression by activating FUBP3, and that nuclear accumulation of FUBP3 beyond basal levels is not required for NLRP3 expression in the presence of Aβ.
Taken together, our results indicate that, although FUBP3 upregulation promoted NLRP3 expression in Neuro2A cells, Aβ did not increase FUBP3 or NLRP3 expression. Aβ may induce FUBP3 nuclear translocation under some conditions, but this translocation is not necessary for FUBP3-dependent NLRP3 expression.

## FUBP3 is required for interleukin-1β secretion and tau phosphorylation
As FUBP3 is indispensable for maintaining NLRP3 protein levels in PNAPP, we next asked whether N3I function was impaired in FUBP3-deficient PNAPP. Although cleaved caspase-1 was undetectable in neurons, and the IL1β level in conditioned media from PNAPP was low (7.177 ± 0.6557 pg/mL), ELISA showed that the expression of FUBP3-targeting shRNA in PNAPP significantly decreased the amount of secreted IL1β (4.817 ± 1.328 pg/mL, ~30% reduction) ( A), suggesting compromised IL1β maturation and/or secretion. While the reduced IL1β secretion might also result from lower IL1b expression, at least in Neuro2A cells, IL1b mRNA levels were unaffected by FUBP3 knockdown.
 To investigate whether FUBP3 inhibition could reduce tau phosphorylation through downregulating Aβ-induced NLRP3 expression, we infected PNAPP with an AAV expressing the FUBP3-targeting shRNA. Similar to NLRP3 inhibition, FUBP3 knockdown significantly reduced p-tau181 and p-tau202/205 levels ( B). As tau phosphorylation at these sites can be catalyzed by a number of kinases such as CaMKIIα (Kobayashi et al., 2017; Liang et al., 2023), we examined the level of phosphorylated CAMKIIα, which is the active form of CaMKIIα ( B). However, no change was found. Similarly, the levels of PME1, which is a phosphatase inhibitor that is induced by conditioned medium from microglia to prevent tau dephosphorylation (Kauko et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2021), were not changed by FUBP3 knockdown ( B).
Together, these findings suggest that FUBP3 in Aβ-producing neurons is required for IL1β secretion and tau phosphorylation; however, it remained unclear whether these effects are mediated by NLRP3.

## FUBP3 is involved in the immune response in Neuro2A cells
FUBP3 function has not been well documented, and therefore we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of FUBP3 in Neuro2A cells with or without Aβ treatment, followed by transcriptomic analysis of the cells. Aβ treatment and FUBP3 knockdown did not affect overall mRNA expression ( A). Differentially expressed genes were defined as |log2(fold change)| > 1 and P < 0.05 (Additional Tables 1–4). In the absence of Aβ, FUBP3 knockdown altered the expression levels of 541 genes, and Aβ treatment without FUBP3 knockdown altered the expression level of 157 genes. In the presence of Aβ, FUBP3 silencing affected 493 genes, one of which was NLRP3, whose mRNA levels were drastically reduced by about ~88% ( B and Additional Table 2). This reduction in NLRP3 mRNA expression was not observed in FUBP3-deficient Neuro2A cells that were not exposed to Aβ (Additional Table 4). Unexpectedly, another member of the NLRP family, the expression of NLRP6, which also forms an inflammasome with ASC and caspase-1 (Lima et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021), showed identical changes to NLRP3 in response to Aβ exposure and FUBP3 knockdown (Additional Tables 2 and 4). The expression levels of several differentially expressed genes identified by the transcriptome analysis, including NLRP6, GSDMD, PPP1R1A, and IL1b, were confirmed by quantitative PCR ( C). Notably, the expression of PPP1R1A, which encodes the inhibitory regulatory subunit of protein phosphatase 1 (PP1), was significantly reduced upon FUBP3 knockdown in Aβ-treated, but not in naïve, Neuro2A cells. Similar to NLRP3 and NLRP6, PPP1R1A expression was unaffected by Aβ treatment in Neuro2A cells transfected with the control siRNA ( C and Additional Tables 1, 2 and 4). A reduction in PPP1R1A expression in PNAPP upon FUBP3 knockdown was also observed (P = 0.0032; D). This decrease in PP1 expression may account for the decrease in phosphorylated tau levels. KEGG analysis indicated that, with or without Aβ treatment, FUBP3 knockdown altered the expression of genes enriched in the immune system and immune/infectious diseases ( E). Enrichment of genes in the same categories was also found in acute Aβ-treated Neuro2A cells, regardless of manipulation of FUBP3 expression levels. Genes involved in cancer and signaling were also enriched ( E). KEGG pathway analysis also revealed that, in Aβ-treated Neuro2A cells in which FUBP3 had been knocked down, pathways involved in the immune system and immune diseases were among the most affected pathways ( F). Additionally, FUBP3 knockdown affected genes associated with the endocrine, system regardless of Aβ exposure ( F).
 In addition to altering the expression of genes involved in immunity, GSEA using the Reactome database suggested downregulation of genes that activate ATM- and Rad3-related (ATR) in response to replication stresses and homology-dependent repair (HDR) through homologous recombination in Aβ-treated and FUBP3-deficient cells compared with Aβ-treated and control siRNA–expressing cells ( G). These FUBP3 functions might be relevant to its DNA 5’ end–trimming activity.

## Discussion
NLRP3 and N3I are strong candidates for mediating the transition from NP to NFT. NLRP3 is crucial for the adverse effects of NP/Aβ in a mouse model of AD exhibiting NPs, while NLRP3 deficiency decreases tauopathy in the brains of mice expressing mutant tau (Ising et al., 2019; Stancu et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024). Additionally, tauopathy has been reported in other neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis (MS), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), which are associated with N3I activation (Gugliandolo et al., 2018; Van Schoor et al., 2022; Amo-Aparicio et al., 2023; Seyedsadr et al., 2023; Shu et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2024). These findings suggest a close association between N3I and tauopathy, making N3I a potential target for preventing tau pathology in neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, however, these earlier studies were conducted on animals in which NLRP3 had been deleted. Although gene deletion is effective in minimizing artifacts and variations in biological studies, it may lead to exaggerated conclusions regarding the function and disease relevance of the depleted gene in medical research. In contrast, in our study, we used shRNA to partially suppress NLRP3, achieving a 50% knockdown in NLRP3 expression in primary cells. This approach may provide a more accurate representation of NLRP3’s function in diseases while avoiding the potential effects of NLRP3 deletion on brain development, thus enhancing the reliability of conclusions regarding the etiologies of geriatric diseases.
Activated microglial N3I may contribute to the formation of NFTs in neurons. It is hypothesized that upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL1β, secreted by microglia upon N3I activation, act as mediators between microglia and neurons, promoting tau phosphorylation (Ising et al., 2019; Beltran-Lobo et al., 2023). However, because these cytokines are secreted into the interstitial fluid and the CSF, and may diffuse to a wide area of the brain, how these cytokines specifically affect some neurons but not others (determining the type of disease that develops) remains unclear. Deleting NLRP3 in mice is expected to alleviate tauopathy in models of multiple neurodegenerative diseases. If Aβ induces tauopathy in neurons through activation of microglial N3I, which in turn promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, neurons such as dopaminergic neurons or motor neurons, for example, may also be affected. This would lead to AD comorbidity with PD, MS, and ALS; however, it is uncommon for patients with AD to simultaneously have other diseases. Our finding that NLRP3 and N3I are also present in neurons, where they mediate IL1β secretion and tau phosphorylation, offers an alternative explanation for the involvement of N3I in different neurodegenerative diseases. The type of disease may be intrinsically determined by the neurons themselves, where tau is highly expressed. Neurons exhibiting higher N3I activity may be more susceptible to NFT formation and pyroptosis. The pro-inflammatory cytokines released by these neurons, along with those from microglia, could exacerbate and propagate the immune response.
Previous studies have demonstrated that neurons also play a crucial role in immunity. In response to double-stranded RNA, neurons secrete various pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, neurons are capable of secreting both clusterin and Aβ, which coat the nuclei of dead or dying cells. These actions may serve as a mechanism to prevent autoimmunity and could potentially form NP seeds (Préhaud et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2022).
Unsurprisingly, N3I thus plays a similar role in neurons (promoting the immune response) to the role that it plays in microglia or macrophages in immune responses. Unexpectedly, however, we found that NLRP3 inhibition reduced tau phosphorylation in neurons. The exact mechanism by which the NLRP3 inflammasome regulates tau phosphorylation remains unclear. While our results suggest that FUBP3 knockdown decreases expression of the phosphatase inhibitor PPP1R1A, potentially facilitating tau dephosphorylation by reducing NLRP3 activity, the observed reduction in phosphorylated tau upon FUBP3 loss or NLRP3 inhibition could also result from alterations in multiple molecular pathways. Alternatively, because cytokines have been shown to induce tau phosphorylation (Ising et al., 2019), the lower levels of neuron-secreted cytokines observed in response to NLRP3 inhibition and FUBP3 knockdown could contribute to the reduced tau phosphorylation. Notably, the duration of NLRP3 inhibition in our experiments was limited to 4 hours, and the impact on extracellular cytokine levels was not tested. Furthermore, our findings suggest that, at physiologically relevant levels (such as in the CSF of patients with viral encephalitis), cytokines do not increase p-tau181 or p-tau202/205 levels. Therefore, the downstream effectors of the NLRP3 inflammasome, other than inflammatory cytokines, in neurons remain to be identified.
Using biochemical methods, we identified FUBP3 as an NLRP3 transcription factor. The requirement of FUBP3 for NLRP3 expression was only clear when Aβ was present. Aβ slightly induced FUBP3 translocation into the nucleus, where FUBP3 could exert its function as a transcription factor. However, FUBP3 was not enriched in the nucleus in PNAPP, where FUBP3 can directly regulate NLRP3 expression, suggesting that further nuclear translocation of FUBP3 beyond basal level appeared to be dispensable for NLRP3 expression. Aβ may activate FUBP3 through other mechanisms, such as enabling FUBP3 to access response elements in the genome. FUBP3 appeared to be preferentially expressed in neurons in both primary cell cultures and mouse brain tissue. At this stage, it is unclear why Aβ triggered FUBP3-dependent NLRP3 expression without upregulating FUBP3, but in the brains of AD mice, neuronal FUBP3 expression increased markedly. We used freshly prepared Aβ monomers to treat the neurons, and Aβ in AD mouse brains may oligomerize into more toxic Aβ species, suggesting that different forms of Aβ may affect FUBP3 expression differently. Furthermore, the neuroinflammation mediated by glial cells in AD mouse brains may promote neuronal FUBP3 expression.
NLRP3 requiring FUBP3 for expression only in the presence of Aβ suggests that exposure to Aβ fundamentally altered the regulatory mechanism of NLRP3 gene expression, even though acute Aβ treatment did not upregulate NLRP3 expression. Because AD pathogenesis is a chronic process, the long-term effect of Aβ on NLRP3 expression in neurons remains to be examined. Furthermore, in vivo evidence is needed to validate FUBP3 as an Aβ-dependent transcription factor for NLRP3 because we did not clearly detect NLRP3 in neurons, even in naïve AD model mice. However, we did find that oxidative stress induced hydrogen peroxide injection strongly enhanced neuronal NLRP3 expression in APP/PS1ΔE9 AD model mice. FUBP3 may play a role in upregulating neuronal NLRP3 expression under these conditions.
While we found that FUBP3 is a transcription factor for Aβ-induced NLRP3 expression in neurons, it may also regulate NLRP3 expression at later stages, such as RNA processing and translation, similar to other FUBP family members. It is unclear at this time which function of FUBP3 is more important for NLRP3 expression, although the promoter activity assay suggests that FUBP3 could bind to and promote activity of the minimal promoter. Of note, FUBP3-dependent promoter activity, as indicated by the luciferase assay results, did not require exposure to Aβ. We speculate that, in the absence of Aβ, this minimal promoter sequence is not spatially accessible to FUBP3, or that other inhibitory mechanisms such as repressors and epigenetic modifications prevent FUBP3 binding to the response element, and that exposure to Aβ lifts these restrictions. However, when the minimal promoter was assessed as part of an artificial construct that had little spatial hindrance, no sites for repressor binding, and limited epigenetic modification, FUBP3 was able to directly interact with the minimal promoter and promote luciferase expression.
An unexpected observation from our study was that FUBP3, as well as FUBP1, may trim the 5′ ends of single-stranded and double-stranded DNA. Although this putative activity requires further investigation, if it does indeed occur then the FUBP family may be involved in the stress-induced cell responses, as exposure of 5′ ends in the genome is normally rare. When stressors such as UV, for example, induce genomic DNA breaks, FUBP family members might participate in DNA repair and enhance the expression of genes required for cell survival. Another source of exposed 5′ ends is nucleic acids from infecting microbes that also cause stress to cells. FUBP3 has been shown to bind with a long non-coding RNA produced by mycobacteria to suppress N3I in macrophages in the periphery (Yao et al., 2022). Given the preferential expression of FUBP3 in neurons under unhealthy conditions in vivo, FUBP3 may participate in a neuron-specific defense mechanism against stress. As FUBP3 regulates NLRP3 expression, and both NPs and NFTs are closely related to N3I, the upregulation of FUBP3 expression in cells associated with these AD pathologies could be a long-term side effect of a repeated neuronal defense response. In line with this speculation, APP upregulation has been demonstrated to protect injured neurons (Hefter and Draguhn, 2017), and tau has been shown to translocate from neurons into microglia to elicit immune responses against invading nucleic acids (Udeochu et al., 2023). These findings point to a potential relationship between AD development and neuronal immune/defense responses. However, in vivo evidence is needed to confirm the conclusions from this study, and the mechanisms by which neuronal NLRP3 regulates tau phosphorylation and the Aβ-dependent functions of FUBP3 should be investigated.
In summary, our findings suggest that neuronal FUBP3 responds to Aβ, and in turn regulates NLRP3 expression, which may facilitate tauopathy in AD. Given the low level of FUBP3 expression in healthy brains and the specific upregulation of FUBP3 in neurons in the brains of AD model mice and aged mice, FUBP3 could be a safe therapeutic target for preventing AD progression.
In this study, we demonstrated that NLRP3 is expressed in neurons and regulates tau phosphorylation. Both basal and induced NLRP3 expression were observed in neurons in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we identified FUBP3 as a crucial transcription factor for NLRP3 expression in neurons, specifically in the presence of Aβ. In primary neurons from AD model mice, FUBP3 was essential for IL1β secretion and tau phosphorylation. Similar to N3I, FUBP3 may play a role in neuron-mediated immune responses and stress-induced responses through its putative DNA binding and 5′-end trimming activities. Given the elevated neuronal expression of FUBP3 in aged brains and brains affected by AD, targeting neuronal FUBP3 is a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent AD progression, particularly among patients with NP but not NFT accumulation in the brain. In this study, although we reported the expression of NLRP3 in neurons in vitro and in AD brains under some conditions, neuronal NLRP3 was not clearly detected in AD brains without further stimuli. It could be because of the low sensitivity of the antibody, the relatively low expression of neuronal NLRP, and the relatively high background signal during immunofluorescence. Moreover, how Aβ induces FUBP3-dependent NLRP3 expression in neurons remains to be investigated.
