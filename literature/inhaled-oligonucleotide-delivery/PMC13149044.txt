Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a nuclear receptor that plays a pivotal role in regulating bile acid, lipid, and glucose metabolism, making it an attractive therapeutic target for liver and metabolic diseases. In this study, we investigated the effects of MHY5456, a synthetic agonist of FXR, on hepatic metabolism and fibrosis. MHY5456 enhanced the transcriptional activity of FXR in a concentration-dependent manner. Treatment of AC2F rat liver-derived cells with MHY5456 resulted in the downregulation of genes involved in lipid accumulation and an upregulation of mitochondrial-related gene expression. Additionally, MHY5456 significantly reduced oleic acid (OA)-induced lipid accumulation. MHY5456 significantly suppressed the expression of fibrosis-related genes and proteins. In vivo, administration of MHY5456 to mice fed a methionine-choline-deficient (MCD) diet alleviated hepatic fibrosis, inflammation, and lipid accumulation. These results show that FXR activation by MHY5456 modulates lipid metabolism and fibrotic pathways, suggesting its potential as a pharmacological candidate for liver and metabolic disorders, including MASLD. Further pharmacological and toxicological studies are needed to confirm its therapeutic relevance.

## INTRODUCTION
MASLD is characterized by the abnormal accumulation of excess fat in the liver in the absence of significant alcohol consumption, certain drugs, or other liver diseases such as viral hepatitis ( ). MASLD is driven by multiple factors, including obesity, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, microbial imbalance, and genetic mutations ( ; ; ). With an estimated global prevalence of 25%, MASLD has emerged as the most common liver disease, posing significant public health challenges ( ). The disease progresses through distinct stages, starting with simple steatosis, where triglyceride (TG) accumulation occurs in hepatocytes without inflammation. Over time, some individuals may develop metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), characterized by liver inflammation, hepatocellular injury, and concurrent fat accumulation. This stage can lead to structural damage, fibrosis, and, in severe cases, cirrhosis, which may eventually progress to hepatocellular carcinoma ( ). Effective management of MASLD is critical, as timely intervention during the early stages can prevent the progression to severe liver complications. Current therapeutic strategies include dietary modifications, weight management, and pharmacological agents such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide), dual or triple incretin receptor agonists (GLP-1/GIP, GLP-1/GIP/glucagon), FXR agonists, and thyroid hormone receptor-beta (THR-β) agonists, which aim to improve metabolic dysfunction, reduce steatosis, and attenuate inflammation and fibrosis in MASH ( ; ).
Lipid metabolism refers to the processes through which the body synthesizes, stores, and breaks down lipids, including fatty acids, TGs, and cholesterol. In the liver, this regulation is critical for maintaining energy balance and overall metabolic health ( ). Dysregulation of lipid metabolism leads to an accumulation of excess lipids, particularly TGs, which is a central feature of many metabolic diseases, including MASLD ( ). Fibrosis, on the other hand, is a pathological response to chronic liver injury, characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, such as collagen, in liver tissue. This process is driven by the activation of HSCs and is closely associated with ongoing inflammation and tissue damage ( ). The relationship between lipid metabolism and fibrosis is bidirectional; lipid accumulation not only initiates inflammation but also activates fibrogenic pathways, driving the progression of fibrosis ( ). Excessive lipid deposition can promote oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokine release, which in turn stimulates the activation of HSCs and the deposition of collagen. Conversely, fibrosis exacerbates metabolic dysfunction by impairing liver function, further disrupting lipid metabolism ( ). Therefore, targeting both lipid metabolism and fibrosis simultaneously may provide a promising therapeutic approach to mitigate the progression of liver diseases associated with metabolic dysfunction ( ).
FXR, encoded by the NR1H4 gene, is a key nuclear receptor involved in regulating bile acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and inflammation, particularly in the liver and intestines ( ). FXR controls bile acid production, excretion, and reabsorption to maintain bile acid homeostasis, which is crucial for digestion and metabolic health. It also regulates lipid metabolism by modulating the synthesis, storage, and degradation of lipids, thus ensuring lipid balance ( ). Through its role in glucose metabolism, FXR helps stabilize blood glucose levels and mitigate insulin resistance, a hallmark of metabolic diseases such as MASLD ( ). Additionally, FXR has protective effects on the liver by modulating inflammation, immune responses, and hepatocellular survival, which are vital for maintaining liver health and preventing liver damage ( ; ). Beyond its metabolic functions, FXR interacts with other key regulators, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) and retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα), to coordinate a wide array of physiological processes. FXR’s role in bile acid and lipid metabolism is further supported by the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (TGR5), which primarily responds to secondary bile acids. TGR5 regulates energy metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and inflammation. While both FXR and TGR5 play essential roles in bile acid signaling, they operate through different mechanisms, with FXR acting as a nuclear receptor and TGR5 utilizing G protein-mediated signaling pathways ( ; ). Recent human studies have shown that FXR expression is reduced in the liver of patients with MASLD, which may contribute to dysregulated bile acid homeostasis, lipogenesis, and inflammation ( ). Furthermore, in MASH patients, decreased FXR signaling has been implicated in enhanced inflammatory responses, suggesting a pathogenic role for FXR downregulation in disease progression ( ).
Recent preclinical and clinical studies have highlighted the potential of FXR agonists in improving hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. However, existing FXR-targeted compounds such as obeticholic acid ( ) and other experimental agents have shown limitations related to efficacy, tolerability, or adverse effects ( ; ), underscoring the need for novel molecules with improved profiles ( ). In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of MHY5456, an agonist designed to activate FXR, in addressing abnormal lipid metabolism and fibrosis. Through a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments, the present study demonstrates that MHY5456 effectively regulates lipid accumulation and exhibits anti-fibrotic properties. In mice with MCD diet-induced lipid accumulation and fibrosis, MHY5456 treatment significantly reduced liver fat content, fibrosis, inflammation, and tissue damage. In AC2F rat liver-derived cell models, MHY5456 enhanced β-oxidation and markedly decreased lipid accumulation induced by OA. Furthermore, in LX2 hepatic stellate cells, MHY5456 suppressed fibrosis induced by TGF-β stimulation. These findings highlight the potential of FXR activation as a therapeutic strategy for alleviating the progression of MASLD, offering a promising avenue for the development of more effective treatments for metabolic liver diseases. Accordingly, we sought to test whether MHY5456 could simultaneously regulate lipid metabolism and reduce fibrosis via FXR activation.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Animals and experimental design
Male C57BL/6J mice aged 9 weeks were obtained from Hyochang Science (Daegu, Korea) and housed in the laboratory animal facility at Pusan National University (PNU). All animal procedures were approved by the Pusan National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PNU-IACUC, approval No. PNU-2024-0412) and conducted in accordance with the PNU animal care guidelines. The mice were maintained under standard conditions: a 12-h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 23 ± 2°C, relative humidity of 60 ± 5%, and ad libitum access to food and water.
The effects of MHY5456 on hepatic lipid accumulation, fibrosis, and inflammation were evaluated using a MCD diet-induced mouse model of MASH. Mice were administered MHY5456 orally at a dose of 5 mg/kg/day for 3 weeks during the MCD diet feeding. On day 22, animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Liver tissues were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered paraformaldehyde for histological evaluation, while additional liver samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80°C for long-term storage and subsequent western blotting, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and biochemical analyses.

## Cell culture experiments
AC2F rat liver-derived cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL and 10 μg/mL, respectively). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. To evaluate the FXR agonist activity of MHY5456, cells were treated with MHY5456 alone, and transcriptional activity as well as related gene expression were analyzed. To assess the inhibitory effect of MHY5456 on OA-induced lipid accumulation, cells were pretreated with MHY5456 for 30 min, followed by treatment with 30 μM OA for 24 h. After treatment, lipid accumulation was assessed by Oil Red O (ORO) staining and TG assay.
To investigate the anti-fibrotic effects of MHY5456, experiments were conducted using LX2 human HSCs, cultured under the same conditions as the AC2F cells. After pretreatment with MHY5456 for 30 min, cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 24 h. RNA and protein samples were then collected for analysis of fibrosis-related markers.

## Cell viability, proliferation and cytotoxicity assay
The cytotoxicity of MHY5456 was evaluated using the EZ-Cytox Cell Viability Assay Kit (DoGen Bio, Seoul, Korea; catalog number EZ-3000). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of MHY5456 for 24 h. Following the treatment period, the EZ-Cytox reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm after 30 min incubation. Control values were set to 100, and all other experimental values were normalized accordingly. This assay is based on the reduction of a water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST) by metabolically active viable cells.

## Triglyceride (TG) assay
TG levels in liver tissue and cells were measured using a commercially available PicoSensTM Triglyceride Assay Kit (BIOMAX, Guri, Korea; catalog number BM-TGR-100), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, liver tissue and cell samples were homogenized in a 5% nonyl phenoxypolyethoxylethanol (NP-40) solution. The samples were then heated to 90°C and subsequently cooled to room temperature, with this process repeated once more. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. TG levels were quantified in the supernatant and normalized to the protein concentration.

## RNA extraction
Gene expression in tissue and cell samples was analyzed using qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA extraction process involved homogenizing liver tissue or cells in TRIzol reagent, followed by the addition of 200 μL of chloroform to the 1 mL homogenized sample. After vigorous shaking and incubating for 15 min, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the aqueous phase (supernatant) was carefully transferred to new tubes. An equal volume of isopropanol was then added to the supernatant, followed by incubation for 15 min and subsequent centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellets were washed once with 75% ethanol before being air-dried and dissolved in diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. The samples were then heated at 56°C for 10 min.

## Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
The isolated RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using a cDNA synthesis kit from GenDEPOT (Katy, TX, USA). For qRT-PCR analysis, SYBR Green Master Mix (BIOLINE, Taunton, MA, USA) and a CFX Connect System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) were employed. Primer sequences for mouse, rat, and human genes were designed using the Primer3Plus program and are provided in . The qRT-PCR data were analyzed using the widely used 2-ΔΔCt method for relative quantification, which involves calculating the fold change in gene expression relative to a control sample after normalization to an internal reference gene. All primer sequences were experimentally validated prior to analysis, and melting curve analysis confirmed a single specific amplification peak for each primer pair, indicating primer specificity and appropriate amplification efficiency.

## Protein extraction
To maintain protein stability, protein extraction procedures were performed at a temperature range of 0-4°C. For liver tissue protein extraction, ProEXTM CETi protein extract solution (Translab, Daejeon, Korea) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Xpert Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Solution [100X], GenDEPOT) and a phosphatase inhibitor was used according to the manufacturer’s guidelines to prevent protein degradation and dephosphorylation. Total cellular proteins were extracted from cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was determined using the PierceTM BCA (bicinchoninic acid) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; catalog number 23227) for accurate quantification.

## Western blotting analysis
For western blotting analysis, the extracted proteins (ranging from 5 to 20 μg) were mixed with 4X sample buffer (Bio-Rad; catalog number 1610747) and heated to 100°C for 5 min to denature the proteins. Equal amounts of protein samples were then separated using 6%-10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the proteins were transferred electrophoretically onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (GVS, Sanford, ME, USA) using the Bio-Rad Western system. The PVDF membranes were blocked for 2 h with either 5% skim milk or 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking buffer. After blocking, the membranes were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS, 50 mM Tris, 137 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-Tween 20) for 10 min each. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies (diluted 1:500 to 1:2000), as listed in . The next day, the membranes were washed three more times with TBS-Tween 20 for 10 min each, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA; catalog numbers GTX213111-01, GTX213110-01) at a dilution of 1:10,000 at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoblot signals were detected using Western Bright Peroxide Solution (Advansta, San Jose, CA, USA) and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All western blotting experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

## Histological analysis
Liver tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently prepared for paraffin embedding. Sections were then stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) to examine cellular structures and evaluate histological changes. H&E staining is a widely used technique for general tissue examination. To assess liver fibrosis and damage, Sirius Red (SR) staining was performed using a commercially available kit (VitroVivo Biotech, Rockville, MD, USA; catalog number VB-3017). SR staining specifically highlights collagen fibers, which are indicative of fibrosis. Images were acquired using a microscope (Leam Solution, Seoul, Korea; catalog number LS30).

## Oil Red O (ORO) staining
ORO staining was performed to detect lipid droplets such as TGs and neutral lipids. For tissue section staining, frozen sections were first prepared using Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound to prevent lipid loss. The tissue was briefly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 24 h. After embedding in OCT compound, the tissue was stored at –80°C. To remove the OCT compound, the tissue sections were soaked in distilled water before proceeding with ORO staining. For cell staining, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and then subjected to ORO staining. ORO staining was conducted using propylene glycol as a solvent. Samples were treated twice with 100% propylene glycol for 5 min each, followed by immersion in ORO solution (0.7% ORO in propylene glycol) for 7 min. The samples were then treated with 85% propylene glycol for 4 min, washed with distilled water, and counterstained with hematoxylin to stain the nuclei. Images were acquired using a microscope (LS30).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the Avidin/Biotin Blocking Kit (Vector; catalog number SP-2001), M.O.M.® (Mouse on Mouse) Elite® Immunodetection Kit, Peroxidase (Vector, Burlingame, CA, USA; catalog number PK-2200), and ImmPACT® DAB Substrate Kit, Peroxidase (Vector; catalog number SK-4105). Liver tissue sections were heated at 60°C for 30 min to melt paraffin, immersed in xylene to remove residual paraffin, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 10 min. Sections were sequentially treated with avidin and biotin blocking solutions for 30 min each, followed by M.O.M. mouse IgG blocking solution for 1 h. Primary antibody against α-SMA (Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA, 1:100, in M.O.M. diluent solution) was applied overnight at 4°C. After incubation with M.O.M. biotinylated anti-mouse IgG reagent for 10 min, sections were treated with the VECTASTAIN Elite reagent (prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol) for 5 min. Color development was achieved using the 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution for 5 min. Finally, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dried at 60°C for approximately 30 min, and mounted with a hydrophobic medium.

## Measurement of transcriptional activity
To assess FXR transcriptional activity in response to MHY5456, a luciferase reporter assay was performed. AC2F cells were transfected with FXR promoter-firefly luciferase (FLUC) plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following transfection, the cells were treated with various concentrations of MHY5456. The transcriptional activity of FXR was measured using the One-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; catalog number E6120). Luminescence was measured as an indicator of FXR transcriptional activity with a luminescence plate reader (Berthold Technologies GmbH & Co., Bad Wildbad, Germany).

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
IF was employed to visualize protein expression through specific interactions between primary and secondary antibodies. Cells were initially fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by three washes with ice-cold PBS. After fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. To reduce nonspecific antibody binding, cells were blocked with a solution containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody α-SMA (Santa Cruz; catalog number sc-32251), diluted 1:100 in blocking buffer. After washing off unbound antibodies with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a secondary antibody (Goat anti-Mouse IgG(H+L) Cross-Absorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa FluorTM 488, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; catalog number A11001) conjugated with a fluorescent tag, in the dark. To visualize cell nuclei, cells were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in PBS for 5 min. Finally, images of the stained cells were captured using a fluorescence microscope (LS30).

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection
To determine whether the inhibitory effect of MHY5456 on lipid accumulation was dependent on FXR, AC2F rat liver-derived cells were transfected with FXR-specific siRNA purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, Iowa, USA). Transfection was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent from Invitrogen, and the final concentration of FXR siRNA was 10 nM. Twenty-three hours after transfection, cells were pretreated with 20 μM MHY5456, while control cells received an equivalent concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the vehicle control. One hour later, OA was added at 30 μM to induce lipid accumulation. After 24 h of OA exposure, intracellular TG levels were measured.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) for multiple-group comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p<0.05) was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## RESULTS

## MHY5456 enhances FXR transcriptional activity in AC2F cells
MHY5456, a benzoxazole-based compound designed as an agonist for FXR, was synthesized, and its chemical structure is presented in . Benzoxazole, a benzene-fused oxazole containing oxygen and nitrogen atoms, provides a versatile scaffold for bioactive compounds. Its derivatives exhibit diverse activities, including antioxidant, anti-tumor, and metabolic regulation via nuclear receptor interactions ( ). Given these properties, benzoxazole derivatives are considered promising candidates for treating liver diseases, metabolic disorders, and inflammation. To assess the safety of MHY5456, a dose-dependent cytotoxicity assay was performed using AC2F rat liver-derived cells. The results indicated no significant cytotoxicity, even at higher concentrations ( ). The agonist activity of MHY5456 was determined by luciferase reporter assay, which showed enhanced transcriptional activity of FXR ( ). These results demonstrate that MHY5456 is a non-toxic compound capable of acting on FXR, supporting its potential as a nuclear receptor agonist for therapeutic applications.

## MHY5456 improves lipid homeostasis and modulates metabolic gene expression in AC2F cells
To examine the effects of MHY5456 on lipid metabolism, AC2F rat liver-derived cells were treated with MHY5456 alone for 2 h or pretreated for 30 min, followed by a 24-h treatment with either MHY5456 alone or in combination with OA ( ). Gene expression analysis showed that MHY5456 treatment alone significantly reduced the expression of lipid accumulation-related genes such as Acaca, Fasn, Scd1, Lpin1, and Dgat2 ( ). In contrast, treatment with MHY5456 resulted in an increase in Cpt1a expression, which is consistent with the activation of β-oxidation pathway, whereas the expression level of Acox1 showed no noticeable change ( ). Additionally, MHY5456 treatment alone significantly increased the expression of mitochondria-related genes such as Cox4i1, Atp6d, mt-Nd6, Cyc1, Atp5g2, Tfam, and mt-Co1, further supporting its association with enhanced mitochondrial-related gene expression ( ). To assess whether MHY5456 mitigates lipid accumulation induced by OA, ORO staining was performed. As expected, OA significantly increased the number of lipid droplets, confirming lipid accumulation. However, treatment with MHY5456 significantly reduced the number of lipid droplets, suggesting a reduction in lipid accumulation ( ). Measurement of TG levels under the same conditions further confirmed this finding, as MHY5456 treatment effectively countered the increase in TG levels induced by OA ( ). To further investigate whether the anti-steatotic effect of MHY5456 is mediated through FXR signaling, AC2F cells were transfected with FXR siRNA prior to OA and MHY5456 treatment ( ). FXR knockdown significantly reduced Nr1h4 expression, confirming efficient silencing ( ). Notably, the TG-lowering effect of MHY5456 observed under OA stimulation was abolished in FXR-deficient cells ( ), indicating that MHY5456-mediated suppression of lipid accumulation is dependent on FXR signaling. Collectively, these results indicate that MHY5456 alleviates OA-induced lipid accumulation and promotes mitochondrial function in AC2F rat liver-derived cells, primarily through reduction of the lipid synthesis pathway.

## MHY5456 suppresses fibrotic activation of HSCs in response to TGF-β stimulation
To evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of MHY5456, cell viability was first assessed in LX2 HSCs to exclude potential cytotoxic effects. MHY5456 treatment showed no significant cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 20 μM ( ). LX2 HSCs were pretreated with MHY5456 for 30 min, followed by TGF-β treatment for 24 h to induce fibrosis. MHY5456 pretreatment markedly suppressed the TGF-β-induced upregulation of fibrosis-related genes, including COL1A2, COL3A1, ACTA2, FN1, and VIM ( ). Additionally, the protein expression of fibrosis markers, such as α-SMA and Vimentin, which were significantly upregulated by TGF-β, was substantially inhibited by MHY5456 treatment ( ). IF analysis further confirmed that TGF-β induced strong α-SMA expression, which was substantially decreased following MHY5456 treatment ( ). These results indicate that MHY5456 exerts a potent anti-fibrotic effect by downregulating key genes and proteins associated with fibrosis.

## MHY5456 attenuates hepatic steatosis and inflammatory responses in MCD diet-fed mice
To assess the in vivo effects of MHY5456 on liver lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, a MCD diet–fed mouse model was employed. Mice were fed the MCD diet for 3 weeks while receiving daily oral administration of MHY5456 at a dose of 5 mg/kg ( ). MHY5456 treatment alone did not significantly affect mouse body weight and had limited impact on the body weight reduction induced by the MCD diet ( ). To examine the effect of MHY5456 on hepatic lipid metabolism, TG levels were measured and found to be elevated in MCD diet-fed mice but significantly reduced by MHY5456 treatment ( ). ORO staining further confirmed significant hepatic lipid accumulation in MCD diet-fed mice, which was markedly reduced in size and number of lipid droplets by MHY5456 treatment ( ). H&E staining showed severe hepatic vacuolization and inflammation in MCD diet-fed mice, which were alleviated by MHY5456 treatment ( ). To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of MHY5456, we examined NF-κB p65 activation and inflammatory gene expression. The MCD diet increased the p-NF-κB p65/NF-κB p65 ratio, indicating enhanced hepatic inflammation, whereas MHY5456 treatment reduced NF-κB p65 activation ( ). Moreover, the expression of Cxcl1 and Nos2 was lower in the MHY5456 group than in the MCD diet group ( ). These findings suggest that MHY5456 effectively reduces both inflammation and lipid accumulation induced by the MCD diet, demonstrating its potential as a therapeutic agent for liver disease.

## MHY5456 alleviates liver fibrosis in mice fed a MCD diet
To evaluate the anti-fibrotic effect of MHY5456, the extent of fibrosis was assessed in the same mouse model. Gene expression analysis of fibrosis markers in mouse liver tissue revealed significant upregulation of Col3a1, Vim, Tgfb1, and Acta2 in response to the MCD diet, confirming the development of hepatic fibrosis. Treatment with MHY5456 significantly reduced the expression of these fibrosis-related genes, indicating its potential to mitigate fibrosis development ( ). At the protein level, analysis of Collagen Iα2 and Vimentin, key indicators of fibrosis, showed substantial increases in these markers following the MCD diet. However, MHY5456 treatment significantly decreased their protein expression, suggesting a protective effect against fibrogenesis ( ). To visualize the extent of liver fibrosis, SR staining was performed on liver tissue sections. The results showed a significant reduction in fibrotic areas in the MHY5456-treated group compared to the MCD diet-only group, further confirming the anti-fibrotic effects of MHY5456 ( ). Consistent with these observations, IHC staining for α-SMA, a marker of activated HSCs, showed strong α-SMA immunoreactivity in the livers of MCD diet-fed mice, while α-SMA staining was visibly reduced in the MHY5456-treated group ( ). These findings collectively support the potential of MHY5456 as a therapeutic intervention for hepatic fibrosis in a diet-induced preclinical model of liver injury.

## DISCUSSION
Fibrotic diseases, characterized by excessive accumulation of ECM proteins, are associated with various chronic conditions, including liver disorders ( ). These conditions result in severe organ dysfunction and are becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide. Despite considerable research efforts, effective treatments for fibrosis remain lacking, prompting ongoing investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying its development. Recent studies have identified the pivotal role of lipid metabolism alterations in the development and progression of fibrosis ( ). In this study, we focused on MHY5456, an agonist of the nuclear receptor FXR, a key regulator of lipid metabolism, to explore its therapeutic potential in preventing liver fibrosis.
Alterations in lipid metabolism play a central role in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis ( ; ). Lipid accumulation due to imbalances in fatty acid oxidation and lipid synthesis contributes to hepatocyte damage, triggering the activation of HSCs and promoting fibrosis ( ; ). The MCD diet, used to induce liver disease, disrupts hepatic lipid metabolism by impairing the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), leading to fat accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction ( ). This cascade triggers oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and HSC activation, contributing to ECM deposition and fibrosis. Elevated levels of TGF-β, a key mediator of fibrosis, exacerbate these pathological processes. In this study, AC2F rat liver-derived cells were used to investigate lipid accumulation because they retain key metabolic characteristics, are stable, and provide reproducible results ( ). AC2F cells allow clear evaluation of MHY5456’s inhibitory effects on lipid accumulation with minimal cytotoxicity or variability. Although human-derived hepatocytes like HepG2 cells offer greater translational relevance, they often show higher variability and less predictable responses ( ). Therefore, AC2F cells were used for initial mechanistic studies, with future work in human hepatocytes needed to confirm clinical relevance. Although the MCD diet is a well-established model for inducing hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, it does not fully recapitulate the metabolic complexity of human MASLD. In this model, the absence of methionine and choline impairs very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) assembly and secretion, leading to hepatic lipid accumulation in the absence of obesity, insulin resistance, and broader systemic metabolic and vascular alterations that are characteristic of human disease ( ). Therefore, these limitations should be taken into consideration when interpreting the translational relevance of the present findings.
Our results demonstrate that MHY5456 significantly mitigates lipid accumulation and improves metabolic regulation in the liver primarily through FXR activation. Although a combination of saturated fatty acids, such as palmitic and stearic acids, with the monounsaturated fatty acid OA is commonly used to better recapitulate the pathophysiological features of fatty liver disease—including impaired lipid metabolism, cellular injury, and inflammation—OA alone was used in this study to focus specifically on lipid accumulation ( ; ). Saturated fatty acids induce apoptosis, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can reduce cell viability and introduce variables that complicate the interpretation of drug effects on lipid metabolism ( ; ). In contrast, OA primarily promotes intracellular TG droplet formation with minimal cytotoxicity, allowing stable and reproducible lipid accumulation ( ). This provides a more controlled condition for evaluating the direct effects of pharmacological interventions. Therefore, OA alone was used to minimize confounding effects from cell injury or inflammation, enabling clearer assessment of MHY5456’s inhibitory effects on lipid accumulation.
In our in vivo mouse model, MHY5456 treatment enhanced fatty acid oxidation and reduced the expression of fatty acid synthesis genes, thereby attenuating hepatic lipogenesis. Consistent with these findings, cell-based experiments demonstrated that MHY5456 improved mitochondrial-related gene expression and suppressed lipid accumulation. Although MHY5456 increased mitochondrial-related gene expression, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as transcriptional changes alone do not confirm functional alterations. Comprehensive evaluation of mitochondrial function would require additional assessments, such as oxygen consumption rate (OCR), membrane potential, biogenesis, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production ( ; ). Thus, the current findings provide only indirect evidence of increased mitochondrial activity, and further functional studies are needed to confirm MHY5456’s effects. Notably, increased mitochondrial activity does not always indicate benefit, as it may also raise oxidative stress and potential cellular damage ( ; ). Thus, additional studies are needed to clarify whether MHY5456-induced mitochondrial activation represents an adaptive or maladaptive response.
In addition to its metabolic effects, MHY5456 exhibited significant anti-inflammatory properties by reducing the protein expression levels of inflammatory markers, including NF-κB p65 and phosphorylated-NF-κB p65 (p-NF-κB p65). Histological analysis using H&E staining further confirmed the structural improvements in liver tissue. The combination of reduced lipid accumulation, suppressed inflammation, and inhibited fibrosis underscores the therapeutic potential of MHY5456 for MASH, a disease characterized by steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of MHY5456 to target both metabolic dysregulation and fibrosis in liver disease.
Our study further emphasizes the importance of developing novel therapeutic strategies for fibrosis by targeting nuclear receptors that regulate lipid metabolism ( ). Given the limitations of current antifibrotic treatments, pharmacological agents that modulate lipid metabolic pathways offer a promising direction for future research. In particular, our findings suggest that targeting FXR may provide an effective therapeutic strategy for addressing multiple aspects of fibrosis progression. Unlike monotherapies that often address a single pathological mechanism, pharmacological modulation of FXR has the potential to influence interconnected pathways involved in fibrosis progression, thereby overcoming some limitations of single-target interventions ( ). In particular, targeting FXR not only restores lipid homeostasis but also modulates inflammation and fibrosis. These findings suggest that MHY5456 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for managing fibrotic disease, providing potential for better outcomes in liver disease models. The ability to influence lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and fibrosis-related pathways further underscores the promise of this approach for future clinical application. As research into the clinical development of FXR agonists continues, a deeper understanding of their long-term safety and efficacy will be crucial for advancing these therapies as a viable treatment option for fibrotic diseases.
In addition to FXR activation, it is important to consider the potential selectivity and off-target effects of MHY5456. Nuclear receptors share conserved ligand-binding domains, and several FXR agonists have been reported to exhibit partial cross-reactivity with other nuclear receptors involved in lipid metabolism, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), liver X receptors (LXRs), and pregnane X receptor (PXR) ( ). Although the present study demonstrates FXR-dependent transcriptional activation and downstream metabolic effects, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that MHY5456 may influence additional nuclear receptor pathways. However, the observed gene expression patterns and metabolic outcomes are largely consistent with canonical FXR signaling, supporting FXR as the primary target. Future studies employing receptor-specific antagonists, reporter assays, or genetic loss-of-function models will be required to rigorously define the selectivity profile of MHY5456 and to distinguish FXR-dependent effects from potential off-target actions.
In summary, activation of FXR represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of dysregulated lipid metabolism and liver fibrosis. Our preclinical findings demonstrate that MHY5456 effectively reduces hepatic fibrosis, improves lipid metabolism, and enhances mitochondrial-related gene expression in both MCD diet-induced mouse and cell models. These therapeutic effects are likely mediated through the modulation of key metabolic and inflammatory pathways, highlighting the compound’s ability to restore hepatic homeostasis under fibrogenic conditions. While these results are encouraging, further studies are needed to validate its efficacy in advanced liver fibrosis models and to comprehensively evaluate its safety. Moreover, understanding its long-term impact on systemic metabolism and potential organ-specific effects will be crucial for translational application. These findings contribute to the expanding evidence base supporting the therapeutic targeting of lipid metabolism and inflammation in fibrotic liver disease, and underscore the potential of FXR agonists, such as MHY5456, for further drug development ( ).
