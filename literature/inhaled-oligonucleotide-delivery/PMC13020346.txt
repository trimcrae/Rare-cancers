This architecture confers several advantageous properties, including enhanced cellular uptake, high nuclease stability, low immunogenicity, and minimal cytotoxicity, making SNAs a promising platform for biomedical applications. Nevertheless, several challenges currently hinder their clinical translation, among which unintended accumulation in non-target organs remains a significant limitation. This review begins by introducing the organs involved in the uptake of nanoparticles, providing a concise overview of the characteristics of NP accumulation in major organs such as the liver, kidneys, and spleen. Furthermore, highlights the potential mechanisms underlying these distribution patterns and extends the discussion to SNAs. Subsequently, reviews the latest strategies for improving the in vivo distribution of SNAs from three primary perspectives: modifying the SNA structure, engineering the surface and modulating the microenvironment surrounding SNAs. Finally, based on the current state of SNA research, discusses key challenges facing existing strategies, as well as the promising prospects of this field. It is hoped that this review will provide valuable insights and guidance for the development and rational design of novel SNAs.

## Introduction
Programmable production and precise manipulation of nanoparticles are the fundamental requirements of nanomedicine. Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs), first developed by Mirkin and colleagues in 1996, represent a unique class of polyvalent nucleic acid−nanoparticle conjugates with broad potential biomedical applications [ ]. Structurally, SNAs consist of a nanoparticle core coated by a dense shell of DNA strands (Fig. A). The nanoparticle core is one critical component, as it significantly influences the physicochemical properties, biological profiles, and potential applications of the SNA [ ]. While gold nanoparticles were originally used as the core, subsequent research has expanded the range of materials to include other noble metals (e.g., silver, platinum), liposomes, and even proteins, which has significantly expanded the versatility of SNAs [ – ]. Alongside the core, the DNA shell also plays a pivotal role in biological interactions and drug delivery, with oligonucleotides comprising three functional regions: an attachment moiety that anchors DNA to the core, a spacer segment that ensures accessibility of the recognition domain, and a programmable recognition domain tailored for specific applications [ , ].
Since their emergence, SNAs have garnered significant attention for their unique nano-bio interactions [ ]. One of the most notable features is their remarkable cellular permeability (Fig. B). SNAs can rapidly penetrate most types of cells, mediated by class A scavenger receptors (SR-As) [ ]. Besides, SNAs are capable of traversing various biological barriers, including the blood-brain barrier (Fig. D) [ ]. Their dense structure affords them exceptional stability, protecting them from enzymatic degradation both in the bloodstream and within cells (Fig. C). This stability is thought to be attributed to the compact arrangement of DNA strands and a localized high concentration of sodium ions that inhibits enzymatic activity [ , ]. Moreover, SNAs exhibit high binding affinity for their targets due to their multifunctional chemical structures, while maintaining a favorable safety profile with low toxicity and immunogenicity [ – ]. Collectively, these characteristics position SNAs as promising candidates for applications in drug delivery, gene delivery, and related fields. It’s worth mentioning that a brain-penetrated RNA interference-based SNAs designed for the treatment of glioblastoma has demonstrated its safety, effective tumor site enrichment, and therapeutic potential in a recent Phase 0 clinical trial (NCT03020017) [ ]. The safety and pharmacodynamic properties of another immunostimulatory SNA compound targeting Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) have been validated in Phase 1 clinical studies, making it a promising candidate for clinical immunotherapy (NCT03086278) [ ]. In addition, a new class of CRISPR-based SNAs developed for genome editing has demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake and nuclear localization, achieving genome editing efficiencies of 32–47%. This novel architecture is poised to significantly broaden the applications and influence of the CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing system [ ].
In spite of these merits, SNAs face several challenges that impede their clinical translation. One significant hurdle is the insufficient dose to the diseased site, leading to accumulation in non-target tissues and organs, particularly in metabolic organs such as the liver and kidneys [ ]. In a study examining the biodistribution of SNAs in glioma-bearing mice, ICP-MS analysis revealed significantly higher accumulation of NPs in the liver and spleen (> 1.40E + 14 nanoparticles per gram of tissue) compared to the tumor tissue (< 1.00E + 13 nanoparticles per gram of tissue) [ ]. On this basis, metallic nanoparticles are resistant to degradation, raising concerns about the long-term risk of toxicity [ ]. These limitations underscore the necessity for elucidating the mechanisms driving organ-specific accumulation and proposing strategies to modulate uptake in non-target tissues.
In this review, we systematically reviewed recent advances in the metabolic distribution of SNAs over the past few years. First, we highlight the biodistribution of SNAs across major organs, with a focus on key metabolic sites such as the liver, kidneys, and spleen. Subsequently, we discuss the potential mechanisms underlying these organ-specific distribution patterns, including protein corona formation, the glomerular filtration barrier in the kidneys, and erythrocyte hitchhiking in the spleen. Subsequently, we provide a systematic overview of recent strategies aimed at enhancing the distribution of SNA. These approaches include direct modifications to the SNA architecture, chemical functionalization of the SNA surface, and alterations to the biological milieu in which SNAs operate. Besides, we outline the advantageous attributes of these strategies while also addressing the limitations identified in research. Finally, inspired by current research progress, we analyze the pivotal challenges and future directions for enhancing the in vivo distribution of SNAs and promoting their clinical translation.

 

## Mapping sna’s journey through the body
Over the past three decades, SNAs have evolved from a novel concept into a diverse class of nanostructures with hundreds of distinct variants [ ]. They have since proven to be more than just chemical tools—not only developed as well-defined diagnostic probes, but also used as gene regulation and immunotherapy agents to directly combat disease [ ]. Besides, some SNAs are built on metallic cores, and certain metal nanoparticles are known to persist in tissues and elicit biological responses. For example, AuNPs have been reported to cause a transient elevation of cytokine gene expression in the liver following the injection into rats [ ], and AgNPs can affect nucleic acid and organic acid metabolism in mouse kidneys [ ]. Thus, gaining a comprehensive understanding of how SNAs behave and distribute within the body is both critically important and urgently needed.
From a microscopic perspective, the cellular uptake of SNAs is predominantly mediated by membrane-associated recognition proteins, which facilitate subsequent internalization via membrane invagination. However, there is no consensus on the precise pathway for SNAs’ cellular entry. It is argued that SR-A and caveolae/lipid-raft have been identified as two principal pathways governing SNAs internalization [ ]. Scavenger receptors are considered to be a subclass of the membrane-bound pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), involved in complex events such as phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and the clearance of apoptotic cells [ ]. Lipid rafts, which are microdomains enriched in sphingolipids and cholesterol, and their invaginated structures known as caveolae, play essential roles in membrane organization, signaling, and cellular homeostasis [ , ]. The two pathways work in concert to facilitate the cellular uptake of SNAs. Interestingly, blocking complement receptors significantly reduces SNAs internalization, whereas inhibiting SR-A does not produce a comparable effect [ ]. These findings indicate that the mechanisms governing SNAs’ cellular entry are more complex than currently understood and warrant further investigation.
From a macroscopic perspective, the general scheme for nanoparticle accumulation and clearance is through three main mechanisms: hepatobiliary, renal, and via the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [ , ]. Systemic delivery studies demonstrate that SNA’s biodistribution patterns are akin to conventional nanoparticle platforms [ ]. Fluorescence and radionuclide imaging studies have shown predominant accumulation observed in the liver, kidneys, and spleen [ , , ]. What sets SNAs apart is their structural flexibility. As polyvalent nucleic acid-nanoparticle conjugates, they offer tunable design features, including adjustable nanoparticle cores (varying in type and size) and customizable oligonucleotide shells (adjustable in sequence, length, and loading density). These design parameters directly shape their physicochemical properties and, consequently, influence how they behave and distribute in the body [ , ]. For instance, certain SNA configurations display exceptional penetrative capabilities to cross challenging biological barriers, including the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [ , ].
The variability in organ-specific nanoparticles arises from intrinsic differences in tissue structure, such as the porous, sinusoidal architecture of the liver versus the tightly regulated filtration barrier of the kidney [ , ], as well as perfusion rates [ , ], cellular phenotypic diversity [ , ], and protein corona formation tendencies [ ]. In recent years, a growing body of research has aimed to unravel the complex interplay between nanoparticles and the diverse microenvironments of major organs [ , ]. In particular, the liver, kidney, and spleen have become focal points in efforts to map nanoparticle biodynamics [ ]. Unlocking the full clinical potential of SNAs will require a deep, mechanistic understanding of these interactions at the organ–nanoparticle interface. ## The liver: primary gatekeeper
Following systemic administration, nanomaterials are rapidly transported to the liver via blood circulation, where they accumulate and are subsequently sequestered [ ]. As the primary clearance organ for most nanoparticles, the liver plays a dominant role in dictating their biodistribution and retention [ , ]. Hepatic uptake is governed by multiple interdependent factors, including blood flow dynamics, spatial localization within hepatic substructures, macrophage density, and nanoparticles’ physicochemical characteristics, particularly size and composition, that influence the interaction with serum protein to redefine their bioidentity (Fig. A) [ , ].
The high uptake of nanoparticles in the liver is attributed to the robust internalization by hepatic macrophages [ ]. The liver hosts a diverse and substantial immune cells, including Kupffer cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), T cells, and B cells [ ]. These cells internalize nanoparticles through multiple pathways: phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis via scavenger, Fc, complement, galactose, folate, and mannose receptors [ ]. A study by Dowty et al. demonstrated that Kupffer cells (47%) exhibit higher dextran endocytosis capacity than splenic macrophages (15%) and peritoneal macrophages (30%), both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. B) [ ]. LSECs also contribute to the liver’s phagocytic capacity. Although individually less efficient, their sheer numbers create a cumulative effect [ ]. Some nanoparticles, such as cage-like organosilica nanoparticles, preferentially localized in LSECs instead of Kupffer cells as confirmed by confocal imaging [ ].
Hepatic hemodynamics further amplify the nanoparticle’s uptake. The liver’s dual blood supply, from the hepatic artery and portal vein, creates a unique vascular environment where oxygen-rich and nutrient-rich blood converge in the sinusoidal network [ ]. These sinusoidal architectures, which occupy a significant portion of liver volume (comprising 50–80%), facilitate slow, high-volume blood flow (1.5 L/min) and prolonged nanoparticle-cell interaction time [ , ]. This dynamic significantly enhances the probability of macrophage contact and uptake. In a study by Kim and colleagues [ ], the hemodynamic influence was quantified through a comparative analysis of quantum dot (QD) uptake in macrophages under physiological flow conditions (in vivo) versus static culture systems (in vitro). Their quantification revealed that 67.1 ± 15.7% of Kupffer cells and only 10.0 ± 6.3% of monocytes were QD-positive. In contrast, monocytes exhibited a comparable level of QD uptake to Kupffer cells under static culture conditions, underscoring the importance of liver-specific flow dynamics (Fig. C).
Another critical determinant of nanoparticles’ fate is the protein corona (PC), which forms spontaneously when nanoparticles are exposed to biological systems [ ]. This corona evolves through a dynamic layering process: an initial loosely-associated “soft” corona with lower binding affinity is gradually displaced by a more stable “hard” corona exhibiting stronger binding interactions [ , ]. Its complexity is influenced by nanoparticle’s physicochemical characteristics (size, shape, curvature, core composition, surface chemistry), protein properties (sequence, structure, concentration), and environmental factors (ionic strength, ligand presence, pH, temperature) [ ]. Additionally, PC profiles also vary across species [ ]. Even slight changes in these factors can significantly alter the composition and stoichiometry of PC, complicating the characterization and introducing uncertainty into its underlying mechanisms. Notably, while nanoparticle features drive corona formation, the corona itself reshapes the nanoparticle behavior [ ], especially during cellular uptake. Proteins within the corona facilitate receptor-mediated recognition by macrophages, engaging pathways such as fibrinogen–integrin, immunoglobulin–Fc receptor, and complement–complement receptor interactions [ ]. At the systemic level, PC also modulates nanoparticle biodistribution. For instance, a study has demonstrated that cholesterol content altered protein abundance within PC, thereby shifting the in vivo localization of nanoparticles (Fig. D, E) [ ]. Though many mechanistic details remain unresolved, mounting evidence points to the protein corona as a central regulator of nanoparticle fate—governing uptake efficiency and whole-body distribution. Understanding and controlling this dynamic interface is critical to advancing predictive models and the clinical translation of nanomedicine.
These biological mechanisms are directly reflected in the liver’s interaction with SNA. As with many nanoparticle platforms, the liver has been consistently identified as a major site of SNA accumulation and retention. Recent studies highlighted that the oligonucleotide sequence present on the SNA surface significantly influences protein corona formation. For instance, guanine-rich (G-rich) SNAs form coronas that are more readily recognized by complement receptors on macrophages, compared to those formed by poly-thymine (poly-T) SNAs. This enhanced recognition promotes aggregation in macrophage-rich liver and spleen (Fig. F) [ ]. The formation of nucleic acid secondary structures further diversifies their biological behavior and distribution. While size and surface charge are well-established determinants of nanoparticle fate, it is increasingly clear that the chemical nature and spatial architecture of surface ligands also play a critical role in shaping how nanomaterials interact with serum proteins and cellular receptors. In this context, the programmable structure of SNAs offers a versatile platform for tuning biological interactions and optimizing delivery.

 

## The kidney: routes of filtration and secretion
Renal clearance of macromolecules and engineered nanoparticles depends on their size, charge, shape, composition, and interactions with kidney compartments [ ]. Compared to hepatic metabolism, renal clearance is more sensitive to the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials. The kidney’s primary filtration process occurs at the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB), a specialized structure located within the glomerulus between the glomerular capillary and Bowman’s space (Fig. A) [ ]. This nanoscale filtration unit consists of three key layers: the endothelium of glomerular capillaries, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and podocyte foot processes [ ]. Together, these layers act as a selective sieve, with the GBM exhibiting the smallest pore size (~ 9.7 nm) and a dense coating of negatively charged molecules, including proteoglycans and glycoproteins [ , ]. These features confer both size- and charge-selectivity, favoring the filtration of small, positively charged nanoparticles [ ].
Intriguingly, renal filtration does not follow simple linear relationships with these physical properties. Nanoparticles with hydrodynamic diameters (HD) of 1–2 nm exhibit peak clearance efficiency, whereas both smaller and larger particles show reduced filtration. This phenomenon may arise from ultra-small nanoparticles (≤ 1 nm) exhibiting stronger interactions with the GFB components, particularly the endothelial glycocalyx (Fig. B) [ ]. Charge-dependent clearance patterns also show unexpected complexity. While positively charged particles are generally favored, some studies report higher clearance rates for strongly negatively charged nanoparticles [ ], suggesting additional, yet unresolved, mechanisms influencing charge-based filtration.
Beyond glomerular filtration, the fate of nanoparticles in the kidney is influenced by their interactions with specific renal cellular components. Some nanoparticles are actively internalized by tubular cells and secreted into the lumen, enabling rapid excretion. Others may adhere to microvilli on tubular epithelia or be phagocytosed by mesangial cells, resulting in retention within renal tissue [ ]. Notably, recent work by Huang et al. identified a novel clearance mechanism in proximal tubular epithelial cells. This process expels internalized gold nanoparticles through the formation of ~ 5 μm balloon-like protrusions that penetrate the dense microvilli layer and release intact gold-containing endocytic vesicles along with mitochondria and other organelles into the tubular lumen, ultimately being released through membrane fission (Fig. C) [ ].
Although SNAs generally exhibit lower renal filtration efficiency and cellular uptake compared to conventional nanocarriers, emerging evidence highlights the critical role of renal metabolism in determining their pharmacokinetics. Ferrer et al. systematically investigated this phenomenon by designing lipid-modified SNAs (LSNAs) using single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) conjugated with diacylglycerol-based phospholipid tails (DPPE-LSNA), and tracking them via employing cyanine 5 (Cy5)-labeled ssDNA with phosphorothioate backbone modifications. Fluorescence quantification revealed a 2-fold higher Cy5-DNA signal intensity in the kidneys of DPPE-LSNA-treated subjects compared to other LSNA formulations (p < 0.05) [ ]. This accumulation pattern suggests that, despite reduced filtration, SNAs can undergo selective renal retention through mechanisms potentially involving lipid-mediated interactions with renal parenchymal cells, highlighting the nuanced and formulation-dependent nature of their renal processing. While nanoparticles smaller than 6 nm are known to be filtered and excreted by the kidneys, our team’s study reveals that SNAs exceeding this size threshold can also achieve efficient renal clearance. We investigated SNAs of various sizes (30, 40, 70, and 100 nm) with identical surface ssDNA coatings. Through positron emission tomography (PET) and BioTEM imaging, we observed their localization in peritubular capillaries and proximal tubular epithelial cells, indicating clearance via tubular secretion rather than physical filtration. Supporting this, pharmacological inhibition of organic anion transporters reduced renal uptake of SNAs by approximately 43.5%. Notably, harnessing this secretion pathway enabled SNAs to mitigate tubular injury and improve outcomes in a murine model of acute kidney injury. These findings build on the traditional filtration paradigm by underscoring the pivotal role of transporter-mediated tubular secretion in the clearance of nanoparticles that exceed the filtration threshold.

 

## The spleen: Non-negligible sequestration
In addition to the liver and kidneys, the spleen is a major site of nanoparticle accumulation, largely due to its substantial structural and functional parallels with the liver. The murine splenic parenchyma comprises three distinct compartments: white pulp, red pulp, and marginal zone (Fig. D). Of these, the red pulp, rich in macrophages and blood-filled venous sinuses, closely resembles hepatic sinusoids in both architecture and macrophage density [ ]. Despite these similarities, both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that hepatic macrophages exhibit significantly greater nanoparticle uptake capacity compared to their splenic counterparts [ ]. The structural homology between the liver and spleen often results in synchronized nanoparticle accumulation patterns, as seen with gold nanoparticles, which preferentially accumulate in both organs due to their robust interaction with the MPS [ ]. These shared pathways highlight the interconnected roles of these reticuloendothelial organs in nanoparticle clearance.
Nevertheless, the spleen possesses unique physiological functions beyond nanoparticle clearance. As a secondary lymphoid organ, it regulates immune surveillance and orchestrates erythrocyte metabolism, which mediates critical immune functions [ ]. The red pulp filters aged erythrocytes based on membrane rigidity—a mechanobiological property that has been harnessed for targeted drug delivery. Ukidve et al. developed an erythrocyte membrane-binding biomimetic nanoplatform that leverages cellular hitchhiking mechanisms (cell-mediated transport) for precision splenic targeting. This platform achieves payload delivery through dynamic erythrocyte-nanoparticle transfer processes at splenic filtration beds [ ]. Yuewen Zhai et al. further fused RBC membranes with liposomes to synthesize iPSC@RBC-Mlipo that possess the biological characteristics of red blood cells, achieving targeted delivery to the spleen (Fig. E, F, G) [ ]. Furthermore, nanoparticle-protein interactions modulate splenic retention. Spleen-targeting nanoparticles exhibit distinct corona signatures that enhance localization [ ]. Nissinen et al. demonstrated that dual PEGylation of mesoporous silicon nanoparticles induces corona protein profiles enriched with CD44, CD93, and CD36 ligands [ ]. These surface markers synergistically promote splenic nanoparticle accumulation while downregulating phagocytic activity in hepatic Kupffer cells, establishing a spleen-selective biodistribution paradigm.
As for SNAs, splenic accumulation is also a key component of their in vivo biodistribution that should not be overlooked. In a seminal study by Äärelä et al., researchers engineered a dodecahedral [ ] fullerene-core multivalent SNA (MSNA-PS) incorporating HER2 mRNA antisense oligonucleotides with phosphorothioate backbone modifications [ ]. Quantitative biodistribution analysis via [18F]-radiolabeling revealed striking organ selectivity in HCC1954 xenograft models, with the spleen identified as the second-highest site of accumulation (Fig. H). Although the study did not explore this splenic enrichment in depth, the findings strongly suggest that the spleen plays a substantial role in SNA pharmacokinetics and should be considered in future biodistribution and targeting strategies.

## Other organs: diverse but subtle
The majority of NPs are delivered via intravenous injection, which is the most commonly used route of drug administration. After entering the bloodstream, they rapidly interact with plasma proteins and are subsequently recognized, taken up, and filtered by the liver, spleen, and kidneys [ ]. Beyond these primary sites, trace amounts of NPs can also reach secondary organs, including the lungs, brain, and bones [ ].
The lungs, as an essential organ for gas exchange, have abundant blood circulation and are rich in capillaries, which allow single red blood cells to pass through. Compared to the endothelial cells of other organs, like the liver, the endothelial cells in the lungs are more intact [ ]. Moreover, the external biological barriers, such as airway epithelial cells and pulmonary surfactants, further limit the possibility of NP extravasation into the lungs [ , ]. Nevertheless, selective lung accumulation has been reported. Vaidya and colleagues suggested that the pKa of certain lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) may influence the formation of their protein corona [ ], which in turn promotes their appearance in the lungs, particularly within the pulmonary endothelial cells. Similarly, lipid nanoparticle-based liposomal spherical nucleic acids (LSNAs) have also exhibited a certain degree of lung uptake [ ].
In the central nervous system (CNS), NP entry is governed by the unique properties of the brain–cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) interface and the BBB. While the brain–CSF barrier permits relatively free solute exchange [ ], the BBB is significantly more restrictive. Therefore, substance that can cross the BBB typically exhibits measurable brain uptake, and several classes of SNAs demonstrate this ability [ ]. As mentioned earlier, certain SNAs exhibit strong penetration ability across various biological membranes, enabling their accumulation in the brain.
Bone presents a different challenge. Although bone is vascularized and relatively rich in blood vessels, its dense cortical structure and complex marrow architecture hinder NP penetration. As a result, most conventional NPs display low affinity for bone targets [ ]. Based on the comprehensive biodistribution results of SNAs by Wang et al., although SNAs show some bone uptake, the level remains significantly lower than that in major solid organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys [ ].
For organs directly contacted with the external environment, such as the respiratory, gastrointestinal tracts and the skin, modifying the administration route (e.g., via inhalation, oral ingestion, or topical application) enables direct drug delivery to the target site, often leading to more favorable therapeutic outcomes [ – ]. Consequently, systemic biodistribution of these organs is often less emphasized.
Overall, although the liver, spleen, and kidneys dominate NP sequestration and clearance, low-level distribution to other organs consistently occurs. Current studies largely describe these observations without providing mechanistic depth. More systematic investigation is needed to clarify how nanoparticle properties, biological barriers, and microenvironmental factors shape NP behavior in these less-studied organs.

## Mastering SNA design for optimal delivery
Building on mechanistic insights into SNA-organ interactions, researchers harness these principles to engineer SNAs with enhanced clinical translation potential [ ]. This paradigm has given rise to three key strategies: structural optimization, surface functionalization, and microenvironmental modulation. These strategies have addressed distinct challenges in biodistribution, targeting, and therapeutic delivery, paving the way for more effective and predictable SNA-based interventions. ## Core–Shell architecture: foundations of control
Modifying the intrinsic structure of SNA remains the most common and fundamental strategy for tuning its behavior [ ]. Owing to its unique structure, SNA possesses a variety of adjustable parameters, including the type and size of its core, as well as the length and the design of the nucleic acid shell [ ].

## Core modifications
Early SNA designs predominantly relied on gold nanoparticle (AuNP) cores due to their ease of synthesis and dense nucleic acid loading. However, AuNPs exhibit significant limitations in clinical applications, notably their tendency to accumulate in the liver driven by opsonization and subsequent macrophage uptake. This results in prolonged tissue retention and potential toxicity, raising concerns for chronic therapeutic use [ ]. Consequently, there has been a gradual emergence of SNAs utilizing other organic particles in place of AuNPs. Resham et al. proposed using liposomes as the core of SNAs (Fig. A, B) [ ]. Liposomal cores offer improved biocompatibility and reduce immunogenicity, while providing structural stability superior to natural lipoproteins. In addition to liposomes, proteins have also been employed as the core of SNAs. Caroline and colleagues developed protein spherical nucleic acids (ProSNAs) by coating the surface of certain cell-impermeable proteins with DNA strands (Fig. C). Compared to natural proteins, this unique structure has enhanced protein cellular uptake, boosted its stability in plasma, and doubled the protein’s circulation time. Moreover, this modification significantly boarder the tissue distribution, with ProSNAs showing particularly high accumulation in major organs, including lung, kidneys, and spleen, regardless of sequence (Fig. D) [ ]. It is important to note that ProSNAs with an all-oligonucleotide shell retain their enzymatic activity in tissue, whereas the native protein loses all function, representing a significant step forward in the development of intracellular protein-based therapeutics.
Beyond core materials, the dimensional parameters of SNAs critically determine their in vivo biodistribution profiles. As with other nanomaterials, SNAs exhibit size-dependent liver tropism. Systematic tuning of core size has emerged as an effective strategy for controlling their in vivo trafficking and tissue deposition. Emerging evidence reveals a size-dependent threshold effect in SNA biodistribution. When core diameters decrease below 10 nm (Fig. E), these ultrasmall SNAs exhibit a dramatic shift in biological behavior [ ]. Ultrasmall SNAs display enhanced renal clearance, reduced hepatic uptake, prolonged circulation, and improved tumor targeting relative to their larger counterparts (Fig. F). These findings establish core size minimization as a powerful strategy for optimizing SNA pharmacokinetics across various platforms. While material constraints still exist, ongoing innovations continue to expand the feasibility of ultrasmall SNA designs.

 

## Shell modifications
Despite extensive research on core modifications in SNA engineering, few studies have aimed to optimize biodistribution through structural adjustments to the SNA itself [ ]. This gap in research reflects the established paradigm that core characteristics exert minimal direct influence on biological fate when compared to the dominant role of the nucleotide shell architecture [ ]. Even correlations between core size and biodistribution appear mechanistically indirect — smaller cores have a greater surface loading density [ ], thereby modifying biointerfacial properties that affect cellular interactions. Thus, modifications to the nucleotide shell are more effective for steering SNA biodistribution in vivo. Unlike replacing the entire SNA’s core, modifying the SNA’s shell preserves the original linkage between the core and the shell. With the advent of efficient synthesis methods such as n-butanol dehydration, the regulation of the SNAs shell has become more straightforward and rapid [ ]. Nucleotide shell modifications can be made from various perspectives, including the nucleotide composition, the base sequence, and the oligonucleotide length.
Building on foundational DNA scaffold designs, researchers have begun optimizing base sequence arrangements. In a study by Alyssa B. Chinen, SNAs synthesized from DNA enriched with different bases were designed separately [ ]. It was discovered that G-enriched SNAs exhibited significantly higher levels of protein adsorption on their surfaces and were more readily phagocytosed by macrophages compared to T-enriched SNAs. Subsequent investigations revealed that G-rich sequences can fold into a special structure recognized by the SR-A receptor, thereby promoting liver uptake via enhanced macrophage interaction [ ]. Interestingly, similar findings were observed in ProSNA studies, reinforcing the idea that the base sequence of nucleotides has a consistent and measurable effect on the in vivo distribution of SNAs [ ]. To minimize hepatic sequestration, designing nucleotide shells with lower G content may be advantageous.
A more in-depth study was conducted by Wang and his team on the base arrangement of the DNA shell [ ]. They demonstrated that modifying the spatial arrangement of DNA bases in the SNA shell could enhance tumor-specific targeting. By designing DNA sequences complementary to microRNAs (miRNAs) overexpressed in cancer cells, the team developed SNAs capable of selectively binding to tumor-associated miRNAs. This approach leverages two synergistic effects: hybridization between the SNA shell and tumor-specific miRNAs promotes localized SNA accumulation, and the binding process simultaneously neutralizes oncogenic miRNAs, addressing both drug delivery and therapeutic functionality (Fig. A). Subsequent experimental validation confirmed the clinical potential of this approach, with tumor tissue exhibiting the highest SNA concentration among all tested biological samples (Fig. B). This result shows a key connection between customized nucleotide sequences and targeted delivery efficiency, highlighting precise base-pairing strategies as a promising route for precision nanomedicine.
Beyond sequence design, structural innovations in the DNA shell itself also play a critical role. In a nanoparticle-related study, researchers encapsulated cores such as Ag2S quantum dots and nanoliposomes within DNA shells to construct architectures analogous to SNAs (Fig. C) [ ]. These DNA coatings served dual functions—shielding the core from premature degradation while simultaneously attenuating their cellular uptake efficiency. The DNA shell altered the pattern of serum protein adsorption on nanoparticle surfaces to induce functional invisibility to livers and other phagocytes. Following enzymatic lability in DNase-rich environments, where multi-enzyme systems degrade the protective layer, nanoparticles enhance their cellular internalization probability. This mechanism effectively circumvents hepatic sequestration and promotes tumor-specific cellular uptake (Fig. D). Although the experimental paradigm incorporates idealized assumptions, it has contributed substantively to nanocarrier design principles and provided a universal strategy for high-efficiency targeted delivery of theragnostic agents in vivo. Notably, the rate of degradation can be programmed by the DNA length, which strongly influences both structural stability and in vivo behavior, with longer oligonucleotides generally improving circulation time.
Emerging research has further expanded the structural components of SNAs beyond conventional DNA frameworks. A notable advancement substituted DNA with small interfering RNA (siRNA) [ , ]. Unlike single-stranded DNA, siRNA is a double-stranded duplex capable of selectively silencing disease-associated genes by degrading complementary mRNA targets. This mechanism has shown particular promise in oncology for suppressing cancer-related gene expression [ ]. The integration of siRNA with SNA addresses two critical challenges: it overcomes siRNA’s inherent difficulty in cellular uptake while simultaneously enhancing tumor-targeting specificity. This dual advantage promotes the selective accumulation of therapeutic agents in malignant tissues. For instance, in a recent study targeting triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), researchers engineered SNAs using siRNA designed to silence the c-Myc oncogene [ ]. In vivo bioluminescence imaging confirmed significantly higher SNAs accumulation in tumor tissue compared to healthy organs, demonstrating the potential of nucleotide modification to precisely control biodistribution patterns.

## Beyond the classic SNA
Beyond conventional architectures involving DNA-encapsulated core particles, emerging strategies employ discrete DNA-polymer conjugates that undergo supramolecular assembly into SNA configurations [ ]. This methodology offers enhanced structural programmability while preserving key SNA attributes, including molecular encapsulation capacity and biostability. One notable approach utilized disulfide bonds as cross-linking agents to drive self-assembly of these conjugates into SNA configurations (Fig. E) [ ]. This strategy provides high extracellular stability, which is beneficial for target cellular uptake (Fig. F). While SNAs tended to dissociate easily upon entering the cell, thereby releasing the encapsulated therapeutic payloads (Fig. G). Such bioresponsive assembly mechanisms warrant systematic investigation, as they modulate both SNA pharmacokinetics (through altered uptake dynamics and biodistribution) and pharmacodynamics (via spatiotemporal control over therapeutic payloads) profiles. Furthermore, Bing Ma et al. employed a similar self-assembled SNA platform in a murine model, which resulted in enhanced tumor uptake and significantly reduced accumulation in off-target organs such as the kidneys compared to the free drug [ ]. Owing to its favorable biodistribution and promising antitumor efficacy, this platform demonstrates strong potential for translation into precision nanomedicine.

 

## Surface functionalization: stealth & targeting
Modification of the outer layer of SNA is a widely adopted strategy to modulate its biodistribution and metabolic profile. By introducing specific functional groups or complementary strands onto the SNA surface [ , ], researchers can directly regulate protein-SNA interactions in vivo, thereby altering the nanostructure’s biological behavior. This approach offers a straightforward and impactful way for engineering SNAs. Furthermore, surface modifications facilitate the integration of additional functionalities, such as tissue or organ targeting capabilities and immunomodulatory properties [ ].
Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is among the most extensively employed surface modifiers for nanoparticles [ ]. PEGylation alters biodistribution primarily by increasing hydrodynamic diameter, thereby reducing renal filtration and prolonging systemic circulation. In parallel, the hydrophilic nature of PEG attenuates hepatic clearance by lowering lipid solubility and minimizing opsonization [ ]. Moreover, PEG forms a steric barrier that limits serum protein adsorption onto nanoparticles. As PEG chain density increases, steric hindrance between adjacent PEG chains compresses the polymer layer into a denser conformation (Fig. A). This structural compaction enhances the shielding effect, creating a more robust barrier against protein interactions [ ]. Consequently, PEGylated drugs exhibit prolonged circulation time, promoting passive accumulation in target tissues via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect (Fig. B, C). Further, PEGylation of SNAs suppresses hepatic and renal clearance, enhancing their systemic distribution, metabolic stability, and target organ enrichment. Higher molecular weight PEGs demonstrate superior efficacy in extending blood residence time. SNAs modified with PEG exclusively on the surface exhibit stronger cell internalization effects compared to dual PEGylation on both shell and core PEG modifications [ ]. However, PEGylation presents clinical translation challenges. The conjugated PEG may distort SNA architecture, perturb protein corona composition, and interfere with immune cell recognition, potentially elevating immunogenic risks [ ]. These limitations underscore the imperative for alternative surface modification strategies to advance clinical viability.
To address these concerns, researchers have explored novel biocompatible coatings that offer functional and stimuli-responsive properties. One notable example involves the use of tumor-targeting amphipathic peptides as SNA surface coatings (Fig. D) [ ]. These peptides, which exhibit superior biocompatibility and reduced immunogenicity, serve a dual functionality—protective shielding of receptor-binding sites and selective activation in tumor microenvironments. Specifically, the peptide contains a protease-sensitive sequence cleavable by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which are overexpressed in many malignancies. Under normal physiological conditions, the coating minimizes nonspecific cellular uptake and extends systemic circulation (Fig. E). However, upon encountering MMP-rich tumor cells, the peptide layer is enzymatically cleaved, exposing the SNA for site-specific cellular internalization (Fig. F). This approach combines the inherent advantages of SNAs with a stimuli-responsive design to achieve targeted therapeutic outcomes.
To optimize the cellular uptake of SNAs, strategies extend beyond attaching non-targeting moieties to minimize off-target absorption. A promising approach involves conjugating functional groups with active targeting capabilities to enhance specific interactions. For instance, conjugating tumor-associated antigens to the outer nucleotide chains of SNAs can improve immune cell recognition and internalization. This principle was demonstrated in a recent study evaluating SNA-based cancer vaccines [ ]. Researchers hybridized peptide-oligonucleotide conjugates containing tumor-associated antigens to the DNA strands of SNAs (Fig. G). This configuration significantly enhanced dendritic cell uptake and lymph node trafficking compared to conventional methods, such as direct antigen attachment or encapsulating them within the core (Fig. H, I).
Although these strategies remain in early-stage development, they highlight the potential of dynamic surface engineering to address longstanding challenges in SNA delivery. Further optimization and translational studies are warranted to expand its biomedical applications.

 

## Environmental tuning: Corona engineering & administration
In contrast to direct structural modifications, strategies aimed at modulating SNA interactions with the physiological environment represent a more intricate and less intuitive approach. Among these interactions, the formation of a protein corona plays a dominant role in shaping SNA biodistribution. The in vivo functionality of SNAs critically depends on the formation of a dynamic and heterogeneous protein corona upon exposure to blood components. As discussed earlier, SNAs are rapidly coated by serum proteins following systemic administration, and the composition of this protein layer ultimately governs their tissue-specific accumulation [ ]. By strategically regulating corona formation, it is possible to improve the precision of SNA delivery.
Recent advances have explored pre-engineering the protein corona to mitigate its interference with SNA biodistribution. One study demonstrated that pre-incubating SNAs with selected proteins in vitro could generate a tailored corona with predefined biological properties (Fig. A) [ ]. For instance, SNAs coated with immunoglobulin G (IgG) are preferentially internalized by macrophages through Fc receptor-mediated endocytosis, whereas SNAs coated with human serum albumin (HSA) exhibit markedly reduced macrophage uptake (Fig. B, C). These findings bridged the theoretical framework of protein coronas with practical SNA design, offering preliminary insights into how corona composition could be manipulated to influence SNA behavior. Although in vivo validation remains limited, the protein corona theory is valuable for exploring SNA distribution and merits further investigation.
Additionally, methods that enhance biodistribution by modifying certain components of the plasma environment are also worth exploring. Studies has demonstrated that, compared with directly injecting target NPs, pre-injecting albumin and subsequently injecting NPs after a certain interval can significantly reduce the uptake of NPs in the liver [ ]. The components of the plasma environment that can be changed are not limited to proteins, but also include others, such as lipids. As previously mentioned, cholesterol can influence the biodistribution of NPs by affecting the protein corona [ ], and inducing hyperlipidemia in mice can significantly alter NP distribution and metabolism. Although these intriguing phenomena and their underlying mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, they undoubtedly offer a novel perspective for future strategies aimed at improving the in vivo behavior of nanomaterials.
Beyond systemic administration, an alternative and more straightforward strategy for directing SNA distribution and achieving therapeutic effects is localized delivery. By applying topically, SNA can accumulate in specific regions of interest while minimizing systemic exposure. This strategy capitalizes on SNA’s exceptional ability to penetrate biological barriers, making it particularly promising for treating cutaneous disorders via gene therapy [ ]. For example, in a study targeting abnormal scar formation, topically administered SNAs carrying antisense oligonucleotides against TGF-β1 successfully penetrated skin layers, delivered therapeutic payloads to cells, and significantly reduced the scar elevation index (SEI) (Fig. D, E) [ ]. Similar applications have demonstrated SNA’s potential in managing psoriasis through localized delivery of genetic therapeutics [ ].
The concept of localized nanoparticle administration is not unique to SNA. Other nanomaterials, such as liposomes, have been adapted for regional applications—for instance, pulmonary delivery via inhalation [ ]. However, SNA distinguishes itself through two critical advantages: superior tissue penetration efficiency and the capacity to transport antisense oligonucleotides that selectively inhibit targeted mRNA’s expression. While the scope of topical application is inherently limited to accessible tissues, these dual capabilities position SNAs as a promising platform for optimizing drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy (Table ).

 

## Hurdles on the path to translation
Despite progress in understanding SNA biodistribution, several obstacles hinder its practical translation. There remains significant scope to improve strategy optimization, mechanistic investigation, and clinical translation in the study of SNAs’ biodistribution.
First, current strategy optimization still faces problems. There is no universal approach to effectively minimize unintended accumulation in non-target organs, and each strategy carries inherent limitations. For example, although surface engineering of SNAs with diverse chemical moieties can extend their circulation half-life and improve biodistribution, these modifications may also disrupt the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles, potentially compromising their biological performance [ ]. Furthermore, many reported conjugation chemistries have been specifically optimized for particular core or nucleic acid shell architectures [ ], and applying these strategies to alternative SNAs constructs frequently leads to diminished colloidal or serum stability. Equally importantly, practical considerations such as synthetic accessibility and reaction yield are often neglected, with multistep protocols and low-yield reactions driving up cost and limiting scalability. In conclusion, these constraints hinder the broad applicability of otherwise promising design strategies.
Secondly, mechanistic understanding remains limited. Significant gaps persist in the mechanistic understanding of SNA–biological system interactions. Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating nanoparticle uptake mechanisms and organ-specific trafficking patterns, key processes—particularly those governing protein corona formation—remain insufficiently characterized. For instance, studies consistently demonstrate that protein coronas play an indispensable role in modulating cellular recognition, governing nanoparticle biodistribution, and directly determining their fate in vivo [ ]. However, current knowledge of protein coronas remains superficial. Most insights are predominantly derived from in vitro studies using simplified biological fluids, while systematic investigations of SNAs behavior in vivo remain limited. This disparity in research focus presents significant challenges, as the biological environment within living systems is inherently more dynamic and physiologically complex than controlled laboratory conditions. Consequently, the true composition and functional impact of protein coronae on SNAs in vivo remain poorly understood, introducing variability and unpredictability that challenge clinical development.
Lastly, the clinical translation of SNAs lacks sufficient attention. The time span of existing biodistribution studies is often limited to short-term outcomes following SNAs administration. Yet in physiological contexts, SNAs undergo prolonged metabolic processing, with distinct degradation pathways for the nucleic acid shell and nanoparticle core [ ]. This issue is especially relevant for SNAs built on inorganic materials such as AuNPs, which may persist in tissues over extended periods. For any NP intended for clinical use, long-term safety and non-toxicity are essential, but addressing these questions requires substantial clinical data and extended monitoring. As a result, relatively few studies have investigated the intermediate and late-phase pharmacokinetics and biosafety of SNAs, leaving critical questions about long-term safety, clearance, and tissue retention unresolved. Substantial hurdles must be overcome to translate SNAs from laboratory research to practical clinical applications.

## Conclusion and prospects
SNAs have garnered growing attention for their unique properties and potential applications. However, their clinical translation remains limited by key challenges, particularly high off-target tissue uptake, which reduces therapeutic efficacy and raises safety concerns. Enhancing the tissue specificity of SNAs has therefore become a critical focus in current research.
The distribution of SNAs in the body is influenced by their interactions with various organs. The liver, kidneys, and spleen are particularly important in SNA uptake and metabolism. The liver is the most prominent organ in SNA uptake. Hepatic cells, such as Kupffer cells and LSECs, take up SNAs influenced by hepatic blood flow and the protein corona formed in circulation. The kidneys also play a role in SNA uptake and filtration. They filter SNAs through the glomerular filtration barrier and interactions with renal tubules, allowing SNAs to be excreted in urine. The spleen, with its reticuloendothelial cell populations, similarly contributes to nanoparticle sequestration. Understanding these organ-specific uptake mechanisms has informed the development of strategies to modulate SNA biodistribution.
Broadly, these strategies fall into three categories. The first and most extensively explored involves the intrinsic structural modification to the SNA. Due to their modular nature, SNAs can be modified in terms of core size, type, and the length, type, and sequence of their nucleic acid shells. These modifications have led to the emergence of novel architectures like liposomal SNAs. The second approach entails external surface functionalization of the SNA, including the attachment of targeting ligands or shielding agents such as PEG or IgG, to influence immune recognition and improve specificity. The third method centers on altering the administration context, such as pre-conditioning the biological environment to favor desirable SNA behavior or modifying the route of delivery. For instance, localized delivery minimizes systemic exposure and enhances site-specific activity, particularly in dermatological or accessible tumor applications. Despite encouraging progress, persistent limitations include the absence of accurate, predictive models for biodistribution and a limited understanding of long-term in vivo metabolism.
Future research should prioritize achieving an optimal balance between SNAs’ functionality and biocompatibility to advance their safe and effective use in biomedical applications. Understanding the biological distribution of SNAs is central to this goal and holds substantial value both for promoting clinical translation and for elucidating the mechanisms of nanomaterial interactions with various organs.
On one hand, SNAs are exceptional biological nanomaterials with extensive applications in drug delivery, gene regulation, and immune activation [ , ]. As SNAs continue to advance, clarifying their metabolism and behavior within the body has become an essential area of focus. Strategic modification of SNAs, especially through tuning their surface chemistry, offers a pathway toward more universal and efficient nanostructures with improved targeting accuracy and reduced off-target uptake. Such refinements will be essential for improving both safety profiles and therapeutic performance.
On the other hand, the structural tunability of SNAs also makes them a valuable probe for studying fundamental organ-nanomaterial interactions. Their well-defined architecture and measurable biodistribution patterns provide a useful model for tracking nanomaterials in vivo and for dissecting organ-specific uptake mechanisms. SNAs are compatible with a wide array of detection technologies due to their dual-core and shell structure. Fluorescence-based methods have proven particularly versatile: SNAs can either be directly labeled with fluorophores or hybridized with fluorescent complementary strands, allowing real-time visualization during in vivo studies. This core-shell architecture further permits differential labeling strategies, where separate fluorophores track spatial distribution patterns of nanoparticle components within biological systems [ ]. For metallic cores containing biologically rare elements like AuNPs, quantitative biodistribution analysis can be achieved through elemental mass spectrometry. This approach provides organ-specific accumulation data by measuring target element concentrations in excised tissues [ , ]. Temporal resolution is enhanced through radioisotope labeling techniques [ ]. Non-invasive longitudinal tracking can be performed via positron emission tomography (PET) [ ] or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) [ ], dynamically mapping systemic SNA redistribution over hours to weeks [ ]. Together, these multimodal detection strategies collectively provide a robust methodological foundation for elucidating SNA pharmacokinetics while minimizing observational artifacts inherent to single-technique approaches.
This review focuses on the biological distribution of SNAs, highlighting strategies to optimize their in vivo behavior through structural design, surface modification, and adaptation to physiological environments. These approaches seek to reduce unintended accumulation in non-target tissues and enhance delivery precision. Advances in analytical technologies, combined with growing mechanistic insights into SNA interactions in vivo, are steadily bridging the gap between preclinical research and clinical translation. With thoughtful, strategy-driven innovation, SNAs are poised to play a transformative role in precision nanomedicine and deliver meaningful benefits to human health.
