The National Institutes of Health has coined the term "nanomedicine" to describe the use of nanotechnology in biological system monitoring, control, diagnosis, and treatment. Nanomedicine continues to receive increasing interest for the rationalized delivery of therapeutics and pharmaceutical agents to achieve the required response while reducing its side effects. However, as nanotechnology continues to advance, concerns about its potential toxicological effects have also grown. This review explores the current state of nanomedicine, focusing on the types of nanoparticles used and their associated properties that contribute to nanotoxicity. It examines the mechanisms through which nanoparticles exert toxicity, encompassing various cellular and molecular interactions. Furthermore, it discusses the assessment methods employed to evaluate nanotoxicity, encompassing in-vitro and in-vivo models, as well as emerging techniques. The review also addresses the regulatory issues surrounding nanotoxicology, highlighting the challenges in developing standardized guidelines and ensuring the secure translation of nanomedicine into clinical settings. It also explores into the challenges and ethical issues associated with nanotoxicology, as understanding the safety profile of nanoparticles is essential for their effective translation into therapeutic applications.

## Introduction
Nanotechnology represents both a captivating concept and a tangible reality [ ]. Back in 1959, Richard Feynman introduced nanotechnology as a scientific concept [ ]. However, little did we know that humanity had been harnessing the power of nanotechnology for centuries without even realizing it. The field of nanotechnology is experiencing rapid growth and has firmly established its presence in several fields including electronics, cosmetics and healthcare. Many pharmaceuticals or drug discovery leads facing solubility concern, which makes them less effective and cause side effects. However, their solubility is improved by enclosing these medications in a lipid or polymer shell, known as nanocarriers. This not only increases bioavailability of drug that enters the body but also reduces the quantity of drug needed for treatment, which in turn lowers costs and toxicity. Additionally, the use of nanocarriers presents new possibilities for targeted treatment. By attaching targeting ligands to the surface of the NPs, to specific cell types, such as immune or cancer cells. This approach has shown potential in the treatment of diseases [ ]. Advancements include targeted drug delivery, improved diagnostics, and the development of smart nanomaterials that enhance the precision and efficacy of treatments. Along with advancement of nanomedicine there are several challenges like complex characterization, safety, toxicity of nanomedicine, harmonization of guidelines and clinical testing approval, which need to be addressed for better treatment [ ]. Engineered nanoparticles have great possibilities to improve diagnosis and therapy precision. Nanotechnology can get beyond limitations related to conventional delivery methods, addressing challenges at both macro and micro scales. These challenges range from biodistribution on a larger scale to intracellular trafficking on a smaller scale. Nanotechnology achieves this by enabling cell-specific targeting, and molecular transport by employing various other innovative approaches [ ]. Under the scope of these initiatives, significant support has been provided to drive the exploration and enhancement of nanotechnology and nanoparticles (NPs) have played a significant role in advancing research and development in this field. NPs can improve the stability and solubility of enclosed substances, aid in the transport across membranes, and extend the duration of circulation, thus enhancing both safety and effectiveness [ , ]. Due to these factors, research on NPs has gained widespread attention and has yielded promising outcomes in both in vitro and in-vivo studies [ , ].
In the last few centuries, material science and medicine were being impacted by the multidisciplinary field of nanotechnology [ ]. Nanomedicine, within the realm of nanotechnology's impact on healthcare, encompasses a range of market trends [ ]. Research and industry were revolutionised by the invention of nanoparticles, nanowires, and quantum dots, which led to advancements such as drug delivery systems (products that utilize nanocarriers to deliver approved drugs, novel drug candidates, and diagnostic components) [ ]. Nevertheless, despite the significant research efforts driven by the National Nanotechnology Initiative, the current number of nanomedicines available to patients is considerably lower than the initial expectations for the field. This disparity can be ascribed, to some extent, to a gap in the process of translating findings from animal studies to their effective use in humans [ , ]. This translational gap arises due to a limited comprehension of the difference in physiological and pathological characteristics between the human and animals. Specifically, the impact of these disparities on the action and efficacy of nanomedicines within the human body remains inadequately understood [ ]. Not only species differences but also heterogeneity among patients hinders the clinical translation of nanomedicines. Presently, there is limited research on nanomedicines and specific patient population interaction, stratified based on individual characteristics. As a result, very few authorized nanomedicines are suggested as initial treatments and many of that show efficacies in just a small percentage of patients. This is mainly due to NPs' effectiveness can be affected by the understudied variability in disease biology and patient characteristics.
Nanotoxicology, as a specialized branch of toxicology, focuses on assessing the potential toxic effects of structures or particles at the nanoscale, specifically those with a diameter smaller than 100 nm [ ]. Broadly speaking, the increased toxicity observed in certain nano-sized chemicals, as opposed to their bulk counterparts, can be attributed to the significantly higher surface-to-volume ratio exhibited by NPs. This elevated ratio renders NPs remarkably reactive, leading to their enhanced toxicity [ ]. This phenomenon goes beyond drugs and includes the physiological conditions in different tissues of the human body. Nanomaterials are thought to have the potential to permeate cellular membranes, biological barriers, and tissues more effectively than larger-sized materials [ ]. Contrarily, extensive biokinetic studies have predominantly shown that particle size exerts a limited influence on biodistribution [ ]. Therefore, acquiring a thorough comprehension of the interactions among nanomaterials used in medicine and the human body can help mitigate some of these challenges. It is crucial to prioritize the evaluation of occupational and environmental risks linked to the production and disposal of nanomaterials to ensure a responsible and sustainable path forward. Nanotechnologies could have an effect on health, safety, and the environment raises concerns that could impede their commercial adoption and market expansion [ ]. As a result, the question arises about the current state and future trajectory of nanotechnology. In this review paper, we have focused on shedding light on the influence of nanomaterial properties that contribute to nanotoxicity. Initially, we have discussed the types of NPs and the crucial role of nanomaterial properties in determining their toxic potential. Additionally, size, shape, surface charge, content, and surface functionality have been found to influence NP biological reactions. Designing safer nanoparticles with lower toxicity requires understanding these interactions [ ]. Next, we have examined the intricate mechanisms by which nanoparticles exert toxicity. These mechanisms involve interactions with cellular membranes, oxidative stress generation, inflammatory responses, autophagy dysregulation, and interference with cellular signalling pathways. A comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms is essential to mitigate potential health risks associated with nanomaterial exposure. Additionally, the different assessment methods employed to evaluate nanotoxicity have been discussed. These methods include in-vitro cell-based assays, in-vivo animal models, and advanced techniques such as omics-based approaches and high-throughput screening. An in-depth evaluation of these methods provides valuable insights into the diverse approaches used to assess nanotoxicity and guides future research in this field. Lastly, the regulatory challenges of nanomedicine have been discussed [ , ].

## Types of nanoparticles
Inorganic, lipid-based, and polymeric nanocarriers are most commonly used in nanoparticle-based medication delivery (Fig.  ). These carriers can transport conventional pharmaceuticals, nucleic acids, gene editing cargos, and antibodies. Nanomedicine has the potential to transform medication delivery, enabling more effective and focused therapy [ ]. Nanotechnology has enabled the production of nanomedicines, nano-sized drug delivery devices that improve treatment efficacy, side effects, and patient outcomes. This section covers nanomedicines for drug delivery and their benefits and drawbacks in a simple yet comprehensive manner (Table  ). Nanomaterials improve medication bioavailability, controlled release, and tissue targeting due to their unique characteristics. Nanomedicines can overcome biological barriers like the blood–brain barrier, increase medication solubility, and extend circulation. These benefits could greatly improve cancer, infectious, and chronic disease therapy. There are barriers and restrictions to overcome alongside these promising advances [ ]. 

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Inorganic NPs synthesized from inorganic materials like gold, iron, and silica have found extensive application in drug delivery and imaging. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), in various forms such as nanospheres, nanorods, nanostars, nanoshells, and nanocages are meticulously designed and can be precisely engineered to exhibit diverse properties [ ]. Their application in restoring vision in mice afflicted with degenerated photoreceptors garnered significant attention [ ]. Biodegradable inorganic NPs show promise in the diagnosis and treatment of conditions like atherosclerosis and cancer [ , ]. The targeted release is enabled by dynamic nano assemblies using two-dimensional inorganic NPs like layered double hydroxides, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), graphene, black phosphorus, transition metal oxides (TMOs), and transition metal carbides and nitrides [ ]. Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) can be employed to deliver therapeutics directly into cells' cytoplasm, evading detection by pump transporters in multi-drug-resistant cancer cells. They can also be utilised for targeted therapeutic delivery via external magnetic fields, lowering the toxicity to healthy tissues. For example, IONPs can carry medications to the CNS and inner ear, which are protected by blood-organ barriers and contain fragile tissues [ , ]. Mark G et al. described the pharmacokinetic performance and the desired characteristics of the IONPs. In contrast, the blood half-life of these particles was determined to be only 6 min, indicating the rapid uptake and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS). In the same report, the authors also investigated the blood half-life of a second ultra-small IONP type (size < 10 nm, while AMI-25 was 72 nm) and found that these particles remain in the bloodstream with half-lives of 81 min, indicating that IONP size plays a crucial role in particle clearance. Apart from IONP size, also the coating and surface charge play a major role in their blood clearance, pharmacokinetics and biodistribution [ ]. Wu L et al. reported the IONPs have toxic effects on human health, especially when exposed for long periods or in high doses. Potential side effects of IONPs included oxidative stress in several tissues, particularly the heart. Additionally, elevated levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and ROS in the serum, liver, heart, and spleen [ ]. Iron oxide, especially magnetite (Fe3O4) or maghemite (Fe2O3) in FDA-approved nanomedicines, effective in imaging, drug delivery, and thermal-based therapies [ ]. Examples like calcium phosphate and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNPs) find applications in gene and drug delivery [ , ]. Quantum dots, mainly composed of semiconducting materials like silicon, show promising potential in in-vitro imaging and are under exploration for in-vivo diagnostics [ , ]. Titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2NPs), employed in cosmetics, food additives, and pharmaceuticals, exhibit cytotoxic effects, genotoxicity, and oxidative stress [ ], potentially inducing indirect genotoxicity by interfering with deoxyribose nucleic acid (DNA) repair mechanisms [ ].
While inorganic NPs have shown promising strides in preclinical studies, their transition to clinical application encounters a daunting hurdle often referred to as the "valley of death". This critical phase poses a substantial challenge, impeding their seamless integration into practical medical use. Out of the FDA-approved nanomedicines, only six are based on inorganic nanoparticles, with the majority of them being iron oxide-based NPs. The limited clinical application of these inorganic nanoparticles may be attributed to their inherent low solubility and concerns regarding toxicity, especially in formulations containing heavy metals. In-vitro studies conducted on Feraheme, an FDA-approved medication for treating iron deficiency in chronic kidney disease, demonstrated elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ]. Polymeric micelles, particularly those derived from pluronic-based copolymers, offer a promising avenue to mitigate certain toxic properties exhibited by inorganic NPs [ ]. The degradability of inorganic NPs can be controlled and adjusted by incorporating organic moieties or doping them with inorganic species, such as MOFs [ ]. Hence, the inherent toxic characteristics resulted in limited application of inorganic NPs in biological applications. This inspired research on merging them with other suitable nanosystems, including cell membrane masks or lipid-based nanosystems, to mitigate potential toxicity and enhance their overall biocompatibility [ ].

## Lipid-based nanoparticles
Lipid-based nanoparticles, which are typically spherical in shape and contain a lipid bilayer that encapsulates aqueous compartments, provide several benefits as a drug delivery system. These benefits include the ability to transport substantial payloads, biocompatibility, excellent bioavailability, and the simplicity of formulation [ ]. As a result, lipid-based NPs have emerged as the predominant class of FDA-approved nanomedicines, owing to their versatility and efficacy [ ]. Among the various subsets of lipid-based nanocarriers, liposomes are particularly prominent. Liposomes are typically composed of phospholipids, enabling the formation of unilamellar or multilamellar vesicular structures. This unique property of liposomes enables them to efficiently carry and deliver a wide range of drugs, including hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds. Different things can affect how stable liposomes are, both in the lab and in living things. These include the size of the nanoparticles, their surface charge, the type of lipids they contain, the number of lamellae they have, and any changes to their surface that involve ligands or polymers [ , ]. To stop the reticuloendothelial system from taking them up quickly, liposomes are often modified on the outside to make them last longer in the bloodstream and carry drugs better. These modifications have played a crucial role in facilitating the clinical utilization of liposomes. The nanodispersions made up of uniformly dispersed nanoparticles in a liquid media, with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm. Moreover, the surface area increases with decrease in particle size, which improves the rate of breakdown and absorption of the dispersed materials. In pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and culinary goods, these dispersions are frequently employed to increase the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of active substances [ ]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are a notable subgroup within lipid-based NPs that are extensively employed for nucleic acid delivery, offering improved stability, protection, and targeted delivery capabilities. LNPs exhibit similarities to liposomes, but they possess a distinct feature in that they form micellar structures within the core of the particle. The distinctive structure of this morphology can be altered by manipulating the formulation and synthesis factors [ ]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are typically formed by including four essential elements. These lipids, known as cationic or ionizable lipids, can bind to negatively charged genetic material and help it to escape from endosomes. Phospholipids are utilized to provide structural integrity to the particles, while cholesterol is incorporated to enhance stability and enable membrane fusion. Additionally, PEGylated lipids are included to enhance stability and prolong circulation time [ , ]. The phytosomes are lipid-based delivery vehicles that are utilised to encapsulate nutraceuticals obtained from plants. Plant extracts containing phytochemicals react with phosphatidylcholine or other polar molecules in an aprotic solvent to generate these phyto-phospholipid complexes. The complex that is produced as a result of this interaction increases the absorption rate of both polar and nonpolar natural substances. Phytosomes are frequently used in pharmaceuticals and herbal remedies to enhance natural component potency and absorption through improved cellular uptake and degradation prevention [ ]. LNPs have proven to be highly efficient in delivering nucleic acids, and their production is simple and uncomplicated. Additionally, their tiny size and ability to remain stable in serum make them essential in individualized genetic therapy applications [ , ]. Ionizable LNPs are an ideal platform for delivering nucleic acid treatments because they have the distinctive feature of being almost neutral in charge at physiological pH. However, in the acidic environment of endosomal compartments, they acquire a positive charge, facilitating endosomal escape and enabling efficient intracellular delivery [ , ]. Despite the numerous advantages, LNP systems may encounter limitations such as low drug loading capacity and suboptimal biodistribution, leading to disproportionate accumulation in the liver and spleen [ ].

## Polymeric nanoparticles
Polymeric nanomaterials (NMs) exhibit versatility in their assembly into various pharmaceutical nanosystems, including NPs, dendrimers, polymeric micelles, and drug conjugates. In this category, polymeric nanoparticles include both vesicular systems called nano capsules and matrix systems [ ]. The natural polymeric characteristics of nanoparticles have additional benefits that are worth exploring, such as enhanced biological compatibility, biodegradability, and reduced immunogenicity [ , ]. Polymeric NPs can be produced using natural or synthetic materials, as well as monomers or preformed polymers, providing a diverse range of structures and properties. This versatility allows for precise control over multiple NPs characteristics. Polymeric nanoparticles (NPs) are ideal for delivering substances since they are biocompatible and can be easily manufactured. Various techniques, including emulsification [ ], nanoprecipitation [ ], ionic gelation [ , ] and microfluidics [ ], are employed to synthesize polymeric NPs, resulting in distinct final products with specific attributes. Polymeric nanoparticles are capable of transporting a diverse array of substances, including both water-repellent and water-attracting molecules, as well as materials with different molecular weights such as tiny molecules, large biological molecules, proteins, and vaccines [ – ]. This versatility makes polymeric nanoparticles very suitable for co-delivery purposes. By modulating features like structure, strength, response, and surface charge, it is feasible to accurately control the loading efficiency and release kinetics of these medicinal substances. Among the various types of polymeric NPs, polymeric micelles show promise as drug carriers and in cancer treatment [ , ]. The nanomicelles are small, spherical bionanomaterials created when amphiphilic molecules that have both hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts self-assembled in a liquid medium. Their sizes usually vary from 5 to 100 nm, with a hydrophilic shell around a hydrophobic core because they hold poorly water-soluble drugs in their core and release them in a specific site of interest. This increases the drug's bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Surfactants such as pluronic and tetronic can form micelles when their concentration reaches a certain threshold, known as the critical micellar concentration. These micelles possess distinct characteristics. However, high molecular weight pluronics might accumulate in tissues limiting their application [ ]. Polymersomes, newer in the nanoparticle realm, mimic vesicles with stable structures and higher cargo space compared to liposomes [ ]. Yet, hurdles like potential toxic by-products from polymers, aggregation risks, and scaling limitations hinder their clinical transition [ ]. Dendrimers, structured like branched tree polymers, offer precise control over their mass, size, shape, and surface chemistry [ ]. Meanwhile, polymeric nano gels prove instrumental in scaffold development, wound healing, and minimally invasive cancer treatments [ – ].

## Toxicity-related attributes of nanomaterials
The relationships between nanomaterials and biological systems are influenced by many fundamental features essential to the nanomaterials (Table  ). These properties include the chemical composition of the core nanomaterials, the presence of impurities, the implementation of functionalization strategies, and the surface properties such as surface charge, hydrophobicity, and the presence of ligands. Additionally, the size and shape of the nanomaterials play a significant role, as does their tendency to agglomerate [ ]. Furthermore, the bio corona is formed through the binding of biomolecules over the surface of the nanomaterial, which also influences nano-bio interactions [ ]. Collectively, these properties determine how nanomaterials interact with biological systems at various levels, influencing their behavior, uptake, distribution, and potential toxicity [Fig.  ]. In this section, we have delved into a detailed exploration of nanotoxicity, focusing on the toxicological impacts of different properties exhibited by nanomaterials. Specifically, how these properties influence cellular uptake and underlying mechanisms of nanomaterial toxicity have been elucidated [ ]. By examining these review aims to offer a thorough comprehension of the correlation between the features of nanomaterials and their possible detrimental impacts on biological systems [ ]. 

## Chemical composition
The chemical composition of the nanomaterials plays a pivotal role in influencing their toxicity. This composition determines crucial factors such as dissolution characteristics, redox capability, ionization properties, and affinity to macromolecules. These features collectively define the potential for toxicity and contribute to understanding the underlying mechanisms of how nanomaterials may exert harmful effects. For instance study conducted on nano-ZnO (zinc oxide) showed that it is embryotoxic and this toxicity was ascribed to its property of dissolution into toxic Zn2+ ions [ ]. In a recent study, Mirshafiee et al. compared the toxicity of 29 rare earth oxide nanoparticles (REOs) and TMOs in liver cells. Pro-oxidative TMOs induced apoptosis in hepatocytes, while REOs triggered pyroptosis in Kupffer's cells and other macrophages. These differences in cell death pathways are likely linked to variations in solubility, redox properties, and catalytic activity [ ].
Core nanomaterials like nano-CuO (copper oxide), nano-Ag (silver), and quantum dots have been identified as sources of potential risk due to their ability to degrade and release toxic ions, posing significant threats to biological systems [ ]. However, stable metallic nanomaterials, exemplified by AuNPs, exhibit superior biocompatibility, as demonstrated in research showing no toxicity towards RAW264.7 macrophages. Au NPs also reduce ROS levels, indicating potential for mitigating inflammatory responses [ ]. In contrast, soluble metallic nanomaterials release toxic ions and induce ROS, emphasizing their high toxicity. Non-metallic nanomaterials, such as MSNPs, demonstrate lower toxicity, attributed to unique surface silanol groups that interact with biomolecules [ ]. Liposomes, known for favorable biocompatibility, have successfully transitioned into clinical applications as drug carriers, optimizing therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse effects [ ].
Deliberate material design tactics such as surface coating, grafting, and element loading play a vital role in determining the toxicological aspects of nanomaterials. Element doping involves introducing a small percentage of foreign atoms, typically metallic, into pure nanomaterials to modify their electronic, magnetic, optical, and redox properties. Carefully controlled doping can enhance or tailor nanomaterial functionalities. However, the introduction of dopant atoms often increases the generation of ROS, leading to elevated oxidative stress. Rational doping strategies, creating heterojunction structures, can improve electron transfer kinetics, enhancing ROS generation efficiency. Doping can also impact nanotoxicity by influencing nanomaterial dissolution. For example, iron doping has been shown to alleviate the toxicity of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) by slowing down dissolution, reducing the release of toxic ions, and mitigating adverse effects on biological systems [ – ].
Nanomaterial safety is influenced by the presence of impurities, such as metal catalyst residues in carbon nanotubes (CNTs), contributing to CNT toxicity by enhancing ROS generation. Controlling these impurities is crucial for safe nanomaterial use. In the case of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)-coated gold nanomaterials, toxicity is linked to residual CTAB. Proper removal of CTAB is essential to ensure the safe use of these nanomaterials and minimize potential adverse effects [ , ]. Nanotoxicity can also be influenced by unanticipated organic contamination, as demonstrated in a study by Chen et al. In this study, molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) nanosheets were synthesized with a method chosen to minimize organic contamination, resulting in low observed toxicity. The presence of lipopolysaccharides (LPS), common contaminants highly resistant to sterilization methods, is a significant concern. LPS contamination in nanomaterials can activate Toll-like receptor 4 on immune cells, leading to increased proinflammatory cytokine release. Accurate assessment of nanomaterial toxicity requires consideration and mitigation of LPS contamination to avoid misinterpretations related to inflammation-inducing capabilities [ , ].

## Size
In the interaction between NPs and cells, size emerges as the most crucial physical property [ , ]. Nanoparticle size directly influences distribution, diffusion, and cellular response [ ]. The larger surface area of smaller NPs enhances their reactivity and catalytic potential in biological environments [ , ]. AuNPs, around 10–16 nm, enter the cytoplasm, while those below 6 nm may penetrate the cell nucleus, potentially increasing toxicity [ ]. Although NPs around 50 nm demonstrate optimal internalization efficiency, studies indicate that smaller NPs (15 to 30 nm) and larger NPs (70 to 240 nm) may exhibit lower cellular uptake rates [ ]. Nanomedicines can enter cells through six significant pathways based on their size viz., phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, independent endocytosis, and direct cell infiltration [ ]. Caveolin-dependent and clathrin-dependent endocytosis pathways result in caveolae (50–100 nm) and intracellular vesicles (100–500 nm), respectively, making them primary mechanisms for nanomaterial internalization in the tens to a few hundred nanometers range. In contrast, macropinocytosis forms leaky macropinosomes (0.5–1.5 μm), facilitating the uptake of larger nanomaterials, typically in the range of hundreds of nanometers up to 250 nm [ ]. Cellular uptake of nanomaterials is influenced by factors beyond size, including surface hydrophobicity, ligands, and cell type. For instance, herceptin-modified gold NPs (Her-AuNPs) at 40–50 nm showed higher cellular uptake than smaller (2–10 nm) and larger (80–100 nm) counterparts. This difference is attributed to various factors such as smaller Her-AuNPs have lower ligand density and faster diffusion rates, while larger ones, despite forming multiple interactions, face challenges in efficient membrane wrapping. Thus, ligand density, binding avidity, and membrane wrapping ability, in addition to size, contribute to nanomaterial cellular uptake [ ].
The optimal size range for NPs in bio-applications and phagocytosis uptake is typically greater than 10 nm but smaller than 100 nm. In a work by Zhao et al. MSNPs, showed that 100 nm MSNPs could be absorbed onto human red blood cells, while larger 600 nm MSNPs induced local membrane deformation [ ]. The choice of NPs size in biomedical applications varies based on specific requirements; for example, 50 nm NPs are commonly used for efficient drug delivery [ ]. NPs size significantly impacts organ responses and organism reactions upon exposure [ ]. In terms of cellular interactions, size is crucial for particle recognition and clearance. Smaller particles 10 to 50 nm is effectively cleared by the kidneys, influencing their removal from the body. Considering these size-related impacts on health, researchers prioritize size as a primary parameter in characterizing NP toxicity. Studies have shown that smaller TiO2NPs (20 nm) induce more pronounced lung inflammatory responses than larger particles (250 nm) [ , ]. This was attributed to the fact that smaller nanoparticles have a greater surface area and increased surface activity, which enhances their ability to interact with various substances and initiate chain reactions within biological systems. Recent studies have shown that size of nanoparticles influence their biodistribution, clearance, and cellular uptake. Small nanoparticles (< 100 nm) may accumulate in non-target tissues and organs, such as the liver, spleen, or lungs, potentially leading to organ toxicity. Also found dextran NPs and IONPs, folic acid NPs causes oxidative stress and inflammation [ ]. Smaller hydrogels (< 200 nm) are more readily internalized by cells, which enhances curcumin's cytotoxicity against cancer cells like MCF-7 but its increase off-target effects in healthy tissues. Larger hydrogels (> 500 nm) have slower cellular uptake and reduced acute toxicity, but they accumulate in organs and cause chronic toxicity [ ]. Recent findings also suggest that accumulation of NPs can cause histopathological changes in the liver, with silicon dioxide nanoparticles SiO2NPs demonstrating greater toxicity than silver silicon dioxide (Ag-SiO2) zinc oxide silicon dioxide nanoparticles (ZnO-SiO2NP) [ , ].

## Shape
As shown in Fig.  , nanomaterials exhibit diverse shapes like nanospheres, nanotubes, nanorods, nanowires, nanocubes, nanosheets etc. The shape influences toxicokinetics and in-vivo toxicity [ , ]. Adjusting the aspect ratio of rod-like MSNPs can reduce liver distribution and promote kidney excretion, regardless of administration route. In oral ingestion, MSNPs generally cause minimal organ damage, except for aspect ratio-dependent renal toxicity, leading to severe kidney damage [ , ]. 
Nanoparticle shape significantly influences cellular uptake, cytotoxicity, in-vivo biofate, and organ toxicity. Computational simulations indicate that nanospheres exhibit the fastest endocytosis rate, followed by nanocubes, nanorods, and nanodisks, attributed to minimal changes in membrane binding energy for symmetrical nanospheres [ ]. Experimental findings on gold nanomaterials support this, showing lowest cell viability for nanospheres, followed by prismatic-shaped nanogold (AuPr) with slightly higher viability. Cubic and rod-shaped nanogold demonstrated comparable bioavailability within the concentration range of 10–100 nm [ ]. Despite lower cell internalization, AuNPs induced significant cell death due to damage caused by their sharp edges [ ]. Nanoparticle shapes also impact crystal plane exposure, biocorona formation, and catalytic capability, leading to distinct toxic potentials [ ].

## Surface properties
Nano-biological interactions primarily manifest between nanomaterials and the surrounding environment, where surface characteristics exert a profound influence on behavior [ ]. Crucial determinants of toxicity encompass surface charge, hydrophobicity, and specific surface atoms or functional groups. Notably, in the realm of biomedicine, nanoparticles often undergo post-synthesis surface modifications to augment colloidal stability, biocompatibility, safety, and pharmacokinetic profiles. These modifications alter primary interfacial properties, subsequently shaping toxicological profiles [ , ]. Surface engineering to render nanomaterials hydrophilic and electro-neutral enhances their capacity to traverse mucus barriers by reducing the effects of contact with hydrophobic and negatively charged mucin strands, prevalent in mucosal barriers. On the other hand, nanoparticles that have hydrophobic surfaces and are positively charged are beneficial for crossing epithelial barriers. Hence, the intricate interplay of nanomaterial surface properties significantly influences absorption dynamics, contingent upon the specific biological context [ , ]. Surface characteristics, including bio-corona formation and colloidal support, affect nanomaterial dissemination and clearance in the body. These properties dictate interactions with biological components, such as proteins, shaping nanomaterial behavior in terms of biodistribution and elimination [ , ]. For instance, AuNPs modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG) exhibit enhanced dispersion in the bloodstream, lowering separation results in a more consistent distribution all across the body. This contrasts with polyethylene imide (PEI)-modified GNPs, prone to agglomeration and sequestration by mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS) cells, resulting in liver and spleen accumulation and limited excretion [ ]. Surface properties of nanomaterials are pivotal not only for their toxicokinetics but also for cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and cytotoxicity. Positively-charged nanomaterials exhibit enhanced cellular internalization due to stronger electrostatic interactions with cytomembranes [ , ]. Surface modifications influence biocorona formation, affecting cellular uptake mechanisms [ , ]. Surface ligands direct nanomaterial targeting capabilities, allowing precise localization to specific cellular compartments [ , ]. Manshian et al. demonstrated a correlation between surface hydrophobicity and cell membrane damage, emphasizing its role in cytotoxicity [ ]. Liu et al. proved that ligands and surface charges affect cellular metabolic processes and cytotoxicity [ , ]. Surface vacancy density in tungsten disulfide and MoS2 nanosheets impacts ferroptotic potential, highlighting the potential of surface passivation to modulate cytotoxic effects[ , , ]. Surface coatings, such as PEG and bovine serum albumin reduce ROS generation and protect against toxicity in AgNPs [ , ].

## Toxicity related to the biological transformation of nanomaterials

## Agglomeration situation
Nanomaterials exist in various forms, including agglomerates (loosely bound particles) and aggregates (chemically fused particles). The European Union's nanomaterial definition encompasses agglomerates and aggregates (AA) with specific size criteria (1–100 nm) [ ]. However, this definition is primarily regulatory and doesn't fully address the potential hazards. The toxicological implications of AA are not fully understood, requiring further research [ ]. The agglomeration state of nanomaterials significantly influences cellular uptake pathways. Agglomerates may follow distinct internalization mechanisms compared to individual nanoparticles, impacting interactions with cell receptors and membrane proteins [ ]. However, within biological systems, agglomerates/aggregates can exhibit high toxic potentials, as seen in an inhalation study with multi-walled CNTs causing respiratory tract pathology due to persistent agglomerates [ , ]. Bahadar H et al. reported that, although titanium oxide is chemically inert, studies have shown that titanium dioxide NPs have toxic health effects in experimental animals. These effects include DNA damage, genotoxicity, and lung inflammation. Specifically, titanium dioxide NPs (< 100 nm) can induce oxidative stress and form DNA adducts. In addition to genotoxicity, titanium dioxide NPs (5–200 nm) have negative effect on immune function and causes toxic effects on the liver, kidneys, spleen, myocardium, and interferes in regulation of glucose and lipid homeostasis in experimental animals [ ]. Generally, agglomerates show reduced cellular uptake due to increased particle size, but in certain cell types like macrophages, larger agglomerates can enhance the delivery of smaller pristine NPs, potentially leading to heightened toxicity effects [ ]. The agglomeration state of nanomaterials significantly influences biodistribution and elimination. Unstable nanomaterials forming large agglomerates post-intravenous injection are sequestered in MPS-rich organs, hindering efficient elimination through hepatobiliary or renal pathways [ ]. In contrast, individual NPs exhibit widespread distribution, faster excretion, and greater tissue penetration, especially when their size is below 250 nm. The impact of agglomeration on nanotoxicity is a debatable topic. While agglomerates may mitigate hazards by reducing specific surface area and interactions with cells [ , ], conflicting findings suggest increased toxicity in certain cases. For example, agglomerated CNTs in a rope-like structure showed higher cytotoxicity, possibly due to their larger size, stiffness, and solidity causing more severe cellular damage [ ]. The impact of agglomeration on nanotoxicity is intricate and varies based on factors like nanomaterial type, cell type, and exposure conditions. Further research is required to comprehend the mechanisms governing the diverse effects of agglomeration on nanotoxicity. The formation of a biocorona, where biomolecules absorb onto nanomaterial surfaces, alters their characteristics, influencing interactions with biological systems and contributing to complex biological effects [ , ].

## Biocorona formation/evolution
Another approach to reducing the ultra-high surface energy of primary nanomaterials involves the rapid adsorption of specific biomolecules onto their surfaces such occurrence is frequently denoted as the biocorona formation. The formation of biocorona is strongly influenced by the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials, including factors such as size, surface chemistry, shape, and other characteristics. Additionally, the surrounding biological environments in which the nanomaterials are present also play a crucial role in biocorona formation [ , ]. The adsorbed biocorona transforms the nanomaterial’s identity, influencing diverse nano-bio interactions [ , ]. This complex biocorona, with a stable inner layer and dynamic outer layer, significantly impacts nanomaterial biokinetics and biofate [ ]. Preferential adsorption of desopsonins like albumin and apolipoprotein enhances nanomaterial resistance to MPS cell sequestration [ ]. Glucose-coated iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs@Glc) accumulate in the liver, while polyethylene glycol-coated iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs@PEG) show wider distribution, linked to biocorona composition. IONPs@PEG degrades due to faster albumin degradation and macrophage-related lysosome accumulation. At the cellular level, biocorona formation on nanomaterials reduces nonspecific adhesion to cell membranes, preserving membrane integrity and influencing cellular uptake efficiency. Compared to bare nanomaterials, the biocorona acts as a protective layer, hindering direct interactions and potential damage to cell membranes. Additionally, the altered surface properties and interactions of nanomaterials in the biocorona affect cellular uptake mechanisms, introducing changes in internalization [ , ]. The biocorona also influences the nanoparticle's toxicity by altering its bioavailability, cellular interactions, and immune response. It is because of toxic surfaces or exposure to reactive sites, modulating the particle's potential to cause oxidative stress, inflammation, or cytotoxicity [ ]. Mustafa G et al. reported the nanocarrier and its related medications caused only minimal harm to neuronal cells, marking a notable breakthrough in treating motor abnormalities in the studied rodents. Abnormal forms of huntingin protein lead to significant cellular damage, manifesting as synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial toxicity, and impaired axonal transport. These harmful effects further influenced by biocorona formation around the nanocarriers, which alter their interaction with cells and potentially contribute to toxicity, particularly in sensitive neuronal environments [ ]. Nanomaterials can influence immune responses by maintaining or altering attached protein conformation in the biocorona, impacting immune recognition and responses [ , ]. For instance, planar MoS2 nanosheets maintain the native structure of absorbed immunoglobulin G (IgG), activating the NF-κB signalling pathway and inducing an inflammatory reaction. Nano topography with the size of 68 nm nanogold on a polymer film induces fibrinogen unfolding, activating monocytes and causing inflammation, while a size of 38 nm nanogold reduces inflammatory responses. Biocorona on nanomaterials alters gene expression related to metabolism and phospholipidosis in human hepatocytes [ ]. In rat aortic endothelial cells, it stimulates the ER stress pathways in the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to an induction of apoptosis via the AKT/caspase 3 route. Additionally, it causes fibrosis in rat lungs by prolonging the stimulation of the TGF-β/Smad 2 pathway. These examples demonstrate the wide range of impacts that biocorona has on cellular pathways in different biological systems [ , ]. One important factor in how the body reacts to nanoparticles exposure is the immune system. Immune cells have the ability to identify nanomaterials as foreign substances, which might cause immunological dysregulation or activation. The immune system's dysregulation in reaction to nanoparticles can have an impact on cytotoxic results by causing both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects. ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β are released when this imbalance happens, and these can trigger inflammatory cascades that lead to cellular damage, apoptosis, and necrosis. Moreover, immune cell function including that of macrophages, dendritic cells, and T-cells directly impacted by nanoparticles, which could result in modified immunological responses, persistent inflammation, and immune-mediated cytotoxicity [ ]. Alaa A et al. reported the immunotoxicity that manifest as chronic inflammation, allergic reactions, autoimmune responses, or weakened defense against pathogens. Prolonged immune activation can lead to cytotoxic effects, such as tissue damage, apoptosis, and necrosis, while immune suppression can allow nanoparticles to persist in the body, potentially leading to chronic diseases or cancer [ ]. In a recent study, investigating the interaction between polystyrene nanoparticles (PS NPs) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) proteins, intriguing findings emerged regarding the altered cytotoxic effects. The study found that the FBS protein corona had a vital role in minimizing the blocking of autophagic flow, decreasing the formation of autophagosomes, and mitigating lysosomal destruction in RAW264.7 cells caused by PS NPs. The findings emphasize the substantial influence of the FBS protein corona on the cellular reaction to PS NPs and provide insight into the possible processes that explain the observed cytotoxic effects [ ]. To investigate the autophagic flux-blocking mechanism, the study examined the intracellular localization of PS NPs. In this study, red-fluorescent PS NPs (F-PS NPs) was used and fluorescent NP-corona complexes (F-PS@FBS) was prepared by incubating them with FBS. Both F-PS NPs and F-PS@FBS were co-localized with lysosomes, resulting in yellow fluorescence. Notably, the fluorescence yield was more prominent in the F-PS NPs group compared to the F-PS@FBS group (Fig.  A). In the effort investigating lysosomal damage caused by in RAW264.7 cells, the use of acridine orange (AO) dye revealed intriguing results. Exposure to PS NPs led to a notable increase in cells with permeable, leaky lysosomes (79.4%) compared to the control group (4.1%). However, when PS NPs were coated with FBS (PS@FBS group), the lysosomal damage was significantly reduced (28.6%) [ ]. These findings suggest that surface modification with FBS holds promise as a potential protective measure against lysosomal damage induced by PS NPs, as depicted in Fig.  B. In normal cells, cathepsin B exhibited a spot-shaped pattern within lysosomes. However, in cells treated with PS NPs, cathepsin B staining appeared diffuse throughout the cytoplasm, indicating leakage and increased lysosomal membrane permeability. Remarkably, this effect was notably suppressed when cells were exposed to PS@FBS, evident by the reduced green fluorescence (Fig.  C). This suggests that PS@FBS exposure effectively inhibits cathepsin B leakage and helps maintain lysosomal membrane integrity [ ]. Bare PS NPs reduced lysosomal acidity and impaired degradation capacity in RAW264.7 cells. In contrast, PS@FBS reversed these effects, indicating FBS corona protection against lysosomal damage (Fig.  D). Moreover, the exposure of RAW264.7 cells to PS NPs resulted in a notable decrease in the activity of lysosomal acid phosphatase (to 37.6%), indicating impaired lysosomal degradation capacity, as depicted in (Fig.  E) [ ]. 

## Biodegradation
Nanomaterials are exposed to several challenging conditions when they come into contact with the microsomal enzymes in hepatic cells and acidic, oxidative, and ionic conditions within lysosomes, especially in the MPS. These surroundings present difficulties for the structural stability of nanomaterials in addition to their deterioration process [ – ]. Biodegradation of nanomaterials has significant implications for their toxicological profiles, which are influenced by factors such as the extent and rate of degradation, as well as the toxicity of both the intact nanomaterials and their degradation products [ ]. The degradation process affects the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials, potentially affecting their interactions and cellular uptake. Moreover, the release of degradation products can introduce new toxicological properties, depending on their composition and bioactivity. Therefore, biodegradation is crucial in shaping the overall toxicological characteristic and potential adverse effects of nanomaterials. In the case of MSNP, a decrease in their aspect ratio results in an early degradation rate when exposed to simulated body fluids. This accelerated degradation has been identified as a contributing factor to increased systemic absorption, higher distribution in the liver, and significant renal damage after taking the medication orally [ ]. The biodegradation of nanoparticles is absolutely important for defining their toxicity mechanisms, including that of nanosilver [ ]. The mechanism of toxicity associated with nanosilver is primarily attributed to its dissolution [ ]. The toxicity mechanism of nanosilver involves its dissolution process. Nanosilver particles undergo dissolution in the acidic lysosomes, converting them into Ag+ ions. This dissolution process leads to enhanced lysosomal membrane permeabilization, resulting in the release of lysosomal contents and Ag+ ions into the cytoplasm [ ]. The presence of Ag+ ions and lysosomal contents in the cytoplasm contributes to mitochondrial damage, generation of ROS, and induction of cell apoptosis. During the dissolution in the lysosome, unstable Ag+ ions can undergo binding with organic acids and transform into Ag–O– species [ , ]. Upon interaction with cysteine-contained proteins in the cytoplasm, Ag–O– can further convert into Ag–S– species. This speciation change may induce apoptosis involving mitochondria and facilitate the excretion of silver from the cells. Both AgNPs and Ag+ ions reduced the viability of THP-1 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner, as measured by the CCK-8 assay, which reflects mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity (Fig.  A). Ag+ ions exhibited stronger toxicity compared to AgNPs at the same dose. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for AgNPs were 14.6 μg/mL at 24 h and 8.3 μg/mL at 48 h, while the IC50 values for Ag ions at 24 and 48 h were 5.3 μg/mL and 2.9 μg/mL, respectively. The live-dead assay was employed to assess the live or dead cell ratio following treatment (Fig.  B) [ ]. At 24 h, both 10 μg/mL of AgNPs and 5 μg/mL of silver ions exhibited strong cytotoxicity, resulting in a higher number of cell deaths [ , ]. 

## Mechanisms of nanoparticle toxicity
Nanotoxicology, a critical aspect of nanoscience, investigates the potential harmful effects of engineered NPs via. Different mechanism, especially in biomedical applications (Fig.  ). Nanoparticles (NPs) are used a lot in nanomedicine, but their small size and distinctive characteristics make people worry about their safety [ – ]. Size, shape, functional groups at the surface, and dose-dependent features are some of the things that affect toxicity [ , ]. Chemically synthesized NPs may exhibit high toxicity due to synthetic chemicals, while biosynthesized NPs with biocompatible surface groups show lower toxicity. The choice of synthesis method and surface functional groups significantly impact NPs toxicity [ ]. The report by Waris A et al. suggests that larger metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) interact with various anatomical organs, for example, MNPs concentrate in the Bowman’s space, and the high reabsorption capacity of renal tubular epithelial cells causes them to accumulate in the kidneys, leading to damage and disruption of normal kidney function [ ]. Zhao H et al. suggested that NPs exposed to the kidney may provoke glomerular swelling, thickening of the basement membrane, degeneration, and necrosis of renal tubular cells. These adverse effects are likely related to oxidative stress, inflammation, autophagy, DNA damage, and ER stress induced by NPs. The earlier studies on engineered nanomaterial (ENM)-induced nephrotoxicity, with a focus on elucidating the potential molecular mechanisms of nanoparticle toxicity in the kidneys, facilitate the safer design and application of ENMs [ ] In terms of human health, dose-dependent NPs toxicity describes how a particle's deleterious effects rise with increasing concentrations or prolonged exposure. Because of their small size, nanoparticles have the potential to cause harm by more quickly entering cells and organs. They might be safe or even helpful at lower concentrations, but at greater concentrations, they can lead to oxidative stress, inflammation, damage to DNA, and even death of cells [ ]. 
The toxicity of a nanoparticle is dependent on its size, kind, and mode of exposure (ingestion, inhalation, etc.). Long-term or high-dose exposure increases the risk of cancer and damages the respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurological systems [ ]. Meng J et al. reported the toxicity of ZnO NPs with varying sizes and shapes, as well as their negative consequences. They have compared the harmful effects of ZnO NPs, short ZnO nanorods (s-ZnO NRs), and long ZnO NRs (l-ZnO NRs) using zebrafish larvae and human neuroblastoma cells SH-SY5Y. Their levels of neurotoxicity and developmental toxicity were comparable, with the low dose exhibiting somewhat more toxicity than the high dose. The low dose of l-ZnO NRs (0.1, 1, 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) showed the lowest toxicity and high doses of l-ZnO NRs have been found to cause significant developmental and neurotoxicity, including the disruption of locomotors activity and the expression of neurodevelopmental and Parkinson’s disease (PD)-responsive genes. Additionally, l-ZnO NRs induces dopaminergic neuronal loss and apoptosis in the zebrafish brain. The nanorods activate the production of ROS, and the excessive accumulation of ROS leads to mitochondrial damage and apoptosis, ultimately resulting in PD-like symptoms. Overall, l-ZnO NRs poses a significant potential risk for the onset and development of PD when administered at relatively high doses [ ]. Contrarily, it should be noted that certain biosynthesized NPs can also demonstrate toxicity when they interact with cells. Toxicity can occur due to multiple mechanisms, such as the breakdown of nanoparticles into less complicated forms or their build up within cells [ ]. The objective of nanotoxicology is to identify potential hazards and assess the safety of nanomedicines. This following section has examined current literature and provided updated insights into the toxic effects associated with nanoparticles. In this section, the key mechanisms underlying the toxicity of nanoparticles are discussed. Specifically, focused on potential of nanoparticles for direct interaction with the cell membrane, their ability to dissolve and release toxic ions, and their potential to induce oxidative stress (Fig.  ). 

## Nanoparticle binding to the cell exterior
Researchers conducted studies to assess the potential adverse effects of exposure to NPs on various organs [ , ]. According to some research, NPs can enter the human body through inhalation, ingestion and topically where they can damage cells, tissue, and organs that are nearby. Current research has prioritized the investigation of the mechanisms behind the toxicity of nanomaterials, specifically their binding to the outer part of cells. The interaction of NPs with cells or exterior part of cells such as cell membrane, metabolism of body, ribosomes, mitochondrial function and electron transport chain system affects their toxicity [ ]. The potential of NPs to produce ROS can be influenced by the charge present on the surface, which is often a primary cause of nanotoxicity [ ]. The degree of immune cell activation and cytokine release can vary based on the NPs surface charge, potentially affecting the overall toxicity [ ]. Sukhanova et al. reported that positively charged NPs exhibit significant toxicity to human health due to their unique interactions with cellular structures. The capacity of NPs to pass through cell membranes is enhanced by the electrostatic attraction between the positively charged NPs and the negatively charged cell membrane glycoproteins. This enhances their uptake by cells more effectively than negatively charged or inert nanoparticles, resulting in increased cytotoxicity [ ]. One more primary mechanism of toxicity is reported by Hu W et al. the compact binding of positively charged NPs to negatively charged intracellular components such as DNA. This binding can cause DNAdamage, disrupt cell cycle progression, and lead to prolonged cell cycle phases, which may contribute to carcinogenic processes [ ]. Heiden et al. reports have shown that positively charge polyamidoamine dendrimers demonstrate toxic effect towards zebrafish and mice embryos [ ]. According to Yu-Lan H et al. chitosan NPs showed harmful effect (via dose dependent and duration of exposure) on cells, zebrafish and mice embryo, led to impact on mortality [ ]. Further, Sandra J et al. reported that chitosan NPs effects on immunotoxicity and hemocompatibility, as well as a number of conflicting findings about how they affected the generation of ROS throughout cells. This case study led to the conclusion that although chitosan NPs exhibited cytotoxic profiles in RAW 264.7 cells, their impact in the ROS differed when examined at non-cytotoxic dosages [ ]. Upon intravenous administration, particles pass through the reticuloendothelial system, ultimately accumulating in the liver and spleen within minutes. NPs with size < 5 nm are preferably filtered out by the kidneys, while NPs with size > 150 nm are absorbed by the liver and spleen. Graphite oxide-zolendronic acid NPs shows cytotoxic effect on Human hepatic cell lines (WRL 68) and causes significant cell death with IC50 values of 932.9 μg/ml [ ]. Niosomes, a type of nanocarrier, has been used to transport drugs and gene agents like siRNA, microRNA, shRNA, lncRNA, and DNA. They are also being studied for targeted drug delivery. Fluorescent imaging studies have shown that niosomes improves cellular uptake through electrostatic interactions with cell membranes. However, the choice and optimization of surfactants used in niosomes are crucial, as they can have cytotoxic, haemolytic and irritant effects [ ]. Stealth NPs should be internalized by various cell types, leading to cytotoxic effects. According to studies conducted by Isibor et al. NPs can enter cells and cause an excessive production of reactive oxygen compounds, which harms proteins, DNA, and lipids. By triggering signalling cascades and chemical mediators, they set up inflammatory reactions. Cell membranes can be struck directly by and damaged by cationic nanoparticles. Prolonged accumulation and biodistribution of NPs within organs can result in persistent inflammation [ ]. Zhang X et al. reported the metallic nanoparticles like silver (AgNPs) direct binding of NPs to the exterior part of a cell or cell membrane can disrupt its integrity, leading to physical damage [ ]. Shi H et al. reported that titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles can induce ROS, leading to oxidative stress, which damages cell membranes as well as cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and DNA [ ]. Research by Kumah E et al. Showed that stealth NPs can cause oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and inflammatory reactions in cells, though their precise pathways depend on their size, shape, and surface chemistry [ ].
The shape of NPs affects how they interact with cell membranes. For instance, rod-shaped or needle-like nanoparticles may penetrate cell membranes more easily than spherical ones, potentially causing more damage and increasing toxicity. Different shapes can influence the mechanism and efficiency of cellular uptake. Shapes that are taken up more readily may cause higher intracellular concentrations of NPs, leading to greater toxicity [ ]. The shape of NPs affects their behaviour in the bloodstream and their distribution across different tissues. Non-spherical NPs may have altered clearance rates and tissue penetration, potentially leading to accumulation in certain organs and increased local toxicity [ ]. Different shapes of NPs tend to have prolonged circulation times and can accumulate in organs such as the lungs and liver, increasing the risk of chronic toxicity. Their shape can hinder their clearance from the body, leading to bioaccumulation and potential long-term health effects [ ]. Shape control is crucial as different shapes interact with cell membranes and tissues in unique ways, affecting cellular uptake, membrane damage, and biodistribution, all of which contribute to nanotoxicity.

## Dissolution to toxic ions
Nanoparticle dissolution, a critical factor influencing safety and toxicity, varies based on surface modifications even for identical composition and size [ ]. Dissolution often precedes cellular uptake, relying on ion channels and transporters [ ]. Toxic element release from nanoparticles, either within organisms or the environment, can lead to toxicity. Specific ion effects include binding to proteins and enzymes, impairing functions [ – ]. Metal ions may directly damage membranes or genetic material and induce oxidative stress [ – ]. Extensive research has been conducted to thoroughly investigate the significance of dissolution as a mechanism of NPs toxicity across a wide range of NPs [ ]. For instance Hang et al. conducted experiments revealing that lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) NPs underwent dissolution, releasing lithium, nickel, manganese, and cobalt ions. Toxicity observed in S. oneidensis correlated with exposure to equivalent concentrations of nickel and cobalt ions, highlighting the significance of dissolution in NMC toxicity [ ]. Additionally, equistoichiometric NMC with different morphologies showed varying toxicity, suggesting a potential role of transition metal coordination on crystal faces. However, when dosed based on surface area, all NMC morphologies exhibited the same toxicity trend, indicating that differences in dissolution based on crystal face presentation did not significantly impact toxicity to S. oneidensis [ ].
A detailed in-vitro toxicology study was performed utilizing 12 distinct cellular models covering diverse organ systems. The study included 10 distinct assays to investigate cytotoxicity, embryotoxicity, epithelial integrity, cytokine production, and oxidative stress. The results revealed a hazard ranking among the tested nanomaterials, with nano-ZnO exhibiting the highest toxicity, followed by nano-SiO2 and nano-TiO2 in most tissues. However, in the embryotoxic tissue, nano-TiO2 was found to be weakly embryotoxic, while nano-ZnO and nano-SiO2 did not exhibit embryotoxic effects. The increased toxicity observed with nano-ZnO was attributed to its ability to dissolve and release toxic Zn2+ ions, in contrast to nano-TiO2, which remains insoluble, and nano-SiO2, which produces non-toxic degradation products. These highlight the importance of considering the specific chemical properties and dissolution behavior of nanomaterials in determining their toxicity and potential adverse effects on different tissues and organs [ , ].

## Oxidative stress
Exposure to nanoparticles frequently leads to oxidative stress, which is marked by a disparity between the formation of ROS and the body's antioxidant defences [ ]. ROS, by-products of various biochemical reactions, can target enzymes, DNA components, and biomolecules, leading to mutations and cellular damage [ ]. ZnONPs have been shown to induce oxidative stress, causing DNA damage and apoptosis in human liver cells [ ]. Oxidative stress is directly linked to neurodegenerative illnesses like Alzheimer's disease. Oxidative damage in neurons is caused by an excess of ROS and the body's decreased ability to neutralise them in Alzheimer's disease. The oxidative stress leads to detrimental processes that affect the health and function of neurons, such as lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage. This damage causes neurons to degenerate and undergo apoptosis (cell death) more quickly over time, which exacerbates cognitive decline and memory loss that are hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease. Thus, oxidative stress plays a major role in the development of neurodegenerative illnesses such as Alzheimer's [ ]. ZnONPs also decrease cell viability and induce apoptosis in primary astrocytes, accompanied by elevated intracellular ROS levels [ ]. Super paramagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles used in various applications, can disrupt iron homeostasis in the neurological system, leading to oxidative stress-related disorders [ ]. These findings underscore the role of oxidative stress as a mechanism of nanotoxicity. Efforts are underway to understand the relationship between nanomaterial electronic structures, ROS production, and toxicity. Li et al. found a direct correlation between abiotic ROS generation by silver metal oxide nanoparticles and their toxicity to Escherichia coli. In another study, nickel oxide (NiO) was observed to have adverse effects on male reproductive health, impacting sperm count, motility, morphology, DNA damage, and inducing oxidative stress [ ]. Nanomaterials, such as nanodiamonds, can also trigger oxidative stress in organisms [ ]. Extremely harmful oxidative stress levels cause damage to the mitochondrial membrane and malfunction of the electron chain, which ultimately results in cell death. The depletion of antioxidants or the increased production of ROS is two important reasons that favour the pro-oxidant effects of tailored nanomedicine. It is well reported that the main mechanism of toxicity of carbon-based nanomaterials is oxidative stress [ , ]. A study conducted by Domínguez et al. found that nanoparticles with a size of 15 nm caused the formation of ROS in the intestines of Daphnia magna in a manner that depended on the dosage. On the other hand, nanoparticle with a size of 5 nm showed lesser ROS production but a higher level of genes associated with oxidative stress [ ].

## Methods for nanotoxicity assessment
Nanotoxicity assessment is crucial for developing safe nanomaterials. It involves studying potential adverse effects on living organisms. This includes evaluating physicochemical properties, interactions with biological systems, and assessing toxicity at various levels. Insights into mechanisms like oxidative stress and genotoxicity inform safer nanomaterial design. Methods include in-vitro studies for cellular-level understanding, in-vivo studies using animal models, ex-vivo studies for localized effects, and advanced techniques like imaging. Computational modeling and structure activity relationship analysis also contribute to predicting and assessing nanotoxicity, enabling the development of safer nanotechnologies [ ]. Various methods of nanotoxicity assessment are provided in the following sections.

## In silico based nanotoxicity assessment
Predictive modelling and in silico tools for nanosafety aim to forecast nanomaterial characteristics, understand interactions with cells, predict biological impacts, and assess potential toxicity [ ]. Nanoinformatics, combining informatics and nanomaterials data, is rapidly advancing [ ]. The development of predictive modelling frameworks aids in foreseeing negative effects, mitigating dangers to the environment and human health [ ]. Although the ideas of accessible, readily available interconnected and reusable data are widely accepted, there are still differing interpretations of these principles. To address this, mature approaches are being developed in the field of life sciences to ensure the effective implementation of data sharing and reuse [ ]. Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) techniques create precise predictive models for nanomaterial physicochemical characteristics, significantly advancing nanotechnology [ ]. ML techniques enable high-throughput screening, drastically reducing time and labor for material testing, accelerating exploration of nanomaterial characteristics and potential applications [ ]. Singh et al. employed an ML-based approach to assess the impact of physicochemical descriptors on intracellular interactions, emphasizing their role in controlling nanomaterial activity in the human body [ ]. The study using Madin-Darby canine kidney cell line identified descriptors triggering epigenetic modifications, highlighting the significance of nanomaterial characteristics in understanding their influence on cellular behavior and potential consequences on cellular transformations [ ].
Quantitative structure–property and structure–activity connections are used for safety and risk assessment has gained popularity due to their ability to predict the activity or toxicity of chemical compounds [ – ]. Guidelines enhance the reliability and regulatory acceptance of QSAR/QSTR models, making them crucial tools in safety assessment [ ]. The 3D-QSAR molecular docking technique aids in forecasting molecular properties and biological interactions of medications, providing valuable insights into their actions. For NPs, Quantitative Structure-Nanotoxicity Relationship (QNAR) models have emerged to better understand nanomaterial toxicity [ ]. Nanoinformatics, like the NanoTox platform, utilizes QNAR models to assess nanotoxicology, providing an open-source platform for accessing nanomaterial toxicity reports [ ]. ROS production is a key outcome in nanotoxicity evaluation, particularly concerning mitochondria and cellular oxidative metabolism [ ]. Single-walled CNTs have potential therapeutic uses but also pose mitochondrial nanotoxicity risks, emphasizing the need for nanoinformatics-based risk–benefit assessments [ ]. MoS2 shows promise in biomedical applications but may have limitations, as indicated by electrophysiological tests and molecular dynamics simulations [ ]. Computational models assessing nanotoxicity on mitochondrial channels contribute to understanding the safety and efficacy of nanomaterials in biomedical applications [ ]. The creation of ISD3, a computational pharmacokinetic model, and the NanoSolveIT.eu project's integration of multiscale models demonstrate advancements in in-silico approaches for nanotoxicity assessment [ ]. In-silico approaches, particularly physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, offer valuable insights into the pharmacokinetic characteristics and potential toxicities of nanomedicines [ , ]. PBPK modeling has diverse applications across scientific disciplines, emphasizing its potential to enhance our understanding of nanomedicine behavior and guide decision-making [ ]. Computational approaches are being used to study the possible harmful effects of nanoparticles. Nevertheless, certain limitations continue to exist in terms of their validation and application for direct clinical implementation. Hence, the utilization of in-vitro and in-vivo models remains pivotal in assessing the toxicity and therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicine.

## In-vitro methods for nanotoxicity assessment
Cell-based evaluations are essential for treating diseases and assessing drug toxicity [ ]. Toxicity testing depends critically on stem cells including embryonic stem cells, cardiac stem cells, and fibromatosis-derived stem cells [ – ]. Ethical concerns led to interest in alternative sources, such as induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from skin or blood cells, offering an ethical solution for their biomedical application [ ]. In the same context iPSCs, are pluripotent and valuable for studying human biology, disease modelling, and regenerative medicine [ ] but ethical concerns, limit their applications [ ]. Integrating genetic variation into cardiotoxicity testing enables a more comprehensive assessment of drug safety [ ]. In nanotoxicity assessment, iPSCs serve as an innovative in-vitro model, evaluating the negative effects of NPs on cellular function [ ]. iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells (iPSC-HLCs) are proposed for hepatotoxicity studies, while iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes assess NPs toxicity, showcasing iPSC adaptability in preclinical drug toxicology [ ]. In addition cancer cell lines are frequently employed to assess nanotoxicity, especially in studying potential NPs toxicity particularly by determining the IC50 [ ]. However, inconsistencies in methods, including cell culture media, NPs incubation period, and supplementation, pose challenges [ ] such as the addition of serum to cell growth medium can impact the assessment of positively charged NPs toxicity due to protein corona formation. Standardizing methodologies is crucial for achieving consistent and reliable results [ ]. Addressing variables like culture media, exposure times, and incubation conditions are essential to maintain consistency and interpret results across different nanotoxicity studies.
Tissue engineering has replaced animal testing for toxicity evaluation through the development of 3D culture systems, providing a more physiologically relevant representation of cellular behavior [ , ] addressing the limitations of conventional 2D cultures such as the lack of crucial elements found in biological tissues, resulting in a more reliable platform [ ]. It offers increased precision in toxicity evaluation, simulating in-vivo settings more accurately [ ]. Similarly coculture systems, enhance the accuracy of toxicity assessment and drug testing [ ]. Microfluidic systems present dynamic models for cell culture, providing more physiologically appropriate settings for nanotoxicity assessment and screening. These systems offer fine control over fluid flow, enabling controlled examinations of nanomaterial-cell interactions, making them versatile platforms for studying nanotoxicity [ ]. Despite the insights from in-vitro platforms, in-vivo models remain crucial for analysing systemic effects and complex interactions of nanomaterials. Upholding ethical standards and following guidelines for experimental animal research is essential for advancing our understanding of nanotoxicity.
High-content screening (HCS) combines automated microscopy with advanced image analysis to simultaneously quantify multiple cellular parameters [ , ]. This technique is pivotal in drug discovery, enabling precise identification of drug-induced toxicity mechanisms with high sensitivity. By integrating HCS into screening workflows, it significantly improves drug safety assessments and minimizes the risk of failure, particularly in evaluating drug-induced liver injury. HCS leverages diverse cellular readouts to detect phenotypic changes, including alterations in protein expression, post-translational modifications, and cellular morphology. Employing fluorescent proteins, immune fluorescence [ ] and various fluorescent probes such as DNA dyes, labelled receptor ligands, and "click" chemistry reagents, HCS offers a distinct advantage over other cell-based methods through its multiplexing capability, providing a comprehensive analysis of cellular responses [ ].

## In-vivo methods for nanotoxicity assessment
Selecting appropriate animal models for in-vivo toxicological studies remains challenging due to the complexity of human physiology and diverse responses to toxins [ ]. Researchers are exploring nonmammalian organisms like zebrafish, nematodes (Caenorhabditis elegans), and fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) as alternative in-vivo models for nanotoxicity studies, offering advantages such as cost-effectiveness, shorter life cycles, ease of manipulation, and ethical considerations compared to mammalian models [ , ]. Recent studies using Drosophila melanogaster as a model organism revealed that the choice of nanoparticle carrier significantly influenced toxicity profiles, emphasizing the importance of considering carrier characteristics in nanomedicine formulation design [ ]. Fruit flies have been validated for monitoring genotoxicity endpoints and assessing the safety of polymeric nanocarriers, revealing oxidative stress and cell cycle arrest associated with poly-lactic acid nanoparticles [ – ]. Non-mammalian in-vivo models, including zebrafish and aquatic organisms like Artemiasalina and Daphnia magna, have demonstrated their usefulness in evaluating the safety of polymeric nanomaterials, serving as effective tools for preliminary toxicological screenings or complementary approaches [ ]. Still, every nonmammalian model has its constraints when compared to more intricate models like rodents [ ]. Mice and rats, which are frequently employed in preclinical nanotoxicity investigations due to their genomic resemblance to humans, are subjected to evaluations that concentrate on alterations in tissue structure, apoptosis, and inflammation in key organs. Attention is given to systems prone to NPs accumulation, like the liver's hepatic sinusoid and Kupffer cells. It's crucial to mimic intended applications and exposure routes for appropriate assessment of drug nanocarriers, including ingestion, injection, transdermal delivery, and inhalation [ – ].
The fit-by-design approach underscores the importance of choosing appropriate animal models based on the intended application of nanomedicine. For therapeutic nanomedicines, models mimicking targeted diseases are recommended, while diagnostics can be tested on healthy animals for acute exposure results, indicating potential risks in clinical settings. Chronic exposure may necessitate adjustments to therapeutic regimens for reassessing nanomedicine safety and efficacy [ ]. Bioimaging techniques allow real-time evaluation of NPs biodistribution, eliminating the need for sacrificing animals for tissue biopsies. Non-invasive bioimaging provides valuable insights into spatiotemporal NPs behavior, reducing the number of animals required for experimentation [ ]. While in-vivo preclinical models are valuable for studying nanotoxicological mechanisms, physiological differences between species pose challenges in translating findings to humans. Consideration of species-specific variations is crucial in interpreting and extrapolating preclinical data to human scenarios [ ].

## Innovative nanotoxicological assays
The integration of HTS, high-content analysis (HCS), validated in-vitro assays, and mathematical modeling proposed by Nel et al. forms a powerful platform for assessing nanomaterial interactions and predicting toxicological end points. This approach allows rapid screening and analysis of numerous nanomaterials across different concentrations and cell types, minimizing variability and saving time and costs [ ]. The combined use of HTS and HCS facilitates efficient and cost-effective evaluations of potential adverse effects of nanomaterials, enhancing safety assessments and making nanoparticle research more accessible [ ]. However this integration in nanotoxicity assessment is still in its early stages and presents inherent complexity [ ]. To address some of these challenges, microfluidic approaches, known as organ-on-chip strategies, have emerged as a promising alternative. Organ-on-chip platforms provide a more physiologically relevant and dynamic environment, enabling the replication of organ-level functions and interactions [ ]. By utilizing these innovative microfluidic systems, researchers aim to overcome limitations associated with traditional in-vitro assays and better simulate the complex responses and interactions of nanomaterials within the human body. By replicating the functions and interactions of organs in a dynamic and physiologically relevant environment, these microfluidic systems enable researchers to better understand the complex responses and interactions of nanomaterials within the human body [ ].

## Microfluidic approaches in nanotoxicology
Microfluidic devices offer unique opportunities for HTS in nanotoxicology studies. These versatile platforms, by mimicking physiological conditions more accurately, enable efficient evaluations of nanomaterials, advancing nanomedicine and enhancing safety and efficacy understanding. In-vitro HTS models serve as crucial links between animal studies and clinical trials, providing cost-effective assessments of potential treatments [ ]. Microfluidic devices developed for various organs provide valuable insights into the behavior and effects of NPs in specific physiological contexts [ ]. Multiorgan-on-chip and body-on-chip approaches show promise in reducing reliance on animal models in nanotoxicity studies. Ongoing developments in microfluidic systems extend to evaluating various nanoparticle-related toxicities and therapeutic applications, showcasing their expanding utility [ ]. The increasing demand for organ-on-chip devices in drug toxicity assessment, particularly in healthcare, reflects their potential in nanotoxicity evaluation. Additionally, leveraging omics techniques allows researchers to gain comprehensive insights into molecular responses and mechanisms underlying nanoparticle toxicity [ ].

## Omics-based nanotoxicity assessment
Omics analyses provide a comprehensive approach to nanotoxicity assessment, allowing for the detection of subtle molecular changes and a more thorough understanding of nanomaterial safety [ , ]. Toxicogenomics, examining genomic responses to toxic exposures reduces animal testing and supporting regulatory decisions [ , ]. It generates extensive data on genetic changes, altered pathways, and networks, offering a comprehensive mapping of nanotoxicological pathways [ ]. Integrating multiple omics data points enable holistic analysis of various molecular components, providing a more thorough understanding of toxicological responses. Hepatoxicity assessments using iPSC-HLCs revealed concentration-dependent gene expression changes in response to AgNPs [ ]. Toxicogenomic alterations were observed in Daphnia magna exposed to AgNPs, indicating molecular responses and impacts on biological pathways [ ]. Caenorhabditis elegans exhibited toxicogenomic changes depending on the manufactured form of ZnONPs, emphasizing the significance of understanding nanoparticle forms [ ]. Engineered nanomaterial exposure induced epigenomic alterations associated with cell cycle regulation, chromosomal organization, and DNA damage response, providing insights into cytotoxicity mechanisms [ ]. Gene toxicity associated with common adverse effects was identified across different engineered nanomaterials, highlighting the impact of surface modifications on reducing toxicity [ ]. While omics platforms hold promise for nanotoxicity assessment, challenges such as standardization, model selection, dosimetry, and bioinformatics expertise need addressing for widespread adoption [ ]. To minimize such limitations confirmatory tests and integrated omics-machine learning approaches, such as metabo-transcriptomics, offer promise for comprehensive nanotoxicity assessment, providing valuable insights and supporting regulatory decisions [ ]. These advanced methods enhance the evaluation of potential toxicities in nanomedicines, emphasizing the need for robust regulatory frameworks.

## Regulatory challenges of nanomedicines
Regulating nanomedicines faces a key challenge as regulatory bodies, like the FDA, rely on safety data from bulk materials. This approach overlooks the distinct pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic activities of nanomedicines, leading to potential discrepancies in safety and efficacy assessments. Consequently, conventional regulations may not adequately address the unique characteristics of nanomedicines, posing a hurdle in ensuring their safety and efficacy [ ]. Nanomedicine classification varies globally, with some regulators labelling them as medicines and others as medical devices. This inconsistency results in different regulatory standards, allowing a nanomedicine to meet approval in one country but not in another, creating potential disparities in safety and efficacy assessments [ ]. The main challenges faced in the regulation of nanomedicines are outlined in Fig.  . 
The diverse structure, form, size, and clinical applications of nanomedicines pose challenges for regulatory characterization. Common measurement techniques, such as dynamic light scattering, may not accurately capture the size of non-spherical materials like rods [ ]. Additionally, techniques like transmission electron microscopy, widely used for size measurement, may alter the nanomaterial's form compared to its state in the human body. Protein corona formation in the bloodstream further complicates accurate size reporting [ ]. The absence of clear nanometrology standards in the literature adds to the challenge [ ]. Without robust clinical regulatory guidance, preclinical nanomedicine development and characterization is unlikely to see significant changes. Scaling up nanomedicine manufacturing presents challenges in stability, necessitating stringent protocols for approval. Establishing Critical Quality Attributes (CQA) is crucial for controlling processes and understanding complex structures. The intricate nature of nanomedicines makes consistent manufacturing difficult, impacting quality, efficacy, and safety.
Consortiums like the US and EU Nanotechnology Characterization Laboratories offer testing facilities, but researchers often access them later in the development pipeline [ – ]. Regulating nanotoxicology and cellular responses poses a significant challenge for regulators [ ]. Existing nano pharmaceutical laws are lacking. These products follow conventional medicine principles. Vanessa Sainz et.al reported that Food & Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency provided guideline to nanomedicine experts to assess the safety of nanomaterials by considering their size and potential interactions with cells, protein molecules, tissues, and systems [ ]. The study explained how nanoparticles affect the body and alter equilibrium. Also, it was suggested, that even if a nanocarrier is licensed, it is considered a new drug entity and must be registered with the relevant authority after preclinical and clinical studies [ ]. The Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW) is developing and applying creative approaches to urgent clinical requirements, and the legal structure predicts regulatory concerns by adopting the best scientific standards. However, more comprehensive efforts are required to coordinate efforts amongst regulatory agencies. Both the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCTAS/NIH) in the US and the Innovative Medicines Initiative (IMI) in Europe may surely advance regulatory science in novel technologies, precision medicine, and, implicitly, nanomedicines [ ]. The primary challenges to the translation of nanomedicine are the complexity of regulations, safety issues, and scaling up manufacturing constraints. Because they frequently evade established regulatory frameworks, NPs are difficult to approve and their long-term impacts on the environment and human body are yet unknown. Increasing output in order to keep quality constant presents more difficulties and raises expenses. Furthermore, as nanomedicine is interdisciplinary, biology, chemistry, and engineering must work closely together. The unexpected behaviour of NPs within biological systems complicates clinical trials and makes the path to widespread clinical application more difficult [ ]. Advances in targeted nano-delivery of bevacizumab using nanoparticles hold great potential to improve cancer treatment. However, the translation of nanomedicine faces significant challenges, including complex and non-standardized regulations, difficulties in predicting safety and efficacy, manufacturing inconsistencies, and high development costs. Addressing these hurdles is crucial for bringing these innovative therapies to clinical practice [ ]. Traditional toxicity measures, such as large-scale animal testing, are considered unethical, costly, and impractical for nanotoxicity assessment. In-vitro methods offer a more efficient and controlled approach, but many common 2D assays overlook the complexity of the human body [ ].
There is growing evidence that traditional in-vitro assays for small compounds are inadequate for nanomaterials, as NPs can interact with reagents, leading to false positives. The unique properties of nanomaterials further complicate testing, hindering progress in regulatory guidance development [ , ]. In the development of nanomedicines for clinical use, thorough preclinical safety data, including adverse effects, is crucial for drug delivery mechanisms and actions [ ]. High doses in nano form may lead to toxic effects on specific cells or organs, posing risks for patients with conditions like chronic kidney disease or diabetes, and raising concerns about antibiotic resistance. The smaller particle size increases mobility, allowing potential crossing of the BBB, posing risks to long-term brain function or causing edema [ ]. The lack of sufficient safety data on these aspects can lead to approvals that may later be reconsidered. Regulating the pharmacokinetics of nanomedicines poses a significant challenge, diverging from the expected course of small drug molecules [ ]. This variation leads to an extended duration of the presence of the substance in the body, which raises worries about possible health risks if nanomedicine products were easily accessible without a prescription [ , ]. The lack of conclusive toxicity information complicates decisions on market entry supervision. The introduction of nanosimilars further challenges regulatory bodies, as generic approaches are deemed invalid due to the complex nanoparticulate structures of nanomedicines [ ]. The absence of established CQA complicates identifying identical or novel parameters. The responsibility for formulating nanomedicine guidelines requires a consultative process involving stakeholders, but the lack of scientific expertise and diverse nanomedicine modes of action hinders effective regulation, highlighting the need for federal-level laboratories and risk assessment protocols. While existing infrastructure is in place, the integration of guidance from national bodies into regulatory frameworks remains incomplete [ ]. The outlined challenges pose significant limitations on the future of nanomedicine, impeding the production of safe and high-quality nanomedicine products. The absence of nanomedicine-specific safety protocols hinders the effective control of NPs. The lack of clear and consistent guidance from governing agencies raises doubts about realizing the full potential breakthrough success of nanomedicine.

## Conclusion
The rapidly evolving subject of nanomedicine has the potential to significantly alter healthcare. With all of the scientific and technological advancements in the upcoming years, it is anticipated that the number of medications based on nanotechnology will increase. High hopes for nanomedicine are bolstered by the discipline's expanding body of research as well as expanding commercial tendencies. The future of nanomedicines promises to transform therapeutic interventions and diagnostics by enabling precision medicine through targeted drug delivery and enhanced imaging. For instance, nanomedicines have potential application as signal amplifier or transducer for the detection of biomolecules or pathogens. Additionally, nanomedicines demonstrated unique application as contrast agents in magnetic resonance imaging and to monitor the in-vivo distribution of therapeutic agents [ ]. Recent insights highlight the growing progress of biomaterials for cartilage and bone regeneration. However, challenges are associated with generation of stable and appropriate functional bone and cartilage implants such as technical approaches, clinical assessments, vascularization, mechanical stability, and defect fitting [ ]. However, toxicities associated with nanomedicines due to various factors such as size, shape, surface chemistry, dose and interaction with biological systems must not be neglected. Ensuring the safety of nanomaterials requires addressing gaps in toxicity data, standardizing assessment methods, and establishing clear guidelines for risk assessment and management. Safe nanomaterial usage requires understanding nanotoxicity mechanisms and creating robust evaluation methods, and overcoming regulatory difficulties. Therefore, the European medical authority has organized an expert group on nanomedicines with representatives from the European regulatory network and academia to give scientific information. To keep up with nanotechnology, future research should investigate nanoparticle exposure's long-term consequences, develop new evaluation methods, and improve regulatory frameworks.
