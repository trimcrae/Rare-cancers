## Abstract

## Background
Early detection of pathogen-associated diseases are critical for effective treatment. Rapid, specific, sensitive, and cost-effective diagnostic technologies continue to be challenging to develop. The current gold standard for pathogen detection, polymerase chain reaction technology, has limitations such as long operational cycles, high cost, and high technician and instrumentation requirements.

## Aim of review
This review examines and highlights the technical advancements of CRISPR-Cas in pathogen detection and provides an outlook for future development, multi-application scenarios, and clinical translation.

## Key scientific concepts of review
Approaches enabling clinical detection of pathogen nucleic acids that are highly sensitive, specific, cheap, and portable are necessary. CRISPR-Cas9 specificity in targeting nucleic acids and “collateral cleavage” activity of CRISPR-Cas12/Cas13/Cas14 show significant promise in nucleic acid detection technology. These methods have a high specificity, versatility, and rapid detection cycle. In this paper, CRISPR-Cas-based detection methods are discussed in depth. Although CRISPR-Cas-mediated pathogen diagnostic solutions face challenges, their powerful capabilities will pave the way for ideal diagnostic tools.

## Introduction
Pathogens are organisms that cause diseases in hosts. These organisms are taxonomically diverse and comprise viruses, bacteria, unicellular, and multicellular eukaryotes , . Some pathogens can cause large-scale infectious diseases that lead to human morbidity and mortality . Pathogens must be identified quickly to diagnose, cure, and control the associated diseases. Nucleic acid amplification detection tests (NAATs) and next generation sequencing (NGS)-based pathogen diagnostics are the most widely used methods for detecting pathogen , , , . These methods have become the gold standard since they can recognize and differentiate between even a few nucleic acid copies , , , . However, the limitations of NAATs such as complex thermal cycle process , , of PCR, the complex primer design and unstable single base resolution of loop-mediated amplification (LAMP) block their application. Regarding NGS, sequence read length restrictions and the requirement for a deep knowledge of biology when reading the analyzed data make this approach unavailable for point-of-care testing (POCT) , . Gene chip technology is of high interest for pathogen detection because this method can greatly increase the number of tests that can be performed in parallel , but its specificity and sensitivity depend on stringently designed probes that complement the target . Electrical biosensors such as biomolecule-functionalized graphene sensing platforms , and optical sensors such as surface plasmon resonance-based sensing platforms , offer advantages including high selectivity, sensitivity, and ease of integration, but they require specialized equipment and are expensive. Thus, a better approach for pathogen nucleic acid testing should be developed. CRISPR-Cas-based method may be an ideal approach that meets the requirements of sensitivity, specificity, low cost, rapidity, ease-of-use, low equipment needs, and easy delivery to users .
Doudna et al. (2012) showed that the CRISPR-Cas system could be used to combine and cut specific sites of target DNA in vitro , thereby laying a mechanistic foundation for CRISPR-Cas usage for nucleic acid detection. The integration of CRISPR-Cas reactions with nucleic acid amplification in a single reaction to achieve point-of-care (POC) detection , the use of the CRISPR-Cas system for viral or bacterial detection and CRISPR-Cas-based biosensing technology have been extensively covered in recent reviews. On this basis, we systematically discuss recent advances and applications of the CRISPR-Cas system in pathogen detection and analyze each module of this technology. We also carefully analyze the factors influencing the performance of these pathogen detection technologies and discuss the challenges and prospects for clinical translation.

## Detection techniques based on the working principles of CRISPR-Cas proteins

## Detection methods based on combination with targets
Cas9 can be programmed to target double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and cause a double-strand break . The detection mechanism specificity is determined by a 20 nt single-guide RNA (sgRNA) that is complementary to the target dsDNA . The application range can be expanded by changing the sgRNA. A complex composed of Cas protein and sgRNA needs to recognize a specific motif (the protospacer-adjacent motif [PAM]) on dsDNA for recognition and combination to initiate enzymatic cleavage by Cas9 protein . Researchers have developed CRISPR-Cas9-based methods to rapidly detect Zika virus (ZIKV) and Escherichia coli (E. coli)
 .
dCas9, which does not have enzymatic cleavage ability, but can still specifically recognize and bind to target dsDNA , can be used as well. Split luciferase can recover its enzymatic activity when the N- and C-terminal halves (NFluc and CFluc, respectively) are linked together . Zhang et al. fused NFluc and CFluc to the N-terminus of dCas9, so that when a pair of adjacent target sites in the substrate DNA is bound by dCas9, luciferase becomes catalytically active and emits light in the presence of D-luciferin. This approach has been used to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). The high specificity of this approach is ensured by two sgRNAs targeting two sequences spaced approximately 20 bp apart. However, this method relies on CRISPR-dCas9 for detection without additional signal amplification, therefore, the method's sensitivity needs to be improved. Besides, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) , SARS-CoV-2 can be detected under the help of dCas9.

## Detection methods based on “collateral cleavage” activity
Unlike nucleic acid assays based on CRISPR-Cas9, assays based on CRISPR-Cas12/Cas13/Cas14 rely on “collateral cleavage” activity triggered by target binding . Both Cas12 and Cas14 are RNA-guide DNases, but Cas12 has higher specificity for dsDNA, whereas Cas14 has higher specificity for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) . These proteins not only specifically recognize and cleave dsDNA or ssDNA sequences using guidance by CRISPR RNA (crRNA) but also non-specifically cleave surrounding non-target ssDNA reporters . Cas12-based platforms such as one-HOur Low-cost Multipurpose highly Efficient System (HOLMES) and DNA Endonuclease Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter (DETECTR) can reliably detect pathogens, such as human papillomavirus (HPV) and Japanese encephalitis virus. Broughton et al. performed simultaneous reverse transcription and isothermal amplification using loop-mediated amplification (RT-LAMP) for RNA and detected the product using “collateral cleavage” by Cas12 to develop a platform named SARS-CoV-2 DETECTR to rapidly diagnose SARS-CoV-2. Another platform named POC-CRISPR combined Cas12 with reverse transcription and recombinase polymerase amplification (RT-RPA) to achieve POC SARS-CoV-2 detection using droplet magnetofluidics. Simplified bead-based sample preparation, nucleic acid amplification, Cas12 reaction, and assay result reporting are integrated within an automated, portable POC-CRISPR device that can be used to detect unprocessed clinical nasopharyngeal swab eluates within 30 mins. Cas14 was identified almost exclusively in the extreme biophilic super-plant “DPANN” , . Previously described Cas proteins are mostly between 950 and 1400 amino acids, whereas Cas14 has only 400–700 amino acids , and is the smallest RNA-directed nuclease discovered to date. Cas14 can target and cut ssDNA. This process is mediated by a seed sequence interaction that occurs near the middle of the ssDNA target . The purified Cas14a-sgRNA complex can perform RNA-guided nucleic acid cleavage in vitro in the presence of ssDNA substrates with high specificity for SNPs , .
While Cas9, Cas12, and Cas14 can detect DNA-type nucleic acids, Cas13 can detect RNA-type nucleic acids ( ). Cas13 is an RNA-directed ribonuclease that can generate multiple cleavage sites in the single-stranded region of an RNA target with specific base preference. Importantly, Cas13 remains active after cleavage , . Cas13 also has “collateral cleavage” activity and can perform trans-cleavage of bystander RNA in target RNA-dependent situations . In contrast to Cas12, Cas13 does not require a PAM sequence but has a protospacer flanking site, where a guanine base directly following the protospacer negatively affects complementation between crRNA and the target single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) . The “collateral cleavage” activity of Cas13 is widely used in pathogen detection. A classic method is the specific high-sensitivity enzymatic reporter unlocking (SHERLOCK) platform developed by Zhang et al. , which can detect ZIKV and Dengue virus (DENV) in patient samples. Furthermore, STOP (SHERLOCK testing in one pot), a streamlined assay that combines simplified viral RNA extraction with isothermal amplification and CRISPR-mediated detection, has been developed to detect SARS-CoV-2. STOP has similar sensitivity to that of reverse-transcription–quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assays . Minimally instrumented SHERLOCK (miSHERLOCK) provides built-in sample preparation, amplification, and signal detection from unprocessed patient saliva with sensitivity comparable to that of RT-PCR, making this approach ideal for POCT . Finally, the SHERLOCK parasite rapid extraction protocol (S-PREP) combines isothermal, lyophilized, one-pot SHERLOCK assays with a simplified sample preparation protocol for rapid and sensitive POC detection of Plasmodium parasites.
 

## Nucleic acid amplification or biosensing technology fuels signal amplification based on CRISPR-Cas detection technology
Because pathogen nucleic acid concentrations in clinical samples are often low, the amplification degree of the detection signal produced by CRISPR-Cas alone is restricted. Thus, nucleic acid amplification or sensing technology is typically used to improve sensitivity.

## Methods combined with PCR
PCR is a classic nucleic acid amplification technology that has been widely added to CRISPR-based detection methods. By combining dCas9 with PCR, Zhang et al. detected Mtb nucleic acid concentration as low as approximately 5 × 10−13 g/mL. Dueñas et al. combined PCR with the Cas12a reaction to achieve pan-Leishmania detection and species-specific Leishmania (Viannia) detection by targeting the highly conserved Leishmania 18S ribosomal RNA gene (18S rDNA) and a region of kinetoplast DNA (kDNA) minicircles in Leishmania (Viannia). In addition to enriching targets, PCR assists in quantitative pathogen detection. For example, the SARS-CoV-2 genome contains an open reading frame gene (O gene) and a nucleoprotein gene (N gene), with the O gene encoding 16 non-structural proteins and the N gene encoding nucleocapsid proteins . These genes are frequently utilized as targets for real-time RT-PCR (rRT-PCR) detection owing to their conservation , , . The fluorescence change (FC) ratio, which is the ratio of the FC at 10 min to that at 5 min after initiating fluorescence readings from mixed reactions, was defined by Huang et al. to quantitatively analyze target O and N genes . This approach helped resolve diagnostic uncertainty owing to high cycle threshold (Ct) values in traditional RT-qPCR assays. In this assay, the rRT-PCR reaction product is transferred to a physically isolated space and mixed with Cas12 reaction components, which generate fluorescence. The slope and FC can be calculated as needed at any point during the assay, and the FC ratio was chosen as the standard assay readout.

## Methods combined with isothermal nucleic acid amplification
To address thermocycling difficulties, isothermal nucleic acid amplification methods have been added into the workflow of several CRISPR-Cas methods. Mukama et al. combined LAMP with a Cas12 reaction to achieve single-copy detection of the Pseudomonas aeruginosa acetyltransferase gene in complex samples (milk and serum). Although the amplified LAMP substrate is DNA, Chandrasekaran et al. transcribed LAMP products to achieve substrate compatibility and combined the transcribed products with Cas13 to develop a platform named DIagnosticS with Corona Virus Enzymatic Reporting (DISCoVER). This method allows highly specific SARS-CoV-2 detection within 35 mins with a sensitivity of 40 copies/µL. To avoid cross-contamination caused by multiple liquid-handling steps , “one-pot” detection was developed. Temperature is a major challenge for single-tube assays. For example, the cleavage activity of wild-type AapCas12b disappears above 60 ℃, whereas the reaction conditions for LAMP are from 60 to 65 ℃ . Thus, if the temperature of the isothermal nucleic acid amplification phase and the ideal CRISPR-Cas reaction temperature is consistent, the amplification and CRISPR reactions can be combined. Otherwise, temperature control equipment should be used between these two reaction steps. Recently, a compact and portable microfluidic DISCoVER device was developed that contains separate temperature-controlled reaction and detection chambers ( A). Gravity-driven steps can be used to carry out Cas reactions and amplification reactions in the same apparatus. Furthermore, a single-step assay combining RT-RPA and Cas12 reaction can be loaded into commercially available microfluidic digital chips to develop a digital CRISPR-Cas‐assisted assay called digitization‐enhanced CRISPR/Cas‐assisted one-pot virus detection (deCOViD) . In this assay, the reaction components can be discretized within digital reaction wells so that the reaction occurs independent of the sample concentration. The RApid DIgital Crispr Approach (RADICA) offers absolute nucleic acid quantification by partitioning individual reactions into 10,000 compartments on a high-density chip, thereby combining the advantages of quantitative digital PCR, isothermal amplification, and Cas12 reactions. The digital warm-start CRISPR (dWS-CRISPR) assay, similar to RADICA, enables absolute nucleic acid quantification by dispensing the reaction mixture into micro-reactions. Unlike RADICA, dWS-CRISPR combines the Cas12 reaction with low-temperature reverse transcription dual-priming isothermal amplification that requires a warm-start reaction to prevent premature target amplification at room temperature, which might lead to inaccurate quantification . The droplet Digital RT-LAMP Enhanced Cas12b-based RNA Detection platform (ddRECD) , which is methodologically thermally stable owing to the usage of Cas12b enzymes rather than Cas12a enzymes, shares this benefit of preventing premature amplification at room temperature. The Cas12a-based Visual Detection (Cas12aVDet) platform also uses “one-pot” detection ( B). In this method, the RPA reagent is placed into a tube with Cas12a attached to the tube wall. After the RPA reaction, Cas12a is added to the reaction by centrifugation to initiate the cleavage reaction, which produces visible fluorescence. Besides, Lu et al. developed a similar approach by mixing the raw amplification reaction materials and the Cas12 reactants in one tube to realize fast and sensitive SARS-CoV-2 detection using a suboptimal PAM sequence.
 

## Methods combined with nucleic acid amplification-free sensors
Nucleic acid amplification procedures suffer from risks of aerosol contamination. Amplification-free biosensors such as electrochemical biosensors, optical biosensors, and piezoelectric plate biosensors greatly reduce this risk. Most CRISPR-Cas biosensing systems have high sensitivity and selectivity, rapidity, low cost, and ultra-high resolution up to single-base variation , , , , . Nanopore technology, for example, is a single-molecule detection technology that does not require labeling or amplification. This method detects a current change caused by the target as it passes through the nanopore , , , . Yang et al. combined dCas9 with solid-state nanopores to develop a method that can be applied to DNA typing diagnosis and rapid pathogen detection ( A). When control sample with bare DNA is added to the flow cell, which contains a 20 nm SiN nanopore, a current trace signal is observed. Target DNA binding to CRISPR-Cas triggers a different current signal; therefore, the position and shape of the current spike separates the different nucleic acid populations, i.e., DNA bound to CRISPR-Cas protein and unbound DNA. Solid-state CRISPR-Cas12a-assisted nanopores (SCAN) non-specifically cut nearby cyclic DNA reporters, changing the molecules from a ring-like to a linear shape. This causes current changes as the molecules pass through nanopores. Indeed, this method has been used for sequence-specific identification of HIV DNA . However, preparing nanopores is expensive and time-consuming; therefore, it is difficult to prepare large batches of nanopores. Additionally, entangled long DNA fragments may influence the method sensitivity .
 Microfluidics is an important tool for clinical diagnosis that can be combined with the CRISPR-Cas system. An automated multiplexing CRISPR microfluidic chip was combined with a custom-designed benchtop fluorometer for rapid and low volume (∼10 μL) Ebola virus detection 
( B). With this method, Ebola RNA can be detected within 5 mins. The good linear relationship (Pearson’s R = 0.96) between the fluorescence signal and the input target concentration also allows quantitative detection. Furthermore, the use of microfluidics enables low sample volume consumption; thus, microfluidic-based methods can be used for finger-prick tests. Microfluidics are characterized by fast and miniaturized features that provide an excellent platform for detecting pathogens .
Finally, by designing an allosteric DNA molecule that acts as a binding unit that catalyzes the DNA reaction to generate linear signal amplification oligonucleotide sequences , Shen et al. developed the APC-Cas platform that uses allosteric probes (AP) and Cas13 to reliably detect Salmonella enteritidis cells. This method is more sensitive and specific than real-time PCR ( C). APs are composed of an aptamer domain, a primer domain, and a T7 promoter domain. Allosteric transformation is triggered by target pathogen recognition followed by DNA sequence extension and RNA transcription to generate a large amount of ssRNA, which can be cut by Cas13 to generate a fluorescence signal.

## Approaches for reaction component processing in CRISPR-Cas based assays
Various reaction components are cleverly combined to create a solid technical foundation. Hsieh et al. significantly improved the signal-to-noise ratio and reaction rate of the Cas12a-Dx platform by optimizing the Cas12a enzyme, reaction buffer, substrate label, substrate concentration and reaction temperature. Therefore, we hope to support the performance of this technology by comprehensively comparing the optimization strategies of various CRISPR-Cas-based nucleic acid detection schemes.

## Sample processing module
In nucleic acid testing, sample collection and processing affects the assay cost, cycle time, and interpretation of the final results. Methods with simple operation and low costs are preferred. The method developed by Guk et al. for MRSA detection does not require nucleic acid extraction from bacteria, instead directly uses cell lysates to detect DNA from pathogenic bacteria. Ni-NTA magnetic beads are used in this method to directly pull down cell lysate samples that have not been genetically purified. The DISCoVER platform is another assay that does not require RNA extraction. In this platform, low concentrations of the shelf-stable reducing agent Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) and ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid are paired and used to protect the target RNA and rLAMP while promoting virus inactivation and inhibiting RNase activity in the sample. In addition, the pairing reagent disrupts mucin formation to reduce the viscosity of saliva, making it easy for users to handle samples . All the reagents are commercially available, inexpensive, and stable at room temperature. The CRISPR-MTB method developed by Ai et al. combines magnetic beads, chemical lysis, and heating to optimize the DNA extraction process. The extraction efficiency of this combined strategy is comparable to that of the column method, but the time is shorter. A scheme called heating unextracted diagnostic samples to obliterate nucleases (HUDSON) paired with SHERLOCK has been developed , which used heating and chemical reduction to lyse virus particles and inactivate high concentrations of RNases. HUDSON-treated urine or saliva can be directly added to the RPA reaction without dilution or purification, which does not inhibit subsequent amplification or detection. However, the HUDSON assay is not applicable for parasite detection. HUDSON-treated simulated whole-blood samples produce minimal signal detected by SHERLOCK, likely from not accessing the intracellular parasitic nucleic acid. However, treating the samples with 50 mM dithiothreitol and 10 mM ethylene glycol-bis(2-aminoethylether)-N,N,N,N-tetraacetic acid followed by incubation at 95 °C for 10 mins produces a strong SHERLOCK signal .

## Nucleic acid amplification module
Nucleic acid amplification improves assay sensitivity. Thus, how to maximize the signal amplification is the goal of this step. Buffer components are important. For example, in Cas12aVDet, NEBuffer 3.1 inhibits the RPA reaction to some extent. However, NEBuffer 2.1 does not . Primers also play an important role . To avoid non-specific amplification products being detected by the subsequent Cas reaction, the primers should maintain minimal base overlap with crRNA. Alternatively, the PAM sequence can be introduced through primers in the HOLMES assay , which allows the method to detect any SNP site independently of the sequence.

## CRISPR-Cas reaction module
CRISPR-Cas reaction module, has a significant impact on assay sensitivity, specificity, and even successful pathogen identification. Notably, Cas proteins from various origins perform differently. For example, to find the most suitable Cas12a for HOLMES that provides a high signal-to-noise ratio, Li et al. tested 10 Cas12a proteins and found that LbCas12a, OsCas12a, Lb5Cas12a, and FnCas12a showed good performance. Of these, LbCas12a was selected for subsequent use. Given the different cleavage preferences of Cas proteins from different sources, Cas proteins can be combined to achieve parallel detection of multiple pathogens in one reaction. Using dinucleotide cleavage screening, Gootenberg et al. found that four dinucleotide reporters, AU, UC, AC, and GA, can be used to, respectively, measure LwaCas13a, CcaCas13b, LbaCas13a, and PsmCas13b activity. Using these four Cas proteins expands the detection range and allows simultaneous detection of four targets ( A).
 Combining CRISPR-Cas proteins with auxiliary CRISPR-related enzymes can also boost sensitivity. The CRISPR type-III effector nuclease Csm6 can assist in signal amplification of Cas13-based detection methods . Interestingly, other enzymes such as endonucleases can conditionalize Cas protein activity. Han et al. showed that restriction endonucleases such as MseI and exonucleases such as ExoI enhance the Cas12a trans-cleavage activity when cleaving reporter DNA, while terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferases such as TdT act as inhibitor.
Another important component of the CRISPR-Cas reaction, crRNA, also correlates with methodological performance. Ding et al. introduced a pair of crRNAs in the all-in-one dual CRISPR-Cas12a (AIOD-CRISPR) method to achieve dual CRISPR-Cas12a detection. Doubling the amount of one crRNA did not improve the detection efficiency, whereas the experiment with two kinds of crRNAs could detect as low as 1.2 copies of the template within 40 mins. Importantly, this copy level was undetectable by experiments using a single crRNA. Fozouni et al. similarly demonstrated that a combination of two or three crRNAs can be used to detect viral target RNAs in the attomole range and reduce loss of detection owing to off-target effects and naturally occurring viral mutations.
Given the importance of reporter gene concentration for fluorescence readings, AIOD-CRISPR also optimized the required ssDNA-FQ reporters. Using a higher ssDNA-FQ reporter concentration effectively shortened the time to reach saturated fluorescence intensity . Mukama et al. used a biotinylated 5′-TTATT-3′ ssDNA reporter gene with 1–9 thymine residues attached to the 5′ end in the CRISPR-Cas and loop-mediated Isothermal Amplification (CIA) method A. A fragment complementary to the reporter gene was fixed to the test line. When the target was present, it specifically bound to Cas12a and triggered “collateral cleavage” activity, resulting in no signal at the test line. When the length of the reporter gene and the complementary probe exceeded nine bases, the fragments combined to produce a signal at the test line. The best reporter concentration to distinguish a signal was 20 nM. Sun et al. combined the RPA and Cas12 reactions with digital microfluidics to establish a nucleic acid detection platform called RCD and found that 5 bp DNA reporters with a “TCCCT” sequence achieved the optimal signal-to-noise ratio. Furthermore, the rate of trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a was increased by using hairpin DNA reporters, which have improved affinity (Km) . Compared to Cas-based detection using a conventional fluorophore/quencher reporter system, horseradish peroxidase-labeled oligonucleotides can increase the limit of detection (LOD) by approximately 30-fold . In addition, optimizing the reaction conditions is also significant. Wei et al. determined that the optimal reaction conditions for Cas14a1-based RNA detection sensors are 10 mM Mg2 + in 20 mM Tris, and 20 mM NaCl buffer (pH 8.5) at 37 ℃. The real-time fluorescence response intensity of the sensor was maximized by these conditions. The effectiveness of the above-mentioned components in improving the performance of CRISPR-based detection schemes can be assessed using figure of merit (FOM) values, which are defined as the product of the LOD and the CRISPR reaction time .
 

## Diverse output modes of CRISPR-Cas-based assays
At present, CRISPR-Cas-related pathogen nucleic acid detection technology mostly uses reporting systems based on fluorescence generation and quenching or non-fluorescence methods. In this section, we would discuss the benefits and drawbacks of each.

## Fluorescence-based reporting system
Fluorescence methods are widely used because of rapid analysis and high sensitivity. Chen et al. incubated fluorophore- and quencher-labeled ssDNA reporters, clinical samples, and pre-assembled LbCas12a–crRNA complexes in a fluorescence plate reader. In this system, a strong fluorescence signal was obtained in the presence of the target. SYBR Green I (SG Ⅰ) can also be used as a fluorescent marker. Guk et al. incubated ternary dCas9-sgRNA-target genomic DNA complexes with Ni-NTA magnetic nanobeads. The beads were washed to remove unbound complexes, followed by the addition of SG I to produce fluorescence in the presence of target ( B). Although SG I has high sensitivity, high signal-to-noise ratio, and simple operation, primer design is technically difficult, which limits SG I applications . To overcome these shortcomings, Sun et al. introduced a metal–organic framework ( C). The ssDNA product of the CRISPR-Cas9-triggered strand displacement amplification-rolling cycle amplification (SDA-RCA) reaction is complementary to the fluorescently labeled DNA probe, resulting in an increased distance between UiO-66 quencher and the fluorescent probe and enhanced fluorescence. Additionally, various fluorescent dyes enable multiplexing. Combinatorial Arrayed Reactions for Multiplexed Evaluation of Nucleic acids (CARMEN) ( D), a scalable combinatorial array reaction for multiple evaluation of nucleic acids, can simultaneously differentiate 169 human-related viruses and at least 10 published genome sequences . In the CARMEN approach, nanoliter droplets containing CRISPR-based nucleic acid detection reagents self-assemble into a matrix of microwells to pair with droplets containing amplified samples, thereby testing their crRNA repeatedly against each sample. To fulfill public health needs, microfluidic CARMEN (mCARMEN) has been developed. This method can test hundreds of samples for multiple respiratory viruses and variants in a day, while also quantifying viral genomic copies by combining the CRISPR-based diagnostics and microfluidics into a streamlined workflow. Compared to CARMEN, mCARMEN eliminates the need for droplet generation, color coding, custom microscopes, chips, and data analysis expertise. The high-throughput CARMEN and mCARMEN assays are of great significance for POCT for pathogens, but these approaches require multiple crRNA screenings for optimal detection and specific equipment to achieve assay output.
In contrast to traditional fluorescent organic dyes, Bao et al. used quantum dots (Qdots) as reporters to develop a new probe system. The unique electrical and optical properties of Qdots have advantages of stable signal intensity and high quantum yield. In oligonucleotide-conjugated gold nanoparticle (AuNP) systems, DNA hybridization causes AuNPs to aggregate, resulting in a red-shift of the surface plasmon resonance peak , . This system can be combined with CRISPR-Cas systems to construct visual biosensors owing to the strong molar absorption coefficient of AuNPs , , , for nucleic acid detection , . For example, CASCADE , is a sensing platform for SARS-CoV-2 detection using Cas13 and AuNPs. After ssRNA coupled to AuNPs are cleaved by target-triggered Cas13, AuNPs aggregate, causing a visible color change. This AuNP-based colorimetric method utilizes minimal lab equipment and is promising for POCT .

## Reporting systems based on non-fluorescent methods
Lateral flow systems are another output method that are portable, easy to operate and store, and easy to commercialize. The CIA platform ( A) uses a lateral flow system as the output method . A DNA probe sequence complementary to the reporter is fixed at the test line of the lateral flow bar to capture the reporters. When the target is present, the reporter is cleaved, resulting in signal loss at the test line. A novel paper-based device combining RT-LAMP with Cas12a reaction can successfully detect low nucleic acid concentrations by visualizing assay results via lateral flow signals . Furthermore, lateral flow systems can easily, quickly, and qualitatively detect pathogens, thereby avoiding the use of expensive fluorescent equipment. However, these lateral flow systems are not quantitative, and the risk of contamination is high.
Similarly, the NASBA-CRISPR Cleavage (NASBACC) platform, which can effectively detect ZIKV, uses a colorimetric method based on chemical reactions 
( B). The toe switch, a regulator that precisely controls gene expression, is an important element of the NASBACC. When activated by the RNA trigger sequence, the toe switch induces translation of the regulatory enzyme LacZ, thereby converting yellow substrate into a purple product. A colorimetric approach is also used by wearable freeze-dried, cell-free (wFDCF) sensors . Reaction components such as toe switches, transcription factors, riboswitches, fluorescent reporters, and CRISPR-Cas12a complexes are integrated into flexible materials such as silicone elastomers and textiles. These materials can be used to manufacture SARS-CoV-2 detection masks to detect respiratory droplets and aerosolized samples. Additionally, the CRISPR-dCas9-based assay developed by Bengtson et al. can be used to detect Leishmania DNA by colorimetric reading of DNAzymes (G-quadruplex–hemin complexes).
Optical methods can also be used as a reporting system. Nucleic acid amplification on the surface of a silicon microring resonator (SMR) biosensor can provide rapid, unlabeled detection of biomolecules attached to the surface of the sensor. Amplification changes the refractive index and can be used for real-time multiplexing . Koo et al. quickly differentiated two tick-borne diseases with similar clinical symptoms caused by different pathogens, namely, scrub typhus and severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome ( C). In this method, RNA is first reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Then, the cDNA is extended using primers fixed on the SMR sensor and polymerase. Nucleic acid amplification causes a shift in the wavelength of the SMR sensor. dCas9 that is specifically bound to the target can further increase the molecular weight and amplify the signal. Compared with SMR alone, SMR sensors combined with dCas9 can detect pathogens with high sensitivity within 20 mins. This method is 100 times more sensitive than RT-PCR alone. Electrical signals can also be used as a reporting system, as demonstrated by Yang et al. . When the target is combined with CRISPR-Cas flow through the nanopore, the resulting localized spikes have a short duration (∼25–60 μs) and a much deeper blockade level (up to 1.5nA) than bare DNA, owing to the volume of the CRISPR-Cas protein. Optics and electricity-based reporting methods are more objective and the results are easier to interpret, but these methods frequently rely on complicated and sophisticated machines.

## Discussion
The SARS-CoV-2 outbreak highlighted the importance of creating an accurate, rapid, and cost-effective nucleic acid test to detect infectious pathogens. The CRISPR-Cas system can be used alone or in combination with nucleic acid amplification and sensors to detect pathogen nucleic acids ( ). Indeed, these systems can be used to detect HPV, Mtb, ZIKV, and other pathogens ( ). The CRISPR-Cas-based methods show a high-order signal amplification ability especially with the help of isothermal amplification that can achieve exponential amplification, which are suitable for detection of samples containing a small amount of nucleic acid. Furthermore, CRISPR reactions can resolve false positive signals caused by non-specific amplification products in amplification reactions through sgRNA recognition. Additionally, the cost of pathogen nucleic acid detection assays based on CRISPR-Cas can be as low as $0.1–1 per test , , while RPA detection methods cost approximately $4.3 per test and paper-based capillary electrophoresis microdevices cost more than $1.6 per test . Compared to previous technologies , , , , , , , CRISPR-Cas-based methods have high sensitivity, specificity, efficiency, low cost, ease of operation, and portability ( )
 Although CRISPR-Cas-based methods have the potential to become excellent pathogen detection systems, they are not yet commonly used mainly owing to various obstacles such as the integrated sample preparation, the establishment of one-pot assays and the standardization of quantitative analysis. However, the cutting-edge studies may provide potential solutions to these barriers to clinical translation.

## Integrated sample preparation.
Direct testing of raw samples facilitates more accurate results, but currently nucleic acid extraction of samples is necessary for most CRISPR-based assays, especially for types of samples such as bacteria and parasites. Therefore, integration of sample processing within portable devices is key to achieving POCT. Sample handling should be as simple as possible without interfering with downstream pre-amplification reactions or Cas reactions, thus facilitating integration into small devices. Simplified nucleic acid processing methods such as HUDSON and S-PREP have the potential to be integrated. Furthermore, the POC-CRISPR incoporates sample preparation with minimal manual operation via facile magnetic-based nucleic acid concentration and transport, which has been adapted into a compact thermoplastic cartridge within a palm-sized yet fully-integrated and automated device. Lyophilisation lysis reagents, isothermal amplification reagents and Cas reaction reagents can be lyophilised and integrated into the wFDCF system to create masks capable of detecting SARS-CoV-2 in droplet samples . Future research should concentrate on creating a fully automated procedure (raw sample-in-answer-out) by seamlessly integrating the sample preparation and CRISPR test , which can be applied to a wide range of pathogens such as viruses, bacteria and parasites.

## The establishment of the one-pot assays
One-pot approach can achieve a faster detection speed and lower the danger of contamination , , . AIOD-CRISPR is the first method to incubate all the components of an isothermal amplification and CRISPR reaction in a single-tube reaction that can be performed at a single temperature (37 °C) . However, the compositional complexity of single-tube reactions makes it difficult to achieve optimum performance. An optimization of a single-tube RT-RPA-CRISPR-based assay protocol demonstrates that reverse transcription buffer and RNase inhibitors can significantly improve its detection performance. In the RT-RPA-CRISPR single-tube reaction, the reverse transcribed cDNA tends to bind to RNA, resulting in a delayed RPA reaction and reduced CRISPR activity. Using the ribonucleic acid endonuclease function of RNase H may solve this problem . Besides, the addition of glycerol also helps to improve the sensitivity of the one-step one-pot assay . To avoid interactions between isothermal amplification and CRISPR reactions, Hu et al. instead devised a light-controlled CRISPR assay that activates crRNA by UV irradiation for 30 s after the completion of the RPA reaction, resulting in an increase in sensitivity of more than two orders of magnitude compared to the traditional one-pot assay. In addition, attaching the amplification and Cas reaction components to different locations in the same tube is a strategy for constructing a two-step one-pot method , . Similarly, digital CRISPR integrates each reaction step into a different region of the sensor, thus enabling a step-by-step CRISPR-based pathogen detection technology for POCT. The development of a low-cost, rapid and portable one-pot test is the way forward.

## Standardization of quantitative analysis
Quantitative analysis includes analyzing test results and performance comparisons quantitatively. Firstly, for the quantitative analysis of performance comparisons, new method is necessary to be compared with the gold standard methods to determine the detection performance of it. For example, CRISPR-based detection of SARS-CoV-2 is often compared with qRT-PCR to validate its sensitivity and specificity . And for quantitative comparison of performance before and after optimization of new methods, FOM may enable this. Second, for the quantitative analysis of test results, the CRISPR-Cas-based approach relies on the fluorescently labeled nucleic acid reporters being cleaved when its “collateral cleavage” activity is initiated for quantitative detection , , which causes quick saturation of the fluorescent signal, making quantitative analysis difficult. Furthermore, the combination with nucleic acid amplification technology , , , , and increase levels of the target would result in increased activation and faster accumulation of signal when measured by fluorescence output . Extra amplification complicates quantification and the window period for quantification has yet to be verified in numerous experiments. Digital CRISPR, which divides the reaction solution into thousands of discrete single molecules for the CRISPR reaction, is an advance of the quantitative detection. The well containing the target (positive well) generates a fluorescent signal while no signal is detected from a well without a target (negative well). By fitting a Poisson distribution, the analyte concentration can be calculated by counting the number of positive or negative wells .
Although translating pathogen nucleic acid detection technology based on CRISPR-Cas to clinical settings has numerous hurdles, these approaches have the potential to become optimal pathogen nucleic acid identification assays that will aid clinical diagnosis and patient health monitoring.
