## Abstract

## Background
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The human colon, which hosts a vast and complex microbial ecosystem, plays a crucial role in health and disease, with approximately 20% of human cancers linked to microbial dysbiosis. Bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) serve as critical mediators of host-microbe communication, influencing both physiological and pathological processes.

## Methods
In this narrative review study, we surveyed the literature published on the multifaceted roles of BEVs, derived from various bacterial strains, in the pathogenesis and treatment of CRC, using databases such as Google Scholar, Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Web of Science.

## Results
BEVs carry a diverse cargo, including proteins, nucleic acids, toxins, and signaling molecules, that can traverse host barriers, modulate immune responses, and influence cancer progression. Their biogenesis varies by bacterial type, involving complex processes such as membrane blebbing, autolysin-mediated wall weakening, and explosive cell lysis. BEVs can suppress tumor growth, positioning them as promising candidates for innovative cancer therapies. Moreover, engineered BEVs have the potential to serve as sophisticated drug delivery vehicles for chemotherapeutic or photothermal agents, as well as novel vaccine platforms. Although tumor regression and enhanced T-cell responses mediated by BEVs have been demonstrated in numerous preclinical studies, their clinical translation remains challenging. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of BEVs derived from diverse bacterial strains, focusing on their roles in CRC pathogenesis and therapy, while highlighting key barriers to their clinical applications.

## Conclusions
While promising, challenges remain in standardizing isolation, ensuring safety, and optimizing tumor-targeting efficiency for clinical translation. Future research focusing on advanced engineering and rigorous validation is crucial to harness the full therapeutic potential of BEVs in CRC management.

Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Bacterial extracellular vesicles, Outer membrane vesicles, Explosive OMVs, Explosive OIMVs

## Background
 Colorectal cancer (CRC) has undergone a substantial shift in its mortality ranking among individuals under the age of 50 [ – ]. While it was the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death in both men and women in the late 1990 s, it has now become the primary cause in men and the second most common in women [ ]. The human gastrointestinal tract, specifically the colon, contains a highly complex microbial ecosystem. This microbial community, which constitutes approximately 97% of the body’s total bacterial load, has been increasingly recognized as a critical factor in human health and disease. Notably, dysfunctions within this microbial ecosystem have been linked to about 20% of human cancers [ ].
The host-gut microbiome axis is a sophisticated ecological system characterized by dynamic interactions between host tissues and resident gut microorganisms, facilitated by the exchange of signaling molecules and metabolites that are critical for regulating fundamental physiological and pathological processes such as immune function, stress responses, and the balance of cell proliferation and cell death. This axis ensures homeostasis by maintaining epithelial barrier integrity, supporting immune tolerance, and producing vital metabolic products, including short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, and bile acid derivatives. When the gut microbial community is disrupted, a phenomenon known as dysbiosis, these stabilizing interactions are disturbed, leading to chronic inflammation, perturbation of signaling pathways, and immune dysregulation, all of which contribute significantly to cancer development and progression, particularly in gastrointestinal tissues. Thus, harmonious communication between the host and its microbiota is essential for maintaining health, whereas disruption of this balance can initiate and promote oncogenic processes through diverse molecular pathways [ – ].
Compared to healthy individuals, CRC patients typically exhibit reduced diversity and richness in their gut microbiome. This imbalance, or dysbiosis, is characterized by an increased abundance of specific pathogenic bacteria, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Bacteroides fragilis, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Streptococcus gallolyticus. Concurrently, there is a notable decline in beneficial, butyrate-producing bacteria such as Roseburia and members of the Lachnospiraceae family [ , ].
Bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) are membrane-bound nanostructures released by both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria during normal physiology and stress. As crucial intermediaries in host–microbe communication, these vesicles can traverse physical barriers and deliver diverse molecular cargo. BEVs significantly influence the host’s immune system by interacting with receptors like Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which can trigger pathways leading to either inflammation or immune evasion. Furthermore, they facilitate disease progression by delivering toxins and virulence factors directly into host cells, entering through various endocytic mechanisms or direct membrane fusion, thereby altering host cell functions and immune responses [ – ]. The intricate molecular mechanisms of BEVs enable them to exert contrasting effects on cancer, either promoting or suppressing tumor growth. By interacting with both the tumor microenvironment (TME) and the immune system, BEVs emerge as innovative tools for improving treatment outcomes across a wide range of cancers. Their functional adaptability positions them as a promising platform, capable of serving as powerful immune modulators, targeted drug delivery vehicles, and essential vaccine components in cancer therapy [ , ]. Moreover, BEVs may represent a critical link between the immune system, bacteria, and CRC cells, with this connection potentially mediated through mechanisms such as dysbiosis, alterations in intercellular signaling pathways and metabolite profiles, and modulation of immune responses [ , ].
Despite their recognized importance in various biological processes, the potential impact of BEVs on cancer development and progression remains largely unexplored [ , ]. To address this knowledge gap, the present narrative review provides a comprehensive overview of the roles and effects of BEVs derived from various bacterial strains in the pathogenesis and treatment of gastrointestinal cancers, with a particular focus on CRC. In addition, we examine the potential of BEVs in cancer vaccine development, explore their engineering for therapeutic applications, and assess their promise as CRC biomarkers. Accordingly, databases such as Google Scholar, Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Web of Science were searched up to September 2025 using primary keywords such as “Extracellular Vesicles,” “Colorectal Cancer,” “Bacterial Extracellular Vesicles,” and “Cancer Vaccine,” together with relevant synonyms and combinations.

## Cargo and composition of BEVs
BEVs are tiny, membrane-bound structures released by both harmful and beneficial bacteria. They carry a wide range of compounds that originate from their parent bacterial cells and facilitate the transport of various molecules, including proteins, functional enzymes (e.g., autolysins), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), lipoteichoic acid (LTA), and peptidoglycans (PG) [ ]. Additionally, BEVs may transport toxins and glycoproteins involved in crucial bacterial processes, including adhesion, invasion, survival, and evasion of the host immune system (e.g., outer membrane protein A (OmpA)). Phosphatidylserine, sphingomyelin, ceramide, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidylglycerol are among the lipids commonly found in BEVs. Their lipid composition resembles that of the bacterial membrane and can vary in response to factors such as iron availability and oxidative stress. BEVs also carry a broad spectrum of nucleic acids, including chromosomal and plasmid DNA, as well as diverse RNA species such as short RNAs (sRNA), messenger RNA (mRNA), microRNA (miRNA), and other non-coding RNAs. The biogenesis of BEVs can influence the specific nucleic acids encapsulated within them, particularly in Gram-negative bacteria, where certain subtypes are known to bundle cytoplasmic material [ , ]. Metabolites, a collection of small molecules, including microbial metabolites, are another key component of BEVs that play potential roles in interkingdom communication and bacterial physiology. Specific enzymes and toxins such as phospholipase C, alkaline phosphatase, esterase lipase, serine protease, cholera toxin, adenylate cyclase toxin, cytolethal distending toxin, vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA), and effector proteins like PhoP-activated genes (PagJ and PagK1) are frequently packaged in BEVs to facilitate bacterial pathogenicity [ ]. BEVs also carry polysaccharides and PG, both key components of the bacterial cell envelope. Their inclusion in BEVs is noteworthy, as these molecules may influence the structural integrity of the vesicles and the ability to interact with the host immune system [ ].

## Biogenesis of BEVs

## Gram-negative bacteria
Recent findings indicate that the biogenesis of EVs in Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria involves distinct cellular processes and molecular machinery, resulting in the production of structurally and functionally diverse EVs populations [ , ]. The structural organization of Gram-negative bacteria includes an inner membrane (IM) and an outer membrane (OM), with the PG layer situated within the periplasmic space between them. Lpp (Lipoprotein, also Braun’s lipoprotein) is a major, highly abundant protein, crucial for linking the OM to the PG cell wall, providing structural integrity, and controlling periplasm width and cell stiffness, acting as a barrier against antibiotics. Notably, the OM exhibits a bilayer structure where the external leaflet is dominated by LPS and the internal leaflet is primarily composed of phospholipids. Conversely, the IM consists of a phospholipid bilayer. Gram-negative bacteria are known to release two primary types of BEVs with overall diameters spanning approximately 20–250 nm: outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) and outer-inner membrane vesicles (OIMVs) [ , ].
The defining characteristic that distinguishes OMVs from OIMVs lies in their membrane composition. OMVs are primarily composed of OM and periplasmic proteins, notably lacking IM and cytoplasmic components. OIMVs contain components from both the IM and OM, as well as periplasmic proteins. Unlike other vesicle types, OIMV formation is triggered by autolysin-mediated weakening or errors in PG synthesis. This process involves bulging of the IM into the periplasm, followed by pinching off from the cell surface, incorporating a portion of the OM [ , ] (Fig. ). Several mechanistic models have been proposed for the biogenesis of OMVs. The reduced OM–PG crosslinking model (or tether‑loss model) suggests that weakening the covalent and non-covalent connections between the OM and the underlying PG, due to defects in lipoproteins such as Lpp and OmpA, reduces mechanical coupling. This allows the OM to bulge outward under turgor pressure and undergo vesiculation through scission. Key evidence includes hypervesiculation (a 10- to 100-fold increase) observed in Lpp, OmpA, or tolA mutants, where OMV production correlates directly with tether disruption. Evidence strongly supports the OM–PG crosslinking model in wild-type cells during exponential growth or quorum sensing, periods characterized by peak signaling lipid levels, and in transporter mutants. Its predictive accuracy diminishes under acute stress conditions. Additionally, the model predicts a significant increase in OMV production, approximately 50–200 nm in size, in mutants exhibiting defective OM–PG tethering, with yields often 10–50 times higher than those observed in wild-type cells. Cargo is expected to be predominantly associated with OM components (LPS, OM proteins, lipoproteins), reflecting local OM protrusions and limited selective incorporation of periplasmic material [ , ].
 The lipid/protein microdomain, or intercalation, model proposes that the clustering of integral membrane proteins, such as OmpA, Pseudomonas quinolone signals, unsaturated branched-chain fatty acids, or Lipid A, facilitates membrane branching and vesicle scission. Localized enrichment of outer leaflet lipids (e.g., phospholipids via VacJ/Lpt mutants) or curvature-inducing proteins and amphipaths (e.g., PQS in P. aeruginosa) promotes asymmetric bilayer expansion, resembling a “bilayer couple” mechanism, thereby enabling spontaneous budding from discrete OM regions without PG detachment [ , ]. This model predicts persistent, physiologically relevant levels of vesiculation during growth, with the frequency correlating to the abundance or activity of curvature-inducing lipids or proteins, such as elevated vesiculation under high PQS conditions. Supporting evidence includes phospholipid accumulation in OMVs from vacJ mutants (enriched 5–10-fold), fluorescent lipid tracing to budding sites, and conserved microdomain markers such as cardiolipin across diverse species. The model is strongly supported in wild-type cells during standard growth and quorum-sensing, as well as in mutants with alterations in lipid trafficking or specific signaling lipids (e.g., VacJ/Yrb, PQS in Pseudomonas). It releases vesicles within the size range of approximately 20–100 nm, containing highly selective cargo such as enriched signaling lipids, specific OM proteins (e.g., OmpF), or toxins. These functionally specialized OMVs contrast with the heterogeneous vesicle populations observed in tether‑loss models ​ [ – ].
The periplasmic pressure/misfolded protein model proposes that substantial periplasmic accumulations, such as misfolded proteins resulting from DegP or SurA chaperone defects, PG fragments, or debris, elevate local osmotic and turgor pressure. This increase deforms the OM, ultimately leading to vesicle fission that expels the load as a detoxification mechanism. Supporting evidence includes antibiotic stress, such as vancomycin treatment, which increases OMV production twenty‑fold with PG cargo, as well as sigmaE stress responses. This model is primarily supported under envelope stress conditions, including thermal shock, antibiotic exposure, the stationary phase, or in quality‑control mutants, rather than during routine growth. It predicts stress-responsive, high-frequency production of relatively large vesicles that can better accommodate bulky cargo, including periplasmic proteins such as DegP and other chaperones, misfolded OM proteins, PG fragments, and even DNA. These features distinguish them from the predominantly OM-focused cargo seen in other models and help explain stress-induced functions such as virulence factor export [ , ]. The model is firmly associated with the activation of envelope stress responses and the buildup of misfolded proteins or PG fragments within the periplasm. Accordingly, OMVs are enriched in periplasmic proteins such as chaperones and proteases, together with misfolded OM proteins, PG fragments, and other stress-associated cargo, consistent with a role in detoxification and export. Under this framework, basal vesiculation in unstressed cells is expected to be minimal, whereas acute stress conditions or mutations that impair envelope quality control drive pronounced, often transient increases in OMV production. The elevated accumulation of misfolded proteins or PG fragments necessitates a higher OMV flux to relieve periplasmic pressure and facilitate the export of potentially deleterious material [ , , ].
The classification of OMVs and OIMVs can be further refined to include explosive OMVs (EOMVs) and explosive OIMVs (EOIMVs) when their biogenesis involves a rapid, burst-like event [ , ]. When cells encounter genetic, phototoxic, or antibody-induced stress, endolysins are activated, leading to degradation of the PG layer and subsequent explosive cell lysis. This process results in the formation of two distinct types of vesicles: (1) EOMVs, which arise directly from cell protrusion and explosion following lysis (Fig. ), and (2) EOIMVs, which form through the self-assembly of IM components and cytoplasmic contents, producing vesicles enclosed within the OM [ , ](Fig. ).

## Gram-positive bacteria
Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick PG layer surrounding their cytoplasmic membrane, which acts as a barrier to the release of BEVs. Nevertheless, cytoplasmic membrane-derived vesicles (CMVs) from Gram-positive bacteria have garnered increasing research interest [ ]. Several non-exclusive hypotheses have been proposed to elucidate the biogenesis of CMVs. The first suggests that CMVs are released from the cytoplasm and traverse the cell wall under turgor pressure, with the rate of efflux determined by pore size and cell wall thickness [ ]. A second model proposes that enzymatic modification of the cell wall reduces membrane rigidity, thereby facilitating CMVs release [ ]. A third hypothesis states that CMVs undergo dynamic deformation to pass through constricted pores when precursor molecules exert a propulsive force against a weakened or degraded PG wall. This mechanism is thought to initiate PG encapsulation during CMV release [ , ].
Generally, CMVs in Gram-positive bacteria originate from the cytoplasmic membrane and should cross the thick PG layer for successful release (Fig. ). This process involves the activity of enzymes that remodel the cell wall, such as autolysins and penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Cell wall-degrading enzymes weaken the PG layer, enabling CMVs to pass through. For instance, in Staphylococcus aureus, these enzymes remodel the cell wall to facilitate BEVs transit, while in Bacillus subtilis, a phage-encoded endolysin creates pores in the PG, promoting BEVs release [ – ]. Genetic mutations, particularly in broad gene regulators like sigB and two-component systems, can profoundly influence the biogenesis and characteristics of BEVs. Additionally, specific peptides, such as the phenol-soluble modulins in S. aureus, play a crucial role in BEVs formation by disrupting the bacterial membrane [ , ].
Under stress conditions, Gram-positive bacteria can release explosive cell membrane vesicles (ECMVs) through a mechanism known as bubbling cell death. This process contrasts with vesicle formation in Gram-negative bacteria, as the substantial PG layer in Gram-positive organisms facilitates the generation of intracellular membrane vesicles and ghost cells without requiring complete hydrolysis of the cytoplasmic membrane. Importantly, ECMVs are structurally distinct from CMVs, which are formed through blebbing [ , ](Fig. ).

## Isolation and characterization

## Isolation of BEVs
One of the key steps in utilizing BEVs to investigate their structure, composition, or theranostic potential is their isolation. To date, various methods have been employed for this purpose, each leveraging distinct characteristics such as size, density, and surface markers. Ultracentrifugation, density gradient, size exclusion chromatography, filter concentration, precipitation, and affinity precipitation are among the most commonly applied techniques for BEVs isolation; each is described individually below.

## Differential ultracentrifugation (DUC)
DUC remains the most widely employed method for isolating BEVs across various studies [ ]. This technique, first employed by Johnston to isolate EVs from reticulocytes [ ], relies on straightforward principles, and through sequential centrifugation steps, different subpopulations of BEVs are separated from culture supernatants. Initially, using low-speed centrifugation, the culture medium is depleted of bacteria and cell debris, and then BEVs are precipitated by applying ultracentrifugation [ , , ]. It is important to note that, in addition to particle size and shape, density and cargo composition also influence EVs’ separation by this method [ , , ]. Accordingly, larger/denser vesicles tend to sediment at lower centrifugal speeds, whereas smaller/less dense particles often require higher speeds to precipitate [ ]. In a recent study, Sawant et al. reported that the DUC method is an efficient approach for isolating BEVs from both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, yielding a higher BEVs recovery than the precipitation method without notable differences in vesicle size [ ]. Moreover, they indicated that BEVs yield does not increase with extended bacterial culture duration, because the exponential growth phase ends and the bacterial population reach the stationary phase. Although this method is cost-effective, scalable, and convenient for isolating BEVs, it involves a lengthy procedure, and the high gravitational forces applied during centrifugation can damage the vesicles and promote aggregation, thereby compromising their structural integrity and potentially impairing their biological function [ , , ]. Moreover, co-precipitation of contaminants such as bacterial appendages, which co-sediment with BEVs due to similar sedimentation properties, may interfere with downstream analyses or biological assays [ , ]. Thus, optimizing centrifugal force is essential for efficient bacterial removal with minimal impact on BEVs yield and quality. Accordingly, the initial low-speed centrifugation step should be carefully selected to ensure effective bacterial clearance; otherwise, higher speeds may induce bacterial lysis and lead to BEVs contamination with cellular debris [ ]. In this regard, plating the supernatant following low-speed centrifugation has been proposed as a practical approach to achieve thorough bacterial clearance [ ]. It is worth noting that the optimal centrifugation speed may vary depending on the bacterial strain and its growth medium. In many BEV studies, researchers have primarily focused on the final pellet, while intermediate centrifugation pellets are often discarded [ – ]. However, as highlighted in the MISEV guideline, these intermediate fractions can be retained and compared with the final pellet in terms of their composition and the biological activity of interest [ ].

## Density gradients (DG)
Density gradient (DG) centrifugation, first introduced by Lindersotrm-Lang, is another technique that has been used to isolate and purify BEVs [ , , ]. This technique benefits from the different buoyancy densities of BEVs, soluble proteins, and other particles to isolate them from each other [ , ]. In this method, following the loading of the sample either beneath the gradient (bottom-up) or on top of the gradient (top-down), ultracentrifugation is applied to allow BEVs to migrate to positions within the gradient where their buoyant density matches that of the surrounding medium [ ]. Since various extracellular particles, contaminants, and cell-free genetic materials possess distinct buoyant densities, they accumulate at different positions within the gradient after centrifugation [ ]. Sucrose and OptiPrep (iodixanol-based) are two dense media that have been widely used for this aim [ ]. Compared to sucrose, Iodixanol has isotonic properties and can therefore better preserve the physical and biological integrity of EVs [ , ]. Depending on the bacterial strain and BEVs density, the centrifugation time and force should be carefully optimized to ensure efficient separation of BEVs [ ]. Moreover, the precise preparation of gradient solutions is another key factor that must be considered to obtain reliable results [ ]. In a study, Xue et al. applied both top-down and bottom-up density gradient centrifugation methods to isolate BEVs from human feces [ ]. They found that although both approaches yielded BEVs ranging from 90 to 300 nm in size, the bottom-up method resulted in higher particle and protein recovery rates. However, there were no significant differences in the particle-to-protein ratio between the two methods. The researchers also reported that a centrifugation time of 7 h was sufficient to achieve the required purity, and extending the duration to 15 h did not further improve protein recovery. Although this technique can enhance BEVs purity, contamination by particles with similar densities persists. Additionally, the procedure is technically demanding and requires experienced technicians. Prolonged ultracentrifugation may also compromise the structural integrity of BEVs [ , , ].

## Size exclusion chromatography (SEC)
SEC is another method for BEVs isolation that separates particles according to their hydrodynamic radius [ ]. This method involves passing the BEVs-containing supernatant through a porous stationary phase composed of materials such as cross-linked dextrans (Sephadex), agarose (Sepharose), polyacrylamide (BioGel), or allyl dextran (Sephacryl) [ ]. Compared to larger particles that bypass the pores and elute first, smaller particles enter the pores and elute later [ ]. SEC offers high purity by removing proteins and small molecules. It is important to note that selecting an SEC column with an appropriate pore size plays a pivotal role in minimizing BEVs contamination by larger particles [ , ]. To date, several commercially available SEC-based kits, including qEV (iZON) and PURE-EVs (Hansa Biomed), have been developed, through which the process of BEVs isolation can be simplified [ , ]. Recently, SEC has been used alone or in combination with other methods to isolate BEVs [ , ]. In a study, researchers used a Sephacryl S-1000 column to isolate OMVs from Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, successfully separating different OMV subpopulations, including large and small vesicles [ ]. This technique is convenient, cost-effective, and may provide more reproducible results [ , ]. Moreover, compared to ultracentrifugation, it better preserves the integrity of BEVs [ ]. However, it is associated with lower yield and limited scalability. Moreover, it dilutes EV fractions, necessitating concentration [ ]. In addition, its size-based separation can lead to the co-isolation of similarly sized contaminants [ ].

## Filter concentration (FC)
Another size-based method for isolating BEVs is filtration chromatography (FC). In this technique, membrane filters with different molecular weight cut-offs (MWCOs) are used to separate BEVs from other particulate contaminants [ , ]. Initially, low-speed centrifugation or large-pore filters are applied to remove cells and debris. Subsequently, filters with smaller pore sizes are used to isolate BEVs. Filtration membranes composed of hydrosart, cellulose, or polyethersulfone are available in a range of MWCOs such as 10–100 kDa, enabling partial size-based separation in addition to concentration [ , ]. This method offers advantages such as speed, simplicity, and ease of operation [ , ]. However, it cannot effectively distinguish BEVs from similarly sized particles. Moreover, trapping of EVs within filter pores presents another challenge, potentially leading to filter clogging and EVs loss. The applied force during this process may also affect EV’s structural integrity [ , , ]. Therefore, selecting appropriate centrifugal forces and filter pore sizes is highly important. Additionally, the choice of filter material is another critical factor to consider. In this context, the low protein-binding nature of hydrophilic PVDF membranes helps minimize non-specific binding of EVs during filtration [ ].
Tangential flow filtration (TFF) is another filtration-based approach used to concentrate bacterial culture supernatants or isolate BEVs. Using depleted brain heart infusion (BHI) medium with TFF increased Helicobacter pylori protein detection by 3.4-fold, while reducing contaminants tenfold [ ]. Unlike conventional dead-end filtration, TFF directs the liquid to flow parallel to the membrane surface, enabling continuous filtration and reducing membrane clogging [ , ]. The AKTA Flux system, developed by Cytiva, is a versatile tangential flow filtration platform equipped with hollow fibre modules and has been applied to the isolation of EVs [ ]. Although TFF has been widely used to isolate EVs from sources such as cell culture conditioned media [ – ], more studies are needed to evaluate this technique for the isolation of BEVs. However, it appears that this method holds promise for industrial-scale BEVs isolation.

## Precipitation
This method relies on the high concentration of salts or polymers that disrupts EVs’ solubility or surface charge, thereby facilitating their sedimentation [ ]. Ammonium sulfate and polyethene glycol are two precipitant agents that have been widely used [ , ]. Following the treatment and incubation of bacterial culture supernatant with these agents, BEVs can be easily isolated by low-speed centrifugation. ExoQuick (System Biosciences), Total exosome isolation reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Invitrogen), and ExoPrep (HansaBioMed) are four examples of precipitation-based EVs isolation products [ ]. In a recent study, Wang et al. compared three methods for isolating BEVs from Mycobacterium tuberculosis: DUC, polymer-based precipitation (using EX0011, China), and a filtration-based approach (using EXODUS H600, China) [ ]. Although all methods isolated BEVs with diameters below 200 nm, the precipitation method yielded the highest particle count and protein concentration. Furthermore, SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that BEVs isolated by these methods contained both shared and distinct protein profiles. Although the precipitation method is fast, cost-effective, convenient, and suitable for large volume isolation, it often yields EVs with limited purity due to the co-precipitation of non-specific proteins [ , ]. Therefore, when high-purity BEVs are required for downstream applications, this approach may not be suitable, and further purification using alternative methods may be necessary. Moreover, BEVs isolated by this method are often contaminated with precipitation agents, which should be removed before subsequent procedures to prevent interference [ , , ].
Researchers have also developed a rapid and cost-effective precipitation-based method for isolating BEVs, which leverages the electrostatic interaction between negatively charged BEVs and positively charged epsilon‑poly‑L‑lysine (ε‑PL), thereby facilitating their efficient pelleting by centrifugation [ ]. Subsequent purification involves resuspension of the pellet in a specific buffer followed by ultrafiltration to remove ε‑PL. Notably, BEVs isolated using this ε-PL-based technique are comparable in key characteristics such as size, morphology, protein profile, and bioactivity to those obtained through the more complex and time-consuming UC method [ ].

## Affinity precipitation
Affinity precipitation or Immunoaffinity is another method for BEVs isolation that benefits from the specific binding of EVs surface recognition molecules to surface proteins (antigens) on the BEVs [ ]. Proteins or membrane-associated molecules that are uniquely or highly enriched on the EVs surface, and that lack soluble counterparts in the extracellular environment, can be utilized as markers for immunoaffinity-based isolation [ , ]. In this regard, tetraspanin molecules such as CD63, CD9, and CD81 have been widely targeted for EV isolation, and several commercial kits, including the Exosome-Human CD63 Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher) and the Exosome Isolation Kit CD81/CD63 (Miltenyi Biotec), have been developed [ ]. Other surface markers can also be targeted; for example, the Exosome-Human EpCAM Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher) is a commercially available kit that enables the isolation of EpCAM-positive EV subpopulations [ ]. Solid matrices such as beads, membranes, and plates have been used to immobilize binding molecules [ ]. A widely adopted application of this method involves submicron-sized magnetic beads functionalized with specific antibodies. When mixed with a sample, these antibody-coated beads bind to target surface molecules on BEVs, forming magnetic bead–BEV complexes. This interaction allows efficient magnetic separation of the desired BEVs, resulting in enhanced specificity. The technique also extends to genetically modified BEVs, such as those engineered with a His-tag. For instance, it has been successfully employed to isolate OMVs containing OmpA-His6 from E. coli culture medium [ ]. Although this method is easy to use and yields BEVs with high purity, it is costly and may exclude certain subpopulations [ , ]. Therefore, it is necessary to assess the distribution of the target molecule across EV subtypes and other extracellular particles to ensure accurate capture specificity [ ]. Moreover, dissociation of EVs from the matrix and binding molecules may require elution conditions that could alter the structural integrity of EVs [ , ].

## Characterization of BEVs
After isolating BEVs, their characterization is essential both to confirm the presence of BEVs and to evaluate the contributions of non-BEV components. Common characterization approaches include EV quantification (e.g., particle number, total protein, total lipids, and total RNA), analysis of protein composition, evaluation of morphological features, and determination of size distribution [ ]. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) is a valuable tool for characterizing BEVs, providing size distribution and concentration measurements by tracking their Brownian motion in solution. However, it cannot distinguish EVs from similarly sized contaminants in scatter mode and may therefore underestimate EVs [ , ]. Moreover, because NTA measures hydrodynamic diameter, it can result in an overestimation of EVs’ size [ , ]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) is another technique widely used to characterize BEVs’ size distribution and membrane properties. This technique measures fluctuations in scattered light caused by the Brownian motion of EVs in suspension and may differentiate vesicles based on their lipid membrane organization. By observing how the scattering intensity changes with temperature, DLS can reveal differences in vesicles produced under various environmental conditions, such as different growth temperatures, which is a sign of membrane remodeling. This makes DLS useful not only for size measurement but also for assessing vesicle membrane phase transitions and heterogeneity [ ]. However, DLS has limitations, including difficulty in resolving polydisperse samples and an inability to distinguish vesicles from aggregates [ ]. Therefore, it is often combined with complementary techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for morphological characterization and small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) for structural analysis [ – ]. This enables determination of vesicle size, concentration, and zeta potential. Tunable resistive pulse sensing (TRPS) is a highly sensitive and accurate method that analyzes vesicles at the single-particle level, providing detailed size distributions and particle counts. Notably, it has demonstrated superior performance compared to optical density measurements in studies of BEVs [ ]. The method requires careful calibration due to pore variability but is relatively fast, uses small sample volumes, and can analyze EVs directly in biological fluids without prior purification [ – ]. Flow cytometry, especially advanced forms like nano-flow cytometry (nFCM), is a highly sensitive and multiparametric method for characterizing BEVs. It enables single-particle analysis by detecting scattered light and fluorescence signals, allowing precise measurement of EV size, concentration, and surface markers. This technique can distinguish BEV subpopulations and analyze their phenotypes using fluorescent antibodies or dyes. While conventional flow cytometry struggles with the small size and low refractive index of EVs, nFCM and bead-based approaches improve detection sensitivity and specificity. This technique offers rapid, high-throughput quantification and phenotyping of BEVs in complex biological samples, making it valuable for studying BEV roles in host-microbe interactions and disease. However, careful optimization and controls are necessary to minimize background noise and ensure reproducibility [ , ]. Electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy (AFM) are established techniques for visualizing EVs. AFM provides images of surface topography and measures mechanical properties at the single-vesicle level. TEM offers high-resolution visualization of vesicle morphology, often revealing their characteristic cup-shaped appearance, and can be combined with immunolabeling to detect specific surface proteins [ , ]. Although TEM provides valuable morphological information, sample preparation may alter the native morphology of EVs. In this regard, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) can overcome the limitations of TEM by more effectively preserving the native morphology of vesicles [ , ]. For biochemical and molecular characterization of BEVs, researchers utilize various methods. Western blotting is a key technique for identifying protein markers associated with BEVs. In Gram-negative bacteria, LPS and OmpA are commonly assessed, whereas LTA serves as a characteristic marker in Gram-positive bacteria. Mass spectrometry provides a comprehensive proteomic profile of BEVs cargo, enabling the identification of specific BEV proteins and differentiation from potential contaminants [ , ].

## BEVs in CRC pathogenesis and therapy

## Role of BEVs in the pathogenesis of CRC (pro-tumorigenic)
The gut–liver, gut–brain, and gut–lung axes represent interconnected communication networks through which the gut microbiota and its metabolites influence systemic health. These axes are essential for maintaining homeostasis, modulating immunity, regulating metabolism, and shaping disease susceptibility via bidirectional signaling. A prominent example is the gut–liver axis, which is anatomically connected by the portal vein, enabling microbial products such as LPS, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and tryptophan derivatives to directly reach the liver [ , ]. Once there, these metabolites can trigger hepatic immune responses, ranging from inflammation to tolerance. The integrity of this system depends heavily on the gut’s mucosal and vascular barriers. Disruption of the gut vascular barrier (GVB) enhances microbial translocation and inflammatory signaling, thereby contributing to the development of liver diseases and promoting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ , ]. The gut–brain axis is a critical communication network linking the gut microbiota, the enteric nervous system (ENS), and the central nervous system (CNS). Signaling occurs through multiple pathways, most notably the vagus nerve, which conveys microbial and hormonal cues to the brain, thereby influencing stress responses. Microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and tryptophan catabolites play key roles by modulating neurotransmitter synthesis, gut hormone secretion, and immune activity, ultimately impacting mood, cognition, and systemic homeostasis [ , ]. Furthermore, the microbiota helps regulate the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, and its disruption, often stemming from gut dysbiosis, can lead to peripheral immune activation, causing neuroinflammation and implicating the axis in various neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, and psychiatric disorders [ , ]. The gut–lung axis functions as an interconnected regulatory system that links intestinal health to respiratory defense. Immune cells that are primed and educated in the gut actively migrate to the lungs, where they shape mucosal immunity and tolerance by altering cytokine profiles and antigen presentation. This systemic connection is also dependent on microbial metabolites that circulate from the gut to modulate inflammation. For example, gut-derived SCFAs are known to influence the function of alveolar macrophages, impacting the response to inhaled threats and changing susceptibility to respiratory infections. Consequently, perturbations to the gut environment, such as those caused by antibiotics or dietary changes, have direct clinical implications, indirectly influencing the progression of lung diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and the effectiveness of the immune response [ ]. CRC often metastasizes to the brain, lungs, and liver, but the liver is by far the most common site due to its unique anatomical connection: the portal vein provides a direct vascular route for circulating tumor cells from the colon. To establish a new tumor, CRC cells first adhere to the hepatic sinusoidal endothelium using molecules like integrins, selectins, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). Once attached, they extravasate into the liver tissue. Their subsequent growth is critically dependent on manipulating the hepatic microenvironment and evading immune attack, a process often involving specific signaling pathways like the Wnt/β-catenin route [ , ]. CRC tumor cells can reach the lungs through multiple routes: they may enter the circulation and bypass the liver, or they can travel directly from rectal tumors via the inferior vena cava. Successfully forming lung metastasis is a demanding process, requiring the cells to adapt to the pulmonary capillary environment, effectively adhere to endothelial cells, and survive the local immune response. Interestingly, the lung tropism of these metastases can be influenced by specific RAS signaling pathway alterations; however, cytogenetic studies indicate that these particular molecular changes are not as common in lung metastases as they are in CRC that has spread to the brain or liver [ , ]. Although brain metastasis from CRC is relatively uncommon, its incidence is rising in clinical practice. The ability of CRC cells to survive the harsh brain microenvironment is driven by key molecular changes, notably the amplification of insulin receptor substrate 2 (IRS2) and altered β-catenin signaling. To reach the brain, CRC cells must successfully cross the formidable blood–brain barrier (BBB), often utilizing CXCR4/CXCL12-mediated chemotaxis and adhesion to the endothelial lining. Once established, these cells adapt to the low-oxygen, nutrient-scarce environment by shifting their metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation and activating crucial survival pathways, including PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β -catenin. The presence of KRAS mutations and certain genetic alterations on chromosome 13q is a particularly strong predictor of a greater propensity for these brain metastases [ , ].
The involvement of BEVs in the pathogenesis of CRC is characterized by their ability to modulate inflammatory processes, induce DNA damage, alter metabolic pathways, and influence bacterial colonization. Long-lasting inflammation harms the lining of the intestines, fostering conditions that encourage tumor growth. TLRs activation by BEVs on host cells is a key step in the induction of chronic inflammation, subsequently activating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Figs. and ) (Table ) [ ]. Enriched levels of F.nucleatum-derived BEVs (FnEVs) are found in CRC tissues. These FnEVs promote the progression of tumors associated with colitis by activating the TLR4 and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which in turn elevates the expression of the inflammatory cytokines Interleukin-8 (IL-8) and CXCL1 [ ]. Moreover, FnEVs drove inflammation by promoting F4/80 + inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) + M1-like macrophages and elevating key cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and iNOS. They also caused barrier leakage through receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), a known regulator of chronic NF-κB signaling. Furthermore, FnEVs induce oxidative stress, triggering Akt/mTOR pathway activation and subsequent mitophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction in CRC cells [ , ]. FnEVs also promote glycolysis in CRC cells by upregulating Angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4), thereby creating a nutrient-rich environment that favors bacterial colonization. The study reveals a novel mechanism by which EVs from tumors prime the TME for pathogenic bacteria. These FnEVs carry the FomA adhesin, which they transfer to the surface of CRC cells via membrane fusion. The presence of FomA on CRC cells then enables F. nucleatum to adhere to itself (auto-aggregation) through interaction with FN1441 and establish colonies within the tumor. This forms a positive feedback loop that ultimately accelerates CRC progression in vivo and in vitro [ ].
 BEVs are capable of delivering bacterial toxins and virulence factors, leading to the stimulation of immune cells and the subsequent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8. For example, H.pylori-derived BEVs contain cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) and vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA), subsequently activate NF-κB and initiate inflammatory responses implicated in gastric cancer development. This inflammatory milieu is thought to promote tumor initiation and progression by establishing a supportive niche for neoplastic cells [ , ].
BEVs can also alter host cell metabolism to support tumor development. For instance, butyrate-producing bacteria like Ruminococcus flavefaciens release BEVs carrying metabolites that block histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity. While normal cells metabolize butyrate via β-oxidation, CRC cells predominantly use glycolysis. This metabolic shift leads to butyrate buildup and HDAC inhibition in CRC cells, a complex effect that can either suppress or promote cancer depending on the specific conditions. In some models, butyrate-induced HDAC inhibition reduces proliferation, angiogenesis, and invasion and can promote apoptosis through pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin modulation and downstream targets like p21, Bax, and other cell cycle regulators. In other contexts, especially with microenvironmental stress or chemoresistance, HDAC inhibition can contribute to therapeutic resistance or adaptive survival [ , ].
BEVs derived from the periodontal pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis can stimulate multiple TLRs, including TLR2, TLR4, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9. In contrast, BEVs from commensal Bacteroides species dampen inflammation by activating TLR2 and TLR4 to promote IL-10 production. However, microbial imbalances (dysbiosis) can disrupt this anti-inflammatory signaling, shifting the response toward pro-inflammatory outcomes [ , ].

## Therapeutic potential of BEVs (anti-tumorigenic)
The therapeutic potential of BEVs in CRC is increasingly recognized. Their multifaceted activity includes modulating the immune system, directly targeting cancer cells, and reshaping the TME to inhibit cancer progression [ ] (Figs. and ) (Table ). BEVs act as potent immune stimulators due to the presence of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including LPS and bacterial nucleic acids. These molecules are recognized by host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), leading to the activation of immune cells within the TME and fostering antitumor immune responses [ , ]. Researchers have also demonstrated a dose-dependent effect of OMV treatment on tumor growth in mice. A high dose of 3.52 × 109 OMVs led to complete remission in most mice bearing MB49 and EMT6 tumors. Lower doses of 8.80 × 108 and 2.20 × 108 OMVs inhibited MB49 tumor growth by 94% and 63%, respectively. Furthermore, OMV treatment significantly enhanced the presence of stem cell-like CD8 + T cells, including neoantigen-specific (SIINFEKL-Kb+) ones, within MC38-OVA tumors compared to controls [ ].
Researchers found that BEVs from Limosilactobacillus Johnsoni and Limosilactobacillus Mucosae reversed the detrimental changes caused by LPS, which included decreased villus length, reduced expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin), anti-inflammatory markers (Arg1, IL-10, CD163+ cells), and increased expression of gut inflammatory markers (iNOS, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α). Specifically, the EVs were shown to modulate gene expression in both macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells, notably restoring barrier integrity markers and reducing pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signals (Caspase-3, Bax) in vivo and in vitro [ ].

E. coli-derived OMVs have shown the ability to accumulate in CRC tumors, where they stimulate the production of antitumor cytokines like CXC-motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), resulting in tumor regression with minimal toxicity [ ].
Research suggests that BEVs, particularly OMVs, hold promise for use as effective anti-cancer agents in vivo and in vitro. For instance, in CT26 colon adenocarcinoma models, E. coli msbB−/− OMVs have induced complete tumor regression via IFN-γ-dependent mechanisms [ ].
The ability of BEVs to induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy), mediated through the Akt–PI3K–AMPK–mTOR signaling pathway, highlights another potential strategy for triggering cancer cell death. For example, BEVs derived from E. coli (strain A5922) have been shown to activate these processes in HT-29 colon cancer cells, resulting in a direct cytotoxic effect. This finding underscores the potential utility of BEVs as therapeutic agents or preventive systems in CRC [ ].
Probiotic BEVs are powerful modulators of the host immune system, actively promoting anti-tumor responses. Specifically, BEVs from Lactobacillus species have been shown to activate M1 macrophages and enhance the expression of CD8 + T cells and IFN-γ, both of which are critical components for a robust anti-cancer immune response [ , ]. Additionally, these BEVs can influence dendritic cells to produce Th1-type cytokines, further enhancing the body’s cellular defenses against tumors [ , ]. The protective effects of probiotics extend to their BEVs, which are vehicles for important proteins like p40 and p75. Upon delivery, these proteins activate epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling, conferring several benefits to intestinal epithelial cells, including resistance to apoptosis and enhanced integrity of their tight junctions. Strengthening of the intestinal barrier is important to maintain gut homeostasis and can indirectly influence cancer progression through its effects on inflammation [ ].
Resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in CRC cells is directly correlated with increased glycolysis, specifically through the upregulation of Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Kinase 2 (PDK2). Research demonstrates that Lactobacillus plantarum-derived BEVs effectively reverse this metabolic shift and circumvent 5-FU resistance by attenuating PDK2 expression via p53-p21-dependent signaling, ultimately leading to inhibited proliferation and apoptosis. This finding, supported by modifying PDK2 levels in both sensitive and resistant cell lines, establishes the PDK2 signaling pathway as a viable therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in CRC [ ].
Additionally, BEVs derived from Lactobacillus acidophilus and S. aureus have exhibited cross-species antitumor activity without notable toxicity [ , ]. Research indicates that BEVs isolated from the probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (ATCC 53103) can trigger apoptosis in HepG2 (liver cancer) cell lines in vitro [ ]. This process occurs through the activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways, characterized by the upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes such as Bax, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9, ultimately resulting in cell cycle arrest. The extent of this apoptotic effect is dose-dependent and may vary based on the specific cancer type being targeted [ , ].
Researchers used BEVs from Akkermansia muciniphila and purified Amuc_2172 in different tumor models to assess their role in tumorigenesis. A key part of the investigation involved evaluating the in vitro and in vivo immune activation caused by Amuc_2172, specifically its ability to stimulate CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). The mechanism of action was further explored by investigating Amuc_2172’s acetyltransferase activity and subsequent target genes. The Amuc_2172 enters colorectal cells via micropinocytosis and acetylates Lys14 on histone H3 (H3K14ac). This acetylation at the HSPA1A locus drives the transcription and secretion of HSP70 (heat-shock protein 70) by the cancer cells. High levels of secreted HSP70 then boost the immune activity of CTLs in vitro and in vivo [ ].

## Conflict findings
A key aspect of E. coli’s involvement in CRC is its paradoxical nature. Certain strains can be pathogenic drivers of the disease, while others show promise as therapeutic agents [ ]. Cytolethal Distending Toxins (CDTs) are secreted by a wide range of Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, Shigella, Actinomycetes, and Helicobacter. CDT has three subunits (CdtA, CdtB, and CdtC); the active subunit, CdtB, is analogous to DNase I and is responsible for causing damage to host DNA. E. coli-derived BEVs contain colibactin and CDTs. Colibactin initiates double-strand DNA breaks, while CDTs promote chromosomal instability by mimicking DNase I activity. This DNA damage triggers host cell cycle arrest and senescence. Furthermore, CDT from Campylobacter jejuni actively promotes colorectal tumorigenesis through two mechanisms: by damaging host cell DNA and by enhancing the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, which amplifies the aberrant WNT signaling pathway characteristic of CRC. The internalization of the essential active subunit, CdtB, is facilitated by the CdtA and CdtC subunits mediating the toxin’s interaction with the cell membrane [ ].
A key finding is that E. coli-derived BEVs trigger the upregulation of TLR2, TLR4, and TLR5 on intestinal epithelial cells, resulting in increased IL-8 secretion and enhanced bacterial internalization. Suppression of antitumor immunity by BEVs can occur through the induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [ ]. B. fragilis-derived BEVs, particularly those enriched with polysaccharide (PSA), have been shown to promote the development of IL-10-producing Tregs. This IL-10 production establishes an immune-tolerant environment that can hinder the effective clearance of cancer cells [ ].

## Potential of BEVs as a cancer vaccine
BEVs have been recognized over the past decade as a highly promising platform for cancer vaccine development, owing to several key advantages. Their notable ability to stimulate the immune system, their inherent safety due to a lack of proliferative potential, and their versatility in transporting and presenting tailored target antigens on their membranes are particularly noteworthy. These BEV-based cancer vaccines often incorporate genetic engineering to load or display foreign proteins, which then act as antigens to activate a robust immune response. This approach leverages the natural immunogenicity of BEVs while aiming to minimize potential side effects [ ]. Therefore, BEVs hold promise for development as effective immunotherapies or as innovative vaccine platforms targeting gastrointestinal malignancies [ , ]. In this regard, vaccination with BEVs from Clostridium perfringens, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Bacillus anthracis, M. tuberculosis, and S. aureus has consistently yielded positive outcomes. Specifically, in mouse models, these BEV vaccines have demonstrated efficacy in prolonging or improving survival rates following lethal infection challenges. Furthermore, they have proven effective in reducing bacterial load and attenuating the inflammatory response under sub-lethal conditions [ – ]. The potential of BEVs to elicit adaptive memory immune responses has been well demonstrated through the development of several BEV-based vaccines. Clinically approved vaccines targeting Neisseria meningitidis serogroup B are the most prominent examples [ , ]. The FDA-approved meningococcal OMV vaccine, Bexsero®, protects against invasive meningococcal disease caused by N. meningitidis serogroup B. Initially approved by the FDA on January 23, 2015, Bexsero® is indicated for active immunization in individuals between 10 and 25 years old. It stands out as the first broadly protective meningococcal serogroup B vaccine approved in the United States. Its efficacy is attributed to its composition: an aluminum hydroxide-adjuvanted vaccine containing three purified recombinant protein antigens (NadA, NHBA, and fHbp) derived from the N. meningitidis group B strain NZ 98/254, along with the PorA subtype P1.4 OMV antigen [ , ]. Although Bexsero® is not specifically indicated for cancer treatment, recent reviews on cancer vaccines highlight the importance of novel antigen delivery systems and immunostimulatory platforms. OMV-based vaccines like Bexsero® provide a model for bacterial vesicle-based immunotherapies that could be adapted for cancer vaccines due to their ability to induce robust T cell responses and modulate the immune microenvironment [ ](Fig. ).
 

## Potential of BEVs as CRC biomarkers
The potential of BEVs as biomarkers for CRC is gaining attention. Distinct microbial compositions of gut BEVs have been observed in CRC patients compared to controls. Specifically, BEVs from genera like Alistipes and Ruminococcus show differential levels linked to CRC progression and tumor site, making them useful for diagnosis and prognosis. When combined with clinical data, BEV profiling significantly boosts CRC detection performance [ ]. Proteins found on BEVs have also emerged as biomarkers for CRC detection and prognosis. Studies demonstrate that CRC patients display higher levels of fecal EV proteins CD147 and A33 than healthy controls. Other notable BEV proteins linked with CRC include ADAM10, CDH17, CPNE3, Galectin-4, GPC-1, HSP60, HSP70, KRT19, and SRC, with individual variability. These BEV proteins’ unique expression profiles make them strong candidates for developing innovative, non-invasive CRC diagnostic methods [ , ].
Evidence increasingly shows that circular RNAs (circRNAs) with abnormal expression in CRC cells facilitate oncogenic processes by repressing tumor suppressor gene expression, thus aiding tumor initiation, invasion, and metastasis. These circular RNAs are formed through a back-splicing process, generating a covalently closed loop that lacks traditional 5’ and 3’ ends. This structural feature confers remarkable stability on circRNAs, contributing to their resistance against exonuclease degradation compared to linear forms [ , ]. For example, plasma EVs from CRC patients contain significantly increased levels of hsa_circ_0003270 (circGAPVD1), a circRNA linked to the circGAPVD1-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network, which correlates with TNM staging and offers promise as both a diagnostic and prognostic indicator [ ]. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are defined as transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides without protein-coding capacity. CRNDE-h, a key lncRNA, is significantly upregulated in the serum EVs from CRC patients and is strongly linked to both regional lymph node and distant metastases. It demonstrates a sensitivity of 70.3%, a specificity of 94.4%, and an area under the ROC curve of 0.892, exceeding traditional markers such as CEA. Meanwhile, several lncRNAs, including H19, HOTTIP, and HULC, are notably downregulated in CRC serum EVs. Reduced levels of HOTTIP, in particular, show potential as a predictive biomarker, highlighting the diagnostic and prognostic importance of lncRNAs in CRC [ , ]. Researchers investigated the role of ribosomal protein L22-like 1 (RPL22L1) by silencing RPL22L1 in human CRC cell lines and assessing its effects on cell growth, migration, the cell cycle, and apoptosis. The impact of RPL22L1 knockdown on tumor growth was further confirmed through in vivo xenograft models. Rescue experiments provided additional support, revealing that the mTOR pathway mediates RPL22L1’s effect on promoting cell cycle progression. Furthermore, tumor-bearing mice with reduced RPL22L1 expression showed inhibited CRC growth. These results suggest that RPL22L1 could serve as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target, offering new avenues for cancer treatment development.

## Synthetic and engineered BEVs
Surface modification of OMVs with calcium phosphate to mitigate toxicity has enabled effective TME reprogramming and enhanced antitumor efficacy in preclinical investigations [ ]. Furthermore, combining engineered BEVs with therapies like chemotherapy, gene therapy, or photothermal therapy can boost antitumor effectiveness. Their small size facilitates passive tumor targeting, thereby enhancing the efficiency of treatment delivery [ ]. Through bioengineering techniques, these vesicles can be transformed into novel drug delivery vehicles for cancer therapy [ , ]. Engineered BEVs can deliver nucleic acids, such as siRNA, offering a protected pathway to silence key cancer-related genes within target cells, enhancing RNA stability and bioactivity. Furthermore, like other nanomaterials, BEVs can be functionalized to carry photothermal agents, enabling targeted destruction of cancer cells upon light irradiation [ – ].

E. coli-derived BEVs are popular due to their easy genetic modification. Engineered E. coli BEVs, in which endotoxin-associated genes have been removed, have demonstrated promising anti-cancer activity by safely eliciting immune responses. LPS modification on these BEVs, achieved by deleting the msbB gene, yields OMVs with reduced endotoxin activity. This modification minimizes the risk of excessive cytokine secretion while preserving their antitumor efficacy, as shown by concentration-dependent tumor regression in murine colon adenocarcinoma models [ , ].
A promising new approach involves engineering BEVs derived from E. coli and N. meningitidis to display tumor-specific antigens such as FAT1 and Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor Variant III (EGFRvIII). These antigens are fused to surface proteins like Nm-fHbp. Preclinical research has demonstrated that this method effectively induces the production of tumor-targeting antibodies and inhibits tumor growth, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy [ , ]. High-pH treatment and sonication are effective methods for converting E. coli-derived OMVs into Synthetic Bacterial Vesicles (SyBVs). These SyBVs exhibit reduced RNA and DNA content, thereby significantly lowering the risk of activating toll-like receptors TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9. Importantly, SyBVs retain their adjuvant properties and can work synergistically with tumor-derived EVs to inhibit melanoma growth [ ]. Moreover, BEVs can be used to cloak synthetic nanoparticles like PLGA and gold nanoparticles, thereby improving their stability and biodistribution. Notably, H. pylori-derived OMVs have shown enhanced targeting of gastric epithelial cells, while E. coli-derived OMVs have demonstrated the capacity to enhance dendritic cell activation [ ].
The immunomodulatory potential of BEVs can be further enhanced through engineering strategies, such as the expression of TLR agonists like flagellin. Crucially, modified BEVs contribute to elevated levels of IFN-γ and IL-17, cytokines essential for mounting a robust antitumor immune response. A clear illustration of this is the observation that E. coli-derived OMVs can induce IFN-γ-dependent tumor regression in murine models [ , ]. To enhance the effectiveness of BEV-based cancer vaccines, some designs include additional proteins like listeriolysin O, which facilitates the antigen presentation to the immune system. Others express immune modulators like SIRPα and GM-CSF to promote phagocytosis by immune cells and activate antigen-presenting cells [ , ]. In preclinical studies using murine models of colon and melanoma cancers, these vaccines have demonstrated tumor growth reduction by inducing both trained innate immunity and robust T-cell responses [ , ].

## BEVs reporting standards
Transparent reporting is essential to enhance the reproducibility and reliability of EV research, including BEV studies, to facilitate progress toward clinical applications [ ]. Although no specific guidelines currently exist for BEVs, general recommendations from the MISEV guidelines and the “Cell culture-conditioned medium-derived extracellular vesicles (CCM-EVs)” task force of the Rigor and Standardization Subcommittee of ISEV can provide valuable guidance [ , , ]. In this regard, given the diversity of BEV sources, including in vitro mono- or polymicrobial cultures, body fluids, or feces, detailed reporting of the source is essential [ ]. Moreover, bacterial growth parameters such as media composition, culture temperature and pH, oxygenation, culture format (e.g., standing, shaking, or bioreactor), and growth phase at the time of harvest should be carefully documented [ , ]. For biofluids, the collection, pre-processing, storage, and transport conditions of samples can influence bacterial richness and the recovery of BEVs and therefore should be reported in detail [ ]. Although ISEV recommends minimizing storage before EVs separation or concentration, particularly when samples remain unfiltered [ ], all media storage conditions, including temperature and filtration, should be thoroughly documented. Since different isolation approaches can yield BEVs with varying purity or enrich specific BEV subpopulations, which may affect downstream analyses, all details of isolation methods should be reported [ , ]. For example, when ultracentrifugation is used for BEV isolation, the ISEV recommends documenting parameters such as rotor type, tube type, centrifugation time, speed, temperature, sample volume, and brake setting [ ]. Similar recommendations should also be applied to other isolation methods. Once BEVs are isolated, characterization is essential to confirm their presence, identify non-EV components, and evaluate the impact of preanalytical variables on BEVs [ ]. In this context, quantitative measures of BEV sources (e.g., media or biofluid volume, bacterial counts, or fecal mass) and BEVs themselves (e.g., protein content, lipid content, or particle number) should be provided. Furthermore, BEV characterization ideally extends beyond size and macromolecular content, incorporating universal markers such as LPS for Gram-negative bacteria and LTA for Gram-positive bacteria, with appropriate controls to account for non-vesicular LPS contamination [ ]. Additional considerations include BEVs’ storage conditions, such as storage buffer, temperature, storage duration, use of cryoprotectants, number of freeze–thaw cycles, and thawing method, all of which should be reported in detail [ ]. Finally, submission of experimental details to the EV-TRACK database (http://evtrack.org/) has also been recommended by ISEV to promote transparency, facilitate reproducibility, and enable cross-study comparisons [ , ]. 

## Limitations and further directions
 Despite the potential of BEVs as therapeutics for CRC, several challenges must be addressed before clinical translation. BEVs represent a heterogeneous population, and their physicochemical and functional characteristics can be influenced by multiple factors, including the bacterial strain, environmental conditions, and growth media composition. Since different BEV isolation methods yield heterogeneous populations that may vary in both content and function, the standardization of isolation techniques is crucial [ , ]. The operational definitions of OMVs, OIMVs, EOMVs, and EOIMVs remain heavily dependent on specific laboratory methods and the bacterial species under study. Because isolation techniques often fail to distinguish between these biogenesis pathways, most “OMV” preparations are in fact heterogeneous mixtures of various vesicle subtypes. This structural diversity poses a significant hurdle for researchers; when cytoplasmic markers like DNA or internal proteins are detected, it is difficult to determine whether they were actively packaged into a complex OIMV or are simply contaminants from cell lysis. Without clearer differentiation, functional data attributed to these vesicles remains challenging to interpret with precision [ , ]. While BEVs hold considerable promise as cancer therapeutics owing to their inherent immune-stimulatory properties and versatility as drug carriers, their clinical translation remains constrained by safety concerns and targeting inefficiencies [ , ]. The safety profile of BEVs is still under active investigation, with emerging data raising important concerns. For instance, BEVs derived from methicillin-resistant S. aureus have been shown to trigger severe IgE-mediated systemic hypersensitivity and anaphylactic shock in animal models [ , ]. Additionally, BEVs originating from Pseudomonas aeruginosa and E. coli can induce apoptosis and inflammation through mitochondrial disruption, suggesting an inherent cytotoxic potential in non-cancerous cells [ , ]. 
One of the most serious safety concerns associated with BEVs is the natural presence of PAMPs, such as LPS, that are present on OMVs from Gram-negative bacteria. LPS has the potential to elicit serious immunogenic responses, such as febrile reactions and endotoxic shock, which restrict the safe clinical application of BEVs. In addition, BEVs may contain bacterial virulence factors and toxins that can exacerbate infections or cause cytotoxicity [ ]. Because different subpopulations of BEVs may contain varying quantities of these components, their heterogeneity poses significant challenges.
Rapid clearance of BEVs by the mononuclear phagocytic system and their inefficient delivery to tumor sites represent major challenges. Similar to other nanoparticles, only a small fraction of administered BEVs reaches the tumor. Consequently, enhancing tumor-targeting efficiency remains a critical priority. Furthermore, although BEVs can serve as potent immunostimulants in cancer therapy, their immunogenicity may also provoke undesired systemic inflammation or adverse immune responses. Therefore, the key lies in fine-tuning their properties to maximize therapeutic immune activation while minimizing harmful inflammatory effects [ , ]. 

## Conclusion
 In CRC research, BEVs perform essential functions, notably in the remodeling of the TME and immune modulation. Furthermore, they are gaining prominence as potential biomarkers and novel therapeutic delivery platforms. Our survey and search in the Clinical Trials database (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06395246) only revealed a single recruiting study, which aims to assess the impact of two distinct dietary protocols, Daily Caloric Restriction (DCR) and the combined Early Time-Restricted Feeding and DCR (eTRE), on metabolic health in obese individuals. Nevertheless, BEVs represent a versatile and highly promising strategy for both direct therapeutic action and as smart carriers in CRC therapy. They also hold considerable potential as biomarkers. However, further studies are required to overcome challenges related to safety, off-target effects, the standardization of isolation methods, and immunogenicity, as well as to accelerate their clinical translation.
