## Abstract

## Introduction:
Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), is a common cause of death in humans worldwide. The slow growth rate of Mtb (~20 hours) makes progress in research slow and diagnosis difficult.

## Areas covered:
Imaging technologies that can quantify viability and infectious load can have a profound impact on progress toward new therapeutics and vaccines and allow rapid diagnosis. Although imaging can quantify bacterial loads throughout an entire animal in real-time, sensitivity is a key limitation. Fluorescent and bioluminescent recombinant strains have been used within the TB field and in other bacteria but have a threshold of ~103 bacteria in mice. Reporter enzyme fluorescence (REF) is a new and very sensitive Mtb imaging technology that uses BlaC, a highly specific surface-localized β-lactamase, in combination with fluorogenic or bioluminescent probes. As such, REF can reduce the threshold to 10–100 bacteria in vivo and detect 10 bacteria within 10 minutes in vitro.

## Expert opinion:
Recombinant reporter systems for imaging bacteria should continue to improve and may reach similar thresholds, but at present, REF remains the most sensitive approach. Furthermore, new more sensitive probes can be readily developed, suggesting that REF will ultimately allow detection of single bacteria in an infected host.

Keywords: Optical imaging, fluorescence, bioluminescence, bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, respiratory infection, animal models, β-lactamase

## Introduction
Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), is one of the oldest diseases known to mankind, dating back to ancient civilizations. Symptoms of active TB include chronic cough, hemoptysis (bloody sputum), fever, fatigue, weight loss, and night sweats. Despite significant scientific breakthroughs, TB continues to be a leading infectious agent. While reported cases dropped after the advent of antibiotics and improved living conditions, the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic, the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains, and the COVID-19 pandemic have contributed to increasing case numbers and deaths. TB remains the most frequent cause of death in humans due to infectious diseases, with 10.8 million cases reported in 2023, including 8.2 million newly diagnosed cases of TB. Despite recent increases in cases, TB-associated deaths have decreased to 1.25 million from 1.34 million before the COVID-19 pandemic [ ]. Drug-resistant Mtb strains pose great challenge since mortality has remained somewhat constant and has not shown any signs of decrease. Mtb is primarily a respiratory pathogen that causes extrapulmonary infection in ~16% of cases [ , ]. In these cases, it is possible to have pulmonary infection at the same time as extrapulmonary infection. Extrapulmonary TB commonly affects the cervical lymph nodes (lymphadenitis) but can also occur in the spine (spinal TB), bones and joints, pleura, gastrointestinal tract, and central nervous system (CNS), where it causes TB meningitis, encephalitis, or presents as an abscess or tuberculoma. TB in the CNS is particularly challenging as it has nonspecific symptoms and can mimic other forms of meningitis [ ]. Interestingly, Mtb can become dormant or latent, observed in the majority of cases (~90%), is typically asymptomatic and often remains undetected until it becomes active or reactivates [ ]. While recent studies have advanced our understanding of TB, the underlying mechanisms of pathogenesis remain poorly understood. A deeper understanding of pathogenic mechanisms would facilitate the development of more effective treatments, vaccines, and diagnostics for TB.
Detection of Mtb is vital for limiting the spread of TB and increasing the efficiency of therapeutics against the disease. According to the Center for Disease Control (CDC), TB diagnosis can be done via blood or skin testing, chest radiography, and/or bacteriological examination. While these methods are somewhat effective, they often require extensive training, are relatively time-consuming, and can be costly. The slow growth rate of Mtb, around 16–24 hours under optimal conditions, is a major reason why diagnosis normally involves multiple tests and usually takes weeks to months to be finalized [ ]. Blood and skin tests are often time-consuming and lack sensitivity, while culture-based diagnostics take several months and frequently become contaminated during growth. Non-culture-based techniques can include sputum or biopsy smear microscopy, nucleic acid amplification, IFN-gamma release assays, and even mass spectrometry. Nucleic acid amplification provides high specificity but requires specialized facilities and is expensive. Due to the length of time required for a definitive diagnosis and the low sensitivity of diagnostic tests other than culture, treatment is often delayed, leading to the increased risk of spread, hospitalization, and mortality [ ]. As such, rapid and accurate diagnostics for TB are crucial to combat the continued spread of this disease.
Diagnostic strategies include a number of new approaches, including genomic assays (e.g. GeneXpert) [ , ], lateral flow assays (LFAs), nanoparticle-based assays, florescent staining methods, and optical technologies [ ]. LFAs using luminescent upconverting reporter particles (UCPs) have been designed to detect multiple cytokines and chemokines, IFN-γ, various Interleukins (ILs), C-C motif ligand 4 (CCL4), and C-X-C motif ligands 9 and 10 (CXCL9, CXCL10) [ ]. Nanoparticles have also been utilized as a method for directly detecting Mtb, rather than just the host response. Quantum dots (QDs) and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have been used to diagnose along with specific oligonucleotides [ ]. These have been utilized to target Early Secretory Antigenic Target 6 (ESAT-6), creating a highly specific and sensitive sandwich-format fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) biosensor that rapidly distinguishes Mtb from Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) strains [ ]. A study designed a fluorogenic trehalose analog that specifically detected mycobacteria and other corynebacteria by a metabolic conversion of DMN-Tre to trehalose monomycolate followed by incorporation into the mycomembrane of Actinobacteria, causing an increase in fluorescence of greater than 700-fold [ ]. Optical technologies have become a valuable tool for tracking infectious agents, particularly those using fluorescent and bioluminescent probes, allowing researchers to visualize and track infections in vivo and in real time.
Optical imaging has been widely used in brain cancer studies, growth monitoring of tumors, neurodegenerative diseases, atherosclerosis, myocardial infarctions, the effects of chemotherapy on tumor size, and respiratory infections such as TB [ , ]. Some of the optical-based diagnostics include plasmonic-based photonics, silicon photonics, photonic microring sensors, microfluidic-based photonics, electrochemiluminescence platforms, smart imaging, and FRET-based platforms [ ]. These technologies generally involve a light source and detector that convert optical signals into measurable data, indicating the presence of pathogens or specific proteins. Combining whole-body imaging with fluorescent or bioluminescent probes for recombinant strains of Mtb can yield insight into the mechanisms of infection within the body along with disease progression, all without the need to sacrifice animals. Clearly, optical imaging in real time would have the capacity to observe extrapulmonary spread within infected animals as well as measurement of the bacterial load within specific organs. The noninvasive and nondestructive nature of optical imaging affords the ability to monitor the same animal and bacteria over time, thus reducing animal-to-animal variability and the number of animals required for individual experiments. However, the primary limitation remains the sensitivity of optical imaging technologies. In order for optical imaging technologies to greatly benefit our understanding of Mtb biology within the host, a threshold of 1–10 bacteria in the lungs is needed since that is the infectious dose. Without this threshold, optical imaging will miss many of the early events during disease and may not pick up upon early organ/tissue infection upon extrapulmonary spread.
Overall, there is an urgent need for rapid, affordable diagnostic platforms capable of detecting and quantifying Mtb in real time, impacting studies on virulence, therapeutics, and vaccine efficacy [ ], but ultimately diagnosis directly within infected individuals in a noninvasive manner. Furthermore, early and accurate detection of drug-resistant Mtb, which could be accomplished through optical imaging technologies, is critical to preventing spread from outbreaks and the creation of even more drug-resistant cases/strains as well as allowing rapid evaluation of therapeutic outcomes during treatment.

## Strategies for sensitive imaging of TB
The use of noninvasive imaging technologies for whole-body studies is invaluable to the understanding of pathogenesis, evaluation of new drugs, and the development of fluorescent and bioluminescent probes in real time and in various models [ , , ]. Whole-body imaging has two main configurations: epi-illumination and trans-illumination. Epi-illumination is configured with the light source on the same side as the detector, while trans-illumination has it placed on the opposite side from the detector. Trans-illumination is more complex, but results in a more accurate image due to decreases in skin autofluorescence and better tissue volume sampling [ , , ]. With respect to TB, both trans- and epi-illumination can help reduce the cost of development for treatments and vaccines, often by reducing the number of animals needed and the analysis time needed for experiments [ ].
One way to take advantage of epi- and trans-illumination is through the use of optical imaging systems. Optical imaging system can be simply composed of a sensitive charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, an optical-filter system, illumination facility for fluorescence imaging, a macro-lens in combination with a computer-controlled object stage, a light-tight imaging chamber, and software for imaging and controlling the device. Relevant characteristics from the interaction between light and tissue properties should be considered in imaging resolution. This applies especially to tissue thickness as excited light decreases exponentially as depth increases, and shorter wavelengths are highly absorbed by hemoglobin and other cells, resulting in significant autofluorescence.
Whole body imaging of animal models is commonly accomplished within vivo imaging systems (IVIS) that allow collection of photons from bioluminescence or fluorescence at different wavelengths. Recently, our group has been working on improvements to IVIS systems for fluorescence that use delivery of light into the animal from a mucous membrane and collection of light that is returned in with a CCD camera. This same system could be used to collect light from bioluminescent sources, as well. This system is composed of a microendoscope (fiber bundle) that, when in contact with the trachea (or lungs) of an infected animal that has been given a fluorogenic substrate or infected with a fluorescent reporter bacterial strain [ , – ]. This approach has shown the potential to improve thresholds of detection in the lungs but could also be used to improve detection in other organs that have accessible mucous membranes, such as the eyes or intestine. Microendoscopy overcomes a major limitation of whole-body imaging, since the thickness of tissue leads to low sensitivity for optical targets located deep within the body [ ]. The flexibility of the fiber allows for its placement in different anatomical regions, enabling illumination of areas inaccessible by conventional methods, such as the lungs and stomach. This setup allows light to be delivered directly to targeted areas, facilitating the detection of fluorescent or bioluminescent sources [ ]. It also reduces the need for invasive procedures, such as diagnostic exams and surgeries, and minimizes the number of animals required for a study by allowing multiple evaluations within a single subject [ ] A microendoscopic fiber has been developed to illuminate mouse lungs for the detection of Mtb substrates, coupled with the IVIS for whole-body imaging using both internal and external light sources ( ) [ , , , ].

## Fluorescence imaging in Mtb
Fluorescence-based techniques are powerful for detection in a variety of different studies, including using fluorescent molecules and culturing of cells and bacteria. Traditional fluorescence microscopy for in vivo studies or disease progression and efficacy of treatments or vaccines requires sacrificing animals [ ]. Developing fluorescence-based techniques to detect Mtb can be approached via recombinant genes or fluorogenic probes for both in vitro and in vivo work. One approach to making these techniques more efficient is optimization of emission, ranging from 500 to 700 nm, to enhance diagnostic sensitivity [ , , ]. An example of this is a tdTomato reporter, which is widely used due to its red fluorescence emission and high photostability, showing minimal photobleaching during imaging [ ]. Additionally, the use of other fluorescent proteins such as GFP are less ideal due to their inability to penetrate mammalian tissues, leading to very high thresholds of detection in the range of 108 bacteria or only near the surface, such as during skin infections. In contrast, near infrared fluorescent probes allow in vivo work and detection thresholds of 103-104 bacteria. A summary of many fluorescent and bioluminescent systems that have been used to detect Mtb is shown in . In vitro and in vivo limits of detection and improvement in signal levels with different detection methods as compared to conventional imaging with GFP have been examined in many cases.
 Previous studies in Mtb evaluated eGFP, mCherry, mPlum, Katushka, mKate, mKeima and tdTomato fluorescent reporter genes [ – ] to evaluate the impacts of longer excitation wavelengths on detection under different conditions [ , ]. BCG expressing GFP could be detected only at 107 colony forming units (CFU), while tdTomato could be detected around 105 CFU, confirming that longer wavelengths display better tissue penetration and reduced scattering [ , ]. Use of a fiber bundle for both illumination and collection of signal could further improve thresholds of detection since it can transmit light directly into the lung using the mucous membrane as a fiber optic system, dramatically enhancing sensitivity for fluorescence detection [ ]. The fiber microendoscope can detect tdTomato with a resolution of 4 μm and a 750 μm diameter field-of-view with a threshold of detection of 104 CFU in subcutaneous samples and 107 CFU in lung samples in this way [ ]. Another fiber bundle used a 0.66 mm outer diameter fiber bundle, consisting of 10,000 individual optical fibers with 3 μm core-to-core spacing, inserted through an intratracheal catheter until it reached tracheal wall for imaging via an IVIS [ ]. The IVIS can be used to guide proper fiber positioning, enabling a threshold of detection of 103 CFU with tdTomato [ , ]. Intravital illumination, in combination with trans-illumination, reduces lung autofluorescence is only minimally invasive [ ].
Optical imaging using only recombinant strains encounter physiological limitations that impact the limit of detection, including oxygen levels and ATP levels within the bacteria, but also metabolic load from high levels of protein overproduction and potential toxicity of the reporter protein for the bacteria. Each of these issues can impact virulence when conducting pathogenesis studies, potentially confounding data obtained. It is important to explore different imaging strategies to identify those that overcome these and other challenges associated with the approach. Heart and blood absorption significantly impact fluorescence detection, along with factors such as the position of the mouse, the wavelengths chosen, and asymmetry between the ventral and dorsal sides. Intravital-illumination addresses the issue of the excitation light having to pass through other organs, reducing light loss to surrounding tissues. A divergence angle of 40 degrees for the fiber’s internal illumination source and source locations at 0.7 mm and 1.7 mm from the tracheal bifurcation was selected as input parameters for optimizing an optical model [ ]. The resulting model confirms the experimental observations that efficient illumination and collection of the resulting fluorescent signal emitted can be accomplished using a fiber bundle to transmit and collect light via the mucous membranes of the lung.

## Bioluminescence imaging
Bioluminescence and chemiluminescence imaging approaches are widely used in the development of therapeutics and diagnostics. Bioluminescence is emission of visible light due to enzymatic reactions ( ), often involving the catalysis of luciferin by luciferase [ ]. One of the first bioluminescence systems used for imaging bacteria was the bacterial bioluminescence reporter system, based on expressing the bacterial luxCDABE operon isolated from Photorhabdus luminescens [ ], which encodes both the substrate synthesis genes and a heterodimeric luciferase [ , ]. Thus, bacterial bioluminescence does not require delivery of an exogenous substrate, since the luxCDE genes code for synthesis of the necessary substrate to produce light. In bacteria with bioluminescence, LuxAB encodes the heterodimeric luciferase which catalyzes the monooxygenation of aliphatic aldehydes into their corresponding acids along with the redox cofactor FMNH2 [ , ]. Due to this mechanism of light production, bacterial viability usually correlates very well with bioluminescence. In some cases, cell density can regulate luciferase-gene expression via quorum sensing [ ]. Salmonella expressing the bacterial bioluminescence operon was the first to demonstrate noninvasive optical imaging of infections [ ]. An advantage of bioluminescence over fluorescence is that there is very little background as compared to the background from autofluorescence in mammalian tissues observed with fluorescent imaging. Bioluminescence is also normally dependent upon ATP for light production, resulting in a rapid loss of signal upon cell death, making bioluminescence a good correlate of viability.
 Two primary luciferases have been used in Mtb, click beetle red luciferase (CBRlux) and firefly luciferase (FFlux) [ ]. Viability of the bacteria by CFU correlates well with bioluminescence. Expression of these bioluminescence constructs did not appear to alter growth rate under conditions that have been examined. Luminescence can be measured intracellularly within macrophages and in infected mice, resulting in a threshold 103 CFU for CBRlux and 104 for FFlux. Imaging pulmonary infections is more difficult due to light scattering and tissue depth, where both systems were able to detect 106 CFU, resulting in 10-fold brighter signal for CBRlux as compared to FFlux. Furthermore, since these luciferases produce luminescence at somewhat different, but overlapping, wavelengths, they can be simultaneously quantified in mixed infections, offering the potential to examine mutant and wild type bacteria in the same animal. These mixed infections may include drug-susceptible, wild type bacteria, drug-resistant bacteria, or mutants of specific genes of interest that may be understudied or understood poorly.

## Reporter enzyme fluorescence (REF)
The use of fluorescent reporter genes and/or enzymes with fluorescent substrates allows for the use of effective optical imaging both in vitro and in vivo. One such system that can be utilized for detection of Mtb is the REF system ( ). REF is a strategy for detecting Mtb using BlaC, a class A β-lactamase (type 2b) constitutively expressed by Mtb encoded by the blaC gene with very robust catalytic activity [ ]. β-lactamases cleave β-lactam antibiotics by hydrolyzing the amide group of the β-lactam ring. Mtb is resistant β-lactam antibiotics due to expression of the blaC gene. It has been shown that blaC is the only gene in the Mtb genome that encodes significant resistance to β-lactamases [ ]. Mutating BlaC in H37Rv makes the strain susceptible to β-lactam activity [ , , ]. The Mtb BlaC active site is significantly larger than other β-lactamases [ ]. This is due to a rearrangement of conserved side chains, the insertion of four residues in the active site, and several amino acid substitutions in the active site [ ]. Furthermore, there is increased flexibility of Mtb BlaC due to the R164A mutation compared to other class A β-lactamases such as TEM-1, the β-lactamase in E. coli [ ]. These differences allow for the design of fluorogenic probes with high specificity to BlaC over other β-lactamases, including TEM-1, including those with 120,000 times the specificity [ , ]. The custom-designed substrates CDC-OMe, CDG-OMe and CDG3 ( ) are specifically cleaved by BlaC, but not by β-lactamases present in other bacterial species [ , ]. The REF test utilizes BlaC, thus requiring a viable bacterium for translocation of the enzyme [ , ]. Since REF measures catalytic activity of BlaC, which is dependent upon ATP [ , , ], is a surface localized, constitutive expressed enzyme that is highly conserved in all Mtb strains examined to date, and displays very high activity, REF performs more sensitively for diagnosis of TB than conventional molecular- and microscopy-based methods [ , ]. Although the majority of probes for REF have been fluorogenic, caged luciferin probes have also been developed ( ) that release luciferin upon lactam cleavage and produce light in the presence of exogenous luciferase expressed by transgenic mice or within the bacteria themselves. Recombinant expression of luciferase within the bacteria provides the most localized signal at the site of infection when a caged luciferin probe is used [ ]. It should be noted that these methods may be more useful for active TB than for latent TB, as latent TB has slower metabolism and relatively minimal activity while dormant. There are a variety of technologies used to test latent TB, including tuberculin skin tests, RNA sequencing, proteomics, interferon-gamma release assays, cytokine detection, and computed tomography [ , ]. BlaC has been shown to be a valuable biomarker for the presence of viable Mtb during infections in humans and animals [ , , – , ] and its continued development for REF imaging with new probes should continue to improve sensitivity of detection during infection.

## Fluorogenic probes for rapid point-of-care diagnosis of TB
There have been a variety of fluorogenic probes developed to allow detection of Mtb in vitro using REF, including the CDC and CDG family of probes that are cleaved by BlaC [ , ]. In order for a TB diagnostic test to be successful at the point-of-care substrate, the test must be ASSURED: affordable, sensitive, specific, user friendly, rapid, robust, equipment free, and deliverable to users [ ]. The CDC and CDG probes use cephalosporin as a backbone linked to either a blue (CDC) or green (CDG) fluorophore ( ). Since BlaC shows greater flexibility in the substrate-specificity loop, methoxy was substituted onto the lactam ring to produce a substrate that is more specific for BlaC over other β-lactamases, yielding CDC-OMe. Cleavage of CDC-OMe by BlaC produces umbelliferon that emits a strong blue fluorescence when excited at 400 nm [ ]. Enzyme kinetics studies revealed that BlaC has over 1,000-fold higher catalytic efficiency, 2.1x104 s−1M−1, with CDC-OMe than TEM-1, 16 s−1M−1 [ ]. Additional probes have been constructed from this backbone with slightly higher catalytic efficiency but did not have the same specificity for BlaC over TEM-1, suggesting that CDC-OMe would perform the best for specificity in the presence of other β-lactamases [ ]. Due to the high background from excitation and emission fluorescence of umbelliferone, this fluorophore was replaced with Tokyo Green, yielding the probe CDG-OMe ( ). Enzyme kinetics for CDG-OMe found a catalytic efficiency of 2.3x105 s−1M−1 for BlaC as compared to 33 s−1M−1 for TEM-1, a nearly 7000-fold specificity [ ]. CDG-OMe proved able to detect Mtb with as few as 10 CFU, proving high sensitivity in addition to specificity [ ].
Since these studies suggest the flexibility of the BlaC active site can be used to develop new probes with improved specificity, a cyclopropyl group was added in close proximity to the methoxy group on the lactam ring, yielding CDG3 ( ). The cyclopropyl group improved specificity, with a 120,000-fold higher catalytic efficiency for BlaC, 2.4 × 105 s−1M−1, as compared to 2 s−1M−1 for TEM-1 [ ]. CDG-OMe generates a 218-fold increase in fluorescence in the presence of BlaC, while CDG3 generates a 214-fold increase [ , ]. Based on this data, CDG3 has improved selectivity and sensitivity, making it a nearly ideal probe for detection of Mtb. CDG3 is also very stable, with a spontaneous degradation rate of only 1 × 10−7 s−1 while also being able to detect 10 CFU of Mtb in sputum [ ]. Strategies for CDG3 stabilization have been explored to allow transport of the reagent to the remote sites where diagnosis of Mtb would have the greatest impact [ ]. The addition of lactose as an excipient increases stability without affecting the overall performance of the probe, allowing a lyophilized probe to be used in REF assays [ ]. REF using CDG3 as a fluorogenic substrate for Mtb detection in sputum is extremely sensitive, approaching or exceeding that of culture methods, currently considered the most sensitive diagnostic approach. The substrate (ex: 493 nm and em: 535 nm) proved to be 10,000-fold more specific for BlaC over other β-lactamases and exhibits sensitive detection of 10 CFU in human sputum having a good correlation with change in fluorescence, 1000-fold better than acid fast staining, the most common diagnostic approach used today [ , ].
Sputum based point-of-care diagnosis of patients with REF imaging is now achievable due to the high selectivity and specificity of the substrates that have been developed. Ongoing studies are focused on optimization of methods to ensure stability of the REF diagnosis system for transport to remote sites where TB is most prevalent and a point-of-care assay would have the greatest impact. The CDG3 REF substrate has been validated in a laboratory based assay using 160 sputum samples from suspected TB patients from different countries, resulting in a sensitivity of 89% for smear-positive, culture positive samples and a specificity of 82% [ ]. Further studies are geared toward development of a low-cost, portable, battery operated, LED fluorescent system based that can be used to detect fluorescence within a freshly expectorated patient sputum sample to quantify the infection on site. The same sputum-based system has the potential to be adapted to urinary and stool samples by modifying or supplementing new reagents to enhance sensitivity and specificity for Mtb detection. Even lymph node aspirates and similar diagnostic samples could be directly analyzed in a similar fashion but may require a slightly modified reagent system or fluorogenic substrate to better avoid the hemoglobin wavelengths that may interfere with detection of the CDG3 substrate.

## Probes for in vivo BlaC detection
Probes for REF imaging have been designed based off the CDG and CDC family of probes specifically for efficient detection of Mtb in vivo [ , ]. These probes, designated CNIR family probes, have emission in the near infra-red fluorescence spectrum, a eukaryotic cell transport domain, and the cephalosporin backbone from the previous probes ( ). The CNIR probes have been examined with the TEM-1 and BlaC enzymes to ensure that they function well for broad β-lactamase detection [ , , , , ]. Using REF for detection of Mtb in vivo with the CNIR series of probes, signal is dependent upon the PK/PD profile of the probe. CNIR signal is controlled by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) where cleavage of the β-lactam ring separates the near infrared fluorophore from a quencher, yielding fluorescence upon excitation at long wavelengths that can penetrate tissues efficiently [ , ]. Four main compounds were initially synthesized and tested, including CNIR5 ( ), and tested for detection of Mtb within J774A.1 murine macrophages. Quantification of bacteria when signal is present yielded a threshold of detection between 1 × 105 and 5 × 105 bacteria in the lungs [ ].
 The CNIR5 probe (Cy5.5-based) has an emission wavelength in the range of 690 nm, which is sufficiently long to detect the presence of the molecule within mice. CNIR5 resulted in the lowest threshold of detection at 48 hours post-delivery of probe, of 6 × 102 CFU in vitro and 104 in vivo, resulting in an increase of 24-fold in fluorescence [ ]. The extended period of time to obtain optimal signal suggests that the probe must first be distributed to the infected tissues, taken up by the infected cells and amplify signal over time by cleavage from BlaC within the infected cells. Incubation of CNIR5 with Mtb showed an increase in fluorescent signal of ~3-fold [ ]. The CNIR probes have acetylated D-glucosamine between the lactam ring and quencher ( ) attached to the carboxylate end of the cysteine via a γ-amino-butyric acid linker. This sugar was included to improve uptake in eukaryotic cells [ ], though no significant difference in signal has been observed whether it was present or absent in probes that have been tested, suggesting further investigation of modifications to improve uptake of probes into mammalian cells is needed. The synthesis of CNIR800, which is based on the long wavelength fluorophore IRDye800, was expected to improve sensitivity as compared to shorter wavelength probes, like CNIR5 ( ). The maximum excitation and emission peaks for cleaved CNIR800 are 745 nm and 795 nm respectively, which improve signal penetration through mammalian tissue and reduce light scattering [ , ]. CNIR800 has a limit of detection of 103 CFUs in infected mice, a 10-fold increase in sensitivity compared to CNIR5 [ ]. Additionally, maximal signal is observed as early as 5–7 hours post-administration of CNIR800 as compared to 24–48 hours for CNIR5, demonstrating much faster pharmacokinetics [ ]. Administering CNIR800 24 hours post infection and using intravital excitation in the lungs showed a threshold of detection at 100 CFU, suggesting the potential for an even lower limit of detection [ ]. As such, the use of near infrared fluorogenic probes enhances the potential of the REF system for in vivo studies, especially in combination with intravital excitation.

## Improvements to sensitivity for imaging TB
Fluorescent and bioluminescent probes have been successfully used for real-time imaging to demonstrate the spread of Mtb from the lungs to other organs/tissues, but both are limited for the earliest time point at which the disseminated bacteria can be imaged due to thresholds >104 bacteria/organ. Some of the most common areas of dissemination for Mtb include but are not limited to: extrapulmonary infections such as the cervical lymph nodes or the spine, pleural tuberculosis, and gastrointestinal infections amongst others [ , – ]. Recombinant engineered Mtb strains expressing green or red fluorescent protein, GFP and tdTomato, which emit at 510 nm and 581 nm, respectively, have been extensively examined for thresholds of detection and the ability to evaluate antimicrobials in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Longer wavelengths offer a clear advantage for in vivo imaging since hemoglobin and other tissues absorb less of the emitted light [ , – ]. The ability to excite fluorescent proteins in the lungs of mice via traditional methods is limited by the excitation wavelength, which is shorter than the emission wavelength, further reducing sensitivity. It is of note that translating the mouse model to humans is not a perfect fit, as the amount of probe and the depth of the tissue is different. The use of alternative ways to increase light to the lungs is one way which may allow for the use of these probes in humans for detection of Mtb. Use of a fiber bundle imaging system to allow for intravital illumination mitigates reduced excitation wavelength delivery and the autofluorescence produced by excitation of the fluorophore, enhancing the sensitivity of Mtb detection through a greater differential between signal and noise [ , ]. Fiber bundles have allowed for the detection of probes very efficiently, lowering the detection threshold to 10 CFU in lungs of mice [ , ]. REF uses BlaC as a reporter enzyme to detect the presence of Mtb in vitro and in vivo. Specialized probes, CDG and CDC series, take advantage of the unique active site of BlaC in Mtb, having more flexibility than other β-lactamases [ , , ]. Experiments with these probes can be done in real-time to understand dissemination of Mtb, with the low limit of detection making these probes nearly optimal for in vivo and in vitro detection. The CNIR series of probes has also been used for real-time experiments, with the far-red wavelengths displaying low background and a threshold of detection ~104 in the lungs [ ]. The CNIR800 substrate (ex: 745 nm, em: 795 nm) could detect 3.9 × 102 CFU in infected mice, a nearly 100-fold improvement over CNIR5 (ex: 649 nm, em: 690 nm) and with a maximal signal between 5 and 7 h [ ], a faster pharmacokinetics as compared to CNIR5 with 24–48 h for maximal fluorescence [ , ]. In addition to targeting the lungs, there are some NIR probes which have been utilized to target the bowels, especially in the assessment of inflammatory bowel disease [ ]. Unfortunately, there is a lack of clinical trials with these probes. Most of the probes discussed utilize catalysis to cause fluorescence or bioluminescence in the detection of Mtb. In addition to catalysis-based probes, there is the possibility of utilizing affinity-based probes, such as those targeting IrtAB, an ABC transporter found on the membrane which uptakes iron [ , ]. While targeting transporters like this may prove valuable to in vitro work, the lack of catalysis requires probes to be constantly on, preventing them from being used for in vivo work as effectively. Despite this, these probes may hold the key to understanding dissemination and pathogenesis of Mtb as well as guiding detection technologies and speeding progress toward improved treatments and vaccines.

## Future directions
The ability to track Mtb using fluorescence and bioluminescence in vitro and during infections has transformed the field of TB research. Although these technologies are already heavily used in nearly all aspects of TB research, there remains a great deal to be done. Point-of-care detection of Mtb at thresholds of 1–10 bacilli could redefine TB diagnosis, and we expect that we are nearing this achievement. Assessing treatment outcomes in real time and diagnosis using fluorescence and bioluminescence have been successfully applied in laboratory settings, but additional work is needed to translate these laboratory systems to robust tools that can be used in the field. Key directions to improve these systems include further development of probes to improve specificity and sensitivity. Specificity may be improved through additional structural changes on the CDG3 backbone, as well as modifications to the fluorophore wavelength of emission that is used to avoid interference from mammalian tissues. Sensitivity can also be improved through the development of long-wavelength luminogenic substrates for probes similar to CDG3, avoiding autofluorescence that can increase background. Recently developed fluorescent and bioluminescent reporters also have a great deal of potential to improve pre-clinical systems that can be applied to all areas of TB research, particularly those that allow stable expression of dual fluorescent and bioluminescent signals that are stronger than the earlier versions of these reporters. Lastly, fiber-bundle illumination and detection could reduce background improve fluorescence- and bioluminescence-based detection, particularly in organs that can be accessed in a minimally invasive manner using the mucous layer as a fiber optic system.

10.

## Conclusions
Optical imaging is an invaluable tool for the detection of pathogens, tracking movement of the bacteria to different organs/tissues, evaluating therapeutic strategies, and assessing impacts of vaccines on disease. Imaging can be done using fluorescence and/or bioluminescence, each of which has advantages and disadvantages.
Fluorescent imaging of infections in vivo is relatively straightforward to use in cases where recombinant strains can be constructed, or specific probes can be developed. However, fluorescence is limited by tissue complexity, light scattering, and background fluorescence. Using substrates that emit light in near-infrared wavelengths, trans-illumination instead of epi-illumination, pairing fluorescent probes with bioluminescent probes, and combining these technologies with optical fiber bundles may greatly improve sensitivity.
Bioluminescence has the advantage of very low background and, in most cases, the requirement of ATP for light production. As such, there is rapid signal decay upon bacterial death. Bioluminescent techniques may also provide a rapid method to quantify Mtb during infection, but the current thresholds of detection are not sufficient to follow the preferred infection dose of 10–100 bacteria in mice. Delivering either fluorogenic or luminogenic substrates in vivo in combination with the fiber bundle system may ultimately decrease thresholds of detection, but additional research is needed to support widespread use. With respect to in vitro work, REF imaging has proven highly sensitive and specific to the detection of Mtb. The REF system meets most of the criteria for use as a TB diagnostic, including being affordable, sensitive, user friendly, rapid, robust, and accessible, though a reader to sensitively detect signal is still required. Development of low-cost hand-held battery-operated readers could alleviate this issue and allow widespread use of REF for diagnosis. Furthermore, new substrates could make this system even more sensitive, both for in vitro and in vivo detection.
Technologies for detecting Mtb face challenges related to the slow growth rate and difficulty in obtaining diagnostic specimens. The use of recombinant strains, fluorogenic or luminogenic substrates and REF have yielded new insight into more sensitive strategies to detect Mtb during infection and in diagnostic specimens. Recombinant reporters and/or probes can be used for either fluorescent or bioluminescent detection and some of these can be used in conjunction with intravital fiber optics to yield limits of detection as low as 10–100 CFU, suggesting that even lower thresholds will ultimately be reached.

11.

## Biosafety and regulatory considerations
Laboratory development of imaging technologies for TB requires implementation of biosafety measures to protect personnel working with the bacteria and infections in animals though adherence to biosafety level three practices and use of biosafety level three facilities. As these technologies are translated to clinical settings, precautions for working with suspected TB patients include the use of negative-pressure rooms and N95 respirators for healthcare workers. Closed containment systems, such as sealed sputum cups, are preferred for diagnostic specimens, since they help to avoid accidental exposure of diagnostic personnel and imaging systems should allow containment of potential aerosols as well as facile decontamination between samples or patients. New diagnostic methods for TB throughout most of the world require assessment and approval by the World Health Organization (WHO) and many markets recognize CE mark approval from the European Union (EU), but within the U.S. FDA approval is required. Often regulatory assessment by these agencies can take many years and delay widespread use of new TB diagnostics, particularly those that are the most innovative.

12.

## Expert opinion
Imaging technologies have the potential to dramatically change the landscape for TB research by facilitating progress toward new therapeutics and vaccines. In addition, imaging has the ability to rapidly detect and quantify TB, which can be applied to diagnostic specimens immediately and ultimately within infected patients, dependent upon sufficiently sensitive probes and detection equipment being developed. Research is ongoing to develop such probes and fiber bundle systems display the potential to produce thresholds of detection that could be useful directly in patients, particularly applicable to pediatric TB patient populations. Application of an imaging system to pediatric TB diagnosis is a critical need since infants and young children often cannot be diagnosed effectively using conventional methods [ – ]. GeneXpert is a molecular diagnostic test for TB that displays potential to improve pediatric diagnosis [ , ], but the costs per test, complicated equipment, need for high-quality infrastructure and technical complexity limit its widespread use at the point-of-care. REF is very low cost, can be used with battery operated readers, and requires little/no technical expertise to apply, suggesting its potential for use as a true point-of-care diagnostic strategy for TB in virtually any setting.
New recombinant systems and modified probes for imaging are constantly being developed in the Mtb field and other biological systems that require more thorough examination and direct comparison to the existing methods in Mtb. Stability of recombinant systems must be addressed, since in vivo plasmids are readily lost, reducing the correlation of signal with bacterial numbers. This issue can be readily addressed by constructing chromosomally encoded recombinant markers, but construction is time-consuming in Mtb due to the organism’s slow growth rate. Combined fluorescent and bioluminescent markers could potentially improve specificity and detection thresholds by allowing better separation of signal from background fluorescence. The spectral unmixing algorithms used by IVIS can be further improved to more accurately correct for the heterogeneity present in real world mammalian tissues. Ultimately, a combination of iterative improvement of recombinant systems, REF probes and imaging equipment would likely result in the best and most sensitive strategies for imaging Mtb, where thresholds of detection need to reach a single bacterium in order to truly replace conventional tracking methods that use colony forming units. The progress to date where thresholds have already been improved 100- to 1,000-fold over earlier methods suggests that we should be optimistic about the potential of imaging to change how we quantify infectious agents during disease.
We expect that within the next 5 years improved, stable fluorescent and bioluminescent recombinant systems will be available in several of the fully virulent Mtb strains. These systems can be readily used for pre-clinical studies toward new therapeutics and vaccines, as well as in pathogenesis studies, facilitating progress within the TB field. Both the REF and fiber bundle systems will continue to be improved, generating thresholds that should reach 1–10 bacteria in vivo, and efforts will be ongoing to translate these systems so that they can improve outcomes for suspected TB patients, both adults using clinical samples and pediatric patients using a combined probe and fiber bundle system. As prototype systems are developed, they will need to be independently evaluated by diagnostic laboratories in endemic regions to rigorously determine performance characteristics as compared to existing methods. These imaging strategies have the potential to be transformative for diagnosis of TB as well as within nearly every area of research on Mtb since there is the potential to replace quantification methods for viable Mtb in research. However, acceptance of imaging strategies to quantify bacterial numbers, regardless of how sensitive the methods are, will be dependent upon validation of the approach under an extremely diverse set of experimental conditions by several independent groups.

## Article highlights
Rapid detection of Mtb is critical to limiting the spread of TB and improving the efficacy of therapeutics.
Noninvasive imaging technologies are valuable for understanding pathogenesis, evaluation of new drugs, and the development of fluorescent and bioluminescent probes that allow sensitive detection of infections and diagnosis.
Optical imaging has broad applications for monitoring tumor growth, neurodegenerative diseases, and respiratory infections, including TB.
The use of endogenous enzymes present in infectious agents, combined with fluorogenic probes can allow reporter enzyme fluorescence (REF) for sensitive optical detection in vitro, using clinical samples or laboratory assays, and during infection.
In addition to REF, recombinant strains carrying fluorescent or luminescent reporters, continue to improve and are leading to more sensitive strategies to detect Mtb for pre-clinical studies developing new therapeutics and vaccines.
