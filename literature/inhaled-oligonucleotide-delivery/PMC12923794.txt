## Abstract
Lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI) is a serious complication in critical clinical situations. Despite its importance, the specific role of type II natural killer T (NKT) cells in LIRI remains unclear. In this study, we establish a LIRI mouse model and demonstrate that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages (AMs) and alleviate LIRI through AMs-mediated mechanisms. This protective effect is absent in Jα18−/- mice, indicating the essential role of invariant NKT (iNKT) cells. Further analysis shows that interleukin-10 (IL-10) secreted by iNKT cells upregulates AT-rich interaction domain 3 A (Arid3a) in macrophages, which then promotes the transcription of DNA damage inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4). This cascade enhances M2 polarization of macrophages, potentially contributing to lung protection and alleviating LIRI. These findings suggest that NKT cells may offer a therapeutic target for LIRI in the future.

 
Subject terms: Monocytes and macrophages, NKT cells, Respiratory tract diseases

## Introduction
Lung ischemia-reperfusion injury is a common and severe pathophysiological process in clinical practice, involved in various diseases such as shock, pulmonary embolism, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation, particularly in lung transplantation . As the only effective treatment for end-stage lung disease, lung transplantation faces the challenge of LIRI, which significantly increases the risk of graft dysfunction, affecting both transplant success rates and patient survival , . The restoration of blood flow during LIRI triggers severe oxidative stress and excessive inflammatory responses, leading to further tissue damage . Given the complex pathogenesis of LIRI, including multiple immune cells and inflammatory mediators, modulating the immune response has become a key strategy to mitigate lung injury.
Alveolar macrophages are highly abundant innate immune cells in the airways. In the inflammatory process of LIRI, the activation of alveolar macrophages serves as a crucial trigger . Depending on the microenvironment, macrophages can polarize into pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes . For example, toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) drive macrophages toward classical M1 activation, while interleukin-4 (IL-4) and IL-13 promote alternative M2 polarization . Notably, IL-10 can also induce M2 macrophages with anti-inflammatory and tissue repair properties . Earlier studies have demonstrated that the in vivo induction of M2 macrophages and the adoptive transfer of in vitro-generated M2 macrophages or extracellular vesicles derived from M2 macrophages can both alleviate acute lung injury – . Furthermore, mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy, which promotes macrophage M2 polarization, has entered phase II clinical trials for lung injury treatment. These trials have shown that MSCs not only improve oxygenation in patients but also significantly reduce protein and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) , . Therefore, promoting M2 macrophage polarization may be a potential strategy for alleviating LIRI.
Natural killer T cells are an important subset of unconventional T lymphocytes, possessing characteristics of both NK cells and T cells. They recognize antigens presented by CD1d molecules on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) via the T cell receptor (TCR). Once activated, NKT cells quickly produce cytokines and act as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity – . NKT cells are classified into type I and type II subsets based on TCR diversity. Type I NKT cells are commonly referred to as invariant NKT (iNKT) cells because they express a specific semi-invariant TCR αβ chain. In contrast, type II NKT cells express more diverse TCRs encoded by variable gene segments and are often referred to as variant NKT (vNKT) cells , . Sulfatide, a 3-sulfated galactosylceramide, is specifically recognized by a subset of type II NKT cells in both humans and mice. The sulfatide-CD1d complex binds to the TCR of type II NKT cells, leading to their proliferation and activation. Thus, sulfatide is commonly used to assess the quantity and function of type II NKT cells . Sulfatide-activated type II NKT cells can regulate the functions of iNKT cells, playing an immunoregulatory role in various diseases . To our knowledge, the role of type II NKT cells in LIRI remains unreported. Although their precise function in LIRI is still unclear, studies have shown that sulfatide can alleviate ischemia-reperfusion injury in the liver and kidney by activating type II NKT cells in these organs , . Type II NKT cells are also present in the lungs of humans and mice, and previous studies have demonstrated that sulfatide can activate lung type II NKT cells in a mouse model of asthma . This suggests that sulfatide may activate type II NKT cells in lung ischemia-reperfusion injury and exert a protective effect on lung damage. Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells reduce the secretion of inflammatory factors like IFN-γ from iNKT cells, while enhancing production of IL-10 . An experiment indicates that the protective effect of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells on renal ischemia-reperfusion injury is associated with an enhanced IL-10 pathway . Moreover, sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviate experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) and inhibit the immunogenic maturation of pulmonary dendritic cells in an iNKT-dependent manner , .
Based on these observations, we hypothesize that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells, which in turn drives the M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages, alleviating lung ischemia-reperfusion injury. In this study, we used CD1d−/− and Jα18−/− mice, along with alveolar macrophage depletion and adoptive cell transfer in a LIRI mouse model, to investigate the role of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells. We further combined RNA sequencing with functional assays to explore how sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells modulated iNKT cells, leading to M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages. Our findings aimed to provide new insights into potential strategies for mitigating LIRI through immune modulation.

## Materials and methods

## Experimental animals
Male mice aged 8–12 weeks were sourced from the Animal Experiment Center of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China), including wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J and BALB/c strains. CD1d−/− mice (NKT cell-deficient, BALB/c background) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA), Jα18−/− mice (iNKT cell-deficient, C57BL/6J background) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China), IL-10RA−/− mice were from Wuhan University (Dr. Qin Pan) and the IL-10−/− mice were from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Dr. Wei Wang). All mice were housed at 22 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle at the Animal Experiment Center of Wuhan University.
Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Wuhan University (Approval No. WP20220407). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

## Lung ischemia-reperfusion injury mouse model
To create a lung ischemia/reperfusion injury (LIRI) model, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and ventilated using an animal ventilator (PhysioSuiteTMRoVent, KENT) set to 150 breaths/min, with a tidal volume of 0.4 ml and a peak inspiratory pressure below 20 cmH₂O. Heparin (20 U/kg) was administered via external jugular vein injection. A left thoracotomy was performed at the fourth rib to expose the left pulmonary hilum, which was clamped with a microvascular clip for 1 h. The clamp was then released to initiate reperfusion. After 4 h, the mice were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium, and the left lung, BALF and blood were collected for analysis. During surgery, rectal temperature was maintained between 36.5 °C and 37.5 °C. In sham-operated controls, only thoracotomy was performed without pulmonary hilum occlusion.

## In vivo sulfatide, anti-CD1d, and Dasatinib intervention
Sulfatide (Matreya) was prepared by mixing with PBS and 0.5% polysorbate-20 (wt/vol) for the experimental solution, while the control solution consisted of PBS with 0.5% polysorbate-20 alone. Each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 20 μg sulfatide or an equal volume (200 μL) of control solution . For anti-CD1d treatment, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 500 μg of anti-CD1d . Dasatinib was administered intravenously at a dose of 10 mg/kg .

## Pulmonary histology and myeloperoxidase (MPO) immunohistochemistry
The left lung was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was then performed. Pathological scoring of lung injury was conducted following the method described in reference , . For immunohistochemical analysis of MPO, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 95 C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by blocking with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-MPO antibody (1:100, Abcam). The next day, sections were washed with PBS, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, Abcam) at room temperature for 30 min, developed with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

## Lung wet/dry weight ratio
The lungs were weighed immediately to obtain the wet weight, then dried to a constant weight. The lung wet/dry weight ratio was calculated as an indicator of pulmonary edema.

## Pulmonary microvascular permeability and oxidative stress assays
Pulmonary microvascular permeability was assessed using the Evans blue dye extravasation method, as described in previous studies . Oxidative stress levels were evaluated by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in lung tissues using an MDA assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Blood gas analysis
Femoral arterial blood samples were collected and analyzed using the i-STAT 300 blood gas analyzer (Abbott) to perform blood gas analysis and calculate the oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂).

## Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid collection
After clamping the right pulmonary hilum with a microvascular clip, bronchoalveolar lavage was performed using a catheter. PBS containing EDTA (0.6 mM) was instilled in 0.4 mL volumes, repeated three times, following established protocols . Collected BALF samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was stored at −80 °C for cytokine analysis. Levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-10 in BALF samples were measured using ELISA kits following the protocol provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. The recovered cells were stained with Wright-Giemsa stain (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) for differential cell counting.

## Isolation of AMs
Primary murine AMs were isolated from BALF obtained by lavage with PBS containing EDTA . Briefly, the BALF samples were centrifuged, and the cell pellet was washed once with PBS. The viability of the suspended AMs was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion assay, confirming a viability of over 95%. The cells were then seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 60 min. Non-adherent cells were gently removed by washing the wells three times with PBS. The purity of AMs was determined to exceed 90% by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Immunofluorescence stain
Cells were seeded onto pretreated coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After three 5-min washes with PBS, samples were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Anti-Arg-1 (1:1000, Proteintech) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After washing three times with PBS, samples were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (1:500, Abcam) for 50 min at room temperature in the dark.
Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) with microwave heating for 30 seconds and subsequently cooled to room temperature. Samples were re-blocked with 10% goat serum for 10 min and incubated with anti-CD68 (1:50, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After three PBS washes, samples were incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:500, Abcam) for 50 min at room temperature in the dark.
Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Coverslips were mounted using antifade mounting medium and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

## Depletion of AMs
After anesthesia with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), mice received 60 µl of clodronate liposomes (Yeasen) or an equivalent volume of PBS via intranasal administration. Flow cytometry conducted 48–72 h later confirmed that alveolar macrophage depletion in the lungs exceeded 85% (Supplementary Fig.  ) .

## RNA quantification
Total RNA was isolated from samples using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mRNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme). RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (novoprotein) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. β-actin was used as an internal control for normalization. The relative mRNA expression of target genes in each sample was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers are detailed in Supplementary Table  .

## Type II NKT cells isolation and cytokine secretion
To isolate type II NKT cells, lungs of mice were collected. Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the harvested tissues. Type II NKT cells were identified and sorted as double-positive cells (APC-Cy7-TCR-β⁺ and PE-sulfatide/CD1d tetramer⁺) using flow cytometry, reaching a final purity ~80% after two rounds of sorting (Supplementary Fig.  ).
A 96-well plate was coated with 5 μg/mL anti-CD3ε antibody (BioLegend) under appropriate conditions. Following coating, type II NKT cells (3×105 cells/well) isolated from mice in different groups were seeded into the wells containing complete culture medium. The medium consisted of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 5 mM Hepes. Soluble anti-CD28 antibody (2 μg/mL, BioLegend) was added to the culture. Cells were incubated under normoxic conditions at 37 °C for 72 h. At the end of the culture period, supernatants were collected, and cytokine concentrations were measured using IFN-γ-ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## iNKT cells isolation, adoptive transfer, and cytokine secretion
To isolate iNKT cells, mice were euthanized, and spleens or lungs were collected. Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the harvested tissues. iNKT cells were identified and sorted as double-positive cells (PE-Cy7-TCR-β⁺ and PE-PBS57/mCD1d⁺) using flow cytometry, achieving a final purity exceeding 90% (Supplementary Fig.  ). The purified iNKT cells were subsequently transferred into recipient mice via tail vein injection at a dose of 1 × 106 cells per mouse.
A 96-well plate was coated with 5 μg/mL anti-CD3ε antibody (BioLegend) under appropriate conditions. Following coating, lung iNKT cells (2 × 105 cells/well) isolated from mice in different groups were seeded into the wells containing complete culture medium. The medium consisted of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 5 mM Hepes. Soluble anti-CD28 antibody (2 μg/mL, BioLegend) was added to the culture. Cells were incubated under normoxic conditions at 37 °C for 72 h. For experiments simulating hypoxia-reoxygenation in vitro, cells were incubated under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) for 6 h, followed by reoxygenation in a normoxic environment for 48 h. At the end of the culture period, supernatants were collected, and cytokine concentrations were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Preparation of BMDMs
To obtain BMDMs, femurs and tibias of mice were flushed with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and filtered through a 70 µm strainer to collect bone marrow cells. After treatment with red blood cell lysis buffer, the bone marrow cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates containing DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, R&D Systems). On the second day, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. Fresh M-CSF and medium were added on the fourth and sixth days. After 7 days of culture, adherent cells were harvested and analyzed for the expression of F4/80 and CD11b markers by flow cytometry, confirming that the purity of BMDMs was greater than 90% (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## iNKT cells co-cultured with BMDMs
iNKT cells isolated from mouse lungs were co-cultured with BMDMs at a 1:1 ratio . CD3/CD28 (BioLegend) was added to the complete medium, and the cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) for 6 h, followed by reoxygenation in a normoxic environment for 48 h. Subsequently, the polarization state of the BMDMs was assessed.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
RNA-seq was performed on AMs isolated from the BALF of LIRI mice treated with either vehicle or sulfatide to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and evaluated for quality and integrity using a spectrophotometer and Bioanalyzer. High-quality RNA samples were selected for library preparation, involving mRNA enrichment, reverse transcription to generate cDNA, fragmentation, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Sequencing was performed on the MGI platform. RNA-seq data were quality-checked using FastQC and trimmed to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences with Trimmomatic. The cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, with significant DEGs identified based on P < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 0.585. Visualization of the results was conducted using ggplot2 to generate a volcano plot.

## Western blotting
Proteins were extracted from cells, quantified, and separated by molecular weight using SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked and incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-β-actin (1:5000, Abclonal), anti-REDD1 (1:2000, Proteintech), anti-Arid3a (1:1000, Proteintech), and anti-Arg-1 (1:5000, Proteintech). After primary antibody incubation, the membrane was treated with an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:10000, Abclonal) or HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, Proteintech). Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrate and visualized via imaging.

## Small interfering RNA transfection for BMDMs
After BMDM induction, siRNA transfection was conducted when cells reached approximately 70% confluency. si-DDIT4 (50 nM), si-Arid3a (50 nM), or negative control siRNA (si-NC) were transfected using the CALNP™ RNAi in vitro transfection reagent (D-nano Therapeutics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The culture medium was replaced with antibiotic-free medium, and then the siRNA-transfection complexes were gently added to each well, followed by gentle mixing. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh complete medium, and gene knockdown efficiency was assessed 48 h post-transfection using RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis. The siRNA Sequences were listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Identification of DEGs and DDIT4-associated genes from GSE122070 dataset
The GSE122070 dataset from the GEO database was analyzed to identify DEGs in BMDMs under LPS and LPS plus IL-10 treatments, as well as genes correlated with DDIT4 expression. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, with significant DEGs defined by the thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1. Spearman correlation analysis was then conducted to determine genes significantly associated with DDIT4 expression, selecting the top 20 for detailed investigation. Visualization of the results was carried out using pheatmap.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
BMDMs were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, and the reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Cells were lysed, and chromatin was fragmented to 200–500 bp using sonication. The lysates were pre-cleared with protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Arid3a (Proteintech) or IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as a negative control. Immunoprecipitated complexes were captured on agarose beads and washed with buffers. After de-crosslinking, the precipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR and PCR to verify Arid3a binding to the DDIT4 promoter region. The primers used for are listed (Supplementary Table  ).

## Dual-luciferase reporter activity assay
A dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted to investigate the interaction between Arid3a and the DDIT4 promoter region. HEK293 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 80% confluence. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with plasmids containing either the wild-type DDIT4 promoter construct or a mutant DDIT4 promoter construct, along with a thymidine kinase promoter-Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid for normalization and either an Arid3a expression plasmid or an empty vector as a control. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Adoptive transfer of AMs or BMDMs
Using a hemocytometer, AMs harvested from the BALF were counted and resuspended in PBS. A total of 3 × 105 AMs or an equivalent volume of PBS (60 µl) was transferred intranasally into the recipient mice . After 12 h, a lung ischemia/reperfusion injury model was established in the mice, and samples were collected. Similarly, BMDM cells were intranasally transferred into the lungs of mice (2 × 106 cells per mouse) . After 12 h, lung ischemia/reperfusion surgery was performed, and samples were collected.

## Tetrameric CD1d-sulfatide complexes
Biotinylated mouse CD1d monomers (NIH Tetramer Core Facility) were combined with sulfatide in PBS containing 0.5% polysorbate-20 at a protein-to-lipid molar ratio of 1:3 and left to incubate overnight at room temperature. The next day, 80 μg of streptavidin-PE (eBioscience) was added to 200 μg of CD1d-sulfatide complexes and incubated for 4 h at room temperature. The resulting tetramers were then stored at 4 °C until further use.

## Flow cytometry analysis
To analyze alveolar macrophages in mice via flow cytometry, lung tissue was finely minced and digested with collagenase I (Gibco) at 37 °C for 1 h. Following digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm nylon mesh to remove large debris and tissue fragments. Residual red blood cells were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and lung mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated by centrifugation as previously described . For viability staining, Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Pharmingen) was applied. To block nonspecific binding, cells were incubated with anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (BD Pharmingen) before staining with specific antibodies or isotype controls: FITC-CD45 (BD Pharmingen), APC-CD11c (BD Pharmingen), and APC-Cy7-Siglec-F (BioLegend) or PE-Cy7-Siglec-F (BioLegend). AMs were identified as CD11c+Siglec-F+ cells. To evaluate AMs polarization, cells were stained with BV421-CD206 (BioLegend). Intracellular staining for CD206 was performed using Fix/Perm buffer (eBioscience), following the manufacturer’s instructions.
For quantifying type II NKT cells and iNKT cells in the lungs and assessing cytokine production, lung MNCs were prepared and stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Pharmingen), followed by CD16/CD32 blocking. Cells were then stained with isotype controls or specific antibodies: FITC-CD45 (BD Pharmingen), APC-Cy7-TCR-β (BioLegend), and PE-labeled PBS57/CD1d tetramer or PE-labeled sulfatide/mCD1d tetramer. Type II NKT cells were identified as TCR-β+ sulfatide/mCD1d+ and iNKT cells were identified as TCR-β+PBS57/CD1d tetramer+ cells. For intracellular cytokine staining, lung MNCs were stimulated with leukocyte activation cocktail with BD GolgiPlug (BD Pharmingen) for 4–6 h. After stimulation, cells were washed and stained intracellularly for APC-IFN-γ, APC-IL-4, Alexa Fluor 647-IL-17A (all from BD Pharmingen), APC-IL-10 (BioLegend) and APC-YES1(SantaCruz) following the manufacturer’s protocol.
To identify which cell acts as CD1d+ antigen-presenting cells for sulfatide, fluorescently labeled sulfatide conjugated with 5-TAMRA (synthesized and verified by Anhui GuoPing Pharmaceutical) was used (Supplementary Fig.  ). The labeled sulfatide was intraperitoneally injected into mice. After 6 h, lung tissues were harvested for analysis. Lung MNCs were isolated and stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Pharmingen) to assess cell viability, followed by CD16/CD32 blocking to prevent non-specific binding. The cells were then incubated with isotype controls or specific antibodies: FITC-CD45 (BD Pharmingen), APC-CD11c (BD Pharmingen), APC-Cy7-F4/80 (BioLegend), APC-CD64 (BioLegend), BV421-Mertk (BioLegend), or APC-CD19 (Elabscience). Dendritic cells were defined as CD45+CD11c+F4/80-, macrophages as CD45+CD64+Mertk+, and B cells as CD45+CD19+. After antibody incubation, cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry.
To assess polarization in BMDMs, cells were first stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Pharmingen), followed by CD16/CD32 blocking. Cells were then stained with isotype controls or specific antibodies: FITC-CD11b (BD Pharmingen) and APC-Cy7-F4/80 (BioLegend). BMDMs were identified as F4/80+CD11b+ cells. Additional staining for polarization marker, BV421-CD206 (BioLegend), alongside isotype controls.
All staining reactions were performed at 4 °C. Data acquisition was conducted on a flow cytometer (Epics Altra; Beckman Coulter), and results were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

## Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis
First, the Read10X function was used to load the 10X mixed data from the GSE190201 and GSE142845 datasets, and Seurat objects were created for each sample. Antibody capture data (ADT) was normalized using the CLR method, and HTODemux was applied to remove doublets. The data was filtered based on nCount_RNA, nFeature_RNA, and the percentage of mitochondrial genes (percent.mt), with filtering criteria: nFeature_RNA > 500, nFeature_RNA < 4000, and percent.mt<10, retaining cells with nCount_RNA ≥ 3. Subsequently, Seurat objects from different samples (spleen, 4w_α-Galcer mice and adipose tissue) were merged, and the Harmony algorithm was used to correct for batch effects. PCA was performed for dimensionality reduction, selecting the top 50 principal components for further analysis, followed by UMAP for visualization. FindNeighbors and FindClusters were used for clustering analysis to identify distinct cell populations. Differential gene expression was analyzed using FindMarkers, selecting genes with logFC>0.25 and p_val_adj<0.05. A bubble plot was generated to visualize gene expression differences across cell populations, with bubble size and color representing gene expression levels. Finally, ggplot2 was used to generate bar plots showing the proportions of cell populations in different sample groups.

## Statistics and reproducibility
All data were presented as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA), employing either Student’s unpaired t-test or one-way ANOVA. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered for differences between groups. Sample sizes and number of replicates are reported in the Figure Legends. All relevant information, including oligo sequences, can be found in Supplementary Data  , while antibody catalog numbers and other reagents used are listed in Supplementary Data  .

## Results

## Intervention of sulfatide alleviates lung ischemia-reperfusion injury
To determine the function of sulfatide in LIRI, a mouse model of lung ischemia-reperfusion injury was established (Fig.  ). Compared to sham group, ischemia-reperfusion could cause pathological lung injury in mice (Fig.  ). Previous studies have shown that the application of sulfatide at different time points (3, 16, and 48 h) prior to liver ischemia-reperfusion consistently alleviated liver injury, with no significant differences in serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels across the time points . We also administered sulfatide to mice at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h prior to lung ischemia reperfusion to observe whether its effect on LIRI was influenced by the timing of treatment. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , there was no statistically significant difference in lung injury scores between LIRI mice administered sulfatide intraperitoneally at 0 h (immediately before ischemia) and 1 h or 72 h before ischemia. However, compared to the 0 h group, the lung injury scores were notably reduced in the 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h groups, with the lowest score observed in the 6 h group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we chose to administer sulfatide 6 h prior to ischemia. At 6 h after intraperitoneal injection of sulfatide, we clamped the left pulmonary hilum of the mice for 1 h, followed by 4 h of reperfusion, and then assessed lung injury, function, and inflammation indices. Our data clearly illustrated that sulfatide had a significant protective effect against LIRI. We observed a reduction in lung injury scores and decreased myeloperoxidase (MPO) expression, which reflects neutrophil activity and is a marker of inflammation in lung tissue (Fig.  ). Additionally, the lung wet-to-dry ratio, Evans blue dye leakage (used to assess vascular permeability), and MDA levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, were all reduced (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, the oxygenation index was elevated (Fig.  ). Compared with LIRI mice that intervened by vehicle, LIRI mice treated with sulfatide showed a significant reduction in neutrophil numbers in BALF (Fig.  ). BALF analysis also revealed decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, and IFN-γ, along with an increase in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig.  ). Overall, these experimental findings provided strong evidences that sulfatide effectively mitigated LIRI in mice.
 

## Sulfatide promotes M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages in LIRI
AMs are the most abundant immune cells residing in the alveoli and can polarize into M1 or M2 phenotypes in response to different microenvironments, playing a crucial role in regulating pulmonary inflammation . Therefore, we investigated the effect of sulfatide on the polarization of AMs in LIRI. We performed phenotypic analysis of AMs using flow cytometry, identifying the AM population by CD11c⁺Siglec-F⁺ marker (Fig.  ). The results showed that, compared to sham mice, the proportion of CD206⁺ cells in AMs was significantly reduced in LIRI mice. However, sulfatide notably restored the proportion of CD206⁺ AMs in LIRI mice (Fig.  , ). We further confirmed macrophage phenotypic changes through immunofluorescence staining. AMs were isolated from BALF using the adherence method, with CD68 used as a macrophage marker for identification and localization. The results indicated that, compared to untreated LIRI mice, the proportion of Arg-1⁺ cells among CD68⁺ cells was increased in LIRI mice following sulfatide intervention (Fig.  , ). Additionally, sulfatide increased the mRNA levels of Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1 in AMs compared to vehicle-treated LIRI mice (Fig.  ). AMs used for mRNA analysis were also isolated from BALF and verified by flow cytometry, with a purity >90% (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, the flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and mRNA expression analyses consistently demonstrated that sulfatide significantly promoted the polarization of AMs towards the M2 phenotype in LIRI.
 

## Sulfatide mitigates LIRI and promotes M2 polarization of AMs in a manner dependent on the activation of type II NKT cells
While sulfatide is widely recognized for its ability to activate specific type II NKT cells, as evidenced by increased cell proliferation and enhanced cytokine secretion , its capacity to activate type II NKT cells specifically in the context of LIRI remains unconfirmed. Therefore, our study sought to investigate whether sulfatide could similarly activate type II NKT cells in LIRI. Supplementary Fig.  shows the gating strategy for type II NKT cells in flow cytometry, where cell populations were first gated based on forward scatter (FSC-A) and side scatter (SSC-A), followed by the selection of single cells, live cells, and leukocytes to obtain the lymphocyte subset. Among lymphocytes, TCR-β+ sulfatide/CD1d-tetramer+ cells were defined as type II NKT cells. Given the low frequency of these cells, we validated the specificity of sulfatide/CD1d tetramer staining in WT, Jα18−/−, and CD1d−/− mice. The tetramer specifically labeled TCR-β+ type II NKT cells in WT and Jα18−/− mice, with only minimal background in CD1d−/− mice, confirming the robustness of this approach (Supplementary Fig.  ). As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , compared to vehicle, sulfatide increased the presence of lung type II NKT cells in LIRI mice. Additionally, the proportion of IFN-γ+ type II NKT cells in the lungs of LIRI mice treated with sulfatide was notably higher than that in LIRI mice treated with vehicle, and both groups were higher than the sham groups. Furthermore, we isolated lung type II NKT cells by flow cytometric sorting and cultured them in vitro (Supplementary Fig.  ). After 72 h, IFN-γ levels in the culture supernatants were measured by ELISA. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , type II NKT cells from sulfatide-treated LIRI mice secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-γ than those from vehicle-treated mice, further supporting that sulfatide enhances IFN-γ production by type II NKT cells in lung ischemia-reperfusion injury. We also measured IL-4+ and IL-10+ type II NKT cells in the lungs, and results indicated no significant difference between LIRI mice treated with sulfatide and those treated with vehicle (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, CD69, an early marker of T cell activation, was markedly increased in lung type II NKT cells of LIRI mice treated with sulfatide compared to LIRI mice treated with vehicle, which also serves to demonstrate sulfatide’s potent activation effect on type II NKT cells in LIRI (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, sulfatide significantly activated type II NKT cells in the LIRI, as evidenced by increased type II NKT cells presence, enhanced IFN-γ secretion, and upregulated CD69 expression. Furthermore, we identified lung dendritic cells as CD1d+ antigen-presenting cells responsible for presenting sulfatide, thereby mediating type II NKT cell activation in this setting (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Thus, is the ability of sulfatide to alleviate lung injury and promote M2 polarization of AMs dependent on the activation of type II NKT cells? Next, we employed CD1d−/− mice reconstituted with iNKT cells, thus creating a type II NKT cell–deficient model, to investigate the role of type II NKT cells in sulfatide-mediated improvement of LIRI and the M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages (Supplementary Fig.  ). As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , compared to vehicle treatment, sulfatide significantly reduced lung injury score and MPO expression in WT mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells, while no noticeable improvement was evident in CD1d−/− mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells. Quantitative analyses of lung wet-to-dry ratio, Evans blue leakage, MDA levels, and PaO2/FiO2 ratio further confirmed that sulfatide exerted a protective effect in WT mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells, but showed no prominent effect in CD1d−/- mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). Analyses of neutrophil count and expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-10 in BALF indicated that, following sulfatide treatment, inflammation was significantly reduced and the IL-10 level was elevated in WT mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells, whereas type II NKT cell–deficient mice showed no such changes (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings suggested that sulfatide-mediated lung protection in LIRI depended on type II NKT cells. Further flow cytometry results demonstrated that, compared to vehicle treatment, sulfatide raised the proportion of CD206+ cells among AMs in WT mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, this effect was absent in CD1d−/− mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). RT-qPCR results showed that, after sulfatide intervention, WT mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells exhibited elevated M2-associated genes (Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1) (Supplementary Fig.  ). No notable changes occurred in CD1d−/− mice with adoptively transferred iNKT cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results indicated that sulfatide promoted M2 polarization of AMs, relying on type II NKT cells. In conclusion, sulfatide’s promotion of M2 polarization of AMs and its alleviation of lung injury required the presence of type II NKT cells.

## Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviate LIRI through alveolar macrophages
Studies have shown that M2 polarization of macrophages can alleviate acute lung injury . We have confirmed that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote the polarization of AMs toward the M2 phenotype as described above. However, the precise contribution of AMs to sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cell–mediated protection against LIRI remains unclear. To investigate whether sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviated LIRI through AMs, we used clodronate liposomes to deplete AMs in mice and then performed adoptive transfer of vehicle- or sulfatide-pretreated AMs into AM-depleted LIRI mice (Fig.  ). The results showed that, compared to LIRI mice, AM-depleted LIRI mice exhibited exacerbated lung injury, as evidenced by increased lung injury scores (Fig.  ), Evans blue leakage (Fig.  ) and MDA levels (Fig.  ), lower oxygenation index (Fig.  ), and elevated neutrophil counts as well as IL-1β and IFN-γ levels in BALF (Fig.  ). Additionally, sulfatide reduced the lung wet-to-dry ratio and TNF-α levels in the BALF of LIRI mice; however, in AM-depleted LIRI mice, sulfatide intervention did not significantly decrease either the wet-to-dry ratio or TNF-α levels (Fig.  ), suggesting that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells might exert its protective effect in LIRI through AMs. Further results demonstrated that, in AM-depleted LIRI mice, adoptive transfer of AMs from sulfatide-pretreated donors resulted in significantly greater alleviation of lung injury compared with transfer of AMs from vehicle-pretreated donors, as shown by decreased lung injury scores (Fig.  ), lower MPO expression in lung tissue (Fig.  ), reduced lung wet-to-dry ratio, Evans blue leakage and MDA levels (Fig.  ), increased oxygenation index (Fig.  ), and lower neutrophil counts as well as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ levels in BALF, along with increased IL-10 (Fig.  ). These results provided additional evidences that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviated LIRI via AMs.
 

## Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells inhibit ischemia-reperfusion-induced lung injury in mice through iNKT cells
Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells have been shown to alleviate asthma , hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury , and inflammatory liver disease through iNKT cells. Our study investigated whether sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells exerted protective effect mediated by iNKT cells in LIRI. We administered sulfatide or vehicle to WT and Jα18−/− LIRI mice to assess lung injury (Fig.  ). Results showed that Jα18−/− LIRI mice had lower lung injury scores, MPO expression and Evans blue dye extravasation, along with an increased oxygenation index compared to WT LIRI mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Neutrophil counts, TNF-α and IFN-γ levels in BALF were also significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that iNKT cells contribute to the development of LIRI. Moreover, in Jα18−/− LIRI mice, sulfatide failed to alleviate ischemia-reperfusion-induced lung injury, as there were no significant differences in lung injury scores, MPO expression, lung wet-to-dry ratio, Evans blue dye extravasation, MDA levels, oxygenation index, or neutrophil counts, TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-10 levels in BALF compared to vehicle-treated Jα18−/− mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings suggested that the protective effect of sulfatide on LIRI need iNKT cells.
 In the rescue experiment, we injected lung iNKT cells from LIRI mice that pretreated with sulfatide or vehicle (106 cells per mouse) via the tail vein into WT mice and CD1d−/− mice. After 12 h, the mice underwent lung ischemia-reperfusion surgery, and postoperative lung injury was assessed (Fig.  ). The results showed that, in both WT and CD1d−/− groups, adoptive transfer of iNKT cells from sulfatide-pretreated LIRI mice resulted in significantly less lung injury than transfer of iNKT cells from vehicle-pretreated mice. This was evidenced by lower lung injury scores, MPO expression, lung wet-to-dry ratio, Evans blue dye extravasation and MDA levels of left lung, decreased neutrophil counts and TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ levels in BALF, as well as increased oxygenation index and IL-10 levels in BALF (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings further confirmed the protective effect of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells on LIRI needed the presence of iNKT cells.

## Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote M2 polarization of AMs in an iNKT cell-dependent manner in LIRI
We further investigated whether sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promoted the polarization of AMs toward the M2 phenotype through iNKT cells in a mouse model of lung ischemia-reperfusion injury. Sulfatide significantly enhanced the M2 polarization of AMs in WT LIRI mice (Fig.  ). However, in Jα18−/− mice, sulfatide treatment did not increase the proportion of CD206+ cells among AMs or upregulate the mRNA expression of M2-related genes (Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1), compared to the vehicle-treated LIRI group (Fig.  ). This suggested that iNKT cells were needed for sulfatide-induced M2 polarization of AMs in LIRI. Moreover, adoptive transfer experiments further supported this role of iNKT cells. In WT and CD1d-/- LIRI mice, transfer of iNKT cells from sulfatide-pretreated donors, compared with vehicle-pretreated donors, led to a significant increase in CD206⁺ AMs as well as elevated mRNA expression of Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1 (Fig.  ). These findings confirmed that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promoted the M2 polarization of AMs in an iNKT cell-dependent manner.

## Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells reduce IFN-γ secretion and enhance IL-10 production in iNKT cells
The above study demonstrated that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviated lung injury and promoted the M2 polarization of AMs in LIRI relying on iNKT cells. However, the precise mechanism by which sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells exerted its effects via iNKT cells remained unclear. Although previous studies have shown that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells could inhibit the function of iNKT cells , its specific impact on iNKT cells in the context of LIRI has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, we further investigated the effects of sulfatide on iNKT cell abundance and function during LIRI. Compared to sham mice, LIRI mice exhibited a significant increase in both the proportion and number of iNKT cells in the left lung tissue (Fig.  ), as well as a marked rise in IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-17A, and IL-10- producing iNKT cells (Fig.  ), suggesting that ischemia-reperfusion might activate lung iNKT cells. Sulfatide reduced the proportion and number of total iNKT cells and IFN-γ-producing iNKT cells in the lungs of LIRI mice (Fig.  ). Although the proportion and number of IL-4- and IL-17A-producing iNKT cells showed a decreasing trend, this change was not statistically significant (Fig.  ). In contrast, sulfatide treatment increased the proportion and number of IL-10-producing iNKT cells (Fig.  ). Further analysis revealed that, compared to vehicle-treated LIRI mice, lung iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated LIRI mice had elevated IL-10 levels and reduced IFN-γ levels in the culture supernatant (Fig.  ). These findings indicated that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells modulated cytokine secretion of iNKT cells in LIRI, involving reduced IFN-γ secretion and enhanced IL-10 production.
 

## Sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviate lung injury and mediate the polarization of AMs towards M2 phenotype in LIRI by promoting IL-10 secretion from lung iNKT cells
We performed adoptive transfer of iNKT cells sorted from WT or IL-10−/− mice into Jα18−/− mice. Twelve hours later, these mice were intraperitoneally injected with either vehicle or sulfatide, followed by lung ischemia-reperfusion surgery. Samples were collected to assess lung injury and macrophage polarization to determine whether sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells exert its effects through IL-10 production by iNKT cells (Fig.  ). In Jα18−/− LIRI mice with adoptive transfer of WT iNKT cells, sulfatide treatment significantly reduced lung injury scores, MPO expression, lung wet-to-dry ratios, vascular permeability (Evans blue dye leakage), and oxidative stress (MDA levels), while increasing the oxygenation index (Supplementary Fig.  ). Sulfatide also reduced neutrophil infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ) while increasing IL-10 levels in BALF (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, in Jα18−/− LIRI mice receiving IL-10−/− iNKT cells, the protective effects of sulfatide on lung injury were significantly weakened compared to mice receiving WT iNKT cells. These mice displayed higher lung injury scores, increased MPO levels, elevated wet-to-dry ratios, greater vascular permeability, increased MDA level, more neutrophil infiltration, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ), alongside reduced IL-10 levels in BALF (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings concluded that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviated LIRI by promoting IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells.
 Furthermore, analysis of M2 polarization markers in AMs revealed that, in Jα18−/− LIRI mice receiving WT iNKT cells, sulfatide increased the proportion of CD206+ cells in AMs and elevated the mRNA levels of Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1 than vehicle (Fig.  ). And compared to Jα18−/− LIRI mice receiving WT iNKT cells, the effect of sulfatide on M2 polarization was markedly reduced in Jα18−/− LIRI mice receiving IL-10−/− iNKT cells. This was evident by less presence of CD206⁺ AMs and decreased mRNA levels of Arg-1, Fizz1, and Ym1 in AMs (Fig.  ). These results suggested that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells enhanced M2 polarization of AMs via IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells.
To further confirm the direct role of iNKT cell–derived IL-10 in macrophage polarization, we co-cultured iNKT cells with BMDMs in vitro. We simulated ischemia-reperfusion process in mice by applying hypoxia-reoxygenation treatment. BMDMs were placed in a hypoxic environment (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) for varying time periods (0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h) and then transferred to a normoxic condition for 2 h of reoxygenation, with TNF-α levels in the culture medium measured thereafter. Results showed a significant increase in TNF-α levels from 0 to 6 h of hypoxia, after which the rate of increase slowed, indicating that BMDMs undergo a marked inflammatory response within the first 6 h of hypoxia (Fig.  ). Based on this, we selected 6 h of hypoxia as the optimal condition for the in vitro experiments. Next, we isolated lung iNKT cells from LIRI mice (WT and IL-10−/− mice) treated with vehicle or sulfatide, subjected them to 6 h of hypoxia followed by 48 h of reoxygenation in CD3/CD28-containing medium, and measured IL-10 levels of the culture medium. Results indicated that, compared to vehicle, sulfatide treatment significantly increased IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells in WT LIRI mice, while iNKT cells from IL-10−/− LIRI mice did not show a similar IL-10 elevation (Fig.  ). We then co-cultured the isolated lung iNKT cells (from WT or IL-10−/− LIRI mice) with WT or IL-10RA−/− BMDMs in a 1:1 ratio. The results demonstrated that BMDMs co-cultured with iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice exhibited significantly enhanced immunofluorescence intensity of Arg-1, MFI of CD206 and mRNA expression levels of CD206 and Arg-1 compare to BMDMs co-cultured with iNKT cells from vehicle-treated WT LIRI mice, indicating that iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice promoted macrophage M2 polarization (Fig.  ). Furthermore, compared to BMDMs co-cultured with iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice, BMDMs co-cultured with iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated IL-10−/− LIRI mice and IL-10RA−/− BMDMs co-cultured with iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice showed lower Arg-1 immunofluorescence intensity and CD206 mean fluorescence intensity (Fig.  ). CD206 and Arg-1 mRNA levels were also significantly decreased (Fig.  ). These results suggested that IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated LIRI mice directly acted on macrophages to promote their M2 polarization.

## IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells following sulfatide intervention promoted M2 polarization of AMs in LIRI mice via Arid3a-DDIT4 axis
To explore how IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells after sulfatide intervention induced M2 polarization of AMs in LIRI mice, we isolated AMs from vehicle- and sulfatide-treated LIRI mice and performed RNA sequencing. The volcano plot revealed significant alterations in multiple genes related to metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation (Fig.  ). To further clarify the role of IL-10 from iNKT cells in macrophages, we cross-referenced these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with the GSE122070 dataset, which compared IL-10 plus LPS–treated BMDMs with LPS-treated BMDMs. The Venn diagram identified 100 commonly upregulated and 98 downregulated genes in AMs from sulfatide-treated LIRI mice and IL-10 plus LPS–treated BMDMs (Fig.  ). By querying The Human Protein Atlas database, we found that among these 198 genes, DDIT4 showed significantly higher expression in macrophage subsets characterized by high levels of CD68, Marco, Mrc1, and CD163 compared with other subsets. CD68 is a macrophage marker ; Marco is preferentially expressed in alveolar macrophages compared to interstitial and monocyte-derived macrophages ; Mrc1 is a hallmark of M2 polarization ; and IL-10 promotes CD163 expression in macrophages . Therefore, the CD68+Marco+Mrc1+CD163+ macrophage subset is likely IL-10–responsive M2-like alveolar macrophages. Based on this, we hypothesized that IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells promoted M2 polarization of AMs in LIRI mice by upregulating DDIT4. To test this hypothesis, we first stimulated BMDMs with IL-10 (100 ng/ml) for 8 h under two conditions: 6 h of hypoxia followed by 2 h of reoxygenation, or continuous normoxia, to assess whether IL-10 could enhance M2 polarization. Under hypoxia-reoxygenation (H/R) condition, IL-10 significantly upregulated CD206 and Arg-1 mRNA levels, increased Arg-1 immunofluorescence intensity, and elevated CD206 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) by flow cytometry (Figs.  and ). In normoxia, IL-10 similarly enhanced Arg-1 mRNA expression, with a trend toward increased CD206 mRNA, MFI for CD206, and Arg-1 immunofluorescence, although these changes did not reach statistical significance (Figs.  and ). These findings suggested that IL-10 stimulation for 8 h promoted M2 polarization in BMDMs. We next examined DDIT4 expression under these conditions. Compared to normoxia, hypoxia-reoxygenation led to elevated DDIT4 mRNA and protein levels, with IL-10 further boosting DDIT4 expression (Fig.  ). The upregulation pattern of DDIT4 corresponded with markers of M2 polarization in macrophages. To confirm the effect of DDIT4, we used small interfering RNA (siRNA) to knock down its expression, with successful knockdown verified by PCR and Western blot (Fig.  ). In BMDMs with reduced DDIT4 expression, IL-10-induced M2 polarization was attenuated, as indicated by decreased mRNA levels of CD206 and Arg-1, lower Arg-1 immunofluorescence intensity, and reduced MFI of CD206 (Figs.  and ). These results suggested that IL-10 drived M2 polarization in BMDMs by DDIT4 under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions.
 In this study, we observed that IL-10 increased DDIT4 mRNA levels, potentially by promoting its transcription or enhancing mRNA stability. To further investigate the regulatory mechanisms of DDIT4, we conducted a correlation analysis. These results revealed that the gene most closely correlated with DDIT4 was Arid3a, a transcription factor (Fig.  ). Bioinformatics predictions confirmed Arid3a as a potential transcription factor for DDIT4 (Fig.  ). Consequently, we decided to further examine the specific role of Arid3a in the regulation of DDIT4 expression by IL-10. As shown in Fig.  , hypoxia-reoxygenation increased the mRNA and protein levels of Arid3a in BMDMs compared to those in normoxic conditions, and IL-10 further enhanced Arid3a expression in BMDMs under hypoxia-reoxygenation. Knocking down Arid3a expression in BMDMs using si-Arid3a revealed that, compared to si-NC-treated BMDMs, the ability of IL-10 to induce DDIT4 expression under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions was reduced (Fig.  ). This indicated that IL-10 enhanced the expression of DDIT4 by upregulating Arid3a. The JASPAR database was used to predict the two specific binding sites within the DDIT4 promoter region where Arid3a was most likely to bind (Fig.  ). ChIP-qPCR analysis showed a significant enrichment of Arid3a at site1 and site2 compared to the IgG control (Fig.  ). The luciferase reporter assay indicated that mutations in either site1 or site2 significantly reduced the activity of the DDIT4 promoter in the presence of Arid3a, suggesting that Arid3a directly enhanced the transcription of DDIT4 through these binding sites (Fig.  ). Under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions, IL-10 significantly increased CD206 and Arg-1 mRNA levels in BMDMs; however, this effect was markedly reduced following Arid3a knockdown (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence staining for Arg-1 further supported this finding, showing reduced Arg-1 expression in Arid3a-knockdown BMDMs under IL-10 stimulation (Fig.  ). Additionally, flow cytometry revealed that IL-10 induced an increase in the MFI of CD206 in BMDMs, which was significantly suppressed with Arid3a knockdown (Fig.  ). These findings suggested that IL-10 enhanced DDIT4 expression by directly upregulating Arid3a, thereby promoting M2 macrophage polarization.
Based on our in vitro observations that IL-10 promoted DDIT4 transcription by upregulating Arid3a, thereby driving macrophage M2 polarization, we next sought to validate this mechanism in vivo. Specifically, we utilized a LIRI mouse model to investigate the effects of IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells after sulfatide intervention on the expression of DDIT4 and Arid3a in mouse macrophages and their M2 polarization. First, Jα18−/− mice were intranasally instilled with clodronate liposomes to deplete AMs. After 48 h, 106 iNKT cells from WT or IL-10−/− mice were adoptively transferred via tail vein injection, and 2×10^6 BMDMs transfected with si-NC, si-DDIT4, or si-Arid3a were administered intranasally. Twelve hours later, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 20 µg sulfatide or an equivalent volume of vehicle control. Six hours after this, the mice underwent lung ischemia-reperfusion surgery or thoracotomy alone, followed by sample collection for analysis (Fig.  ). As shown in Fig.  , both mRNA and protein levels of Arid3a in adoptively transferred BMDMs of LIRI mice were significantly higher compared to sham mice, and sulfatide further enhanced Arid3a expression in these macrophages. However, when iNKT cells were unable to secrete IL-10, the stimulatory effect of sulfatide on Arid3a expression in adoptively transferred BMDMs was diminished (Fig.  ). Moreover, even with DDIT4 knockdown in macrophages, sulfatide continued to promote Arid3a expression in adoptively transferred BMDMs of LIRI mice (Fig.  ). These findings indicated that sulfatide promoted Arid3a expression in lung macrophages through IL-10 produced by iNKT cells, whereas DDIT4 did not affect Arid3a expression. Similarly, Fig.  showed that compared to sham mice, DDIT4 expression in adoptively transferred BMDMs of LIRI mice was also significantly elevated, with further enhancement by sulfatide. However, this enhancement was reduced when IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells was absent, and it was notably weakened with Arid3a knockdown (Fig.  ). These results demonstrated that iNKT cells regulated DDIT4 expression via IL-10–mediated upregulation of Arid3a following sulfatide intervention. In addition, compared to vehicle intervention, sulfatide significantly promoted the mRNA and protein levels of Arg-1 and the mRNA of Fizz1 in adoptively transferred BMDMs of LIRI mice (Fig.  ). However, in mice with IL-10−/− iNKT cells, this enhancing effect of sulfatide on Arg-1 and Fizz1 was diminished, indicating that sulfatide boosted Arg-1 and Fizz1 expression in BMDMs via IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells (Fig.  ). Furthermore, after knocking down DDIT4 or Arid3a in BMDMs, there was no significant difference in the effect of sulfatide on Arg-1 and Fizz1 expression between LIRI mice with WT iNKT cells and LIRI mice with IL-10-/- iNKT cells (Fig.  ). As noted earlier, IL-10 promoted DDIT4 and Arid3a expression. These findings suggested that IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells might promote M2 polarization of macrophages in airway largely through DDIT4 and Arid3a. Furthermore, considering that Arid3a acted as a transcription factor for DDIT4 and promoted its expression in mice, but DDIT4 did not affect Arid3a levels, this implied that sulfatide might enhance Arid3a expression by stimulating IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells. This increase in Arid3a levels subsequently raised DDIT4 levels, which promoted M2 polarization of macrophages.
 

## YES1 as a potential mediator of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cell regulation of iNKT cell function
We further preliminarily explore how sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells influence iNKT cell function. Three single-cell RNA-seq datasets of murine iNKT cells from the GEO database were analyzed: splenic iNKT cells from untreated mice, splenic iNKT cells four weeks after α-GalCer stimulation, and adipose iNKT cells from untreated mice. α-GalCer could induce memory-like iNKT cells capable of secreting IL-10 , , whereas adipose iNKT cells were more prone to secrete IL-10 compared with splenic cells , with untreated splenic iNKT cells serving as a baseline. Comparison across these conditions allows us to identify key subsets and candidate genes associated with IL-10 secretion. iNKT cells can be classified into four subsets: NKT1 (high expression of T-box transcription factor, T-bet, encoded by TBX21), NKT2 (high expression of promyelocytic leukemia zinc finger, PLZF, encoded by ZBTB16), NKT17 (high expression of retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor γt, RORγt, encoded by RORC), and NKT10 (high expression of E4-binding protein 4, E4BP4, encoded by NFIL3), with NKT10 being capable of producing IL-10 , . Our analysis similarly identified four subsets across the three datasets (Supplementary Fig.  ), with NFIL3 expression most prominent in cluster 1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, the proportion of cluster 1 was markedly increased in α-GalCer-stimulated splenic iNKT cells and in adipose iNKT cells compared with untreated splenic cells (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that NKT10 was largely concentrated within this subset. Based on this, we performed intersection analysis of differentially expressed genes between cluster 1 and other clusters, as well as genes upregulated in α-GalCer–stimulated splenic or adipose iNKT cells relative to untreated splenic iNKT cells, yielding 96 shared genes potentially linked to IL-10 production (Supplementary Fig.  ). Among these genes, YES1 was of particular interest, as previous studies showed it could promote T cell differentiation toward IL-10–producing phenotypes . Functional validation revealed that sulfatide significantly upregulated YES1 expression in lung iNKT cells during LIRI (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, inhibition of YES1 with Dasatinib abolished the effects of sulfatide-induced IL-10 production and IFN-γ suppression by iNKT cells, as well as the reduction in numbers of iNKT cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggested that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells might modulate iNKT cell function via YES1.

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that sulfatide alleviated lung ischemia-reperfusion injury by promoting M2 polarization of alveolar macrophages in an iNKT cell–dependent manner. Mechanistically, sulfatide activated type II NKT cells, which in turn stimulated IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells. IL-10 from iNKT cells subsequently upregulated Arid3a in macrophages, enhancing DDIT4 expression and driving M2 polarization. The absence of either type II NKT cells, iNKT cells, or IL-10 markedly impaired this protective effect, highlighting the critical role of this cellular and molecular cascade. These findings not only elucidated a previously unrecognized interplay between type II NKT cells and iNKT cells in regulating macrophage function during LIRI, but also revealed the IL-10–Arid3a–DDIT4 axis as a key mediator of M2 polarization. Overall, our results provided mechanistic insights and suggested that targeting the type II NKT–iNKT–M2 macrophage pathway might represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for mitigating lung ischemia-reperfusion injury.
Alveolar macrophages are central to the onset and progression of LIRI. These cells can polarize into an inflammatory M1 phenotype, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines that aid in pathogen defense but also contribute to tissue damage. Alternatively, they can shift to an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, providing anti-inflammatory effects that help alleviate LIRI . This functional plasticity makes AMs a promising therapeutic target for managing LIRI. Our data showed that sulfatide promoted M2 polarization in AMs, with a corresponding reduction in lung injury in the mouse LIRI model. Importantly, adoptive transfer of alveolar macrophages derived from sulfatide-pretreated LIRI mice effectively alleviated lung ischemia-reperfusion injury, highlighting the critical role of AMs in mediating sulfatide’s protective effects against lung ischemia-reperfusion injury. Interestingly, we also found that AMs depletion with clodronate liposomes exacerbated LIRI, consistent with findings from Nakamura T. et al. . However, Prakash A. et al. reported that AM depletion alleviated LIRI in mice . What could explain this discrepancy? Upon closer analysis, we noted differences in the methods of clodronate liposome administration across studies. Our study, alongside that of Nakamura et al., employed intranasal or intratracheal administration, which selectively depleted AMs. Conversely, Prakash et al. used intraperitoneal injection, which depleted not only AMs but also circulating monocytes and monocyte-derived macrophages. AMs not only serve as a primary defense mechanism in the lungs but also play a crucial role in maintaining airway homeostasis by limiting inflammatory responses. Consequently, AM depletion might lead to enhanced neutrophil recruitment, thereby exacerbating lung injury . In contrast, monocytes promote neutrophil migration and extravasation in LIRI via the C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) - C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) axis – . Additionally, IL-1β secreted by monocytes induce pyroptosis in human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (HPMEC) via the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R)/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB)/NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) pathway . These mechanisms may help explain the divergent outcomes observed across studies, but further investigation is warranted.
NKT cells rapidly secret cytokines upon stimulation, playing a crucial role in inflammation. Previous studies have shown that iNKT cells exacerbate LIRI . However, the role of type II NKT cells in LIRI remains poorly understood. Sulfatide, a glycolipid, binds to CD1d to form a complex specifically recognized by TCR on type II NKT cells . Our data demonstrated that, in a mouse model of LIRI, sulfatide activated type II NKT cells within the lung. Additionally, in LIRI mice lacking type II NKT cells, sulfatide failed to reduce lung injury and promote M2 polarization of AMs. Our findings provide evidence that, in the context of LIRI, sulfatide alleviates lung injury and induces M2 polarization of AMs in a type II NKT cell-dependent manner. In summary, these results suggest a potential mechanism wherein sulfatide prevent LIRI by increasing M2 polarization of AMs through type II NKT cells stimulation. However, sulfatide did not protect lung injury and promote M2 polarization in AMs of Jα18−/− mice (which lack iNKT cells) even when they were subjected to LIRI. Interestingly, adoptive transfer of iNKT cells obtained from sulfatide-pretreated LIRI mice produced the opposite effect: it promoted M2 polarization in alveolar macrophages and attenuated lung injury in CD1d−/− mice (which lack iNKT cells and type II NKT cells). These findings strongly suggest that the capacity of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells to enhance M2 polarization in AMs, thereby mitigating LIRI, depends on the presence of lung iNKT cells.
Studies have shown that activation of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells induces anergy in iNKT cells , effectively preventing inflammatory liver disease , hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury , experimental autoimmune hepatitis , and asthma . Compared to normal iNKT cells, these anergic iNKT cells show functional changes, including reduced proliferative capacity, decreased IL-4 and IFN-γ production, and increased IL-10 secretion , . This phenomenon is similar to what has been observed in Th1 cells, where IL-10-producing Th1 cells can arise from IFN-γ-producing Th1 cells . This functional shift is mediated by CD46, which interacts with the serine/threonine kinase SPAK to upregulate IL-10 production, a process that is dependent on IL-2 , . Our study demonstrated that sulfatide significantly reduced the number of iNKT cells and altered their cytokine profile—increasing IL-10 secretion and decreasing IFN-γ levels—in LIRI mice. This effect might be related to increased YES1 expression. As a unique subset of T cells, whether sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells regulate iNKT cells via the CD46/IL-2 axis and whether this axis is associated with YES1 expression require further investigation. Moreover, previous studies have shown that IFN-γ can inhibit IL-10 secretion , . Recently, Hu X. et al. further demonstrated that in macrophages, IFN-γ inhibited the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase–protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt) signaling pathways, leading to reduced cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) activation, increased glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) activity, and suppression of activator protein 1 (AP-1) function, which in turn decreased IL-10 production . Therefore, it remains to be further studied if the reduction of IFN-γ secretion by iNKT cells contributes to the increased IL-10 secretion.
Additionally, the effect of sulfatide was notably diminished in Jα18−/− LIRI mice which recieved IL-10−/− iNKT cells, as evidenced by less alleviation of lung injury and weaker promotion of M2 polarization in alveolar macrophages. Furthermore, in co-culture experiments with iNKT cells and BMDMs, we observed that lung iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated IL-10−/− LIRI mice exhibited a reduced capacity to induce M2 polarization of BMDMs compared to lung iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice. In addition, previous studies have shown that IL-10 can promote macrophage M2 polarization . These findings collectively suggest that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells induce M2 polarization of macrophages by promoting IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells. Interestingly, when BMDMs lacked the IL-10 receptor alpha (IL-10RA), the effect of lung iNKT cells from sulfatide-treated WT LIRI mice to induce M2 polarization in BMDMs was also significantly reduced, supporting that IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells, induced by sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells, acts directly on BMDMs to promote M2 polarization. In summary, our findings strongly suggest that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells, which then directly acts on macropahges to induce M2 polarization. In addition to promoting IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells, we found that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells also led to reduced IFN-γ production by iNKT cells. Previous studies have shown that sulfatide-activated type II NKT cells alleviate hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting IFN-γ secretion from iNKT cells . Therefore, sulfatide-activated type II NKT cells may mitigate LIRI by reducing IFN-γ production by iNKT cells. However, as this study primarily focuses on the role of IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells induced by sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells in LIRI, we have not directly tested this potential mechanism. Future studies could explore the effect of reduced IFN-γ in modulating inflammation and injury on LIRI, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the immunoregulatory mechanisms of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells in this context. It is also worth noting that, although sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells inhibit IFN-γ secretion by iNKT cells, type II NKT cells produce IFN-γ upon activation by sulfatide. So why can sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells alleviate LIRI? First, the role of IFN-γ secreted by iNKT cells in LIRI is still unclear. Secondly, studies have shown that, compared to type I NKT cells, type II NKT cells produce lower levels of IFN-γ when their TCRs are stimulated, which may be the reason .
DDIT4, also known as regulated in development and DNA damage responses 1 (REDD1), is regulated by various physiological and pathological conditions, including hypoxia, oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, DNA damage, and glucocorticoid stimulation. DDIT4 inhibits the activity of the tuberous sclerosis complex 1/2 (TSC1/TSC2) complex, thereby suppressing mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) activation, making it a negative regulator of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) . Excessive activation of mTOR inhibited the response of M2-like macrophages to IL-4 or other M2 stimuli , , whereas inhibition of mTOR promoted the conversion to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype . Previous studies have shown that IL-10 upregulate DDIT4 expression and promote oxidative phosphorylation in macrophages through mTOR inhibition . Consistent with these findings, our results also indicated that IL-10 upregulated DDIT4 expression under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions, and that DDIT4 promoted M2 polarization of BMDMs. Furthermore, several independent studies support a crucial role for DDIT4 in macrophage M2 polarization. Li K. et al. demonstrated that paeonol upregulated DDIT4 expression in macrophages, thereby inducing M2 polarization and accelerating diabetic wound healing . Similarly, Yang H. et al. reported that lysophosphatidic acid enhanced DDIT4, leading to decreased miscarriage risk by enhancing M2 polarization of decidual macrophages . However, a study in a model of diabetic atherosclerosis found that IL-6 and TNF-α production was reduced when DDIT4 was knocked out in macrophages, which stood in contrast to our conclusion . Regarding the function of DDIT4, it has been proposed that transient induction of DDIT4 rapidly inhibits mTORC1 activity, conferring beneficial outcomes within a limited time window. In contrast, sustained expression of DDIT4 may lead to adverse consequences due to the activation of complex cellular compensatory responses . To resolve this discrepancy, further analysis of the GSE122070 dataset revealed that DDIT4 mRNA expression in BMDMs was elevated at 6 h following LPS and IL-10 co-treatment compared to LPS treatment alone, with no significant differences observed at the 3- or 12-h time points (data not shown). These results suggest that IL-10 induces DDIT4 expression in a transient manner, which may explain the differences in results obtained under different experimental conditions. Additionally, our data indicated that under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions in BMDMs, Arid3a was a transcription factor for DDIT4, promoting its expression and leading to macrophage M2 polarization. Similarly, Wang et al. also found that Arid3a promoted M2 polarization of THP-1 . Earlier researches have suggested that DDIT4 is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) under hypoxia , and IL-10 regulates DDIT4 through signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) . Therefore, future studies can further explore whether HIF-1α, STAT3, and Arid3a cooperatively regulate IL-10-induced DDIT4 expression in BMDMs under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions.
We also found that sulfatide promoted the expression of Arid3a and DDIT4 in macrophages in LIRI mice, but this effect was diminished in LIRI mice where iNKT cells could not secrete IL-10. This suggests that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote Arid3a and DDIT4 expression through IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells. Moreover, when si-Arid3a-transfected BMDMs were adoptively transferred into LIRI mice with depleted AMs, sulfatide failed to upregulate DDIT4 levels in BMDMs. In contrast, when si-DDIT4-transfected BMDMs were transferred into the same model, sulfatide still promoted Arid3a expression. This indicates that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells regulate DDIT4 expression via Arid3a, while Arid3a expression is independent of DDIT4. Furthermore, when BMDMs transfected with either si-Arid3a or si-DDIT4 were adoptively transferred into LIRI mice with depleted AMs, the ability of sulfatide to promote M2 polarization of macrophage in the airway was reduced, suggesting that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells promote macrophage M2 polarization through Arid3a and DDIT4. In conclusion, these findings preliminarily suggest that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells enhance Arid3a expression through IL-10 secretion by iNKT cells, which subsequently upregulates DDIT4 and ultimately promotes M2 polarization of macrophages.
However, our study has several limitations. First, the findings are derived from mouse models, and further studies are needed to determine whether these mechanisms are applicable to humans. Due to experimental constraints, we are unable to generate conditional knockout mice for DDIT4 and Arid3a in alveolar macrophages. Although we address this by adoptively transferring si-DDIT4- or si-Arid3a-transfected BMDMs into alveolar macrophage–depleted mice, more comprehensive in vivo validation is required to fully elucidate the roles of DDIT4 and Arid3a. Second, the Jα18−/− mice used in this study are from an earlier strain reported to have restricted TCRα diversity . Nonetheless, our adoptive transfer experiments demonstrate that sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells act through iNKT cells to promote alveolar macrophage M2 polarization and attenuate LIRI, supporting the robustness of our conclusions. Third, CD1d−/− mice are maintained on a BALB/c background, whereas Jα18−/− mice are on a C57BL/6 background. Although strain differences may influence immune responses, appropriate controls are included throughout, thereby minimizing their impact on data interpretation. Thus, our central conclusions remain valid.

## Conclusion
Our study highlights the role of sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells in mitigating LIRI. Specifically, we demonstrated that sulfatide-activated type II NKT cells alleviate LIRI by promoting M2 polarization of AMs through IL-10 secretion from iNKT cells. Moreover, our findings indicate that IL-10 secreted by iNKT cells upregulates Arid3a expression in macrophages, subsequently increasing DDIT4 levels, which plays a crucial role in enhancing macrophage M2 polarization. These insights suggest that targeting M2 polarization of AMs through sulfatide-reactive type II NKT cells may serve as a promising strategy for LIRI intervention. This therapeutic approach has the potential to improve LIRI outcomes and paves the way for future clinical investigations.
