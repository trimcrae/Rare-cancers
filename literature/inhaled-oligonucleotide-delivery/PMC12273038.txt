## Abstract
The architecture of the mammalian lung is both intricate and distinct. The respiratory system consists of a complex network of semirigid airway tubes, stretching from the trachea to the alveoli, highly vascularized sacs responsible for gas exchange. This system demands precise regulation. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) functions as a receptor and regulatory hub for various cellular processes, including metabolism, proliferation, and autophagy, and plays a pivotal role in lung development and regeneration, continuing to influence cellular processes into early childhood. Over the past decade, studies have identified abnormally elevated mTOR activity in adult lung diseases such as acute lung injury and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, leading to the approval of mTOR inhibitors for clinical use. However, during fetal lung development and the postnatal period, mTOR often exhibits a dual role. Its dynamic expression requires careful adaptation to temporal and spatial variations. The safety and efficacy of mTOR inhibitors during these developmental windows remain uncertain, as the role of mTOR becomes increasingly complex in response to the dramatic changes in lung tissue. This review aims to analyze the regulatory mechanisms and functional roles of the mTOR pathway throughout various stages of lung development and adult pulmonary diseases, highlighting the need for caution in using mTOR inhibitors during critical developmental phases. Careful evaluation is essential when considering pharmaceutical interventions for abnormal lung development and pediatric pulmonary disorders.
Keywords: Lung development, Asthma, ALI, COPD, mTOR inhibitors, Rapamycin, Autophagy

## Background
The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) plays a vital role throughout the human life cycle, from the formation of fertilized eggs to embryonic and neonatal lung development, and later in conditions like cancer and geriatric diseases such as Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). While mTOR itself rarely mutates in cancer, it is easily influenced by oncogenic factors within specific microenvironments and is frequently activated in immune disorders, obesity, and aging-related conditions [ ]. Rapamycin, an mTOR-specific inhibitor, was approved in 1999 for preventing kidney transplant rejection and has proven effective in treating lung diseases like lymphangioleiomyomatosis [ , ] However, the complexity of mTOR feedback regulation and its involvement in multiple signaling pathways limit its clinical applications. mTOR inhibitors are associated with several adverse effects, with dyslipidemia being the most common, along with pro-inflammatory responses. Moreover, the interplay between the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) pathways adds to the complexity of regulation [ , ]. Numerous studies are underway to develop inhibitors with improved selectivity, reduced toxicity, and enhanced pharmacokinetic profiles. Modifying the delivery method could mitigate side effects, underscoring the need for personalized treatment approaches.
Human lung development encompasses 24 levels of bronchial branching, coordination between bronchoalveolar structures and pulmonary vasculature, and progresses through the embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, alveolar, and microvascular stages of maturation [ , ]. This intricate process is essential for forming a blood-gas barrier to enable effective gas exchange in the lungs. Different mTOR complexes are engaged at specific times and locations during this development. As the cellular microenvironment dictates the fate of developing lung cells, mTOR senses environmental cues such as nutrient levels, growth factors, and oxidative stress. In response, mTOR governs key processes including cell proliferation, autophagy, apoptosis, senescence, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Although mTOR inhibitors generally show protective effects in adult-related diseases, their roles in fetal and neonatal lungs are more variable and complex, requiring cautious application based on developmental needs.
This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the functions and regulatory mechanisms of mTOR in lung development, from early stages to aging. While mTOR may not be the primary driver, it serves as a central regulatory hub. The aim is to elucidate how mTOR coordinates lung development across different temporal and spatial contexts, offering insights into its potential as a therapeutic target for lung development and disease. Given the abundance of literature on the mTOR-cancer relationship, this review will not delve further into that aspect.

## mTOR across the lifespan of the lung
The human lung follows distinct developmental stages, each marked by unique physiological states. mTOR plays a pivotal role across the entire lifespan, influencing processes from fetal lung development to childhood and adolescent asthma, adult pulmonary fibrosis, COPD in aging, and pulmonary infections at various life stages. Aberrant mTOR regulation, triggered by various factors, can significantly disrupt lung development and contribute to a broad spectrum of respiratory diseases (Fig.  ).

## Fetal and childhood period

## Fetal lung development
Lung growth and development are highly complex processes that demand the precise coordination of cellular activities, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and cell death [ , ]. mTOR is a key regulator in this context, governing these cellular activities, supplying energy, and sustaining the pluripotency of embryonic stem cells throughout the developmental process [ ]. Both mTORC1 and mTORC2 are active from the earliest stages, including the formation of the fertilized egg and its initial mitotic division [ – ]. In mTOR-/- mice, impaired cell proliferation in embryonic and extraembryonic compartments leads to lethality soon after implantation [ ].
Fetal lung development relies on the interconnected growth of bronchioles, alveoli, and the vascular system, all modulated by mTOR signaling, which controls nutrient sensing and autophagy. The human lung contains 24 levels of bronchial branching, which form and fold new tissue layers during embryogenesis. Additionally, the strict coordination between bronchoalveolar structures and pulmonary vasculature is essential for establishing the blood-gas barrier needed for effective gas exchange. Lung maturation occurs through the embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, alveolar, and microvascular stages, continuing even postnatally [ ] (Fig.  ). Notably, mTORC1 and mTORC2 have opposing roles in angiogenesis: mTORC1 suppresses it, while mTORC2 promotes it [ , ]. Achieving the right balance between these two complexes is essential for proper fetal lung development. In cases of gestational diabetes, the dynamic assembly of mTORC1 and mTORC2 shifts toward mTORC1, impairing fetal lung vascular formation and disrupting lung development [ ]. mTORC1 activation drives the differentiation of stem cells in a sequential manner and influences tracheal extension and branching by modulating mitochondrial function during lung development, ensuring proper pulmonary formation [ ].
 A key factor promoting tracheal growth is fibroblast growth factor-10 (FGF-10), which is highly expressed at the developing tip of the trachea. Similar to FGF-10, mTORC1 exerts strong signaling activity within the progenitor cells of the anterior airway epithelium. Rapamycin inhibition of mTORC1 results in cell cycle arrest in this region, leading to reduced formation of new tracheal branches [ ]. Moreover, mTORC1 participates in tracheal branching via Extracellular-regulated kinase 1/2(ERK1/2), which acts as a downstream effector of Spry2 and Shh. Interestingly, Spry2 plays a dual role in this context: it activates ERK1/2 to inhibit mTORC1 and promote branching morphogenesis, while also activating mTORC1 to suppress branch formation [ , ]. Thus, the “time-driven” expression of mTORC1 is critical for regulating the complex processes of tracheal extension and branching during lung development. Understanding the specific pathways through which mTOR modulates various cell populations at distinct developmental stages is therefore essential for further insights into lung formation.
The sprouting of fetal pulmonary blood vessels is primarily driven by the hypoxic environment of the maternal body, with deviations in oxygen partial pressure adversely affecting angiogenesis and potentially causing vascular malformations [ , ]. The transcription factor hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), which detects low oxygen levels, is composed of hypoxia-regulated and nuclear translocation subunits that are believed to depend on mTORC1. Vascular development is mediated by epithelial cell secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A). Under hypoxia, the hypoxia-regulated HIF subunit stabilizes and translocates to the nucleus through a translocation subunit, thereby regulating VEGF-A production [ ]. In lung tissue, three HIF types are expressed: HIF1 plays a key role in regulating vascular signal transduction; HIF2 is critical for tracheal maturation and air-blood barrier formation; and HIF3 serves as a competitive inhibitor of HIF1 and HIF2. Thus, the HIF system operates as a “time-driven” factor, orchestrating the cyclical branching of pulmonary vasculature [ ]. mTOR likely supports this by stabilizing HIF, modulating its transcription and translation, and providing feedback through mTORC1 [ , ].
Lung development requires synchronized tracheal and vascular growth. The mTORC1 pathway, influenced by Spry2, regulates VEGF-A through direct control of HIF-CBP/p300 transcription. As a result, the FGF-10-Spry2-mTORC1-STAT3/HIF-1-VEGF-A signaling cascade coordinates tracheal and vascular growth and morphogenesis [ , ]. This coordinated development is essential for expanding the surface area of the air-blood barrier, ensuring efficient gas exchange in neonates post-birth. Fetal lung development also involves the secretion of fluid into the airways to maintain the lung’s three-dimensional structure, with rapid postnatal fluid clearance essential for complete gas exchange in newborns. This absorption process is mediated by Na + channels in lung epithelial cells, which are regulated by catecholamines. Inhibition of mTORC2 can reduce Na + currents, hindering fluid absorption and potentially leading to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and “wet lungs“ [ ]. mTORC1, on the other hand, regulates this process by modulating the translation of Na + channel transcripts [ ].
Maintaining alveolar stability relies heavily on the secretion and function of pulmonary surfactant, a process also linked to mTOR signaling. A study using mice with a targeted deletion of the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1) gene via fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4)-Cre demonstrated that TSC1-mTORC1 is likely critical for the synthesis of surfactant proteins A and B [ ]. These findings suggest that mTORC1 acts as a mechanical sensor in lung epithelial cells, and its activation during mechanical ventilation can adversely affect lung function. Specifically, the deletion of TSC2, a negative regulator of mTORC1, in epithelial cells compromises lung compliance during mechanical ventilation, while mTORC1 inhibition has been shown to reduce physiological lung injury by preventing surfactant dysfunction [ ]. Despite this, the precise mechanisms by which mTOR regulates lung compliance and surfactant activity remain unclear. Although autophagy has been identified as playing an evolutionarily conserved role in the maturation of lamellar bodies in alveolar epithelial cells, how mTOR influences this process has yet to be elucidated [ ].
Thus, during fetal development, mTOR senses external signals to direct the coordinated growth of bronchi and vasculature in the lungs. A deeper understanding of mTOR’s temporal and spatial regulation will be essential for developing strategies to prevent and treat disorders stemming from abnormal fetal and neonatal lung development.
mTOR is pivotal in lung development spanning from embryonic to postnatal stages, by regulating the extension and branching of pulmonary blood vessels and airways, as well as coordinating their growth. A critical balance between mTORC1 and mTORC2 is necessary for these processes. ## BPD
Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), a common respiratory disorder in premature infants, is characterized by long-term respiratory complications that can persist into adulthood. Despite progress in understanding the mechanisms underlying BPD—such as dysregulated proliferation, apoptosis, transformation, and transdifferentiation—targeted and effective treatments remain elusive. Accumulation of autophagosomes in the lungs of BPD mice has been linked to impaired pulmonary surfactant secretion during disease progression [ ]. Additionally, elevated light chain 3B-II (LC3B-II) levels can interfere with the transition of alveolar type II epithelial cells into type I cells, further affecting lung development [ , ]. Research indicates that this autophagosome buildup results from impaired autophagic flux and reduced degradation, triggered by excessive oxygen exposure, a common factor in BPD development. Notably, rapamycin administration can partially restore autophagic flux and support lung development under these conditions [ ]. Similarly, Rptor inhibitors have shown potential in protecting against oxygen-induced lung injury in neonatal mice by enhancing autophagy and reducing apoptosis, consistent with the earlier findings [ ]. Recent studies in a baboon model of BPD have highlighted the time-dependent regulation of autophagy, largely driven by decreased upstream Adenosine 5‘-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity and increased mTORC1 pathway activation. This dysregulation of autophagy plays a significant role in BPD pathogenesis [ ]. Activation of mTORC1 disrupts the critical alveolarization phase of lung development, leading to simplified lung architecture and impaired function, which exacerbates the structural and functional deficits associated with BPD.

## Asthma
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder that primarily affects the airways, with airway remodeling being a key feature of the disease. Research dating back to 2003 has identified elevated mTOR expression in asthma mouse models, strongly associated with airway remodeling [ ]. Studies have shown that reducing p-Akt and p-mTOR levels can mitigate airway smooth muscle fibrosis, subsequently inhibiting asthma progression [ , ].
mTOR plays a critical role in maintaining the homeostasis and function of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) within the pulmonary system. It regulates the accumulation and composition of specific APC subsets in the lungs. mTOR deficiency in Cluster of differentiation 11b + T cells (CD11b+) dendritic cells alters the immune response, leading to a Th17/neutrophil phenotype, increased IgE levels, and the development of allergic asthma [ ]. Clinical and animal studies have confirmed a marked increase in serum mTOR levels during acute asthma attacks, which decrease during remission. Rapamycin has demonstrated efficacy in reducing asthma symptoms, comparable to budesonide, a conventional asthma medication, suggesting that targeting mTOR may be a promising therapeutic approach [ – ].
mTOR-dependent autophagy is intricately linked to inflammation and immune regulation, playing a significant role in airway inflammation and asthma [ , ]. Although the precise mechanisms by which rapamycin exerts its protective effects, particularly through autophagy, remain unclear, it has been shown to alleviate inflammation and restore cytokine balance between Th17/Treg and Th1/Th2 responses [ ]. For instance, Hua [ ] demonstrated that rapamycin effectively inhibits eosinophil differentiation, providing protection against allergic airway inflammation. Additionally, Mushaben’s research highlighted that rapamycin reduces IgE levels and CD4 + T cell numbers in asthma models without significantly affecting airway hyperresponsiveness [ ]. Administering rapamycin early in the development of dust mite-induced asthma has been shown to reduce airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and goblet cell hyperplasia. However, delayed treatment (2 weeks after a 4-week induction period) did not produce the same protective effects [ ]. Rapamycin has also been found to ameliorate cigarette smoke-induced asthma by counteracting the cellular senescence induced by the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway, thus reducing allergic asthma symptoms [ ].
Recent research has revealed that in the early stages of asthma, Interleukin 33(IL-33) may suppress mTOR and potentially trigger autophagy in airway epithelial cells. This autophagy activation leads to the production of pro-allergic cytokines, promoting type II immune responses and perpetuating airway inflammation associated with asthma [ ]. Inhibiting autophagy through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway has shown some therapeutic efficacy in asthma treatment [ ]. Notably, the Silent information regulator 1(SIRT1) inhibitor EX-527 significantly reduced airway inflammation in allergic mice by activating the mTOR pathway and suppressing autophagy [ ]. Similarly, the administration of chloroquine (50 mg/kg) effectively attenuated allergic airway inflammation by inhibiting autophagy through mTOR activation in allergic mice [ ].
Given these findings, it is plausible that rapamycin could enhance early inflammation control and reduce airway remodeling in asthma. However, its effectiveness may diminish once the disease has fully manifested. The variability in experimental outcomes involving rapamycin might be due to differences in treatment protocols, subject characteristics, or medication duration. In early asthma models, mTOR inhibition may act as a protective feedback mechanism following initial injury, rather than being the cause of the injury itself. Thus, further investigation is necessary to clarify these mechanisms and optimize therapeutic strategies.

## Adult period

## Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal disease characterized by lung tissue scarring due to excessive collagen deposition, leading to the gradual decline of lung function [ – ]. Although the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved pirfenidone and nintedanib for IPF treatment, their therapeutic efficacy remains limited [ , ]. A genome-wide association study revealed a strong correlation between reduced DEPTOR expression and an increased risk of IPF, underscoring the critical role of the mTOR signaling pathway in pulmonary fibrosis development [ ].
The activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway plays a central role in IPF by promoting excessive collagen synthesis in myofibroblasts, which accelerates fibrosis progression [ , ]. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a specific marker of pathogenic fibroblasts, distinguishes them from non-pathogenic fibroblasts. In 2020, researchers developed a ligand targeting FAP, successfully delivering PI3K inhibitors to collagen-producing fibroblasts, thereby reducing collagen production. This innovation presents a promising therapeutic avenue for IPF [ ]. Furthermore, studies have shown that fibroblasts lacking CD148 exhibit hyperactivation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, decreased autophagy, and accumulation of p62 protein aggregates, which exacerbate pulmonary fibrosis. Therefore, CD148 upregulation can counteract fibrosis, providing a potential new treatment strategy for IPF [ ].
Chronic autophagic stress can drive fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts [ ], with reactive oxygen species (ROS) serving as key inducers of prolonged autophagy via mTORC2. This pathway promotes cell aging and differentiation into myofibroblasts, contributing to fibrosis [ ]. Additionally, mTOR activation can damage lung epithelial cells and facilitate EMT, further exacerbating the pathological progression of IPF [ , ]. In summary, the complex role of the mTOR signaling pathway in pulmonary fibrosis calls for further investigation into the relationship between autophagy and fibrosis, as well as the interactions between the mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes. Understanding these dynamics may offer novel insights into the development of more effective IPF treatments.

## Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)
Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is characterized by the abnormal infiltration of smooth muscle-like cells into the lung parenchyma, leading to cystic lung destruction [ ]. Mutations in the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) genes in tumor cells activate the mTOR pathway, resulting in uncontrolled cell proliferation and disruption of lymphatic vessel formation [ ]. This ultimately leads to lung remodeling and respiratory failure. Recent advances in the molecular understanding of LAM have identified new diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, alongside more effective treatment strategies. The FDA has approved sirolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, for the treatment of LAM, and ongoing research is aimed at optimizing the use of mTORC1 inhibitors for disease management [ , ].

## Pulmonary hypertension
The mTOR pathway is also significantly involved in pulmonary vascular remodeling, particularly in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [ ]. mTOR activation occurs in the right ventricle (RV) in response to pressure overload, but prolonged activation can impair both the structure and function of the RV. While both mTORC1 and mTORC2 are implicated in pulmonary vascular remodeling, mTORC1 is thought to play a more dominant role [ ]. Since current mTOR inhibitors cannot selectively target mTORC1 or mTORC2, inhibition of mTORC2 may diminish the overall therapeutic effect. This challenge can be addressed by inhibiting downstream changes in mTORC2 activity. For example, imatinib, which inhibits Platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFRs), enhances the efficacy of PAH treatments by mitigating mTORC2-related effects [ , ].
Further, tascinorilimus and SIRT1 have been shown to prevent chronic pulmonary hypertension by reducing mTOR activity, with SIRT1 achieving this effect by restoring TSC2 expression [ , ]. In addition, BI-749,327, a selective Transient Receptor Potential Canonical6(TRPC6) blocker, has emerged as a potential treatment for respiratory diseases like PAH and PH, effectively suppressing PDGF and serum-induced AKT and mTOR phosphorylation in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) [ ]. Research has also indicated that the absence of Methylation-Controlled J protein(MCJ) exerts protective effects against post-hypoxia pulmonary hypertension, mediated by the ROS/mTOR/HIF-1α axis, making it a promising therapeutic target [ ]. Inhibiting Src (Tyr416) phosphorylation with drugs like Protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) or Berberine (BBR) has been shown to suppress hypoxia-inducible factor-1 α (HIF-1 α) expression via the Akt/mTOR pathway, thus preventing the proliferation and migration of PAH-PASMCs [ ].
However, despite rapamycin’s potential effectiveness in treating pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure in younger populations, studies suggest it does not yield the same benefits in elderly mice. This is possibly due to significant weight loss caused by rapamycin in older subjects, indicating the need for further research into its specific mechanisms in aging populations [ ].

## COPD
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a condition characterized by persistent airflow limitation, driven primarily by small airway fibrosis, airway inflammation, and emphysema. Currently, there are no specific prevention or treatment strategies available for COPD [ – ]. The pathogenesis of COPD is closely tied to processes such as cellular aging, increased production of ROS, impaired autophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction, stem cell depletion, and DNA damage [ – ].
Inflammation and cellular senescence are key components of COPD progression, with increased mTOR activity playing a central role. Studies in both patients with COPD and mouse models have demonstrated that mTOR activation in airway epithelial cells correlates with reduced autophagy [ ]. In transgenic mice, overexpression of mTOR in pulmonary vascular or alveolar epithelial cells is sufficient to induce cellular senescence, closely mimicking the lung changes seen in COPD [ , ]. Cigarette smoke exposure, a major cause of COPD, induces oxidative stress and triggers cellular senescence through mTOR activation. Rapamycin has shown promise in reducing pulmonary inflammation and emphysema in cigarette smoke-exposed mice, offering potential therapeutic benefits [ – ].
In COPD, reduced mitochondrial autophagy impairs bacterial phagocytosis, which is thought to be mediated by the PI3K-mTOR pathway [ , – ]. One study found that rapamycin restored glucocorticoid responsiveness in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with COPD, indicating its potential to improve treatment efficacy [ ]. Additionally, research has linked the accumulation of autophagy-related proteins LC3B and p62 to the severity of emphysema, suggesting that these proteins could serve as predictive biomarkers for COPD progression [ ].
The clinical potential of mTOR inhibitors in COPD treatment is promising, though further comprehensive basic and clinical research is needed. The intricate nature of mTOR complexes and their involvement in nearly all cellular processes highlights the need for a deeper understanding of its structure-function relationships to optimize therapeutic strategies for COPD.

## Cross the whole period

## ALI
Acute lung injury (ALI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by damage to the pulmonary capillaries and alveolar epithelium, along with increased vascular permeability and various non-cardiogenic factors. With mortality rates reaching up to 38.5%, the extent of alveolar epithelial damage is a key prognostic factor, as the alveolar-capillary membrane, immune system, and hemostatic mechanisms are primary sites of injury. ALI can be triggered by numerous factors, including lipopolysaccharides (LPS), staphylococcal toxins, influenza virus, paraquat exposure, and traumatic brain injury, all of which are associated with excessive mTOR activation [ – ].
Extensive research has highlighted the role of mTOR-mediated autophagy in ALI pathogenesis, with rapamycin and the knockdown of Rptor or mTOR often reversing disease progression [ , – ]. Rapamycin has shown efficacy in reducing neutrophil aggregation, interstitial pulmonary edema, and microvascular permeability in murine models of LPS-induced ALI. It also decreases levels of Tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α), IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, improving survival rates, likely through the activation of autophagy and inhibition of the Nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway [ , ]. Hu [ ] found that in mice, p-mTOR expression in the pulmonary epithelium is upregulated while autophagy is suppressed, potentially through Toll-like receptor-4(TLR-4) signaling. Rapamycin was able to alleviate LPS-induced lung epithelial damage by restoring autophagy.
Other mTOR pathway regulators have also demonstrated protective effects in ALI models. The endoplasmic reticulum stress inhibitor 4-PBA has shown protection in ALI by modulating autophagy via the AKT/mTOR pathway [ ]. Similarly, the mycelial extract SS-1 reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and suppressed myeloperoxidase activity in LPS-induced ALI through the TLR4/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [ ]. The selective SIRT1 inhibitor EX-527, upstream of mTOR, effectively reduced plasma TNF-α and IL-6, significantly alleviating LPS-induced lung injury [ ]. Furthermore, Protein-tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) inhibitors attenuated abnormal neutrophil aging by inhibiting CXC chemokine receptor 4(CXCR4) signaling via the AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby preventing or ameliorating lung injury [ ]. Metformin has also demonstrated anti-inflammatory and protective effects in ALI, possibly through mTOR inhibition via the AMPK-mTOR pathway [ ].
Various immune cells, such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, play critical roles in the pathogenesis of ALI. High mobility group protein B1(HMGB1) promotes the maturation of pulmonary dendritic cells and enhances their antigen-presenting capabilities via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, resulting in increased adhesion and chemotaxis of these cells, which exacerbates ALI progression [ ]. Additionally, the activation of the NOD-like receptors family pyrin domain containing protein3(NLRP3) inflammasome is regulated by Triggering receptors expressed in myeloid cells-1(TREM-1) through the mTOR/HIF-1α/glycolysis pathway, with TREM-1 further worsening inflammation by inducing macrophage necroptosis via mitochondrial fission mediated by mTOR-dependent dynamin-related protein 1(DRP1) phosphorylation [ , ]. Moreover, spermidine-mTOR siRNA nanocomplexes have shown synergistic anti-inflammatory effects by promoting macrophage autophagy and shifting their phenotype from type-1 macrophage (M1) to type-2 macrophage (M2), thereby mitigating LPS-induced ALI [ ]. Extracellular vesicles from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells, which release microRNA-377-3p, target Rptor to induce autophagy and reduce lung injury [ ]. Additionally, Caveolin-1(CAV-1) knockdown has been shown to inhibit autophagy-related AKT/mTOR signaling by disrupting the CAV-1/NF-κB axis, activating AMPK, and reducing inflammatory responses, thereby alleviating ALI [ ].
However, contrary to expectations, rapamycin treatment of LPS-induced ALI in mice did not show significant protective effects in Ye’s study [ ]. Similarly, Fielhaber et al. found that while rapamycin could inhibit NF-κB and neutrophilic inflammation in LPS-induced ALI, it paradoxically exacerbated lung damage and increased cellular apoptosis, which may be linked to variations in dosage and timing of LPS exposure [ ]. Chen et al. [ ] also demonstrated that rapamycin or Torin 1 could promote pneumonia by increasing endothelial cell contraction and permeability while improving LPS-induced ALI.
Thus, mTOR inhibitors may offer protection in nearly all forms of ALI by promoting autophagy, reducing inflammation, and enhancing immune responses. Enhancing autophagy in the early stages of sepsis may provide a novel approach to treating sepsis-induced ALI. However, due to the complex etiologies of lung injury, the involvement of different cell types, and the varying stages of disease progression, further research is essential to better understand the effects of mTOR-related therapies and establish clear selection criteria.

## Pulmonary infectious disease
mTOR plays a critical role in regulating lung diseases caused by specific pathogens, including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Severe acute respiratory syndrome-associated coronavirus (SARS-CoV), SARS-CoV-2, and Middle East respiratory syndrome-related coronavirus (MERS-CoV). In the case of MERS-CoV, mTOR activity is inhibited by nonstructural protein 1, leading to the induction of autophagy. This pathogen also downregulates lysosome-related gene expression, impairing lysosome formation and acidification, which disrupts autophagic flux and ultimately induces apoptosis [ – ].
The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, presents significant global public health challenges. Dysregulation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway has been observed in patients with COVID-19, raising concerns about its role in disease progression [ ]. Studies have shown an upregulation of this pathway in COVID-19, suggesting that inhibitors targeting PI3K/Akt/mTOR may help mitigate excessive inflammatory responses, protect alveolar cells, and improve patient outcomes through antiviral effects [ ].
Given the varied etiologies of lung diseases, the involvement of distinct cell types in injury mechanisms, and the different stages of disease progression, further research is essential to clarify the effects of mTOR-related therapies and establish clear selection criteria for their use (Table  ).
 

## Characteristics of mTOR
The mTOR is a highly conserved serine/threonine kinase that belongs to the PI3K-related kinase (PIKK) superfamily. It forms two distinct protein complexes: mTORC1 and mTORC2. The core components of mTORC1 include mTOR, regulatory-associated protein of mTOR (Raptor), and mLST8, while mTORC2 comprises mTOR, rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR), and mLST8. These structural differences confer distinct functional roles, meaning (Rictor) that drugs targeting the mTOR pathway can influence both complexes.
mTORC1 activation is initiated by growth factors, amino acids, sufficient energy, and oxygen levels. Once activated, it influences downstream effectors such as S6 kinase 1(S6K1), 4E-binding protein (4EBP), Sterol-regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP), and methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2(MTHFD2), leading to enhanced protein synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, lipid production, and nucleotide biosynthesis. In addition, mTORC1 promotes cell activity while suppressing autophagy and protein degradation during starvation or energy depletion, thus maintaining cellular homeostasis of glucose, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and amino acids [ ]. Under stress conditions like hypoxia, energy depletion, or DNA damage, AMPK and TSC signaling pathways inhibit mTORC1, allowing cells to adapt by promoting autophagy and other compensatory mechanisms [ ]. Through this intricate signaling network, mTOR coordinates cellular responses to external stimuli and nutrient availability, ensuring optimal cellular function (Fig.  ).
 The modulation of autophagy by mTORC1 in mammalian cells has been extensively studied [ , ]. Autophagy involves a series of stages: the formation of autophagosomes, their fusion with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, degradation of autolysosomal contents, and lysosome regeneration. When mTORC1 is inhibited, it enhances the activity of the ULK complex, VPS34 complex, and other autophagy-related factors, driving autophagosome membrane elongation and maturation [ , ]. mTORC1 also activates the lysosomal regulator transcription factor EB (TFEB), promoting the transcription of lysosome-related and autophagy-related genes, thereby increasing lysosome abundance and enzyme levels. Furthermore, mTORC1 modulates the formation of the STX17-SNAP29-VAMP8 complex via post-translational modifications of SNARE proteins, facilitating the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes [ – ]. Research has revealed that mTOR regulation can alter lysosomal metabolite kinetics. Inhibiting mTOR activity reduces the efflux of essential amino acids from lysosomes, potentially enhancing their role in autophagy completion [ ]. Additionally, a recent study found that mTOR promotes the formation and separation of autophagolysosomal tubules through the PI3KC3 pathway, contributing to lysosome regeneration [ ].
mTOR induces a sophisticated negative feedback regulation in cells, particularly in the context of autophagy. During starvation, mTOR inhibition accelerates lysosome biosynthesis and facilitates autophagy. As autophagy progresses, degradation byproducts such as amino acids accumulate, triggering a feedback loop that reactivates mTOR. This mTOR activation limits autophagosome production while promoting the formation of new lysosomes from active autolysosomes, thus increasing lysosome numbers and preparing the cell for subsequent autophagic cycles. This delicate balance maintains autophagy at a controlled level, preventing excessive breakdown of cellular components and ensuring cell survival [ , ]. Disruptions at any stage of this autophagic cycle can impair the overall process, underscoring the importance of mTOR in maintaining consistent autophagic flux.
In addition, mTORC2 plays a critical role in regulating autophagy through its own negative feedback mechanism, complementing mTORC1’s regulatory functions. Inhibition of mTORC1 enhances autophagy but also suppresses Grb10, leading to increased activation of mTORC2 via insulin/IGF-1 receptor signaling. mTORC2, in turn, phosphorylates and activates Akt, which upregulates mTORC1 to stabilize its activity within a certain range, thereby fine-tuning the autophagic process [ – ]. Most mTOR inhibitors currently used in clinical settings primarily target mTORC1, but if mTORC2 is also affected, this could potentially alter drug efficacy. Beyond its role in the insulin/PI3K pathway, mTORC2 influences actin cytoskeleton remodeling and cell migration by phosphorylating members of the AGC protein kinase family. It also regulates apoptosis, glucose metabolism, and cell survival through factors like FOXO1, Glycogen synthase kinase-3(GSK3), and Serum/glucocorticoid regulated kinase 1(SGK1), maintaining a positive feedback loop with Akt [ , ].
Thus, mTOR acts as a pivotal “molecular switch” in the regulation of autophagy, orchestrating this complex process. Alterations in mTOR-driven autophagy are implicated in various pathological conditions, making mTOR a potential molecular target for the prevention and treatment of autophagy-related diseases.
Extracellular signals such as energy levels, ROS, growth factors, and amino acids upregulate mTOR expression via pathways involving AMPK, PI3K, and AKT. The two mTOR complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2, subsequently drive cellular proliferation by enhancing key processes like protein, lipid, and nucleotide synthesis, as well as glucose metabolism. Additionally, mTOR plays a critical role in inhibiting autophagy by modulating various stages, including phagophore nucleation, elongation, autophagosome formation, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and lysosome reformation. This inhibition promotes cell survival but can also trigger cell death under certain conditions. Moreover, mTORC1 and mTORC2 regulate cellular proliferation and apoptosis through intricate feedback mechanisms, ensuring cellular homeostasis and balanced cell growth

## mTOR as a therapeutic target for lung diseases

## Clinical application
mTOR activation plays a critical role in various immune diseases, cancers, obesity, and aging-related disorders, significantly influencing these pathways [ ]. Currently, only a limited number of mTOR inhibitors are available in clinical practice, all belonging to the first generation. These inhibitors are primarily used for cancer treatment or preventing transplant rejection. Rapamycin, the first mTOR inhibitor approved by the FDA in 1999, serves as an immunosuppressant to prevent organ rejection. Second- and third-generation selective mTOR inhibitors have not yet been introduced into clinical settings [ ]. Dysregulation of the mTOR pathway is evident in nearly all lung diseases, with many lung cells showing increased mTOR activity (Table  ). Rapamycin and its derivatives often exhibit protective effects; however, due to inherent limitations, their clinical use is currently restricted to lung cancer, LAM, and certain immune-related pulmonary disorders [ , , ]. The clinical use of mTOR inhibitors is associated with numerous adverse effects, including pro-inflammatory responses and dyslipidemia, frequently observed in post-transplant patients [ ]. Other side effects may include thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, stomatitis, and diarrhea, with rare occurrences of interstitial pneumonia [ ]. Prolonged use of rapamycin and its derivatives post-transplantation may also induce insulin resistance, increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes, a metabolic side effect commonly seen in clinical practice [ , ]. Recent experimental studies have explored mTOR-related and small-molecule drugs, though their potential toxic side effects remain unclear, necessitating further research to evaluate safety and clinical viability. Interestingly, some studies have reported reduced mTOR activity in certain lung diseases, suggesting a protective decrease in mTOR feedback during early disease stages [ ] (Table  ). A protective decrease in mTOR feedback is believed to occur during the early stages of the disease.
Research on mTOR inhibitors is primarily focused on cancer treatment and overcoming drug resistance. Ongoing investigations are exploring ATP-competitive mTOR and dual-site inhibitors, with numerous drugs in clinical trials for various diseases [ , ]. However, research into the application of these inhibitors in non-cancerous diseases remains limited. Moderate inhibition of mTOR may offer new diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities for these conditions. Certain natural compounds, such as epigallocatechin gallate, resveratrol, sulforaphane, curcumin, cryptotanshinone, and caffeine, are known to regulate the mTOR pathway either directly or indirectly. These compounds influence mTOR through effects on cell cycle regulation, autophagy, and apoptosis [ – ]. Further insights into the mechanisms of these agents could lead to more effective strategies for preventing and treating mTOR-related diseases.

## Compounds in preclinical studies
Given the significant systemic side effects associated with mTOR inhibitors, nebulized inhalation presents a promising alternative for treating pulmonary conditions. This route allows for the direct delivery of low doses to the lungs, reducing systemic side effects while achieving higher local concentrations of phosphocyclic acid. This targeted approach has shown potential in inhibiting lung tumors and improving survival rates in an in situ mouse model [ ]. Additionally, metformin inhalation, another drug known to inhibit mTOR, has been shown to enhance survival rates in mice infected with influenza without notable adverse effects [ ]. Recent research has focused on developing rapamycin nanoparticles to improve the treatment of respiratory diseases such as COPD and asthma [ ]. Another study highlighted the use of solid lipid nanoparticles incorporating rapamycin for inhalation therapy, which could reduce metabolism, extend the drug’s half-life, and offer a promising alternative treatment for lymphangioleiomyomatosis [ ]. This inhalation-based delivery system offers a targeted, efficient method to optimize mTOR inhibition while minimizing the risks of systemic toxicity.

## Conclusions
The mTOR signaling pathway plays a critical role in regulating numerous physiological and pathological processes, making it a key modulator of the respiratory system throughout life, from fetal lung development to aging. Its influence extends to both the development and progression of various pulmonary diseases. While mTOR inhibitors, particularly small-molecule drugs, have demonstrated considerable efficacy in cancer therapy, their application in common lung diseases remains limited due to a wide range of side effects. However, certain natural compounds that mildly inhibit mTOR activity may offer protective effects for the lungs. Inhalation therapy using mTOR inhibitors presents a promising treatment strategy for lung diseases, offering the potential for improved efficacy and reduced systemic side effects. Given the complex etiologies and mechanisms underlying diseases such as COPD, BPD, and asthma, the bidirectional modulation of mTOR suggests that therapeutic interventions must be carefully tailored to different stages of disease progression. This nuanced approach may optimize treatment outcomes across varying lung conditions. Due to its substantial potential in advancing the understanding of pulmonary diseases, mTOR continues to be a valuable target for developing novel therapies for human lung disorders.
