The DOHaD hypothesis was originally formed based on epidemiological studies, such as those examining the Dutch famine in the aftermath of World War II [ ]. These studies revealed that individuals exposed to nutritional deprivation during critical windows of fetal development were more likely to experience increased risks for various chronic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic disorders, later in life [ ]. Building on the DOHaD hypothesis, numerous studies have explored how various environmental insults, including toxicants [ ], diet [ ], stress [ , ], diabetes [ , ], smoking [ ], and alcohol consumption [ , ] lead to adverse health effects in the offspring.
Multiple studies have demonstrated that variations in maternal nutrition, such as protein restriction or high-fat intake, can influence the epigenetic landscape of the offspring. In a low-protein mouse model, van Straten and colleagues demonstrated that 204 gene promoter regions were differentially methylated in the livers of fetuses derived from low-protein dams compared to control dams [ ]. Similarly, other research has shown that the expression of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR-⍺) and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) increased in the livers of offspring produced from low-protein dams, while the methylation levels of the associated promoters significantly decreased [ , ]. A study on maternal high-fat diet (HFD) in rats demonstrated increased triglyceride content in the offspring’s liver, accompanied by upregulation of the hepatic cell cycle inhibitor CDKN1A, and decreased methylation within its first exon [ ]. Similarly, in a maternal undernutrition mouse model, it has been reported that offspring from undernourished dams developed obesity and glucose intolerance, accompanied by differential expression of lipogenic genes and alterations in methylation within two identified CpG islands in the liver [ ].
While the effects of maternal diet on offspring are relatively well understood, the specific impact of dietary factors on the epigenetic landscape of germ cells and, consequently, on the resulting embryos warrants further evaluation. Schutt and colleagues demonstrated that a protein-restricted diet in rats resulted in altered amino acid metabolism in the oocyte along with abnormal cumulus oocyte complex mitochondrial ultrastructure, and differential oocyte expression of genes related to mitochondria biogenesis [ ]. Similarly, an HFD in mice resulted in oocytes with increased pyruvate oxidation and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). Two-cell embryos derived from HFD compared to control females displayed a global decrease in 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) levels, while 5-formylcytosine (5fC) levels were increased [ ]. Another HFD study in mice similarly found that oocyte spindle morphology was altered, mitochondrial distributions were abnormal, and ROS levels were increased [ ]. Interestingly, staining for 5mC revealed a decrease in 5mC from HFD compared to control oocytes [ ]. In another study involving HFD, researchers found increased DNA methylation at the leptin promoter and reduced methylation at the Ppar-⍺ promoter in oocytes [ ].
While these studies collectively demonstrate that maternal dietary changes can influence both the oocyte and offspring’s health, there is a notable gap in our understanding of how specific dietary components, such as the methyl group donor methionine, affect the whole-genome methylation landscape of the oocyte. Furthermore, little is known about how these methylation changes are inherited by the embryo and whether they impact the development of both the embryo and the offspring. The knowledge gap is particularly significant in livestock species, as most work to date has utilized rodent models. In a previous study in cows, we demonstrated that adding dietary methionine at the preimplantation stage of embryo development altered the expression of 276 genes in the collected embryos, highlighting the potential impact of maternal methionine supplementation on embryonic development [ ]. Additionally, our recent work has shown that paternal methionine supplementation significantly altered DNA methylation in sperm cells, leading to differential gene expression in the resulting embryos [ ]. Building on these findings, we hypothesized that maternal methionine supplementation will alter the DNA methylation patterns in oocytes and that these alterations could be reflected in the embryo’s epigenome.

## Materials and methods
This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for the reporting of animal research. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Wisconsin-Madison (Protocol ID: A006488-R01).

## Supplementation of rumen-protected methionine (RPM) in diets of ewes
In this study, Polypay sheep was used as a large animal model as it has long been known for its reproductive efficiency, twinning rate, and prolificacy. In previous studies, we utilized this model to study the effects of paternal and maternal methionine supplementation on epigenetic and phenotypic changes in offspring [ , ]. Eight Polypay twin ewe pairs (16 total ewes) aged 2 years and each having previously lambed once, were selected from the University of Wisconsin research flock ( ). Within each twin pair, one ewe was randomly assigned to a treatment group (n = 8) and the other to the control group (n = 8) using a random number generator. The control group was fed a general basal diet [ ], while the treatment group received the same basal diet supplemented with 0.44% (3 g) of RPM (RPM Smartamine; Adisseo, Alpharetta, GA, USA). The experimental methionine dose was determined based on our previous studies on methionine-induced epigenetic changes in sheep sperm [ , ]. Methionine was chosen for supplementation due to its role as a methyl donor and its potential to modulate DNA methylation [ ]. Each ewe was fed individually, receiving 0.45 kg of their assigned diet once daily over 147 days. Methionine supplementation was continued throughout the study, including during the ovum pick up (OPU) and embryo flush procedures, to ensure consistent dietary exposure. Outside of the daily feeding period, ewes were group-housed with ad libitum access to forage, water, and an additional 1 kg of the basal diet.
 

## Ovarian stimulation protocol
To maximize the number of follicles available for OPU, the ewes underwent ovarian stimulation prior to the procedure. On Day 0, a controlled internal drug release (CIDR) was inserted vaginally into each ewe. On Day 9, 1.0 cc of Estrumate was administered to induce luteolysis. Starting on Day 10.5, ewes received 3.0 cc of Folltropin via intramuscular injection, followed by two 2.0 cc injections on Day 11. Ovarian aspirations were performed on Day 12, and the CIDR was removed.

## Ovum pick up
Ovum pick up was performed 76 days after the start of methionine supplementation via laparotomy on Day 12 of the ovarian stimulation protocol. The ewes were fasted for 24 hours before surgery. General anesthesia was induced with an intravenous injection of 2–4 mg/kg of Propofol, followed by maintenance with inhalation of the anesthetic, isoflurane. Once intubated, the ewes were then placed in a Trendelenburg position to facilitate optimal access to the ovaries before incisions were made. A 2 cc injection of Bupivacaine was administered at the laparoscopic incision sites for local anesthesia, and Flunixin Meglumine was given intravenously for postoperative pain relief and to minimize swelling. Within 5 minutes of Bupivacaine administration, approximately 3 1-inch incisions were made in the abdominal wall, spaced about 3 inches apart. A laparoscope was inserted into one incision, and a small amount of CO2 gas was used to slightly inflate the abdomen. A second laparoscope, equipped with a dissecting tool, was inserted through a second incision to grab the ovarian ligament. Both laparoscopes were used for visualizing the ovaries, and abdominal pressure was continuously monitored to ensure it did not exceed 10 cm H2O. A trocar attached to an OPU kit connected to a vacuum pump was inserted through the third incision, and follicular aspiration was performed, starting from the bottom of each ovary and moving upwards. Following aspiration of each ovary, the collection tube was rinsed with warmed flush media by inserting the OPU needle tip into the flush media. Prior to the release of the ovarian ligament, the ovaries were then rinsed with heparinized saline (1 ml/50 ml), and the musculature and skin were sutured. Following the OPU, oocytes were washed in ABT Complete Flush Media (ABT360, Pullman, WA, USA) and evaluated for quality according to the standards outlined by the International Embryo Technology Society (IETS). Grade 1 and 2 oocytes were then pooled by treatment and placed into a 0.5 mL tube in 1–2 uL of media for DNA extraction and sequencing. To provide sufficient DNA amount for whole genome bisulfite sequencing and unbiased representation, collected oocytes were sequenced as a pool rather than a single oocyte. Three treatment and four control pools were made out of all collected oocytes. Briefly, two treatment pools had oocytes from three methionine-fed ewes each, and the other one had oocytes from the remaining two treatment ewes. Additionally, three control pools had oocytes from two control ewes each, and the remaining control consisted of only one ewe’s oocytes, since the last control ewe did not yield any oocytes. A pooling strategy was applied, considering a similar number of oocytes represented in each pool, ranging from 17 to 21 oocytes.

## Synchronization and superovulation protocol
To maximize the number of embryos for DNA methylation analysis, the same ewes used for OPU underwent estrus synchronization and superovulation prior to breeding. On Day 0, a CIDR was inserted vaginally into each ewe. On Day 7, the CIDR was replaced with a new one, and 1.0 cc of prostaglandin (EstroPLAN) was administered to induce luteolysis. Starting on Day 12, Folltropin was administered twice daily for four days. The CIDR was removed on Day 14.5, and 1.5 cc of PG600 was given to stimulate ovulation. On Day 16, ewes were introduced to a control yearling Polypay ram for natural mating. Each ram was randomly selected to breed with four ewes and was fitted with marking harnesses to confirm mating success.

## Embryo flushes
Embryo collections were performed by GenOvations (Lodi, WI, USA) via laparotomy 70 days after the OPU procedure. Prior to surgery, ewes were fasted for 24 hours, and general anesthesia was administered as described above. Once intubated and in a Trendelenburg position, local anesthetic and Flunixin were administered as previously described, and a small (~1.5 cm) incision was made in the abdominal wall. A laparoscope was inserted through the incision to visualize the uterus, and the incision was then enlarged to approximately 7 cm to allow access through the subcutaneous tissue, abdominal musculature, and peritoneum. The uterine horns were then exposed, and a 20-G Foley catheter was inserted into the distal third of each uterine horn. A total of 50 ml of saline was flushed through each horn and collected into a filtered Petri dish via the Foley catheter. The uterine horns were repositioned into the abdomen, and the musculature and skin were sutured closed. Following the flush, blastocysts were washed in ABT Complete Flush Media (ABT360), evaluated using the IETS grading system, and placed into a 0.5 mL tube containing 1- 2 uL of media for DNA extraction and sequencing. Three treatment and five control pools were made. Briefly, two treatment pools had embryos from two methionine-fed ewes each, and the other one had embryos from one treatment ewe, as the remaining three did not yield any embryos. Additionally, three control pools had embryos from two control ewes each, and the remaining two control pools consisted only of one ewe’s embryos each. A pooling strategy was applied, considering a similar number of embryos represented in each pool, containing 10 to 13 embryos.

## Whole-genome DNA methylation analysis of oocytes and embryos
Oocyte and embryo samples were processed for whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) using the EZ DNA Methylation Direct Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), which enables bisulfite conversion directly from cells without the need for prior DNA purification. Following bisulfite conversion, whole genome amplification was performed using the Pico Methyl-Seq Library Prep Kit (Zymo Research) to ensure sufficient DNA for downstream analysis. The amplification was followed by adapter ligation and PCR amplification to prepare the sequencing libraries. Sequencing was performed at the Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center, The University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, using the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with two 25B lanes, using V1.0 sequencing kits. Fastq files were generated and demultiplexed using the bcl2fastq v2.20 Conversion Software (Illumina) for downstream analysis.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Following WGBS, the 150 bp paired-end reads for 8 blastocyst stage embryo pools (5 control, 3 treatment) and 7 oocyte pools (4 control, 3 treatment) were processed using FastQC v0.12.1 to generate sequence quality reports. TrimGalore v0.6.1 and Cutadapt v4.8 were utilized for trimming any low-quality bases, reads, and adapter sequences [ ]. The resulting clean reads were then aligned to the ‘ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0’ reference genome assembly from the NCBI database using Bismark v0.24.2 and Bowtie2. Methylation count reports were subsequently generated for cytosines located within CpG islands [ , ].
Differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) within CpG islands of both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes from oocytes and embryos were identified using the methylKit R package. Comparisons were performed between control and treatment groups for embryo pools, oocyte pools, and their respective mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [ ]. For nuclear genome analyses, we excluded cytosines on sex chromosomes and those with fewer than 10 reads. Read coverage across samples was then normalized using median normalization. Mitochondrial cytosines were extracted and analysed separately to detect DMCs between control and treatment groups in both embryos and oocytes, and to assess potential overlaps. The same minimum threshold of 10 read counts and normalization were also applied to the mtDNA analysis. Differential methylation was assessed using a beta-binomial model followed by an ‘F’ test [ ]. An FDR-based multiple testing correction was applied to the p-values, and a dual threshold was used to determine significant DMCs. Cytosines were considered significant if they met both criteria: a q-value < 0.01 and a methylation difference > 15% [ ].
After detecting DMCs, the ‘ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0’ reference genome annotations were used with the R packages ‘genomation’ and ‘GenomicRanges’ to analyse the distribution of methylation differences across gene components (e.g., promoters, introns, exons, intergenic regions). Additionally, the corresponding genes associated with the identified DMCs were determined, along with their distances from transcription start sites [ , ]. DMCs were associated with a gene, if they are located within or ±20 Kb proximity of a gene.

## In-vitro embryo production
To assess whether the genes identified as differentially methylated in embryos have functional roles in early embryonic development, two candidate genes harbouring DMCs within their introns were selected for siRNA knockdown experiments. To validate the effects across species, bovine ovaries were sourced from a local abattoir. In vitro embryo production was performed as previously described by Tribulo et al. [ ]. Briefly, cumulus – oocyte complexes (COCs) were collected and washed in oocyte collection medium (OCM) [ ]. Only oocytes surrounded by at least one layer of cumulus cells were selected for in vitro maturation. Groups of 50 COCs were matured for 24 hours in 500 µL of oocyte maturation medium (OMM), overlaid with 250 µL of light mineral oil (FUJIFILM Irvine Scientific, Santa Ana, CA, USA).
Following maturation, COCs were washed 3 times in HEPES-TALP and transferred in groups of 200–250 to fertilization dishes containing 1700 µL of IVF-TALP [ ]. Frozen – thawed semen was processed using a discontinuous gradient (50% upper layer and 90% lower layer) with ISolate Sperm Separation Medium (FUJIFILM Irvine Scientific) to enrich for motile sperm. The resulting sperm pellet was washed with 1 mL of HEPES-TALP, centrifuged, and resuspended in IVF-TALP to a final concentration of 17 × 106 sperm/mL. Sperm were then added to the fertilization dish to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 106 sperm/mL. Penicillamine, hypotaurine, and epinephrine (PHE) were added to support fertilization. After 11 hours of fertilization, presumptive zygotes were denuded of cumulus cells by vortexing, washed 3 times in HEPES-TALP, and transferred in groups of 40 to SOF-BE2 culture medium [ ], where they were briefly held prior to electroporation.

## Electroporation with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)
Electroporation was performed approximately 12 hours post-fertilization, at the zygote stage, to ensure delivery of siRNAs before embryo cleavage. For this procedure, zygotes were removed from the culture plate in groups of 40 and washed twice in HEPES-TALP, followed by 3 washes in Opti-MEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Each group was then passed through 2 sequential 5.7 µL drops containing Opti-MEM supplemented with custom-designed siRNAs (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA) at a final concentration of 10 µM. This concentration was selected based on preliminary testing of multiple siRNA concentrations, where 10 µM yielded optimal delivery and knockdown efficiency.
Zygotes were then transferred in 5.7 µL of the siRNA/Opti-MEM solution to an electroporation slide equipped with platinum plate electrodes (15 mm length, 1 mm gap, 1.5 mm height; Bulldog Bio, Portsmouth, NH, USA). Electroporated control zygotes were transferred in 5.7 uL of Opti-MEM alone. Electroporation was performed using the following parameters: poring pulses − 20 V, 6 pulses, 1.5 ms duration, 50 ms intervals, 10% decay rate, and transfer pulse − 3 V/mm, 5 pulses, 50 ms duration, 50 ms intervals, 40% decay rate.
Following electroporation, zygotes were washed 3 times in HEPES-TALP and returned to a fresh culture plate containing 500 µL of SOF-BEII medium [ ], overlaid with 250 µL of light mineral oil. Embryos were assessed on Day 7.5 post-fertilization for blastocyst formation to determine developmental competence. Blastocyst rates were analysed in R using binomial logistic regression to assess the effect of siRNA electroporation (https://www.r-project.org/). A Wald test was applied to assess overall significance among treatment groups. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Resulting blastocysts were then collected for downstream gene expression analysis.

## Gene expression analysis
To evaluate if electroporation of zygotes with siRNAs of interest altered gene expression in embryos, RNA was extracted from day 7.5 blastocysts using the RNAqueous-Micro Kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad, CA, USA), and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using iTaq SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad). The Beta Actin gene was used as an endogenous control, and primers for the genes of interest, SCRIB and CERS3, were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). Cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized to the endogenous beta-actin gene to evaluate gene expression. The fold change for each gene of interest was calculated as 2−ΔΔCT using the estimated ΔΔCt value ± standard error [ ]. To compare the gene expression levels of siRNA-electroporated cells and control cells, a t-test was performed on the ∆Ct values of each gene.

## Results

## Effects of maternal diet on DNA methylation patterns in oocytes
To determine the impact of methionine supplementation on DNA methylation patterns in oocytes, we performed WGBS on samples from 3 treatment pools and 4 control pools. A total of 49,010 cytosines in CpG contexts were identified, of which 2,056 were differentially methylated between the treatment and control groups (FDR < 0.01; methylation difference > 15%). Of these, 2,034 were hypomethylated and 22 were hypermethylated (Supplementary file 1; ). All hypermethylated DMCs were located in intergenic regions, while hypomethylated DMCs were distributed across introns (1%), exons (3%), promoters (1%), and intergenic regions (95%) (Supplementary file 1; ). Out of 2,034 hypomethylated DMCs, 211 were located within ±20 Kb of a transcription start site (TSS), which were associated with 15 uncharacterized genes (Supplementary file 1). Interestingly, one of the uncharacterized genes was predicted to resemble collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1) through sequence similarity.
 
Additionally, mitochondrial DNA methylation was analysed to assess the impact of the methionine-enriched diet. Between the treatment and control groups, 17 DMCs were identified, with 12 DMCs being hypermethylated and 5 being hypomethylated (Supplementary file 1; ).

## Effects of maternal diet on methylation patterns in embryos
To investigate whether methionine supplementation in the maternal diet affects DNA methylation in embryos produced from the same methionine-treated ewes used for OPU, we performed WGBS from 3 treatment pools and 5 control pools, each consisting of 10–13 embryos. No morphological differences were found between the embryo groups. Of the 200,250 cytosines analysed, 113 were differentially methylated between the treatment and control groups (FDR < 0.01; methylation difference > 15%). Of these, 112 DMCs were hypermethylated, and only one DMC was hypomethylated (Supplementary file 2; ). Approximately 93% of hypermethylated DMCs were located in intergenic regions, with 4.5% in exons and 2.5% in introns (Supplementary file; 2, ). Some of the hypermethylated genes are beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase 4 (B4GALT4), LOC132659112 (COL1A2-like), LOC101118284 (OR5W2-like), LOC114118352 (HNRNPR-like), and ceramide synthase 3 (CERS3). On the other hand, the single hypomethylated DMC, which exhibited a 47% methylation difference between the treatment and control groups, was found in the intron of the scribble planar cell polarity protein (SCRIB) gene. Additionally, only one hypermethylated CpG-DMC, which was not mapped to any gene, and a single hypomethylated DMC within the SCRIB gene were found to be located in CpG islands in this study. Consistent with the DNA methylation trends observed in the genome, the mitochondrial DNA revealed a total of 22 DMCs, all of which were hypermethylated (Supplementary file 2; ).
 

## Overlaps of oocyte and embryo DMCs
To determine whether methylation marks from the oocyte are inherited by the embryo, we analysed the overlap of DMCs between the two groups. Three cytosines were found to be differentially methylated in both the oocytes and embryos. These DMCs, however, were hypomethylated in the oocyte but became hypermethylated in the embryos. All three DMCs were associated with unannotated genes. Interestingly, in the mitochondria, 5 hypermethylated DMCs were present in both oocytes and embryos. Two were located within the CYTB gene, 2 were intergenic in relation to KEF53_t09, and one was within the KEF53_t10 gene.

## Electroporation of embryos with siRNAs
To investigate the functional relevance of DMCs identified in embryos, zygotes were electroporated with siRNAs targeting two genes, SCRIB and ceramide synthase 3 (CERS3), associated with intronic DMCs. In addition to SCRIB harbouring the only hypomethylated CpG-DMC and CERS3 being the top functionally characterized gene with a gene body CpG-DMC (Supplementary File 2), these genes were also selected for their potential regulatory roles in early development and for intronic methylation changes that may influence gene expression.
For SCRIB, across four biological replicates, a total of 331 embryos were electroporated with siRNA, alongside 277 electroporated controls (no siRNA) and 169 non-electroporated controls. Embryos electroporated with SCRIB siRNA exhibited a substantially reduced blastocyst rate of 13.83%, compared to 30.20% in electroporated controls (p = 0.001) and 30.45% in non-electroporated controls (p = 0.001). There was no significant difference in blastocyst rate between electroporated controls and non-electroporated controls (p = 0.95) ( ).
 
Similarly, for CERS3, across five biological replicates, 442 embryos were electroporated with siRNA, with 332 electroporated controls and 289 non-electroporated controls. The average blastocyst rate in CERS3-targeted embryos was 12.49%, compared to 21.37% in electroporated controls (p = 0.005) and 23.20% in the non-electroporated controls (p = 0.005). Similarly to SCRIB, there was no significant difference in blastocyst rates between electroporated and non-electroporated controls (p = 0.69) ( ). No significant morphological differences were observed among the blastocysts formed in the different groups for CERS3 and SCRIB.
Gene expression analysis of blastocysts revealed a reduction in transcript levels in response to electroporation, with SCRIB-electroporated embryos showing an average 43.2% reduction in expression (p = 0.34) and CERS3-electroporated embryos showing a 60.1% reduction (p = 0.06), relative to electroporated controls.

## Discussion
Maternal nutrition plays a crucial role in shaping offspring development through epigenetic mechanisms; however, the direct effects of dietary interventions on oocyte DNA methylation and subsequent embryonic epigenetics remain incompletely understood. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate DNA methylation in the oocytes as well as the embryos produced from the same methionine-supplemented mature ewes. Notably, we identified 2,056 DMCs in the oocytes of methionine-treated versus control ewes, with 2,034 being hypomethylated. While a methyl-donor diet would be expected to increase methylation, the observed predominance of hypomethylation in germ cells is intriguing and aligns with our previous findings in sperm. Gross et al. [ ] demonstrated that methionine supplementation in prepubertal rams resulted in 426 hypomethylated and 398 hypermethylated DMCs in sperm using RRBS, and Townsend et al. [ ] identified 134 hypomethylated and 32 hypermethylated DMCs using RRBS in a separate cohort of rams. This consistent trend suggests that excess methionine may disrupt methylation homeostasis in germ cells, potentially due to inhibition at key points in the one-carbon metabolism pathway, including altered SAM:SAH ratios and MTHFR inhibition, as outlined in greater detail by Townsend et al. [ ]. Comparable observations in other species further support these findings. Pogrinby and colleagues found that feeding methionine to middle-aged rats for 14 days led to substantial DNA hypomethylation, accompanied by increased plasma homocysteine levels [ ]. Similarly, Osorio et al. reported that peripartal cows supplemented with methionine showed reduced global hepatic DNA methylation but exhibited hypermethylation in the PPARA promoter region, consistent with our findings [ ]. Collectively, these studies suggest that although methyl donors are typically linked to increased methylation, excessive methionine intake may paradoxically induce widespread hypomethylation by disrupting the balance of the one-carbon cycle.
In contrast to the widespread hypomethylation observed in oocytes, we found that nearly all DMCs in embryos were hypermethylated, with only a single hypomethylated DMC. This shift in methylation status is consistent with the well-established process of epigenetic reprogramming that occurs following fertilization. Upon fertilization, the parental genomes undergo extensive demethylation, erasing most epigenetic marks to establish a pluripotent state in the early embryo. Then, during gastrulation in the embryo, a new epigenetic landscape is established, which plays a key role in driving cellular differentiation in the conceptus [ ]. The erasure of oocyte methylation patterns in the embryo aligns with this process. However, studies on imprinted genes [ ], transposable elements [ ], and transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in germ cells [ , ] indicate that certain epigenetic marks can escape reprogramming and potentially influence embryonic and postnatal development. The hypermethylation observed in embryos suggests that, although much of the oocyte’s epigenetic signature is likely reset, certain loci may be preferentially retained or remethylated in response to maternal methionine supplementation. This warrants further investigation into their potential functional roles in fetal programming.

## Effects of maternal diet on methylation patterns in oocytes and embryos
While over 2,000 DMCs were identified in the oocytes produced from methionine-supplemented versus control ewes, only 211 DMCs, all of which were hypomethylated, were found to be associated with 15 uncharacterized genes. Although these genes were uncharacterized and mostly unannotated, some of them have clear indications for their role in reproduction by simply being highly expressed, particularly in reproductive tissues. For instance, out of 15 uncharacterized genes, LOC121818470, LOC121818464, LOC132659095, LOC121818463, LOC121816055, and LOC132659093 were observed to be highly expressed in sheep oviduct and ovaries (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Furthermore, one of these 15 genes, namely LOC132659098, was predicted as a collagen alpha-1(III) chain-like gene. The original gene collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1) is a structural protein and core component of structural support for blood vessels, uterus, and bowel in the extracellular matrix of mammals [ ]. Previously, several studies found an association between COL3A1 gene expression levels and the regulation of ovarian function by affecting cell proliferation in granulosa cells, especially cumulus cells [ ]. Additionally, the functional annotation of COL3A1 suggested its direct involvement in utero embryonic development (GO:0001701), extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198), TGF-β receptor signalling (GO:0007179), and integrin-mediated signalling pathways (GO:0007229), which are essential for oocyte activation, maturation, fertilization, and early embryonic development [ ]. This highlights the possibility that dietary influences affecting DNA methylation in oocytes may not only impact methylation status in the immediate context of the oocyte but could also contribute to fertilization and fetal programming.
To examine the effects of maternal diet on embryo DNA methylation, we performed WGBS on embryos produced from the same methionine-treated and control ewes used for oocyte collection. Given that epigenetic marks are heritable throughout generations [ ] and that paternal methionine supplementation can lead to changes in embryonic gene expression [ ], we hypothesized that embryos would also exhibit differential methylation patterns in response to maternal methionine supplementation. Like the oocytes, many genes harbouring DMCs in the embryos remain unannotated; however, several genes are involved in key functions such as nervous system development, glycolipid and sphingolipid biosynthesis, hormonal control, and cellular polarization.
One of the annotated genes harbouring DMCs in the embryos is β-1,4-Galactosyltransferase 4 (B4GALT4). The B4GalT family encodes a set of type II transmembrane glycoproteins that transfer galactose to similar acceptor sugars [ ]. Specifically, high expression of B4GALT4 has been linked to colorectal cancer metastasis and poor prognosis [ ]. Notably, B4GALT4 is more highly expressed in in vivo-derived bovine embryos compared to in vitro embryos [ ], suggesting it may play an important role in developmental processes. Given its potential involvement in cellular interactions and altered expression and methylation patterns in embryos, further research into its function during embryonic development is warranted.

## SCRIB and CERS3 knockdowns and implications for early embryo development
SCRIB, which plays a central role in cellular organization and polarity [ ]. Additionally, the Scribble complex regulates key cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation, by engaging multiple signal transduction pathways that establish apical-basal and planar cell polarity [ ]. Loss of proper epithelial organization, as seen when Scrib is disrupted, can lead to defects in embryonic morphogenesis in mice [ ] and is a hallmark of epithelial cancers [ , ]. Scrib null mice are neonatal lethal, suffering from severe neural tube and abdominal wall closure defects [ , , ]. The SCRIB gene has also been reported by the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) to be involved in the Hippo signalling pathway, tight junctions, and viral carcinogenesis, with molecular functions including signalling adaptor activity (GO:0035591), protein kinase binding (GO:0019901), and cadherin binding (GO:0045296). In humans, mutations in SCRIB have been linked to severe neural tube defects, such as craniorachischisis and spina bifida [ , ]. In our study, SCRIB knockdown in bovine embryos resulted in a significant reduction in blastocyst development, suggesting that its expression is crucial even during the earliest stages of embryogenesis. While its role in later morphogenesis is well established [ ], our findings extend the importance of SCRIB to earlier developmental windows, possibly by affecting cellular polarity and organization during early cleavages. Furthermore, the potential sensitivity of SCRIB to nutritional or environmental inputs during gametogenesis or early development may provide a mechanistic link between maternal diet and embryonic viability. Furthermore, its dysregulation may have profound consequences for offspring’s health, particularly in relation to neural and epidermal development.
Additionally, an intron of CERS3 was found to be hypermethylated in embryos produced from methionine-treated ewes and was therefore selected as another candidate for knockdown in embryos. While CERS3 is predominantly expressed in the testis and skin [ ], it plays a critical role in the synthesis of very long chain ceramides, which are essential components of lipid membranes and signalling molecules [ ]. One study demonstrated that the synthesis of very long chain ceramides by CERS3 is a crucial early step for skin barrier formation, as mutations in the gene lead to disturbed epidermal permeability barrier function [ ]. Similarly, Jennemann and colleagues demonstrated that Cers3 knockout mice possess severe skin barrier defects leading to death shortly after birth, accompanied by a deficiency in epidermal keratinization and an increase in transepidermal water loss [ ]. The CERS3 gene has also been reported to be involved in sphingolipid metabolism and the sphingolipid signalling pathway by KEGG, with molecular functions such as protein binding (GO:0005515) and sphingosine N-acyltransferase activity (GO:0050291). In our study, the knockdown of CERS3 in bovine embryos led to a roughly 10% reduction in blastocyst formation rates, suggesting that CERS3 may be essential for successful early embryonic development. Ceramides regulate cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis through multiple signalling pathways [ ]. Therefore, reduced expression of CERS3 may disrupt these essential processes during early embryonic development. Moreover, the sensitivity of CERS3 to methylation changes in response to maternal diet, along with prior observations of altered CERS1 expression following paternal methionine supplementation [ ], highlights the broader relevance of the ceramide synthase family in early development and its potential responsiveness to nutritional inputs.

## Mitochondrial DNA methylation in oocytes and embryos
We observed differential methylation changes in mitochondrial DNA in both oocytes and embryos produced from methionine-treated versus control dams, with 5 DMCs common to both. Mitochondria and the nuclear epigenome engage in bidirectional crosstalk, where mitochondrial processes mediate epigenetic changes, and nuclear epigenetic regulation influences mitochondrial function. This relationship is key in stress responses, longevity, and cellular metabolism [ , ].
Mitochondria are critical in supplying the intermediate metabolites required for nuclear epigenetic modifications. The regulation of these processes relies heavily on the availability of methyl donors, with the one-carbon cycle being central to linking metabolism and epigenetic regulation. In this pathway, methionine acts as a key methyl donor, providing S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) for DNA methylation reactions. SAM is generated through the methionine-homocysteine cycle in the cytosol. However, this cycle depends on the folate cycle and ATP, which are both reliant on mitochondrial metabolism [ , ]. Given that methionine is an essential component of the one-carbon cycle, it is plausible that dietary supplementation of methionine could influence mtDNA methylation. Studies investigating other dietary interventions, such as adherence to a Mediterranean diet, resulted in hypomethylation of the D-loop region of mtDNA and hypermethylation of MT-CO2 [ ]. Similarly, L-carnitine supplementation in aged women led to increased methylation in the D-loop of mtDNA in platelets [ ].
Importantly, alterations in mtDNA methylation have been associated with various multifactorial diseases, including cardiovascular diseases [ , ] and obesity [ ]. Given these associations, it is of significant interest to explore how nutritional interventions, such as methionine supplementation, might influence mtDNA methylation and contribute to metabolic and developmental outcomes. While this study provides valuable preliminary data, further research is needed to fully validate the impact of methionine on mtDNA methylation and its implications for offspring development. Our findings, however, contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that mitochondrial epigenetics may play a central role in DOHaD and that dietary factors influencing the methylation landscape of both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA could have long-lasting effects on health outcomes.
Limitations of this study include challenges associated with the inherently low DNA quantity in oocytes and embryos, which resulted in suboptimal sequencing depth. This led to reduced and variable alignment rates across samples, with average alignment rates of 27% for oocytes and 33% for embryos. This variability introduced challenges in downstream analysis, as the total number of cytosines available for methylation assessment was lower than expected. Consequently, some biologically relevant DMCs may have been undetected, potentially underestimating the full extent of methionine-induced methylation changes. Future studies could benefit from optimized library preparation to improve sequencing efficiency and provide a more comprehensive assessment of DNA methylation dynamics in these low-input cell types.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this study provides novel insights into the impact of maternal methionine supplementation on DNA methylation patterns in oocytes and embryos, highlighting potential epigenetic mechanisms through which maternal diet influences offspring development. Compared with hypermethylation in embryos, the widespread hypomethylation observed in oocytes suggests a complex interplay between maternal nutrition and epigenetic reprogramming. The identification of differentially methylated genes involved in key developmental processes, including neurodevelopment, cellular organization, and metabolism, underscores the potential long-term implications of maternal dietary interventions on fetal programming. Furthermore, the observed changes in mitochondrial DNA methylation reinforce the role of mitochondrial epigenetics in linking maternal nutrition to offspring health.
