## Abstract

## Background
Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and other immune-activating strategies represent the main approach to cancer treatment; however, immune resistance in many solid tumors limits the immune therapy response and can cause strong toxic side effects. In solid tumors, the development of effective anti-tumor immune responses is hindered by limited immune cell infiltration and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). To overcome current immunotherapy challenges, we proposed a novel approach providing local and stable treatment levels to activate or revitalize anti-tumor immunity, to achieve the effect of TME infiltration and sustained presence in solid tumors.

## Methods
Using genetic engineering methods to synergistically activate immunity in the TME, we programmed macrophages to express therapeutic payloads, including interleukin (IL)-12 and the signal regulatory protein alpha-Fragment crystallizable fusion protein (SIRPα-Fc), a CD47 ICI. Co-culture studies were performed to evaluate the effects of the genetically engineered macrophages (GEMs) on the T cells and GEMs themselves in vitro. We evaluated the tumor response, cellular response, and cytokine response. The GEMs were administered to a mouse model of tumor-cell transplantation, where they were retained and expressed as lentiviral payloads.

## Results
The IL-12 secreted by the GEMs provided effector signals for T cells, thereby enhancing the tumor resident anti-tumor macrophages and CD8+T-cell populations. In addition, the secretion of SIRPα-Fc enhanced the phagocytic activity of the macrophages toward tumor cells and promoted their antigen presentation function. The combination therapy of dual proteins produced significant synergistic effects in solid tumor models and further enhanced memory immunity. The GEMs also improved the efficacy of ICIs in the ICI-resistant gene engineering tumor models and demonstrated significant anti-tumor efficacy in the metastasis models.

## Conclusions
Our study showed the potential clinical application of GEMs in the treatment of tumors.

## Highlight box

## Key findings
• We engineered a cell-based immunotherapy platform—genetically engineered macrophages (GEMs)—that co-loads interleukin-12 with a CD47-blocking SIRPα-Fc fusion. These living vectors pierce solid tumours and sustain release of both payloads at the disease site.

## What is known and what is new?
• In solid tumours, scant immune cell entry coupled with a deeply immunosuppressive milieu forms a dual roadblock; this "cold" and hostile microenvironment cripples therapeutic immunity and fuels systemic toxicity.
• We introduce a strategy that secures high, sustained drug concentrations inside the tumour, re-igniting silenced immunity while ensuring deep and durable tumor microenvironment penetration.

## What is the implication, and what should change now?
• Our findings position GEMs as a readily translatable therapeutic for clinical oncology.

## Introduction
The use of immunotherapy in the treatment of solid tumors is an emerging field. Currently, the effectiveness of immunotherapy is limited, but it holds promise for improving patient outcomes ( ). T-cell adoptive cell therapy [e.g., chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) immunotherapy] has produced exciting results in the treatment of hematological malignancies, but its application to solid tumors has been proven challenging due to cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, autoimmune diseases, the risk of secondary malignancy, and the risk of infection ( , ). Therefore, an antigen independent cell therapy that persists in tumors represents a promising approach for restoring endogenous immune cell infiltration and anti-tumor function.
Interleukin (IL)-12 is mainly secreted by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, and is known for its anti-tumor properties ( ). It plays a critical role in initiating the cytokine cascade that leads to interferon gamma (IFN-γ)-mediated immune activation ( ). Strictly speaking, “cellular immunity” refers to immunity driven exclusively by T lymphocytes. After encountering their cognate antigen, naïve T cells undergo clonal expansion and differentiate into armed effector cells that display distinct surface receptors and antigen specificity. These effector subsets fall into three functional sets: cytotoxic T cells, helper T (Th) cells, and regulatory T cells. IL-12 was initially shown to support the Th1 cell response and recruitment of cluster of differentiation 4-positive (CD4+) and cluster of differentiation 8-positive (CD8+) T cells ( - ). Although the high-dose administration of recombinant IL-12 is effective in the treatment of cancer, high systemic IL-12 peak concentrations can have severe side effects ( , ).
Macrophages, myeloid-derived innate immune cells involved in antigen presentation and phagocytosis, are among the most abundant immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and their functional plasticity enables them to switch between anti-tumor and pro-tumor roles depending on the context. This plasticity has driven numerous approaches to deplete or repolarize tumor-associated macrophages ( , ). The phagocytic activity of macrophages can be enhanced by reducing the anti-phagocytic cell “don’t-eat-me” signal.
The most important anti-phagocytic axis is based on the binding of cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47) on tumor cells to signaling regulatory protein alpha (SIRP-α) on macrophages ( ). CD47 binds to the SIRP-α on the surface of macrophages and inhibits their phagocytic ability, thereby enabling tumor cells to evade recognition and surveillance by the immune system ( ). The CD47 blocking antibody has been used in the treatment of hematomas and solid tumors, but its further efficacy is limited by side effects such as blood toxicity ( ). In addition, clinical trials have shown that CD47-targeted monotherapy has limited efficacy in the treatment of most solid tumors, with the exception of a few hematomas. Further research on combinations of the CD47 blockade and other immunotherapies in the treatment of solid tumors is necessary ( , ).
This study established a multi-protein genetically engineered macrophage (GEM)-based immunotherapy platform capable of co-delivering the immune-activating cytokine IL-12 and the CD47 antagonist SIRPα-Fc directly to solid tumors. This highly promising, precision immunotherapy acts rapidly and efficiently against tumors. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1591/rc).

## Methods

## Cell lines and primary cell cultures
Mouse ovarian epithelial carcinoma cells (ID8) cells and 293T cells purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Virginia, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 containing 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 100 µg/mL of streptomycin, and 100 U/mL of penicillin at 37 °C in 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). Bone marrow-derived myeloid (BMDM) cells were harvested from the tibial bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice and prepared according to the conventional method. The cells were differentiated with 5 ng/mL of mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (mM-CSF) to increase the purity of the F4/80+ macrophages, and cytokines were continuously supplemented to maintain cell vitality. Fresh medium was exchanged every 48–72 hours, and fully differentiated BMDMs were collected on day 7.
The M2-polarized macrophages were harvested after treatment with 50 ng/mL of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 hours, and then stimulated with 20 ng/mL of IL-4 for 24 hours. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from mouse peripheral blood using gradient centrifugation. Total T cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 mice using Dynabeads untouched mouse T-cell kit of magnetic sorting technology based on the biotin streptavidin system, and activated with anti-CD28 (5 µg/mL) and anti-CD3 (5 µg/mL) beads (P5988960, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and IL-2 (8 ng/mL) in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium for 24 hours at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

## Design and construction of viral vector
The artificial IL-12 gene was cloned into a viral vector by linking p35 and p40 through a glycine-serine linker (G4S). The CD47 fusion protein (SIRPα-Fc) comprised the mouse SIRP-α N-terminal V domain linked to mouse IgG2a Fc, which is similar to the human IgG1 Fc region and has the complete effector function. The C-terminus of the SIRPα-Fc protein carried a His tag. To construct a vector encoding mouse GEM, the SIRPα-Fc gene was inserted into the restriction enzyme cleavage sites of the viral vector, and the thosea asigna virus 2A (T2A) self-cleaving peptide from the porcine teschovirus-1 polyprotein was inserted between IL-12 and SIRPα-Fc.

## Packaging of lentiviruses and construction of cell lines
To package virus protein X (Vpx) in lentivirus, HEK293T cells were co transfected using a four-plasmid system. On the basis of the standard three plasmids (transfer plasmid (carrying the target gene and puromycin resistance gene), packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G), this system additionally adds Vpx. The addition of expression plasmid Vpx can significantly enhance the infection efficiency of the virus on difficult to transduce cells such as myeloid cells. The plasmids were mixed in optimized proportions and transfected, and the collected virus supernatant was concentrated by ultracentrifugation. Collect virus supernatant 48 and 72 hours after transfection, filter through 0.45 µm membrane and concentrate by ultracentrifugation.
The virus titer was determined using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) method, expressed in transduction units (TU/mL). To construct a cell line expressing the target gene, the target cells were co incubated with virus supernatant and poly gel (8 µg/mL). After 24 hours, the medium was replaced with a complete medium containing an appropriate amount of puromycin (determined through preliminary experiments) for screening. The transfection rate was identified to be above 90% and used for the following experiments.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Takara, RR820A, Beijing, China), according to a standard protocol. RNA was converted to complementary DNA using a TaKaRa reverse recording kit (Takara, RR047A, China). The 7900HT Quick RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, USA) and SYBR Premix EX Taq were used to analyze messenger RNA (mRNA) expression. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

## Cytokine detection
The concentrations of mouse cytokine IL-12 (Elabscience, E-EL-M3062, Wuhan, China) in the serum were measured at days 1, 4, 7, and 10. Additionally, the His-tag (Genscript Biotech, L00436, Nanjing, China) levels of the BMDMs transfected with or without lentivirus were detected. The IFN-γ (Elabscience, E-EL-M0048, China) levels in the supernatant of the PBMCs co-cultured with the conditional medium (CM) of the GEMs for 24 hours were measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Flow cytometry
The 1×106 ID8 cells were collected and dispensed into 1.5-mL Eppendorf (EP) tubes, and incubated with phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-mouse CD47 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25-0479-42) for 30 minutes on ice under darkness. After being washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were suspended in 500 µL of PBS containing 1% BSA and analyzed using a Becton Dickinson FACSCalibur flow cytometer (San Jose, CA, USA). To verify the phagocytic effect of the GEMs, the ID8 cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE). After co-culturing, the GEMs were isolated using the mouse macrophage Magnetic Cell Sorting Kit (MACS, 130-049-601, Miltenyi, Germany). The data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.4.2 software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

## In vitro phagocytosis activity assay in macrophages
The BMDMs were seeded into a 48-well plate (80,000 cells/well) and incubated in cell culture media for 24 hours. The ID8 cells were incubated in PBS containing 10 µM of CFSE (CFDA-SE, V12883, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 minutes and washed three times with PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The BMDMs or treated GEMs were co-cultured with the CFSE+ID8 cells for 24 hours. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD LSR (Laser Scanning) flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) flow cytometer, and the data analysis was conducted using FlowJo.

## In vivo therapeutic evaluation of GEMs in the ID8 model
Male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks old, average weight 18±2 g) were inoculated with 7×106 ID8 cells intradermally on day 0. To verify the synergistic effect of different GEMs (the isolate secreted IL-12 or SIRPα-Fc or combo), the mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with GEMs or 100 µL of PBS on day 17, using a Random Number Table for group assignment.
To verify the synergistic anti-tumor effect of the GEMs with programmed death-1 inhibitor antibody (αPD-1) (BE0146, Amyjet Scientific, Wuhan, China), the mice were divided into four groups based on different treatments: the PBS group (n=10), which received 100 µL of PBS intravenously on day 17; the αPD-1 group (n=10), which received 100 µg of αPD-1 intraperitoneally on days 17, 21, 25, and 29; the combo GEM group (n=10), which received 8×106 GEMs with IL-12 or SIRPα-Fc intravenously on day 17; and the αPD-1+ combo GEM group (n=10), which received the combined treatment. To confirm that the GEMs delivered immune regulatory proteins locally to the tumor, the mice were divided into three groups based on different treatments: the PBS group (n=10), which received 100 µL of PBS intravenously on day 17; the control group (n=10), which received 8×106 BMDMs transfected with vector-lentivirus intravenously on day 17; and the combo GEM group (n=10), which received 8×106 GEMs with IL-12 or SIRPα-Fc intravenously on day 17. Starting from day 1, all the mice received the treatments three times at 4-day intervals until the tumor burden reached an endpoint of 1,500 mm3.
All the operations performed on the mice were approved by the China Medical University Standards for the Laboratory Animals Welfare and Ethical Review Committee (permit number: 2023PS1077K). All animal research was conducted in accordance with the Guiding Opinions on the Treatment of Laboratory Animals and the Laboratory Animal Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Animal Welfare issued by the National Standard GB/T35892-2018 of the People’s Republic of China. A protocol was prepared before the study.

## Tumor accumulation, pharmacokinetics, and biodistribution of immune regulatory proteins
The C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with 7×106 ID8 cells intradermally on day 0, and injected with the combo GEM treatment on day 17. Blood, spleen, liver and lung tissues, and tumors, were collected at scheduled time points (days 18, 21, 24, and 27) after the mice were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane inhalation and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Organs were harvested and homogenized using Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (T-PER; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a protease inhibitor tablet. The organ and tumor accumulation or circulating amounts of cytokines were measured.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence
The tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The paraffinized sections were separated using xylene before rehydration with an ethanol gradient. After antigen retrieval in 10 mM of citric acid buffer, the tissue sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 10 minutes and sealed at 37 °C for 1 hour. The tissue sections were then incubated overnight with the following antibodies: ki-67 (1:500, Abcam, ab15580, Cambridge, UK), CD8a (1:500, Abcam, ab217344, UK), F4/80 (1:500, CST, #30325, Danvers, USA), CD163 (1:500, Abcam, ab182422, UK), Perforin (1:500, Abcam, ab16074, UK), IFN-γ (1:500, CST, #98139, USA), IL-12 (1:500, Abcam, ab80682, UK), and His-Tag (1:500, CST, #12698, USA). Next, the membranes were incubated with an appropriate secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 hour. The tissue sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies. After nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, the sections were imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The images were captured using a DX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The resulting data were analyzed using Image Pro Plus software (version 6.0; Media Cybernetics, Rockville, USA).

## Statistical analysis
The statistical analysis was performed using Prism version 7.03 or greater (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data are presented as the mean value ± standard error. P values were calculated for the bar graphs using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance, or Kruskal-Wallis test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The levels of significance were denoted as follows: *, P≤0.05; **, P≤0.01; ***, P≤0.001; ****, P≤0.0001; ns, no significance.

## Results

## GEMs expressed immunomodulatory proteins
To generate the GEMs, mouse BMDMs were transduced with lentivirus encoding SIRPα-Fc, mIL-12, or SIRPα-Fc/mIL-12 ( ). The BMDMs containing exogenous IL-12 encoding stably expressed and secreted IL-12 during normal passage in vitro ( ). The expression of the target gene was detected by qRT-PCR using self-designed SIRP-Fc primers ( ). The secretion of SIRPα-Fc by the GEMs was further confirmed by His-ELISA assay of the culture supernatant of the GEMs. The target protein was present in the supernatant of the SIRPα-Fc and Combo GEM cells, but not in the mIL-12 GEMs ( ).
 To evaluate whether SIRPα-Fc in the GEM cell supernatant could bind to CD47 on the surface of tumor cells, an antibody competition assay based on flow cytometry was performed ( ). The results showed that CD47 was highly expressed on the ID8 tumor cells at the baseline. After co-incubation with the supernatant of the GEMs encoding the SIRPα-Fc gene, the fluorescence intensity of CD47 staining was significantly reduced, but it did not decrease after co-incubation with mIL-12 GEM cells ( ). These results showed that the GEMs effectively secreted the SIRPα-Fc and IL-12 proteins.

## GEMs achieved anti-tumor function by secreting target proteins and M2 reduction
We then examined the anti-tumor biological function of the GEMs. Under normal circumstances, IL-12 is mainly produced by APCs and stimulates peripheral blood lymphocytes to promote inflammation. A key function of IL-12 is to promote the production of IFN-γ by target lymphocytes. Therefore, we collected PBMCs co-cultured with BMDMs or GEMs ( ). The control group or SIRPα-Fc GEMs induced minimal secretion of IFN-γ, while the GEMs secreting mIL-12 induced the PBMCs to produce a large amount of IFN-γ ( ). Next, we directly examined the effect of the GEMs on the sorted T cells. After co-culturing, the control group or SIRPα-Fc GEMs showed a certain degree of T-cell activation, but the GEMs secreting mIL-12 showed a more enhanced effect, as detected by the typical surface markers of T-cell activation, such as CD25 and CD69 ( ). Additionally, the GEMs secreting mIL-12 had a stronger inhibitory effect on the T-cell exhaustion phenotype (LAG3, the marker of T-cell exhaustion; ). To verify the phagocytic effect of the GEMs, we labeled the ID8 cells with CFSE and detected the macrophages by flow cytometry. After co-culturing, the GEMs secreting SIRPα-Fc showed a stronger phagocytic ability, demonstrating that the secretion of SIRPα-Fc promoted the anti-tumor phagocytosis of the GEMs ( ).
 We also explored whether the GEMs reduced the M2 macrophages in the TME in vitro. We treated the GEMs with ID8 or T cells for 24 hours, harvested the CM of the GEMs, and then exposed the M2-polarized macrophages to this CM for another 24 hours to assess phenotypic changes in the M2 cells ( ) It should be noted that the BMDMs were harvested after treatment with 50 ng/mL of LPS for 24 hours and then with 20 ng/mL of IL-4 stimulation for 24 hours. As show, we found that the mRNA expression levels of IL-10 and TGF-B (the markers of the M2-polarized macrophages) were significantly higher, and those of iNOS and CD80 (the markers of the M1-polarized macrophages) were significantly lower after co-culturing with the CM of ID8 cells, but no such significant difference was observed after the isolated treatment of the CM of the GEMs. However, after co-culturing with the CM of the ID8 and GEMs, the mRNA expression levels of IL-10 and TGF-B decreased significantly, while those of iNOS and CD80 increased significantly. Further, after the T-cell treatment, we found that only those co-cultured with the CM of the T cells and GEMs displayed a significant shift in the phenotype of the BMDMs. The mRNA expression levels of IL-10 and TGF-B were significantly decreased, while the those of iNOS and CD80 were significantly increased ( ). We then examined the mechanism underlying the M2 reduction. There may be two, independent, explanations for the loss of M2 identity: (I) tumor-cell debris internalized by macrophages may trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines that actively repolarize neighboring M2 cells toward an M1 phenotype; and (II) GEMs may license T cells to produce IFN-γ, which then acts on macrophages to consolidate M1 programming.

## GEMs can reduce M2 macrophages and activate T cells to inhibit tumor growth
To evaluate the anti-tumor effects of the GEMs in vivo, we subcutaneously injected ID8 cells into the flanks of mice and examined the tumor burden based on the tumor volume ( ). The results showed that throughout the experimental period, there was no significant difference in the body weight among the mice in the different treatment groups, indicating that the different treatment methods did not affect the mice’s weight and there was no difference of the blood counts and liver/kidney function; that is, no obvious toxicity was observed ( and Figure S1). All the GEMs significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo, but the GEMs secreted in combination with the two anti-tumor proteins exhibited the most significant tumor inhibitory effect. Compared with the PBS treatment, both the GEMs secreting SIRPα-Fc and mIL-12 inhibited tumor growth. The GEMs secreting mIL-12 showed a stronger tumor inhibitory effect compared to the GEMs secreting SIRPα-Fc ( ). The combined secretion of the proteins better alleviated tumor proliferation ( ). This may be related to the ability of the GEMs secreting both proteins to reduce tumor-promoting M2 macrophages ( ). Moreover, the expression of CD8+ and IFN-γ was the highest in the combined protein secretion group ( ). These results indicated that the GEMs reduced the M2 macrophages and promoted T-cell activation to inhibit tumor growth in vivo.
 

## GEMs enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of αPD-1 and promoted memory immunity
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as αPD-1 and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitor antibody are representative of immune oncology drugs that have shown significant therapeutic outcomes in the treatment of advanced cancer. The combination therapy of GEMs and αPD-1 may further activate the anti-tumor activity of T cells. We evaluated the efficacy of αPD-1combined with the GEM therapy in a mouse ID8 tumor model ( ). Compared with the αPD-1 monotherapy or the GEM therapy, the αPD-1 + GEM therapy had the most significant anti-tumor effect ( ), and the combination therapy resulted in the complete recovery of 60% (3 of 5) of the tumor-bearing mice ( ). The combination therapy promoted the anti-tumor activation of effector T cells, including the increased expression of CD8a, perforin, and IFN-γ ( ). Due to their ability to promote anti-tumor phagocytosis by macrophages and initiate the antigen presentation, the GEMs promoted the memory immunity. Compared with the αPD-1 monotherapy, the GEM monotherapy increased the proportion of memory T cells (CD44+CD3+T) in the spleen of the mice ( ).
 

## GEMs delivered immune regulatory proteins locally to the tumor and the pre-metastatic microenvironment
It has been proven that BMDM cells are elevated in the circulation of mice and humans, and highly enriched in the tumor and metastatic microenvironment (tumor cells need to enter the circulation and reach the pre metastatic lung before reaching the metastatic site). We performed the GEM treatment on the tumor-bearing mice, with PBS and empty vector transfected macrophages as controls, and confirmed IL-12 production by ELISA ( ). After the intravenous (i.v.) administration of the GEMs to the tumor-bearing mice, the IL-12 levels reached their peak at the tumor site 4 days after the treatment ( ). Similar trends were also observed in the spleen ( ), lungs ( ), but there was no significant difference in the liver ( ) and while, the IL-12 levels reached their peak at the tumor site 4 days after the treatment of the serum ( ). Notably, IL-12 at the tumor site continued to be expressed after 10 days of treatment ( ), and this expression persisted until at least day 17 after treatment ( ). Further, SIRPα-Fc continued to be expressed in the tumor until the 17th day after the treatment ( ). These data collectively indicated that the GEMs effectively delivered the immune regulatory proteins to the tumor sites and had a long-term therapeutic effect. Meanwhile, the GEMs delivered immune regulatory proteins to the microenvironment prior to metastasis, which may disrupt the immune evasion of disseminated tumor cells.
 

## Discussion
This study described a multi-protein GEM immunotherapy platform that can locally deliver the immune-activating factor IL-12 and CD47 blocker SIRPα-Fc fusion protein to solid tumors. Macrophages are present in many solid tumors, which suggests that the TME of solid tumors supports the long-term residence of macrophages. However, the entry, infiltration, and persistent presence of macrophages in solid tumors are significant barriers to natural T cells and immunotherapy (such as ICI and CAR-T therapy) ( - ). Our in vivo studies showed that the GEMs survived in solid tumors and were transported to subcutaneous tumors ( ). The GEMs exhibited strong anti-tumor activity in vivo and improved the therapeutic effect in solid tumors. The results showed that the secretion of SIRPα-Fc by the GEMs enhanced the phagocytic activity of macrophages, increased the anti-tumor activity of effector T cells, and reduced immune suppression in tumor tissues, promoting the long-term immunity of tumors. This demonstrated the potential of GEMs in the treatment of solid tumors.
Blocking the CD47-SIRPα signaling axis can enhance the tumor phagocytic ability of macrophages and lead to the depletion of M2 macrophages in vitro ( , ). The CD47 blockade not only affects macrophage function, but also induces DC maturation ( ). However, CD47 is widely expressed in many cells and can cause serious side effects when treated with CD47 antibodies. The expression of CD47 on red blood cells or platelets can prevent anti-CD47 antibodies from reaching the required therapeutic dose in tumor tissue and may induce severe anemia ( ). Compared with traditional antibodies, the SIRPα-Fc fusion protein has a smaller molecular size, which facilitates tissue penetration and distribution ( ). The binding rate of SIRPα-Fc to human red blood cells is extremely low, which can reduce the risk of anemia, but can still induce a high incidence of thrombocytopenia ( ). Therefore, lower doses or local administration are necessary, but this may further reduce efficacy.
IL-12 plays a central role in anti-tumor immunity. A single pulse induces naïve CD4+ cells to become Th1 effectors, drives natural killer (NK) cells to a highly cytotoxic state, and converts macrophages from the tissue-repair to a microbicidal M1 mode. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes respond with accelerated proliferation, stronger killing kinetics and long-term memory formation. These cellular changes are accompanied by a surge of IFN-γ, granzyme, and perforin that directly drives cancer-cell apoptosis while also up-regulating major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and costimulatory molecules on APCs. The resulting chemokine gradient recruits additional waves of effector leukocytes, turning an immunologically “cold” tumor into an inflamed, growth-controlled lesion. Brempelis et al. showed that compared with control GEMs, IL-12 GEMs amplified tumor-cell killing and established a pro-inflammatory milieu in the tumor slice, reflected by elevated IFN-γ and the chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 ( ). Although early IL-12 clinical trials were associated with toxicity, recent trials have shown that the local delivery of IL-12 is well tolerated, promotes CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and increases serum IFN-γ ( ).
Recent treatment methods have focused on improving the targeting of IL-12 to tumors via the use of nanoparticles or oncolytic viruses ( - ). Early phase studies are now testing new engineered extracellular vesicles as living-drug couriers that ferry antisense oligos, small interfering RNA, or tumor antigens to malignant tissue ( ). Encouragingly, these first-in-human trials suggest that such extracellular vesicle-based regimens can target tumors more precisely, awaken durable anti-cancer immunity, and inflict markedly fewer off-target toxicities than traditional chemotherapy or radiotherapy ( ).
Moreover, synthetic-biology-engineered cells can be programmed to secrete immunomodulatory cytokines on demand. IL-12-armed myeloid cells (IL-12-GEMy cells) can up-regulate both adaptive-immunity activation genes and antigen-processing/presentation pathways; this molecular switch recruits waves of activated T lymphocytes, NK cells, and DCs into the lung parenchyma, thereby converting the metastatic niche into an immunologically hostile microenvironment ( ). Therefore, disrupting the CD47-SIRPα “don’t-eat-me” signal restores macrophage phagocytosis, while IL-12 simultaneously promotes the activation of T and NK cells; together, the two interventions are expected to converge on the tumor from both innate and adaptive angles, producing a cooperative-and potentially deeper-anti-tumor response.
Macrophages have good tumor chemotaxis and retention abilities, and act as “active drug carriers”. They could be the ideal carriers for CD47 inhibitors and IL-12 delivery. They can limit the serious side effects of CD47 inhibitors or IL-12 through local secretion and accumulation in the TME. Based on this logic, we designed GEMs in this study. The GEMs secreted SIRPα-Fc, which can bind to CD47 and enhance the phagocytic effect of macrophages on tumor cells. In addition, the GEMs were also beneficial for the production of memory T cells ( ), thereby promoting the long-term inhibitory effect of tumors, demonstrating the synergistic effect of the CD47 blockade and IL-12 immune activation. Our results indicated that the positive synergistic effect of IL-12 immune activation and the CD47 blockade may be applied to solid tumors. In addition, although the GEMs need to be evaluated in humans in vivo, our preliminary results indicated that the GEMs were resistant to immune suppression bias ( ). The secretion of IFN-γ driven by the mouse GEMs and its effect on the tumor burden (in mice) indicated that the GEMs can deliver their effective load and stimulate an anti-tumor immune response. The production of local anti-tumor proteins may also be effective for metastatic or multifocal diseases ( ).
The GEM platform could also improve existing therapies. The local expression of IL-12 in tumors derived from engineered NK cell lines or CAR-Ts themselves can enhance the anti-tumor effect of CAR-Ts ( , ). Although the innovative CAR-T technology can improve tumors targeting limited antigens and kill low antigen density tumors, the TME promotes the rapid depletion of T cells ( , ). As phagocytic APCs, GEMs can present evolved antigens to T cells, which may activate endogenous T cells and achieve long-term anti-tumor activity. In addition, binding with the T-cell ICI PD-1 will further enhance anti-tumor ability of T cells ( ). Therefore, GEMs are not only a well-defined concept validation therapeutic payload, but the secretion of persistent anti-tumor proteins in tumors may also have the potential to reproduce efficacy with fewer doses (relative to rapidly expanding CAR-Ts) and fewer interventions than current local delivery methods (e.g., transient plasmid electroporation). Further, GEMs can be engineered with hypoxia-inducible promoters or chimeric receptors designed to bind proteins enriched in the TME to further restrict payload expression to the TME. In addition, we showed that macrophages engineered to secrete modified IL-12 and SIRPα-Fc are protected against xeno-rejection.
There are some limitations in this research. Conditional payload expression is particularly important for the GEM platform given that intravenously delivered macrophages initially enter the lungs. Obtaining sufficient human macrophages is the biggest obstacle to macrophage-based cell therapy, and it is often difficult to manipulate human macrophages to perform genetically, which is another major challenge faced by GEM products ( ). Thus far, the anti-tumor activity of our GEM formulation has been validated solely in the murine ID8 ovarian-cancer model. To broaden the evidence base, we will extend the pipeline to multiple solid-tumor lineages—both in vitro and in vivo—with particular emphasis on PD-L1 checkpoint-refractory clones, thereby establishing that GEM therapy can overcome resistance to immune-checkpoint blockade.

## Conclusions
We proposed a cell immunotherapy platform whereby GEMs can penetrate solid tumors and stably deliver therapeutic proteins. These drug carrier cells can be used alone or in combination with other immunotherapies to stimulate anti-tumor immune responses. Our GEM platform combined IL-12 cytokines with the CD47 blocker SIRPα-Fc fusion protein to effectively deliver these anti-tumor proteins to tumor sites through macrophages, addressing issues related to anti-tumor proteins in the treatment of solid tumors, and exerting strong anti-tumor effects in mouse tumor models. The GEM therapy affected the innate and adaptive immune responses, and exerted synergistic anti-tumor effects in solid tumors. The GEMs improved the anti-tumor efficacy of solid tumors by enhancing macrophage phagocytosis, recruiting more DCs to tumor tissues, and increasing the proportion of CD8+T cells and memory T cells. Our study showed the potential clinical application of GEMs in the treatment of tumors. We intend to extend the application of this therapy to a wider range of malignancies.
