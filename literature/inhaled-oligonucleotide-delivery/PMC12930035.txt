## Introduction
Air pollution contributes to serious health problems. Among air pollutants, PM2.5 is the predominant contaminant consisting of harmful fine particles with a diameter of less than 2.5 μm ( ). Previous research has found that PM2.5 contributes to the occurrence of a broad spectrum of diseases such as respiratory disorder, cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer's disease, and cancer ( , , ). PM2.5 is a heterogeneous mixture of various pollutants, with the characteristics of large relative surface area, small particle size, and strong ability to adsorb toxic substances ( ). Toxic substances such as heavy metals, carbon-containing particles, nitrates, sulfates, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, bacteria, and viruses present on PM2.5 can invade and stagnate in the smallest airways and alveolar ends. This directly leads to lung damage, inducing and promoting diseases of the respiratory system ( , ). Studies have confirmed that long-term or short-term exposure to significant amounts of PM2.5 can significantly increase the chances of respiratory infections and exacerbate existing pulmonary disorders, including lung cancer, asthma, and COPD ( , , ).
Some researchers suggest that the uptake of PM2.5 by alveolar macrophages can promote local inflammation in the lungs, thereby triggering a systemic inflammatory response ( ). In the lungs, the small size of PM2.5 particles allows them to cross the alveolar wall and enter the surrounding capillaries and lymphatic vessels, thus entering the systemic circulation. This process can lead to structural and functional damage to the alveoli ( ). Inhalation of PM2.5 activates pulmonary redox metabolism, induces local inflammation, and stimulates the pulmonary arc reflex. Resident alveolar macrophages take up PM2.5, leading to oxidative stress and the release of inflammatory mediators—a central mechanism driving the local inflammatory response ( ). These inflammatory signals are then transmitted via the bloodstream to secondary organs through bioactive intermediates such as oxidized lipids, inflammatory cytokines, and chemokines. This process further promotes the generation of abundant free radicals in the lungs, rapidly disrupting the oxidative-antioxidant balance and significantly impairing lung function ( , ). A corresponding increase in the body's stress response to lung oxidative damage occurs as antioxidant levels in lung mucus rise compensatorily ( ). Although the exact mechanisms by which PM2.5 induces lung damage remain unclear, our previous works and increasing evidence have reported that PM2.5-stimulated lung damage is inextricably linked to its positive pro-inflammatory effect ( , , ). Studies have demonstrated that phagocytosis of PM2.5 by alveolar macrophages leads to activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent release of the key pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β ( ). Jones et al. confirmed that the induction of NLRP3 in alveolar macrophages activates Caspase1, which stimulates the release of inflammatory factor IL1β and aggravates lung damage ( ). Recent studies have confirmed that NLRP3-related pyroptosis is related to acute lung injury and the release of various inflammatory factors ( , ).
Pyroptosis is a form of regulated cell death marked by cell swelling, pore formation in the lipid membrane, and the secretion of pro-inflammatory molecules. ( , ). The inflammasome, composed of NLRP3, ASC (an adapter protein with a caspase recruitment domain), and Pro-caspase1, is essential for pyroptosis ( ). As the primary effector cells of the alveoli, macrophages are involved in defense and immunity. When the cell membrane recognition receptor receives an inflammatory signal, it activates the NLRP3 inflammasome complex and Caspase1 ( ). Studies have also confirmed that PM2.5 activates the NLRP3/Caspase1 signaling pathway to induce lung inflammation ( ). Therefore, pyroptosis may be a fundamental mechanism within PM2.5-stimulated lung damage.
Given the involvement of NLRP3 in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases, many studies have been undertaken to identify NLRP3 inhibitors. Some of the inhibitors identified include MCC950, CY-09, Tranilast, OLT1177, and Oridonin ( ). MCC950 is a specific inhibitor of NLRP3 that interacts with the NACHT domain, inhibiting ATP hydrolysis and blocking NLRP3 oligomerization ( ). Research has demonstrated that MCC950 exhibits strong inhibitory activity on inflammation in mice with spontaneous colitis, cholestatic liver injury, acute pancreatitis, dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis, and allergic rhinitis effect ( , , , ). In our previous works, we observed that MCC950 reduces Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated lung inflammation in mice ( ), but there are only limited studies on MCC950 and PM2.5-stimulated acute lung damage.
Herein, we established a murine model of acute lung injury induced by short-term, high-dose exposure to PM2.5, along with in vitro experiments in which MH–S macrophages were stimulated with PM2.5 to trigger pyroptosis. These approaches were used to evaluate the role of PM2.5 in NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis. We further employed both cellular and animal models to assess the pulmonary toxicity of PM2.5 and to explore the potential anti-inflammatory mechanisms of MCC950. The results of this study will help inform future clinical investigations into respiratory diseases associated with environmental pollution.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## PM2.5 sampling and suspension preparation
PM2.5 monitoring and collection were conducted on the rooftop of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University in Shijiazhuang, China. The PM2.5 sampler was positioned approximately 500 m in altitude from the main road, at a height of 35 m above ground level, with an air flow rate set at 100 L/min. The measured PM2.5 concentration was 115 μg/m3. Glass fiber filters were used to collect particles originating from vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, and environmental dust.
PM2.5 samples were collected using a high-pressure infrared large-flow air sampler (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The glass filters contained in the sampler were treated with a KQ-400KDB ultrasonic cleaner (5 × 30 min). After extraction, the samples were promptly filtered, frozen, dried, and concentrated, then stored at –20 °C for subsequent analysis. The elemental composition of PM2.5 was analyzed using an energy-dispersive spectrometer. In our previous studies, PM2.5 samples were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope with a heated tungsten filament electron source, operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Quantitative compositional analysis revealed the following elemental mass fractions: carbon (4%), oxygen (24%), sodium (7%), magnesium (2%), aluminum (6%), silicon (39%), gold (5%), sulfur (4%), potassium (3%), calcium (5%), and barium (2%) ( , ).. A PM2.5 suspension at a concentration of 2 mg/mL was prepared in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

2.2

## Animals and model of acute lung injury (ALI)
Male C57BL/6JNifdc mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–24 g; VAF/SPF grade) were obtained from the Beijing Laboratory Animal Research Center. The C57BL/6J inbred strain was selected due to its genetic homogeneity and well-established role as a standard model in pulmonary research, particularly for acute lung injury and inflammation, ensuring reproducibility and direct comparability with existing literature. The use of VAF/SPF-grade animals was essential to exclude confounding effects of common respiratory pathogens, thereby ensuring that observed lung injury and inflammatory responses could be unequivocally attributed to PM2.5 exposure.
To further minimize physiological variability, only male mice were used. Female sex hormones such as estradiol are known to modulate pulmonary inflammation, and the estrous cycle could introduce fluctuations that confound PM2.5-specific effects. The use of males provides a stable baseline for clearer mechanistic analysis. Additionally, mice at the young adult stage (6–8 weeks) were employed to ensure full immune maturity without age-related functional decline or chronic low-grade inflammation, making this life stage suitable for modeling acute PM2.5-induced injury while avoiding aging as a confounding factor.
All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a controlled environment (23 ± 2°C, 55 ± 5% humidity, 12/12  h light/dark cycle) with free access to an irradiated growth-maintenance diet (compliant with Chinese National Standard GB/T 14924.1) and water. Mice were co-housed with five individuals per cage. After one week of acclimatization, they were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 8 per group): a control (Control) group, a PM2.5–exposed (PM2.5) group, a PM2.5 + MCC950–treated (PM2.5 + MCC950) group, and a PM2.5 + dexamethasone–treated (PM2.5 + Dex) group, the latter serving as a positive control.
During the initial phase of the study, we established the model by administering a single, high-dose intratracheal instillation of PM2.5 to mice, exposing them to concentrations of 4, 8, or 16 mg/kg for a 24-hour period. Following anesthesia induced by an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), mice were positioned in a supine posture on a surgical platform. The tongue was gently extended, and a blunt-tipped 3 mL intravenous catheter was carefully advanced into the trachea under cold light guidance. A PM2.5 suspension was then instilled intratracheally, after which the mice were gently rotated to facilitate uniform distribution of the solution within the pulmonary tissue.
Our results identified 8 mg/kg as the optimal concentration for establishing the acute lung injury (ALI) model ( ). Histological analysis at this dose confirmed pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration and other pathological alterations characteristic of ALI ( , PM2.5 group). Mice exhibited expected signs of acute respiratory toxicity, including wheezing and dyspnea, supporting the successful induction of a significant yet sublethal toxic response. No mortality was observed throughout the study period. The single high-dose instillation protocol was adopted to align with the aim of modeling acute exposure. In contrast to repeated low-dose regimens, this approach elicits a rapid and well-defined peak effect (Cmax–driven), which allows clear observation of acute pathophysiological outcomes within a focused timeframe, while reducing the number of handling events and associated procedural stress. Although the protocol intentionally evokes a transient period of compound–related stress, it minimizes cumulative physical stress and potential local tissue injury from repeated instillations, thereby providing a clearer attribution of the observed effects to the test substance rather than to procedural confounders. 
Animals in the Con group were injected with PBS (100 μL) intratracheally and received 50 μL of PBS intraperitoneally. Dexamethasone was employed as a positive control to benchmark the efficacy of MCC950. As a well-established anti-inflammatory agent, dexamethasone effectively alleviates PM2.5-induced respiratory symptoms, including wheezing and dyspnea, and reduces inflammatory cell infiltration in lung tissue. Twelve hours prior to PM2.5 (8 mg/kg) administration, animals in the experimental groups were injected with MCC950 intraperitoneally ( ) (50 mg/kg; MedChem Express Cat. No. HY‑12815, China) or dexamethasone ( ) (1 mg/kg; Shiyaoyinhu, China).
The mice were sacrificed 24 h after receiving 8 mg/kg PM2.5. Prior to sacrifice, the animals were placed under deep anesthesia via an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). Cervical dislocation was then performed, and lung tissue and blood samples were collected for subsequent assays.The Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Hospital of Qinhuangdao, China, approved animal care and experimental protocols (Approval no: 2023SZDW002).

2.3

## Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) harvesting
Tracheal intubation was performed on the mouse's right lung which was ligated followed by lavaging the airway and left lung of the mouse with 300 uL PBS, 3 times. The recovered liquid was acquired followed by centrifugation at 1200 xg for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were kept at −80 °C for cytokine assays.

2.4

## Hematoxylin & eosin staining
After collecting the BALF, the right lung tissues were excised and fixed with phosphate-buffered formalin (4%) for 72 h. Fixed lung samples underwent dehydration, paraffin embedding, and microtome sectioning. The 4 μm tissue slices were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE, Solarbio, China) to assess the basic morphologies and inflammatory response within the lung tissue.

2.5

## Immunofluorescence imaging
The expression of M1 macrophage markers CD80 and GSDMD in mice lung tissue was detected by immunofluorescence ( ). Briefly, mice lung tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and treated with rabbit anti-CD80 (cat. no. ab215166, Abcam, China, 1:100), mouse anti-GSDMD (cat. no. 2E6C7, Proteintech, China, 1:50), and anti-rabbit-conjugated AlexaFluor® 488 (cat. no. ab150077, Abcam,1:500), anti-mouse-conjugated AlexaFluor® 594 (cat. no. ab150116, Abcam, 1: 500) marker. DAPI (Report Biotech, China) staining was conducted for nuclei identification. Images of marker and nuclei localization were captured with a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

2.6

## Cell culture and treatment
MH-S cells were supplied by the American Type Culture Collection (BFN60807584, Manassas, USA). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell seeded in 6-well plates that achieved the logarithmic growth phase (2x105 cells per well) were exposed to various concentrations of PM2.5 for 8 h to stimulate the cells. MCC950 or dexamethasone was added to the medium 2 h before treatment with PM2.5. Both cells and medium were collected after the co-cultivation (PM2.5 + MCC950 / dexamethasone) period of 8 h. Cells were divided into control (Control) group, PM2.5 (PM2.5) group, MCC950 (MCC950) group, PM2.5 + MCC950 (PM2.5 + MCC950) group and PM2.5 + dexamethasone (PM2.5 + Dex) group according to different intervention drugs.

2.7

## Cell viability assay (CCK-8)
MH-S cells (1x104 cells/well) were grown into 96-well plates for 24 h. MH-S cells were stimulated with PM2.5 (0, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL), MCC950 (0, 1, 3, and 9 nM) or dexamethasone (0, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL) and cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 kit (Dalian Meilun Biotechnology). The drug concentrations selected were: 100 μg/ml PM2.5, 3 nM MCC950, and 10 μg/mL dexamethasone. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a Tecan Spark 10 M microplate reader (Switzerland).

2.8

## Nlrp3 siRNA transfection
Nlrp3 siRNA (RiboBio, China) and RFect transfection reagents (BAIDAI, China) were used to knockdown the NLRP3 expression in MH-S cells with nonspecific siRNA used as the control. After being starved for 24 h, cells underwent transfection with Nlrp3 siRNA or non-specific siRNA for a further 24 h. Subsequently, cells were exposed to PM2.5 (100 μg/mL) and/or MCC950 (300 nM) for 8 h. Western blotting was used to assess transfection efficiency after 48 h.

2.9

## Electron microscopic observation of cell pyroptosis
Briefly, different groups of MH-S cells were grown in 6-well plates for 8 h with an appropriate medium. Then cells were digested with trypsin (0.25%, T1300, Solarbio, China) and washed 3 times with PBS. Cells subjected to various treatments were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde and analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (H-600, Hitachi, Japan).

2.10

## ELISA
IL1β, IL6, IL18 and TNFα levels in mouse serum and MH-S cell culture medium were detected using ELISA kits (Neobioscience, China). OD values at 450 nm were recorded using a TECAN SPARK microplate reader (Tecan Group, Ltd.).

2.11

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)
TriQuick Reagent (Solarbio, China) was employed to isolate total RNA from lung tissue of the ALI model mice or from MH-S cells. cDNA synthesis was carried out using Reverse Transcription Reagents (GeneCopoeia, China). The target genes were amplified with primer pairs ( ) via qPCR Mix 2.0 (GeneCopoeia, China). The PCR reaction was conducted with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30 sec (initial denaturation), then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 sec, 55 °C for 30 sec, and 72 °C for 30 sec, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The mRNA levels were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method. gapdh amplification products (Sangon Biotech, China) were used as the internal reference. 

2.12

## Protein extraction and immunoblotting
Cells and lung tissue were lysed, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 xg for 20 min at 4 °C. Proteins in the supernatant were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Inc.). The protein solution was mixed with loading buffer, boiled for 10 min and kept at ‑80 °C. Equal protein volumes (15 µL) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with skimmed milk (5%) in TBS-Tween for 2 h and incubated at 4℃ overnight with antibodies diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer. The next day, membranes were rinsed and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20) for 2 h at 25 °C. Target protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin. Chemiluminescence images were captured using the Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, Cytiva), and protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ v1.8.0 (NIH, USA).
Primary antibodies included NLRP3 (cat. no. 15101, Cell Signaling Technology‌ (CST), USA,1:1000), Pro-Caspase1 (cat. no. 2225, CST, 1:1000), Caspase1 (cat. no. 4199, CST, 1:1000), IL1β (cat. no. 12242, CST, 1:1000), IL18 (cat. no. 67775, CST, 1:1000), GSDMD-NT (cat. no. DF13758, Affinity, China, 1:1000), NFκB (cat. no. 8242, CST, 1:1000), Phospho-NFκB p65 (Ser536) (cat. no. 3033, CST, 1:1000), IκB (cat. no. 4812, CST, 1:1000), Phospho-IκB (Ser32) (cat. no. 2859, CST, 1:1000), β‑actin (cat. no. 20536–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:5000). Secondary antibodies included Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) HRP (Cat. no. S0001, Affinity, 1:10000) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) HRP (Cat. no. S0002, Affinity, 1:10000).

2.13

## In vivo assessment of caspase-1 activity
The FAM-FLIVO probe (Catalog No. #980, ImmunoChemistry Technologies, LLC), a fluorescently labeled and cell-permeable inhibitor of activated caspases, was employed to detect pyroptosis in live animals by targeting caspase activity ( ). Upon covalent binding to active caspases, this non-cytotoxic probe generates a quantifiable fluorescence signal, enabling the spatial visualization of pyroptosis.The lyophilized probe was reconstituted in 50 µL of sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution. Twenty-four hours after the final PM2.5 challenge, the FLIVO working solution (100 µL per mouse) was administered via tail vein injection. The probe was allowed to circulate for a precise period of 30 min. Following this circulation period, mice were anesthetized in an induction chamber supplied with 4–5% isoflurane in oxygen and then maintained under 1–3% isoflurane via a nose cone on the imaging stage. The thoracic and abdominal fur was carefully removed with an electric animal clipper to minimize background autofluorescence and ensure unimpeded optical access. The mice were then positioned in a supine posture within the imaging chamber of an IVIS® Lumina LT Series III system (PerkinElmer).The resulting signal intensity, quantified as total radiant efficiency ([p/s/cm2/sr] / [µW/cm2]) within a defined region of interest (ROI) over the thoracic cavity, was used to assess the relative levels of pyroptosis in the pulmonary tissue.

2.14

## Statistical analysis
Data were obtained from 3 independent experiments. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation(SD). Group means were compared using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Differences between two groups were assessed using the two-sample Student’s t-test. Statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9, with P < 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

## Results
3.1

## MCC950 attenuates PM2.5-stimulated ALI and inflammatory cytokines in vivo
The ability of MCC950 to alleviate inflammation in a PM2.5-induced ALI was assessed through HE-staining of treated mice lung tissues. When compared to the Con group, at 24 h post-PM2.5 exposure, HE-staining of lung tissue revealed significant pathological changes, characterized by alveolar septal thickening and edema, extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in both the alveolar and interstitial spaces, and focal areas of alveolar hemorrhage ( 
A,C). A significant increase was observed in the number of macrophages and the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α in the bronchial alveolar lavage fluid (BALF) following PM2.5 exposure compared to the Con group ( 
E-I). Corresponding increases in these inflammatory factors were also detected in serum( 
J-M). Pretreatment with MCC950 significantly suppressed the infiltration of inflammatory cells and mitigated alveolar and interstitial congestion and edema induced by PM2.5. Consistent with these findings, ELISA confirmed that MCC950 effectively reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α) in both bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and serum ( 
A,C,E-M). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that MCC950 treatment markedly attenuated the PM2.5-induced increase in macrophage numbers within lung tissues ( 
B,D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MCC950 not only protects the lungs from PM2.5-induced damage but also effectively alleviates the associated inflammatory response. Histological assessment demonstrated no detectable toxicity or inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs of mice following intraperitoneal administration of MCC950 at the study dose. Consequently, the MCC950 group was omitted from the follow-up study. ( 
A). Compared to the PM2.5 group, pre-treatment with either MCC950 or dexamethasone significantly attenuated key histopathological alterations, such as inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar edema, and septal thickening. Importantly, MCC950 exerted protective effects comparable to those of the reference drug, dexamethasone ( 
A,C).

3.2

## MCC950 inhibited PM2.5-stimulated inflammation by regulating the NF-κB/IκB /Nlrp3 pathway in vivo
To elucidate the mechanism by which MCC950 reduces lung inflammation, we analyzed the expression levels of key mediators—NF-κB, IκB, and NLRP3—in the Con, PM2.5, PM2.5 + M, and PM2.5 + D groups using Western blot and quantitative PCR (qPCR). PM2.5 exposure significantly increased the expression of NLRP3 (protein and gene) and upregulated p-NF-κB and p-IκB in mouse lung tissues compared with the Con group. These collective findings suggest that the NLRP3-mediated NF-κB/IκB inflammatory pathway was activated upon PM2.5 challenge. ( A-C). MCC950, a specific NLRP3 inhibitor, suppressed the expression of p-NF-κB and p-IκB, an effect likely mediated through the reduction of NLRP3 in lung tissue. Similarly, dexamethasone also effectively suppressed this pathway. Therefore, MCC950 inhibited PM2.5-stimulated inflammation by regulating the NF-κB/IκB /Nlrp3 pathway. 

3.3

## MCC950 inhibits pyroptosis-related protein and mRNA expression in vivo
Exposure to PM2.5 induced severe inflammatory cell infiltration in mouse lung tissue. Given that NLRP3, as noted in the introduction, is a key initiator of pyroptosis, we hypothesized that this observed pathology might be associated with this form of inflammatory cell death. To test this, we further investigated the relationship between the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 and pyroptosis. To provide direct evidence for pyroptosis, we performed dual-color immunofluorescence staining for GSDMD in mouse lung tissues following PM2.5 exposure. Macrophages were identified using an anti-CD80 antibody, while pyroptotic cells were labeled with an anti-GSDMD antibody. The results demonstrated that PM2.5 induced macrophage pyroptosis, as evidenced by the co-localization of CD80 and GSDMD. This effect was markedly attenuated by pretreatment with MCC950. The experimental rationale centered on a direct evaluation of MCC950 ( A,B) consequently, a dexamethasone control was omitted from the subsequent experimental design. GSDMD and Caspase-1 are key mediators of pyroptosis, while IL-1β and IL-18 represent major downstream pro-inflammatory factors. To further substantiate the occurrence of pyroptosis, prompted by our earlier data showing elevated IL-1β and IL-18, we analyzed the expression of central pyroptosis executioners—GSDMD-NT, pro-Caspase-1, and Caspase-1—at the protein and transcriptional levels after PM2.5 exposure. As anticipated, PM2.5 exposure up-regulated the expression of pro-Caspase-1 and GSDMD-NT—the pore-forming executioner of pyroptosis—and significantly increased the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. These results suggest that PM2.5 promotes inflammatory factor release by activating the canonical Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis pathway, thereby exacerbating lung injury. Conversely, MCC950 treatment down-regulated pro-Caspase-1 expression and reduced levels of downstream GSDMD-NT, IL-1β and IL-18, indicating that MCC950 alleviates Caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis ( C-K). 

3.4

## MCC950 inhibits PM2.5-induced inflammatory cytokine production by targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome in vitro
To further elucidate the mechanism by which MCC950 mitigates pyroptosis, we established an in vitro model in which MH-S cells were stimulated with PM2.5. First, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays were performed to assess cytotoxicity across a range of PM2.5 concentrations. Based on these results and consistent with previous findings from our group, a concentration of 100 μg/mL ( A) was selected for its ability to significantly upregulate NLRP3 expression without compromising cell viability ( ). This concentration, in combination with an 8-hour exposure period, was established as the definitive in vitro treatment protocol. Based on CCK-8 assay results, the final working concentrations of MCC950 (3 nM) or Dexamethasone (10 μg/mL) were selected ( B,C). Both compounds were added to the culture medium 2 h prior to PM2.5 exposure. The MCC950-only group showed no significant difference from the Con group in terms of inflammatory cytokine release, indicating that the compound itself does not provoke an inflammatory response. This, along with the CCK-8 data, confirmed its suitability as a vehicle control and provided the rationale for excluding it from later stages of the study. Compared to the Con group, PM2.5 exposure significantly increased the release of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α from MH-S cells into the culture supernatant. Pretreatment with MCC950 significantly suppressed this PM2.5-induced effect, resulting in lower levels of all four cytokines in the PM2.5 + M group compared to the PM2.5 group. MCC950 exhibited comparable efficacy to dexamethasone in reducing the secretion of inflammatory factors in the cell supernatant, indicating its effectiveness in alleviating PM2.5-induced inflammation ( D-G). 

3.5

## NLRP3 is the key to PM2.5-stimulated MH-S pyroptosis
Following exposure to PM2.5, the expression of NLRP3 in MH-S cells was markedly up-regulated, accompanied by an increase in the levels of the pyroptosis executioner protein GSDMD-NT. We observed that MCC950 pre-treatment downregulated the levels of both NLRP3 and its downstream effector GSDMD-NT, thereby likely reducing pyroptosis. Notably, dexamethasone, while able to reduce NLRP3, showed no inhibitory effect on GSDMD-NT, suggesting its anti-inflammatory action is mediated through other pathways, distinct from blocking core pyroptosis execution. To unequivocally define the biological role of MCC950, a dexamethasone group was omitted in the subsequent experimental design ( ). These in vitro findings were consistent with observations in vivo, indicating that PM2.5 promotes pyroptosis through upregulation of NLRP3 expression. Based on these results, it was hypothesized that NLRP3 may play a critical role in PM2.5-induced pyroptosis. To further investigate the pivotal function of NLRP3, we generated NLRP3-knockout cells using siRNA transfection ( B, I). Subsequent exposure of these cells to PM2.5 resulted in a significant reduction in NLRP3 expression, along with a corresponding decrease in GSDMD-NT levels ( C, J). Given that Caspase-1 is the executioner protease of the canonical pyroptosis pathway and is produced through the proteolytic cleavage of pro-Caspase-1, we subsequently quantified the levels of its active form. The production of the effector cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, which are direct downstream targets of Caspase-1, was also evaluated. As shown in and K-N, PM2.5 exposure increased NLRP3 expression and significantly up-regulated the protein levels of its downstream effectors, Caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18. Conversely, Nlrp3 knockout down-regulated the expression of these components. These results indicate that NLRP3 is a key factor in triggering pyroptosis. This conclusion is further supported by the pre-treatment with MCC950, a specific NLRP3 inhibitor, which produced a similar suppressive effect. Collectively, the data in panels A-N confirm that MCC950 exerts its anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the pyroptosis promoter NLRP3. 

3.6

## MCC950 inhibits the occurrence of pyroptosis via the NF-κB/NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD axis in vitro
Collectively, our results demonstrate that MCC950 mitigates PM2.5-induced lung injury by reducing NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis in MH-S cells. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, we conducted the following experiments based on these findings. Building on in vivo observations that MCC950 inhibits NLRP3 expression, we sought to determine whether this effect involves the classical NF-κB/IκB inflammatory pathway, of which NLRP3 is a downstream inflammasome component. We therefore evaluated the activation status of this pathway and its key factors in vitro. Quantitative western blotting and qPCR showed that PM2.5 exposure promoted the phosphorylation of NF-κB/IκB and up-regulated the expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD-NT, and IL-1β, confirming that PM2.5 promotes pyroptosis via this pathway. Conversely, in MH-S cells, MCC950 suppressed the PM2.5-induced activation of the NF-κB/IκB/NLRP3 axis and downregulated both mRNA and protein levels of Caspase-1, GSDMD-NT, and IL-1β. ( ). These findings indicate that MCC950 inhibits pyroptosis via the NF-κB/NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD axis in PM2.5-treated MH-S cells. 

3.7

## MCC950 inhibits the occurrence of pyroptosis in cell and animal models
Active Caspase-1 is a key initiator in the classical cell proapoptotic signaling pathway. Using animal in vivo imaging technology, we measured the expression of active Caspase-1 in mice lungs after PM2.5 exposure. The FAM‑FLIVO probe entered the mice through the tail vein circulated to the lungs and fluorescence intensity was captured using the imager. Our data directly show that PM2.5 exposure significantly enhanced Caspase-1 activity in mouse lung tissue compared to the Con group, indicating widespread pyroptosis. This enhanced activity was reduced by MCC950 pre-treatment, yet remained higher than in the Con group. Collectively, these findings indicate that MCC950 attenuates, but does not fully prevent, PM2.5-induced pyroptosis ( A-D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to examine the ultrastructural changes associated with pyroptosis in MH-S cells following PM2.5 exposure. Characteristic morphological features of pyroptosis were observed: the cells exhibited swelling and rounding, with numerous bubble-like protrusions forming on the plasma membrane. These membrane-associated blebs are indicative of GSDMD-NT pore formation, which disrupts membrane integrity. The perforations lead to further osmotic swelling, eventual plasma membrane rupture, and the release of mature inflammatory mediators ( E). Following MCC950 pretreatment, MH-S cells maintained plasma membrane integrity and showed no morphological signs of pyroptosis. This result supports our central conclusion that MCC950 effectively attenuates pyroptosis in macrophages. 

## Discussion
Environmental exposures are a major determinant of chronic disease, contributing to approximately two-thirds of all global cases of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Notably, chemical pollution alone is responsible for an estimated 16% of deaths worldwide ( ). Air pollution ranks as a top-five global risk factor, with PM2.5-related mortality being a primary concern ( ). In 2019, prolonged exposure to PM2.5 pollution was linked to 4.14 million deaths globally (95%UI: 3.45–4.80), accounting for 62% of all deaths attributed to air pollution. Over the past decade, deaths attributable to ambient PM2.5 have risen by approximately 23% (95%UI: 13.9–34.4) worldwide. Moreover, future calculations are expected to yield even higher figures. This is substantiated by a 2020 estimate, which established that PM2.5 exposure alone was responsible for 9 million premature deaths annually worldwide ( ). The toxicity of PM2.5 is attributed to its translocation from the lung epithelium into the circulation, contributing to endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress ( ). Studies have confirmed that PM exposure can reduce monocyte survival rates within 15 min of exposure. The potential mechanism involves PM-induced generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), which decreases mitochondrial membrane potential. This drop in potential subsequently activates key factors including Caspase-3 and Caspase-9, thereby triggering Gasdermin E (GSDME) cleavage and initiating pyroptosis ( ). Our previous research found that the small PM2.5 particle size contributes to a strong ability to adsorb toxic substances ( ). The particles are able to carry many harmful components such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and directly damage lung tissue( ). Previous research has found that in the mouse ALI model, PM2.5 exposure directly upregulated NLRP3 expression in mice lungs. MCC950 is an NLRP3 inhibitor with multiple pharmacological properties. Nevertheless, the interaction between MCC950 and PM2.5-stimulated lung damage has not been elucidated. We established a PM2.5-stimulated ALI mouse model and MH-S cell model to explore the roles and underlying mechanisms of MCC950. The findings indicate that MCC950 ameliorates PM2.5-stimulated ALI by inhibiting NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis. This finding contributes new knowledge to the search for alternative approaches to mitigate respiratory damage arising from pollutant exposure.
It has been demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure induces oxidative stress in sera and lung tissues of mice, leading to disruption of airway and alveolar epithelial cell integrity, and subsequently, the secretion of inflammatory factors ( ). Macrophages are critical effector cells of the lung immunity, and they promote lung damage through their unique functional heterogeneity ( ).We found that PM2.5 exposure promoted the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in sera and airways of mice. Results of the animal experiments demonstrated that severe pathological damage occurred in the lung tissues of mice following short-term (24 h) and large-dose (8 mg/kg) PM2.5 exposure, with acute leukocyte infiltration, thickened alveolar walls, alveolar congestion, and interstitial pulmonary edema. This shows that PM2.5-induced inflammation directly damages lung tissue and recruits a substantial number of macrophages to infiltrate. This pathological change is not diffused but mainly limited to local lesions around the trachea, accompanied by significant macrophage infiltration as noted from the immunofluorescence results. This pathological presentation is consistent with the known morphological features of pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory cell death. Pyroptosis is a lytic and inflammatory type of programmed cell death. In the canonical Caspase-1-dependent pathway, activated Caspase-1 performs two key functions: it cleaves the pro-inflammatory cytokines pro-IL-18 and pro-IL-1β into their mature forms, facilitating their release to activate immune and inflammatory responses ( ). Concurrently, Caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), and its N-terminal domain (GSDMD-NT) oligomerizes to form plasma membrane pores. This perforation disrupts osmotic balance, leading to cell swelling, lysis, and the release of inflammatory content, thereby defining the hallmark features of pyroptosis ( ). We employed small animal in vivo staining to directly assess activated Caspase-1 in mouse lung tissue following PM2.5 exposure, which confirmed that PM2.5 promotes pyroapoptosis in murine lung cells. In complementary in vitro experiments, stimulation of MH-S cells with PM2.5 led to the observation of characteristic pyroapoptotic morphology via transmission electron microscopy. As a specific NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor, MCC950 significantly attenuated pyroptosis. Comprehensive experimental data demonstrated that MCC950 treatment not only inhibited NLRP3 expression but also downregulated the NF-κB/IκB axis. This consequently suppressed the Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway and reduced the release of the downstream inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Furthermore, the preemptive application of MCC950 also diminished the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, suggesting that NLRP3 may engage with additional inflammatory pathways and highlighting the broader anti-inflammatory potential of MCC950. MCC950 alleviated this pathological change, especially in the reduction of inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the involvement of macrophages in the anti-inflammatory actions of MCC950 is still not clear.
Pyroptosis triggers a cascade that amplifies the inflammatory response, which can indirectly induce severe oxidative stress and an “inflammatory storm” in surrounding cells, thereby exacerbating tissue injury. Numerous studies have demonstrated that inhibition of pyroptosis effectively mitigates lung tissue damage ( , ). An earlier study confirmed that NLRP3 deficiency in lung macrophages reduces cytokine secretion and inflammatory cell infiltration in PM2.5-stimulated lung damage ( ) Our immunofluorescence double-labeling confirmed that macrophages in mouse lung tissue underwent pyroptosis following PM2.5 exposure. The subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines from these pyroptotic cells exacerbated tissue injury. Pro-inflammatory macrophages play a central role in the inflammatory response. The regulation of macrophage polarization is a pivotal factor in modulating the body's overall inflammatory reaction. Therefore, targeting M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophages represents a promising therapeutic strategy for future research.
In vitro, siRNA technology was used to reduce the expression of NLRP3 and slow down PM2.5-induced inflammation. These results explain the pathological changes resulting from PM2.5 exposure. In vivo, when MCC950 was introduced prior to PM2.5 exposure, it reduced NLRP3 activation and pyroptosis initiation. MCC950 significantly reduced lung tissue damage and inhibited the inflammatory response in bronchial lavage fluid. Electron microscopy demonstrated that MCC950-preprotected MH-S cells had a significant reduction in pyroptosis induced by PM2.5. The results show that MCC950 inhibits NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and exerts a protective effect on lung injury.
In addition, PM2.5 can also directly activate the innate immunity of macrophages. The deposition of PM2.5 particles into the airways directly damages macrophages. The damaged cells trigger pattern recognition receptors (PAMPs) by releasing danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby triggering signaling cascades, activating NF-KB, and promoting NLRP3 inflammasome formation ( ). TNFα and IL6 are essential downstream pro-inflammatory factors. Activated macrophages mainly secrete TNF-α, an early pro-inflammatory cytokine released during lung damage ( ). On the other hand, IL-6 serves as a key endogenous cytokine responsible for systemic inflammatory activation. It mediates and amplifies inflammatory responses and is a crucial biomarker of inflammatory status and disease progression ( ). Therefore, we further studied how MCC950 prevents PM2.5-stimulated lung damage. Both in vivo and in vitro assays demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure increases the expression of p-NFκb/IκB and the release of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. PM2.5 activates the NF-κb/IκB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and activates NLRP3, thereby inducing the pyroptosis response in macrophages. The supplementation of MCC950 prior to PM2.5 exposure inhibited NLRP3 activation, blocked the NF-κb/IκB/signaling pathway, inhibited macrophage pyroptosis, and alleviated PM2.5-stimulated ALI.
In summary, our findings demonstrated that PM2.5 activated NLRP3-mediated macrophage pyroptosis by modulating the NF-κb/IκB/NLRP3 axis. In addition, MCC950 inhibited macrophage pyroptosis by targeting NLRP3 and alleviated PM2.5-induced lung damage.
