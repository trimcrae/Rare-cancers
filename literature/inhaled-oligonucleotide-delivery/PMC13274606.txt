## Abstract
Age-related decline in oocyte quality increases the risk of infertility, miscarriage, and birth defects. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key contributor to this decline. Here, we report that oocyte-specific deletion of Uba3, which encodes the catalytic subunit of the E1 NEDDylation-activating complex, causes sterility in mice. Fully grown, germinal vesicle–stage Uba3 conditional knockout oocytes exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction, including elevated reactive oxygen species, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and depletion of mitochondrially encoded RNA transcripts. Proteomic analysis identified alterations in mitochondrial-associated proteins, including enrichment of mitochondrial matrix and respiratory chain components and reduced abundance of electron transport chain complexes. These defects were associated with reduced levels of the mitochondrial RNA polymerase, POLRMT [polymerase (RNA) mitochondrial DNA directed]. We further show that POLRMT is directly modified by NEDDylation, which alters its stability by antagonizing ubiquitylation and degradation. Notably, NEDD8 levels decline with age in both mouse and human oocytes. Together, these findings identify NEDDylation as a regulator of oocyte quality and connect this pathway to mitochondrial transcription in oocytes.

Evidence from mouse and human oocytes implicates NEDDylation in oocyte quality, fertility, and reproductive aging.

## INTRODUCTION
Age-related decline in oocyte quality reduces female fertility and increases the risk of adverse reproductive outcomes. As more women delay childbearing, use of assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) continues to rise ( ). However, limited knowledge of the mechanisms that maintain oocyte quality hinders improvements to ART outcomes. Oocyte quality depends on meiotic and developmental competence, which is progressively acquired during oocyte growth within the ovarian follicle ( ). Mitochondria are central to this process because they provide the energy to meet the metabolic demands of meiotic maturation, fertilization, and early embryogenesis. In oocytes, mitochondrial dysfunction increases oxidative stress, reduces energy production, promotes mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, and is associated with aneuploidy and lower live birth rates, particularly with advancing maternal age ( – ).
Protein posttranslational modifications (PTMs) help maintain proteome integrity and change during aging ( – ). Among these, ubiquitin-like (UBL) modifications such as SUMOylation and NEDDylation regulate protein stability, localization, and function through E1-E2-E3 enzyme cascades. Oocyte-specific deletion of the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) E2 conjugation enzyme, Ube2i, causes sterility and defective oocyte development during folliculogenesis ( , ). Other studies suggest that dysregulation of SUMOylation and ubiquitylation correlates with reproductive aging, supporting the idea that multiple PTMs help preserve oocyte quality ( ).
NEDDylation is a lesser studied UBL but is emerging as a mediator of age-related metabolic disease ( ). The NEDD8 (neural precursor cell expressed developmentally down-regulated protein 8) activating enzyme (NAE) is a heterodimer composed of the regulatory subunit (NAE1) and the catalytic subunit (UBA3). Global loss of Uba3 causes peri-implantation embryonic lethality in mice ( ). Studies of zebrafish, worms, and flies implicate NEDDylation in ovary development, germline maintenance, and meiosis ( – ), but its role in mammalian oocyte development remains unclear. In fully grown mouse oocytes, pharmacologic inhibition of NEDDylation disrupts meiotic progression and causes metaphase I (MI) arrest ( ). We therefore asked whether NEDDylation is required for oocyte growth during folliculogenesis to maintain female fertility and whether components of this pathway change with reproductive aging in mouse and human oocytes.

## RESULTS

## Oocyte-specific NEDDylation knockout mouse line
To generate loss-of-function in NEDDylation, we created a conditional Uba3 allele that removes the predicted active site and catalytic cysteine residue (fig. S1). This active cysteine residue is required for NEDD8 recognition by the E1; therefore, recombination negates the NEDDylation cascade ( ). We generated an oocyte-specific Uba3 deletion [conditional knockout (cKO)] by crossing Uba3flox/flox mice with Gdf9-icre+ mice, a well-characterized conditional Cre recombinase model active in oocytes of primordial follicles, which comprise the nongrowing oocyte pool ( ). This time point was chosen for recombination because NEDD8 transcript levels are highest in oocytes of nongrowing follicles, then decrease but remain relatively constant throughout folliculogenesis (fig. S2, A and B) ( ). NEDD8 protein was also detectable by immunofluorescence in oocytes of different follicle stages (fig. S2, C to G). We confirmed oocyte-specific loss of UBA3 protein by immunohistochemistry on ovary sections from 3-week-old control and Uba3 cKO mice ( ). We additionally measured relative Uba3 mRNA levels in fully grown, prophase I–arrested germinal vesicle (GV)–stage oocytes (FGO) from control and Uba3 cKO mice, as well as in somatic granulosa cells, which do not express cre ( ). Despite loss of UBA3, NEDD8 immunofluorescence in FGO did not differ between genotypes (fig. S3, A to C).
 We then validated functional disruption of NEDDylation using two assays. Prior studies used changes in molecular weight of the canonical NEDDylation target, cullin-1 (CUL1), as a readout for monitoring pathway activity ( , ). Immunoblotting of control and Uba3 cKO FGO showed that control oocytes retained the previously validated higher–molecular weight CUL1-NEDD8 band ( ), whereas this band is absent in Uba3 cKO FGO, indicating loss of CUL1 NEDDylation after Uba3 deletion (fig. S3D). We also assessed protein levels of the reported oocyte NEDDylation targets, EMI1 (early mitotic inhibitor 1) and FZR1 (fizzy and cell division cycle 20 related 1). Previously, it was shown that acute inhibition of FGO with MLN4924 (pevonedistat), a small-molecule inhibitor of NEDDylation, decreases FZR1 protein levels and increases EMI1 protein levels ( ). Similarly, Uba3 cKO oocytes showed the same pattern relative to controls, further supporting disruption of NEDDylation in Uba3 cKO oocytes (fig. S3, E to H).

## NEDDylation is essential for female fertility
In 6-month breeding trials, Uba3 cKO females produced no pups ( ). Histologic analysis showed that ovaries from sexually immature 3-week-old Uba3 cKO ovaries were similar to controls ( ). By 6 weeks of age, during the transition to sexual maturity, Uba3 cKO ovaries contained significantly fewer follicles, and many showed signs of atresia ( , and fig. S4). At 8 weeks, after the onset of sexual maturity, Uba3 ovaries lost most of their follicles, weighed significantly less than controls, and contained numerous atretic follicles ( , and fig. S4). By 12 weeks of age, Uba3 cKO ovaries were nearly devoid of all follicles (fig. S4). Across all follicle types, Uba3 cKO ovaries showed a 68% reduction in follicle number at 8 weeks of age and a 95% reduction in follicle number at 12 weeks of age, accompanied by a significant increase in atretic follicles in Uba3 cKO ovaries (89% increase at 8 weeks and 92% at 12 weeks) (fig. S5). Consistent with this progressive depletion phenotype, cleaved caspase-3 (CC3) analysis at 4 weeks of age showed a significant increase in CC3-positive follicles in Uba3 cKO ovaries compared to controls (fig. S5). The CC3 signal was primarily confined to granulosa cells rather than oocytes (fig. S5, A to D), indicating that oocyte apoptosis was not the predominant feature in these follicles. Together, these data show that Uba3 cKO females undergo progressive loss of follicles beginning shortly after 3 weeks of age, culminating in ovarian failure in early adulthood.
Because Uba3 cKO ovaries remain histologically similar to controls at 3 weeks of age, we selected this early time point to identify oocyte defects that arise before overt follicle loss. We first tested whether mice ovulated a similar number of oocytes using exogenous hormone stimulation. Compared to controls, Uba3 cKO mice ovulated significantly fewer oocytes ( ). Most ovulated Uba3 cKO oocytes recovered from the oviduct ampulla were morphologically degenerated or failed to resume meiosis, as assessed by germinal vesicle breakdown (GVBD) and progression to metaphase II (MII) with first polar body extrusion ( and fig. S6).
Previous studies showed that acute inhibition of NEDDylation in FGO with MLN4924 reduces meiotic maturation ( ). To determine whether developmental loss of NEDDylation produces a similar phenotype, we performed in vitro maturation assays using FGO from sexually immature control and Uba3 cKO mice. Uba3 cKO oocytes showed a marked reduction in meiotic competence, with only 2% extruding a polar body and reaching MII, compared to controls ( ). In contrast to the prior study, which reported similar GVBD rates between the MLN4924-treated and control oocytes ( ), Uba3 cKO oocytes showed impaired GVBD, with only 40.3% undergoing GVBD compared to 85.4% of controls ( ). In addition, 44.8% of Uba3 cKO oocytes were nonviable after 18 hours of culture, significantly more than controls ( ). Together, these findings indicate that developmental loss of NEDDylation causes more severe defects than acute pharmacologic inhibition of FGO, supporting an additional requirement for NEDDylation during folliculogenesis.

## UBA3-deficient FGO have an altered proteome
Because NEDDylation regulates proteins through PTM, we next asked whether loss of Uba3 altered the oocyte proteome, which could explain the lack of meiotic competence. We therefore performed proteomic profiling on FGO isolated from 3-week-old control and Uba3 cKO ovaries (n = 3 pools of 20 oocytes from three mice per genotype). Of the 3454 proteins identified in at least two samples, 187 were significantly down-regulated and six were significantly up-regulated [|log 2 fold change (log2FC)| > 1; Padj < 0.05] ( ). Cellular component (CC) gene ontology analysis of the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) revealed strong enrichment for mitochondrial-associated categories. Enriched terms in the Uba3 cKO FGO included “mitochondrial matrix,” “respiratory chain complex,” “oxidoreductase complex,” “NADH dehydrogenase complex,” “respiratory chain complex I,” and “mitochondrial nucleoid” ( ).
 To examine this mitochondrial signature further, we analyzed lysates from pooled control and Uba3 cKO FGO and probed for subunits of the five mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes. Uba3 cKO FGO showed significantly reduced levels of complex I and complex IV. Mean levels of complexes II, III, and V in the Uba3 cKO trended lower than controls but did not reach statistical significance ( ). These findings identify disruption of the mitochondrial proteome, including loss of ETC components, as a prominent early defect in Uba3 cKO oocytes.

## UBA3-deficient FGO have dysfunctional mitochondria
Recent studies show that functional loss of ETC complexes increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and impairs cellular homeostasis ( ). To test whether the mitochondrial changes identified in the proteomic analysis were associated with functional defects, we measured mitochondrial ROS with MitoTracker CMXRos in FGO isolated from sexually immature mice ( , and fig. S7). FGO showed two chromatin configurations [surrounded nucleolus (SN) and nonsurrounded nucleolus (NSN)], and both are competent to resume meiosis and reach MII ( ); we therefore analyzed both configurations together. Uba3 cKO FGO showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than control FGO, indicating increased mitochondrial ROS ( ). To exclude delayed oocyte growth as an explanation for this difference, we measured oocyte diameter and found no difference between genotypes ( ).
 Excess ROS can damage mtDNA and disrupt mitochondrial ultrastructure ( ). Abnormal mitochondria morphologies in oocytes, including vacuolized and “onion-like” mitochondria, are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and aging ( ). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of 3-week-old control and Uba3 cKO ovaries showed that Uba3 cKO oocytes had marked mitochondrial ultrastructure abnormalities ( ). Mitochondrial number and mtDNA abundance did not differ between genotypes ( and fig. S8A); however, Uba3 cKO FGO showed altered ratios of unhealthy mitochondria to healthy compared to controls, including vacuolized and onion-like structures ( ). Uba3 cKO vacuolized mitochondria were significantly larger than mitochondria with healthy morphology in both controls and knockout FGO (fig. S8B).
To determine whether these structural defects were accompanied by impaired mitochondrial function, we measured mitochondrial respiration using a Seahorse Extracellular Flux (XF) assay with 30 FGO per well ( ). Uba3 cKO FGO showed significantly lower basal respiration than control FGO ( ) and significantly reduced adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) production rates ( ). Consistent with these findings, acute inhibition of NEDDylation in wild-type FGO treated with MLN4924 also reduced basal and maximal respiration rates as compared to controls (fig. S9, A to C). These results support the conclusion that mitochondrial dysfunction arises directly from loss of NEDDylation and not from nonspecific deterioration in Uba3 cKO oocytes.
Uba3 cKO FGO responded differently than controls to mitochondrial inhibitors in the Seahorse XF assay ( and fig. S9, D to G). Rotenone (R), which inhibits complex I, and antimycin (A), which inhibits complex III, were significantly less effective at decreasing measured oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in Uba3 cKO FGO compared to control FGO. Carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) also differentially affected Uba3 cKO FGO, causing a more significant increase in OCR in Uba3 cKO FGO. Together, these findings are consistent with altered ETC function in Uba3 cKO oocytes. Given the enrichment of mitochondrial nucleoid–associated proteins in the proteome analysis, we next asked whether mitochondrial transcription was affected in Uba3 cKO oocytes.

## UBA3 loss affects oocyte mitochondrial transcription
To define a pathway linking UBA3 loss to mitochondrial dysfunction in Uba3 cKO oocytes, we analyzed bulk oocyte RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from control and Uba3 cKO FGO. Consistent with the mitochondrial defects identified by proteomic analysis, which showed reductions in several key mitochondrial proteins, in the RNA-seq analysis, multiple mitochondrial encoded transcripts were significantly down-regulated in Uba3 cKO FGO compared to controls (log2FC < −1; Padj < 0.05) ( , bolded genes). Because mitochondrial transcription depends on nuclear-encoded factors such as POLRMT [polymerase (RNA) mtDNA directed], TFB2M (transcription factor B2 mitochondrial), and TFAM (transcription factor A mitochondrial), we also examined their transcript abundance in the RNA-seq dataset. None of these genes showed altered mRNA levels in Uba3 cKO oocytes ( ), and oocyte nuclear transcription was unaffected in the Uba3 cKO (fig. S10). A subset of genes was validated by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) (fig. S11), which included Nd5 [mitochondrial encoded NADH (reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) dehydrogenase 5], which is part of complex I (fig. S11D). In the proteomic dataset, none of the proteins were detected at sufficient abundance for comparison. However, given the marked loss of mitochondrial transcripts in the RNA-seq, together with the extensive mitochondrial phenotype, we therefore tested whether the loss of UBA3 affected POLRMT, the sole mitochondrial polymerase required for mitochondrial transcription.
 To test whether POLRMT protein levels were altered in individual Uba3 cKO FGO, we collected 3-week-old FGO and performed POLRMT immunofluorescence followed by confocal imaging ( ). Uba3 cKO FGO showed significantly reduced POLRMT signal relative to controls, indicating that loss of Uba3 reduces POLRMT protein abundance ( ). In control oocytes, POLRMT signal was higher in SN oocytes than in NSN oocytes, although the basis of this is unknown ( ). Despite this difference, both SN and NSN Uba3 cKO FGO showed significantly lower POLRMT levels than the corresponding controls. These findings led us to test whether POLRMT is directly modified by NEDDylation.

## NEDD8 directly modifies mitochondrial RNA polymerase (POLRMT)
To investigate whether NEDD8 directly modifies POLRMT, we first performed in silico analysis using the NEDDylation predictor program DTL-NeddSite ( ). This analysis identified nine candidate human NEDD8-modified lysine residues with a prediction score greater than 70% ( ). Analysis with the ubiquitylation prediction tool ESA-UbiSite ( ) showed that two of the nine sites overlapped with the predicted ubiquitin-modified lysines (K154 and K1205), including one with a score greater than 90% ( ).
To test whether NEDD8 modifies POLRMT directly, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells transfected with hemagglutinin (HA)–tagged POLRMT and Myc-tagged NEDD8. We used HEK-293T cells because the limited number of oocytes precluded co-IP in mouse oocytes. Co-IP showed that NEDD8 associates with POLRMT in this system ( ). To test the contribution of the predicted NEDD8-binding sites, we mutated all candidate lysine residues in POLRMT to arginine. In cells expressing Myc-tagged NEDD8 and mutant HA-tagged POLRMT, co-IP was lost, indicating that these predicted sites contribute to the POLRMT-NEDD8 interaction ( ). We further used fluorescence-based proximity ligation assay in wild-type oocytes to confirm the association between NEDD8 and POLRMT in oocytes ( ).
Because prior studies have reported antagonism between NEDD8 and ubiquitin ( , ), we next tested whether loss of NEDDylation increases POLRMT ubiquitylation and proteasome-dependent degradation. HEK-293T cells expressing HA-tagged POLRMT were treated with vehicle [dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)], MLN4924 to inhibit NEDDylation, or MLN4924 plus the proteasome inhibitor MG132. After immunoprecipitation (IP) of HA-tagged POLRMT, we immunoblotted for ubiquitin and HA. Inhibition of NEDDylation increased POLRMT ubiquitylation and reduced POLRMT protein levels relative to vehicle-treated cells ( ). Addition of MG132 increased ubiquitylated POLRMT, as expected, and restored POLRMT protein levels to near control levels ( ). These data support a model in which NEDDylation stabilizes POLRMT by limiting its ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation.
To further test this model in oocytes using an orthogonal approach, we performed confocal immunofluorescence. Wild-type FGO were treated with vehicle, MLN4924, or MLN4924 plus MG132. POLRMT fluorescence decreased in MLN4924-treated oocytes in a dose-dependent manner relative to untreated controls, although the reduction was less pronounced than in Uba3 cKO oocytes, likely reflecting acute pharmacologic inhibition rather than developmental loss of NEDDylation ( ). Addition of MG132 partially restored POLRMT levels. These findings are consistent with the in vitro data and support a proteosome-dependent reduction in POLRMT after inhibition or loss of NEDDylation.
 

## NEDDylation is altered in aged mouse and human oocytes
Mitochondrial decline in aged mouse and human oocytes is well established ( , ). Similar to our Uba3 cKO FGO, aged mouse TEM data show changes in the percentage of swollen, vacuolized mitochondria ( ), and in human GV oocytes, POLRMT transcripts show a modest negative correlation with maternal age (R = −0.34; P = 0.048) ( ). In contrast, expression of NEDDylation pathway genes is largely maintained during reproductive aging of human GV oocytes ( ). NEDD8 abundance has not previously been examined in mouse or human GV-stage oocytes. We first quantified NEDD8 levels in FGO from young (3 weeks old) and aged (10 months old) wild-type mice and found that NEDD8 was significantly lower in aged oocytes relative to young controls ( ). To test whether this pattern was conserved in human, we quantified NEDD8 protein in human GV oocytes from individuals aged 26 to 40 years old by confocal microscopy ( ). NEDD8 levels showed a moderate negative correlation with age (R2 = 0.49; P = 0.01), indicating that NEDD8 declines with reproductive age in human oocytes. These findings are consistent with the broader decline in mitochondrial competence that accompanies oocyte aging and indicate that loss of NEDDylation recapitulates key features of this phenotype. Together, these mouse and human data support reduced NEDDylation during reproductive aging and suggest that impaired NEDDylation may contribute to the loss of mitochondrial quality that accompanies oocyte aging (fig. S12).
 

## DISCUSSION
Mitochondrial function is key to oocyte quality to meet the metabolic demands of meiotic maturation, fertilization, and early embryogenesis. Reproductive aging is associated with reduced membrane potential and ATP production, increased ROS, and higher mtDNA mutation burden in oocytes ( , , ). Prior studies showed that acute inhibition of NEDDylation or small interfering RNA–mediated knockdown of Nedd8 in wild-type mouse FGO causes MI arrest without affecting GVBD and that MLN4924 treatment of mouse blastocysts reduces embryo quality and blastocyst hatching while increasing oxidative stress ( , ). We extended these studies by examining the consequences of developmental loss of NEDDylation during oocyte growth using an oocyte-specific Uba3 cKO mouse model. Oocyte-specific deletion of Uba3 causes sterility and produces poor-quality oocytes with defects in meiotic resumption, increased oxidative stress, and broad mitochondrial dysfunction including impaired mitochondrial respiration, reduced ATP production, increased ROS, and reduced mitochondrial transcription. These findings show that NEDDylation is an essential regulator of oocyte quality and mitochondrial homeostasis during their development in ovarian follicles.
Our data support a model in which NEDDylation maintains mitochondrial transcription by stabilizing POLRMT. Alongside reduced mitochondrial transcripts, Uba3 cKO oocytes have reduced POLRMT abundance. Our biochemical studies showed that loss of NEDDylation increases POLRMT ubiquitylation and its proteasome-dependent turnover. This antagonism is different than NEDDylation’s canonical role in the activation of cullin-ring ligases, CRLs, which promote ubiquitin-mediated degradation ( ). There are a few reports of antagonism between NEDDylation and ubiquitin-mediated degradation of non-cullin targets ( , ). Although it remains unclear whether POLRMT NEDDylation also occurs in somatic cells, the interaction observed in HEK-293T cells suggests that it may extend beyond oocytes. Based on the proteomic analysis, POLRMT destabilization alone does not fully account for the Uba3 cKO phenotype, which extends beyond mitochondria to include alterations in trafficking, ribosomal and secretory pathways, cytoskeletal organization, and chromosome-associated structures. Further studies are needed to distinguish primary from secondary effects of UBA3 loss and to identify additional NEDDylation targets in oocytes.
Uba3 cKO females undergo almost complete follicle depletion by 3 months of age, with granulosa cell apoptosis beginning before sexual maturity. Neither proteomic nor transcriptomic analysis of mutant oocytes showed enrichment of cell death–related pathways, and CC3 was not detected in oocytes in histologic sections. These findings suggest that follicle loss is secondary to oocyte dysfunction rather than primary oocyte apoptosis, at least at the FGO stage. Whether depletion of the primordial follicle pool reflects mitochondrial defects or a distinct mechanism remains to be determined. Primordial follicle oocytes are thought to suppress complex I to maintain low levels of ROS ( , ). Because recombination in our mouse model begins at this stage, we speculate that NEDDylation may contribute to the developmental transition from a dormant mitochondrial program with suppressed complex I to the respiratory state of growing oocytes, which includes complex I. Reduced complex I in Uba3 cKO FGO is consistent with this possibility, although it remains to be directly tested.
Uba3 deletion disrupts NEDDylation at the pathway level, and this model provides a useful discovery platform for identifying NEDDylation-dependent oocyte processes. In this study, we focused on mitochondrial transcription as a major NEDDylation-sensitive process. However, there are likely additional nonmitochondrial pathways and developmental programs that are affected in the Uba3 cKO, for instance, the impaired follicle health and reduced ovulation. Resolving these questions will require more refined models to define relevant stages and target proteins.
The Uba3 cKO phenotype is more severe than that of several oocyte-specific models with mitochondrial dysfunction, including dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and mitofusin-1 (Mfn1). Uba3 cKO females were sterile and showed nearly complete follicle loss by 12 weeks of age, whereas Drp1 knockout females were subfertile, while Mfn1 knockout females showed reduced follicle numbers at 8 weeks of age ( , ). Uba3 cKO FGO do not show reduced expression of Mfn1 or Drp1 in the transcriptome dataset, and MFN1 is not significantly changed in the proteomics, suggesting that the Uba3 cKO mitochondrial phenotype is not likely explained by reductions in these.
Uba3 cKO mice also share some phenotypic overlap with oocyte-specific knockouts of known NEDDylation targets, such as CRLs. For example, knockout of Dcaf13, which encodes the substrate receptor of the CRL4 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, causes a delayed reproductive phenotype, with follicle depletion at 5 months rather than by 12 weeks as in our Uba3 cKO mice ( ). In addition, Uba3 cKO oocytes show 30-fold more differentially expressed transcripts than Dcaf13-deficient oocytes. Together, these comparisons suggest that the Uba3 cKO reproductive phenotype reflects a greater disruption of oocyte homeostasis rather than loss of a single downstream NEDDylation target, although disruption of the NEDDylation CRL-axis likely contributes.
Our findings may also have relevance to reproductive aging. A recent proteomic study of human GV oocytes reported changes in mitochondrial proteins along with reduced proteasome and chaperone networks, consistent with impaired proteostasis in aging oocytes ( ). Declining NEDDylation and other PTMs may contribute to these defects. In our study, NEDD8 protein levels declined with age in both mouse and human GV oocytes. Although the consequences of NEDD8 decline are currently unknown, NEDDylation is likely impaired once NEDD8 falls below a critical threshold. Together, these observations raise the possibility that reduced NEDD8 in aging oocytes, like loss of UBA3 in mice, contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction in part by impairing mitochondrial transcription.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Experimental design
This study tested whether oocyte NEDDylation is required for female fertility, mitochondrial function, and oocyte quality. To address this question, we generated an oocyte-specific Uba3 cKO mouse model and compared mutant and control ovaries and oocytes at different ages. We focused the mechanistic analysis primarily on 3-week-old fully grown FGO because these preceded overt follicle depletion and atresia, to identify primary oocyte defects. Primary outcomes included fertility, ovulation, meiotic competence, mitochondrial structure and function, proteomic and transcriptome, and POLRMT abundance and regulation. Additional studies examined age-related changes in NEDD8 in mouse and human GV-stage oocytes. Sample sizes, numbers of biological replicates, statistical tests, and inclusion criteria are provided below for each experiment.

## Animals
All experimental procedures were performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and with prior approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee animal protocols at Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) (protocol no. AN-4762). Mice were housed in rooms programmed with 12-hour light/dark cycle in microisolator cages, with food and water provided ad libitum. The Uba3 floxed allele was generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technology at the BCM Genetically Engineered Rodent Models (GERM) Core. Forty-five founders were screened by PCR and verified by genomic DNA sequencing, six males had correctly positioned loxP sites, and one founder male was selected and outcrossed for two generations to F1 C57/Bl6/N;129SvEv7 to verify germline transmission and reduce carrying over any potential off-target edits. Genomic DNA was extracted from ear or tail snips and genotyped by PCR using gene-specific primers (table S1).

## Source of human oocytes
The use of immature GV human oocytes was approved by BCM Institutional Review Board (protocol no. H-33461). Oocytes were sourced from women undergoing assisted reproduction treatment at BCM Family Fertility in Houston, Texas, USA. Only immature oocytes unsuitable for fertilization were used. All patients gave informed consent prior to collection and use of their immature oocyte(s) and were deidentified prior to use.

## Tissue processing and immunohistochemistry
Mice were anesthetized by isoflurane (Abbott Laboratories) inhalation and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Ovaries were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Electron Microscopy Services) overnight followed by standard paraffin processing and embedding at the BCM Human Tissue Acquisition and Pathology Core. For immunohistochemistry, paraffin-embedded blocks were serially sectioned (5 μm) and underwent standard citrate-based antigen retrieval, nonspecific blocking, antibody incubation, and detection with VECTASTAIN ABC solution (Vector Laboratories) with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate (Vector Laboratories). Sections were counterstained with Harris Hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich), dehydrated using an alcohol gradient, and mounted with Permount (Thermo Fisher Scientific). See table S2 for antibody concentrations.

## Fertility analysis
Mating pairs of 6-week-old Uba3 cKO female or control littermate and a known fertile 8-week-old wild-type male were housed in a continuous breeding cage. The presence of newborn pups was monitored daily for 6 months, and any resulting litters were weaned at 3 weeks. The number of pups per litter and litters per month were recorded for five females per genotype, with statistical analysis by unpaired t tests between genotypes.

## Mouse oocyte collection, culture, and ovulation
For experiments using FGO, mice were injected with 5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) intraperitoneally (ProSpecBio) 48 hours prior to oocyte collection. Ovaries were punctured to release oocytes using 26-gauge needles into minimal essential medium containing 2.5 μM milrinone (Sigma-Aldrich, M4659) to prevent meiotic resumption and cultured in Chatot, Ziomek, and Bavister (CZB) medium (Millipore Sigma, MR-019-D) in a humidified incubator (5% CO2 and 37°C).
For ovulation induction, 3-week-old female mice were injected intraperitoneally with 5 IU of PMSG (ProSpecBio) and, 47 hours later, injected intraperitoneally with 5 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG; Pregnyl, Merck Pharmaceuticals). Mice were euthanized 17 to 18 hours after the hCG injection, and oocytes were harvested from the oviduct ampulla. The total number of oocytes at each stage per mouse was recorded following removal of cumulus with hyaluronidase (0.1%) after a brief incubation.
For oocyte in vitro maturation experiments, 4-week-old female mice were injected intraperitoneally with 5 IU of PMSG 48 hours prior to oocyte collection into EmbryoMax CZB medium (Millipore Sigma) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine. Oocytes were cultured for 18 hours in a humidified incubator, and total oocytes in each stage [GV, GVBD (MI), polar body (MII), and dead] per mouse were recorded.

## Histology and follicle quantification
Ovaries were collected and fixed as described in the “Tissue processing and immunohistochemistry” section above. Ovaries at 3 weeks of age were serially sectioned (5 μm) and stained with periodic acid–Schiff and hematoxylin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for morphometric analysis. High-resolution images were obtained using a digital camera (AxioCam 105, Zeiss). For morphogenetics, follicles in every fifth section were counted manually; to avoid double counting, only follicles with a visible oocyte nucleus were counted. A correction factor for follicles smaller than the antral stage was applied to all samples uniformly ( ). Follicles were classified into the following categories based on previous studies: primordial (type 2), primary (types 3a and 3b), secondary (type 4), and antral (types 6 to 8) ( ). Atretic follicles were classified based on discontinuous basement membrane, collapsed zona pellucida, pyknotic nuclei in granulosa cells, shrunken oocytes, and irregular follicle size.

## RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
RNA was isolated from FGO from 6-week-old females using the Arcturus PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 200 ng of total RNA with the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies). qRT-PCR assays were carried out with Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Melt curve analysis was performed to verify a single amplification peak. qRT-PCR primer sequences (Integrated DNA Technologies) are listed in table S3. Relative transcript levels were calculated using the cycle threshold (2−ΔΔCT) method ( ). Gapdh was used as a reference gene to normalize RNA, and the mean of the control samples was used for calculating relative transcript level of each sample.
For quantification of relative mtDNA content, DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN) from PMSG-stimulated FGO from 6-week-old females. qRT-PCR assays were performed, and the relative abundance of 18S (nuclear) compared to Nd2 (mitochondrial) was reported as relative mtDNA content (primer sequences in table S3).

## RNA sequencing
For oocyte RNA-seq, ~150 FGO were pooled from n = 3 to 4, 3-week-old mice of each genotype, and RNA was extracted using the PicoPure isolation kit (Arcturus) using the manufacturer’s protocol. Long noncoding RNA-seq was performed at Novogene, with sample quality control, library preparation, and initial processing. Reads were trimmed and filtered using fastp 0.23.4 and aligned to Mouse Genome Assembly GRCm39 using STAR 2.7.11a. Gene expression was quantified from mapped reads using featureCounts Version 2.0.6 and imported into DESeq2 1.42.0 for differential expression analysis. Counts were normalized, scaled, and plotted using pheatmap Version 1.0.13 in RStudio.

## Proteomics
For oocyte mass spectrometry, 20 FGO were collected from n = 3, 3-week-old mice of each genotype. After collection, oocytes were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further processing. The oocyte pellet was dissolved in 50 mM final ammonium bicarbonate buffer. The solution was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and boiled on a heat block at 95°C for 5 min and then cooled at room temperature. The protein digestion was carried out using 50 ng of trypsin and LysC enzyme mixture at 37°C overnight. The enzyme reaction was neutralized by adding 1% final formic acid. The peptides were concentrated in a SpeedVac and dissolved in loading buffer (0.015% DDM prepared in 0.1% formic acid). Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was conducted on a nanoElute2 UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) coupled online to timsTOF Ultra 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) equipped with a CaptiveSpray nanoelectrospray ion source. The data acquisition was performed in diaPASEF mode. The peptide samples were directly loaded onto a 25 cm–by–75 μm PepSep Ultra C18 column (Bruker Scientific LLC) and separated using a 30-min total run time at 250 nl/min. The diaPASEF acquisition scheme included mass/charge ratio (m/z) range (400 to 1000 m/z) with a mobility range (0.64 to 1.45 1/K0). The MS/MS method used 24 data-independent acquisition (DIA) windows, resulting in a cycle time of 0.96 s. The diaPASEF raw data were processed using DIA-NN (Version 2.2.0) in a library-free search mode. The fasta file containing 17,228 mouse proteins was downloaded from UniProtKB (downloaded 21 November 2024). The precursor ions were generated from fasta in silico digest with deep learning–based spectra, retention times, and ion mobilities prediction. The proteolytic enzyme trypsin/P was selected, allowing for up to one missed cleavage. Variable modification of oxidation (M) and protein N-term acetylation was allowed. Parameter specifics include peptide lengths ranging from 7 to 30, precursor charges from 1 to 4, precursor m/z from 300 to 1800, and fragment ion m/z from 200 to 1800. The MBR was enabled and precursor identification was set at 1% false discovery rate. The gene product inference and quantification were done with label-free iBAQ approach using “gpGrouper” algorithm ( ). The iBAQ protein expression was median normalized and log-transformed for further statistical analysis. DEPs between groups were determined with the empirical-Bayes moderated t test as implemented in limma, with trend and robust options enabled ( ). Adjusted P values were calculated with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. DEPs were plotted using ggplot2 4.0.2 and pheatmap Version 1.0.13 in RStudio. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using EnrichGO as part of clusterProfiler Version 4.18.4.

## Immunofluorescence
FGO from PMSG-stimulated mice were manually denuded with 100-μm Stripper Micropipette (Cooper Surgical) and stained using a previously established protocol ( ). In brief, FGO were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Electron Microscopy Sciences), permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), blocked in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× PBS, and incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Antibodies used are listed in table S2. FGO were incubated with secondary antibody (1:1000, Invitrogen) at room temperature (RT) for 1 hour.
Like mouse oocytes, human GVs were fixed in 4% PFA, permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS, and incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4°C. GVs were then incubated with secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen) at RT for 1 hour. FGO and GVs were imaged with Zeiss LSM 880 confocal scanning microscope at the nucleolar plane, and the fluorescence signal was quantified using the “Measure” tool of Fiji.
For detection of ROS, prior to fixation, FGO from 3-week-old females were incubated in MitoTracker Red CMXRos 1:1000 (Invitrogen, M7512) in M2 medium for 30 min. Oocytes were then fixed in 4% PFA in 1× PBS for 20 min, washed three times in 1× PBS, and incubated in 20 μM Hoechst-33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min. Mounting and imaging were performed as previously described. Slides were imaged immediately following preparation to reduce the toxic effects of prolonged dye exposure. Dead or damaged oocytes were not imaged.
For proximity ligation assay, anti-NEDD8 and anti-POLRMT antibodies were conjugated to Duolink In Situ Probemaker MINUS (Millipore Sigma, DUO92010) and PLUS (Millipore Sigma, DUO92009) lyophilized oligonucleotides, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. FGO from PMSG-stimulated mice were incubated overnight at 4°C with NEDD8-MINUS or POLRMT-PLUS as negative controls, or both probes diluted 1:100 in provided Duolink antibody diluent. To visualize fluorescence, the Duolink In Situ Detection Reagents Red kit (Millipore Sigma, DUO92008) was used. FGO were washed and incubated in ligase-ligation and polymerase-amplification buffers according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. FGO were imaged as described above.

## In vitro transcription assay
FGO from 3-week-old mice were cultured in the presence of 2 mM 5-ethynyl uridine (5EU) (Click-IT RNA Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit, Invitrogen) in CZB medium supplemented with 2.5 μM milrinone and l-glutamine according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. In brief, oocytes were cultured in 5EU for 2 min and then immediately fixed in 4% PFA for 20 min and briefly washed in 1× PBS. The oocytes were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in BSA-PBS for 15 min, incubated in the Click-IT reaction cocktail according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedure, and imaged as previously described ( ).

## Transmission electron microscopy
Ovaries from 3-week-old mice were fixed in Karnovsky’s fixative overnight, secondarily fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a series of ethanol of increasing concentration, and embedded in epoxy resin. Tissue was sectioned at 100-nm thickness using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and Reynold’s lead citrate, and imaged with a JEOL JEM-1400Flash TEM operating at 120 kV of acceleration voltage and equipped with a high-contrast pole piece and an AMT NanoSprint15 Mk-II sCMOS electron detector. The number of mitochondria per image was manually counted for eight nonoverlapping images at 2000× magnification per mouse (n = 3 per genotype). Mitochondria were categorized based on ultrastructure features as normal, onion-like, or vacuolized. Normal mitochondria lacked both a large vacuole and dark swirling cristae. Onion-like were smaller than average and displayed a circular morphology with dense, swirling cristae. Vacuolized mitochondria contain a large electron-lucent space occupying more than 30% mitochondrial volume.

## Seahorse XF assay
OCR of FGO was measured using an XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience). XFe96 culture plates were hydrated 24 hours prior to oocyte collection using Seahorse XF Calibrant medium (Agilent). PMSG-stimulated FGO from 3-week-old mice (30 per well) were placed in the XFe96 culture plate with Seahorse XF Media pH 7.4 supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine (Agilent). The cartridge was loaded with four individual inhibitor solutions (0.5 μM rotenone, 3.0 μM trifluoromethoxy carbonyl cyanide phenylhydrazone, 1.5 μM oligomycin, and 0.5 μM antimycin A). Standard instrument injection, mixing, and measurement times were used. Total ATP production rates were calculated from Seahorse data using previously published methods ( ).

## Immunoblotting
For oocyte immunoblots, FGO from 3-week-old PMSG-stimulated mice were collected and pooled for 200 oocytes per sample (~4 ovaries per sample). Samples were prepared by combing 200 oocytes with lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) sample buffer (Invitrogen) and sample reducing agent (Invitrogen). For HEK-293T immunoblots, 20 μl of IP eluant or 10 μg input protein was combined with LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen) and sample reducing agent (Invitrogen). Samples were then boiled for 10 min at 70°C, cooled to room temperature, and loaded into NuPAGE 4-12% bis-tris protein gels (Invitrogen) and electrophoresed in Mops SDS running buffer (Invitrogen) under reducing conditions. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). After transfer, membranes were allowed to dry. Nonspecific antigen blocking was carried out by incubating membranes in 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 hour at RT. Primary antibody (see table S2) was diluted in blocking buffer and incubated with rocking at 4°C overnight. Membranes were rinsed in TBST three times for 10 min each. Secondary antibody [1:10,000; anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP) Promega reference no. W402B or anti-rabbit HRP Promega reference no. W4011] was diluted in TBST and incubated with rocking for 1 hour at RT. Membranes were rinsed in TBST three times for 10 min each. The SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34577) and iBright 1500 (Invitrogen) were used to image.

## Cell culture with transient transfection and IP
HEK-293T cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Waltham, MA). The plasmid used to overexpress HA-tagged POLRMT in our study, pRP[Exp]-Hygro-CMV > hPOLRMT[NM_001407805.1]*(1-41aa/HA/42-1237aa), was constructed and packaged by VectorBuilder. POLRMT plasmid design was adapted from Surovtseva et al. ( ). The mutagenized HA-tagged POLRMT plasmid with lysine to arginine mutations at nine predicted NEDD8 sites was also constructed and packaged by VectorBuilder. The expression plasmid for myc-NEDD8 was a gift from Y. Xiong ( ). Plasmids for transfection were prepared using a maxiprep kit (QIAGEN). For transient transfection, HEK-293T cells were grown to 80% confluence and then transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 reagent following the manufacturer’s recommendations (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were grown for 48 hours and then harvested in 1 ml of 1% NP-40 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysate (1 mg) was immunoprecipitated using 25 μl of Pierce Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

## Drug treatment
FGO were collected from 3-week-old PMSG-stimulated mice, cultured in CZB medium (Millipore Sigma), and treated with DMSO-only (control), 1 to 2 μM MLN4924 (Millipore Sigma, 5054770001), or 1 to 2 μM MLN4924 + 100 μM MG132 (Millipore Sigma, M7449) for 2 hours. HEK-293T cells were treated with DMSO-only, 4 μM MLN4924 only, or 4 μM MLN4924 + 100 μM MG132 for 8 hours. Medium was changed, and cells were washed with 1× PBS before subsequent downstream experiments. Optimal concentrations for drug treatment were adapted from previous publications ( , ).

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. GraphPad Prism 10 was used for statistical analysis, including linear regression. The number of replicates or numbers of mice are indicated in the figure legends. The difference between two groups was determined by Student’s t test and differences between multiple groups by analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc testing. Statistical significance between genotypes in percent-of-whole data was determined by chi-square analysis. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay data were log-transformed prior to statistical analysis by Student’s t test. All t tests were two-tailed. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
