## Background
This study aims to investigate the impact of high altitude (HA) hypoxia on ferroptosis in lung tissue and the evaluate the preventative effect of Dimethyl fumarate (DMF) on lung inflammation.

## Methods
Proteomic analysis was performed in plasma of volunteers ascending to high altitude, lung tissue of ALI rats, co-cultured lung epithelial (BEAS-2B cells) and macrophages (THP-1 cells) under hypoxia, either individually or in co-culture setting. DMF was pre-treated with rats or BEAS-2B cells before ferroptosis indexes and inflammatory cytokines were determined. Knock-down or overexpression of SLC7A11 in BEAS-2B cell was performed to further verify the role of DMF in alleviating ferroptosis and inflammation in ALI.

## Results
Proteomic analysis of human plasma, rat lung tissue and lung epithelial cells identified Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) enriched in the ferroptosis. HA exposure increased inflammatory response and lung injury, which could be alleviated by DMF. Co-culture of two cell types lead to a more pronounced ferroptotic response in BEAS-2B cells and an elevated level of cytokine expression in THP-1 cells under hypoxia condition, which could also be ameliorated by DMF. Knockdown of SLC7A11 results in a reversal of ferroptosis and macrophage mediated inflammation, which were improved by increasing Nrf2 expression through DMF treatment.

## Conclusion
This study revealed that a reciprocal regulatory relationship between ferroptosis of lung epithelial cells and macrophage-mediated inflammation was one of the critical mechanisms contributing to HA exposure triggered ALI. Furthermore, DMF could alleviates hypoxia induced ALI by upregulating Nrf2/SLC7A11 pathway, making it a potential protective agent against HA hypoxia induced ALI.

## Introduction
Exposure to high altitudes generates a reduction in the partial pressure of oxygen, triggering a hypobaric hypoxia condition. This condition produces pathophysiological alterations in an organism and can lead to tissue and organ damage, and even mortality [ – ]. Furthermore, high-altitude hypoxia affects the respiratory system, cardiovascular system, and nervous system, giving rise to related illnesses such as high-altitude headache, acute mountain sickness (AMS), central sleep apnea syndrome, high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE), and high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) [ ]. Hypobaric hypoxia can cause severe lung injury within the respiratory system [ ]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these injuries induced by high altitude remain unclear, impeding the development of effective treatments. To date, various preventative and therapeutic measures have been employed against injury induced by hypobaric hypoxia at high altitudes, with drugs being one of the primary approaches [ ]. Nevertheless, these therapies primarily target symptoms rather than addressing the pathogenesis. Therefore, it is imperative to identify key molecular mechanisms that can prevent and treat injuries caused by hypobaric hypoxia at high altitude.
In response to hypobaric hypoxia, generated by exposure to high altitude (>2500 m), the inflammatory process is rapidly activated in both human subjects and animal models [ ]. Hypoxia disrupts the delicate equilibrium between oxidation and antioxidant mechanisms in the body [ ]. The maintenance of oxygen homeostasis plays a crucial role in preserving lung integrity and functionality [ ]. The hypobaric hypoxia environment at high altitude induces the recruitment of inflammatory cells into the alveolar space, inducing an inflammatory phenotype in the lung. This occurs not only in individuals exposed to hypobaric hypoxia but also in rodents subjected to alveolar hypoxia that mimics the conditions observed in pulmonary diseases, ultimately eliciting detrimental inflammatory effects within the pulmonary system [ , ]. Hypoxia and inflammation are intricately linked at molecular, cellular, and clinical levels. The inflammatory response induced by hypoxia includes recruitment of immune cells, activation of downstream signaling pathways, as well as release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines [ , ].
There is an increasing recognition that inflammation and cell death reciprocally influence each other, forming a self-amplifying loop of these two factors, thereby exacerbating inflammation [ ]. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent programmed non-apoptotic form of cell death [ ], occurs concurrently with the induction of neutrophil-mediated inflammation [ ]. Ferroptosis plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of acute lung injury (ALI), and pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis has been reported to effectively ameliorate the inflammatory response associated with lipopolysaccharide-induced lung injury [ , ]. Additionally, ferroptosis has been demonstrated to facilitate early inflammation following liver, kidney, and intestine injuries [ , ]. It has also been reported that iron supplementation at high altitudes induces lung tissue injury through excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induction of inflammatory responses [ ]. However, whether ferroptosis contribute to the inflammatory response in ALI induced by hypobaric hypoxia at high altitude remains unclear.
Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) functions as a pivotal regulator of cellular antioxidant responses by stimulating the transcription of a diverse range of genes that can alleviate oxidative stress [ ]. Due to continuous exposure to various pollutants and oxidants in the external environment, the maintenance of redox balance by Nrf2 is crucial for airway health. Researchers have discovered that Nrf2 deletion results in high susceptibility and severity of insults in various respiratory diseases, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), respiratory infections, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and lung cancer [ ]. Therefore, many studies focus on the benefits of Nrf2 inducers, in the therapeutic intervention for oxidant-associated lung diseases. Dimethyl fumarate (DMF), an oral fumaric acid ester, is recognized for its pharmacological properties as an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agent [ ].Its anti-oxidant effect is attributed to its ability to activate the Nrf2 pathway and so reduce oxidative stress [ ]. DMF has been approved for treating multiple sclerosis (MS), psoriasis and dermatologic conditions [ ]. The approval of DMF to treat these diseases has opened doors to new avenues for research in using the DMF in other inflammatory and autoimmune conditions and little is known regarding the role of Nrf2 in high altitude induced ALI.
Hence, the objective of this study is to investigate the underlying mechanism of ALI induced by high altitude and the preventative effect of DMF. Through proteomic analysis, we identified ferroptosis as a key player in the development of hypoxia induced ALI. Through rat and in vitro model, we revealed a reciprocal regulatory relationship between ferroptosis of lung epithelial cells and macrophage-mediated inflammation. Besides, Nrf2/Slc7a11 is involved in the pathogenesis of ALI and can promote M1 polarization of macrophages, leading to worsening symptoms of ALI. Promoting Nrf2 expression suggests its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of ALI, which could be considered a potential therapeutic target for hypoxia induced ALI.

## Methods and materials

## Subjects enrolled in the study
Our study initially recruited 40 Chinese male volunteers residing primarily at low altitude. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were described in our previous study [ ]. These participants underwent a gradual ascent from the plain area (1400 m, Kashgar, China), to an altitude of 3700 m, followed by further ascent to 5000 m after a seven-day adaptation period at 3700 m. At each altitude, all enrolled participants completed a questionnaire including age, weight, height and smoking habits as well as symptoms such as headache, gastrointestinal upset, fatigue/weakness, dizziness/light-headedness, and sleep disturbance. The presence of acute mountain sickness (AMS) at high altitude was determined using the 2018 Lake Louise scoring (LLS) according to the questionnaire. Participants were categorized into three groups based on AMS occurrence at two altitudes, that were severe AMS (sAMS), moderate AMS (mAMS) and non-AMS groups [ ]. Blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning at each altitude after obtaining their informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethic Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval identifier: S2021-623-01) and all protocols followed the ethical guidelines.
We implemented comprehensive contingency plans to prevent, monitor, and manage altitude sickness. Key details of our contingency plans include pre-Ascent preparation and screening, graded ascent and acclimatization, continuous monitoring of AMS, on-site medical support and facilities, intervention and evacuation protocols. During the whole ascending process, no cases progressed to HAPE or HACE, and all volunteers completed the study and returned safely to low altitude.

## Animals and High-Altitude model establishment
A total of 24 male Sprague Dawley rats (SPF grade), weighing between 210 g and 230 g, aged between 35d and 42d, were purchased from SiPeiFu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The rats were housed in the animal center of People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital, maintained at a relative humidity of 50%, with a temperature range of 21–24℃, and subjected to a 12-hour light-dark cycle. The rats were acclimatized for a period of 3–5 days on a standard diet prior to the commencement of the experiments. This study was approved by the Animal Care Committee of the PLA General Hospital, adhering to the Guidelines for the Use and Care of Research Animals publicized by the National Institutes of Health.
The rats were randomly allocated into six groups (with six rats in each group): the normal control group (Con group), the DMF-Con group, the Fer1-Con group, the hypobaric hypoxia induced ALI group (HH-ALI group), the DMF-HH-ALI group, and the Fer1- HH-ALI group. Rats in the Con and DMF-Con group remained at low altitude outside of the hypobaric hypoxia chamber. Rats in the HH-ALI group were exposed to the hypobaric chamber (Yantai Binlun Hyperbaric Oxygen Chamber Co., Ltd.) simulating an altitude of 5000 m (with a pressure of 54 kPa, which is 53.3% of atmospheric pressure) for 48 h as experimental model. The chamber comprises several key components: the main chamber, the vacuum pumping system, the oxygen supply system, the connecting pipelines, and the communication system. It is designed to simulate high altitude by reducing the ambient atmospheric pressure through air evacuation. The internal pressure is maintained via the airtight sealing of doors and windows. The rate of altitude change within the chamber is consistently controlled at 0.5 m/s. Upon reaching an altitude of 5,000 m, the oxygen concentration inside the chamber is around 11%.
Prior to exposure to hypobaric hypoxia, rats in DMF-HH-ALI group were orally administered DMF via intragastric route (100 mg/kg). Rats in Fer1-HH-ALI group were injected intraperitoneally with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 4 mg/kg). The injection or intragastric administration in these groups lasted for 10 consecutive days, prior to hypoxia exposure and once per day. Twenty-four hours after their final administration, rats in these group were placed in the hypobaric chamber simulating an altitude of 5000 m or under common condition for 48 h. The temperature and humidity in the chamber were strictly maintained at 20 ± 2℃ and 50–60%, respectively. The rate of ascent and descent was consistently regulated at a velocity of 0.5 m/s. Rats were provided with food and water within the chamber during hypobaric exposure. Subsequent procedures were conducted after returning to normal atmospheric pressure outside the hypobaric hypoxia chamber.

## Cell culture and processing
The human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B cells, purchased from Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM medium. The human leukemia monocytic cells (THP-1 cells, purchased from Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in 1640 medium. Both media contain 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin (100 units/mL) and the two cell lines were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. BEAS-2B cells were exposed to hypoxia for either 24–48 h in a hypoxic incubator with 5% CO2 and 1% O2. THP-1 cells was treated with 100 µL of PMA (10 µg/mL) for 24 h to polarize them into adherent M0 macrophages.
In addition to culturing the two types of cells separately under hypoxia conditions, we also co-cultured them through transwell chambers with a membrane pore size of 0.4 μm. Specifically, BEAS-2B cells were seeded in six-well plates, while M0 macrophages were subsequently placed in transwell chambers, which were then inserted into the six-well plates inoculated with BEAS-2B cells. Subsequently, they were incubated either under normal conditions or within the hypoxic incubator set at an oxygen concentration of only 1%, for a total of either 24–48 h to establish hypoxic co-cultured models.
Then we pre-treated BEAS-2B cells with Fer-1 or DMF (MCE, China), both at a concentration of 20 μm for 24 h followed by co-culturing these two cell lines under hypoxic condition for 48 h as the treatment group. Either knockdown or overexpression of the SLC7A11 gene was performed in BEAS-2B cells before DMF (20µM) was added to medium for 24 h. The treated BEAS-2B cells were then co-cultured with M0 macrophage under hypoxia condition for another 48 h.

## Proteomic analysis of plasma and lung tissue
The plasma samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm to obtain supernatant, while the Top14 Abundant Protein Depletion Mini Spin Columns (Thermo Fisher, USA) were used to minimize the interference of high-abundance proteins on the detection of low-abundance proteins in plasma. For lung tissue and cell pretreatment, pulverization was performed using liquid nitrogen, followed by reconstitution with PBS, and subsequent fragmentation via ultrasound. The supernatant was obtained by centrifuging the lung tissue homogenate at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4℃. In both cases, protein solubilization was achieved by resuspending them with 200 µl of 8mmol/L urea in a 10kD filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) tube. After discarding the filtrate following centrifugation at 14,000 g for 10 min, reductive alkylation treatment and neutralization of dithiothreitol (Sigma, USA) with iodoacetamide (Sigma, USA) were carried out on the samples prior to trypsin digestion for peptide generation. The desiccated peptides were ultimately resuspended in a solution containing 0.1% formic acid to facilitate proteome analysis using LC-MS/MS coupled with a Quadrupole Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer in conjunction with the Ultimate-3000 HPLC system. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode for data acquisition.

## Data processing and bioinformatic analysis
The Q-Exactive raw files were identified against the Uniprot human database and analyzed with Thermo Proteome Discoverer (V2.2). Preprocessing of raw data included post-sum normalization, logarithmic transformation and normal distribution imputation performed with Perseus. Then the data were imported into Perseus for further analysis, excluding missing values exceeding 80% in each group. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using a two-tailed Student’s test with p values < 0.05 and fold change > 1.5 or < 0.67. The screened DEPs from plasma or lung tissue were visualized through heatmaps, volcano plots, principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) using Oebiotech online tool (https://cloud.oebiotech.cn/task/) or R software.

## Histomorphology of lung tissue
The inferior lobe of the right lung was surgically excised and subsequently fixed in 10% formalin solution, followed by embedding in paraffin, sectioning, and staining with hematoxylin and eosin for subsequent histological analysis.

## Transmission electron microscopy
The apex of the left lung was excised, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, followed by fixation in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution. Subsequently, the tissue specimens underwent dehydration through an acetone series and were embedded in Epon 812 epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate prior to imaging using a JEM-1400Flash transmission electron microscope for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis. The cells were detached using a cell scraper, rinsed with PBS, and fixed following the same protocol as described above.

## Immunofluorescence
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) in lung tissues, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), Arginase (Arg) and Nrf2 in THP-1 cells was analyzed using immunofluorescence assay. Tissue samples or cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using Triton X-100 before circles were drawn around to prevent antibody diffusion. The primary antibodies (Nrf2, iNOS and Arg) were then incubated at 4 ℃ overnight. The dye solution was then added to the circles, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a dark incubator for 30 min. The slides were placed in PBS (PH7.4) and washed on a decolorizing shaker 3 times, for 5 min each time. After the slices were slightly dried, DAPI dye was added into the circles and incubated for 10 min at room temperature away from light. The slide was again placed in PBS and washed for 3 times, 5 min each time. Finally, the slices were slightly dried and sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching tablets. The sections were observed and analyzed under fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse C1) and images were collected. Additionally, fluorescence microscope was also used to observe ROS levels analysis in BEAS-2B cells stained with DCFH-DA probe. The cells were washed in a well plate, followed by addition of the probe solution and incubation for 20 min. After washing, Hoechst stain was added, and the cells were incubated for 10 min. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

## Flow cytometry
ROS levels analysis in BEAS-2B cells, and M1/M2 macrophages were identified through flow cytometric analysis. Briefly, the cells were incubated with CD86 (M1) and CD206 (M2) antibodies. Then cells were washed and fixed before permeabilization. As for ROS level analysis, cells were collected and subjected to DCFH-DA staining for 20 min at 37℃, followed by three times washes with serum-free medium and resuspension in 1 mL PBS. The fluorescence was analyzed using a BD FACS Aria II flow cytometer (USA).

## Malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH) and Fe2 + detection in the lung tissues and BEAS-2B cells
Then GSH, SOD, MDA, and Fe2+ were measured in both lung tissue and BEAS-2B cells after treatment. The right lung tissues of rats were surgically excised and then rinsed with pre-cooled aseptic saline solution. Subsequently, the lung tissues were homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to centrifugation at 4℃ to obtain supernatants for subsequent analysis. The BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 6-well plates, subjected to pancreatic enzyme digestion post-intervention, cleaned with PBS and the resulting cell pellets were obtained through centrifugation for subsequent analysis. The levels of GSH (Beoytime, China), SOD (Dojindo, Japan), MDA (Dojindo, Japan), and Fe2+ (Dojindo, Japan) in the lung tissues and BEAS-2B cells were quantified according to manufacturer’s instructions provided with various assay kits.

## Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
In Boster’s ELISA kit of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, in lung tissue or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples, ELISA kit of and High mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) in lung epithelial cellsat specific dilution ratios were added and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. The supernatant was then removed and the well was washed. Biotinylated antibodies (1×) were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After washing off the excess antibody solution, HRP antibody (1×) was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Following this step, TMB substrate was added and incubated at 37 °C for 10 to 20 min in the dark. Finally, stop solution was introduced to terminate the reaction, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm.

## Total RNA extraction and Real-Time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from either lung tissues or BEAS-2B cells using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration of the isolated RNA was quantified by spectrophotometry at 260 nm, and its purity was assessed by calculating the 260/280 ratio (1.9–2.1). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using a random hexamer primer and a SuperScripts Reverse Transcriptase (RT) Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme, China). RT-PCR was conducted with gene-specific primers and SYBRs Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-red), utilizing sample cDNAs equivalent to 2 µg total RNA used as templates.

## Protein extraction and Western blot analysis
Lung tissues and cells were homogenized and total protein was extracted using RIPA Lysis Buffer Kit. The protein concentration was determined using BCA assay. Protein samples were denatured by adding sample buffer and heated at 100 °C for 7 min, followed by separation with 12% sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membrane was then blocked for 1 h in a solution of 5% heated, filtered skimmed milk in TBS-T, then incubated overnight with the primary antibodies of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11 (SLC7A11), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4), interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), HMGB1, and β-actin separately (CST, America) (dilution 1:1000) at 4 °C. After three rinses with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the secondary goat anti-rabbit or goat-anti-rabbit antibodies (Huaxingbio, China) (dilution 1:10000) diluted in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Antibody against β-actin (diluted to a ratio of 1:1000) served as loading controls. Visualization of proteins was achieved using ECL Plus reagent while band intensity measurements were performed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA) and the ratios between the protein of interest and β-actin were recorded. Average data from each rat within the same group were calculated before being analyzed and compared among groups.

## Cell viability
To assess cell viability, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was employed. Briefly, cells were exposed to hypoxia environment, Fer-1 or DMF. Then the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 10³ cells per well. At the designated time points, 20 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). Cell viability was standardized to that of the untreated controls.

## Lactatedehydrogenase (LDH) release assay
LDH release was measured according to LDH assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells seeded in 96-well plate were exposed to hypoxia environment, then 10 µl of LDH regent was added to a 96-well plate as the maximum enzyme activity control. After 1 h incubation, the suspension was removed to a new 96-well plate. After adding reaction solution, the absorbance of each well was determined at double-wavelengths of 490 nm and 630 nm using microplate reader (Thermo, USA).

## Statistical analysis
The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc.), and the results were presented graphically. All experiments were repeated three times. Power analysis was performed to determine the sample size of volunteers. Repeated measures One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and LSD-t post-hoc tests were used to compare parameters among different groups. Student T-test (significance level set at p < 0.05) were employed to determine the distinct levels of parameters between two groups.

## Results

## Ferroptosis pathway enriched in deps through plasma proteomic analysis between the plain area and high altitude
We initially recruited 40 participants, who gradually ascended from the plain area to the altitudes of 3700 m and 5000 m. Among them, eight individuals developed AMS upon reaching 3700 m, and AMS symptoms persisted even at an altitude of 5000 m after a seven-day adaptation period at 3700 m. This group was referred as the severe AMS (sAMS) group. Proteomics analysis was employed to compare DEPs in plasma samples collected from these 8 participants at low altitudes with their plasma samples collected after reaching two high altitudes, which means prior to and following the onset of acute mountain sickness (AMS). Our analysis using Heatmap and PLS-DA demonstrated a clear distinction in classification before and after ascent to the high altitude within the sAMS group (Fig.  A&B). A total of 73 DEPs were identified, comprising 18 up-regulated and 55 down-regulated proteins (Fig.  C). GO analysis revealed that the DEPs were mainly enriched in cellular components in cellular matrix and vesicle lumen, biological processes including granulocyte activation and neutrophil mediated immunity, as well as molecular functions like cell adhesion molecule binding and endopeptidase activity (Fig.  D). KEGG analysis revealed that the significantly enriched pathways primarily encompassed ferroptosis, focal adhesion, HIF-1 signaling pathway and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig.  E).

 

## Ferroptosis pathway enriched in lung tissue and lung epithelial cells via proteomic analysis after exposure to hypoxia
The rats in the experimental group were exposed to a hypobaric hypoxia chamber for 48 h, simulating an altitude of 5000 m and establishing a rat model known as the HH-ALI group. The DEPs in lung tissues between the control group (Con group) and the hypoxia-exposed group at 48 h (HH-ALI group) were compared using protein profiling. PCA demonstrated distinct separation of these proteins between Con and HH-ALI groups, while clustering analysis also revealed clear separation between them (Fig.  A&B). A total of 357 DEPs were identified, with 199 up-regulated and 158 down-regulated proteins observed (Fig.  C). GO analysis revealed that the DEPs were mainly enriched in the cellular components including fibrinogen complex and extracellular space, biological processes including negative regulation of endopeptidase and acute-phase response, as well as molecular functions including heme binding and serine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity (Fig.  D). KEGG analysis indicated enrichment of pathways related to ferroptosis, complement and coagulation cascades and platelet activation (Fig.  E). Consequently, our findings highlight ferroptosis as the common enriched pathway involved in both AMS development and high-altitude induced ALI. However, further investigation is required to elucidate the role of ferroptosis in high altitude induced ALI.

 

To simulate the microenvironment of hypoxia-induced ALI in vivo and investigate the mechanism of ferroptosis during ALI pathogenesis, BEAS-2B cells were subjected to normoxia and different time points of hypoxia, both individually cultured and co-cultured with THP-1 cells in vitro. The DEPs of BEAS-2B cells and THP-1 cells were compared between the co-culture control group (Con-co-culture group) and the 48 h-hypoxia-exposed co-culture group (Hypoxia-co-48 h group) using proteomic profiling. As for BEAS-2B cells, ferroptosis, glutathione metabolism, glycosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and oxidative phosphorylation were mainly enriched among the DEPs (Fig.  A, B). Meanwhile, neutrophil degranulation, neutrophil activation involved in immune response, positive regulation of cytokine production and metabolism related pathways were enriched in DEPs of THP-1 cells (Fig.  C, D).

 

## Ferroptosis in lung tissue of hypoxia-induced ALI rat model
After hypoxia exposure for 48 h, rat lung tissues were collected for histological examination using HE staining. Compared to the Con group, the lung tissue in the HH-ALI group exhibited alveolar interstitial thickening, hemorrhage, and inflammatory cell infiltration and the lung injury score significantly increased (Fig.  A). Increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines including IL-1 and IL-6, was observed in lung tissue of HH-ALI group, compared with Con group (Fig.  B). These findings collectively suggest successful construction of an ALI model induced by hypobaric hypoxia with an elevated level of inflammatory response in the lung tissue. By utilizing electron microscopy, we observed that the lung tissue in the HH-ALI group exhibited condensed mitochondrial membrane densities and smaller volume, as well as the diminished or vanished of mitochondria crista, compared to that in the Con group, indicating mitochondrial changes (Fig.  C). The fluorescence intensity of ROS in lung tissue was significantly higher in HH-ALI group than that in Con group (Fig.  D). Furthermore, Fe2+ levels were notably elevated in the HH-ALI group compared to those in the Con group (p = 0.02) (Fig.  E). Excessive accumulation of iron ions can disrupt redox reaction, resulting in production of many reactive oxygen free radicals within cells, which represents one mechanism underlying ferroptosis occurrence. We further assessed the lipid peroxidation product MDA and discovered a significant increase in its levels in the HH-ALI group when compared to the Con group (p = 0.0003). Moreover, GSH level and SOD activity were significantly lower in the HH-ALI group than those observed in Con group (p<0.0001, p = 0.017) (Fig.  E). Collectively, these findings together suggested that high altitude exposure induced iron overload, lipid peroxidation and redox imbalance in lung tissue. Furthermore, our results revealed a significant decrease in the expression levels of ferroptosis inhibiting proteins SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH1 in the HH-ALI group, while the expression of the ferroptosis promoting protein ACSL4 was found to be increased (Fig.  F, G). These findings strongly support the occurrence of ferroptosis in hypoxia-induced ALI.

 

## Ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells and inflammatory response in macrophage under hypoxia induced ALI in vitro model
Under hypoxic and co-culture conditions, electron microscopy revealed that BEAS-2B cells exhibited reduced mitochondrial volume, attenuated or absent cristae, and increased membrane electron density compared to normal condition. Notably, these changes were most pronounced in the group exposed to 48 h of hypoxia and co-cultured with M0 macrophages (Fig.  A). In addition, compared with BEAS-2B cells cultured individually under the same hypoxic treatment conditions, cells in the co-culture group demonstrated a significantly enhanced response, particularly in the Hypoxia-co-48 h group. The levels of ROS fluorescence intensity were higher under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxia, and further elevated during co-culture as opposed to individual culture (p < 0.05) (Fig.  B). Hypoxia also diminished the level of antioxidant enzyme substrate GSH and SOD activity in both individual culture and in co-culture groups of BEAS-2B cells. The GSH level and SOD activity in the Hypoxia-co-48 h group exhibited a significant decrease compared to that observed in Hypoxia-48 h group (p < 0.05) (Fig.  C). Hypoxic exposure resulted in a significant decrease in ferroptosis inhibitory molecules, including SLC7A11 and GPX4 (p < 0.05), indicating induction of ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cells (Fig.  D). Notably, mRNA and protein expression of SLC7A11, GPX4 and FTH1 exhibited the most pronounced decrease in the Hypoxia-co-48 h group (Fig.  D). These findings suggest that when co-cultured with M0 macrophages, hypoxia can potentiate ferroptosis induction specifically within BEAS-2B cells. Considering the expression levels of ferroptosis-related proteins, analysis of ROS fluorescence and antioxidant ability, it can be inferred that the induction of ferroptosis was most pronounced within BEAS-2B cells after exposure to hypoxic conditions for 48 h when co-cultured with macrophages.

 

## DMF alleviates inflammatory response through inhibiting ferroptosis during hypobaric hypoxia induced ALI in rat
We investigated the preventative effect of DMF and Fer-1 in ferroptosis through ALI rat model. Rats in the DMF-HH-ALI or Fer1-HH-ALI group were pre-treated with DMF or Fer-1 and subsequently exposed to a hypobaric hypoxia chamber for 48 h. Compared to the HH-ALI group, rats in the DMF or Fer1 preventative group exhibited reduced interstitial thickening, hyperemia, inflammatory cell infiltration and lung injury score in the lung tissue (Fig.  A; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Decreased expression of TNF-α and IL-6 was observed in both preventative groups (Fig.  B, Supplementary Fig. 1B). In addition, the proportion of M1-polarized macrophages in BALF increased significantly, with the mRNA expression of iNOS also increased significantly. While the M2-polarized macrophages decreased, with the Arg mRNA expression also decreased (Fig.  B). It could be observed that compared to the HH-ALI group, DMF or Fer1 exhibited increased mitochondrial volume and decreased mitochondrial density in lung tissue, indicating alleviation of mitochondrial contraction (Fig.  C, Supplementary Fig. 1C). Next, we further investigated the regulation of Nrf2 on lipid peroxidation, which plays critical role in ferroptosis. We found that lipid peroxidation product MDA expression was reduced (p < 0.0001), while antioxidant GSH content increased significantly (p < 0.0001), along with an increase in SOD inhibition rate (p = 0.046) in the DMF-HH-ALI and Fer1-HH-ALI group (Fig.  D; Supplementary Fig. 1D). Furthermore, there was a decrease in ROS expression levels within rat lung tissue from the DMF-HH-ALI and Fer1-HH-ALI group, suggesting a tendency towards balanced redox (p = 0.02) (Fig.  E, Supplementary Fig. 1E). This indicates that DMF or Fer-1 could improve antioxidant capacity in lung tissue by effectively ameliorating lipid peroxidation occurrence and reducing ferroptosis. In addition, SLC7A11, GXP4, and FTH1 protein and mRNA levels were significantly upregulated after DMF or Fer-1 treatment (Fig.  F, Supplementary Fig. 1F, G). Collectively, these findings suggest that the inhibition of ferroptosis by DMF may ameliorate inflammatory response in ALI induced by hypobaric hypoxia.

 

## The protection of DMF against inflammatory response of macrophage and ferroptosis of lung epithelial cells through SLC7A11
Firstly, we investigated the influence of hypoxia exposure on the viability of M0 macrophage. As shown in Fig.  A, exposure to hypoxia for 12 h increased the viability of macrophages, but prolonged exposure to 24 h significantly suppressed cell viability (p < 0.01). After hypoxia exposure for 48 h, the release of LDH in the supernatant of macrophages significantly increased (p < 0.05). The mRNA and protein level of IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α in macrophages gradually increased with prolonged exposure to hypoxia, indicating an enhanced inflammatory response due to hypoxia. Compared to the group solely exposed to hypoxia (p < 0.05), co-culturing macrophages with BEAS-2B cells under hypoxic conditions resulted in further upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages (p < 0.05), with the highest expression and secretion of inflammatory factors by macrophages were observed in the Hypoxia-co-48 h group (Fig.  B, C). The secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines exhibited a consistent pattern (Fig.  D). Furthermore, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence and RT-PCR analysis showed that M1-polarized THP-1 cells (CD86+, iNOS) were significantly increased, while M2-polarized THP-1 cells (CD206, Arg) were decreased in Hypoxia-co-48 h group (Fig.  E). Therefore, we consider the Hypoxia-co-48 h group as the in vitro ALI model since this condition induced the most severe inflammatory response in macrophages.

 

To further validate the preventative effect of DMF on inflammatory response and ferroptosis, we constructed the ALI invitro model with SLC7A11 knock-down or overexpression in lung epithelial cells. The viability of BEAS-2B cells treated with different concentrations of DMF or Fer-1 was detected by CCK-8 assay to determine the optimal dosing concentration. Our results indicate that there was no significant effect on cell viability at concentrations ranging from 0 to 20 µmol/L. However, when the concentration exceeded 40µmol/L for either DMF or Fer-1, respectively, cell viability was significantly inhibited (p < 0.001) (Fig.  A; Supplementary Fig. 2A). Therefore, a concentration of 20 µmol/L DMF or Fer-1 was selected to construct the cell model. The immunofluorescence analysis to evaluate the effect of DMF on nuclear translocation of Nrf2 revealed that Nrf2 in the nucleus was significantly reduced under hypoxia exposure, which was then increased by the DMF pretreatment (Fig.  B). After pre-treatment with DMF or Fer-1, GSH level increased, while SOD activity, Fe2+ and ROS level significantly decreased in BEAS-2B cells when compared with the hypoxia-co-culture group, indicating an enhanced antioxidant capacity upon administration of DMF or Fer-1. These changes could be reversed by knock-down of SLC7A11 (Fig.  C, D, E; Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). Furthermore, protein expression involved in ferroptosis including GPX4, SLC7A11 and FTH1 was increased in BEAS-2B cells pre-treated with DMF or Fer-1, which was also alleviated after administration of si-RNA of SLC7A11 (p < 0.05) (Fig.  F, G; Supplementary Fig. 2D). However, after DMF pretreatment and overexpression of SLC7A11, GPX4 significantly increased compared to DMF pretreatment alone (Fig.  H), along with decreased ROS level, Fe2+ and increased SOD, GSH level (Fig.  J-M). We also investigate how ferroptotic epithelial cells drive macrophage inflammatory response through detecting HMGB1 originated from BEAS-2B cells. Hypoxia exposure significantly increased the expression and secretion of HMGB1, while DMF pretreatment could alleviate its expression (Fig.  I). Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the inhibition of ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cells through DMF could be effectively reversed by the reduced expression of SLC7A11 and then reversed by overexpression of SLC7A11.

 

Furthermore, M1-macropahge (CD86+, iNOS) also decreased and M2- macrophage (CD206, Arg) increased after DMF pre-treatment. After inhibiting SLC7A11 expression in BEAS-2B cells, the proportion of co-cultured M1-macorphage increased while M2-macrophage decreased (Fig.  A). We also observed significantly reduced mRNA, protein expression and secretion levels of IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α in macrophages in Hypoxia-co-DMF/Fer1 group (Fig.  B, D, F). However, the decrease of inflammatory cytokines was partially reversed by SLC7A11 knock-down (Fig.  C, E, G; Supplementary Fig. 3). In addition, following SLC7A11 overexpression and subsequent DMF pretreatment, the mRNA, protein expression and secretion levels of IL-1 and IL-6 again decreased (Fig.  H). These findings suggest that DMF inhibition of ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cells under hypoxia can alleviate the expression of inflammatory factors and M1/M2 proportion of co-cultured macrophages, and could be reversed by decreasing SLC7A11 expression.

 

## Discussion
In the present study, we demonstrated for the first time that DMF can alleviate the hypoxia-induced ALI by upregulating Nrf2/SLC7A11 pathway in ferroptosis of lung epithelial cells (Fig.  ). We collected plasma samples from volunteers before and after their arrival at the plateau for proteomic analysis. In addition, a hypoxia-induced ALI rat model and an in vitro model based on co-culture of lung epithelial cells and macrophages were established. The proteomic analysis results from the human plasma, rat and cell model revealed distinct protein enrichment pathways associated with ferroptosis. Then our particular interest lies in investigating the underlying mechanism by which ferroptosis contributes to lung injury following exposure to high altitude hypoxia. Subsequently, we confirmed the occurrence of ferroptosis in ALI rat lung tissue following exposure to the hypobaric hypoxia, which could be alleviated by Nrf2 activator, DMF. Furthermore, using a co-culture model of lung epithelial cells and macrophages, we demonstrated that hypoxia induced ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells, could exacerbate macrophage inflammatory response, which could also be alleviated by DMF. Moreover, we discovered that the function of DMF to alleviate inflammatory response and polarization of macrophage was partially dependent on SLC7A11. Based on these findings, we supposed that DMF was a possible preventative target against hypoxia induced ALI.

 

Hypoxic environments at high altitudes exert a significant impact on the health of residents, primarily through severe lung injury, and life-threatening risks [ – ]. Studies have demonstrated that exposure to high altitude induces significant alterations in iron metabolism, disrupting in iron homeostasis and leading to toxicities such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis [ – ]. Chen et al.’s study observed the occurrence of ferroptosis in lung injury induced by chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) [ ]. Initially, we conducted proteomic analysis on clinical samples and identified proteins exhibiting differential abundance in plasma before and after high-altitude exposure, which were found to be enriched in the pathway associated with ferroptosis. We also established a rat model and an in vitro model of ALI under HA conditions. Increased cytokines in rat lung and macrophage indicated the occurrence of inflammatory response. The DEPs identified through proteomic analysis in the lung tissue and BEAS-2B cells before and after exposure to hypoxia were found to be enriched also in ferroptosis.
Ferroptosis is a recently discovered form of regulated cell death characterized by oxidative damage [ , ]. Given that hypoxia is frequently associated with increased ROS levels and oxidative stress [ , ], it is crucial to comprehend how hypoxia specifically regulates ferroptosis under different cellular conditions [ ]. Notably, diverse responses to ferroptosis under hypoxic conditions have been observed among cells from various diseases, with some undergoing ferroptosis while others inhibit this process [ ]. HA exposure has been shown to induce redox imbalance, inflammatory response, and ferroptosis in adipose tissue as evidenced by elevated levels of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA along with decreased GSH levels [ ]. Considering the involvement of ferroptosis in acute lung injury (ALI), it may serve as a significant therapeutic target for ALI treatment [ ]; however, the role of ferroptosis in high-altitude ALI remains unclear. Our study observed changes in iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation, as evidenced by elevated Fe2+ content, MDA and ROS in the lung tissue following high-altitude hypoxia induced ALI, which was consistent with previous reports [ ]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that exposure to acute hypobaric hypoxia exposure leads to a significant elevation in ROS and MDA levels within 1.5 h, specifically observed in the lung tissue of rats [ ]. The association between Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation, intracellular ROS accumulation, as well as a decline in intracellular GSH levels, represents distinctive characteristics indicative of ferroptosis [ – ].
Our current study also observed a decrease in the expression of ferroptosis-inhibitory proteins, namely SLC7A11, FTH1, and GPX4, alongside an increase in ACSL4 expression. GPX4 is a protein involved in the regulation of ferroptosis by inhibiting lipid peroxidation [ ]. Diminished activity of GPX4 prevents the metabolism of lipid peroxides through GPX4-catalyzed reduction reaction, resulting in Fe2+-mediated oxidation of lipids via a Fenton-like mechanism and subsequent generation of a substantial quantity of ROS, thereby promoting ferroptosis [ , ]. SLC7A11 serves as the primary plasma membrane transporter for extracellular cystine, responsible for facilitating the reverse transport of cystine/glutamate, subsequently participating in cysteine and glutathione synthesis and inhibiting GSH biosynthesis [ – ]. As iron metabolism-related genes, FTH1 exhibits a more potent ferroxidase oxidase activity, thereby facilitating the cellular absorption of surrounding iron ions. ACLS4 plays a pivotal role in the biosynthesis and remodeling of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), facilitating the activation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and modulating their transmembrane properties [ ]. These genes collectively regulate the intricate biological process of ferroptosis by governing iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation metabolism, which are crucial for maintaining cellular balance [ ] .
Dysfunction of lung epithelial cells is considered a crucial contributing factor to ALI pathology, and lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) have been widely used in ALI research [ – ]. CIH-induced lung injury resulted in significant accumulation of ROS, upregulation of ACSL4 expression, downregulation of GPX4 expression, and occurrence of ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cell [ ]. During hypoxia-reoxygenation, viability reduction in lung epithelial cells was accompanied by upregulated iron-related factors, as well as downregulated genes involved in inhibiting ferroptosis (Nrf2, SLC7A11, and GPX4), along with increased expression of inflammatory cytokines [ ]. Thus, we further elucidated the cellular-level mechanism of ferroptosis in hypoxic lung injury. In our study, after culturing the lung epithelial cell individually under hypoxic conditions for 48 h, we observed a negative correlation between the duration of hypoxia and antioxidant activities of GSH and SOD. Additionally, there was a positive correlation between the duration of hypoxia and ROS in BEAS-2B cells indicating oxidative stress under hypoxia condition. Our results also indicated a certain degree of ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cell based on gradually decreased expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 along with mitochondria-related changes.
The role of ferroptosis is crucial in initiating inflammation and exacerbating lung damage [ ]. Studies have indicated a close association between ferroptosis, arachidonic acid metabolism, and inflammatory response [ ]. Furthermore, consuming macrophages or neutralizing IL-1β in vivo can effectively eliminate ferroptosis-mediated liver cancer growth [ ]. Wang et al. demonstrated that modulation of ferroptosis can ameliorate HAPE induced by hypobaric hypoxia [ ]. Considering the crucial role played by macrophage in inflammatory responses [ , ], we further established an in vitro model by co-culturing THP-1 derived M0 macrophages with BEAS-2B cells to simulate the microenvironment within lung tissue. Under hypoxic conditions, we observed an upregulation of inflammatory cytokines in individually cultured THP-1 cells, which is consistent with previous literature reports. Under 1% O2 condition, PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages secreted enhanced levels of IL-1β [ ]. In another study involving co-culturing of THP-1 cells with liver cells, it was observed that the stimulation effect on hepcidin production mediated by hypoxia was significantly augmented compared to individual-culture of liver cells [ ]. Subsequently, co-culturing BEAS-2B cells with differentiated M0 stage THP-1 cells under hypoxic conditions was performed to investigate the interplay between these two cell types. Our findings also unveiled that this co-culture exacerbated the inflammatory response in THP-1 cells, as evidenced by increased levels of inflammatory cytokines compared to individual culture of THP-1 under hypoxia. Furthermore, co-culturing BEAS-2B and THP-1 cells under hypoxic conditions intensified ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cells, as evidenced by increased levels of ROS, decreased levels of GSH, SOD, ferroptosis-related proteins and mitochondria-related alterations compared to individual culture of BEAS-2B cells under hypoxia. Considering these findings collectively, we consider that macrophage inflammation exacerbates ferroptosis of BEAS-2B cells exposed to hypoxia, while ferroptosis of BEAS-2B cells further promotes the inflammatory response induced by hypoxia in macrophages.
Hypoxia could regulate ferroptosis through various pathways, the role and mechanism of hypoxia-regulated ferroptosis vary among different diseases [ , ]. Nrf2 has been reported to be associated with multiple cell death ways due to its role in regulating genes maintaining redox homeostasis [ ]. Under normal condition, Nrf2 is degraded by the proteasome via the KEAP1-Cullin 3 dependent mechanism, thereby maintaining intracellular concentrations at low levels [ ]. Upon occurrence of oxidative stress, Nrf2 is activated and exerts its antioxidant function [ ]. The activation of Nrf2 signaling plays an important role in protecting cells and tissues from oxidative stress-induced damage; however, when unfavorable factors such as oxidative stress persist, a decrease in Nrf2 expression can occur, leading to reduced binding activity with antioxidant response elements and disruption of the antioxidant balance, resulting in tissue damage [ ]. Multiple genes associated with iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation were transcriptionally regulated by Nrf2. Previous studies have found that Nrf2/SLC7A11 regulates ferroptosis in various diseases, such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and cancer in the lungs, brain, heart, and liver [ , , ]. Therefore, pharmacological modulation of the Nrf2 pathway to target the upstream regulators of ferroptosis could be considered as an optimal strategy for treating ferroptosis-related diseases.
Our study investigated the alterations in Nrf2 expression in BEAS-2B cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions, both in separate and co-culture settings and found a significant decrease in Nrf2 expression under hypoxia condition both individually cultured and co-cultured (Supplementary Fig. 4). Then we found that DMF (Nrf2 agonist) pretreatment increased the protein level of SLC7A11, GPX4, FTH1, while also elevating GSH and SOD levels, reducing ROS accumulation and suppressing inflammatory cytokine expression in lung tissue of ALI rat model. Consistent with previous studies, DMF preventative treatment in BEAS-2B cells could significantly increase the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, which was significantly reduced under hypoxia exposure. DMF also alleviate the inflammatory cytokine expression and secretion, indicating that Nrf2 can improve the inflammatory response of extracellular hypoxic ALI through ferroptosis. However, the molecular mechanism by which Nrf2 regulates ferroptosis in hypoxic ALI and the influence on the inflammatory response of ALI remains unclear.
There is a dearth of research investigating the potential regulatory role of Nrf2 in BEAS-2B cells under hypoxic conditions on ferroptosis through SLC7A11 and its subsequent impact on macrophage-mediated inflammatory response. In our current study, we employed siRNA to lower SLC7A11 expression in BEAS-2B cells. It was observed that the ferroptosis and macrophage-mediated inflammatory response in BEAS-2B cells, which were ameliorated by upregulating Nrf2 expression through DMF at an early stage, were then reversed upon decreased SLC7A11 expression. This suggests that the Nrf2/SLC7A11 axis regulates inflammation response in hypoxia induced ALI by modulating ferroptosis. Reduced levels of GSH and GPX4 as well as increased ROS levels in BEAS-2B cells indicate that continuous co-culture under hypoxia leads to diminished Nrf2 expression, resulting in impaired cystine transport due to decreased SLC7A11 expression. Consequently, downstream GSH synthesis is affected and GPX4 activity dependent on GSH as a cofactor is reduced, leading to persistent accumulation of lipid peroxides and ultimately causing ferroptosis in BEAS-2B cells and also impacts the inflammatory response during hypoxia induced ALI.
We also conducted a preliminary exploration of how lung epithelial cells undergoing ferroptosis drive the inflammatory response of macrophages. Damage-associated molecular pattern molecules (DAMPs) are endogenous danger signals that alert the innate immune system and shape the inflammation response to cell death [ ]. HMGB1 is a prototypic DAMP and can be released by dead, dying, or injured cells and once released HMGB1 can further bind its receptors to mediate immunity response [ ]. It has been demonstrated that HMGB1 is a mediator of ferroptotic cell death-induced inflammation that is positively regulated by autophagy [ ]. Thus, we examined the expression and secretion of HMGB1 in BEAS-2B cells following hypoxia exposure and found that hypoxia significantly enhanced both the expression and secretion of HMGB1, whereas DMF pretreatment alleviates these effects. Our results suggest that ferroptotic BEAS-2B cells release HMGB1, which may trigger the inflammatory response of macrophage; however, further investigation is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
DMF is a cell-permeable ester with well-characterized pharmacokinetics, primarily due to its use in treating multiple sclerosis (MS) and psoriasis [ ]. However, its clinical translation to high-altitude environments still requires careful consideration of three key factors: ​​bioavailability​​, ​​dosing feasibility​​, and ​​potential off-target effects​​. Orally administered DMF is rapidly hydrolyzed by intestinal esterases to its active metabolite, monomethyl fumarate (MMF), which is responsible for the majority of its biological effects, including Nrf2 activation [ ]. For high-altitude use, this proven oral bioavailability is a significant advantage. In addition, DMF is lipophilic and stable at neutral pH but may degrade under acidic conditions [ ]. An oral medication is vastly more feasible in remote or field settings than an intravenous or inhaled formulation. The existing commercial capsules (e.g., Tecfidera®) are designed for gastric acid resistance to minimize the common side effect of flushing [ ]. However, further research is needed to confirm whether high-altitude hypoxia directly impacts DMF’s gastrointestinal (GI) absorption or first-pass metabolism. Our data, showing that DMF was pre-treated in rats and cells, suggests a prophylactic strategy would be most effective. A feasible protocol could involve initiating dosing 24–48 h before ascent. This would allow sufficient time for Nrf2 activation and upregulation of antioxidant genes like SLC7A11 to establish a protective cellular state before hypoxia exposure. While DMF’s primary mechanism involves Nrf2 activation, its metabolite MMF may interact with off-target pathways, raising concerns about unintended effects in hypoxic environments. The most frequent adverse effects are flushing and gastrointestinal events (abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea) [ , ]. These are often transient and can be mitigated by dose titration. In the context of high-altitude travel, where GI upset is already common, this side effect profile requires careful monitoring. A more serious concern is lymphopenia [ ], which occurs in a subset of patients on chronic DMF therapy and increases the risk of opportunistic infections. This is likely less relevant for short-term, prophylactic use (e.g., 5–10 days for a high-altitude trip) as significant lymphopenia typically develops over months. DMF also has off-target, Nrf2-independent effects, such as activating the hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCAR2), which mediates the flushing response [ ]. While this is not harmful, it is a confirmatory sign of the drug’s activity.
There were still limitations for our study. Firstly, our study enrolled only male volunteers from low-altitude regions, which might compromise the sample’s representativeness. Thus, our future research will recruit participants across genders (males and females), age groups (e.g., 18–35 vs. 36–50 years), and altitude backgrounds (e.g., lifelong high-altitude residents, intermediate-altitude natives, and lowlanders) to examine how these factors modulate AMS susceptibility and associated molecular pathways.

## Conclusion
At present, the investigation of inflammation in ferroptosis is still in its nascent stages. Our study firstly found ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells may contribute to the exacerbation of inflammation in macrophages during the hypoxia-induced ALI. Then we demonstrated that DMF exhibits significant protective effects against hypoxia induced ALI, in a Nrf2/SLC7A11 dependent manner, and that it significantly decreases histopathological changes, inflammatory cytokines secretion and M1 macrophage polarization both in vivo and in vitro. To our knowledge, we have demonstrated a novel synergistic interplay between ferroptosis and inflammation in hypoxia-induced ALI. Therefore, the inhibition of ferroptosis through Nrf2/SLC7A11 in hypoxic lung epithelial cells can partially attenuate the inflammatory response associated with lung injury. Our study offers valuable insights for the identification of novel therapeutic targets in high altitude ALI and contributes to the development of personalized therapies for hypoxia-induced ALI.
