Current treatments primarily target the endothelin, nitric oxide, and prostacyclin pathways [ – ]. However, these vasodilatory agents fail to reverse the underlying vascular remodeling, resulting in persistently high mortality [ ]. Consequently, the clinical landscape has shifted towards disease-modifying therapies targeting pro-proliferative cascades [ ]. Notable breakthroughs include sotatercept, an activin signaling inhibitor that restores transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) balance [ ], and tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) pathway [ ]. Despite these promising advances, the intricate upstream regulatory networks governing these critical pathways remain largely unclear. Therefore, identifying novel molecular regulators is critical for developing next-generation targeted therapies for PAH.
CircRNAs are a class of non-coding RNA characterized by a covalently closed loop structure [ ], which confers them with high stability and resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation, compared to linear RNAs [ ]. This exceptional stability facilitates their cellular accumulation, thereby enabling them to exert sustained regulatory functions [ ]. While circRNAs can interact with several proteins or serve as translation templates [ ], they function prominently as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), competitively binding miRNAs, hence regulating the expression of miRNA target genes [ ]. Accumulating research has identified the association between circRNAs and diverse cardiovascular diseases, such as atherosclerosis [ , ] and ischemia/reperfusion injury [ , ]. However, the specific role of circRNAs in PAH and their potential regulatory effects on vital signaling cascades, such as the BMP pathway, remain largely unexplored.
Dysfunction of the BMP pathway is widely acknowledged to play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of heritable and sporadic PAH [ , ], given its potent anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory effect under physiological circumstances [ ]. SMAD family proteins, acting as the primary intracellular signal transducers for the BMP pathway, are regulated by SMAD-specific E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (SMURF1), which targets SMAD 1/5/9 (also known as SMAD 1/5/8) for ubiquitin-mediated degradation [ ]. Collectively, this regulatory interplay maintains the homeostasis of the BMP pathway, ensuring it functions within a physiological range.
In this study, we identified that circATXN2, derived from the back-splicing of Ataxin-2 (ATXN2) gene exon 18 and 19, increased significantly in lung tissue from PAH mice and hypoxia-treated PASMCs. It serves as a molecular sponge that interacts with miR-138-5p, thereby promoting the expression of SMURF1 in the BMP pathway. Moreover, in vivo silencing of circATXN2 was found to effectively attenuate PAH progression.

## Materials and methods

## Animal model and samples
Tagln-Cre transgenic male mice (8 weeks old, weighing ~ 20 g) were obtained from Cyagen Bioscience Inc. (Guangzhou, China). Wild-type C57BL/6 J male mice were obtained from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China). All animals were housed in a controlled environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, and a light/dark cycle of 12/12 h. All animal experiments conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA) and the local ethics review board.
Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus vector 9 (AAV9) was employed to infect Tagln-Cre mice in order to specifically knock down the circATXN2 expression in SMCs. After two weeks for AAV9 infection in room air (21% FiO2), two groups of male mice including hypoxia (Hyp) + si-circATXN2 negative control (AAV9-si-NC) and Hyp + si-circATXN2 (AAV9-si-circATXN2) were housed in hypoxia (10% FiO2), accompanied by weekly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor antagonist (SU5416) subcutaneous injection. Wild-type littermates were used as normoxia (Nor) group housed in room air.

## Echocardiography
Four weeks after hypoxia induction, echocardiography was performed using a 14-Hz ultrasound probe (Hewlett Packard Sonos 5500, USA). Crucial hemodynamic parameters associated with PAH were comprehensively assessed, encompassing right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), acceleration time (AT), ejection time (ET), and the pulmonary artery velocity time integral (PAVTI). These parameters were consistently measured over six consecutive cardiac cycles and performed by a single, experienced echocardiographer who remained unaware of the treatment protocol, ensuring a rigorous blinding approach during data collection.

## Right ventricular systolic pressure
Following the administration of continuous isoflurane inhalation anesthesia at a concentration range of 1–2.5%, the mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. A midline incision was made to expose the trachea, and the mice were intubated and connected to a small animal ventilator. A sternotomy was performed to expose the heart. A polyethylene catheter (24-gauge, 0.75 × 19 mm) was inserted directly into the right ventricle via the apex. The catheter was connected to a pressure transducer to record the right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP). After the measurement, mice were euthanized, and heart and lung tissues were collected for further analysis.

## Circular RNA high-throughput sequencing and computational analysis
Total RNA was extracted from the lung tissues of mice in the control and PAH groups (n = 3 per group) and dispatched to LC-Bio (Hangzhou, China) for high-throughput sequencing analysis, which was executed on an Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform utilizing the PE150 sequencing mode. Subsequent to sequencing, data underwent quantile normalization and data processing procedures, carried out employing the R software package. The ensuing datasets were then subjected to thorough analysis by LC-Bio. To predict circRNAs, we employed the computational pipeline known as CIRC explorer, which facilitated the retrieval of back-spliced junction reads. The quantification of circRNAs expression levels was predicated on the number of back-spliced reads per million mapped reads, with reference to the GRCm39 genome.

## Cell culture and treatment
Primary mouse pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) were isolated from male C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks old). Briefly, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the lungs were rapidly excised. The main pulmonary arteries were dissected under a sterile dissection microscope. To ensure the purity of the smooth muscle cell population, the adventitia was carefully stripped off with forceps, and the endothelium was removed by mechanical denudation using a fine iron wire. The remaining medial tissues were cut into fragments (< 1 mm3) and subjected to enzymatic digestion with Type I collagenase (2 mg/mL, Beyotime) and Trypsin (0.125%, Gibco) at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation. The digested tissue suspension was centrifuged, and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell purity was verified by immunofluorescence staining using antibodies against α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; 1:800, Proteintech) as a smooth muscle marker and CD31 (1:50, Proteintech) as an endothelial marker. Only cells exhibiting > 95% α-SMA positive and CD31 negative staining were used. To prevent phenotypic switching from contractile to synthetic states, only PASMCs from passages 2 to 5 were utilized for all experiments. For hypoxic treatment, cells were incubated in a Tri-Gas incubator (Baker Bugbox M, ME, USA) set at 3% O2, 5% CO2, and 92% N2 for 24 h.

## Cell transfection
To explore the role of circATXN2, miR-138-5p and SMURF1 in PAH and the connection between them, PASMCs were transfected by synthetic si-circATXN2, oe-circATXN2, miR-138-5p mimics, miR-138-5p inhibitors, si-SMURF1, oe-SMURF1 and their negative controls (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) respectively using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, NY USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RNA extraction, reverse transcription, quantitative real time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from tissue and cells using total RNA extraction reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), according to the guidance of manufacturers. cDNA was synthesized by HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme) for mRNA quantification. Meanwhile reverse transcription was performed using Bulge-Loop miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (RiboBio) for miR-138-5p quantification. qRT-PCR was employed using SYBR Green qRT-PCR Kit (Yeasen, Nanjing, China) on a 7900HT thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, MA, USA). The miR-138-5p relative expression was normalized to U6 while the remaining target were normalized to 18S and both of them calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Table . 

## Immunoblotting
Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a cocktail of protease inhibitors. After centrifuging at 13,000 × g for 20 min at 4˚C, a BCA kit was used to measure the protein concentration of the resulting supernatant. Then, equal amounts of protein were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by being transferred onto PVDF membrane. Then the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST buffer for 2 h, and subsequently incubated with targeting primary antibodies at 4˚C overnight. Membranes were washed with TBST buffer afterwards and then incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h in room temperature. Protein bands were finally visualized using ECL reagents (Yeasen) and recorded by a Syngene Bio Imaging Device (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). ImageJ software was used for quantification of protein levels.

## Cell proliferation assay
The level of cell proliferation was determined by two assays using CCK-8 Kit (Cellcook, Guangzhou, China) and EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) respectively. In the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates, then 10 μl CCK-8 reagent was added into each well after necessary treatments. The optical density was quantified at 450 nm by a microplate reader. For the EdU assay, cells were seeded in 24-well plates, and the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit was employed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, cells from various experimental groups were incubated with EdU for one hour. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were treated with click additive solution containing azide 488 and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in darkness. The labeled cell nuclei were observed under a fluorescence microscope following staining with DAPI.

## Cell migration assay
Cell migration capability was assessed through both wound healing and Transwell assays. In the Transwell assay, treated cells were transferred to the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates (Corning, NY, USA) with 8-μm pores, using serum-free medium. The lower chambers were filled with full medium, serving as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-h incubation, cells on the upper surface of the upper chambers were gently removed using cotton swabs, while the migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.4% crystal violet (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The number of these cells was subsequently counted under a light microscope at high-power fields. In the wound healing assay, treated cells were cultured in 6-well plates (Corning) until reaching approximately 90% confluence. After overnight serum deprivation, wounds were created by gently scratching the cell monolayer with the tip of a sterile 200 μl pipette. The widths of the wounds were then photographed and measured at random areas using a light microscope and ImageJ software, respectively.

## RNase R treatment
5 μg extracted RNA was mixed with 15U RNase R and reaction buffer (Beyotime) at 37 ˚C for 30 min, followed by incubation at 70 ˚C for 10 min to inactivate RNase R. The treated RNA was reversed to cDNA and subjected to qRT-PCR.

## RNA immunoprecipitation
Cells were washed with PBS and then lysed in cell lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with RNase inhibitor (Beyotime). To ensure complete nuclear lysis and chromatin shearing, the lysate was subjected to brief sonication on ice. Subsequently, the lysate was subjected to centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4˚C, and the supernatant was then incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with either anti-AGO2 or anti-IgG antibodies at 4˚C for 8 h. The beads were thoroughly washed and then treated with Proteinase K to eliminate proteins. Finally, the isolated RNA was extracted using total RNA extraction reagent (Vazyme). The purified RNA underwent analysis through agarose gel electrophoresis and qRT-PCR.

## Fluorescent in situ hybridization
The FISH assay was performed in PASMCs, Cy5-labeled circ-NC or circATXN2 probes and FITC-labeled miR-NC or miR-138-5p were designed and synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio Co. Ltd. The signals of the probes were detected by Fluorescent in Situ Hybridization Kit (RiboBio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in glass-bottom confocal dishes (Biosharp, Beijing, China). The cells were washed in PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, and then permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After the pre-hybridization buffer was added to each sample at 37˚C for 30 min, the hybridization buffer containing different probes was then utilized. Hybridization was performed in a humid chamber at 37˚C overnight. The cells were washed with hybridization washing buffer I three times at 42 ˚C for 5 min, and then sequentially washed with hybridization washing buffer II and hybridization washing buffer III once at 42 ˚C for 5 min, respectively. The cells were then counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio) and examined with a confocal microscope (ZEISS, LSM 900, Germany). The mean fluorescent intensity was calculated by Image J software and subjected to statistical analysis for significance.

## Immunofluorescence staining
After deep anesthesia, mice were perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed lungs were embedded in paraffin and then sliced into 5-µm sections. The lung sections were dewaxed with xylene and rehydrated using gradient ethanol. The slices were further treated with citrate buffer at 100 °C for 15 min to facilitate antigen retrieval. Then the lung sections were permeabilized using 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 10 min and blocked with 2% goat serum at RT for 1 h. After that, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody including anti-α-SMA (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at 4 ˚C overnight. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with a Cy3-conjugated anti-rabbit at RT for 1 h, followed by labeling the cell nuclei with DAPI (Servicebio). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX73, Japan). The mean fluorescent intensity was calculated by Image J software and subjected to statistical analysis for significance.

## Dual luciferase reporter assay
To construct a luciferase reporter vector, we synthesized fragments containing the wild-type or mutant sequences of the SMURF1 3’ UTR, which were then inserted into a firefly luciferase reporter vector, respectively. We also constructed the plasmid of miR-138-5p, miR-NC, and SMURF1 3’ UTR-NC. HEK-293 T cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured to 50%-70% confluence before transfection. 0.2 μg plasmids were transfected by using lipofectamine 3000. At 48 h after transfection, the cells were lysed, and luciferase activity levels were measured using Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme). Firefly luciferase activity levels were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity levels.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Data acquisition and analysis were conducted by blinded investigators with respect to the experimental groups. Biochemical analyses involved at least three samples per group for each evaluation, whereas in vivo studies entailed a minimum of six mice per genotype. Unless otherwise mentioned, all experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). The normality of the variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were determined by Student’s t-test while one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests was utilized for comparisons among three or more groups. Calculations were conducted using Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.), and significance was determined with a threshold of P < 0.05.

## Results

## CircATXN2 is upregulated in PAH models and hypoxic PASMCs
The strategy, semaxanib (VEGFR selective inhibitor) administration combined with chronic hypoxia (Su-Hyp) [ ], was utilized to establish mouse PAH models. Hemodynamic measurements confirmed the successful induction of PAH (Fig.  A). The lungs were then harvested and underwent high-throughput sequencing. Overall, 13,613 circRNAs were detected, and a total of 56 differentially expressed (fold change (> 2 or < 0.5) and P < 0.05) circRNAs were identified (28 upregulated and 28 downregulated) (Fig.  B, C). Ten circRNAs were then filtered out based on the criteria: reads per million mapped reads (RPM) > 1 and number of mapped junctions (NMJ) > 20. CircATXN2 was finally identified as a top candidate based on its sequence conservation, PASMC enrichment, and reaction to hypoxia (Fig. S1A-D). Validation by qRT-PCR showed that circATXN2 was predominantly expressed in PASMCs compared to PAECs (Fig.  D). Furthermore, circATXN2 expression in PASMCs was significantly induced by hypoxia (3% O2, 24 h) (Fig.  E, Fig. S1E), mirroring the upregulation observed in vivo. Meanwhile, we also detected the expression of the circATXN2 precursor, ATXN2 hnRNA. The results showed a synchronous increase in ATXN2 hnRNA levels under hypoxia (Fig. S1F), suggesting that the upregulation of circATXN2 is driven by the transcriptional activation of its host gene ATXN2 under such pathological stress. 
We subsequently characterized the structural properties of circATXN2. Divergent primers amplified the target sequence only from cDNA, whereas convergent primers amplified both cDNA and genomic DNA (gDNA), ruling out genomic rearrangement (Fig.  F). RNase R digestion assays demonstrated that circATXN2 was resistant to exonuclease degradation, whereas linear ATXN2 mRNA was significantly depleted, further confirming its circular structure (Fig.  G). Additionally, Sanger sequencing validated the back-splicing junction, confirming that circATXN2 is formed by the circularization of exons 18 and 19 of the ATXN2 gene (Fig.  H).
Finally, we examined the localization and conservation of circATXN2. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays revealed that circATXN2 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of PASMCs (Fig.  I). Tissue distribution analysis indicated that circATXN2 is highly enriched in lung tissue compared to other major organs (Fig.  J). Moreover, sequence alignment showed that the circATXN2 sequence is highly conserved across species, including mice, rats, humans, pigs, and rabbits (Fig.  K). Collectively, these data identify circATXN2 as a stable, conserved, and hypoxia-responsive circRNA that is enriched in PASMCs during PAH development.

## Silencing circATXN2 alleviates hypoxia-induced PASMC proliferation and migration
To address its role in PAH, we silenced circATXN2 expression in PASMCs using specific siRNAs (targeting the back-splicing junction (BSJ) sequence) and assessed the phenotypic effects under hypoxic conditions. Three distinct siRNAs, targeting different sites on the circATXN2 BSJ sequence, were designed to avoid off-target effects. qRT-PCR indicated that si-circATXN2 could effectively knock down circATXN2 but not linear ATXN2 mRNA (Fig. S2A, B). CCK-8 assays showed that while hypoxia promoted cell growth, this effect was significantly attenuated by circATXN2 knockdown (Fig.  A). This observation was further confirmed by EdU incorporation assays, which demonstrated that silencing circATXN2 markedly suppressed hypoxia-induced DNA synthesis and proliferation (Fig.  B, E). We next evaluated the impact of circATXN2 on PASMC motility, another key driver of vascular remodeling. Both wound healing (Fig.  C, F) and Transwell assays (Fig.  D, G) revealed that hypoxia-induced migration was significantly repressed following circATXN2 knockdown. Of note, silencing circATXN2 had no appreciable effect on apoptosis or autophagy (Fig. S2C-H), indicating that the observed reduction in PASMC viability was primarily attributable to suppressed proliferation and migration rather than enhanced cell death. 

## CircATXN2 acts as a miR-138-5p sponge
To explore the mechanism underlying the regulatory role of circATXN2, we first investigated its potential to bind miRNAs, based on the premise that cytoplasmic circRNAs predominantly function as miRNA sponges. We conducted RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays of AGO2. The results revealed a significant enrichment of circATXN2 in the AGO2-precipitated complex compared to the IgG control, substantiating its potential interaction with the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) (Fig.  A). Furthermore, the RIP assays showed a much higher enrichment efficiency for circATXN2 compared to linear ATXN2 mRNA, confirming the specificity of the circATXN2-AGO2 interaction (Fig. S3A). Intersecting predictions from miRanda, RNAhybrid, and TargetScan yielded five candidate miRNAs (miR-138-5p, miR-667-5p, miR-5120, miR-5126, and miR-6337) containing binding sites for circATXN2 (Fig.  B). These miRNAs were subsequently biotinylated as baits for RNA pulldown, and circATXN2 was specifically and significantly enriched only by miR-138-5p (Fig.  C). Additionally, only miR-138-5p is broadly conserved across species according to TargetScan, leading us to focus on the promising circATXN2/miR-138-5p axis. 
Sequence analysis predicted six potential binding sites for miR-138-5p within the circATXN2 sequence (Fig.  D), and FISH assays visualized the co-localization of circATXN2 and miR-138-5p in the PASMC cytoplasm, providing spatial evidence supporting their potential interaction (Fig.  E). Moreover, qRT-PCR demonstrated that miR-138-5p expression was inversely regulated by circATXN2 in PASMCs (Fig.  F, G), providing functional evidence of their interaction.
To confirm that circATXN2 functions via the miR-138-5p axis, we performed functional rescue experiments. The suppression of hypoxia-treated PASMC proliferation and migration was observed after transfection with the miR-138-5p mimic. Importantly, these inhibitory effects were then effectively rescued by circATXN2 overexpression (Fig.  H-M, Fig. S3B). Together, these findings provide compelling evidence that circATXN2 promotes PASMC proliferation and migration by acting as a sponge for miR-138-5p.

## CircATXN2 promotes PASMC proliferation and migration via the miR-138-5p/SMURF1 axis
Five bioinformatic tools were utilized to identify the specific downstream target of miR-138-5p, and four genes were predicted as potential candidates by the intersection of these algorithms (Fig.  A). Notably, qRT-PCR revealed that SMAD ubiquitination regulatory factor 1 (SMURF1) mRNA was significantly downregulated by miR-138-5p, whereas the other candidates showed no obvious changes (Fig.  B). 
Consistent with the mRNA results, we observed a negative regulation wherein miR-138-5p overexpression significantly downregulated SMURF1, and vice versa (Fig.  C, D). As a well-known negative regulator of the BMP pathway, SMURF1 targets R-SMADs for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation [ ]. We validated this specific relationship in PASMCs, and observed that SMURF1 negatively regulates the level of SMAD1, one of the R-SMADs [ ] (Fig.  E-G). To further assess the specificity of this regulation within the BMP signaling context, we also evaluated the expression of inhibitory SMADs (i-SMADs), specifically SMAD6 and SMAD7 [ ]. Our results showed that the protein levels of SMAD7 were not significantly altered by the modulation of SMURF1 (Fig. S4A-D). Given that SMAD1 is a direct substrate of SMURF1, these findings suggest that circATXN2 exerts its function primarily by regulating the stability of R-SMADs via SMURF1, without significantly disrupting the i-SMAD feedback loop. To validate the direct interaction between miR-138-5p and SMURF1 mRNA, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. The results showed that miR-138-5p significantly suppressed the luciferase activity of the vector containing the wild-type SMURF1 3’-UTR, but not the mutant form (Fig.  H).
Finally, to verify the hypothesis that circATXN2 modulates PASMC behavior by regulating SMURF1, we assessed whether elevating SMURF1 expression could reverse the phenotypic effects caused by circATXN2 silencing under hypoxia treatment. As anticipated, circATXN2 knockdown significantly suppressed PASMC proliferation and migration, while these inhibitory effects were partially counteracted by the overexpression of SMURF1 (Fig.  I-L, Fig. S5A-C).
In conclusion, we demonstrated that circATXN2 exerts its pathological pro-proliferative effects in PASMCs by preventing the downregulation of SMURF1 through miR-138-5p.

## SMC-specific knockdown of circATXN2 ameliorates pulmonary vascular remodeling and improves right ventricular function in vivo
To substantiate the therapeutic potential of targeting circATXN2 in vivo, we utilized a Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) system carrying siRNA (targeting the BSJ sequence) to specifically silence circATXN2 in SMCs. Tagln-Cre mice were subjected to chronic hypoxia exposure combined with semaxanib injection to establish the PAH model.
Immunofluorescence staining validated the transduction efficiency and SMC specificity of the AAV9 vector, as evidenced by the co-localization of the AAV9-GFP signal with the SMC marker α-SMA in pulmonary arteries (Fig.  A). Accordingly, qRT-PCR verified that circATXN2 expression was significantly knocked down in the lung tissues of the AAV9-si-circATXN2 group (Fig.  B). We next evaluated the effect of circATXN2 silencing on hemodynamic parameters and vascular remodeling. As expected, mice in the PAH group exhibited significantly elevated right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP). Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated that right ventricular function was impaired in the PAH group, as evidenced by decreased pulmonary acceleration time/ejection time (AT/ET) ratio and pulmonary artery velocity time integral (PAVTI). Additionally, PAH mice displayed severe pulmonary vascular remodeling characterized by medial wall thickening. Notably, all these functional impairments and the pathological phenotypes were significantly alleviated by SMC-specific circATXN2 silencing (Fig.  C-G). Finally, to validate the molecular mechanism in vivo, we examined SMURF1 levels in lung tissue lysates of mice. Consistent with our in vitro findings, immunoblot analysis revealed that the upregulation of SMURF1 protein in PAH mice was effectively attenuated by circATXN2 knockdown (Fig.  H). The overall experimental paradigm and timeline for this successful in vivo therapeutic intervention are summarized in Fig.  I. 
Collectively, these data demonstrate that reducing circATXN2 in SMCs attenuates the severity of PAH via the circATXN2/SMURF1 axis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Discussion
This study identified that circATXN2 drives PASMC proliferation and migration by interfering with the BMP pathway both in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, circATXN2 upregulates SMURF1 expression by acting as a molecular sponge for miR-138-5p. Such accumulation of SMURF1 accelerates the ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of R-SMADs, ultimately impairing the homeostatic BMP pathway. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report that circATXN2 promotes PASMC proliferation and migration, contributing to the pathogenesis of PAH (Fig.  ). 
CircRNAs, distinct from linear RNAs, are characterized by a covalently closed loop structure lacking 5' caps and 3' poly-A tails [ ], which confers them with high resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation [ ]. Such stability facilitates the cellular accumulation of circRNAs, rendering them ideal candidates for regulating gene expression [ ]. While ample literature has suggested that aberrant circRNA expression is closely associated with diverse cardiovascular diseases [ – ], such as myocardial infarction [ , , ] and cardiac fibrosis [ , ], the specific roles of circRNAs in the progression of PAH remain largely unexplored. Through high-throughput sequencing and confirmatory experiments, we identified circATXN2 as a significantly upregulated transcript among the differentially expressed circRNAs in the lung tissues of PAH mice. Our data substantiate that circATXN2 acts as a critical pathogenic factor, expanding the current understanding of the non-coding RNA landscape in PAH.
It is widely acknowledged that the homeostatic maintenance of the BMP pathway in PASMCs is crucial for pulmonary vascular health [ ], and its dysfunction is a primary driver of PAH. SMURF1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, acts as a potent negative regulator in this cascade by targeting R-SMADs, the major effectors in the BMP pathway, for degradation [ ]. Although the upregulation of SMURF1 is a known pathological feature of PAH [ ], the upstream post-transcriptional mechanisms responsible for its accumulation have not been fully characterized. A key finding of our study is the identification of the circATXN2/miR-138-5p axis as a novel upstream regulator of SMURF1. We demonstrated that circATXN2 acts as a molecular sponge for miR-138-5p, thereby relieving the repression of SMURF1. This interaction bridges the gap between aberrant circRNA expression and BMP pathway inactivation, offering a mechanistic explanation for the sustained SMURF1 upregulation driving pulmonary vascular remodeling. This observation adds a layer of complexity to the functional role of miR-138-5p in PAH. Previously, Le Ribeuz et al. characterized miR-138-5p as a pathogenic driver that promotes PAH by silencing the potassium channel KCNK3 [ ]. Interestingly, our findings suggest that miR-138-5p may exert pleiotropic effects that are highly dependent on the cellular context and the relative abundance of its ceRNAs. By demonstrating that circATXN2 effectively shields SMURF1 from miR-138-5p-mediated suppression, our study not only elucidates the specific cause of SMURF1 elevation but also highlights the circATXN2/miR-138-5p/SMURF1 axis as a pivotal regulatory node in PAH.
There are also several limitations in this study. First, although we validated the pathogenic role of circATXN2 in Tagln-Cre mice and primary PASMCs, the expression pattern and diagnostic value of circATXN2 in large-scale clinical samples from PAH patients remain to be further investigated. Second, regarding in vivo hemodynamic assessments, we employed an open-chest right heart catheterization approach. This method inherently abolishes negative intrathoracic pressure, which can result in lower absolute RVSP readings compared to closed-chest measurements or telemetry, although the relative pathological differences between groups remained robustly significant. Third, regarding the downstream mechanisms, while our findings highlight the regulation of SMAD1 by the SMURF1 axis and its independence from the i-SMAD (SMAD6/7) feedback loop, the BMP signaling cascade is inherently complex. We did not exhaustively assess additional BMP signaling components, such as other R-SMADs (SMAD5/8) [ ] or non-canonical alternative pathways (e.g., p38/MAPK) [ ]. Considering that these alternative pathways might concurrently maintain or alter the overall signaling homeostasis, how they specifically interplay with the circATXN2/SMURF1 axis during PAH progression remains to be fully elucidated in future studies. Finally, while we confirmed that hypoxia induces circATXN2 upregulation, the precise upstream regulatory mechanism, such as the potential involvement of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) or specific splicing factors, was not fully elucidated in this current work. Future studies are warranted to explore these upstream mechanisms and to verify the conservation of this axis in human PAH pathology.
In conclusion, the present study refines the current understanding of non-coding RNAs in PAH by identifying circATXN2 as a critical regulator of pulmonary vascular remodeling. We demonstrate that circATXN2 acts as a cytoplasmic sponge for miR-138-5p to modulate PASMC proliferation and migration via the SMURF1/BMP signaling axis. These insights into the mechanistic role of circATXN2 in PAH pathogenesis underscore its potential as a promising therapeutic target for future clinical interventions.
