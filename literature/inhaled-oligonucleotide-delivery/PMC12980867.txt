In clinical bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples, RCN3 was significantly higher in organizing pneumonia patients undergoing active fibrotic organization than in idiopathic PF patients in a stable fibrotic state. ## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is characterized as excessive scarring of the lungs, leading to irreversible destruction of the lung architecture and high mortality due to limited therapeutic options [ ]. PF frequently occurs after acute lung injury (ALI) due to infection, mechanical damage, or allergic reactions. Dysregulated initiation of the injury-repair process, with early accumulation of inflammatory cells, cytokines, and fibrous exudates, progresses to fibrosis under disrupted signaling driven by anti-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors [ ]. Furthermore, post-injury PF is causally associated with poor prognosis of acute pneumonia-induced pulmonary disorders such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and acute organizing pneumonia (OP) [ , ]. Despite extensive research, the incidence of PF after acute pneumonia has barely improved, underscoring the need to identify early-phase profibrotic mediators that could be targeted to rebalance repair in ALI.
Dysregulated epithelial-fibroblast crosstalk is a core determinant for the initiation and progression of pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. Excessive fibroblast activation post-ALI is driven by dysregulated release of profibrotic cytokines from aberrantly activated epithelial cells [ , ]. These profibrotic epithelial-derived cytokines, including transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), promote proliferation and activation of fibroblast to myofibroblast. Activated fibroblasts then release additional pro-fibrotic mediators to promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Although TGFβ is a key mediator in the reciprocal epithelial-fibroblast paracrine loop driving fibrotic remodeling [ ], the early epithelial paracrine signals that precede TGFβ in response to epithelial injury remain incompletely defined.
Reticulocalbin 3 (RCN3) is an ER chaperone protein in the secretory pathway bearing a signal sequence at its N-terminus, six EF-hands, and an HDEL ER-retention signal at its C-terminus [ , ]. RCN3 is required for alveolar type II (AT2) surfactant secretion during perinatal lung development [ ], and modulates injury–repair by limiting ER-stress–induced epithelial apoptosis and facilitating fibroblast activation [ – ]. Earlier in vitro work also noted chaperone-associated release of RCN3 alongside the maturation and secretion of paired basic amino acid–cleaving enzyme 4 (PACE4), with RCN3 functioning as a chaperone that specifically binds the PACE4 precursor [ ]. Building on this, our secretomics analysis of LPS-exposed pulmonary epithelial cells identified RCN3 within the epithelial secretome, leading us to hypothesize that secreted, stress-responsive RCN3 functions as an early paracrine mediator of pulmonary epithelial-fibroblast communication after epithelial injury.
In this study, we validated the stress-responsive secretion of RCN3 in both mouse models and human samples, and investigate its function and mechanism as a paracrine mediator of epithelial-fibroblast crosstalk in post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis. We show that epithelial RCN3 is exported via an N140-glycosylation-dependent ER-Golgi pathway and engages TGFβR1–Smad2/3 signaling in fibroblasts. Furthermore, early intratracheal neutralization of RCN3 attenuates initial fibrotic remodeling after LPS-ALI, supporting the concept that secreted, stress-responsive RCN3 acts as an early epithelial paracrine mediator with potential preventive therapeutic relevance in post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics statement
The study protocol involving human participants was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (No. 2021-KE-295). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committees of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (No. 2021-Dong-11) and the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Animal care and experiments were performed in strict accordance with institutional guidelines.

## Patient study design sample collection
This study was based on a retrospective study on existing ILD patient cohort, in which bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and clinical data were collected between January 2019 to December 2021 in the department of respiratory and critical care medicine, Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital. A total of 26 patients (IPF, n = 18; OP, n = 8) were included in the analyses, following excluding the patients with cancer, a history of corticosteroid use, sarcoidosis, pneumoconiosis, or incomplete data. IPF and OP patients were identified based on the published criteria of the corresponding societies. Non-ILD controls (n = 9) were subjects undergoing bronchoscopy with BAL for evaluation of incidentally detected pulmonary nodules, with normal HRCT and lung function; interstitial lung disease and active infection were excluded. The BALF and pulmonary function data were collected within two days after admission. The clinical data included gender, age, smoking history, vital signs, underlying diseases, laboratory examining data and pulmonary function test (PFT) were obtained from medical records.

## Reagents and drugs
Reagents and drugs used were human/mouse recombinant RCN3 (Abcam, ab276552/ab277008), human TGF-β (Sigma, GF346), LPS (Sigma, L6529), SIS3 (MCE, HY-13013), anti-RCN3 antibody (Abcepta, AP5788c), TGFβ receptor inhibitors-LY364947 (Abcam, ab141890) /LY2109761 (MCE, HY-12075), TGFβR2 siRNA (Santa Cruz, sc-36657), control siRNA-A (Santa Cruz, sc-37007), MiQumn G25 centrifugal desalting column (Chromsep, 110607), anti-His antibody (CST, 12698), anti-TGFβR1 antibody (Abcam, ab235578), anti-TGFβR2 antibody (Abcam, ab259360), anti-Smad3 (phospho S423 + S425) antibody (Abcam, ab52903), anti-Smad3 antibody (Abcam, ab208182), anti-Histone H3 antibody (CST, 9715).

## Mouse models
All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. All mice (male, 8–12 weeks old) were bred and housed in a specific pathogen-free mouse facility at constant temperature (18–23 °C) and humidity (40–60%) in sterilized plastic cages. A 12 h-light/12 h-dark cycle was used. The intratracheal administration of LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) was performed in 8-week-old C57BL6/J at the dose of 5 mg/kg in 50 µl saline (25 µl×2), and sterile saline was instilled at the same procedure as a control.

## Isolation and culture of mouse embryo fibroblast (MEF)
Embryos were harvested from pregnant Rcn3 knockout and wild-type mice at embryonic days 12.5 to 14.5 (E12.5–E14.5). The embryos were washed three times with DPBS, minced into small pieces, and allowed to adhere to the bottom of a Petri dish. A small amount of complete medium was added. Fibroblasts migrated from the tissue explants and were cultured for further experiments after 5 to 7 days.

## Cell culture
Human primary lung fibroblasts (CP-H011) were purchased from the Wuhan Procell Life technology company and Lonza (American, CC-2512); Cells were cultured in fibroblast medium (CM-H011, Procell). BEAS-2B cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, United States). 293T, HeLa, and HBE cell lines were purchased from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resources (NICR, China). These cells had been proven to be negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS (Gibco, United States), 100 U/ml of penicillin and 100 µg/ml of streptomycin at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2.

## Quantitative real‑time PCR (qPCR)
RNA was isolated from cells or tissues with Trizol (Invitrogen, 15596018) according to the standard protocols, and the cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, R037A). QPCR assays on these cDNA was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, RR820) and data were normalized to house-keeping genes (RPL19 for mouse samples and GAPDH for human samples) and analyzed by the 2−△△Ct method relative to saline/vehicle treated control groups. The data were derived from at least three independent experiments performed in triplicate, and the primers used in qPCR are shown in Table .

## Immunoblotting assay
Protein lysates (30–50 µg) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors were denatured in loading buffer and separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 90 min at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent imaging system (SINSAGE). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

## Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Concentrations of RCN3, TGF-β1, FGF2, and CTGF in murine BALF, as well as RCN3 levels in human BALF, were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Cloud-Clone Corp., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The specific kits used were: mouse RCN3 (SEH243Mu), mouse TGF-β1 (SEA124Mu), mouse FGF2 (CEA551Mu), mouse CTGF (SEA010Mu), and human RCN3 (SEH243Hu). All samples were measured in duplicate.

## CCK8 assay
Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured for 24 h, followed by serum starvation for 12 h. Cells were then exposed to rhRCN3 (0.1 µg/mL) or 10% FBS. At 24 h post-treatment, cell proliferation was assessed by adding 10 µl of CCK8 solution to each well and incubating for 2 h at 37 °C (CCK8 assay kit, LabLead, CK001). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to calculate the cellular proliferation rate.

## SMAD-binding element (SBE) reporter assay
293T cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h of culture and 12 h of serum starvation, cells were transfected with SBE reporter or negative control (BPS Bioscience, 60654). At 24 h post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing rhRCN3 (0.1–1 µg/mL), TGFβ1(0.2 or 2 ng/ml) or a combination of rhRCN3 (0.1 µg/mL) and TGFβ1(2 ng/ml). Luciferase activity was measured 24 h later using a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Beyotime, RG027).

## Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay
The interaction between RCN3 and TGFβR1 was analyzed using Biolayer Interferometry (BLI) on an Octet RED system (ForteBio). Recombinant human RCN3 (Abcam, ab276552) and the extracellular domain of TGFβR1 (prokaryotically expressed and purified) were used. Briefly, rhRCN3 was biotinylated using a Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Scientific, 21329) and subsequently desalted using desalting columns (Thermo Scientific, 89889). The biotinylated RCN3 (100 µg/mL) was immobilized onto Streptavidin (SA) biosensors (ForteBio, 18-5001). Following a wash step with binding buffer, the sensors were immersed in binding buffer containing serially diluted TGFβR1 protein (6.25, 25, 50, 100, and 200 ng/mL). Association and dissociation phases were monitored for 10 min at 25 °C. A reference sensor loaded with RCN3 but exposed to binding buffer alone was used to correct for baseline drift. Data were analyzed using the Octet Data Analysis software.

## Proteomic profiling of the injured epithelial secretome

## Protein extraction
Lyophilized culture medium samples (approximately 100 mg) were quickly ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1 mL phenol extraction buffer. Subsequently, 1 mL of saturated phenol with Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) was added. After vigorous shaking, the mixture was kept at 4 °C for 30 min. The upper phenolic phase was separated from the aqueous phase by centrifugation at 7100 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The upper phenolic phase was transferred to a fresh tube and mixed with five volumes of pre-cold 0.1 M ammonium acetate-methanol. Following overnight precipitation at − 20 °C, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. For the wash step, the pellet was resuspended twice with pre-cold methanol and twice with ice-cold acetone. Following another round of centrifugation, the pellet was collected, air-dried and resuspended with 300 µL lysate solution. After incubation of 3 h at room temperature, the solution was centrifuged to remove any insoluble fraction and the resulting supernatant contained the total extractable protein. The total protein concentrations were quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay.

## SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis
Protein samples (10 µg) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue using the eStain LG (GenScript, Nanjing, China) and imaged using an automatic digital gel image analysis system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

## Protein digestion
Protein samples (50 µg each) were adjusted to equal volumes and concentrations. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and samples were incubated at 55 °C for 30–60 min. After cooling to room temperature, iodoacetamide (IAA) was added to a final concentration of 10 mM, and samples were incubated in the dark for 15–30 min at room temperature. Proteins were precipitated by adding six volumes of pre-cooled acetone and incubating at − 20 °C for at least 4 h or overnight. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 8000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and residual acetone was allowed to evaporate for 2–3 min. The pellet was redissolved in 100 µL of 50 mM NH₄HCO₃. Trypsin was added at a protein-to-enzyme ratio of 50:1 (w/w), and digestion was performed at 37 °C for 12 h. The reaction was quenched by adjusting the pH to 3 with phosphoric acid. Peptides were desalted using SOLA™ SPE cartridges, dried under vacuum, and resuspended in buffer containing iRT peptides (1:10) prior to analysis.

## Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry
Proteomic analysis was performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to an EASY-nLC™ 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Peptides were loaded onto a C18 column (15 cm × 75 μm) and separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using the following linear gradient of mobile phase B: 5–22% for 0–20 min; 22–37% for 20–24 min; 37–80% for 24–27 min; and held at 80% for 27–30 min. The ion mobility range was set from 0.7 to 1.3 V·s/cm², and the collision energy ranged from 20 to 59 eV. MS/MS spectra were acquired in the mass range of 100–1700 m/z.

## Database search and bioinformatics
Raw data were searched against the UniProt Human database using Spectronaut Pulsar™ 18.4 (Biognosys, Switzerland). The search parameters were set as follows: fixed modification, Carbamidomethyl (C); variable modifications, Oxidation (M) and Acetyl (Protein N-term); enzyme, Trypsin; missed cleavages allowed, 2. The False Discovery Rate (FDR) cutoff for both precursors and proteins (Q-value) was set at 0.01.

## Statistical analyses
Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified based on a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 and a P-value < 0.05, with a requirement of at least 4 unique peptides. Functional annotation was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway databases. Enrichment analyses were conducted to characterize the biological functions of the DEPs.

## High‑throughput RNA sequencing and data analyses
RNA-Sequencing analyses of the total RNA from CP-H011 cells with rhRCN3 (0.1 µg/mL) treatment for 24 h and control (n = 4 per group) were performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). In brief, total RNA was extracted using the Mir Vana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion), and the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7 was subjected to library construction using TruSeq Stranded mRNA LTSample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). RNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina sequencing platform (HiSeqTM 2500 or Illumina HiSeq X Ten), and 125 bp paired-end reads were generated. Quantification of mRNA transcript abundance was performed by normalized expression values as fragments per kb per million reads using cufflinks, and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTseq-count. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a foldchange (FC) > 1.5 and FDR < 0.05 using DEseq2. Hierarchical clustering was performed to visualize gene expression patterns across groups. Gene Ontology analysis on these DEGs prioritized by strength (Gene-ratio: the ratio of a number of deferentially expressed genes between the number of genes associated with the GO term) and FDR p values corrected by BH procedure (< 0.05). The most affected processes were presented.

## Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay
To identify RCN3-interacting proteins, immunoprecipitation was performed on both secreted and intracellular fractions. For the analysis of secreted proteins, cell supernatants were collected from 293T cells transfected with RCN3-flag or control plasmid. The supernatants were pre-cleared with 30 µl protein A/G agarose beads for 1.5 h at 4 °C to eliminate nonspecific binding proteins and incubated with 1 µg of anti-flag antibody overnight at 4 °C. Immune complexes were captured using 50 µL of Protein A/G beads, washed at least three times with TLB buffer, and eluted by boiling in 2 × loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. The samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie brilliant blue staining, and the immunoprecipitants were identified using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).
Separately, to analyze intracellular interactions, cytoplasmic and membrane proteins fractions were extracted from CP-H011 cells transfected with RCN3-his or control plasmid using cell membrane protein and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime, P0033). Cytoplasmic or membrane protein lysates (1 mg) were similarly pre-cleared and immunoprecipitated with 1 µg anti-his antibody overnight at 4 °C. The immune complexes were captured, washed, and eluted as described above, followed by analysis via immunoblotting.

## Cellular immunofluorescent staining (IF)
HeLa cells were seeded in 35 mm confocal dish (5 × 104 cells/dish). Forty-eight hours after transfection with RCN3-wildtype, RCN3-without-HDEL, or RCN3-N140Q plasmids, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 8 min. Then, the cells were blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002, 1:500). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. The primary antibodies used were: anti-RCN3 (1:200, sigma, HPA050402), anti-Golgi97 (1:100, Invitrogen, A-21270), anti-Flag (1:500, sigma, F1804), anti-Flag (1:500, CST, 14793) and anti-Calnexin (1:100, CST, 2679). The secondary antibodies (all 1:500, from ZSGB-BIO) included: Alexa Fluor 488-Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (ZF0511), Alexa Fluor 488-Goat anti-Mouse IgG (ZF0512), Alexa Fluor 594-Goat anti-Mouse (ZF0513) and Alexa Fluor 594-Goat anti-Rabbit (ZF0516).

## Histological staining
Following euthanasia, lung tissues were harvested and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissues were subsequently dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin according to standard procedures. Section (3-µm thick) were cut and mounted on slides. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using commercial solutions (BASO, BA-4097 and BA-4098). Masson’s trichrome staining was performed using Masson’s trichrome staining Kit (Solarbio, G1340) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, paraffin-embedded tissues were performed through standard protocols. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies: anti-αSMA (1:4000, Abcam: ab124964) and anti-TGFβ1 (1:500, Abcam: ab215715). Whole-slide images were acquired using a Pannoramic 250 FLASH scanner (3DHISTECH).

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as the mean ± SD. To compare continuous variables, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test the normality of the data. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed by the two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. Statistical comparisons of three or more groups were performed by using the one-way ANOVA (Bonferroni post hoc test) for normally distributed variables, one-way Kruskal–Wallis tests (Dunn’s post hoc test) for non-normally distributed variables, or two-way ANOVA (Tukey post hoc test). The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

## Results

## Secretomics identifies RCN3 as an early epithelial paracrine mediator
To identify early paracrine mediators during lung injury-repair, we profiled the secretome of LPS-treated human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells by secretory proteomics. We detected 145 upregulated proteins (fold change ≥ 1.5; ≥4 unique peptides), with GO enrichment in extracellular matrix and collagen-associated components. Among these, 50 were involved in repair-related processes, and from this subset, 6 lung-related proteins were identified, including CTGF, FGF2, HLA-B-associated transcript 3 (BAT3), Tenascin C (TNC), RCN3 and Chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1/YKL40) (Fig. A and B). Notably, CTGF and FGF2 are well-established mediators in the injury-repair process that are known to be critically involved in PF [ ]. Immunoblotting of conditioned media confirmed increased secretion for the other four lung-related candidates (BAT3, TNC, RCN3 and YKL40); RCN3 showed the most robust induction, nominating it as an early epithelial paracrine mediator candidate in response to epithelial injury (Fig. C).
To evaluate the potential clinical value of secretory RCN3, we measured BALF RCN3 in organizing pneumonia (OP) patients with active organization (n = 8), stable idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) (n = 18), and non-ILD controls (subjects undergoing bronchoscopy for pulmonary nodule evaluation with normal pulmonary diagnostic outcomes, n = 9). The baseline characteristics of the patients, including laboratory data at admission, are shown in S-Table 1. BALF RCN3 was higher in OP than in stable IPF and controls (Fig.  D), consistent with increased epithelial RCN3 during acute/subacute activity. These data are exploratory and stage-anchored and support the hypothesis that secreted RCN3 participates in early-phase responses following epithelial injury.

 

## Full-length RCN3 is secreted through the ER-Golgi secretory pathway
To validate the regulated RCN3 secretion process, we obtained mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) from Rcn3−/− mice (from our prior study [ ]). A ~ 48 kDa RCN3 protein band was detected in cell lysates (CL) and conditioned medium (CM) from wildtype MEFs (WT) but not knockout (KO) MEFs (Fig. A, top panel). Likewise, RCN3 was detected in CL and CM from human lung epithelial BEAS-2B cells and mouse lung fibroblasts (LF) (Fig. A, bottom panel). Secreted RCN3 was confirmed by mass spectrometry of a ~ 48 kDa band immunoprecipitated from WT MEF conditioned medium using an anti-RCN3 antibody (S-Figure 1).

 

To confirm whether full-length RCN3 is secreted, we constructed a double-tagged RCN3 molecule with Flag at the N-terminus after the signal peptide and Myc at the C-terminus preceding the HDEL (ER retention signal) sequence. Secreted RCN3 was immunoprecipitated by both Flag and Myc antibodies but not by anti-HDEL (Fig.  B), suggesting loss of the HDEL retention signal during secretion. Consistently, mass spectrometry identified a full-length of secreted RCN3 lacking HDEL in CM from flag-RCN3 transfected 293T cells (Fig.  C). Additionally, the deletion of the HDEL decreased intracellular RCN3 and markedly enhanced secretion, consistent with HDEL-dependent ER retention of RCN3 for its chaperone role (Fig.  D).
To further clarify the secretory pathway of RCN3, we studied the effect of Brefeldin A (BFA), which blocks trafficking between the ER and Golgi and thus inhibits intracellular transport of secretory proteins. BFA treatment inhibited RCN3 secretion (Fig.  E), indicating that RCN3 is secreted through the ER-Golgi secretory pathway. Consistently, immunofluorescence assays verified the colocalization of RCN3 with ER and Golgi marker proteins, while intracellular RCN3 lacking the HDEL signal was barely visible (Fig.  F). These results support the secretion of RCN3, through the canonical ER-to-Golgi-dependent secretory pathway.
Because N-linked glycosylation is required for proper protein folding and ER-Golgi transport, we used the NetNGlyc prediction tool and identified Asn140 (Asn-Ala-Thr) as a putative N-glycosylation site. It was confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis of PNGase F-treated RCN3 enriched via FLAG-IP from CL and CM of 293T cells expressing FLAG-RCN3 (Fig.  G and S). To evaluate the significance of N140-Glyc modification for RCN3 secretion, we constructed N140-Glyc mutants (N140A and N140Q). Because the third Thr is required for N-Glyc, we also constructed a T142A mutant. The two bands for WT RCN3 were reduced to a single band upon treatment with the deglycosylating enzyme PNGase F, and similar changes were observed in all three of the glycosylation mutants. Furthermore, all mutants exhibited significantly decreased RCN3 secretion in CM, although the N140Q and T142A mutants showed comparable expression within cells (CL) (Fig.  H). Additionally, in immunofluorescence assays of Hela cells, the N140Q mutant displayed an aggregated punctate distribution in the cytoplasm and partially co-localized with the ER but not the Golgi (Fig.  I). The ER retention of the N140Q mutant was accompanied by a marked increase in the ER stress marker GRP78 (Fig.  J). These observations are consistent with the possibility that N140-Glyc modification regulates RCN3 secretion and further supports the secretion of RCN3 via the ER-Golgi secretory pathway. Together, these observations indicate that RCN3 secretion occurs through the ER-Golgi pathway and is critically dependent on N-glycosylation at N140.

## Alveolar RCN3 secretion precedes TGFβ1 during LPS-induced ALI
To explore the biological function of secretory RCN3 during lung injury-repair, we examined the dynamics of RCN3 secretion in BALF after intratracheal LPS administration (5 mg/kg). In C57BL/6J mice, LPS-induced ALI was evident by day 2, resolved by day 4, and progressed to a fibrotic repair phase by days 7–14, as shown by H&E staining (Fig.  A and B). The transcription levels of fibrotic genes (Collagen I α1, Collagen I α2, TGFβ1, and α-SMA) were markedly upregulated on days 7 and 14 after LPS exposure, while the transcription of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) was sharply enhanced on day 2 and then declined, coinciding with the pathological changes (Fig.  C). Notably, RCN3 levels in BALF increased on days 2 and 4 and declined thereafter, whereas TGFβ1 levels in BALF were upregulated on days 7 and 14 (Fig.  D and E). Consistent with TGFβ1, CTGF and FGF2 levels in BALF were also upregulated on days 7 and 14 (S-Figure 3). Thus, RCN3 peaks earlier than TGFβ1, CTGF and FGF2 in BALF following LPS-ALI, suggesting that BALF RCN3 pool would act as an early responder to epithelial injury in the ALI–repair continuum. We further supported this hypothesis using mouse and human epithelial cell models (MLE12 and BEAS-2B), both of which exhibited increased RCN3 secretion after LPS exposure (Fig.  F and G).

 

## Secreted epithelial RCN3 activates pulmonary fibroblasts without promoting proliferation
To test whether secreted RCN3 activates fibroblasts, we treated human primary lung fibroblasts (CP-H011) with recombinant human RCN3 (rhRCN3, 0.1 µg/ml) (S-figure 4 A). RhRCN3 significantly enhanced expressions of fibrotic genes, including Collagen I α1, Collagen I α2, α-SMA, CTGF and TGFβ1, but not proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA). TGFβ1 (2 ng/ml) similarly promoted the transcription of these fibrotic genes, while co-treatment of rhRCN3 with TGFβ did not further enhance fibroblast activation induced by TGFβ alone (Figs.  A and B). Consistent with the PCNA expression results, rhRCN3 did not increase CP-H011 cell proliferation (Fig.  C).
To further validate the role of RCN3 in mediating epithelial-fibroblast interactions after injury, we collected the CM from 293T cells transfected with RCN3-WT or -N140Q mutant, which we applied to culture lung fibroblast CP-H011 for 24 h. CM from RCN3-WT-293T, but not RCN3-N140Q-293T cells, increased the expression of Collagen I α1, Collagen I α2, α-SMA, CTGF and TGFβ1 in fibroblasts (Fig.  D). In a complementary epithelial model, BEAS-2B cells were challenged with LPS for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh medium and collected 24 h later. The CM was then incubated with either IgG or anti-RCN3 antibodies for RCN3 neutralization and subsequently applied to CP-H011 fibroblasts. LPS-IgG-CM significantly upregulated both fibrotic genes and PCNA, while RCN3 neutralization (LPS-RCN3 AB-CM) significantly blunted the induction of fibrotic genes but did not reduce PCNA expression (Fig.  E). Together, these data indicate that epithelial RCN3, released under injury or stress, acts as a paracrine activator of lung fibroblasts to enhance fibrotic gene expression without increasing proliferation, and that this activity depends on N140-glycosylation–mediated secretion.

 

## Secreted RCN3 engages TGFβR1 to activate canonical TGFβ–Smad signaling
To explore how secreted RCN3 modulates fibroblast activation, we performed RNA-seq on CP-H011 fibroblasts treated with rhRCN3 versus vehicle. A total of 543 differentially-expressed genes (328 upregulated and 215 downregulated) were identified (foldchange (FC) > 1.5, FDR < 0.05), including multiple genes primarily involved in fibrosis (Fig. A). The rhRCN3-dependent Gene Ontology (GO) processes were enriched in collagen-containing extracellular matrix components (Fig. B), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) processes were enriched in the TGFβ signaling pathway (Fig. C). Concordantly, rhRCN3 treatment markedly increased the phosphorylation of Smad3, but not AKT, Erk1/2 or JNK, in fibroblasts (Fig. D). Furthermore, SMAD-binding element (SBE) reporter assays confirmed that rhRCN3 significantly activated TGFβ signaling, albeit to a lesser extent than TGFβ1, and showed no significant additivity upon co-treatment with TGFβ1 (Fig. E and S-Figure 5A–B). Consistently, RCN3 exposure caused a marked nuclear translocation of Smad3 in fibroblasts, similar to TGF-β1 treatment (S-figure 6 A-B). Together, these data indicate that RCN3 selectively activates canonical TGFβ–Smad3 signaling. In addition, rhRCN3 further upregulated transcriptions of TGFβR1/2 and TGFβ1 in fibroblasts and this effect was significantly attenuated by the Smad3 inhibitor SIS3 (S-Figs. 4B and S, 5 and 6, 7 A-C), consistent with canonical Smad-dependent amplification [ ].
It is well-established that TGFβ1 initiates canonical TGFβ signaling by binding to TGFβR1/TGFβR2 heterodimers, leading to TGFβR1-mediated phosphorylation of Smad2/3 [ , ]. We therefore investigated whether RCN3 engages TGFβR1 or TGFβR2. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay revealed that TGFβR1, but not TGFβR2, interacts with RCN3 (Fig. F). Pharmacologic inhibition with LY364947 (TGFβR1-selective) and LY2109761 (TGFβR1/2) similarly blunted rhRCN3-induced Smad3 phosphorylation (Fig. G). Because no specific inhibitor for TGFβR2 is available, we used siRNA to suppress TGFβR2 expression. As expected, TGFβR2 knockdown failed to inhibit RCN3-induced Smad3 phosphorylation, while TGFβR1 knockdown markedly blunted this response (Fig. H and I).
To assess receptor engagement biophysically, we performed Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI, Octet-RED system), which demonstrated a concentration-dependent interaction between RCN3 and TGFβR1 (estimated dissociation constant (KD) = 26.91 nM, Fig.  J). Moreover, protein–protein docking analysis (ClusPro 2.0) yielded high-confidence models in which RCN3 residues 60–70 and 80–93 form a putative interface with the extracellular domain of TGFβR1 (Fig.  K and S-figure 8). We note that these structural predictions are hypothesis-generating and do not exclude indirect association within larger complexes; interface mutagenesis and orthogonal structural methods will be needed to establish a direct-binding footprint and its physiological relevance.

 

## Extra-alveolar RCN3 is sufficient to trigger fibrotic remodeling in vivo
To evaluate the profibrotic role of secretory RCN3 in vivo, we subjected C57BL/6J mice to intratracheal administration of recombinant mouse RCN3 (rmRCN3, 0.1 mg/kg, two doses) (Fig.  A). At day 14, lungs from rmRCN3-treated mice showed fibrotic remodeling by Masson’s trichrome staining and increased α-SMA and TGFβ1 by immunohistochemistry (Fig.  B). Correspondingly, COL1A1 and α-SMA mRNA and protein levels were elevated, and COL1A2, CTGF, and TGFβ1 mRNA were increased (Fig.  C and D). In contrast, the expression of inflammatory response-related factors, including IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, was not significantly different indicating that rmRCN3-induced-fibrosis is not related to inflammation (S-figure 9).

## Early neutralization of alveolar RCN3 attenuates fibrosis after LPS-ALI
Given the above evidence, we hypothesized that limiting alveolar RCN3 early after ALI could be beneficial against subsequent fibrotic remodeling. To test this, RCN3 antibody (2 µg/mouse) or control-IgG was administered intratracheally to C57BL/6J mice on days 2 and 4 after intratracheal LPS (Fig.  E). Intratracheal administration of the RCN3 antibody significantly depleted RCN3 in BALF after LPS exposure (S-figure 10). At 7 days post-LPS, the RCN3 antibody group exhibited less pulmonary fibrotic remodeling than the IgG control group, as shown by Masson’s staining and immunohistochemical detection of α-SMA and TGFβ1 (Fig.  F), accompanied by reduced expression of Collagen I, Collagen II, and TGFβ1 (Fig.  G and H). By day 14 post-LPS, fibrotic changes had largely resolved, and no significant difference between groups was observed, consistent with an early-phase window of efficacy for RCN3 neutralization (S-figure 11 A–11B). These in vivo results support a model in which excess alveolar RCN3 contributes to early-phase fibrotic remodeling post-ALI, and early neutralization can attenuate this response. While these findings nominate RCN3 as a potential therapeutic target for post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis, comprehensive pharmacological characterization (pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and off-target profiling) was not undertaken in this study.

 

## Discussion
Dysregulated epithelial-fibroblast crosstalk underlies the initiation and progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Here, we identify secreted, stress-responsive RCN3 as an early epithelial paracrine mediator that engages TGFβR1-Smad3 signaling to activate fibroblasts in PF post-injury (schematized in Fig.  ): during the early injury-repair phase following ALI, epithelial cells secrete RCN3 via an N140-glycosylation–dependent ER–Golgi pathway, thereby increasing alveolar RCN3, which in turn activates fibroblasts through TGFβR1–Smad3 signaling. The resulting myofibroblast activation is accompanied by upregulation of profibrotic factors (including TGFβ1), reinforcing extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. Within this framework, early neutralization of alveolar RCN3 attenuated initial fibrotic remodeling, nominating epithelial RCN3 as a potential early-window therapeutic target in post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis.

 

Epithelial-fibroblast interaction is essential for the initiation and progression of the lung injury-repair process, while its imbalance leads to aberrant fibrotic remodeling. In response to epithelial injury or stresses, epithelial cells release paracrine cues that activate fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, which in turn release more factors to promote EMT and fibroblast proliferation [ ]. In our models, CM from LPS-challenged BEAS-2B cells activated primary human lung fibroblasts, while RCN3 neutralization significantly blunted this effect. Consistent with the in vitro results, intratracheal RCN3 neutralization in the early repair phase mitigated pulmonary fibrosis post-LPS-induced ALI in vivo. Clinically, BALF from patients showed higher RCN3 in acute/subacute OP versus stable IPF and controls, and consistently our animal time course places RCN3 early (day 2) and canonical mediators (TGFβ1, FGF2 and CTGF) later (day 7–14). These findings support that RCN3 is an early epithelial injury signal that contributes to the initiation of the injury–repair process. We note that the BALF analysis is exploratory and stage-anchored in a small cohort and should not be interpreted as a marker of progressive fibrosis; larger, stage-defined cohorts (early IPF, silicosis, post-ARDS, PASC) are needed to test its relevance to disease progression.
RCN3 was originally characterized as an ER chaperone in the secretory pathway, facilitating protein maturation and secretion (e.g., PACE4 and surfactant proteins), contributing to epithelial and fibroblast homeostasis in pulmonary development and injury [ – ]. While our earlier publications emphasized its intracellular roles in ER homeostasis, the present study defines a secreted, stress-responsive paracrine role of RCN3 beyond the intracellular functions. Epithelial-derived RCN3 is exported via a glycosylation-dependent ER–Golgi pathway, acts through the engagement of TGFβR1 to activate canonical Smad2/3 signaling, and is validated by in vivo target engagement. Temporally, RCN3 precedes canonical profibrotic mediators (e.g., TGFβ1, FGF, and CTGF) in BALF following ALI, highlighting its early-window translational potential.
According to secretion routes, prior in-vitro work by Tsuji et al. described the chaperone-associated secretion of RCN3 accompanying the secretion and maturation of PACE4, in which RCN3 functions as a chaperone protein by selectively binding to the PACE4 precursor protein and a portion of extracellular RCN3 was subsequently processed by secretory PACE4 [ ]. Beyond the chaperone-associated release and its known intracellular functions, our study delineates a stress-responsive, regulated secreted pool of RCN3 that links epithelial injury to fibroblast TGFβR1-Smad2/3 signaling. Importantly, we do not infer physiological dominance of regulated export over chaperone-associated release. Quantitative partitioning of secretion routes under defined epithelial stressors will be addressed in future studies.
Our study further establishes that stress-responsive RCN3 secretion occurs through the ER-Golgi pathway and is critically dependent on N-glycosylation at N140, and the deglycosylation mutant was barely exported. Loss of N140 glycosylation leads to ER retention, GRP78 induction, and loss of fibroblast-activating capacity in conditioned medium, thereby linking the N-glycan to the generation of a bioactive secreted species. Whether N-glycosylation is also required for receptor engagement upon exogenous application remains to be determined using deglycosylated or purified mutants.
TGFβ1 signaling is critical in both physiologic wound healing and pathologic fibrosis. TGFβ1 binds to TGFβR2, which recruits TGFβR1 to form a heterotetrametric signaling complex that activates downstream canonical (Smad3) or non-canonical (AKT, ERK, STAT3) signaling pathways [ ]. Our RNA-seq, pathway enrichment, p-Smad3, SBE-reporter, and nuclear translocation data support selective activation of canonical TGFβ1-Smad3 signaling by RCN3, with upregulation of TGFβ1 and TGFβR1/2 that is SIS3-sensitive, consistent with Smad-dependent amplification. Furthermore, co-immunoprecipitation shows association of RCN3 with TGFβR1 (not TGFβR2); either TGFβR1 inhibition or knockdown blunts RCN3-induced Smad3 phosphorylation. BLI assay indicates a concentration-dependent interaction between RCN3 and TGFβR1 with a KD of 26.91 nM, indicative of moderate affinity. Docking analysis suggests RCN3 residues 60–70 and 80–93 as a putative interface region. Although BLI and co-IP support TGFβR1 engagement consistent with functional dependence, they do not exclude the possibility of indirect association within a multiprotein complex. Definitive validation of direct binding and precise interface mapping will require site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted RCN3 patches combined with high-resolution structural determination (e.g., cryo-EM). Given that TGFβ1–TGFβR2 binding exhibits sub-nanomolar affinity (KD ≈ 0.1 nM) [ ], whereas RCN3–TGFβR1 interaction is in the tens-of-nanomolar range (KD =26.91 nM). The observed absence of additivity upon co-stimulation may reflect transient TGFβR1 occlusion, receptor-complex stoichiometric constraints, or rapid negative regulation. These possibilities should be systematically evaluated using order-of-addition experiments and comprehensive two-dimensional dose–response matrices.
Temporally, RCN3 levels in BALF from ALI mice peaked at day 2 and returned to baseline by day 7 post-LPS exposure, whereas TGFβ1 levels peaked at day 7, indicating that RCN3 release precedes TGFβ1 during injury-repair. In fibroblasts, both rhRCN3 and CM from RCN3-overexpressing cells upregulated TGFβ1 and TGFβR1/2, suggesting that the early RCN3 pool likely enhances TGFβ signaling. This amplification is consistent with previously described positive-feedback loops in canonical TGF-β signaling [ ]. Of note, although RCN3-driven upregulation of TGFβ1 and TGFβRs is Smad3-dependent, this does not necessarily imply direct Smad3 binding to their promoters. Whether this regulation involves direct Smad-complex occupancy at TGFβ1/TGFβR loci or occurs indirectly will be addressed in future studies using ChIP-qPCR and ChIP-seq.
In vivo, the intratracheal administration of rmRCN3 caused obvious pulmonary fibrosis without significant inflammatory response. Although this experiment is pharmacological gain-of-function, intended to test sufficiency and not to model physiological concentrations, these results indicate that RCN3 is a profibrotic paracrine factor rather than an inflammatory activator. Conversely, intratracheal neutralization of RCN3 after LPS-ALI significantly attenuated fibrosis at day 7 post-LPS, whereas by day 14 fibrosis had largely resolved with no difference between groups. These observations indicate that RCN3 neutralization acts primarily within an early post LPS-induced injury window, consistent with its peak secretion on day 2. Future studies employing additional clinically relevant lung injury models (e.g., ventilator-induced lung injury, abdominal sepsis and bleomycin), delayed antibody administration, and later endpoints (days 21–28) will be required to test the generalizability of these observations and the potential efficacy of RCN3 neutralization against established or progressive fibrosis. In addition, while we document on-target engagement (BALF RCN3 depletion) within the effective window, we did not perform a comprehensive pharmacological characterization (pharmacokinetics, biodistribution and potential off-target effects). These analyses will be addressed during future therapeutic optimization.

## Conclusions
Secreted RCN3 is a stress-inducible, early epithelial paracrine mediator that links epithelial injury to fibroblast activation in post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis. RCN3 is released via an N140-glycosylation–dependent ER–Golgi pathway and rises earlier than TGFβ1 following LPS-ALI. Secreted RCN3 engages TGFβR1 to activate canonical Smad2/3 signaling in fibroblasts, thereby promoting myofibroblast differentiation. In vivo, recombinant RCN3 is sufficient to elicit lung fibrosis, whereas intratracheal neutralization markedly attenuates the initial fibrotic response following LPS-induced lung injury. Together, these data support secreted, stress-responsive RCN3 as a potential early-window therapeutic target in post-ALI pulmonary fibrosis.

## Limitations
Our present data do not establish: (1) the relative contributions of regulated versus chaperone-associated RCN3 release during epithelial injury; (2) structural-level proof of direct RCN3–TGFβR1 binding; (3) efficacy in established disease and generalization beyond LPS-ALI; (4) primary epithelial validation of secretion triggers and crosstalk; (5) the functional significance of RCN3 glycosylation for receptor binding or signaling capacity; (6) comprehensive antibody pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and potential off-target profiling.
Future studies will address interface mutagenesis and structural mapping, quantify secretion routes under defined epithelial stressors, validate these findings in human primary alveolar epithelial cells (hPAEpCs or iPSC-AT2), and extend investigations to broader etiologies with delayed dosing and day 21–28 endpoints, alongside comprehensive pharmacological profiling during therapeutic optimization.
