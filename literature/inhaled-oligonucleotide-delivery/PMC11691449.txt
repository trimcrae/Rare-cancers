## Abstract
Parkinson’s disease is primarily caused by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra compacta. Ferroptosis, a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, plays a vital role in the death of dopaminergic neurons. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in dopaminergic neurons have not yet been completely elucidated. NADPH oxidase 4 is related to oxidative stress, however, whether it regulates dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis remains unknown. The aim of this study was to determine whether NADPH oxidase 4 is involved in dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis, and if so, by what mechanism. We found that the transcriptional regulator activating transcription factor 3 increased NADPH oxidase 4 expression in dopaminergic neurons and astrocytes in an 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6 tetrahydropyridine-induced Parkinson’s disease model. NADPH oxidase 4 inhibition improved the behavioral impairments observed in the Parkinson’s disease model animals and reduced the death of dopaminergic neurons. Moreover, NADPH oxidase 4 inhibition reduced lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation in the substantia nigra of the Parkinson’s disease model animals. Mechanistically, we found that NADPH oxidase 4 interacted with activated protein kinase C α to prevent ferroptosis of dopaminergic neurons. Furthermore, by lowering the astrocytic lipocalin-2 expression, NADPH oxidase 4 inhibition reduced 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6 tetrahydropyridine-induced neuroinflammation. These findings demonstrate that NADPH oxidase 4 promotes ferroptosis of dopaminergic neurons and neuroinflammation, which contribute to dopaminergic neuron death, suggesting that NADPH oxidase 4 is a possible therapeutic target for Parkinson’s disease.

## Introduction
The main neuropathology of Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra compacta (SNc), which causes progressive worsening of PD symptoms (Guo et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023). While many potential mechanisms for the death of dopaminergic neurons in patients with PD have been examined, the underlying molecular mechanism has not yet been fully elucidated (Jankovic and Tan, 2020).
Previous studies have shown increased iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation in the SNc of patients with PD and animal models of PD (Zucca et al., 2017; Angelova et al., 2021). These features are characteristic of a novel form of regulated iron-dependent cell death called ferroptosis that involves excessive lipid peroxidation and disturbance of antioxidant systems (Dixon et al., 2012). Moreover, evidence has shown that ferroptosis is connected to several human disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases (Karuppagounder et al., 2018; Alim et al., 2019; Bao et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b). Because tumor cell lines (e.g., neuroblastoma) serve as in vitro models of PD, dopaminergic neuronal death in PD was long thought to be mediated only by apoptosis (Ziv et al., 2001). However, with increasing use of primary cultures and the discovery of other forms of cell death, it has been suggested that ferroptosis plays a significant role in dopaminergic neuronal death in PD (Galluzzi et al., 2018; Mahoney-Sánchez et al., 2021). Notably, ferroptosis has been linked to dopaminergic neuronal death in several PD models, as cell death was successfully reversed by treatment with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a classic ferroptosis inhibitor) (Do Van et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2021; Mahoney-Sanchez et al., 2022). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in PD remain largely unexplored.
NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) is a member of the NOX family of proteins, which are distributed extensively throughout the central nervous system (Ma et al., 2017; Boonpraman and Yi, 2024). The H2O2 produced by NOX4 is an important substrate for the Fenton reaction, which is a crucial step in ferroptosis (Jiang et al., 2021). Emerging evidence has demonstrated an association between abnormal NOX4 expression and neurodegeneration. In the APP/PS1 mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), NOX4 inhibition by GLX351322 prevented synaptic and memory impairment by reducing amyloid levels and oxidative stress in the hippocampus (Tao et al., 2021). Another team reported significantly increased NOX4 expression in the cortical astrocytes of patients with AD and APP/PS1 mice (Park et al., 2021). Moreover, NOX4 inhibition or deletion alleviated neuronal injury after traumatic brain injury and intracerebral hemorrhage in vivo (Ma et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2020). Notably, earlier studies showed that NOX1 and NOX2 negatively affect PD pathogenesis (Wu et al., 2003; Choi et al., 2012; Ma et al., 2017). Recently, Boonpraman et al. (2023) reported that NOX4 promotes astrocytic ferroptosis in the hippocampus in a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6 tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD model. They found that the astrocytic NOX4 regulated the expression of myeloperoxidase and osteopontin, which induced mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation. However, the role of NOX4 in dopaminergic neurons remains unknown.
Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore whether NOX4 inhibition alleviates the loss of dopaminergic neurons by inhibiting ferroptosis in an MPTP-induced PD model.

## Methods

## Ethics statement
The Institutional Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine approved all experiments involving animals on December 29, 2022 (approval No. AIRB-2022-0240), and all animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th ed., National Research Council, 2011).

## Animals and drugs
One hundred and fifty specific pathogen–free male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, weight 20–25 g) were purchased from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China, license No. SCXK (Hu) 2022-0004) and housed five to a cage in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment under a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to drinking water and food.
Because male mice are more sensitive to MPTP than female mice (Yu et al., 2023), we induced subacute PD in male mice only. The mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 30 mg/kg/d MPTP (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat# M0896) once a day for 5 consecutive days; the control mice were injected with saline. Starting on day 6 of the experiment, the mice were injected i.p. with the selective NOX4 antagonist GLX351322 (5 mg/kg/d; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA, Cat# HY-100111) every 24 hours for 5 days. All mice were subjected to behavioral tests and sacrificed on day 11, as shown in Additional Figure 1A.

## Behavioral tests
Mice underwent 3 days of behavioral training, and their motor coordination was tested by rotarod test on day 11 (Zhao et al., 2021). Briefly, each mouse was placed on a rotating device (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain, Cat# LE8205) whose speed gradually increased from 4 to 40 r/min over 300 seconds, and the latency to fall was recorded. All mice were tested three times at 20-minute intervals. Results are shown as the average value from three trials.
For the open field test (Zhao et al., 2021), the mice were allowed to acclimate to the testing room for 1 hour before testing. Subsequently, each mouse was placed in the center of a cubic box under bright lighting for 5 minutes. The movements of each mouse in 5 minutes were recorded using SMART video tracking software (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA).

## Cell culture, treatment, and transfection
MN9D and PC12 cells are often used to simulate human dopaminergic neurons (Bai et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021). Mouse dopaminergic neuron MN9D cells (Cat# BNCC359140, RRID: CVCL_M067) were obtained from the BeNa Culture Collection (Henan, China). The cells were grown in high-glucose DMEM from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The MN9D cells were treated with or without GLX351322 (5 µM; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-100111), bisindolylmaleimide I (BIM; protein kinase C [PKC] inhibitor, 5 µM; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-13867), erastin (5 µM, MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-15763), and 1-methy-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+, 0.5 mM, Sigma, Cat# D048) for 24 hours.
Rat pheochromocytoma PC12 cells (Cat# TCR 9, RRID: CVCL_0481), which are often used as an in vitro PD model, were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PC12 cells were treated with or without 0.5 mM MPP+ for 24 hours.
Sprague–Dawley rats (SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.) were anesthetized by 1% isoflurane inhalation on postpartum day 1–3, and astrocytes were extracted from their brains, as previously described (Wang et al., 2021). First, the newborn rat cerebral cortices were collected and incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes in 0.25% trypsin. The dissociated cells were then cultured in DMEM medium with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS. Then, cells were seeded into a poly-D-lysine–coated T75 flask and incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C. The medium was changed every 3–5 days. On day 10, the cells had reached confluence and were shaken at 200 r/min overnight to remove oligodendrocytes and microglia. The remaining adherent cells, consisting of highly purified astrocytes, were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cheng et al., 2020). The astrocytes were then treated for 24 hours with or without 0.5 mM MPP+.

## Immunofluorescence
Mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with 1% sodium pentobarbital (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Lianyungang, China) at a dose of 100 mg/kg and perfused with PBS, followed by perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were quickly harvested, placed in a 4% PFA solution at 4°C for 24 hours, and then dehydrated in a 30% sucrose solution for at least 48 hours. After the brains had sunk to the bottom of the tube, indicating complete dehydration, they were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and serially sliced into 30-µm coronal sections using a cryostat microtome (Leica CM1950). After blocking and permeabilization, the brain slices were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies ( ). The following day, the brain slices were incubated with secondary antibodies ( ) for 1 hour at room temperature. Then, the slices were washed thrice and stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Finally, images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6B, Wetzlar, Germany) or a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8).
 Immunofluorescence analysis of MN9D and PC12 cells was conducted after MPP+ treatment. The MN9D and PC12 cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 minutes at room temperature, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked using 5% BSA for 1 hour, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight ( ). The next day, the cells were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, washed thrice, and stained with DAPI (Beyotime). Finally, images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8).

## Immunofluorescence image analysis
TH-positive dopaminergic neurons were quantified in six sections of the SNc using the unbiased stereological cell count method (Bido et al., 2021). Images were acquired using an upright fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6B), and cells were counted using ImageJ software 1.52v (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012)
Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive cells, IBA-1-positive cells, and lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) and GFAP double-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software (Neal et al., 2020). Cell counts were obtained from at least three different sections from each group. The percentage of double-labeled cells was then calculated by dividing the number of GFAP and Lcn2 double-labeled cells by the number of GFAP-positive cells.

## Quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was isolated from MN9D cells and mouse substantia nigra (SN) tissue using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary deoxyribonucleic acid was synthesized using 5X Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using 2X SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) and a StepOnePlusTM Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used in this experiment are listed in . Except for interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), gene-specific primers for all other genes designed using ORIGENE (https://www.origene.com.cn/). The IL-1β primer sequence was reported earlier (Fu et al., 2021), and the GAPDH primer was sourced from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).
 

## Western blot assay
Brain tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes and centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 20 minutes. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were mixed with loading buffer and denatured at 100°C for 10 minutes. Then, 20 µg protein for each sample was subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% milk at room temperature for 1 hour and incubated with the primary antibodies ( ) overnight. The next day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (Huabio, Hangzhou, China) at room temperature for 1 hour. The protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the immunoblot images were examined using ImageJ.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was conducted as described previously (Ma et al., 2021). First, MN9D cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 minutes and lysed on ice for 10 minutes. The lysates were then sonicated to obtain DNA fragments with a mean length of 200–500 bp. The lysates were incubated with 20 µL protein G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 3 µg anti-transcription factor 3 (ATF3) antibody or 3 µg rabbit anti-IgG control antibody at 4°C overnight ( ). The next day, the beads were washed with low-salt wash buffer, high-salt wash buffer, LiCl buffer, and TE buffer (Abclonal, Wuhan, China, Cat# RK20258). The protein/DNA cross-linking was then reversed to release the DNA. After purification, the DNA was amplified by qPCR. The primers used for ChIP are listed in .
 

## Coimmunoprecipitation
Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2019b). First, MN9D cells or brain tissues were briefly lysed in 1% NP40 lysis buffer (Beyotime) at 4°C for 30 minutes. Then, 2 µg anti-NOX4 antibody or 2 µg rabbit anti-IgG control antibody ( ) were incubated with 20 µL protein G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 1 hour. The cell or tissue lysates were mixed with antibody-coated magnetic beads at 4°C overnight. The following day, the magnetic beads were washed thrice with PBS. The proteins were eluted from the beads by boiling in 1X SDS-PAGE sample buffer and analyzed by immunoblotting, as described earlier in this section.

## Proximity ligation assay
Proximity ligation assay (PLA) was conducted using Duolink® reagents (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MN9D cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, and then incubated at 4°C overnight with paired primary antibodies ( ). Following a buffer wash, secondary antibodies conjugated with oligonucleotides (anti-rabbit PLUS and anti-mouse MINUS) were applied. The PLA negative control experiment was labeled one antibody and IgG to rule out nonspecific binding of the antibody used in the experiment. The images were detected by laser scanning confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SP8).

## Transmission electron microscopy
MN9D cells and brain tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight. The samples were then refixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (TED PELLA, Redding, CA, USA) for 1 hour, stained with 2% uranyl acetate (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) for 30 minutes, and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series. The samples were then embedded in 100% acetone for 2 hours and sliced into 90-nm ultrathin sections. Next, the samples were stained with 4% uranyl acetate (Sigma) and 1% lead citrate (Sigma). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai G2 Spirit 120 kV, Netherlands) was used to detect the samples’ ultrastructure.

## Perls’ iron staining
Perls’ iron staining was performed as previously described (Song et al., 2021). Briefly, brain sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 5 minutes and washed with ddH2O for 30 seconds. Following three PBS washes, the brain slices were submerged in 2% HCl-potassium ferrocyanide (Macklin, Shanghai, China). The brain sections were then immersed in 1% hydrogen peroxide and 99% methanol (Sinopharm, Beijing, China) for 20 minutes to remove endogenous peroxidase activity. The results were detected using an Olympus x61 microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was measured using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8; APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA). Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. After treatment with MPP+ or erastin, 100 µL medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by 2 hours of incubation. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

## Transfection
ATF3 knockdown in MN9D cells was performed using RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an ATF3-specific siRNA (RIBOBIO, Guangzhou, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 pmol siRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM medium, then mixed with 1.5 µL RNAiMAX. This mixture was added to MN9D cells in a 12-well cell culture plate for 48 hours. The siRNA target sequence is shown in .
 

## Measurement of lipid peroxidation and ferrous iron
MN9D cells were incubated with 1 µM C11-BODIPY 581/591 fluorescent probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D3861) for 30 minutes. The cells were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DM4000), fluorescent cells were quantified using ImageJ, and the extent of lipid peroxidation was evaluated by calculating the green-to-red fluorescence ratio.
Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (Beyotime, S0131), 4-HNE levels (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, H268-1-2), superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels (Beyotime, S0101), glutathione/glutathione disulfide (GSH/GSSG) ratio (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan, Cat# G263), and ferrous iron levels (Dojindo, I291) were measured using kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For the MDA assay, SN tissues or cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was harvested. MDA solution was added to the standard and samples, the mixtures were heated at 100°C for 15 minutes and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes, and the MDA level in the supernatant was measured by reading the absorbance at 532 nm using a microplate reader. For the 4-HNE assay, SN tissues or cells were lysed in lysis buffer and centrifuged, and the supernatant was harvested by centrifugation, added to a 4-HNE conjugate–coated plate, and incubated for 10 minutes. Then, avidin-horse radish peroxidase and color-substrate solution were added to the plate, which was reincubated for 30 minutes. Finally, the substrate solution was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader. For the SOD assay, SN tissues or cells were lysed in lysis buffer, and the supernatant was harvested via centrifugation. Then, working solution was added to the samples, and the mixture was heated at 37°C for 30 minutes. Finally, the SOD levels were detected by reading the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. To determine the GSH/GSSG ratio, 5% 5-sulfosalicylic acid dihydrate was used to remove proteins from the sample. The samples were then centrifuged at 8000 × g at 4°C for 10 minutes, and the GSH and GSSG levels in the supernatant were measured by detecting absorbance at 405 nm using a microplate reader. For the ferrous iron assay, assay buffer was added to the SN tissues, and the mixture was homogenized by ultrasonication for 5 minutes and centrifuged at 16,000 × g at 4°C for 10 minutes. The ferrous iron level in the supernatant was measured by adding 100 µL probe solution to each standard and sample, heating at 37°C for 1 hour, and detecting absorbance at 593 nm using a microplate reader.

## Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as mean ± SEM for at least n = 3. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while differences among more than two groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for significance.

## Results

## NOX4 participates in the progression of 1-methyl-4-phenyl-

## 1,2,3,6 tetrahydropyridine-induced Parkinson’s disease
To investigate whether NOX4 is involved in PD progression, we first explored NOX4 expression levels in the SN and striatum of mice treated with MPTP. Our results showed that NOX4 expression in the SN (P = 0.0129) and striatum (P = 0.0228) was increased in MPTP-treated mice compared with the control group ( A–D). Next, we conducted an immunofluorescence assay to determine which cell type in the SN region expresses NOX4 and to further detect changes in NOX4 expression between the MPTP and control groups. Notably, we found that NOX4 was expressed mostly in dopaminergic neurons, and at low levels in astrocytes, in the MPTP group ( E and Additional Figure 1A). Moreover, NOX4 expression levels were elevated in dopaminergic neurons (P = 0.0073) and astrocytes (P = 0.0017) in the MPTP group compared with the control group ( F and G). Consistent with the in vivo results, NOX4 expression was also increased in MN9D cells (P = 0.027) (mouse dopaminergic neuron cells) and primary astrocytes after MPP+ treatment compared with control group (P = 0.0107; H–K). These results suggest that NOX4 may be involved in PD progression.
 To directly evaluate the role of NOX4 in PD development, we treated mice with GLX351322, a selective NOX4 antagonist that crosses the blood-brain barrier (Additional Figure 1B). GLX351322 treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the expression of p22phox, which is essential for NOX4 activity, in the SN compared with the MPTP group (P < 0.0001; Additional Figure 1C and D), indicating that GLX351322 has strongly inhibits NOX4 in the brain. Next, we conducted tyrosine hydroxylase immunofluorescence and western blot (WB) assays and found that NOX4 inhibition ameliorated dopaminergic neuron loss in the SN (21%, P = 0.0424), decreased TH+ fiber intensity in the striatum (18%, P < 0.0435) ( J–L), and rescued TH protein levels in the SN (20%, P = 0.0452) and striatum (30%, P = 0.0469) in the MPTP-induced PD model ( L–N and Additional Figure 1E–G). Furthermore, compared with the MPTP group, the MPTP + GLX351322 group exhibited a greater fall latency in the rotarod test (P = 0.0456; O) and traveled farther during the open field test (P = 0.0233; P and Additional Figure 1H). These results demonstrate that NOX4 is involved in MPTP-induced PD progression.

## ATF3 binds to the NOX4 promotor and positively regulates NOX4 expression
ATF3 promotes intracellular H2O2 production (Lu et al., 2021); therefore, we speculated that ATF3 might mediate H2O2 production by regulating NOX4 expression in dopaminergic neurons. qPCR analysis showed that ATF3 (tissues: P = 0.0332; cells: P = 0.0009) and NOX4 mRNA (tissues: P = 0.0398; cells: P = 0.0367) levels increased in mouse SN tissue and MN9D cells after MPTP or MPP+ treatment compared with the control groups ( A and B). Consistent with this, immunofluorescence and WB assays showed that ATF3 (MN9D cells P = 0.0004; PC12 cells P = 0.0009) and NOX (MN9D cells P = 0.0068; PC12 cells P = 0.0048) protein levels were markedly increased in MN9D and PC12 cells subjected to MPP+ compared with the control groups ( C–H). Moreover, siRNA-mediated silencing of ATF3 in MN9D cells reduced NOX4 expression (si-Ctrl versus si-ATF3#1, P = 0.0228; si-Ctrl versus si-ATF3#2, P = 0.0458; I and J). These results suggest that ATF3 may upregulate NOX4 expression at the transcriptional level in MPP+/MPTP-induced PD.
 To further assess the possibility that ATF3 modulates NOX4 transcription, we analyzed the NOX4 promoter using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net) and found that the NOX4 promoter contains two potential ATF3 binding sites ( K). Notably, ChIP analysis showed that ATF3 directly bound to these two binding sites in the NOX4 promotor after MPP+ treatment ( L). These findings suggested that MPP+/MPTP treatment enhanced the expression of ATF3, which bound the NOX4 promoter region and promoted NOX4 transcription.

## NOX4 inhibition alleviates dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis in vivo
Although dopaminergic neuron ferroptosis in PD has been shown to primarily involve disruption of iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation, whether NOX4 can modulate iron metabolism or lipid peroxidation in the SN remained unclear. We first investigated the effect of NOX4 inhibition on the number of iron-positive cells in the SN by Perls’ iron staining. The result showed that there are more brown granules in the neuronal cytoplasm in the SN of PD model mice than control mice, and GLX351322 treatment substantially decreased the number of iron-positive cells compared with the MPTP group (P = 0.00328; A and B). Because ferrous iron, rather than ferric iron, is implicated in ferroptosis, we next assessed the ferrous iron content of the SN and found that it was much lower in the MPTP + GLX351322 group than in the MPTP group (P = 0.0021; C). To further explore the causes of changes in iron metabolism in the SN, we examined the expression of iron transporters in the SN and striatum, including the iron importer transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), the divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), the iron exporter ferroportin (FPN), and the ferrous iron storage protein ferritin heavy chain (FTH). Our results showed that TfR1, DMT1, and FTH expression in the SN (TfR1 P = 0.0441, DMT1 P =0.0031, FTH P = 0.0262) and the striatum (TfR1 P = 0.0451, DMT1 P = 0.0359, FTH P = 0.003) was increased in the MPTP group compared with the control group, while NOX4 inhibition by GLX351322 reversed this effect (SN: TfR1 P = 0.0443, DMT1 P = 0.0251, FTH P = 0.0175; striatum: TfR1 P = 0.0107, DMT1 P = 0.0239, FTH P = 0.0314). However, there was no significant difference in FPN expression among the three groups ( D–F). The changes in TfR1, DMT1, FTH, and FPN mRNA expression levels mirrored the changes seen at the protein level (Additional Figure 2A). We next performed TEM to assess neuronal ultrastructure. The results showed shrunken mitochondria and reduced mitochondrial cristae in MPTP-induced PD mice, and these effects were partially restored by NOX4 inhibition ( G). These data suggested that pharmacological inhibition of NOX4 mitigated iron accumulation in the SN by regulating iron transport.
 To further investigate the role of NOX4 in lipid peroxidation in a mouse model of PD, we used a dihydroethidium probe to examine ROS production in the SN and found that ROS levels in the SN were markedly increased in the MPTP group compared with the control group (P = 0.0003), while GLX351322 treatment reduced ROS levels to a level comparable to that seen in the MPTP group (P = 0.0071; Additional Figure 2B and C). Next, we examined the expression of biomarkers of ferroptosis, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), and ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1). WB analysis showed that ACSL4 expression was upregulated (SN P = 0.0268, striatum P = 0.0133) and GPX4 expression was downregulated (SN P = 0.0065, striatum P = 0.008) in the MPTP group compared with the control group, in both the SN and the striatum. These changes were reversed by treatment with GLX351322 (SN ACSL4 P = 0.0494, GPX4 P = 0.0352; striatum ACSL4 P = 0.031, GPX4 P = 0.0418; Figure
3H, I, Additional Figure 2D and E). Notably, the expression of another important ferroptosis suppressor protein, FSP1, did not differ significantly among the three groups (Additional Figure 2F and G). Additionally, levels of the lipid peroxidation byproducts MDA (P < 0.0001) and 4-HNE (P = 0.0012) were elevated in the MPTP group compared with the control group. However, MDA (P = 0.0186) and 4-HNE (P = 0.0354) expression levels decreased after GLX351322 treatment in the MPTP-induced PD group ( J and K). In contrast, the SOD level (P = 0.0488) and GSH/GSSG ratio (P = 0.0002) were downregulated in the MPTP group compared with the control group, and GLX351322 treatment restored both the SOD level (P = 0.0349) and the GSH/GSSG ratio (P = 0.0104; L and M). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of NOX4 alleviated MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis by regulating iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation in vivo.

## The NOX4 antagonist GLX351322 mitigates 1-methy-4-phenylpyridinium induced ferroptosis in MN9D cells
Next, we explored the effect of the NOX4 antagonist GLX351322 on MPP+-induced ferroptosis in vitro. CCK-8 analysis showed that MPP+ and erastin (a ferroptosis inducer) decreased MN9D cell viability, and this effect was reversed by treatment with GLX351322 or Fer-1 (a classic ferroptosis inhibitor) ( A and B). To assess lipid peroxidation we used a C11-BODIPY fluorescent probe that converts from red to green upon lipid peroxidation. The green/red ratio was significantly increased in the MPP+ group compared with the control group (P = 0.0072) indicating an upregulation in lipid peroxidation after MPP+ treatment. Furthermore, treatment with GLX351322 (P = 0.0466) or Fer-1 (P = 0.0172) reduced the green/red ratio in the MPP+ group, indicating that NOX4 or ferroptosis inhibition reduced MPP+-lipid peroxidation in vitro ( C and D). TEM analysis showed that MPP+ treatment resulted in mitochondrial shrinkage and a reduction in mitochondrial cristae, while these changes were reversed by GLX351322 and Fer-1 in vitro ( E). Next, expression levels of the critical ferroptosis-related proteins GPX4 and ACSL4 were examined by WB. MPP+ treatment led to ACSL4 upregulation (P = 0.03) and GPX4 downregulation (P = 0.0022), while treatment with GLX351322 (ACSL4: P = 0.0303, GPX4: P = 0.0199) or Fer-1 (ACSL4: P = 0.0041, GPX4: P = 0.0049) reversed these changes in vitro ( F–H). Furthermore, levels of the lipid peroxidation byproducts MDA (P = 0.0042) and 4-HNE (P = 0.0123) were elevated in the MPP+ group compared with the control group. However, NOX4 inhibition reduced the levels of both MDA (P = 0.0483) and 4-HNE in vitro (P = 0.0173; I and J). The SOD level (P = 0.0074) and GSH/GSSG ratio (P < 0.0001) in MN9D cells were decreased in the MPP+ group compared with the control group, while NOX4 inhibition reversed these changes in vitro (SOD: P = 0.0400, GSH/GSSG ratio: P = 0.0327) ( K and L). In conclusion, these findings suggest that NOX4 inhibition alleviated MPP+-induced dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis by in vitro.
 

## The NOX4/PKCα axis modulates ferroptosis in vitro and in vivo Parkinson’s disease models
Previous studies have demonstrated that PKCα regulates ferroptosis induced by erastin in differentiated Lund human mesencephalic (LUHMES) cells, and that oxidative stress can activate PKC (Tuttle et al., 2009; Do Van et al., 2016). Therefore, we examined changes in p-PKCα and PKCα expression after treating the MPTP-induced PD model with GLX351322. WB analysis showed that p-PKCα protein expression was increased in the MPTP group compared with the control group (P = 0.0083), while NOX4 inhibition by GLX351322 reversed this change in vivo (P = 0.0159; A and B). Immunofluorescence staining showed colocalization of NOX4 and PKCα in MN9D cells (Additional Figure 3A), and Co-IP demonstrated a direct interaction between NOX4 and PKCα was observed in MN9D cells and mouse brains ( C and D). Furthermore, an in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA), which detects protein pairs located < 40 nm away from each other (Soderberg et al., 2006), showed a distinct interaction pattern between NOX4 and PKCα in MN9D cells ( E). No PLA signals were detected in MN9D cells under negative control conditions, when either of the two primary antibodies was omitted (Additional Figure 3B).
 To further explore the role of PKCα in MPP+-induced ferroptosis, we first examined cell viability and found that treatment with the PKC inhibitor BIM rescued MPP+-induced cell death ( F). Detection of ultrastructural changes by TEM showed that MPP+ treatment caused mitochondrial shrinkage and reduced mitochondrial cristae, while these changes were reversed by BIM treatment in vitro ( G). C11-BODIPY fluorescence analysis suggested that MPP+ increased intracellular lipid peroxidation in MN9D cells (P = 0.0069), and that this effect was reversed by BIM treatment (P = 0.0246) ( H and I). Moreover, PKC inhibition by BIM alleviated the MPP+-induced increase in 4-HNE (P = 0.0484) and MDA (P = 0.0204) levels and the decrease in SOD levels (P = 0.0049) and the GSH/GSSG ratio (P = 0.0209) in MN9D cells ( J–M). Taken together, these results suggest that NOX4 interacts with and activates PKCα, thereby inhibiting MPP+-induced ferroptosis in MN9D cells.

## NOX4 inhibition alleviates neuroinflammation in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease by downregulating astrocytic Lcn2 expression
Because NOX4 localizes partially to astrocytes, we hypothesized that NOX4 could be related to neuroinflammation in our PD mouse model. Therefore, we quantified microglia and astrocytes in the SN by immunofluorescence staining with IBA-1 for microglia and GFAP for astrocytes. The results showed that microglia (P = 0.0002) and astrocyte (P < 0.0001) counts increased in response to MPTP exposure, and that this effect was ameliorated by NOX4 inhibition in vivo (IBA-1: P = 0.0031, GFAP: P = 0.0016; A–D). We next examined IL-1β and TNFα mRNA levels and found that both (IL-1β, P = 0.0215; TNFα, P = 0.0196) were significantly upregulated in the MPTP group compared with the control group, while GLX351322 treatment reversed this effect in vivo (IL-1β, P = 0.0365; TNFα, P = 0.0347; E and F). These results revealed that NOX4 antagonist GLX351322 inhibited neuroinflammation in MPTP-induced PD models.
 Next, we assessed the localization and expression level of the iron-binding protein cytokine lipocalin-2 (Lcn2), which is associated with apoptotic cell death, cellular uptake of iron, and glial activation in the brain (Ferreira et al., 2015; Mann et al., 2022). Interestingly, we detected a significant increase in Lcn2 localization to GFAP-positive cells in the SN of the MPTP group compared with the control group (P < 0.0001), and this effect was reversed by GLX351322 treatment (P = 0.0239; G and H). Moreover, WB analysis showed that Lcn2 expression was upregulated in response to MPTP treatment (P = 0.0301) but returned to control levels after NOX4 inhibition in vivo (P = 0.0468; I and J). A previous study showed that Lcn2, which is secreted by astrocytes, is selectively toxic to neurons (Bi et al., 2013). Therefore, we asked whether dopaminergic neurons express the Lcn2 receptor (Lcn2R). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that Lcn2R localized to dopaminergic neurons (Additional Figure 4), suggesting that Lcn2 could be toxic to dopaminergic neurons in vivo. In conclusion, NOX4 activates neuroinflammation in MPTP-induced in vivo PD models through upregulating astrocytic Lcn2, which may directly induce dopaminergic neuron death.

## Discussion
In this study, we identified a crucial role for NOX4 in the pathogenesis of PD. First, NOX4 expression was increased in dopaminergic neurons and astrocytes in an MPTP-induced mouse model of PD. Second, NOX4 inhibition mitigated the dopaminergic neuron loss and improved behavioral performance in the MPTP-induced PD model. Third, NOX4 expression was mediated by ATF3 in dopaminergic neurons. Fourth, NOX4 interacted with and activated PKCα to promote dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis. Finally, NOX4 promoted neuroinflammation by upregulating astrocytic secretion of Lcn2.
NOX4 belongs to the family of NADPH oxidases, which are widely expressed throughout the central nervous system. Recent studies have demonstrated that NOX4 causes neuronal and astrocytic ferroptosis in animal models of intracerebral hemorrhage and AD, respectively (Park et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2023). After intracerebral hemorrhage, NOX4 levels increased, and knocking down NOX4 prevented neuronal ferroptosis by restoring GSH levels. Similarly, NOX4 expression was upregulated in astrocytes in patients with AD and in an APP/PS1 animal model of AD, and NOX4 overexpression in human astrocytes decreased GSH levels. Moreover, previous studies have reported that NOX1 and NOX2 promote PD progression (Wu et al., 2003; Choi et al., 2012). However, little is known about the role of NOX4 in dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis; therefore, we systematically explored the function of NOX4 in a PD model in the current study.
We found that NOX4 expression was significantly elevated in dopaminergic neurons and astrocytes in an MPTP/MPP+-induced PD model. These data suggest that NOX4 may be involved in PD pathogenesis. To directly evaluate the role of NOX4 in PD development, we introduced a selective NOX4 antagonist, GLX351322, which has been reported to protect against synaptic and memory dysfunction in APP/PS1 mice (Tao et al., 2021). We found that GLX351322-mediated NOX4 inhibition reduced dopaminergic neuron death in the SNc and improved behavioral dysfunction in MPTP-induced PD mice. These results demonstrate that NOX4 promotes PD pathogenesis.
To understand the mechanism underlying the increase in NOX4 expression in dopaminergic neurons after exposure to MPTP or MPP+, we first examined NOX4 expression at the mRNA level. Given that NOX4 mRNA expression was elevated and that the transcriptional factor ATF3 promotes intracellular H2O2 synthesis, we hypothesized that ATF3 could promote NOX4 transcription and induce H2O2 production. Our results showed that ATF3 knockdown significantly decreased NOX4 expression, while ChIP result showed that ATF3 directly binds to two sites in the NOX4 promotor to regulate NOX4 transcription. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that ATF3 promotes ferroptosis by transcriptionally repressing SLC7A11, which is important for GSH synthesis (Wang et al., 2020; Koppula et al., 2021). This could help explain why GSH levels were reduced in the PD model.
Ferroptosis is a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation (Liu et al., 2023b). Studies have implicated ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, PD, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, and Friedreich’s ataxia (Do Van et al., 2016; Stockwell et al., 2017; La Rosa et al., 2020; Bao et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023). In addition, earlier studies showed that lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation are increased in the SN in patients with PD and in PD animal models (Zucca et al., 2017; Angelova et al., 2021). Here we found that pharmacological suppression of NOX4 increased dopaminergic GSH levels and decreased lipid peroxidation to alleviate dopaminergic neuronal death in an MPTP-induced PD model. Similarly, Boonpraman et al. found that NOX4 knockout can alleviate astrocytic lipid peroxidation in the hippocampus in an MPTP-induced PD model (Boonpraman et al., 2023). A previous study showed that H2O2 stimulates TfR1 expression at both the post-transcriptional and translational level (Andriopoulos et al., 2007). Additionally, H2O2 can regulate the expression of hepcidin, which reduces iron accumulation in the brain by downregulating the expression of iron transport proteins (Millonig et al., 2012; Du et al., 2015). In our study, we found that NOX4 regulates iron metabolism in the SN by altering the expression of iron transporters, including TfR1, DMT1, and FTH. However, to our surprise, FPN expression in the SN was not regulated by NOX4. Previous studies have reported that NOX4 regulates the expression of hepcidin in liver cells (Millonig et al., 2012; Silva et al., 2018), where it binds to FPN and renders it inactive. This could be a tissue-specific effect. NOX4 and PKCα play a role in lipid peroxidation (Li et al., 2011; Park et al., 2021; Boonpraman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a; Xie et al., 2023). Similar to previous studies, we found that NOX4 inhibition reversed the changes in the expression levels of the lipid peroxidation-related proteins GPX4 and ACSL4 seen in the MPTP-induced PD model. However, there was no difference in the expression levels of FSP1, another potent ferroptosis suppressor protein, among the three groups. These findings suggest that GPX4, rather than FSP1, plays a vital anti-oxidant role in dopaminergic neuronal ferroptosis.
PKCα is a serine/threonine kinase that modulates numerous cellular functions, such as apoptosis, proliferation, synaptic plasticity, and memory formation (Mishra and Dey, 2021). A previous study showed that PKCα knockdown inhibited erastin-induced ferroptosis in differentiated LUHMES cells (Do Van et al., 2016). In contrast, another team reported that PKCα inhibition sensitizes neuroblastoma stem cells to etoposide by stimulating ferroptosis (Monteleone et al., 2021). These results suggest that PKCα may play different roles in ferroptosis in different cells. In our study, we found that NOX4 interacted with and activated PKCα, while PKCα inhibition alleviated MPP+-induced cell death by preventing ferroptosis in MN9D cells. Moreover, we found that PKCα is significantly linked to lipid peroxidation in dopaminergic neurons, which is similar to its function in diabetic embryopathy (Li et al., 2011). Notably, a recent study demonstrated that AD-associated gain-of-function variants in PKCα drive cognitive decline in a mouse model (Lorden et al., 2022). Taken together, these lines of evidence suggest that PKCα may be a promising therapeutic target for PD.
We found that NOX4 plays a similar role in the pathogenesis of PD as it does in AD. Iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation are associated with both PD and AD progression. Our results suggest that NOX4 inhibition decreases iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation (Masaldan et al., 2019; Ashraf et al., 2020). Furthermore, activation of PKCα, which is downstream of NOX4, promotes the progression of both PD and AD (Lordén et al., 2022). Because of these similarities, NOX4 could be a shared therapeutic target for two neurodegenerative diseases: PD and AD. However, NOX4-mediated ferroptosis of dopaminergic neurons in PD may rely on a disease-specific mechanism. SNCA is considered to be an autosomal dominant pathogenic gene causing PD and has been demonstrated to be closely associated with ferroptosis of dopaminergic neurons (Angelova et al., 2020; Mahoney-Sanchez et al., 2022). A previous study reported that H2O2 promotes oligomerization of the α-synuclein A53T mutant (Kang and Kim, 2003). Additionally, GKT136901, a NOX1/4 inhibitor, prevents α-synuclein dimer formation (Schildknecht et al., 2014). A53T mutant α-synuclein increases intracellular ROS levels and promotes H2O2-induced cell death (Jiang et al., 2007). Therefore, we speculate that NOX4 and α-synuclein may mutually promote each other’s expression and contribute to dopaminergic neuron ferroptosis in PD.
Given that we observed NOX4 localization to astrocytes in the SN, we further investigated its role in astrocytes. Previous studies have reported that NOX4 is closely associated with peripheral inflammation (Moon et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019a; Yoo et al., 2020). Therefore, we speculated that elevated NOX4 expression in astrocytes may promote the neuroinflammation in the MPTP-induced PD model. We examined the GFAP- and IBA1-positive cells and cytokines in the SN. We found that the numbers of GFAP-positive cells and IBA1-positive cells, as well as IL-1β and TNFα mRNA levels, decreased after NOX4 inhibition compared with the MPTP group. NOX-induced oxidative stress plays a vital role in regulating the expression of the iron-binding protein Lcn2 (Liu et al., 2022), and NOX1-generated ROS production drives Lcn2 expression in colonic epithelial cells (Makhezer et al., 2019). Moreover, NOX4 is the major isoform of NADPH oxidases expressed by astrocytes (Cooney et al., 2013; Park et al., 2021). Therefore, we asked whether NOX4 inhibition would reduce Lcn2 expression in astrocytes in the MPTP-induced PD model. Our results showed that Lcn2 expression decreased in the SN after GLX351322 treatment compared with the MPTP group. Lcn2 is a secreted protein that has been reported to affect brain iron metabolism, neuroinflammation, and apoptosis (Ferreira et al., 2015; Lim et al., 2021; Mann et al., 2022). Thus, NOX4 may promote neuroinflammation by upregulating astrocytic secretion of Lcn2.
Moreover, we found that the Lcn2 receptor, Lcn2R, was expressed by dopaminergic neurons. Previous studies have demonstrated that Lcn2 secreted by astrocytes is selectively toxic to neurons and mediates iron import into neurons (Bi et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2021). Therefore, we hypothesized that Lcn2 might be directly toxic to dopaminergic neurons and interfere with their iron metabolism, which could help explain the loss of dopaminergic neurons and aberrant iron metabolism observed in the SN in PD mice. Notably, recent studies have reported that Lcn2 has opposite effects on ferroptosis in different diseases (Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023a). Therefore, the effect of upregulated astrocytic Lcn2 expression on dopaminergic neuron ferroptosis in PD models deserves further study.
There are several limitations to our study. We showed that the inhibition of NOX4 by GLX351322 alleviated dopaminergic neuron loss and behavioral impairment in the MPTP-induced PD model. However, the effect of this treatment should be verified in other PD models, such as the 6-OHDA-induced PD model and the A53T model. Second, based on previous studies, we treated MPTP-induced PD mice with only 5 mg/kg per day of GLX351322. Future experiments should address whether there is a better therapeutic concentration of GLX351322 to treat PD. Third, although we found that NOX4 inhibition alleviated ferroptosis in MN9D cells treated with MPP+, future studies should explore the effect of GLX351322 on primary dopaminergic neurons.
In conclusion, we confirmed the significance of NOX4 in PD-related ferroptosis and neuroinflammation. Considering the close relationships among ferroptosis, neuroinflammation, and PD pathogenesis, NOX4 is a potential therapeutic target for PD.
