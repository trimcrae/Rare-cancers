## ABSTRACT
Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) transplantation is considered one of the most promising regenerative strategies for treating degenerative musculoskeletal diseases, yet its underlying therapeutic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we demonstrate that transplanted MSCs regulate apoptosis and DNA damage repair (DDR) in senescent nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) by releasing apoptotic extracellular vesicles (ApoEVs), thereby delaying the process of intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD). Mechanistically, we found that NPCs in degenerated discs exhibit abnormal subcellular localization of the deubiquitinase ubiquitin specific peptidase 5 (USP5), with excessive cytoplasmic retention leading to aberrant ubiquitination and degradation of the E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1). Following transplantation into the degenerative disc microenvironment, MSCs undergo extensive apoptosis in the short‐term and release ApoEVs enriched in highly acetylated USP5. These vesicles promote nuclear translocation of USP5 in NPCs, which stabilizes E2F1 by preventing its ubiquitin‐mediated degradation. This cascade reduces DNA damage and apoptosis in NPCs and enhances their functional activity. Overall, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which apoptotic donor MSCs exert therapeutic effects through intercellular communication, specifically by modulating recipient NPCs apoptosis and DDR pathways. This study underscores the critical role of donor cell apoptosis in the therapeutic efficacy of stem cell transplantation and provides new insights for optimizing regenerative medicine strategies.

## Materials and Methods
2.1

## Human Nucleus Pulposus (HNP) Tissue Samples
Clinical samples of HNP tissue were obtained from patients who underwent thoracolumbar fractures or acquired scoliosis. Informed consent of this study was obtained from all the participants. Magnetic resonance images (MRI) degree of disc degeneration was evaluated according to the Pfirrmann grading system. The research protocol using samples in this experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

2.2

## Isolation and Culture of NPCs and MSCs
The human NP tissues were isolated and cut into small pieces of 1 mm3, then digested in 0.2% type II collagenase (Gibco, USA) for 12 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. The obtained cells and partially digested tissues were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) twice and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and then were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with the F12 nutrient mixture and 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141C) at 37°C with 5% CO2. NPCs of passage 2–3 were used in subsequent experiments. The senescence cell model was established by exposing NPCs to tert‐Butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 100 µM; Sigma–Aldrich, A13926) for 12 h followed by replacing with normal medium in in vitro experiments. Sprague Dawley rats (2 weeks) were purchased from the Hunan SJA Laboratory animal Co., Ltd (Changsha, China). MSCs were harvested from the femoral bone marrow cavity when rats were euthanized by overdose of 2% (w:v) pentobarbital. The obtained bone marrow blood was cultured in MSC complete medium (Cyagen, USA) at 37°C with 5% CO2. Then, the culture medium was changed every 3 days. The cells were passaged at a 1: 3 ratios at 80%–90% confluence, and cells from the passage 2–3 were used in further experiments.

2.3

## Isolation and Characterization of ApoEVs
To isolate ApoEVs in vitro, MSCs were cultured in fresh medium (10% exosome‐depleted FBS, Gibco, USA) and treated with 200 nM staurosporine (STS, Cell Signalling Technology, 9953s) for 12 h. The conditioned medium was harvested and centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min to pellet non‐adherent cells. The supernatant was then subjected to a centrifugation step at 2000 × g for 10 min to pellet apoptotic bodies and cell debris. Subsequently, the supernatant was further centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C to pellet ApoEVs. The ApoEVs was washed twice and then resuspended in PBS for further use. The total protein content in ApoEVs was quantified using BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). The particle concentration and size of ApoEVs were measured by Flow NanoAnalyzer (Nanofcm, China). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan) was used to measure morphologies and contents of ApoEVs. The obtained ApoEVs were stored at −80 for later use.

2.4

## Internalization of ApoEVs Into NPCs In Vitro

To assess the internalization of ApoEVs by NPCs, NPCs were seeded in a six‐well plate and maintained at 37°C overnight. The pelleted ApoEVs were pre‐labelled with the 10 µM DiR dye (MCE, HY‐D1048) and then were co‐cultured with NPCs for 6 and 12 h, respectively. After fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, the cells were used for cytoskeleton staining (Beyotime, C2201S), and the cell nucleus were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002). The internalization of ApoEVs were observed by the laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000).

2.5

## DNA Damage Assay by Comet Assay
After the conditional pre‐processing, NPCs 1 × 106/mL were lysed and the DNA was de helix using Comet Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Electrophoresis was carried out with a voltage of 25 V for 20 min in the 4°C freezer. Each gel was then neutralized and stained with 20 µL Propidium Iodide solution in the dark for 15 min. The gels were observed using inverted phase contrast microscope and were analysed by CometScore 2.0 software. The DNA percentage in the tail and tail length were used as descriptors of DNA damage.

2.6

## Plasmid and siRNA Transfection and Lentiviral Transduction
The plasmids used in this study were purchased from Miaoling Biology (Wuhan, China). Full‐length E2F1‐WT and E2F1‐mutants (Δ1, Δ2 and Δ3) were cloned into the pLV3‐CMV‐HA‐C vectors. Full‐length USP5‐WT and USP5‐mutants (D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, C335A, K94R, K509R, K825R and USP5 NLS mutant) were cloned into the pLV3‐CMV‐Myc‐C vectors as indicated. Ub‐WT and Ub mutants (K11, K48, K48R and K63) were cloned into the pCDNA3.1‐CMV‐His‐C vector. These plasmids were transfected into HEK‐293T cells following the E‐Poly Transfection Reagent (D‐nano therapeutics, China) instructions. These plasmids were introduced into NPCs via virus transfection to achieve stably expression. The construction of lentiviral vectors, as well as plasmid packaging and purification, was outsourced to GeneChem (Shanghai, China). NPCs were infected with the lentivirus according to the manufacturer's instructions provided with the lentiviral transduction reagent (GeneChem). Chemical interventions and protein assays were conducted 48 h post‐ transfection. Knockdown of E2F1 and USP5 in NPCs was conducted by siRNAs transfection (Tsingke, China). Si‐E2F1, si‐USP5#1, si‐USP5#2 and control si‐NC were transfected using E‐Poly Transfection Reagent (D‐nano therapeutics, China) following to the manufacturer's protocols. The siRNA sequences were listed in Table .

2.7

## Immunoprecipitation‐Mass Spectrometry (IP‐MS) Analysis
To identify interacting proteins of E2F1, NPCs lysates were collected and immunoprecipitated with protein A/G magnetic beads conjugated to the corresponding antibodies. Mass spectrometry (MS) was performed by SpecAlly Life Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The resulting MS/MS raw data were analysed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). The mean and SD of the protein fold change were calculated, and the fold change of proteins beyond the cutoff line (>4) was identified as significant.

2.8

## RNA Sequencing (RNA‐Seq) Analysis
RNA samples were extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, USA). A cDNA library was constructed, followed by sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq platform. Differential gene expression analysis was carried out using the DESeq R package, with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.05 and |log2foldchange| > 1.5. Finally, enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed in R software using the clusterProfiler R package.

2.9

## Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay
To verify the protein–protein interactions, we performed Co‐IP using protein magnetic beads (HY‐K0201, HY‐K0206, MCE). Briefly, we collected the cell lysate after intervention and lysed them with RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Then, the protein lysates were coimmunoprecipitated with protein magnetic beads at 4°C overnight on a rotator. After washing with wash buffer twice, the proteins samples were separated from beads with 5× SDS loading buffer (Seven, SW163) at 95°C for 10 min and analysed with a Coomassie blue staining and Western blot assay.

2.10

## Western Blot Assay
Total protein or nuclear protein were extracted from cells by Whole Cell Protein Extraction Kit (Seven, China, SW) or Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China, P0027), and the protein concentration was measured by using the BCA protein assay kit (Seven, China, SW101). Then, protein sample of each group was subjected to the SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto the PVDF membrane. After blocked with 5% non‐fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against Caspase 3 (1:1000, R23315, Zen‐bio), cleaved‐caspase 3 (1:1000, R23727, Zen‐bio), Annexin V (1:1000, R22841, Zen‐bio), GAPDH (1:5000, 10494‐1‐AP, Proteintech), ACAN (1:1000, A8536, Abclonal), COL2A1 (1:2000, 28459‐1‐AP, Proteintech), MMP13 (1:1000, 18165‐1‐AP, Proteintech), Tubulin (1:10000, T40103, Abmart), E2F1 (1:2000, R24159, Zen‐bio), BCL2 (1:1000, 381702 Zen‐bio), BAX (1:1000, 380709, Zen‐bio), γH2AX (1:1000, AP0687, Abclonal), poly‐Ub (1:2000, 10201‐2‐AP, Proteintech), HA (1:5000, 51064‐2‐AP, Proteintech), Myc (1:2000, 60003‐2‐Ig, Proteintech), His (1:5000, 66005‐1‐Ig, Proteintech), USP5 (1:2000, 10473‐1‐AP, Proteintech), Ace‐lys (1:2000, YK0020, Immunoway), H3 (1:2000, 17168‐1‐AP, Proteintech), Alix (1:1000, YM8063, Immunoway), TSG101 (1:1000, DF8427, Affinity), CD9 (1:1000, AF5139, Affinity), HSP60 (1:2000, 15282‐1‐AP, Proteintech) and Annexin A (1:2000, 21990‐1‐AP, Proteintech) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with TBST (Tris‐buffered saline and 0.1% Tween 20), the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000, SA00001‐2, SA00001‐1, Proteintech) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system.

2.11

## IVDD Animal Model Induction
This animal experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Huazhong university of science and technology (No. 4097). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act. Sprague Dawley rats (6–8 weeks, male) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Hunan, China). Briefly, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% (w:v) pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). After sterilization with povidone iodine, coccygeal intervertebral discs (Co 6/7) were percutaneously punctured by a 21G needle at a depth of 5 mm, followed by rotation at 360° and holding for the 30 s. After 1 week, the experimental groups received weekly intradiscal injections of 2 µL PBS, MSCs (1 × 106/mL) or ApoEVs (50 µg/mL) for four consecutive weeks.

2.12

## MRI and X‐Rays Evaluation
After anesthetizing, the rats were positioned prone for MRI scans and x‐rays to observe signal and structural changes in the IVDs. Specifically, SD rats were maintained under anaesthesia using 2% isoflurane inhalation, after which they were carefully placed in the prone position for the imaging procedure. Sagittal T2‐weighted images were evaluated according to the Pfirrmann grade (Pfirrmann et al. ). The disc height index (DHI) was measured by Image J software (Han et al. ).

2.13

## Histological, Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence Analysis
The specimens were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified using a 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution, and embedded in paraffin. The samples were sectioned into 5‐µm thick slices and subjected to histological examination using haematoxylin–eosin (H&E), safranin O‐fast green (S‐O) and alcian blue staining techniques. The histological features were evaluated based on established histologic grading criteria (Norcross et al. ).
For immunofluorescence analysis, tissue sections or cell slide were prepared following the same protocol. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ACAN (1:200, A8536, Abclonal), COL2A1 (1:200, 28459‐1‐AP, Proteintech), MMP13 (1:200, 18165‐1‐AP, Proteintech), E2F1 (1:200, R24159, 201080, Zen‐bio), γH2AX (1:200, AP0687, Abclonal), USP5 (1:200, 10473‐1‐AP, Proteintech) and then probed with coraLite488‐conjugated or coraLite594‐conjugated anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibody (1:200, SA00013‐2, SA00013‐1, SA00013‐4, Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature.

2.14

## Statistical Analysis
All of the data were analysed by GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA) The quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data of multiple independent groups were analysed by one‐way ANOVA. Student's t‐test was used to analyse the differences between the two groups. p value < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

## Results
3.1

## Transplanted MSCs Underwent Apoptosis and Alleviated IVDD
MSCs have been extensively investigated in both preclinical studies and clinical trials for the treatment of degenerative musculoskeletal disorders. To further elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms underlying MSC transplantation, we established a rat model of IVDD induced by fine‐needle puncture of the coccygeal discs (Figure  ). Following intradiscal injection of MSCs, we performed MRI and x‐ray examinations to evaluate disc degeneration. Specifically, the Pfirrmann grading system and the DHI were used to quantitatively assess structural changes in the IVD after MSC treatment. As shown in Figure  , the MSC transplantation group showed significant improvements in NP signal intensity on T2‐weighted MRI images and IVD height after 4 weeks of treatment comparing with the PBS‐treated group. Histological analyses using H&E, S‐O and alcian blue staining revealed that the integrity of the IVD and the content of NP tissue in the MSC group were notably better than in the PBS group (Figures  and ). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining indicated that MSC transplantation partially mitigated disruptions in ECM degradation and synthesis within the IVD (Figure  ).
 Interestingly, the MSC‐mediated repair process was accompanied by a decrease in MSC number over time (Figure  ). To evaluate MSC apoptosis at the tissue level, TUNEL staining was performed 48 h post‐transplantation. Prior to injection into the disc, MSCs were pre‐labelled with DiR (Red), and TUNEL‐positive (Green) apoptotic MSCs were observed in the NP region (Figure  ). These findings suggest that transplanted MSCs can delay IVDD in the rat tail model during apoptosis, potentially highlighting a link between MSC apoptosis and their significant therapeutic effects.

3.2

## ApoEVs Released by MSCs Alleviated IVDD
Recent studies have demonstrated that apoptotic cells secrete a distinct subtype of EVs known as ApoEVs, which may serve as critical mediators of intercellular communication. In this study, we hypothesized that ApoEVs derived from apoptotic MSCs contribute to the attenuation of IVDD. To generate ApoEVs, MSCs were treated with 200 nM STS for 12 h to induce apoptosis under controlled conditions. Under optical microscopy, apoptotic MSCs exhibited reduced cell volume and developed vesicular and budding projections, which gradually detached to form ApoEVs (Figure  ). The apoptotic state of the MSCs was further confirmed via TUNEL staining (Green) and Annexin V‐FITC (Figure  ). To further isolate ApoEVs, we utilized a sequential centrifugation process. First, the apoptotic MSCs suspension was centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min to remove cells, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min to eliminate cell fragments. Finally, it was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min to pellet the ApoEVs, which were then resuspended in PBS (Figure  ). The shape and size distribution of ApoEVs were evaluated using TEM and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). As shown in Figure  , ApoEVs were predominantly oval‐shaped with diameters ranging from 100 to 200 nm, averaging approximately 125 nm. Western blot analysis confirmed the expression of apoptotic markers Caspase 3, Cleaved‐caspase 3 and Annexin V in ApoEVs (Figure  ).
 We subsequently investigated the internalization of ApoEVs by NPCs in vitro. Confocal microscopy revealed that ApoEVs were efficiently internalized by NPCs within 6–12 h of co‐culture, with the level of internalization progressively increasing over time (Figure  ). Moreover, treatment with ApoEVs markedly alleviated ECM metabolic dysregulation in senescent NPCs, including both matrix degradation and synthesis abnormalities (Figures  and ). To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ApoEVs in vivo, we administered them directly into degenerated IVDs (Figure  ). After 4 weeks, the ApoEVs treatment group exhibited significant improvements in NP signal intensity on T2‐weighted MRI images and in IVD height compared to the PBS group (Figure  ). Additionally, the ApoEV‐treated group demonstrated superior preservation of disc tissue integrity and recovery from ECM degradation and synthesis disruption compared to the PBS group (Figures  and ). These findings suggest that ApoEVs have substantial therapeutic efficacy in delaying IVDD.

3.3

## ApoEVs Regulate NPCs Apoptosis and DDR Through E2F1
We subsequently explored the potential mechanisms by which ApoEVs delay IVDD. RNA‐seq of aging NPCs pre‐ and post‐ApoEVs treatment revealed significant changes in gene expression profiles (Figures  and ). Specifically, 2543 differentially expressed genes were identified post‐ApoEVs intervention, with 1913 genes significantly upregulated and another 630 downregulated (Figure  ). To identify the upstream transcription factors regulating these gene expressions, we utilized Chip‐X and Position Weight Matrices (PWMs) analysis, which uncovered 22 transcription factors involved in the regulatory processes (Figure  ). Among these, E2F1 emerged as the predominant regulator, with its downstream target genes significantly enriched in NPCs apoptosis and DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways (Figures  and ).
 To confirm the hypothesis that ApoEVs exert therapeutic effects by modulating E2F1, we evaluated apoptosis and DDR‐related markers in senescent NPCs following ApoEVs treatment. As demonstrated in Figures  and , ApoEVs intervention reduced TBHP‐induced NPCs apoptosis. Comet assays and immunofluorescence further verified that ApoEVs alleviated TBHP‐induced DNA damage (Figure  ). Given the critical role of the E2F family of transcription factors in regulating the cell cycle and maintaining genomic integrity, we subsequently evaluated the proliferative capacity and cell cycle dynamics of NPCs (Øvrebø et al. ). We found that ApoEVs enhanced the proliferative capacity of senescent NPCs and alleviated cell cycle arrest, thereby establishing a prerequisite for improving NPC functional activity (Figure  ).
To further understand the role of E2F1 in regulating NPCs apoptosis and DDR, we conducted overexpression and knockdown experiments. Overexpression of E2F1 reduced NPCs apoptosis and DNA damage, whereas E2F1 knockdown led to increased apoptosis and DNA damage in NPCs (Figures  and ). These findings underscore the pivotal role of E2F1 in regulating NPCs apoptosis and DDR. Notably, TBHP treatment decreased E2F1 protein levels in NPCs, but ApoEVs partially restored E2F1 expression (Figures  and ). As a nucleus transcription factor, we investigated whether ApoEVs directly deliver E2F1 and found no direct evidence for this (Figure ). Thus, we hypothesize that ApoEVs regulate E2F1 protein levels in NPCs through an exogenous mechanism.

3.4

## ApoEVs Inhibit E2F1 Ubiquitination and Degradation in NPCs
We observed significant variations in E2F1 protein levels across IVD tissues with differing degeneration grades (Pfirrmann grades II–IV), and noted a progressive decline in E2F1 abundance concomitant with increasing degeneration severity, suggesting aberrant E2F1 degradation in degenerated discs (Figures  and ). Moreover, prior transcriptomic KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis and proteasome pathways (Figure ). Subsequently, in an in vitro senescent NPCs model, we assessed E2F1 protein stability and found that TBHP treatment induced rapid E2F1 degradation, resulting in a significantly shortened protein half‐life. In contrast, ApoEVs treatment partially enhanced E2F1 protein stability, extending its half‐life compared to TBHP treatment alone. The ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway and autophagy‐lysosomal pathway are two major proteolytic mechanisms that maintain protein homeostasis. Further experiments revealed that the proteasome inhibitor MG132, but not the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), prevented E2F1 degradation induced by CHX in senescent NPCs (Figure  ). Endogenous Co‐IP analysis indicated increased ubiquitination of E2F1 in senescent NPCs and degenerative NP tissues, while ApoEVs treatment partially reduced E2F1 ubiquitination, preventing its protein degradation (Figure  ).
 To further elucidate the specific molecular components within ApoEVs involved in the regulation of E2F1 ubiquitination, we performed endogenous Co‐IP using an anti‐E2F1 antibody, followed by MS analysis of the pulled‐down protein complexes (Figure  ). A total of 1140 differentially expressed candidate proteins were identified in NPCs, among which five deubiquitinases (DUBs) were specifically characterized (Table and Figure  ). Based on the protein profile dataset of MSC‐derived ApoEVs assessed by You et al. (You et al. ), we highlighted one significant DUB, USP5 (Figure  ). USP5 has been confirmed to be present and expressed in ApoEVs, suggesting that ApoEVs may regulate E2F1 ubiquitination and degradation through USP5 delivery (Figure  ). Consistent with the MS results, Co‐IP assays in HEK‐293T cells demonstrated that HA‐tagged full‐length E2F1 interacts with Myc‐tagged USP5 (Figure  ). Notably, interactions between endogenous E2F1 and USP5 were also observed in NPCs (Figure  ). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that E2F1 and USP5 predominantly co‐localize in the nuclei of NPCs (Figure  ). E2F1 consists of a C‐terminal transactivation domain, a DP dimerization domain, and an N‐terminal domain (Wu et al. ). Co‐IP experiments using three E2F1 truncation mutants demonstrated that the mutant lacking the N‐terminal domain lost the ability to interact with USP5 (Figure  ), indicating that the N‐terminal domain of E2F1 is essential for its interaction with USP5. USP5 comprises five distinct domains: cUBP, nUBP, USP, UBA1 and UBA2 (Avvakumov et al. ). To identify the domain responsible for the interaction with E2F1, we constructed five truncated USP5 mutants (Figure  ). Co‐IP results showed that the USP5 mutant containing the ZnF‐UBP domain interacts with E2F1, while the mutant lacking this domain lost its ability to interact with E2F1 (Figure  ). These findings suggest that USP5 interacts with E2F1 through the ZnF‐UBP domain and the N‐terminal domain of E2F1, providing further insight into the regulatory mechanisms.

3.5

## USP5 Prevents E2F1 Degradation Through Deubiquitination
Previous studies have shown that mutation at the C335A residue of USP5 disrupts the structural integrity of the region surrounding its catalytic site, thereby altering the overall conformation of the protease. This structural change compromises the ability of USP5 to form a stable complex with ubiquitin (Ub) substrates and abolishes its deubiquitinating activity toward polyubiquitin chains (Zhang et al. ; Scott et al. ; Scott et al. ). To elucidate the functional role of USP5 in regulating E2F1 protein stability, we generated a catalytically inactive mutant, USP5 (C335A) and performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays to inhibit protein synthesis and assess E2F1 degradation dynamics (Figure ). As shown in Figure  , E2F1 exhibited a significantly extended half‐life in NPCs overexpressing wild‐type USP5, whereas the catalytically inactive mutant USP5 (C335A) did not enhance E2F1 half‐life. Additionally, NPCs with USP5 knockdown demonstrated a more rapid degradation rate of E2F1 protein (Figure  ). These findings suggest that USP5 plays a critical role in regulating E2F1 protein stability, and the DUB activity of USP5 is essential for this regulation. Subsequently, we explored the mechanisms by which USP5 enhances E2F1 stability. Upon overexpressing USP5 in HEK‐293T cells, we observed that wild‐type USP5 inhibited the polyubiquitination of E2F1 in a dose‐dependent manner, with increasing USP5 levels correlating with decreased polyubiquitination of E2F1 (Figure  ). Furthermore, overexpression of wild‐type USP5 rather than the USP5‐C335A mutant effectively suppressed E2F1 polyubiquitination (Figure  ). Additional USP5 knockdown experiments reinforced the crucial role of USP5 in regulating E2F1 ubiquitination (Figure  ).
 Polyubiquitin chains are primarily formed through two distinct linkages: Lys48 and Lys63. Lys48‐linked polyubiquitin chains serve as the main signal for proteasomal degradation, while Lys63‐linked chains are involved in various non‐proteasome‐related cellular functions (Kulathu and Komander ). Previous studies indicate that USP5 predominantly cleaves K11, K48 and K63 types of polyubiquitin chains. To determine which type of E2F1 ubiquitination is influenced by USP5, we co‐transfected HA‐E2F1, Myc‐USP5 and lysine‐specific ubiquitin variants (K11, K48 and K63) into HEK‐293T cells. The results demonstrated that USP5 specifically cleaves the K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains of E2F1 protein (Figure  ). Similarly, when we co‐transfected HA‐E2F1, Myc‐USP5 and the lysine‐specific ubiquitin mutant (K48R) into HEK‐293T cells, we found that the K48R mutant was not subject to cleavage by USP5 (Figure  ). In conclusion, these findings indicate that USP5 enhances E2F1 protein stability in NPCs by selectively removing K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains from E2F1.

3.6

## Acetylation of NLS Sequence at K94 Enhances USP5 Nuclear Translocation
DUBs are typically localized in the cytoplasm, where they are responsible for removing ubiquitin modifications from target proteins, thereby regulating their stability and activity. Emerging evidence indicates that many DUBs are capable of translocating into the nucleus, where they participate in the regulation of DDR and cell cycle progression by catalysing the deubiquitination of nuclear substrates. In our study, we observed that the overall protein levels of USP5 progressively declined with increasing severity of IVDD in NP tissues (Figure ). Interestingly, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a marked shift in the subcellular distribution of USP5 in senescent NPCs, characterized by increased cytoplasmic retention and reduced nuclear localization as degeneration advanced (Figure  ). This redistribution pattern was further validated in an in vitro model of senescent NPCs, suggesting that impaired nuclear translocation of USP5 may hinder its deubiquitinating activity in the nucleus (Figure  ). The nuclear localization signal (NLS) is a critical prerequisite for a protein entry into the nucleus. Utilizing the NLStradamus database, eNLS mapper and protein subcellular localization prediction tools (PSORT), we analysed potential NLS sequences within USP5. Notably, the PRKK sequence within USP5 was found to be highly conserved across multiple species in various databases (Figure  ). To evaluate the effect of the NLS, we constructed lysine mutant plasmids by substituting lysine with arginine and observed that USP5Mut exhibited significantly reduced nuclear distribution compared to USP5WT, resulting in increased cytoplasmic retention (Figures  and ). The NLS mutation in USP5Mut led to a loss of DUB activity in the nucleus, causing a marked increase in the ubiquitination level of E2F1 (Figure  ).
 Research indicates that the nuclear entry of DUB is mediated by post‐translational modifications. For example, the DUB OTUD3 can be acetylated by the acetyltransferase CBP, facilitating its nuclear translocation (Zhou et al. ). To assess whether acetylation affects the nuclear‐cytoplasmic distribution of USP5, we treated NPCs with Trichostatin A (TSA, a class I and II HDAC inhibitor) and Nicotinamide (NAM, a SIRT family deacetylase inhibitor). Our findings revealed that the acetylation level of USP5 significantly decreased in aging NPCs, which correlated with its abnormal nuclear‐cytoplasmic distribution (Figure  ). Additionally, the deacetylase inhibitors significantly promoted the acetylation of USP5 and increased its protein levels in the nucleus (Figure  ). Subsequent immunofluorescence confirmed that the nuclear translocation of USP5 was associated with elevated acetylation levels (Figure ). Using the iGPS platform (www.pail.biocuckoo.org), we identified three lysine residues in USP5 (Lys94, Lys509 and Lys825) that are subject to acetylation, and these residues are highly conserved across various species (Figures  and , Table ). To further clarify which specific lysine residues influence the nuclear‐cytoplasmic distribution of USP5, we substituted arginine for each lysine residue (Arg94, Arg509 and Arg825). Our results indicated that USP5K94R, USP5K509R and USP5K825R significantly reduced the acetylation level of USP5 (Figure  ). Notably, USP5K94R exhibited impaired nuclear translocation, leading to decreased nuclear distribution, which was not observed with USP5K509R and USP5K825R mutants (Figure  ). Additionally, endogenous ubiquitination experiments confirmed that USP5K94R failed to maintain its DUB activity, resulting in increased ubiquitination levels of E2F1 (Figure  ). Interestingly, Lys94 is located precisely within the NLS sequence of USP5, suggesting that acetylation at this site is crucial for influencing its nuclear‐cytoplasmic distribution. In summary, these results indicate that the NLS sequence is essential for nuclear translocation of USP5 and its DUB activity. Furthermore, increased acetylation at the Lys94 site within the NLS enhances the nuclear translocation of USP5 in aging NPCs.

3.7

## ApoEVs Are Superior to Microvesicles in Delaying NPCs Apoptosis and DDR
We further investigated the differences in protein cargo among various types of EVs released by MSCs. Specifically, we isolated ApoEVs, microvesicles (MVs) and exosomes (Exo) and characterized them (Figure ). Western blot analysis revealed that USP5 was present in ApoEVs and MVs, but not in Exo (Figure  ). To further assess the acetylation status of USP5 across different EVs, Dot blotting analysis demonstrated that the acetylation level of USP5 was significantly higher in ApoEVs compared to MVs. This suggests that ApoEVs may offer a distinct advantage over MVs in delivering acetylated USP5, which could play a crucial role in delaying IVDD (Figure  ). We then examined whether differences in USP5 acetylation levels in EVs were linked to the apoptotic state of MSCs. Using STS to induce apoptosis in MSCs, we found that while overall acetylation levels in apoptotic MSCs did not significantly change, the acetylation level of USP5 increased compared to that in normal MSCs. This finding explains why USP5 acetylation levels were higher in ApoEVs than in MVs (Figure  ). To further explore the therapeutic potential of ApoEVs in IVDD, we treated senescent NPCs with ApoEVs and MVs separately. Compared to MVs, ApoEVs exhibited a superior capacity to promote NPCs proliferation and more effectively alleviated cellular senescence‐associated phenotypes, including cell cycle arrest, apoptosis and DNA damage (Figure  ). To further confirm the functional importance of USP5 within ApoEVs, we silenced USP5 expression in MSCs and subsequently isolated ApoEVs (ApoEVs‐siUSP5) from these cells. Dot blotting analysis revealed a marked reduction in USP5 protein content within ApoEVs following USP5 knockdown, accompanied by a concomitant decrease in acetylation levels. When senescent NPCs were treated with ApoEVs‐siUSP5, the vesicles exhibited significantly impaired therapeutic efficacy compared to control ApoEVs and ApoEVs‐siNC, as evidenced by reduced cell proliferation and limited alleviation of senescence‐associated phenotypes. These results underscore the essential role of USP5 as a key effector molecule within ApoEVs, driving their protective function in senescent NPCs (Figure ).

## Discussion
Apoptosis and DDR are two fundamental processes that occur in cells under normal physiological conditions (Huang et al. ). Apoptosis plays a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and eliminating cells with DNA damage or mutations (Rausch et al. ; Ma et al. ). However, in the adverse microenvironment of degenerated IVDs, various endogenous and exogenous factors can induce severe DNA damage, potentially triggering excessive apoptosis of NPCs and disrupting tissue homeostasis. Severe apoptosis in NPCs significantly affects cell viability and proliferation, leading to imbalances in synthesis and degradation metabolism, further exacerbating IVDD. This unfavourable microenvironment presents new challenges for subsequent biotherapy. MSC transplantation has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy in regenerative medicine. However, the adverse local microenvironment can diminish the therapeutic effects of MSCs. Interestingly, Liu et al. has confirmed that locally injected MSCs in a MI model gradually disappear, accompanied by apoptosis, yet this seems to have no impact on the therapeutic efficacy of the MSCs. EVs may play a therapeutic role in this context. EVs, which primarily include Exo, MVs and ApoEVs, are generally regarded as signalling carriers involved in intercellular communication and signal transduction (Eitan et al. ; Todorova et al. ). Their low immunogenicity, ability to target homologous cells, longer retention time and capacity to cross biological barriers have made them a focus of extensive research (Rezaie et al. ; Wang et al. ; Dou et al. ). However, ApoEVs have received relatively little attention due to their larger and variable size and complex contents (Wang et al. ). Recent studies have shown that circulating ApoEVs play a key role in resisting osteoporosis and maintaining bone homeostasis, suggesting that apoptosis may drive cellular proliferation during tissue regeneration (Liu et al. ). ApoEVs derived from MSCs improve cardiac function by promoting angiogenesis, indicating that vesicles play a significant role in intercellular communication under both physiological and pathological conditions (Liu et al. ; Todorova et al. ; Martinez and Andriantsitohaina ). To better elucidate the relationship between the apoptosis of implanted cells and the regeneration of recipient cells, we conducted MSC transplantation therapy in degenerated IVDs. Our study confirmed that degenerated IVDs induce apoptosis in transplanted stem cells and reduce their numbers; however, this does not interrupt the therapeutic effect of MSCs. Given that MSCs undergo apoptosis, we subsequently assessed the crucial role of ApoEVs as primary communication mediators in treating IVDD. Current research has primarily focused on Exo and MVs, with limited understanding of the mechanisms by which ApoEVs delay IVDD. In this study, we found that MSC‐derived ApoEVs promote the proliferation and anabolic metabolism of NPCs by alleviating DNA damage and apoptosis. MSC secretory profiles are highly responsive to microenvironmental cues, a feature that has been harnessed to optimize MSC‐based therapies. For instance, hypoxic preconditioning enhances MSC survival within the avascular niche of the IVD (Peck et al. ). Even unmodified MSCs can become “licensed” by injury‐induced local signals in vivo to secrete reparative factors. External stimuli likewise influence both the yield and composition of EVs released by host cells (Zou et al. ). Notably, embryonic stem cells (ESCs) cultured under serum‐free conditions produce greater quantities of ApoEVs than those treated with STS, and these vesicles retain pluripotency‐associated proteins from their parent ESCs (Beyer and Pisetsky ). Li et al. further demonstrated that adipose‐derived MSCs induced to undergo apoptosis by agents such as STS, deacetyl‐butyrate phosphate, hydroxyurea, or sublancin B yield ApoEVs enriched in miR‐21‐5p (Li et al. ).
ApoEVs contain exceptionally high levels of nuclear components such as PCNA, spliceosomal factors, telomere‐associated proteins and genomic DNA, far exceeding the amounts found in conventional Exo or MVs (Huang et al. ; Zou et al. ; Lanna et al. ; Lei et al. ; Pavlyukov et al. ). Such extensive nuclear cargo loading arises from environment‐induced changes in MSC secretion and renders ApoEVs an excellent model for investigating vesicular transfer of nuclear material. Moreover, ApoEVs carrying DNA repair enzymes (e.g., PARP1) may bolster genomic stability and foster DDR in senescent cells (Huang et al. ). In our study, we observed that ApoEVs mitigate ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of the nuclear transcription factor E2F1, thereby protecting NPCs from excessive apoptosis and enhancing their capacity for DNA repair.
The ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway is a critical process for protein quality control, wherein DUBs mediate the reversible removal of ubiquitin chains, playing a vital role in maintaining cellular homeostasis (Gao et al. ). This study identifies USP5 as a key DUB involved in the deubiquitination of the transcription factor E2F1, thereby delaying the onset of IVDD. Previous research has confirmed that USP5 participates in various cellular pathways, including DDR, apoptosis inhibition and inflammation regulation (Nakajima et al. ; Nostramo et al. ). USP5 removes ubiquitin from target proteins by cleaving the isopeptide bond between the carboxyl terminus of proximal ubiquitin molecules and the lysine residues of distal ubiquitin molecules, resulting in the release of monoubiquitin (Clague et al. ). Additionally, USP5 specifically removes ubiquitin modifications from substrate proteins, preventing their degradation and being crucial for maintaining the stability of the free ubiquitin pool (Park and Ryu ). Based on the linkage sites between ubiquitin and protein substrates, nine types of ubiquitination have been identified, primarily including M1, K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, K63 and G76. Each type regulates distinct functions, with K11 and K48‐linked ubiquitination being primarily associated with proteasomal degradation. USP5 can cleave seven types of ubiquitin linkages, namely, K6, K11, K29, K48, K63, linear linkages and G76‐K48 branched chains, exhibiting a higher affinity for unmodified K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains compared to linear and K63‐linked chains (Chen et al. ; Wilkinson et al. ). Given that E2F1 is primarily conjugated with K11, K48 and K63‐linked ubiquitin chains, we found that USP5 inhibits E2F1 ubiquitination‐mediated proteolysis by specifically degrading K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains (Dubrez ).
Typically, DUBs are located in the cytoplasm and participate in the removal of ubiquitin modifications from substrate proteins, thus regulating protein stability. Recent studies have shown that DUBs can enter the nucleus, where they modulate DDR and cell cycle processes by catalysing the deubiquitination of substrates (Zhou et al. ). Generally, the nuclear translocation of DUBs is mediated by post‐translational modifications, and some proteins require the NLS for this process. For instance, studies have indicated that the DUB USP22 contains an NLS, allowing it to shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm. In the cytoplasm, USP22 catalyses the deubiquitination of KPNA2 (nuclear import protein), facilitating the nuclear translocation of IRF3 in response to viral infection and subsequent downstream gene expression; in the nucleus, USP22 catalyses the deubiquitination of CCND1 (G1 phase cyclin D1), thereby regulating cell cycle entry into the G1 phase (Cai et al. ). The mechanism of USP5 nuclear translocation remains unclear, this study preliminarily establishes that USP5 participates in the nuclear translocation process dependent on its own NLS, laying the groundwork for the subsequent removal of transcription factor ubiquitination in the nucleus.
We found that apoptotic MSCs secrete ApoEVs with significantly higher levels of USP5 compared to MVs. Furthermore, high acetylation levels of USP5 appear to be significantly enriched in ApoEVs. Under conditions of DNA damage, protein phosphatase 2 (PP2A) catalyses the dephosphorylation of the DUB OTUD6A at the S70/71/74 sites, promoting nuclear translocation of OTUD6A, which is essential for its recruitment to sites of DNA damage to exert its deubiquitinating function (Zhao et al. ). The DUB OTUD3 can be acetylated by acetyltransferase CBP, promoting nuclear translocation of OTUD6A, facilitating the deubiquitination of PPARδ, and enhancing its stability and activity, further regulating the expression of OXPHOS‐related genes (Zhou et al. ). Similarly, this study found that the acetylation level of USP5 is reduced in degenerated NPCs, accompanied by barriers to nuclear translocation. The acetylation of the NLS sequence at K94 of USP5 can enhance its nuclear translocation process. Additionally, compared to MVs, ApoEVs released from apoptotic MSCs appear to be more conducive to the nuclear translocation of USP5, providing an essential basis for the removal of ubiquitination from the nuclear transcription factor E2F1 and regulating NPCs apoptosis and DDR.
Despite the important findings of this study, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations that warrant consideration. First, in vitro experiments cannot fully replicate the complex microenvironment of the degenerative IVD, which is characterized by low glucose, hypoxia, acidic pH and oxidative stress. Consequently, the apoptosis of MSCs induced in vitro may differ from that triggered by the pathological conditions within the disc in vivo. Second, this study primarily focused on elucidating the role of the USP5–E2F1 pathway in mediating the therapeutic effects of ApoEVs on IVDD. However, given that ApoEVs carry a broad array of bioactive cargos, including proteins and nucleic acids, their mechanisms of action likely extend beyond USP5‐mediated regulation of NPCs apoptosis and DDR. Further investigations are needed to characterize other functional cargos within ApoEVs, their downstream signalling pathways, and the broader spectrum of affected cellular phenotypes, in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of their regenerative potential. Finally, while this study emphasized the downstream effects of ApoEVs enriched in hyperacetylated USP5, it did not explore the upstream mechanisms governing MSC apoptosis and alterations in USP5 acetylation. These aspects remain to be clarified in future research.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this study emphasizes the complex dynamics between extracellular communication of transplanted MSCs, NPCs apoptosis and DDR in the context of IVDD. We demonstrate that the adverse microenvironment of degenerated discs trigger NPCs excessive apoptosis and DNA damage by inducing aberrant ubiquitination degradation of E2F1, disrupting tissue homeostasis. However, the ApoEVs derived from apoptotic transplanted MSCs emerge as key mediators in mitigating NPCs apoptosis and promoting DDR. The significant presence of the DUB USP5, with its high acetylation levels within these ApoEVs, provides a promising therapeutic avenue for IVDD. These findings enhance our understanding of the potential therapeutic role of ApoEVs in improving IVDD.
