## Abstract
Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based therapy regenerates damaged structures of the respiratory system and restores lung function, thus providing a promising therapeutic approach for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Understanding the communication between injured alveolar cells and MSCs can improve the efficiency of MSC-based therapies. The present study analyzed the untargeted metabolomics of the supernatant of AEC-II injury induced by cigarette smoke extract and identified 205 differential metabolites. Phenotypic assays indicated that phenylacetylglutamine (PAG) significantly promoted the migration and mitochondrial function of bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs). Whole-transcriptome sequencing (WT-seq) was used to analyze the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) and mRNA expression profiles of BMSCs treated with PAG. The upregulated lncNRA NONRATT006276.2 (NRT6276.2) and its trans-regulated gene, microtubule-associated protein tau (Mapt), were identified based on the lncRNA-mRNA co-expression network and bioinformatics analysis. The knockdown of NRT6276.2 or Mapt inhibited the positive effects of PAG on BMSCs. Furthermore, Mapt overexpression reversed the phenotype of BMSCs inhibited by silencing NRT6276.2. In conclusion, PAG enhanced the migration and mitochondrial function of BMSCs by regulating the NRT6276.2/Mapt pathway. This study clarified the positive effects of PAG produced by injured lung cells on transplanted MSCs, providing a potential new strategy to enhance the efficiency of MSC-based therapies.

## Introduction
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized by airway obstruction and inflammation and accounts for approximately 55% of all chronic respiratory disease cases [ ]. The occurrence of COPD is closely associated with smoking, air pollution, and occupational exposure to harmful substances. Other risk factors include genetic susceptibility, abnormal inflammatory responses, and lung development [ ]. Generally, COPD is diagnosed only after the appearance of symptoms, which indicates that more than 50% of lung function is lost, limiting the window for optimal treatment. Irreversible loss of lung function can lead to respiratory failure and an increased strain on the heart [ ], which makes COPD the third leading cause of mortality worldwide [ ]. Bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids can alleviate airway function and remain the most commonly used therapeutic approaches for COPD. However, these drugs are unable to improve the function of damaged lungs.
Currently, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) transplantation has multiple benefits in the treatment of COPD, attracting sustained interest and attention. First, MSCs secrete various cytokines and inhibit inflammatory responses, thereby improving airway function [ ]. Secondly, MSCs can promote the regeneration of lung tissue and repair damaged alveolar and airway structures, thereby improving lung function [ , ]. A three-year study showed that the progression of pathological changes in COPD patients was slowed down after bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs) treatment [ ]. A meta-analysis of 392 patients from 11 clinical studies indicated that patients with COPD showed significant improvements in forced expiratory volume in the one second and 6 min walking test after MSCs therapy [ ]. To date, the mechanisms underlying the robust restoration and regenerative capabilities of MSCs are not well understood. The majority of studies have concentrated on the beneficial effects of MSCs on damaged lung epithelial and endothelial cells, including anti-apoptosis, anti-inflammation [ , ], secretion of paracrine factors (cytokines and extracellular vesicles) [ ], nanotube formation for mitochondrial transfer [ ], as well as regulation of tRNA-derived small RNAs [ ]. However, the upstream molecules that activate MSCs, especially signals from damaged lung cells, are often overlooked and require further research.
Our previous study showed that the supernatants of injured alveolar epithelial type II (ATII, AEC-II) cells induced by cigarette smoke extract (CSE) improved the migration and mitochondrial function of BMSCs [ ]. We proved that the exosome long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) TCONS_00064356 in the supernatant is the key signaling molecule [ ]. Recent studies have shown significant differences in metabolite profiles between patients with COPD and healthy non-smokers, and metabolites with apparent changes may be closely related to the progression of COPD [ ]. Compared to the COPD model group, treatment with UC-MSCs upregulated the metabolic pathways [ ]. Interestingly, many differential metabolites (DMs) were identified in ATII cells (A549) directly exposed to silica [ ]. Lactate produced by ATII cells shifts alveolar macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype in acute lung injury [ ]. These findings indicate that metabolites secreted in the microenvironment play important roles in pathological changes in lung diseases.
Here, we hypothesized that the metabolites secreted from injured AEC-II promote the migration and mitochondrial function of BMSCs. A total of 205 DMs were identified in the supernatant of AEC-II injury using untargeted metabolomics, and among the three candidates, phenylacetylglutamine (PAG) promoted the migration and mitochondrial function of BMSCs. Next, lncNRA NONRATT006276.2 (NRT6276.2) and its trans-regulated gene microtubule-associated protein tau (Mapt) were identified upregulated in AEC-II treated with PAG by whole transcriptome sequencing (WT-seq) analysis. Finally, we demonstrated that PAG produced by AEC-II injury promotes the migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs by regulating the NRT6276.2/Mapt pathway. In summary, the present study demonstrated that injured AEC-II exerted positive effects on MSCs through metabolites and identified downstream responsive molecules. This study elucidated a novel mechanism for MSC-based COPD therapies, from the perspective of damaged lung cells rather than the MSCs themselves, which is different from the conventional research ideas.

## Materials and methods

## Cells and cell culture
Rat AEC-II cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), and cultured in DMEM-F12 (Sangon, Shanghai, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Wistar rat bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs) were obtained from the Stem Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured with α-MEM medium (Sangon) supplied with same concentrations of FBS and antibiotics. All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2.

## Preparation of CSE
Briefly, the CSE solution was prepared from 3R4F Research Cigarettes (University of Kentucky, USA) as previously described [ ]. After filtered using a 0.22 μm pore membrane (Millipore, USA), the solution was considered as 100% CSE.

## Untargeted metabolomics analysis
Supernatant collection: AEC-II cells were treated with 1% CSE for 48 h to construct a COPD cell model as described in our previous study [ ]. Then, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS three times to exclude the compounds from CSE. Next, injured AEC-II cells were further cultured for 48 h, and the resulting supernatant was collected for untargeted metabolomics. The supernatant from cells without CSE treatment was used as the control. Six replicates were used for each group.
Pretreatment of samples: 200 µL of the sample was added with 800 µL methanol and acetonitrile (1:1), and extracted by low-temperature ultrasonic (5 ℃, 40 kHz) for 30 min. After centrifugation and nitrogen-blow, the samples were redissolved with 120 µL of acetonitrile and water (1:1). Next, the solution underwent another low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 5 min (5 ℃, 40 kHz) and centrifugation for 10 min. The supernatant was then transferred to an injection vial with an inner tube for analysis.
Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis: Ultra high-performance LC-tandem Fourier transform MS system (UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X) was used for metabolite identification. The chromatographic column used for compound separation was ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (Waters, Milford, USA). Mobile phases A: water and acetonitrile (95:5); mobile phases B: acetonitrile, isopropanol and water (47.5:47.5:5). For MS analysis, the sample was ionized by electrospray, and the mass spectrum signal was collected in the positive and negative ion scanning modes. The raw data were processed by the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) to match the MS information and metabolite identification. The human metabolome database (HMDB) and Metabolite Link databases were used for identification and annotation of metabolites. P < 0.05 were used to screen the DMs. MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca) was used for the unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA), supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and pathway enrichment analyses.

## Preparation of metabolites’ solution
Preparation of 30 mM Uric Acid (UA) solution: 27.3 mg UA was dissolved in 5.11 ml H2O, supplemented with 0.3 ml NaOH (1 M). After sterilization with a 0.22 μm filter, 30 mM UA was diluted 41.6 times with medium to yield a 720 µM UA solution. For PAG, 10 mg PAG was dissolved in 0.378 ml DMSO to obtain a 100 mM PAG solution, which was then diluted 1380 times with medium to obtain a 100 µM PAG solution. For N-Methyl-D-Aspartic Acid (NMDA), 10 mg NMDA was dissolved in 0.679 ml DMSO to obtain a 100 mM NMDA solution, which was then diluted 100 times with medium to obtain a 100 µM NMDA solution. The final pH values of the three metabolites’ solutions were all 7.3. UA, NMDA, and PAG were all purchased from MedChemExpress Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

## Cell viability assay
BMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 103 cells/well. The compounds UA, NMDA, and PAG were incubated with BMSCs for 48 h respectively, and an MTT kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used to detect cell viability, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Wound healing assay
BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well. Then, a longitudinal scratch was made in the middle of each well using a pipette tip after the confluence reached ~ 90%. Next, the floating cells were removed and the three compounds (UA, NMDA, and PAG) were incubated with BMSCs. Scratches were captured at the time points of 0, 24, and 48 h. The width of the scratches was measured, and the relative migration distance was used to represent the migration ability.

## Transwell assay
Briefly, BMSCs were collected and resuspended in medium without FBS. Subsequently, 2 × 105 cells in 100 µL medium were seeded into a chamber (8 μm pore membrane) inserted in a 24-well plate. Next, 600 µL medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the plate. After 24 h, the cells remaining in the inner layer of the chambers were removed and washed, while the migrated cells in the outer layer were fixed and stained with a crystal violet solution (Servicebio). The cells that migrated from random fields of view were imaged and quantified.

## JC-1 staining assay
Mitochondrial membrane potential was detected by JC-1 staining. Briefly, alive cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution (Beyotime) for 20 min at 37℃/5% CO2. Subsequently, cells were washed with JC-1 buffer (Beyotime) twice, then maintained with fresh medium. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). The red-to-green fluorescence ratio was used to represent the mitochondrial membrane potential.

## ATP measurement
Briefly, the collected cells were lysed and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4℃. Next, 20 µl supernatant were incubated with 100 µl ATP working solution (Beyotime), and the Luminescence was detected using a MultiMode microplate reader.

## WT-seq analysis
AEC-II cells were incubated with 7.2 µM PAG for 24 h, and cells without treatment were used as the control. Three replicates were used for each group. Cells were lysed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was extracted using the MJzol Animal RNA Extraction Kit (MagBeads) and purified using the RNAClean XP Kit (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library construction and sequencing were performed by SHBIO Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, cDNA library was prepared according to Illumina standard instruction (VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina®), and sequenced with an Illumina novaseq6000 follwoing manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina). Raw reads were filtered using Seqtk before mapping to the genome using Hisat2 (version 2.0.4) [ ]. The gene fragments were counted using Stringtie (v1.3.3b) followed by trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization [ , ]. Differentially expressed lncRNAs (DELs) and differentially expressed mRNAs (DEms) were screened following the criteria: absolute fold change [FC (abs)] ≥ 2 and P < 0.05.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Prediction of lncRNA targets: Genes less than 10 kb away from the lncRNA were predicted to be target genes for cis-regulation. BLAST was used to select sequences complementary to lncRNAs, and the complementary energy between the two sequences ≤-30 was used to screen the trans-regulatory genes using RNAplex [ ].
Construction of the lncRNA-mRNA co-expression network: The cis- and trans-regulated genes of DELs were compared with the DEms, and the overlapping genes were considered DEms regulated by DELs. Next, the lncRNA-mRNA co-expression network was visualized using Cytoscape software.
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses: GO classification analysis (geneontology.org) and KEGG pathway analysis (www.genome.jp/kegg/) were performed to annotate the biological roles of the DEms regulated by DELs.

## Cell transfection
BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well. After cell confluence reached ~ 80%, the plasmids or siRNAs were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cells were collected to detect RNA and protein expression levels. A plasmid containing the full-length Mapt gene (pCDHCMVMCSEF1GFPT2APuro-Mapt) and a blank plasmid (pCDHCMVMCSEF1GFPT2APuro) were synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. siRNAs targeting NRT6276.2 (si-NRT6276.2:5’-GCT TTA GCT AGG CTC CAC TGT-3’) and Mapt (si-Mapt: 5’-CGG GAC TGG AAG CGA TGA CAA-3’) were synthesized by Ribobio (Guangzhou, China).

## qPCR
BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well. After the cells reached approximately 90% confluence, PAG was added for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA (1000 ng) was used for reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA (1 µL) was then added to a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 10 µL ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and 1.6 µL forward and reverse primers. The thermal cycle parameters were as follow: 95 ℃ for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s, 60 ℃ for 30 s. The relative expression level of the gene was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table .

## Western blot
BMSCs were seeded in a 60 mm dish, and the proteins were collected after the cell confluence reached ~ 90%. The protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein concentration determination kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 20 µg protein was used for gel electrophoresis, and the separated proteins in gels were then transferred to a 0.22 μm membrane (Millipore). Following 2 h blocking with non-fat milk (Beyotime), the membranes were incubated with the first antibodies respectively overnight at 4 ℃. The secondary antibody was then added, followed by further incubation for 2 h at room temperature. The band signals were enhanced using an ECL kit (Beyotime) and recorded using a chemiluminescence imaging system. GAPDH (#AC027, 1:10000), MAPT (#A23490, 1:1000), and secondary antibodies (#AS014, 1:10000) were purchased from ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
FISH assay was conducted to examine the location of NRT6276.2 expression in BMSCs. Briefly, cells were incubated with NRT6276.2 probe, synthesized by Servicebio Co., Ltd., then stained with DAPI (ABclonal). Finally, fluorescence intensity of samples was examined using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Statistical analysis
Differences between the two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test (two-tailed). Data are presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10.1.2, CA, USA).

## Results

## Metabolic profile analysis of supernatant from injury AEC-II induced by CSE
A total of 1018 metabolites were identified in the positive and negative models. The PCA results showed that the samples from the two groups were partially separated (Fig.  A), and they were completely separated under supervised OPLS-DA analysis (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that the metabolic profile of injured AEC-II significantly changed after CSE damage. A total of 205 DMs were screened using a threshold of p < 0.05. The FC values of most DMs varied from 0.9 to 1.1, and the change in amplitude of 36 DMs (17.56%) was higher than 10% (Fig.  C, Table ). The heatmap plot summarizes the relative abundance levels of all DMs in each sample. The metabolic profiles of samples from the same group were similar, and they were clustered together in the g1 and g2 groups (Fig.  D). HMDB classification of compound analysis indicated that these DMs could be categorized into 20 subclasses, including amino acids, peptides, and analogs (33.3%); carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates (7.6%); fatty acids and conjugates (7.1%); and benzoic acids and derivatives (2.0%) (Fig.  E). In total, 10.6% of DMs were not available in the HMDB database.
 The detailed information of the top 20 upregulated compounds according to the FC value is listed in Table  . Most of them belonged to endogenous metabolites according to HMDB and KEGG annotation, and only 7 (in bold) of them were available (with CAS number). In addition to UA, a purine derivative, the other 6 endogenous metabolites were classified as amino acids, peptides, and analogues, of which 4 of them belonged to dipeptides: Gamma-Glutamylmethionine, Gamma-Glutamylalanine, Gamma-Glutamyltryptophan, Gamma-Glutamylphenylalanine (Table  ). Next, the other two metabolites, glutamate analogues (NMDA) and amino acid derivative (PAG), and UA were preferentially selected for further investigation. The abundance changes of the three metabolites are presented in Fig.  F.
 

## PAG promotes the migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs
Next, we tested the cytotoxicity of the three candidates, and their doses were designed based on physiological serum concentrations. As shown in Figs.  A and 180–360 µM UA, 5-100 nM NMDA, and 0.32–7.2 µM PAG were non-toxic to BMSCs, and the viability of BMSCs was reduced by ~ 25% in 720 µM UA group. Wound healing assay results indicated that 3.6 and 7.2 µM PAG significantly promoted the migration ability of BMSCs after treatment for 24 h, but this effect was inhibited by 360 and 720 µM UA (Fig.  B, C). NMDA did not affect the migratory ability of BMSCs (Fig.  B, C). Consistently, the transwell assay indicated that, 7.2 µM PAG promoted the migration ability of BMSCs, and 360 and 720 µM UA had the opposite effect (Fig.  D, E). Therefore, the PAG was used for further experiments. As shown in Fig.  A-C, 3.6 and 7.2 µM PAG significantly enhanced the mitochondrial membrane potential and upregulated ATP level. The qPCR result indicated that, genes involved in mitochondrial transfer and ATP synthesis, Miro1, Uqcr10, and ATP5e, were all significantly increased after BMSCs treated with 7.2 µM PAG, and 3.6 µM PAG showed a weaker effect (Fig.  D). These results indicated that PAG significantly promoted the migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs in a dose-dependent manner within the range of 3.6‒7.2 µM. Therefore, the increased levels of PAG regulated by AEC-II injury promote changes in the phenotype of BMSCs.
 

## Identification and screening of potential responsive LncRNA and mRNA regulated by PAG
To investigate the potential mechanism of PAG, BMSCs treated with 7.2 µM PAG were analyzed by WT-seq. The results identified 267 upregulated and 220 downregulated lncRNAs using filters of FC (abs) > 2 and p < 0.05, respectively (Fig.  A). In total, 108 upregulated and 109 downregulated mRNAs were identified using the same filters (Fig.  C). As shown in Figs.  B and D, the lncRNAs underwent more changes than the mRNAs in number and changed the amplitude of genes. Therefore, lncRNAs exhibit greater sensitivity to PAG treatment than mRNAs, and they may be the first responders earlier than mRNAs.
 The predicted cis- and trans-regulated mRNAs of the DELs are shown in Table . These mRNAs were then compared with DEms, and 13 DEms were found to overlap. Next, lncRNA-mRNA co-expression network was constructed in Fig.  E. The network covered 26 DELs and 13 DEms, of which RGD1566226, Dnajb13, AC118147.2, Kcng4, and Acer2 were trans-regulated by several lncRNAs (Fig.  E). The remaining eight DEms were regulated by one lncRNA in a cis- or trans-regulatory manner (Fig.  E). Expression changes in the 26 DELs and 13 DEms are listed in Tables  and , respectively.
 As shown in Fig.  A, DEms regulated by DELs were involved in various biological processes, of which Mapt is involved in locomotion. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that Mapt was also enriched in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway (Fig. B), which is involved in the regulation of mitochondrial function. Consequently, we inferred that Mapt and its upstream lncRNA NRT6276.2, may be PAG responders.
 

## Knockdown of NRT6276.2 or Mapt inhibits the positive regulatory effects of PAG on BMSCs
Next, the expression levels of NRT6276.2 and Mapt were detected in the PAG group. As expected, these genes were significantly upregulated following PAG treatment (Fig.  A). BMSCs were transfected with siRNAs targeting NRT6276.2 and Mapt, and the results confirmed their successful knockdown (Fig.  B, C). Additionally, the knockdown of NRT6276.2 resulted in a significant reduction in Mapt expression, while NRT6276.2 expression did not change after downregulating Mapt (Fig.  B). Further results of phenotype assays indicated that the knockdown of NRT6276.2 significantly inhibited the pro-migration ability of PAG on BMSCs (Fig.  D-G). JC-1 staining results indicated that, silence of NRT6276.2 reduced the red fluorescence level, but the green fluorescence level was not increased correspondingly (Fig.  H). This change resulted in the decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential in PAG + si-NRT6276.2 group, compared with the PAG + si-NC group (Fig.  I). Silence of NRT6276.2 also inhibited the ATP production of BMSCs (Fig.  J). The expression levels of Miro1, Uqcr10, and ATP5e, which were enhanced by PAG, were reduced by the downregulation of NRT6276.2 (Fig.  K). Similarly, Mapt knockdown also resulted in the reductions of migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs, as well as the expression of the three genes (Fig.  D-K). The above results confirmed that, NRT6276.2 and Mapt are both downstream responders of PAG.
 

## Overexpression of Mapt reverses the phenotypes of BMSCs inhibited by si-NRT6276.2
To investigate the effect of Mapt overexpression on the phenotypes of BMSCs modulated by the downregulation of NRT6276.2, BMSCs were transfected with NRT6276.2 siRNA and a Mapt overexpression vector. The results confirmed that the NRT6276.2 expression level was significantly decreased in the si-NRT6276.2 group, compared with the NC group (Fig.  A). Downregulation of NRT6276.2 caused an obvious reduction in Mapt levels, and overexpression of Mapt reversed this reduction (Fig.  A, B). Phenotype assay results showed that knockdown of NRT6276.2 significantly inhibited the migration ability, mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP level, which were then reversed by Mapt overexpression (Fig.  C-I). Consistently, the expression levels of Miro1, Uqcr10, and ATP5e were inhibited by si-NRT6276.2, then enhanced by the overexpression of Mapt (Fig.  J). These results indicate that Mapt is a downstream target of NRT6276.2, and PAG promotes the migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs by activating the NRT6276.2/Mapt pathway. The location of NRT6276.2 in BMSCs was detected by FISH assay, and the result indicated that NRT6276.2 was mainly located in the cytoplasm and was also present in the nucleus (Fig.  K).
 

## Discussion
Metabolites produced by cells are vital molecules in intercellular communication and signal transmission. The changes in the metabolic profile of injured AEC-II reflect the internal changes in metabolic pathways and the potential effects on adjacent cells, including the retained MSCs after transplantation. In the present study, we focused on the 20 DMs with the highest FC values (> 1.1). Among the seven available endogenous metabolites, relevant research on four dipeptides (Gamma-Glutamylmethionine, Gamma-Glutamylalanine, Gamma-Glutamyltryptophan, Gamma-Glutamylphenylalanine) is very limited, so we considered the other three metabolites.
Uric acid (UA) is the final product of purine metabolism. Serum UA levels are positively correlated with the severity of COPD and could be a biomarker of COPD [ , ]. Its level in COPD patients range from 3.07 to 5.27 mg/dl (182‒313 µM), which is higher than that in the controls (2.65‒4.93 mg/dl; 157‒293 µM) from the data of 2797 participants [ ]. In addition, the level of UA/albumin in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from patients with sarcoidosis or hypersensitivity pneumonitis is 2.2‒4.6 times that in the serum [ ]. Therefore, the dose of UA was set as 180, 360 and 720 µM following above data. NMDA is an analog of glutamate that can induce acute lung injury by activating the NMDA receptor [ ]. Blockage of NMDA attenuates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. NMDA is often used as an agonist of the NMDA receptors, thereby exerting a similar damaging effect on lung function [ ]. Currently, NMDA has not been identified as the endogenous metabolite in lung tissues, and its level in rat endocrine glands and hypothalamus were 8.4 ± 1.2 and 5.6 ± 1.1 nM/g tissue respectively [ ]. In addition, the blood NMDA level in aplysia californica is 5.1 ± 1.1 nM [ ]. which is a reference value since its levels in the brain tissues of A. californica and rats are close [ ]. Therefore, NMDA doses were set at 5, 50, and 100 nM. PAG is usually identified as a gut microbiota-dependent metabolite, and its blood levels are associated with the risk of cardiovascular disease [ ]. Plasma PAG levels are significantly higher in non-survivors of acute respiratory distress syndrome than in survivors [ ]. In healthy humans, plasma PAG concentrations range from 0.32 to 3.6 µM [ ]. Therefore, the two critical doses were considered, and 7.2 µM (twice the highest concentration) was also included. Among the 205 DMs, only the 3 metabolites were investigated, and other candidates including the dipeptides, also deserve further investigations to explore the comprehensive mechanisms.
In summary, the significant increases in UA, NMDA, and PAG levels in damaged AEC-II reflect the progression of respiratory diseases, which is consistent with current relevant studies. PAG significantly promoted the migration ability and mitochondrial function of BMSCs, providing evidence for the positive effect of lung alveolar epithelial cell injury in COPD on transplanted MSCs. Currently, there is no direct evidence for the effect of PAG on migration and mitochondrial function. PAG is able to dose-dependently (1 nM-50 µM) induce a transient and suboptimal cAMP generation, and exerts as a partial agonist of β2-adrenergic receptor (β2AR) [ ]. The role of β2AR inhibitor of PAG (10–300 µM) is proved where PAG co-exists with β-agonists [ ]. The serum concentration of PAG in 95% of patients with cardiovascular disease varies from 0.08 to 7.4 µM, and only 5% of patients exhibit a higher concentration (9.9–23.7 µM) [ ]. Therefore, the endogenous PAG is not sufficient to exert the role of β2AR inhibitor, and the role of partial agonist is more possible. Interestingly, activation of β2AR promotes mitochondrial biosynthesis and increases mitochondrial quantity and activity [ ]. The adequate role of β2AR in PAG’s pro-mitochondrial function on BMSCs, especially the dose-dependent effect, would be investigated in the future studies.
lncRNAs also play an important role in the phenotype of BMSCs [ , ]; therefore, we focused on lncRNAs. Compared to human lncRNAs, rat lncRNAs have been less extensively investigated, and a combination of lncRNA-mRNA co-expression networks and bioinformatic analysis [ , ] is beneficial for the selection of potential lncRNAs. NRT6276.2 is located at chr10:103946886–103,948,314, and there are no reports indicating its potential role. The trans-regulation of mRNAs by lncRNAs is not constrained by spatial location and occurs more frequently than cis-regulation [ ]. In the present study, NRT6276.2 trans-regulated Mapt and four other DEms, which were also regulated by other 10 DELs. The single trans-regulation of NRT6276.2 on Mapt, provided a clear and adequate mechanism between them, avoiding potential competitive regulation by other DELs. There are several ways for lncRNAs to regulate mRNA, including the recruitment of transcription activation or inhibition factors [ ], interaction with DNA to alter the conformation of chromatin [ ] formation of an R-loop structure to regulate mRNA transcription [ ]. We found that the downregulation of NRT6276.2 decreased the mRNA level of Mapt, whereas the level of NRT6276.2 was not changed after the downregulation of Mapt. This confirmed that NRT6276.2 is an upstream regulator of Mapt. NRT6276.2 was located in the nucleus and cytoplasm, so NRT6276.2 is able to exert potential transcription regulatory role in the nucleus. The precise regulatory mechanism of NRT6276.2 requires further systematic investigations.
Silence of NRT6276.2 enhanced red fluorescence level of JC-1 aggregates, but the green fluorescence level of JC-1 monomers was not increased correspondingly. Li et al. also found similar phenomenon under certain conditions, which may be related to the incomplete breakdown of membrane potential [ ]. The significant increase of green fluorescence of JC-1 (mitochondrial depolarization) often indicates the initiation of apoptosis or oxidation injury [ , ]. The slight change induced by si-NRT6276.2 showed a moderate effect on the mitochondrion, which is insufficient to induce the serious damage of mitochondrion.
The expression of Mapt stabilizes microtubules, which are important components of the cytoskeleton and are crucial for the shape and migration ability of cells [ ]. Mapt, also known as Tau, plays an important role in the central nervous system by regulating axonal transport and promoting neurite outgrowth [ ]. Knockdown of Mapt or Tau results in reduced motility of Schwann cells [ ] and glioblastoma cells [ ], and the upregulation of Tau significantly enhances the migration ability of gastric cancer cells [ ], which is consistent with the results of the present study. Tau is localized within mitochondrial subcompartments [ ], suggesting a potential Tau-dependent regulation of mitochondrial functions. The phosphorylation of Tau at the AT8 sites may benefit mitochondrial movement, but the hyperphosphorylation of AT8 sites may cause axonal degeneration [ ]. In addition, Tau is often abnormally modified under pathological conditions and forms protein aggregates, impairing mitochondrial functions, including mitochondrial transport and ATP production [ , ]. These reports indicate the dual effects of Tau on mitochondrial function, which are closely related to its phosphorylation levels and modification methods. The present study found that PAG treatment enhanced mitochondrial function and Mapt levels, and that overexpression of Mapt reversed the mitochondrial function inhibited by knockdown of NRT6276.2. The phosphorylation level of Tau may be regulated by PAG treatment or by downregulating NRT6276.2.
KEGG pathway analysis indicated that Mapt was enriched in the MAPK pathway. Activation of MAPK regulates mitochondrial function, including mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production [ , ]. This pathway is involved in the regulation of cell proliferation and migration abilities [ ]. Additionally, the activation of p38/MAPK pathway is associated with phosphorylation of Tau [ ]. These findings indicate that the MAPK pathway may be involved in the phenotypic changes in BMSCs that are regulated by PAG.
Clinical trails indicate that, infusion of MSCs improves lung function of patients with COPD [ , ]. In animal models of COPD, intra-bronchial or intra-tracheal administration of MSC increases MSC population within lung tissues, and shows a better repair effect, compared with intravenous infusion of MSCs [ ]. The present study confirmed the active role of PAG released from injury lung cells on the function of MSCs. Multiple-dose of PAG through inhalation administration after MSCs implantation might enhance the function of MSCs and improve lung function. Currently, the clinical research background of PAG is still unknown. The application of PAG for the treatment of COPD is confronted with a series of challenges, including safety evaluation, local pharmacokinetics and the administration of dosage and dosage form.
One of the limitations of the present study is that the concentrations of the three metabolites were set according to serum levels. Further quantification of the levels in the supernatants of AEC-II or rat lung tissues would be helpful to confirm the effects of these metabolites. Secondly, the role of PAG between injury lung cells and transplanted MSCs was not confirmed in the rat COPD model. Thirdly, the study was performed using rat-derived MSCs and AEC-II cells, and further verification is required on human cell lines to ascertain the role and mechanism of PAG. Supplement of these assays (in vivo animal model and in vitro human cell line phenotype) benefits to exclude the interspecies differences and promotes the clinical translation of PAG. Another limitation is that the underlying mechanism for NRT6276.2 upregulating of Mapt was not investigated and will be considered in a future study.

## Conclusions
The present study provided a metabolic profile of CSE-induced injury in AEC-II cells, and the enhanced levels of UA, NMDA, and PAG were associated with the progression of respiratory diseases. PAG significantly enhances the migration and mitochondrial function of BMSCs by regulating the NRT6276.2/Mapt pathway. This study elucidated the positive effects of PAG produced by injured lung cells on transplanted MSCs from the perspective of the lung microenvironment in COPD. This not only provided a new mechanism to support the effects of MSC-based therapies but also developed a potential new strategy to further enhance the efficiency of MSC-based therapies.
