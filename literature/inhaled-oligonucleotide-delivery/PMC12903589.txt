## Abstract

## Background
Differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs) is critical pre-requisition for the progression of heterotopic ossification (HO). However, the factors that initiate MPC differentiation remain elusive. This study aims to investigate the temporal crosstalk between macrophages and MPCs during HO, and identified the critical regulatory mechanism.

## Methods
Single-cell transcriptome data of mouse ossified hindlimb Achilles’ tendon was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). MPC and macrophage clusters were visualized, followed by pseudo-time analysis, gene set variation analysis, transcription factor activity analysis, and cell communication analysis. Achilles’ tendon transection and dorsal burn injury was used to induce HO to validate the bioinformatic results. Macrophage-specific delete of Spp1 mouse model was established to observe the effects of macrophage-derived SPP1 on extracellular matrix remodeling and chondrogenesis of MPCs. Pharmacological blocking SPP1-Integrin receptors axis was performed both in vitro and in vivo to identify the essential role of SPP1.

## Results
Single-cell transcriptome profiling analysis revealed Col12a1+ MPCs and SPP1+ macrophages were the critical clusters to initiate and maintain HO. Mechanistically, integrin receptors, especially Itgαv and Itgβ5, were the essential mediator to associate SPP1+ macrophage with MPCs to drive chondrogenesis. In vitro experiments revealed that Spp1 was involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and chondrocyte differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells. Single cell RNA sequencing combined with in vivo experiments further confirmed that knocking out Spp1 in macrophages significantly alleviated cartilage formation within mouse Achilles’s tendon tissues and HO progression. Specific inhibiting SPP1-Itgαvβ5 axis or knocking out Spp1 gene in macrophages alleviated the progression of HO.

## Conclusions
Our findings revealed a novel crosstalk between MPC and macrophages via SPP1-integrin receptors axis to promote ECM remodeling and chondrogenic differentiation of MPCs during HO, which may be a potential target for a medical intervention to treat HO.

## Introduction
Heterotopic ossification (HO) has been a pathologically ectopic formation, which significantly compromises people’s quality of life [ ]. HO frequently occurs after deep burns, total hip arthroplasty, fractures, and central nerve system injury, and tendon is the most common tissue to develop HO [ ]. Histologically, HO develops mainly by means of endochondral ossification, including inflammation, chondrogenesis, osteogenesis, and maturation [ ].
Increasing studies have identified focal and external macrophages as the critical mediators to trigger local inflammation and endochondral ossification [ ]. The accumulation and activation of macrophages obviously affect local hypoxic microenvironment and angiogenesis, which will induce chondrogenic differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs) [ ]. M1 and M2 macrophages have been reported to participate in the progression of HO. During the crosstalk between macrophages and MPCs, M1 macrophages often initiate inflammatory response after local tissue injury, followed by inflammation relief mediated by M2 macrophages [ ]. Depletion of macrophages has been shown to evidently alleviate the process of HO [ ]. Previous studies suggested that HO correlated closely with inflammation [ , ]. M1 macrophage-mediated inflammation could enhance osteogenic gene expression and angiogenesis, and thus promote cartilage differentiation and bone formation in injured tissue [ ]. In fact, many studies depleting macrophages decreased M2 not M1 macrophages, indicating the vital role of M2 macrophages in the formation of HO [ ]. Nevertheless, M2 macrophages are typically inflammation-inhibitory, which did not support the known information that inflammatory response triggers HO. Therefore, different types of macrophages may play a different role along with different stages of HO, and it’s rather important to identify the key types of macrophages and potential mechanism that contribute to HO.
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has provided us an alternative to map the types, subsets, and states of macrophages in normal and injured tendon, which can provide a deep insight into physiological and pathological processes [ ]. Therefore, in this study, we used scRNA-seq data to chart a comprehensive census of cells from normal and injured mouse tendon tissue in a temporal manner and investigated the critical role of SPP1+ macrophages in mediating MPC chondrogenesis during HO. Mechanistically, integrin receptors, especially ItgαV and Itgb5, were the essential mediators to associate SPP1+ macrophage with endochondral ossification of MPCs. Finally, specific inhibiting SPP1-integrin receptors signaling axis alleviated the progression of HO both in vitro and vivo.

## Methods

## scRNA-seq data acquisition and analysis 
The scRNA-seq data analyzed in this study were obtained from NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data repository [ ]. 10X Genomics platform was used for scRNA-seq analysis, and Seurat package was utilized to process the data [ ]. Following removal of low-quality cells, high-quality cells were normalized and scaled utilizing the “NormalizeData” and “ScaleData” functions to obtain linear conversion. The top 2000 variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA), and the 30 most significant principal components were adopted for cluster analysis. Harmony package’s “Runharmony” function was employed to eliminate batch effects by integrating scRNA-seq data from multiple samples [ ]. The resulting clusters were visualized using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm, and cells were categorized based on the expression of typical markers, such as MPC (Pdgfra, Prrx1), Myeloid (Lyz2, Cd14), endothelial cells (Pecam1, Sox18), DC (Cd209a, Flt3), pericyte (Myh11, Rgs5, Acta2), T cells (Cd3d, Ccr7), B cells (Ms4ab, Ms4a1), Nerve (Mbp, Mpz), granulocyte (Csf3r, S100a8, S100a9) and erythrocyte (Hbb − bs, Hba − a2).

## Pseudo-time analysis of MPC and myeloid clusters
Monocle 2 package was used for pseudo-time analysis of the cell timing sequence in each sample [ ]. ScRNA-seq data were processed normalization, filtering low quality cells, and selecting highly variable genes. For clustering, differential expression analysis was carried out on the genes expressed by more than 10 cells. The DDRTree method was applied to identify pseudo-time-dependent gene expression across all cells. Heatmaps were used to visualize the expression of all the identified pseudo-time-dependent genes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the gene modules that changed dynamically with pseudotime were performed using the ClusterProfiler package.

## Gene set variation analysis
A nonparametric approach, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), was employed to evaluate the relative enrichment of gene sets across samples [ ]. Pathway analyses were mainly conducted on functional gene sets outlined in the molecular signature database64 and were exported using the GSEABase package (version 1.46.0). Different gene sets were identified utilizing the Limma package. To identify differences in biological function and pathway activity, a threshold value of p < 0.05 and |logFC| >0.2 was employed.

## Cell communication analysis
The CellChat R package (http://www.cellchat.org/) was used to analyze cell-cell communication networks from single-cell sequencing data [ ]. By combining single-cell expression profiles with known ligands, receptors, and their accessory factors, the strength of intercellular communication was calculated. We utilized network analysis and pattern recognition methods to predict the major signal inputs (incoming) and outputs (outgoing) of cells, as well as the coordinating functions between these cells and signals. To identify the ligands that affect the osteogenesis of MPC cells, we utilized Nitchnet software to predict the ligand activities that impact the formation of Col12a1 + MPCs. This method can predict ligand-target gene connections between interacting cells by combining cell expression data, signals, and prior knowledge of gene regulatory networks.

## Transcription factor activity analysis
Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) is a method based on co-expression and motif analysis for the reconstruction of gene regulatory networks and cell state identification from single-cell transcriptomics data [ ]. We utilized SCENIC software to analyze cell subtype-specific transcription factors in single-cell datasets. By constructing expression matrices and annotation files, we imported them into the SCENIC software to build co-expression networks, transcription factor-target gene networks, and finally calculated the activity of transcription factor regulons.

## Flow cytometry
After burn/tenotomy the soft tissue around the injury site was dissected from the posterior compartment between the muscular origin and calcaneal insertion of Achilles tendon at the indicated time points (1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 days after HO surgery). Tissue was firstly digested for 30 min in 0.25% Type 1 Collagenase and 0.3% Dispase II (Gibco) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium at 37 °C under constant agitation at 120 rpm. Digestions were subsequently quenched with 10% FBS RPMI and filtered through 40 μm sterile strainers. Specimens were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 and subsequently stained using the following antibodies: CD11b (MAB-FITC, E-AB-F1081C), Ly6G (BL4, E-AB-F1108F), F4/80 (PE, E-AB-F0995D), SPP1 (CoraLite® Plus 488, CL647-22952). Stained and washed samples were run on a FACSAria II for cell sorting or LSRFortessa for analysis (FONG CYTE 2080) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

## Isolation, culture, and differentiation of MPCs
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital. The acquisition of mouse tendon MPCs was reported previously [ ]. 4-6-week C57BL/6 mice were purchased and the Achilles’s tendon tissue were collected. Then, the tendon tissue was cut into pieces, followed by digested with 0.2%-0.3% type Ⅰ collagenase at 37℃ for 60 min. After remove undigested chunks of tissue using 0.2 μm filter net, the cells were then planted onto 25 cm2 culture flask and cultured with low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (L-DMEM), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Fig. ).
To isolate bone marrow-derived MPCs, C57BL/6 male mice aged 6–8 weeks were sacrificed and the femur was excised from mice, and the complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gbico), 1% w/v penicillin/ streptomycin and 50 ng mL − 1 M-CSF (Novoprotein) was used to flush the bone marrow out to the culture plates, and the cells were cultured for 3 days. After reaching 80% confluence, the cells were collected for the following experiments.
The osteogenic differentiation medium (ODM) contained high-glucose DMEM (H-DMEM) with dexamethasone (10− 8 M), vitamin C (50ng/mL), β-glycerol phosphate (10 mM), and FBS (10%). On day 7 of culture, the ALP activity of MPCs was tested with a detection kit (Servicebio, GP1050). On day 14, the calcium deposits generated by MPCs were evaluated using ARS kit (CTCCBIO SCIENCE, JD001). Put cells into three cylinders of anhydrous ethanol for 5 min and two cylinders of xylene for 5 min. Finally, mount the cells with neutral balsam, followed by microscope observation and analysis.
The chondrogenic medium was composed of H-DMEM with sodium pyruvate (1 mM), 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium, vitamin C (50 ng/ml), dexamethasone (10− 7 M), and TGF-β3 (10 ng/ml). At day 14, cells were stained with Alcian blue (Servicebio, GP1040), followed by microscope observation and analysis. For in vivo chondrogenesis, micro-mass cultured MPCs were induced by CDM with or without SPP1 and si-Mef2c for 3 d. The MPCs were then wrapped in the Transwell membrane and implanted subcutaneously into Nu/Nu mice. The specimens were dissected from recipient mice and analyzed 4 weeks later [ ].
For adipogenesis, the differentiation medium consisted of solution A and solution B. Solution A contained α-MEM with 10% FBS, dexamethasone (50 mM), rosiglitazone (100 nM), 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (500 nM), insulin (10 mg/mL), and penicillin/ streptomycin (1%). Adipogenic differentiation was detected by Oil Red O staining (0.5%) after 7 days of stimulation.

## RNA reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was prepared using TRIzol (TaKaRa). Firstly, HiScript ® III RT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to reverse the mRNA from NP cells. Then, on a Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA), the relative expression of acquired cDNA was visualized using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). In this study, β-Actin was used as a reference gene. Relative gene expression was quantified using the formula: 2−△△Ct.

## Establishment of burn/tenotomy HO model
This method has been established previously [ ]. Briefly, after anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, dorsal hair of the mice (6–8 weeks) was closely clipped, and an aluminum block which was heated to 60 °C was imposed to the dorsal region (over 30% of the total body surface area) for continuous 18 s to trigger a partial-thickness burn injury. Subsequently, each mouse received dorsal hind limb tendon transection at the midpoint of the Achilles tendon under sterile condition, followed by closing the skin with 3 − 0 sterile suture. After operation, all the mice received pain management by means of subcutaneously injecting buprenorphine (Buprenex, Reckitt Benckiser Pharmaceuticals) every 12 h for the following 2–3 days.

## Establishment of macrophage-specific Spp1 deficient mice
All mice were generated on a C57BL/6 background. Lyz2Cre and Spp1flox/flox mouse strains were obtained from the GemPharmatech company (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd, Nanjing China) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Spp1flox/flox mice were crossed with Lyz2Cre mice to generate Spp1flox/flox; Lyz2Cre mice. Only littermate mice were used when comparing WT and Spp1 knockout. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the animal center of the authors’ institution.

## Macrophage preparation and polarization assay [ ]
Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were collected from either Spp1flox/flox mice or Spp1flox/flox; Lyz2Cre mice (6–8 weeks) by flushing the femurs and tibias with PBS in a 1-ml sterile syringe. Then), 1 × 107 bone marrow cells were plated in 10-cm dish and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2 mM l-glutamine, 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Servicebio), and recombinant mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (50 ng/ml; GB310046-M, Servicebio). The culture medium was replaced on day 3, and the cells were allowed to differentiate for 7 days. The isolated macrophages were treated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (50 ng/ml) for 24 h for induction to the M1 phenotype.

## Transcriptome sequencing (mRNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis
BMDMs were collected from either Spp1flox/flox mice or Spp1flox/flox, and then were seeded onto six-well (105 cells/well) for 24 h to allow adhesion to the plate. The BMDMs were then treated with M-CSF (10 ng/mL) for an additional 24 h. Total mRNA was extracted from NPCs using TRIZOL solution and immediately frozen at -80 °C. The mRNA-seq analysis of human NPCs was carried out in partnership with Genekinder Medicaltech (China). Following this, the R package DESeq2 was utilized to identify and analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Further analysis focused on mRNAs exhibiting differential expression, defined by a log2(FC) value greater than 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05. Additionally, gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted to identify significant enrichments with a threshold of p < 0.05. The GO analysis encompassed biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC).

## Single cell RNA sequencing using 10× genomics
Achilles’s tendon tissues collected from the extremity injury site were digested for 45 min at 37 °C using 0.3% Type 1 Collagenase and 0.4% Dispase II (Gibco) in RPMI medium, with constant agitation at 120 rpm. The digestion was stopped by adding 10% FBS RPMI, and the mixture was filtered through 40 μm sterile strainers. The cells were washed with PBS containing 0.04% BSA, counted, and then resuspended at approximately 1000 cells/µl. Cell viability was determined using Trypan blue exclusion on a Countess II (Thermo Fisher Scientific) automated counter, and only samples with over 85% viability were selected for further Single cell RNA sequencing using 10× genomics. Bioinformatics analysis methods were performed as described above.

## Score according to ossification-related hallmark genes
Module scores and the enrichment fraction for ossification‐related gene expression in single cells were determined using AUCell. The ossification‐related hallmark gene was obtained from https://www.gseamsigdb.org/.

## Micro-CT assessment
Injured mouse hind limbs were collected and imaged 9-weeks post-injury using Micro-CT scanner (Skyscan1176, United States Bruker Siemens Inveon, Eschborn, Germany), with the following parameters: voltage of 50 kV, current of 450–500 µA, and isotropic resolution of 9 μm. The obtained images were constructed with NRecon software (Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium) using the following parameters: ring artifact correction, 8; smoothing, two; and beam hardening correction, 30–40%. Bone parameters analyzed for femur bone samples included ectopic bone volume (BV) [ , ]. In addition to total ectopic bone volume, analyses were also performed regarding the cortical thickness of native bone at the proximal tibia. After identifying the fuse point between tibia and fibula at the mid-diaphysis, a region 0.350–3.800 mm above this anatomical landmark was isolated and then quantified at 1250 HU for mean cortical thickness (Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium) [ , ].

## Histology analysis
After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for four weeks, the tendon tissue underwent decalcification, paraffin-embedding, and section of 3–4 μm thick sagittal sections before staining with Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green (SOFG). The details of H&E and SOFG have been described previously [ , ].

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
For IF staining of cells, after fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, the cells was permeabilized using 0.15% Triton X-100 for another 5 min, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with CD206 mouse monoclonal antibody (AG2664, Beyotime), iNOS rabbit polyclonal antibody (GB11119, Servicebio), COL2A1(AF0135, Affinity, USA), Sox9(AF6330, Affinity, USA), ACAN (DF7561, Affinity, USA), Col12a1(AF04360, AIFang biological, China). Secondary antibodies included anti-rabbit/mouse FITC/Cy3 IgG (GB21401 and GB22301, Servicebio). Finally, microscopic examination and photography were performed. For IF staining of deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections. The primary antibodies utilized include, F4/80 rabbit polyclonal antibody (GB113373, Servicebio), SPP1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (GB11500, Servicebio), Col12a1(AF04360, AIFang biological, China), COL2A1(AF0135, Affinity, USA), Sox9(AF6330, Affinity, USA), Integrin alpha V rabbit polyclonal (GB11293-2, Servicebio), Integrin α5 polyclonal antibody (YT5589, Immunoway), Integrin beta 5 polyclonal antibody (AF0185, Affinity), Integrin beta 1 polyclonal antibody (AF5379, Affinity), and COL12A1 polyclonal antibody (YT1010, Immunoway). Secondary antibodies included anti-rabbit/mouse FITC/Cy3 IgG (GB21401 and GB22301, Servicebio). Finally, microscopic examination and photography were performed [ , , ].

## Protein extraction and western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from treated MPCs. Using super-RIPA lysis buffer with protease (P1005, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (C0002, TOPSCIENCE), mice treated MPCs were lysed after preparation. After quantification, collected protein was loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel (10%, PG112, Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd, China) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (HVLP04700, Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich). After blocking the membranes with 5% dry skimmed milk, the primary antibodies were added to immerge the membranes, followed by the incubation with secondary antibodies. Antibodies included ACAN (#DF7561, Affinity), SOX9 (#AF6330, Affinity), MEF2C(#AF5390, Affinity), Smad3(#AF6362, Affinity), p-Smad3 (#AF8315, Affinity), and GAPDH (#AF7021, Affinity).

## Statistical analysis
In this study, independent experiments or repeated measurements were conducted with sample sizes of three, four, or five. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analysis. Normality hypothesis and the homogeneity of variance of data were determined by Shapiro’s test and Levene’s test before comparisons. Student’s t-test was performed to assess the presence of statistical differences between groups. Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post-test. The differences at P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (P < 0.05). The significance level was presented as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

## Results

## Cell characteristics from mice achilles’s tendon tissues during the progression of HO
To investigate the temporal changes of cell populations during the progression of HO, we performed an unbiased single-cell sequencing analysis of mice Achilles’s tendon tissues of control and HO group based on the published date obtained from NCBI’s Gene expression omnibus (GEO) data repository (GSE126060) [ ]. Single cell RNA sequencing was performed using the 10× genomics platform. The scRNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat package Seurat package [ ]. The cell clusters were then visualized by means of Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), and totally 15 different cell clusters identified. Cells were grouped based on the expression of classical markers (Fig. , A-C).
In addition, we analyzed the distribution differences between control group and HO group. There was a significant increase number in the proportion of MPCs and macrophages in HO group (Fig. , D). Furthermore, we examined the temporal changes of all these cell clusters, and found that the proportion of MPCs decreased at the third after operation, but increased since the seventh day after the operation, whereas the proportion of macrophages peaked at the third after operation and decreased until the 21st day after operation (Fig. , E).

## Tendon-derived extracellular matrix (ECM)-related MPCs possessed multiple osteogenesis-related functions to contribute to HO
In HO, MPCs frequently undergo inappropriate activation, leading to osteogenic differentiation [ ]. In mice with dorsal burn and Achilles tenotomy, burn injury will enhance the osteogenic capability of MPCs and causes heterotopic bone formation [ ]. Therefore, to further deeply examine the role of MPCs in HO, we firstly focused on MPCs in the control and HO groups. The MPCs clusters were visualized using UMAP, and a total of 3 major MPCs clusters were identified: Col12a1+MPCs, Clec3b+MPCs, and Mki67+MPCs (Fig. , A and B; Fig. , A-D). A comparison analysis between the control and HO group suggested that the cell ratio of Col12a1+MPCs was elevated in HO group, while the ratio of Clec3b+MPCs was decreased in HO group (Fig. , E). Temporal analysis demonstrated a gradually increased tendency of Col12a1+MPCs in tendon tissue after trauma (Fig. , C). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) analysis revealed that Col12a1+MPCs possessed multiple osteogenesis-related function, including bone trabecula formation, bone trabecula morphogenesis, ossification, chondrocyte proliferation, and bone modeling (Fig. , F). IF staining of Achilles tendon showed consistent tendency of Col12a1+MPCs in tendon tissue after trauma (Fig. , D).
We then conducted trajectory analysis using single-cell sequencing data collected from the injury site at four different time points after injury. We identified five major states on changes in MPCs gene expression, which was plotted in pseudotime (Fig. , E). Three major types of MPCs were also presented in pseudotime, with Mki67+MPCs differentiating into Col12a1+MPCs and Clec3b+MPCs along the trajectory (Fig. , F). Representative gene expression of cell differentiation was also mapped in pseudotime. The results showed that cells at the branch of Col12a1+MPCs trajectory possessed makers of extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism which participated in chondrogenesis and endochondral ossification (Fig.  , G). Analysis of gene-related function changes over pseudotime at each branch point demonstrated that Col12a1+MPCs trajectory showed the close relationship with cartilage development and ossification, while Clec3b+MPCs trajectory was associated with would healing and angiogenesis (Fig. , G). Furthermore, analysis of transcript factors suggested that Col12a1+ MPCs expressed more genes related to chondrogenesis and endochondral ossification, including Mef2c, Sox9, Mkx, Hoxo9, and Tead1(Fig. , G and H). The results above indicated that tendon-derived Col12a1+ MPCs may be the critical cell cluster to trigger and maintain HO.
 

## Spp1+ macrophages were associated with HO progression
Macrophages have been another major cell cluster responsible for HO [ ]. However, limited information is available on the impact of different macrophage subpopulations on aberrant differentiation of MPCs [ ]. To explore the correlation between macrophages and HO and to identify the critical macrophage subclusters, we visualized macrophages via UAMP, and found five major types of macrophages, including IL-1β+ macrophages, Cxc3cr1+ macrophages, Mrc1+ macrophages, Spp1+ macrophages, and Isg15+ macrophages (Fig. , A and B; Fig. , A). The ratio of Spp1+ macrophages increased at the third day after tenotomy, peaked at the seventh day, and maintained until 21st day (Fig. , B). The ratio of Il-1β+ macrophages increased at the third day after tenotomy, and gradually decreased since the seventh day. In contrast, the ratio of Mrc1+ macrophages remained at a low level until the 21st after tenotomy (Fig. , B). IF staining of injured tendon tissue indicated SPP1+ macrophages showed obvious increase at day 7 after operation (Fig. , B and C). Using flow cytometry, we also observed an early infiltration of Spp1+ macrophages three days after tenotomy at the injury site, which peaked at day 7, and remained present later until three weeks after injury, consistent with the results of single-cell sequencing data (Fig. , C and D).
Then, we performed trajectory analysis on single-cell sequencing data obtained from the extremity injury site at days 0, 3, 7, and 21 post-injuries. Five major types of macrophages were also presented in pseudotime, with Mrc1+ macrophages located at the differentiation starting branch, and differentiating into Spp1+ macrophages and Il-1β+ macrophages along the trajectory (Fig. , E). Additionally, representative genes expression that changed dynamically with pseudotime of the three major types of macrophages was also mapped in pseudotime (Fig. , F). The results showed that cells at the branch of Mrc1+ macrophages trajectory possessed makers of classical M2 macrophages which were involved in myeloid leukocyte differentiation, response to external stimulus, angiogenesis, and hemopoiesis (Fig. , G). Nevertheless, Il-1β+ macrophages expressed makers of classical M1 macrophages, such as Tnf, IL-1β, and Nfκb1, which are involved in the regulation of inflammation and chemotaxis, Nfκb signaling, and Tnfα signaling (Fig. , G). As for Spp1+ macrophages, these macrophages were characterized by both M1 and M2-related genes, such as Arg1 and glycolysis-related genes (Ldha, Eno1, and Pkm). Gene function analysis over pseudotime showed that the trajectory of Spp1+ macrophages was closely related to the regulation of ECM, glycolysis, and wound healing (Fig. , G). Given that alternations of ECM have been reported to be associated with osteogenesis [ ], we deduced that Spp1+ macrophages could be the critical clusters that contributed to HO progression.

## Integrin receptors mediate the communication between SPP1+macrophage and MPCs, which drives the development of HO
Given the potential relationship between macrophages, MPCs, and HO, we next analyzed the interaction between Spp1+ macrophages and MPCs. Interaction frequency and strength suggested a high crosstalk between macrophages and MPCs in HO group (Fig.  , A). CellChat analysis indicated that pathways related to VEGF, L1CAM, and CADM appeared predominantly in the control group, whereas pathways related to IL6, TNF, RANKL, and SPP1 were more prominent in the HO group (Fig.  , B). Further, CellChat analysis indicated that the binding of Spp1 to integrin receptors (Itgav, Itga5, Itgb1, and Itgβ5) may play more critical role in mediating the crosstalk between Spp1+ macrophage and Col12a1+ MPCs, compared to Tgfβ-Acvr1/Tgfβ1/2 axis (Fig.  , C). Analysis of gene crosstalk indicated that Spp1 from macrophage correlated closely with endochondral ossification-related genes within MPCs (Col1a2, Col3a1, Crabp2, and Tnc) (Fig.  , D). The signaling pathway analysis using CellChat suggested that SPP1 signaling pathway between Spp1+ macrophage and Col12a1+ MPCs was significantly activated (Fig.  , E). Notably, Integrin receptors (Itgav, Itga5, Itgb1, and Itgβ5) were widely expressed in MPCs, which may provide the basis for the bioeffects of SPP1 (Fig.  , F).
 Taken together, integrin receptors axis may be the essential mediator to associate SPP1+ macrophage with MPCs to drive HO [ ].

## SPP1+ macrophage is involved in ECM regulation and chondrogenesis during HO
Cartilage formation and endochondral ossification were the major mechanism of new bone formation during HO [ ]. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of macrophage-derived SPP1 on chondrogenesis of MPCs. We firstly established macrophage-specific Spp1 deficient mice (Spp1Lyz2) and isolated the BMDMs from WT and Spp1Lyz2 mice, respectively (Fig. , A). Subsequently, we performed the bulk RNA sequencing, and GO analysis demonstrated that multiple signaling pathways related to chondrogenesis were affected, such as ECM organization, cartilage development, cellular calcium ion homeostasis, and integrin binding (Fig. , B). Furthermore, we created a partial-thickness burn injury in the dorsal skin of WT and Spp1Lyz2 mice, respectively. Then, seven days after burn damage, we isolated and cultured the primary BMDMs from the two kinds of mice, and the BMDMs were subsequently co-cultured with MPCs (Fig. , C). Alcian blue staining showed that MPCs cultured with BMDMs from Spp1Lyz2 mice exhibited lower ability to chondrogenesis than from Spp1flox/flox mice (relative density: 1:) (Fig. , D and E). We further collected the co-cultured MPCs and performed bulk RNA sequencing. Totally 1024 upregulated and 880 downregulated DEGs were found (Fig. , F). Heat map demonstrated that MPCs cultured with BMDMs from Spp1Lyz2 mice expressed lower level of chondrogenesis-related genes, including Sox9, Spp1, and Mmp9 (Fig. , G). GSEA analysis revealed that compared to MPCs cultured with BMDMs from Spp1Lyz2 mice, MPCs co-cultured with BMDMs from Spp1flox/flox mice showed significantly activated signaling, including carpal bone ossification and chondrocyte differentiation (Fig. , H). The results of KEGG further confirmed that SPP1+ macrophage is involved in ECM regulation and chondrogenesis (Fig. , I).
 

## ScRNA-seq analysis reveals that deletion of Spp1 in macrophages could attenuate the progression of HO in vivo
Given the critical of SPP1+ macrophage in triggering HO, we established the Achilles tendon HO model using Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice, respectively. Then, seven days after surgery, we isolated the tendon tissue from Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice, respectively and performed scRNA-seq to examine the expression profiles at the single-cell level and to confirm the cell populations contributing the progression of HO. Totally 11 clusters were identified in mouse Achilles tendon tissue visualized by means of UMAP (Fig. , A). To annotate the cells, marker genes representing each cluster were identified using Seruat [ ]. Pdgfra and Prrx1 were used to determine MPCs, while Lyz2 and Cd68 were used to find macrophages or monocytes (Fig. , B). The cell markers for all the clusters were showed in the dot pot (Fig. , C). The cell distribution in the tendon tissue of Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice was also evaluated (Fig. , D). Correlation analysis revealed the interactions and communication relationships between different cell subpopulations, aiding in understanding how these subpopulations collectively participate in the biological processes of HO progression (Fig. , E). Based on the cellular markers, macrophages were further divided into six major subclusters, Ly6c2+ macrophages, Cd36+ macrophages, Mrc1+ macrophages, DC, Stmn1+ osteoclast, and Ctsk+ osteoclast (Fig. , A and B). Cell distribution analysis showed the cell subcluster ratio in Spp1Lyz2 mice (KO) and WT mice, respectively (Fig. , C). Then, we evaluated the expression of Spp1 in different macrophage subclusters and confirmed that the expression of Spp1 was well knocked out in macrophages (Fig. , D). In addition, the function of the three macrophage subclusters was evaluated. Mrc1+ macrophages featured by endocytosis and cell proliferation, Cd36+ macrophages featured by wound healing and cell migration, while Ly6c2+ macrophages mainly functioned as response to INF-β and antigen presentation (Fig. , E). Given the increased ratio of Cd36+ macrophages and Mrc1+ macrophages in Spp1Lyz2 mice (KO), we deduced that Spp1 deficiency in macrophages facilitate the tendon repair and immune homeostasis.
Subsequently, we examined the MPCs, and grouped MPCs into four subclusters based on their cellular markers, including Clec3b+ MPCs, Col12a1+ MPCs, Tnmd+ MPCs, and Mki67+ MPCs (Fig. , A and B). As showed in the results above, Col12a1+ MPCs were the major contributor to endochondral ossification. Therefore, we focused on Col12a1+ MPCs. Cell distribution analysis showed that the relative ratio of Col12a1+ MPCs decreased in Spp1Lyz2 mice, whereas the relative ratio of Tnmd+ MPCs increased in Spp1Lyz2 mice (Fig. , C). Tnmd has been reported to correlate with tendon regeneration [ ]. Therefore, the results showed that knocking out Spp1 in macrophages decreased the osteogenic differentiation potential of MPCs and facilitated to the tendon repair. We further evaluated the gene expression of chronogenesis and osteogenesis and found differential expression levels of Sox9 and Runx2 across various MPC populations (Fig. , D). Specifically, there are noticeable variations between Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice, suggesting decreased osteochondrogenic differentiation potential in the absence of spp1 in macrophages (Fig. , D). Ossification scores also suggested the reduced potential to ossification in Achilles tendon HO model of Spp1Lyz2 mice (Fig. , E). In addition, we evaluated the radiological and histological changes of Achilles tendon HO model from Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice, respectively. Micro-CT indicated that compared to WT mice, Spp1Lyz2 mice showed decreased formation ectopic bone after two months of surgery (Fig. , F and G). Especially, the floating HO in Spp1Lyz2 mice nearly disappeared (Fig. , F and G). H&E and SOFG staining revealed that, at four weeks post-surgery, the Achilles tendon from WT mice displayed significant cartilage formation, while the Achilles tendon from Spp1Lyz2 mice exhibited reduced cartilage formation. (Fig. , H). Furthermore, IF staining demonstrated evident reduction of chondrocyte marker, SOX9 and ACAN, in Achilles tendon from Spp1Lyz2 mice (Fig. , I and J). Together with the results from above, these findings suggest that in the burn/tenotomy model of trauma induced HO, deletion of Spp1 in macrophages could effectively attenuate the progression of HO in vivo.

## Specifically blocking SPP1-Itgαvβ5 axis effectively inhibits chondrogenesis in vitro
The results above suggested that integrin receptors, mainly including ItgaV, Itga5, Itgb1, and Itgb5, play a critical role in mediating the promotive effects of Spp1+ macrophages on HO. Integrin receptors act as cell adhesion receptors located in the cell membrane, which links the extracellular matrix (ECM) to the cytoskeleton and facilitate the transmission of biochemical and mechanical signals between cells and their surroundings [ ]. Each integrin heterodimer comprises an α-subunit and a β-subunit in a noncovalent complex. To further explore the key receptor subunit complex in mediating Spp1 signaling, we designed the siRNA for ItgaV, Itga5, Itgb1, and Itgb5, respectively. The results of RT-qPCR showed that silencing these for integrin receptor subunits alleviated the expression of chondrogenesis-related genes in MPCs, including Sox9, Acan, and Col2a1 (Fig. , A). Notably, knocking down ItgaV and Itgb5 showed the most inhibitory effects of chondrocyte differentiation of MPCs (Fig. , A). The results suggested that the Spp1-ItgaV/b5 axis may be the primary mediator to associate macrophages with MPCs. Cilengitide is an arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptide that targets integrins can specifically prevent the oligomerization of integrin subunits, including alpha v, beta3 (αvβ3), and beta5 (αvβ5) [ ]. Therefore, we chose Cilengitide to blocking Spp1-intergain receptor signaling. To explore the affinity of Cilegentide to these integrin receptors, we performed the molecular docking assay based on Auto Dock Tools1.5.6 and Auto Dock Vina v.1.2.0 softwares. The results showed that compared to other integrin receptors, the binding energy between Cilegentide and ItgaV and Itgb5 was the lowest, indicating the better affinity and specificity of Cilegentide to those two receptors (Fig. , B). Further, the docking results were visualized in three dimensions using PyMOL2.3.0 software. Molecular docking analysis demonstrates that Cilengitide binds spontaneously to both ITGAV and ITGb5 subunits. With ItgaV, it forms seven hydrogen bonds (VAL256, ILE191, PHE189, ALA126, VAL128) and one salt bridge (ASP192), supported by a binding energy of -9.5 kcal/mol. Similarly, Itgb5 engagement involves four hydrogen bonds (SER529, SER534, SER59) with a binding energy of -8.5 kcal/mol (Fig. , C). These results above demonstrated that Cilengitide exhibited the strongest binding affinity for Itgαv and Itgβ5, providing a structural basis for its potent inhibitory effect on the SPP1-αvβ5 axis.
 Chondrogenesis assay indicated that recombinant mouse SPP1 promoted the chondrocyte differentiation, as shown by the protein expression of ACAN and SOX9 (Fig.  , D and E). However, the administration of Cilengitide obviously suppressed this effect (Fig.  , D and E). IF staining for SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1, in MPCs induced by chondrocyte differentiation medium (CDM) suggested similar results that treatment with Cilengitide effectively alleviated the pro-chondrogenesis effects induced by SPP1 (Fig.  , G-J). In addition, alcian blue staining further confirmed the inhibitory effect of Cilengitide on SPP1-indued chondrogenesis (Fig.  , K). To further confirm the therapeutical effect of SPP1-integrin axis inhibition, MPCs were divided into two groups: SPP1 group and SPP1+Cilengitide group. Then, MPCs were cultured for chondrogenic differentiation and subsequently transplanted into nude mice recipients (Fig.  , L). The results showed that, pretreatment with Cilengitide in MPCs led to a significant reduction in chondrogenic capacity in vivo (Fig.  , M and N). Collectively, specifically blocking SPP1-Itgαvβ5 axis using Cilengitide could effectively inhibit chondrogenesis in vitro.
Cartilage formation and endochondral ossification were the major mechanism of new bone formation during HO. Our results further showed that Mef2c and Sox9 were primarily located in Col12A1+ MPCs (Fig. , A). Analysis of transcription factors indicated Mef2c may be the critical gene mediating endochondral ossification and new bone formation of Col12A1 + MPCs (Fig. , H). Temporal expression of Mef2c indicated that Mef2c was highly expressed at day 7 after injury (Fig. , B). Given that integrin receptors mediated the crosstalk between Spp1+ macrophages and MPCs, we evaluated the effects of integrin receptors on the expression of Mef2c. The results showed that silencing integrin receptors decreased the expression of Mef2c, with ItgαV and Itgβ5 being the most obvious (Fig. , C). These results above may suggest that Mef2c could function as the mediator of SPP1- ItgaVb5 to promote cartilage formation and endochondral ossification during HO. To systematically delineate the signaling pathways downstream of SPP1–Itgαvβ5, we focused on major intracellular regulators of chondrocyte differentiation. Treatment of MPCs with recombinant SPP1 robustly induced phosphorylation of both ERK1/2 and Akt, as determined by Western blot analysis (Fig. , D and E). This activation was entirely abolished by pre-treatment with Cilengitide, confirming the essential role of this integrin heterodimer in transducing SPP1-mediated signaling (Fig. , D and E). To evaluate the functional relevance of these pathways in chondrogenic regulation, we performed pharmacological inhibition experiments using the MEK/ERK inhibitor PD98059 (2 µM) or the Akt inhibitor MK-2206 (2 µM). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that either inhibitor effectively suppressed SPP1-induced upregulation of key chondrogenic genes, including Mef2c, Sox9, Acan, and Col2a1(Fig. , F and G).
We further investigated the clinical relevance of our findings in other forms of heterotopic ossification, namely ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) and ossified ligamentum flavum (OLF). Transcriptomic analysis of human OPLL tissues (GEO: GSE94683) identified 2,386 upregulated and 1,923 downregulated genes compared to normal ligament controls (Fig. , A and B). Among these differentially expressed genes (DEGs), SPP1 was significantly elevated in ossified specimens (Fig. , C). Functional enrichment analysis revealed activated biological processes including extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, extracellular structure organization, ossification, and mesenchymal progenitor cell differentiation (Fig. , D). KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed involvement of ossification-related pathways such as calcium signaling, AKT signaling, MAPK signaling, and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. , E). Similarly, in OLF tissues, we identified 782 upregulated and 533 downregulated DEGs, with SPP1 again showing marked overexpression in ossified samples (Fig. , A). GO term analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in ECM organization, extracellular structure organization, ossification, and chondrocyte differentiation (Fig. , B). Corresponding KEGG analysis highlighted activation of AKT signaling and ECM-receptor interaction pathways (Fig. , C). These consistent findings across different HO subtypes strengthen the proposition that SPP1-mediated ECM remodeling represents a common pathogenic mechanism in ectopic bone formation. We further assessed integrin receptor expression in human PLL and LF tissues. Immunofluorescence staining revealed markedly elevated expression of ItgαV, ITGα5, and ITGβ5 in ossified PLL compared to normal controls (Fig. ). Similarly, ossified LF tissues exhibited significantly higher levels of ItgαV and ITGβ5 compared to normal ligament samples (Fig. ).
Collectively, these findings suggest that OPLL and OLF may share pathogenic mechanisms with Achilles tendon-derived HO, characterized by local microenvironmental alterations featuring extracellular matrix remodeling and endochondral ossification, along with activation of a conserved regulatory network centered on SPP1-integrin receptor-mediated AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. Our results firstly established a novel SPP1–Itgαvβ5–(ERK1/2/Akt)–Mef2c signaling axis that mechanistically links macrophage-derived SPP1 to the transcriptional reprogramming of MPCs during chondrogenesis, providing a unified framework for understanding ectopic bone formation across different anatomical sites.

## Specifically blocking SPP1-Itgαvβ5 axis alleviates the progression of HO in vivo
Subsequently, we explored the therapeutic effect of cilengitide on ectopic bone formation in vivo via local administration of cilengitide at the injury site (once a week) following burn and tenotomy (Fig. , A and B) [ ]. At the ending of model experiment (8 weeks after surgery), micro-CT scanning was performed and the results revealed a significantly reduced volume of ectopic bone in the cilengitide group compared to the HO group (Fig. , C and D). H&E and SOFG staining demonstrated the reduced cartilage precursor at the injury site 4 weeks following burn and tenotomy (Fig. , E). Importantly, the ratio of positive cells for chondrogenesis, SOX9 and ACAN, within injured tendon tissue was significantly reduced by cilengitide (Fig. , F and G).
 Therefore, specifically blocking SPP1-Itgαvβ5 axis inhibited endochondral ossification and alleviated the progression of HO.

## Discussion
This present study demonstrated that MPCs, which differentiated into ECM-related cell clusters (Col12a1+MPCs) play a critical role in trigging the progression of HO induced by burn/tenotomy. Furthermore, we found a novel pro-HO macrophage cluster, Spp1+ macrophages, which showed consistent cell ratio changes with Col12a1+MPCs. More importantly, we firstly revealed that Spp1+ macrophages interacted with Col12a1+MPCs via SPP1-integrin receptors to promote HO. Mechanistically, the expression of mef2c induced by SPP1-integrin receptor axis, especially Itgav and Itgβ5, play the critical role in mediating chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation of MPCs. Notably, in another two HO-related diseases, OPLL and OLF, we also found the increased expression of SPP1 and integrin receptors, which further confirmed the importance of SPP1-integrin receptors axis in HO. Finally, specifically blocking SPP1-integrin receptor axis significantly alleviated burn/tenotomy-induced HO, indicating the essential role of SPP1-integrin receptors axis in this pathological process (Fig. ). Our findings in this study may reveal a novel target and pave the footholds for future treatment strategies designing for the treatment of HO.
Previous studies have reported that ECM metabolism and the interaction of ECM with MPCs play important roles in MPC proliferation and differentiation [ , ]. Study from Amanda K et al. showed an early increase in MPC genes associated with signaling pathways of cell adhesion and ECM-receptor interactions at the injury site [ ]. Changes of ECM constitution could cause focal force alternations which will be sensed by MPCs and subsequently MPCs will undergo aberrant cell fate or HO. Inhibition of mechanotransducive FAK and YAP/TAZ signaling by genetic knockout resulted in decreased HO formation [ ]. In this present study, we revealed that MPCs resident in mouse tendon differentiated into Col12a1+MPCs after burn/tenotomy and were maintained at a high level until 21 days following operation. Col12a1+MPCs highly expressed ECM-regulated genes. GSVA analysis suggested that Col12a1+MPCs correlated closely with ECM receptor interaction and glycosaminoglycan metabolism. Importantly, compared other MPC clusters, Col12a1+MPCs expressed higher osteoblast-related transcription factors, such as Mef2c and Sox9, which have been reported to participate in the initiation and progression of HO [ – ]. We deduced that injury induced by burn/tenotomy can result in alternations in local ECM homeostasis and dysfunction of mechanical properties of ECM. MPCs sensed the abnormal tissue stiffness and would undergo differentiation into Col12a1+MPC which further cause ECM remodeling. Therefore, a self-amplifying loop of Col12a1+MPC and ECM remodeling initiated by ECM receptor interaction at local tendon tissue will accelerate injury-induced HO.
MPC differentiation and inflammatory response mediated by immune cells have been two critical pre-requisition for the progression of HO [ ]. As a result, we sought to examine the changes of macrophages during HO. As indicated by the results, a new cluster of macrophages, Spp1+ macrophages were firstly identified to correlated with osteogenic differentiation of MPCs, which showed consistent tendency with Col12a1+MPC. Function analysis revealed that Spp1+ macrophages were involved in the regulation of collagen-containing ECM. Interestingly, glycolysis-related genes, including Ldha, Eno1, Pkm, and Gapdh, were highly active in Spp1+ macrophages, which have been reported to the typical characteristics of M1 macrophages. Nevertheless, Spp1+ macrophages also highly expressed Arg1, a maker of M2 macrophages. As validated by previous studies, Spp1+ macrophages expressed both typical M2 marker CD206 and M1 macrophage regulator activity of STAT1 [ , – ]. In fact, either M1 or M2 macrophages can result in HO formation indicated the critical role of the dual function of SPP1+ macrophages on HO [ ]. We deduced that the M2 macrophages that could trigger HO which was reported in previous studies may be belong to Spp1+ macrophages. In addition, genes related to the extracellular matrix, such as CD44, which may endow Spp1+ macrophages with the ability of ECM regulation [ ]. Notably, this study also revealed that SPP1 not only increased the produce of SPP1 by M1 macrophages, but also promoted the expression of M2 macrophage polarization genes. Current studies have suggested the promotive effects of Spp1+ macrophages on tumor progress and tissue fibrosis, as well as HO revealed by our study [ , ]. The upregulation of SPP1 is also a hallmark of inflammatory diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting the critically pathophysiological role of Spp1+ macrophages [ ]. The results from Sc-RNA seq showed that Spp1+ macrophages expressed M1 type macrophage-related genes, such as Ldha and Eno1, and also expressed M2 type macrophage-related genes, such as Arg1, indicating the unique and plastic phenotype of SPP1⁺ macrophage population. To explore the effects of Spp1 on macrophage polarization, We isolated BMDMs from Spp1WT and Spp1Lyz2 mice and subjected them to classical M1 polarization stimuli (LPS + IFN-γ) (Fig. , A). Under basal conditions, Spp1 deficiency in macrophages indeed led to a noticeable shift in the polarization balance, characterized by increased expression of M1 marker genes (Inos, Tnfα, and Il1β) and a concomitant decrease in key M2 marker genes (Arg1, Cd206, and Ym1/2) (Fig. , B). Importantly, upon stimulation with LPS + IFN-γ, this effect was markedly amplified. The absence of Spp1 resulted in a significantly more pronounced M1-polarized state compared to WT macrophages, while the induction of M2-associated genes was substantially blunted (Fig. , B). Further, in this new submission, we have added immunofluorescence staining and ELISA experiments to validate the results of qRT-PCR, which showed consistent tendency that SPP1 is a critical modulator of macrophage polarization, favoring a transcriptional program that restrains full M1 polarization and supports the expression of certain M2 markers (Fig. , C-E). These data above robustly strengthen our original claim of the hybrid nature of SPP1⁺ macrophages by demonstrating the functional consequence of its loss on polarization plasticity. In a previous study, Karina Schuch et al. reported that Spp1 deficient adipose tissue-derived macrophages expressed more M1 and less M2 markers but less tumor necrosis factor-α compared with WT, consistent with our results in this study [ ]. Nevertheless, the authors found no effect of SPP1 deficiency on macrophage polarization. We deduced that the different results of our study from Karina Schuch et al.’s study may be attributed to the fact that they induced M1 differentiation using only IFNγ [ ]. In tumor-related researches, there are also studies also showing that patients with more SPP1hi macrophages had poorer clinical outcomes, which was similar to those with higher level of M2 macrophages, including colon cancer and squamous cell carcinomas [ – ]. However, Xu et al. found that SPP1 facilitated macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype via stimulating Janus kinase 1/signal transducers and activators of transcription 1 (JAK1/STAT1) signaling in hepatocytes to secrete high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [ ]. Therefore, more efforts are still needed to investigate the key transcription factors that govern the Spp1+ macrophages and determine their function.
To further identify the key mediators of MPC and Spp1+ macrophages interaction in mouse HO model, we investigated the cell-cell communication mechanisms of MPC and Spp1+ macrophages. The results showed that Spp1-integrin receptors (Itgav, Itga5, Itgb1, and Itgβ5) signaling pathway played a critical role in mediating the crosstalk between Spp1+ macrophage and MPCs to promote HO, more significant than TGFβ signaling pathway, a classical pro-HO signaling [ ]. The results of scRNA-seq of tendon tissue from Spp1Lyz2 mice and WT mice demonstrated that specifically knocking out Spp1 in macrophages decreased the number of HO-related MPCs, Col12a1+MPCs, and increased the ratio of tendon repair-related MPCs, Tnmd+ MPCs. In addition, inhibiting the expression of Spp1 in macrophages evidently reduced the ossification score of MPCs. Combined with the radiological and histological results, we could conclude that in the burn/tenotomy model of trauma induced HO, deletion of Spp1 in macrophages could effectively attenuate endochondral ossification and the progression of HO in vivo.
Further, given that SPP1 can be generated by Spp1+ macrophages contributed to HO via integrin receptors, we used recombinant SPP1 protein to stimulate MPCs in vitro, and found that the administration of SPP1 significantly enhanced CDM-mediated chondrogenesis, but these promotive effects were blocked by specific itgavβ5 inhibitor, cilengitide. Mouse HO model further confirmed the therapeutic effect of inhibiting SPP1 signaling on HO formation. SPP1, also named osteopontin (OPN), has been a fascinating, multifunctional protein that functions not only in intercellular communication, but also in the extracellular matrix (ECM) [ ]. SPP1 possesses cytokine, chemokine, and signal transduction functions by virtue of modular structural motifs that provide interaction surfaces for integrin receptors and CD44-variant receptors [ ]. However, very little is known about the effects of SPP1 on HO formation.
In this present study, our findings identified a novel mechanosensitive pathway in heterotopic ossification (HO) wherein macrophage-derived SPP1 activates αvβ5 integrin on mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs), triggering coordinated ERK/MAPK and AKT signaling that converges on Mef2c-mediated chondrogenic programming. This SPP1-αvβ5 axis effectively bridges inflammatory signaling with established mechanotransduction pathways in HO [ ], revealing how biochemical cues amplify mechanical responses in pathologic ossification. The dual activation of ERK and AKT represents a robust regulatory mechanism that ensures sustained chondrogenic commitment, consistent with known integrin-mediated mechanotransduction in skeletal differentiation [ – ]. Furthermore, the convergence of both pathways on Mef2c establishes redundant regulatory nodes that reinforce chondrogenic differentiation, extending previous evidence of integrin-mediated MAPK/AKT signaling in pathologic ossification [ , ]. This coordinated ERK-AKT-Mef2c network reveals greater complexity in HO pathogenesis than previously recognized and offers multiple potential therapeutic targets within the mechanotransduction framework.
In addition to tendon HO in patients with burn or neurogenic injury, more than millions of patients, also suffer from OPLL and OLF [ ]. The conserved upregulation of SPP1 and integrin receptors in OPLL and OLF tissues underscores a shared pathogenic backbone across anatomically distinct forms of HO. Our analyses not only confirm the consistent overexpression of SPP1, ItgαV, and ITGβ5 in these clinical specimens, but also reveal a recurrent pattern of pathway activation, including ECM-receptor interaction, AKT, and MAPK signaling, that mirrors the signaling landscape observed in trauma-induced HO. This molecular convergence suggests that the SPP1–Itgαvβ5–(ERK1/2/Akt)–Mef2c axis identified in our experimental models may represent a common mechanistic core in ectopic bone formation, independent of anatomical site or etiology. Inhibitors of the SPP1-integrin axis, such as cilengitide, may thus exhibit efficacy not only in traumatic HO but also in progressive ligamentous ossification disorders. Furthermore, the detection of SPP1-enriched microenvironments in clinical samples could serve as a biomarker for identifying patients at risk of progressive HO, enabling earlier intervention in a wider spectrum of fibro-osseous pathologies.
ECM constitutes a dynamically organized three-dimensional architecture of structural and regulatory macromolecules that governs tissue morphogenesis and homeostasis through integrated biochemical and biomechanical signaling. By orchestrating cell adhesion, signal transduction, and mechanosensing pathways, the ECM exerts precise control over fundamental cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and phenotypic commitment. This regulatory paradigm finds a striking parallel in tumor pathogenesis, where progressive remodeling of the specialized tumor ECM drives carcinogenesis from localized invasion to metastatic dissemination. Our findings regarding SPP1-mediated ECM remodeling in HO resonate strongly with its well-characterized role in tumor progression. Indeed, cancer-associated macrophages (CAMs) could actively secrete cytokines, such as SPP1, to shape the ‘oncomatrix’, a tumor-specific extracellular matrix with distinct molecular and biomechanical properties that promote malignancy [ ]. In this setting, SPP1 acts not only as a chemotactic signal but also as a modular matrix scaffold that engages integrin receptors or CD44 variants on stromal and cancer cells, thereby activating downstream signaling to drive ECM deposition, crosslinking, and tissue stiffening [ , ]. Critically, these biomechanical alterations reinforce SPP1 expression through a feed-forward circuit, sustaining a self-amplifying cycle of pathological matrix remodeling [ , , ]. Notably, the SPP1-enriched microenvironment in HO mirrors the pro-tumoral oncomatrix in its capacity to direct cellular fate. In both pathological settings, SPP1 macrophages orchestrate a permissive niche through integrin-mediated activation of profibrotic and prochondrogenic signaling, while matrix stiffness and structural topography emerge as pivotal determinants of mesenchymal progenitor cell differentiation. These mechanistic parallels reveal new therapeutic horizons for HO. Targeting the SPP1-integrin axis may simultaneously disrupt biochemical signaling and biomechanical reinforcement circuits driving ectopic bone formation. Furthermore, SPP1-neutralizing antibodies or integrin antagonists currently in oncological development, such as Etaracizumab or Cilengitide, represent promising candidates for repurposing in HO. Finally, quantitative assessment of ECM properties may establish novel biomarkers for HO staging and therapeutic monitoring, mirroring the evolving role of oncomatrix characterization in precision oncology.
There are several limitations that should be acknowledged here. Firstly, in this present study, we used Lyz2-Cre mice as the tool mice. In fact, the Lyz2-Cre mice model has been widely established and was selected based on its common use in the field for studying macrophage-specific functions in numerous high-impact studies [ , – ]. However, the use of constitutive Lyz2-Cre may affect other myeloid cells, such as neutrophils [ ]. Therefore, we cannot formally rule out potential contributions from other Lyz2-expressing cells. Future studies employing more selective models, such as Cx3cr1-CreERT2 or F4/80-CreERT2, would be valuable to further refine the cellular specificity of our findings [ , , ]. Secondly, while our data demonstrated that Cilengitide exhibits optimal affinity for ITGαV and ITGβ5 receptors, and that knockdown of these genes most effectively blocks SPP1-induced chondrogenic differentiation, we acknowledge the potential involvement of other integrin receptors, such as ITGβ3. Future studies employing more selective αvβ5 inhibitors or MPC-specific genetic knockdown of Itgb5 would help further validate the precise contribution of this integrin heterodimer. Thirdly, our study revealed compelling evidence for the involvement of the SPP1-integrin axis in both OPLL and OLF pathogenesis. However, the current findings primarily rely on bioinformatic exploration and immunohistochemical confirmation from clinical specimens. Although we have mechanistically validated this signaling pathway in our tendon-derived HO model, direct in vivo evidence demonstrating the therapeutic efficacy of targeting the SPP1-integrin axis in OPLL or OLF animal models is still lacking. Future studies employing genetically modified mouse models of spinal ligament ossification, combined with localized delivery of SPP1-neutralizing antibodies or integrin antagonists, will be essential to establish the translational potential of this pathway across different ectopic ossification disorders. Finally, although we have demonstrated concurrent upregulation of SPP1, integrin receptors, and chondrogenic markers in human OPLL and OLF samples through transcriptomic analysis and IF staining, our current data lack spatial resolution to definitively colocalize these components at the cellular level. The inability to perform multiplex immunohistochemistry or spatial transcriptomics on these clinical specimens prevents us from directly visualizing SPP1+ macrophages in proximity to integrin-expressing MPCs within PLL and LF tissue. Furthermore, the limited availability of well-preserved human HO samples containing all pathological stages restricted our analysis to demonstrating association rather than spatial interaction. Future studies comprehensively characterized human HO cohorts will be essential to validate the precise cellular interactions proposed in our mechanistic model.

## Conclusion
Altogether, our findings revealed a novel crosstalk between MPC and macrophages via SPP1-integrin receptors axis-mediated chondrogenic differentiation of MPCs during HO, which may be a potential target for a medical intervention to treat HO.
