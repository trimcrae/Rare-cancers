## Background
As the primary source of glucose during fasting, hepatic gluconeogenesis is rigorously regulated to maintain euglycemia. Abnormal gluconeogenesis in the liver can lead to hyperglycemia, a key diagnostic marker and the primary pathological contributor to type 2 diabetes (T2D) and metabolic disorders. Hepatic nuclear factor-4 (HNF4α) is an important regulator of gluconeogenesis. In this study, we identify leupaxin (LPXN) as a novel coactivator for HNF4α. Although previous studies have shown that LPXN is highly correlated with cancer types such as B-cell differentiation and hepatocellular carcinoma progression, the role of LPXN in gluconeogenesis remains unknown.

## Methods
We initially used protein pull-down assays, mass spectrometry and luciferase assays to identify the coactivator that interacts with HNF4α in gluconeogenesis. We further leveraged cell cultures and mouse models to validate the functional importance of molecular pathway during gluconeogenesis by using adenovirus-mediated overexpression and adeno-associated virus shRNA–mediated knockdown both in vivo and ex vivo, such as in ob/db/DIO mice, HepG2 and primary hepatocytes. Following, we used CUT&Tag and chip qPCR to identify the LPXN-mediated mechanisms underlying the observed abnormal gluconeogenesis. Additionally, we assessed the translational relevance of our findings using human liver tissues from both healthy donors and patients with obesity/type 2 diabetes.

## Results
We found that LPXN interacts with HNF4α to participate in gluconeogenesis. Knockdown of LPXN expression in the liver effectively enhanced glucose metabolism, while its overexpression in the liver effectively inhibited it. Mechanistically, LPXN could translocate into the nucleus and was essential for regulating gluconeogenesis by binding to the PEPCK promoter, which controlled the expression of an enzyme involved in gluconeogenesis, mainly through the Gcg-cAMP-PKA pathway. Additionally, LPXN expression was found to be increased in the livers of patients with steatosis and diabetes, supporting a pathological role of LPXN.

## Conclusions
Taken together, our study provides evidence that LPXN plays a critical role in modulating hepatic gluconeogenesis, thereby reinforcing the fact that targeting LPXN may be a potential approach for the treatment of diabetes and metabolic disorders.

## Introduction
Diabetes mellitus is among the most prevalent and rapidly growing diseases worldwide, requiring effective and durable treatments [ , , ]. Hepatic gluconeogenesis is the primary contributor to increased glucose production in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D). Therefore, numerous molecular pathways in the liver contributing to glucose production can be potential pharmacological targets to reduce blood glucose levels. Consequently, efforts are underway to develop drugs that directly inhibit metabolic enzymes involved in gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis [ , ].
Systemic glucose homeostasis involves a delicate balance between glucose production in the liver and its uptake by peripheral tissues [ , , ]. Hepatic glucose production occurs through two distinct pathways, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, with gluconeogenesis being a major contributor to increased glucose levels in patients with end-stage diabetes [ , , ]. Both pathways are primarily controlled by glucagon (Gcg) and insulin, which upregulate and downregulate glucose production, respectively [ , ]. Hepatic nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α) is a critical transcription factor that plays a key role in gluconeogenesis. It regulates the expression of several enzymes essential for glucose production, including phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase). HNF4α functions by binding to specific DNA sequences in the promoter regions of these enzymes, enhancing their transcription and expression, thereby promoting gluconeogenesis [ , , ]. Additionally, HNF4α collaborates with other transcription factors, such as forkhead box O1 and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α, in the liver to regulate the expression of gluconeogenesis-related genes [ , , ].
Moreover, the interaction between cell adhesion molecules and metabolic processes is an emerging area of research, particularly with respect to leupaxin (LPXN), which is homologous to the focal adhesion protein paxillin. As a transcriptional coactivator, LPXN promotes cell migration, adhesion, and invasion in various cancers [ , , , , , , ]. Members of the paxillin family, including LPXN, are characterized by their lipid droplet and LIM protein–protein interaction domains, which are key mediators of intracellular signaling. These structural domains allow attachment to the cytoplasmic structural domains of integrins, facilitating the transduction of extracellular signals into cells. Moreover, paxillin family proteins translocate to the nucleus and form a complex regulatory network with several steroid hormone receptors and transcription factors. These interactions strongly influence the transcriptional regulation of genes in response to external stimuli [ , , , , ]. However, the relevance of LPXN in metabolic disorders and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly defined.
Here, we identified LPXN as a novel coactivator that interacts with HNF4α to participate in gluconeogenesis. Our results revealed LPXN as an essential coactivator of HNF4α, suggesting a regulatory role of LPXN in hepatic gluconeogenesis and glucose metabolism, which may be used to develop novel therapies for metabolic disorders, including T2D.

## Methods
2.1

## Human liver samples
Liver samples were obtained through surgical biopsies from five patients with fatty liver disease and fourteen patients with both Type 2 diabetes and fatty liver disease. Additionally, eleven healthy livers were collected from cadaver donations. The study protocol was approved by the Shanghai General Hospital (2019SQ189) and conducted in accordance with the Belmont Report and the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.2

## Mice and treatment
Seven-week-old male db/db mice (weight, approximately 36 g), ob/ob mice (weight, approximately 35 g), C57BL/6J mice (weight, approximately 22 g), and high-fat diet (HFD) mice (age, 6 months; weight, approximately 49 g) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The mice were bred and maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions (temperature, 22 °C ± 1 °C; humidity, 40%–70%, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and food. The protocols for experiments involving mice were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital (SYXK-2021AW061).
Adenoviruses expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) and LPXN (ADV-GFP and ADV-LPXN, respectively), with a full-length LPXN or GFP cDNA coding sequence, were obtained from GeneChem Technologies (Shanghai, China). Overexpression (OE) of hepatic LPXN or GFP was achieved by tail vein injection of the corresponding virus (2 × 109 plaque-forming units) into normal C57BL/6 mice. Glucose tolerance tests (GTTs), pyruvate tolerance tests (PTTs), and fasting tests were performed on days 7, 10, and 13, respectively. Two weeks after infection, anesthetized mice were fasted overnight and their livers and sera were collected and immediately frozen at −80 °C for further analyses.
A control adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against a negative control sequence (AAV-shNC) and adeno-associated virus encoding Lpxn short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (AAV-shLPXN) were obtained from the HANBIO Technology Company (Shanghai, China) to generate Lpxn knockdown (KD). The sense sequence for the Lpxn shRNA was 5′-GCTCCATTCAAATCTTCAGAT-3′. The top and bottom strand of the shRNA sequence were 5′-AATTCGCTCCATTCAAATCTTCAGATctcgagATCTGAAGATTTGAATGGAGCTTTTTTG-3′ and 5′-GATCCAAAAAAGCTCCATTCAAATCTTCAGATctcgagATCTGAAGATTTGAATGGAGCG-3′.
C57BL/6J mice were intravenously injected with AAV-shLPXN (2 × 1011 plaque-forming units) or control AAV-shNC (2 × 1011 plaque-forming units) via the tail vein. GTTs, insulin tolerance tests (ITTs), PTTs, and fasting tests were performed at weeks 4, 5, 6, and 7. Blood glucose levels were measured in ob/ob, db/db, HFD-induced obese mice, and C57BL/6J mice under feeding conditions and after fasting for 6 or 9 h.
Subsequently, overnight fasted mice were weighed and anesthetized via CO2 inhalation. Whole blood was collected from the eye socket, and liver tissue was collected and weighed. Mice were euthanized through cervical dislocation. Liver tissue and serum samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C for further analysis.

2.3

## In vivo glucose, insulin, and pyruvate tolerance tests
Glucose (1.5 g/kg for C57BL/6J mice and 1 g/kg for obese and diabetic mice), sodium pyruvate in saline (1 g/kg for C57BL/6J and 2 g/kg for obese and diabetic mice) or insulin (0.75 U/kg for C57BL/6J and 1.5 U/kg for obese and diabetic mice) was administered via intraperitoneal injection after overnight (GTT and PTT) or 6-h (ITT) fasting for the GTT, PTT, and ITT. Glucose levels in the tail vein were measured 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after loading. Blood glucose measurements were performed using a blood glucose monitor (FreeStyle Optium Neo; Abbott).

2.4

## Enzyme activity in serum and hepatic tissue
Serum Gcg, insulin, triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol (T-CHO) levels were measured using the Mouse Glucagon ELISA (#ml057708; Mlbio), the Mouse Insulin ELISA (#ml001983; Mlbio), Triglyceride Assay (#A110-1-1), and Total Cholesterol Assay kits (#A111-1-1), respectively. Serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were measured using the ALT/GPT (#C009-2-1; Nanjing Jiancheng) and AST/GOT (#C010-2-1; Nanjing Jiancheng) activity test kits, respectively. Glucose production was measured using a Glucose Assay Kit (#A154-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng). PEPCK and G6Pase activity assays in mouse liver tissue and serum were performed using the PEPCK Activity Kit (#ml037624; Mlbio) and G6Pase Activity Kit (#ml037561; Mlbio), respectively. All tests were conducted according to the manufacturer's instructions.

2.5

## Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractionation
Mice were fasted for 4 h and injected intraperitoneally with Gcg (100 μg/kg body weight) to assess Gcg-induced nuclear translocation in the liver. Blood glucose measurements were performed 30 min, 2 h, and 3 h later. Liver tissues were rapidly excised, and cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were isolated using a commercial kit (78835; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The effect of protein kinase A (PKA) on the liver nuclear translocation of LPXN was investigated by treating mice with Gcg (100 mg/kg) for 30 min with or without pretreatment with the PKA inhibitor H-89 (5 mg/kg) for 2 h. Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were isolated for western blot (WB) analysis.

2.6

## Protein isolation and western blotting
Total protein was extracted from liver tissues using protein lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitor cocktails (cOmplete ULTRA Mini, Roche) and a phosphatase inhibitor (PhosSTOP™, Roche). The total protein was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23229, Thermo). Protein extracts were resolved via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Primary antibodies used were against: LPXN (catalog #PA5-79610, diluted 1:1000; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), PCK1 (catalog #12940, diluted 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technologies (CST), Beverly, MA), GAPDH (catalog #2118, diluted 1:1000; CST, Beverly, MA), α-tubulin (ab7291, diluted 1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and β-tubulin (ab6046, diluted 1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (catalog #7076, diluted 1:5000; CST, Beverly, MA) and anti-rabbit IgG (catalog #7074, diluted 1:5000; CST, Beverly, MA) secondary antibodies. Immunoreactive proteins were detected using the Immobilon Western HRP substrate (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The blots were developed and imaged using an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare). The relative density of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

2.7

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using an RNeasy Mini Kit (CAT No. 74106, QIAGEN). RNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop analyzer (Thermo Scientific). Total RNA was transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a Quantstudio 7 flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara). Relative quantification analysis of gene expression was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the expression level of housekeeping genes ACTB, GAPDH, or U6. The primer sequences are listed in .

2.8

## Histopathological evaluation
Liver samples were fixed in 10% formalin buffer for 24 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5-mm thickness were obtained using a microtome and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff staining and for LPXN immunohistochemistry. Images were acquired using an ImageScope (Leica Biosystems Imaging, USA) or a digital slice scanner (3D Histech, Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary).

2.9

## Proteomic sample preparation and mass spectrometry analysis
Primary hepatocytes were isolated via a two-step perfusion procedure with liver perfusion media (Krebs–Ringer Biocarbonate Buffer, Sigma, K4002) and liver digest buffer (Krebs–Ringer Biocarbonate Buffer with 0.1–0.15% collagenase). Following separation, primary hepatocytes were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °Cand 5% CO2 for 24 h. Then, a protein pulldown was conducted in primary liver cells using HNF4α (Catalog #ab92378; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) as bait and IgG (Catalog #2729S; CST) as a control. The Pierce™ Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Catalog #88804; Thermo) was utilized for this procedure. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to mass spectrometry analysis. Additionally, the protein pulldown samples were examined by WB.

2.10

## Cell culture and treatment
HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HepG2 cells were subjected to fasting conditions, forskolin (20 μM), and Gcg (400 nM) treatment for 24 h, before analysis via quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)-polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or WB to investigate the effect of LPXN on the expression of gluconeogenesis-related genes. LPXN KD was generated by transfecting HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes with either negative control or LPXN small interfering RNA (siRNA; siNC or siLPXN) using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The siRNAs used were as follows: siNC: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′ and siLPXN: 5′-AAGUGAGCCUGGAGGAUUATT-3′ (Public Protein/Plasmid Library; Jiangsu, China).
HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes were infected with adenoviruses expressing GFP (Ad-GFP) (control) or LPXN (Ad-LPXN) for 36 h. Additionally, primary hepatocytes were transfected with siNC or siHNF4α, and an LPXN expression plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 to investigate the effect of LPXN overexpression on gluconeogenesis in HNF4α KD cells. The siRNAs were as follows: siNC: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU/TT-3′, siHNF4α: 5′-CAAAGAAGAUUGCCAACAUTT-3′ (Public Protein/Plasmid Library; Jiangsu, China). HepG2 cells were either infected with Ad-HNF4α for 48 h or treated with or without siLPXN for the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis of HNF4α.

2.11

## Immunoprecipitation assays
HEK293T cells were collected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM Na3VO4, and 1 mM NaF) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Total protein was precleared using bovine serum albumin–coated magnetic beads (Catalog #70024; CST, Beverly, MA). The clarified lysates were then incubated with specific antibodies at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. Antigen–antibody complexes were subsequently precipitated with Protein G magnetic beads at 4 °C for 4–6 h. Finally, proteins were eluted using 5 × SDS sample buffer by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min.

2.12

## Proximity-ligation assay
HEK293T cells were plated at a density of 0.5 × 105 cells per mL and cultured for 24 h. HNF4α and LPXN plasmids were mixed and transfected into the HEK293T cells using Lipo3000, followed by continuous incubation for 24 h. The cells were washed thrice with PBS, fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 1% Triton for 15 min. Blocking was performed using 5% horse serum. Primary antibodies (rabbit HA for HA-HNF4α; mouse Flag for Flag-LPXN) were added and incubated overnight. Plus/Minus probes were added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were washed and incubated with ligase at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing, an amplification reagent was added to amplify the signal. Finally, the cells were washed and stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to counterstain the nuclei. Images were captured using 594 set as the fluorescein isothiocyanate output.

2.13

## Transfection and luciferase reporter assays
HEK293T cells were plated onto 24-well plates and maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Each transfection for the luciferase reporter assays included 100 ng of mPEPCK (−2000) luciferase plasmid, 300 ng of pCDH-CMV empty vector plasmids, 300 ng of HNF4α and LPXN expression plasmids, and 5 ng of pRL-SV40 (Renilla luciferase control reporter vector). The cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and relative luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (FR201-01; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Each group was independently triplicated for statistical reliability.

2.14

## Immunocytochemistry
HepG2 adherent cells were cultured on coverslips in 6-well plates and treated with PBS or Gcg (1 μmol/L) for 24 h. After washing, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min. They were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (in PBS) for 15 min at room temperature and blocked with goat serum for 30 min. For double staining, the cells were incubated overnight with rabbit anti-LPXN monoclonal antibody (1:500, Cat.PA5-79610, Invitrogen) and mouse anti-HNF4α monoclonal antibody (1:100, Cat. MA1-199, Invitrogen). After washing, the cells were incubated with donkey anti-rabbit 594 (1:1000, Cat.A32754, Invitrogen) and donkey anti-mouse 488 (1:1000, Cat.A-21202, Invitrogen) for 1 h. Following another wash, DAPI Fluoromount-G™(Cat. 36308ES11, YEASEN) was added to the slides, and coverslips were placed. The cells were then observed and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8).

2.15

## CUT&Tag chromatin profiling
HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), while siRNA transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen). Each CUT&Tag experiment involved transfection with 2.5 μg of either an HA-empty vector or HA-HNF4α expression plasmid, along with 40 nM of either siNC or siLPXN. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and CUT&Tag DNA was extracted using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (TD904-01; Vazyme). Negative control antibody IgG (Catalog #2729S; CST), positive control antibody H3K4me3 (Catalog #9751S; CST), and target antibody HNF4α (Catalog #MA1-199; Invitrogen) were used in the experiment. All experiments were independently repeated three times to ensure statistical reliability.
CUT&Tag DNA samples were quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and quality assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Next-generation sequencing library preparations were performed following the manufacturer's protocol, with CUT&Tag products used for library construction. Libraries with different indices were then multiplexed and loaded onto either an Illumina HiSeq/Novaseq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) or an MGI2000 instrument (MGI, Shenzhen, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing was performed in a 2 × 150 paired-end (PE) configuration. Image analysis and base calling were carried out using the HiSeq Control Software (HCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 for the HiSeq instrument, NovaSeq Control Software (NCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 for the NovaSeq instrument, or Zebeacall for the MGI2000 instrument. Furthermore, CUT&Tag chromatin profiling bioinformatic analysis was performed. Finally, the impact of LPXN on key gluconeogenic genes was validated through both chromatin profiling and CUT&Tag-qPCR. The primer sequences are listed in .

2.16

## ChIP assays
ChIP assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (catalog # 9003S; CST). Cross-linking was initiated in HepG2 cells with 1% formaldehyde and subsequently halted using glycine. The cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and scraped into PBS. Chromatin fragmentation was performed by sonication in SDS lysis buffer using the Bioruptor (Scientz, Ningbo, China), targeting an approximate DNA size of 500 bp. After sonication, samples were precleared with protein G agarose beads and subjected to immunoprecipitation using either IgG (Catalog #2729S; CST) or anti-HNF4α antibodies (Catalog #ab92378; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C. Cross-linking was subsequently reversed at 65 °C for 2 h, and DNA fragments were purified for qPCR. Primer sequences are listed in .

## Statistical analysis
All experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard error (mean ± SEM), and the data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Two-group comparisons were performed using 2-sided Student's t-test and multiple-group comparisons were performed using ANOVA followed by post hoc tests as appropriate (specified in figure legends). P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1

## LPXN interacts with HNF4α to participate in gluconeogenesis
We used HNF4α as the bait to explore its potential coactivator during gluconeogenesis by protein pull-down assay in primary hepatocytes. Mass spectrometry indicated that the candidate protein was LPXN ( ). Furthermore, immunoprecipitation (IP) of LPXN pulled down HNF4α, confirmed their interaction ( A). Proximity ligation assay (PLA) staining futher proved the interaction between LPXN and HNF4α, as indicated by B, the positive signals (green color) was mainly located near or inside the nucleus where functional HNF4α is typically found, suggesting that the nuclear of LPXN may play a significant role. 
As HNF4α is a key transcriptional factor (TF) for gluconeogenesis, next, we investigated whether LPXN also participated in gluconeogenesis. Luciferase reporter assay, using reporter constructs containing PEPCK promoter, a key enzyme of gluconeogenesis,corroborated that HNF4α enhanced binding activity to PEPCK through interaction with LPXN ( C). Additionally, HNF4α knocked down significantly decreased the expression of gluconeogenic enzymes, PEPCK and G6Pase. Similarly, LPXN KD also reduced PEPCK and G6Pase mRNA expression ( D, E). These results suggest that LPXN directly interacts with HNF4α to enhance its transcriptional activity and participate in gluconeogenesis.

3.2

## LPXN is related to gluconeogenesis in vivo
To elucidate whether LPXN participate in gluconeogenesis in vivo, we performed fasting experiments to mimic gluconeogenesis in vivo. Of note, serum glucagon and insulin levels increased and decreased, respectively, with fasting state ( ). Lpxn expression pattern was similar to that of Pepck and G6pase, both of which increased during fasting ( A, B). Consistent with mRNA levels, LPXN protein levels also increased during fasting as indicated by WB and immunohistochemistry ( C–E and ). 
Similar changes in PEPCK, G6Pase and LPXN at the protein and mRNA levels were also observed in HF diet and db/db mice models; their levels with fasting duration, proved that LPXN was related to gluconeogenesis in vivo ( F–K).

3.3

## Suppression of LPXN ameliorates hyperglycemia and glucose intolerance in obese and diabetic mice
To validate the results from in vivo data, loss function study was proposed following. AAV2/8-shLPXN was used to KD LPXN in db/db mice and showed a 70% reduction in LPXN protein levels in the liver and a corresponding reduction in PEPCK protein expression ( A–C). Additionally, mRNA levels of the gluconeogenic genes Pepck and G6pase were significantly reduced ( D), without changes in body weight and liver weight ( ), compared with AAV2/8-shNC-treated animals. However, plasma TG, T-CHO, AST, and ALT levels were unchanged ( ). 
LPXN inhibition resulted in a reduction in fasting glucose levels in free-fed and 6-h and 15-h fasting db/db mice ( E), accompanied with improved glucose, pyruvate, and insulin tolerance ( F–H). A corresponding reduction was observed in the activity of the gluconeogenic enzymes PEPCK and G6Pase in liver tissue ( I). Inhibition of hepatic LPXN decreased glycogen accumulation in the livers of db/db mice ( J), suggesting that KD of Lpxn in the liver effectively ameliorates hyperglycemia and glucose intolerance.
Similar changes were also observed in ob/ob mouse models. Mice receiving AAV2/8-shLPXN showed reduced fasting blood glucose levels along with improved glucose and pyruvate tolerance ( ). We observed a slight but insignificant trend toward decreased insulin tolerance. The levels of both PEPCK and G6Pase were decreased in liver tissue samples after Lpxn KD ( ). LPXN inhibition in ob/ob mice did not affect body weight; however, liver weight showed a 20% reduction, with modestly reduced lipid levels, without affecting plasma AST and ALT ( ). Histochemical and glycogen assays revealed that inhibition of hepatic LPXN expression resulted in unchanged glycogen and lipid droplet accumulation in the livers of ob/ob mice ( ). This may represent a modest shift in lipogenesis versus lipolysis, likely a secondary effect on the liver. Data from diet-induced obesity (DIO) mice also showed similar results. Mice receiving AAV2/8-shLPXN showed reduced fasting blood glucose levels along with increased glucose and insulin tolerance. Plasma TG and T-CHO decreased without affecting lipid droplet accumulation ( ). Collectively, these data demonstrate that inhibition of hepatic LPXN reduces hyperglycemia and improves glucose tolerance in vivo.

3.4

## Overexpression of LPXN increases hepatic glucose production in wild-type (WT) mice
To link the loss fucntion in vivo, we developed gain of function experiments. WT C57BL/6 mice, injected with ADV-LPXN via the tail vein, showed LPXN upregulation at both protein and mRNA levels compared to those that received ADV-GFP. We also observed increased expression of PEPCK in LPXN-overexpressing livers ( A–C and ). Moreover, the hepatic gluconeogenic enzymes PEPCK and G6Pase were increased in the liver of mice that received ADV-LPXN than in those that received ADV-GFP ( D). 
Blood glucose levels as well as glucose and pyruvate tolerance were higher in mice that received ADV-LPXN under both fed and fasting conditions ( E–G). In contrast, the serum TG and T-CHO levels did not show significant differences ( ). Notably, mice that received ADV-LPXN exhibited a reduced hepatosomatic index ( ). Serum levels of AST and ALT were also reduced in mice that received ADV-LPXN ( ). Thus, OE of LPXN may exhibit some hepatoprotective effects. H&E staining and glycogen assay showed that livers of mice that received ADV-LPXN exhibit different degrees of glycogen granule distribution and glycogen accumulation ( H). These data suggest that the OE of hepatic LPXN promotes gluconeogenesis and hyperglycemia in mice.

3.5

## LPXN regulates gluconeogenesis in vitro
Next, we investigated whether LPXN regulated gluconeogenesis in vitro. HepG2 cells were subject to fasting as described in Section . Consistent with the in vivo data, both mRNA and protein levels of LPXN, PEPCK, and G6Pase were significantly increased in HepG2 cells following fasting and treatment with forskolin or Gcg compared to those in control groups ( ). This effect was also observed in primary hepatocytes ( –M), suggesting that LPXN is associated with gluconeogenesis in vitro.
siRNAs were used to target LPXN in HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes, followed by treatment with Gcg and forskolin for 24 h. Compared to the negative control siRNA (siNC), siLPXN significantly decreased glucose production, as well as PEPCK and G6Pase expression in both cell types ( A–H, ), suggesting that LPXN KD impairs hepatic gluconeogenesis in vitro. 
We used ADV-LPXN to overexpress LPXN in HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes, followed by Gcg and forskolin treatment. LPXN OE in both cell types increased glucose production, as well as PEPCK and G6Pase expression at both protein and mRNA levels ( I–O and ). In addition, results of LPXN KD and OE in HepG2 cells and primary hepatocytes under fasting treatment showed that LPXN regulates hepatic gluconeogenesis in both cell types ( ).

3.6

## LPXN coactivates with HNF4α, mainly in the nucleus, to promote the transcription of gluconeogenic enzymes
Next, we intend to elucidate the phenotype underlying LPXN regulates gluconeogenesis in vitro and in vivo. We performed immunocytochemistry on HepG2 cells treated with PBS and Gcg for 24 h. Compared to the control group, the co-localization of HNF4α and LPXN in the nucleus was more pronounced in the Gcg-treated cells ( A), which showed similar pattern as B. Following, we performed cytoplasmic and nuclear fraction study, the results showed during gluconeogenesis, LPXN expression was significantly increased in the nuclei of liver cells from diabetic db/db mice compared to WT mice. ( A, B). 
Notably, not only in db/db mice, but also in WT mice received Gcg treatment, a greater accumulation of LPXN was observed in the nucleus compared with the saline control group 30 min after WT mice were treated with Gcg ( B, C). Additionally, following Gcg treatment in mice, both Lpxn and Pepck mRNA levels were increased. Thus, the induction pattern suggests that they may be regulated downstream of Gcg signaling ( D and C).
We further investigated the potential molecular pathway to regulate LPXN localization changed during gluconeogenesis. As Gcg-cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-PKA pathway (cAMP-PKA) pathway is a classic pathway in gluconeogenesis. Therefore, we checked whether LPXN nuclear localization was also regulated by cAMP-PKA pathway. Attendant with previous data, nuclear LPXN expression was ignificantly increased following Gcg treatment, which was clearly mitigated by pretreatment with H-89, a PKA inhibitor, suggesting that the cAMP-PKA pathway is critical for promoting LPXN expression in the nucleus. Pre-treating mice with H-89 not only reduced LPXN expression but also decreased Pepck mRNA levels and lowered blood glucose, highlighting the role of LPXN in regulating hepatic gluconeogenesis through the Gcg-cAMP–PKA cascade ( E–H). Additionally, ChIP data demonstrate that LPXN significantly enhances its binding to PEPCK promoter. However, with siRNA-mediated KD of Lxpn, its binding activity was significantly decreased, even with HNF4α treatment ( I). Overall, the results show that LPXN is an essential coactivaor of HNF4α to regulate the activity of gluconeogenic enzymes. This interaction not only enhances binding to the PEPCK promoter, but also significantly increases gluconeogenic enzyme activity, primarily through the cAMP-PKA pathway, thereby promoting gluconeogenesis.

3.7

## LPXN is involved in regulating HNF4α target gene expression and various metabolic processes
To dissect the effect of LPXN on gluconeogenesis, we performed CUT&Tag chromatin profiling experiment with HNF4α OE, and LPXN KD + HNF4α OE. Heatmap analysis of the CUT&Tag results showed marked induction of HNF4α near the TSSs of genes in HepG2 cells treated with HNF4α OE, which was diminished by LPXN KD ( A). To further investigate whether HNF4α-marked genes are dependent on LPXN expression, we analyzed the up-regulated genes and down-regulated genes in siNC + HNF4 vs. siNC + Vector and siLPXN + HNF4α vs. siNC + HNF4α. Overlapping differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from these 2 comparisons identified 76 “LPXN-target” genes, which were up-regulated by HNF4α OE, but down-regulated by LPXN KD ( B–D). GO term analysis and heatmap revealed that these genes are mainly related to gluconeogenesis and glucose-lipid metabolism ( D, ), such as G6PC1, PPARGC1α and PCK1 which were all HNF4α targetome, implying a key role of LPXN in the regulation of HNF4α targetome. Indeed, genes peaks were highly enriched at the promoter region in activated HNF4α but not in quiescent LPXN group ( E). Consistent with our ﬁndings near transcription start sites (TSSs), the mRNA expression of G6PC1, PCK1, and PGC1α were significantly increased in the HNF4α OE group, while a robustly decreased in LPXN KD group ( F, ). Collectively, these results indicate that LPXN depletion compromises HNF4α regulation of its glucose-lipid metabolism-related target genes. 

3.8

## Increased LPXN levels are related to metabolic disorders: evidence from human livers
We investigated the relevance of LPXN in metabolic disorders that are associated with insulin resistance. H&E staining revealed large fat droplets in cells in the fatty liver sections, which displace nuclei to the cell periphery and cause polymorphic hepatocyte formation. In comparison with normal livers, diabetic livers exhibited macrovesicular or microvesicular steatosis, with varying degrees of fibrosis, hepatocyte damage, and necrosis. Compared with the healthy individuals, LPXN positive staining in liver was signifcantly increased in both fatty liver and diabetic patients ( A–C). Moreover, both mRNA and protein level of LPXN were increased in the livers of patients with diabetes than healthy control ( D, E, ), suggesting that LPXN may also regulate gluconeogenesis in human tissues and is more active under conditions of insulin resistance (e.g., fatty liver disease and T2D). 

4

## Discussion
In this study, we identified LPXN as an essential regulator of gluconeogenesis. F shows a schematic representation of the mechanism by which LPXN exerts glucagon-induced gluconeogenesis suppression.
Although LPXN regulates diverse biological pathways, ranging from cell adhesion to hepatocellular carcinoma progression, its modulation of metabolic disorders, especially gluconeogenesis, has not been addressed [ , , , , ]. Therefore, we evaluated the role of LPXN in the liver, as it is the major regulator for glucose metabolism and most metabolic disorders are caused by abnormal liver gluconeogenesis. LPXN in other tissues may also participate in this process. Several hormones, including insulin, Gcg, growth hormones, and cortisol, are the main regulators of glucose metabolism in the liver. Gluconeogenesis is a critical process for whole-body glucose homeostasis and is altered in certain diseases. Therefore, elucidating these alterations may help identify novel therapies for such diseases.
We proposed that the effect of LPXN in the liver is primarily mediated through the cAMP -PKA pathway. Gcg initiates the intracellular cAMP-PKA signaling pathway by binding to the Gcg receptor, leading to the activation of cAMP-driven PKA. This indirectly leads to the phosphorylation of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB). Phosphorylated CREB enters the nucleus, binds to specific cAMP response elements, and activates the expression of upstream genes, including those encoding key gluconeogenesis enzymes, thus promoting gluconeogenesis [ ]. Accordingly, we observed that cAMP-PKA pathway activation by Gcg enhances LPXN expression in the nucleus, while H-89 pretreatment reduces its expression. The effects of Gcg and H-89 on PEPCK further confirm the importance of the cAMP-PKA pathway in LPXN-mediated gluconeogenesis. Furthermore, as demonstrated by a previous study, the cAMP-PKA cascade is crucial for the proper nuclear retention of HNF4α, which regulates the transcription of gluconeogenic enzymes [ ].
The present outcomes of KD/OE of LPXN resemble those reported for HNF4α knockout, which exhibit reduced fasting blood glucose, suggesting an important role for LPXN-HNF4α in gluconeogenesis in the liver. HNF4α is a pivotal transcription factor that regulates glucose homeostasis mainly through its coactivation in the transcriptional regulation of hepatic gluconeogenic enzymes such as PEPCK and G6Pase, genes that are activated at fasting and suppressed in a fed state, and involved in the conversion of pyruvate to glucose [ , ]. A combination of HNF4α and forkhead box O1 is required for reciprocal transcriptional regulation of glucokinase and G6Pase genes in response to fasting and feeding [ ]. H4LivKO mice showed lower levels of fasting blood glucose, improved glucose tolerance, and a reduced response to Gcg challenge. They also showed altered expression of genes involved in glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glycogen metabolism in the liver, owing to reduction of HNF4α binding to the Gcg receptor promoter [ ]. Moreover, the HNF4α motif lies adjacent to the glucocorticoid response element (GRE) at glucocorticoid receptor (GR)-binding sites within regions of open chromatin and modulates glucocorticoid action in the liver [ ]. Thus, the liver GR cistrome is remodeled in the absence of HNF4α, and loss of chromatin accessibility at HNF4α-marked sites results in reduction of GR binding to GRE motifs, which causes maturity-onset diabetes in young individuals [ ]. HNF4α haploinsufficiency in maturity-onset diabetes in young individuals abrogates the liver and pancreatic differentiation of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells [ ]. Future studies are warranted to further examine the role of LPXN-HNF4α in physiological conditions in vivo.
We also examined the role of LPXN in the nucleus, as its nuclear localization is important in modulating gluconeogenesis. Since LPXN is a translocating protein, it is possible that cytoplasmic LPXN participates in this process. Accordingly, we found that LPXN translocates into the nucleus and jointly binds to the PEPCK promoter region with HNF4α, enhancing PEPCK transcription. In the cytoplasm, the PEPCK catalyzes the conversion of oxaloacetate into phosphoenolpyruvate, which is ultimately converted into glucose [ ]. Whereas, the siRNA-mediated KD of Lpxn, showed a significant reduction in the binding activity of HNF4α to the PEPCK promoter. The CUT&Tag-seq chromatin profiling findings provide further evidence that LPXN plays a broader role in HNF4α-mediated metabolic regulation, extending beyond its interaction with the PEPCK promoter. Therefore, the interaction between HNF4α and LPXN is essential to further regulate gluconeogenesis. Consequently, future studies should explore the regulatory role of LPXN in the cytoplasm.
Nonetheless, our study has some limitations. First, although we demonstrated the effect of LPXN on hepatic gluconeogenesis through AAV-mediated knockdown, the therapeutic effects of liver-specific Lpxn knockout in mice require further investigation. Second, although we observed a correlation between LPXN expression and hepatic gluconeogenic function in animal models, its role in gluconeogenesis modulation in humans remains unclear. Therefore, a thorough investigation of the gene polymorphisms and variations of LPXN is necessary. Finally, the evidence for the association between LPXN and gluconeogenesis needs to be strengthened through clinical studies, and the therapeutic potential of LPXN siRNA warrants further investigation.
In conclusion, our findings revealed a novel regulatory pathway involving LPXN in the control of hepatic gluconeogenesis. LPXN interacts with HNF4α and acts downstream of the Gcg-cAMP-PKA cascade to promote gluconeogenic gene expression and glucose production in the liver. Thus, targeted inhibition of LPXN represents a potential therapeutic strategy for managing hyperglycemia and improving glucose metabolism in diabetes and metabolic disorders.
