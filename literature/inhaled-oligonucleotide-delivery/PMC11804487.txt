AMH protects the ovary from doxorubicin by regulating cell fate and the response to DNA damage
".

## Significance
Doxorubicin is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent known for its efficacy in treating various cancers, but it can also cause significant ovarian damage. The exact mechanisms of Doxorubicin toxicity and the protective effects of Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) in the ovary have remained unclear. Using single-cell RNA sequencing we reveal that doxorubicin adversely affects various ovarian cell types, including mesenchymal and preantral granulosa cells. AMH mitigates doxorubicin-induced damage by pausing the differentiation of theca progenitor cells, promoting follicle survival, and reducing the accumulation of DNA damage through Id3 expression, leading to sustained fertility in mice. These findings are essential for the field of oncofertility, providing insights into protective mechanisms at a cellular level.
Keywords: AMH, oncofertility, ovary, chemotherapy

## Abstract
Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) protects the ovarian reserve from chemotherapy, and this effect is most pronounced with Doxorubicin (DOX). However, DOX toxicity and AMH rescue mechanisms in the ovary have remained unclear. Herein, we characterize the consequences of these treatments in ovarian cell types using scRNAseq. DOX-induced DNA damage activates Tp53 class mediators across ovarian cell types. In the mesenchyme, cotreatment with AMH halts theca progenitor differentiation and reduces apoptotic gene expression. In preantral granulosa cells, DOX upregulates the cell cycle inhibitor Cdkn1a and dysregulates Wnt signaling, which are ameliorated by AMH cotreatment. Finally, AMH induces Id3, a gene involved in DNA repair, which is necessary to prevent the accumulation of DNA lesions marked by γ-H2AX. Altogether these mechanisms of AMH protection contribute to sustained fertility in mice, offering promising broad avenues for fertility preservation in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy.

The adverse effects of chemotherapy on future fertility have long been recognized ( , ). Over the past few decades, survivorship of cancer therapies has improved significantly, leading to an increasing need to understand and prevent the adverse long-term impacts of these treatments on fertility, especially in pediatric patients and young adults. The standard of care for preserving reproductive function is gamete or embryo freezing, and increasingly ovarian cortex tissue cryopreservation before cancer therapy ( ). However, these methods are not available to all patient populations, can delay cancer treatment, and in the case of tissue transplantation may risk reintroducing cancer cells ( ). For these reasons, there is a clinical need for treatments that preserve fertility (fertoprotectants) that can be given concurrently with chemotherapy; GnRH analogs are often used for this purpose, with the hypothesis that their suppressive effect on antral follicles may be salutary to the ovary, although their clinical efficacy is limited ( – ).
One potential fertoprotectant that has been proposed is Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH) ( ), a member of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) superfamily of ligands that is produced by granulosa cells of growing follicles and is a feedback inhibitor of follicle activation and preantral follicle growth ( , ). Currently, measurements of circulating AMH are used in the clinic as a surrogate marker of ovarian reserve (the pool of quiescent primordial follicles), and to predict fertility and reproductive outcomes after chemotherapy ( ). In addition to being a biomarker, AMH is a potent suppressor of early follicle development: Studies have shown that treatment with exogenous AMH can provide durable contraception in female mice, rats, and cats ( – ). Because AMH suppresses follicles at an earlier stage than GnRH analogs, it has the potential to be more efficacious in safeguarding the much larger pool of preantral follicles and the ovarian reserve from the damages of chemotherapy. Indeed, we and others have shown that AMH may help protect the ovarian reserve during exposure to chemotherapy ( , – ). Cotreatment with recombinant human AMH (rhAMH) significantly reduced the loss of primordial follicles in mice treated with carboplatin, cyclophosphamide, and Doxorubicin ( ). One presumed mechanism of action is the replacement of the negative feedback normally provided by endogenous AMH from growing follicles on new follicle activation and growth, thus preventing the excessive activation of follicles that has been speculated to occur when treating with chemotherapy ( ). However, it remains unclear whether this is the only mechanism of protection, or whether others could contribute to the observed rescue. The protective effect of exogenous AMH was particularly pronounced in conjunction with Doxorubicin (DOX) ( ) an anthracycline chemotherapeutic drug functioning as a topoisomerase inhibitor ( ) and used to treat a wide variety of cancers. DOX disrupts DNA replication and induces DNA lesions such as double-stranded breaks (DSBs), making it especially harmful to dividing cells. In contrast, in our models, AMH was less effective at protecting the ovarian reserve from the damage of cyclophosphamide ( ), which is particularly damaging to mitotically arrested primordial oocytes, suggesting AMH may be most useful in the context of chemotherapies that target dividing granulosa cells of growing follicles ( ). These results suggest that AMH may protect the ovary through additional mechanisms and cell types beyond primordial follicle activation, such as by inhibiting cell cycle, promoting survival, or enhancing the repair of DNA lesions associated with DOX.
In this study, we aimed to explore the mechanisms behind ovarian damage in prepubertal mice following DOX treatment and elucidate the mechanisms by which AMH may reduce chemotherapy-induced ovarian damage, using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). We found that DOX induced Cdkn1a (p21)-driven DNA-damage response pathways in multiple ovarian cell types, and that unexpectedly, AMH may protect the ovary both by inhibiting progenitor proliferation and differentiation of theca and granulosa cells, but also by preventing the accumulation of DNA damage in these cells through the upregulation of Id3, culminating in the preservation of long-term fertility in mice.

## Results

## Classification of Cell Types in Mouse Prepubertal Ovaries.
To evaluate the response of different cell types to DOX damage and AMH treatment, we treated mice (n = 5) at the prepubertal stage (postnatal day 25) with two weekly doses of DOX (3 mg/kg) ( ). The use of this dose and repeated treatment with DOX allowed us to explore both the acute and chronic effects of DOX and better replicate the clinical protocols using repeated rounds of chemotherapy in pediatric cancer patients. To evaluate the protective effects of AMH during DOX exposure we cotreated mice with recombinant human AMH (rhAMH) (0.75 mg/kg, twice daily), a dose we showed previously could help preserve the ovarian reserve ( ). For controls, we also included a group that received vehicle (saline) or rhAMH alone. Ovaries were collected at either 4 h or 1 wk after the second dose of DOX.
 To examine the protective effect of recombinant rhAMH against DOX-induced ovarian follicle loss ( ), we counted follicles at 4 h and 1 wk after the second DOX dose ( ). Both AMH alone, or as a cotreatment with DOX at 4 h post-DOX, produced a significant increase in the number of primary ovarian follicles and a significant decrease in secondary and antral follicles ( ), consistent with the suppressive effect of AMH on preantral follicle development. At the 1-wk timepoint post-DOX, we observed a substantial reduction in the PMF reserve by 59% in DOX-alone treated mice (5.25 ± 0.71/section) compared to controls (12.76 ± 0.92/section, P < 0.0001). Importantly, AMH cotreatment significantly mitigated this loss, resulting in an 80% recovery in PMF (9.47 ± 0.87/section in DOX + rhAMH, P < 0.001) compared to DOX-only treated mice ( ).
To understand more clearly the mechanisms of DOX injury and AMH rescue at the cellular level we used the 10X Genomics workflow, to generate single-cell transcriptomes from ~10,000 cells in each condition: vehicle control, AMH, DOX, DOX+AMH ( ). After quality check and normalization, we removed clusters of cells that exhibited stress response genes such as Fos, Fosb, and Jun, or a high proportion of mitochondrial and ribosomal gene counts, likely due to tissue processing and dissociation artefacts. Clusters corresponding to oviductal or rete ovarii cells ( , ) (expression of Ovgp1) and reticulocytes (expression of Hbb-bs, Hba-a1, Hba-a2, Hbb-bt) were also excluded from the analysis in this study due to their limited representation. After data cleanup, five major cell types were identified based on previously reported markers ( ): mesenchyme (58%), granulosa (GC) (33%), immune (5%), vascular endothelium (3%), and ovarian surface epithelium (OSE) (1%) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We next reclustered GC cells and resolved subcell types expressing established markers: preantral/cumulus (Gatm+), antral-mural (Inhba+), proliferating (Top2a+), and atretic (Ghr+) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The reclustering of mesenchymal cells also recapitulated previously reported cell subtypes: smooth muscle (Myh11+), stroma (Tcf21+), proliferating (Top2a+), pericyte (Rgs5+), early theca (Hhip+), steroidogenic theca (Star+), and differentiating theca, which expressed markers of both early theca stage and steroidogenic theca (Hhip+ Star+) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Clusters corresponding to cells of the ovarian bursa membrane (Clec3b+, validated by RNAish) and Pgr+ smooth muscle theca cells (Pgr+, validated by RNAish) were also identified (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Finally, a small population of oocytes (226 cells) was identified based on germ cell markers (Gdf9, Zp3, Ddx4), but was excluded from analysis because only 2 were detected in the DOX-treated ovaries.

## DOX Treatment Induced Expression of TP53 Targets in Mesenchymal Cells, Which Were Suppressed by AMH Cotreatment.
To elucidate the cellular response of mesenchyme to DOX, we compared the transcriptomes of the DOX and CON conditions ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Differential Gene Expression (DGE) and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the most significantly enriched pathway was the intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway by TP53 class mediator, notably Cdkn1a, Ccng1, Mdm2, Phlda3, and Bax, which were also among the top 10 markers that were highly differentially expressed in DOX treatment ( and SI Appendix, Table S1). GO analysis also revealed a significant upregulation of pathways that regulate muscle cell differentiation (Wnt4, Lama2, Tmsb4x, Ank2), mitotic DNA checkpoint signaling (Ccng1, Cdkn1a, Rps27l, Mdm2), and smooth muscle cell proliferation (Cdkn1a, Igf1r, Mdm2, Prkg1) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B and Table S2). To investigate further the impact of treatment on proliferating cells, which are susceptible to DOX ( ), we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the proliferating mesenchymal cluster using the M2 mouse collection, containing 2,626 gene sets from pathway databases and biomedical literature. GSEA revealed significant enrichment in TP53 targets (dataset) ( ) and in the cellular response of different cell types to DNA-damaging treatments such as Cisplatin, Methotrexate, Camptothecin (dataset) ( ), ionizing radiation (IR) (Quintens dataset) ( ) ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C and Table S3), all sharing common genes involved in the TP53 pathway as mechanisms of DNA-damage response. Importantly, cotreatment with AMH resulted in a reduced expression of these TP53 target genes as seen in the scRNAseq dataset ( ). Furthermore, we also observed enrichment in the WikiPathways Peroxiredoxin 2-induced ovarian failure pathway (WikiPathways) and ovarian aging (Custom gene sets of aging and senescence signatures extracted from Lengyel ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Similarly, the OSE cluster recapitulated a signature of TP53 target genes upon DOX treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E). Altogether, these data suggest that DOX toxicity in ovarian mesenchymal and ovarian surface epithelial cells elicits a common upregulation of TP53 target genes, leading to cell cycle arrest (Cdkn1a), Apoptosis (Bax, Phlda3), Senescence (Cdkn1a), or activation of TP53 feedback mechanisms (Mdm2, Ccng1) in response to DNA damage, and that AMH may dampen these responses ( ).
 To validate the upregulations of the TP53 target markers in the cell types of interest, we chose to quantify the most prominent genes by fold change of significant markers identified through DGE analysis using RNAish and IHC ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). These markers included Cdkn1a, Trp53inp1, Mdm2, Ccng1, Phlda3, and Bax. For each ovary (N = 5), theca layers from three growing follicles and three random stromal areas per mouse were selected for quantification (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). RNA in situ hybridization (RNAish) quantification ( ) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G) confirmed significant upregulation of Cdkn1a in both theca and ovarian stroma. Other TP53 target genes (Trp53inp1, Mdm2, Ccng1, Phlda3) displayed a significant upregulation in the theca cells, while the ovarian stromal cells showed a trend toward increased expression, although this increase did not reach statistical significance. Notably, cotreatment with AMH significantly reduced the upregulation of TP53 targets (Cdkn1a, Mdm2, and Trp53inp1) in theca cells. Taken together, these data suggest that the upregulation of TP53 target genes (such as Cdkn1a) in response to DNA damage was prominent in mesenchymal cells following treatment with DOX and that cotreatment with AMH significantly attenuated the induction of these gene targets, particularly in theca cells.

## AMH Stalls the Proliferation and Differentiation of Theca Cells, Contributing to the Resistance to DOX.
To investigate further the theca cell response to AMH, we first examined the expression of Amhr2 in prepubertal ovaries. Both the scRNAseq data and the in situ hybridization confirmed the expression of Amhr2 in granulosa, OSE, and importantly, in theca cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). We focused on the steroidogenic theca cells, which showed a significantly reduced expression of TP53 targets when cotreated with AMH and DOX compared to DOX alone. Upon AMH treatment, we identified a new theca cluster in the scRNAseq data uniquely associated with AMH treatment (DOX+AMH, AMH) and not observed in other conditions (CON, DOX) ( ). All theca clusters (CON, DOX, AMH, DOX+AMH) expressed Prss35, a known serine protease marker expressed in the theca layer of developing follicles ( ) ( ). DGE analysis contrasting AMH-treated (DOX+AMH, AMH) from those without AMH (CON, DOX) revealed a significant reduction in the expression of key steroidogenic markers such as Cyp17a1, Cyp11a1, and Hsd3b1 in AMH-treated theca cells ( ), and GO analysis confirmed enrichment of markers involved in steroid metabolic process (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Furthermore, Id3, an inhibitor of differentiation gene ( ), and known target of AMH ( ), was found to be upregulated by AMH treatments ( ). RNAish confirmed the reduced expression of Cyp11a1 and the near complete absence of Cyp17a1 expression in theca cells of growing follicles in response to AMH (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D and E). Taken together, these data suggest that AMH treatment stalls or prevents early theca differentiation into steroidogenic theca.
 We further assessed the effects of AMH on cellular differentiation trajectories using RNA velocity analysis. In control ovaries, RNA velocity vectors formed a strong directional flow originating from early theca, passing through differentiating theca, and ending at the terminally differentiated state of steroidogenic theca, which is marked by high expression of steroidogenic enzymes ( , Top panel). Differentiation rate analysis by RNA velocity in control ovaries identified this steroidogenic theca state as the terminally differentiated stage in their developmental lineage ( , Top panel). In contrast, when AMH samples were combined in the RNA velocity analysis, it revealed an AMH-specific cluster consistent with a stall in differentiation as their terminal cell state, instead of terminating at steroidogenic theca ( , Bottom panel). These findings suggest that AMH alters the cellular differentiation trajectory of progenitor theca cells, causing them to stall in their differentiation process rather than progress to steroidogenic differentiation.
Given AMH’s role in inhibiting proliferation and preventing cell differentiation in granulosa cells ( ), we next examined the expression of proliferation markers Mki67 and Ccne2 in proliferating mesenchymal cells in the scRNASeq dataset. Our analysis confirmed the downregulation of these markers ( ). We further examined AMH’s antiproliferative effect by quantifying the expression of Ki67, a proliferation marker ( ), in theca cells using IHC. The proportion of Ki67+ theca cells was lower in DOX than CON, suggesting DOX may eliminate actively dividing cells. While AMH treatment also resulted in a lower percentage of Ki67+ cells at 4 h, we speculate this was due to temporary inhibition of theca proliferation rather than cell death (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Supporting this interpretation, 1-wk after the final AMH treatment, AMH-treated theca cells had a higher rate of proliferation than controls, including in ovaries cotreated with DOX and AMH, suggesting they survived and resumed proliferation after treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E).
Altogether, these observations suggest that AMH may temporarily inhibit progenitor theca cell proliferation and stall their differentiation toward the terminal steroidogenic cell state ( ). This may impart temporary protection of follicles from DOX-induced damage, which would otherwise eliminate proliferating early theca cells. Following cessation of AMH treatment, theca cells can resume their proliferation and support follicular development.

## DOX Treatment Induced TP53 Targets in Preantral Granulosa Cells, Which Were Rescued by AMH.
Given that preantral granulosa cells are highly regulated by AMH, as evidenced by the distinct shifts in clusters of AMH-treated samples by scRNA-seq ( ), we aimed to understand the impact of DOX on these cells. To elucidate the mechanisms of DOX damage in preantral granulosa cells we performed GSEA, which revealed a significant enrichment of 28 GSEA gene sets (P < 0.05), including notably the upregulation of the TP53 target pathways, as seen in the analysis of mesenchymal cells (SI Appendix, Table S4). GSEA also revealed a significant upregulation of pathways that regulate cell cycle and DNA damage responses including DNA repair, the TP53 pathway, and the Wnt/Beta-Catenin signaling pathway ( ). We validated selected TP53 targets by RNAish which confirmed the significant upregulation of Cdkn1a and Mdm2 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and downstream markers of TP53-induced apoptosis Bax by DOX (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Cotreatment with AMH significantly reduced the upregulation of TP53 targets Cdkn1a and Mdm2, and a similar trend was observed for the apoptosis marker Bax but did not reach statistical significance ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Using IHC, we also validated at the protein level the upregulation of Cdkn1a in DOX-treated samples and the rescue with AMH treatment ( ). Taken together, these findings suggest that DOX-induced DNA damage in preantral granulosa cells may activate the TP53 pathway, resulting in the upregulation of Cdkn1a and Mdm2, and that AMH cotreatment may dampen this TP53 response.
 

## Doxorubicin Treatment Induced the Expression of Wnt6, While AMH Treatment Inhibited Wnt6 and Induced Id3 Expression.
To explore the effect of DOX on cell–cell communication in preantral follicles, we employed CellChat, a computational tool, to analyze intercellular communication networks ( ), and identify signaling pathways altered by DOX. We compared the strength of outgoing and incoming interactions to and from preantral follicle granulosa cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Among the enhanced outgoing interactions, the WNT signaling pathway emerged as the most prominent, indicating that under DOX conditions, preantral follicles upregulated paracrine WNT signals to other adjacent cells, effectively dysregulating this pathway. The WNT pathway plays a critical role in primordial follicle activation and preantral follicle growth ( , ). Given that WNT signaling was found to be altered by DOX treatment in both GSEA and CellChat analyses, and the significant upregulation of Wnt6 by DOX in the DGE analysis, we validated this change of expression by RNAish and quantified its normalized mean intensity across different treatment conditions ( ). Increased Wnt6 expression was observed in preantral follicles in DOX-treated ovaries compared to controls. Conversely, Wnt6 expression was nearly absent in preantral follicles treated with either AMH or DOX+AMH ( ). The contrasting expression patterns of Wnt6 in DOX and AMH-treated samples suggest an antagonism between DOX and AMH on follicle progression, with Dox promoting follicle development, and AMH suppressing their differentiation. In support of this hypothesis, we found inhibition of preantral markers associated with follicle activation and growth Slc18a1, Nr5a2, Aldh1a1, and Kctd14 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) and inhibition of proliferation in granulosa cells with AMH treatment ( ) in our scRNAseq dataset, as well as by qPCR in whole ovaries (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Thus, we speculate that AMH may protect preantral follicles by inhibiting their proliferation and differentiation, which in turn may decrease their vulnerability to DOX cytotoxicity.
To better understand the response of preantral granulosa cells to AMH, we examined the expression of downstream canonical targets of AMH in granulosa cells ( ). The scRNA-seq analysis confirmed the presence of an AMH-specific preantral cluster gene expression signature matching our previous observations in neonatal ovaries ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). In particular, we found a significant increase in Id3 expression, a gene involved in both inhibition of differentiation and also DNA repair. Indeed, ID3 protein is recruited to sites of DNA damage to facilitate double-strand break repair through its interaction with MDC1 ( , ). Using RNAish we confirmed a significant upregulation of Id3 transcript in AMH-treated preantral granulosa cells ( ). Interestingly, Id3 was also strongly induced in the oocytes of AMH-treated preantral follicles (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). We hypothesized that the upregulation of Id3 in the oocytes and granulosa cells of preantral follicles by AMH may facilitate DNA damage repair following DOX treatment.
Finally, to determine whether the follicles suppressed by AMH during doxorubicin exposure could recover, develop, and produce fertilization-competent and developmentally viable gametes after AMH treatment cessation, we conducted a 6-mo longitudinal mating study in adult C57BL/6J female mice previously treated with either DOX alone (N = 18) or DOX in combination with AMH (N = 12) ( ). One week after treatment, the females were paired with males, and kept in cohabitation throughout the study, removing litters at weaning. In accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) guidelines for humane euthanasia practices, females that died or were euthanized due to labor dystocia, a known complication associated with Dox exposure ( ) (N = 10 in Dox, N = 2 in Dox+AMH), were censored from the curve from their time of death to ensure normalized comparisons ( ). The study, which spanned 180 d, showed that rhAMH treatment had a protective effect as evidenced by the significantly increased cumulative litter sizes per mother in the DOX+rhAMH group compared to the DOX-only group by the 6-mo timepoint (P = 0.042) ( ). Furthermore, there was a trend for increased number of litters in the DOX+AMH group compared to DOX alone (P = 0.054) ( ). Additionally, the infertility rate was 50% in the DOX group (4 of 8 mice were fertile) compared to 10% in the DOX+AMH group (9 of 10 mice were fertile), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Our analysis found a trend for increased fertility in several other fertility parameters, including the average time to first litter (DOX = 40.25 d vs. DOX+AMH = 36 d, P = 0.6322), with the first litter occurring on day 20 in the DOX group and day 21 in the DOX+AMH group, the interval between litters (DOX = 39.375 d vs. DOX+AMH = 40.625 d, P = 0.8455), and larger average litter sizes (4.34 vs. 2.35 pups) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S5).

## Cotreatment with AMH Dampened DOX-Induced DNA Damage Response and Enhanced DNA Repair in an Id3-Dependent Fashion.
Next, we wanted to elucidate how DOX-induced DNA damage leads to cell death, and how AMH might be rescuing the DNA-damage response. To examine DOX-induced apoptosis, we performed a TUNEL assay across conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). At 4 h, 24 h, and 1-wk post-treatment we did not observe any signs of apoptosis in primordial oocytes, pregranulosa cells, or granulosa cells of primary follicles in all groups; however, TUNEL staining increased in the growing granulosa cells of growing follicles of the secondary and antral stage with DOX treatment. This was most evident with the increased number of TUNEL-positive granulosa cells at the 4 h time points when comparing the control and DOX-treated ovaries (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In contrast, AMH-DOX cotreatment significantly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive granulosa compared to DOX alone at the 4 h and 1-wk timepoints in antral follicles (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).
To understand how the DNA damage caused by DOX is resolved, we examined the accumulation of DNA damage foci by IHC using an antibody marking γ-H2AX, a marker of double-stranded DNA break (DSB). We observed an increase in γ-H2AX foci in the nuclei of primordial and primary oocytes in the DOX-treated group. Quantification of γ-H2AX positive follicles revealed that AMH cotreatment with Doxorubicin significantly reduced the accumulation of γ-H2AX in oocytes of primordial and primary follicles ( ). In secondary or larger follicles, γ-H2AX was detected in granulosa cells but not in oocytes, suggesting differential susceptibility of germ cells and granulosa cells to DOX-induced DNA damage, which varied by follicle stage. To better understand how granulosa cells of growing follicles may be responding to DNA damage we quantified the ratio of positive granulosa cells per follicle and found a trend for an increased proportion of γ-H2AX positive granulosa cells in all follicle types following DOX treatment, reaching statistical significance in secondary follicles. Importantly, we also observed a trend for AMH cotreatment to reduce the proportion of γ-H2AX positive granulosa cells which was statistically significant in antral follicles ( ). These findings suggest that DOX induced DNA damage in oocytes of primordial and primary follicles and GCs of larger secondary and antral growing follicles, while cotreatment with AMH resulted in a significant decrease in the accumulation of unresolved DSB across these follicle types and cells.
 We next sought to understand whether the protective effect of AMH could be mediated directly on GCs or required other cell types of the follicle (oocyte, theca) to be present. We isolated mitotically active undifferentiated GCs from early-growing follicles collected from ovaries following diethylstilbestrol (DES) stimulation to promote preantral follicle growth in prepubertal day 21 mice. We then treated these cells with either DOX only (0.5 μM for 48 h) or with AMH (10 μg/mL for 76 h including 24 h pretreatment) or vehicle control in culture, and evaluated their ability to clear DNA damage by measuring γ-H2AX as a marker of unresolved DSBs. Similarly to our in vivo results, GCs in culture also had a significantly reduced γ-H2AX accumulation in DOX+rhAMH treated GCs compared to the DOX alone group, as indicated by the percentage of γ-H2AX positive cells ( ). These data suggest AMH can directly promote the resolution γ-H2AX foci in growing granulosa cells exposed to DOX.
To understand how GCs recover from DOX damage we compared AMH+DOX cotreated cells to DOX alone in a clonogenic assay. This allowed us to pretreat cells with AMH (or vehicle) for 24 h, add DOX for 48 h, and monitor their long-term recovery by the number and size of colonies, reflecting clonogenic cell survival, recovery, and proliferation in drug-free medium for 10 d. Total colony growth was quantified by crystal violet staining and absorbance, which was increased in the DOX+AMH group compared to DOX alone (DOX 0.1 µM vs. DOX 0.1 µM + AMH 10 μg/mL, 1.69-fold increase, P < 0.001; DOX 0.5 µM vs. DOX 0.5 µM + AMH 10 μg/mL, 1.42-fold increase, P < 0.05) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Interestingly, AMH pretreatment alone also conferred a subsequent advantage in cell growth (albeit less pronounced) compared to control-PBS-treated cells, even in the absence of drugs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), which parallels our observation of rebound in theca cell progenitors following treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). These data suggest that cotreatment with AMH confers a survival and proliferative advantage to GCs following DOX exposure.
Since Id3 is a bona fide AMH target and was previously shown to facilitate DSB repair ( , ), and DOX is known to induce this type of DNA damage, we sought to determine whether Id3 induction could explain some of the protective effects of AMH. To test this hypothesis we again used primary mouse granulosa cells treated in vitro with an siRNA to knock-down Id3 expression. We confirmed increased ID3 protein expression in the nuclei of GCs after AMH treatment, which was correlated with a reduction in γ-H2AX by immunofluorescent staining following treatment with 0.5 µM of DOX ( ). We also observed an AMH-dependent increase of Id3 transcript, which could be knocked down using an siRNA ( ). Image analysis of immunofluorescence staining showed that treatment with 0.5 µM of DOX induced an accumulation of γ-H2AX staining, which was significantly reduced by treatment with AMH. However, this γ-H2AX accumulation could be significantly rescued by knocking down Id3 ( ), suggesting AMH promotes resolution of DNA damage in part through the upregulation of Id3.

## Discussion
In this study, we report a single-cell atlas describing the ovarian toxicity of Doxorubicin in mice. We used this model to document the response of various ovarian cell types to DOX and/or AMH. We focused on this particular chemotherapy because our previous study suggested that gene therapy and recombinant AMH protein were most effective at protecting the ovarian reserve of mice treated with repeated dosing of DOX ( ).
The mechanism of cytotoxicity of DOX is via the generation of DNA damage, by acting as an intercalating agent that disrupts topoisomerases and causes DNA adducts, single-strand breaks, and double-strand breaks, particularly in actively dividing cancer cells ( ). However, these same types of DNA lesions can also occur in other benign cells, contributing to severe side effects including cardiotoxicity ( ) and fertotoxicity ( , ). In the context of the ovary, genotoxic chemotherapies such as Doxorubicin are thought to contribute to accelerated loss of ovarian reserve through apoptosis of the highly susceptible primordial oocyte ( – ), and/or by accelerating the rate of activation of primordial follicles. It has been suggested that chemotherapy itself may be activating follicles ( ) or that this process could be precipitated by loss of negative feedback inhibitors such as AMH, which are depleted by treatment ( ). However, less is known about the mechanisms of Doxorubicin toxicity across other ovarian cells ( ), and whether the deterioration of the ovarian mesenchyme could in turn adversely impact fertility ( ). Similarly, while there is a presumption that AMH may protect the ovarian reserve by reducing the rate of activation ( , , , ), its salutary effects on growing follicles or ovarian mesenchyme in the context of chemotherapy have been relatively unexplored. Herein, we identified DNA-damage response pathways consistently activated by DOX across various cell types of the ovary and propose multiple protective mechanisms of AMH that may be cell-type specific and contribute to the preservation of ovarian health and fertility ( ).
 The response to DNA damage includes recruitment of the repair machinery to the break site marked by γ-H2AX, signaling of the checkpoint kinases CHK1 and CHK2, and if unresolved, activation of TP53 and its downstream target p21 (Cdkn1a) ( , ), which leads to cell cycle arrest, senescence, and apoptosis ( , ). In the ovary, we observed a consistent upregulation of Cdkn1a following repeated exposure to DOX, suggesting the resulting DNA damage activates the TP53 pathway across ovarian cell types. Other downstream effectors were also induced by DOX including Ccng1, Mdm2, Phlda3, and ultimately resulted in the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway markers such as Bax. Ccng1 and Mdm2 are components of a cell cycle checkpoint control axis that regulates proliferative cell competence, DNA fidelity, and survival, and have been proposed as targets for chemo-sensitization ( ). We found that these pathways were most prominently engaged in both the theca and preantral granulosa cells of the follicle following DOX treatment. This prompted us to investigate the impact of AMH on DOX-induced DNA damage response.
Unexpectedly, we found that AMH cotreatment with DOX prevented the induction of TP53 class mediators such as Cdkn1a and Ccng1 in several mesenchymal and granulosa cell subtypes. AMH+DOX cotreatment also decreased the number of γ-H2AX-positive follicles in ovaries in vivo, and granulosa cells in vitro compared to DOX alone. Specifically, AMH reduced the accumulation of γ-H2AX in the oocyte of primordial and primary follicles and in the granulosa cells of larger growing follicles. This finding was unexpected given that oocytes do not express AMHR2 ( ); we speculate that AMH effects on germ cells may result from juxtacrine signaling between oocyte and granulosa cells, whose nature remains to be resolved ( ). Combined, our findings of reduced γ-H2AX foci accumulation, and reduced DNA damage response suggested that AMH may promote more efficient DNA damage repair. Since AMH induced a strong upregulation of Id3 ( ), a factor that plays a crucial role in DNA repair ( ), we tested whether this could be a mechanism of rescue. We observed a significantly reduced ability of AMH to rescue γ-H2AX foci accumulation in GCs when knocking down Id3 in vitro. We therefore hypothesize that AMH may promote the resolution of DSB in part by inducing Id3. Further work will be required to investigate whether other DNA repair proteins may be regulated by AMH.
Another way by which AMH may avert the accumulation of DNA damage in granulosa and theca cells of growing follicles may be by inhibiting their proliferation, and thus their susceptibility to genotoxic stress. Indeed we have previously shown that AMH could inhibit the proliferation of granulosa, stromal, and ovarian surface epithelial cells ( ), in part by stalling differentiation programs ( , ). Taking advantage of our scRNAseq dataset using RNA velocity we found evidence that AMH stalled the differentiation of thecal progenitor cells, resulting in a decreased expression of steroidogenic enzymes. These results are consistent with previous reports suggesting AMHR2 is expressed in theca cells and that AMH may inhibit steroidogenesis in part by inhibiting transcription of CYP17A1 ( – ). The stall in thecal progenitor differentiation was also accompanied by a reduction in proliferation which could indirectly protect against genotoxic stress ( – ). Importantly, the suppression of theca progenitors by AMH could be reversed, with the theca layer resuming growth 1 wk after AMH treatment was discontinued. Protecting theca progenitors could represent an important component of the oncofertility treatment strategy, given their role in follicle growth and general endocrine health ( ).
Similarly, in granulosa cells, AMH suppresses the differentiation of granulosa cells, in part by inhibiting the WNT pathway ( ). Specifically, we observed a significant upregulation of Wnt6, a driver of follicle activation and growth ( ), by DOX, particularly in preantral follicles, which could be inhibited by AMH cotreatment. We speculate that the upregulation of Wnt6 might contribute to DOX damage by further promoting the growth of follicles. The capacity of AMH to reversibly stall already activated follicles at an early stage, as evidenced by an increased number of stalled primary follicles and fewer secondary follicles after 1 wk of treatment, may represent a mechanism of protection from doxorubicin. These findings suggest that manipulation of the WNT signaling pathway may represent a pharmacological target for intervention in oncofertility.
Importantly, egg quality in females treated with AMH+DOX is sufficient to support future pregnancies. Indeed, the protection of the ovarian microenvironment by AMH during DOX exposure provided functional long-term preservation of fertility, leading to an increased number of pups over 6 mo in a mating study comparing females treated with AMH+DOX to DOX alone. These data suggest that AMH treatment at the time of chemotherapy exposure could preserve ovarian function across the reproductive lifespan, which could be crucial for translating these findings into a clinical setting. We speculate that some of these mechanisms of toxicity and rescue with DOX are likely to be replicated with other chemotherapies, but more studies are needed to ensure the efficacy and safety of AMH in humans.

## Limitations of the Study.
The dosage of DOX was chosen to facilitate the exploration of fertotoxic pathways and avoid mortality in mice and may not be directly translatable to clinical regimens used in pediatric cancer patients. The mechanisms of DOX toxicity may not be broadly generalizable to other chemotherapy regimens. Additionally, oocytes are poorly represented in our scRNAseq data, which reduced our ability to understand the consequences of treatments on these cells. The in vivo data in this study was generated using one strain of the mouse (C57BL/6J), although prior analyses suggest similar responses to AMH in nude mice (Nu/Nu) ( , ). Moreover, it remains unclear if the mechanisms of gonadotoxicity of chemotherapy, and the mechanism of action of AMH are conserved between mice and humans. While the observed increase in overall fertility in mice cotreated with AMH compared to DOX alone provides compelling evidence that AMH may help protect ovarian function during chemotherapy exposure, we acknowledge the possibility that both DOX and AMH may have other systemic influences on other organs that could influence fertility at the whole-animal level. It also remains unclear what the relative contribution of AMH is in preventing the accumulation of DNA damage versus facilitating its repair. Future studies focusing on intact cultured follicles will help isolate and clarify AMH’s direct effects on follicular biology and DNA damage.

## Materials and Methods

## Animals.
This study was conducted under an experimental protocol (2019N000068) approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All experiments were performed in C57BL/6J female mice (Jackson Laboratory).

## Doxorubicin Treatment In Vivo.
Twenty-five-day-old female mice were treated with rhAMH or vehicle (saline) by intraperitoneal injections twice daily (0.75 mg/kg/2xday) for 2 wk. The DOX group received two intraperitoneal injections (3 mg/kg) at 1-wk intervals on days 26 and 33. Mice were euthanized at 4 h, 24 h, and 1 wk after the last DOX (day 33) dose by inhalation of CO2, and then tissue and blood samples were collected.

## Isolation and Culture of Mouse Primary Granulosa Cells.
Twenty-day-old female C57BL/6J mice were treated with 100 μg DES to provoke preantral follicle growth, and 48 h later ovaries were removed and GCs collected by puncturing ovaries with a 25-gauge needle as previously described ( ). Following filtration, the GCs were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium.

## Histological Follicle Counts.
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded ovaries were sectioned serially at 5 μm thickness. Every tenth section (25 per group) were immunostained with DEAD-Box Helicase 4 (DDX4) and Hematoxylin. The follicle stage was classified according to previously accepted definitions ( ). Following quantification, the mean number of follicles per stage per section was calculated.

## Generation of Single-Cell Suspension for scRNAseq.
One ovary from per animal was pooled from each group (N = 5 per group) and digested enzymatically, and mechanically dissociated using the gentleMACS™ Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). The cell suspension was strained, and 10,000 cells from each group were collected for library preparation.

## Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Data Processing.
Samples were processed for scRNAseq using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 (Dual Index) and Chromium Controller (10× Genomics). Libraries were sequenced using the NovaSeq S4 (Illumina) by the Bauer Core Facility at Harvard University. Data analysis was performed using CellRanger (version 7.0.0) with standard parameters. The samples were aligned against the mouse reference transcriptome mm10-2020-A and analyzed using Seurat ( ) (R version 4.2.0, Seurat version 4.1.0).

## Differentially Expressed Genes and Pathway Analyses between Different Treatments.
Differentially expressed genes with adjusted P-values less than 0.05 were used as input for GO enrichment analysis by the clusterProfiler package ( ), and the Enrichplot package was used for the visualization of Functional Enrichment Results ( ). GSEAs were performed using GSEA software ( ).

## Cellular Differentiation Analysis.
RNA velocity ( ) was performed using the Velocyto package, with scVelo using the steady-state model (stochastic option).

## Cell–Cell Interaction Analysis.
Cell–cell communication calculation and analysis was performed using CellChat ( ), an R-based analysis tool. The “CellChatDB.mouse” database was used as the ligand–receptor interaction database.

## IHC.
Ovarian sections were stained with antibodies against pH2AX (cell signaling), Ki67 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), p21 (Millipore Sigma), followed by incubation with diluted horseradish peroxidase-(HRP)-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit IgG antibody (Cell Signaling) or HRP-conjugated donkey anti-Rat IgG antibody (Jackson laboratory), and stained using 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) and chromogen substrate solution (Dako).

## RNA In Situ Hybridization.
In situ hybridizations were performed using ACDBio kits as per the manufacturer’s protocol, and as previously described ( ). Briefly, RNAish was developed using the RNAscope Mutiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 assay or the RNAscope 2.5 HD Reagent Kit (RED) (ACD Bio).

## TUNEL and Immunofluorescence.
TUNEL-assay was performed using the Click-iT Plus (Invitrogen). DNase I-treated sections were used as a positive control. The nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342.
For Immunofluorescence the primary antibodies used were LRH-1 (R&D system), pH2AX (cell signaling), Id3 (Santa Cruz), and for secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (1Abcam). The number of positive cells in the ovary was counted using Image J software (NIH).

## Clonogenic Assay.
Primary GCs (104 cells/well) were plated in six-well plates before treatment. Cells were treated with either Dox alone (0.1 μM and 0.5 μM for 48 h) or with rhAMH (10 μg/mL for 76 h including 24 h pretreatment). The colonies were then grown for 10 d in a drug-free medium. Colonies were fixed and stained with a solution of 80% crystal violet and 20% methanol, then 30% v/v acetic acid was added and absorbance was recorded at 595 to 600 nm in the VICTOR™ Multilabel Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). Three independent experiments were performed.

## Id3 Knockdown with siRNA.
GCs collected from DES-stimulated mice were transfected with 50 nM of siRNA targeting ID3 (Silencer® Select Pre-Designed siRNA) or 50 nM scramble nontargeting control siRNA (Silencer® Select Negative Control) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GCs were treated with Dox only (0.5 μM for 24 h) or Dox (0.5 μM for 24 h) with rhAMH (10 μg/mL for 48 h including 24 h pretreatment). Following fixation and permeabilization, GCs were incubated with primary antibodies against γ-H2AX (cell signaling) and Id3 antibody (Santa Cruz) and secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (Abcam) before being counterstained with Hoechst. Fluorescent images were quantified in ImageJ to calculate the percentage of positive cells using the “Analyze Particles” function.

## Mating Study.
Adult female 6 to 8 wk C57BL/6J mice (N = 18 DOX+saline, N = 12 DOX+AMH) were treated with two doses of DOX 6 mg/kg at a 1-wk interval concurrently with recombinant AMH or saline control cotreatment using an osmotic pump (DURECT Corporation) as previously described ( ). One week after removing the pump, female mice were housed with adult males of proven fertility and housed together continuously for 6 mo. The cumulative number of pups per female was recorded during the mating period.

## Quantification and Statistical Analysis.
Statistical evaluation was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.2.1. For the mating study data, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess differences in the distribution of a continuous variable between the two groups, cumulative over 6 mo. For the quantification of in situ hybridization assay, color deconvolution was performed using the Fiji image processing package to separate the red dot signals from hematoxylin staining (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). For quantification analysis, three random areas of interest (from each category: theca, stroma, or preantral granulosa) were selected for analysis on each slide. For the in situ hybridization assay using RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit, the normalized mean intensity was measured for each slide.
