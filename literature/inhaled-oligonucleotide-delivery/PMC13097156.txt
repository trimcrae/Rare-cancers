3.2

## TBC Exacerbates Colon Inflammation Induced
by Microplastics In Vivo
To simulate daily exposure, mice
were gavaged with PBS, PVC microplastics, or PVC microplastics supplemented
with TBC. The dynamic changes in body weight were recorded throughout
the gavage period. The rate of body weight gain in the PVC + TBC group
was slower than that in the PVC group during the gavage period, and
the final body weight of the PVC + TBC group was lower than that of
the PVC group, although the difference was not statistically significant
( A). The disease
activity index (DAI) score was assessed using a simplified scoring
system to evaluate colonic inflammatory activity. At the end of the
gavage period, the DAI score of the PVC+TBC group was significantly
higher than that of the PVC group ( B). Following euthanasia, colon samples were
collected for analysis. Comparisons of colon length and colon weight
revealed that both were significantly lower in the PVC + TBC group
compared to the PVC group ( C,D). Histological examination of hematoxylin-eosin
(H&E)–stained colon sections showed marked inflammatory
cell infiltration in both groups; however, epithelial disruption and
discontinuity were more pronounced in the PVC + TBC group ( E). Furthermore,
ELISA analysis of inflammatory cytokines demonstrated that the levels
of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β,
and IL-6, were significantly elevated in the PVC + TBC group compared
to the control group, whereas the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine
IL-10 was significantly decreased (p <  0.05)
( F).
 Collectively, these results indicate that long-term
exposure to
microplastics induces colonic inflammation in mice, and the presence
of TBC exacerbates this inflammatory response.

3.3

## Preliminary
In Silico Assessment of TBC Toxicity
ProTox 3.0 prediction
results indicated that TBC may possess potential
carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity
( A). In addition,
physicochemical property predictions from ADMETlab 3.0 suggested that
TBC exhibits high lipophilicity and good membrane permeability ( B). Taken together,
these preliminary predictive findings point toward a potential carcinogenic
risk associated with TBC. Combined with our previous experimental
results showing that TBC exacerbates intestinal inflammation, we hypothesize
that TBC may play a role in the process of inflammation-associated
carcinogenesis in the intestine.
 A total of 358 potential TBC-related target genes
were collected
from the ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, and SuperPred databases. Additionally,
8,795 colorectal cancer (CRC)related target genes and 5256
inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)related target genes were
retrieved from the GeneCards, OMIM, DrugBank, PharmGKB, and TTD databases
( C).The intersection
of these three gene sets yielded 299 overlapping genes ( D), which were considered as
candidate targets mediating the effects of TBC on the progression
from intestinal inflammation to carcinogenesis.
To explore the
interactions among the 299 overlapping targets,
a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed
using the STRING database. The interaction data were visualized using
Cytoscape software, and core genes were identified by calculating
the Betweenness, Closeness, Degree, eigenvector, LAC, and Network
scores using the CytoNCA plugin. Genes with all centrality metrics
exceeding their respective median values were selected as core genes.
Through two rounds of PPI network analysis, 13 core genes were ultimately
identified: AKT2, MAPK1, MAPK3, HSP90AA1, PIK3CD, BCL2, PIK3R1, PIK3CB,
ESR1, CASP3, KRAS, ERBB2, and PTPN11 ( E and ). Further PPI network analysis was conducted among
these 13 core genes to investigate the interactions between the encoded
proteins ( E).

## Enrichment Analysis of Core Targets Involved
in TBC-Mediated Colitis- to-Cancer Transition
Gene Ontology
(GO) enrichment analysis of the 299 candidate genes identified a total
of 2841 significantly enriched GO terms, including 2384 biological
processes (BP), 146 cellular components (CC), and 284 molecular functions
(MF). Based on the false discovery rate (FDR) values, the top 10 terms
for BP, CC, and MF categories were ranked and visualized ( F). KEGG pathway
enrichment analysis revealed 285 significantly enriched pathways;
the top 20 pathways with the lowest FDR values were classified according
to the KEGG pathway categorization system and visualized as a bar
plot ( G). Additionally,
KEGG enrichment analysis was performed for the 13 core genes, identifying
160 significantly enriched pathways. The top 10 pathways with the
lowest FDR values were displayed in a Sankey-bubble plot ( H).
GO enrichment
analysis of the 299 targets indicated significant enrichment in G
protein–coupled receptor signaling pathways, synaptic membrane
components (including presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes), protein
tyrosine kinase activity, and neurotransmitter receptor activity.
KEGG pathway analysis highlighted several pathways closely associated
with colitis-associated carcinogenesis, including neuroactive ligand–receptor
interaction, VEGF signaling, and chemical carcinogenesis–reactive
oxygen species pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis of the 13 core genes
showed significant enrichment in cancer-related signaling pathways,
drug resistance pathways, and inflammation-associated pathways. Specifically,
cancer-related pathways included the estrogen signaling pathway, prostate
cancer, colorectal cancer, and endometrial cancer; drug resistance
pathways included endocrine resistance and platinum drug resistance;
and inflammation-related pathways included the AGE-RAGE signaling
pathway and neurotrophin signaling pathway.
In summary, the
enrichment analysis suggests that TBC may influence
the progression from intestinal inflammation to carcinogenesis through
multiple mechanisms, including cancer-associated signaling pathways,
neuroimmune regulation pathways, and oxidative stress–related
pathways.

## Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Analysis
To investigate the interactions between TBC and core target proteins,
molecular docking was performed using the top nine core targets with
the highest Degree values from the PPI network analysis: MAPK1, AKT2,
ERBB2, KRAS, MAPK3, PIK3R1, PTPN11, PIK3CB, and PIK3CD. Docking simulations
were conducted using the AutoDock Vina software, and binding energies
of the nine protein–ligand complexes were visualized in a heatmap
( A). In molecular
docking, a binding energy lower than 0 kcal/mol indicates that
the ligand may spontaneously bind to the protein. A binding energy
equal to or lower than – 5.0 kcal/mol is generally considered
to reflect a moderate to strong binding affinity between the ligand
and the target protein.
 B). The results revealed
that TBC forms conventional hydrogen bonds with LYS48 and TYR30 in
MAPK3, and engages in hydrophobic interactions with ALA46, VAL33,
ILE25, and ILE50. In the KRAS–TBC complex, hydrogen bonds were
observed with GLU64 and ARG69, accompanied by hydrophobic interactions
with TYR97, VAL104, and MET73. For the PIK3CD–TBC complex,
TBC formed hydrogen bonds with ARG576 and THR607, while interacting
hydrophobically with LEU610, LEU613, and LEU103.
 Root-mean-square deviation
(RMSD) analysis was
employed to evaluate
the dynamic stability of the TBC–protein complexes during molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations. Based on the molecular docking results,
100 ns MD simulations were performed under standard temperature and
pressure conditions for three protein–ligand complexes. The
results showed that the TBC–MAPK3 complex remained relatively
stable within 60 ns ( E), the TBC–KRAS complex reached stability after
approximately 20 ns ( F), and the TBC–PIK3CD complex exhibited stable
fluctuations after 40 ns ( G). The number of hydrogen bonds observed
during the simulations was consistent with the molecular docking findings,
further supporting the stability of the binding interactions between
TBC and the target proteins. Overall, the MD simulation results were
in agreement with the docking data, reinforcing the notion that TBC
forms stable interactions with these key target proteins.

3.6

## TBC Aggravates Tumorigenesis in AOM/DSS-Induced
Mice
Previous studies have explored the process of colitis-associated
carcinogenesis, its promoting factors, and potential therapeutic interventions
by intraperitoneal injection of AOM combined with intermittent DSS
administration in mice. B). Similar to human inflammatory bowel disease (IBD),
AOM/DSS-treated mice exhibited classic clinical features of chronic
colitis, including weight loss, diarrhea, and hematochezia. During
the three cycles of DSS treatment, the disease activity index (DAI)
scores were significantly elevated. Compared to the model group, TBC-treated
mice showed more pronounced weight loss, diarrhea, and fecal bleeding
( D), with a
dose-dependent increase in DAI scores ( A). Morphological examination revealed that
the colon length in TBC-treated AOM/DSS mice was significantly shorter
than that of the untreated model group ( C). Furthermore, the total number of tumors
was markedly increased, and tumor sizes were larger in the TBC-treated
groups compared to the conventional model group ( A,B). Histopathological analysis
of H&E-stained colon tissues showed significant inflammatory cell
infiltration in all AOM/DSS-treated groups, with more severe infiltration
observed in the TBC-treated groups. Additionally, adenocarcinoma formation
was observed in all model groups, but TBC intervention further aggravated
AOM/DSS-induced dysplastic lesions and promoted the progression to
surface tumors or tubular adenomas ( D and E). Overall, these results suggest that TBC
markedly exacerbates AOM/DSS-induced colonic inflammation and tumorigenesis
in mice.
 To further assess the expression of key target
proteins in colonic
tissues, immunohistochemical staining for MAPK3, KRAS, and PIK3CD
was performed.The results showed that KRAS and PIK3CD expression levels
were significantly higher in the colonic tissues of TBC-treated model
mice compared to both the control group and the conventional model
group, while MAPK3 expression showed no significant differences among
groups ( C).

## Transcriptomic Analysis Uncovers Mechanisms
of TBC in Promoting Colitis-to-Tumorigenesis Transformation
To explore the underlying mechanisms, transcriptomic analysis was
performed. Sample correlation matrices were calculated, and a soft
thresholding power (β = 4) was selected to meet the scale-free
network topology criterion ( A). Dynamic tree cutting and module merging (threshold
set at 0.25) identified a total of 18 gene coexpression modules ( B). Module eigengenes
were correlated with the three selected target genes (MAPK3, KRAS,
PIK3CD) using Pearson correlation analysis. The module showing the
strongest correlation with the key target genes was identified as
the core module ( C). Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (adjusted p-value < 0.05) identified by DESeq2 were intersected
with genes from the core module, resulting in the identification of
146 key genes ( D,E). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these 146 genes were conducted
using the “clusterProfiler” R package ( F,G).
 Notably, consistent with previous
network toxicology
analysis results,
the chemical carcinogenesis–reactive oxygen species pathway
was significantly enriched. Moreover, transcriptomic data revealed
newly enriched pathways highly relevant to colitis-associated carcinogenesis,
including pathways related to cytoskeletal organization, tight junction
integrity, and intestinal barrier function.These findings provide
new insights into the mechanisms by which TBC exacerbates the progression
from intestinal inflammation to carcinogenesis.

## Discussion
Long-term exposure to environmental
toxicants poses significant
health risks and is associated with increased incidence of various
diseases, including cancer. As a major citrate-based plasticizer and
a commonly used alternative to phthalates, tributyl citrate (TBC)
is extensively applied in building materials, food packaging, and
pharmaceuticals. In this study, we first demonstrated through animal
experiments that TBC significantly aggravated colonic inflammation
in a PVC microplastic-induced mouse model of colitis. Toxicity predictions
using the ProTox and ADMET databases further indicated that TBC exhibits
potential carcinogenicity, hepatotoxicity, and neurotoxicity, suggesting
that it may contribute to the transition from intestinal inflammation
to colorectal carcinogenesis. Based on these findings, we employed
a network toxicology approach to identify the molecular targets of
TBC and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of its pro-tumorigenic
effects.
Through network toxicology analysis, we integrated
TBC-related
targets with colorectal cancer-associated gene data sets and constructed
a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. A total of 299
potential targets were identified, among which 13 were prioritized
as core targets: AKT2, MAPK1, MAPK3, HSP90AA1, PIK3CD, BCL2, PIK3R1,
PIK3CB, ESR1, CASP3, KRAS, ERBB2, and PTPN11. Molecular docking and
dynamics simulations further revealed stable binding of TBC to MAPK3,
PIK3CD, and KRAS, suggesting their pivotal roles in mediating TBC
toxicity. MAPK1 (ERK2) and MAPK3 (ERK1), as key components of the
MAPK signaling pathway, are involved in inflammation and cell proliferation
and are well-recognized drivers of inflammation-associated carcinogenesis. PIK3CD, encoding the PI3Kδ subunit (p110δ),
regulates IL-10 secretion in B cells and maintains intestinal immune
homeostasis, playing an essential role in inflammatory bowel diseases. Other PI3K family members, including PIK3R1
and PIK3CB, have also been implicated in colitis and inflammation-associated
tumorigenesis.
−

 KRAS, a frequently mutated oncogene in colorectal cancer, is closely
associated with inflammation and tumor progression. Previous studies have suggested that synergy between KRAS
mutations and chronic inflammation may serve as a critical driver
of the inflammation–cancer transition.

Functional enrichment analysis of the 299 targets revealed
significant
involvement in multiple signaling pathways related to colorectal tumorigenesis,
including chemical carcinogenesis via reactive oxygen species (ROS),
neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, and the VEGF signaling
pathway. Excessive ROS production induces oxidative stress, DNA base
oxidation, and genomic instability, thereby increasing the risk of
malignant transformation.
−

 Neurotrophic factors such as
GDNF and BDNF play protective roles in maintaining intestinal epithelial
barrier function, and dysregulation of their signaling pathways may
exacerbate inflammation. VEGF is overexpressed
in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), promoting immune
cell recruitment and cytokine release, thus forming a positive feedback
loop that supports a tumor-promoting microenvironment. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis of the core
genes showed enrichment in colorectal cancer pathways, endocrine resistance,
and platinum drug resistance, suggesting that TBC may not only promote
tumorigenesis but also participate in the development of therapeutic
resistance.
To further validate these findings, we introduced
TBC into the
AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated colorectal cancer mouse model.
The results showed that TBC significantly exacerbated colonic inflammation
and increased tumor burden, supporting its role in promoting the inflammation–cancer
transition. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the expression
levels of PIK3CD and KRAS were significantly elevated in the colonic
tissues of TBC-treated mice compared to the control and untreated
model groups, suggesting that these targets may mediate the carcinogenic
effects of TBC.
Transcriptome sequencing of colonic tissues
from conventional and
TBC-treated AOM/DSS models revealed consistent enrichment of ROS-related
chemical carcinogenesis pathways, further supporting the results from
our network toxicology analysis. ROS not only directly induces DNA
damage leading to carcinogenesis but also disrupts gut microbiota
homeostasis, aggravating inflammation and elevating cancer risk. Moreover, transcriptomic data indicated enrichment
in pathways related to tight junctions and intestinal barrier function.
It is well-established that impaired barrier integrity is a key pathogenic
factor in IBD and correlates with disease severity.
,
 Genetic defects in mucosal barrier-related genes have also been
shown to exacerbate chronic intestinal inflammation and promote tumorigenesis.
,

Integrating these results with the observed overexpression
of KRAS
and PIK3CD, we propose that TBC may activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway
via PIK3CD, affecting epithelial repair and immune regulation, thereby
increasing barrier permeability and promoting chronic inflammation
(graduate thesis). Meanwhile, KRAS mutations may impair epithelial–stromal
interactions and compromise barrier function, further contributing
to colorectal tumor progression.

Plasticizers are ubiquitous in human living environments and have
been detected across multiple media, including drinking water, food,
indoor and outdoor air and settled dust, personal care products, and
pharmaceuticals.
−

 Human exposure to micro/nanoplastics (MPs/NPs) and
their co-occurring chemicals (including plasticizers) typically occurs
via multiple concurrent routesprimarily ingestion, inhalation,
and dermal contact. The present study focuses on ingestion-related
exposure; however, real-world exposure commonly involves simultaneous
multiroute inputs. Future research should therefore prioritize the
systematic capture and quantification of multiroute exposures.
Exposure duration is likewise a key determinant of external validity.
In real-world populations, exposure more closely resembles low-dose,
repeated subchronic–chronic scenarios. Prior work on TBC and
other plasticizers encompasses both short-term/high-dose toxicology
and long-term exposure at environmentally relevant concentrations;
these evidence streams should be distinguished in interpretation yet
regarded as complementary. For different
health end points, exposure dose and timing should be discussed by
category: reproductive and developmental outcomes warrant particular
attention to sensitive windows such as pregnancy and the perinatal
period, whereas chronic metabolic or tumor-related outcomes should
emphasize long-term cumulative dose and time-weighted exposure. In
study design, incorporating multiroute, long-duration, and mixture
exposure models will better reflect real-world human exposure contexts
and improve external validity.
In real-world settings, TBC rarely
exists in isolation. Micro/nanoplastics
(MPs/NPs) commonly serve as its carriers while simultaneously coadsorbing
metals and hydrophobic organic pollutants; once inside the body, these
mixtures may exhibit additive, synergistic, or antagonistic interactions.
Epidemiological and experimental studies suggest that exposure to
multiple metals and organic pollutants is associated with tumorigenesis,
nephrotoxicity, and impaired neurodevelopment in children.
−

 Mixed exposure to various plasticizers, pesticides, and other chemicals
has likewise been linked to adverse reproductive outcomes, pregnancy
complications, neurodevelopmental abnormalities in children, dyslipidemia
in older adults, and elevated cancer risk.
−

 In addition, MPs may act not only as “vectors,”
but also produce composite toxic effects during coexposure by altering
the bioavailability and internal distribution of coadsorbed contaminants.
−

 Accumulating evidence shows that such mixed exposures commonly drive
multiorgan injury and disease progression through amplified oxidative
stress, barrier dysfunction, and inflammatory cascades.
−

 Consistent with these patterns, our results indicate that TBC can
promote intestinal tumorigenesis by enhancing oxidative stress. Based
on this body of evidence, we propose a testable working hypothesis:
under coexposure with metals, other plasticizers, or MPs/NPs, TBC
may synergistically potentiate toxicity via two axes“vector–desorption–redistribution”
and “oxidative stress–inflammation–barrier disruption”thereby
initiating or exacerbating related diseases. This hypothesis warrants
further validation in models that more closely reflect real-world
exposure scenarios (multiroute, low-dose, subchronic/chronic, and
mixture designs).
Despite the comprehensive toxicological assessment
conducted in
this study, we acknowledge a key limitation: the lack of in vitro
experimental validation. While in vivo models provided valuable insights
into the inflammation-promoting and tumorigenic effects of TBC, the
absence of cellular-level assays limits our ability to confirm direct
molecular mechanisms and cell type-specific responses. To address
this gap, future studies should employ in vitro systems to validate
and extend our findings. For example, coculture models of intestinal
epithelial cells (e.g., Caco-2 or HCT116) and macrophages (e.g., RAW264.7
or THP-1-derived cells) could better mimic the inflammatory microenvironment
of the gut. These models would allow detailed investigation of TBC-induced
oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, disruption of
tight junction integrity, and activation of oncogenic pathways such
as MAPK and PI3K/AKT. Furthermore, targeted knockdown of key genes
(e.g., PIK3CD, KRAS) using siRNA or CRISPR/Cas9 techniques could provide
mechanistic evidence for their roles in mediating TBC toxicity. Such
approaches will be instrumental in establishing causal links between
molecular targets and TBC-driven inflammation-to-carcinogenesis transition.
In conclusion, unlike previous network toxicology studies that
relied solely on in silico predictions, our study adopted a comprehensive
strategy combining toxicity prediction, target screening, pathway
enrichment, animal modeling, and multiomics validation. This approach
enabled us to systematically demonstrate that TBC aggravates intestinal
inflammation and promotes tumorigenesis via mechanisms involving ROS
generation, activation of KRAS and PIK3CD signaling, and disruption
of epithelial barrier function. Our findings provide novel mechanistic
insights into the inflammation-to-cancer transition induced by environmental
plasticizers and lay a theoretical foundation for future risk assessment
and toxicological regulation of TBC.

## Conclusions
The impact of microplastics
on intestinal health has garnered increasing
attention; however, the toxicological contributions of their embedded
additives, such as plasticizers, remain largely overlooked. This study
provides the first evidence that tributyl citrate (TBC), a widely
used citrate-based plasticizer present in microplastics, not only
aggravates colitis but also promotes the transition from chronic intestinal
inflammation to colorectal tumorigenesis. Specifically, TBC exposure
accelerates the progression of colitis in the AOM/DSS mouse model,
a well-established surrogate for inflammation-driven colorectal cancer.
By integrating network toxicology, transcriptomics, and experimental
validation, our findings reveal that TBC-induced colitis-associated
carcinogenesis is mediated by key molecular targets including MAPK3,
KRAS, and PIK3CD, and involves critical pathways related to oxidative
stress, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and immune dysregulation.
These results highlight the overlooked carcinogenic potential of plasticizer
exposure within the gastrointestinal environment. Moving forward,
our research will continue to dissect the underlying molecular pathways
and regulatory nodes through which TBC modulates intestinal inflammation
and neoplasia, with the ultimate goal of informing risk assessment
and guiding preventive strategies for plasticizer-related intestinal
diseases.

## Environmental Implication
As substitutes
for phthalate plasticizers, citrate esters are widely
regarded as environmentally friendly and low-toxicity alternatives.
However, with the increasing usage of citrate plasticizers, their
concentrations in the human living environment have risen substantially,
leading to higher exposure frequencies and doses. The long-term health
impacts of such exposure remain largely unknown. This study demonstrates
that TBC may aggravate intestinal inflammation and promote tumorigenesis
via oxidative stress induction and impairment of intestinal barrier
function. These findings underscore the potential human health risks
posed by new-generation plasticizer pollutants and highlight the need
for strengthened public health strategies and environmental monitoring
to mitigate these risks.
