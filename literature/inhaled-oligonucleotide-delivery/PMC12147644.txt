## Highlights
•CHL-C can reduce cell viability in a range of CRC.

## Introduction
Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common type of cancer, and its treatment frequently includes the utilization of drugs based on antibodies and small molecules. The development of CRC has been linked to various signaling pathways, with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway identified as a key target for intervention.

## Objectives
We have explored the impact of imidazopyridine-tethered chalcone-C (CHL-C) in CRC models.

## Methods
To determine the influence of CHL-C on apoptosis and autophagy, Western blot analysis, annexin V assay, cell cycle analysis, acridine orange staining, and immunocytochemistry were performed. Next, the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and the anti-cancer effects of CHL-C in vivo were examined in an orthotopic HCT-116 mouse model.

## Results
We describe the synthesis and biological assessment of the CHL series as inhibitors of the viability of HCT-116, SW480, HT-29, HCT-15, and SNU-C2A CRC cell lines. Further biological evaluations showed that CHL-C induced apoptosis and autophagy in down-regulated β-catenin, Wnt3a, FZD-1, Axin-1, and p-GSK-3β (Ser9), and up-regulated p-GSK3β (Tyr216) and β-TrCP. In-depth analysis using structure-based bioinformatics showed that CHL-C strongly binds to β-catenin, with a binding affinity comparable to that of ICG-001, a well-known β-catenin inhibitor. Additionally, our in vivo research showed that CHL-C markedly inhibited tumor growth and triggered the activation of both apoptosis and autophagy in tumor tissues.

## Conclusion
CHL-C is capable of inducing apoptosis and autophagy by influencing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is globally recognized as the third most common type of cancer, accounting for about 10 % of all cancer cases. It is the second highest cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. This form of cancer is most commonly identified in people aged 50 and above . The term “colorectal cancer” encompasses cancers of both the colon and rectum, constituting cancers of the large intestine. CRC can be treated by drugs comprising of antibodies (Alymsys, Bevacizumab, etc) and small molecules (Regorafenib, Curcumin, Trifluridine, Tipiracil hydrochloride, etc) , . 5‐Fluorouracil is a synthetic fluorinated pyrimidine that is used as a first-line treatment for colorectal cancer , . 
CRC formation has been associated with diverse signaling pathways like EGFR/MAPK, Notch, PI3K, TGF-β, and Wnt/β-catenin, which results in cancer regulation, survival, differentiation, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and autophagy , . Since the Wnt/β-catenin signaling system is abnormally active in most CRC patients, inhibiting this route may prevent CRC from developing, progressing, invading, and metastasizing. Hence, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been determined as one of the potential targets for CRC . The Wnt signaling pathway operates through two distinct mechanisms: the canonical pathway, which is dependent on β-catenin, and the noncanonical pathway, which is regulated by Wnt/Ca2 + and Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP). Within the canonical pathway, the activation of Wnt signaling inhibits the assembly of the destruction complex, which consists of APC, Axin, and GSK3β. Usually, this complex phosphorylates β-catenin, tagging it for degradation. When Wnt signaling is active, β-catenin escapes phosphorylation and subsequent recognition by β-TrCP, avoiding destruction via the ubiquitin–proteasome system , , . Conversely, the non-canonical Wnt pathway functions without reliance on β-catenin and generally does not entail dismantling of the destruction complex or the movement of β-catenin into the nucleus. It operates by the direct attachment of Wnt proteins to Frizzled (FZD) receptors and their co-receptors, triggering pathways like Wnt/Ca2 + and Wnt/ PCP, bypassing the need for β-catenin stabilization and its transfer to the nucleus .
Previous studies have highlighted aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC. Natural compounds such as Quercetin, Silibinin, Triptonide, Curcumin, Resveratrol, Epigallocathechin-3-gallate, and Salinomycin nanocrystals can suppress activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC , , . Based on our observations, compounds bearing imidazolyl and carbonyl (C=O) motifs have demonstrated their efficacy against the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in various cancer cells ( ). A synthetic small molecule named DK419 bearing an imidazole ring, a Niclosamide derivative, has been shown to decrease the viability of CRC cell lines. It has been identified as an inhibitor of gene transcription within the Wnt/β-catenin pathway . In 2017, another elegant study highlighted the effectiveness of a benzimidazole derivative named HI-B1 in combating CRC. HI-B1 demonstrated a dose-dependent effect in experiments using β-catenin/TCF4 luciferase, indicating its capacity to modulate this signaling pathway. Notably, HI-B1 treatment increased apoptosis in CRC cells by inhibiting β-catenin expression. This was further evidenced by the suppression of mRNA levels of cyclin D1 and axin2 in CRC cell lines (DLD1 and CACO2) as measured by quantitative PCR (qPCR) methods. Western blot analysis supported these findings, revealing that HI-B1 disrupts the formation of the β-catenin/TCF4 complex. Moreover, the efficacy of HI-B1 was assessed in various patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, where the compound showed heightened sensitivity in models with elevated β-catenin expression, underscoring its potential as a targeted therapy for CRC by impacting the β-catenin signaling pathway . Additionally, HI-B9 specifically reduced the mRNA expression levels of key genes targeted by the β-catenin/TCF4 complex, including axin-2, cyclin D1, c-myc, and PPAR-γ. These results suggest that HI-B9 and HI-B16 containing carbonyl functional groups disrupted the critical pathways involved in modulating the growth of colon cancer cells, highlighting their potential as targeted therapies for CRC . Another study was performed on thiophene fused triazolo-diazepine analog also bearing a carbonyl group, referred to as JQ-1, caused apoptosis and inhibited the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in SW480 and HCT116 cells. This inhibition was achieved by increasing the expression levels of the NKD2 gene, which plays a role in regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Additionally, JQ-1 could attenuate the proliferation of SW480 cells by down-regulating miR-21, a microRNA known to be involved in cancer progression and metastasis. Moreover, JQ-1 diminished tumor size of SW480 in a mouse xenograft model . 
Based on the above observations, we aim to synthesize an active compound that could target the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC cells. To achieve this, we incorporated the active motifs and synthesized imidazopyridine tethered chalcones, and performed various biological evaluations targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and as an inhibitor of CRC. Imidazopyridine is one of the extensively studied bicyclic heterocycles for its notable pharmacological activity, and is found in various drugs such as Zolimidine, Olprinone and Zolpide , . Similarly, synthetic and natural chalcone compounds have demonstrated a wide range of remarkable modes of action with potential use in treating an array of illnesses , , . In our previous work, we have synthesized trisubstituted imidazopyridine ketones 
 ; in the present work, we have synthesized various imidazopyridine tethered chalcones and evaluated their cytotoxicity with CHL-C being the lead. Compound CHL-C was tested for cell viability against various human colorectal carcinoma (CRC) cells. The compound CHL-C, a member of the imidazopyridine-tethered chalcones (CHL) series, demonstrated a remarkable ability to induce apoptosis and autophagy. It exerted its effects in a time-dependent manner by downregulating key components of the Wnt/β- pathway, while upregulating phosphorylated GSK3β (Tyr216) and β-TrCP. Furthermore, CHL-C has shown effectiveness in inducing apoptosis as well as autophagy in tumor tissues derived from mice. In conclusion, this study presents the synthesis and biological evaluation of the CHL series, highlighting CHL-C's potential to modulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as a promising approach for CRC treatment.

## Materials and methods
All analytical grade chemicals utilized in the study were sourced from Sigma Aldrich. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra, both^1H NMR and^13C NMR, were recorded using an Agilent spectrometer operating at frequencies of 400 MHz for^1H NMR and 100 MHz for^13C NMR, respectively, with chloroform-d (CDCl3) serving as the solvent. Tetramethylsilane (TMS) was used as an internal standard to facilitate the identification and quantification of chemical substances in the NMR spectra. Spectrometer mass spectra were obtained on PE Sciex. API3000 ESI-MS, the elemental analyses were carried out using an Elemental Vario Cube CHNS rapid Analyzer. The progress of chemical reactions was monitored using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on pre-coated silica gel G plates.

## General procedure for the synthesis of imidazopyridine chalcones CHL(A-J)
To synthesize Imidazopyridine chalcone CHL(A-J) derivatives via Claisen-Schmidt condensation, Imidazopyridine ketone (1) (1.0 mmol) was placed in a round-bottom flask with EtOH (15 mL) as the solvent. The mixture was stirred for 30 min at 10 °C and 30 % NaOH solution (1 mL) was added drop-wise. After 30 min, an equivalent amount of aldehyde 2(A-J) (1.0 mmol) was added. The reaction mixture was further stirred at room temperature for 8 h. The completion of the reaction was monitored using TLC. Once the reaction was complete, 20 % HCl wash was administered until the solution reached neutrality. Subsequently, the precipitate was filtered and washed successively with ethanol and hexane and then recrystallized from ethanol CHL(A-J).

## Reagents
Anti-PARP, anti-caspase-3, anti-IAP-1, anti-Bcl-2, anti-Bcl-xL, anti-Wnt3a, anti-FZD-1, anti-β-TrCP, anti-β-actin antibodies were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). ## Cell lines
MCF-7 cells were sourced from Procell Life Science and Technology Co. LTD, based in Wuhan, China. These cells were cultured in either MEM or Leibovitz’s L-15 medium, both supplemented with 2 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and kept in a controlled environment with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. The inhibitory effects of these compounds in MCF-7 cells were determined by Alamar Blue assay. Additionally, human normal colon CCD-18Co cells and human colorectal carcinoma cell lines, including HCT-116, SW480, HT-29, HCT-15, and SNU-C2A were acquired from the Korean Cell Line Bank in Seoul, Korea. HCT-116, SW480, HT-29, HCT-15, and SNU-C2A cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium, enriched with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, to ensure optimal growth and maintenance conditions. CCD-18Co cells were cultured in DMEM high glucose medium, enriched with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, to ensure optimal growth and maintenance conditions.

## MTT assay
CRC cell lines were treated with compound CHL-C at concentrations of 0, 10, 30, and 50 µM for a duration of 24 h. Following the treatment period, cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay, as previously elaborated . The resulting formazan product was solubilized, and its absorbance was measured by VARIOSKANLUX at 570 nm.

## Cell cycle analysis
Human CRC cells were treated with 30 µM for CHL-C (0, 12, 24, 36, 48 h) and fixed overnight with EtOH. Subsequently, as previously detailed, cell cycle analysis was conducted .

## Annexin V assay
The cells underwent time-dependent treatment with CHL-C, followed by an Annexin V assay as previously outlined . Following treatment, cells were harvested and incubated with propidium iodide (PI) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-tagged Annexin V for 15 min at ambient temperature. Subsequently, cells were analyzed using a BD AccuriTM C6 Plus Flow Cytometer.

## Live and dead assay
CRC cells were exposed to 30 µM of CHL-C for varying durations. They were stained with 1 µl/ml Calcein AM as well as 2 µl/ml of Ethidium homodimer-1 (EthD-1), and then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Post-staining, the fluorescence signals were recorded using an Olympus FluoView FV1000 confocal microscope.

## Western blot analysis
CRC cells were exposed to CHL-C at specified concentrations and for predetermined durations. Following treatment, the cells underwent Western blot analysis to detect and analyze various proteins, using a method outlined in previous studies .

## Acridine orange assay
CRC cells were subjected to treatment with CHL-C for specified durations to assess the activation of autophagy. Following the treatment with CHL-C, the cells were stained using acridine orange, a dye that fluoresces differently when bound to various cellular components, making it a useful indicator for autophagy. The staining was performed at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the stained cells were observed using an Olympus FluoView FV1000 confocal microscope.

## Immunocytochemistry
CRC cells underwent treatment with CHL-C, after which the expression of LC3, a key protein marker for autophagy, was analyzed using immunocytochemistry. This process began with the cells being fixed using 4 % paraformaldehyde to preserve their structure and antigens. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100, and immunocytochemistry was performed according to a previously described method .

## MDC staining
CRC cells were exposed to CHL-C, followed by staining with Monodansylcadaverine (MDC) for 40 min at 37 °C to verify the formation of acidic vesicular organelles, a hallmark of autophagy. The stained cells were observed through Olympus FluoView FV1000 confocal microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

## GSK3 β kinase assay
CRC cells were treated with CHL-C (30 μM) for (0, 12, 24, 36, 48 h) and GSK3 β kinase assay was performed by the GSK3 β kinase enzyme system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Ethics statement
This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Sing-Health Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee (protocol number: 2013/SHS/870).

## Acute toxicity studies
In this acute toxicity drug study, eight-week-old NCr nude female mice, supplied by Invivos in Singapore, received intra-peritoneal injections of CHL-C at dosages of 5 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg, and 100 mg/kg, along with a control group receiving a vehicle solution of 0.1 % DMSO. The well-being of the mice was closely monitored on a daily basis for any signs of toxicity, which could include changes in physical appearance, postural alterations such as a hunched back, changes in respiratory rate, muscle spasms, cyanosis, and signs of either stimulation or depression. Additionally, parameters such as alterations in body weight, as well as food and water consumption, were meticulously recorded each day until day 8 of the study. On day 8, to assess the systemic effects of CHL-C administration, terminal blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture from the mice. These samples were then analyzed to evaluate the liver and kidney functions, crucial indicators of the compound's toxicity profile. Liver function was assessed by measuring levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT or SGPT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST or SGOT), while kidney function was evaluated through the measurement of blood urea nitrogen (BUN).

## Orthotopic implantation of colon cancer cells in nude mice
After disinfection, a 0.5–1 cm incision was made in the lower right abdomen of NCr nude mice. Using blunt forceps, the colon was gently pulled out. HCT116-Luc, 0.5 x106 cells in 20 µl was injected into the colon mesentery using a 31G insulin needle. After the cells were successfully injected, the colon was gently plugged back into the abdominal cavity using blunt forceps. The abdominal wall was meticulously sutured closed. Following this, the external skin incision was securely closed using wound clips to ensure proper healing and to minimize the risk of infection. The development and progression of tumors in the treated animals were monitored using a highly sensitive and non-invasive imaging technique. This was achieved by quantifying bioluminescence signals emitted by the tumors, utilizing the Xenogen IVIS system (Caliper Life Sciences, CA).

## In vivo experiments
In the study focused on evaluating the efficacy of CHL-C, eight-week-old NCr nude female mice, obtained from Invivos, were orthotopically implanted with HCT116-Luc cells, engineered to express luciferase. Once the bioluminescence signal emitted by the tumor cells reached a predefined threshold, indicating significant tumor growth, mice were segregated into two groups. The first group received treatment with a vehicle solution consisting of 0.1 % DMSO, serving as the control group, while the other group was treated with CHL-C at a dosage of 50 mg/kg. The treatments were administered intra-peritoneally, three times a week, for a duration of three weeks, to assess the therapeutic efficacy of CHL-C against the tumor. Upon reaching humane end-point criteria, which are predefined conditions to ensure animal welfare, the mice were humanely euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Following euthanasia, vital tissues, including the primary tumor site in the colon, as well as the liver and lung tissues, were carefully excised. These tissue samples were then snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent molecular and histological analyses.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tumors extracted from the experimental mice were initially fixed using 10 % neutral buffered formalin. Once the tumors were embedded in paraffin and sectioned, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was conducted according to previously established protocols .

## Molecular docking
Molecular docking simulation was performed using Autodock4 tools . First, the crystal structure of β-catenin (PDB ID: 1G3J) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Preparation of the protein structure involved the removal of water molecules and heteroatoms, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms using the BIOVIA Discovery Studio software. Later, ligand preparation was performed, and the three-dimensional structure of CHL-C was generated and optimized using suitable software. Previously reported ICG-001 was also generated using suitable drawing software and was similarly prepared. Ligands were assigned partial charges and saved in PDBQT format. AutoDock4 tools were employed to perform molecular docking simulations. The grid was prepared around the binding pocket of β-catenin for both CHL-C and ICG-001. The grid dimensions were 40 Å×40 Å×40 Å and the spacing was 0.675 Å. The Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) was employed to search for favorable binding modes of CHL-C and the ICG-001 within the binding pocket of β-catenin. The number of genetic algorithm runs was set to 10, and other parameters were kept at default settings. Subsequently, the docking outcomes were visualized with the aid of BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer and UCSF Chimera.

## Statistical analysis
The statistical analysis of the experimental data utilized an unpaired t-test with Welch's correction to assess the differences in means between two distinct groups. A p-value below 0.05 was established as the criterion for determining statistical significance.

## Results

## Synthesis of imidazopyridine tethered chalcones CHL(A-J)
As in our previous report, we synthesized 1-[2-methyl-imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine-3-yl]ethenone , , and via Claisen-Schmidt condensation reaction, compounds (CHL) were synthesized by reacting (1) with various substituted aldehydes 2(A-J) in ethanolic NaOH at 0–5 °C. Upon completion of the reaction, the reaction mixture was neutralized with 20 % HCl, followed by filtration to separate the precipitated product. This raw product was subsequently purified through sequential washing with ethanol and hexane. Ultimately, we obtained the target CHL(A-J) compounds as a yellow solid, as depicted in .

## CHL-C promotes apoptotic cell death in tumor cells
The cytotoxic effects of the synthesized CHL compounds on MCF-7 cells were evaluated using the Alamar Blue assay. These compounds demonstrated an ability to inhibit MCF-7 cell viability within a concentration range of 8.48–100 µM. Notably, within this group, the CHL-C-bearing trimethoxy phenyl group was more active than other derivatives ( ). The cytotoxic action of CHL-C in CRC and CCD-18Co cells was examined using an MTT assay. This investigation revealed that CHL-C markedly reduced cell viability across various CRC cell lines, including HCT-116, SW480, HT-29, HCT-15, and SNU-C2A; however, it was only slightly affected in the CCD-18Co cells, as illustrated in A. Next, we examined the effect of CHL-C in apoptotic cell death through diverse assays. It was noted that CHL-C induced arrest in the G2/M phase and triggered both early and late stages of apoptosis, with these effects varying in a time-dependent manner ( B and C). The effectiveness of CHL-C in reducing CRC cell viability was further confirmed via the live and dead assay, as depicted in D. Moreover, as shown in E, CHL-C induced PARP cleavage activation. 

## CHL-C induces autophagy in HCT-116 and SW480 cells
Next, the impact of CHL-C on autophagy activation was conducted using a variety of methodologies, including acridine orange staining, immunocytochemistry, MDC staining, and Western blot analysis. The treatment with CHL-C significantly increased the number of acridine orange-stained cells, highlighting its potent effect in stimulating autophagy, as illustrated in A and B. Further, MDC staining, which specifically labels autophagic vacuoles, showed that CHL-C treatment led to an increase in MDC-stained cells over time, suggesting a time-dependent enhancement of autophagy ( C). D and E demonstrate that CHL-C induced autophagy by down-regulating the expression of LC3, a protein that is crucial for the formation of autophagosomes. 

## CHL-C promotes apoptosis by modulating levels of cell survival proteins
Subsequent investigations focused on determining whether CHL-C could influence apoptosis by modifying the expression levels of proteins central to the apoptotic process. A shows that CHL-C promotes apoptosis through the activation of caspase-9 and caspase-3, which are crucial enzymes in the execution phase of apoptosis. Additionally, CHL-C was found to downregulate anti-apoptotic proteins, such as IAP-1, Bcl-2, and Bcl-xL, as shown in B. Additionally, the involvement of caspase-3 in the apoptosis induced by CHL-C was further validated using a caspase-3 inhibitor, Z-DEVD-FMK. The use of this inhibitor effectively reversed the apoptosis induced by CHL-C, as depicted in C. This reversal underscores the critical role of caspase-3 in the apoptotic process initiated by CHL-C, highlighting its potential as a target for therapeutic intervention. 

## CHL-C induces autophagy-induced cell death
Our further examination delved into the influence of CHL-C on autophagy in CRC cells, specifically its effects on the expression levels of Atg7, Beclin-1, p62, and ULK1. Atg7 and Beclin-1 are known as crucial regulators and are vital for the formation of autophagosomes , . The findings revealed that CHL-C treatment led to an upregulation of Atg7 and Beclin-1 expression, in comparison to untreated cells, as shown in D. Also, phosphorylation of both s555 and s757 ULK1 proteins was increased in a time-dependent manner. This upregulation indicates that CHL-C promotes autophagy in CRC cells by enhancing the pathways responsible for autophagosome formation. However, the expression of the p62 protein, a ubiquitin-binding scaffold protein, was observed to decrease. Additionally, the role of autophagy in the action of CHL-C was further confirmed using an autophagy inhibitor, 3-methyladenine (3-MA). The application of 3-MA effectively counteracted the autophagic effects induced by CHL-C, as demonstrated in E. This reversal underscores the critical role of autophagy in CHL-C's mechanism of action against CRC cells and highlights the potential of modulating autophagy as a therapeutic strategy.

## In silico analysis of CHL-C targeting Wnt/β-Catenin cascade
The Wnt/β-catenin plays a pivotal role in CRC development and progression, making it an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. This study involved a computational analysis of CHL-C, aimed at investigating its potential to act as an inhibitor of β-catenin. This analysis aimed to assess the binding affinity and interaction of CHL-C with β-catenin and compare its efficacy to that of a previously identified inhibitor, ICG-001(29).
The results from molecular docking analysis indicated that both CHL-C and ICG-001 demonstrate strong binding affinities toward β-catenin. Specifically, CHL-C was found to have a binding affinity of −5.54 kcal/mol, suggesting a favorable interaction with β-catenin. Conversely, ICG-001 exhibited a marginally greater binding affinity, with a value of −5.82 kcal/mol. These findings suggest that CHL-C, much like ICG-001, could effectively interact with β-catenin, potentially inhibiting its role in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. This inhibition could hinder the pathway's contribution to CRC development and progression, underscoring the potential of CHL-C as a therapeutic agent in CRC treatment strategies focused on targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Key interactions between CHL-C and β-catenin were observed, including hydrogen bonds forming with GLY-307, LYS-312, LYS-345, and ASN-387 with bond distances of 1.73 Å, 2.10 Å, 2.32 Å and 2.07 Å. Hydrophobic contacts (π-alkyl, alkyl) with TYR-306 and TRP-383 residues in the β-catenin binding site were observed. While compound ICG-001 formed hydrogen bonds with ASN-387 with a bond distance of 3.04 Å, π-cation interaction with LYS-312, and hydrophobic contacts (π-sigma, amide-π stacked, π-alkyl) with LYS-345, VAL-346 and VAL-349 was observed. The strong binding modes and interactions observed for CHL-C and ICG-001 support their role as potential inhibitors for β-catenin ( A-D). 

## CHL-C induces cell death through down-regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway
The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has been recognized as a key focus for the development of new treatment approaches in CRC . Based on the findings presented in A, treatment with CHL-C significantly reduced the levels of key components within the pathway, including β-catenin, Wnt3a, FZD-1, Axin-1, and phosphorylated GSK-3β (Ser9). Interestingly, under identical conditions, there was an increase in the levels of phosphorylated GSK3β (Tyr216) and β-TrCP. We examined GSK3 β activity to confirm the effect of CHL-C on GSK3 β. As shown in B, it was confirmed that the activity of GSK3 β was decreased upon treatment with CHL-C. 
To additionally confirm the influence of CHL-C on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and its role in promoting apoptosis and autophagy, we proceeded with the knockdown of β-catenin. This action confirmed that the apoptotic and autophagic effects induced by CHL-C were indeed mediated through the alterations in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascades. Interestingly, suppression of β-catenin and downstream signals of the β-catenin pathway in cells transfected with β-catenin siRNA, has been shown in C. Moreover, the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway enhanced CHL-C-induced PARP cleavage and cell death, as illustrated in D and E. Additionally, the knockdown of β-catenin also promoted the activation of LC3 by CHL-C, further supporting the induction of autophagy, as depicted in F. The ability of CHL-C to downregulate key components of this pathway and the subsequent confirmation through β-catenin knockdown highlight its potential as a therapeutic agent that targets the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascades in CRC.

## CHL-C suppresses tumorigenesis and metastasis in orthotopic HCT-116 mouse model
We investigated the anti-cancer activity of CHL-C in a CRC mouse model. Tumors originating from HCT-116-Luc cells were grown in NCr nude mice, which were subsequently administered intraperitoneal injections of CHL-C at a dose of 50 mg. The results, as illustrated in A-D demonstrated that CHL-C treatment effectively inhibited tumor growth. Importantly, this therapeutic effect was achieved without any significant alteration in the body weight of the mice, suggesting that CHL-C could target tumor cells without inducing adverse effects typically associated with chemotherapy, such as weight loss. This finding indicates the potential of CHL-C as a promising anti-cancer agent for CRC, offering a balance between efficacy and safety in treating tumor growth. 

## CHL-C modulates the activation of apoptosis and autophagy in tumor tissues
IHC was employed to assess expression levels of key proteins involved in apoptosis, autophagy, cell proliferation, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. According to the results demonstrated in A, there was a notable increase in the expression of cleaved PARP and LC3, markers of apoptosis and autophagy, respectively. Conversely, the expression levels of β-catenin and Ki-67, a proliferation marker, were observed to decrease following CHL-C treatment. Subsequent analysis focused on quantifying the impact of CHL-C on the activation of apoptosis and autophagy in the tumor tissues of the treated mice. Western blotting results, shown in B and C, confirmed the induction of apoptosis and autophagy by CHL-C, as evidenced by the altered expression of related proteins. Furthermore, D demonstrated that CHL-C effectively down-regulated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, aligning with observed effects in vitro. 

## Molecular dynamics simulations
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using Desmond v3.8 to evaluate the stability of the β-catenin and CHL-C complex. The OPLS 2005 force field was utilized to minimize the energy of the docked complex. The complex was placed in an orthorhombic box with dimensions of 10 Å × 10 Å × 10 Å, using the TIP3P water model. The system was neutralized with counter ions, and the physiological salt concentration was maintained at 0.15 M. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, and the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used for calculating electrostatics with a 10 Å cut-off for Lennard-Jones interactions. The SHAKE algorithm was employed to constrain covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The system was minimized and equilibrated before running a 150 ns MD simulation in an NPT ensemble at a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1.01325 bar. The Nose-Hoover thermostat and Martyna-Tobias Klein barostat were used to control the temperature and pressure during the simulation . Throughout the 150 ns simulation, the trajectory, root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuations (RMSF), and inter-molecular hydrogen bond interactions of the β-catenin/CHL-C complex were analyzed to assess structural and dynamic changes.

## Discussion
CRC is ranked as the third most prevalent malignant tumor and contributes significantly to cancer-related mortality in the United States , . A key hallmark of over 90 % of CRC cases is the mutation in β-catenin or APC genes. These genetic alterations lead to the stabilization of β-catenin, thereby enhancing Wnt signaling activity , . Furthermore, mutations in at least one component of the Wnt signaling cascade are present in CRC patients, underlining the pathway's critical role in the disease's pathogenesis. Given this background, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has been identified in numerous studies as a promising target for chemotherapeutic interventions in CRC , , , . In this study, we have shown that CHL-C effectively down-regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in CRC cells and in a preclinical orthotopic cancer model. This suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by CHL-C was observed to mediate both apoptosis and autophagy in the cancer cells. These processes are pivotal in anti-tumor research, representing crucial mechanisms through which cancer cells can be targeted and eliminated. Our findings suggest that CHL-C not only impacts tumor cell viability through direct cytotoxic effects, but also modulates key signaling pathways that govern cell survival and death, offering a dual mechanism of action against CRC.
First, we examined the induction of cell death by CHL-C. Apoptosis is a crucial biological process for removing damaged or unwanted cells, and the ability to specifically induce apoptosis in cancer cells is a potent strategy in anticancer therapy , , , . Interestingly, Yang et al., have previously reported that brassinin exerts its anti-tumor effect in CRC cells by promoting apoptosis . We first confirmed the cytotoxicity of CHL-C on HCT-116, SW480, HT-29, HCT-15, and SNU-C2A cells. Based on these preliminary results, a concentration of 30 µM CHL-C was chosen for in-depth studies. Further analyses confirmed CHL-C's capability to trigger apoptosis through a series of biochemical assays. It was found that CHL-C induced arrest in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle and promoted both early and late stages of apoptosis. Given the pivotal roles of PARP, caspase-9, and caspase-3 in the apoptosis process , , we evaluated CHL-C's impact on these key apoptotic markers. CHL-C was shown to induce PARP cleavage and activation of caspase-9 and caspase-3, while simultaneously inhibiting anti-apoptotic factors such as IAP-1, Bcl-2, and Bcl-xL. These findings collectively demonstrated that CHL-C effectively induces apoptosis in CRC cells, underlining its potential as a therapeutic agent in targeting and eliminating cancer cells through apoptotic mechanisms.
Autophagy, a cellular pathway critical for the removal of misfolded or damaged proteins, plays a significant role in modulating tumor growth . It involves the rapid degradation of damaged cytoplasmic components, that can be encapsulated by autophagosomes and then broken down within autophagic lysosomes , . A key player in the autophagosome assembly process is LC3, which undergoes a transformation from LC3 I to LC3 II during conjugation, marking the formation of autophagosomes . Autophagy-related proteins such as Atg7 and Beclin-1 are pivotal for the initiation and promotion of autophagy, acting as markers of autophagic activity , , . In the context of CRC, evidence suggests that the induction of autophagy can contribute to cell death, offering a potential therapeutic avenue. Cryptotanshinone, for example, has been shown to induce autophagy, leading to cell death in CRC cells . We evaluated the potential role of CHL-C on autophagy in CRC cells. Upon treatment with CHL-C, a notable upregulation in the expression of LC3, Atg7, and Beclin-1 was observed, indicating the induction of autophagy. To further validate the autophagic response to CHL-C, acridine orange and MDC staining were employed. The results demonstrated a marked increase in the proportion of cells stained with both acridine orange and MDC in the CHL-C treatment group, confirming the induction of autophagy. Collectively, these findings underscore CHL-C's capability to promote both autophagy and apoptosis in CRC cells, highlighting its potential as a dual-action anticancer agent.
Activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a common occurrence in CRC and plays a crucial role in regulating both apoptosis and autophagy [6, 36, 49, 50]. Previous research has highlighted the complex interplay between β-catenin and the processes of apoptosis and autophagy, demonstrating that manipulation of this pathway can lead to cell death [50–52]. For instance, withanolide has been shown to induce cell death by altering the β-catenin pathway and activating apoptosis and autophagy [51]. Our findings indicate that CHL-C effectively inhibits activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling, significantly reducing the expression of β-catenin target genes. Further, structure-based bioinformatics analysis showed that CHL-C binds with high affinity to β-catenin, displaying a binding efficacy comparable to that of ICG-001, a known β-catenin inhibitor. Notably, the knockdown of β-catenin was observed to enhance the activation of both apoptosis and autophagy. This approach to targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway presents a promising strategy for regulating cell death processes, offering potential therapeutic avenues for the treatment of CRC.
Next, we also confirmed the anti-cancer activity of CHL-C in the CRC mice model. CHL-C treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth in CRC orthotopic mice model without altering the body weight. The administration of CHL-C also resulted in the induction of apoptosis and autophagy in tumor tissues, echoing the compound's cellular effects observed in vitro. Moreover, the suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by CHL-C, as seen in cellular models, was also evident in the tumor tissues, reinforcing the consistency of the mechanism of action of CHL-C across different experimental settings. The significance of these findings lies in the compound CHL-C's dual action of stimulating both apoptosis and autophagy, pivotal processes for controlling tumor growth by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. The in vivo results bolster the evidence for CHL-C's anticancer efficacy, presenting it as a promising candidate for further development as a novel antitumor agent targeting CRC. The comprehensive analysis, from cellular mechanisms to preclinical efficacy, highlights the potential of CHL-C to serve as a foundation for new therapeutic strategies in the fight against CRC.
We provide insight into the synthesis and comprehensive biological assessment of a novel compound, CHL-C (imidazopyridine tethered chalcones), designed as an anti-CRC agent. The compound demonstrates its therapeutic potential by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and affecting downstream regulatory elements. CHL-C was found to promote both apoptosis and autophagy in CRC cells, pointing to its dual mechanism of action against CRC. An extensive computer-based docking analysis emphasized the capacity of CHL-C to bind to the active site of Wnt/β-catenin, indicating a potential direct interference that might block the activity of this pathway. Western blot analyses further supported this interaction, showing CHL-C's capability to downregulate critical components of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Conversely, it upregulated phosphorylated GSK3β (Tyr216) and β-TrCP, indicating a comprehensive modulation of this signaling cascade. Moreover, in vivo studies demonstrated that CHL-C not only continued to induce apoptosis and autophagy within the tumor environment, but also underscored the compound's effectiveness as a potential therapeutic agent in treating CRC. These findings collectively reveal CHL-C's promising anticancer activities through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, providing a strong foundation for further development and investigation as an anti-CRC treatment.

## Molecular dynamics simulations of β-catenin and CHL-C complex
Following docking studies, the optimum binding pose of the β-catenin/CHL-C complex was subjected to molecular dynamics simulations. The molecular dynamics data provide information on the binding stability of the CHL-C in the binding pocket of the β-catenin during the 150 ns simulation time. A depicts the root mean square deviation (RMSD) for the ligand and protein. The plot demonstrates that the protein RMSD remained stable throughout the simulation with an average RMSD of around 3.5 Å. The ligand's (CHL-C) RMSD was stable throughout the simulation with an average RMSD value of 5.1 Å. The slight increase in the ligand RMSD reflects the flexibility and the dynamic nature of the binding site of β-catenin . 
In our simulations of the β-catenin/CHL-C complex, the RMSF analysis (see B) revealed notable fluctuations in the N-terminal region, with values reaching up to 8 Å. Apart from the N-terminal, all the other residues exhibited RMSF values below 3.7 Å, with fluctuations greater than 3 Å corresponding primarily to loop regions. These regions are inherently more flexible due to their lack of secondary structure constraints. The overall average RMSF of 2.1 Å suggests that the protein maintains a stable structure throughout the simulation, with localized flexibility in expected regions .
In C, we observe several key interactions between the ligand CHL-C and specific residues of β-catenin. Notably, CHL-C interacts with ASN287 and ASN326 through water bridge and hydrogen bond interactions for approximately 20 % of the simulation time. These interactions are crucial for stabilizing the ligand within the binding pocket. Additionally, CHL-C forms water bridge interactions with LEU316, ALA317, and GLN322 for 18 % of the simulation time. These interactions further enhance the stability of the complex, suggesting that the ligand is well accommodated within the binding site of β-catenin.
A particularly strong hydrophobic interaction was observed between CHL-C and ALA323, maintained for over 45 % of the simulation time. Hydrophobic interactions play a significant role in ligand binding by increasing the binding affinity and contributing to the overall stability of the complex. These interaction patterns underscore the potential efficacy of CHL-C as a therapeutic agent targeting β-catenin. The consistent interactions observed during the simulation suggest that CHL-C is a promising candidate for further development.
