However, the role of other metabolites in plaque progression remains unclear. Hereby, we observed elevated α-ketoglutarate levels in granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs) of Ldlr−/− mice on a high-fat diet (HFD), determined by targeted metabolomics. On top of HFD, α-ketoglutarate administration further increased GMP proportion, myeloid cell production, and plaque progression in Ldlr−/− mice. The regulation of α-ketoglutarate in atherosclerosis required the expression of its receptor, oxoglutarate receptor 1 (OXGR1), in bone marrow cells (BMCs), as transplantation of OXGR1−/− BMCs attenuated plaque progression compared to transplantation of OXGR1+/+ BMCs in HFD-fed Ldlr−/− recipients. Using targeted metabolomics, single-cell RNA sequencing and validation experiments, we demonstrated that the α-ketoglutarate/OXGR1 axis upregulated the expression of purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) in GMPs, which promoted de novo purine biosynthesis and reduced the levels of nicotinamide mononucleotide and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), thereby disturbing mitochondrial homeostasis and increasing the production of myeloid cells. Furthermore, proteomics data revealed that PNP treatment regulated the redox status by increasing the expression of NAD kinase (NADK), thereby accelerating NAD consumption. Additionally, PNP promoted the transcriptional activation of NF-κB via ubiquitin, enhancing ROS production and inflammation in lineage−/low cells. Spearman's correlation analysis revealed a positive association between isocitrate and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels in human plasma. Overall, HFD potentiated α-ketoglutarate, contributing to atherosclerosis.

## Introduction
Dyslipidaemia is one of the main risk factors for cardiovascular disease. Compelling evidence suggests that dyslipidaemia induces endothelial cell dysfunction and activates smooth muscle cells and inflammatory cells to propagate inflammation, all of which reinforce atherosclerotic plaque progression and lead to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [ , ]. Lipid-lowering medications, such as statins and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) monoclonal antibodies, are used as first-line medications for dyslipidaemia and are beneficial for reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) [ ]. Decrease in plaque volume by 1% conferred a substantial 25% risk reduction of MACE following lipid-lowering therapies [ ]. Despite the reported benefits, the observed mitigation from lipid-lowering drugs targeting LDL-c in clinical trials have been limited. In a meta-analysis involving 270,288 patients from 34 randomized clinical trials, the greatest benefit of lipid-lowering medications was noted in patients with high baseline LDL-c (100 mg/dL) [ ]. Moreover, in another meta-analysis of 92,180 patients with established cardiovascular disease, the time to prevent 1 MACE per 100 individuals was 17.7 and 47.3 months among patients taking PCSK9 inhibitor-based and ezetimibe-based intensive lipid-lowering therapy, respectively, with statins [ ]. These findings suggest that dyslipidaemia is a complicated metabolic syndrome and LDL-c is not the sole target of lipid-lowering treatment.
With advances in omics technology, our understanding of the metabolic landscape and metabolite reprogramming in humans and mice with dyslipidaemia is improving. One study showed that indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 activity was increased in the intestine of low-density lipoprotein receptor-deficient (Ldlr−/−) mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 13 weeks, as measured by liquid chromatography, which shifted tryptophan catabolism toward kynureinine production in the intestine [ ]. When fed a HFD and a high cholesterol diet, Ido1 deletion from intestinal epithelial cells resulted in increased plaque size in Ldlr−/− mice fed a HFD and a high cholesterol diet compared with their littermates [ ].
We also previously reported that α-ketoglutarate, an intermediate metabolite in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, was increased in haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) from Ldlr−/− mice following a HFD for 12 weeks [ ]. By contrast, exendin-4 administration reduced α-ketoglutarate in HSPCs and inhibited plaque progression in Ldlr−/− mice fed a HFD [ ]. These data indicate that α-ketoglutarate could be involved in the initiation of atherosclerotic development.
However, the effect of α-ketoglutarate differs among various diseases. For instance, using liver metabolomics, one study showed that the α-ketoglutarate level was higher in patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) than in those without NASH, but it decreased in patients with NASH remission [ ], indicating mitochondrial impairment. In cultured primary hepatocytes, exposure to α-ketoglutarate promoted apoptosis via mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) activation [ ]. α-Ketoglutarate promoted skeletal muscle blood flow and hypertrophy [ ] and increased pro-renin receptor expression, facilitating salt reabsorption [ ], both of which were mediated via oxoglutarate receptor 1 (OXGR1). Injection of α-ketoglutarate into wild-type mice triggered adult cardiomyocyte proliferation and regeneration following myocardial infarction [ ]. Furthermore, diverse associations have been reported between circulating α-ketoglutarate levels and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with coronary heart disease [ , ]. Consistent with this, it is unclear whether the effect of HFD on myelopoiesis and plaque progression is mediated by α-ketoglutarate. If so, the mechanism through which it modulates inflammation to facilitate atherosclerosis development remains to be established.
To address these knowledge gaps, Ldlr−/− mice were fed a HFD with or without 2% α-ketoglutarate in the drinking water for 6 weeks and plaque size was evaluated. Lethally irradiated Ldlr−/− mice were transplanted with Oxgr1+/+ and Oxgr1−/− bone marrow cells (BMCs) and the dietary regimen was repeated. Targeted metabolomics and single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) of granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs) sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) were performed to dissect the pathological mechanisms underpinning the involvement of the α-ketoglutarate/OXGR1 axis in plaque progression.

## Results
2.1

## α-Ketoglutarate facilitated the HFD-induced increase in GMPs, myeloid cell production, and plaque progression in Ldlr−/− mice
Previously, we reported that α-ketoglutarate was elevated in HSPCs from Ldlr−/− mice after 12 weeks of HFD [ ]. To explore the kinetics of α-ketoglutarate, HSPCs were isolated by FACS from Ldlr−/− mice fed with chow diet or HFD for 6 weeks and processed for targeted metabolomics. HFD induced 2.8-fold and 2.7-fold increases in plasma cholesterol and triglycerides, respectively, in the Ldlr−/− group compared with the chow diet group (cholesterol: 5.85 ± 1.34 mmol/L vs. 16.2 ± 1.51 mmol/L, p < 0.0001; triglycerides: 1.11 ± 0.52 mmol/L vs. 3.04 ± 1.42 mmol/L, p < 0.0005 [n = 8–10/group]).
 A shows the metabolic profiles of HSPCs in the chow and HFD groups quantified by targeted metabolomics. Of the seven TCA cycle-related metabolites quantified by targeted metabolomics, only α-ketoglutarate was significantly elevated in HSPCs from the HFD group compared with the control group (2.81 ± 1.22 × 10−10 μmol/cell vs. 5.80 ± 1.38 × 10−10 μmol/cell, p = 0.048 [n = 7/group]) ( B– ). Similarly, plasma α-ketoglutarate was 1.4-fold higher in the HFD group than in the chow diet group (451.6 ± 142.5 μmol/L vs. 653.9 ± 141.2 μmol/L, p = 0.008 [n = 8–10/group]) ( C). Moreover, the α-ketoglutarate level was positively correlated with the total cholesterol level (p = 0.025), but not with the triglyceride level in the plasma (p = 0.17) ( D). 
To investigate the effect of α-ketoglutarate on atherosclerosis, Ldlr−/− mice were fed chow diet or HFD for 6 weeks. Starting from week 3, the mice were received saline or 2% α-ketoglutarate in the drinking water until the end of the experiment. In the HFD group, 4 weeks of α-ketoglutarate administration did not alter cholesterol, triglyceride, or fasting blood glucose levels in the blood (all p ≥ 0.15) ( E–G). However, α-ketoglutarate increased myeloid cell number in the blood of Ldlr−/− mice in the HFD group ( H and I). The general characteristics of Ldlr−/− mice in the different groups are summarized in . Plaque size and the number of CD45+ inflammatory cells in the plaques were greater in the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group than in the HFD group (plaque size: 41452 ± 7819 μm2 vs. 79711 ± 9467 μm2, p = 0.004 [n = 17–18/group]; CD45+ cells: 37.9 ± 5.7 cells vs. 55.8 ± 6.2 cells, p = 0.046 [n = 10 per group]) ( J–L).
There was an increase in myeloid cell number in the blood and plaques of Ldlr−/− mice in the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group. Therefore, we compared the proportion of HSPCs, common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), and GMPs in the bone marrow. After 6 weeks of treatment, total bone marrow cell (BMC) number was 1.5-fold higher in the HFD group than in the chow diet group (p = 0.0008 [n = 6–7/group]). The number of BMCs was comparable between the HFD + saline group and the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group (p = 0.36 [n = 7–8]). HFD for 6 weeks induced a 1.5-fold increase in the proportion of HSPCs among BMCs compared with chow diet (0.10% ± 0.03% vs. 0.15% ± 0.03%, p < 0.0001 [n = 15–34]) ( M). FACS analysis revealed that α-ketoglutarate administration did not alter the HSPC proportion of BMCs in the HFD groups (p ≥ 0.28). Similar findings were observed for the proportion of CMPs (p = 0.13 for HFD vs. HFD + α-ketoglutarate) ( N). Compared with the chow diet group, the GMP frequency was 1.1-fold higher in the HFD group (0.97% ± 0.16% vs. 1.11% ± 0.18%, p = 0.022 [n = 15–34/group]). Furthermore, α-ketoglutarate promoted an increase of 1.2-fold in GMP proportion compared with HFD alone (1.11% ± 0.18% vs. 1.32% ± 0.27%, p = 0.0002 [n = 28–34/group]) ( O). Representative FACS plots of GMP proportion are shown in P.

## Transplantation of Oxgr1−/− BMC attenuated plaque progression in Ldlr−/− mice on HFD plus α-ketoglutarate
Oxgr1 encodes the α-ketoglutarate receptor in mice (OXGR1 in humans) [ ]. To explore whether α-ketoglutarate promoted inflammation and progression in atherosclerotic plaques via OXGR1, Oxgr1
−/− mice were established via CRISPR-Cas9 technology, as previously reported [ ] ( A and B). Western blotting verified the dramatic reduction in OXGR1 expression in Oxgr1−/− BMCs compared with wild-type (WT) BMCs ( C). At 9 weeks of age, OXGR1 deficiency in mice resulted in higher fasting blood glucose (4.25 ± 0.51 mmol/L vs. 7.18 ± 0.64 mmol/L, p < 0.0001 [n = 4–5/group]). However, when the mice were challenged with 10% glucose injection, the overall glucose tolerance capacity was comparable between the two groups (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 1288.0 ± 147.5 vs. 1518.2 ± 190.7, p = 0.077 [n = 4–5/group]) ( D). Furthermore, the plasma cholesterol concentration was similar (p = 0.19), but the triglyceride concentration was higher (p = 0.026) in the Oxgr1−/− group than in the WT group ( E and F). Compared with the WT group, neither white blood cell number nor differential white blood cell proportion differed in Oxgr1−/− mice (all p ≥ 0.11, G and H). 
Next, WT and Oxgr1−/− BMCs were transplanted into lethally irradiated Ldlr−/− mice. Four weeks after BMC transplantation, all mice were treated with a HFD with or without 2% α-ketoglutarate for 6 weeks ( I). Overall, plasma cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations were comparable among all groups (both p ≥ 0.37) ( ). According to the FACS analysis, α-ketoglutarate treatment did not alter the proportion of HSPCs, but it increased the proportion of GMPs among the BMCs from Ldlr−/− mice receiving WT BMCs, but not in those injected with Oxgr1−/− BMCs, compared with their corresponding controls ( J and K). Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed that the plaque size was 1.8-fold higher in Ldlr−/− mice transplanted with WT BMCs followed by α-ketoglutarate treatment than in those transplanted with WT BMCs alone (56840.0 ± 20021.1 μm2 vs. 99574.4 ± 47395.3 μm2, p = 0.046 [n = 8–9/group]). By contrast, α-ketoglutarate treatment did not affect plaque size in Ldlr−/− mice receiving Oxgr1−/− BMCs compared with those transplanted with Oxgr1−/− BMCs alone (38649.3 ± 21808.4 μm2 vs. 47932.3 ± 33600.2 μm2, p = 0.49 [n = 8–9/group]) ( L). Immunostaining revealed that the α-ketoglutarate administration elicited stronger inflammation in Ldlr−/− recipients with WT BMCs, as evidenced by the number of Mac3+ and CD45+ cells in plaques ( M– , ).

## scRNA-seq analysis of GMPs identified purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) upregulation by α-ketoglutarate
By FACS sorting, lineage−/lowcKit + Sca-1−CD34+CD16/CD32+ cells containing GMPs were isolated from Ldlr−/− mice on chow or HFD in the presence or absence of α-ketoglutarate treatment. They were subjected for scRNA-seq using BD Rhapsody technology. Normalization and clustering were performed on a cell-by-gene expression matrix. For visualization, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction was displayed for GMPs ( A). On the basis of the expression of canonical cell markers and differentially expressed genes (DEGs), four clusters were identified, including GMPs, GMP/Pro-Neutrophils (GMP/Pro-NEU), GMP/Pro-monocytes (GMP/Pro-Mono), and GMP/Pre-dendritic cells (GMP/Pre-DC) ( A and B). Compared with the mice that received a chow diet + α-ketoglutarate, the fraction of GMPs was reduced, but GMP/Pro-NEU and GMP/Pro-Mono proportions were significantly increased in GMPs from mice in the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group (both p < 0.05, C). On the basis of the DEGs, the top 10 biological processes in the Gene Ontology analysis were identified ( D–G). Of the 10 top biological processes, increased oxidative phosphorylation was seen in GMPs from the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group, but not in the HFD alone group, compared with their corresponding controls ( E and F). On the basis of the DEGs between the HFD + α-ketoglutarate and HFD groups, the top biological processes included leukocyte function regulation and activation ( G). 
The volcano plot depicted the DEGs in GMPs from the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group versus the HFD group, in which 54 were upregulated and two were downregulated ( H). The Venn diagram illustrated the DEG overlap between different pairs of comparisons. Overall, 39 DEGs were screened between the different comparisons ( I). Of the 39 identified DEGs, 32 genes were consistently elevated when compared with its corresponding control ( J– ). Given that PNP is critically involved in metabolism, the implication of 32 metabolites was further dissected in GO analysis of lipid metabolic process, amino and metabolic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, and nucleobase-containing compound metabolite process. The top 10 DEGs that were overlapped in each comparison are presented in J.
To validate the DEG results, Ldlr−/− mice were given a HFD with or without α-ketoglutarate as described above. BMCs were isolated to screen mRNA expression by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) ( ). In line with the scRNA-seq results ( I–K), PNP mRNA was 1.5-fold higher in BMCs from mice in the HFD + α-ketoglutarate group than in mice from the HFD group (p = 0.008) ( L). Western blotting analysis confirmed these findings ( M). Using BMCs obtained from Ldlr−/− mice, PNP protein expression was 1.2-fold higher in mice transplanted with WT BMCs followed by HFD + α-ketoglutarate than in those fed on HFD only (1.2 ± 0.3 vs. 1.0 ± 0.1, p = 0.041 [n = 11–13/group]). By contrast, PNP expression was comparable in mice transplanted with Oxgr1−/− BMCs, regardless of α-ketoglutarate administration (1.1 ± 0.3 vs. 0.9 ± 0.2, p = 0.24 [n = 12–13/group]) ( M and N).

## PNP promoted GMP proliferation and enhanced myeloid cell production via inosine-5′-monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2)
ScRNA-seq showed that PNP expression was most enriched in the GMP/Pro-NEU cluster ( A). To investigate the role of PNP in myeloid cell production, BMCs were cultured with PNP at different concentrations for 1 and 3 days, respectively. Spectral flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportions of HSPCs, GMPs, and Pro-NEU were elevated by PNP in a dose-dependent manner at both time points (all p < 0.05 [n = 5–7/group]) ( B–F). Representative FACS plots of GMPs and Pro-NEU in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-treated and PNP-treated cells are shown in D and F. PNP treatment did not alter the common monocyte progenitors (cMOP) proportion compared with PBS (p ≥ 0.095). After 3 days of PNP exposure, the neutrophil proportion was increased by 1.8-fold and 2.6-fold in the low and high PNP activity groups, respectively, compared with control (PBS: 3.84% ± 1.58%, PNP 0.1 U/mL: 7.11% ± 1.49%, PNP 1 U/mL: 9.95% ± 1.79%, p < 0.002 for both [n = 7/group]) ( G). The monocyte proportion was comparable between the PBS and PNP treatment groups (p ≥ 0.17) ( H). A representative FACS plot of neutrophils and monocytes in PBS-treated and PNP-treated cells is shown in I. 
PNP is an enzyme in the purine salvage pathway. It reversibly catalyses the phosphorolysis of purine nucleosides for purine recycling [ ]. Recently, PNP was discovered to catalyse the phosphorolysis of guanosine/deoxyguanosine and inosine/deoxyinosine into guanine and hypoxanthine, respectively, generating ribose-1-phosphate and deoxyribose-1-phosphate in patients with sickle cell disease and hemolytic vasculopathy [ ]. In the present study, the pentose phosphate pathway was significantly stimulated in GMPs from the HFD group ( F). Furthermore, interconversion has been reported between ribose-1-phosphate and ribose-5-phosphate [ ]. Thus, it has been speculated that PNP participates in the pentose phosphate pathway by providing ribose-5-phosphate. Interestingly, ribose-5-phosphate also links the pentose phosphate and de novo purine biosynthesis pathways [ ]. Within the de novo purine biosynthesis pathway, we previously showed inosine monophosphate (IMP)-induced GMP proliferation [ ]. In parallel, IMPDH2 has been implicated in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the response to DNA damage in different cells [ , ].
To further dissect the impact of the pentose phosphate and de novo purine biosynthesis pathways, lineage−/low cells were stimulated with PNP or PBS for 24 h. Ribose-5-phosphate and IMPDH2 protein levels were quantified by ELISA. After normalization by protein concentration, intracellular ribose-5-phosphate protein levels were 1.5-fold higher in PNP-treated cells than in controls (0.05 ± 0.02 ng/μg protein vs. 0.03 ± 0.01 ng/μg protein, p = 0.032 [n = 11/group]) ( J). As predicted, IMPDH2 protein concentration was 1.2-fold higher in PNP-treated cells than in controls (84.7 ± 17.5 ng/μg protein vs. 69.3 ± 9.6 ng/μg protein, p = 0.036 [n = 11/group]) ( K). IMPDH2 has been reported to regulate cell proliferation [ ]. Thereafter, BMCs were exposed to PNP in the presence or absence of IMPDH2 inhibitor and labeled with 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EDU). By FACS analysis, IMPDH2 inhibition substantially reduced the proliferation of HSPCs, GMPs, and ProNEU ( L and M). IMPDH2 inhibition abrogated the increase in HSPCs, GMPs, ProNEU, and neutrophils induced by PNP ( N–S).

## PNP perturbed redox and mitochondrial homeostasis
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) is a co-enzyme that plays a key role in cellular metabolism, redox status and mitochondrial homeostasis. Recently, Kropotov et al. reported that PNP impairs salvage pathway of NAD by metabolizing nicotinamide riboside (NAD precursor) towards nicotinamide accumulation (Nam) in mammalian cell lines [ ], leading to reduced NAD production. Given the central role of NAD in maintaining cell steady-state, we testified the redox status of GMP exposed to PNP. Indeed, when lineage−/low cells were stimulated with 0.1 U/mL PNP, the NAD level was not altered after 6 h, but it decreased by 26% after 24 h of treatment compared with PBS (p = 0.61 for 6 h; p = 0.013 for 24 h) ( A). Furthermore, NAD precursor, nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), was significantly reduced by PNP compared with control (p = 0.047) ( B). PNP treatment did not affect ATP production at either time point, as quantified by ELISA (p ≥ 0.18 for 6 and 24 h) ( C). PNP treatment did not modify the activity and protein expression of superoxide dismutase (SOD) (both p ≥ 0.12) ( D and E). Neither glutathione peroxidase (GPX) nor catalase differed between the PBS- and PNP-treated groups (both p ≥ 0.39) ( F and G). 
The treated cells were incubated with 5(6)-Carboxy-2′ 7′-dichlorofluroescein (DCFA), and the ROS levels were compared by FACS. As shown in H–J, PNP increased the percentage of Reactive oxygen species (ROS)hi cells in lineage−/low cells in a dose-dependent manner compared with PBS (%ROShi cells: 21.6% ± 4.1% for PBS; 35.3% ± 7.6% for 0.1 U/mL PNP; 47.0% ± 3.0% for 1 U/mL PNP; both p ≤ 0.0003). Moreover, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) intensity of ROS was 1.1-fold and 1.4-fold greater in the 0.1 U/ml and 1 U/mL PNP-treated lineage−/low cells than in the PBS-treated cells, respectively (both p ≤ 0.02) ( H–J).
Next, mitochondrial function was determined by measuring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of lineage−/low cells using a Seahorse Bioscience XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Although there was no difference in OCR between the two groups, ECAR increased by a factor of 1.1 in lineage−/low cells after 24 h of PNP treatment, compared with controls (p = 0.46 for OCR; p = 0.026 for ECAR) ( K–P).
Put together, these data suggest that PNP exposure disrupted redox status and triggered metabolic reprograming in lineage−/low cells.

## Proteomics analysis revealed increased NAD kinase (NADK) expression and NF-κB transcription in PNP-treated lineage−/low cells
To further elucidate how PNP regulated cell fate, lineage−/low cells were treated with PBS or 0.1 U/mL PNP for 24 h and then proceeded for proteomics. By analysis, totalled 119,503 peptides and 7858 proteins were identified and heatmap was shown ( ). The threshold ratio of IMP vs. PBS differential expression (≥1.5 or ≤1/1.5) identified 66 up-regulated proteins and 18 down-regulated proteins, as shown in a volcano plot ( A, ). Through Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) annotation, the up-regulated proteins were mainly involved in transcription, signalling transduction and protein modification ( B). Along with that, GO analysis of biological properties and the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis are shown ( C and D, ). 
The heatmap of dysregulated proteins is presented in . Because of unknown function of 13 up-regulated proteins, they were excluded for further analysis. In the remaining 53 up-regulated proteins, their function was enriched in metabolism and inflammation ( E). Besides that, several ubiquitin proteins involved in post-translational modification were noticed. Of 6 proteins in metabolism, NADK was prominently elevated following PNP treatment ( F). NADK plays a crucial role in redox homeostasis via phosphorylation of NAD + for NADP(H) production [ ]. Furthermore, the key inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB expression was also significantly induced by PNP ( G). To determine the role of ubiquitin in NF-κB activation, lineage−/low cells were exposed to PNP in the presence or absence of pan-ubiquitin inhibitor, MG132, for 24 h, increased NF-κB expression was detected in the nuclear of PNP-treated cells by ELISA ( H).

2.9

## Relationship of LDL-c with TCA cycle-related metabolites in humans
Finally, the relationship of LDL-c with TCA cycle-related metabolites was assessed in healthy humans. Of the 40 participants, 32 (80%) were male. The mean age of the participants was 48.0 ± 13.6 years. Treatment with LDL-c increased isocitrate dehydrogenase expression in BMCs ( G and H). To verify the in vitro findings, the TCA cycle-related metabolites in the fasting plasma samples of healthy participants were quantified by metabolomics. Spearman's correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between the isocitrate level and LDL-c in the blood of humans (r = 0.37, p = 0.018 [n = 40]) ( A). No significant correlation was observed between plasma triglyceride and isocitrate levels (r = 0.11, p = 0.99 [n = 40]) ( B). When adjusting for age and sex, the positive correlation was maintained between isocitrate and LDL-c levels in the blood (p = 0.022).

## Discussion
The main findings of this study are summarized as follows. First, in the presence of HFD, the level of α-ketoglutarate increased in the blood, partially mediated by LDL-c-induced upregulation of isocitrate and isocitrate dehydrogenase within the TCA cycle. Second, acting through OXGR1, α-ketoglutarate promoted PNP transcription and protein expression. PNP is a central protein linking the pentose phosphate pathway, de novo purine biosynthesis, redox and mitochondrial homeostasis. Ultimately, PNP impaired NAD production, but induced IMPDH2 expression, ROS production and NF-κB transcriptional activation, all of which facilitated GMP proliferation and differentiation of inflammatory myeloid cells. The proposed mechanism is illustrated in C.
In the setting of dyslipidaemia, the activation of HSPCs and GMPs in the bone marrow is closely associated with myelopoiesis in the blood and inflammatory cells in plaques [ , ]. In the presence of lipoprotein receptor deficiency, cholesterol homeostasis is perturbed in HSPCs, which in turn activates signalling pathways for HSPC proliferation [ ]. Interestingly, GMP proliferation-induced myelopoiesis can occur independent of HSPC activation [ ]. In a mouse model of transient hyperglycaemia, GMPs were activated by pronounced S100A8 and S100A9 release from neutrophils, resulting in increased myeloid cell production and plaque progression [ ].
Recently, essential metabolic pathways modulating immunity in different diseases have been identified. One study showed that even 3 weeks after starting HFD, 30 metabolites, including acetyl-CoA substrate citrate and adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-citrate lyase, were increased in the aorta of ApoE knockout mice [ ]. ATP-citrate lyase catalyses cytosolic citrate conversion to acetyl-CoA, an acetyl donor for histone acetylation [ ]. These increased metabolites in the aorta promote metabolic reprogramming, such as cholesterol biosynthesis, and elicit epigenetic reprogramming by providing acetyl donors for target gene transcription for trained immunity [ ]. Moreover, metabolites can act through their receptors to directly regulate cell function. 17,18-Epoxyeicosatetraenoic acid is the key component of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids. It allosterically regulates sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 conformation for endothelial nitric oxide synthase activation in endothelial cells [ ].
Consistent with these findings, we showed that, acting through OXGR1, α-ketoglutarate strengthened the atherogenic effects of HFD, increasing GMP differentiation toward atherogenic myeloid cell production and plaque progression. This phenomenon seemed to be independent of cholesterol and triglycerides, as evidenced by comparable plasma cholesterol and triglyceride levels in HFD-fed Ldlr−/− mice with or without α-ketoglutarate treatment. Nonetheless, OXGR1 was obligatory for the regulation of myeloid cell production by α-ketoglutarate, as plaque size was lower in Ldlr−/− mice transplanted with Oxgr1−/− BMCs compared with those transplanted with WT BMCs.
By far, accumulated evidence has shown the beneficial effects of α-ketoglutarate on lifespan expansion [ ], increase fat thermogenesis and lipolysis [ ], enhanced skeletal muscle performance during exercise [ ], and improved cardiac regeneration and function following myocardial infarction in various animal models [ ]. These studies were conducted in wild-type mice and rats. However, clinical studies have not provided any direct evidence of the effects of α-ketoglutarate on atherosclerosis. Therefore, the role of α-ketoglutarate in dyslipidaemia-induced atherosclerosis remains unclear. Furthermore, the optimal dosage and dietary strategy for α-ketoglutarate supplementation require thorough evaluation in future studies. Caution should be taken when using α-ketoglutarate.
Further examination revealed that PNP was upregulated via the α-ketoglutarate/OXGR1 axis. PNP is an enzyme that reversibly catalyses the phosphorolysis of purine nucleosides to the corresponding purine base and ribose 1-phosphate. PNP deficiency accounts for <5% of all severe combined immunodeficiency cases [ ]. Patients with PNP deficiency exhibit severe neutropenia and lymphopenia [ , ]. Thus, PNP inhibitors have been used to treat patients with T-cell lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, and graft-versus-host disease [ ]. Guanosine and its derivatives are endogenous ligands for Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) [ ]. When PNP is abundant, purine nucleosides are catabolized. The excess guanosine and its derivatives activate TLR7 signalling, which modulates macrophages to produce inflammatory cytokines [ ] and triggers GMP expansion [ ].
Hereby, our data demonstrated that PNP served as a central molecule to elicit de novo purine biosynthesis by interconversion of ribose-1-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate. As a result, we detected increased IMPDH2 production ( J and K). IMPDH2 is an enzyme that is critically involved in the synthesis of guanine nucleotides (guanosine triphosphate and guanosine diphosphate). IMPDH2 regulates cell proliferation by providing nucleotides for DNA and RNA synthesis, cellular energy metabolism, and signal transduction [ , ]. In accordance with other studies on cancer cells [ , ], inhibition of the IMPDH2 pathway abrogated GMP cell cycle progression and myeloid cell production ( L–N).
Our data also illustrated that PNP diminished NAD production and rendered a redox state, leading to pronounced ROS production. Low ROS is beneficial for stem cell quiescence and self-renewal capacity [ ]. By contrast, in response to stress, enhanced mitochondrial ROS production drives myeloid progenitor proliferation to accelerate myeloid cell production [ , ]. Our previous study showed that controlling ROS production using N-acetyl-l-cysteine could inhibit HSPC proliferation, myeloid cell production, and plaque progression in Scarb1−/− mice fed on HFD [ ]. Therefore, we speculate that governing PNP-induced ROS production could render inflammation in plaques.
Interestingly, our proteomics data revealed that treatment with PNP increased the expression of NADK. NADK is the key enzyme that phosphorylates NAD to generate NADP and is the only enzyme that converts NAD(H) to NADP(H). Both cytosolic and mitochondrial NADK2 have been identified as having a similar role in maintaining redox homeostasis and metabolism [ ]. Although NADK is not directly related to ROS, it contributes indirectly to ROS production via NADP(H), which acts as a substrate for ROS-producing enzymes. Whether NADK potentiates or represses ROS production is currently under investigation. Our results imply that increased NADK by PNP could accelerate NAD consumption through NAD phosphorylation to produce NADP(H) in cells with reduced NAD levels. This would be important for the design of future therapies.
This study has several limitations. First, FACS delineated an increased GMP proportion in Ldlr−/− mice fed an HFD with α-ketoglutarate compared with those fed an HFD alone. When GMPs were sorted out with conventional surface markers, scRNA-seq analysis identified 4 clusters comprising GMP and 3 subpopulations. We assumed that these 4 clusters are also present in LDLr−/− mice fed on HFD. To date, limited data has revealed the involvement of OXGR1 and GMPs in immune cells in diseases. Second, according to qPCR and western blotting, PNP expression was elevated downstream of the α-ketoglutarate/OXGR1 axis. PNP is an intracellular enzyme. Our results showed that PNP treatment executed the effect of α-ketoglutarate on myeloid cell production partially via increased ROS production and NF-κB activation. Additionally, increased NADK expression was detected following PNP treatment. The manner in which PNP affects redox homeostasis and participates in atherosclerosis remains unclear. Using Protein-Protein Interaction Databases, we identified receptors such as VCAM1 and Integrin α4 interacting with PNP (IntAct Molecular Interaction Database, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact; HitPredict, http://www.hispredict.org; Integrated Interactions Database, https://iid.ophid.utoronto.ca/search_by_proteins/). This indicates the complexity of the PNP regulatory machinery, and further investigation is required to understand how it regulates myelopoiesis. Therefore, genetic approaches such as cell-specific overexpression or knockdown are more appropriate for investigating the effect of PNP on myelopoiesis in future studies. And thirdly, we observed a positive correlation between isocitrate (a precursor) and LDL-cholesterol in healthy subjects. However, it remains unclear whether α-ketoglutarate levels are directly associated with the severity of atherosclerosis or the instability of plaque in patients. Therefore, these are topics that require further investigation in the future.
In conclusion, α-ketoglutarate, the key intermediate TCA cycle metabolite, is elevated upon feeding an HFD in mice. α-Ketoglutarate increased the transcription and expression of PNP via OXGR1, regulating GMP differentiation and inflammatory myeloid cell production. This propagated the atherosclerotic effects of dyslipidaemia on atherosclerosis. Targeting metabolite homeostasis could complement lipid-lowering therapies, improving the therapeutic efficacy of treatments for cardiovascular disease.

## Methods
4.1

## Human study
This study complied with the Helsinki Declaration for the study of human subjects and was approved by the institutional review board of Beijing Youan Hospital. All study subjects provided written informed consent.
Forty healthy individuals were recruited at the Department of Science and Technology, Beijing Youan Hospital. The general information of the participants, including age and sex, was recorded. After overnight fasting, plasma samples were obtained, and the plasma cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations were determined at the central laboratory.

4.2

## Targeted metabolomics
Targeted metabolomics analysis was performed to determine TCA cycle-related metabolite levels, as described previously (LipidALL Technologies, Changzhou, China) [ ].

4.3

## Mice and treatment
All experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University and performed in accordance with the guidelines outlined in Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament regarding the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. At the day of sacrifice, the mice were anaesthetized with 1% isoflurane inhalation.
Male WT C57BL/6J mice, Ldlr−/− mice, and Oxgr1−/− mice on a CD45.2 (H-2kb) background were used in this study. The Ldlr−/− mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Oxgr1−/− breeding mice were provided by Professor Gang Shu, and the colonies were expanded (ViewSolid Biotech, Beijing, China). All mice were maintained in the animal facility at Beijing Youan Hospital, where they had ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory chow.
At 8–10 weeks of age, the Ldlr−/− mice were started on a HFD (Clinton/Cybulsky High Fat Rodent Diet with Regular Casein and 1.25% Added Cholesterol (#D12108C), ResearchDiets, US). Throughout the study, the mice were simultaneously administrated saline or 2% α-ketoglutarate (α-ketoglutaric acid sodium salt (#K1875), Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) in their drinking water for 6 weeks.

4.4

## BMC transplantation
At 9 weeks of age, Ldlr−/− mice on a chow diet were lethally irradiated using 9.0 Gy. Within 24 h after irradiation, equal amounts of WT or Oxgr1−/− BMCs were administered via the tail vein (5 × 106 BMCs/mouse). Four weeks after transplantation, half of the Ldlr−/− mice were switched to a HFD with or without 2% α-ketoglutarate in the drinking water. The remaining Ldlr−/− mice were maintained on a chow diet with or without 2% α-ketoglutarate in the drinking water. Ten weeks after BMC transplantation, the mice were sacrificed for analysis.

4.5

## White blood cell counts
Leukocytes and differential white blood cells were quantified using the Auto Haematology Analyzer (Mindray, BC-2800Vet, Shanghai, China).

4.6

## Glucose tolerance test and lipid profile
The mice were fasted overnight before glucose tolerance test. The following day, the mice were injected with 10% glucose (10 μL/g body weight) intraperitoneally. Blood glucose concentration was measured at baseline, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min after injection of 10% glucose (Accu-Chek®, Roche, US). The plasma cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations in the fasted blood samples were measured as previously described (Liquid Sample Triglyceride Content Assay Kit [#E1003], Liquid Sample Cholesterol Content Assay Kit [#E1005]; Applygen Technologies Inc., China) [ ].

4.7

## ELISA
Plasma α-ketoglutarate levels were determined by ELISA (MLBio, Shanghai, China). The concentrations of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, citrate synthase, malate dehydrogenase, and isocitrate dehydrogenase in the protein lysates extracted from BMCs of Ldlr−/− mice were also determined by ELISA (MLBio). The enzyme activity of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, citrate synthase, malate dehydrogenase, and isocitrate dehydrogenase in BMCs treated with 0 or 100 μg/mL of LDL-c was measured by ELISA (MLBio) according to their catalytic capacity on their substrates.
Protein was extracted from PNP-treated lineage−/low cells to determine the expression of ribose-5-phosphate, IMPDH2, NAD, SOD, and ATP by ELISA, following the manufacturer's instructions (mouse IMPDH2 ELISA kit [#ml601123], mouse NAD ELISA kit [#JK691233], Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, China; mouse SOD ELISA kit [#CSB-E08556 m], Huamei Bioengineering, China; ATP Assay Kit [#HY-K0314], MedChemExpress, US). In addition, the activity of SOD, catalase, and GPX was determined by ELISA (SOD Activity Assay kit [#BC0175], Catalase Activity Assay kit [#BC0205], GPX Activity Assay kit [#BC1195], Solarbio, China). Data were normalized by the number of cells or proteins loaded.

4.8

## FACS
HSPCs were defined as lineage−Sca-1+cKit+ cells (“LSK cells”); CMPs and GMPs were defined as CD34+CD16/32dimcKit + Sca-1−lineage−/low cells and CD34+CD16/32+cKit+Sca-1−lineage−/low cells, respectively. Monocytes and granulocytes were defined as CD11b+CD115−Ly-6G+ and CD11b+CD115+Ly-6G−, respectively. When gated on GMPs, cMOP and Pro-NEU were identified as CD115+CD81− and CD115−CD81+ cells, respectively. Neutrophils and monocytes were defined as CD11b+Ly-6G+CD115− cells and CD11b+Ly-6G−CD115+ cells, respectively [ ].
To assess lineage production, BMCs were treated with PNP (0–1.0 U/mL) for 1 or 3 days. After harvesting, the cells were counted and stained with surface markers for GMP, Pro-NEU, cMOP, neutrophils, and monocytes. The frequency of each cell population was analyzed by spectral flow cytometry (Cytek Bioscience, Shanghai, China). In the spectral flow cytometry experiment, CD3, CD45R/B220, and CD49b were used as lineage markers.
To study cell cycle, 10 μM EDU was added in the culture medium 2 h before harvest. Cells were first probed with antibodies for surface markers for 20 min. After permeabilization and fixation, cells were incubated with fluorescent dye picolyl azide in Click-iT™EdU reaction buffer for 30 min according to the instruction (Cat. 10632, Invitrogen, Beijing, China). EDU + cells were analyzed by FACS.
To assess ROS levels, lineage−/low cells were treated with PNP for 24 h. After harvesting, the cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (Reactive Oxygen Species Assay kit [#S0033S], Beyotime, China) for 15 min at 37 °C prior to FACS.
After 6 weeks of the relevant dietary regimen and α-ketoglutarate treatment, BMCs were isolated from Ldlr−/− mice. After lineage depletion using a Lineage Cell Depletion kit (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), the cells were stained with GMP surface markers for FACS. GMPs sorted by FACS were processed for scRNA-seq or targeted metabolomics.
Isotype IgG antibodies were used as negative controls. To obtain sufficient cells for the analysis, at least 100,000 BMCs were acquired. With the exception of spectral flow cytometry, an anti-mouse lineage cocktail (2B8) was used to exclude lineage+ cells, and the data were acquired using a FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences, the United States) and analyzed using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences, the United States).
FACS antibodies are listed below. 

4.9

## Cell isolation and culture
Freshly isolated BMCs from WT mice were maintained in StemSpan serum-free expansion medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) supplemented with 10 ng/mL stem cell factor (R&D Systems, US) and 10 ng/mL thrombopoietin (R&D Systems). Immediately after seeding, the BMCs were treated with 0 or 100 μg/mL LDL-c for 24 h. The cells were harvested to measure enzyme expression and activity by ELISA. The BMCs were treated with 0–10 unit/mg PNP (#N2415, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 or 72 h. After harvesting, the cells were counted and stained with surface markers for spectral flow cytometry analysis. Lineage−/low cells were isolated from BMCs using a Lineage Cell Depletion kit and exposed to PBS or PNP for different durations. The cells were collected and the protein quantity and activity were measured by ELISA. ROS levels were measured by FACS.

4.10

## RNA extraction and qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent and converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript® III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit (Vazyme, China). Real-time qPCR was performed using the ViiA 7 Real-time PCR System with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix reagent (ThermoFisher, US).
Primer sequences are listed below. 

4.11

## Determination of oxygen consumption
Lineage−/low cells isolated from WT mice were treated with PBS or 0.1 U/mL PNP for 24 h. After optimization, 3 × 105 cells were subjected to measure O2 consumption using a Seahorse Bioscience XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (XF96, Seahorse Bioscience). After measuring the basal respiration, the cells were treated with 1.5 μmol/L oligomycin for the quantification of ATP production and H+ (proton) leak. The maximum respiratory capacity was then assessed by the addition of 1.5 μmol/L FCCP. Finally, mitochondrial respiration was blocked with a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (both at 0.5 μmol/L) and the residual OCR was calculated to measure non-mitochondrial respiration.

4.12

## Nuclear factor (NF)-kB transcription activity assay
THP-1 cells were treated with PBS or 0.1 U/mL PNP for 24 h. Thereafter, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Cat. L4391, Sigma, USA) for 15 min nuclear proteins were extracted using a Nuclear Extraction kit (Ab113474; Abcam, USA). After determining protein concentrations using a BCA kit, 5 μg protein samples were transferred to 96-well plate pre-coated with dsDNA of NF-kB provided in the Kit. After overnight incubation, samples were probed with NF-kB Ab for 1 h followed by goat anti-rabbit HRP conjugate for 1 h. Developer solution was added for colourisation and signal was determined by the absorbance at 450 nm.

4.13

## Western blotting analysis
After total protein extraction, the protein concentration was measured using the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit [#23225], ThermoFisher, US). Equal amounts of protein were loaded and separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against phosphorylase (1:1000, #18009-1-AP, Proteintech) and β-actin (1:1000, #4970S, Cell Signaling Technology, US) overnight at 4 °C. After three times of washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence.

4.14

## Histology
As previously reported [ ], the hearts were perfused with PBS to remove any remaining blood cells, and then with 1% paraformaldehyde for fixation. After dissection, the hearts were processed using standard methods for paraffin embedding. The whole heart was embedded in paraffin with the cutting surface positioned at the base. Heart sections (5 μm thick) were collected from the aortic valve region using a rotary microtome. Cross sections were collected at a distance of about 50 μm from the first open aortic valve leaflet. A total of 100 serial cross sections were placed on ten separate glass slides. On each slide, each adjacent cross section was spaced 50 μm apart.
By Hematoxylin and eosin staining, the aortic valve leaflets were identified and used as landmarks. Lesion sizes were quantified within the corresponding valve region of each cross section and the subsequent three cross sections, covering a total distance of 150 μm. The lesion in each aortic valve region was manually traced to analyze the lesion area in μm2 using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, US). After obtaining the total lesion area of all aortic root areas combined at 0 μm, 50 μm, 100 μm and 150 μm distance of the open aortic valves, the average atherosclerotic lesion area was calculated to present the lesion size in this mouse.

4.15

## Immunohistochemistry
The heart tissue sections were heated at 60 °C for 2 h, dewaxed in xylene, and dehydrated through a gradient concentration of alcohol. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific staining using 3% hydrogen peroxide and 3% bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated with donkey anti-mouse CD45 antibody (1:100, #ab8216, Abcam, England), or goat anti-rabbit α-smooth muscle actin antibody (1:200, #ab5694, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. The slides were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, the sections were visualized using diaminobenzidine solution and counterstained with hematoxylin. For Mac3 staining, sections were probed anti-mouse Mac3 antibody (1:100, #108502, BioLegend) overnight and then incubated with Goat Anti-Rat IgG H&L Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:100, #ab150157, Abcam) for 30 min. As the negative control, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody without prior primary antibody incubation.

4.16

## Proteomics by mass-spectrometry
4.16.1

## Sample preparation
The cells were mixed thoroughly and lysis buffer was added to extract the total protein. Centrifugated the sample at 12,000 g for 5 min and collected the supernatant. The proteins in the supernatant were precipitated using acetone precipitation. The protein precipitate was dissolved in 8 M urea buffer, and the protein concentration was then determined by BCA assay.

4.16.2

## Protein reductive alkylation and digestion
The protein samples were reduced with DTT (dithiothreitol) for 1 h at 56 °C. Then, IAA (iodoacetamide) was added for the alkylation reaction, which was carried out in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The reductive alkylated protein samples were diluted with 100 mM NH4HCO3 to decrease the urea concentration. The proteins were trypsinised at a mass ratio of 1:50 (enzyme to protein) overnight, and the supernatant was desalted using a ZipTip C18 desalting column. The sample was stored frozen at −20 °C after vacuum drying.

4.16.3

## High performance liquid chromatography
The protein samples were injected and separated by an online-coupled Vanquish Neo liquid chromatography mass spectrometer. The peptides were aspirated through the autosampler and bound to the trap column (300 μm × 5 mm, 5 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size, Thermo), before being separated by elution onto the analytical column (150 μm × 150 mm, 2 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size, Thermo). The analytical gradient was established using two mobile phases (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% ACN), with a flow rate of 1.8 μL/min for the liquid phase. 2.2 Astral mass spectrometry detection: the mass spectrometer collected data using the DIA model. Each scan cycle consisted of an MS scan (R = 240 K, AGC = Custom) followed by a DIA scan. The HCD collision energy was set to 25. The filter window for the quadrupole is 2 Da.

4.16.4

## Astral mass spectrometry data analysis
The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using DIA-NN software (version 1.8) and were retrieved from the Human Proteomics Reference Database in UniProt (as of 29 March 2022, containing 20,377 protein sequences). The deep learning algorithm in DIA-NN predicted a spectral library, which was then used to extract the original DIA data and collect the protein quantification information. The precursor ion and protein levels were screened using the final data with a 1% FDR standard. The screened proteomics quantification information was then used for subsequent analysis.

4.17

## Targeted metabolomics for glycolysis and the TCA cycle
GMPs from Ldlr−/− mice were sorted by FACS for targeted omics analyses (LipidALL Technologies). Freshly sorted cells were inactivated in ice-cold chloroform:methanol (1:2, v/v). Lipids were extracted from the cells using a modified version of Bligh and Dyer's protocol, as previously described [ ]. An aliquot of the polar metabolite extract was derivatized with 3-nitrophenylhydrazine for quantification of TCA cycle-related metabolites using the Thermofisher DGLC U3000 connected with Sciex 6500 Plus QTRAP, as previously reported [ ]. Internal standards included d4-citrate, d4-succinate, d3-pyruvate, d4-fumarate, and d3-malate (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Shanghai, China).

4.18

## scRNA-seq
The BD Rhapsody system was used to capture the transcriptomic information of Lineage−/lowcKit + Sca-1−CD34+CD16/CD32+ cells containing GMPs. Single-cell capture was achieved by random distribution of a single cell suspension across >200,000 microwells using a limited dilution approach. Beads with oligonucleotide barcodes were added to saturation to enable the cells to pair with the beads in each microwell. Cell lysis buffer was added to enable the poly-adenylated RNA molecules to hybridize to the beads. The beads were collected in a single tube for reverse transcription. The beads were collected in a single tube for reverse transcription and exonuclease I digestion.
After cDNA synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged on the 5′ end (3′ end of a messenger RNA transcript) with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) and cell barcode indicating its cell of origin. Whole-transcriptome libraries were prepared using the BD Rhapsody single-cell whole transcriptome amplification (WTA) workflow, including random priming and extension (RPE), RPE amplification PCR, and WTA index PCR. The libraries were quantified using a High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent, Beijing, China) on a Bioanalyzer 2200 and the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was performed using an Illumina sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, the United States) on a 150 base pair paired-end run.

4.19

## scRNA-seq data analysis
Fastp with default parameter filtering of the adaptor sequence was applied and low-quality reads were removed to obtain clean data. UMI-tools software was applied for single cell transcriptome analysis to identify the cell barcode whitelist. Specifically, the UMI-based clean data were mapped to the mice genome by STAR mapping with the customized parameters from the standard pipeline of UMI-tools to obtain UMI counts of each sample. We applied stringent quality control criteria to ensure that the selected data were from live single cells. After quality control, the inclusions of dead cells, doublets and low-quality cells, and batch effect were further corrected by the Scanpy package. Cells with ≥200 and ≤ 5000 detected genes, ≤20000 total UMIs, and a mitochondrial transcript fraction below 15 % were retained for downstream analyses. Scanpy package (version: 1.10.3, https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/) was used for cell normalization and regression based on the expression table in accordance with the UMI counts of each sample and the percentage of the mitochondrion rate to obtain scaled data. After selecting the 3000 most highly variable genes, single-cell transcriptomes from all samples were integrated with scVI, which simultaneously corrected batch effects and learned a 30-dimensional latent representation. These embeddings were subsequently visualized with UMAP and subjected to graph-based clustering. With Leiden algorithm (resolution = 0.6). The marker genes were calculated by Scanpy's tl.rank_genes_groups function with the Wilcoxon test algorithm under the following criteria: |log2FC| ≥ 0.25, p ≤ 0.05. The cell type of each cluster was determined by markers from previously published bone marrow single cell studies.
4.19.1

## Differential gene expression analysis
To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among samples, the function scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups with the Wilcoxon rank sum test algorithm was used under following criteria: |log2FC| ≥ 0.25, p ≤ 0.05.

4.19.2

## GO analysis
To delineate transcriptional alterations, we performed pairwise differential gene expression analysis for four comparisons: HFD vs. CHOW, HFD_AKG vs. CHOW, HFD_AKG vs. HFD, and HFD_AKG vs. CA. Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.15 were considered significantly differentially expressed. To elucidate the biological functions of these DEGs, we then performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.16.0). This analysis was conducted against a background of all genes detected in the dataset, with annotations derived from the Mus musculus genome database. GO terms with an adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

4.20

## Statistical analyses
Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or n (%). Statistics analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, US). For normally distributed data, two experimental groups were compared using the unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. If non-normally distributed, the data of two groups were compared using the non-parametric Wilcoxon test. In the experiments involving ≥3 groups, one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett's test was used to compare the treated groups against the control group, while one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni's post hoc test was applied when comparing all groups. The plasma levels of isocitrate were logarithm-transformed, and Spearman's analysis was applied to analyze the correlation of isocitrate with LDL-c in the blood of healthy subjects. Multivariate-adjusted linear regression analysis was conducted for LDL-c against isocitrate. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
