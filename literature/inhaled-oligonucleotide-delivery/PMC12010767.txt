## Abstract
Infection with the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, the etiologic agent of human Chagas disease, results in life-long infection. Infective trypomastigotes circulate in the bloodstream and have the capacity to infect any cell type, including reproductive tissue. This study sought to assess the potential for sexual transmission of T. cruzi in an experimental mouse model. The conditions used in this study, in which acutely infected males and immunosuppressing the females, created a worst-case scenario allowing for the greatest chance of measuring transmission through intercourse. Male BALB/c mice were infected and mated with uninfected females, and the females were subsequently examined for T. cruzi tissue parasitism. A single transmission event of 61 total matings was observed, indicating a low but non-zero risk potential for male-to-female sexual transmission of T. cruzi.
Keywords: Trypanosoma cruzi, Chagas disease, Sexually transmitted infection

## Introduction
Trypanosoma cruzi, the protozoan parasite that causes human Chagas disease, has a wide tissue distribution during acute infection, including reproductive tissue. This paper shows the first study to thoroughly test the sexual transmissibility of this parasite from infected males to uninfected females. The data presented herein show a low but non-zero risk for infection of females by acutely infected male mating partners. Further studies examining sexual transmission of T. cruzi in experimental models and humans are warranted.
T. cruzi is a protozoan parasite that causes Chagas disease, a chronic parasitic infection characterized by life-long infection of the host. T. cruzi is normally transmitted from the feces of a reduviid bug vector deposited during or immediately after a blood meal. Infective trypomastigotes enter the bloodstream via the bite wound or through exposed mucous membranes and subsequently enter host cells. The parasites replicate as amastigotes intracellularly before transforming back to trypomastigotes, escaping the cell and re-entering the bloodstream. During acute infection virtually every tissue and cell type is infected, but immune control of the acute infection results in parasite clearance from most tissues. Because the acute infection does not have a unique clinical presentation, Chagas disease often goes undiagnosed ( ; ). During the chronic phase of infection, T. cruzi persists primarily in muscle tissue, and clinical pathology in the form of cardiomyopathy or megasyndrome of the gastrointestinal tract eventually manifests in 20–30% of infected individuals.
Chagas disease is endemic to Latin America and is maintained in a sylvatic cycle in the southern United States. Vector-borne transmission in the United States rarely occurs, with only 23 documented cases ( ). Other potential routes of infection with T. cruzi in the U.S. include blood donation ( ), organ transplantations ( ; ; ; ; ; ), and congenital transmission ( ). While parasites persist during chronic infection primarily in muscle tissue, parasite reservoirs can also be found in adipose tissue and bone marrow ( ; ). Transmission of T. cruzi has occurred through donated lung, kidney and kidney/pancreas combination, liver, bone marrow, and cord blood ( ; ; ; ; ; ), suggesting that at least in some instances, parasites either persist in non-muscle tissue or that parasites released into the bloodstream have the potential to be a source of organ or tissue-based transmission.
Experimental models have shown T. cruzi infection of reproductive tissue during acute infection. Histological examination of the male and female reproductive tract has demonstrated the presence of parasite nests in a wide array of reproductive tissue ( ; ; ; , ; ). Semen of infected male mice contained T. cruzi and intraperitoneal injection of semen resulted in infection of approximately 75% of recipient mice ( ). The potential for human sexual transmission of T. cruzi was raised after a Draft Guidance Document was published in 2009 by the United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration titled “Use of Serological Tests to Reduce the Risk of Transmission of Trypanosoma cruzi Infection in Whole Blood and Blood Components for Transfusion and Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products.” The use of sperm from anonymous third-party donors creates a situation in which sexual transmission of T. cruzi may need to be evaluated, and whether testing of anonymous donors is an evidence-based decision or only theoretical. Despite the fact that millions of people are infected with T. cruzi, most are never diagnosed because Chagas disease is a disease of poverty and the afflicted have limited access to medical care. Moreover, most individuals are unaware of their infection status either because they are asymptomatic or because their symptoms are not recognized as Chagas disease. This is particularly likely in the United States where the awareness of Chagas among physicians is very low.
In the study described here, we sought to formally assess male-to-female sexual transmission of T. cruzi. While acute T. cruzi infection is rare in adults, for the purpose of these studies mice were examined for the presence of T. cruzi in reproductive tissue during acute infection or were mated during acute infection to provide a “worst-case” scenario.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Mouse and parasite strains used in this study
A pilot study was performed to determine the optimal parasite and mouse strain combination and time point after infection for the mating to occur. Mice for the pilot and subsequent studies were housed in specific pathogen-free housing at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in Atlanta, GA and given free access to food and water. All animals were handled under ethical rules and guidelines outlined in CDC standard operating procedures under an approved Animal Use Protocol. The infection of BALB/c mice with 104 CL strain parasites and C57BL/6 mice infected with 103 Brazil strain parasites were used in this pilot study. Male mice were infected intraperitoneally and sacrificed at 13, 20, and 27 days after infection to cover a variety of time points during the acute infection. At each time point, three mice were sacrificed and testes, cauda epididymis, and gross musculature of the femur were collected. DNA was extracted from these tissues and real-time PCR analysis was performed as described below to estimate relative infection intensity. Tissue from one BALB/c and one C57Bl/6 uninfected mouse was included as a negative control. The Ct value (cycle number at which the fluorescence signal crosses the threshold and becomes detectable) was recorded and used to determine the post-infection time-point of greatest infection intensity to be used in this study. BALB/c mice infected with CL parasites 13 days after infection had the lowest Ct and were therefore chosen as the model for this study.

2.2.

## Mating
To examine male-to-female sexual transmission in this experimental model, 61 male BALB/c mice were each mated for 24–72 h with one female BALB/c mouse. Male mice were infected via intraperitoneal injection of tissue-culture derived trypomastigotes. T. cruzi parasites were propagated in Vero cells grown in tissue culture media (RPMI-10) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum in flasks placed in a 37 °C CO2 incubator. Parasites harvested from flasks were centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 rpm in 15 mL conical tubes, washed once in 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then counted on a hemocytometer. Parasites were re-suspended in 1X PBS at a concentration of 105 viable parasites per mL. Male mice were infected with 0.1 mL intraperitoneally.
At 13 days after infection, one infected male and one uninfected female mouse were placed per cage overnight for mating. A positive mating event was confirmed through either the presence of a vaginal plug or a resulting pregnancy. After mating, females were removed to separate cages for the duration of the study, and males were sacrificed (48–72 h after mating) to assess the presence of T. cruzi parasites in sperm and seminal fluid. At 25 days after mating, female mating partners of infected male mice were immunosuppressed to detect outgrowth of parasites in vivo ( ). Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 mg/kg/d of cyclophosphamide at 2–3 d intervals for a total of 4 doses and survival was monitored daily for 3–4 days. Mice were sacrificed between 31 and 37 days after mating through CO2 inhalation, and then skeletal muscle (gross musculature of the femur) and heart tissue were collected and stored immediately on dry ice and transferred to a —20 °C freezer within 2 h for longer-term storage.

2.3.

## Determination of tissue parasitism
To assess infection status, tissue was removed from storage and minced with a sterile scalpel or using a Cell Crusher™ (Cell-crusher, Cork, Ireland) then DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue specimens underwent Proteinase K digestion and DNA stabilization in Qiagen’s proprietary buffers and DNA was isolated from the lysate by adsorption to silica gel spin columns. After two washes with kit buffers to remove proteins and impurities, DNA was eluted from the column with 200 μl of elution buffer and then stored at −20 °C. For the pilot study, DNA was amplified using a real-time PCR assay using primers from the T. cruzi microsatellite TcZ region (32F, 5′-TTT GGG AGG GGC GTT CA-3′, and 148R, 5′-ATA TTA CAC CAA CCC CAA TCG AA-3′)( ). Because of high background on the real-time PCR and its potential to identify false positives, conventional PCR was used for the study samples, using a T. cruzi minicircle DNA oligonucleotides Try S35 (5′-ATA ATG TAC GGG KGA GAT GCA TG-3′) forward primer and Try S36 (5′-GGG TTC GAT TGG GGT TGG TGT-3′) reverse primer. A negative buffer control was included in each set of extractions and PCR reactions as contamination controls, and T. cruzi DNA was used as a positive control. PCR product was dyed with EZ-VISION® DNA dye (Amresco, Solon, OH) and presence/absence of the 330 bp PCR product was determined by UV transillumination after separation on a 2% agarose gel.

3.

## Results
Tissue from infected male mice (testes, cauda epididymis and gross musculature of the femur) was tested for T. cruzi DNA as described, and each tissue collected tested positive from all male mice (representative data shown in lanes 6, 7, 8, and 11, ). Tissue from female mating partners (heart and gross musculature of the femur) underwent PCR testing as well. Assuming no infection would be observed and using a one-sided, exact binomial test, a sample size of 50 mice was chosen to give an upper bound of the infection rate 95% confidence interval (CI) of 5.8%. In total, 61 mating pairs were used in the study, and one (1) female mouse had T. cruzi parasite DNA in tissue after mating ( , lane 10). Thus, we observed a transmission rate of 1.6%, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.0–7.5%.
 
4.

## Conclusions
The current study demonstrates the potential for sexual transmission of the protozoan parasite T. cruzi. During acute infection, T. cruzi can be found in most all nucleated tissue, including male reproductive tissue, although mouse sperm itself has not been examined due to the difficulty in obtaining ejaculate from that species.
While it is possible that infection in this study came through biting rather than through sexual transmission, there is precedent for genital entry of parasites into the host. A previous study showed that instillation of 5 μl of blood infected with T. cruzi into the external meatus of the vagina and the penis of albino mice resulted in infection of 6 out of 36 females and in 2 out of 14 males ( ). While no infection was established after mating infected males with uninfected females in this study, only 4 mating pairs were used. As seen with the current study, a large sample size is required to detect a single transmission event following a brief mating. Very little is known about the per-act risk of acquiring various sexually transmitted infections. Most of the available data relates to HIV infection. For example, acquiring HIV infection from anal intercourse with an infected partner is estimated to be 5 per 1000 exposures ( ), and 0.1% for vaginal coitus. Given this data and the lack of information about the presence of T. cruzi in semen of chronically infected men, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the sexual transmission of T. cruzi during chronic infection is likely to be a highly rare event.
One limitation of this study is the lack of supporting evidence for the PCR data. Histological examination for amastigote nests or parasitemia would have been valuable as additional measures of infection, but the female mice in our study became very ill after the cyclophosphamide treatment, exhibiting wasting to an extent which prevented taking additional tissue or blood beyond the limited amount of musculature we obtained for PCR. This may have been due to the immunosuppression given so soon after pregnancy, which in itself produces an immunocompromised state. The conditions used in this study, specifically using acutely infected males and immunosuppressing the females, created a worst-case scenario allowing for the greatest chance of measuring transmission through intercourse. Females were examined for vaginal plugs and litters to confirm mating, but matings could only be confirmed in approximately half of the cases. These data may therefore underestimate the rate of transmission in the conditions used.
The data presented herein show a low but non-zero risk of male-to-female sexual transmission of the protozoan parasite T. cruzi in a mouse model. The conclusions are limited by the fact that conditions used in this study were designed to optimize the chance of detecting a positive transmission event. Using acutely infected male mice and immunosuppressing the female mating partners created a scenario that is not truly reflective of a real-world risk of a chronically infected adult transmitting T. cruzi to a sexual partner. The presence of T. cruzi parasites in semen from chronically infected mice has not been examined, nor has the presence of T. cruzi parasites in semen of chronically infected human males. Therefore, additional studies examining sexual transmission in experimental models of chronic infection or infections in persons from non-endemic areas who have sexual partners from an endemic area would be informative, especially those with HIV infections. These studies would help us to better understand the risk of T. cruzi spread through sexual transmission and to determine if implications for the data presented here extend beyond coitus between sexual partners to even include risk of transmission through donated sperm.
