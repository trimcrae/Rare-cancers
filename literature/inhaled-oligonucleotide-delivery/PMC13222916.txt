## Background
Prostate cancer (PCa) clinical management is challenged by tumor heterogeneity, driving the need for robust prognostic biomarkers, with altered glutamine metabolism emerging as a promising target. This investigation aimed to construct a metabolism-based prognostic signature for PCa and translate it into a targeted nanotherapeutic strategy.

## Methods
Transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were analyzed to identify dysregulated glutamine metabolism genes and construct a prognostic model via univariate Cox, LASSO, and multivariate Cox regression with validation in an independent cohort. The model’s characteristics were assessed through tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor mutation burden (TMB), and consensus clustering analyses. The pivotal gene of the model was selected to functionally validate in vitro through proliferation, clonogenicity, migration, and invasion assays. To exploit this vulnerability, a CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform was engineered to co-deliver cisplatin and glutaminase inhibitor CB-839, with evaluation in PCa models in vitro and in vivo.

## Results
A novel five-gene prognostic signature (ASNS, ATP2B4, GLYATL1, SLC6A20, SLC7A9) was established and stratified PCa patients into high- and low-risk groups based on progression-free interval (PFI). High-risk patients exhibited an immunosuppressive TME, higher TMB and TP53 mutations, and activation of proliferation-related pathways. Functional validation identified ASNS as a key oncogenic driver, enhancing PCa tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Therapeutically, CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform demonstrated potent synergistic cytotoxicity by depleting glutathione (GSH) and amplifying oxidative stress. This dual-action mechanism triggered significant apoptosis and tumor suppression with a favorable safety profile.

## Conclusions
Our study establishes a glutamine metabolism-based prognostic signature, pinpointing ASNS as a key driver. The resulting CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform offers a promising chemo-metabolic strategy for enhanced PCa treatment.

## Introduction
Prostate cancer (PCa) ranks as the second common cancers in men worldwide, emerging as a critical public health issue [ ]. Notwithstanding the accessibility of surgical and radiotherapy interventions for localized PCa, a significant proportion of patients advance to manifest aggressive variants [ , ]. The Gleason scoring system is currently applied in clinical practice to assess PCa aggressiveness and guide corresponding treatment [ ]. However, heterogeneity inherent in PCa complicates the approach of differentiating between indolent and aggressive tumors, leading to lack of survival benefit and higher complication rates for distinct PCa patients [ ]. It’s critical for reliable molecular biomarkers to build more accurate prognostic models, improving PCa patient risk stratification and facilitating individualized therapeutic strategies.
Metabolic reprogramming is essential for furnishing biomaterials and energy to sustain tumor cell proliferation and facilitate their adaptation to adverse microenvironments [ ], in which the dysregulation of glutamine metabolism presents an important component [ ]. Evidence has suggested that PCa demonstrated a significant reliance on glutamine for its initiation, progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy [ ]. Glutamine provides energy by recharging the tricarboxylic acid cycle through anaplerotic processes and delivers nitrogen for nucleotide and amino acid synthesis [ ]. Mechanistically, the oncogene c-MYC selectively enhances the expression of glutaminase (GLS), facilitating the accelerated degradation of glutamine in PCa cells [ ]. Genetic knockdown of the glutamine transporter ASCT2 inhibits PCa cells proliferation in vitro and reduces the growth of xenografted prostate tumors [ ]. Interestingly, glutamine serves as an essential precursor for the synthesis of glutathione (GSH) [ ], as GLS-mediated glutaminolysis sustains redox homeostasis in prostate cancer cells, enabling resistance to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage from radiotherapy [ ], while inhibiting glutamine metabolism effectively sensitize prostate cancer to radiation therapy [ ], indicating a promising anti-cancer strategy [ , ]. However, reliable biomarkers from glutamine metabolism that can determine PCa patient outcomes and inform clinical treatment remain absent [ ].
Furthermore, glutamine metabolism has been recognized as fundamentally interconnected with the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ], establishing a complex interplay crucial for immune modulation. Inhibiting glutamine limits cancer cell metabolism while paradoxically enhancing the oxidative metabolism of effector T cells, resulting in a long-lived, highly aggressive anti-tumor response [ , ]. Additionally, glutamine antagonists could alter the pro-tumorigenic to anti-tumor phagocytic function of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in urological malignancies [ , ]. Recent advances in single-cell genomics and machine learning have enabled the identification of metabolic signatures that not only predict prognosis but also characterize immune landscapes and immunotherapy responses across cancer types [ , ]. We propose that a glutamine metabolism-related gene signature can predict PCa aggressiveness and describe the immune milieu of the tumor.
Consequently, our study objects to developing a novel prognostic signature based on glutamine metabolism, identifying a key oncogenic driver, and translating these findings into a therapeutic strategy leveraging the identified metabolic vulnerability and the chemo-sensitizing principle of GSH depletion [ , ]. A tailored nanotherapeutic platform (CB/CDDP@lipo) was developed for the co-delivery of a GLS inhibitor (CB-839) and cisplatin, intended to produce a synergistic chemo-metabolic anti-tumor effect by concurrently targeting the tumor’s metabolic vulnerability and inducing DNA damage. In summary, our study identifies new molecular markers and possible treatment targets to aid in accurate diagnosis, predicting outcomes, and personalized therapy for PCa.

## Materials and methods

## Data acquisition and preprocessing
As illustrated in Fig.  A, the flowchart for this investigation delineated its approach and progression. We accessed the transcriptome expression profiles and the matching clinical follow-up data for the TCGA Prostate Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PRAD) cohort from the National Cancer Institute Genomic Data Commons (GDC) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-PRAD). In the curation process for the data, the samples with incomplete survival information and those with extremely short follow-ups were excluded, resulting in an overall cohort comprised of 483 tumor and 51 corresponding adjacent normal tissue samples. The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB v7.4) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) was utilized to retrieve gene sets related to “glutamine”. A complete glutamine metabolism gene collection was created after integration and deduplication (Table ). For independent external validation, data from the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Prostate Cancer (MSKCC) cohort were downloaded from the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (Study ID: prad_mskcc) (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=prad_mskcc). This validation set included RNA sequencing data (TPM format) and complete Disease-Free Survival (DFS) information for 150 prostate cancer patients, all of whom met the inclusion criteria.

 

## Identification of differentially expressed glutamine metabolism genes
A differential expression analysis utilizing the R package “DESeq2” with raw read counts from 483 tumor samples and 51 surrounding normal tissues in the TCGA-PRAD group identified the key genes with aberrant glutamine metabolism in prostate cancer. Genes were designated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) if their adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) and the absolute log2(FoldChange) > 0.585, indicating a 1.5-fold change, to ensure that critical metabolic genes were not prematurely excluded before Lasso-Cox modeling. The R package “ggplot2” created a volcano plot of gene expression variations. We intersected the previously found DEGs with our glutamine metabolism gene set to precisely filter the gene subset directly implicated in our study focus, which identified 33 glutamine metabolism related DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotations was performed on the 33 core genes using “clusterProfiler” package to determine their similar biological functions and enriched signaling pathways.

## Consensus clustering and molecular subtype identification
In the training cohort TCGA-PRAD, we employed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis on the above 33 glutamine metabolism related DEGs to identify 11 candidate genes related to Progression-Free Interval (PFI), which were visualized using forest plots from the R package “forestplot”. Next, unsupervised consensus clustering was conducted based on the above 11 prognosis-related genes indentified from univariate cox analysis to classify the PCa patients into two molecular subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2), executed by the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package, setting the cluster number (k) from 2 to 6. We employed the Partitioning Around Medoids algorithm with 1000 repetitions to ensure clustering stability. These subtypes were characterized by comparing immune microenvironments and identifying DEGs. Functional enrichment (GO/KEGG), Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were used to comprehend the DEGs’ biological meanings.

## Construction and validation of the prognostic model
The genes discovered in the univariate cox investigation were reduced multicollinearity and variable selection refined using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression. The penalty parameter (lambda) was picked with the lowest partial likelihood deviation, then found a stable collection of prognostic genes using the R package “glmnet” and ten-fold cross-validation. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) helped us calculate multicollinearity in the final gene model. The risk score for PCa samples was calculated following the formula: 

In the formula, n represents the total number of genes in the prognostic signature, Expi represents the log2-transformed TPM expression level for gene i, and βi represents multivariate Cox regression-calibrated coefficient of gene i. The PCa patients were split into high-risk and low-risk groups using the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the “survival” and “survminer” R packages to compare PFI between high- and low- risk groups, with significance assessed via the log-rank test. Time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC) were performed by the “timeROC” R package to evaluate the model’s predictive performance. The robustness was further validated in the independent MSKCC cohort using the same risk score and stratification method.

## Construction and evaluation of the nomogram
A nomogram incorporating the risk score alongside recognized clinical indicators was constructed through the “rms” R package. Calibration charts compared expected and observed survival rates to assess the nompgram’s accuracy.

## Analysis of immune microenvironment, genomic variation, and functional pathway enrichment
We employed the ESTIMATE algorithm from the “estimate” R package in order to estimate the Immune Score, Stromal Score, and the total ESTIMATE Score per sample from which we can estimate the inference of the Tumor Purity. The relative proportions of 22 unique immune cell subtypes were subsequently measured using the CIBERSORT deconvolution technique using the LM22 signature matrix. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was conducted with the “GSVA” R package to assess the infiltration levels of various immune cells and the immune-related pathways activation. We utilized “maftools” package to analyze Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB), which is defined as the number of non-synonymous somatic mutations, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splice site modifications, per megabase of the genome. The “GSVA” R package was used to perform Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) signatures. We employed the “pheatmap” R program to create a heatmap for visualizing the most significantly altered routes.

## Integrative analysis and prediction of therapeutic potential
A Sankey diagram was created utilizing the “ggalluvial” R package to assess the correlation between our risk categorization and the identified molecular subtypes. Drug sensitivities were predicted using the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for a range of therapeutic drugs via the “oncoPredict” R package and were compared with our risk score.

## Cell culture and key reagents
The prostate cell lines (human cancer lines PC3 and DU145 and the immortalized epithelial line BPH-1) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and were confirmed via STR cell sequencing and certified as being free of mycoplasma contamination. PC3, DU145, and BPH-1 cell lines were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, PM150110) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, 164210-50) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122). All cultures were maintained under standard conditions (37 ℃, 5% CO2). Small interfering RNA targeting ASNS (si-ASNS) were procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The specific target sequence (5’ to 3’) for the si-ASNS construct was CGAGTGAAGAAATATCCGTAT. Telaglenastat (CB-839, #HY-12248) and Cisplatin (#HY-17394) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA).

## In vitro functional validation of ASNS

## Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)
PC3 and DU145 cells were plated in 6-well plates to 60–70% confluence and transfected with 50 nM si-ASNS or negative control siRNA (si-NC), with an untreated group as baseline and collected for analysis after 48 h. For qPCR, RNA was extracted with TRIzol, reverse-transcribed with PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix, and analyzed using SYBR Green on a CFX Opus 96 System. ASNS expression was calculated via 2^(-∆∆Ct) using ACTB as control.

## Wound healing assay
A straight-line scratch was made in the mid-point of the monolayer with the tip of a sterile 200 µL pipette. Following the removal of loosely attached cells with PBS washing, the cells were maintained in serum-starved medium. The rate of wound closure was monitored by capturing images at the same location at 0, 12, and 24 h with an inverted microscope.

## Transwell invasion assay
Cell invasion ability was tested using Transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pores (Corning, USA). The upper chamber inserts were first coated with Matrigel (1:8 dilution). Transfected PC3 and DU145 cells (8 × 104) in 200 µL serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chambers, with 700 µL medium containing 20% FBS serving as the attractant in the lower compartments. Following 48 h of incubation, invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and photographed.

## Preparation and characterization of nanoplatform
Drug-loaded liposomes were synthesized via the thin-film hydration technique. In a typical preparation, 50 mg of lecithin, 10 mg of cholesterol, 3.5 mg of stearylamine, and 2 mg of CB-839 were co-dissolved in chloroform. This organic solution was subjected to rotary evaporation to form a uniform lipid film. The drug-loaded vesicles were then hydrated with 2 mL of an aqueous cisplatin solution and sonicated in order to form uniformly sized liposomes. The emerging formulations (CB@lipo, CDDP@lipo, and CB/CDDP@lipo) were kept at 4 ℃. Particle size and zeta potential were measured using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS).

## In vitro therapeutic efficacy assessment of nanoparticles

## Cellular uptake analysis
To track the cellular internalization of the nanoplatform, the liposomes were labeled by encapsulating the fluorescent dye Rhodamine B. PC3 cells in 12-well plates were seeded and incubated with the fluorescently labeled nanoparticles in a time-course for 0.5, 1, 4, and 8 h. The unbound particles were washed off after the incubation times in three washes in ice-cold PBS. Cell nuclei were then counterstained with Hoechst (10 µM), and cellular uptake was visualized with inverted fluorescence microscope.
Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assays

## Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity assays
For ASNS knockdown experiments, PC3 and DU145 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2 × 103 per cells/well). Following siRNA transfection, cell proliferation was monitored at 1–5 day. For drug cytotoxicity assays of nanoplatform, PC3 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 per cells/well) and incubated for 12 h to allow for cell attachment. The cells were then treated with fresh medium containing different nanopaticles with various concentrations (0, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) for 48 h. Following the respective incubation periods, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (10% v/v) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37℃. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The experiments were performed in triplicate independently. Cell viability was determined using the formula: cell viability (%) = [(OD450, sample - OD450, blank)/(OD450, control - OD450, blank)] × 100%.

## Intracellular ROS level detection
PC3 cells were treated with different compositions (4 µM CB-839 and/or 12.5 µM CDDP), and incubated with DCFH-DA probe (10 µM) in serum-free medium at 37 ℃ for 30 min. After the washing with PBS for removing excess inuptaked probe, the fluorescence images was immediately observed with an inverted microscope.

## Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay
Following undergoing different treatments, the PC3 cells were stained with JC-1 staining probe (Beyotime, China) for 25 min at 37 ℃. The mitochondrial membrane potential was checked by looking at the fluorescence change of the JC-1 probe under an inverted fluorescence microscope. A lower red-to-green fluorescence ratio means the mitochondrial membrane is depolarized.

## Apoptosis analysis
After treatment with different formulations, PC3 cells were collected and double-stained with Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and Propidium Iodide (PI) (5 µL) for 15 minuates in room temprature, with immediately analyzing by flow cytometry.

## Live/dead cell staining
After treated with different nanoparticles, PC3 cell viability was assessed by incubating with Calcein-AM (5 µM) and Propidium Iodide (PI) (2 µM) (Beyotime, China), and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

## Xenograft tumor model and in vivo treatment
Male BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old) were obtained from SPF (Beijing) biotechnology Co. Ltd., and animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval number: TCM-LAEC2024094w0929) and local legislation and institutional policies for animal welfare, in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. For the xenograft model, 5 × 106 PC3 cells were injected subcutaneously into the male BALB/c nude mice. Anesthesia was induced and maintained using a vaporized 2% isoflurane/oxygen mixture via an inhalation system during the injection procedure. Once tumors reached an average volume of 50 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned (n = 5 per group) in four groups: PBS control, CB@lipo, CDDP@lipo, and CB/CDDP@lipo (200 µL), with tail vein treatments every two days. The tumor volume, designed as 0.5 × length × width2, and body weight were measured every two days. Throughout the study, any animal whose tumor volume approached the predefined humane endpoint of 1500 mm3 was promptly euthanized to prevent exceeding this limit. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation at the end of the study, using a slow fill rate of 30% of the chamber volume per minute. Vital organs were harvested for further study after respiratory arrest to confirm death. H&E staining, Ki67 immunohistochemistry, and TUNEL apoptosis assays were performed on tumors and major organs.

## Statistical analysi
Continuous variables derived from both public datasets and experimental samples were consistently expressed as Mean ± SD. The unpaired two-sample Wilcoxon test (Mann–Whitney test) was utilized for comparing two independent groups using R software (version 4.5.1). Multi-group comparisons in experimental settings were performed using One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or repeated measures (RM) two-way ANOVA as appropriate via GraphPad Prism 10.1 software, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In both cases, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was employed as a post-hoc analysis to determine the significance of differences between specific treatment groups. For the RM two-way ANOVA applied to CCK-8 assays, the Geisser-Greenhouse correction was applied to adjust for potential violations of sphericity. All experiments were conducted in at least three independent biological replicates. Significance levels were denoted as ns P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P< 0.0001.

## Results

## Identification of the prognostic glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMGs)
The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal prostate tissues and prostate cancer samples in the TCGA-PRAD cohort was demonstrated by a volcano plot (Fig.  B). The DEGs intersected with glutamine metabolism gene collection to yield 33 potential genes termed as glutamine metabolism-related genes (GMGs) for further study as dipicted in venn diagram (Fig.  C). GO enrichment analysis showed that GMGs are involved in amino acid metabolism pathways like “aspartate and glutamate metabolism” and “arginine biosynthesis”, which are downstream routes of glutamine usage (Fig.  D). Based on GMGs, univariate Cox regression first identified 11 glutamine metabolism genes significantly associated with PFI (Fig.  E). Moreover, the correlation heatmap was generated to show the associations between these 11 significant genes (Fig.  F).

## Identification of molecular subtypes based on glutamine metabolism genes
To explore intrinsic heterogeneity in prostate cancer, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering using the above 11 prognosis-associated genes. Optimal stability analysis for k = 2 divided patients into Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 molecular subtypes (Fig.  A and Supplementary Fig. 1). The ESTIMATE method exhibited that Cluster 2 with higher Stromal, Immune, and Overall Scores, indicating reduced tumor purity (p < 0.001). This profile clearly indicated Cluster 2 as “immune-hot” and Cluster 1 as “immune-cold” (Fig.  B, C). A more extensive CIBERSORT deconvolution revealed a complicated TME in Cluster 2 with a greater number of dendritic cells and more Tregs (Fig.  D). ssGSEA showed that Cluster 2 had high infiltration of practically all immune cell types (Fig.  E). We used differential expression analysis to determine the genetic causes of subtype differences (Fig.  F). GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs showed primary enrichment in biological processes like “enzyme inhibitor activity” and “steroid metabolic process” (Fig.  G). A subsequent KEGG enrichment chord plot showed complex DEG linkages with pathways like “biosynthesis of cofactors” (Fig.  H). GSEA showed that the two subtypes’ signaling pathways were very different. Classical PCa-standard pathways like “androgen response” and “MYC targets” were enriched significantly in Cluster 2 (Fig.  I), while pathways that are individually engaged in tumor invasion, metastasis, and immune control, like “epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)”, “inflammatory response”, and “hypoxia”, were enriched significantly in the other subtype (Fig.  J). These findings collectively define two PCa subtypes with disparate biological characteristics. Additionally, PPI network analysis highlighted CD19 and ALB as hub genes within the repression network (Fig.  K).

 

## Construction and evaluation of a prognostic risk model based on glutamine metabolism genes
A refined selection of candidate genes was required in order to build a stable prognostic risk model. LASSO-Cox regression analysis was then employed to improve model robustness, with the optimal λ value was found from ten-fold cross-validation and further narrowed down the candidates to 8 crucial prognostic genes (Fig.  A, B). Stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed five glutamine metabolism genes (ASNS, ATP2B4, GLYATL1, SLC6A20, and SLC7A9) providing the best predictive signature (Fig.  C). The VIF result confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity and ensured the model stability with VIF in each gene below five (Fig.  D). The PCa samples were classified into high-risk (n = 241) and low-risk (n = 242) based on median risk score. Scatter plots illustrated a clear trend where an increasing risk score correlated with a higher incidence of disease progression events (Fig.  E, F). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a considerably reduced PFI in high-risk patients compared to low-risk patients (p < 0.001), highlighting the model’s prognostic significance (Fig.  G). The model predicting PFI at 1, 3, and 5 years had AUCs values of 0.730, 0.730, and 0.710 regarding the time-dependent ROC analysis, respectively, demonstrating good predictive efficacy (Fig.  H).

## Validation and clinical utility of the 5-gene model
The result was validated in the external MSKCC cohort, significantly adhere to the training data results. Kaplan-Meier analysis displayed that high-risk patients presented considerably lower disease-free survival (DFS) than low-risk patients (p = 0.001) (Fig.  A). This validation cohort’s time-dependent ROC analysis showed the model’s constant predictive ability, with AUCs of 0.836, 0.726, and 0.707 at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year DFS endpoints (Fig.  B). The risk score was evaluated for independence from other prognostic variables by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis. The univariate model indicated that risk score, T stage, N stage, and Gleason score were independent prognostic variables (p < 0.05), however Age and M stage were not (Fig.  C). The multivariate model incorporating pertinent parameters indicated that risk score (HR = 1.854, p < 0.001) and Gleason score (HR = 1.777, p < 0.001) were significant predictors of PFI, whereas T_stage (p = 0.055) and N_stage (p = 0.304) lost their independent predictive relevance (Fig.  D). Hence, we created a clinical nomogram that used the risk score and Gleason score to predict 1, 3, 5, and 10-year PFI probability (Fig.  E). Calibration curves showed high concordance between nomogram-predicted survival and observed survival at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig.  F). Time-dependent ROC curves showed the nomogram’s good predictive accuracy at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig.  G). The nomogram consistently surpassed the Gleason score alone in AUC values throughout time, demonstrating its higher prognostic utility (Fig.  H).

 

## Association of the prognostic model with the tumor immune microenvironment and genomic instability
To elucidate the biological mechanisms underpinning our risk model, we analyzed the TME and genomic landscapes of the distinct risk groups. Despite ESTIMATE analysis identifying no discernible variation in the overall ESTIMATE and Immune Scores, the high-risk group exhibited a conspicuous low Stromal Score (p < 0.01) (Fig.  A, B). The result points to the potential for a smaller stromal component in high-risk tumors, although purity of the tumors was not discernibly different based on the groups. Deconvolution by CIBERSORT suggested an immune phenotype in the high-risk group that was multifaceted, with a conspicuously high infiltration by both suppressive immune cells, the immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), and anti-tumor M1 macrophages, in association with a reduced proportion of resting mast cells (Fig.  C). The five model gene’s link with immune cell infiltration confirmed the model’s relationship with the immunological microenvironment, for example, the plasma cells and Tregs (Fig.  D). The ssGSEA analysis results also supported the differences in the immune landscape more broadly. The high-risk group exhibited reduced infiltration of adaptive immune cells, including activated B cells, central memory CD4/CD8 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and immature dendritic and B cells, while CD56dim natural killer cell infiltration was notably increased (Fig.  E).

 

The high-risk group at the genomic level presented with a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) and much higher incidence of TP53 mutation (17% vs. 5%), in keeping with severe genomic instability (Fig.  F and G and Supplementary Fig. 2). The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the high-risk group was characterized by the upregulation of pathways related to cell division and genomic maintenance, such as “CHROMOSOME_SEPARATION” and “HOMOLOGOUS_RECOMBINATION” (Fig.  H). The low-risk group, on the other hand, was extremely enriched in basal metabolism and redox homeostasis processes, for example, “OXIDOREDUCTASE_ACTIVITY_ACTING_ON_THE_ALDEHYDE_OR_OXO_GROUP_OF_DONORS_NAD_OR_NADP_AS_ACCEPTOR”. The low-risk PCa cancers may have a more stable metabolic and antioxidant defense system, while high-risk tumors proliferate unchecked. KEGG pathway analysis showed that the high-risk group had high enrichment of “CELL_CYCLE”, “DNA_REPLICATION”, and “MISMATCH_REPAIR” pathways, supporting their proliferative and genomically unstable characteristics. The low-risk group upregulated cell adhesion-related gene sets such “ADHERENS_JUNCTION”, indicating a more stable and less aggressive condition (Fig.  I). Collectively, these findings affirm that our risk model effectively stratifies PCa into subgroups with distinct biological features, identifying an aggressive subtype characterized by an immunosuppressive TME, heightened genomic instability, and intense proliferative activity.

## Integrative analysis reveals subtype, risk, and therapeutic implications
We first examined molecular subtype risk scores to consolidate this work’s multiple classification approaches. The median risk score of the Cluster 2 was significantly higher than that of the Cluster 1 (p < 0.001) (Fig.  A). A Sankey diagram showed that Cluster 1 patients generally had the low-risk group, while Cluster 2 patients mostly had the high-risk group (Fig.  B). Due to large immunological state disparities, we assessed immune checkpoint molecule expression and T-cell functional markers. The “immune-hot” high-risk Cluster 2 had much higher expression of PDCD1 (PD-1), PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2), and TGFB1 than Cluster 1 (Fig.  C). Importantly, Cluster 2 also showed up-regulation of multiple inhibitory receptors (LAG3, HAVCR2, and TIGIT) and key transcriptional regulators of exhaustion, such as TOX and PRDM1 (Supplementary Figure S3). This multifaceted profile suggests that the high-risk subtype is characterized by an immunologically primed but functionally exhausted microenvironment. The therapeutic relevance was then explored by analyzing the relationship between risk score and drug sensitivity. High-risk patients showed greater sensitivity to Oxaliplatin (R=−0.22), Cisplatin (R=−0.17), Lapatinib (R=−0.25), and Navitoclax (R=−0.16), but potential resistance to MEK inhibitors Trametinib (R = 0.24) and Selumetinib (R = 0.2) (Fig.  D), which exhibiting the potential to guide individualized treatment planning.

 

## ASNS promotes malignant phenotypes of prostate cancer in vitro
Among the identified genes, asparagine synthetase (ASNS) was concentrated on for its substantial prognostic weight and distinctive function as a primary glutamine-utilizing enzyme, which directly represents the central metabolic pathway of our investigation. We initially measured baseline ASNS expression in PCa to determine its biological significance. qPCR analysis showed that PC3 and DU145 PCa cell lines had significantly higher ASNS mRNA levels than immortalized nonmalignant epithelial cell line BPH-1 (Fig.  A). We used siRNA-mediated ASNS knockdown in PC3 and DU145 cells to determine its roles. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR, which demonstrated a significant reduction in ASNS expression at the transcript level in si-ASNS-transfected cells compared to controls (Fig.  B, C). Subsequent functional assays provided descriptive evidence that ASNS knockdown inhibited the malignant characteristics of PCa cells. Proliferation, as shown by the CCK-8 assay, was significantly slowed down in PC3 and DU145 cells following ASNS knockdown (Fig.  D, E). In the colony formation assay, whereas control cells formed dense and large colonies, cells transfected with si-ASNS resulted in sparse colony distribution and restricted colony size (Fig.  F-H). Also, Transwell invasion assays showed limited cell density traversing the membrane in the si-ASNS groups (Fig.  I-K). Wound healing assays displayed persistent wound gaps at the indicated time points in both PC3 and DU145 cells following ASNS knockdown (Fig.  L-N).

 

## Liposomal co-delivery of CB-839 and cisplatin elicits a synergistic anti-tumor effect
Given the lack of clinical inhibitors for ASNS and the therapeutic potential of targeting upstream glutamine metabolism, we rationally selected the GLS inhibitor CB-839, which functionally substitutes for the anti-proliferative effects of ASNS inhibition. Besides, CB-839 leads to GSH depletion, which sensitizes tumors to DNA-damaging agents like cisplatin, faciliating the development of a liposomal nanoplatform (CB/CDDP@lipo) to integrate our bioinformatics discoveries into a practical therapeutic strategy, leveraging a “metabolic-chemotherapy” synergy. Specifically, CB-839 obstructs the conversion of glutamine to glutamate, upsetting a crucial metabolic reliance, while concurrently diminishing the intracellular GSH, thereby rendering cancer cells more susceptible to the oxidative stress elicited by cisplatin. The UV-vis evaluation validated the effective encapsulation of both pharmaceuticals (Fig.  A). The encapsulation efficiencies of CB-839 and Cisplatin (CDDP) were calculated to be 74.2% and 28.6%, respectively, and their loading capacities were 2.46% and 3.78%, respectively (Supplementary Figure S4). The CB/CDDP@lipo nanoparticles with a uniform diameter of 150 nm and a positive surface charge of + 25 mV were good for cellular internalization (Fig.  B, C). Moreover, the nanoparticles demonstrated excellent colloidal stability, maintaining a consistent size over a period of 7 days in relevant biological media (Supplementary Figure S5). Rhodamine B-labeled CB/CDDP@lipo nanoparticles were successfully endocytosed by PC3 cells in subsequent uptake assays (Fig.  D, Supplementary Figure S6A). We subsequently aimed to validate the potential postulated synergistic mechanism in vitro. The CCK-8 assay indicated first evidence of synergy, showing that the CB/CDDP@lipo nanoparticles had a markedly stronger anti-proliferative effect on PC3 cells compared to each individual drug formulation (Fig.  E). Then we directly evaluated the effect on cellular redox equilibrium. The results validated that single-agent treatments with CB@lipo or CDDP@lipo resulted in a moderate reduction in intracellular GSH with combination therapy with CB/CDDP@lipo causing a significant depletion of GSH (p < 0.0001), suggesting a potential compromise of the cell’s principal antioxidant defense (Fig.  F). As a result, PC3 cells subjected to the combination therapy with CB/CDDP@lipo could not mitigate the cisplatin-induced ROS, resulting in a DCFH-DA fluorescence increase (Fig.  G, H). The CB/CDDP@lipo treatment resulted in a dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig.  I, J), a characteristic often associated with severe oxidative damage. Flow cytometry analysis quantified the apoptotic response, with the apoptotic rate in the CB/CDDP@lipo group reaching 58.07%, which was significantly higher than that in the control and CB@lipo groups (Fig.  K, Supplementary Figure S6B, C). This enhanced apoptosis was accompanied by oxidative damage and mitochondrial impairment, suggesting their potential involvement in the synergistic anti-tumor effect. Live/dead staining indicated a majority of PI-positive (dead) cells in CB/CDDP@lipo group further validating the metabolic-chemotherapy synergistic effect (Fig.  L, Supplementary Figure S6D). In conclusion, our findings substantiated that the CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform efficiently induced death in PC3 cells via a synergistic mechanism encompassing GSH depletion, ROS amplification, and mitochondrial disruption, hence affirming chemo-metabolic treatment strategy.

 

## CB/CDDP@lipo effectively inhibits tumor growth in vivo with good biosafety
After demonstrating the significant in vitro synergistic effect, we further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy in vivo and safety of CB/CDDP@lipo in a prostate cancer xenograft model. A preliminary hemolysis assay indicated that even at doses reaching 200 µg/mL, CB/CDDP@lipo did not induce significant hemolysis of erythrocytes, demonstrating favorable blood compatibility of CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform for intravenous adminstration (Fig.  A). Both CB@lipo and CDDP@lipo groups were demonstrated to suppress tumors compared to the PBS control groups, with CB/CDDP@lipo combination therapy exhibiting more effective (p < 0.01) (Fig.  B, C). After the experiment preriod, excised tumors from CB/CDDP@lipo group showed significantly reduced volume and weight compared to other groups (p < 0.001), proving the synergistic effect of CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform in vivo (Fig.  D, E). The improved efficacy was also apparent from the individual tumor growth trajectories in each therapy group as illustrated in Fig.  F. H&E examination of tumor tissues from the CB/CDDP@lipo group demonstrated extensive necrotic areas and a significant reduction in cellularity, aligning with Ki67 immunohistochemistry, which indicated the lowest proliferation index in CB/CDDP@lipo therapy group (Fig.  G, Supplementary Figure S7A). TUNEL studies corroborated these findings, revealing the most intense fluorescence signal in the CB/CDDP@lipo treated tumors, indicating of widespread apoptosis (Fig.  H, Supplementary Figure S7B). Furthermore, no extreme weight loss was detected during the entire treatment duration in any group, indicating low systemic toxicity of CB/CDDP@lipo nanoplatform (Fig.  I). This finding was also corroborated by the H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain), the outcome of which was indicative of no indication of pathological harm or inflammatory response after different treatments (Fig.  J). Collectively, these in vivo experimental results demonstrate that CB/CDDP@lipo effectively suppresses PCa tumor growth with a favorable safety profile.

 

## Discussion
The crucial, position of glutamine metabolism in the case of prostate cancer extends beyond the provision for basic proliferation, presenting pivotal in tumor advancement, therapeutic resistance, and patient prognosis [ ]. The predominant molecular responses that serve to illustrate the dependence include, for instance, PCa progression-associated loss of expression by the KGA isoform of glutaminase in favour of the androgen-independent enzymatically active GAC isoform, which directly underlies resistance to androgen deprivation therapy [ ]. Furthermore, the metabolic pathway remaines central to chemotherapy resistance in that CB-839-mediated inhibition of GLS has recently shown to reduce the proliferative potential of docetaxel-resistant PCa cells [ ], while glutamine deprivation holds the potential to sensitize the cancer cells to radiotherapy by the induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidation stress, and DNA damage [ , ]. The clinical relevance of this pathway is further underscored by its potential for biomarker development [ ]. High GLS expression in tumor, compared to benign prostatic hyperplasia, correlates with shorter overall survival, and co-expression with MYC is linked to a higher risk of biochemical recurrence after radiotherapy [ , ]. Moreover, circulating metabolites, which containing plasma glutamine and serum glutamate, have been associated with disease progression, solidifying the pathway’s prognostic value [ , ]. We sought to build a glutamine metabolism-related gene prognostic signature and integrate it into a targeted nanotherapeutic method to meet the urgent demand for more precise clinical tools for prostate cancer.
Our investigation demonstated a five glutamine metabolism gene prognostic signature consisting of ASNS, SLC7A9, SLC6A20, GLYATL1, and ATP2B4, each representing a unique aspect of the tumor’s metabolic and malignant characteristics. ASNS, identified as the central risk gene in our model, directly utilizes glutamine for the synthesis of asparagine. The elevated expression of ASNS, which is associated with unfavorable prognosis, indicates a significant reliance on this metabolic pathway [ ]. In vitro experiments demonstrated that ASNS is significantly expressed in PCa cells and strongly enhances their malignant phenotypes. This is consistent with recent research indicating that ASNS is upregulated in TP53-mutated CRPC establishing it as a crucial survival dependency that may be targeted [ ]. SLC7A9 and SLC6A20, function as solute carriers that modulate the intracellular amino acid pool [ ]. SLC7A9, a cystine transporter, is implicated as a ferroptosis inhibitor and a contributor to chemoresistance in gastric cancer, attributed to its involvement in GSH synthesis [ ]. SLC6A20 encodes the sodium-dependent imino acid transporter SIT1, which is responsible for the cellular uptake of glycine and proline [ ]. Glycine serves as a rate-limiting precursor for glutathione synthesis, supporting tumor cell survival under oxidative stress [ ], while proline can be directly channeled into the tricarboxylic acid cycle to provide energy or utilized for collagen synthesis to facilitate matrix remodeling [ ]. Pan-cancer analysis revealed that SLC6A20 expression is elevated in prostate cancer and significantly correlates with tumor grade, neutrophil infiltration, and inflammatory subtypes, suggesting its potential role in tumor immune modulation [ ]. Including SLC6A20 in our 5-gene signature highlights the intricate relationship between amino acid transport and prostate cancer progression, since this gene may act as a metabolic-immune hub that supports tumor growth and alters the immunological milieu. In contrast, GLYATL1 and ATP2B4 were identified as protective factors. Similarly, research have indicated that reduced expression of GLYATL1 correlates with poorer prognosis in kidney cancer, while its expression in prostate cancer is regulated by androgens and is elevated in low-grade tumors [ ]. The downregulation of the calcium pump ATP2B4 is associated with a loss of cell polarity and reduced recurrence-free survival in breast cancer, indicating that its high expression reflects a more differentiated and less aggressive tumor state [ ].
Our study also revealed a significant relationship between the risk signature derived from metabolism and the TME. Recent research in lung adenocarcinoma has shown that the integrated Metabolism-Related Signature effectively predict immunotherapy response, with high-risk scores linked to reduced immune activity and “cold” tumor traits [ ]. Key metabolic gene SLC25A1 have been demonstrated to correlate with CD8 + T cell exclusion patterns and resistance to PD-1 therapy. In contrast, our study observed that the “immune-hot” subtype (Cluster 2) was associated with higher risk scores and poorer prognosis, underscoring the TME’s complexity, where the presence of abundant immune cells does not guarantee an effective anti-tumor response. This is further evidenced by the significantly elevated expression of immune checkpoints and T-cell exhaustion markers, such as TOX, LAG3, and PRDM1 in Cluster 2, which suggests that these infiltrating T cells are in a functionally exhausted state. This paradoxical phenomenon, where high immune infiltration coexists with an immunosuppressive landscape, is increasingly recognized as being linked to specific metabolic vulnerabilities within the tumor microenvironment. Recent studies suggest that glutamine metabolism-associated biomarkers, SLC1A5, play a critical role in shaping such suppressive environments [ ]. High expression of these metabolic hubs correlates with increased macrophage infiltration and elevated immune checkpoint levels, ultimately leading to immunotherapy resistance and poor clinical outcomes. This underscores how tumor-driven metabolic remodeling can actively drive immune evasion despite presenting with a “hot” tumor immune infiltration profile. This phenotypic discrepancy reflects the tissue-specific impact of metabolic remodeling on immune regulation across different cancer types [ ]. The intricate interplay indicated that targeting glutamine metabolism represented a promising strategy for TME remodeling [ ]. Inhibiting glutamine uptake can bolster anti-tumor immunity [ ]. For example, inhibiting glutamine transporter ASCT2 with the small-molecule V9302 has been shown to promote autophagy, leading to decreased B7H3 expression and enhanced granzyme B production by CD8 + T cells [ ]. Additionally, combining glutamine depletion or transport inhibition with chemotherapy has been shown to synergistically activate IFN-β and promote CD8 + T cell recruitment, dependent on the identified OTUD4/LDHA signaling axis [ ]. The role of glutamine is also paradoxical. Excessive glutamine metabolism in tumors has been reported to result in toxic ammonia accumulation, leading to lysosomal and mitochondrial damage, which ultimately contributed to T-cell exhaustion [ ]. Severe deprivation of glutamine in the TME can have equally detrimental effects. Studies on hepatocellular carcinoma indicated that glutamine deficiency adversely affected cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) function, leading to a marked decrease in the secretion of effector molecules such as granzyme B and perforin (PRF1) [ ]. This observation was also noted in multiple myeloma, where diminished glutamine uptake results in T-cell immune dysfunction [ ]. Our study highlighted the necessity for a carefully calibrated therapeutic approach that targets tumor cells while preserving essential anti-tumor immune effectors.
Our study aims to translate analytical findings into actionable therapeutic strategies. Notably, prognostic models and functional experiments in prostate cancer cells have confirmed the critical role of ASNS. However, clinically viable ASNS inhibitors are currently lacking. CB-839, a GLS inhibitor, effectively mimics the therapeutic impact of ASNS inhibition by reducing intracellular glutamate and aspartate production, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation, which has been validated in clinical trials [ , ]. Therefore, we designed a co-encapsulated nanoliposome integrating CB-839 and cisplatin to construct a “metabolism-chemotherapy” synergistic nanoplatform, termed CB/CDDP@lipo. In vitro and in vivo experimental results robustly demonstrate this dual-target synergistic anti-tumor effect. Cisplatin induces DNA damage and generates substantial ROS, while concurrently delivered CB-839 inhibits glutamine metabolism and suppresses GSH synthesis, significantly enhancing intracellular oxidative stress, which led to extensive cellular damage and pronounced apoptosis, achieving a synergistic anti-tumor effect where 1 + 1>2.
Despite the promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our model demonstrated consistent performance across independent TCGA and MSKCC datasets, it remains limited by its reliance on retrospective data from public repositories. The integration of prospective clinical samples from our own institution will be essential to mitigate these database-specific biases and further refine the model’s clinical utility. Second, the reliance on an immunodeficient xenograft model prevented an evaluation of the nanomedicine’s interplay with the host immune system. Future research in immunocompetent models is thus necessary to test the platform’s potential for dual-action TME remodeling, which may reveal whether targeting GLS can directly eliminate tumor cells while also alleviating immune suppression, thereby achieving a tripartite synergy of chemo-metabolic-immunotherapy. Finally, our investigation focused on a dual-agent synergy, but preclinical evidence suggests that GLS inhibitors can also be effectively combined with other metabolic modulators like metformin or L-asparaginase. Exploring such multi-drug combinations with our nanoplatform presents an important avenue for future research.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study has successfully designed and validated a novel, five-gene prognostic signature derived from glutamine metabolism. Through a comprehensive bioinformatics to preclinical workflow, we have demonstrated that the model accurately stratified PCa patients and provided deep insights into the tumor immune landscape. Our findings indicated that the key signature gene ASNS acted as a pro-tumorigenic factor and served as a potential therapeutic target. Building on this, the engineered synergistic “metabolic-chemotherapy” nanomedicine, CB/CDDP@lipo, was confirmed to have potent anti-tumor efficacy and favorable safety profile in preclinical models. These studies contributed to understanding the molecular basis of prostate illness and established a strong foundation for improved patient risk assessment and the development of novel combination therapies.
