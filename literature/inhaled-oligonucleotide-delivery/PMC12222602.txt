## Abstract
Mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), composed of MPC1 and MPC2, plays a pivotal role in regulating cancer metabolism. While previous studies have implicated MPC1 in tumor progression, the specific function of MPC2 in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains largely unclear. In this study, we found that reduced MPC2 expression was significantly associated with advanced TNM stage and poor patient prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that MPC2 suppresses RCC cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, concurrent low expression of MPC2 and MPC1 was correlated with significantly shorter overall survival, suggesting their combined prognostic value. Gene set enrichment analysis indicated that both MPC1 and MPC2 are negatively associated with the Bmi1 signaling pathway. Mechanistically, inhibition of the MPC complex—either genetically or pharmacologically—led to increased Bmi1 protein levels by reducing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. These findings identify the MPC complex as a potential tumor suppressor and prognostic biomarker set in RCC, functioning in part through modulation of Bmi1 stability.

## Introduction
Kidney cancer ranks among the top ten cancer-related causes of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 2% of all malignancies. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most prevalent histological subtype, contributes to nearly 400,000 new cases annually [ , ]. Given its high incidence and clinical heterogeneity, there is an urgent need to identify novel and sensitive biomarkers for prognostic evaluation and therapeutic targeting.
Aberrant pyruvate metabolism has emerged as a hallmark of tumorigenesis [ ]. The mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), a heterocomplex composed of MPC1 and MPC2, mediates the transport of pyruvate from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix, thereby regulating cellular metabolic homeostasis [ , ]. As a rate-limiting component of glycolytic flux, MPC plays an essential role in tumor metabolism [ ]. Loss of MPC has been shown to promote a stem cell-like gene expression pattern in cancer cells by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [ ]. Structurally and functionally conserved, MPC1 and MPC2 are integral mitochondrial membrane proteins essential for oxidative metabolism. MPC1 has been reported to suppress tumor growth and is correlated with favorable prognosis in several malignancies, including cholangiocarcinoma [ ] and prostate cancer [ ]. Mechanistic studies further revealed that MPC1 impairs cancer cell proliferation by inhibiting the STAT3 pathway and interacting with mitochondrial STAT3 in non-small cell lung cancer [ ]. Furthermore, MPC2 overexpression in colorectal cancer cells inhibited aerobic glycolysis through activation of the mTOR pathway [ ], reinforcing the antitumor potential of the MPC complex. In our previous work, we showed that reduced MPC1 expression enhanced RCC cell invasiveness via the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) pathway [ ]. However, the specific functional role and underlying mechanisms of MPC2 have not been well illustrated in RCC.
In this study, we found that low MPC2 expression was significantly associated with advanced TNM stage and poor overall survival (OS) in RCC patients. Additionally, overexpression of MPC2 inhibited cell proliferation in 786-O RCC cells. Interestingly, patients with concurrent low expression of both MPC1 and MPC2 exhibited markedly reduced OS compared to those with higher expression. Mechanistically, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified the B cell-specific Moloney murine leukemia virus integration site-1 (Bmi1) signaling pathway as negatively enriched in both MPC1-high and MPC2-high subgroups. Subsequent experiments revealed that inhibition of the MPC complex decreased the K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of Bmi1, suggesting a novel post-translational regulatory mechanism linking MPC function to RCC progression.

## Materials and methods

## Patients and samples
A tissue microarray comprising 150 tumor tissues and 30 corresponding adjacent noncancerous tissue samples was obtained from BioChip (Shanghai, China). All patients were followed up with postoperatively. Clinical characteristics of the RCC specimens are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All enrolled patients underwent surgical resection, and the diagnosis of RCC was confirmed by postoperative pathological examination. Tumor staging was performed according to the American Joint Commission on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification system. Additionally, an independent cohort consisting of 509 tumor tissues and 100 normal kidney tissues was retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database for validation purposes.

## Gene set enrichment analysis
A total of 509 kidney cancer samples from the TCGA database were analyzed. Samples were stratified based on MPC1 or MPC2 expression levels, selecting the top 50 and bottom 50 samples for each gene. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon-Mann‒Whitney test. The C6 gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and the R package clusterProfiler were utilized to identify significantly enriched pathways. Enrichment significance was evaluated using normalized enrichment score (NES), nominal p-value, and false discovery rate (FDR) q-value.

## Colony formation assay
For colony formation assay, 1000 cells per well were seeded into 6-well plates containing 2 mL of complete DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 7 days until visible colonies formed. Colonies were then fixed and stained with 0.01% crystal violet, followed by counting under an inverted microscopy.

## Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and scoring
IHC was performed as described previously [ ]. Paraffin-embedded human tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit polyclonal anti-MPC2 (ab236584) and anti-MPC1 (ab74871; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by detection using an avidin-biotin-peroxidase system (DAKO). The extent of staining was scored as follows: 0 (0–4% positive cells), 1 (5–24%), 2 (25–49%), 3 (50–74%), and 4 (75–100%). Staining intensity was graded as 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). Scores for extent and intensity were multiplied to obtain a final staining score (range 0–12). Weak and moderate intensities were classified as low expression, while strong intensity was classified as high expression (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The optimal cut-off value for MPC2 was determined by X-tile software [ ], with a threshold score of 3 (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

## Cell culture and lentiviral infection
The human RCC cell Line 780-O was purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For MPC2 overexpression, full-length human MPC2 cDNA was cloned into a lentiviral vector and transduced into 786-O cells (1 × 106 cells/well). For gene knockdown, 786-O cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were transfected with siRNAs targeting MPC1 or MPC2 or with a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC) (Genechem, Shanghai, China) for 48 h. The siRNA sequences were as follows: MPC1-RNAi: 5’-UCCCTCTUTTUCTATTCTTTU-3’; MPC2-RNAi: 5’- AACCATTGGGACCTAGTTTAT − 3’.

## CCK-8 assay
786-O cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1000 cells per well and cultured for 24, 48, 72, 96 h and 120 h. At each time point, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## Western blotting
Western blotting was conducted as previously described [ ]. Primary antibodies included anti-MPC2 (ab236584 1:500), anti-Bmi1(MAB33341 1:500), anti-Flag (Cell Signaling Technology 1:1000), anti-His (Cell Signaling Technology 1:1000), anti-ubiquitin (Cell Signaling Technology 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech 1:1000).

## Tumor xenograft model
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Army Medical University. A total of 1 × 106 RCC-overexpressing cells or control cells suspended in 200 µL of PBS were subcutaneously injected into 5-week-old male nude mice. The maximal tumor diameter did not exceed 1.5 cm. After 30 days, mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation, and tumors were excised and weighed.

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
qPCR was conducted as previously described [ ]. Primer sequences were as follows: Bmi1 F 5’-CGTGTATTGTTCGTTACCTGGA-3’, R, 5’-TTCAGTAGTGGTCTGGTCTTGT-3’; GAPDH F, 5-AATCCCATCA CCATCTTCCA-3, R, 5’-TGGACTCCACGACGTACTCA-3; E-cdaherin: F: 5’-CCCACCACGTACAAGGGTC-3’, R: 5’-ATGCCATCGTTGTTCACTGGA-3’; Vimentin: F:5’-GGGACCTCTACGAGGAGGAG-3’, R: 5’- CGCATTGTCAACATCCTGTC − 3’.

## Cycloheximide (CHX) Chase assay
To assess protein stability, RCC cells were treated with 100 µg/ml cycloheximide (CHX) for 0, 1, 2–4 h. Protein samples were then collected at indicated time points and subjected to western blot analysis to determine protein half-life.

## In vivo ubiquitination assay
786-O cells were transfected with HA-tagged ubiquitin plasmid (HA-Ub) along with indicated vectors and pretreated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 µM for 4 h; MCE HY-13259) and/or the MPC inhibitor UK5099 (MCE PF-1005023). Whole-cell lysates were prepared using a denaturing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1% SDS, and protease inhibitor) and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, diluted with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5), sonicated, and centrifugated. Lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation and western blotting.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
Co-IP was performed as previously described [ ]. Cells were transfected with relevant plasmids for 48 h, lysed in IP buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitors, and incubated with anti-Flag M2 magnetic beads (M8823, Sigma-Aldrich), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoprecipitants were analyzed by western blotting.

## Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 software. Pearson’s χ2 test was applied to evaluate the correlation between MPC2 expression and clinicopathological features. Differences in MPC2 expression between RCC and adjacent tissues, as well as across TNM stages I, II, and III, were assessed by unpaired t-test. OS differences were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests. The relationship between MPC1 and MPC2 was assessed by linear regression. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were employed for survival analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results

## Reduced MPC2 expression is associated with advanced TNM stage and poor prognosis in RCC
To investigate MPC2 expression in RCC, IHC staining was performed on 30 RCC tissues and matched adjacent corresponding paracancerous tissues. MPC2 expression was significantly higher in paracancerous tissues than in RCC tissues (Fig.  A and B). The proportion of MPC2-high cells was also markedly lower in RCC tissues compared to adjacent tissues (p = 0.030; Supplementary Table 1). Consistent with these findings, analysis of the GEPIA database revealed reduced MPC2 mRNA expression in RCC samples, supporting our IHC results (Fig.  C). Collectively, these data suggest that MPC2 may be involved in RCC tumorigenesis.
 Next, we explored the relationship between MPC2 expression and TNM stage in a cohort of 150 RCC patients (Supplementary Table 2). We observed that MPC2 expression was significantly higher in patients with TNM stage I than in those with TNM stage II and III (Fig.  D and E). Additionally, MPC2 expression was negatively correlated with tumor size (p = 0.009) but showed no significant association with sex, age, location, lymph node metastasis or histological grade (Supplementary Table 3).
Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with low MPC2 expression had significantly poorer OS compared to those with high MPC2 expression (Fig.  F, p = 0.038). This association was further validated using data from the TCGA database, which showed a similar trend (Fig.  G, p < 0.001). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate analyses identified MPC2 as an independent prognostic factor for patients with RCC (Table  ). These findings suggest that MPC2 is closely associated with tumor progression and patient prognosis.
 

## MPC2 suppresses RCC cell growth both in vitro and in vivo
As MPC2 expression was negatively correlated with tumor size, we further explored its potential tumor-suppressive function. 786-O renal carcinoma cells were transduced with lentivirus to establish MPC2-overexpressing (OEMPC2) and control (mock) cell lines (Fig.  A). The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that MPC2 overexpression significantly inhibited cell proliferation in vitro (Fig.  B). In contrast, the knockdown of MPC2 using siRNA (siMPC2) in 786-O cells led to a significant increase in proliferation compared to negative control siRNA (siNC) cells (Fig.  C and D). To evaluate the tumor-suppressive effect of MPC2 in vivo, OEMPC2 and mock cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice to establish xenograft models. Tumors derived from OEMPC2 cells were significantly smaller in both volume and weight than those derived from mock cells (Fig.  E and F), indicating that MPC2 suppresses tumor growth in vivo.
 

## The combined expression of MPC1 and MPC2 serves as a prognostic biomarker for RCC
In humans, the two components of the MPC complex, MPC1 and MPC2, have been reported to facilitate pyruvate transport across the mitochondrial membrane [ ]. The MPC complex is closely associated with tumor metabolism and cell proliferation. To further explore the relationship between MPC1 and MPC2 in RCC, we analyzed their expression patterns in our clinical cohort. The expression levels (Fig.  A and B) and intensity (Fig.  C) of MPC1 were positively correlated with those of MPC2, consistent with findings from the TCGA database (r = 0.298, P < 0.001; Fig.  D). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further revealed that patients with concurrent low expression of MPC1 and MPC2 had significantly shorter OS than those with higher expression (Fig.  E). In vitro, decreased MPC2 expression was observed in MPC1 knockdown (siMPC1) cells than that in control cells, and MPC1 expression was also downregulated in siMPC2 cells (Fig.  F), suggesting a reciprocal regulatory relationship between MPC1 and MPC2. Together, these findings indicate that MPC1 and MPC2 are closely linked at both the expression and functional levels, and their combined expression may serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker set for RCC.
 

## MPC complex inhibits RCC progression via suppression of the Bmi1 pathway
GSEA based on the KIRC-TCGA dataset revealed that genes associated with the Bmi1 signaling pathway were negatively enriched in MPC1-high and MPC2-high tissue samples (Fig.  A and B), suggesting reduced Bmi1 pathway activity in these groups. Bmi1 is a well-established stemness biomarker and has also been reported to be associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. To determine whether MPC2 influences the growth and stemness of RCC cells, we established Bmi1- overexpressing 786-O cells (OEBmi1; Fig.  C). CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that cell proliferation and stemness were significantly increased in OEBmi1 cells compared to mock controls (Fig.  D and E). However, the expression levels of EMT markers, including E-cadherin and Vimentin, remained unchanged between OEBmi1 and mock cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results suggest that the MPC complex promotes tumor growth and stemness, potentially through regulation of the Bmi1 pathway.
 

## UK5099-mediated Mpc inhibition stabilizes bmi1 by reducing ubiquitin-mediated degradation
Increased Bmi1 expression was observed in RCC cells transfected with siMPC1 or siMPC2 compared to those transfected with siNC (Fig.  A and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Notably, treatment with UK5099, a pharmacological inhibitor of the MPC complex, led to a significant increase in Bmi1 protein levels without affecting Bmi1 mRNA levels (Fig.  B and C and Supplementary Fig. 3B), suggesting that MPC complex-mediated regulation of Bmi1 occurs at is dependent on the post-transitional level. We speculate that the inactivation of the MPC complex contributes to the stabilization of the Bmi1 protein. To further test this hypothesis, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that MPC inhibition via UK5099 significantly prolonged the half-life of Bmi1 protein in RCC cells (Fig.  D). Consistently, Co-IP assays showed that UK5099 treatment markedly decreased the ubiquitylation levels of Bmi1 (Fig.  E). These findings collectively indicate that inhibition of the MPC complex stabilizes Bmi1 by suppressing its ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation.
 

## Discussion
The MPC complex plays a crucial role in transporting pyruvate from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix, thereby regulating biological processes such as angiogenesis, immunosuppression and proliferation [ , ]. Consequently, dysregulation of either MPC1 or MPC2 impairs MPC complex function and promotes tumor progression. In our previous study, we demonstrated that reduced MPC1 expression was significantly associated with advanced tumor stage and poorer OS. In this study, we further showed that MPC2 is downregulated in RCC and that its aberrant expression inhibits RCC cell proliferation. Notably, a combined analysis of MPC expression revealed that low levels of both MPC2 and MPC1 in RCC patients were correlated with poorer prognosis compared to other molecular subtypes.
Previous studies have demonstrated that MPC suppresses cancer cell growth by inhibiting aerobic glycolysis, commonly referred to as the Warburg effect [ , ]. Inhibition of MPC activity using UK5099 increased aerobic glycolysis and upregulated hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) expression, thereby inducing resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [ ]. Loss of either MPC1 or MPC2 destabilizes the MPC complex, resulting in the depletion of both proteins. In the present study, we found that the MPC1 protein expression was positively correlated with MPC2 levels in RCC. Moreover, dysregulated MPC2 expression inhibited RCC cell proliferation, consistent with the tumor-suppressive role previously described for MPC1 in RCC [ , ]. In fact, similar findings have also been reported in colon cancer [ ], hepatocellular carcinoma [ ], and prostate cancer [ ]. Additionally, the MPC complex couples glycolysis to glucose oxidation through mitochondrial pyruvate import [ ]. Overexpression of either MPC1 or MPC2 reduces free NADH levels, resulting in decreased proliferation and mobility of melanoma cells [ ]. As discussed above, MPC1 and MPC2 appear to share a common underlying mechanism in suppressing tumor progression.
Although Bmi1 has been extensively reported to induce EMT formation in various cancers, including endometrial, lung, nasopharyngeal, and hepatocellular cancers [ – ], our findings revealed no significant changes in EMT markers (E-cadherin, Vimentin) in Bmi1-overexpressing RCC cells. This discrepancy suggests that the role of Bmi1 in regulating EMT is context-dependent and may be influenced by tumor type and cellular microenvironment. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that Bmi1-mediated EMT often requires cooperation with other factors, such as SOX4 [ ], NF-κB [ ], Akt/β-catenin [ ], or TGF-β signaling [ ], which may be inactive or less prominent in RCC. Moreover, microRNA-429 has been shown to suppress EMT and metastasis in RCC by directly targeting BMI1 [ ], suggesting that although Bmi1 has EMT-inducing potential in renal cancer, its function may be controlled by complex post-transcriptional regulation. Therefore, Bmi1 may primarily promote RCC cell progression by enhancing cell proliferation and stemness rather than driving EMT. Further investigation is warranted to explore the molecular cofactors and contextual determinants that modulate Bmi1’s EMT-inducing capacity in renal cancer. BI-44, a small-molecule inhibitor of Bmi1, has shown promising antitumor efficacy in preclinical cancer models [ ]. However, the underlying mechanisms regulating Bmi1 expression and stability remain incompletely understood. A previous study showed that epigenetic regulation of Bmi1 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation led to a reduction in BCL-2 levels in acute myeloid leukemia [ ], underscoring the critical role of Bmi1 post-translational modification in cancer biology. In the present study, we uncovered a novel regulatory mechanism of Bmi1 ubiquitination. Additionally, MPC deficiency has been shown to promote colon cancer metastasis by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [ ], which is also implicated in Bmi1 ubiquitination via βTrCP-mediated activity [ ]. Besides, cells treated with UK5099 showed a significantly higher proportion of cancer stem cells, including Bmi1+ subpopulations, which exhibited elevated AP-1 activity that drives tumor invasiveness and metastasis [ ]. These findings support the notion that Bmi1 may serve as a potential biomarker for cancer stemness and metastatic progression. Moreover, our data reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which UK5099 regulates Bmi1 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, suggesting its potential application in modulating cancer stem cell populations via targeting Bmi1.
The MPC complex plays a pivotal role in directing CD8+ T-cell differentiation towards a memory phenotype. Adoptive transfer of CAR T cells preconditioned with an MPC inhibitor led to superior and durable antitumor efficacy, suggesting that the MPC complex contributes to the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment [ ]. Here, we demonstrated that the MPC complex is associated with the activation of the Bmi1 signaling pathway, which facilitates the elimination of residual cancer stem cells following PD1 blockade and promotes antitumor immune responses [ , ]. Moreover, the observed correlation between MPC2 expression and PD-L1 levels (Supplementary Table 3) further implicates the MPC complex in the regulation of immune checkpoint pathways. Taken together, these findings suggest that the MPC complex serves not only as a novel prognostic biomarker but also as a potential immunotherapeutic target.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study is the first to demonstrate that MPC2 is significantly downregulated in RCC tissues, and its low expression is strongly associated with advanced TNM stage and poor prognosis. Functionally, MPC2 suppresses RCC cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, combined low expression of MPC1 and MPC2 predicts worse patient outcomes, supporting their potential as a prognostic biomarker pair. MPC complex inhibition stabilizes Bmi1 by suppressing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby facilitating tumor progression. These findings highlight a novel MPC–Bmi1 axis and suggest the MPC complex as a promising therapeutic target in RCC.
