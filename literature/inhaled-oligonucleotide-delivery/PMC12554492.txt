## Background
CMTM4 is involved in various cellular processes, such as cancer progression and immune regulation. However, its precise role in cervical cancer remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the impact of CMTM4 on HeLa cell behavior, tumorigenesis, myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) generation, and immunosuppressive effects in the context of cervical cancer.

## Methods
HeLa cells were genetically engineered to overexpress CMTM4 or to achieve CMTM4 knockdown. Subsequently, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to systematically evaluate cell proliferation, apoptosis, tumorigenesis, as well as the behavior of immune cells. MDSCs were isolated and treated to analyze the effects of CMTM4 on their function.

## Results
Immunohistochemical and Western blot analyses indicated a diminished expression of the CMTM4 protein in cervical cancer tissues relative to adjacent non-cancerous tissues. In vitro experiments revealed that silencing CMTM4 enhanced the viability of HeLa cells and reduced apoptosis, whereas its overexpression produced the opposite effects. Furthermore, CMTM4 overexpression mitigated the immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs. However, the introduction of exogenous MDSCs counteracted the inhibitory impact of CMTM4 on cervical cancer cell proliferation. Mechanistically, CMTM4 knockdown resulted in increased phosphorylation levels of AKT, STAT3, and ERK, while its overexpression led to decreased phosphorylation of these proteins (p-AKT, p-STAT3, and p-ERK). In vivo experiments demonstrated that CMTM4 overexpression significantly reduced tumor volume and weight, downregulated the expression of proliferation markers such as PCNA and Ki67, and inhibited the activation of the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway. Additionally, CMTM4 overexpression was associated with a reduction in MDSC infiltration within cervical tumor tissues.

## Conclusions
CMTM4 emerges as a critical regulator in cervical cancer, influencing both tumor cell behavior and the immune microenvironment.

## Key findings
• The study found that overexpressing CMTM4 in HeLa cells regulates cell proliferation and apoptosis, modulates the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway, inhibits tumorigenesis, reduces myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) infiltration in mouse tumors, and blocks the immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs. However, exogenous MDSCs can reverse CMTM4’s inhibitory effect on cervical cancer growth.

## What is known and what is new?
• It has been established that CMTM4 participates in multiple cellular processes, including cancer progression and immune regulation.
• This research specifically uncovers the role of CMTM4 in cervical cancer, detailing its effects on HeLa cell behavior, tumorigenesis, MDSC generation, and immunosuppressive functions, which was previously unclear.

## What is the implication, and what should change now?
• CMTM4 serves as a crucial regulator in cervical cancer, influencing both tumor -cell behavior and the immune microenvironment. This suggests that CMTM4 could be a potential therapeutic target for cervical cancer treatment. Additionally, more in-depth studies are required to understand the interaction between CMTM4 and MDSCs to optimize treatment strategies for cervical cancer.

## Introduction
Cervical cancer is a prevalent malignancy that poses a significant threat to women’s health and life. Its incidence continues to rise annually, necessitating urgent attention and intervention ( , ). In 2020, a global estimate of 604,000 incident cases of cervical cancer was reported, leading to approximately 342,000 fatalities ( ). Despite the improvement in effective screening for early detection of cervical cancer and continuous development and enhancement of multidisciplinary treatment, the prognosis for patients with intermediate and advanced cervical cancer remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of less than 50% ( , ). Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the occurrence and progression of cervical cancer, as well as identify efficacious tumor markers and therapeutic targets to impede or prevent recurrence while enhancing patient survival.
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) originate from immune regulation and response of the immune system during pathological conditions ( ). MDSCs are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells (IMCs), consisting of progenitors of macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells ( ). It has been reported that MDSCs promote tumor progression by suppressing the immune system ( ). In tumor-bearing conditions, differentiation and activation of IMCs are suppressed, leading to their accumulation ( ). The expansion and activation of these immature cells result in up-regulated expression of a series of immunosuppressive factors ( ). Recently, it has been demonstrated that MDSCs mediate inhibition of antitumor immune response through various mechanisms including secretion of immunosuppressive mediators ( , ). Increased numbers of MDSCs have also been observed in cervical and endometrial cancers, and their increase has been associated with reduced survival or a poor response to chemotherapy or radiotherapy ( ).
CKLF-like MARVEL transmembrane domain-containing family (CMTM) proteins play a crucial role in the occurrence, development, metastasis and invasion of various tumors ( ). CKLF-like MARVEL transmembrane domain-containing protein 4 (CMTM4) belongs to a relatively conservative member of the CMTM family, which is located on chromosome 16q22.1 and distributed in clusters with CMTM1, CMTM2 and CMTM3 ( ). CMTM4 is widely present and expressed in a variety of normal human tissues and organs, but its expression is low or absent in many primary tumor tissues ( - ). The study has shown that the abnormal expression level of CMTM4 protein in various tumors may be related to the occurrence, development and prognosis of tumors ( ). In addition, CMTM4 protein is involved in the regulation of T cell function and immune response and plays an important role in the regulation of tumor microenvironment and tumor immune escape ( ). However, the functional role CMTM4 plays and the molecular mechanism in the progression of cervical cancer have not been elucidated in detail. Here, we suggested the anticancer effect of CMTM4 in cervical cancer, both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, we for the first time, demonstrated that CMTM4 could further inhibited tumor progression in cervical cancer through the modulating the immunosuppressor activity of MDSCs. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-480/rc).

## Methods

## Clinical specimens
Twenty paired samples of paracancerous and cervical cancer tissues were obtained from patients undergoing surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Qinghai University between February 2020 and October 2022. All tumor specimens originated from surgical resections, with no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy administered before excision. Histopathological and clinical examinations verified the pathological status of each sample. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Qinghai University (P-SL--2023185), and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

## HeLa cell culture and transfection
HeLa cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (#CL-0101, Wuhan, China) and maintained in RPMI1640 medium (#PM150110, Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#C04001-500, Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The liquid was changed once every 2 days. When the cell density was 80% to 90%, digestion and passage were performed. The cells with a number of growth stages were inoculated into 96-well plates with 2×105 cells per well. The cells were transfected with 50 nM pcDNA-normal control (NC), pcDNA-CMTM4, NC siRNA, or CMTM4 siRNA for 24 h using Lipofectamine 2000 (#1901433, Invitrogen, Camarillo, CA, USA).

## Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry
HeLa cells were inoculated on 6-well plates, the cells were digested and collected by pancreatic enzymes. After washing with PBS, the cells were added with 5 µL AnnexinV-FITC and PI, respectively, and incubated for 20 min away from light. Apoptosis was detected by Cytoflex flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA).

## Cell cycle progression were detected via flow cytometry
HeLa cells were inoculated on 6-well plates, the cells were digested and collected by pancreatic enzymes. 1 mL of 75% pre-cooled ethanol was added to the cell precipitation, fixed at 4 ℃ overnight, centrifuged at 350 g for 5 min, sucked up the supernatant, washed twice with PBS, and collected the cell precipitation. The staining solution was prepared into Rnase A:PI at 1:9 volume. The staining solution was 500 µL PI/RNase A and suspended the cells at room temperature for 30 min, and then detected by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA).

## Animals
Fifty-four female C57BL/6 mice (SPF grade, aged 5–6 weeks) were obtained from Dashuo Animal Experiment Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The animals were housed at 24±1 ℃ with 55%±5% relative humidity under a 12 h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Qinghai University (P-SL--2023185), in compliance with national guidelines for the care and use of animals. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration.

## Establishment and grouping of animal models
The mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n=6) using the random number table method: the control group, the AD-CMTM4 group, the AD-CMTM4 group, and the AD-CMTM4 + MDSCs group. HeLa cells were diluted into suspension (1.0×107 cells per milliliter), and the mouse tumor model was established by subcutaneous inoculation (0.1 mL per mouse) on the armpit of the right forelimb. Twenty-four hours later, 100 µL PBS or recombinant adenovirus containing 2.0×108 PFU/mL (AD-CMTM4) or MDSCs were injected into the tail vein of HeLa tumor-bearing mice. The mice resumed normal feeding, and the inoculation sites were monitored for leakage. Body weight, food intake, behavioral status, and tumor morphology were recorded every three days. Tumor diameter was measured using vernier calipers. Twenty-four days after successful model establishment, the mice were anesthetized via inhalation with 3% isoflurane for induction and 1.5% for maintenance, followed by euthanasia through carbon dioxide inhalation. The tumor tissue was removed and kept at −80 ℃.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) stain
Tumor tissue sections (4 µm) were routinely dewaxed and hydrated with gradient ethanol. The samples were treated with antigen repair solution at 95-99 ℃ for 40 min and cooled at room temperature for 20 min. After washing 3 times, CD3 antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #GB11014, 1:100), Gr1 antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #GB11014, 1:100), CD11B antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #Ab133357, 1:200), PCNA antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #Ab133357, 1:200), or Ki67 antibody (BIOHUAAN, Jinan, China, #HA7211157, 1:200) was added and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. Incubation using an HRP marked goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #GB23303, 1:100) was performed for 30 min on the next day. Then, a working solution of horseradish peroxidase-labeled streptavidin was drip-added to the sections and incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Then the EnVision detection and color development kit was used for DAB color development, hematoxylin re-staining, gradient ethanol dehydration, and xylene transparency for observation. IHC images were evaluated microscopically (RX50, LABEX, England).

## Western blot analysis
The RIPA kit (Signaling Technologies, Inc., Danvers, MA, USA) was employed for the extraction of proteins from tumor tissues, while the protein quantification was carried out using the BCA kit (Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd., P0009). Total protein (30 µg per sample) was resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat dried milk overnight at 4 ℃, the membranes were probed with target-specific primary antibodies. Following three washes with TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween), the membranes were incubated for 1.5 h with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; cat. no. ab6721). Protein bands were detected using an ECL system (Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) with β-actin serving as the loading control. Band intensities were quantified using Quantity One software (Bio-Rad).

## Analysis of MDSCs generation
HeLa cells were transfected with 50 nM NC siRNA, CMTM4 siRNA, pcDNA-NC or pcDNA-CMTM4 for 24 h, respectively. The transfected HeLa cell supernatant was co-cultured with MDSCs cells for 24 h. Then, the cells and supernatant were co-cultured with the bone marrow cells of normal BALB/c mice by Transwell apparatus (Corning Life Sciences, Corning, NY, USA), and the proportion of MDSCs generation was detected after 72 h, and the BM cells cultured in whole medium were the control group. At the same time, GM-CSF (50 ng/mL, PeproTech GmbH, Hamburg, Germany, #315-03) was added to maintain the normal differentiation of myeloid cells.

## Detection of immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs in vitro
Mouse T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) via negative magnetic selection (#130-096-535, Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CD11b+Gr1-MDSCs (t-MDSCsWT, t-MDSCsNC, and t-MDSCsCMTM4) in different tumor tissues was separated respectively, and were co-cultured with T cells in a ratio of 3:1. CD11b+Gr1 cells isolated from the spleen of normal mice were used as a control. T cells were stimulated using magnetic beads conjugated with anti-human CD3 and CD28 antibodies (#11131D, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were harvested after 72 h. T cell proliferation was assessed using bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU). T cell apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
ELISA is performed to detect secreted flow inflammatory cytokines in T cell culture supernatants. Mouse IFN-γ ELISA Kit (#ZC-37905, ZCIBIO, Shanghai, China), Mouse TNF-β ELISA Kit (#ZC-39025, ZCIBIO, Shanghai, China), and Mouse IL-10 ELISA Kit (#ZC-37962, ZCIBIO, Shanghai, China) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Statistical analysis
The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Considering the small sample size, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data. The results of the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the data followed a normal distribution, suggesting that the variables were parametric. The data were analyzed using Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P<0.05.

## Results

## The protein expression of CMTM4 was down-regulated in cervical cancer tissues
We first investigated the expression of CMTM4 in 20 cervical cancer specimens between tumor and paracancer tissues using IHC stain and western blot analysis. As shown in , IHC results showed that paracancer tissues had a higher CMTM4 expression in comparison to cervical cancer tissues ( ). Further, CMTM4 protein expression was decreased in cervical cancer tissues compared with paracancer tissues ( ).
 

## CMTM4 regulated HeLa cell proliferation and apoptosis
As shown in , CMTM4 silencing promoted and CMTM4 overexpression inhibited the viability of HeLa cells. Meanwhile, Flow cytometer analysis showed that inhibition of CMTM4 expression decreased and CMTM4 overexpression increased apoptosis of HeLa cells ( ). Inhibition of CMTM4 expression decreased the percentage of HeLa cells in the G0/G1 phase, whereas overexpression of CMTM4 enhanced the percentage of HeLa cells in the G0/G1 phase ( ). In siRNA CMTM4-transfected HeLa cells, the expression levels of apoptosis-related protein Bax and cleaved-caspase-9 were reduced, while the expression of Bcl-2 was elevated ( ). The CMTM4 overexpression group showed the opposite results ( ).
 

## CMTM4 modulated the activity of the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway in HeLa cells
The AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and apoptosis, and its dysregulation can lead to uncontrolled proliferation and evasion of apoptosis. Based on our findings that CMTM4 significantly affected the proliferation and apoptosis of HeLa cells, we hypothesized that CMTM4 may exert its effects by modulating the activity of the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway.
The results showed that the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway is frequently dysregulated in cancer cells, leading to the development of malignant traits including uncontrolled cell proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, increased cell migration and invasion, and resistance to therapeutic interventions. Our data showed that compared with the NC siRNA group, CMTM4 silencing promoted the phosphorylation levels of AKT, STAT3, and ERK ( ). Meanwhile, compared with the pcDNA-NC group, CMTM4 overexpression inhibited the expression of p-AKT, p-STAT3, and p-ERK ( ).

## CMTM4 overexpression inhibited HeLa tumorigenesis and decreased AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway in vivo
To examine the effect of CMTM4 in vivo, recombinant adenovirus overexpressing CMTM4 (AD-CMTM4) was used to treat HeLa-bearing mice. The CMTM4 overexpressed HeLa-bearing mice displayed a significant reduction in tumor volume and tumor weight ( ). Meanwhile, the expression of a proliferation marker protein PCNA and Ki67 was decreased by CMTM4 overexpression ( ). Our study further confirmed that the overexpression of inhibited tumor growth in vivo. In addition, consistent with the in vitro results, the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway was also affected in vivo. The expression levels of p-AKT, p-STAT3, and p-ERK in tumor tissues were significantly higher than those in the skin tissues of the blank control group ( ). Overexpression of CMTM4 markedly reduced the phosphorylation levels of these signaling molecules in the tumor tissues of HeLa-bearing mice ( ).
 

## CMTM4 overexpression reduced MDSC infiltration in mouse cervical cancer tumors
In order to further investigate the effect of CMTM4 on the tumor microenvironment in mice, we mainly examined the changes of MDSC proportion in spleen, blood and tumor after CMTM4 treatment in mice. Using CD11b+Gr1+ to label MDSCs, flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant reduction in the percentage of MDSCs in the spleen, blood and tumor tissue of cervical cancer mice treated with AD-CMTM4 compared to those treated with AD-NC group ( ). Furthermore, tumor tissues were analyzed by IHC stain for the expression of CD11b and Gr1 ( ). The results suggested that the percentage of CD11b and Gr1 positive cells in tumor tissues decreased significantly compared with that in the AD-NC group ( ). These findings suggested that overexpression of CMTM4 could effectively inhibit MDSCs infiltration in vivo.
 

## Effect of CMTM4 on MDSC generation induced by cervical cancer in vitro
The flow cytometry analysis revealed that the supernatant derived from CMTM4-silenced HeLa cells significantly increased the proportion of MDSCs compared to the control group ( ). Conversely, the supernatant obtained from HeLa cells overexpressing CMTM4 markedly decreased the proportion of MDSCs produced ( ). The results indicated that CMTM4 demonstrated a notable suppressive impact on the generation of MDSCs in vitro.

## CMTM4 overexpression blocked the immunosuppressive effects of MDCSs
Next, the MDSCs isolated from tumor tissues of mice bearing cervical cancer in different groups were subsequently co-cultured with T cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the MDSCs in tumor tissues of wild-type mice (t-MDSCSWT) significantly augmented T cell apoptosis compared with the control group. Meanwhile, compared with MDSCs in tumor tissues of mice in the AD-NC (t-MDSCSNC) group, co-culture of MDSCs in tumor tissues of mice overexpressing CMTM4 (t-MDSCSCMTM4) with T cells significantly reduced the level of T cell apoptosis ( ). ELISA was used to measure the levels of pro-inflammatory factors in cell supernatants. The data showed that after t-MDSCSCMTM4 were co-cultured with T cells, the content of IFN-γ and TNF-β in the supernatant was significantly increased, while the content of IL-10 was significantly decreased compared with t-MDSCSNC group ( ). In addition, the proliferation of CD3+ T cells in spleen and tumor tissues of different tumor-bearing mice was detected by flow cytometry. The results showed that the proliferation of CD3+ T cells in tumor and spleen tissues of tumor-bearing mice in CMTM4 overexpression group was significantly increased compared with the AD-NC group ( ). The number of CD3+ T cells in tumor tissues was detected by IHC. Compared with the AD-NC group, the number of CD3+ T cells in tumor tissues of CMTM4-overexpressed mice was significantly increased ( ).
 

## Exogenous MDSCs reversed the inhibitory effect of CMTM4 on the growth of cervical cancer
Finally, we sought to explore the effects of exogenous MDSCs on CMTM4-mediated HeLa progression. We observed that the compared to the AD-NC group, mice overexpressing CMTM4 exhibited a significant increase in tumor volume over time, accompanied by a notable augmentation of tumor size ( ). However, treatment with exogenous MDSCs effectively counteracted the inhibitory impact of CMTM4 overexpression on tumor growth ( ). In addition, the co-administration of exogenous MDSCs significantly attenuated the expression levels of proliferation markers PCNA and Ki-67 in tumor tissues, as compared to the group with CMTM4 overexpression alone ( ).
 

## Discussion
CMTM4 has shown anti-tumor properties in a variety of cancers. It was reported that CMTM4 was commonly down-regulated and acted as a suppressor of tumor growth in clear cell renal cell carcinoma ( ). Elevated levels of CMTM4 mRNA were indicative of an unfavorable prognosis in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma ( ). Additionally, lung cancer patients with negative expression of CMTM4 experienced shorter survival duration compared with the patients with positive expression of CMTM4 ( ). This study showed that CMTM4 expression was reduced in cervical cancer, suggesting that CMTM4 may play an important role in the development of cervical cancer. Furthermore, we demonstrated that CMTM4 suppressed cell proliferation. The orderly progression of the cell cycle is a critical determinant of cell proliferation, with deviations in cell cycle dynamics potentially impacting cellular growth. Previous studies have implicated CMTM4 as a significant modulator of cell cycle progression and division in HeLa and SW480 cells ( , ). Our findings further support this notion, as we observed that CMTM4 exerted an inhibitory effect on the cell growth by promoting accumulation in the G2/M phase. The AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway is also involved in cell cycle regulation ( , ). Prior research has demonstrated that CMTM4 triggers cell cycle arrest by activating the AKT/STAT3/ERK pathway in colorectal cancer ( ). Our study reveals that CMTM4 suppresses the activity of the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway. Consequently, the significant inhibition of AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway activation by CMTM4 may contribute to the observed cell cycle arrest in cervical cancer cells, warranting further investigation.
These findings hold substantial therapeutic potential for the treatment of cervical cancer. Given that CMTM4 was downregulated in cervical cancer and was demonstrated to suppress cell proliferation and cell cycle progression, strategies aimed at modulating CMTM4 expression or activity warrant exploration. For instance, gene therapy approaches could be developed to upregulate CMTM4 expression in cervical cancer cells, potentially resulting in cell cycle arrest and inhibition of tumor growth. Furthermore, the investigation of small molecule drugs that can either mimic the function of CMTM4 or enhance its activity in suppressing the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway is merited. Such therapeutic interventions targeting CMTM4 modulation may offer a novel avenue for improving treatment outcomes in patients with cervical cancer.
T cells, a subset of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), play a crucial role in the immune system by identifying and eradicating pathogens, aberrant cells, and tumor cells to maintain homeostasis ( , ). Research indicates that dysregulation or aberrant levels of T cells are closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression ( ). T cells play a crucial role in immune surveillance and elimination of cervical cancer cells ( , ). Cervical cancer is often associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. The HPV employs various strategies to evade host T cell immune surveillance, thereby facilitating the persistence of infection and the development of cancer ( ). It was reported that engineered T cells targeting HPV E7 mediate regression of human cervical cancer in a murine model ( ). The study revealed that γδ T cells, a critical subset of T cells, played a significant role in anti-tumour immunity by effectively targeting and killing HPV+ and cancer-derived organoids through the regulation of BTN-family members BTN2A1/3A1 ( ). The preceding research demonstrated that CMTM4 functions to stabilize PD-L1, leading to the negative regulation of CD8+ T cells and suppression of T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity ( ). Moreover, CMTM4 positively regulates PD-L1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines ( ). In immunocompetent mice, CMTM4 suppression reduced HCC tumor growth and enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration ( ). Our results showed that CMTM4 inhibited cervical cancer growth in mice by promoting CD3+ T cell infiltration in spleen and tumor tissue.
The presence and activity of MDSCs in cervical cancer have been shown to have a detrimental impact on the immune system ( , ). MDSCs promote tumor progression by suppressing tumor-specific T cell responses, enhancing angiogenesis, and facilitating metastasis. Research demonstrated that treatment with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) reduced the accumulation of MDSCs and enhanced the presence of CD8+ T cells in BALB/C mice with U14 cervical tumors ( ). Patients with cervical cancer who has a high presence of MDSCs in the tumor microenvironment tend to have a worse prognosis ( ). Furthermore, METTL3-mediated m6A RNA modification was linked to the promotion of MDSC expansion, impacting tumor progression and prognosis in cervical cancer. This process also promoted the differentiation of CD33+ cells into MDSCs within the tumor microenvironment ( ). Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that tumor-derived granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)-induced MDSCs promoted cervical cancer stemness through prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production ( ). Furthermore, Swainsonine, an inhibitor of alpha-mannosidase, was implicated in exacerbating the progression of cervical cancer by increasing the population of MDSCs ( ). Here, our data suggested that CMTM4 blocked the immunosuppressive effect of MDCSs and promoted the proliferation of CD3+ T cells in vivo and in vitro. To further understand the role of CMTM4 in cervical cancer immune evasion, we will explore the mechanistic relationship between CMTM4 and MDSC suppression in the future. Specifically, we aim to identify the potential pathways involved, such as signaling cascades or molecular interactions that mediate the inhibitory effect of CMTM4 on MDSCs. This investigation will provide a more in - depth understanding of how CMTM4 influences the immune microenvironment in cervical cancer and may uncover novel therapeutic targets.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our study has unequivocally demonstrated that CMTM4 plays a regulatory role in HeLa cell proliferation and apoptosis by modulating the AKT/STAT3/ERK signaling pathway. The overexpression of CMTM4 has been shown to inhibit HeLa tumorigenesis and mitigate MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, highlighting its considerable therapeutic potential in the treatment of cervical cancer by affecting both tumor cell dynamics and the tumor microenvironment.
Looking ahead, several avenues for future research can be pursued. Clinically, prospective cohort studies should be conducted to assess the expression levels of CMTM4 in cervical cancer patients and their correlation with clinical prognosis. Experimentally, further in-depth investigations into the molecular mechanisms, including the identification of upstream regulators and downstream effectors of CMTM4, could provide deeper insights into its functional role. Additionally, further in vivo and in vitro experiments utilizing diverse cell lines and animal models are necessary to generalize these findings and comprehensively elucidate the role of CMTM4 in cervical cancer.
