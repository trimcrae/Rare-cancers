## Summary
Peritoneal carcinomatosis (PC) in gastric adenocarcinoma (GAC) is the most common metastatic site and leads to a short median survival. Exosomes have been shown to remodel the microenvironment, facilitating tumor metastases. However, the functional component in GAC cell-derived exosomes that remodel the landscape in the peritoneal cavity remains unclear. To address this, we performed in-depth proteomic profiling of ascites-derived exosomes from patients with PC, and we found that Galectin-3 was highly enriched in exosomes derived from malignant ascites. exosomal Galectin-3 was the crucial regulator of PC. Blockage of exosomal Galectin-3 significantly inhibited tumor metastases and prolonged overall survival. Exosomal Galectin-3 activated cancer-associated fibroblasts through integrin α1β1/FAK/Akt/mTOR/CXCL12 signaling. Combined inhibition of the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis and exosomal Galectin-3 enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, leading to significantly diminished PC progression and durable antitumor responses. These findings provide a rationale for clinical strategy of targeting exosomal Galectin-3 to treat PC.

## Introduction
Peritoneal carcinomatosis (PC; also called malignant ascites, or implants in the peritoneal cavity) from gastric adenocarcinoma (GAC) is associated with poor patient survival (≤6 months). The dismal prognosis is due to the suboptimal therapeutic landscape and inherent therapy resistance. Incorporation of immunotherapy has benefited only a small subset of patients. , Lack of novel therapeutics for PC is a major unmet need in the clinic. Therefore, deep dives into the molecular mechanisms of PC and immunology could uncover therapeutic targets.
Several reports suggest that cancer cells secrete many factors for self-sustaining proliferation and release exosomes that remodel the tumor microenvironment for successful dissemination. , , , , , , , , Exosomes promote lung, liver, and brain metastases by modulating the microenvironment, which involves the modification of endothelial cells to allow vascular leakiness and activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to create a tumor-promoting microenvironment. , Exosomes also promote PC by disrupting the mesothelial barrier, thus promoting adhesion to mesothelial cells and leading to mesothelial-mesenchymal transition in GAC. , Nevertheless, the precise molecular and mechanistic contributions of exosomes to PC in the ecosystem of the peritoneal cavity remain unclear.
Galectin-3 (Gal-3) in tumors has been implicated in cancer progression and metastasis through cell proliferation, growth, and immune responses. However, cancer cell-derived exosomal Gal-3 in the pre-metastasis stage is less understood. In the current study, using in-depth proteomic profiling of exosomes derived from PC, we identified Gal-3 as the payload in exosomes. Gal-3 contributed to the immunosuppressive microenvironment through CAF activation, which was associated with the dysfunction of T cells. Perturbation of exosomal Gal-3 and other related molecules considerably reduced PC, suggesting a potential therapeutic targeting strategy for PC in GAC.

## Results

## Patient ascites-derived exosomes promote peritoneal carcinomatosis
To confirm exosomes as a major contributor to PC, we purified exosomes from patient ascites. Exosomes isolated from ascites had the 100-nm size distribution and cup-shaped morphology that are typical for exosomes ( A and S1B). We detected commonly used exosome biomarkers, including CD9, CD63, and CD81, using flow cytometry analysis of exosomes ( A, 1B, and C). The high expression of these exosome markers was further validated by Western blot analysis ( C).
 Meanwhile, we observed that SCID mice pre-injected with ascites-derived exosomes showed significantly increased progression of PC ( D–1G and D), consistent with several previously reported observations that exosomes promote tumor metastases. , , , We observed a high abundance of fibroblasts in the peritoneal cavity. We also confirmed that αSMA expression was increased in the exosome-pretreated group ( H and E). Next, we injected exosomes into the peritoneal cavity of SCID mice for 2 weeks without cancer cells and observed that exosome treatment enhanced αSMA expression in the peritoneum ( I, 1J, and F). In addition, gene set enrichment analysis identified upregulated transcriptional pathways related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition, inflammatory responses, and hypoxia in short-term survivors ( G). Collectively, these observations showed that exosomes promoted PC in a patient-derived orthotopic model.

## The core proteome of exosomes is identified through liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry
We next analyzed protein profiles in matched sets of ascites-derived exosomes and cells from four patients with PC using LC-MS/MS proteomics ( A and A; ). In total, an average of 772 and 1480 protein features were identified, respectively, in ascites-derived exosomes and ascites-derived cells ( B; and ). We previously reported that patients with PC have extensive intertumoral heterogeneity. We next explored the ascites-derived proteomic datasets for conserved features within this known heterogeneity, revealing 146 proteins and 858 proteins that were ubiquitously identified in the proteome of ascites-derived exosomes and ascites-derived cells, respectively ( C and S2D; ). Of these 90 proteins in both ascites-derived cells and ascites-derived exosomes ( E; ). Protein-protein interaction analyses revealed a highly interconnected network of ubiquitous proteins in ascites-derived cells and exosomes ( ). Gene Ontology analyses of the 146 proteins identified in the exosome sample set indicated highest enrichment of extracellular and vesicle cellular components; vesicle-mediated transport, negative regulation of protein metabolic process, and secretion biological processes; and signaling receptor binding and extracellular matrix structural constituent molecular functions ( B, A, and S4B).
 To examine the specific exosomal markers derived from cancer cells, we systematically established a comparative strategy between the universal exosomal proteins from the proteomic dataset and the genes from the cancer cell cluster of two single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of PC in GAC. , We observed that 14 proteins were co-expressed in the exosomal proteomics dataset and the cancer cell cluster of the scRNA-seq datasets ( C). We have previously reported that Gal-3 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of GAC, and Gal-3 expression is correlated with diffuse GAC. In the current study, we found that Gal-3 was highly expressed in the cancer cell cluster compared with other molecules ( D). Furthermore, the above-mentioned 14 proteins were also co-expressed in ascites-derived cells and ascites-derived exosomes ( C). Prominent Gene Ontology categories of the above-mentioned 90 proteins co-expressed in ascites-derived cells and exosomes were related to extracellular exosomes, extracellular vesicles, cell adhesion molecule binding, signaling receptor binding, vesicle-mediated transport, and negative regulation of endopeptidase activity ( ).

## Galectin-3 is enriched in exosomes derived from patient ascites
To further validate exosomal Gal-3 expression in patient ascites, we isolated exosomes from the ascites fluid of 54 patients and determined the level of exosomal Gal-3 by Western blot analysis. We found that Gal-3 was present on the exosomes derived from patient ascites and PC cells ( E and A). Next, we validated Gal-3 expression in AGS and KP-Luc2 cells by Western blot analysis. Then we generated Gal-3 knockout (KO) cell lines in human AGS and murine KP-Luc2 cells using the Lenti-CRISPR/Cas9 system ( B). The exosomes isolated from these cells showed the size distribution and cup-shaped morphology that is typical of exosomes ( F, 2G, and C). Furthermore, we observed that Gal-3 was highly expressed in exosomes derived from AGS and KP-Luc2 normal control (NC) but not Gal-3 KO cells ( H, D, and S6E).

## Exosomal galectin-3 promotes peritoneal carcinomatosis in a patient-derived orthotopic model
To determine whether exosomal Gal-3 promotes PC, we injected exosomes into the peritoneal cavity of SCID mice for 2 weeks and then orthotopically injected patient-derived cell line GA0518 ( A). To specifically block Gal-3 from cancer cell–derived exosomes, we generated exoTD-139 ( A). We found that AGSNC cell–derived exosomes significantly promoted PC compared with AGSGal−3 KO exosomes. ExoTD-139 impeded PC caused by AGSNC exosomes ( B and 3C). Decreased deposition of αSMA and fibroblast activation protein (FAP) was observed in the AGSGal−3 KO exosome treatment group ( D). A more targeted evaluation revealed that mice pretreated with AGSGal−3 KO exosomes exhibited decreased expression of Acta2, Col1a1, Col1a2, and Fap ( E). In addition, a significant increase in CD206+ cells and Irf1, Irf5, Ccl5, Cd80, and Cd86 expression was observed in mice pretreated with AGSGal−3 KO exosomes ( D, B, and S7C). Collectively, these results suggest that exosomal Gal-3 remodeled the tumor microenvironment and promoted PC through CAF activation and M2 macrophage infiltration.
 

## Exosomal galectin-3 promotes cancer-associated fibroblast activation and CXCL12 expression
To validate the effect of exosomal Gal-3-induced CAF activation, we first isolated CAFs from three patient samples of ascites ( A). We observed that AGSNC exosomes induced robust activation of FAK and AKT signaling compared with AGSGal−3 KO exosomes ( B). We further observed that Gal-3High exosomes significantly enhanced proliferation and invasion of both human and mouse CAFs ( C and A–S8C). Similarly, we observed that KP-Luc2NC exosomes induced the activation of FAK and AKT signaling in a time-dependent manner in mouse CAFs ( D). Compared with Gal-3Low exosomes, Gal-3High exosomes from ascites significantly stimulated CAFs to robustly express ATCA2, COL1A1, COL1A2, FN, CCL2, CXCL12, IL6, and VIM, all of which were differentially expressed genes in the CAF clusters based on a scRNA-seq dataset ( D). We further confirmed that ATCA2, COL1A1, COL1A2, and FN expression levels were significantly enhanced in human and mouse CAFs treated with NC exosomes compared with Gal-3 KO exosomes ( E and S8F). CXCL12, but not CCL2 or IL6, was significantly increased in AGSNC exosome–treated CAFs. ExoTD-139 significantly reversed the effect of AGSNC exosomes on both GF0818 and GF1026 cells ( G and S8H).
 

## The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment in the peritoneal cavity
Next, we found that both exosomal Gal-3 and CXCL12 were significantly increased in patients who were cytopathological positive for PC ( E; ). CXCL12 was positively correlated with exosomal Gal-3 in patient-derived ascites ( F). Additionally, we found that CXCL12 was a poor prognosticator in the cohort with 156 patients with PC ( G). CXCL12 was increased in the CAF cluster of short-term survivors and was associated with poor prognosis in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) STAD cohort ( H and A). CXCR4, the receptor of CXCL12, was also significantly correlated with poor prognosis in the TCGA STAD cohort ( B). Next, we stratified patients with PC into three groups according to CXCL12 and CXCR4 expression using a bulk RNA-seq dataset of PC patient samples ( C–S9E). Gene set enrichment analysis showed that the PIK3-AKT-mTOR pathway was upregulated in CXCL12High patients ( F). Additionally, we observed that the percentage of lymphocytes at baseline was lower in patients with CXCL12High ( G).
We also observed that CXCR4High and CXCL12High patient samples were associated with immunosuppressive signatures in the bulk RNA-seq dataset of PC patient samples ( A and S10B). Similar to results reported previously, 
CXCL12 was highly expressed in the inflammatory CAF cluster, which was expressed only in short-term survivors ( C–S10E). Next, we observed that CXCR4 was highly expressed on CD8+ T cells from patient-derived ascites ( F). CXCL12 inhibited the migration of purified CD8+ T cells, indicating that CXCL12/CXCR4 directly inhibited the mobilization of CD8+ T cells ( G). These results suggest that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is related to the immunosuppressive microenvironment in the peritoneal cavity.

## Exosomal galectin-3 induces cancer-associated fibroblast activation through integrin α1β1/FAK/Akt/mTOR signaling
We next examined the effects of integrins on exosomal Gal–3–induced signaling, given the potential role of integrins as Gal-3 binding receptors. , , 
ITGA1, ITGA5, ITGAV, and ITGB1 were highly expressed in the CAF cluster ( A). The expression of these genes in human CAFs was further enhanced when the CAFs were treated with AGSNC exosomes. ExoTD-139 reversed the expression of ITGA1 and ITGB1, but not ITGAV and ITGA5 ( B–S11F). AGSNC exosomes induced the robust activation of FAK, AKT, and mTOR signaling in human CAFs. Integrin α1 or integrin β1 knockdown by siRNAs significantly diminished the exosomal Gal-3-induced signaling and decreased CXCL12 expression ( A–5C and G). FAK inhibition suppressed the invasion, proliferation, and exosomal Gal-3–triggered activation of FAK/Akt/mTOR signaling ( D–5F). These results suggest that exosomal Gal-3 induces CAF activation through integrin α1β1/FAK/Akt/mTOR signaling.
 

## ExoTD-139 combined with AMD3100 suppresses peritoneal carcinomatosis through increased T cell infiltration
Recent studies showed that CXCR4 and its ligand, CXCL12, modulate the immune microenvironment in pancreatic cancer models. Inhibition of the CXCR4/CXCL12 pathway enhances T cell access to the tumor microenvironment. , , Having observed CAF activation in the PC microenvironment, we aimed to investigate the effects of dual inhibition of exosomal Gal-3 (exoTD-139) and CXCR4 (AMD3100) on tumor progression using an immunocompetent mouse model. We observed that the combination treatment significantly impeded tumor progression and increased mouse survival compared with control ( A–6D and A–S12F). Exosomal Gal-3 (NC) increased the overall levels of fibrillar Col1, as examined using staining on serial tissue sections, whereas exoTD-139 (i.e., blockade of exosomal Gal-3) reversed overall levels of fibrillar Col1 in the peritoneal cavity ( C). We explored the TCGA STAD dataset using immune algorithms and found that the expression of CXCR4 was positively correlated with CAF infiltration ( A). We also observed that T cells were significantly decreased in mouse bone marrow but increased in peritoneal lavage ( E, 6F, and B). This finding is in line with a previous study showing that CXCR4 blockade promoted the mobilization of T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells from the bone marrow and lymph nodes into the periphery. We observed that the immunosuppressive marker PD-1, but not CTLA4, was decreased with the combination treatment compared with the control ( C and S13D). AGSGal−3 KO exosomes significantly enhanced CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and Granzyme B+ CD8+ T cells accompanied by increased IFNG and IL2 expression ( ). Collectively, these results suggest that treatment with the combination of exoTD-139 and AMD3100 suppresses the progression of PC by increasing the mobilization and cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells.
 

## Combination of exoTD-139, AMD3100, and αPD-1 induces durable antitumor responses
The Tuveson group reported that AMD3100 induces rapid T cell accumulation among cancer cells and acts synergistically with α-PD-L1 to greatly diminish pancreatic cancer cells. Because we observed CAF activation and PD-1+CD8+ T cell upregulation, we sought to explore the antitumor efficacy of exoTD-139, AMD3100, and αPD-1 combination treatment ( A). KP-Luc2 cells expanded rapidly in the peritoneal cavity of C57BL/6 mice pre-incubated with KP-Luc2NC exosomes, whereas treatment with exoTD-139/AMD3100 or αPD-1 only partially attenuated tumor growth. However, combination treatment led to cancer regression and durable antitumor responses ( B and A–S15D). Combination treatment elicited the expression of T cell genes and Granzyme B, IFN-γ, and Perforin cytokines, confirming robust T cell activation ( C–7E).
 Taken together, these results show that exosomal Gal-3 promotes CAF activation and decreases the cytotoxic function of T cells through an immunosuppressive microenvironment ( F).

## Discussion
A common metastatic site of GAC is the peritoneal cavity. PC affects ∼45% of patients with GAC , who often have a short survival duration of ≤6 months. , Therefore, new therapeutic targets coupled with ablative technologies are widely anticipated to lessen or eliminate the onset of PC and prolong PC patient survival, but new therapeutic targets must first be identified. In the current study, we explored the role of exosome cargo in cancer progression and the function of exosomes in organotrophic metastases in GAC.
Previous reports have shown that tumor cells remodulate the microenvironment of a target organ that would support successful metastatic colonization. , , , , , , , , , Among tumor-secreted factors, tumor-derived exosomes are crucial players in cell-to-cell communication, and exosomes reshape distant microenvironments, driving organ-specific metastases. , Exosomes engage in multiple systemic pathophysiologic processes, such as reprogramming stromal recipient cells and regulating and suppressing anticancer immunity, to establish supportive interactions with organ-specific niches and subsequent metastases. Several reports indicate that exosomes play this role in lung, liver, and brain metastases, consolidating the principles of exosome-mediated microenvironment modulation. , , , , , , Several groups have reported that exosomes promote PC by destroying the mesothelial barrier and initiating tumor invasion into the peritoneal cavity in both GAC and ovarian cancer. , Others have reported that GAC-derived exosomes promote cancer cell adhesion to mesothelial cells or induce mesothelial-mesenchymal transition. , Although previous studies have shown that extracellular Gal-3 promotes tumor invasiveness and Gal-3, expressed in cancer cells, inhibits cancer metastasis in melanoma. However, it is important to note that extracellular Gal-3 and cancer cells Gal-3 differs from exosomal Gal-3. , While several studies have shown the presence of Gal-3 in exosomes from bladder and colon cancer, , the specific function of exosomal Gal-3 has not been thoroughly explored. Additionally, the role of exosomal Gal-3 may vary across different tumor types, highlighting the need for a tumor-specific investigation of its functional role. In the current study, we used multi-omics analyses combined with patient-derived orthotopic and syngeneic mouse models to reveal that exosomal Gal-3 induced CAF activation to form a fibroblast niche, which further suppressed T cell function in the peritoneal cavity.
Our research echoes similar findings observed in other contexts, particularly in the realm of gastric cancer. Building upon the strides made in pancreatic cancer research regarding the efficacy of CXCL12/CXCR4 and anti-PD-1 therapies, our investigation sought to determine whether analogous strategies hold promise for the treatment of GAC. Our study pioneered the isolation and characterization of exosomes extracted from the ascites of patients afflicted with GAC. Employing cutting-edge mass spectrometry techniques, we identified Gal-3 as a prominent component of these ascites-derived exosomes. Noteworthy parallels emerge with previous studies that have highlighted the role of Gal-3 in orchestrating cellular processes, including myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix modulation, albeit in non-neoplastic conditions.
Central to our findings is the conspicuous overexpression of Gal-3 on both cancer cells and cancer-derived exosomes within the GAC milieu. Functionally, exosomal Gal-3 emerges as a key protagonist in driving GAC progression by fostering CAF activation, instigating niche formation, and dampening antitumor immune responses within the peritoneal cavity. This discovery not only provides valuable insights into the dynamic remodeling of the peritoneal microenvironment but also unveils potential therapeutic targets with implications for GAC management. At the mechanistic level, our investigations unveil a signaling cascade wherein exosomal Gal-3–induced CAF activation proceeds through the integrin α1β1/FAK/Akt/mTOR/CXCL12 axis. Notably, the consequential dysregulation of the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway exacerbates the immunosuppressive milieu within the peritoneal cavity by impeding T cell mobilization and cytotoxic functions. These findings underscore the intricate interplay between exosomal cargo, stromal components, and immune effectors, thereby delineating promising avenues for therapeutic intervention in GAC akin to those observed in PC. A limitation of our current study is the lack of investigation into the origin of CAFs and the role of exosomal Gal-3 in mesothelial to mesenchymal transition which has been demonstrated as a resource of CAF in previous studies. , , This is an important aspect that warrants further exploration in our future studies.
In summary, our results showed that exosomal Gal-3 remodels the microenvironment, promoting the progression of PC. CAF activation triggers an immunosuppressive microenvironment through CXCL12 secretion. Additionally, simultaneous inhibition of exosomal Gal-3, CXCR4, and PD-1 produces the most striking PC suppression, which has implications for developing rational therapeutic strategies.

## CAF isolation
Patient ascites cells (IP-039-1 for GF1026; IP-024-1 for GF818) were pelleted and washed twice with PBS and then resuspended in 80 μL of MACS buffer 1:20 diluted autoMACS rinsing solution (Miltenyi Biotech, Gaithersburg, MD; Cat# 130-091-222) with PBS per 107 total cells. Then, 20 μL of human anti-fibroblast microbeads (Miltenyi, order no. 130-050-601) per 107 cells was mixed with the cells and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by washing once with MACS buffer. The cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of MACS buffer and applied to the separation column and magnetic separator. After washing with MACS buffer, the cells from microbeads were flushed out from the microbeads and cultured with Dulbecco modified Eagle medium. Cells were passed twice, followed by immortalization with lentiviral infection that expresses hTERT (pHIV7-CNPO-Tert) and SV40 large-T (pHIV7-CNPO-Tag; a gift from Dr. Jiing-Kuan Yee, City of Hope, CA). The immortalized CAFs were used for downstream experiments. Patients’ information was shown in . For the CAFs isolated from Trp53Loxp/Loxp; CDH11Loxp/Loxp; Tff1-CreERT2 (kindly provided by R Johnson’s lab), fresh tumor tissue was minced with sterilized lancets, digested with collagenase IV (17104019, Gibco, 4 mg/mL) and dispase II (17105041, Gibco, 4 mg/mL)/RPMI at 37°C for 0.5 hour, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, and resuspended in FASC buffer (PBS/2% FBS ) as a single-cell suspension. The subsequent single-cell suspension was stained with CD140a (PDGFRα)-PE (135905; BioLegend) for fibroblast sorting. Samples were filtered through a 40 μm mesh and then sorted with an Aria II sorter (BD Biosciences) at the North Campus Flow Cytometry Core Laboratory of MDACC.

## Ex vivo assays with human T cells
Patient ascites lymphocytes were isolated from Buffy Coats using Ficoll PAQUE (GE Healthcare) and were cryopreserved until further use. After thawing, patient ascites lymphocytes were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 50μM β-mercapoethanol for 48 hours. T cells were treated with 1 μg/mL anti-CD3e (BioLegend) and 1 μg/mL anti-CD28 (eBioscience) for 24 hours, followed by exosome treatment for another 48 hours. Cells were incubated with the human antibodies CD45 (BioLegend, Cat# 982316), CD3 (BD, Cat# 557832), CD8 (BD Bioscience, Cat# 557945), and Granzyme B (BD, Cat# 563389). Data were acquired using Gallios 561 (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo Ver 10.

## Patient-derived orthotopic model
Mouse work was performed in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and AAALAS guidelines (MD Anderson animal protocol 00000232-RN03), and the study was compliant with all relevant ethical regulations regarding animal research. 10 μg exosomes were intraperitoneally injected into the 8-12 weeks female SCID mice peritoneal cavity 3 times per week for 2 weeks. For the orthotopic injection, SCID mice were first anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation and then laid in a supine position. A 6- to 10-mm incision was made in the midline of the mouse abdomen, and the stomach was exteriorized with forceps. Then, 1 × 105 GA0518 cells with mCherry-Luciferase in 30 μL of matrigel were injected into the subserosal layer of the stomach with a 27-gauge needle. The stomach was returned into the abdominal cavity and the skin was closed with wound clips. Tumor growth and PC were monitored every week through bioluminescence imaging of surgery mice.

## In vivo syngeneic mouse model
All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the approved protocol (00001488-RN02) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. For experimental exosome education, exosomes (10 μg) were intraperitoneally injected 3 times per week for 2 weeks. 8-12 weeks female C57BL/6 mice were the intraperitoneally injected with 1 × 106 KP-Luc2 cells. One week after tumor cell injection, mice were randomly grouped to receive treatment with PBS, AMD3100 (30 mg/mL) inserted with ALZET osmotic pumps (1007D or 2002, Charles River) subcutaneously, or αPD-1 intraperitoneally injected with 300 μg anti-PD-1, three times per week for 3 weeks. The tumor luminescent density was monitored every week. At the endpoint of the experiment, the mice were euthanized. Tumor tissue was subjected to single-cell isolation and flow cytometry analysis and total RNA extraction for qRT-PCR.

## Method details

## Isolation of extracellular vesicles by density gradient flotation
Microvesicles were depleted from ascite samples by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 20 minutes, followed by 16,500 × g for 30 minutes; resulting supernatant was filtered through a pre-wetted 0.22-μm vacuum filter. Microvesicle-depleted biospecimens were densified by mixing with OptiPrep iodixanol solution (Sigma D1556) to a final density of 1.16-1.30 g/mL and loaded into the bottom of a polycarbonate ultracentrifuge tube and overlaid with 0.5- to 2-mL aliquots of iodixanol/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution in the 1.20-1.01 g/mL (35-0% wt/vol) range, proceeding from the highest to lowest density to form a single- or multi-step density fractionation gradient as needed. Ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 × g for 4 hours at 8°C. Vesicles were collected from the top of the tube, proceeding downward to recover volume equal to 90% of overlaid gradient volume. Density of harvested fractions was assessed against a standard curve based on sample absorbance at 250 nm using a NanoDrop microvolume spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE). Vesicle harvests were stored at -80°C.

## Proteomic profiling of extracellular vesicles
ECV-derived protein digestion and identification by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was performed using established protocols. The nanoAcquity UPLC system coupled in-line with a WATERS SYNAPT G2-Si mass spectrometer was used for the separation of pooled digested protein fractions. The system was equipped with a Waters Symmetry C18 nanoAcquity trap-column (180 μm × 20 mm, 5 μm) and a Waters HSS-T3 C18 nanoAcquity analytical column (75 μm × 150 mm, 1.8 μm). The column oven temperature was set at 50°C, and the temperature of the tray compartment in the auto-sampler was set at 6°C. LC-HDMSE data were acquired in resolution mode with SYNAPT G2-Si using Waters Masslynx (version 4.1, SCN 851). The capillary voltage was set to 2.80 kV, sampling cone voltage to 30 V, source offset to 30 V, and source temperature to 100°C. Mobility used high-purity N2 as the drift gas in the IMS TriWave cell. The pressure was 4.50 mbar in the helium cell, 2.47 × 10−2 mbar in the Trap cell, 2.90 mbar in the IMS TriWave cell, and 2.53 × 10−3 mbar in the Transfer cell. IMS wave velocity was 600 m/s, helium cell DC 50 V, Trap DC bias 45 V, IMS TriWave DC bias 1 V, and IMS wave delay 1000 μs. The mass spectrometer was operated in V-mode with a typical resolving power of at least 20,000. All analyses were performed using positive mode ESI using a NanoLockSpray source. The lock mass channel was sampled every 60 seconds. The mass spectrometer was calibrated with a [Glu1] fibrinopeptide solution (300 fmol/μL) delivered through the reference sprayer of the NanoLockSpray source. Accurate mass LC-HDMSE data were collected in an alternating low-energy (MS) and high-energy (MSE) mode of acquisition with mass scan ranging from 50 m/z to 1800 m/z. The spectral acquisition time in each mode was 1.0 seconds with a 0.1-second inter-scan delay. In low-energy HDMS mode, data were collected at a constant collision energy of 2 eV in both the Trap cell and the Transfer cell. In high-energy HDMSE mode, the collision energy was ramped from 25 eV to 55 eV in the Transfer cell only. The RF applied to the quadrupole mass analyzer was adjusted such that ions from 300 m/z to 2000 m/z were efficiently transmitted, ensuring that any ions observed in the LC-HDMSE data <300 m/z arose from dissociations in the Transfer collision cell. The acquired LC-HDMSE data were processed and searched against a protein knowledge database (Uniprot) through the ProteinLynx Global Server (PLGS, Waters Company) with a 4% false discovery rate.

## Exosome production from GAC cell line
The cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 48 hours at a confluency of 70-80% and washed twice with PBS. The conditioned medium was collected and followed by differential centrifugation at 800 × g for 5 minutes and 2,000 × g for 10 minutes, and then filtered using a 0.2-μm filter. Exosome isolation was performed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 3 hours at 4°C. After ultracentrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS.

## Nanoparticle tracking analysis
The size distribution of exosomes was determined based on the Brownian motion using a NanoSight LM10 (Malvern), which is equipped with a high-sensitivity sCMOS camera and Blue488 laser. The temperature was set at 25°C during measurement of exosomes. The syringe pump speed was set to 20. A 90-second delay followed by three captures of 30 seconds each was employed for each acquisition.

## Transmission electron microscopy of exosomes
Exosomes were fixed in 2.5% EM grade glutaraldehyde. Formvar-carbon coated mesh nickel grids were treated with poly-l-lysine solution for 5 minutes, and excess was removed by the filter paper. The grids were allowed to dry. Drops of suspended exosomes were deposited on the formvar-carbon coated mesh nickel grids. Exosomes were adsorbed for 1 hour at room temperature. Grids were rinsed with PBS 5 times for 3 minutes each and incubated in filtered 1% uranyl acetate in distilled water for 1 minute. The excess was removed by filter paper, and grids were allowed to dry at room temperature. Grids were examined in a JEM 1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, USA, Inc, Peabody, MA) at 80 kV. Digital images were captured using the AMT Imaging System (Advanced Microscopy Techniques Corp, Danvers, MA).

## Flow cytometry-based analysis of bead-bound exosomes
A total of 5 × 109 exosomes were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS and mixed with 10 μL of aldehyde/sulfate beads (Invitrogen). Then, the samples were incubated at room temperature with rotation for 15 minutes. Subsequently, exosomes were added with 400 μL of PBS and rotated overnight at 4°C. Samples were added to 200 μL of 1M glycine and rotated at room temperature for 1 hour on the following day. The bead-bound exosomes were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the samples were resuspended in 100 μL of 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for blocking and rotated at room temperature for 1 hour. After blocking, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes, supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was resuspended in 50 μL of 2% BSA containing unconjugated primary antibodies CD9 (BD, Cat# 558749), CD63 (BD, Cat# 564221), and CD81 (BD, Cat# 559519). The samples were rotated at room temperature for 1 hour. Bead-bound exosomes were washed twice in 200 μL of 2% BSA, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and bead-bound exosomes were rotated for 1 hour at room temperature with 20 μL of 2% BSA containing secondary antibody. Then the bead-bound exosomes were washed three times with 200 μL of 2% BSA, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes. Data were acquired using the Gallios 561(Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo Ver 10.

## Generation of exoTD-139
For in vivo experiments, approximately 109 total exosomes (measured by NanoSight analysis) were incubated with 300 μg TD-139 (Selleck Chem (Cat# S0471)) with rotation, at room temperature, for 24 h. Samples were then washed two times with PBS with a 30 kDa Amicon Ultra filter (EMD Millipore) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Notably, freshly prepared exosomes were used for every single assay reported in this manuscript, in both in vivo and in vitro experiments.

## Protein extraction and Western blot analysis
Ascites cells and human/murine cell lines were treated with lysis buffer, prepared in reducing SDS Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-world), and denatured at 95°C for 20 minutes. The samples were then subjected to electrophoresis using Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies Gal-3 antibody TIB166 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA), CD9 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 13174), CD63 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 52090), CD81 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 99507), TSG101 (Abcam, Cat# ab83), pFAK (Abcam, Cat# ab39967), FAK (Cell Signaling, Cat# 3285), pAkt (Cell Signaling, Cat# 4060), Akt (Cell Signaling, Cat# 4691), Integrinα1 (Abcam, Cat# ab78479), Integrinβ1 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-8978), pS6 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 5364), S6 (Cell Signaling, Cat# 2317), and actin (Cell Signaling, Cat# 4970) were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by secondary antibody incubation and washing, and then target proteins on the membranes were detected by chemiluminescence.

## Quantitative reverse-transcriptase PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from cells using Direct-zol RNA Kit (Zymo Research, Cat# R2051), processed for cDNA synthesis using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4368814), and subjected to qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4367659). Expression levels of the indicated genes were normalized to the expression of Gapdh as a housekeeping gene. The qRT-PCR primers are listed in . For the TaqMan_qPCR array, total RNA was extracted from the cells of mouse peritoneal lavage in KP-Luc2NC exosomes and NC exoTD-139, AMD3100, and αPD-1 treatments. qPCR array (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4367786) was performed on biological replicates according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Generation of Gal-3 KO GAC cell lines using Lenti-Crispr/Cas9
The CRISPR/Cas9 system was used to knock out Gal-3 in AGS and KP-Luc2 cell lines. Guide RNA design followed the principles listed on the Massachusetts Institute of Technology website (http://crispr.mit.edu) or German Cancer Research Center’s E-Crisp website (http://www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/desighcrispr.html). Guide RNAs for Gal-3 were inserted into V2mO (pLentiCrispr.V2. mOrange). Guide RNA sequences for Gal-3 KO are listed in . Briefly, 50 μL of each primer at 100μM were added together, and each pair of guide RNA sequences was formed into duplexes in a heating block heated to 100°C and cooled down to room temperature on its own. The duplexes were then used as inserts to ligate using T4 DNA ligase (NEB, Ipswich, MA) into V2mO (pLentiCrispr.V2mOrange) cut by BsmbI (NEB, Ipswich, MA). The ligates were transformed into Stabl3 competent cells, and resultant clones were screened by the size of inserts and verified by sequencing. Plasmids with the right inserts were then co-transfected with pCMV.Dr8.2 and pCMV-VSV.G at a ratio of 10:10:1 into HEK293T cells with 70% confluency using JetPrime (Polyplus, France). Supernatants were harvested 24 hours later, and consecutively two more times, 48 hours and 72 hours later. Lentiviral supernatants were centrifuged, and clear upper solution was filtered and used for immediate transduction or kept in a -80°C freezer for later use. Target AGS and KP-Luc2 GAC cells were seeded in a 6-well plate with 70% confluency, lentiviral supernatants were added with 8μM polybrene, and transduced cells were selected by puromycin at a concentration determined by killing curves for 3-6 days. Surviving cells were then subjected to single-cell cloning by FACS sorting or by dilution into 96-well plates. Positive clones were screened and verified by Western blot. Gal-3 cDNA in frame with 3xFlag marker was generated. Gal-3 cDNA generated by Q5 PCR with primers hGal3.XbaBamH.R with hGal3.g12.dg3.F3 was used to generate the p3xFlag-Gal3.dg3 plasmid. Clones were subjected to digestion and sequencing to verify that three nonconsecutive nucleotides were altered in the cDNAs of the gRNA1 binding site of the three plasmids.

## Immune cell population analysis from bone marrow and PC by flow cytometry
For detection of immune cell response in vivo, fresh mouse ascites cells were filtered by 70-mm cell strainers, washed, and resuspended. Cells were stained in the dark on ice for 30 minutes with 100 μL antibody cocktail per sample with FACS buffer (PBS/2% FBS) containing 20% Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, Cat# 566349) and 50 mg/mL anti-mouse CD16/CD32 FC block (BD Biosciences, Cat# 553142). Cells were washed twice with FACS buffer, then fixed and permeabilized with Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience, Cat# 00-5523-00). Cells were washed twice with 1× permeabilization buffer (eBioscience, Cat# 00-8333-56) and stained on ice for 30 minutes with intracellular antibody cocktail in 1× permeabilization buffer. Cells were washed twice with 1× permeabilization buffer and washed with FACS buffer. Cells were incubated with the mouse antibodies CD45 (BioLegend, Cat# 103107), CD3 (BioLegend, Cat# 100329), CD8 (BioLegend, Cat# 100730), PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat# 135205), CTLA4 (BioLegend, Cat# 106309), Granzyme B (BioLegend, Cat# 372217), Peforin (BioLegend, Cat# 154315), IFNγ (BioLegend, Cat# 505825), F4/80 (BioLegend, Cat# 123118), and CD206 (BioLegend, Cat# 141717). Data were acquired using Gallios 561 (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo Ver 10.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
For detection of CXCL12 secretion, patient ascites samples were measured according to the kit manufacturer’s instructions (R&D, Cat# DY350). A total of 100 μL per well of horseradish peroxidase–conjugated streptavidin (BD Biosciences) diluted in PBS containing 0.1% BSA was then added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Plates were developed with tetramethylbenzidine (Pierce) and stopped with 0.5N H2SO4. The plates were read at 450 nm with a BioTek plate reader.

## Immunofluorescence
Human ascites samples, cell lines, and mouse tissue sections were blocked for 1 hour with 4% cold water fish gelatin (Aurion) without permeabilization in room temperature and stained overnight at 4°C with Gal-3 antibody TIB166 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA), αSMA (Abcam, Cat# 5694), FAP (Invitrogen, Cat# PA5-99313), CD206 (Abcam, Cat# Ab64693), CD8 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat #98941S), PD-1 (Abcam, Cat# 214421), and Granzyme B (Abcam, Cat# ab255598) antibody followed by secondary antibodies. Slides were then mounted with DAPI-containing Vectashield Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, Cat# H-1200-10) and visualized under the Nicon T2 confocal laser scanning microscope.

## Invasion and proliferation
Briefly, 1 × 104 GF0818 or GF1026 cells in 50 μL of culture medium containing 0.5% FBS were plated on the upper well of a transwell insert (Corning) with or without 10 μg exosomes. Then, 600 μL of culture medium with 20% FBS was gently added to the lower well, followed by incubation of the cells at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 48 hours. The culture media and cells inside of the insert were removed with cotton-tipped applicators, and the migrated cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.2% crystal violet. The migrated cells were counted under an invert microscope. For the CAF cell proliferation assay, 2 × 103 GF0818 or GF1026 cells in 200 μL of culture medium containing 10% FBS were plated on a 96-well plate (Corning) with or without 10 μg exosomes. Incucyte was used to detect cell proliferation for the indicated timepoints.

## Chemotaxis assays
For CXCL12 cross-inhibition of chemotaxis, recombinant human CXCL12 (R&D Systems, 350-NS) was loaded in the upper wells of a Boyden chamber plate (VWR), and the lower chambers were flowed in 240 μL of chemotaxis buffer with 1,000 ng/mL CXCL10 (R&D Systems, 266-IP). The Boyden chamber plates were incubated at 37°C for 3 hours. The number of cells in the lower chamber was calculated with a Guava bench-top flow cytometer.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analyses of flow cytometry and immunostaining quantifications were performed with one-way analysis of variance with the Tukey multiple comparison test, unpaired two-tailed t test, or Fisher exact test using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Expression levels of indicated genes among TCGA STAD cohort samples were based on the RNA Seq V2 RSEM data. TCGA data were downloaded from cBioPortal. Kaplan-Meier plots were drawn for survival analysis and the log-rank Mantel-Cox test was used to evaluate statistical differences. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Error bars represented standard error of the mean when multiple visual fields were averaged to produce a single value for each animal, which was then averaged again to represent the mean bar for the group in each graph.
